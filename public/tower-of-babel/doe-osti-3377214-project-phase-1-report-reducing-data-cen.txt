Executive Summary
This report describes a U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Geothermal-funded project
to investigate how data center cooling loads can be managed by Cold Underground Thermal
Energy Storage (Cold UTES). The project was led by the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR) in collaboration with Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), the University of
Chicago, Princeton University, and industrial advisors. Several modeling methodologies were
used to encompass a range of modeling techniques and assumptions. The intent of this work is to
describe the methods and results of the analyses and provide a broad consensus on the value and
opportunity for Cold UTES for data center cooling.

Key Findings
Cold Underground Thermal Energy Storage (Cold UTES) is an ultra-long-duration grid energy
storage technology. With Cold UTES, low-cost grid power is converted to cold thermal energy
and stored in subsurface systems, including boreholes and reservoirs, at the point of use. Cold
UTES is one approach within the general category of engineered geothermal systems.

The basis of the study is a data center with 1 GWe electric demand for computing and auxiliary
loads. This is modeled as 1 GWth of thermal load that is rejected to the environment with a
cooling system that does not consume water. The electric power required for cooling the data
center varies as the air temperature changes. In cold weather only dry coolers are used, with an
electrical load for cooling as low as 10 MWe. In hot summer hours, chillers and dry coolers are
required, which raises the electrical load for cooling to as much as 300 MWe.

Cold UTES for peak-hour cooling of data centers is studied for deployment in Maricopa County
in Arizona and Loudoun County in Virgina using thermal storage capacities from 4 GWh-th to
over 1,000 GWh-th. The two sites have different power and transmission systems, available grid
energy resources, daily and seasonal load profiles, weather conditions, and grid regulatory
requirements.

Some of the key findings are:

1. Cold UTES Can Reduce Peak Electrical Cooling Load
Large peak electrical loads have been identified as a barrier to getting new grid interconnection
agreements for data centers in some locations. This modeling work shows Cold UTES reducing
electrical peak load for cooling by ~200 MWe for a 1-GWe data center in Virginia.

2. Cold UTES Can Increase Computing Capacity
Reducing peak loads means that more computing capacity (IT + auxiliary loads) may be installed
for a given grid interconnection limit, which may provide significant economic value to the data
center operator. Two independent methods estimated that Cold UTES facilitates an increase in
computing capacity of 9%–20%.

3. Cold UTES Can Reduce Annual Energy Costs
Electricity purchases are one of the largest ongoing annual expenses for data centers. Cold UTES
shifts electricity use for cooling from peak to off-peak hours when power is much less expensive.

 v

In Virginia, the annual electricity cost for cooling is reduced by 70%, roughly ~$20 million per
year.

4. Cold UTES Can Reduce Cooling System Costs
In some scenarios, Cold UTES can remove the need for mechanical chillers at the data center.
Eliminating the large investment in chiller equipment could reduce the cooling system cost
despite the investment in Cold UTES.

5. Cold UTES Can Reduce Power System Costs for Grids
Grid infrastructure and fuel cost savings are evaluated using specialized grid modeling software
for energy storage discharge durations ranging from 4 hours to 4,000 hours of full load data
center cooling capacity. The change in grid infrastructure resulting from the use of Cold UTES is
identified and the value is found to be between about $90 million and $390 million.

Why This Study Is Needed
The escalating energy demands of data centers present challenges to the grid, energy
infrastructure, and local communities. Particularly contributing to this demand are hyperscale
(i.e., very large scale) data centers driven by advancements in artificial intelligence (AI). The
biggest grid challenge is accommodating new loads, particular peak load, for a given grid (Chen
et al. 2025). In many scenarios, grids cannot accommodate new data centers without expensive
and time-consuming grid infrastructure upgrades such as capacity expansion and transmission

 vi

expansion. This forces data center developers to identify alternative ways to reduce/eliminate
capacity requirement to a grid, such as on-site power generation, which also presents challenges
related to supply chain delays, permitting issues, and associated environmental and social
impacts.

The other major reason this work is needed is to address data center water consumption. Most
conventional cooling systems that reject heat from data centers consume both water and
electricity. Efficiently designed evaporative systems in low-humidity environments are widely
deployed and consume small quantities of electrical energy—e.g., <10% of the IT capacity
(Shehabi et al. 2024). However, these systems consume large quantities of water, which is a
pressing issue in many regions (Lei et al. 2025; Damante 2026; Good and Boehmer 2026; Gorey
2025; Tempest 2026; Williamson 2026). Alternatively, dry-cooled heat rejection systems avoid
water consumption at the expense of an increased electrical load for cooling: we find this can be
as much as 30%–40% of the IT load for a large data center with direct-to-chip liquid cooling.
These peak loads coincide with the hottest, most expensive hours of the year, driving up costs
and grid stress.

Thus, cooling system energy and water consumption are critical deployment barriers for data
centers. The Cold UTES technology investigated in this report presents an opportunity to address
these key factors.

Introducing Cold Underground Thermal Energy Storage
Cold Underground Thermal Energy Storage (Cold UTES) uses the subsurface to provide long-
duration energy storage with the potential to provide flexible, scalable, low-cost, and non-water-
consumptive cooling to data centers, or similar industries with high cooling energy needs. As
shown in Figure ES-2, Cold UTES utilizes the ground to store cold thermal energy when excess
energy is available from the grid or ambient temperature is low, typically in the winter or off-
peak nighttime periods, and discharge the cooling flow for data centers when needed, mostly
during the summer and peak hours.

Cold UTES uses either borehole thermal energy storage (BTES), which uses an array of
boreholes that have been drilled a few hundred feet deep, or reservoir thermal energy storage
(RTES), which uses underground reservoirs that are several thousand feet deep, as illustrated in
Figure ES-3. The marginal cost of energy capacity is very low (close to zero) for UTES, which
facilitates energy storage capacities that are suitable for seasonal dispatch, but diurnal and
weekly dispatch are also possible.

Project Objective and Approach
The project objective is to quantify the potential benefits of Cold UTES to data center
owners/operators and grid stakeholders. A comprehensive analysis required the development of
several independent models that provide different perspectives on cost and value:

Facility-level models: Two models are used to assess the costs and benefits to data center owners
and operators.
 • NLR developed a techno-economic analysis (TEA) model to simulate the annual
 performance of a data center cooling system (with and without Cold UTES) as a function
 of time over the full range of weather conditions to evaluate energy usage, peak loads,
 and IT capacity expansion. Economic models assess the cost of the cooling system,
 electricity consumption, and levelized cost of cooling over the lifetime.

 • The University of Chicago developed DCGen (Gnibga and Chien 2026a), a data center
 model-driven tool. DCGen generates detailed AI training data center designs for present,
 and future (2027, 2029). These models include full IT (computing, networking, storage),
 power (data center and rack-level), and cooling systems (conventional—coolant
 distribution units, chillers, dry coolers), for 10 MWe to 1 GWe scale.

Grid-level models: Capacity expansion tools are used to assess the costs and benefits to the
electrical grid.
 • Due to the complexity of grid system models, two models: ReEDS™ by NLR and GenX
 by Princeton University are adopted and validated against each other. After the
 validation, ReEDS is then used to evaluate the impact of deploying 1 GWe of data center
 computing load (IT power plus auxiliary loads). The model is executed with and without

 viii

 Cold UTES, and several discharge durations are considered to determine the impact on
 power system cost and generation capacity.

The models are applied to case studies to evaluate Cold UTES at data centers in Virginia and
Arizona. The large concentration of data centers in Virginia required modeling the PJM region,
which is an independent system operator (ISO) region encompassing a large area from the mid-
Atlantic seaboard region though Ohio and into part of Illinois. Phoenix also has a large
concentration of data centers, many of which are in the Salt River Project (SRP) utility area.

Sensitivity analyses are conducted with each model to assess the influence of key variables, such
as discharge duration, subsurface properties, assumed costs, and data center design variables.

Summary of Results
Throughout the analysis in this report, we model a baseline data center with a 1 GWe compute
capacity (IT load plus ancillary load) that rejects heat with a dry-cooling system, as illustrated in
Figure ES-4. In this summary, the technical cooling system (TCS) supply temperature is 30°C in
Virginia and 40°C in Arizona.

During peak hours, UTES is used to absorb the rejected heat from facility, which otherwise would be directly released
 into the ambient. In non-peak hours the heat is pulled from the ground and released to atmosphere via the dry

Peak Electrical Cooling Load Reduction
We use the NLR TEA model to assess the hourly power consumption of the data center. As
shown in Figure ES-5, the partial power usage effectiveness (PUE) (the ratio of the IT load plus
electrical cooling load to IT load) varies as a function of the hour of the year and reaches its peak
in summer. When Cold UTES is not included, the peak partial PUE reaches 1.22 and 1.17 in
Virginia and Arizona, respectively, but the average partial PUE is relatively low compared to
reported PUE ranges for the broader data center fleet (Shehabi et al. 2024), 1.036 for both cases.
It is worth noting that the average PUE is the typically reported metric, whereas the peak PUE
determines installed capacities and strongly influences operational costs. The lower partial PUE
value in Arizona is due to a higher cooling fluid temperature used in the data center.

Increased Capacity for IT Load
A data center is typically subject to a ceiling capacity for its total load, which generally includes
IT load, electrical load for cooling, and other ancillary load (such as lights and power distribution
loss). When Cold UTES is used to reduce load for a day, the reduced cooling capacity may be
used to increase the IT capacity. UChicago’s DCGen tool estimates the IT load may be increased
about 10% for each location, as shown in Figure ES-9. NLR’s TEA model uses a different
approach and assumptions but obtains consistent results, with potential IT load increases of 9-
20% depending on design.

Annual Energy Cost Saving
Our analysis using the NLR TEA model finds that the annual energy cost is significantly reduced
when peak-hour electricity consumption is reduced and shifted to off-peak time periods. This
cost reduction occurs because electricity prices are the highest in peak time periods. Figure ES-7
compares annual cooling electricity cost for the baseline data center with and without Cold
UTES. When a Cold UTES system is adopted, the annual cooling energy cost is reduced from
~$30 million/year to ~$10 million/year for Virginia and from ~$22 million to ~$9 million for
Arizona. The reduced energy consumption during peak hours contributes to the majority of
energy cost savings. The reduction of use of peak power (mostly from simple cycle combustion
turbines) enables companies that track annual estimated off-site emissions to calculate the
reduction in grid marginal emissions.

Cooling System Cost Saving
Data centers are designed to operate reliably and without interruption, with some data centers
aiming to operate 99.9% of the year. This requires the cooling system to be designed to meet
peak loads in the summer, which are often only a few hours. For the remainder of the year, the
cooling capacity is underutilized. By reducing the peak electrical load for cooling during these
peak hours, Cold UTES can decrease the overall cooling infrastructure size and associated
capital expenditures.

The NLR TEA model characterizes the cooling system lifetime cost with the levelized cost of
cooling (LCOC). Our analysis in Figure ES-8 indicates that using Cold UTES can reduce the
data center cooling system LCOC by 71% and 66% for Virginia and Arizona, respectively.
Savings are due to the elimination of mechanical chillers (which are replaced by UTES) and a
reduction in electricity costs by avoiding peak hours.

Increased Data Center Building Lifetime
Data center shells (building, cooling, and power infrastructure) historically had operational lives
of 10 years (Barroso et al. 2019). Our study shows that the rapid increase of power density due to
AI accelerators requires rapid expansion of cooling infrastructure; this so-called “gray space”
rapidly grows to dominate the data center interior and exterior space in as little as two years,
crowding out the IT hardware. Cold UTES reduces the growth of cooling gray space, enabling
the data center shell lifetime to extend data center lifetime by 1–2 generations (2–6 years) under
moderate annual IT power growth of 12%.

Power System Cost Saving for Grids
NLR’s ReEDS capacity expansion model is used to assess whether Cold UTES can benefit the
electrical grid. In Figure ES-10, the net present value (NPV) of the power system cost savings is
given as a function of Cold UTES storage duration for Virginia and Arizona. In both cases, the
adoption of Cold UTES leads to net savings of power system cost compared to a scenario with
the same total data center capacity additions without Cold UTES. The net saving peaks at
~1,000 hours of Cold UTES discharge duration for Virginia and 2,000 hours for Arizona. This
demonstrates that storage built at a seasonal timescales maximizes value.

  Figure ES-10. Breakdown of the change in net present value power system costs through 2050
 from varying durations of Cold UTES (RTES) for Virginia and Arizona. White circles indicate the
 total net change in costs.
We also project the impact of Cold UTES storage duration on installed generation by technology
category in 2050, along with projected load increase. For a given projected capacity portfolio in
2050, reconfiguring the baseline data center cooling system to include Cold UTES is projected to
affect the overall grid expansion portfolio, as shown in Figure ES-11, with notable reductions in
the capacity of solar, batteries, and gas combustion.

The difference between the two scenarios is a result of the different weather conditions, annual

Despite the many factors at play, results demonstrate that Cold UTES can have a substantial

Sensitivity Study
The sensitivity of Cold UTES performance to key design parameters is explored. Initial findings
include:

 • Cold UTES has the greatest impact on peak loads and costs if the UTES is only charged
 with dry coolers because dry coolers have low power consumption. Charging the UTES
 with mechanical chillers does not reduce peak loads as significantly.

 • The peak electrical cooling load reduction is more pronounced in locations with colder
 and/or longer winters. The colder weather in Virginia enables a greater percentage of the
 total cold energy storage to occur with dry cooling compared to Arizona, which relies on
 chiller cooling.

 • There are limited hours to charge the UTES with dry coolers in hot environments, such as
 Arizona, which may necessitate the use of chillers for charging. This may diminish the
 benefits of Cold UTES with respect to peak cooling reduction and associated system
 benefits, but Figure ES-10 shows that when considering grid benefits, the NPV is higher
 in Arizona than Virginia.

 • The TCS supply temperature has a strong influence on the value of Cold UTES. Data
 centers with low TCS supply temperatures have large peak loads due to frequent use of
 mechanical chillers, and Cold UTES can significantly reduce costs and peak loads if

 xv

 enough cold energy can be stored. Data centers with high TCS supply temperatures rarely
 use mechanical chillers, which reduces the opportunity for Cold UTES to reduce
 expenditure on electricity for cooling.

 • Reservoir permeability and well flow rate influence RTES pumping power, number of
 wells, and cost, and require optimization to maximize Cold UTES benefits when
 designing for a specific location.

Current State of Cold UTES Technology and Adoption Readiness
DOE applies Technology Readiness Level (TRL) and Adoption Readiness Level (ARL)
frameworks to systematically evaluate the maturity and commercialization potential of emerging
energy technologies.

Cold UTES leverages mature geothermal storage concepts—specifically BTES and RTES. The
core components (drilling, heat exchangers, pumps, and control systems) are all commercially
available (TRL10) and have been demonstrated at scale in analogous applications.

For data center cooling applications in the 25- to 100-MW range, Cold UTES can be credibly
assigned a TRL of 8, which reflects the existence of full-scale, operational systems with
analogous technical requirements, even if not yet deployed at hyperscale or in every climate. For
larger systems (100–500 MW+), the TRL is lower due to scaling risks that have not yet been
fully validated.

While the technical maturity of Cold UTES is relatively high, its adoption readiness is lower, due
to challenges with realizing the value proposition, market acceptance, resource maturity, supply
chain, permitting, and social acceptance. Based on these factors, Cold UTES currently stands at
ARL 5–6.

DOE Office of Geothermal, in partnership with the project team, is evaluating key next steps to
advance TRL and ARLs through a commercial-scale demonstration to resolve technical
uncertainties, provide operational data, validate business models, engage stakeholders, and build
investor confidence.

1 Data Center Cooling and Introduction of Cold
  Underground Thermal Energy Storage (Cold UTES)
Data centers are emerging as a significant and rapidly growing load within the global power
system. Over the past decade, data center electricity consumption has increased substantially,
with a marked acceleration in recent years and a wide projected range through 2028 reflecting
uncertainty in workload growth, technology deployment, and efficiency improvements (Shehabi
et al. 2024). Historically, electricity consumption remained relatively stable at the global scale
during 2010–2018, as improvements in hardware efficiency and system-level optimization were
largely offset by increasing demand (Masanet et al. 2020). Since then, however, electricity
consumption has increased rapidly, with industry estimates indicating that U.S. data center
electricity consumption nearly doubled between 2020 and 2023 (EPRI 2026), highlighting the
accelerating scale of data center electricity demand. Growth is occurring not only in average
electricity consumption but also in peak demand, driven in part by cooling loads associated with
increasing adoption of dry-cooling technologies in response to community concerns about water
consumption and operator water-reduction goals. Figure 1 illustrates the rapid escalation in
projected data center-related load growth within the PJM region, with forecasts shifting from
essentially flat in 2021 to more than 40% peak load growth over a 15-year horizon by 2025.
While conventional servers continue to represent the dominant share of total load, artificial
intelligence (AI)-specialized servers exhibit a rapidly increasing share and are projected to
become an increasingly important driver of future demand growth (Figure 2). In parallel,
infrastructure-related energy use, including cooling, power delivery, and auxiliary systems,
remains a persistent and non-negligible fraction of total facility load (Shehabi et al. 2024).

A key underlying driver of these trends is the rapid evolution of compute hardware, particularly
AI-accelerated workloads. Graphics processing unit (GPU) and central processing unit (CPU)
power levels increase over time, with GPUs exhibiting a substantially faster growth trajectory.
This escalation in power density directly increases heat generation and imposes more stringent
thermal management requirements. Cooling system design is thus increasingly shifting toward
liquid-based thermal management approaches capable of supporting higher heat fluxes and
higher operating temperature regimes, as conventional air-cooling becomes progressively
constrained at emerging compute densities. These trends reflect a broader transition in data
center cooling strategies toward warm-water and other high-efficiency liquid cooling
architectures.

These evolving thermal requirements are directly reflected in facility-level cooling performance
and operational efficiency. Figure 3 shows hourly partial power usage effectiveness (PUE)
values ranging approximately from 1.0 to 1.2, with clear increases occurring when ambient dry-
bulb temperature exceeds ~20°C. Under these conditions, data centers transition from
economizer or free-cooling operation to mechanically assisted cooling strategies, including
chiller-based systems, resulting in higher auxiliary energy consumption and elevated PUE. This
behavior demonstrates that cooling system performance is strongly influenced by environmental
conditions, with ambient temperature governing cooling mode transitions and driving temporal
variability in overall facility energy efficiency.

1.1 Data Center Cooling: Trends
Data center cooling systems are organized as a hierarchical thermal management chain spanning
facility-, rack-, and chip-level subsystem. Facility-level systems typically include air- or liquid-
cooled chillers and associated heat rejection equipment, which define the overall thermal
capacity of the data center and strongly influence site-level energy efficiency (e.g., Figure 3). At
the rack level, coolant distribution units (CDUs) and facility water system (FWS) loops regulate
heat transport between facility infrastructure and IT equipment. At the chip level, direct-to-chip
cooling or air-based heat sinks provide localized heat removal at the processor interface. Figure 4
illustrates representative cooling configurations, including air-cooled chillers, FWS loops,
CDUs, and technical cooling systems (TCS), highlighting the progression from conventional air-
based cooling toward hybrid and liquid-cooled architectures. The coupling between these layers
determines the overall effectiveness of heat removal and the achievable operating temperature
range of IT equipment.

The selection of cooling architecture and facility scale has a direct impact on overall data center
efficiency, commonly quantified using PUE. Figure 5 presents distributions of annual average
facility PUE values across small, midsize, and large-scale facilities under different cooling
technology configurations based on Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory modeling results
(Shehabi et al. 2024). Unlike hourly PUE (e.g., Figure 3), which can vary with weather
conditions and operational loading, these annualized values reflect integrated facility
performance across operational scenarios and climate conditions. The plots show a clear
stratification of PUE across cooling technologies. Liquid cooling systems exhibit the lowest
mean PUE values (approximately 1.14–1.18), followed by hybrid configurations (approximately
1.32–1.43), and water-cooled chillers (approximately 1.65). Air-cooled chiller and direct
expansion (DX) systems show the highest PUE values, approaching approximately 1.95–1.99.
These differences highlight the strong dependence of facility-level efficiency on cooling
technology selection. In addition, larger data centers tend to exhibit lower and more stable PUE
values compared to smaller facilities, reflecting scale-related improvements in infrastructure
efficiency and operational stability.

1.2 Data Center Cooling: Challenges
While these hierarchical design choices and technology selections significantly influence data
center efficiency, they also expose fundamental constraints that limit further performance
improvements. As cooling systems scale to meet growing thermal demands, trade-offs between
competing resource requirements become increasingly pronounced. In particular, one of the most
critical challenges in modern data center cooling is the balance between energy consumption and
water use, and mitigation strategy of peak load.

 4

1.2.1 Dry Cooling vs. Wet Cooling
Figure 6 quantifies the cooling-related electrical load derived from the PUE modeling framework
for a representative 1-GW IT load, where electrical load for cooling is estimated as 0.75 of the
infrastructure loads derived from PUE. The framework in (Shehabi et al. 2024) incorporates
climate-dependent simulations across geographically distributed U.S. weather conditions and
includes auxiliary infrastructure loads such as uninterruptible power supply (UPS), power
distribution, fans, pumps, and cooling systems within the PUE calculation. Dry-cooling systems
exhibit higher electrical power requirements, ranging from approximately 0.07 to 1.9 GW,
whereas wet-cooling systems achieve lower energy consumption but at the expense of
substantially increased water use (see water usage effectiveness [WUE] in Figure 6(b)). This
reflects a fundamental resource trade-off in which reductions in cooling energy demand are
achieved through evaporative processes that increase water consumption, with potential
implications for operational cost and resource management. In terms of water use, dry-cooling
systems exhibit near-zero consumption under most operating conditions, with only minor non-
zero contributions arising from auxiliary facility-level water systems, while wet-cooling systems
show significantly higher water use, ranging up to approximately 4.1 with a mean of 2.24, driven
by evaporative heat rejection processes. Overall, cooling-related energy and water use are
influenced by both system design and ambient climate conditions, which modulate the
effectiveness of evaporative cooling processes and contribute to variability across operating
environments.

Water consumption has emerged as a major constraint for data center expansion in many regions,

water resources (Damante 2026; Gorey 2025; Williamson 2026; Tempest 2026; Good and

configurations with minimal site water use and evaluates how Cold UTES can enhance system-

1.2.3 Peak Load
Data center cooling demand contributes directly to peak electricity load, amplifying system
demand during periods of high ambient temperature. As outdoor temperatures rise, cooling
system efficiency declines and mechanically driven cooling becomes more dominant, increasing
total facility power consumption. This temperature-driven demand is strongly coincident with
system peak conditions, placing additional stress on the electric grid, particularly in regions with
high cooling requirements. Figure 8 illustrates how electricity price behavior varies across
Phoenix, Texas, and Virginia based on a combination of modeled and real-time regional datasets.
Texas exhibits pronounced summer price spikes, reaching several thousand dollars per MWh,
while Phoenix shows similarly strong seasonal increases, with peak values reaching
$6,800/MWh during periods of sustained high temperatures. In contrast, Virginia exhibits
comparatively stable price levels throughout the year, generally within a narrower range. The
timing of these elevated price periods is consistent with seasonal peak demand conditions, which
are typically associated with higher ambient temperatures and increased cooling requirements.
These patterns suggest that peak cooling demand can play a significant role in shaping electricity
price variability, particularly in regions with strong seasonal temperature extremes. Elevated

 6

demand during high-temperature periods can contribute to increased system loading, price
volatility, and higher marginal energy costs. Data center operators may thus face substantially
increased operating expenses during peak periods. Under extreme conditions, grid constraints
and elevated electricity prices may also necessitate demand response actions, including the
reduction or deferral of IT workloads, linking thermal management to grid interaction, energy
cost, and computational availability.

1.3 Emerging Cooling Storage Technology—Cold UTES
As a direct response to the data center cooling challenges, we propose an emerging cooling
storage technology called Cold Underground Thermal Energy Storage (Cold UTES). This
technology utilizes the subsurface ground or reservoir as a storage media for cooling energy for
data centers. One example schematic is given in Figure 9. Cold UTES may be used to store the
cold energy in the winter and dispatch it to data center cooling in the summer. Water is used in
the data center closed loop between the rack room and Cold UTES system, so there is no water
consumption when paired with dry-cooling technology. The operation of Cold UTES can be
flexible in the way that it can be charged during any off-peak hours when needed, and, if
designed well, discharge cooling power to data center whenever needed, mostly in summer. By
doing so, Cold UTES is capable of reshaping the electrical load for cooling profile illustrated by
the bottom plot of Figure 9: It dramatically lowers the summer peak load.

More technical details on Cold UTES can be found in Section 2. In this report, we will carry out
techno-economic analysis (TEA) to quantify the benefits of Cold UTES for data center
operators/owners, and grid impact analysis of Cold UTES when deployed at scale in a given
region.

Importance of Data Center Cooling Temperature
The water temperature in the data center TCS loop, FWS loop, and HRL directly affects energy
efficiency, equipment reliability, and operational costs. The different constituencies (data center
operators, IT equipment owner/operators, IT equipment vendors) have different incentives for
choosing water temperatures. In data centers with direct-to-chip liquid cooling, modern servers
can function reliably across a wide range of operating points, such as S20 (meaning a 20°C
supply water to IT racks), S30, S35, and recently S45 (meaning 45°C supply water to IT racks).
The higher IT rack water inlet temperatures have two advantages: (1) higher rack supply
temperatures reduce chiller energy consumption, and (2) higher rack inlet temperatures produces
higher water exit temperatures, increasing the number of hours when the data center’s heat can
be removed through only dry coolers fans, a practice known as free cooling. For example,
hyperscale operators now adopt S30 and S35 (supply water to IT racks) to balance efficiency and
reliability, while firms like NVIDIA are advocating S45 (supply water to IT racks) for high-
density workloads (ASHRAE 2024; Bhargava et al. 2026). These trends frame an ongoing
exploration of multiple operating points rather than a single ideal temperature. On the other hand,
the Open Compute project (Mills et al. 2024) is standardizing facility supply temperatures,
critical to support a wide range of co-location tenants with different needs. A data center co-
location operator might wish to meet this standard of 30°C facility water to be acceptable to as
many tenants as possible. However, this facility supply temperature dictates a lower facility

 11

return temperature, reducing the ability to utilize free cooling, and thereby increasing cooling
costs.

In Section 2, we study data centers cooled with 20°–45℃ facility supply temperature range. In
Section 3, we study S45 equipment rack supply. We consider supply and return temperatures of
45℃ and 56℃, respectively, in the TCS, and supply and return temperatures of 41℃ and 53℃
respectively in the FWS loop.

2.1 UTES Background
UTES is described by the U.S. Geological Survey as a subset of a larger suite of geologic energy
storage technologies that are capable of storing excess energy for later use to meet seasonal
demand or provide baseload power when renewable energy sources are variable (Buursink et al.
2023). UTES systems broadly comprise three subsystems: a source of heating or cooling, the
geological storage formation, and a heat exchanger or generator to deliver heating, cooling, or
electricity to the end user (Witter et al. 2026). When storing hot thermal energy, energy can be
sourced from solar thermal, nuclear, or biomass plants, as well as waste heat from industrial
operations. Storage of cold thermal energy relies on mechanical or free cooling from chillers or
coolers to chill liquid before it is stored in the subsurface.

UTES storage media range from deep sedimentary formations to shallow aquifers to buried tanks
or boreholes and can broadly be grouped into open loop and closed loop technologies. Open loop
technologies store fluid within the pore space of the subsurface, while closed loop technologies
store the heat transfer fluid in sealed containers like boreholes, pits, or tanks. Open loop
technologies take advantage of vast subsurface formations, allowing for large-scale energy
storage with less drilling, piping, or storage infrastructure than closed loop technologies.
However, open interaction between the water and subsurface can present operational and
environmental risks such as mineral scaling, energy losses to groundwater flow, and potable
reservoir contamination. Closed loop technologies tend to avoid these risks, often presenting
long lifespans with low maintenance, but require higher upfront costs to drill, pipe, or build the
enclosed storage medium.

UTES technologies have a long history of development and use, particularly in Europe. Aquifer
thermal energy storage (ATES) has seen widespread adoption, with over 3,000 systems in
operation globally, the majority located in the Netherlands (Atkinson et al. 2023). Almost all
operating ATES systems operate at low temperatures (< 40°C), providing direct-use heating and

 13

cooling. Pit thermal energy storage (PTES) and tank thermal energy storage (TTES) have been
used for district heating and energy storage around the world and are often paired with
concentrating solar thermal (CST) plants. Borehole thermal energy storage (BTES) systems have
been deployed throughout Europe and Canada for heating and cooling applications. RTES,
sometimes called geological thermal energy storage (GeoTES), has been researched extensively
(Akindipe et al. 2024; Atkinson et al. 2023; Dobson et al. 2023; McTigue et al. 2023; Sharan et
al. 2021; Witter et al. 2025) but is less technologically mature. This report focuses on RTES and
BTES and their ability to provide cooling for data centers. Figure 12 depicts ATES, BTES, and
RTES technologies accessing their respective subsurface formations.

2.1.1 Description of Reservoir Thermal Energy Storage
RTES systems are open loop and are characterized by laterally extensive, porous, and permeable
strata, saturated with brackish or saline (non-potable) water. These formations include shallow to
deep brackish aquifers, sedimentary formations, or depleted oil and gas reservoirs. An RTES
system is an open loop system that flows water or brine as the heat transfer fluid through the
formation (Winick et al. 2025). Figure 13 shows the primary components of a simple RTES
system. Wells connect the subsurface storage formation to a heat exchanger and power block,
while heating or cooling can be provided from several energy sources.

The geophysical, chemical, and hydrological characteristics of the chosen storage reservoir
directly impact the safety, longevity, and efficiency of RTES systems. Key characteristics
include depth, porosity, permeability, salinity, thermal conductivity, and reservoir thickness.

 14

Storage reservoirs should also possess a relatively impermeable caprock and baserock for
insulation and to minimize fluid migration and heat loss. Recommended depths can vary based
on regional differences and the intended application. The reservoir should be deep enough that
there is a caprock present, and so there is minimal impact on potable water sources or protected
areas. In hot storage projects, greater depths and the increased temperatures correlated with them
may be preferable, with depths from 400–1,000 m being most common. However, greater depth
must be balanced against the increased drilling costs and parasitic energy losses to pumping
required. For cold storage, shallower depths (and thus lower drilling and pumping costs) along
with cooler subsurface temperatures are preferable. For an RTES cooling system, the limiting
lower temperature bound is defined by the freezing point of water, below which the RTES
functionally becomes unworkable as there is no longer a practical means to physically move heat
through the system (Winick et al. 2025). At any depth, geological stability, limited groundwater
flow (<25 m/yr), and the absence of faults are important to prevent thermal and fluid losses.
Higher porosity (>20%) and moderate permeability (100-500 mD) are also important for
balancing efficient heat transfer while avoiding heat loss from free convection. Brackish aquifers
are often preferred to reduce competition and restrictions around potable water, with target
salinity above 1,000 mg Cl-/l, although it is important to consider that the risk of corrosion in
piping increases with higher salinity.

2.1.2 Description of Borehole Thermal Energy Storage
BTES systems are closed looped configurations that typically target shallow depths, up to 300 m.
BTES systems typically consist of many closely spaced vertical boreholes drilled into the
subsurface. These shallow subsurface formations may or may not have the same permeability
and porosity needed to function as a volumetrically significant RTES system, and thus these
closed looped systems rely instead on conductive heat transfer with the near-wellbore rock
volume (Winick et al. 2025). To enhance heat exchange, boreholes are often backfilled with
thermally conductive grout.

Various borehole piping types and field configurations can be constructed. A common piping
scheme is the single U-tube boreholes, which are a single pipe inserted into the borehole with a
180-degree turn at the bottom. These are the industry standard, and are easy to handle, fast to
install, and require minimal piping material. Double U-tube boreholes are harder to install and
use more material but can provide better heat transfer and lower pressure drop, making them
more efficient. Coaxial boreholes consist of two concentric pipes, where fluid flows down one
and up the other, and generally provide the highest heat transfer and lowest pressure loss but can
be more expensive and complex to install. Boreholes can be arranged into any number of field
configurations based on the available surface area and storage needs. Common configurations
include square grids, linear series, concentric rings, and hexagonal grids. The design and spacing
of boreholes is critical for performance. Figure 14 depicts a BTES system providing seasonal
storage for heating and cooling to a commercial building. This example system uses single U-
tube boreholes in a hexagonal configuration.

 Figure 14. Example configuration of a U-tube BTES system operating for seasonal storage.
 Figure from (Mitali et al. 2022).

As a closed-looped technology targeting shallow formations, BTES can be deployed almost
anywhere. However, there are some key considerations for siting a feasible system. The thermal
conductivity of the subsurface greatly influences the outcome of the system, and the rock

 16

hardness can impact the drilling process. Further key considerations for BTES siting include
minimal groundwater flow (<25 m/yr) to limit thermal losses and sufficient surface area to
accommodate the drilling of large borehole fields.

The design point of each component and the full system is first calculated. This information is

The cooling system design of the baseline data center (with no UTES) is first calculated by

2.4.5 Modes of Operation
Efficiently cooling a data center requires different equipment and operational modes depending
upon the ambient temperature.

Baseline Data Center:

 • Low ambient temperatures: The chillers are bypassed and all cooling is provided by the
 dry coolers, as in Figure 19b. Dry coolers typically operate at part-load and therefore
 consume very little electrical power. Electrical power consumption for a data center that
 rejects 1-GWth of heat is illustrated in Figure 23, and in this example, dry cooler mode
 can occur up to ambient temperatures of 20°C.

 • Intermediate ambient temperatures: Some dry coolers are used to pre-cool the FWS fluid,
 which is then cooled to the required temperature by the chillers as in Figure 20. Heat
 from the chiller condensers is then rejected by the remaining dry coolers. Chillers
 typically operate at part-load, reducing their power consumption. Dry coolers typically
 operate at maximum fan speed in order to deliver the maximum pre-cooling possible (as
 this is more efficient than cooling with the chillers). As the ambient temperature

 29

 increases, the number of dry coolers available for pre-cooling decreases. In the example
 in Figure 23, pre-cooling occurs during ambient temperatures of 20°–36°C.

 • High ambient temperatures: The FWS fluid is cooled entirely by the chillers and
 condenser heat is rejected by the dry coolers, which occurs above 36°C in Figure 23.

Figure 23. Cooling system electricity consumption for a data center that rejects 1-GWth heat with
 chillers and dry coolers as a function of ambient temperature
When integrating Cold UTES the objective is to avoid disrupting the delivery of cooling to the
data center, and minimize modifications to the baseline cooling system design. Therefore, the
cooling system capacity and design is the same as for the baseline data center. Since the same
cooling equipment is being used, this equipment must be operated differently to the baseline
system in order to create spare cooling capacity that can be used to charge the UTES.

Charging Cold UTES:

 • Low ambient temperatures: The dry coolers operate at maximum fan speed. The number
 of dry coolers necessary to cool the data center in these conditions is first calculated. The
 remaining dry coolers may then be used to charge the Cold UTES. These dry coolers run
 at full fan speed and cool the UTES fluid to the maximum extent; at colder ambient
 temperatures, the UTES injection temperature is also reduced. This ensures the maximum
 cold energy is stored. As the ambient temperature increases, the number of dry coolers
 available to charge the UTES decreases. This is represented in Figure 24a which shows
 the total electrical energy required to cool the data center and charge the UTES, while
 Figure 24b shows the cooling delivered to the UTES which decreases as ambient
 temperatures increase.

As the ambient temperature rises, it will reach a point where it is too warm to charge the UTES
to the required temperature using only dry coolers. Chillers may then be used to charge the
UTES although this increases the electrical power input. Results explore the impact and benefit
of using chillers for UTES charging.

 30

 • Intermediate ambient temperatures: The chillers and dry coolers are operated at full load
 assuming that the dry coolers are all used to reject heat from the chiller condenser. The
 total cooling capacity is calculated, and this will exceed the cooling requirement of the
 data center. The “spare” dry coolers can be either allocated to charge the UTES or to pre-
 cool the chillers that are allocated to the data center.

 o All spare equipment allocated to charge the UTES: The spare chillers charge the
 UTES and the dry coolers reject the condenser heat. This results in a significant
 increase in electrical power with the result that the UTES charge rate is
 maximized, as shown in Figure 24 with the 100% charge rate curves.

 o A fraction of spare equipment is allocated to pre-cool the data center chillers: This
 results in more efficient cooling of the data center. Fewer chillers/dry coolers are
 available to charge the UTES, which leads to a reduced charging rate, but the
 change in electrical power is more manageable, as shown in Figure 24 with the
 50% charge rate curves.

 • High ambient temperatures: The chillers and dry coolers are operated at full load, and the
 total cooling capacity is calculated, which will typically exceed the cooling requirement
 of the data center. The number of chillers and dry coolers to cool the data center is
 calculated, assuming that all dry coolers are used to reject heat from the chiller
 condenser. The remaining equipment can be allocated to charge the UTES. The UTES is
 charged to a lower temperature than the FWS system, so the chiller and dry cooler
 performance is recalculated to meet this temperature requirement.

 • Very high ambient temperatures: During the hottest hours of the year, cooling the data
 center requires the full cooling capacity of the cooling system. There is no spare capacity
 available to charge the UTES.

It should be noted that the charging rate of the UTES also depends on the IT capacity, which can
also vary with time. In time periods where the IT capacity is reduced, the data center requires
less cooling and therefore the spare cooling capacity that can be allocated to charge the UTES
increases.

Discharging the Cold UTES:

 • The Cold UTES is discharged to meet the full cooling requirement of the data center.
 Thus, dry coolers and chillers are not operated when the UTES discharges, and the
 cooling performance does not therefore depend on ambient temperature. The UTES flow
 rate is adjusted to meet the required data center cooling capacity.

2.5 Case Study Descriptions
Twelve case studies were coordinated with the grid modeling team: six in Virginia and six in
Arizona. These regions are chosen because of the large projected growth in data centers (see
Appendix E) as well as their variations in climate, grid system, and other factors. Cases 1 and 2
are set in Virginia, while Cases 3 and 4 are set in Arizona. Cases 1 and 3 consider a nominal data
center design with different TCS supply temperatures. Cases 2 and 4 investigate the scenario
where the cooling system capacity is kept the same as Case 1 and 3, respectively, but the IT
compute capacity is oversized. The charge rate—as described in Section 2.4—is varied by case.
For Case 1, 1-s1, 3, and 3-s1, the UTES charge rate is chosen to smoothly bring chillers online,
whereas, for cases where the IT capacity is oversized, the UTES charge rate is maximized in

Results from the grid models are provided in Section 4. We present results for the same cases
using the system model below for comparison. There are several key differences between the
system model analysis and the grid model analysis:

 1. The grid models run these 12 case studies assuming a constant, non-varying load of 1
 GWe, whereas the system model here uses a variable load, as described in Appendix D.2.

 2. The grid models use a sophisticated dispatch approach that determines whether or not to
 use the chillers to charge the UTES. The system model may use chillers to charge the
 UTES, in which case they are used to the maximum extent possible (i.e., the model does
 not use a sophisticated dispatch to work out the optimal times to charge the UTES with
 chillers). Therefore, we also consider the case where chillers are not used to charge the
 UTES, which may only then be charged with the dry coolers.

 3. The system model is used to investigate the sensitivity of the results to several key
 parameters, such as well cost, flow rate, TCS supply temperature, and permeability.

2.6.3 Increasing IT Capacity
The previous subsections show that Cold UTES can reduce peak electrical loads, reduce
electricity costs, and reduce the LCOC. A further value proposition is that reducing peak loads
allows for more IT capacity to be installed for a given grid connection capacity. Here, the grid
connection capacity is given by the baseline data center design and equals the maximum IT
capacity plus ancillary load (1 GWe) plus the peak electrical cooling load that occurs at the
maximum ambient temperature. The grid connection capacity is 1.27 GWe in Case 1 and 1.30
GWe in Case 3.

The hourly power consumption is shown for the baseline Case 1 data center in Figure 36a for the
peak day – i.e. the day in which the peak total power occurs. In this example, the power
consumption is always less than the grid connection because the peak IT load does not coincide
with the peak ambient temperature. But if the peak IT load occurred during peak temperatures
the grid connection limit would be reached.

This ‘spare’ connection capacity can potentially be used by increasing the IT capacity if it can be
ensured that the grid connection limit is not exceeded. Cold UTES enables peak electrical
cooling loads to be minimized and can therefore ensure IT capacity and utilization is maximized
without exceeding the grid connection limit. For example, Figure 36b shows how Cold UTES
can be used to increase the IT capacity by 25% while keeping the cooling system capacity the
same and keeping peak electrical loads under the grid connection capacity on the peak day.

2.7 System Model Results for 12 Case Studies
In this section, techno-economic results for the 12 cases outlined in Table 4 are presented.

Peak electrical load for cooling is shown in Figure 38 and shows that RTES significantly reduces
peak electrical cooling load in Virginia. This is because there are plentiful hours with low
ambient temperatures, so that UTES can always be charged using dry coolers—even if chillers
are allowed to be used. If chillers are used for UTES charging, then peak electrical loads increase
in Arizona. There are not enough hours with low ambient temperatures to create a large UTES
capacity by charging with dry coolers only. Chillers are used to charge the UTES but their large
power requirements, in addition to the UTES pump power, leads to an increase in maximum
electrical load for cooling. To reduce peak electrical cooling loads in Arizona, the UTES can
only be charged by the dry coolers and not chillers (Figure 38b).

The LCOC for the 12 cases is shown in Figure 39 and shows that UTES can reduce the LCOC in
most cases. Installing UTES increases the total capital costs (Figure 39a) but if the chillers are
not used for charging, then the required chiller capacity can be reduced (Figure 39b). This is
particularly true in Virginia: chillers are not used for charging UTES and are not required to cool
the data center (as the data center is cooled with dry coolers or UTES). Therefore, in this
scenario, chiller capital costs can be reduced. This is not the case in Arizona, where chillers are
still required to cool the data center in some hours of the year.

 45

UTES also reduces electricity costs for cooling. These operational costs are a significant
component of the data center LCOC, therefore, UTES enables the LCOC to be reduced even if
capital costs increase.

Annual expenditure on electricity for cooling is shown in Figure 40, which illustrates how UTES
is able to reduce the LCOC by reducing electricity costs. The bars are divided into expenditure in
the 20% most expensive hours and 80% least expensive hours. For a conventional data center,
the 20% most expensive hours generate most of the cooling electricity cost. UTES dispatches in
the hottest hours, which tend to correlate with the most expensive hours. Discharging UTES
requires less electricity than running chillers in the hottest hours, so electricity costs are
significantly decreased due to reduced expenditure in the most expensive hours. However,
expenditure in the least expensive hours can increase, because these are the hours when UTES is
charged and therefore the total electricity consumption in those hours can increase.

The 12 case studies capture a range of sensitivities. The TCS supply temperature has a
significant influence on cooling system operation. Increasing the TCS supply temperature (e.g.,
from 30°C in Case 1 to 40°C in Case 1-s1, and from 30°C in Case 3 to 40°C in Case 3-s1; see
Table 4) reduces the peak electrical loads for cooling because the chiller evaporator temperature
is higher, leading to improved chiller COP. As a result, capital costs are reduced (smaller chiller
and UTES capacities are required) while operational costs also decrease (the chillers are used
less frequently), leading to an overall reduction in LCOC for the baseline data center. The benefit
of using UTES to reduce LCOC therefore decreases for higher TCS supply temperatures.

In Cases 2 and 4, the IT plus ancillary capacity is increased while the cooling system capacity is
kept constant. In Case 2a, the capacity is increased to 1,100 GWe in Virginia. However, the peak
electrical cooling load does not increase noticeably, as the peak load is associated with
discharging the UTES rather than temperature-dependent chiller operation. However, in Case 2b,
the capacity increases to 1,200 GWe, which leads to chillers charging the UTES (Figure 38a) and
therefore a significant increase in UTES peak electrical cooling load compared to Case 2a.

In Case 2-s1-a and Case 2-s1-b, the TCS supply temperature is increased (while keeping the
cooling system capacity constant), which reduces the use of chillers and helps reduce peak loads
and LCOC. In Arizona, chillers are always used to charge the UTES, leading to large peak
electrical loads for cooling – particularly when the IT capacity is increased in Case 4. But these
loads are consumed during low price hours, which leads to lower electricity costs. If the chillers
are prevented from charging the UTES (e.g. Figure 38b) then the peak loads can be reduced.
LCOC appears to reduce when IT capacity increases: the capital cost is the same but the quantity
of cooling increases to match the increased IT load. Electricity costs increase, but the increase is
small when UTES is used because the additional electricity consumption has been shifted to low
price hours.

2.9 Model Limitations and Future Work
Numerous assumptions and simplifications have been made in the development of the system
model, and future work could improve the model in several ways.

Currently, the model only considers heat that has been rejected from the liquid-cooled chips.
However, other equipment (such as transformers, memory boards, corridors) are cooled with air
(even in ‘liquid-cooled data centers’). This air is likely to be cooled to a different temperature to
the TCS loop, particularly if high TCS temperatures are used.

The data center cooling system comprises many chillers, dry coolers, and UTES wells, which
have been assumed to operate identically. Part-load models are also simplifications in order to
ensure the model runs quickly. Improved system models will consider more detailed part load
models, transient effects, and more complex control systems where equipment can be operated at
different operational points. Furthermore, more realistic controls should be enacted to ensure that
the cooling system can get from one operating point to another in a realistic way.

Several simplifying assumptions are made to approximate UTES behavior. For example, the
recovery efficiency and discharging temperature are assumed to be constant. The discharging
temperature is also assumed to be constant. Both of these variables are likely to vary as a
function of time depending on the design and operation. This assumption considerably simplifies

 54

the model by removing the need for computationally complex reservoir models. Future work
should consider improving the UTES models: first, by incorporating simple improvements so
that the model can still run in a reasonable amount of time, and second by considering
integration with sophisticated subsurface simulation tools.

A simplified dispatch approach has been taken that assumes perfect foresight of electricity prices
and weather conditions. Including a forecasting model and error would improve the accuracy of
results that quantify the benefit of UTES. Furthermore, considering a wider range of operating
conditions, including extreme events, could provide more context about UTES value.

Future work should include manufacturer data, experimental data, and operational data to
validate the models. Inclusion in digital twins may help to demonstrate the benefits of Cold
UTES without requiring the costs and complexities of a physical demonstration.

3 A Focused Study of Data Center Cooling Costs and
  IT Capacity
In this section, we model an AI training data center operating at S45 (i.e., 45°C supply water to
IT racks) to explore opportunities to increase interconnection power, reduce cooling costs, and
increase data center lifetime. First, we reduce annual peak cooling power through Cold UTES,
freeing up a portion of the interconnection capacity for additional IT equipment, thus improving
data center compute performance. Second, we aim to lower electrical loads for cooling,
particularly during grid peak periods and when electricity is costly, thereby reducing cooling
costs. In many occasions, mitigation of peak electricity consumption would help reduce
environmental impacts per local energy policies. Third, we examine how Cold UTES-enabled
cooling can improve data center power scalability and extend data center lifetime.

3.1 Considerations: Why Add Cold UTES to Data Centers?
3.1.1 Efficient Power Capacity Utilization
First, interconnection capacity must be shared over IT equipment, power supply, and cooling.
While IT and power supply loads are relatively constant, cooling power varies due to external
weather conditions (Stojkovic et al. 2025), with pronounced peaks during hot periods of the day
or year. The power allocated for peak cooling cannot be used for IT equipment, directly reducing
the productivity of the data center. Our first goal is to reduce this peak power, and free up more
grid interconnect capacity for IT load.

Second, peak data center power can coincide with high grid stress, increasing cost for the data
center, and decreasing grid reliability. In other words, this poor alignment increases the
contribution of data center load to grid resource requirements, potentially causing grids to deny
or defer data center connection (Lin et al. 2024). And locally, this poor alignment can both
increase power costs and attributed carbon emissions for the data center. Our second goal is to
reduce this poor alignment to help the grid and reduce data center costs.

3.1.2 Efficient Power Supply and Heat Removal
Third, the power density of AI computing equipment is growing rapidly, but the corresponding
power distribution and cooling systems are not keeping pace (see Figure 44). In an AI training
data center today, the so-called “gray space” elements (everything other than IT equipment)
consume over 50% of floor and roof space. Because data centers each have a given physical size,
growing IT power density means that power distribution and cooling systems fill an ever larger
space fraction, eventually exceeding the available space, and preventing further increase in IT
load or potentially causing the data center to be shuttered as no longer cost-effective. Our third
goal is to reduce the space requirement for power distribution and cooling and enable them to
scale to higher power and extend the lifetime of data centers.

 (a), flexibility to reduce grid stress (b), and efficient cooling (c).

3.1.3 Approach
We address these challenges using a Cold UTES system design. This technology operates
without water consumption, making it well suited for water-stressed regions, and enables long-
term thermal energy storage in underground formations such as natural reservoirs or shallow
rock. The study in this section considers three regions: Arizona, Texas, and Virginia. First, we
optimize Cold UTES operation (charge and discharge rates) throughout the year with the highest
air temperatures over the 2020–2024 period, freeing up power capacity to increase data center IT
load. Second, we shift the electrical load for coolingfrom hot periods of the day/year to cooler
periods when electricity is less expensive and fewer high-value fossil fuel resources are being
used, reducing data center cooling costs and carbon emissions. Third, the use of Cold UTES
reduces the required capacity and space footprint of certain cooling equipment (chillers and dry
coolers) along with their associated power distribution equipment. Harnessing this opportunity,
we investigate to what extent current data center buildings can accommodate more power growth
with the increasing IT power density, thereby extending their operational lifetime.

Cold UTES integration into the data center cooling system is shown in Figure 45. Heat
exchangers isolate water loops in both systems. During charging, the Cold UTES system is
connected to the heat rejection loop. Water is extracted from hot wells, cooled by unused
capacity of the dry coolers, and injected into cold wells. During discharge (during peak periods),
Cold UTES connects to the technology cooling system loop, supplying chilled water directly to
the cooling system on hot days (Katsura et al. 2024; Oh et al. 2025; Winick et al. 2025). When
this happens, the chillers, dry coolers, and attached water pumps are shut down, reducing power
demand, energy consumption/costs and associated carbon emissions. Cold UTES is used to
provide chilled water to the data center precisely when conventional cooling systems operate
inefficiently.

 loop and discharge in the technology cooling system loop.
We used the cooling system model (Gnibga and Chien 2026b) to define references for data
center power, costs and operational carbon impacts. We then integrated the Cold UTES model
(Gnibga and Chien 2026b) into the system, which allows the electrical loads for cooling to be
shifted from hot periods to cooler periods, thereby reducing peak cooling power demand and
improving IT capacity. In addition, the Cold UTES system allows electrical loads to be shifted to
periods when electricity prices and carbon intensity are favorable, reducing cooling costs and
carbon impact. Furthermore, Cold UTES reduces gray space utilization, because it reduces
cooling and associated power distribution hardware requirements. We used space models in

thermal energy storage (Cold UTES) to electrical energy storage using a battery model (Gnibga

The analytical models and optimization methodology used in this study are detailed in (Gnibga

peak cooling power, costs, carbon impact, and data center lifetime.

3.1.4 Accurately Modeling Data Center IT Configurations: DCGen
The data center configurations used in both Sections 2 and 3 are based on DCGen, a model-
driven tool that generates realistic and detailed data center designs. Modern data centers are
complex energy systems in which IT equipment, power distribution, and cooling infrastructure
are tightly integrated and mutually dependent. Furthermore, they are evolving rapidly, with each
new generation of AI hardware (annual) requiring significant data center redesign. DCGen
captures these cross-layer dependencies, enabling what-if case studies grounded in realistic
industry models (Gnibga and Chien 2026b) rather than simplified or outdated assumptions
(Barroso et al. 2019).

The DCGen tool models both rack-level and facility-level data center characteristics, including
rack power density, rack layouts by node type, power distribution and cooling systems optimized

for either space or power efficiency, as well as power demand and space utilization of each

By grounding our data center designs (IT equipment, power distribution, and cooling

3.3.1 Increased IT Power Capacity
Interconnection capacity is a critical resource. This is particularly true as U.S. power grids are
increasingly short of power (Environmental Resources Management (ERM) 2025; Lin et al.

for IT load, cooling, and power conversion losses. Consequently, the share of this budget
available for IT racks (compute, networking, storage) is constrained by peak cooling demand
(generally the hottest days). In this section, we examine how Cold UTES can expand IT capacity.
Note that the work described here focus on Nvidia’s recommended high-temperature water inlet
temperature, and was published in (Gnibga and Chien 2026b) in June 2026. The studies in
described in Section 2.6.3 parallel those described here, but with a different cooling water inlet
temperature focus.

3.3.4 Cooling Electrical Load During Grid Peak Periods
A popular load shifting technology is battery energy storage. We compare the environmental and
cost implications of shifting electrical loads with Cold UTES and Li-ion batteries. Cold UTES
stores thermal energy, using excess cooling capacity to generate cold efficiently, and harnessing
large capacity for long-duration storage. For example, in Arizona, Cold UTES stores up to
579,766 MWth for seasonal needs. In contrast, a battery stores electricity, so charging or
discharging does not impact the power consumed by cooling equipment; rather, it shifts
temporally the power draw from the electrical grid. Batteries can reduce power costs (charge
when electricity prices are low) and enable more efficient use of valuable fossil fuel assets
(charge when few fossil fuel resources will be used). In terms of capacity, the battery requires at
most a capacity of 793 MWhe (Arizona) to support both daily and seasonal operations.

3.3.5 Increased Data Center Lifetime
A key element for data center scalability (lifetime) is the ability to house required equipment.
Traditionally, data center buildings have 10- to 20-year lifetimes (Barroso et al. 2019), but with
rapid power density growth, they can be unusable in just a few years. We assume that more
power can be brought into the data center building and explore how space constraint affects
lifetime. Specifically, we explore how Cold UTES affects this game. The data center lifetime
analysis is conducted using DCGen (Gnibga and Chien 2026a) (see Appendix C).

power density across the four generations (2022, 2024, 2027, 2029) described above, assuming
unlimited power availability (i.e., no external constraints from the power grid). The data center
IT power is estimated to 209 MW in 2022, 1 GW in 2024, 2.32 GW in 2027, and 3.24 GW in
2029. We first analyze space requirements with conventional cooling. Figure 54 shows data
center floor (first bars) and outdoor (second bars) space used in three locations, classified by IT,

3.4 Summary Results and Limitations
We evaluate the benefits of Cold UTES for data center cooling. Focusing on grid interconnection
capacity shows multiple benefits across three regional locations: improvements in power
efficiency, reductions in on-peak power demand (e.g., 44.4% in Texas) and cooling costs (e.g.,
3.4% in Arizona). In addition, Cold UTES improves IT power capacity by 9.8% across the three
regions. Further, Cold UTES can extend data center lifetime by reducing cooling space footprint.
In Virginia, data centers can sustain 12% annual power growth and higher rack densities for
lifetime extension to 2029. In Arizona and Texas, the lifetime extension is through 2027.

Our analysis relies on several modeling assumptions and limitations. First, we perform an offline
optimization over a five-year horizon (2020–2024), assuming complete knowledge of typical
weather conditions and seasonal patterns, electricity pricing, carbon intensity, and local grid peak
loads. This approach provides valuable insights into UTES operation, but online strategies that
reflect uncertainty regarding future weather and grid conditions are required to realize these
benefits. Second, in modeling cooling, we assume that no thermal energy losses in water loops
and ignore transient dynamics. Additionally, we assume that the water temperature in UTES
wells remains constant throughout the year, ignoring leakage of heat and fluid. In addition,
enabling lower temperature charging of UTES would increase its potential. Finally, the simple
model UTES pump power demand may overestimate its actual power use, reducing the Cold
UTES systems benefits.

3.5 Future Work
Future directions are several-fold. First, we have explored the opportunity of Cold UTES to
increase data center effectiveness, focusing on grid interconnection capacity as a key resource
limit. These studies took IT load as a given. However, there is opportunity to increase capacity
by reshaping IT load, and we can optimize Cold UTES-based data center operation for the metric
of total IT capacity (integrated over time). This may reveal additional potential benefits of Cold
UTES. Second, we have used simple models of the other cooling elements and power
distribution elements. However, these have broader operation ranges, whose optimization can
further increase total IT capacity (Chien et al. 2022; Gnibga et al. 2024). We will explore the
opportunities to further increase both IT capacity and total IT capacity (integrated over time).
Finally, the third future direction is to create online control for these scenarios, enabling
evaluation of Cold UTES in a real data center prototype.

4.1 Background and Grid Modeling
To assess the potential power system impact of adding Cold UTES (exclusively modeled as
RTES in this section) to data center cooling systems, the cooling system model described in
Section 2 is incorporated into two capacity expansion models: the Princeton University GenX
model and the NLR Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS) model. These models
produce potential future power system designs by minimizing the investment and operational
costs of the electricity system. The cooling system model with RTES is incorporated into these
models such that the models will see the endogenously determined hourly cooling system
electricity demand, which becomes an additional load that the power system must meet. These
capacity expansion models optimize the operations of the cooling system and RTES alongside
the power system investments and operations to minimize total electricity system costs.

GenX (Jenkins and Sepulveda 2017) is an open-source optimization tool designed to support
power system planning by determining the least-cost mix of electricity generation, storage, and
transmission resources needed to meet future demand under various policy and technical
constraints. Written in Julia using the JuMP optimization framework, GenX co-optimizes
investment and operational decisions across a range of resource types—including thermal,
renewable, storage, and demand-side flexibility—while capturing key operational details such as
unit commitment, hourly dispatch, reserves, and network flows. Its formulation balances
computational tractability with sufficient operational fidelity to represent the interactions
between variable renewables, flexible loads, and energy storage that are central to modern grid
planning questions. GenX has been widely used in academic research and policy analysis and
continues to be actively developed and maintained as part of an open-source community.

ReEDS (Avraam et al. 2025) is NLR’s capacity planning model for the power sector, simulating
the possible evolution of the bulk power system—generation, storage, and transmission—from
present day through 2050 or later. ReEDS is a capacity expansion and dispatch model primarily
used for the contiguous U.S. electric power sector that relies on system-wide least-cost
optimization to estimate the type and location of future generation and transmission capacity.
The model represents the U.S. at high spatial resolution and incorporates a wide range of
conventional and renewable generation technologies, energy storage, and demand-side resources.
ReEDS leverages advanced dimensionality reduction techniques, such as optimized
representative day selection (Brown et al. 2025) to maintain computational tractability while
capturing key interactions between conventional generators, variable renewables, storage, and
system operations. The model is open source and written in Python and GAMS.

While GenX and ReEDS are both capacity expansion models, they have some key feature
differences listed in Table 7 that impact their results. Input data and scenario assumptions are
harmonized between the two models to ensure any differences in the models’ results are
primarily due to feature and structural design differences. Two models were used in this work to
be able to compare outputs and improve confidence in modeling a new technology type for this
class of model.

4.2 Grid Analysis Scenarios and Assumptions
The grid analysis work presented here uses the same cases as presented in Table 4 of Section 2.5
except that an additional retrofit scenario is considered. The retrofit scenario assumes that 5,000
MW of data centers are retrofitted with RTES in 2035, but otherwise uses the same assumptions
for RTES as its similarly named scenario (e.g., same cooling loop temperatures).

Additional inputs and assumptions are needed to represent the grid systems, and those are
described here. Specifics on the data center load growth assumptions used to inform the grid
scenarios are provided in Appendix F.

The capacity expansion models use existing generator fleet data from the U.S. Energy
Information Administration’s (EIA) Annual Energy Outlook (AEO) 2025 National Energy
Modeling System unit database (EIA 2025a) and EIA’s March 2025 Preliminary Monthly
Electric Generator Inventory (EIA 2025b). Fuel price projections for gas, coal, and uranium are
from the AEO 2025 reference scenario (EIA 2025a) and technology performance and cost
projections from the 2024 Annual Technology Baseline, using the moderate case assumptions
(NLR 2024). Load growth projections and hourly profiles are from Evolved Energy Research
(Jones et al. 2024) and have a national average 1.8% compound annual growth rate. Wind, solar,
and geothermal site-specific resource availability and cost as well as hourly site-specific wind

 73

and solar hourly generation potential are from the reV model using the reference siting
assumptions (Maclaurin et al. 2021; Lopez et al. 2025).

The models project the evolution of the electricity system through 2050, modeling every 5 years
(2030, 2035, etc.). To ensure sufficient firm capacity to meet future demand, planning reserve
margin requirements are enforced based on the NERC 2024 Long-Term Resource Assessment
(NERC 2025). State and federal policies as of August 2025 are included, except for the Clean
Air Act regulations for new and existing power plants. While endogenous power plant
retirements are allowed, fossil power plant retrofits for CCS or hydrogen and transmission
expansion are disallowed in order to improve consistency between the two models.

To estimate the potential power system impacts from RTES, we compare scenarios without
RTES to scenarios with RTES. The change in power system capacity, generation, and costs
between the case without RTES and the cases with RTES thus provide an estimate of the value
of RTES to the power system.

To investigate the impact of RTES duration on grid value, RTES cases with 4*, 8, 12*, 24*, 72*,
168, 1,000, 2,000, and 4,000 hours of duration are considered. Due to the computational burden
associated with GenX’s higher temporal resolution, durations listed with an asterisk are only
considered in ReEDS, while the rest are considered in both GenX and ReEDS. Assuming that the
temperature of the subsurface surrounding the RTES wells would stabilize after a few injection
cycles, we apply 100% thermal recovery efficiency. This is consistent with the findings of Pepin
et al. (2021) that cold RTES systems in different geologic regions across the contiguous United
States have very high thermal recovery efficiencies, with 96.3% to 99.3% recovery after 5 years
of operation. We consider the impact of RTES systems for data centers in Virginia and Arizona
on regional grids.

For the cases where RTES is located in data centers in Virginia, we model the PJM system,
which is represented using 23 regions, as shown in Figure 57. We add the data center load to the
region in northern Virginia and explicitly model the cooling system, with and without RTES, for
that load.

4.3 Modeling Methodology of Cold UTES in Grid Models
We incorporate a simplified model of RTES in a data center cooling system into the two capacity
expansion models. To assess the potential grid benefits of RTES without detailed RTES cost
data, we compare the cost of meeting additional data center demand with and without no-cost
RTES. This approach allows us to focus on the benefits of RTES to the grid irrespective of the
cost of RTES, which is not readily available.

4.3.1 Reservoir Thermal Energy Storage Model
We incorporate RTES into the GenX and ReEDS models using the approach shown in Figure 59.
The objective of these optimization models is to minimize electricity costs while meeting data
center cooling demand. We assume thermal cooling demand is constant and equal to the
electrical non-cooling load of the data center, which includes computing and lighting. For
instance, for a data center with 1,000 MW of electrical non-cooling load, we assume the thermal
cooling load is 1,000 MW. Electricity demand needed to supply the cooling will be a function of
ambient conditions and the use of RTES (if available in a given scenario).

In the design considered, heat is rejected from the data center through the dry cooler, chiller, and
RTES system (if available). To minimize data center water consumption, we focus on a cooling
system with a dry cooler rather than an evaporative cooler, as discussed in Section 2. We assume
a constant mass flow rate for the working fluid in the facilities water system loop.

Although the data center only has a single cooling system, we represent the cooling system as

The cooling demand increases the planning reserve requirements within the models, requiring
additional capacity buildout to serve the additional peak demand requirements. The magnitude of
this peak demand increase is a function of the ambient temperature at peak demand (which
determines the efficiency of the cooling system), the planning reserve margin requirement set by
NERC, and the utilization of the RTES system (this planning reserve concept is illustrated in
Figure F-6 in Appendix F).

RTES is modeled with a simple tank model based on the volume of water in the cold well and is
characterized by its duration, which is the ratio of maximum energy storage capacity (MWh) to

facilities water system loop mass flow rate is assumed to be fixed, thermal energy balance
dictates the relationship between the facilities water system loop temperature difference across
the RTES heat exchanger and the RTES loop mass flow rate. The electricity consumption to

4.4 Grid Benefits of Cold UTES
We find that RTES could offer appreciable power system cost savings to serve new data center
load. Figure 60 shows the net present value of system cost savings through 2050 as a function of
duration for each of the cases. The bulk power electricity system cost of adding data center
capacity in these scenarios ranges from $5,271-6,036/kW (in net present value). These values
indicate that including RTES for a dry-cooled data center reduces the electricity system cost of
adding that data center capacity by 1.3%–6.8%. A comparison of these ReEDS outputs with
GenX outputs is provided in Figure F-9 in Appendix F, which shows good alignment.

RTES duration can have a significant impact on grid savings. At the low end of duration,
increasing the duration from 4 hours to 12 hours increases the grid savings by 5%–32%, and the
maximum grid savings occurred with durations of 2,000 hours. Although we did run scenarios
with 4,000 hours of duration, these scenarios did not see additional cost savings beyond the
2,000-hour duration scenarios and are therefore not shown in Figure 60. Scenarios that had
increased IT capacity (Cases 2 and 4) experienced greater benefits from longer durations than
those with the default amount of IT capacity due to more efficient cooling system operation
when long-duration RTES was available.

In summary, the grid modeling of RTES for the Virginia and Arizona grid systems highlights
that RTES can provide meaningful grid savings through both reducing demand during high
summer stress periods and by shifting cooling load and electricity generation to more efficient
periods. The value of RTES depends on both the specifics of the grid system and RTES design,
the climate, and whether the grid has more need for firm capacity in the summer or winter.

4.5 Model Limitations and Future Work
This work focuses on the grid value of Cold UTES for case studies in Virginia and Arizona. The
work relies on state-of-the-art capacity expansion models that consider many aspects of
electricity system expansion. The scope of the work presented here is fairly narrow, as there are
many more variations of the Cold UTES and data center systems that could be explored.
Additionally, the scenarios here only considered a single electricity future, and alternative

 85

futures, such as those with different fuel prices, technology costs, demand growth projections,
and state and federal policies could have meaningful impacts on the value of Cold UTES for the
electricity system. Finally, the Cold UTES is modeled with perfect foresight and perfect
coordination with the electricity system, both of which are unlikely to occur in real-world
systems.

We recommend that future work focus on:
 • Estimating the value of Cold UTES under a broader range of grid scenarios, such as
 high/low gas prices, high/low generation and storage technology prices, limited
 renewable generation availability, and with alternative state energy policies
 • Estimating the value of Cold UTES in a wider range of locations, such as Texas, Illinois,
 and Georgia, or across the full contiguous United States
 • Converting the electricity system outcomes to retail rate impacts for varying classes of
 customers
 • Investigating the potential for other types of UTES, including Hot UTES, to provide
 value to the electricity system.

As shown by the recent and rapid expansion of energy storage on the grid, we expect that the
need to understand how energy storage might impact grid investment and operation decisions
will only increase over time.

5 Findings and Pathway Forward
Cold UTES represents a novel advancement in energy storage and demand-side management. Its
ability to provide flexible, non-water-consumptive cooling, convert low-value energy into a
critical industrial asset, and create new economic opportunities by leveraging existing
geothermal technologies makes it a key tool for managing the escalating energy demands of data
centers and ensuring grid resilience and an economically robust future. This report assesses the
benefits of Cold UTES by assuming a 1-GW data center baseline design at two different
locations in Arizona and Virginia. This section summarizes key findings and presents a potential
pathway forward.

5.1 Findings: Cold UTES Technology Value Proposition
This technology offers substantial value to the data center operators (facility-level value) and the
overall electricity grid (grid-level value). To assess Cold UTES value to these different
stakeholders, several independent models were developed. Facility-level models were NLR’s
TEA model and University of Chicago’s DCGen, which were used to assess the costs and
benefits to data center owners and operators. Grid-level models used were NLR’s ReEDS
capacity expansion model and Princeton University’s GenX model, and these capacity expansion
tools were used to assess the costs and benefits to the electrical grid. Cold UTES value is
sensitive to a range of factors, such as location, cooling system design, and geology, as described
in detail in earlier chapters. General findings are reported below.

5.1.1 Facility-Level Value (for Data Center Operators)
Cold UTES provides direct advantages to data center facilities, improving operational efficiency
and reducing costs.

1. Increased IT Capacity:
 o Maximizing IT capacity for a given grid interconnection is a critical objective for data
 center operators seeking to maximize growth rate (added IT capacity within a given
 time frame) and capital efficiency (IT capacity per capital expenditures). Cold UTES
 provides power-efficient cooling and temporal flexibility that enables significant
 increases in IT capacity (~10-25%).
2. Reduced Electric Utility Power Costs:
 o Monetization of Strategically Sourced, Low-Cost or Stranded Energy: Cold UTES
 allows data centers to utilize low-cost or stranded electricity, such as curtailed
 renewable energy or excess nighttime generation, converting and storing it for high-
 value cooling. This enables data centers to maximize the value of competitively
 priced electricity, including off-peak generation from fossil fuels and nuclear power.
 o Demand Charge Reduction: By shifting electrical loads for cooling away from peak
 demand periods, data centers can significantly lower their monthly demand charges
 (measured in $/MW-month).
 o Energy Cost Savings: Cold UTES enables facilities to consume cheaper, off-peak
 electricity (measured in $/MWh) to charge the storage system, subsequently
 discharging stored cold thermal energy during expensive peak hours to reduce the

 87

 cost of purchased power. This leverages time-of-use pricing and economic arbitrage
 opportunities.
3. Water Resource Benefits:
 o Non-Water-Consumptive Cooling: Cold UTES is a non-water-consumptive cooling
 solution, significantly reducing or eliminating the need for municipal/freshwater in
 data center operations. This is particularly valuable in water-stressed regions and
 aligns with energy efficiency goals.
 o Reduced Environmental Impact: By decreasing reliance on water-intensive cooling
 methods and monetizing strategically sourced, low-cost energy, Cold UTES lowers
 the overall environmental footprint of data centers—a key requirement to obtaining
 social license to operate.
4. Reduced Cooling System Capital Costs:
 o Lower Required Equipment Capacity and Optimized Sizing: Data centers typically
 install oversized equipment to address extreme design temperatures and peak
 demands. Cold UTES reduces this necessity, as it can be pre-charged during off-peak
 periods to handle peak electrical loads for cooling. By reducing peak hour loads and
 mitigating the derating of cooling equipment during hot weather conditions, Cold
 UTES lessens the need for oversized cooling infrastructure. This translates directly
 into reduced capital expenditure for cooling systems and the backup generators that
 serve that load.
 o Operational Flexibility: Enables dynamic switching between Cold UTES and
 conventional cooling based on operational needs, electricity pricing, or demand
 response signals.

5.1.2 Grid-Level Value (for the Grid and Utilities)
Cold UTES offers advantages to the broader electricity grid, enhancing stability, resilience, and
enabling greater renewable energy integration.

  1. Grid Stability and Demand Management:
 o Reduced Need for New Transmission and Generation Infrastructure: By managing
 demand, Cold UTES reduces the need for new power plant capacity, particularly
 expensive peaking plants.
 o Peak Load Reduction and Reshaping: Cold UTES directly reduces and shifts data
 center electrical loads for cooling, which can constitute up to 40% of a data center’s
 total energy consumption at peak times. This alleviates strain on the grid during
 critical periods. Enabling the shifting of demand from peak to off-peak hours
 (diurnally and seasonally) can help balance demand more effectively. Shaping and
 broadening the load curve can reduce sharp peaks and valleys. Data centers equipped
 with Cold UTES can shed load by relying on stored thermal energy, making their
 cooling demand dispatchable and assisting with resource adequacy and grid
 reliability. This facilitates real-time load following and frequency regulation by
 allowing rapid adjustments to cooling demand, responding to grid signals.

 88

 o Addresses Rapidly Growing Demand: Helps manage the anticipated significant
 growth in U.S. electricity demand over the next decade, particularly from data centers
 and AI.
  2. Enhanced Grid Resilience and Infrastructure Optimization:
 o Non-Wires Alternative: Increases the effective load carrying capacity of the existing
 transmission system, allowing more customers to be served without the need for new
 transmission construction. This represents an avoided transmission cost.
 o Reduced Grid and National Security Risks: Mitigates exposure to fuel supply/pricing
 volatility, supply chain risks, and associated delays with gas turbine deployment.
 o Virtual Power Plant Functionality: Cold UTES systems can function as virtual power
 plants, providing grid services such as reducing resource adequacy requirements and
 offering dispatchable load.
  3. Economic Value to Utilities and Customers:
 o Avoided Capital and Operational Costs: Reduces the need for new generation and
 grid infrastructure (transmission lines, batteries), leading to significant cost savings
 for utilities and ratepayers.
 o Financial Arbitrage: Provides financial value to the utility/power system operator
 through avoided generation, transmission costs, and reduced peak energy costs.
 o Increased System-Wide Value: Contributes to a more robust, efficient, and cost-
 effective energy system overall. When system level costs are reduced, this translates
 to savings for rate payers.

5.1.3 Value for Strategic and Economic Development
Beyond direct benefits to data centers and the electric grid, Cold UTES offers broader strategic
and economic advantages to the United States.

 1. Strategic Alignment with National Goals:
 o Supports federal goals for energy dominance, AI leadership, and infrastructure
 development without stressing existing grid capacity or water resources.
 Addresses the challenges associated with rapid AI growth.
 2. Economic Opportunity and Synergy with Oil and Gas Sector:
 o New Economic Pathways: Opens vast sedimentary basins, many of which are
 historically prolific in oil and gas production, to an entirely new economic
 opportunity.
 o Repurposing Infrastructure: Offers a pathway for oil and gas operators to extract
 prolonged value, revenue, and profit from aging oil field infrastructure,
 potentially extending the life of fields nearing decommissioning. This minimizes
 initial subsurface costs and accelerates deployment by leveraging existing assets
 and infrastructure (wells, pipelines, etc.).
 o Location-Agnostic Application: The underlying UTES technologies, especially
 BTES, are generally location-agnostic, meaning suitable geologic formations for

 89

 UTES exist across broad geographic regions and can be adapted to various
 depths.

5.2 Project Modeling Limitations
The following are some of the intentional limits placed on the scope of this work:
 • Limited data center deployment location. While data center developers are pursuing
 projects across the U.S., it was not possible to cover all possible locations and design
 scenarios such as using other alternative on-site energy sources. However, our models
 developed for this project can be used to analyze new deployment scenarios with new
 input parameters/conditions so as to assist with future feasibility and optimization studies
 of Cold UTES technologies.
 • Limited analysis of the design space for Cold UTES and its integration. There are a large
 number of design parameters that would be required for system design and operation
 optimization. Our initial analysis only conducts a few of them.
 • Lack of real-world design and operation data of hyperscale data centers. Our model takes
 a rigorous theoretical approach and incorporates as much realistic inputs as possible from
 engaged stakeholders/experts. However, specific design values and operational data from
 operating data centers was not available as it was deemed by data center companies to
 potentially compromise their competitive positions.
 • Lack of comparison of the Cold UTES technology against or in addition to other energy
 storage technologies. The analysis focuses on the assessment of Cold UTES for peak
 electrical cooling load reduction and does not include a comparison or integration with
 other alternative short-term or long-duration storage options, except some grid impact
 analysis showing that Cold UTES may be complementary with other battery storage
 technologies in some scenarios. The integration of Cold UTES with short duration
 batteries could be a promising area in which to identify further data center owner (behind
 the meter) value.
 • Lack of operational performance data of Cold UTES technologies for model validation.
 Cold UTES for data center cooling has not been demonstrated in the field in the U.S., and
 its performance projection mainly comes from numerical modeling.
 • Limitations in the newly developed TEA model. Reduced-order physical
 models/correlations are adopted to model performance of key components of the data
 center cooling systems. In particular, the models for both RTES and BTES technologies
 are mostly empirical and require future high-fidelity physics-based modeling.
 • Limitations in the adopted grid model. Grid models are complex and are used to make
 projections into the future with projections subject to various errors including component
 pricing changes, technology evolution, and variations in load growth. The analysis
 presented here makes use of one particular grid scenario, along with a set of assumptions
 on technology cost, fuel price, demand growth projects, and policies.

5.3 Cold UTES Technology Outlook—Technology and Adoption
 Readiness
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) applies Technology Readiness Level (TRL) and
Adoption Readiness Level (ARL) frameworks to systematically evaluate the maturity and
commercialization potential of emerging energy technologies. These assessments provide a
common language for understanding technical progress, identifying remaining risks, and
prioritizing investments that can accelerate deployment. For Cold UTES, assessing the
technology readiness against these frameworks can provide an objective view of the current state
of the technology as well as help to guide DOE’s strategic support and ensure that public
investment delivers maximum impact

5.3.1 Current State of Cold UTES Technology Readiness (TRL)
Cold UTES leverages mature geothermal storage concepts—specifically borehole thermal
energy storage (BTES) and aquifer/reservoir thermal energy storage (ATES/RTES)—to provide
long-duration, non-water-consumptive cooling for data centers. The core components (drilling,
heat exchangers, pumps, and control systems) are all commercially available (TRL10) and have
been demonstrated at scale in analogous applications:

 • BTES: Epic Systems 3 in Wisconsin operates a 3.5-MW data center cooled by a large
 BTES field. The Marine Corps Logistics Base 4 in Albany, GA, has demonstrated full-
 scale geothermal UTES for building cooling, albeit at smaller scale and not specifically
 for “cold” storage.
 • ATES: Equinix’s Amsterdam facility 5 uses ATES to deliver 12 MW of cooling to its
 AM4 and AM3 data centers, with similar systems deployed elsewhere in the Netherlands.

These precedents establish that the underlying technology is robust, potentially scalable to
hyperscale sizes, and operational in real-world settings. For data center cooling applications in
the 25- to 100-MW range, Cold UTES can be credibly assigned a TRL of 8: “actual system
completed and qualified through test and demonstration.” This reflects the existence of full-scale,
operational systems with analogous technical requirements, even if not yet deployed at
hyperscale or in every climate. For larger systems (100–500 MW+), TRL may be lower due to
scaling risks that have not yet been fully validated.

While the technical maturity of Cold UTES is relatively high, its adoption readiness is lower,
reflecting both strengths and remaining challenges:

5.3.3 The Case for Moving the Technology Toward Pilot Demonstration
Investment in a pilot-scale demonstration of Cold UTES would be transformative for both TRL
and ARL, unlocking the pathway to widespread commercial adoption:

 • Advancing TRL: A successful pilot at the 25- to 100-MW scale, integrated with a real
 data center and operated over multiple seasons, would elevate Cold UTES to TRL 9—
 “actual system operated over the full range of expected conditions.” This would help
 resolve remaining technical uncertainties related to scale, integration, and control
 strategies, and provide operational data to inform future deployments.
 • Advancing ARL: Demonstrating Cold UTES in a high-visibility, U.S.-based data center
 would address key adoption risks—validating business models, engaging early adopters,
 and streamlining permitting and stakeholder processes. This would enable Cold UTES to
 reach ARL 8–9, where all major adoption risks are resolved and the technology is ready
 for broad commercial deployment.

5.3.4 Technology Outlook Conclusion
Cold UTES is a technically mature, high-impact solution for data center cooling, with strong
potential to address urgent energy and water challenges. While the core technology is currently
at TRL 8 and ARL 5–6 for 25- to 100-MW applications, a pilot demonstration is logical and
critical next step to achieve full commercial readiness. Such an investment would not only
advance TRL and ARL to their highest levels, but also catalyze a new era of resilient
infrastructure for the digital economy.

5.4 Pathway Forward
With the demonstrated values brought by Cold UTES in various representative case studies in
the report, Cold UTES is shown to have strong potential to address the most critical barriers of
data center deployment—water consumption and peak load. In the meantime, future commercial
deployment of Cold UTES would benefit from advancement in four major technical areas:

 93

drilling cost, characterization of local geology/chemistry, system integration, and business
justification.

 • Drilling cost. Drilling technologies have been widely used in geothermal and oil/gas
 industries. Drilling for Cold UTES will not be a major barrier for deployment. However,
 cost reduction of drilling would greatly improve the overall system economics and is a
 current industrial focus.
 • Characterization of local geology/chemistry. Among the two UTES technologies
 (RTES and BTES), RTES concerns chemistry. For both UTES technologies, geological
 conditions will have a great impact on UTES system performance such as system design
 and charging and discharging efficiencies. When geological conditions are not ideal,
 UTES would not be technically or economically viable. Thus a sufficient geological
 characterization of the local subsurface is a critical reference for system design and
 feasibility assessment.
 • System integration. For a given data center, Cold UTES will directly interact with the
 traditional facility cooling loops. The integrated cooling system will be required to
 consistently meet the cooling needs of computing racks for a given weather condition and
 cooling system design/operation. The integration system control will be required to
 enable flexible adjustment of the electric cooling load to maximize the economic return.
 • Business justification. Commercial adoption of Cold UTES requires high-fidelity
 performance prediction over the system’s lifetime, minimizing system uncertainties such
 as component reliability over time, and economic metrics related to business priorities.

There are no scientific/engineering breakthroughs needed for Cold UTES development, so
development of pilot/demonstration of Cold UTES will be the next critical step in mitigating
commercial risks of Cold UTES technologies. These risks include:
 • Lack of a standard site characterization protocol to assess the geology/chemistry and
 establish its impact to the drilling/system cost, system design optimization, and projected
 system performance.
 • Lack of successful engineering designs of Cold UTES technologies for a given required
 performance.
 • Lack of track record of system integration with data center cooling systems at applicable
 locations and business cases.
 • Lack of understanding on system uncertainties on cost, performance, operation
 flexibility, and component reliability.
 • Lack of validated techno-economic model for business justification.
 • Lack of validated grid impact model for value proposition to the national grids.

Appendix A. Data Center Energy System Terminology
A.1 Definitions

Energy Loads

IT Load (or Power):

 Electrical power consumed by information technology (IT) equipment, including
 compute (CPUs/GPUs), memory, storage, and networking equipment typically embodied
 in servers, storage systems, and network switches. Throughout this report, a “1-GWe data
 center” implies a data center with 1 GWe of installed IT equipment, excluding cooling
 and other supporting infrastructure.

Ancillary Load:

 Electrical power consumed by non-IT infrastructure required to support data center
 operation, excluding cooling systems. Ancillary components include power distribution
 units (PDUs), uninterruptible power supply (UPS), transformers, lighting, monitoring,
 and control systems. Although UPS and transformers do not directly consume power,
 electrical conversion and standby losses contribute to the ancillary load.

Power Distribution Load (or Power):

 Electrical power consumed by power distribution units (PDUs), uninterruptible power
 supply (UPS), and inside machine room transformers.

Cooling Thermal Load:

 The thermal power generated within the data center that must be removed by the cooling
 system to maintain allowable operating conditions. This is assumed to equal the IT load
 plus the ancillary load. Thermal load (MWth) is distinguished from electrical load
 (MWe).

Cooling Electrical Load (or Power):

 The electrical power required to operate the cooling system that removes the cooling
 thermal load, including mechanical chillers, coolant distribution units (CDUs), dry cooler
 fans, and circulating pumps.

Total Facility Electrical Load:

 The total electrical power consumed by the data center at a given operating point.

 Total Facility Load = IT Load + Ancillary Load + Cooling Electrical Load

Peak Facility Power:

 105

 The maximum instantaneous total facility electrical load, including IT load, ancillary
 load, and cooling electrical load.

IT Capacity:

 The maximum power that can be supplied to IT racks in the data center.

Interconnection Capacity:

 The maximum rated electrical power that the data center is contractually and physically
 permitted to draw from the electrical grid at the point of interconnection.

Load Factor:

 The ratio of average electrical load to peak electrical load over a defined period.

 Ratio of average electrical load to capacity (maximum supportable) electrical load.

Cooling Fluid Loops:

Cooling fluid loops are piping networks that distribute heat transfer fluid around the data center
and therefore transfer cold or hot energy from one location to another.

Facility Water System (FWS) Loop:

 The FWS loop circulates temperature-controlled fluid between the data center internal
 heat exchangers (which include CDUs CRAHs, etc.) and chillers, dry coolers, or the
 UTES heat exchanger, depending on the operational mode. For this project, the fluid is
 treated as pure water, which it often is in operational data centers.

Technology Cooling System (TCS) Loop:

 The TCS loop is cooled by the FWS loop. In liquid-cooled data centers, the fluid in the
 FWS is a liquid and heat is exchanged between the TCS and FWS in a CDU. The cold
 fluid in the FWS is used for direct-to-chip liquid cooling. In air-cooled data centers, the
 fluid in the FWS is air, which is driven through data center aisles and racks to cool the
 chips. Heat is exchanged between the FWS fluid and air in a Computer Room Air
 Handler (CRAH). Many data centers require both liquid and air cooling. For this project,
 the fluid is treated as pure water, although it may often be a mixture of water and 25%
 propylene glycol (25% PG) in operational data centers.

Heat Rejection Loop (HRL) / Condenser Loop:

 For liquid-cooled chillers, heat is rejected from the chiller condenser into the liquid of the
 HRL. This hot liquid then rejects its heat to the environment, through dry coolers or
 cooling towers. This loop may also be called the Condenser Loop. When the liquid-
 cooled chillers are not operating (in cool weather), the HRL and the FCS may be merged

 106

 and the chillers bypassed. With air-cooled chillers, the HRL does not exist; heat in the
 refrigerant vapor is transferred directly to the cooling air through he air-cooled
 condenser.

Underground Thermal Energy Storage (UTES) Loop:

 The UTES loop circulates fluid between the UTES system and chiller evaporators, dry
 coolers, or CDUs depending on the operational mode for either data center cooling or
 UTES charging.

Chip Cooling System:

Air-Cooled Chips:

 In air-cooled data centers, fans drive cold air through cold aisles and through the
 computing racks themselves to cool the chips. The air is cooled by cold water from the
 FWS cooling loop. Heat is exchanged between the water and air in a Computer Room Air
 Handler (CRAH).

Computer Room Air Handler (CRAH):

 The CRAH is a heat exchanger that exchanges heat between the primary cooling loop and
 cold air in the cold aisles of air-cooled data centers. Recent in-room heat exchangers
 (IRHX) and air-assisted liquid cooling (AALC) are variants of CRAHs.

 107

Direct-to-Chip Liquid Cooling or DLC Cooling:

 In liquid-cooled data centers, cold fluid in the TCS cooling loop is distributed to the cold
 plates of high-power electronics (e.g., CPUs and GPUs) to cool them.

Coolant Distribution Unit (CDU):

 The CDU is a heat exchanger that exchanges heat between the primary cooling loop and
 the secondary cooling loop for direct-to-chip liquid cooling.

Data Center Cooling Equipment:

Dry Cooler:

 A dry cooler is a heat exchanger for rejecting heat from a liquid to the ambient air. Air is
 moved with fans. Dry coolers do not consume water in the cooling process. The liquid is
 cooled to a temperature that approaches the ambient dry-bulb temperature.

Cooling Tower:

 A cooling tower is used to cool a liquid by rejecting heat to the ambient air. A portion of
 the liquid evaporates, which facilitates efficient heat rejection at the expense of
 consuming water. The liquid is cooled to a temperature that approaches the ambient wet
 bulb temperature.

Liquid-Cooled Chiller:

 A mechanical chiller in which the condenser rejects heat to water in the heat rejection
 loop. The warmed water is then cooled in a dry cooler or cooling tower.

Air-Cooled Chiller:

 A mechanical chiller in which the condenser rejects heat directly into the ambient air.
 The air is moved by fans that are part of the chiller system. Some air-cooled chillers have
 a ‘free-cooling’ mode in which the chiller compressor is bypassed during low ambient
 temperatures to reduce electrical consumption.

Underground Thermal Energy Storage (UTES):

 An underground structure that can accept heat or cold – thermal energy – and persist it
 for hours, days, or weeks.

Data Center Power Distribution Equipment:

Power Distribution Units (PDUs):

 Electrical equipment that control and distribute electrical power, typically sourced from
 Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS), to IT racks.

 108

Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS):

 Electrical equipment that ensures continuity of power availability. UPSs are typically
 deployed at the facility and power distribution levels of the data center.

Main Switchboards (MSBs):

 A central electrical hub that takes incoming power and divides it into multiple circuits for
 different parts of the building’s electrical loads.

Backup Generators:

 On-site generators (gas, diesel, or batteries) used in event of a grid power service
 disruption. In flexible load scenarios, they may be operated in a larger set of
 circumstances.

Data Center Equipment and Space Categorization

Server & Server Racks:

 A server is a computer equipped with processing units and, in some specialized cases,
 accelerators dedicated to compute-intensive tasks. Servers are installed in standardized
 metal frames, or server racks, which organize multiple servers into a compact and
 structured unit, enabling efficient use of space, power, and cooling within a data center.

Storage Racks:

 They are filled with storage nodes optimized for I/O performance, whose role is to store
 and manage data. They feature large collections of storage bays containing rotated hard
 disk drives and solid-state-disks, capable of holding many terabytes of data.

Networking Racks:

 They house network devices such as switches and the cabling that connects them, forming
 a reliable network infrastructure that enables data communication among servers and with
 external networks. In some data center configurations, network devices are installed
 within server racks or deployed as Top-of-Rack switches.

Pod or Module:

 IT racks ( servers, storage, networking) are typically organized into rows, with multiple
 rows sharing cooling supplied by one or more coolant distribution units (CDUs) and
 power delivered by one or more power distribution units (PDUs). This fundamental
 arrangement of racks, power, and cooling infrastructure is known as a pod or module.

 109

Gray Space:

 The space required for non-IT racks or equipment. This space is allocated with an
 overhead for “access to equipment”. Space can be inside data center building or on site.
 Typically includes power distribution/conversion (e.g., PDUs) and cooling equipment
      (e.g., CDUs).

White Space:

 The space requirement for IT racks, including computing, storage, and networking.

Metrics:

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE):

 The ratio of the total facility electrical load to IT load – i.e. (IT load + ancillary load +
 electrical cooling load) / IT load. The PUE tends to be an annual average.

Peak PUE:

 The maximum PUE that occurs during the year.

Partial PUE:

 The partial PUE reflects only the contribution of the cooling power and is therefore the
 ratio of the IT load plus electrical load for cooling to the IT load. Ancillary loads are not
 included in the partial PUE, and the purpose of this metric is to emphasize the magnitude
 of electrical cooling loads relative to the IT load.

Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE):

 The total water consumption of the data center divided by the IT power consumption.

Levelized Cost of Cooling (LCOC):

 The LCOC reflects the average cost to deliver a unit of cooling ($/MWh-th) and is a
 useful metric to compare different cooling systems. The LCOC compares the total cost of
 the cooling system over its lifetime to the quantity of cooling delivered, as defined in
 Appendix D.11.The ratio of the total cost of the cooling system to the annual thermal
 energy that is cooled. The total cost includes capital cost, operations and maintenance
 (O&M) cost, and the cost of electricity to power the cooling system. Annual O&M and
 electricity costs are used, while the capital cost is annualized (or levelized) by
 considering the lifetime of the cooling system and the discount rate.

 110

A.2 Data Center Energy Use
Data centers consume electricity primarily through IT equipment for computation, data storage,
and networking, and the electrical energy is ultimately converted into heat that should be
managed to maintain performance and reliability. Auxiliary systems, such as PDUs and UPS
systems, contribute additional heat through electrical conversion losses, while other facility
systems, including lighting and ventilation, produce relatively minor heat loads. Modern data
centers often operate at high rack power densities, where even small increases in server load can
significantly increase cooling demand, driving higher cooling requirements and energy
consumption. Effective thermal management is thus critical to preventing hardware overheating
or failure, maintaining IT performance, and optimizing operational efficiency.

While air cooling has historically been the primary method for managing heat in data centers,
increasing processing power and the growing use of high-performance GPUs have raised chip-
and rack-level power densities, making air cooling alone insufficient for effective thermal
management. Liquid-cooling technologies, such as cold plates and immersion cooling, are
increasingly adopted because they enable targeted heat removal at the component level and can
support higher numbers of servers and process cores due to superior heat transfer capabilities
(Alissa et al. 2025). Liquid cooling can also enable recovery of waste heat for other uses
(ASHRAE 2021). Despite these advantages, many other components of the data center continue
to generate heat that still relies on air cooling, so most facilities employ a hybrid approach
combining air and liquid cooling (Figure A-1). The optimal ratio of liquid to air cooling depends
on factors such as IT equipment mix, rack power density, facility design, climate conditions, and
potential opportunities for heat reuse. By understanding the strengths and limitations of different
cooling systems, operators can optimize both IT performance and energy efficiency while
maintaining hardware reliability.

Data center cooling technologies can be categorized based on the location and method of heat
removal into primary heat removal systems, cooling production equipment, heat rejection
systems, distribution systems, and economization strategies.

A.3 Primary Heat Removal Technologies
Direct-to-Chip (Cold Plate) Cooling
Direct-to-chip cooling removes heat directly from CPUs, GPUs, and other high-power
components using liquid-cooled cold plates (Figure A-2 (a)). Coolant is circulated through a
closed loop and transfers heat to a facility water system or heat exchanger. This approach enables
significantly higher rack power densities than air cooling and improves thermal control at the
component level. Direct-to-chip cooling is increasingly deployed in AI-specialized and high-
performance computing environments and is often integrated with air-cooled systems for
residual heat removal.

Immersion Cooling
Immersion cooling submerges IT equipment in a dielectric fluid that absorbs heat directly from
electronic components (Figure A-2(b) and (c)). Heat is removed through fluid circulation and
transferred to an external heat exchanger. Immersion cooling offers very high heat transfer
efficiency and supports extreme rack power densities, while reducing reliance on traditional air-
handling systems. Adoption is currently concentrated in AI-focused and specialized facilities due
to hardware compatibility requirements and operational considerations.

A.4 Cooling Production Technologies
Air-Cooled Chiller
Air-cooled chillers generate chilled water using refrigerant compressors and reject heat directly
to ambient air. These systems are relatively simple to install and do not require cooling towers or
condenser water systems. Air-cooled chillers are commonly used in small and midsize data
centers and in locations with limited water availability, though their efficiency decreases during
high ambient temperatures.

Water-Cooled Chiller
Water-cooled chillers produce chilled water and reject heat through condenser water systems

performance at scale than air-cooled chillers. Water-cooled chiller plants dominate large co-
location and hyperscale facilities and are well suited for integration with economization and heat
recovery strategies.

Direct Expansion (DX) Cooling Units
DX systems use refrigerant circulated directly between indoor cooling units and outdoor
condensers to remove heat. These systems are compact and commonly deployed in smaller data
centers, edge facilities, and legacy installations. While DX systems offer simplicity and lower
initial cost, they generally provide lower efficiency and limited scalability compared to chilled-
water systems.

Adiabatic Cooling Systems
Adiabatic cooling systems enhance air- or water-based cooling by using evaporative processes to
reduce ambient air temperature during peak conditions. These systems reduce energy
consumption and water usage compared to traditional evaporative cooling towers. Adiabatic
solutions are often used to improve chiller or dry cooler performance in warm climates.

 114

A.5 Heat Rejection Technologies
Dry Cooler
Dry coolers reject heat using ambient air without evaporative water loss. They are typically
paired with water-cooled chillers or liquid-cooling systems and eliminate the need for water
treatment. Dry coolers are well suited for cooler climates and facilities seeking to minimize
water consumption.

Cooling Tower
Cooling towers reject heat through evaporative cooling of condenser water. They enable lower
condenser temperatures and higher chiller efficiency, particularly in large facilities. Cooling
towers require water treatment and ongoing maintenance but remain a dominant heat rejection
method in hyperscale data centers.

Thermosyphon System
Thermosyphon systems reject heat from liquid-cooling loops using natural circulation driven by
density differences, without mechanical pumping. Heat evaporates in an evaporator, rises to a
condenser where it releases heat to the ambient environment, and returns by gravity.
Thermosyphons are typically used alongside dry coolers or chillers, most effective in cooler
climates, and suitable for systems with higher supply and return temperatures.

Heat Recovery
Heat recovery systems capture waste heat from data center cooling processes for reuse in
building heating, district energy systems, or industrial applications. These systems are
increasingly viable in liquid-cooled data centers due to higher return water temperatures.

A.6 Distribution Systems
Compute Room Air Handler (CRAH)
CRAH units circulate air across chilled-water coils to remove heat from data center spaces. They
are commonly used in chilled-water facilities and provide precise temperature and humidity
control. CRAHs are typically deployed with hot-aisle and cold-aisle containment.

 116

Computer Room Air Conditioner (CRAC)
CRAC units use DX refrigeration to cool and circulate air within data center spaces. They are
self-contained and widely used in smaller or legacy facilities but are generally less efficient than
CRAH systems.

In-Row Cooling
In-row cooling units are placed between server racks to reduce air mixing and improve cooling
effectiveness. They are well suited for high-density environments and can be air- or liquid-
cooled.

Rear Door Heat Exchanger
Rear door heat exchangers mount directly to server racks and remove heat from exhaust air using
liquid-cooled coils. These systems reduce room-level cooling demand and serve as a hybrid air-
liquid solution for high-density racks.

A.6 Economization and Energy-Saving Technologies
Air-Side Economizer
Air-side economizers use outdoor air for cooling when ambient conditions permit, reducing
mechanical cooling energy. Proper filtration and humidity control are required to maintain
indoor air quality.

Water-Side Economizer
Water-side economizers produce chilled water using cooling towers or dry coolers without
operating chillers. These systems are widely deployed in water-cooled plants and offer
substantial energy savings in suitable climates.

Refrigerant Economizer
Refrigerant economizers allow DX systems to bypass compressors during favorable ambient
conditions. While simpler than chilled-water economizers, their energy savings potential is more
limited.

Appendix C. DCGen: A Generative Tool for Canonical
Data Center Configurations
C.1 DCGen: A Generator of Canonical Data Center Designs
Modern data centers consist of complex IT, power, and cooling systems. These systems have
complex interdependencies that create both static and dynamic requirements that determine
appropriate configurations and affect performance capabilities of a data center. As the
complexity and performance requirements have increased, with growing power density and data
center scale, it is increasingly difficult for researchers to create realistic data center designs. As a
result, many studies have been done using outdated (Barroso et al. 2019) or unrealistic designs.

To solve this problem, we created DCGen, a model-driven tool that generates realistic data
center designs that each include full IT, power, and cooling systems. Because data centers are
increasingly specialized to workload, DCGen generates four data center types: AI training,
mixed AI training and inference, AI inference, and cloud. For each type, data centers of different
scales can be generated (1 MW, 100 MW, 10 GW, etc.). DCGen is a data center design generator
and model generator that makes the following key contributions:

 • It captures rack- and data center-level properties of computer servers, networking, etc.,
 enabling a representative modeling of power and energy dynamics. These properties are:
 rack arrangements per node type (e.g., GPUs, storage…), rack power density, IT power
 capacity, “white space” (or IT space) requirements
 • It models cooling and power distribution hardware setup optimized for space or power,
 and the associated power demand and “gray space” (or non-IT space) requirement,
 enabling systematic power-efficiency and space management analysis
 • It provides projections to future data center configurations (2027, 2029), allowing
 modeling and study of future data center systems.

The data center designs output are JSON-encoded, incorporate canonical IT configurations and
commercial “gray space” components, and can be directly input into other simulation tools.

The tool can generate configurations based on two parameters (design targets): (1) a specific
number of racks (representing the desired compute capability), and (2) a target electrical power
use. DCGen takes as input a target compute capability or power capacity, one of four canonical
data center types (AI training, mixed training and inference, AI inference, and cloud), the date of
operation (2024, 2027, or 2029), the specification of optimization objective (space or power),
redundancy and safety margins for cooling and power systems. It then generates the data center
configuration (including IT, cooling and power systems), output in JSON. Moreover, DCGen
provides detailed metrics such as rack power density, electrical loads, and space requirements as
described above, enabling a wealth of research.

DCGen was used in the research throughout the Cold UTES project, and generally enables
computing and data center research using realistic IT, cooling, and power system configurations.

D.2 Data Center Load Profiles
Overview and Purpose
A library of data center load profiles was developed for this project. This library consists of
synthetic annual electrical load time series developed for data center planning and grid
integration analysis. The load shapes were generated using the Shape Maker tool (Grahovac et al.
2026). The tool addresses a critical gap in the energy research and planning ecosystem: the lack
of readily available, high-fidelity electricity load shape data for data centers. Rather than relying
on proprietary metered data that is difficult to obtain and share, Shape Maker generates synthetic
load profiles grounded in physical observation and validated by industry expertise, enabling
meaningful energy analysis without access to sensitive operational data.

 126

The library provided here comprises 90 distinct annual load profiles, each delivered as a 15-
minute resolution time series spanning a full calendar year. Each profile represents a specific
combination of facility size, usage type, cooling technology, noise level, and diurnal pattern, and
is expressed as a fraction of nominal IT capacity so that it can be scaled to any facility size. The
profiles also include disaggregation of the total facility electrical demand into individual power
components, including IT sub-loads, lighting, and power conversion losses.

Modeling Approach and Methodology
The load shapes are built on a two-level generative architecture: a daily load profile generator
and an annual load profile assembler that iterates over each day of the year, constructing the full
time series from daily building blocks. Each daily profile is controlled by a rich set of
parameters:

 ● Base load level — the minimum continuous facility draw, around which all variation is
 defined
 ● Peak parameters — each peak is defined by a start time, a duration, and a part-load
 fraction, with support for multiple peaks per day
 ● Noise — a uniformly distributed random variation added at every time step, scaled as a
 percentage of the base load
 ● Transition duration and smoothness — control the steepness and length of the ramp
 between baseline and peak, enabling everything from sharp step-like switching to gradual
 behavioral shifts
 ● Weekday/weekend differentiation — separate peak timing windows, load levels, and
 frequency distributions are applied depending on the day type
 ● Holiday handling — recognized U.S. federal holidays are automatically treated as
 weekend-type days.

The annual generator iterates day by day, with day-to-day variability in noise intensity
modulated within a minimum-to-maximum band, producing a natural heterogeneity across the
annual calendar consistent with real facility operations. A base load correction is applied
internally to ensure that, after accounting for all diurnal peak contributions over weekdays and
weekends throughout the year, the time-averaged load equals the intended annual utilization
target. This yields a physically consistent load series without requiring the user to manually solve
for the underlying baseline parameter.

Load Shape Archetypes
The library covers a structured combination of facility and operational characteristics across four
dimensions: facility size, usage type, cooling technology, and load variability pattern. Together,
these produce 90 distinct annual load profiles.

Facility size reflects the scale of the data center and influences both the degree of load
aggregation and the range of cooling technologies considered:

 ● Large — includes all usage types and both cooling technologies

 127

 ● Medium — includes all usage types and both cooling technologies
 ● Small — includes all usage types; air cooling only.

Usage type reflects the computational workload hosted by the facility, which determines the
characteristic base load and temporal utilization pattern:

 ● Inference — moderate and relatively steady utilization, serving real-time model inference
 requests
 ● Training — high utilization driven by intensive, time-bounded model training jobs
 ● Mixed — intermediate utilization reflecting a blend of inference and training workloads.

Cooling technology affects the breakdown of IT power subcomponents, particularly the
contribution of server fans:

 ● Air-cooled — conventional rack cooling using server fans, available for all facility sizes
 ● Liquid-cooled — direct liquid cooling eliminating fan power, available for large and
 medium facilities.

Load variability style is defined by two orthogonal dimensions, noise level and diurnal pattern,
applied uniformly across all facility types.

Noise level captures the degree of short-term variability in the load signal:

 ● Low noise (3%–7% of baseline) — reflects large, highly aggregated facilities whose
 statistical pooling smooths moment-to-moment fluctuations
 ● High noise (12%–18% of baseline) — reflects smaller or more specialized facilities with
 more volatile demand.

Diurnal pattern defines the time-of-day shape of the load. This library represents three diurnal
patterns:

 ● Flat — no meaningful time-of-day variation, characteristic of facilities running
 continuous, always-on workloads such as large-scale model training
 ● Business diurnal — a 10%-above-baseline peak during business hours (8 AM–5 PM) on
 weekdays, reflecting facilities whose compute demand tracks human working schedules,
 such as enterprise inference workloads
 ● Customer diurnal — a 10%-above-baseline evening peak (4–9 PM) on both weekdays
 and weekends, modeling facilities whose load tracks consumer internet activity patterns.

Power Component Disaggregation
Each load profile in the library is disaggregated into individual power flow components,
decomposing the total facility electrical demand into three classes:

 ● IT load components — sub-elements of the IT load that scale proportionally with total IT
 demand, including server processors (CPUs and GPUs, 75% of IT load), embedded

 130

 server fans (10%), other server components including memory (10%), and miscellaneous
 IT equipment such as storage and networking (5%). Note that while these fans provide
 cooling services, they are metered at the server plug load, and thus for the purposes of
 facility accounting are typically accounted as IT load for the purposes of PUE
 calculation. For liquid-cooled facilities, fan power is eliminated and the remaining
 components are rescaled accordingly. Values for component share of AI specialized
 server draw for training taken from (Govind et al. 2023; Newkirk et al. 2025), and with
 inference values derived from (Patel et al. 2024). Values for conventional servers based
 on (Barroso et al. 2019).

 ● Flat loads — fixed quantities expressed as a percentage of nominal IT capacity regardless
 of instantaneous utilization, with lighting as the primary example, ranging from 0.1% of
 IT capacity for large facilities to 1% for small ones.

 ● Load-following efficiency losses — overhead from power conversion infrastructure that
 scales with IT load, principally power distribution losses (default 90% efficiency) and
 UPS losses (default 95% efficiency), each contributing a proportional overhead term to
 the total facility draw. See (Barroso et al. 2019) for more detailed discussion.

D.3 Heat Exchanger Model
Heat exchangers are assumed to be counter-flow devices defined by the user in terms of an
average approach temperature – that is, the temperature difference between the hot fluid and the
cold fluid. This provides an intuitive way to set the temperatures in the system. The heat
exchanger design point performance is quantified in terms of the effectiveness and the number of
heat transfer units (NTUs). Off-design performance is approximated using the effectiveness-
NTU approach. In off-design conditions, the mass flow rates and inlet temperatures may change.
From this information, the NTU is updated and the effectiveness found from established
correlations for counter-flow heat exchangers. The outlet temperatures are then calculated.

A pressure drop is also incurred in the heat exchanger and this is simply defined as a fixed
fractional drop in pressure.

D.8 RTES Model
Reservoir thermal energy storage (RTES) operates cyclically, with periods of cold fluid injection
for energy storage followed by production to supply data center cooling. In physical systems,
RTES performance is governed by the coupled evolution of temperature and pressure within the
reservoir. Key performance metrics include recoverable cooling capacity, produced fluid
temperature, thermal breakthrough, and reservoir pressure response. In this study, RTES is

 134

represented using a reduced-order formulation for system-level techno-economic analysis of data
center cooling. Rather than resolving the full spatial and temporal evolution of temperature and
pressure, the model captures RTES behavior through four primary components:

 1. Seasonal energy balance to determine required storage capacity

 2. Bulk volumetric heat capacity formulation to determine required reservoir volume and
 well spacing

 3. Empirical reservoir impedance relationship to estimate hourly reservoir pressure drop and
 pumping energy

 4. Hourly dispatch algorithm that governs charging and discharging periods.

This approach enables computationally efficient integration of RTES into hybrid cooling system
simulations while preserving the primary dependencies of storage capacity and pumping
requirements on reservoir properties.

D.9 BTES Model
Borehole thermal energy storage has been implemented with simplifying assumptions for general
results showcasing the overarching influence of using BTES. The design of a BTES system
relies heavily on the ground thermal properties, the layout of the system, the borehole design, the
operating conditions and the borehole depth. The focus of this simple model is to determine the
number of boreholes needed to meet the cooling design requirements of the data center.
Determining the number of boreholes directly relates to the capital cost and operational cost of
the system, and therefore a good first estimate for comparison with other cooling
systems/technologies.

A suitable path forward for the BTES model for this work was to estimate the amount of heat
transfer per length of borehole and use this to calculate the number of boreholes needed for the
specific load. Importantly, heat transfer per length of a borehole has been researched for various
types of subsurface formations and found to vary greatly depending on the pipe diameter, flow
rate, subsurface details, and type of tube within the borehole (U-tube, double U-tube). A
generally good estimate for double U-tubes is between 20 – 80 W/m.

Detailed BTES estimate
One way to enhance the estimation of the number of boreholes is by using GHEDesigner, an
open-source python code developed to quickly optimize the borehole field and layout based on
user information. The code can run for multiple years and can optimize many different borehole
layouts and pipe configurations. Importantly, this code uses pygfunction, another open-source
tool used for calculating the thermal response of a borehole taking into account more accurately
the subsurface temperatures and drifts. While both of these tools have benefits, they are not
specifically geared toward long-term energy storage. Further limitations of this code are that the
user must input the load over the year, and the user must provide limits on specific values such
as the borehole length, and the exit fluid temperature of the borehole system. While the code runs
quickly it is granular, resulting in monthly temperatures, which is not fine enough for this
particular study. The code, however, does estimate the number of boreholes needed and was used
to verify the methods in this simplified system code.
To gain a more informed understanding of the subsurface response a detailed finite element
model was created. Using FEFLOW a subset of the borehole field was modeled to understand

 140

the fluid temperatures in the borehole and the resulting ground temperatures. Large models using
this software are computationally expensive, for instance a seasonal storage simulation with 100
borehole heat exchangers may take 8 hours of simulation time. Knowing this the FEFLOW
model was carefully constructed to allow for insight into a borehole field without modeling a full
configuration.
A steady-state model was utilized as a calibration to establish subsurface conditions before the
BTES field and borehole heat exchangers (BHEs) were installed for further simulations. The
transient-state model occurs during operation of the field, while the steady-state model is used as
the basis for further simulation. The boundary condition for average surface temperature at the
starting season for the transient model was 18oC based on the case study for Virginia. This
temperature was also used to calibrate the steady-state model conditions.
FEFLOW was then used in three different approaches to estimate the response of a BHE and
extrapolate to a field. First a single BHE was used to approximate seasonal storage depletion
rates and rapidly test the design and subsurface parameters that are relevant to storage
optimization. Second, a multi-BHE 5 spot layout was created to understand the center-field BHE
behavior. This approach allowed for analyzing the storage capacity and quantifying the bottom-
field thermal losses through observation points located beneath the central borehole. Lastly, an
edge loss layout was designed to approximate the thermal losses at the edge of a BTES field.
This provides insight into the behavior and effective capacity of the edge boreholes over time.

D.10 Dispatch Algorithms
RTES operation over the annual simulation horizon (8,760 hours) is determined using an hourly
dispatch algorithm that identifies active charging (injection) and discharging (production)
periods. Operational feasibility is enforced through an annual energy consistency check, ensuring
that the total discharged energy does not exceed the fraction η of energy previously injected.
This framework avoids explicit resolution of intra-seasonal thermal front evolution, transient
storage limits, or time-dependent breakthrough dynamics.

The preliminary hourly storage power 𝑄𝑄[𝑡𝑡] is first computed based on system conditions, which
may include ambient temperature, cooling demand, electricity tariffs, or wholesale electricity
prices. Active charging and discharging hours are then selected using a ranking-and-trimming
procedure, prioritizing hours according to one of three dispatch strategies:

 1. Temperature-based dispatch:
 Charging occurs during the coldest hours, and discharging during the hottest hours,
 ensuring that storage is utilized when most effective for supplying cooling. Hourly
 ambient temperatures are sorted, and energy is cumulatively allocated until the total
 recoverable energy is reached.

 2. Tariff-based dispatch:
 Charging is prioritized during off-peak electricity tariff periods, and discharging during
 peak periods. Hours are ranked according to the tariff schedule, and energy is allocated
 until the seasonal energy constraint is satisfied.

 3. Price-based dispatch:
 RTES responds to wholesale electricity price signals. Charging is favored during low-
 price hours, and discharging occurs during high-price hours. Cumulative energy
 allocation ensures that the seasonal recovery efficiency is respected.

for BTES, a more complete analysis is required.

A limitation of the BTES model is the assumptions for the subsurface properties and temperature
drift within the subsurface. The BTES model in this work does not include specific details on the
piping materials or ground properties. Further, the transient nature of the fluid flow and the heat
transfer is not explicitly included in this work. Additional details on the BTES model,
limitations, and alternative approaches can be found in Appendix C.

E.1 BTES Case Study Results
Key design parameters for the BTES are the flow rate per borehole (1 kg/s), borehole length (250
m), diameter (0.17 m), spacing (3.5 m), and cost (30 $/m). Other parameters, such as the
temperature of the ground and fluid, the thermal conductivity of the ground, fluid, and borehole,
and borehole design (single U-loop, double U-loop, etc.) affect the heat transfer per unit length.
Together these factors affect the thermal performance and the number of boreholes.

BTES results for the 12 case studies are shown in Table E-1 and Figure E-2 and only consider
the scenario where chillers do not charge the UTES. The figure shows the electrical cooling load,
the LCOC, and the annual cooling cost. The BTES results follow similar trends to the RTES
results.

BTES uses conductive heat transfer (unlike RTES which uses advection) and this impacts the
energy transfer in the systems and impacts the electrical loads for cooling and associated costs.
The lower heat transfer rates in BTES systems increase the number of boreholes and therefore
increases capital costs and the LCOC compared to RTES. However, BTES has lower pumping
consumption compared to RTES, so peak electrical cooling load and operational costs are
reduced– but not enough to outweigh the increased LCOC. Importantly, borehole cost is
uncertain and has a strong influence on LCOC, as shown in Figure E-1.

E.2 Sensitivity Results for BTES
When the Cold UTES system uses BTES, results generally follow the same trends as those
presented for RTES. However, there are key differences regarding flow rates and costs; those
results are summarized here.

Sensitivity to Borehole Flow Rate and TCS Supply Temperature
Pumping power for BTES systems is relatively low and does not vary significantly with flow
rate. Therefore, BTES flow rates do not have a strong influence on peak cooling power or the
potential increase in IT capacity. However, increasing the flow rate reduces the number of
boreholes, which therefore reduces the LCOC.

Appendix F. Additional Inputs and Outputs for Grid
Impacts
This appendix contains additional inputs and outputs from Section 4 on the bulk electricity
system modeling and impacts of Cold UTES.

The Cold UTES grid impacts focus on two regions: Loudoun County, Virginia, and Maricopa
County, Arizona. Loudoun County lies within the PJM Interconnection and has one of the
highest concentrations of data centers in the United States, with continued growth expected due
to its well-established infrastructure. Maricopa County, served by Salt River Project (SRP), is a
fast-growing region for new data center development and is expected to see data center demand
double in the next 5 years. These regions offer a contrast in utility models: PJM operates as an
ISO with competitive wholesale electricity markets, whereas SRP is a non-ISO, vertically
integrated utility with a captive ratepayer base. This distinction affects how each region would
value system- or grid-level solutions. Additionally, the two regions provide geographic and
climatic diversity, which influences the value proposition of replacing adiabatic cooling with
Cold UTES.

For each region, we estimate IT capacity for low, medium, and high data center growth scenarios
from 2025 to 2050. Data center capacity values are sourced from the 451 Research Datacenter
Knowledge Base, which maintains a database of data center locations and facility characteristics.
The dataset is limited to stand-alone data centers and for this analysis we exclude cryptocurrency
mining facilities and small or embedded data centers. Data centers in our data set are classified
by space type:

 • Hyperscale: large, single-occupant facilities owned and operated by tech giants)
 • Powered Shell: landlord provides building, power, and connectivity while the tenant
 installs IT and cooling equipment
 • Wholesale: dedicated, customizable blocks of space and power leased to a single
 customer under long-term contract)
 • Retail: smaller portions of space such as racks or cages leased to multiple customers with
 shared infrastructure)
 • Telco: facilities owned and operated by telecommunications or service providers.

Each projection scenario spans three time periods: a present-day baseline (2025), near-term
growth (2025–2030), and long-term growth (2030–2050). The present-day baseline represents
the IT capacity of data centers operational at the end of 2025 and is identical across all three
scenarios.

Near-term growth rates are derived from the location database, which captures operational
facilities, facilities under construction, and planned projects based on available announcements.
For each county, we calculated the annual rate of IT capacity growth by space type from 2024
through 2027 and used these rates to project capacity from the 2025 baseline through 2030.
These rates define the medium scenario; the low and high scenarios apply a 30 percent variance
below and above the medium growth rate, respectively.

 153

Long-term growth rates (2030–2050) are applied uniformly across space types. The high
scenario assumes 6 percent annual growth, estimated from historical LBNL 2024 Energy Use
Report data. The medium scenario assumes 3 percent annual growth, and the low scenario
assumes 1 percent annual growth.

F.1 Maricopa County
Maricopa County has experienced significant growth in data center development in recent years,
ranking second nationally in planned data center capacity (JLL, 2024). The market is dominated
by wholesale and hyperscale facilities, reflecting demand from large cloud and technology
companies seeking dedicated, high-capacity space. Much of the recent and anticipated
construction is driven by the rapid expansion of AI workloads, which require high-density
compute infrastructure and substantially greater power per rack than traditional data center
operations. Maricopa County's desert climate also introduces distinct operational considerations,
particularly around water use. With limited water resources, reducing water consumption in data
center cooling is a key priority for both operators and local stakeholders.

Figure ES-9 shows the annual cost savings in Virginia and Arizona, respectively for 2035
through 2050. These values are presented to be more comparable to the cost savings reported in

balance constraint in each time step and multiplying it by the electricity used for cooling in that
time step, and then summing the values across the year. The savings are then the difference in
costs between the scenario with RTES and the scenario without RTES. The capacity cost is
calculated by taking the reserve margin price (the shadow price from the reserve margin

cost savings is again the difference in costs between the scenarios with and without RTES.

day) rather than using the approach of marginal value times quantity consumed.

 • Section 2 uses 2024 Cambium data for electricity prices in a price-taker approach.

 so it will include newer inputs for fuel prices, demand growth, policy, interconnection
 queue limits, etc.
 • Section 2 focuses on 2030 results, while Section 4 considers results across the lifetime of
 the system, including how the value of the system changes over time.
