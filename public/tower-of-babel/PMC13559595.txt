Dietary risk factors for lung cancer: an umbrella review of systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zirong Wen, Zhimou Zhang, Chenyang Wu, Dawei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer poses a substantial global public health burden, and dietary factors represent potentially modifiable targets for primary prevention. This study evaluates the methodological quality, risk of bias, and reliability of evidence related to dietary risk factors for lung cancer through an umbrella review and provides a comprehensive overview of the identified risk factors.MethodsThis review protocol has been registered on PROSPERO. A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews to collect meta-analyses on dietary factors and lung cancer risk published up to March 2026. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) tool, and the evidence strength of each outcome was rated based on the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system.ResultsThere were 57 meta-analyses included, covering 57 diet-related factors. The association between folate and reduced risk of lung cancer is supported by moderate-quality evidence, while most other factors (such as beta-carotene, red and processed meat, etc.) have low or very low quality evidence. A healthy dietary pattern is associated with a lower risk of lung cancer risk, while a Western/meat diet pattern and a high glycemic load diet are positively associated with risk.ConclusionsThe quality of evidence for the association between most dietary factors and lung cancer risk is low, with only folate showing moderate-quality evidence, indicating that current research is not sufficient to support strong causal inference. In the future, it is necessary to conduct well-designed prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to provide a higher-quality scientific basis for precise nutritional prevention of lung cancer.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 57 dietary factors examined in this umbrella review, only folate intake was supported by moderate-quality evidence for reducing lung cancer risk [risk ratio (RR) =0.47, 13 randomized controlled trials (RCTs)]; all other factors (including beta-carotene, red/processed meat, vitamins, and dietary patterns) had low or very low evidence quality. Healthy dietary patterns (e.g., Mediterranean) were associated with lower risk, whereas Western/meat, pro-inflammatory, and high glycemic load diets were associated with higher risk. No significant association was found for 19 factors (e.g., lycopene, green tea, dairy).

What is known and what is new?

• Individual meta-analyses have suggested links between various nutrients/foods and lung cancer, but results are inconsistent.

• This is the first umbrella review to systematically grade the evidence across all dietary risk factors. It demonstrates that the vast majority of associations rests on low or very low certainty evidence, with folate being the only exception. It also clarifies which dietary patterns show more consistent signals than single nutrients.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should avoid presenting weak associations as causal; smoking cessation remains the core intervention, and overall healthy dietary patterns can be recommended with caution.

• Researchers should prioritise well-designed prospective cohorts, Mendelian randomisation studies, and randomised trials targeting dietary patterns rather than individual nutrients to improve evidence quality for nutritional prevention of lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer, originating from the alveolar epithelium or bronchial mucosa, is characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis (1). Its clinical manifestations are often insidious and stage-dependent: early-stage disease may be asymptomatic or present with nonspecific symptoms, whereas advanced disease is associated with systemic manifestations and poor survival (2). Lung cancer remains a major global public health burden. According to GLOBOCAN 2024 estimates, it accounted for approximately 2.6 million new cases and 1.9 million deaths worldwide and remained the leading cause of cancer mortality (3). Although smoking is the principal risk factor, lung cancer also occurs in non-smokers, highlighting the potential importance of modifiable environmental and lifestyle factors, including diet (4,5). Given the poor prognosis associated with late-stage diagnosis, identifying modifiable risk factors for primary prevention is of considerable importance (6,7).

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported associations between dietary exposures and lung cancer risk. Mediterranean and prudent dietary patterns have been associated with a lower risk of lung cancer (8), whereas high-fat diets and greater red and processed meat consumption may be associated with increased risk (9,10). High glycemic load (GL) diets have also been proposed as potential risk factors, possibly through insulin-related pathways (11). Biological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and exposure to carcinogenic compounds generated during meat processing or high-temperature cooking, may partly explain these associations (8,12-14).

Recent prospective evidence has suggested inverse associations between lung cancer risk and higher intakes of total fruits and vegetables, vegetables, fruits, cruciferous vegetables, green leafy vegetables, and citrus fruits (15-21). Similarly, adherence to the Mediterranean diet and higher intake of dietary fiber and selected vitamins, such as vitamin D, have been associated with a lower risk of lung cancer (17,22-26). These associations may be related to antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cell-cycle regulatory effects.

However, findings across conventional meta-analyses remain inconsistent. Effect sizes, study quality, heterogeneity, and susceptibility to bias vary substantially, and conclusions regarding the same exposure, such as β-carotene, may differ across studies. Moreover, the certainty of evidence for dietary factors has not been assessed using a uniform framework. Therefore, an umbrella review is needed to systematically synthesize existing meta-analyses, evaluate their methodological quality and potential biases, and determine which dietary associations are supported by more robust evidence.

In this umbrella review, we synthesized meta-analyses of dietary risk factors for lung cancer incidence. To our knowledge, this is the first umbrella review to comprehensively assess the certainty and methodological quality of evidence across dietary factors associated with lung cancer risk. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (27) (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1439/rc).

Methods

Design and registration

This umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the methodological recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis for Umbrella Reviews (28) and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews (29). The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251177129).

Eligibility criteria and information sources

We included meta-analyses evaluating associations between dietary factors and lung cancer risk. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews were searched from inception to March 2026, and reference lists of eligible studies were manually screened. Meta-analyses of interventional and observational studies were eligible; non-English publications and studies based on animal experiments or cell culture models were excluded.

When multiple meta-analyses assessed the same exposure, we selected the most recent study if publication dates differed by >24 months. For studies published within 24 months, priority was given to the meta-analysis including the largest number of prospective cohort studies; if these were equal, the study with the higher A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) score was selected. Where the most recent meta-analysis lacked dose–response data, an additional eligible study reporting such data was retained.

Search strategy

The database search strategy was as follows: ((((“Diet” (Mesh)) OR ((Diets) OR (food))) AND ((“Risk Factors” (Mesh)) OR (((((((((((Risk Factor) OR (Population at Risk)) OR (Populations at Risk)) OR (Risk Scores)) OR (Risk Score)) OR (Risk Factor Scores)) OR (Risk Factor Score)) OR (Health Correlates)) OR (Social Risk Factors)) OR (Social Risk Factor)) OR (Incidence)))) AND ((“Lung Neoplasms” (Mesh)) OR (((((((((Pulmonary Neoplasm) OR (Pulmonary Neoplasms)) OR (Lung Neoplasm)) OR (Lung Cancer)) OR (Lung Cancers)) OR (Cancer of Lung)) OR (Pulmonary Cancer)) OR (Pulmonary Cancers)) OR (Cancer of the Lung)))) AND (systematic review OR meta-analysis) (20).

Methodological quality assessment and data extraction

Two reviewers independently assessed methodological quality using AMSTAR and extracted data; disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework and classified as high, moderate, low, or very low (30).

Extracted data included study population, dietary exposure, author and publication year, number and design of included studies, sample size and cases, effect estimates [risk ratio (RR), odds ratio (OR), or hazard ratio (HR)] with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), meta-analytic model, heterogeneity, and small-study effects. Dose-response results were also extracted when available. Where reported by the eligible meta-analyses, we additionally extracted results stratified by smoking status, histological subtype, and molecular subtype. However, because these subgroup definitions were inconsistently reported and subtype-specific estimates were generally unavailable, no separate quantitative synthesis according to histological or genomic subtype was prespecified.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Effect estimates were synthesized using fixed- or random-effects models, as appropriate. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Egger’s or Begg’s tests were used to assess small-study effects when at least 10 studies were available (31). Sensitivity analyses were undertaken, where possible, for outcomes supported by high- or moderate-certainty evidence. A P value <0.10 indicated statistically significant heterogeneity, whereas P<0.05 was considered statistically significant for other analyses. Analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 and Stata 19.0.

Moderate-quality evidence

A meta-analysis published by Qin summarized 13 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which suggested that higher folic acid intake was linked to a lower risk of lung cancer (RR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.23–0.94). The study involved 49,406 participants, including 16,718 lung cancer patients (53).

Low-quality evidence

Seven associations were supported by low-certainty evidence. Beta-carotene supplementation was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer (RR 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06–1.26) (32). Higher intakes of red and processed meat and processed meat alone were also associated with increased risk (RR 1.20, 95% CI: 1.09–1.33; and RR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.20, respectively) (8). In contrast, higher intakes of fruit and vitamin E were associated with reduced risk (RR 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.94; and RR 0.84, 95% CI: 0.76–0.93, respectively) (16,51). Higher dietary inflammatory index scores were associated with increased lung cancer risk (RR 1.14, 95% CI: 1.07–1.22) (17) (Figure 2).

Very low-quality evidence

Most associations were supported by very-low-certainty evidence (Figure 3; Table 1). Potential inverse associations were reported for vitamin C, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), soy, isoflavones, fruits and vegetables, carrots, vitamins A and D, calcium, tea, citrus fruits, healthy dietary patterns, yogurt, dietary fiber, nuts, cruciferous vegetables, flavonoids, and soybeans (15,17,24,25,34-42,49,50). Healthy/prudent dietary patterns were associated with lower lung cancer risk (OR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.51–0.83), whereas Western/meat-based dietary patterns were associated with higher risk (OR 1.39, 95% CI: 1.17–1.65) (43).

Potential positive associations were reported for high meat/protein diets, coffee intake, alcohol-related dietary patterns, red meat, high glycemic index (GI) or GL diets, and fruit consumption in a Japanese population (8,10,11,17,44,47). Dose-response findings for vitamin C, PUFAs, soy, and isoflavones are summarized in Table 1 (36,37,50). Detailed effect estimates, sample sizes, study designs, and certainty ratings are provided in Table 1 and Table S1.

Non-significant associations

This study extracted 19 dietary risk factors that were not associated with lung cancer incidence, including lycopene, magnesium, processed meat (non-smoking population), white meat (non-smoking population), organ meat (non-smoking population), fish (non-smoking population), heterocyclic amines (HCAs, non-smoking population), cholesterol, fish, acrylamide, calcium, vitamin D, green tea, vegetables, iron, cautious diet pattern, traditional diet pattern, dairy products, and fat. Currently available evidence does not indicate a significant link between these factors and lung cancer incidence (Table 1). These smoking-stratified results were reported by a limited number of source meta-analyses. Most eligible meta-analyses did not provide separate estimates for current, former, and never-smokers, and adjustment for smoking intensity and duration was inconsistent. Moreover, sufficiently detailed data according to histological subtype or genomic alterations, including kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements, were not available for quantitative synthesis.

Heterogeneity, publication bias, and methodological quality

Among the 57 included meta-analyses, 10 did not report heterogeneity data. Of the remaining studies, 21 had a mean I2 value <50%. Publication-bias data were unavailable for 33 meta-analyses; among the 24 studies reporting Egger’s test, 21 showed no evidence of publication bias and 3 suggested possible publication bias.

The median AMSTAR score was 8 (range, 2–9) (Table 1; Table S2). Most associations were rated as low or very low certainty according to GRADE, and only folic acid was rated as moderate certainty. Sensitivity analyses for the folic acid outcome showed a consistent direction and statistical significance of the association (Table S1).

Discussion

Key findings

This umbrella review synthesized evidence from meta-analyses of dietary factors and lung cancer risk. Lung cancer remains a major global health burden, and diet has received increasing attention as a potentially modifiable factor beyond smoking (3,55-57). However, the certainty of evidence was low or very low for most associations. Only folic acid was supported by moderate-certainty evidence for an inverse association with lung cancer risk. Therefore, the observed associations between dietary exposures and lung cancer should not be interpreted as causal.

Overall, healthy/prudent, Mediterranean, and other plant-rich dietary patterns were associated with lower lung cancer risk, whereas Western/meat-based, pro-inflammatory, alcohol-related, and high-glycemic diets were associated with higher risk (8,11,17,43,47). These findings are broadly consistent with previous evidence, but substantial heterogeneity, residual confounding, particularly by smoking, and differences in dietary assessment limit confidence in individual associations. An additional interpretive limitation is the heterogeneity of lung cancer itself. The associations summarized in this umbrella review largely relate to overall lung cancer incidence, although adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, small-cell lung cancer, and molecularly defined tumors may differ substantially in their relationships with tobacco exposure and other etiologic factors. For example, squamous cell carcinoma is strongly associated with smoking, whereas some oncogene-driven adenocarcinomas, including ALK-rearranged tumors, occur more frequently in never-smokers. Therefore, pooled associations for overall lung cancer may conceal subtype-specific or smoking-dependent relationships. The available meta-analytic literature did not permit reliable separation according to histological or genomic subtype, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly. Biological plausibility should be interpreted in the context of the certainty of the epidemiological evidence. Experimental or mechanistic findings may generate hypotheses but cannot establish that a dietary exposure causes or prevents lung cancer. Accordingly, detailed mechanistic interpretation is most appropriate for associations supported by relatively stronger evidence, whereas mechanisms related to low- or very-low-certainty associations should be regarded as speculative.

Associations between dietary patterns and lung cancer risk

The associations discussed in the following sections were predominantly supported by low- or very-low-certainty evidence. The biological pathways described below should therefore be viewed as possible explanations requiring confirmation, rather than evidence that these dietary factors causally influence lung cancer development. Regarding dietary patterns, they can be broadly categorized into two main types: healthy and unhealthy patterns. Healthy patterns include healthy/moderate dietary patterns, the Mediterranean diet, the alternate healthy eating index, the healthy eating index, and the dietary approaches to stop hypertension. Our results indicated that higher adherence to these dietary patterns was linked to a lower risk of lung cancer (17,43). Conversely, unhealthy patterns include Western/meat dietary patterns, inflammatory dietary patterns, alcohol consumption patterns, the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGL), and the dietary guidelines index (DGI). Greater adherence to these dietary patterns was linked to an increased risk of lung cancer (11,17,43).This study identified a significant association between healthy/cautious dietary habits and lung cancer incidence. This dietary pattern primarily consists of fruits, vegetables, and minimally processed grains. These foods contain high levels of dietary antioxidants, fiber, and anti-inflammatory compounds including flavonoids, carotenoids, polyphenols, and vitamins, which may affect different stages of cancer development (58). Additionally, animal-based experimental studies have explored the potential chemopreventive role of plant-derived polyphenols in lung cancer (59). Another 2025 UK Biobank study incorporating genetic risk and proteomic data found that higher overall plant-based diet index was associated with reduced lung cancer risk, whereas an unhealthy plant-based diet index was associated with elevated risk; proteins enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways may partly mediate these associations (60). The alternative healthy eating index, healthy eating index, and dietary approaches to stop hypertension are highly similar to this healthy/cautious dietary pattern (17), thus sharing similar preventive mechanisms.

The Mediterranean diet, first proposed by Keys et al. in the 1980s and regarded as one of the healthiest dietary patterns (61), may also reduce lung cancer risk. Its benefits are well-documented (62,63) and it emphasizes fruits, vegetables, seafood, legumes, nuts, whole grains, moderate red meat, dairy and wine, with limited processed foods and sugary drinks. It is linked to lower risk of lung, colon, stomach, breast, rectal, esophageal and oral cancers (64-70). Core mechanisms include: (I) Strong antioxidant effects from polyphenols, carotenoids, and vitamins C and E in olive oil, vegetables, fruits and nuts (71), which neutralize free radicals and reduce oxidative DNA damage from tobacco smoke and pollution (50,51,72). (II) Systemic anti-inflammatory effects via olive oil polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids from fish, and spice sulfides; these inhibit nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), lower C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin (IL)-6, and create a tumor-suppressive microenvironment (37,73,74). (III) Regulation of cell growth and apoptosis by phytochemicals from fruits, vegetables, legumes and whole grains, potentially upregulating p53 and suppressing abnormal proliferation (60,75). (IV) Improved metabolic health and gut microbiota: high fiber, low GL and healthy fats help maintain weight and insulin sensitivity, while gut microbial short-chain fatty acids exert anti-inflammatory and immune-regulatory effects (11,76).This study found that Western/meat-based and inflammatory dietary patterns are linked to a higher risk of lung cancer. Western or meat-based and pro-inflammatory dietary patterns typically involve frequent intake of red and processed meat, refined grains, and sugar-rich foods. The overall composition of these diets may contribute to their potential carcinogenic effects. In particular, the carcinogenicity of meat has been linked to several suspected carcinogens, including heterocyclic aromatic amines, N-nitroso compounds, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Many of these substances are formed during meat processing or high-temperature cooking (77). In addition to red and processed meat, recent prospective evidence has highlighted ultra-processed foods as an emerging dietary risk marker. In a large cohort study, higher ultra-processed food consumption was associated with increased risks of lung cancer, including both non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), independent of major lung cancer risk factors and overall diet quality (78). Both dietary patterns involve high consumption of refined grains, which may reduce dietary fiber intake and thereby weaken protective effects against lung cancer. A prospective comprehensive study examining the impact of carbohydrate quality and quantity on lung cancer reported that higher intakes of dietary fiber (RR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.54–0.72) and whole grains (RR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.64–0.83) were linked to a reduced risk of lung cancer (79). Furthermore, high consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages was linked to a 23% higher risk of lung cancer (RR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.04–1.46) (79). This study found that dietary patterns involving alcohol consumption were linked with a higher incidence of lung cancer. The potential mechanisms of the effect of alcohol on lung cancer mainly include the carcinogenic effect caused by the alcohol metabolite acetaldehyde (80), oxidative stress induced by alcohol (81-84), and the induction of cytochrome P450 by alcohol, which affects the metabolism of other carcinogenic precursors (81,85). Recent evidence on alcohol-related exposures remains heterogeneous. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of wine consumption and lung cancer risk reported no statistically significant association in the overall analysis, although subgroup findings varied by smoking status and study design; therefore, alcohol-related dietary patterns should be interpreted cautiously and in the context of smoking and other lifestyle confounders (86). Moreover, a meta-analysis found that a daily intake of 30 g of alcohol slightly increases lung cancer risk compared with non-drinking.A 2025 population-based cohort study reported that higher dietary GI was associated with increased risks of lung cancer, NSCLC, and SCLC, whereas GL showed an inverse association with lung cancer and NSCLC after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that carbohydrate quality should be interpreted together with overall dietary quality. The GL is determined by multiplying the carbohydrate content per serving of food by its GI value (11,87). The mechanisms linking dietary GI and GL to lung cancer development mainly involve glucose metabolism, insulin regulation, and insulin resistance. Long-term intake of high-GI or high-GL foods may induce repeated insulin responses, which may promote insulin resistance and increase the risk of lung cancer (88).

Associations of vitamins and other micronutrients with lung cancer risk

We classify vitamins A, C, D, E, beta-carotene, calcium, folic acid, flavonoids, and isoflavones as dietary trace organic nutrients. Our results suggest a protective association between vitamin A intake and lung cancer incidence. Experimental studies show that dietary antioxidants generally inhibit cancer cell growth (89,90), and vitamin A may contribute to lung cancer prevention through antioxidant activity, induction of detoxification enzymes, inhibition of cell proliferation, and involvement in DNA methylation and repair (91). Both cohort and case-control studies link dietary vitamin A and beta-carotene to reduced lung cancer risk, although beta-carotene supplementation may increase risk (92).

We also found that vitamin D may help reduce lung cancer risk. Beyond its recognized role in bone health (93), vitamin D has anti-tumor properties (94,95), significantly inhibiting proliferation of small cell lung cancer cell lines and inducing apoptosis (95,96), restraining metastatic spread in animals (94,97), and inhibiting angiogenesis and malignant cell migration. Moreover, combined vitamin D and calcium intake reduces lung cancer risk more than vitamin D alone (39), likely because vitamin D receptor (VDR) and calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) share downstream elements (98,99); higher extracellular calcium stabilizes 1,25(OH)2D3 via CYP27B1 and CYP24A1 (100,101), and 1,25(OH)2D3 upregulates CaSR (99,102). Clinical observations further indicate that calcium intervention lowers lung cancer risk in individuals with higher circulating vitamin D levels (103).Our research suggests that vitamins C and E are inversely associated with lung cancer incidence. Vitamin C and vitamin E are among the most common antioxidants found in fruits and vegetables, and they may have chemopreventive effects (50). It is widely believed that vitamins C and E can scavenge free radicals and reduce oxidative DNA damage, thereby inhibiting carcinogenesis (50,104-106).Our research suggests dietary folic acid may be linked to decreased lung cancer incidence. Folate, a water-soluble B vitamin, acts as a cofactor in one-carbon metabolism, providing methyl groups for DNA and other compounds (107,108). Supplementation can enhance immune function and anticancer defense (109), reduce genotoxic effects (110), and lower cancer risk by supporting DNA repair and genomic integrity (111). Major dietary sources are green leafy vegetables, legumes and citrus fruits; their alkaline nature may reduce dietary acid load (DAL). Elevated DAL is associated with higher cancer risk (112), possibly by inhibiting folate activity and disrupting DNA integrity, thereby promoting lung cancer. Folate may counteract these harmful effects. Preclinical studies show folate can inhibit early carcinogenesis by correcting DNA hypomethylation and repairing precancerous lesions (113).

Our research indicates a potential protective association between flavonoid intake and lung cancer. Flavonoids, a class of polyphenolic compounds, can scavenge free radicals and neutralize various oxidants (25,114,115), which may contribute to lower cancer risk. However, this protection is not solely due to direct antioxidant activity (116,117), as dietary flavonoids generally have low bioavailability (plasma concentrations are low), though this varies: isoflavones and quercetin show relatively high bioavailability, while tea polyphenols and anthocyanins are very low (116,118). Flavonoids may also act by modulating enzymatic metabolic pathways—inhibiting carcinogen-activating phase I enzymes (e.g., cytochrome P450) and stimulating phase II detoxifying enzymes [e.g., uridine diphosphate (UDP)-glucuronosyltransferase, glutathione S-transferase, quinone reductase] (119).

Our research suggests soy isoflavones may protect against lung cancer in women. Lung tissue has the highest estrogen receptor transcript levels among non-reproductive tissues (120), with estrogen receptor β highly expressed in normal lung and lung cancer cell lines (121,122). Estradiol stimulates lung adenocarcinoma cell proliferation, an effect suppressed by estrogen receptor antagonists or aromatase inhibitors (123,124). Soy products are major sources of dietary phytoestrogens, primarily isoflavones. Acting like selective estrogen receptor modulators (e.g., tamoxifen), soy isoflavones competitively bind estrogen receptors, preferentially estrogen receptor β, exerting weak estrogenic activity in some tissues and anti-estrogenic effects in others (125-128). They also exhibit estrogen receptor-independent actions: modulating tumor-related signaling pathways, stimulating immune responses, and providing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (129-138). Animal studies show isoflavone supplementation reduces lung tumor incidence and prolongs survival, especially in female mice (139-141).

On the contrary, the present analysis suggests that beta-carotene intake may be associated with a modest increase in lung cancer risk. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have similarly reported that beta-carotene supplementation may elevate lung cancer risk, particularly among smokers and individuals exposed to asbestos (142,143). This may be because carotenoids have a paradoxical effect, potentially enhancing oxidative stress caused by tobacco smoke. Beta-carotene can easily form pro-oxidant oxidation products at high concentrations, especially in the oxidizing environment of the lungs of smokers, which is characterized by increased cellular oxidative stress and decreased antioxidant defenses (32). Antioxidant supplementation may disrupt the redox balance, exert harmful effects on cellular function, and thus promote carcinogenesis (144).

Fruits, vegetables, and lung cancer risk

Previous case-control and cohort studies have confirmed that the consumption of fruits and vegetables may be linked to a reduced risk of lung cancer (17-21). Among them, vegetables rich in carotenoids are particularly noteworthy for their potential to reduce lung cancer risk. An international review jointly conducted by the World Cancer Research Fund and the American Institute for Cancer Research indicated that the intake of fruits and carotenoid-rich foods may be linked to a reduced risk of lung cancer, and the intake of non-starchy vegetables may also reduce this risk (with an evidence level of “limited-suggestive”) (145). As mentioned above, flavonoids, which are widely present in fruits and vegetables, may also play a preventive role against lung cancer.

Our research suggests that cruciferous vegetables may help reduce the risk of lung cancer. Cruciferous vegetables, named for their four equally sized petals arranged in a cross shape, include cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, and other members of the Brassicaceae family. They contain various anti-cancer components, including glucosinolates [precursors of isothiocyanates (ITCs)] and indole-3-carbinol (I3C), both of which may help lower lung cancer risk (15). Animal research has shown that ITCs primarily inhibit the development of lung cancer by suppressing the activity of phase I enzymes (such as cytochrome P450) that metabolize tobacco-related carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and by enhancing the detoxifying activity of phase II enzymes (such as glutathione S-transferase, GST) (146,147). Our study found that carrot intake is inversely associated with lung cancer incidence. Evidence from earlier research suggests that carrots may contribute to the prevention of cancer (148-150). The carotenoids found in carrots may lower the risk of lung cancer through several biological mechanisms. Firstly, carotenoids have been reported to inhibit tumor development (151,152). Existing evidence suggests that retinoic acid has an inhibitory effect on lung cancer cells. It can increase the expression of microRNA miR-512-3p, and the overexpression of miR-512-3p not only inhibits the adhesion, migration, and invasion of tumor cells in non-small cell lung cancer A549 and H1299 cell lines, but also reduces the expression of the DOCK3 protein (153). Furthermore, certain types of carotenoids can be converted into vitamin A, which plays an important role in cell differentiation and may contribute to cancer prevention (154).

Our results indicate that the intake of citrus fruits is inversely associated with lung cancer incidence. Citrus fruits provide a diverse mixture of components that may possess anticancer properties, including vitamin C, flavonoids, hesperidin, carotenoids, folate, and dietary fiber (155). Numerous studies have reported that certain major components can exert anticancer effects via multiple mechanisms. Vitamin C represents one of the key components among these compounds. Because this vitamin cannot be synthesized by the human body, it must be regularly obtained from dietary sources. Although vitamin C is present in various foods, only a limited number of fruits and vegetables contain high concentrations, with citrus fruits recognized as an important source (156). This nutrient may therefore be one of the main components responsible for the anticancer effects of citrus fruits. Vitamin C can scavenge free radicals and reactive oxygen species and also regenerate other antioxidant vitamins (157). Other biological mechanisms suggest that vitamin C may reduce inflammation and oxidative DNA damage, and may also exert cytotoxic effects on cancer cells (158-160). Other components mentioned above may help reduce lung cancer risk through similar biological mechanisms, and will not be discussed further here.

Our research has found that dietary fiber and yogurt may independently or synergistically play a preventive and protective role against lung cancer. The beneficial effects of dietary fiber and yogurt are largely attributed to their prebiotic and probiotic characteristics, which may modulate the gut microbiota either independently or through synergistic interactions (161-163). Although dietary fiber cannot be directly digested by humans, it can be metabolized by intestinal microbiota, resulting in the formation of short-chain fatty acids (76). Recent studies suggest that these metabolites may influence host immune responses and metabolic processes not only in the intestinal environment but also in other organs, including the lungs (76,164,165). Animal experiments have demonstrated that a high-fiber diet can reshape the pulmonary immune environment by altering the composition of intestinal and lung microbiota (164). Yogurt is a nutrient-rich food that typically contains probiotics of specific strains, which can promote the health of intestinal microbial communities. As immune modulators, probiotics can also regulate cytokine secretion, as well as the proliferation and differentiation of immune cells (163). Yogurt is often considered to have a potential role in the prevention of lung diseases. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that certain probiotic strains can inhibit lung metastasis, enhance natural killer cell activity, and exhibit anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory effects (166,167). Our current research results indicate that the combination of prebiotics (dietary fiber) and probiotics (yogurt) may be more effective in preventing lung cancer than either component alone. This suggests that increasing the consumption of prebiotics and probiotics may contribute to lung cancer prevention (24).

Red meat and processed meat are significant risk factors for lung cancer

Red and processed meats contain considerable amounts of heme iron and saturated fat, and may also contain some carcinogens, potentially serving as significant dietary risk factors for various cancers (8). Studies have found that heme iron in red and processed meat can induce cytotoxicity, promote epithelial cell apoptosis and proliferation, trigger lipid peroxidation, free radical formation, and DNA adduct formation in epithelial cells, and catalyze the formation of nitroso compounds, thereby promoting carcinogenesis (168). High consumption of animal protein has been significantly associated with an elevated risk of inflammatory bowel disease (169), a condition that itself is linked to a higher risk of colorectal cancer (170). Additionally, animal-derived products may contain antibiotic residues, which can disrupt the composition and function of the gut microbiota (171) and potentially influence local and systemic immune responses (171). Together, these factors contribute to the development of carcinogenesis (172). Proteins from animal sources are rich in branched-chain and aromatic amino acids, metabolic components frequently linked to the development of insulin resistance (173,174). Insulin resistance has been linked to an increased risk of various cancers. High consumption of red meat increases insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I) levels (175,176). IGF-I is involved in cell growth, carcinogenesis, and tumor progression (177). The high level of total fat present in meat can promote the release of bile acids, cholecystokinin, and prostaglandins, thereby elevating cancer risk (178-180). Furthermore, persistent organic pollutants (POPs) can accumulate in animal fat cells (181,182), potentially increasing the risk of chronic diseases, including cancer, by disrupting endocrine pathways (182). They are also present in lipoproteins, and the levels of POPs in lipoproteins from cancer patients are higher than those in healthy individuals (183).

Unsaturated fatty acids as potential protective dietary factors against lung cancer

Our research suggests that nut consumption can reduce lung cancer incidence. Nuts are nutrient-dense, providing protein, unsaturated fatty acids, dietary fiber, antioxidants and other bioactive components (184). Their anticancer mechanisms involve multiple pathways. Nut intake lowers low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) and essential fatty acid levels, thereby reducing cholesterol and cholesterol ester accumulation in cancer cells and suppressing tumor cell proliferation (185). Higher nut consumption also improves endothelial function (186), lipid peroxidation (187) and insulin resistance (188)—factors implicated in cancer initiation and progression (189). Moreover, antioxidant compounds in nuts (190) reduce oxidative DNA damage (185) and cell proliferation (191), attenuate inflammatory responses (192), and lower circulating insulin-like growth factor 1 (193). These components may also promote apoptosis (194), inhibit angiogenesis (195) and modulate gut microbiota (196). Collectively, current evidence indicates that nut consumption may play a significant role in cancer prevention and control.Our research has found that the consumption of PUFAs, abundant in fish, may reduce lung cancer incidence. Fish and PUFA intake is linked to decreased risk of various cancers (73,197,198). n-3 PUFAs, especially eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), prevent lung cancer through dual anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor mechanisms (199-202). They regulate eicosanoid metabolism by replacing arachidonic acid in cell membranes, reducing pro-inflammatory mediators [prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), leukotriene B4 (LTB4)] and increasing anti-inflammatory ones [prostaglandin E3 (PGE3), leukotriene B5 (LTB5)] (203-206), while inhibiting NF-κB (207,208) and activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) (209-212), thus suppressing chronic inflammation that drives lung cancer progression (213,214). Directly, n-3 PUFAs induce oxidative stress and apoptosis in lung cancer cells (e.g., DHA increases lipid peroxides and downregulates MKP-1) (212,215-218), inhibit the COX-2/PGE2 signaling axis and its pro-proliferative pathways (219-222), and downregulate invasion/metastasis molecules such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (223-225). In smoking-related lung cancer, they alleviate tobacco carcinogen (NNK)-induced inflammation and tumorigenesis and upregulate tumor suppressors like p21 (226,227). Metabolites such as resolvins and protectins actively promote inflammation resolution and improve the tumor microenvironment (228-230), and n-3 PUFAs can enhance chemotherapy drug accumulation and toxicity (e.g., doxorubicin, cisplatin) in lung cancer cells, reversing drug resistance (215,216,231,232).

Tea and coffee as dietary factors associated with lung cancer risk

Firstly, our research has found that tea intake is inversely associated with lung cancer incidence. Globally, tea ranks as the second most widely consumed beverage after water (233,234) and is prepared from Camellia sinensis leaves. From a biological perspective, tea is not a single substance. It is rich in various bioactive components including catechins, flavonoids, lignans, and phenolic acids. In recent years, tea has garnered attention due to its health benefits, particularly its protective effects against various cancers (235-237). Animal experiments and in vitro studies have inducated that tea may protect against the development and progression of lung cancer through its polyphenolic antioxidants (235,238). The mechanisms of action include the inhibition of nitrosation, cell proliferation, and tumor progression, and the induction of cancer cell apoptosis (239). In contrast, we found that coffee may contribute to a modest increase in lung cancer risk. Coffee, a widely consumed beverage worldwide, is made from ground and roasted coffee beans. A possible explanation for its carcinogenic mechanisms is as follows: coffee is a complex mixture of bioactive components, including caffeine, various phenolic compounds, and chlorogenic acid (240). Caffeine can affect DNA damage repair mechanisms and cell-cycle regulation, potentially contributing to increased cancer risk (241,242). In contrast, caffeine has been reported to suppress certain cellular and molecular components of the immune system, and numerous human studies have shown that coffee consumption is linked to levels of pro-inflammatory biomarkers (243-245). In addition, the roasting process of coffee produces substantial amounts of acrylamide, a carcinogenic compound (246).

Clinical implications and future research

Current evidence does not justify dietary supplementation or single-food interventions specifically for lung cancer prevention. Smoking cessation should remain the primary preventive strategy. Clinicians may encourage adherence to general healthy dietary patterns for overall health benefits, while avoiding causal claims regarding individual nutrients or foods. Future studies should use standardized dietary assessments and biomarkers, rigorously control for smoking and socioeconomic factors, and evaluate nutrient-nutrient and nutrient-gene interactions. Large prospective cohorts, individual-participant-data meta-analyses, Mendelian randomization studies, and randomized trials of dietary-pattern interventions are needed to strengthen causal inference. Future prospective cohorts and individual-participant-data meta-analyses should report associations separately for never-smokers, former smokers, and current smokers, with detailed adjustment for smoking duration, intensity, cumulative exposure, and time since cessation. Whenever feasible, analyses should also be stratified by major histological types and molecular alterations, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), KRAS, and ALK status. Such stratification will be necessary to determine whether dietary factors represent general risk markers, correlates of tobacco-related behavior, or subtype-specific etiologic exposures.

Conclusions

This umbrella review, the first to systematically integrate and evaluate meta-analytic evidence on diet-related factors and lung cancer risk, identified multiple statistically significant associations. However, methodological limitations substantially constrain the strength of causal inference. As a secondary study, this review is highly dependent on the quality of included meta-analyses, most of which are observational and susceptible to residual confounding (e.g., inadequate adjustment for smoking intensity and socioeconomic status), recall bias, and measurement bias. According to GRADE criteria, only folate intake demonstrated moderate-quality evidence for reduced lung cancer risk; the vast majority of nutrients, foods, and dietary habits—including vitamins, red meat, and the Mediterranean diet—were classified as low or very low evidence. Significant heterogeneity across meta-analyses in inclusion criteria, exposure definitions, effect size models, and dose-response methods further reduced the comparability and precision of pooled estimates (significant heterogeneity observed in 21 studies). Publication bias, language bias, and overlapping primary studies may also affect the robustness of findings. Nonetheless, the review suggests that overall dietary patterns (e.g., healthy or prudent dietary patterns) show more consistent protective associations than individual nutrients, whereas red meat, processed meat, and high GL diets may represent potential risk factors. Overall, high-quality evidence in the field of diet and lung cancer prevention remains limited. Future research should prioritize well-designed prospective cohorts and randomized intervention trials to provide a stronger scientific basis for nutrition-based prevention strategies.

A further major limitation is that lung cancer was generally treated as a single outcome in the source meta-analyses. Lung cancer comprises histologically and molecularly distinct diseases, and dietary associations may differ between adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, small-cell lung cancer, and tumors defined by alterations such as EGFR mutation, KRAS mutation, or ALK rearrangement. Because most eligible meta-analyses did not report sufficiently detailed subtype-specific results, we could not perform separate analyses by histological or genomic subtype. Smoking-stratified results were also limited and inconsistently reported, although findings specifically derived from non-smoking populations were identified where available. Thus, some observed dietary associations may reflect residual confounding by smoking or smoking-associated behaviors rather than independent dietary effects. Future studies should prioritize analyses jointly stratified by smoking exposure, histological subtype, and molecular characteristics.
