Systematic analysis of the ShRZFP gene family and its association with tiller regulation in sugarcane
Authors: Huiwen Zhou, Hanmin Luo, Xiaoyan Liu, Xinyi Li, Rui Yan, Lihang Qiu, Ting Luo, Haifeng Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUbiquitination modification is a core regulatory mechanism of plant growth, development and stress response. The CHY zinc finger domain-containing RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase (RZFP) subfamily plays a unique role in plant signal integration, but its systematic research in sugarcane remains unreported. Tillering is a key agronomic trait determining sugarcane yield, while its molecular regulatory mechanism is yet to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to systematically characterize the RZFP gene family in sugarcane and explore its association with tillering regulation.ResultsA total of 47 ShRZFP genes were identified from the sugarcane XTT22 genome. Phylogenetic analysis divided ShRZFP proteins into 3 groups with independent expansion characteristics; segmental duplication was the main driver of family expansion, dominated by purifying selection during evolution. ShRZFP proteins harbored 2–10 conserved motifs, with consistent gene structures within the same evolutionary branch. Light-responsive elements were the most abundant cis-acting elements in ShRZFP promoters. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) screened 6 candidate tillering-related ShRZFP genes enriched in the yellow module, whose co-expressed genes were significantly enriched in the photosynthesis pathway. Upstream transcriptional regulatory network prediction revealed that candidate ShRZFP genes may be regulated by transcription factors (TFs) including WRKY, AP2/ERF, TCP and DBB. qRT-PCR validation showed these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes were significantly responsive to key tillering-related phytohormones, suggesting their putative involvement in the negative regulation of sugarcane tillering.ConclusionThis study is the first systematic analysis of the ShRZFP gene family in sugarcane, which fills the research gap of this gene family in sugarcane, and provides valuable candidate genes and a preliminary theoretical basis for molecular improvement of sugarcane tillering traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09235-w.

Introduction

Ubiquitination modification is a key post-translational regulatory mechanism that plays an important role in plant growth, development and stress response [1]. The core of this process is executed by the ubiquitin/26S proteasome system (UPS), which precisely regulates the homeostasis of intracellular proteins by specifically recognizing and degrading target proteins [2]. Ubiquitination modification can change the abundance of target proteins (mainly through degradation pathways), subcellular localization or biological activity. This modification process is sequentially catalyzed by three types of enzymes: E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme and E3 ubiquitin ligase [3]. In the ubiquitination cascade reaction, E3 ubiquitin ligase plays a core role, responsible for specifically recognizing substrates and catalyzing the transfer of ubiquitin molecules from E2 enzymes to substrate proteins [4]. Due to the need for E3 enzymes to specifically recognize substrates, this enzyme family shows high diversity in organisms. For example, the number of predicted E3 enzymes in Arabidopsis thaliana is about 1400 [5], while that in Oryza sativa is about 1300 [6].

Specifically, based on the characteristic domains they contain, E3 ubiquitin ligases can be divided into four major categories: HECT (homologous to the E6-associated protein carboxyl terminus) type, U-box type, RING (really interesting new gene) type, and CRLs (cullin-RING ligases) type. Among them, HECT-type, RING-type, and U-box-type E3 ubiquitin ligases belong to single-subunit E3 ubiquitin ligases, while CRLs-type E3 ubiquitin ligases are multi-subunit E3 ubiquitin ligases [7, 8]. HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligases first bind to ubiquitin via the cysteine (Cys) residues in their characteristic domain to form a thioester intermediate, and then transfer and covalently conjugate ubiquitin to target proteins. The number of genes encoding HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligases varies significantly among different species; for example, only 7 genes encoding HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligases have been identified in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana [9, 10]. The characteristic U-box domain of U-box-type E3 ubiquitin ligases consists of approximately 70 amino acids, and lacks the canonical cysteine (Cys) and histidine (His) residues essential for zinc ion chelation [11, 12]. The characteristic RING domain of RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligases is a cysteine (Cys)-rich sequence composed of 40 to 60 amino acids. Eight spatially conserved Cys and His residues within the domain act as metal ligands to chelate two zinc ions, thereby mediating the transfer of ubiquitin to target proteins [13]. CRLs-type E3 ubiquitin ligases have high structural and functional polymorphism, and their functional complexes are assembled from a cullin scaffold protein, the RBX1 (RING Box 1) protein responsible for binding E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes, and a cullin-binding substrate recognition subunit. Five canonical cullin proteins (CUL1, CUL2, CUL3a, CUL3b, and CUL4) have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana, all of which are core components of CRLs-type E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes [14, 15]. Notably, among all E3 ligase types, the RING finger protein family is dominant in quantity, and hundreds of members have been identified in many species such as Glycine max, Arabidopsis thaliana, Populus tomentosa, Oryza sativa and Vitis vinifera [6, 8]. These ligases play pivotal regulatory roles in a wide range of core biological processes in plants, including phytohormone signal transduction, abiotic and biotic stress responses, organ morphogenesis, and the establishment of important agronomic traits [16–19].

Tillering is an important agronomic trait that contributes to plant architecture formation and yield formation in gramineous crops. Several key studies in Oryza sativa have identified several RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligases as important regulators of tillering-related traits. For instance, the RING-type E3 ligase IPI1 exerts tissue-specific dual regulation on rice tiller number and panicle development by mediating differential polyubiquitination of IPA1: it stabilizes IPA1 in shoot apexes to repress excessive tillering, while promoting IPA1 degradation in young panicles to regulate panicle branching, with loss-of-function ipi1 mutants exhibiting a significant increase in tiller number [17]. Another RING-type E3 ligase LATA1 modulates rice tiller angle by regulating auxin biosynthesis and asymmetric distribution, thus controlling shoot gravitropism and plant architecture establishment [18]. In addition, the RING-type E3 ligase OsRGLG6 has been demonstrated to target and ubiquitinate OsOTUB1, a key regulator that simultaneously controls rice tiller number and grain number per panicle, highlighting the essential role of RING-type E3 ligases in coordinating rice plant architecture and yield traits [19]. Despite these advances, current studies on tillering regulation by RING-type E3 ligases mainly focus on canonical RING domain-containing members, while the functions of RING-CH type E3 ligases (RZFP subfamily) harboring a CHY zinc finger domain in tillering regulation remain largely unknown.

In the RING finger E3 ubiquitin ligase family, members containing CHY zinc finger domain form a distinct and functionally important subfamily, which belongs to the single-subunit type according to the number of subunits. Such RING-CH type ligases retain the core catalytic activity mediated by the RING domain. They also expand their functions through the CHY zinc finger module. They thereby play a unique role in integrating multiple signaling pathways and accurately responding to environmental signals. Among them, CHYR1, the first member cloned in Arabidopsis thaliana, has been shown to promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and ABA-induced stomatal closure, thereby enhancing plant drought resistance [20]. Subsequent studies have further revealed the core functions of such proteins in plant growth, development and stress response. For example, AtCHYR2 localized in the cytoplasm of Arabidopsis thaliana is proved to be a key positive regulator of the glucose signaling pathway, whose expression is induced by sugar, and affects the main root growth of Arabidopsis thaliana by regulating stomatal aperture and photosynthesis [21]. In Cucumis sativus, CsCHYR1 positively regulates drought adaptability through the ABA signaling pathway, which can interact with protein kinase CsSnRK2.6 and mediate the ubiquitination and degradation of transcription factor CShTAF1, thereby promoting stomatal closure to enhance drought resistance [22]. Overexpression of MiRZFP34 from Mangifera indica confers responsiveness to drought and low-temperature stress. Heterologous expression of MiRZFP34 also promotes early flowering in Arabidopsis thaliana. Mechanistically, MiRZFP34 upregulates the expression of key flowering pathway genes, including AtAP1, AtFT and AtSOC1. It also interacts with the mango flowering regulatory proteins MiAP1-1, MiAP1-2 and MiCAL2. Through these regulatory actions, MiRZFP34 integrates the flowering regulatory and stress response pathways [23]. GmCHYR16 in Glycine max negatively regulates the tolerance of soybean to bicarbonate stress by targeting and degrading the transcription factor GmERF71 [24].

However, systematic analysis of the CHY zinc finger-containing RZFP subfamily remains lacking in sugarcane, an economically important cash crop. As a major branching trait of gramineous plants, tillering number and growth status directly affect the cane yield per unit area [25]. Our previous studies revealed that ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis is closely associated with tiller development in sugarcane, and RZFP34 was identified as a key gene involved in this process [25]. We thus hypothesize that this gene may mediate ubiquitination modification and participate in the occurrence and development of sugarcane tillers.

Against this background, the present study aimed to conduct a genome-wide systematic analysis of the ShRZFP gene family in sugarcane, and explore their potential association with tillering regulation. We characterized the evolutionary characteristics, expression patterns and phytohormone response profiles of this family, preliminarily screened tillering-related candidate ShRZFP genes, and laid a foundation for further dissecting the molecular mechanism of ubiquitination modification in regulating sugarcane tillering.

Materials and methods

Identification, physicochemical Properties and subcellular localization prediction of ShRZFP Family

The genome data of the modern cultivated sugarcane hybrid Saccharum spp. cv. XTT22 (Xintaitang 22, 2n = 111, aneuploid allo-autopolyploid) were retrieved from the high-quality haplotype-resolved genome assembly reported by Zhang et al. [26], with the genome dataset obtained from the Saccharum Genome Database (ScDB, https://sugarcane.gxu.edu.cn/scdb/) [27]. As a representative elite cultivar in Chinese sugarcane breeding, XTT22 has a typical genetic composition of modern sugarcane hybrids, which is an optimal model for studying the molecular basis of key agronomic traits in allo-autopolyploid sugarcane. The hidden Markov model (HMM) of the RZFP gene family containing the CHY zinc finger domain (PF05495) was downloaded from the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/download/Pfam/), and the simple hmm search tool in TBtools-II (v2.458) [28] was used to initially screen candidate ShRZFP proteins in the sugarcane genome. The candidate protein sequences were submitted to the NCBI Conserved Domain Database [29] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) and InterPro database [30] (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) to verify whether they contain both CHY zinc finger domain and RING finger domain, so as to determine the final members of the ShRZFPs family. Arabidopsis thaliana TAIR10.1 protein sequences were obtained from the Arabidopsis thaliana genome database TAIR [31] (https://www.Arabidopsis.org/); Oryza sativa AGlS1.0, Solanum lycopersicum ITAG2.4 and Zea mays Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0 protein sequences were downloaded from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/); and Populus trichocarpa protein sequences were obtained from the Phytozome datebase [32] (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Ptrichocarpa_v4_1). Subsequently, the same identification method was used to identify RZFP members in Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Populus trichocarpa, Zea mays and Solanum lycopersicum respectively.

The physicochemical properties such as the number of amino acids, relative molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, protein length and hydrophilicity of sugarcane RZFPs proteins were analyzed using TBtools-II (v2.458) [28]. The subcellular localization of RZFPs proteins was predicted by the online tool WoLF PSORT [33] (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Chromosomal localization, phylogenetic tree construction and gene gain and loss analysis of ShRZFP gene family

According to the XTT22 genome data, the position information of ShRZFP genes on chromosomes was extracted using TBtools-II (v2.458) [17]. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree of RZFP proteins from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa and Saccharum spp. was constructed using TBtools-II (v2.458) [28]. The JTTDCMut+G4 model was adopted, the bootstrap number was set to 5000 replicates, and all other parameters were set as default. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using the iTOL online platform [34] (https://itol.embl.de/).

The construction of the species phylogenetic tree was performed using the Timetree online website [35] (https://timetree.org/). Based on the calculation method of Notung software, TBtools-II (v2.458) [28] was used to analyze the gene loss and gain events of RZFP genes in six species: Saccharum spp., Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Solanum lycopersicum, Zea mays and Populus trichocarpa.

Analysis of conserved motifs, conserved domains and gene structure of the ShRZFP family

The structural information of ShRZFP genes was extracted from the sugarcane genome annotation file (GFF format). The MEME Suit [36] (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) was used to predict conserved motifs, with the maximum number of motifs set to 10, and other parameters remained default. TBtools-II (v2.458) [28] was used to visualize motifs and gene structures.

Analysis of cis-elements, collinearity and duplication events in promoter region of ShRZFP gene family

The 2000 bp sequence upstream of the start codon (ATG) of ShRZFP genes in the XTT22 genome was extracted as the promoter region using TBtools-II (v2.458) [28], and the obtained sequences were submitted to the PlantCARE online database [37] (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) for cis-acting element prediction. After screening the prediction results to exclude general basic promoter elements such as TATA-box, GC-box and CAAT-box as well as elements with unknown functions, TBtools-II (v2.458) [28] was used to visualize the retained specific functional elements.

Based on the sugarcane genome sequence and its annotation information, the MCScanX tool in TBtools-II (v2.458) [28] was used to perform collinearity analysis within the sugarcane species, and further conduct interspecific collinearity comparison between Saccharum spp. and Oryza sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana. For the identified collinear gene pairs, the ratio of non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka) to synonymous substitution rate (Ks) was calculated using TBtools-II (v2.458) [28], to analyze the duplication types of the ShRZFP gene family and the selection pressure during evolution.

Expression profiles of ShRZFP genes in different tissues based on RNA-seq data

Transcriptome data of XTT22 across different tissues (seedling stem, seedling leaf, mature leaf roll, leaf blade, and internodes at different maturity stages) were obtained from the Sugarcane Genome Database [17] (https://sugarcane.gxu.edu.cn/scdb/), with raw sequencing data available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession no. PRJNA988946 [16]. Three biological replicates were set for seedling tissues, and one biological replicate was included for mature vegetative tissues. Gene expression levels were represented by FPKM values, and the expression heatmap was plotted using TBtools-II (v2.458) [28].

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) of the ShRZFP genes during sugarcane tillering stages

Based on the transcriptome data of sugarcane leaves and tiller buds at three key tillering stages (T1: tillering initiation stage, axillary buds begin to germinate underground; T2: early tillering stage, axillary buds grow to 0.5–1 cm underground; T3: peak tillering stage, axillary buds emerge to 2 cm in length) obtained in the early stage of our research group (raw sequencing data deposited in NCBI SRA under accession no. PRJNA723212 [38]), weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the FPKM-normalized gene expression matrix. A total of 18 independent samples were included in this analysis, with 3 biological replicates set for both leaf and tiller bud samples at each of the three tillering stages. The WGCNA analysis was implemented using the WGCNA shiny module of TBtools-II (v2.458) [39].

To improve the accuracy of network construction, the expression matrix was first preprocessed: low-expression and low-variation genes with an average FPKM value lower than 1 and a coefficient of variation less than 0.1 in more than half of the samples were excluded. Then, a weighted co-expression network was constructed, and the soft thresholding power was set to 9 to ensure the constructed network conformed to scale-free network distribution characteristics. Genes were hierarchically clustered based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM) to identify co-expression modules.

To screen modules associated with tillering development traits, we calculated the module eigengene (ME) of each module and performed Pearson correlation analysis between MEs and the phenotypic matrix. Modules with a significance level of P < 0.05 were defined as trait-related modules for subsequent analysis. Meanwhile, the module membership (MM) of each gene and gene significance (GS, defined as the correlation between gene expression level and target tillering traits) were calculated for all genes.

For this study focusing on the ShRZFP gene family, we prioritized trait-related modules that contained the highest number of ShRZFP family members as the focal module for in-depth analysis. All ShRZFP genes in the focal module were selected as candidate genes for subsequent functional enrichment analysis, co-expression network construction and expression validation.

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis was performed on the ShRZFP candidate genes and their co-expressed genes using TBtools-II (v2.458) [17], and the enrichment results were visualized via the OmicShare platform [40]. For the focal module, the TOM subnetwork was exported with a connection weight threshold set to 0.05 to generate edge and node files for visualization. The co-expression network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software [41], and the betweenness centrality (BC) value of each ShRZFP candidate gene node was calculated via the CytoNCA plugin of Cytoscape to evaluate the connectivity characteristics and potential regulatory roles of these candidate genes in the network.

Analysis of the upstream transcriptional regulatory network of ShRZFP genes

Transcription factor (TF) binding sites of the ShRZFP candidate genes were predicted via the Plant Transcription Factor Database [42] (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/) with Zea mays as the reference species and a screening threshold of E-value < 1 × 10⁻⁵ to obtain potential transcriptional regulatory relationships. TFs annotation was performed on genes in the co-expression module containing ShRZFP genes, and Pearson correlation analysis of expression levels between ShRZFP genes and candidate transcription factors was conducted using the OmicShare platform [40], with P < 0.05 set as the threshold for significant correlation. The transcriptional regulatory network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software [41].

Hormone response expression pattern analysis of ShRZFP genes by qRT-PCR

In this study, XTT22 was used as the test material, which was provided by the Sugarcane Research Institute of Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Healthy sugarcane stalks were selected, and only one healthy bud segment was taken from the upper part of each stalk, cut into single bud segments, rinsed with clean water, and soaked for 30 min; the single bud segments were planted in flowerpots with a specification of 24 × 19.5 × 26.5 cm, 3 single bud segments per pot, and 11 kg of peat nutrient soil was filled in each pot. The flowerpots were placed in a greenhouse in the experimental field of the Sugarcane Research Institute of Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences for cultivation. The microclimate conditions suitable for sugarcane growth in the greenhouse were set as follows: average temperature 35 °C, humidity 75%, light intensity 1880 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. After the plants grew normally to the tillering stage in the greenhouse, hormone response expression pattern analysis was carried out.

At the early tillering stage of sugarcane, 200 mg·L⁻¹ gibberellic Acid 3 (GA₃), 200 mg·L⁻¹ paclobutrazol (PP333), 400 mg·L⁻¹ indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), 400 mg·L⁻¹ zeatin (ZT), 200 mg·L⁻¹ abscisic acid (ABA) and 50 mmol·L⁻¹ ethylene (ETH) were sprayed on the leaves respectively; spraying was carried out at 9 am every day for 3 consecutive days. The first true leaf of plantlets was collected at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h after treatment, and total RNA was extracted and qRT-PCR analysis was performed with reference to published methods [28]. PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser and SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kits were purchased from TaKaRa Bio (Tokyo, Japan). All qRT-PCR primers for the candidate ShRZFP genes and the internal reference Actin gene were designed using the IDT PrimerQuest™ Tool (https://sg.idtdna.com/PrimerQuest/Home/Index). A 20 µL qRT-PCR reaction system was prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions, and amplification was performed on an ABI Vii7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) to detect the expression levels of ShRZFP genes. The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate the relative expression level of genes, and the Actin gene was used as the internal reference gene. Each experiment had 9 replicates (3 biological replicates × 3 technical replicates), and the primer sequences used are shown in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 20.0 statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 24); significance test was performed by one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA), and multiple comparison of significance differences between experimental groups was performed by Duncan’s method; GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software Inc., USA) was used for graphing.

Results

Identification, chromosomal localization, physicochemical properties and predicted subcellular localization of the ShRZFP family

Through systematic analysis of the XTT22 genome, we identified a total of 47 genes encoding RING-type (RING-H2_Pirh2-like) E3 ubiquitin ligases containing CHY zinc finger domain, which were uniformly named as the sugarcane RZFP (RING Zinc Finger Protein) gene family, and numbered as ShRZFP01 to ShRZFP47 in turn according to their localization information on chromosomes (Fig. 1; Table S2). The XTT genome contains 111 chromosomes [26], and these 47 ShRZFP genes are distributed on 32 of them, showing a wide and uneven distribution characteristic in the complex polyploid genome of sugarcane. Among them, 31 members are located in SoChr (Saccharum officinarum subgenome) and 15 members are located in SsChr (Saccharum spontaneum subgenome), and one unclassified member is located in the ambiguous genomic region (Ind), suggesting that the gene family may have experienced different degrees of expansion and retention during the evolution of sugarcane subgenomes.

Systematic analysis of the physicochemical properties and predicted subcellular localization of the ShRZFP family members (Table S3) revealed extensive heterogeneity in the protein physicochemical properties of this family. For example, the number of encoded amino acids ranges from 250 (ShRZFP10) to 1307 (ShRZFP22 and ShRZFP26), indicating high diversity in protein molecular size and domain composition among family members. Theoretical isoelectric point analysis showed that most members are alkaline, which may be related to the amino acid composition of their functional domains; instability index analysis indicated that the instability index of most members is higher than 40, suggesting that they are easily degraded in vivo, which may be closely related to their function as E3 ubiquitin ligases that require rapid turnover to achieve precise signal regulation. Subcellular localization prediction showed that the family members are localized in multiple locations such as nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplast, peroxisome and cytoskeleton. This diverse localization pattern strongly suggests that sugarcane RZFP proteins may be involved in a variety of different biological processes, and this functional differentiation provides an important structural basis for the family to play complex and precise regulatory roles in sugarcane growth, development and environmental response.

Phylogenetic analysis and gene gain and loss events of ShRZFP gene family

A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on a total of 77 RZFP protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa and Saccharum spp. The results showed (Fig. 2A) that all RZFP proteins can be divided into three groups (I-III), and each evolutionary group contains RZFP proteins from the three species of Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa and Saccharum spp. Specifically, Group I comprised 19 RZFP proteins, including 12 from sugarcane; Group II contained 21 proteins, 11 from sugarcane; Group III included 37 proteins, 24 from sugarcane. As monocotyledons, Oryza sativa and Saccharum spp. were closely clustered in the RZFP protein phylogeny, while sugarcane was distantly related to the dicot Arabidopsis thaliana. This pattern indicates that RZFP protein evolution is consistent with the monocot–dicot phylogenetic divergence, and the family has evolved conservatively within monocots.

Based on the species divergence times of the six selected species (Saccharum spp., Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Populus trichocarpa, Zea mays and Oryza sativa), we constructed a species divergence tree and further combined 114 RZFP protein sequences to analyze gene gain and loss events of the RZFP family during species differentiation. The results showed (Fig. 2B) that the divergence time between dicotyledonous and monocotyledonous plants was about 160.0 million years ago; among them, Populus trichocarpa and Arabidopsis thaliana in dicotyledonous plants have the closest evolutionary relationship, and Saccharum spp. and Zea mays, both C4 crops, have the closest evolutionary relationship in monocotyledonous plants. About 125 million years ago, differentiation occurred within dicotyledonous plants, and the ancestors of Arabidopsis thaliana, Populus trichocarpa and Solanum lycopersicum separated successively, and then evolved independently to form the current crop groups. 49.1 million years ago, Oryza sativa in monocotyledonous plants diverged from Saccharum spp. and Zea mays, which are also C4 crops. Further analysis of the changes in the number of RZFP gene family members in each species showed that Arabidopsis thaliana had the largest number of gene losses, while Saccharum spp. showed a more significant gene family expansion, which may be related to its characteristics as a polyploid crop.

Analysis of conserved motifs, conserved domains and gene structure of the ShRZFP family

Motif and domain analysis results showed (Fig. 3B) that there are differences in the types of motifs contained in different members of the ShRZFP family. The number of motifs in different ShRZFP proteins ranges from 2 to 10, and most family members contain motif 1, motif 8 and motif 9, motif 10 suggesting that these 4 motifs play a fundamental role in the function of RZFP proteins and together form their conserved domains. The remaining motifs are present only in certain family members, and such proteins may have achieved functional expansion by acquiring additional domains, thus it is speculated that ShRZFP family proteins have functional specificity.

Gene structure analysis results showed (Fig. 3C) that the number of exons of the ShRZFP genes ranges from 8 to 15. The ShRZFP genes in the same evolutionary branch have highly similar number, length and distribution pattern of exons; at the same time, the length of untranslated regions (UTR) of some genes is significantly different. As a key region for post-transcriptional regulation of genes, the difference in UTR length may affect the stability of mRNA, translation efficiency or microRNA binding efficiency, suggesting that ShRZFP family members may have different regulatory mechanisms in different tissues, different developmental stages or different environmental conditions.

Analysis of cis-elements in promoter region of the ShRZFP gene family

Systematic analysis of cis-acting elements in the promoter region of the 47 ShRZFP genes (Fig. 4; Table S4) identified four major types of core functional elements: light response, plant hormone response, growth and development regulation, and biotic/abiotic stress response. Among them, light response elements were the most numerous, followed by plant hormone response elements, then stress response elements, and the least growth and development elements. After excluding common basic conserved elements such as CAAT-Box and TATA-Box, a total of 45 types and 1177 cis-acting elements were identified; among them, the promoter of ShRZFP20 contained the largest number of elements (39), and the promoter of ShRZFP11 contained the smallest number of elements (11).

From the overall distribution characteristics of elements, light response elements are widely distributed in the promoters of all ShRZFP genes, and account for the highest proportion in multiple genes such as ShRZFP02, ShRZFP10, suggesting that light signal is the core environmental factor regulating the expression of ShRZFP family genes. Plant hormone response elements are also relatively enriched in the promoters of multiple ShRZFP genes, among which the number of methyl jasmonate response elements is particularly significant, indicating that this hormone signal plays an important regulatory role in the expression of the ShRZFP family members.

The distribution of growth and development-related elements and biotic/abiotic stress response elements is obviously gene-specific. For example, a small number of genes (ShRZFP01, ShRZFP42, ShRZFP46, ShRZFP47) do not contain these two types of regulatory elements. Among growth and development elements, the number of meristem expression and endosperm development-related elements is the largest; among stress response elements, the anaerobic stress response element (ARE) plays a key role.

Analysis of collinearity, duplication events and Ka/Ks ratio of ShRZFP genes

In the Saccharum spp. genome, a total of 112 collinear relationships were identified among 42 ShRZFP gene members (Fig. 5A, Table S5). Among them, 106 gene pairs originated from whole-genome duplication or segmental duplication (WGD/SD), and only 1 gene pair was generated by tandem duplication events, indicating that segmental duplication is the main way to drive the expansion of the ShRZFP gene family. It is worth noting that most collinear relationships occur in the same homologous groups (HG), suggesting that the expansion of the ShRZFP family mainly depends on segmental duplication, and there may be functional redundancy among its members. Ka/Ks ratio analysis of these collinear gene pairs showed that the ratio ranges between 0.065 and 1.428 (Table S5): the Ka/Ks ratio of 3 gene pairs is greater than 1, the ratio of 2 gene pairs is close to 1, and the ratio of most other gene pairs is less than 1. This indicates that the Saccharum spp. ShRZFP gene family is mainly subjected to purifying selection during evolution, and a few genes may have experienced positive selection or neutral evolution.

Interspecific collinearity analysis results showed (Fig. 5B) that there are a large number of collinear gene pairs between the Saccharum spp. and Oryza sativa, but no collinear gene pairs with Arabidopsis thaliana were detected; 7 RZFP genes in the Oryza sativa genome and 40 ShRZFP genes in the Saccharum spp. genome form 49 collinear gene pairs in total. Expression profiles of ShRZFP genes in different tissues

Gene expression profile analysis showed (Fig. 6) that the expression characteristics in various tissues indicate that the expression level of most ShRZFP members is still generally low. Among the ShRZFP members with relatively high expression levels, their expression levels in seedling and premature tissues are significantly higher than those in mature tissues; and in mature tissues, the expression level in leaves is significantly lower than that in stem tissues. This feature suggests that ShRZFP members are more active in vigorously growing tissues and may be more likely to participate in the physiological and metabolic regulation of this period.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) of ShRZFP genes during different sugarcane tillering stages

To further clarify the internal association between ShRZFP genes and Saccharum spp. tillering regulation, WGCNA analysis was carried out based on the transcriptome data of leaves and tiller buds at the tillering stage to screen core ShRZFP genes associated with tillering traits. The WGCNA algorithm was used to analyze the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in leaves and tiller buds at the tillering stage. The results showed that a hierarchical clustering tree was constructed based on the correlation coefficients among 11,026 genes, and a total of 20 co-expression modules were obtained, with different colors representing different modules (Fig. 7A). The number of genes contained in each module is different, among which the turquoise module contains the largest number of genes (2770), and the grey module contains the smallest number of genes (17). According to the screening criteria of P < 0.05, multiple modules are highly correlated with tillering stage traits (Fig. 7B). Screening the modules to which ShRZFP members belong found that 13 ShRZFP members are significantly correlated with the process traits of the sugarcane tillering stage, among which the yellow module contains the largest number of ShRZFP members (6, namely ShRZFP16, ShRZFP23, ShRZFP24, ShRZFP27, ShRZFP29, ShRZFP46). With P < 0.05 as the significance threshold, the yellow module is highly positively correlated with leaves at T1 stage and T3 stage. Six ShRZFP genes in the yellow module were selected as candidate genes to construct a co-expression network diagram of these candidate genes and their associated genes. The results showed (Fig. 7C; Table S6) that these 6 ShRZFP family members formed a total of 4200 co-expression relationships with 1140 genes out of the 11,026 genes used in the WGCNA analysis. The CytoNCA APP was used to calculate the betweenness centrality (BC) of each ShRZFP gene node. The size of the circle in the figure is positively correlated with the BC value. The higher the BC value, the more core regulatory role the gene may play in the co-expression network. Among them, ShRZFP27 has the largest BC value, and ShRZFP24 has the smallest BC value.

KEGG enrichment analysis of the 6 ShRZFP members and their co-expressed 1140 genes showed (Fig. 7D) that these genes are significantly enriched in 10 pathways, and the top five pathways are Photosynthesis proteins, Metabolism, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, Energy metabolism, and Photosynthesis. This indicates that these genes are highly enriched in the pathway network centered on photosynthesis.

GO enrichment analysis showed (Fig. 7E) that these differentially expressed genes are significantly enriched in three levels: cellular component, molecular function and biological process. At the cellular component level, differentially expressed genes are mainly enriched in terms such as “plastid membrane”, “organelle outer membrane” and “plastid thylakoid”; at the molecular function level, they are mainly significantly enriched in terms related to “chlorophyll binding”, “tetrapyrrole binding” and “pigment binding”; at the biological process level, differentially expressed genes are mainly enriched in processes such as “response to radiation”, “photosynthesis, light harvesting”, “response to light stimulus”, “photosynthesis, light harvesting in photosystem I” and “photosynthesis, light reaction”.

Analysis of the upstream transcriptional regulatory network of ShRZFP genes

To elucidate the potential transcriptional regulatory mechanism of ShRZFP family genes involved in sugarcane tillering development, we predicted the TF binding sites in the promoter regions of six candidate ShRZFP genes (ShRZFP16, ShRZFP23, ShRZFP24, ShRZFP27, ShRZFP29, and ShRZFP46) during tillering stages. The results showed (Fig. 8A; Table S7) that a large number of TF binding sites were present in the promoter regions of six candidate ShRZFP genes. A total of 49 TF members from 16 families were predicted to form 92 potential transcriptional regulatory relationships with the six ShRZFP genes, among which the AP2/ERF, MYB, TCP, bZIP, and MADS-box families were the candidate families with high enrichment levels. ShRZFP27 had the largest number of corresponding potential transcriptional regulatory relationships, and was predicted to be targeted by 34 TFs, followed by ShRZFP46, which was predicted to be targeted by 18 TFs. Among them, the TF MADS1 was predicted to bind to the promoter regions of all six ShRZFP genes, while GTL1, TFIIIA, HY5, and GBF6 were predicted to bind to the promoter regions of four ShRZFP genes (ShRZFP16, ShRZFP23, ShRZFP24, and ShRZFP29).

Based on the results of WGCNA, we performed TF family annotation on the co-expressed genes clustered in the yellow module together with the six candidate ShRZFP genes to obtain a candidate TF set. Combined with the transcriptome data of sugarcane at key tillering stages, Pearson correlation analysis of expression levels was performed between the six candidate ShRZFP genes and the above candidate TFs. The results showed (Fig. 8B; Table S8) that a total of 37 TFs were significantly correlated with the expression levels of ShRZFP genes (P < 0.05). Among them, multiple members from the TCP, bZIP, ERF, WRKY, and DBB families showed a significant strong positive correlation with ShRZFP genes, suggesting that these TFs may exert a transcriptional activation effect on ShRZFP genes. Some members from the Dof and ERF families showed a significant negative correlation with ShRZFP genes, indicating that they may have a transcriptional inhibitory effect on ShRZFP genes.

We further analyzed the expression patterns of the above candidate TFs in tiller buds and functional leaves at three sugarcane tillering stages (T1, T2, T3) (Fig. 8C). The candidate transcription factors exhibited distinct expression specificity across different tissues and tillering developmental stages, and the majority of them presented elevated expression levels in functional leaves at the T1 and T3 stages. Their spatiotemporal expression patterns were highly consistent with the corresponding associated ShRZFP genes, which provided expression-level support for the potential functional synergism between them. Among them, TF families including bZIP, ERF, MYB and TCP were predicted to have potential transcriptional regulatory relationships with ShRZFP genes. Transcription factors from these families showed high expression levels and significant correlation with the expression levels of multiple ShRZFP genes, which preliminarily indicated that these families may play a major regulatory role in the expression of ShRZFP genes during sugarcane

tillering.

qRT-PCR verification of hormone response expression pattern of ShRZFP genes

According to the WGCNA analysis results, 6 ShRZFP genes (ShRZFP16, ShRZFP23, ShRZFP24, ShRZFP27, ShRZFP29, ShRZFP46) in the yellow module were selected in this study to verify their hormone signal response expression patterns by qRT-PCR (Fig. 9; Table S9).

Analysis of expression characteristics of ShRZFPs under various phytohormones. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation of three biological replicates. Different lowercase letters above columns indicate significant differences between different time points (*P* < 0.05)

Under IAA, ABA and GA treatments, the expression trends of these 6 ShRZFP members are relatively consistent, showing a change rule of first increasing and then decreasing. Among them, ShRZFP46 has the most significant response to IAA treatment, and its expression level is significantly up-regulated by 7 times compared with the control at 48 h; ShRZFP24 has the most intense response to ABA treatment, and its expression level is significantly up-regulated by 7 times at 24 h; ShRZFP46 has the most obvious response to GA treatment, and its expression level is significantly up-regulated by 7 times at 24 h. The response patterns of these 6 ShRZFP members to the other three hormones (PP333, ZT, ETH) are obviously differentiated. Under PP333, ZT and ETH treatments, ShRZFP46 shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, with the expression level reaching the peak at 24 h. In summary, these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes in the yellow module show a complex “consistent and differentiated” expression pattern in response to different hormone treatments, which strongly suggests that they may play different but synergistic regulatory roles in the Saccharum spp. tillering regulatory network.

Discussion

In the RING finger E3 ligase family, members containing CHY zinc finger domain form a distinct and functionally important subfamily. As a zinc finger binding domain, CHY can interact with DNA, RNA, proteins or lipids [43]. In recent years, more and more studies have reported the functions of RING finger E3 ligases containing CHY domain in regulating plant growth, development and responding to various stress factors [21, 22, 44]. This study systematically analyzed the evolutionary characteristics, structural differentiation and spatiotemporal expression patterns of the sugarcane ShRZFP gene family.

The genome of the modern sugarcane hybrid cultivar XTT22 is composed of two ancestral subgenomes: Saccharum officinarum (abbreviated as SoChr, accounting for 75.73% of the genome) and Saccharum spontaneum (abbreviated as SsChr, accounting for 17.92% of the genome), as well as recombinant chromosomes (abbreviated as RecChr, 6.35%). Among them, the SsChr subgenome, as the wild ancestral subgenome, provides a key genetic basis for stress resistance and tillering trait formation in sugarcane [26]. Based on the highly heterozygous polyploid genome of XTT22 (111 chromosomes), this study systematically identified 47 ShRZFP genes. Although the number of ShRZFP genes in the SsChr subgenome (15) was significantly lower than that in the SoChr subgenome (31), the distribution density of this gene family in the SsChr subgenome was at a high level relative to the 17.92% genomic proportion of the SsChr subgenome in XTT22. The gene density of this family in SsChr was nearly twice that in SoChr, which is closely related to sugarcane nobilization domestication and subgenome functional differentiation. During sugarcane breeding, genomic regions from wild SsChr related to tillering and stress resistance were preferentially retained. As the ShRZFP family is putatively involved in tillering and stress regulation, these genes from SsChr were positively selected and preserved during domestication, providing evolutionary evidence for their role in sugarcane tillering domestication.

Gene family evolution is mainly driven by whole-genome duplication, segmental duplication and tandem duplication, and these duplication events are the core forces driving the expansion of gene numbers during evolution [45–47]. Among them, segmental duplication was the core driving force for the expansion of the ShRZFP family (111 collinear gene pairs were derived from segmental duplication), which is consistent with previous conclusions [26]. In addition, collinear relationships mainly occurred in homologous groups, with abundant inter-subgenomic (between SoChr and SsChr) collinear events. This reflects the exchange and recombination of genetic material between the two ancestral subgenomes of sugarcane after interspecific hybridization, and also suggests that this family can achieve functional coordination between the SoChr and SsChr subgenomes through inter-subgenomic collinearity, to meet the adaptive requirements of the complex polyploid sugarcane genome.

The results of Ka/Ks ratio indicate that the ShRZFP family is mainly subjected to purifying selection during evolution to maintain the stability of its core ubiquitination function; the Ka/Ks ratio of 3 other gene pairs is greater than 1, suggesting that a few family members can achieve functional innovation through positive selection, thereby adapting to environmental stress and artificial selection pressure after sugarcane polyploidization [47]. This also indicates that most collinear gene pairs are constrained by purifying selection, and their catalytic functions may be highly conserved, while a few members complete functional differentiation and innovation through positive selection.

Previous studies have shown that RZFP proteins in plants are usually localized in multiple organelles, which suggests that this protein family can be involved in various plant physiological activities [48]. The subcellular localization prediction results of this study show that sugarcane ShRZFP proteins are localized in 5 organelles, which also suggests that the sugarcane ShRZFP family may have multiple functions. At present, the functions of E3 ubiquitin ligase genes in tillering regulation of other crops have been reported: OsPAGN1 negatively regulates the number of rice tillers, and its deletion mutant pagn1 shows a phenotype of significantly increased tiller number [49]; APC/CTE regulates rice tillering by mediating the degradation of MOC1. High concentration of GA can activate APC/CTE, promote the degradation of MOC1 in axillary meristems, and then inhibit tillering, while moderate ABA signal antagonizes GA-induced MOC1 degradation through the SnRK2-APC/CTE regulatory module, maintains the size of axillary meristems, and thus promotes tillering [50]; OsPUB77 can ubiquitinate OsBZR3, a key transcription factor in the BR signaling pathway, and promote its degradation. The decrease of OsBZR3 content will inhibit the BR signaling pathway, eventually leading to the decrease of rice tiller number [51].

Our previous study showed that RZFP34 is a key gene positively correlated with GA3 content and the white module negatively correlated with tiller number. It is speculated that it may participate in the ubiquitination and degradation of DELLA proteins by cooperating with the gibberellin receptor GID1, thereby regulating the gibberellin signaling pathway and affecting sugarcane tiller development [25]. Based on the above research basis, this study conducted WGCNA analysis on ShRZFP genes and found that the family genes are enriched in the yellow module, which is highly positively correlated with leaves at T1 stage (tillering initiation stage) and T3 stage (tillering peak stage); at the same time, these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes and their co-expressed genes are significantly enriched in photosynthesis-related pathways. Therefore, it is speculated that the putative main expression site of these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes is leaves, and they may participate in regulating the sugarcane tillering process at the tillering initiation stage and tillering peak stage by regulating photosynthesis, which is consistent with the relevant research conclusions in Arabidopsis thaliana [20].

In this study, multiple transcription factor families including WRKY, ERF and TCP that may be involved in the transcriptional regulation of these candidate ShRZFP genes were screened through promoter binding site prediction and gene expression correlation analysis. Previous studies have shown that the above transcription factor families play key regulatory roles in plant lateral branching and tillering development. Light can inhibit lateral branch development in Arabidopsis thaliana by regulating the expression level of the TCP family gene BRC1 [52]; in rice, the TCP family member TB1 exerts a negative regulatory effect on tiller formation by modulating the transcription of key genes in photosynthetic pathways [53], while OsWRKY53 can directly bind to the promoter of OsTB1 and activate its transcription, and the two synergistically negatively regulate rice tillering [54]. Furthermore, OsWRKY36 negatively regulates rice tiller number by repressing the expression of MOC2, a gene encoding a key photosynthetic enzyme [55]. NGR5, an AP2/ERF family transcription factor that acts as a core regulator of nitrogen response, whose transcript level is regulated by gibberellin signaling, participates in the regulation of rice tiller bud outgrowth by integrating photosynthetic carbon signals and nitrogen signals [56].

B-box proteins are a class of zinc-finger transcription factors containing a conserved B-box domain, which exert key regulatory roles in light-mediated plant growth and development [57]. Previous studies in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana have confirmed that BBX family members can precisely regulate lateral branch development: BBX4 positively regulates the number of branches in Arabidopsis under short-day conditions [58]; AtBBX10 also acts as a positive regulator of branch number in Arabidopsis [59]. The regulatory function of this family in tillering has also been verified in non-model crops: TaCOL-B5, a gene mapped in wheat that encodes a homolog of Arabidopsis BBX6, significantly promotes wheat tillering when overexpressed [60].

Transcriptome data from this study showed that members of the above families exhibited high expression levels at the T1 and T3 tillering stages. Among them, multiple members of the BBX family (DBB) had significantly higher expression levels than those of other transcription factor family members, suggesting that this family may participate in the regulation of germination and growth of sugarcane tiller buds by responding to light signal-mediated developmental pathways. Taken together, the above research evidence shows that the transcription factor families including WRKY, ERF, TCP and BBX (DBB subfamily) screened in this study have been widely confirmed to participate in the regulation of plant lateral branching/tillering development, and can mediate the transcriptional regulation of genes related to photosynthetic pathways. Combined with the WGCNA analysis result that the gene set co-expressed with these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes was significantly enriched in the photosynthesis pathway in this study, as well as the high expression characteristics of the above transcription factors at key stages of sugarcane tillering, it is preliminarily suggested that these transcription factor families (especially the BBX/DBB subfamily) may couple photosynthetic metabolism with tillering development processes through transcriptionally regulating these candidate ShRZFP genes, and are potentially involved in the molecular regulation of sugarcane tillering. The specific regulatory mechanism remains to be verified by subsequent experiments.

The results of this study showed that the expression of these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes could be significantly up-regulated by IAA, ABA and GA, indicating that sugarcane ShRZFP genes may be downstream targets of IAA, ABA and GA signaling pathways, which is consistent with previous research conclusions [25, 50]. Notably, numerous studies have confirmed that these three hormones (IAA, ABA and GA) can negatively regulate plant branching/tillering processes [61, 62]. Paclobutrazol (PP333), a gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis inhibitor, has been demonstrated in previous studies to antagonize the inhibitory effect of GA on sugarcane tillering, thus promoting tiller formation in sugarcane [25]. In the present study, PP333 was found to significantly down-regulate the expression of most these candidate ShRZFP genes, which is consistent with the expression pattern of ShRZFP genes up-regulated by GA treatment. Combined with the conclusions from the previous study of our team [26], and based on the above expression correlation evidence, it can be preliminarily speculated that these 6 candidate ShRZFP genes may serve as potential negative regulators of sugarcane tillering, and may participate in the modulation of sugarcane tillering process via mediating IAA, ABA and GA signaling pathways. The specific regulatory function of these genes remains to be verified by further in vivo and in vitro functional experiments. In addition, phytohormone response analysis showed that the expression of several ShRZFP genes could be significantly down-regulated by ZT. This expression pattern is highly consistent with the functional property of ZT as a positive regulator of branching/tillering [63, 64], further supporting the above speculation that these candidate ShRZFP genes may have potential negative regulatory roles in sugarcane tillering.

Overall, both ethylene and IAA can significantly up-regulate the expression of these 6 candidate sugarcane ShRZFP genes. Previous studies in rice have confirmed that ethylene can indirectly activate the SOR1-mediated signaling pathway by inducing auxin accumulation, ultimately inhibiting rice root growth [65]. Combined with the results of this study that both ethylene and IAA can significantly up-regulate the expression of ShRZFP genes, it is speculated that a similar regulatory pathway may exist in sugarcane, that is, ethylene regulates the expression of ShRZFP genes by inducing auxin accumulation, thereby participating in the regulation of physiological processes related to sugarcane tillering.

Some limitations exist in this study. The functional validation of RZFP genes is still insufficient in the current work, and further experimental verification is needed to clarify their exact biological roles. In future work, we will use a genome-wide panel or worldwide diverse sugarcane genotypes for in-depth functional validation of the 6 identified ShRZFP genes, and perform pan-gene family analysis of the RZFP family with published sugarcane reference genomes to comprehensively elucidate their biological functions in sugarcane.

Taken together, this study represents a basic genome-wide identification and candidate gene screening of the sugarcane ShRZFP gene family. It systematically characterizes this family for the first time in sugarcane, fills the corresponding research gap, and provides valuable candidate genes and data support for future functional investigations.

Conclusion

Based on the highly allopolyploid genome of sugarcane XTT22, this study systematically characterized the RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase gene family containing the CHY zinc finger domain (ShRZFPs), and for the first time identified 47 ShRZFP genes that were unevenly distributed across 32 chromosomes. Phylogenetic and structural analyses revealed that this gene family mainly expanded via segmental duplication: purifying selection dominated its evolutionary process, with positive selection driving functional innovation in a small number of members. The conserved motifs and differentiated gene structures provided structural support for their functional divergence. Spatiotemporal expression analysis demonstrated tissue specificity of ShRZFP genes. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified 6 candidate ShRZFP members associated with sugarcane tillering stages, which were enriched in the yellow module, and their co-expressed genes were predominantly concentrated in the photosynthesis pathway. Hormone response assays showed that ShRZFP genes were up-regulated by tillering-negative hormones (IAA, ABA, GA) and down-regulated by PP333 and ZT, suggesting that these genes act as negative regulators of sugarcane tillering and participate in the tillering regulatory network by mediating multiple hormone signaling pathways. Taken together, this study systematically characterized the evolutionary, structural and expression characteristics of the ShRZFP family in sugarcane, revealed the potential association between this family and sugarcane tillering, and fills the corresponding research gap of this gene family in sugarcane. This work also provides reliable candidate gene resources and a theoretical reference for dissecting the molecular mechanism of sugarcane tillering regulation and advancing sugarcane molecular breeding.
