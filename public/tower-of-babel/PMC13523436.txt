Surgical treatment of giant diagonal branch aneurysms accompanied by obstructive coronary artery disease with guidance from 3D printed models: a case report
Authors: Zhenyu Zhai, Qing Li, Qiwen Mo, Zirong Xia, Zhen Xia, Minjing Zuo, Heng Yang, Yu Tao, Jianhua Yu, Yanhua Tang, Juxiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery aneurysms are focal dilatations that exceed the diameter of normal adjacent vessels by a factor of at least 1.5-fold. Multimodal imaging is essential when attempting to improve the efficacy of curative interventions in aneurysm patients as it can facilitate the comprehensive functional and anatomical characterization of these intricate vascular lesions.Case summaryIn this report, we describe the case of a patient with giant diagonal branch aneurysms accompanied by obstructive coronary artery disease. Successive coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and surgical resection were performed to effectively treat these conditions, and these procedures were aided by the use of three-dimensional (3D) printed models of the heart.ConclusionEmploying computed tomography-guided reconstruction procedures in combination with the use of multiplanar, multicolor 3D printed models can enable surgeons to better assess the dimensions, source, and relationships of target vessels with adjacent tissues, thereby ensuring that preoperative planning can be completed more effectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06130-y.Keywords: Giant coronary aneurysm, Computed tomography, Three-dimensional printed model, Case reports

Introduction

Coronary artery aneurysms refer to focal dilatations where the arterial diameter exceeds that of the surrounding healthy coronary segments [1]. The abnormal aneurysm-associated expansion of the vessel lumen is defined by a diameter that is more than 1.5-fold greater than that of the largest coronary vessel lumen or that of adjacent normal vessels [2, 3]. The most frequently affected site is the right coronary artery, followed by the proximal portion of the left anterior descending artery, while lesions of the left circumflex artery are somewhat less prevalent [4, 5]. In the clinic, the identification of patients affected by coronary artery aneurysms remains relatively rare such that they are detected in just 0.3–4.9% of adults undergoing coronary angiography [6].

In adults, atherosclerosis remains the leading cause of coronary abnormalities [6, 7], whereas in children and adolescents, the predominant etiological basis for such abnormalities is Kawasaki disease [8–10]. The incidence of such aneurysms can also be influenced by a diverse array of other risk factors including trauma, dissection, autoimmune conditions (e.g., arteritis, periarteritis nodosa, or systemic lupus erythematosus), syphilis, mycotic emboli, and complications associated with certain procedures (e.g., atherectomy, laser-based procedures, or angioplasty) [11, 12]. Coronary artery aneurysms can, when they arise, contribute to a range of adverse outcomes such as mechanical obstruction, dissection, embolization, thrombosis, the erosion of adjacent structures, and rupture [13]. Given the unpredictable nature of the progression of coronary aneurysms, surgical resection of the affected segment remains a primary approach to the management of this condition [14, 15].

Patients with coronary artery aneurysms face complex anatomy and high intervention risks. Traditional imaging methods often fail to fully capture aneurysms and their spatial relationships. 3D printing technology addresses these limitations by creating physical models from imaging data, enhancing visualization of complex structures. It is now effectively used in managing structural heart and coronary artery diseases [16, 17]. Research and case reports have proven its effectiveness in preoperative assessments for coronary stenting and Coronary Artery Fistula (CAF) interventions, and in enhancing doctor-patient communication and clinical education [18, 19]. For example, 3D printed models have been used to guide transcatheter interventions for CAF, create in vitro aneurysm models to test device performance, and assess lesion rupture risk [19, 20]. This case report explores the use of 3D printing for preoperative anatomical assessment and personalized treatment planning in coronary artery aneurysm patients, based on prior research.

Case report

Preoperative characteristics

A 73-year-old woman who had experienced a fall from an elevated position 10 years previously presented with aggravated chest pain and dyspnea after activity that had been present for more than one month. Echocardiography revealed severe dilatation of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) with thrombosis, measuring ~ 76 × 63 mm, although left ventricular function remained normal (Fig. 1A). Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a giant aneurysm (~ 67 × 57 mm) located in the diagonal branch, suggesting Kawasaki disease as a potential etiological basis (Fig. 1B). Coronary angiography (CAG) further confirmed the presence of a giant diagonal branch aneurysm with severe ~ 85% stenosis of the proximal right coronary artery (Fig. 1C-D). Given the lack of congruity among these imaging results CT-guided reconstruction and multiplanar, multicolor 3D printed models were used to achieve better spatial visualization.

B Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed ~ 67 mm x 57 mm aneurysm located in the diagonal branch. C Coronary angiography (CAG) detected a giant diagonal branch aneurysm. 3D printing and surgical plan formulation

The scans underwent processing by importing the patient’s CT data in the Digital Imaging and Communication of Medicine format into commercial software (Mimics Research 21.0, Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) to reconstruct a 3D model of the heart. Image segmentation was the core step in this workflow, primarily accomplished through techniques such as layer masking and region growing. Segmentation was performed jointly by trained biomedical engineers and a cardiologist with 10 years of experience in cardiac CT, to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Subsequently, a cardiovascular physician, under the supervision of the cardiologist with 10 years of experience in cardiac CT (SM-E), evaluated each scan to determine the optimal visualization approach for highlighting the coronary arteries and abnormalities. This involved intentionally excluding certain structures to enhance clarity. The decision to excise segments of the cardiovascular anatomy was justified by the unobstructed visual access and ability to trace the coronary arteries. The models utilized in this case report were scaled to a 1:1 ratio. Before constructing the 3D model, multidimensional data verification and comparative analysis were conducted. A cardiologist visually confirmed the model’s anatomical authenticity and dimensional accuracy.

Following image segmentation and the creation of a basic 3D mesh model, the mesh data underwent post-processing in Materialise 3-matic Research 11.0, which included surface smoothing, removal of excess tissue, fusion of overlapping structures, hollowing of internal cavities, and uniform wall thickness calibration. A standard STL file was then exported with a fixed shell thickness of 3 mm for all samples. These STL files were imported into a Stratasys Polyjet 850 multimaterial full color 3D printer for fabrication, with materials chosen based on the mechanical properties of cardiovascular tissue and preoperative simulation needs. Using the printer’s multi-material capability, medical photopolymer resins were applied selectively: soft resin for elastic structures like cardiac chambers and vessels, and rigid resin for aneurysm walls and sclerotic lesions. Consistent with physiological conventions, left heart structures were colored red and right heart structures blue, creating a personalized 3D model that reflected the individual’s anatomy. The models were then assessed for quality by a biomedical engineer with seven years of 3D printing experience and reviewed by a cardiologist to ensure accurate anatomical representation based on CT scans, focusing on dimensional accuracy, structural integrity, morphological consistency, key dimension errors, and structural identification accuracy. The criteria included: dimensional accuracy assessed at five anatomical sites using a digital caliper (0.01 mm accuracy), with deviations from the original STL model ≤ 0.25 mm; visual inspection for structural integrity under 500 lx, ensuring no delamination, cracks, or support residue, and clear vessel lumens; morphological consistency requiring the model’s cardiac chambers, valves, and great vessels to match CT anatomy without artifacts; key dimensions measured at 3–5 landmarks, with errors under 1.5 mm or relative errors < 5%; and structural identification by physicians on the unlabeled model, with the correct identification rate recorded.

The 3D-printed model depicted a giant diagonal branch aneurysm with a maximum diameter of about 80 mm, while preoperative CTA measured it at approximately 67 × 57 mm, with discrepancies due to different measurement planes. Intraoperative direct inspection confirmed that the maximum aneurysm diameter was about 8 cm, consistent with the 3D-printed model result. This approach revealed a giant diagonal branch aneurysm with a maximum diameter of ∼8 cm that was accompanied by severe stenosis of the proximal RCA (Fig. 2).

(A) 3D reconstruction using Materialise 3-matic (Leven, Belgium) software in the front view. B-D The 3D printed model in the front view and lateral view, respectively. The green part in aneurysm is thrombus

Based on the 3D printed model, a comprehensive understanding of the anatomical morphology of the anomalies and its adjacent tissues, including left main (LM) coronary artery, left anterior descending (LAD)artery, left circumflex (LCX) artery, diagonal branch, ascending aorta, pulmonary artery (PA), right ventricle (RV), left ventricular (LV), and left atrium (LA), can be obtained (Figs. 1F, G). The direct observation of the patient’s distinct coronary lesion geometry and the surrounding structural configuration through physical cardiac models facilitates a more objective assessment of anatomical conditions by the surgical team. Consequently, clinicians are able to devise targeted surgical strategies that align with the specific characteristics of the lesion and develop the surgical plan by assessing the anomalies size and its spatial relationship with neighboring tissues. From the model, surgeons could observe that the giant aneurysm located in the proximal diagonal branch accompanied by a severe stenosis in the middle segment of the right coronary artery. In this case, the initial step involved the surgical excision of the giant coronary aneurysm, followed by right coronary artery revascularization through the implementation of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), utilizing the great saphenous vein, which simulated on the 3D printed model before the surgery.

Surgical steps and perioperative follow-up outcomes

The identified giant diagonal branch aneurysm was surgically excised in a beating-heart procedure under cardiopulmonary bypass, after which coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was performed utilizing the great saphenous vein to the posterior descending branch (PDB), as was simulated preoperatively using the 3D printed model (Fig. 3A-C). Subsequent pathology evaluations confirmed the presence of a diffuse atherosclerotic process in the resected surgical specimen (Fig. 3D). After the procedure, the patient was discharged in a good state.

Discussion

Coronary aneurysms are inherently complex, and the use of individually prepared 3D-printed models can provide clinicians with greater insight into the visuospatial characteristics of the defect as they relate to surrounding anatomical structures. This, in turn, can facilitate greater anatomical comprehension conducive to superior preoperative planning, as in the present case. When treating aneurysm patients, it is important that clinicians be able to recognize relational structures in order to more effectively assess the positioning, characteristics, and progression of the target aneurysm, ensuring that the degree of abnormality and potential risk to the patient can be adequately established. These insights can guide the clinical decision-making related to aneurysm management. In prior reports, 3D printing technologies have successfully been utilized for the reconstruction and replication of the coronary arterial tree, emphasizing the value of this approach to coronary artery visualization [21, 22]. In contrast to visual observation of 3D models on a screen, a 3D-printed model offers tactile feedback and allows for preoperative rehearsal, helping the surgeon familiarize themselves with the patient’s unique anatomy in a risk-free setting—especially important for the rare coronary anatomical variation in this case [23].

There have been few clinical studies and reports on using advanced 3D printing for preoperative assessment and surgery in coronary aneurysm patients. Most research has focused on 3D printing for interventional therapy in coronary artery fistulas and aneurysms or surgical planning for ventricular and other aneurysms [24–26]. However, its use in complex cases like a giant diagonal branch aneurysm with severe proximal right coronary artery stenosis is even less common. Both the current case and Kamio et al. highlight the importance of 3D modeling and printing in preoperative planning for rare, complex coronary artery aneurysms. Kamio et al. addressed a giant left anterior descending artery aneurysm with additional systemic aneurysms using a two-stage surgical approach [27]. In contrast, the present case involved a giant aneurysm from the diagonal branch with severe right coronary artery stenosis, managed with a single-stage surgery. These cases demonstrate that patient-specific 3D printing is a valuable, adaptable tool for individualized surgical planning in complex coronary artery disease. In the present case, based on the patient’s clinical complaints, we comprehensively evaluated the high-risk features including thrombosis formation, aneurysm rupture and myocardial ischemia jointly induced by giant coronary artery aneurysm and obstructive coronary artery disease. After multidisciplinary team discussion, surgical treatment was ultimately determined as the optimal therapeutic strategy for this patient, and state-of-the-art 3D printing technology was applied for systematic preoperative evaluation of this case. In this case, our approach led to a successful surgical procedure and the subsequent discharge of the patient in satisfactory condition.

Due to the rarity and complexity of the coronary pathology in this case, few clinicians possess the necessary surgical experience. Even experienced cardiac surgeons may struggle with unique anatomical variations, and relying solely on traditional imaging for intraoperative decisions can heighten the risk of anastomotic errors and vascular injuries, potentially leading to reoperation or long-term graft occlusion. Literature indicates that redo coronary artery bypass grafting significantly raises hospital costs (by an average of USD 9,845) and is linked to higher rates of complications such as infection, thromboembolism, and stroke [28], with venous thromboembolism increasing hospitalization costs by about USD 23,150 [29]. The 3D-printed model used in this study was funded by our research team after obtaining informed consent from the patient and family, resulting in no out-of-pocket expenses for the patient. While 3D-printed models incur some production costs, they offer significant advantages for both clinicians and patients. For clinicians, they allow for precise preoperative anatomical assessments and optimal surgical planning, overcoming traditional imaging limitations. For patients, they reduce clinical risks like intraoperative injury and reoperation, and help them understand their condition better through the physical model. This understanding improves treatment adherence and long-term prognosis. Thus, the clinical benefits of 3D printing technology outweigh its production costs compared to the expenses and burdens of reoperation and complications. It is an ideal individualized medical adjunct for complex and rare coronary artery disease. It should be emphasized that this technology is not a mandatory routine cost for all coronary artery surgeries but rather an individualized medical option based on the rarity and anatomical complexity of the disease.

In a prior study reported by Matthew Lee, the feasibility of employing 3D-printed models of the heart to replicate coronary artery anatomy and provide a better understanding of coronary abnormalities was demonstrated [30]. In the previous study, the use of white resin for 3D-printed cardiac models hindered the visual distinction of intracardiac structures, possibly causing some anatomical abnormalities to be missed.

This study also faces limitations: a standardized workflow for image reconstruction and visualization of complex coronary lesions is yet to be developed. Consequently, some image details, such as right coronary artery compression and subtle structures around the thrombus, were not clearly visible due to initial reconstruction parameters and display conditions. Second, variations in imaging principles, spatial dimensions, and measurement systems among the CTA-based 3D model, echocardiography, and original CT images led to minor but acceptable measurement discrepancies. However, intraoperative inspection confirmed the aneurysm size matched the 3D-printed model, proving its anatomical accuracy. Third, the patient, due to age and financial constraints, opted out of gold-standard postoperative coronary angiography, undergoing only noninvasive tests like echocardiography and chest CT. This lack of long-term angiographic data limits the assessment of anastomotic patency over time.

Future research could focus on: creating a standardized protocol for multicenter image reconstruction and 3D printing; optimizing reconstruction thresholds and improving color detail annotation; integrating multimodal imaging to minimize measurement errors; extending postoperative follow-up with invasive coronary angiography to assess long-term graft patency; and conducting cost-effectiveness analyses and exploring multi-material and multi-color printing strategies to validate the clinical value of this technique for complex coronary lesions.

Conclusion

The present case underscored the value of utilizing modern 3D printing technologies to aid the preoperative assessment and surgical management of patients with giant diagonal branch aneurysms accompanied by obstructive coronary artery disease.
