Optical variability and optical–mid-infrared dust lags in Type 1 changing-look AGNs 2026 Optical variability and optical–mid-infrared dust lags in Type 1 changing-look AGNs – 3
Y. Tao, J. Tang, and X. Wei

Abstract
Changing-look active galactic nuclei (CL AGNs) show large changes in luminosity and optical spectral state on time-scales of a few years and probe time-dependent accretion in the disc–BLR–torus system. We present a systematic statistical study of their optical variability in a well-defined Type-1 phase, using g g - and r r -band light curves from the Zwicky Transient Facility for 165 CL AGNs. A subsample of 34 objects also has NEOWISE W ​ 1 W1 and W ​ 2 W2 light curves, which we use to measure optical–mid-infrared time lags. We use structure functions and a damped random-walk model to characterise variability amplitudes and time-scales on rest-frame scales of tens to a few hundreds of days, and examine their dependence on black hole mass, luminosity and Eddington ratio. In the Type-1 phase, the short-time-scale ( ∼ 30 \sim 30 d) optical variability amplitude shows little dependence on black hole mass, luminosity or Eddington ratio. By contrast, the longer-term amplitudes on 150 150 – 300 300 d time-scales and the DRW time-scales increase slowly with black hole mass and luminosity and still show no clear dependence on Eddington ratio. The sample shows a ubiquitous ‘bluer-when-brighter’ trend and larger variability at shorter wavelengths, consistent with continuum variability from a multi-temperature disc. For the NEOWISE subsample, the dust lag–luminosity relation inferred from the optical–MIR lags is similar to that of normal Type-1 AGNs. Overall, CL AGNs in the Type-1 phase behave like normal Type-1 AGNs within the standard disc–BLR–dusty-torus framework, but are more prone to large continuum reconfigurations on year-like time-scales.



1
Introduction

Active galactic nuclei (AGNs) are multi-component systems powered by accretion on to a central supermassive black hole (SMBH), comprising an accretion disc, a broad-line region (BLR), a narrow-line region (NLR) and a surrounding dusty torus, and emitting from radio to X-rays. In the unified model, the observed diversity of AGNs is attributed mainly to differences in accretion-flow geometry, dust obscuration and line-of-sight orientation, rather than to intrinsically distinct central engines. In this framework, the optical/UV continuum is usually dominated by thermal emission from a multi-temperature blackbody disc, while the infrared (IR) radiation is produced by reprocessing of the disc emission by the dusty torus. AGNs show significant variability on time-scales from days to years, tracing fluctuations in the accretion flow and the reverberation response of the dust and BLR, so variability has become a key tool for probing both accretion physics and spatial structure. In recent years, structure-function (SF) statistics and damped random-walk (DRW) models have been widely used to describe variability amplitudes on different time-scales, characteristic time-scales and their scaling relations with black hole and accretion properties in large AGN samples
(
24
;
18
, e.g.)
.

Changing-look active galactic nuclei (CL AGNs) are a class of sources that undergo dramatic luminosity and spectral-type transitions on time-scales of years or less: their broad Balmer emission lines and continuum can switch between Type-1 and Type-2 classifications, often accompanied by large-amplitude variations in optical and mid-infrared (MIR) brightness. This behaviour challenges the traditional static picture in which AGN types are set solely by geometry and obscuration, and is now widely thought to reflect non-stationary evolution of the accretion disc and its surrounding structures on humanly accessible time-scales. Previous work, from intensive monitoring of individual objects to statistical studies of larger samples, has explored possible physical drivers of CL events, including rapid changes in accretion rate, restructuring of the disc–corona system, variable line-of-sight obscuration and various instability or disc-wind scenarios. However, most of these studies have focused on “before-and-after” comparisons of spectra and light curves across the state change itself, whereas the variability behaviour of CL AGNs while in the Type-1 state is much less well characterised
(
10
;
15
;
20
;
19
, e.g.)
.

Existing large-scale variability studies have mainly concentrated on “normal” Type-1 AGNs, and have established general trends linking variability amplitude to wavelength, luminosity, black hole mass (
M_{\rm BH}
) and Eddington ratio (
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
). However, it remains unclear whether CL AGNs in their Type-1 phase follow the same statistical relations or instead form a distinct sub-population with more extreme amplitudes and time-scales. The dramatic changes in the central continuum in CL AGNs must also affect the ionisation state and thermal balance of the BLR and dusty torus. This raises the question of whether the optical-to-MIR dust lag–luminosity relation (dust lag–
L_{\rm bol}
) in CL AGNs departs from the near-/mid-IR radius–luminosity (
R
–
L
) relations measured for normal Type-1 AGNs, and how the torus responds in time to large changes in the ionising continuum. In addition, colour-dependent variability patterns—such as the “bluer-when-brighter” trend and the tendency for larger variability amplitudes at shorter wavelengths—are widely reported in normal Type-1 AGNs and are usually interpreted as a natural outcome of thermal fluctuations in a multi-temperature blackbody disc. Whether CL AGNs retain this behaviour while in the Type-1 phase remains to be tested with statistical samples
(
21
;
29
;
23
, e.g.)
.

Motivated by these considerations, we present a systematic analysis of the optical variability properties of 165 CL AGNs in the Type-1 phase, based on
g
- and
r
-band light curves from the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) and on physical parameters compiled from the literature. For a subsample of 34 sources with NEOWISE
W1/W2
coverage, we also measure optical-to-MIR time lags. By applying both structure-function (SF) statistics and DRW modelling within a unified framework, we characterise the intrinsic variability amplitudes and characteristic time-scales from tens to hundreds of days, and examine their scaling relations with
M_{\rm BH}
, bolometric luminosity (
L_{\rm bol}
) and
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
. We further study the colour-dependent variability between the
g
and
r
bands and derive empirical
\tau_{\rm W1,W2}
–
L_{\rm bol}
relations from the optical–MIR lags, thereby testing whether the inner dust radius of CL AGNs in the Type-1 phase departs from the standard
R
–
L
scaling established for normal AGNs.

2
Sample and data

Our starting point is a parent sample of changing-look active galactic nuclei (CL AGNs) compiled from recent systematic studies.
5
;
6
identified 617 CL AGNs from large spectroscopic surveys, and
4
reported 52 newly confirmed candidates. We merge these catalogues into a combined list of 669 unique CL AGNs, removing duplicates by matching source names and coordinates and adopting the most recent redshift and spectral classification available in the literature.

For consistency, we use the sample definitions and the published “on/off” state classifications provided in these works, and do not attempt an independent spectral re-classification. In particular, we take the identifications of broad-line appearance and disappearance, together with the associated “on-state” (Type-1) and “off-state” (Type-2) labels and documented spectral histories, directly from the original authors’ analyses. For each source, we require that at least one spectrum classified as Type-1 in the literature falls within the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF;
1
;
17
) observing baseline, and we define the time interval around this spectroscopic epoch as the “Type-1 phase” used in our variability and dust-lag analysis. Because of the limited cadence of spectroscopic monitoring, additional CL transitions within spectral gaps or outside the ZTF baseline cannot be strictly ruled out; throughout this paper, “Type-1 phase” therefore refers to the on-state interval constrained by the available spectra and photometry, rather than a guarantee of the instantaneous spectral type at all times. Applying these criteria and basic consistency checks, we select 165 CL AGNs that are in a spectroscopically confirmed Type-1 phase within the ZTF baseline and meet the quality requirements for our variability analysis. These objects constitute our main optical sample. Their basic properties (coordinates, redshifts, black hole masses and luminosities) are listed in Table A1.

The optical variability data are taken from the public ZTF data releases. For each of the 165 CL AGNs, we extract
g
- and
r
-band light curves from the ZTF difference-photometry database using their celestial coordinates, and retain only those photometric epochs that fall within the spectroscopically defined Type-1 phase. Our variability analysis relies primarily on the
g
-band light curves, while the
r
-band data are used mainly to quantify colour-dependent variability.

To investigate optical-to-mid-infrared (MIR) time lags, we supplement the optical data with NEOWISE photometry in the
W1
(3.4
\mu
m) and
W2
(4.6
\mu
m) bands
(
25
;
16
)
. Starting from the 165 Type-1 CL AGNs, we select those with sufficiently long temporal baselines, an adequate number of visits in the NEOWISE Single Exposure database and high photometric quality in both
W1
and
W2
. This yields a MIR subsample of 34 sources, for which the
g
-band and
W1/W2
light curves are used in the time-lag analysis described in Section
3

3
Methods

3.1
Data pre-processing

We use the 165 ZTF
g
-band Type-1 light curves selected in Section
2
as our starting sample. For the structure-function (SF) analysis we apply only basic quality cuts, excluding data points with missing time, magnitude or uncertainty, as well as those with non-positive or anomalously large photometric errors.

For the light curves used in the DRW modelling, we adopt a standard pre-processing pipeline in addition to these basic cuts
(
13
, cf.)
. First, we perform a single pass of
3\sigma
clipping on each light curve to remove isolated outliers. Next, multiple
g
-band measurements obtained within the same observing night are combined into a single epoch using an error-weighted average. We then bin observations within adjacent three-day windows. This yields a more uniform temporal sampling and noise level for the subsequent DRW fits.

For the optical–MIR lag analysis with
JAVELIN

(
28
;
27
)
, we apply a unified pre-processing scheme to both the ZTF and NEOWISE data. On the optical side, starting from the raw magnitude light curves, we use the same quality-control and nightly averaging steps as above and then convert the magnitudes to flux densities. On the MIR side, we start from the NEOWISE
W1/W2
Single Exposure magnitudes, filter out low-quality or contaminated measurements using the official quality flags, and average multiple exposures within a single visit (error-weighted) to obtain a representative photometric point. These measurements are likewise converted to flux densities with associated uncertainties to serve as input to
JAVELIN
. Throughout this study, all time intervals are transformed to the rest frame,
t_{\rm rest}=t_{\rm obs}/(1+z)
.

3.2
Variability characteristics

3.2.1
Structure function

To characterise the optical variability amplitude, we use the first-order SF
(
3
;
14
, e.g.)
. For each light curve transformed to the rest frame, we compute the time lag
\Delta t_{ij}=t_{j}-t_{i}
, the magnitude difference
\Delta m_{ij}=m_{j}-m_{i}
and the corresponding variance of the measurement errors,
\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2}
. The time lags are grouped into logarithmic bins of width 0.25 dex over the range
15
–
500
d, and bins containing fewer than 50 pairs are discarded. Within each bin, the SF is defined as

SF(\Delta t)=\sqrt{\langle(\Delta m_{ij})^{2}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdots\rangle
denote averages over all pairs in the bin. We then linearly interpolate the binned SF to obtain values at rest-frame time-scales of 30, 150 and 300 d (denoted
SF_{30}
,
SF_{150}
and
SF_{300}
, respectively), which are used as measures of variability amplitude in the subsequent correlation analysis.

3.2.2
Damped random-walk model

We employ a DRW model
(
8
;
13
)
to describe the longer-term variability behaviour. Treating each single-band light curve as a continuous-time stochastic process, we estimate the short-term variability amplitude
\hat{\sigma}
and the characteristic damping time-scale
\tau
. From the fitted
\hat{\sigma}
and
\tau
, we derive the asymptotic SF amplitude as

SF_{\infty}=\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}.

3.2.3
Optical colour-dependence metrics

To quantify colour-dependent variability, we first match quasi-simultaneous
g
- and
r
-band observations by requiring
\lvert\Delta t_{\rm rest}\rvert\leq 0.5
d, and treat each matched pair as a single epoch. We then perform a weighted linear regression of the form

\ (g-r)=a+\beta g

to obtain the colour–brightness slope
\beta
. In our convention,
\beta>0
corresponds to a “bluer-when-brighter” trend. Before fitting, we apply
3\sigma
clipping on the residuals to remove strong outliers, and we retain only light curves with at least eight quasi-simultaneous pairs for the statistical analysis.

In addition, we compare the variability amplitudes in the two bands using their SF values at 30, 150 and 300 d. We define an amplitude-based colour-dependence metric as

\Delta_{\rm chrom}\equiv SF_{g}(\Delta t)-SF_{r}(\Delta t).

A positive value of
\Delta_{\rm chrom}(\Delta t)
indicates that the variability amplitude in the blue (
g
) band is systematically larger than that in the red (
r
) band on the given time-scale, providing a complementary statistical measure of colour dependence.

3.3
MIR versus optical analysis

To estimate the time lag between the optical and MIR variability, we use the public code
JAVELIN

(
28
;
27
)
. Under the DRW assumption,
JAVELIN
models the optical light curve as the driving continuum, and treats the
W1/W2
light curves as reprocessed signals obtained by convolving the driver with a top-hat transfer function that applies smoothing, scaling and a bulk time shift. The posterior distributions of the time lags are explored via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. We restrict the prior on the observed-frame lag to positive values, adopting a uniform search range of
0
–
400
d. The final lag
\tau
and its
1\sigma
uncertainty are taken as the median and the 16th–84th percentile range of the posterior distribution, respectively.

We then visually inspect the
JAVELIN
posteriors and fit residuals for all sources in a uniform manner. We retain only those lag measurements that show a well-defined single peak within the
0
–
400
d range, where the main peak is clearly separated from both zero and the upper prior boundary, and the fractional uncertainty is not excessively large. Cases with strongly multi-modal posteriors, posteriors dominated by aliases caused by the roughly semi-annual NEOWISE sampling cadence, or results consistent with zero lag are considered unreliable and excluded from the subsequent dust lag–luminosity analysis.

To characterise the relation between the MIR time lag and the bolometric luminosity, we fit the following relation in logarithmic space:

\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\tau}{\mathrm{d}}\right)=a\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{L_{\rm bol}}{10^{11}L_{\odot}}\right)+b,

where
a
and
b
are the slope and intercept of the lag–luminosity relation. Unless otherwise noted, all time lags quoted in this paper are in the rest frame, i.e.
\tau\equiv\tau_{\rm obs}/(1+z)
.

5
Discussion

5.1
Optical variability and physical parameters in the Type-1 phase

For the subsample of 165 CL AGNs in the Type-1 phase constructed in this work, we measured structure-function amplitudes on multiple time-scales (
SF_{30}
,
SF_{150}
,
SF_{300}
and
SF_{\infty}
) and the DRW damping time-scale (
\tau
) from the ZTF
g
-band light curves. We then compared these variability metrics with the supermassive black hole mass (
M_{\rm BH}
), bolometric luminosity (
L_{\rm bol}
) and Eddington ratio (
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
). Overall, we find no significant correlations between the short-time-scale amplitude
SF_{30}
and any of these physical parameters. In contrast,
SF_{150}
,
SF_{300}
and
SF_{\infty}
show weak-to-moderate positive correlations with both
\log M_{\rm BH}
and
\log L_{\rm bol}
. The damping time-scale
\tau
likewise increases systematically with
\log M_{\rm BH}
and shows a weaker positive trend with
\log L_{\rm bol}
, as illustrated in Fig.
9
. After accounting for the strong correlation between
M_{\rm BH}
and
L_{\rm bol}
, we do not find any significant independent correlation with
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
in the residuals.

These behaviours are qualitatively consistent with recent optical-variability statistics based on large samples of normal Type-1 AGNs, for example the ZTF-based DRW analysis of SDSS AGNs by
2
and the ZTF variability study of Swift/BAT-selected Seyferts by
7
, in which the long-term variability amplitude and characteristic time-scale are found to increase slowly with
\log M_{\rm BH}
and
\log L_{\rm bol}
, while the influence of
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
appears secondary. Our results indicate that, even when restricted to CL AGNs that have undergone dramatic spectral transitions, their properties within the spectroscopically confirmed Type-1 window do not show strong departures from these trends; instead, they lie largely within the scatter of the relations defined by normal Type-1 AGNs. Taken together, the
SF_{30}
and
SF_{150}/SF_{300}
results suggest the presence of two variability regimes. On short (
\sim 30
d) time-scales the fluctuations behave almost like local, stochastic “noise” that does not “remember” the global black hole mass or luminosity, whereas on longer (
\gtrsim 150
d) time-scales the variability amplitude grows and begins to correlate with
M_{\rm BH}
and
L_{\rm bol}
, consistent with fluctuations that propagate over a substantial fraction of the accretion disc.

A common interpretation is that optical variability on rest-frame time-scales of tens to hundreds of days and longer primarily reflects large-scale power fluctuations associated with the characteristic geometric scales and thermal/viscous time-scales of the accretion disc. In this picture,
M_{\rm BH}
and
L_{\rm bol}
modulate the long-term variability amplitude and the DRW damping time-scale by setting the typical disc radius, temperature profile and available energy reservoir. By contrast, the single-epoch estimate of
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
mainly traces the mean accretion state averaged over longer-term evolution; given the current sample size and error budget, its independent contribution to long-term variability is not clearly isolated. Within this framework, our Type-1 CL AGN subsample appears to occupy the same
M_{\rm BH}
–
L_{\rm bol}
variability scaling relations as normal Type-1 AGNs, rather than forming a population of strong outliers.

It should be emphasised that our correlation analysis and regression results are still limited by measurement uncertainties in
M_{\rm BH}
and
L_{\rm bol}
and by the finite dynamic range of the sample. More precise black hole mass estimates and a broader coverage of parameter space in future work will be needed to test whether CL AGNs exhibit systematic offsets at the extreme ends of these relations.

From a physical standpoint, the observed trends are naturally explained in the context of continuum models dominated by accretion-rate fluctuations. In such models, perturbations within the disc propagate on thermal or viscous time-scales, and the amplitude and time-scale of long-term variability are controlled mainly by the black hole mass and total radiative power, rather than by the instantaneous
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
inferred from a single spectrum. The qualitative agreement between the SF and DRW scalings found here for CL AGNs in the Type-1 phase and those reported for normal Type-1 AGNs suggests that, even after undergoing large on/off transitions, the statistical properties of disc variability in the subsequent Type-1 phase remain embedded within the unified accretion-fluctuation paradigm, rather than pointing to a fundamentally different variability mechanism.

5.2
Colour dependence

Using the quasi-simultaneous ZTF
g
- and
r
-band measurements, we first fitted the linear slope
\beta
of the colour–brightness relation,
(g-r)
versus
g
, for each source as defined in Section
3.2.3
. In parallel, we quantified the long-term chromaticity of the variability by comparing the SF amplitudes of the two bands at fixed rest-frame time-scales of 30, 150 and 300 d, through

\Delta_{\rm chrom}=SF_{g}(\Delta t)-SF_{r}(\Delta t).

The distributions of
\beta
and
\Delta_{\rm chrom}(\Delta t)
are shown in Fig.
5
and Fig.
6
, respectively.For the majority of CL AGNs within the Type-1 phase we find
\beta>0
, and the medians of
\Delta_{\rm chrom}(\Delta t)
at
\Delta t=30
, 150 and 300 d are all slightly positive, with only a small fraction of objects consistent with zero or mildly negative values. Thus, on rest-frame time-scales of tens to a few hundreds of days, Type-1 CL AGNs show both a clear “bluer-when-brighter” trend and systematically larger variability amplitudes in the blue (
g
) band than in the red (
r
) band. Statistically, this behaviour is in good qualitative agreement with the BWB phenomenon widely reported for normal Type-1 AGNs.

In the standard picture of a multi-temperature blackbody accretion disc, such trends arise naturally: when the inner disc continuum, which dominates the optical
g/r
bands, heats up, the relative flux enhancement is stronger at shorter (bluer) wavelengths, while red, effectively less variable components — such as host-galaxy starlight — dilute the amplitude of the observed colour changes. Our results therefore suggest that, even for CL AGNs that have undergone large on/off transitions, the colour–brightness behaviour during the spectroscopically confirmed Type-1 phase is still governed by similar continuum components, characterised by stronger variability at bluer wavelengths, rather than by a colour-variability mode that is fundamentally different from that of normal Type-1 AGNs.

We caution, however, that our colour analysis does not explicitly decompose the host-galaxy light and emission-line contributions. For some low-redshift objects, the flux from broad Balmer lines and strong narrow lines (e.g. [O
iii
]) in the
g/r
bands is non-negligible, and the reverberation response of these lines to continuum variations may affect the measured values of
\beta
and
\Delta_{\rm chrom}
to some extent. The constraints presented here should therefore be regarded as global, statistical statements about the combined emission from continuum plus lines, rather than a precise diagnosis of the bare disc continuum alone. Future work combining high-S/N spectral decomposition with multi-band variability monitoring will be needed to disentangle more cleanly the respective colour responses of the continuum and emission lines in CL AGN samples.

5.3
Dust lag–luminosity relation and the unified picture of CL AGNs

Mid-infrared variability of CL AGNs has also been explored using
WISE
/
NEOWISE

light curves, but so far only for relatively small samples
(e.g.
12
).
Motivated by these results, we make use of the long-term
NEOWISE

W1/W2

light curves for our CL AGN sample.
From the parent sample of 165 Type-1 CL AGNs, we selected a subset of 34 objects that show high-quality variability in the NEOWISE
W1/W2
bands. We used
JAVELIN
to model the optical-to-mid-infrared (MIR) time lags (
g\rightarrow W1/W2
) under the assumption that the variability is driven by a damped random-walk process. For each source, we retained only those lag measurements with unimodal posterior distributions whose primary peak is clearly separated from both zero and the upper edge of the adopted prior range, and whose uncertainties are not excessively large. Cases that are clearly affected by aliases introduced by the roughly semi-annual NEOWISE sampling, or dominated by spurious zero-lag solutions, were rejected.

The resulting log–log fits between the rest-frame
W1/W2
lags and
L_{\rm bol}
yield dust lag–luminosity relations with slopes of order
\sim 0.3
. After accounting for statistical and systematic uncertainties, this slope is somewhat shallower than the naive prediction of a simple dust-sublimation-radius scaling,
R_{\rm sub}\propto L^{0.5}
. However, it is consistent, within the errors, with recent dust lag–luminosity relations for normal Type-1 AGNs based on near-infrared reverberation mapping and WISE/NEOWISE monitoring, which typically yield slopes in the range
\sim 0.3
–0.4
(
22
;
9
;
26
, e.g.)
. Our CL AGN data points also lie broadly within the intrinsic scatter of these empirical radius–luminosity relations.

Taken together, these results suggest that, within the spectroscopically confirmed Type-1 windows sampled here, the characteristic size of the dusty torus in CL AGNs does not show any strong systematic offset from that of ordinary Type-1 AGNs. In other words, CL AGNs approximately follow the same dust radius–luminosity (
R
–
L
) scaling as normal Type-1 systems. In the context of the unified AGN model, this favours a picture in which the on/off transitions of CL AGNs are driven primarily by large changes in the ionising radiation from the inner disc and the illumination state of the broad-line region (BLR), while the outer dusty torus, once a new quasi-stable Type-1 state is reached, retains an average radius–luminosity relation similar to that of normal Type-1 AGNs.

Several important limitations should be kept in mind. First, our dust-lag subsample comprises only 34 sources with a relatively limited luminosity range, and the individual lag measurements remain significantly affected by the sparse NEOWISE sampling and by systematic uncertainties. We therefore cannot exclude the possibility of weak systematic offsets in the scatter or zero-point of the
R
–
L
relation for CL AGNs that might emerge from larger samples or higher-precision lag measurements. Our current results show only that, when CL AGNs are in a spectroscopically confirmed Type-1 phase, the typical scale of their dusty tori broadly obeys the standard
R
–
L
scaling within the unified AGN framework. Whether the torus undergoes substantial evolution in covering factor or inner radius during the Type-2 phase remains an open question, and will require future intensive multi-wavelength reverberation-mapping campaigns to address in detail.

6
Conclusions

Using CL AGN samples compiled from recent literature, we have constructed a set
of 165 sources that have a spectroscopically confirmed Type-1 phase within the
ZTF baseline and analysed their
g/r
-band variability. A subsample of 34 objects
with high-quality NEOWISE
W1/W2
coverage was used to study optical–mid-infrared
time lags. Our main conclusions are as follows.

1.

Optical variability scaling.

On a short rest-frame time-scale of
\sim 30
d, the structure-function amplitude
SF_{30}
shows no significant dependence on
M_{\rm BH}
,
L_{\rm bol}
or
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
. However, on longer (
\sim 150
–
300
d) time-scales the structure-function amplitudes
SF_{150}
,
SF_{300}
and
SF_{\infty}
, as well as the DRW damping time-scale
\tau
, increase slowly with
\log M_{\rm BH}
and
\log L_{\rm bol}
. while still showing no clear independent dependence on the Eddington ratio
\lambda_{\rm Edd}
. These
trends are qualitatively consistent with large-sample optical variability
statistics for normal Type-1 AGNs, and the Type-1-phase CL AGNs lie largely
within the intrinsic scatter of the relations defined by normal AGNs.

2.

Colour-dependent variability.

Quasi-simultaneous ZTF
g/r
photometry shows that most Type-1 CL AGNs have
positive colour slopes
\beta
in the
(g-r)
–
g
relation, and positive

\Delta_{\rm chrom}
at rest-frame time-scales of 30–300 d.
They therefore display the same “bluer-when-brighter” behaviour and larger
variability amplitudes at shorter wavelengths as normal Type-1 AGNs,
consistent with continuum variability dominated by a multi-temperature
accretion disc rather than a distinct colour-variability mode.

3.

Dust lag–luminosity behaviour.

For the 34-source NEOWISE subsample, the rest-frame
W1/W2
lags follow a
lag–luminosity relation that is broadly consistent, within the
uncertainties, with recent dust lag–luminosity relations for normal
Type-1 AGNs. Our CL AGN measurements fall within the scatter of these
empirical near-/mid-IR radius–luminosity (
R
–
L
) scalings and do not
show strong systematic offsets in the Type-1 phase

(
22
;
9
;
26
, see also)
.

Taken together, these results indicate that CL AGNs in a Type-1 phase are best
understood as systems undergoing strong temporal evolution within the standard
disc–BLR–dusty-torus configuration, rather than as a separate population
detached from the normal Type-1 sequence in terms of variability statistics or
dust geometry. Their distinctive feature lies in the large-scale reconstruction
of the continuum and broad-line emission on year-like time-scales, while their
Type-1 disc variability and torus scaling relations remain broadly compatible
with those of normal Type-1 AGNs
(
19
, e.g.)
. The
present work is limited by the sample size, the coverage of parameter space and
the cadence of the photometric data; larger CL AGN samples, denser optical–MIR
monitoring and broader multiwavelength campaigns (including X-rays) will be
needed to disentangle more fully the relative roles of accretion-rate
fluctuations and obscuration geometry in the changing-look phenomenon.
