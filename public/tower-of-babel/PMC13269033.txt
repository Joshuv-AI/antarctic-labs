A High Sensitive Detection of Droplet Digital PCR for the Diagnosis of Pulmonary Tuberculosis
Authors: Shangying Yang, Mingxiang Huang, Yunfan Xiong, ChunXia Tu, Ye Xu, Kanglin Wan, Xiaohong Chen, Fan Zhang, Xiaoqin Liao, Qiaoqian Chen, Jing Zhang, Jie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRapid and accurate diagnosis of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in the patients with pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is essential for the patients management and infection control, particularly in cases with negative results from conventional bacteriological tests, as early detection enables timely initiation of treatment and interruption of disease transmission.Patients and MethodsWe evaluated the diagnostic performance of a dual-target droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assay, targeting IS6110 and IS1081, using bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples collected from 506 hospitalized patients including 397 PTB and 109 non-PTB from August 2024 to December 2024. The performance of ddPCR was compared with that of MGIT960 culture, real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR), multi-color melting curve analysis (MMCA), and GeneXpert MTB/RIF (Xpert).ResultsThe ddPCR assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 89.7% (95% CI: 86.3–92.3%), specificity of 89.0% (95% CI: 81.5–94.0%), positive predictive value (PPV) of 96.7% (95% CI: 94.5–98.1%), and negative predictive value (NPV) of 70.3% (95% CI: 61.8–77.6%). The overall concordance rate was 89.5% (95% CI: 86.5–91.9%), with a Kappa value of 0.768 (95% CI: 0.712–0.824). The superior diagnostic performance of ddPCR was further supported by an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.893 (95% CI: 0.855–0.931). In a subgroup analysis of 216 bacteriologically negative PTB cases, ddPCR exhibited a sensitivity of 81.9%, which was significantly higher than that of Xpert (34.3%), MMCA (17.6%), and qPCR (31.0%) (all P < 0.001).ConclusionThe dual-target ddPCR assay targeting IS6110 and IS1081 offers a rapid, sensitive, and accurate method for the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis. Its cost-competitiveness and manageable workflow make it a promising tool for clinical practice.Keywords: pulmonary tuberculosis, droplet digital PCR, diagnosis, high sensitive

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Despite the first-ever global decline in TB incidence and mortality, the disease continues to pose a substantial and persistent public health challenge. China ranks the fourth among the 30 countries with a high TB burden globally.1 Of particular concern, bacteriologically negative pulmonary tuberculosis (BN-PTB) has emerged as a major bottleneck in TB control owing to its diagnostic complexity and concealed clinical risk. BN-PTB refers to active pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosed on the basis of indirect evidence, such as clinical manifestations, radiological findings, or interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), in the absence of microbiological confirmation by sputum smear microscopy or Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) culture. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), BN-PTB accounted for approximately 36–37% of newly notified pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) cases worldwide in 2024, with marked regional variation.2,3 Because of atypical clinical presentations, non-specific imaging features, and the inherent limitations of current diagnostic methods, BN-PTB patients are frequently misdiagnosed or missed altogether. Delayed diagnosis may lead to progression to bacteriologically positive PTB (BP-PTB), resulting in irreversible pulmonary damage. Moreover, despite low detectable bacillary loads, BN-PTB patients may still contribute to ongoing transmission and experience increased mortality, underscoring their significance as both a key target population and a major diagnostic challenge.

Although MTB culture remains the diagnostic gold standard, its prolonged turnaround time of 2–6 weeks is incompatible with the need for early clinical decision-making. Molecular diagnostic technologies, such as Xpert MTB/RIF (Cepheid, USA), have therefore been widely implemented, enabling rapid detection of MTB within approximately 2 hours. While Xpert MTB/RIF exhibits high specificity in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples from smear-negative PTB patients with scant or absent sputum, its sensitivity remains suboptimal at approximately 67%.4 The next-generation Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra further improves analytical sensitivity, particularly for paucibacillary specimens; however, its high cost limits widespread implementation in many low- and middle-income settings. Consequently, there remains an urgent need to develop diagnostic approaches that are both highly sensitive and cost-effective for the identification of BN-PTB.

Droplet-based digital PCR (ddPCR) is an emerging nucleic acid detection technology that partitions samples into thousands of nanoliter-scale droplets, enabling absolute quantification based on Poisson statistics. This approach offers high analytical precision, excellent reproducibility, and obviates the need for external standard curves. Accumulating evidence suggests that, although ddPCR is still in the early stages of clinical application for TB diagnosis, it demonstrates superior sensitivity compared with conventional methods such as culture and Xpert in extra-pulmonary TB, smear-negative PTB, and tuberculous pleurisy.5–7 In addition, ddPCR enables quantitative assessment of drug resistance–associated mutations and mycobacterial burden, providing potential value for treatment monitoring and latent infection evaluation.8,9 Nevertheless, systematic assessments of ddPCR performance for detecting MTB in BALF specimens from patients with suspected PTB remain limited.

In our previous work, we developed a highly sensitive dual-target MTB ddPCR assay targeting two multicopy insertion sequences, IS6110 and IS1081 in MTB genome. By integrating a short-amplicon design with a multitarget detection strategy, this assay achieved excellent sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy.10 In the present study, BALF samples were collected from patients with suspected PTB, and the diagnostic performance of this ddPCR assay was systematically compared with that of conventional methods, including MGIT 960 culture, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), multi-color melting curve analysis (MMCA), and Xpert, for the detection of MTB-specific DNA. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of ddPCR for PTB identification and to determine its potential value in guiding clinical diagnostic decision-making.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Sample Collection

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuzhou Pulmonary Hospital of Fujian Province (Fuzhou Tuberculosis Prevention and Treatment Hospital), Fujian, China (Approval No. 2024–008-02). All patient-related information was processed anonymously to ensure confidentiality.

From August 2024 to December 2024, a total of 523 BALF samples were consecutively collected from non-duplicated inpatients admitted to the Tuberculosis Department of Fuzhou Pulmonary Hospital, Fujian Province, China. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinically suspected tuberculosis with symptoms such as subacute cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, fatigue, chest pain, weight loss, or other manifestations suggestive of TB; (2) chest computed tomography (CT) or X-ray findings showing cavities, unilateral or bilateral patchy, nodular, or cord-like pulmonary shadows, pleural thickening, or pleural adhesions; (3) supportive laboratory findings, including elevated absolute lymphocyte count or erythrocyte sedimentation rate, positive tuberculin purified protein derivative (PPD) test, positive tuberculosis antibody or T-SPOT test, and abnormal TBNK lymphocyte profiles; (4) availability of BALF samples subjected to ddPCR, MGIT 960 culture, qPCR, MMCA, and Xpert testing; (5) age >12 years with negative HIV.

Tuberculosis diagnosis was established according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Tuberculosis (WS 288–2017) issued by the Health Industry Standard of the People’s Republic of China. Diagnostic classification was based on etiological evidence (including bacteriological and molecular biological methods), combined with epidemiological history, clinical manifestations, radiological findings, auxiliary examinations, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses. According to the criteria, patients were categorized into three groups: culture-positive PTB, culture-negative but clinically diagnosed PTB, and non-PTB cases. Culture-positive PTB cases were defined as the patients with clinical PTB who tested positive for MTB by culture and were classified as BP-PTB. Culture-negative but clinically diagnosed PTB cases were defined as the patients with negative results in three sputum smear examinations and one culture test, but with radiological or pathological findings consistent with PTB, compatible clinical symptoms, and exclusion of other pulmonary diseases, these cases were classified as BN-PTB. Non-PTB cases were defined as the patients in whom tuberculosis was excluded after comprehensive differential diagnosis.11

Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) Samples

BALF samples were obtained by experienced tuberculosis specialists using bronchoscopy, with lavage performed at the sites of pulmonary lesions. Each BALF specimen was divided into five equal aliquots according to standard operating procedures and transported to the laboratory for MTB culture, qPCR, MMCA, Xpert, and ddPCR detection.

MGIT 960 Culture and Strain Identification

MTB culture was performed using the Bactec MGIT 960 system (Becton Dickinson, Cockeysville, MD, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 mL of BALF was mixed with 4% NaOH at a ratio of 1:2. The mixture was transferred to a sterile 50 mL centrifuge tube, vortexed thoroughly, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 40 mL of 0.01 M pH 7.2 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added, followed by centrifugation at 3000×g for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was gently resuspended in 1 mL PBS and incubated at room temperature for an additional 10 min. A 0.5 mL aliquot was inoculated into a MGIT culture tube and incubated in the MGIT 960 system for up to 42 days. Culture results were automatically reported by the instrument. Positive cultures were confirmed by Ziehl–Neelsen acid-fast staining, and MTB species identification was performed using the MPT64 antigen detection kit (Genesis, Hangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

qPCR analysis was performed using the Mycobacterium tuberculosis Complex Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (fluorescent probe method; Da’an Gene, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BALF samples were mixed with an equal volume of 4% (w/v) NaOH, gently agitated, and incubated at room temperature for approximately 30 min. After centrifugation of 1 mL of the mixture at 3000×g for 15 min, the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was treated with DNA extraction solution and incubated at 100 °C for 15 min to lyse bacterial cells. Two microliters of extracted DNA were used as the template in PCR reactions, which were performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cycling conditions were set according to the kit protocol, and MTB DNA levels were determined based on cycle threshold (Ct) values.

Multicolor Melting Curve Analysis (MMCA)

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Lab-Aid 824 MTB Nucleic Acid Extraction Maxi Kit (Xiamen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Briefly, 2 mL of a mixture containing 4% (w/v) NaOH and 0.5% N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NALC) was added to 2 mL of BALF, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. After centrifugation at 3000×g for 15 min, the supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of the precipitate was mixed with Lysis Buffer F5. The mixture was loaded into reagent strips and processed using the Lab-Aid 824s nucleic acid extractor for thermal lysis and DNA purification. PCR amplification and melting curve analysis were performed using the Mycobacteria Identification Kit (Xiamen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) on the SLAN-96S real-time PCR system (Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Xpert MTB/RIF Assay

The Xpert MTB/RIF assay (GXMTB/RIF-CN-50) was conducted using the GeneXpert system (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. BALF samples were mixed with the GeneXpert sample reagent at a volume ratio of 1:2 in sterile containers, vigorously shaken, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. During incubation, samples were shaken again after 5–10 min to ensure complete liquefaction. Subsequently, 2 mL of the processed sample was transferred into the test cartridge, which was loaded into the GeneXpert instrument for automated analysis.

Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) Assay

Genomic DNA extraction for ddPCR was identical to that used for the MMCA assay. ddPCR was performed using the TargetingOne Digital PCR System (TargetingOne, Beijing, China), with each sample analyzed in eight parallel reactions. All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the protocol previously described in reference [10].

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software was used for data analysis. When the measurement data was normally distributed, it was represented by “mean ± SD”, and inter-group differences were assessed using the Student’s t test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%). Diagnostic performance was evaluated in terms of detection rate, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall agreement. Agreement between different diagnostic methods was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic, with kappa values interpreted as follows: < 0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement. Before statistical analysis, the normality of continuous data was evaluated. All statistical tests in this study were two-tailed, with a P-value of less than 0.05 defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the Participants

A total of 523 patients with suspected PTB were initially recruited. According to the predefined exclusion criteria, 14 patients were excluded due to the absence of the results of MTB culture or Xpert testing. Additionally, one patient was clinically diagnosed with concurrent MTB and NTM infections, one patient had urinary tract tuberculosis, and one patient had an invalid specimen. Ultimately, 506 patients were included in the study, of whom 397 were diagnosed with PTB and 109 with non-PTB based on a composite reference standard. Among the 397 PTB patients, 181 were classified as BP-PTB, and 216 as BN-PTB. Within the 109 non-PTB cases, 69 were diagnosed with lung infections caused by the other pathogens, 18 with NTM lung disease, 13 with lung cancer, 7 with pneumoconiosis, and 2 with other conditions (Figure 1).

Flow chart of patient enrollment and test results. A total of 506 patients were ultimately included for further analysis. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples were tested using ddPCR, Xpert, MMCA, qPCR and MTB culture. Patients were diagnosed with PTB and nonpulmonary tuberculosis (non-PTB) as described in the Methods.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. Of the study population, 350 (69.2%) were male. The mean age of all participants was 52 years (range, 12–85 years). Specifically, 341 individuals (67.4%) reported cough and sputum production lasting more than 2 weeks, 18 had diabetes mellitus, and 15 had a history of tuberculosis. Overall, the cohort was predominantly male, and PTB patients were younger than non-PTB patients. No statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups regarding gender, marital status, clinical symptoms, hypertension, or prior tuberculosis history (P > 0.05). In contrast, significant differences were detected in age distribution (χ2 = 25.72, P < 0.001), prevalence of diabetes mellitus (χ2 = 8.81, P = 0.012), and Dust exposure history (χ2 = 9.20, P =0.010).

Detection Results and Diagnostic Performance of MTB Culture, qPCR, MMCA, Xpert, and ddPCR (with Clinical Diagnosis as the Reference Standard)

The composite reference standard for final clinical diagnosis was established based on comprehensive integration of clinical manifestations, chest CT imaging features, pathological diagnosis, and various laboratory detection methods for MTB. The detection results of MTB culture, qPCR, MMCA, Xpert, and ddPCR for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) in BALF samples are summarized in Figure 2A. Among the 506 enrolled patients, the positivity rate of ddPCR demonstrated a significantly higher overall than that of MTB culture, qPCR, MMCA, and Xpert (72.73% vs. 35.77%, 42.30%, 32.02% and 47.04%), respectively, all P < 0.001) (Figure 2B). A total of the 397 patients with a final diagnosis of tuberculosis were simultaneously tested using all five methods, yielding 21 distinct combinations of positive and negative results (Figure 2C and D), of which, the results showed that 118 samples (29.7%) were positive by all five assays, while 97 samples (24.4%) were positive exclusively by ddPCR. Among ddPCR-negative samples, positivity was rare and scattered across other methods: one sample was positive by MTB culture, qPCR, and Xpert; one by MTB culture alone; five by qPCR alone; one by MMCA alone; and two by Xpert alone.

Using the final clinical diagnosis as the reference standard, ddPCR achieved a sensitivity of 89.7% (95% CI: 86.3–92.3), which was significantly higher than that of MTB culture (45.1%; 95% CI: 40.3–49.9), qPCR (53.9%; 95% CI: 49.0–58.8), MMCA (40.8%; 95% CI: 36.0–45.7), and Xpert (59.7%; 95% CI: 54.8–64.4). All pairwise comparisons between ddPCR and the other methods were statistically significant (χ2 = 179.477, 124.365, 209.390, and 94.334, respectively, all P < 0.001). Although the specificity of ddPCR (89.0%; 95% CI: 81.5–94.0) and its positive predictive value (PPV; 96.7%; 95% CI: 94.5–98.1) were slightly lower than those of MTB culture, qPCR, MMCA, and Xpert, agreement analysis demonstrated that ddPCR showed substantial concordance with the clinical diagnosis (kappa = 0.768). In contrast, Xpert (kappa = 0.388), qPCR (kappa = 0.335), MMCA (kappa = 0.229), and MTB culture (kappa = 0.240) exhibited only fair agreement. Detailed diagnostic performance metrics are presented in Table 2. ROC curve analysis further confirmed the superior diagnostic performance of ddPCR, which achieved the largest area under the curve (AUC = 0.893), exceeding those of Xpert (0.794), MTB culture (0.728), qPCR (0.770), and MMCA (0.704) (Figure 3).

Comparison of qPCR, MMCA, Xpert, and ddPCR Using MTB Culture as the Reference Standard

When MTB culture was used as the reference standard, the diagnostic performance of qPCR, MMCA, Xpert, and ddPCR was evaluated (Table 3). The sensitivity, specificity, and kappa values were 81.2% (95% CI: 75.1–86.3), 100% (95% CI: 96.7–100.0), and 0.764 (95% CI: 0.682–0.846) for qPCR; 68.5% (95% CI: 61.4–74.9), 100% (95% CI: 96.7–100.0), and 0.621 (95% CI: 0.531–0.711) for MMCA; and 90.1% (95% CI: 85.2–93.7), 99.1% (95% CI: 94.9–100.0), and 0.864 (95% CI: 0.799–0.929) for Xpert, respectively. Notably, ddPCR achieved the highest sensitivity at 98.9% (95% CI: 96.2–99.9), with a specificity of 89.0% (95% CI: 81.5–94.0) and the highest overall agreement with MTB culture (kappa = 0.891; 95% CI: 0.835–0.947). Although the specificity of ddPCR was slightly lower than that of qPCR, MMCA, and Xpert, its sensitivity exceeded that of all other molecular assays. Taken together, ddPCR demonstrated the best overall concordance with MTB culture among the four molecular detection techniques when both sensitivity and specificity were considered.

Detection of BALF Samples from BN-PTB Patients

The diagnostic performance of ddPCR was further evaluated in the subgroup of BN-PTB patients by means of comparing with qPCR, MMCA, and Xpert. Among the 216 BN-PTB patients, ddPCR demonstrated a significantly higher sensitivity than Xpert, qPCR, and MMCA (81.9% vs. 34.3%, 31.0% and 17.6%), respectively; all P < 0.001) (Figure 4A). Across these BALF samples, 11 distinct combinations of positive and negative results were observed (Figure 4B and C), reflecting substantial variability among the four assays. Only 33 samples (15.3%) were positive by all four methods, indicating limited concordance in this subgroup. The most prevalent pattern was positivity exclusively by ddPCR, accounting for 97 samples (44.9%), nearly half of all BN-PTB cases. In contrast, positivity by a single assay other than ddPCR was rare, occurring in only 5 samples (2.3%) for qPCR, 2 samples (0.9%) for Xpert, and 1 sample (0.5%) for MMCA. Additionally, 31 samples (14.4%) tested negative by all four methods.

Discussion

Bacteriologically negative pulmonary tuberculosis (BN-PTB) constitutes a substantial proportion of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) cases and remains a major diagnostic challenge in clinical practice. Owing to scant or absent sputum production and a low mycobacterial burden, the sensitivity of conventional diagnostic assays including sputum smear microscopy, culture, and the other methods is often limited, leading to frequent false-negative results.12–14 The absence of a definitive etiological gold standard further complicates the accurate identification of BN-PTB, resulting in delayed diagnosis, inappropriate treatment decisions, and continued transmission. These challenges underscore the urgent need for more sensitive and reliable diagnostic approaches for this patient population.

Flexible bronchoscopy allows direct sampling of lesion-involved bronchi and alveoli, enabling bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) to be obtained as a diagnostic specimen.15 Compared with sputum, BALF collection reduces oropharyngeal contamination and increases the likelihood of capturing Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), particularly in patients with dry cough, sputum-scarce disease, or bacteriologically negative presentations.16 Accordingly, BALF was selected as the primary specimen in this study to ensure both feasibility and diagnostic reliability when evaluating different detection methods, especially in BN-PTB cases.

From an etiological standpoint, tuberculosis diagnosis ultimately depends on the detection of MTB. In patients lacking typical clinical manifestations, the absence of microbiological confirmation severely limits diagnostic certainty. Importantly, BN-PTB does not represent a distinct pathological entity but rather reflects the limitations of existing diagnostic tools in detecting low-abundance pathogens in conventional specimens. Although advanced molecular techniques such as Xpert and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) have improved etiological diagnosis of PTB, their performance remains suboptimal in samples with low bacterial loads. As a result, etiological evidence remains insufficient in a subset of suspected PTB cases. With continued advances in molecular techniques, particularly the increasing clinical application of droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), a growing proportion of BN-PTB cases can now be microbiologically confirmed. Consistent with this paradigm, 177 of 216 BN-PTB patients in the present study were positive tested by ddPCR and were ultimately diagnosed with PTB, supporting the notion that many BN-PTB cases reflect detection failure rather than the true absence of MTB infection.

In this cohort of 506 patients suspected PTB, 35.77% were classified as BP-PTB, 42.69% as BN-PTB, and 21.54% as non-PTB. Comparative analyses revealed significant differences in demographic characteristics and comorbidities among the groups. Patients with PTB were generally younger than non-PTB individuals, with a higher disease burden observed in younger age groups, consistent with previous reports.17 Diabetes mellitus was more prevalent in BP-PTB, supporting its role as an important risk factor for increased mycobacterial burden. Individuals with BN-PTB exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of dust exposure, indicating that dust exposure impairs respiratory immunity, increases the risk of tuberculosis infection, and is associated with the insidious onset and delayed diagnosis of BN-PTB.18 Overall, the cohort exhibited a male predominance, a high prevalence of prolonged cough, and a relatively low rate of documented TB contact history, which was mainly confined to PTB patients—findings consistent with real-world clinical settings.

In terms of diagnostic performance, ddPCR demonstrated a markedly higher sensitivity for MTB detection in BALF than MTB culture, qPCR, MMCA, and Xpert. Notably, ddPCR achieved the highest sensitivity in BN-PTB patients (81.94%), a population in which conventional methods performed poorly. In culture-positive cases, the sensitivity of ddPCR approached that of culture itself, highlighting its robustness across a wide range of bacterial loads. The superior performance of ddPCR observed in this study can be attributed to two key factors. First, the intrinsic technical advantages of ddPCR contribute substantially to its high analytical sensitivity. By partitioning each sample into tens of thousands of nanoliter-scale droplets, ddPCR effectively reduces competition among templates and mitigates the inhibitory effects of complex sample matrices. This partition-based amplification enables precise detection of low-abundance targets and improves quantification accuracy, making ddPCR particularly well suited for specimens with low MTB burden.19–21 Second, the assay design employed in this study further enhanced detection performance. Rather than relying on a single amplicon, the ddPCR assay integrates three independent targets—two within IS6110 and one within IS1081—thereby increasing effective target coverage. Given the variability in copy number and genomic conservation of insertion sequences across MTB strains, multi-target detection reduces the risk of false-negative results caused by low copy number or sequence variation at a single locus.22–24 Moreover, the interpretation criterion defining positivity by any detected target further minimizes missed detections, especially in paucibacillary samples. The combination of droplet-based amplification and multi-target coverage thus exerts a synergistic effect, maximizing sensitivity in BN-PTB diagnosis.25–28

In this study, the specificities of MTB culture, qPCR, and MMCAs were all 100%, while that of Xpert was nearly perfect (99.1%). In contrast, ddPCR exhibited relatively lower specificity (89.0%). We systematically reviewed the 12 ddPCR-positive specimens from the clinically confirmed non-PTB group. A considerable proportion of these patients had clinical and radiological features highly suggestive of subclinical or low-burden tuberculosis infection, including 5 cases with positive interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) results accompanied by micronodules and tree-in-bud opacities on chest CT; 4 cases with a history of previous tuberculosis treatment and patchy or linear opacities on chest CT; 1 case with pathological findings suggestive of focal granuloma formation; 1 case with a history of dust exposure; and 1 case presenting with hemoptysis. Given the ultra-high analytical sensitivity of ddPCR capable of detecting trace amounts of MTB DNA, these unexpected positive signals in the non-PTB cohort likely reflect undiagnosed low-burden tuberculosis infections10,22,29 or residual microbial DNA from prior anti-tuberculosis treatment. These findings underscore the need for cautious interpretation of ddPCR-positive results at very low bacterial loads, particularly in patients lacking definitive bacteriological confirmation. Furthermore, they highlight the importance of integrating molecular testing outcomes with clinical, radiological, pathological, and immunological evidence to prevent overdiagnosis and unnecessary anti-tuberculosis therapy.

Consistent with previous studies, ddPCR also outperformed Xpert MTB/RIF in smear-negative or sputum-scarce PTB cases and demonstrated a strong association between declining IS6110 copy numbers and effective anti-tuberculosis therapy.6,30 When evaluated against the final clinical diagnosis, ddPCR yielded the largest area under the ROC curve among all tested methods, indicating superior overall diagnostic performance. Importantly, ddPCR substantially reduced missed diagnoses and significantly improved detection rates in BN-PTB patients, a group for whom early and accurate diagnosis remains particularly challenging.

ddPCR-positive but culture-negative cases likely represent true MTB infections missed by culture, supported by consistent clinical features, quantifiable MTB DNA levels, and rigorous quality controls. Current molecular tests perform poorly in BN-PTB due to low bacterial load and inhibition, whereas ddPCR significantly improves MTB detection in this challenging subgroup. We propose implementing ddPCR as a reflex test for Xpert-negative or indeterminate samples in patients with high clinical suspicion, preserving Xpert’s cost-effectiveness while enabling early diagnosis. Clinical actions should be guided by a tiered framework integrating ddPCR results, symptoms, and imaging findings to optimize treatment decisions and reduce transmission risk.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, all patients were recruited from a single specialized center, which may limit generalizability. Multicenter studies involving larger and more diverse populations are warranted to further validate these findings. Second, Longitudinal assessment was not performed due to ethical considerations and clinical guidelines that preclude repeated sampling in successfully treated patients. Consequently, the long-term prognostic value of ddPCR can only be evaluated in specialized research cohorts with predefined serial sampling protocols.

In summary, the multi-target ddPCR assay developed in this study demonstrates high sensitivity and analytical stability for MTB detection in respiratory specimens, with excellent performance in BN-PTB. Given its favorable turnaround time, competitive cost relative to Xpert, and manageable operational workflows, ddPCR represents a promising tool for improving the etiological diagnosis and management of pulmonary tuberculosis.
