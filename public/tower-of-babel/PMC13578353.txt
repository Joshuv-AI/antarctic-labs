A H-MnO2 nanoplatform for tumor microenvironment remodeling and multimodal synergistic therapy in prostate cancer
Authors: Heng Zhang, Deqi Jiang, Luyao Huang, Lixiao Zhen, Zhipeng Yan, Chao Zhang, Maokun Sun, Yunji Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs is inherently dose-dependent, and their clinical application is often limited by systemic toxicity and poor tumor selectivity. Manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanomaterials have attracted considerable interest in nanomedicine due to their favorable biocompatibility, efficient cellular uptake, and capacity to remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME). Docetaxel (DTX) remains a first-line treatment for advanced prostate cancer; however, its considerable side effects restrict therapeutic outcomes. To enhance the efficacy of DTX while reducing its toxicity, we encapsulated DTX into hollow MnO2 nanoparticles pre-loaded with the photosensitizer IR825, constructing a multifunctional nanoplatform designated H-MnO2@IR825/DTX. This nanoplatform leverages the catalase-like activity of H-MnO2 to catalyze the decomposition of tumor-overexpressed H2O2 into O2, thereby alleviating hypoxia and providing an endogenous oxygen source for photodynamic therapy (PDT). Under near‑infrared (NIR) irradiation, H-MnO2@IR825/DTX generates both localized hyperthermia for photothermal therapy (PTT) and a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which synergizes with the controlled release of DTX to achieve multimodal antitumor treatment. Mechanistically, the ROS burst not only activates the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway but also triggers pyroptosis via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/Gasdermin D (GSDMD) signaling axis, as validated by both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Meanwhile, the decomposition of MnO2 depletes glutathione (GSH) and generates Mn2+ for chemodynamic therapy (CDT), further amplifying oxidative stress. Collectively, H-MnO2@IR825/DTX integrates TME remodeling with PTT, PDT, CDT, and chemotherapy, eliciting potent synergistic antitumor effects against prostate cancer through ROS-dependent apoptosis and pyroptosis, while exhibiting low toxicity to normal cells. This rationally designed nanoplatform represents a promising strategy for effective and safe prostate cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58396-z.

Introduction

The incidence rate and mortality rate of prostate cancer are increasing year by year, and it has become a worldwide men’s health problem. In China, the incidence rate of prostate cancer has risen to the 6th place in the incidence rate of malignant tumors in men, accounting for 2.1% of the deaths of malignant tumors in men, and more and more patients with prostate cancer have already progressed to the advanced stage of prostate cancer at the time of the initial diagnosis1–3. The 2019 edition of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommends ADT therapy in combination with docetaxel as a first-line standard of care for patients with metastatic prostate cancer treated for the first time and able to tolerate chemotherapy4. However, docetaxel chemotherapy regimen has the disadvantages of greater systemic toxicity and insufficient targeting5,6, while long-term application of chemotherapy can cause tumor cells to develop multidrug resistance, leading to a decrease in the efficacy of chemotherapy, and it is also an important factor that restricts the clinical application of oncology chemotherapy in the long term7,8. Selection of targeted drug carriers or optimization of drug delivery modes can achieve synergistic and toxicity-reducing effects and provide therapeutic efficiency of chemotherapeutic drugs9. Nanomaterials have been widely valued and explored due to their properties such as small size at the nanometer level and relatively large surface area, which can be coupled with different biomolecules or loaded with a variety of imaging agents and antitumor drugs10.

The microenvironment of a tumor is mainly composed of many different proteins and cells, such as epithelial cells, stromal cells, and external stroma11,12. The tumor microenvironment has many characteristics compared to the normal physiological environment, such as higher permeability, insufficient oxygen, acidic environment, and abnormal blood vessel growth13,14. Nanomaterials, on the other hand, can take advantage of the abundant blood vessels in the tumor microenvironment to penetrate and reach the tumor tissue, and stay in the tumor tissue for a longer period of time without being rapidly metabolized due to their own high permeability and retention effect15. Strategies of optical therapy have received increasing attention due to their higher targeting and noninvasiveness, and optical therapies that utilize exogenous photosensitizers to enhance the therapeutic effect of light radiation have emerged, including photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT)16. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is an emerging therapeutic technology that employs photosensitizing agents activated by specific-wavelength external light irradiation to generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS)—primarily singlet oxygen (1O2)—through a series of photochemical and photobiological reactions, destroying the double-stranded structure of DNA and causing apoptosis17,18. The fundamental mechanism of PDT relies on light-activated photosensitizers that, upon excitation by irradiation, mediate the conversion of molecular oxygen in the tumor microenvironment into singlet oxygen (1O2), a highly reactive cytotoxic agent. The antitumor efficacy of PDT is predominantly attributed to the direct cytotoxicity of singlet oxygen and its modulation of the tumor microenvironment.The content of ROS in tumor cells is significantly higher than that in normal tissue cells. Tumor cells can down-regulate the production of ROS by overexpressing antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione, glutathione peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase to maintain ROS at a specific physiological level to sustain the survival and proliferation of tumor cells19. In contrast, therapeutic doses of ROS can inhibit tumor cell proliferation and invasion, and cause DNA damage and apoptosis in tumor cells20,21. Hydroxyl radical (· OH) generated in chemodynamic therapy is one of the reactive oxygen species (ROS), which has stronger oxidative damage ability than H2O2 or superoxide anion in reactive oxygen species22–24.Enhancing ROS-dependent death signaling by increasing oxidative damage in tumor cells can effectively inhibit tumor progression25,26. Elevated ROS levels not only lead to tumor cytotoxic injury but also reverse chemoresistance and increase the anticancer effect of chemotherapy27. It was found that MnO2 nanoparticles could play a chemokinetic role in inducing apoptosis of tumor cells by catalyzing the conversion of H2O2, which is highly concentrated in the tumor microenvironment, to highly toxic •OH via a Fenton-like reaction. At the same time, MnO2 reacts with H+ or GSH in tumor cells and breaks down into Mn2+ to reduce the GSH content in the cells that resist oxidative stress damage, which strengthens the anti-tumor efficacy28,29.

Building upon this rationale, we engineered a hollow MnO2 (H-MnO2) nanocarrier to modulate the hypoxic TME. H-MnO2 possesses catalase-like activity, catalyzing the conversion of TME-overexpressed H2O2 into O2, thereby improving oxygen availability for ROS-generating therapies (PDT/CDT). Concurrently, DTX was passively targeted and loaded to enhance its bioavailability and therapeutic index. Furthermore, the photosensitizer IR825 was immobilized onto the H-MnO₂ surface. This nanoconstruct (H-MnO2@IR825/DTX) leverages the photothermal effect and ROS generation triggered by NIR irradiation of H-MnO2@IR825, synergizing with DTX chemotherapy for multimodal antitumor action combining tumor targeting, PTT, PDT, and chemotherapy.

Experimental section

Synthesis of H-MnO2 nanoparticles

Silica nanospheres sSiO2 was first prepared by Stober method, and then the prepared sSiO2 sample (50 mg) was dispersed into ultrapure water (50 mL), and the sample was added to potassium permanganate solution (6 g dissolved in 200 mL ultrapure water). After completion of addition under ultrasonic conditions, the reaction was continued for 12 h, followed by centrifugation at 11000 rpm for 30 min to collect the precipitate, which was subsequently washed three times with distilled water to obtain SiO2@MnO2 nanoparticles. The samples were dissolved in aqueous Na2CO3 solution (2 mol/L), centrifuged at high speed and stirred at 60℃ for 12 hours to obtain H-MnO2. After washing with distilled water for three times, the samples were stored in DMSO and stored in the refrigerator at 4℃ until use.

Synthesis of H-MnO2@IR825

The photosensitizer IR825 was loaded onto the surface of H-MnO2 nanoparticles, and 50mg IR825(Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., LTD.) was added to 10ml DMSO(Beijing Solaibao Technology Co., LTD.) by shaking until dissolved. 10mg H-MnO2 was weighed and added to the solution and stirred continuously for 24 h at room temperature (22-24℃) by magnetic force. After washing the mixture with deionized water, the mixture was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 30min. After washing again with deionized water, the concentrated H-MnO2@IR825 nanosamples were added to DMSO and stored at 4℃ in the dark.

Synthesis of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX

The chemotherapy drug docetaxel DTX was embedded in hollow mesoporous H-MnO2@IR825 nanoparticles, and 15 mL (1mg/mL) H-MnO2@IR825 nanoparticles were first lyophilized and then added to 10 mL (0.5 mg/mL) DTX solution and sonicated for 5 min. The mixture was then shaken overnight at room temperature (22-24℃ ) in the dark, washed twice with deionized water, and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min. After that, the concentrated H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles were diluted to 1mg/mL with deionized water and stored at 4 ℃in the dark.

Material characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were acquired using a JEM-2100F transmission electron microscope. Element mapping were performed to analyze the distribution of Mn, O and C. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) analysis was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer. Fourier transform-infrared spectra were obtained using a Thermo ls50 instrument (Thermo Scientific, Inc., USA).Dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano Z; Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) was used to record the hydrodynamic particle size distribution and zeta potential of the NPs.

Oxygen generation properties of the nanoparticles

To evaluate the oxygen generation capability of the H-MnO2 nanoparticles, a series of experiments were conducted. In the first setup, H-MnO2 solutions of varying concentrations (0, 100, and 200 ppm) were prepared in EP tubes, each containing 1 mL of the nanoparticle solution with a fixed hydrogen peroxide concentration of 200 µM. The mixture was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube using a pipette gun, and place the tube in a water bath set at 37℃ and maintain a stable ambient temperature.The dissolved oxygen concentration was measured every minute for 240 minutes using a portable dissolved oxygen meter (WLDO-300, Shanghai Water Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.). The oxygen electrode probe was placed directly into the reaction system to monitor oxygen generation in real time.

To further investigate the effect of hydrogen peroxide concentration on oxygen production, a second set of experiments was performed using a fixed H-MnO2 concentration of 200 ppm. EP tubes containing 1 mL of the nanoparticle solution were prepared, and hydrogen peroxide was added to achieve final concentrations of 200, 500, and 1000 µM. The oxygen generation performance was continuously monitored for 240 minutes following the same procedure.

Evaluation of temperature elevation ability of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX

The photothermal properties of the H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles were systematically evaluated. To assess the concentration-dependent photothermal heating effect, 1 mL aliquots of nanoparticle solutions at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 200 ppm were placed in a 96-well plate. Each sample was irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 2 W/cm2, and temperature changes were recorded over 30 minutes using a thermographic camera (FOTRIC 220S).

The effect of laser power density on photothermal performance was investigated using a fixed nanoparticle concentration of 200 ppm. Under identical conditions, samples were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation at power densities of 1, 2, and 3 W/cm2, and temperature variations were monitored over 30 minutes.

Photothermal stability was examined by subjecting 1 mL of the nanoparticle solution (200 ppm) to six cycles of laser irradiation (808 nm, 2 W/cm2) with alternating 5-minute on/off intervals. Temperature changes were recorded throughout the 60-minute experiment.

Photodynamic detection of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles

To verify the photodynamic effect of the synthesized nanoparticles H-MnO2@IR825/DTX, the experimental results were evaluated by the detection probe SOSG singlet oxygen fluorescence probe, which is highly selective for 1O2. SOSG did not show any significant response to •OH and the superoxide radical •O2-, and the probe could emit a green fluorescence similar to that of fluorescein in the presence of singlet oxygen. 1 mL of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanosolution (200 ppm) was configured with hydrogen peroxide concentrations of 200, 500, and 1000 µM. The solution was added to a 96-well plate with a pipettes gun, and then it was placed under an 808 nm laser with a power density of 1 W/cm2 for 0, 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 20, and 30 min. The Settings were: excitation wavelength 504 nm, emission wavelength 525 nm; The fluorescence intensity was scanned three times with a fluorescent microplate reader. The time was taken as the abscissa, and the fluorescence value A/A0 of the obtained nanoparticle solution was taken as the ordinate to obtain the singlet oxygen generation curve of the nanoparticle H-MnO2@IR825/DTX reaction with different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide.

Detection of IR825 and DTX loading in H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles and cumulative release of IR825 and DTX

The ultraviolet-visible(UV-Vis) absorption spectra of H-MnO2@IR825 nanoparticles were acquired using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Standard solutions of IR825 were prepared at concentrations of 35, 40, 45, 50, and 62.5 μg/mL. Absorbance measurements were performed using the UV-Vis spectrophotometer to establish a calibration curve for IR825. Following nanoparticle synthesis, the absorbance of IR825 remaining in the supernatant (after centrifugation) was determined. This value was used to calculate the loading efficiency of IR825 onto the H-MnO2@IR825 nanoparticles.

The UV absorption peak of DTX solution was measured by UV spectrophotometer (UV) at 282 nm. DTX solution with concentrations of 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000 ug/mL was prepared, and the standard curve of DTX solution was drawn. The drug loading of DTX was calculated by examining the OD value of the supernatant solution of the prepared H-MnO2@IR825/DTX sample.

In the in vitro experiment, the acidic environment was simulated and buffer solutions with pH values of 6.0 and 7.5 were prepared. H-MnO2@IR825 was added to the dialysis bag, and then it was shaken at 200 revolutions per minute in 10 mM PBS at 37℃for 24 h. At fixed time points, 1 milliliter of dialysate was taken to measure its ultraviolet value, and the IR825 drug release rate curve was plotted. In the same way, the DTX drug release rate curve was detected and plotted.

Establishment of animal tumor models

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Committee of Shandong Provincial Third Hospital (No. DWKYLL-2024004) and performed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, body weight approximately 20 g) were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Xino Medical Technology Co., Ltd. and housed in the Animal Experiment Center of the Third Hospital of Shandong Province under specific pathogen‑free conditions.

The PC3 prostate cancer xenograft model was established by subcutaneous injection. PC3 cells were harvested, resuspended in precooled PBS at a concentration of 1×105 cells per 200 μL, and inoculated into the right axillary region of each nude mouse.

Sample size calculation: Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.2), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80% (1-β = 0.80), a minimum of 5 mice per group was required. To account for potential dropouts, we included 6 mice per group (n = 6).

Randomization: When the tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, the tumor‑bearing mice were randomly assigned to seven experimental groups (n = 6 per group) using a computer‑generated random number sequence. The groups were as follows: Group 1: PBS (control); Group 2: H-MnO2@IR825; Group 3: DTX (free docetaxel); Group 4: H-MnO2@IR825/DTX; Group 5: PBS + laser (control); Group 6: H-MnO2@IR825 + laser; Group 7: H-MnO2@IR825/DTX + laser.

All treatments were administered via tail vein injection. For groups requiring laser irradiation (Groups 5–7), irradiation was performed 24 hours after injection as described in Section "Establishment of animal tumor models".

Tumor volume analysis in nude mice

Twenty-four hours after the injection of nanomedicine into the tail vein, the laser irradiation group received 808nm wavelength laser irradiation for 10min at a power of 2W/cm2 for 14 days at an interval of 1 day. For the size of the tumor in nude mice, the long diameter (A) and short diameter (B) of the tumor bearing mice were measured every other day with a vernier caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated according to the formula V=A×B2/2.

Biological distribution of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles

First, H-MnO2@IR825/DTX solution was dispersed in PBS (1 mg/mL), then DiR solution was added, and magnetic stirring was performed (dark, 25℃, 6 h). Free DiR was removed by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 15 min) and washed three times with PBS to obtain H-MnO2@IR825/DiR. When the tumor volume reached 150 mm3, two tumor-bearing nude mice were injected with 100 μL DiR and 100 μL MnO2@IR825/DTX/DiR, respectively, through the tail vein.According to the detection time point, the whole animal imaging system (LVIS Luwoma Series III, USA) was used to detect the fluorescence intensity.

Detection by infrared thermography of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX in vivo

The laser irradiation group was irradiated with 808nm wavelength laser at 2W/cm2 for 10min at 24h after injection of nanomedicine. One nude mouse in group 5, 6 and 7 was anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane. During the process of laser irradiation, the temperature of the tumor site was monitored and recorded by infrared thermal imaging.

Evaluation of histology

The laser irradiation group was irradiated with 808nm wavelength laser at 2W/cm2 for 10min at 24h after injection of nanomedicine. One nude mouse in each group was anesthetized by inhalation, and the tumor was removed after death. The tumor tissue was immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen, and the difference in ROS production in the tumor was detected by frozen section.

At the experimental endpoint, mice were deeply anesthetized using a high concentration of isoflurane (5%) in an induction chamber until the loss of consciousness and cessation of reflexes. Following confirmation of deep anesthesia, cervical dislocation was promptly performed to ensure death.This method complies with the euthanasia guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) and makes every effort to minimize the animals’ suffering.

The removed tumors were subjected to a series of histological assessments, including hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining for morphological changes of tumor cells, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining for apoptosis, and PCNA staining for proliferation. Detection of Hif-1ɑ expression difference in living animal tumors, the expression level of Hif-1ɑ is closely related to the invasion of tumors, detection of its expression difference is of great significance for evaluating the therapeutic effect and prognosis of tumors.

To investigate whether the ROS burst induced by multimodal therapy triggers pyroptosis in prostate cancer cells, the expression levels of key pyroptosis-associated markers were assessed. Pyroptosis is a pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death typically initiated by activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent cleavage of Gasdermin D (GSDMD).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on tumor tissue sections to detect the expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, and GSDMD. Additionally, western blot analysis was conducted to quantify the protein levels of NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD, and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in tumor tissues from nude mice of each experimental group. Actin was used as the loading control.

Biotoxicity testing of materials in vivo

At the experimental endpoint, the liver, kidneys, heart, and lungs were taken for HE testing to investigate whether the nanoparticles used in the experiment were biologically toxic to nude mice.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20 software. Significant differences were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t-test. Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX

TEM revealed that the prepared H-MnO2 nanoparticles exhibited a spherical morphology with uniform size (approximately 100 nm, Figure S1). The morphology of H-MnO2 nanoparticles did not change significantly after the loading of IR825 and DTX on the surface of H-MnO2 nanoparticles, indicating that the adsorption of drugs did not affect the structural stability of H-MnO2 nanoparticles (Fig. 1A and 1B).The composition and purity of the prepared nanoparticles were determined by qualitative and quantitative elemental analysis using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 1C). Through EDS analysis, it was found that the prepared H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles were mainly composed of Mn, C and O elements, and the proportion of Mn element was about 10.85%, while the proportion of C element and O element was about 78.35% and 10.22%, respectively (Fig. 1C, 1D and 1E). The samples of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles were analyzed by X-ray energy spectrometer. By XPS spectroscopy, two significant peaks at 642.2 eV and 653.2 eV binding energies were identified, which correspond to the Mn2p 3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 signals of Mn2+ ions, respectively (Fig. 1G and 1H). Consistent with the XPS results of H-MnO2, the structure of H-MnO2 did not change after drug loading.

The infrared spectrum analysis of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles using infrared spectroscopy can explore the molecular structure characteristics of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX and the vibration mode of its chemical bonds in detail. The spectral band of H-MnO2 loaded with IR825 and DTX overlaps with that of H-MnO2, and the results show that the structure of H-MnO2 is not changed after loading drugs (Fig. 1I).The Zeta potential of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX sample was measured by nanoparticle size potential analyzer. The results showed that the Zeta potential value of the sample was about -27.1 mV, while the Zeta potential value of H-MnO2 was about -32.6 mV, which proved that DTX was successfully loaded on the prepared H-MnO2 nanoparticles (Fig. 1J).Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed the hydrodynamic diameters and polydispersity index (PDI) of the nanoparticles: H-MnO2 exhibited a size of 112.4±8.6 nm (PDI = 0.156); H-MnO2@IR825 showed a size of 156.3±10.2 nm (PDI = 0.182); and H-MnO2@IR825/DTX had a size of 198.7±12.4 nm (PDI = 0.208) (Fig. 1K). The nanoparticles were detected by ultraviolet spectrophotometer, and the results showed that DTX was successfully loaded into the nanoparticles, and the drug release rate was detected later (Fig. 1L).

Based on the above results, it was confirmed that we successfully loaded IR825 and DTX on H-MnO2.

Oxygen generation properties of the nanoparticles

The oxygen generation capacity of H-MnO2 nanomaterials was evaluated in the presence of H2O2 using a portable dissolved oxygen meter. When H2O2 was added to deionized water at a final concentration of 200 µM, a slight gradual increase in dissolved oxygen content was observed over time, attributable to the spontaneous decomposition of H2O2 at 37℃. However, upon introduction of H-MnO2 nanoparticles, the dissolved oxygen level rose markedly and rapidly. This oxygen production exhibited a clear dependence on the concentration of H-MnO2, with higher nanoparticle concentrations leading to faster rates of oxygen release. Furthermore, when varying concentrations of H2O2 were introduced into a fixed concentration (200 ppm) of H-MnO2 solution, the dissolved oxygen content increased more rapidly at higher H2O2 concentrations (Fig. 2A). These results demonstrate that H-MnO₂ exhibits catalase-like activity, efficiently catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2 into O2 and thereby enhancing oxygen solubility in aqueous environments. Photothermal characteristics of the nanoparticles

The photothermal properties of the constructed nanoparticle H-MnO2@IR825/DTX were studied. Firstly, the photothermal heating of the solution with different concentrations of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles was detected. The solution concentration was set at 0, 50, 100, and 200ppm, and the solution temperature increased with time under 808 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 2B). When the concentration of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles was diluted to 200 ppm, the temperature of the reaction solution increased with the laser power density, and it became stable after a certain degree of increase, with good irradiation time and concentration dependence (Figure S3). The H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles were subjected to six cycles of photothermal stability experiments with the laser density set at 2 W/cm2 for 10min each cycle (laser irradiation for 5min and laser off for 5min). The nanoparticle solution achieved a similar peak temperature in each cycle, indicating photothermal therapy stability (Fig. 2C). The FOTRIC thermograph was used to monitor the heating reaction of the nanoparticle solution, and the photos were taken at an interval of 2 minutes (Fig. 2D). It was observed that the high concentration of the nanoparticle solution showed an obvious heating reaction under laser irradiation. The results of photothermal performance experiments show that the synthesized H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles are relatively stable photothermal conversion agents.

Photodynamic characteristics of the nanoparticles

The photodynamic properties of the synthesized nanoparticles H-MnO2@IR825/DTX were verified. The fluorescence intensity of the singlet oxygen fluorescent probe of SOSG was detected by fluorescent microplate reader. To evaluate the ability of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles to react with different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (200, 500, 1000 µM) to form singlet oxygen under 808nm laser irradiation. The experimental results showed that the fluorescence intensity gradually increases with the increase of laser irradiation time, indicating that the H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles could effectively generate singlet oxygen under laser irradiation. The results also showed that the generation of singlet oxygen was positively correlated with the concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the generation of reactive oxygen species was also dependent on laser irradiation time (Fig. 2E). The experimental results provided strong evidence for its application in tumor therapy.

ROS production experiment based on PC3 cells

The reactive oxygen species (ROS) production of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles in prostate cancer PC3 cells was evaluated by ROS detection kit. The PC3 cells were treated with DCFH-DA fluorescence staining, and the differences in ROS production between the different experimental groups were observed by fluorescence microscopy. The experimental results showed that the fluorescence intensity of the control group treated with PBS was the weakest, indicating that there was no significant intracellular ROS production in the absence of nanoparticles and laser irradiation stimulation. However, the H-MnO2@IR825 nanoparticles and H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles produced reactive oxygen species in the cells without receiving laser irradiation, indicating that metal Mn had a Fenton-like reaction to produce reactive oxygen species in the tumor cells. The fluorescence intensity of H-MnO2@IR825 nanoparticles combined with laser irradiation was significantly enhanced, revealing that H-MnO2 can catalyze the production of oxygen from hydrogen peroxide in the tumor microenvironment, which provides sufficient raw materials for photodynamic therapy (Figure S9).

Biodistribution of the H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles

The biodistribution and metabolic behavior of DiR in animals were monitored using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system (IVIS). Following tail vein injection of free DiR, the fluorescence signal was rapidly metabolized; no signal was detectable at the tumor site, and fluorescence was observed primarily in the bladder and subsequently excreted in the urine within 24 hours. In contrast, after tail vein injection of H-MnO2@IR825/DTX/DiR (10 mg kg-1), the fluorescence signal of DiR at the tumor site gradually increased over time, and significant nanoparticle accumulation in the tumor region was observed 12 hours post-injection (Fig. 5E). This tumor‑specific accumulation is attributed to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of the nanoparticles. Compared with normal tissues, tumor tissues exhibit wider vascular wall gaps and compromised structural integrity, allowing nanoparticles of appropriate size and surface characteristics to preferentially extravasate and be retained. Thus, the nanodrug achieves passive accumulation in tumor tissues via the EPR effect without the need for an active targeting ligand. These results demonstrate that the constructed nanodrug exhibits favorable tumor-specific accumulation and can potentially release DTX locally to exert its antitumor effect.

In vivo photothermal characteristics of the nanoparticles

To study the photothermal effect of the nanoparticles in vivo, one nude mouse from each treatment group was selected for the experiment 24 hours after tail vein injection of the nanoparticles. After inhalation anesthesia, the nude mice were irradiated with 808nm laser for 10min at a power density of 2W/cm2. The tumor sites of these nude mice were monitored in real time by infrared thermography, and the temperature changes of the tumor sites were recorded in detail. This significant photothermal effect indicates a high enrichment of nanoparticles inside the animal tumor. The H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoparticles with stable heating properties in animals, provided new ideas and methods for tumor diagnosis and treatment.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed an innovative H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoplatform that integrates tumor-targeted drug delivery with multimodal synergistic therapy and more importantly intelligent modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME). This system achieves not only high docetaxel loading and pH-triggered release but also exhibits excellent photothermal stability and robust photodynamic performance.

A key innovation of this platform lies in its TME-responsive therapeutic synergy. The catalase-like activity of H-MnO2 effectively alleviates tumor hypoxia by catalyzing the decomposition of overexpressed H2O2 into O2, thereby enhancing the efficacy of both photodynamic therapy (PDT) and chemodynamic therapy (CDT). Meanwhile, H-MnO2 decomposition depletes glutathione (GSH) and generates Mn2+ for CDT, further amplifying oxidative stress. Under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, the nanoplatform simultaneously activates photothermal therapy (PTT), PDT, and CDT, which, together with the controlled release of docetaxel, results in a powerful cooperative antitumor effect, as validated by both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Mechanistically, the multimodal treatment alleviates hypoxia (downregulating HIF-1α). More importantly, our study reveals that the ROS burst induced by the nanoplatform triggers pyroptosis via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway, both in vitro and in vivo. This pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death further amplifies the antitumor immune response and contributes significantly to the overall therapeutic efficacy.

It is important to acknowledge that the present study is entirely preclinical, conducted using subcutaneous xenograft models in immunodeficient mice. While the H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoplatform demonstrated potent antitumor efficacy and a favorable safety profile in these animal models, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to human patients. Future clinical trials will be essential to validate the safety, tolerability, and therapeutic efficacy of this nanoplatform in prostate cancer patients.In addition to the need for clinical validation, the clinical translation of the H-MnO2@IR825/DTX nanoplatform faces challenges related to scalability and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) compliance. Therefore, future work should focus on developing scalable manufacturing processes, potentially including continuous flow synthesis or one-pot methods, while establishing robust quality control criteria for particle size, polydispersity, drug loading, and sterility. Achieving GMP-grade production will be a critical step toward regulatory approval and eventual clinical application. These considerations provide a realistic perspective on the path from bench to bedside.

Collectively, this rationally designed nanoplatform represents a promising and translatable strategy for effective and low-toxicity treatment of advanced prostate cancer, with its multifaceted mechanisms involving apoptosis, proliferation suppression, hypoxia relief, and ROS-dependent pyroptosis.
