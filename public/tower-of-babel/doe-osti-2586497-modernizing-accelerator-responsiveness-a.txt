Executive Summary
Accelerators increasingly use artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) software and workflows
for a variety of tasks, from optimization to fault detection and recovery. Efficient and sustainable application
of these technologies necessitates specialized and facility-specific infrastructure commitments. Accelerator
facilities also introduce unique radiation and security hazards, placing additional demands on operational
infrastructure. These needs further escalate the prioritization of effective collaboration models and associated
funding mechanisms and legal frameworks.
 We present an assessment of AI/ML in the accelerator technology ecosystem in the U.S., with an em-
phasis on funding mechanisms, partnership frameworks with universities and industry, as well as legal and
security protections. We examine existing multilateral collaborations and technology initiatives, and develop
prescriptions for the successful development and maintenance of such entities. We present recommendations
to enable long-term growth of AI/ML technologies requiring collaborative frameworks and formal licensing
or security agreements. Lastly, we explore collaboration models in use across other scientific program offices
and technical domains, and identify two models that are poised for success within the existing accelerator
ecosystem.
 Successful collaborations provide a formal framework to support open communication between facilities,
research groups, and industrial partners. They also provision adequate funding for sustained community-wide
workforce development, and they meet practical demands on controls and data infrastructure, security and
licensing protections. This report covers the status of a few current collaborations and technology products,
and it compares various models to other, successful collaborations that allow for long-term support and
reliability. A taxonomy of AI/ML collaboration components has been created. Deficiencies in specific
components are identified where possible. Our assessment of current collaboration models finds them to be
highly informal, leading to many independent silos working towards the same goals in incompatible ways.
National laboratories and their management firms have widely varying views on such collaborations, often
conflicting with the perspectives of industrial and academic partners. We provide recommendations for
leadership structures and maintenance models based on existing frameworks across technological domains.
 Collaborative infrastructure development often fails without clear programmatic guidance, organizational
support, and dedicated long-term resource management. The advancement of accelerator facility operations
requires recognition of the need for both dedicated R&D and operational resources, and subsequent coordi-
nation and contracting mechanisms to adequately distribute and direct these resources. This report presents
several recommendations for developing general multilateral contract mechanisms in addition to increasing
total resource allocations.
 Collaborations are a challenge to manage and maintain in any rapidly advancing technical field, and even
the most robust examples of such collaborations rely on dedicated support to enable continued development.
Several key components are required for a long-term collaboration to be successful, but people are the
key. Strong leadership is essential, either in the form of individuals or a managing entity. Addressing the
multi-faceted and multi-scale challenges presented by accelerator operations with rapidly changing AI/ML
technologies will require dedicated leadership and long-term vision.

1 Introduction
Particle accelerators are critical components of several important industries in the US, including health,
research, industry, and security. They are critical components for a variety of specialized tasks, including food
irradiation, cargo inspection, and materials analysis. These small facilities provide stand-alone manufacturing
or inspection services that require extremely high uptime with minimal cost or online processing. For security,
there is interest in mobile installations that can operate across a wide variety of situations.
 Larger accelerator facilities are a key component of America’s research and development resources, pro-
viding tools critical for research, industry, and defense. For example, light sources, such as Argonne National
Lab’s APS, are multi-user facilities that provide services to a number of clients, supporting both private and
public sector clients. Many of these light source facilities have numerous beamlines dedicated to pharmaceu-
tical and biological research, and have led to discoveries in a number of different fields, while other beamlines
have led to significant advances in the development of advanced and nanoscale materials. X-ray beamlines
make extensive use of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques for data analysis and
experimental tasks, but the operation and control of the beamlines and accelerator facilities themselves are
significantly less automated.
 Operating such a machine, with its associated facility, personnel, and engineering requirements, entails
the integration of a number of interested parties, spanning physics specialists, conventional facilities (e.g.
HVAC, electric service), and controls engineers. Each group has their own requirements and interests, leading
to a set of complex requirements. These accelerators require a highly trained and specialized operations staff,
as well as a customized set of tools for controlling and monitoring.
 Developing a model for sustainable development of ML and AI for use in operations is critical for the US
to stay at the forefront of technological development, while also allowing for future-proofing the accelerator
industry from economic or policy turmoil. We provide here a guide for implementing such a model within
the diverse ecosystem of private and public entities involved in the operation of accelerators. We summarize
the current state and use of ML and AI in operations of accelerator facilities based on interviews, literature
review, and policy analysis. Particular emphasis is placed on the current state of the industry scientifically
and financially, and on what members of the community and industry can do to better collaborate.

2 Background
Collaborations are challenging entities to create, manage, and maintain. In the course of this business plan
development, one quotation really stands out:

 A sustainable collaboration is one that continues to grow. – Andy Goetz, a founder of the Tango
 Controls software collaboration

 Without growth, either in membership, intellectual property, or member buy-in, there is little benefit
to any of the members, investors, or users of tools and IP generated from a collaboration. While there is
a general consensus that the sharing of knowledge is valuable, it is increasingly difficult to share technical
skills and data without formal agreements and cost accounting.
 But even with these obstacles in the way, members of the scientific and industrial community agree
that the most important thing is people. People drive project success and the ability for knowledge to be
disseminated. Without strong supporters and leaders in a community, a project is destined to fail, regardless
of its merits.
 Artificial intelligence and machine learning software projects have additional challenges, as discussed
below, due to even more stringent, and legally ambiguous, data sharing requirements. Such concerns are
readily apparent in presentations at recent ML for accelerator conferences, where strict interpretation of EU
privacy laws restrict the public dissemination of data and models used for research. Extracting the most
value out of ML and AI collaborations will most likely involve the sharing of techniques, not the sharing of
previously developed solutions.

2.1 Market Analysis
The particle accelerator market sector consists of a large patchwork of specialized and small businesses,
supported by larger consumer and industrial technology. Software and control infrastructures use a wide
mix of everything from National Instruments LabVIEW, industrial control software such as Siemens’ WinCC,
Beckhoff and Rockwell Automation PLC controls, and specialized, open, distributed control systems such
as EPICS and TANGO.
 An additional challenge in this market sector is ongoing siloing, where operators and users believe that
it is better to develop a specialized tool for their own uses as opposed to joining a larger group or using
other available software and hardware tools. While this is true in some cases, and can lead to cost savings in
healthy organizations with formal succession plans in place, such a mindset has hindered the development of
open collaborations. This, in conjunction with siloed funding and management chains within both industry
and government, adds additional disincentive to collaboration.
 Examples of this can be seen on several large collaborations that grew out of single-facility needs, such as
the development model seen in the Bluesky collaboration [1]. The collaboration leadership and development
is generally by a single entity, limiting both the usability and growth of such tooling to additional facilities.
This sort of organic growth has been the norm for most collaborative projects in the accelerator space, limiting
growth of the collaboration to those parties with both similar requirements and a previous relationship with
the lead entity.

2.1.1 The Accelerator Ecosystem in the US
Particle accelerators are widely used in a number of industries, with a corresponding wide range of re-
quirements for users. While every user strives to achieve maximum efficiency of their equipment, usually
characterized by up-time or operational hours, different users have varying degrees of acceptance for lost
efficiency in operations. A good example of these differences can be seen in discussions with experimental
operators on specific beamlines at large facilities, where a large number of visiting scientists might operate
the same beamline, leading to a high learning curve and lost experimental time at the beginning of an
experimental period to tailor both software and workflows. There has recently been a major push on the
experimental side to provide more robust machine learning models and digital twins of the experiments to
help mitigate these issues.

 5

 Similarly, medical and security machine needs are much more occasional, as downtime between operational
periods can be high. In contrast, these facilities often operate with a wider range of operational settings,
requiring more time for reconfiguration. When these machines are in operation, users expect single-button
operation with the ability to run without focusing on the mechanics of operation (e.g. what gate charge
voltage does an RF power supply require for optimal operation).
 Accelerators are seeing increasing use as substitutes for radioactive sources, with major government and
private programs aimed at replacing radioactive sources with accelerator-based sources to mitigate several
types of risk to safety, health, and the environment. The use of linear accelerators as X-ray sources has also
helped popularize the development of commercially available, off-the-shelf units.

2.1.2 Government-Funded Accelerator Facilities
The US Department of Energy (DOE) ecosystem of national labs and facilities is a complicated framework
of interlocking jurisdictions and entities. For large-scale experimental facilities, there is an additional layer
of complexity to determining responsibility due to a complicated web of funding sources and policy require-
ments. In general, operational funds are requested on an annual basis by a managing subcontractor for each
facility, with accelerator divisions often not holding priority over others. This leads to a bottom-up approach
to funding, where operational requests are developed at a relatively low level of the organizational structure,
often not taking into account long-term, wider scale facility infrastructure and labor cost requirements.
 For example, data storage and handling (for state-of-the-art machine learning workflows) requires quality
control on the level of that seen in experiments. Most older facilities, and mindsets about accelerator control
in general, don’t recognize the need for higher quality operational data. Control and monitoring variables
are often saved at slower than 1 Hz rates, meaning machine learning techniques to perform fast monitoring
and detection do not have data available to train against.
 These needs are tightly interconnected with the technology infrastructure installed at a facility as a
whole, as the accelerators are heavily reliant on IT infrastructure. Without a push for higher performing
infrastructure, accelerator and machine learning groups often need to focus on interpolating data and working
with lower performing, non-specialized infrastructure to accomplish specialized archival and processing needs.

2.1.3 Private Accelerator Facilities and Infrastructure
Private accelerator facilities consist of those used in research universities, manufacturing, security, and med-
ical applications. Security and medical vendors are dominated by a small number of large manufacturers.
Examples include IBA, ProTom, Varian (now Siemens) for medical applications, as well as Altair and Leidos
for security applications. Smaller firms provide support infrastructure and equipment specialized for use in
the particle accelerator market, such as Dimtel for particle beam monitoring and control.
 These private firms, due to the small size of the market and need to prevent losing business to their
own customers (e.g. national labs, universities), often develop closed tooling and systems that require
manufacturer support and maintenance to maintain a revenue stream while being able to support users.
Integrating such systems with custom tooling and with additional infrastructure, such as those required for
machine learning, can be a particular challenge. Due to the need to maintain a competitive advantage, in
consort with the small market size, there is little incentive to develop more open tools and interfaces for use
by third parties.
 The lack of third-party interfaces in this equipment means that integrating machine learning techniques
into such equipment for long-term operations requires either a close relationship with the vendors, creating
a development path forward for future market opportunities and expansion, or more often the equipment
users developing custom tooling allowing for higher level integration and control with the rest of the system.
Another option would be direct financial support from interested parties.

2.2 Introduction to Funding Mechanisms
Accelerator facilities rely on a number of funding sources for operations, with often even industrial facilities
receiving some form of government funding for operations through targeted grants. These grants often come
with significant restrictions or time limitations of one to five years. Depending on government policy and
budget stability, recent years have seen significant risk to relying on such funds for new work, especially as

 6

the use of continuing resolutions in lieu of a formal budget limits the types of projects and expenses that
can be undertaken.
 Much of the foundational research for accelerators was built on these government grants, both within the
USA and outside of it. Operations funding for large US facilities is usually provided through annual requests
for facilities operations by lab subcontractors, which is a separate pool of funds than those used for research
and development efforts. This allocation split causes distinct challenges for the use of machine learning in
operations, as the development of new techniques and computing resources are not generally considered a
valid use of available funding resources.
 Funding for the introduction of new techniques and modalities for operational facility control is also a
challenge due to the regular need for additional computing and technology infrastructure. While experimental
and science groups often have access to cutting-edge computing resources, upgrading existing facilities to
maintain operational flexibility remains relatively low priority. Funding allocations for such infrastructure
maintenance and upgrade work is a significant challenge.
 Paying for membership in formal collaborations has been a particular challenge for these groups as the
collaborations often tend to have associated legal implications for access to IP or data. Previously, lab
contractors set aside a larger pool of discretionary funding for use in such ventures, but in recent years
such costs have shifted to budgetary line items or been cut, leading to a corresponding lack of flexibility.
Collaborations tend to require some level of travel or workshop attendance, similar to that seen in science
activities, but this has not been traditionally seen in facility and machine operations.

2.3 Introduction to collaborations
Several forms of collaborations have grown to assist different types of entities to work together across the
particle accelerator market space. These have a variety of structures, with a variety of origins. Most grew
out of short-term experimental projects, with a corresponding funding end date after which the collaboration
becomes relatively defunct. A few have been developed that we will term successful, including several controls
software groups and knowledge sharing conferences.
 Generally speaking, a successful collaboration requires:

  1. Strong leadership with directed goals
  2. An ability to share data and ideas in a standard manner; this is especially true if working with industry
  3. An agreement on how to govern the collaboration and any resultant products

 There are a number of significant complications in arranging collaborations between government facilities,
industry, and academia, in addition to funding and legal concerns. Government facilities tend to not have
models in place to allow for their staff and funding to work towards a multi-institution collaboration, instead
relying on unilateral contracts and agreements on a per lab operator basis, with each subcontractor levying
their own contract restrictions and requirements. In addition, there is no IP model in place that allows for
open or third-party knowledge sharing, outside of open source development licenses, with each lab requiring
independent review of such agreements.
 General-use open source licenses tend to require internal review as well, with highly complex copyright
requirements that don’t make sense for successful, long-term collaboration. This is particularly true of
products that use software libraries with commercial usage restrictions or copy-left requirements. Creating
software projects without consideration of such restrictions can immediately freeze certain industrial or
academic partners from involvement before even considering the creation of a collaboration. An example of
copyright complications can be seen in the file header below from the EPICS Area Detector project.

 The most successful software and hardware collaborations in the accelerator space have generally been
through projects that have a single institution home, which then grow to have additional major parties with
interest in the project. After a period of time, most of these have formed governing councils for the project
that tend to have no ability to formally direct the work of the collaboration as a whole, but do provide a space

providing maintenance. Examples of such organizations are the PCI-SIG[2] and MPEG[3]. The licensing
terms and membership cost for such organizations tend to be seen as too high for the operations teams in
government and academic facilities that tend to run particle accelerators, although other divisions at the
same facilities tend to be active members of such groups for computing, semiconductor technology, or video
handling.

2.4 Partnership agreements and models
It is necessary to highlight the extent to which the number of different partnership agreement models has
proliferated for working with national lab subcontractors, and the lack of any clear path towards how a
national lab accelerator facility can be involved in a third-party managed collaboration with multilateral
MOU requirements.
 As an example of the complexity non-government partners need to navigate, in 2017 there were eight
(8) different partnership agreement models available, of which none generally involved a lab joining an ex-
isting collaboration on an equal or subservient footing with another non-national-lab entity (summarized
below) [20]. While several of these mechanisms have characteristics beneficial for multilateral data shar-
ing and collaboration between entities, shared software and collaboration does not cleanly fit within these
guidelines unless a collaboration is itself a non-profit with the resources to manage such agreements.

CRADAs The participants collaborate by providing personnel, services, facilities, or equipment and pool
 the results from a particular R&D program.
SPPs SPPs permit DOE Laboratories to conduct work for other Federal agencies and nonfederal entities
 on a 100% cost reimbursable basis.
Agreement for Commercializing Technology (ACT) ACT contracts allow research and technology
 transfer projects with more-flexible intellectual property arrangements and other terms and condi-
 tions used more traditionally in industry agreements, including fixed pricing, advance payments, and
 performance guarantees.
SBIR and STTR These are two U.S. Government programs where DOE programs can set aside a fraction
 of their funding to be competitively awarded to small businesses to perform R&D projects. The small

 8

 businesses are encouraged to commercialize the technology, and they retain the rights to technology
 that they develop.
Technology Licensing Agreements (TLAs) Because of the laws and policies governing the licensing
 of federally funded research and DOE policies regarding IP, TLAs may include provisions such as
 march-in-rights, government use rights, and indemnification provisions.

Technical Assistance Agreements (TAAs) These agreements allow Laboratory scientists and engineers
 to help members of the small business community solve important challenges at reduced cost to the
 small business.
Material Transfer Agreements (MTAs) MTAs protect biological materials and tangible research prod-
 ucts provided to or by the Laboratory from further transmittal. The agreement normally requires return
 or destruction of materials and products at the end of the agreement.
User Agreements These agreements expedite access to DOE scientific user facilities. Prospective non-
 proprietary users may propose independent or collaborative research. Access to User Facilities is also
 available on a full-cost-recovery basis for proprietary research that is not intended for publication.

 The above agreements require contractors and partners to have specialized staff and oversight, while also
adding significant complexity to personnel interactions. Project work timelines and deliverables are a key
component of a number of these agreements, blurring the lines between research and product delivery.
 ML and AI work, like much fundamental research, have unknown final boundaries to the work, leading
to complexity in arranging such relationships. Most of these contract vehicles do not provide the overhead
necessary for workforce development and training, leading to additional knock-on effects years later.

2.5 Introduction to industry
An increasing number of particle accelerators are fixed and mobile deployments of packaged industrial units.
While these units are a critical part of the USA particle accelerator market, the opportunity for collaboration
between such installations is minimal. This is primarily due to the nature of the clients that use such tools,
including defense and security, such that meeting the requirements of academic and government users often
runs directly counter to those of the main customers. This has a direct effect on industry’s ability to join
large collaborations while staying profitable, as they can end up competing with national labs if they open
source their IP. This leads to both a low return on investment, as well as a constantly shifting set of customer
requirements.
 Industry has an odd position when it comes to its relationship with DOE labs and machine learning. As
mentioned above, the model of data access at most laboratories and facilities limits industry to the position
of subcontractor for specific lab contracts or grants. As a result of this situation, companies can struggle to
find a footing in this market sector. Excellent discussions of these issues can be found in a Snowmass 2022
report on “Nurturing the Industrial Accelerator Technology Base in the US” [22] and in an NAPAC 2022
proceeding on “Facilitating Machine Learning Collaborations Between Labs, Universities, and Industry” [1].
In particular:

 “...companies initially successfully acquired expertise, equipment, experience, and motivated cus-
 tomers base, all at a great cost and through decade-long efforts. Yet at the end . . . a
 business cannot sustain itself without directed long term investment by the funding
 agencies, as it is practiced in Europe and Asia. The collapse of these companies created
 supply chains gaps, from which to this day our field has never fully recovered.” — Nurturing the
 Industrial Accelerator Technology Base in the US

 Successful software projects use open source development models, but the overhead of maintaining such
projects strains profitability and long-term success for small businesses. Many of the popular open source
software projects in the AI and ML space were developed as single-party tools by large business (e.g. OpenAI,
Google, Meta) and open sourced – not collaborations that grew into a larger project with proprietary support
vendors.

2.6 Introduction to Policy
Federal and regional policies have a major effect on the operations and deployment of particle accelerators.
The most striking example is the story of the failure of the Superconducting Super Collider. However, any
large-scale project in the market sector that is canceled after a long period of planning has severe knock-on
effects for the mid- and long-term revenue and planning projections of supplier firms. Outside of large firms
where the particle accelerator market is a secondary focus, most suppliers are highly reliant on a smaller
number of consistent, high-value contracts.
 New facilities outside of the national lab space are also subject to high levels of regulatory oversight,
with new isotope manufacturing and energy research facilities often navigating a complex and conflicting set
of regulatory agencies and safety requirements. Lack of expertise of some regulatory authorities in working
with accelerator facilities can also be problematic.
 Much of the ML research in the accelerator space is still being performed within the national lab in-
frastructure, centered at a couple facilities in particular, with strong leadership being provided by a small
number of individuals. Industry involvement is minimal, with no official industry organization existing for
equipment and services suppliers, and only a few boutique firms specializing in ML usage in the accelerator
space. Larger ML-specific firms are beginning to express interest in increased participation, but long funding
lead times and policy requirements, with minimal incentives from accelerator facilities and operators, make
this challenging.
 There is an inherent conflict between the goals of the funding agencies and the goals of the organizations
performing the work. Policy-level organizations have broad scientific and/or security goals. These goals
do not mesh well with the needs of long-term operations at a multi-disciplinary facility that sometimes
operates across multiple funding agencies. AI/ML research for operations falls squarely into this priorities
gap, despite its increasing importance for maximizing accelerator uptime and usage. AI/ML research in
process engineering can be seen in other market sectors, such as large-scale energy systems.
 Recent changes in policy may help re-prioritize the use of overhead and line item budgets for the devel-
opment of collaborative software tools across facilities and industry, but the future is uncertain. Without
a re-prioritization to focus on AI/ML in operations across all interested parties, it is likely that trained
personnel will not be available in the workforce outside of AI and ML subject-matter experts in specialized
firms.
 Privacy and security concerns for data sharing also need to be addressed if a collaboration is to be
successful between disparate entities, both national and international. One long-term decision made on
some AI/ML projects in the accelerator community will help buffer projects from drastic policy swings –
third-party software repository ownership to avoid possible loss of code availability.

2.7 Introduction to AI/ML usage
In machine learning, having an agreement for how data is stored and handled is critical. In the larger world
of machine learning, several standards already exist, usually driven by what proprietary tools are being used
by the industrial partners involved. For example, Google developed Tensorflow, Meta developed pytorch,
and Microsoft and Apache both have different model storage standards, ONNX and TVM, respectively.
Higher level frameworks include Keras, initially developed by a Google engineer, and the OpenAI/Farama
Foundation Gym and Gymnasium, respectively. Each of these have developed with different licensing and
development structures, leaving no ‘optimal’ choice for any particular use case.
 Differences in facilities and machines make it very difficult to share models, but sharing machine learning
techniques can be eminently useful for operators. This can be a particular challenge due to differing regulatory
and privacy environments.
 Machine learning usage in particle accelerators can be categorized into three levels of time granularity:
near real-time, intermediate (∼ 1 Hz) scale, and delayed or offline processing. Some tasks, such as image pro-
cessing, segmentation, and classification can occur at a variety of time scales, depending on use case. These
classifications are helpful to understand how the use of ML and AI in the particle accelerator community is
different from that used in industry. Industry has generally focused on two major fields of research: image
processing and language processing. At particle accelerators, while image processing is used in operations
for some tasks such as sample alignment, most of these tasks occur outside the operational space. These
tasks are most often seen in the experimental, medical, and detector portions of machines. Operations needs

 10

include beamline and control optimization, and recently have seen significant success in using Bayesian opti-
mization techniques. Recent work has addressed early detection of error states and alarms, both online and
in post-processing.
 Operations data is also not generally treated with the same priority as experimental data, leading to sig-
nificant differences in data granularity and archival terms. Previously, mainly figures of merit or summation
metrics such as beam power and device performance on longer timescales were all that was archived, both to
minimize data storage needs and also due to a lack of consideration for the need for higher resolution data.
The lack of high quality archival datasets leads to significant challenges for the training of smart systems,
requiring either a significant amount of pre-processing of human readable machine log data, with its own
errors, or interpolation and use of intensive manual labeling of datasets.
 This is one reason that optimization techniques have become the main focus of contemporary ML in
operations. The other is that collaborations between facilities requires both desire for high quality data on
a system with its own unique qualities and agreements in data usage between a number of disparate parties.
These parties include funding entities (e.g. DOE), facility operations (subcontractors), division management
and staff, while also following any required privacy and data handling requirements levied by government
and regional authorities.
 Several labs have become key, unofficial “centers” for machine learning in accelerator operations. These
are primarily SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory (SLAC) and Jefferson Laboratory (JLAB), with Fermi
National Accelerator Laboratory (FNAL) struggling to build up an AI/ML infrastructure for reasons given
below. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) has been actively providing support for cutting
edge technologies, and develops a significant portion of the simulation codes used in operations, but is much
less actively involved aside from being the home center to funding for several key projects (i.e. the WarpX
computing framework, and the SciDAC FastMATH Institute).
 The federal government has determined that AI and ML are key technologies for development in the
USA, with the National Science Foundation (NSF) creating a pilot program called the National Artificial
Intelligence Research Resource (NAIRR). The NAIRR report of January 2023 recommends that significant
investment be put towards AI/ML across the government, but with a more broad focus on non-critical (i.e.
facility, operations, and internal) usage [4]. The NAIRR report calls for a focus on leveraging a large amount
of private computing resources, which is a significant challenge for the particle accelerator space.

2.8 Introduction to Legal Implications
Collaboration and data sharing agreements can have significant legal implications for the entities involved,
generally due to liability or licensing requirements. While some of this was covered in previous sections, it is
important to keep such issues in mind as collaboration structures are further discussed. Much of the analysis
is how to help interested parties collaborate together in a manner comfortable for them, both for legal
expectations and long-term risk management. The following list of questions emerged in our conversations
with relevant stakeholders.

 • How do legal issues and regulations affect collaboration governance and ownership models?
 • Liability — how are any external partners protected? Is there any risk to the involved parties?

 • Industry — commercial vs non-commercial licenses? Liability in licenses?
 • Standardization in language across licenses for tooling. This is a particular challenge across national
 borders.
 • Do standard licenses or prior art exist that should be followed? Can the collaboration or member
 parties provide the resources necessary to research these?

 • Intellectual Property — Microsoft has recently provided enormous support to open source projects,
 but they also have a business built on proprietary for-profit consumer and enterprise software. Can a
 fundamental research community build a relationship with such a partner while still maintaining open
 software?

 11

 There is currently no settled case law or legal understanding of how AI/ML tools will be treated in either
civil or criminal spheres. For conservative organizations, the risk of using such tools, let alone sharing risk
with others for work at their facilities, is high. Such obstacles can be overcome either through a use of
formal agreements or a complete lack of agreements, depending on how one views the use of software. An
organization that uses open software often agrees to take all the risk of such use on themselves, for example.

3.1 Collaborations in Software
We surveyed a number of different collaboration models and structures across the software and technology
space, and created a taxonomy. The taxonomy helps to understand the different models and to determine
whether one can classify any collaboration as a long-term success. These collaborations represent a broad
range of projects in or tangent to the particle accelerator community, including compilers, inter-lab data
sharing initiatives, and standards organizations.
 It is worth keeping in mind that business models in the US are not the same as those in other countries,
and different forms of organization have different needs. For example, in Austria a non-governmental (NGO)
structure exists [17]. Europe also sponsors large extra-governmental entities such as CERN [6] and ITER [13].
The only collaboration model that we have found to be international without official agreements outlining
member party requirements is that used in ad hoc open source software projects.

 Community, collaborative and non-profit software projects benefit from having an umbrella organization

3.3 State of AI/ML
Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are in widespread use across particle accelerator ex-
perimental facilities, as well as security and defense installations. For the particle accelerator community,
production use cases for facility operations are primarily limited to image recognition and processing. The
development of such technologies in the private sector has strongly impacted both funding and research
priorities within scientific and academic institutions. Operational facilities and control systems have very
different requirements from the cutting-edge of technology, though, with a need for reliability and quantifi-
ability. This differs greatly from usage for language processing and image processing, where a larger degree
of error is generally acceptable.
 Identifying areas within operations that would benefit directly from the current state of AI/ML research
is a challenge outside of needs that mimic those in industry (e.g. image and language processing). Many of
the needs fall under reliability and control theory, with theories that have been generally well-developed for
decades. The meteoric rise of available computing power has seen many of these compute-intensive methods
find their way into wider use, with machine learning techniques often wrapping these algorithms or providing
optimizations to the parameters used.
 ML and online learning techniques also include real-time, model-based controls. Similar to the issues
discussed above, such techniques are relatively rare in operations due to the conservative nature of such
organizations, limited R&D budgets, and the risk of damage to equipment. The equipment used for controls
has also led to a number of limitations or workarounds being necessary. This is particularly true with PLCs
(Programmable Logic Controllers) and proprietary vendor-provided hardware. The widespread use of such
equipment leads to only a few opportunities for machine learning wrapped around them — optimization
techniques, slow control, and protocol and failure analysis. PLCs in particular are still using proprietary
tooling and communication architectures.
 One important point was consistent across every interview: generally, no one knows who owns the data
or models created within the community. This problem leads to other negative consequences, in particular
for risk-adverse organizations. This confusion regarding IP ownership is independent of funding rules or
organization size. A significant advantage of the accelerator community, which was brought up in several
interviews, is that scientific collaborations often enjoy a feeling of openness, regardless of internal policies
or specific regulations on IP. Individuals in the community often benefit from this advantage, in spite of
organizational challenges and bureaucratic processes.
 Industry, academia and government are converging on the use of pytorch [16] as their AI/ML framework
of choice. This is in part due to challenges inherent in using TensorFlow [21], due to Google leading the
project. The pytorch framework and organization is growing rapidly, and its ease of use has been noted by
many organizations.
 ML for operational control is still not mainstream for operations in US facilities, with facilities only
recently implementing test projects for portions of their ML needs (e.g. deployment techniques, data analy-
sis). Most of these prototype projects are focused on optimization techniques, looking at ways to minimize
system downtime or assist with machine alignment and start-up. One of the most popular tools in use across
multiple facilities, internationally, is the SLAC-developed xopt/badger toolkit [10, 11].
 AI/ML is very different from other technologies as to where cutting-edge research is occurring. Much of
the most advanced research has come out of large companies, such as Tesco, Microsoft, or Google, and not
out of research institutions. This leads to important differences in how collaborations should be structured,
as is discussed below.
 While experiments have been increasingly using AI for sophisticated data analysis of complicated detector
and material geometries, the current operational needs are more limited. Machine vision is used in some
portions of accelerators for tasks including identification, segmentation, and advanced image processing,
 Most accelerators using ML in operations are limited in their ability to use bleeding edge technology due
to insufficient processing resources from within the control system. While some groups are able to leverage

 14

other computing resources in a shared facility, these often come with access restrictions due to both funding
(i.e. requiring funding to reserve computer time) and data movement (i.e. the computing resources are
not within the accelerator network). There has been a significant effort among academia, industry, and
government to push processing power to edge devices, but this shift would increase complexity with regard
to security, software development and controls.
 Operations staff at accelerators generally have access to a different set of computing resources than those
available for wider scientific computing needs. This is often due to the need for computing with access to
control system data, on networks and with handling needs more similar to those in an industrial factory. In
addition, it is a challenge for such divisions to obtain upgrades or expanded resources from facility managers
due to the separation between R&D funding vs operations. Most accelerator facilities do not budget and
reserve funds in multi-year blocks for upgrades to infrastructure.
 This lack of focus has a clear effect on the ability for operations to use AI/ML techniques. For example,
while even most consumer gaming GPUs are now able to perform functionally for operations research, such
small purchases might not fit the traditional view of computing needs by IT staff. This can lead to delays
in research results and product development. Facilities and businesses that have focused on providing such
resources for their staff have seen greater benefits in a shorter period of time.
 Collaborative work has assisted in much of this work as facilities that have limited their resources have
sometimes been able to shift the funding for such work to external contracts or other businesses. However,
such collaborations often have no clear path for monetization and growth, or they include IP restrictions
that make research ownership unclear.

4 Analysis
4.1 Successful and Unsuccessful Collaborations
The collaboration components listed in Table 1 interact in ways that highlight how different collaborative
models can be evaluated, depending on the stated goals. For example, a research collaboration focused on
solving a specific problem in operations using AI/ML for a linear accelerator might not be valid for analogous
goals in the synchrotron ring of an X-ray lightsource or a collider. Hence, this example requires a different
structure than what a more general software collaboration might use. An example of the latter is provided
by the open source Xopt [10] collaboration.
 Across multiple interviews and discussions, we have heard consistently from community stakeholders that
“people need money to collaborate,” which is not the same as being funded to work towards a specific project
goal. Collaboration needs to be a goal in itself for it to be successful in the long term.
 The Linux Foundation [14] recently created the High Performance Software Foundation (HPSF) [12],
which has provided a home for some scientific projects in the particle accelerator space that have reached
the end of their funding life cycle, including some funded by the SciDAC program [18] and/or the Exascale
Computing Project (ECP) [7]. Such a non-profit open source model still requires funding and resources to
be put towards the development of the projects if they are to continue being successful. An example of such
a project is the WarpX collaboration [23, 2, 9], based out of Berkeley Lab [5], discussed as a case study
below.
 A common collaboration model for large entities is that used by technology transfer offices, where a
number of license mechanisms can be used to move technology from the institution to outside parties. This
mindset that the work performed at an institution should be monetized, rather than accepting a shared
responsibility to community collaborations, is particularly challenging for AI/ML technologies, even within
the specialized sphere of accelerator operations. We were unable to find any examples of academic- or
government-led collaborations in AI/ML, likely due (at least in part) to these unhelpful points of view and
associated contract mechanisms.
 We next consider three successful software collaborations in the particle accelerator community, so as to
highlight how these efforts have grown or been developed in the current funding and regulatory environment.

 15

4.1.1   Case Study: Xopt [10]
Xopt is an optimization toolkit that is used at an increasing number of facilities to perform Bayesian op-
timization for a variety of control needs, both operational and scientific. The tool is coupled with a GUI
front end known as Badger [11]. xopt was originally developed by SLAC engineers and staff, in particular
Dr. Christopher Mayes and Dr. Ryan Roussel. This project has been successful for a number of reasons,
including:
 • Monetary and personnel support from a large facility (SLAC)
 • An enthusiastic advocate pushes for the tool’s use across multiple facilities (Dr. Roussel)
 • Sufficient generality to fill a need for multiple facilities across academia, industry, and government
 Xopt provides a toolkit that is of significant value to the accelerator operations community. The project
leadership (i.e. specific individuals) provide strong support for Xopt users. The SLAC National Accelerator
Lab [19] actively provides resources for the deployment and development of the tool in both travel and
personnel funds, and the project leadership are strong evangelists for the software. Deployment and usage
varies across different facilities. Several other Bayesian optimization toolkits are available and would likely
have been deployed independently by different accelerator groups if Xopt did not provide these resources for
communication and development support.

4.1.2   Case Study: WarpX [2, 23, 9]
WarpX was initially developed at Berkeley Lab as a part of the Exascale Computing Project (ECP), with
support from internal lab funding, and has grown to include support from European agencies, in addition to
other Department of Energy funding streams. Since the end of ECP funding, the governance and funding
model have shifted. For example, the project has become a founding member of the Linux Foundation’s High
Performance Software Foundation [12]. The Linux Foundation model is such that each project is classified
as a Linux Foundation project.
 The WarpX collaboration has been successful due to its access to a number of funding sources from
multiple funding offices, strong personalities leading the development who regularly reach out to users and
partners, and multi-country interest in the success of the project leading to additional buy-in. Even with
such strong support, there is concern within the project that the new funding model will not provide the
resources necessary to continue development without stronger internal lab and funding agency support.
 Various industry entities are helping to increase usage of the tool by additional partners, but project
leadership does not have a model in place to handle members who are not lab or academic.

4.1.3 Case Study: EPICS [8]
The Experimental Physics and Industrial Control System (EPICS) is an ecosystem of software packages
designed for distributed control of large facilities, such as particle accelerators. The framework is widely
used, with a robust system of packages and interfaces. Most of the key interfaces and protocols were
developed by specific individuals with interest in continuing project development, keeping the project alive.
Government funding has generally been available for project-based support, but long-term funding has only
been available on an ancillary basis, through individuals contributing their time.
 Project governance is multinational, with no single source of funding or development leadership. There
are specific individuals, in particular Michael Davidsaver, Andrew Johnson, and Mark Rivers, who are key
developers and provide project leadership in an ad hoc manner. While the project does have an official
membership and leadership model, the lack of a single funding stream has led to several major components
experiencing non-project-based development planning.
 An example of this can be seen in the development of the main GUI and interface tool, Control System
Studio, that is now developed primarily by the European Spallation Source and ORNL, while the C++ and
python libraries are generally developed by Argonne National Lab and Osprey DCS. This model leaves the
project reliant on funding for specific individuals to introduce new features or maintain current systems,
and would not be financially sustainable as an open source software ecosystem outside of the fact that the
software has been deployed at a number of facilities that rely on it. Even so, many facilities are still using
versions of the software developed 10 years ago, and the dual language (i.e. Java and C++) development
paths can lead to bugs and features confusion.

 16

 Legally, code ownership and licensing is generally ambiguous, as was shown earlier in this discussion,
with significant questions as to how other software packages integrate in with more standard licenses. For
example, in the plugins related to imaging (AreaDetector), where integrating vendor libraries is a significant
challenge. The use of a general open source license that allows for wide usage and dissemination has been a
help to the project’s ability to bring in new partners, but development is not supported by a large industrial
partner.

5 General Collaboration Models
A business plan to support a strong collaboration in AI/ML for particle accelerator operations requires:
 • A clear set of priorities and well-scoped goals.
 • Champions in each organization with dedicated resources.
 • Upper-level management support within all participating organizations.
 • A decadal support modal that provides guaranteed funding and a corroborating usage model
 We propose two alternatives for a successful collaboration in accelerator operations, depending on im-
mediate need. A third option exists and is discussed below, but would require additional discussion and
legal frameworks, that would likely be insurmountable if collaborating between industry, academia and gov-
ernment. In the future, these recommended models could be used to pivot ongoing projects, akin to how
the WarpX and Xopt projects shifted ownership throughout their development. Due to open questions about
data ownership and veracity for AI/ML models, there are significant legal and IP considerations that need
to be ironed out.
 Additional options were considered, but several governance and funding models are incompatible with
the partners and entities involved in accelerator operations or the long-term operation of large facilities.
This is particularly true for collaboration models proposed by the National AI Task Force, which rely on
NSF-style funding and assume the use of large-scale industrial compute infrastructure, such as Microsoft
Azure or Amazon Cloud.
 Due to the physical infrastructure and network security requirements for accelerator operations and
controls, the use of cloud computing resources are typically not applicable for anything but delayed processing
and/or offline optimization paradigms. Control systems also generate a large amount of redundant data,
contributing to high costs for offsite data movement and storage. While these challenges can in principle
be resolved through systems analysis and design, the development of such techniques and infrastructure
represent their own dedicated and facility-specific effort.

5.1 Option 1: Institution-home Leadership
The most likely model to succeed would adopt many aspects of the NSF strategy, with the establishment
of an AI/ML for accelerator operations research center with close ties to one or more extant facilities. This
model has similarities to the previous organization of the US Particle Accelerator School, with direct buy-in
and funding support from leadership at other accelerator facilities, while also allowing for the development
of umbrella legal frameworks that do not limit the collaboration’s ability to work with others. Such a model
would enable organic growth of software expertise and support across multiple institutions. As an example,
SLAC could serve as a host institution for an AI/ML operations research lab that leverages existing support
on the Xopt project to seed a broader multi-institutional research program. Subsequent collaborative funding
efforts, analagous to SCIDAC, could provide dedicated long-term funding while also furnishing additional
means for multi-institutional outreach and adoption.
 However, it would require an initial ‘seed funding’ period and multi-institutional coordination to provide
the initial experimental data, simulation tools, controls interfacing, and model frameworks. Furthermore,
implementing even this seed environment would require a data-sharing agreement from participating orga-
nizations.
 This model also requires the lead institution to accept a loss of control over the work in return for a larger
number of industry and academic partners. Attracting interest from industry partners requires less restrictive
contract mechanisms alongside long-term investment. Introducing a multi-lateral contract mechanism that
is general enough to support collaborations without concrete deliverables could mitigate these concerns;

 17

however, the collaboration model described as Option 2 may still be more favorable for supporting long-term
growth.

5.2 Option 2: Non-profit Home
The development of a third-party home for AI/ML research in accelerator operations provides significant
benefits for working across a variety of partners. The Linux Foundation has shown some success with this
model for a number of open source projects, and other fields, such as Quantum Computing, have found such
entities to provide the ability to do unbiased research and communications beneficial. Another example of a
successful model would be that used by Lightsources.org, run out of Diamond Light Source.
 This model relies on individuals from multiple institutions working together to develop the necessary
pseudo-corporate framework for a collaboration, while also helping members find and obtain funding to sup-
port collaborative needs. The Machine Learning Applications for Particle Accelerators (MaLAPA) workshop
series, which initially started as the ICFA Beam Dynamics Workshop on Machine Learning Applications,
serves as an example of a proto-nonprofit model that requires more secure funding to establish further
collaboration.
 Software projects, both open source and otherwise, that fall into this model have all been successful solely
due to strong individual leadership and personalities. Identifying and promoting these individuals to lead a
collaborative effort in operations is a problem of resources. This is especially true for a legal entity such as
a non-profit that requires resources to manage its internal affairs.

5.3 Option 3: Project Promotion
A final option for collaboration would be to ‘promote’ a current project into a collaboration by raising
additional long-term support and expanding the scope of the effort. There are several groups interested in
AI/ML in accelerator operations, but this option would require essentially a VC spin-off of a current project
for success. As a successful component of any long-term collaboration is strong individual leadership, this
option presents a challenge to convince these leaders to change their focus.
 This option also entails significant challenges for IP and legal ownership of code and data, requiring clear
understandings to be in place between all the partners involved. This is particularly true if the chosen project
is from an academic or government setting, and was developed without consideration for use in industry.

6 Conclusion
Accelerators are increasingly using ML techniques for a variety of tasks, from optimization to fault detection
and recovery. Efficient and sustainable application of these technologies necessitates specialized and facility-
specific infrastructure commitments. Accelerator facilities introduce unique radiation and security hazards,
placing unique demands on operational infrastructure. These needs further escalate the prioritization of
effective collaboration models and associated funding mechanisms and legal frameworks.
 Successful collaborations are a challenge to manage and maintain in industries with high need, and even
the most robust examples of such collaborations rely on dedicated support to enable continued development.
Several key components are required for a long-term collaboration to be successful, but people are the key.
Even the most wildly successful project will not succeed without strong leadership, either in the form of
individuals or a managing entity.
 Enticing industry and academia involvement in government projects is a greater challenge, as protections
need to be in place to protect the work and IP of the non-government entities. For example, some European
institutions have contracting models that are of a different form from those pursued by US government
accelerator facility contractors. Moving such work to a third-party entity to manage development and
licensing would likely be a successful model for future collaboration.
