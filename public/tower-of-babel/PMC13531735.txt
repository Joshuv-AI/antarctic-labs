Rapid recovery vs standard care in laparoscopic appendectomy: a prospective study on patient-reported outcomes
Authors: Tiia Mönttinen, Maija-Liisa Kalliomäki, Johanna Laukkarinen, Mika Ukkonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic appendectomy for uncomplicated acute appendicitis traditionally requires postoperative hospitalization. Rapid recovery and same-day discharge protocols have been increasingly adopted, yet evidence regarding patient-reported outcomes is limited. This study aimed to compare a rapid recovery program with standard postoperative care, focusing on patient satisfaction, pain experience, and postoperative functional interference with daily activities.MethodsIn this prospective, non-randomized observational study with sequential group allocation, 96 consecutive adults undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy for uncomplicated acute appendicitis at a single tertiary hospital were enrolled. Patients were allocated sequentially into standard care (n = 62, enrolled first) or a rapid recovery program (n = 34, enrolled in a subsequent phase). Outcomes included length of hospital stay, postoperative pain intensity (NRS 0–10), pain interference with daily activities, sleep quality, and overall satisfaction, assessed via structured telephone interviews conducted 3–5 days post-discharge.ResultsBaseline demographics and comorbidities were comparable between groups. Median hospital stay was significantly shorter in the rapid recovery group compared with standard care (12 vs. 18 h, p < 0.001). All patients reported high overall satisfaction with care and pain management. Patients in the rapid recovery group reported lower worst pain (p = 0.049) and least pain (p = 0.006), while median pain was slightly higher (p = 0.004). Pain outcomes showed a mixed pattern that does not support a definitive conclusion regarding superiority of pain management in either group. Sleep disturbance due to pain was less common in the rapid recovery group (18% vs. 42%, p = 0.022). Nearly all patients expressed confidence in the rapid recovery protocol and would recommend it to others.ConclusionRapid recovery following laparoscopic appendectomy is feasible, well accepted, with high patient satisfaction and a modest reduction in hospital stay.Trial registrationISRCTN registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03913-0.Keywords: Appendectomy, Patient reported outcomes, Length of stay, Morbidity, Pain management

Introduction

Acute appendicitis, the most prevalent emergency surgical condition, requires effective management to ensure optimal patient recovery. Despite the ongoing debate about surgical versus antibiotic treatment in uncomplicated acute appendicitis, operative treatment remains still the standard of care [1]. In pursuit of better resource utilization, postoperative recovery protocols have evolved. Among these innovations, rapid recovery programs and outpatient care have been introduced – this is an approach designed to expedite patient recuperation while ensuring safety and efficacy [2–7].

Since the introduction of the rapid recovery programs, the focus has been on shortening hospital stays, enhancing patient satisfaction and pain management [8–11]. While feasibility and safety data are encouraging, patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) – including pain experience, functional interference, and satisfaction – have received little systematic attention in this context. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically assessed PROMs after rapid recovery protocols in adults undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy for uncomplicated appendicitis.

The primary aim of this prospective study was to describe and compare patient-reported pain intensity and satisfaction between a rapid recovery program and conventional postoperative care following laparoscopic appendectomy. Length of hospital stay was a secondary endpoint. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of prior PROM data in this setting, we did not hypothesize superiority of the rapid recovery program on patient-reported outcomes, but anticipated that it would be non-inferior to standard care on satisfaction and pain experience.

Patients and methods

The aim of this prospective, non-randomized study with sequential group allocation was to compare the conventional postoperative management to the rapid recovery program after laparoscopic appendectomy, with the goal of evaluating their respective impact on patient-reported outcomes. Consecutive adult patients undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy at Tampere University Hospital, Finland, were enrolled in the study. The study hospital is responsible for all level emergency care in the hospital district with the catchment area exceeding 500,000 inhabitants. Inclusion criteria were: age 18–65 years, uncomplicated appendicitis confirmed intraoperatively (no perforation with either peritonitis or abscess), and a responsible adult at home for the first 24 h. Pregnant patients or those unable to give written informed consent were excluded.

Patients were recruited from the study hospital emergency department, where they received both written and oral informed consent. Patients were informed about the study protocol and planned postoperative discharge. Recruitment occurred in two phases: the standard care group was recruited first (August 2019 – November 2022), followed by the rapid recovery group (November 2022 – March 2023). Prior to this study, the new protocol had not been implemented at the hospital. Once the power analysis criteria were met, the hospital staff were briefed on the new protocol, and the recruitment of the rapid recovery program group began. This sequential design was chosen to reduce contamination bias (i.e., to prevent knowledge of the rapid recovery protocol from influencing discharge decisions in the standard care group). However, it introduces potential for temporal confounding, implementation bias, and learning-curve effects. Three patients with missing age data were excluded, resulting in a final analysis population of 96 patients.

Surgery

In both study groups, surgery was planned for the next available slot so that the time from hospital admission to surgery should be less than eight hours. A single dose of parenteral antibiotics (cefuroxime 1.5 g and metronidazole 500 mg) was administered before the operation. Surgery was performed using a standard laparoscopic technique with a 30° laparoscope. During the surgery, three incisions (two 5 mm and one 10 mm) for non-cutting bladeless trocars were made. Standard instruments were used: monopolar electrocautery, ligation clip device, retrieval bag, and supporting laparoscopic instruments were used. Local infiltration with ropivacaine (10 mL) was used for analgesia. Dissolvable sutures were used for all wounds. Antibiotics were discontinued after the operation. No drains or urinary catheters were used postoperatively. All patients were operated on either by a consultant surgeon or a resident surgeon with a minimum of three years of experience in emergency abdominal surgery.

Postoperative pain management at the recovery room and hospital ward included paracetamol (1 g three times per day), ibuprofen (600 mg three times per day), and opioids (oxycodone, 1 mg/10 kg, up to 3 times per day), unless these were contraindicated. Patients were given a normal diet after the operation.

Anaesthesia

Anaesthesia was standardized across both groups. Induction used propofol (2 mg/kg), fentanyl (2–3 μg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) intravenously. Maintenance used sevoflurane and fentanyl as needed. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed with sugammadex or neostigmine as appropriate. Prophylactic ondansetron (4 mg i.v.) was administered for postoperative nausea and vomiting. Postoperative analgesia in the recovery unit included paracetamol (1 g i.v. if not given orally pre-operatively), dexketoprofen (50 mg i.v.), and intramuscular oxycodone (1 mg/10 kg); oral opioids and rescue analgesia were available as needed. This protocol was identical in both groups and is therefore unlikely to have differentially affected postoperative pain or discharge timing.

Standard care group

Patients were discharged from the surgical ward following a morning ward round (8–10 a.m.). Surgeons informed patients about surgical findings and postoperative care. At discharge, patients received a prescription for postoperative pain management (ibuprofen 600 mg and paracetamol 1 g, each up to three times daily unless contraindicated).

Discharge criteria for the standard care group were not formally protocolized; discharge was at the attending surgeon's discretion following the morning ward round, once the patient was assessed as clinically ready.

Rapid recovery program group

Patients in the rapid recovery group did not meet the operating surgeon postoperatively. During pre-operative recruitment, the recruiting physician explained the typical laparoscopic appendectomy procedure, possible complications, and anticipated recovery. Discharge was nurse-led. Nurses instructed patients on wound care at home and criteria for returning to the emergency department or contacting the ward. Discharge criteria included: recovery from anaesthesia; ability to eat, urinate, and walk; stable vital signs (systolic blood pressure > 100 mmHg, heart rate < 100/min, body temperature < 38 °C); and adequate pain control with paracetamol (1 g, up to three times daily) and ibuprofen (600 mg, up to three times daily).

Follow-up call

A structured telephone interview was conducted with all patients 3–5 days after discharge. All interviews were conducted by the same researcher (TM). The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study to capture dimensions relevant to the rapid recovery experience and has not been formally validated as a patient-reported outcome instrument; an English-language version is provided as a supplementary file. The questionnaire included:

Five-point Likert scale: sufficiency and clarity of information received; perceived safety of treatment; overall satisfaction with care; likelihood to recommend the treatment; appropriateness of postoperative pain management.

Electronic medical records were reviewed for follow-up data including readmissions, reoperations, morbidity, and mortality up to 30 days post-discharge for all patients.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26 for Windows or R version 4.1.12. Values are presented as absolute values with percentages or medians with interquartile ranges and ranges. Two-tailed Chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests were used to compare categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U-test to compare continuous variables. The length of hospitalisation was defined as time from emergency department admission to hospital discharge. Because no prior studies had reported PROMs after rapid recovery appendectomy, the power analysis was based on an anticipated reduction in length of hospital stay (alpha = 0.05, 80% power). Patient-reported outcome analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature; the study was not powered for these outcomes, and this distinction is maintained consistently throughout. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied; the risk of type I error due to multiple testing is acknowledged. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 99 patients were initially enrolled; three with missing age data were excluded, resulting in a final analysis population of 96 patients. All patients had uncomplicated acute appendicitis. 34 patients (median age: 35 [IQR 24–46] years; 71% female) underwent the rapid recovery program and 62 (median age: 34 [IQR 26–50] years; 42% female) the standard postoperative recovery process. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups and are summarised in Table 1. The rapid recovery group included a higher proportion of female patients (71% vs. 42%, p = 0.013); however, sex was not associated with pain outcomes in this cohort (p = 0.466) and is therefore unlikely to have confounded the pain comparisons.

The median length of hospital stay was significantly shorter in the rapid recovery program (12 h [IQR 9–15, range 2–33] vs. 18 h [IQR 13–26, range 3–61], p < 0.001). Only one patient suffered from a postoperative complication – this patient developed a laparoscopic trocar site hernia one week after the operation, necessitating subsequent surgery. Healthcare contacts within 30 days post-discharge were comparable between groups (rapid recovery protocol: 6 patients [18%] vs. standard care: 13 patients [21%], p = 0.902).

Pain-related outcomes are summarised in Fig. 1 and Table 2. Patients in the rapid recovery group reported lower worst pain (median 5 [IQR 3–6] vs. 5 [IQR 4–6], p = 0.049) and lower least pain (median 1 [IQR 0–2] vs. 2 [IQR 1–3], p = 0.006), but slightly higher median pain (median 3 [IQR 2–4] vs. 1 [IQR 1–2], p = 0.004) compared with the standard care group. These pain comparisons are exploratory; no definitive conclusion regarding superiority of pain management in either group can be drawn from these findings. This mixed pattern likely reflects differences in the pain environment between home and hospital rather than a straightforward improvement in pain management. Fewer patients in the rapid recovery group reported sleep disturbance due to pain (18% vs. 42%, p = 0.022). There were no statistically significant between-group differences in pain interference with sitting, standing, or walking (Table 1).

All patients in both groups reported high overall satisfaction (median 5 out of 5, p = 0.756) and satisfaction with pain management (median 5, p = 0.357). Ninety-five percent of patients in the rapid recovery group expressed overall confidence in the program. One patient expressed dissatisfaction with the rapid recovery approach – this individual also had the longest hospital stay in that group. All patients, including this individual, indicated they would recommend the rapid recovery protocol to other patients (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Many elective laparoscopic procedures are performed using rapid recovery programs, but their application in emergency surgery remains less established. Laparoscopic appendectomy, where recovery is generally straightforward, is an appealing candidate for such approaches [2–14]. The present study examined patient-reported outcomes in this context. Our main findings are that rapid recovery was feasible and well-accepted, with high patient satisfaction in both groups, and a statistically significant, if modest, reduction in hospital stay.

The high satisfaction observed in both groups is a noteworthy finding. This may partly reflect the structured pre-operative information provided to all participants, which was more comprehensive than typical clinical practice. The equivalent satisfaction between groups suggests that information quality, rather than discharge timing alone, may be a key driver of patient experience. This should be considered when implementing rapid recovery pathways: counselling and information provision are likely as important as the logistical aspects of the program.

The pain findings require careful interpretation. The rapid recovery group reported lower worst and least pain, but higher median pain scores. This pattern may reflect that patients discharged earlier experience their peak pain at home rather than in hospital, where analgesia is more readily available. It does not clearly support a conclusion of improved pain management. Objective data on analgesic consumption – including total opioid-equivalent use, rescue analgesic requirements, and post-discharge prescription refills – were not collected in this study. Such data would be important to characterise postoperative pain management more robustly and should be included in future studies.

The reduction in hospital stay, while statistically significant, was approximately six hours – smaller than reductions reported in several earlier studies [2–7]. This is likely attributable to implementation barriers: some patients in the rapid recovery group were discharged the following morning despite meeting discharge criteria, reflecting surgeons' tendency to instruct overnight stays and nurses' compliance with those instructions. If discharge criteria were applied consistently, the reduction in stay could be substantially greater, with meaningful implications for bed availability and costs. A formal economic analysis was not undertaken, and would be an interesting topic for future research.

Morbidity and mortality were left out of main outcomes, as postoperative morbidity and mortality after laparoscopic appendectomy for uncomplicated disease is generally low and a new treatment protocol is not likely to increase typical complications or deaths; in our recent study with large study population, less than 2% of patients experienced postoperative complications [15]. Most complications occur in patients with complicated disease and may often manifest days after discharge. Majority of complications do not require reinterventions. Typical severe complications, such as intra-abdominal bleeding from a trocar site, are likely to be diagnosed before discharge regardless of the discharge program. The most common complication that may require intervention, postoperative abscess at the surgical site, is usually diagnosed after discharge and requires readmission regardless of the discharge group. In this study, only one patient suffered from a postoperative complication requiring readmission, which standard care would not have prevented. Rapid recovery program is most suitable for previously healthy patients with uncomplicated appendicitis, while older and more fragile patients, who may be at higher risk, are likely to require standard care.

Study limitations

The study had some limitations. First, the two groups were enrolled in sequential phases rather than concurrently, making this effectively a prospective non-randomised before–after comparison rather than a controlled trial. Such a design is susceptible to temporal confounding, implementation bias and changes in discharge culture over time. Furthermore, the two groups differed not only in discharge timing, but also in the nature of postoperative surgeon contact, the personnel responsible for discharge, and the content of counselling provided. These additional differences may independently influence satisfaction, reassurance, and symptom reporting, and cannot be separated from the effect of earlier discharge. We chose sequential rather than concurrent enrolment to reduce contamination bias – specifically, to prevent knowledge of the rapid recovery protocol from influencing surgeons' discharge decisions in the standard care group. This was a deliberate methodological choice, but it comes at the cost of reduced internal validity. Second, all interviews were conducted by the same researcher, who was not blinded to group allocation; this represents a potential source of bias. Third, the telephone questionnaire was not formally validated as a patient-reported outcome instrument; future studies should employ validated PROMs (e.g., EQ-5D or SF-36) to strengthen comparability. Finally, the questionnaire was developed specifically for this study to capture dimensions relevant to the rapid recovery experience.

Conclusion

This prospective non-randomised study demonstrates that a rapid recovery program following laparoscopic appendectomy for uncomplicated appendicitis is feasible and well-accepted by patients. High satisfaction was observed in both groups. The program was associated with a statistically significant, though modest, reduction in hospital stay.
