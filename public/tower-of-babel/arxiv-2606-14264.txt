Universal L 2 L^{2} -approximation using median digital-net algorithms
Ziyang Ye, Xiaoqun Wang, Zexin Pan

Abstract
We propose a median digital-net algorithm for L 2 L^{2} -approximation of non-periodic functions over [ 0 , 1 ] s [0,1]^{s} , inspired by the recently developed median lattice algorithms for the periodic setting. The algorithm requires no smoothness or weight parameters but only a sufficiently large candidate Walsh index set K K . It proceeds in three stages: generating multiple estimates of the Walsh coefficients in K K using independent randomized digital-net samples; taking the respective median of both the estimates and their absolute values; then, based on these median values, identifying the dominant coefficients and constructing a truncated Walsh series as the final approximation. We prove that if the target function has dominating mixed partial derivatives up to order α \alpha , all having finite Vitali variation of fractional order λ \lambda , then the algorithm achieves an L 2 L^{2} -error of 𝒪 ⁡ ( M − α − λ + ϵ ) \mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha-\lambda+\epsilon}) with high probability, where M M is the total number of function evaluations and ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 is arbitrarily small. Furthermore, the implied constant grows at most polynomially in the dimension s s under suitable decay conditions on the ANOVA components of the target function. On the implementation side, we provide both parameter-dependent and -independent constructions of the index set K K , and employ the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform and Gray code to accelerate the algorithm. Numerical experiments support the theoretical analysis and demonstrate that the proposed algorithm remains effective in high-dimensional settings.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the
L^{2}
-approximation of non-periodic functions on the unit cube
[0,1]^{s}
. This problem is of crucial importance in high-dimensional applications such as computational finance
[
7
,
6
]
, machine learning
[
2
,
1
]
, and uncertainty quantification
[
22
,
16
,
14
]
. However, the curse of dimensionality causes computational costs to grow exponentially with dimension, rendering traditional grid-based methods infeasible. This challenge motivates the search for novel function space assumptions and specialized algorithms.

To address this issue, quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods offer a particularly promising direction. QMC methods are equal-weight quadrature rules that use low-discrepancy point sets, such as digital nets
[
23
,
12
]
and lattices
[
29
,
11
]
, to tackle
the curse of dimensionality. Although well-established for numerical integration, adapting these methods to function approximation remains challenging. Existing results focus mainly on periodic functions, where the natural synergy between lattices and the Fourier series is exploited.

Pioneering work by Kuo et al.
[
20
]
established an
L^{2}
-approximation algorithm for functions in Korobov spaces (periodic Sobolev spaces). Their approach used a single rank-1 lattice of
M
nodes, achieving an error rate of
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha/2+\epsilon})
for arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
, where
\alpha
is the smoothness parameter of the Korobov space. This rate is nearly optimal among algorithms restricted to function evaluations on a single rank-1 lattice
[
4
]
, but falls short of the near‑optimal rate
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha+\epsilon})
without this restriction. More recent approaches, including multiple rank-1 lattices
[
17
]
, subsampled lattices
[
3
]
, multiple shifted lattices with least-squares recovery
[
5
]
, and median lattices
[
27
,
26
]
, all match this near-optimal rate with high probability.

We focus particularly on the median lattice algorithms introduced by Pan et al.
[
26
]
. Building on earlier work
[
27
]
, their approach uses the median‑of‑means to estimate many Fourier coefficients from only a few randomized lattices. Its key advancement is universality: the algorithm requires no prior knowledge of the smoothness and weight parameters of the Korobov space. By leveraging the universal property of median QMC (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
24
,
25
]
) and choosing a parameter‑independent index set of Fourier coefficients, the method achieves the near‑optimal error rate without parameter tuning.

To extend lattice-based methods to non-periodic settings, a common strategy is to employ the tent transformation to map functions into periodic spaces
[
30
,
8
,
19
]
. Recent works have used this transformation to establish optimal error bounds for half-period cosine spaces
[
13
]
, yet a direct digital-net-based approach, which does not require transforming the function, remains largely unexplored. To address this, we draw inspiration from the median QMC framework of
[
25
,
26
]
and develop a universal median digital-net algorithm for the
L^{2}
-approximation of non-periodic functions.

The proposed approach builds upon the Walsh series representation of non-periodic functions, analogous to the Fourier series in the periodic case. Specifically, we consider target functions that admit an absolutely convergent Walsh series expansion

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x}),

where
\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}
denotes the multivariate Walsh function and the Walsh coefficients are

\widehat{f}(\bm{k})=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\bm{x})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

To recover the dominant Walsh coefficients of
f
, we employ digital nets, exploiting the structural correspondence between digital nets and Walsh functions. The procedure follows a three-stage design paralleling the one in
[
26
]
. First, for each coefficient in a preselected candidate index set
K
(chosen to capture the dominant Walsh coefficients), we compute
R
independent estimates using random digital-net samples of size
N=2^{m}
. We then apply a median-of-means estimator to both the coefficient estimates and their absolute values. The former yields the final coefficient estimates, while the latter serves as a selection criterion to identify the
N
coefficients with the largest absolute values.
Finally, we construct a truncated Walsh series as an approximation to
f
using the median estimates.

Following
[
25
]
, we assume
f
belongs to a function space equipped with a norm based on the fractional Vitali variation introduced in
[
9
]
. However, our algorithm uses unweighted index sets instead of the weighted index set construction in
[
25
]
. This choice slightly degrades the tractability, but it endows the algorithm with universality: it eliminates the dependence on specific weight parameters while maintaining strong convergence guarantees.

We prove that if
f
possesses dominating mixed derivatives up to order
\alpha
, each having finite fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
(formally defined in Section
2.3
), the algorithm achieves an
L^{2}
-error of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\alpha-\lambda+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
with high probability. This convergence rate holds whenever the candidate index set
K
contains the dominant Walsh coefficients determined by
\alpha
and
\lambda
, and
R
grows logarithmically in the size of
K
. In particular, there exists a universal choice of
K
such that for all admissible
\alpha
and
\lambda
, the probabilistic error bound is
\mathcal{O}((s\log M/M)^{\alpha+\lambda-\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
, where
M=NR
is the total number of function evaluations and the implied constant is independent of the dimension
s
.

To enable efficient computation, we leverage the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform and the structure of the index set
K
to significantly reduce the cost of estimating Walsh coefficients, thereby making the universal approximation approach practical for large-scale problems.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
reviews the necessary background, including digital nets, Walsh functions, ANOVA decomposition, and the function norm used in
[
25
]
. Section
3
presents the universal median digital-net algorithm, constructs the ideal candidate index set under full knowledge of the parameters, and states the probabilistic
L^{2}
-error bound (Theorem
3.9
) along with the tractability result. Section
4
focuses on the practical implementation, providing two concrete constructions of the index set
K
and discussing acceleration strategies via the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform and optimized sorting based on Gray code. Section
5
reports numerical experiments that validate the theoretical findings. Finally, Section
6
offers a concluding discussion. The appendix contains the omitted proofs.

Notation and background

Throughout the paper,
f:[0,1]^{s}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the target function to be approximated, where
s
represents the dimension of its domain. We assume that
f
is nonzero.
Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the natural numbers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. In particular, we define
\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}=\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
.
Let
\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\ell}=\{0,1,\ldots,\ell\}

\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{<\ell}=\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. We further denote
1{:}s=\{1,\ldots,s\}
. For a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and a subset
u\subseteq 1{:}s
,
\bm{x}_{u}
denotes the subvector of
\bm{x}
indexed by
u
, while
\bm{x}_{u^{c}}
denotes the subvector indexed by
1{:}s\setminus u
. The indicator function
\bm{1}\{A\}
equals
1
if event

A
occurs and
0
otherwise. The notation
\mathbb{U}(S)
denotes the uniform distribution over a set
S
.
The cardinality of a set
K
is
|K|
. Furthermore, for a finite
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we define
\|K\|=\sum_{\ell\in K}\ell
. For vector
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
, its support is denoted by

\bm{s}(\bm{k})=\{j\in 1:s|k_{j}\neq 0\}.

We assume the target function
f
is continuous, denoted as
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
, with
\|f\|_{\infty}=\max_{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f(\bm{x})|
. The
L^{p}
-norm of
f
is defined by
\|f\|_{L^{p}}=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{s}}|f(\bm{x})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)^{1/p}
, and
L^{p}([0,1]^{s})
denotes the space of functions with finite
L^{p}
-norm.

To denote the classical partial derivatives of
f
, we adopt the following notation

f^{(\bm{\alpha})}(\bm{x})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{j}}}{\partial x_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}\right)f(\bm{x}),\quad\forall\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}.

For integrand
g
defined on
[0,1]^{s}
, QMC methods approximate the integral

\mu(g):=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}g(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\quad\text{by}\quad\widehat{\mu}(g)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}g(\bm{x}_{i})

with
\bm{x}_{0},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1}\in[0,1]^{s}
. In this paper, we will choose
\bm{x}_{i}
to be the randomized base-2 digital nets described below, and we denote the number of points in the digital net by
N=2^{m}
.

2.1
Digital net and complete random design

For
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}}
, we express
i
in its binary form
i=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}i_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
and denote
\vec{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Analogously, for
a\in[0,1)
, we approximate its infinite binary expansion
a=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{-\ell}
by truncating to
E\in\mathbb{N}
digits, yielding
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{E})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
. Here, we adopt the convention that binary expansions do not contain an infinite tail of
1
’s, ensuring that the representation is unique.

Furthermore, for any
k,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with binary representations
k=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}k_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
and
t=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}t_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
, we define the
bitwise XOR
operator
\oplus
as
w=k\oplus t
, where the binary representation
w=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}w_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
satisfies
w_{\ell}=k_{\ell}+t_{\ell}\mod{2}
for all
\ell
. For the
s
-dimensional case, the operator
\oplus
is applied component-wise.

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
E\in\mathbb{N}
, a
base-
2
digital net
consists of
2^{m}
points in
[0,1]^{s}
determined by
s
generating matrices
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
. The deterministic points
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{is})
are constructed via

\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}\mod{2}\quad\text{for }i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}},j\in 1{:}s,

where
\vec{x}_{ij}
represents the first
E
bits of
x_{ij}
. Typically, for unrandomized digital nets we have
E\leqslant m
.

To incorporate randomness, we set the precision
E
such that
E\geqslant m
and modify the construction as follows

\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}+\vec{D}_{j}\mod{2},

(2.1)

where
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
is a random matrix and
\vec{D}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
is a random vector. The vector
\vec{D}_{j}
is called the
random digital shift
and consists of independent Bernoulli (0.5) entries. Regarding the randomization of
C_{j}
, we employ the
complete random design

[
24
]
, in which every entry of
C_{j}
is sampled independently from
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\})
.

Now, let
\{\bm{x}_{i}^{E},i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}}\}
denote the
E
-bit precision points generated by equation (
2.1
) with matrices
\bm{C}_{m,E}:=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{s}\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
and shifts
\bm{D}_{E}:=\{\vec{D}_{1},\ldots,\vec{D}_{s}\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{E}
. We denote the QMC estimator for an integral
\mu(g)
as

\widehat{\mu}_{\bm{C}_{m,E},\bm{D}_{E}}(g):=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}g(\bm{x}_{i}^{E}).

(2.2)

In this paper, our theoretical analysis is primarily based on the idealized case where
E=\infty
. In practical algorithms,
E
is taken to be sufficiently large. The main theorem is nevertheless established for finite
E
, with the proof combining the
E=\infty
analysis and stability arguments for the finite-precision setting.

2.2
Walsh functions and error analysis for base-2 digital
nets

Walsh functions are the natural Fourier basis for the analysis of base-2 digital nets. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in[0,1)
, the
k
’th univariate Walsh function
\operatorname{wal}_{k}
is defined by

\operatorname{wal}_{k}(x)=(-1)^{\vec{k}^{\top}\vec{x}},

where
\vec{k}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
and
\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
are the binary expansions of
k
and
x
, respectively. Since
\vec{k}
has only finitely many nonzero digits, the inner product
\vec{k}^{\top}\vec{x}
is well-defined. It follows that if
k<2^{E}
, then
\operatorname{wal}_{k}(x)
is constant on elementary intervals of the form
[t/2^{E},(t+1)/2^{E})
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{E}}
, taking values in
\{-1,1\}
.

For the
s
-dimensional case, the multivariate Walsh function
\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}:[0,1)^{s}\to\{-1,1\}
is defined as the tensor product of univariate Walsh functions

\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{wal}_{k_{j}}(x_{j})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}^{\top}_{j}\vec{x}_{j}},

where
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
. These functions satisfy

\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}}(\bm{x})=\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{x}),\quad\forall\bm{k},\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}.

The functions
\{\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\}_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}
constitute a complete orthonormal basis for
L^{2}([0,1]^{s})

[
12
]
. Therefore, any
g\in L^{2}([0,1]^{s})
can be expressed via the Walsh expansion

g(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\widehat{g}(\bm{k})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})

(2.3)

with Walsh coefficients given by

\widehat{g}(\bm{k})=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}g(\bm{x})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where the series in (
2.3
) converges in the
L^{2}
-sense. Using this expansion, we
obtain the following error decomposition for the QMC estimator.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
satisfies
\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{\ell})|<\infty
. For any
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
, let
g(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})
. Then
\mu(g)=\widehat{f}(\bm{k})
and

\widehat{\mu}_{\bm{C}_{m,\infty},\bm{D}_{\infty}}(g)-\mu(g)=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}}Z(\bm{\ell})W(\bm{\ell})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}),

(2.4)

where
\widehat{\mu}_{\bm{C}_{m,\infty},\bm{D}_{\infty}}(g)
is given by equation (
2.2
) with
E=\infty
, and

\displaystyle Z(\bm{\ell})=\bm{1}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{\ell}_{j}^{\top}C_{j}=\bm{0}\mod{2}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad W(\bm{\ell})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{\ell}_{j}^{\top}\vec{D}_{j}}.

Proof.

In the
L^{2}
-sense, we have

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{x})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\widehat{f}(\bm{\ell})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{x})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\widehat{f}(\bm{\ell})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{k}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\widehat{f}(\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{k})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{x}).

(2.5)

This implies
\mu(g)=\widehat{g}(\bm{0})=\widehat{f}(\bm{k})
. Since
f
is continuous with
\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{\ell})|<\infty
,
[
12
, Theorem A.20]
shows that

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})

holds pointwise. Consequently, equality (
2.5
) holds pointwise, and equation (
2.4
) follows from the same argument as in
[
24
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

2.3
ANOVA decomposition and function norm

In this subsection, we focus on the function norm considered in
[
25
, Section 3]
. First, we employ the following ANOVA decomposition
[
21
]
for
f\in C([0,1]^{s})

f=\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}f_{u},

where each component
f_{u}
depends only on
\bm{x}_{u}
and satisfies
\int_{0}^{1}f_{u}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=0
for any
j\in u
. This decomposition is unique in the
L^{2}
-sense.
Furthermore, if we enforce
f_{u}\in C([0,1]^{s})
, then
f_{u}
is given by the following recursive formula

f_{u}(\bm{x})=\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}f(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{u^{c}}-\sum_{v\subsetneqq u}f_{v}(\bm{x}),

(2.6)

with
f_{\varnothing}(\bm{x})=\mu(f)
. By an abuse of notation, we write
f_{u}(\bm{x})=f_{u}(\bm{x}_{u})
, where the latter
f_{u}:[0,1]^{|u|}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
|u|
-dimensional function.

The ANOVA component
f_{u}
has the Walsh expansion

f_{u}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{s}(\bm{k})=u}\widehat{f}(\bm{k})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x}),

(2.7)

since
\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}
depends only on
\bm{x}_{\bm{s}(\bm{k})}
and
\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=0
whenever
j\in\bm{s}(\bm{k})
.

Next, we introduce the fractional Vitali variation with
\mathfrak{p}=2
from
[
9
]
to quantify the variation of the ANOVA components
f_{u}
. For a function
g:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and a subcube
J=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[a_{j},b_{j})\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
, the alternating sum over
J
is given by

\Delta(g,J)=\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}d}(-1)^{d-|v|}g(\bm{a}_{v},\bm{b}_{v^{c}}),

where
(\bm{a}_{v},\bm{b}_{v^{c}})
denotes the vertex of
J
with
x_{j}=a_{j}
for
j\in v
and
x_{j}=b_{j}
for
j\in 1{:}d\setminus v
. For
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
is defined as

V_{\lambda}^{(d)}(g)=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{Vol}(J)\left|\frac{\Delta(g,J)}{\operatorname{Vol}(J)^{\lambda}}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where the supremum is over all partitions
\mathcal{P}
of
[0,1)^{d}
into subcubes and
\operatorname{Vol}(J)
is the volume of subcube
J
. When
d=0
, we set
V_{\lambda}^{(0)}(g)=|g|
by convention.

Furthermore, we recall the partial fractional Vitali variation introduced in
[
24
]
. For
v\subseteq 1{:}d
, define the partial integral operator
I_{v}:L^{1}([0,1]^{d})\to L^{1}([0,1]^{|v|})
as

I_{v}(g)(\bm{x}_{v})=\int_{[0,1]^{d-|v|}}g(\bm{x}_{v},\bm{x}_{v^{c}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{v^{c}}.

Let
D_{v^{c}}
be the class of functions

D_{v^{c}}:=\left\{\rho_{v^{c}}:\rho_{v^{c}}(\bm{x})=\prod_{j\in v^{c}}\rho_{j}(x_{j}),\rho_{j}\in C([0,1]),\rho_{j}\geqslant 0,\|\rho_{j}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1\right\}.

The partial fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
with respect to
v\subseteq 1:d
is given by

V_{\lambda}^{v}(g):=\sup_{\rho_{v^{c}}\in D_{v^{c}}}V_{\lambda}^{(|v|)}(I_{v}(g\rho_{v^{c}})).

By convention,
V_{\lambda}^{1{:}d}(g)=V_{\lambda}^{(d)}(g)
. An important special case arises when
\lambda=1
, where we have the estimate

V^{v}_{1}(g)\leqslant\|g^{(\bm{1}_{v},\bm{0}_{v^{c}})}\|_{L^{2}}

(2.8)

if the weak derivative
g^{(\bm{1}_{v},\bm{0}_{v^{c}})}
exists and belongs to
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
.
Here
(\bm{1}_{v},\bm{0}_{v^{c}})
denotes the vector whose
j
-th entry equals
1
if
j\in v
and equals
0
otherwise.

We now define the function norm for the target function
f
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\lambda\in(0,1]
and a nonempty subset
u\subseteq 1{:}s
, define

\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}=\sup_{v\subseteq u}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\alpha}_{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{|u|}\\
\alpha_{j}=\alpha\ \forall j\in v\\
\alpha_{j}>0\ \forall j\in u\setminus v\end{subarray}}V_{\lambda}^{v}(f_{u}^{(\bm{\alpha_{u}})}),

(2.9)

where
f_{u}
is the ANOVA component of
f
given by (
2.6
). When
\lambda=1
and the weak mixed derivative
f^{(\alpha+1,\dots,\alpha+1)}
exists and belongs to
L^{2}([0,1]^{s})
, equation (
2.8
) implies the bound

\|f\|_{u,\alpha,1}\leqslant\sup_{\bm{\alpha}_{u}\in\{1:(\alpha+1)\}^{|u|}}\|f_{u}^{(\bm{\alpha}_{u})}\|_{L^{2}}.

We further set
\|f\|_{\varnothing,\alpha,\lambda}=|\mu(f)|
and define the global variation norm

\|f\|_{s,\alpha,\lambda}=\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}.

For nonzero
f
,
\|f\|_{s,\alpha,\lambda}\neq 0
and we can define the
relative variation
of ANOVA component of
f_{u}
as

\gamma_{u}=\frac{\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}}{\|f\|_{s,\alpha,\lambda}},

(2.10)

which satisfies
\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\gamma_{u}=1
.

3
The median algorithm and its error

In this section, we introduce the median digital-net-based algorithm and construct the pre-selected index sets for Walsh coefficients used in our analysis. We then present the main result, Theorem
3.9
, which provides a probabilistic error bound. While the proof of this theorem relies on the error decomposition in (
3.18
), the complete technical derivation is deferred to Appendix
C
. Furthermore, we investigate the tractability properties of our median digital-net-based algorithm.

3.1
The universal median digital-net-based algorithm

We first present an idealized algorithm that utilizes complete random designs with precision
E=\infty
and assumes no rounding errors are present. This idealized algorithm (Algorithm
3.1
) is inspired by
[
26
, Algorithm 3.1]

.

where

is the modulus of continuity of

.

Proof.

The construction of the digital net points
\bm{x}_{i,r}
in Algorithm

and
\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E}

ensures that each entry

Now for any

\displaystyle|\widehat{f}_{N,r}(\bm{k})-\widehat{f}^{E,\epsilon}_{N,r}(\bm{k})|

\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon+\omega_{f}(\sqrt{s}2^{-E}).

(3.6)

We note that

3.2
Anisotropic index sets for theoretical analysis

In this subsection, we define anisotropic index sets used purely for theoretical analysis, followed by a discussion of their relevant properties.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we write the binary representation of

differently by

k=\sum_{j=1}^{v}2^{c_{j}-1},

\displaystyle|K_{m,\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta}|

3.3
Error analysis: the main result

By the orthonormality of the Walsh basis, the squared
L^{2}
error admits the following decomposition

\displaystyle\|A^{E,\epsilon}_{m}(f)-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{\bm{k}\in H^{E,\epsilon}_{N}}|\widehat{f}^{E,\epsilon}_{N,\mathrm{med}}(\bm{k})-\widehat{f}(\bm{k})|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\setminus H^{E,\epsilon}_{N}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k})|^{2}.

(3.18)

The first term is controlled by establishing probabilistic bounds on the coefficient estimation errors. For the second term, a decomposition analogous to
[
26
, Inequality (
\clubsuit
)]
ensures that significant Walsh coefficients are included in the index set
H^{E,\epsilon}_{N}
with high probability. These estimates lead to our main result, formulated in Theorem
3.9
below; the complete proof is provided in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\lambda\in(0,1]
,
\beta\geqslant 1
,
\delta\in(0,1/10)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
N=2^{m}
. For any
f
with
\|f\|_{s,\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
, let
\bm{\gamma}
denote its associated relative variations. Assume that the finite index set
K\supset K_{m,\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta}
and the precision
E
satisfies (
3.4
). Furthermore, let
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, and let
R
be an odd natural number chosen sufficiently large such that

\frac{|K|+N}{2}(8\delta(1-2\delta))^{R/2}+\frac{N}{2}(20\delta(1-5\delta))^{R/2}\leqslant\delta^{\prime}.

(3.19)

Then for any

\displaystyle\|A^{E,\epsilon}_{m}(f)-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leqslant C_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma}}(m)^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)}}{N^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+\theta-1}}\|f\|_{s,\alpha,\lambda}^{2}+8N\epsilon_{1}(\|f\|_{\infty}+\epsilon_{1}),

where the constant

is given by

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta}=

\displaystyle\frac{6-3\delta-\delta^{2}}{\delta(1-\delta)}\Upsilon_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta}

\displaystyle+\frac{(1-\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\delta^{2}}\max\left\{64\Upsilon_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta},\frac{1}{4}\Theta_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\eta}^{2}\right\},

with
\eta=(1-1/(2\alpha+2\lambda))\theta
, and
\epsilon_{1}
,
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma}}(m)
,
\Upsilon_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta}
and
\Theta_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\eta}
are defined in (
3.6
), (
3.9
), (
3.13
) and (
3.16
), respectively.

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Given a failure probability tolerance
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, the condition (
3.19
) is satisfied if

(|K|+N)(8\delta(1-2\delta))^{R/2}\leqslant\delta^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad N(20\delta(1-5\delta))^{R/2}\leqslant\delta^{\prime}.

A sufficient condition for these requirements to hold is

(3.20)

In particular, if
|K|
satisfies the growth bound

|K|\leqslant N(C\log N)^{s+1}

for some constant

3.4
Tractability analysis

In this subsection, we give a dimension-independent probabilistic error bound for the idealized algorithm (Algorithm
3.1
). This corresponds to Theorem
3.9
with
\epsilon_{1}=0
(i.e., no finite‑precision error). According to the theorem, this is achieved if
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma}}(m)
,
\Upsilon_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta}
, and
\Theta_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\eta}
are bounded independently of
s
. Such independence holds under the tractability condition
[
25
, condition (25)]
, which requires the existence of positive weights
\{\gamma_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}\}
satisfying

\gamma_{u}\leqslant\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}\quad\forall u\subseteq\mathbb{N}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\infty}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}<\infty.

(3.22)

Under this condition, by an argument analogous to
[
25
, Remark 3]
, it holds that

\Upsilon_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\theta}\leqslant\delta^{-2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)}\exp(C_{\infty}\beta^{2(\alpha+\lambda)}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta})

with
C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}
as defined in (
3.14
) and

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma}}(m)\leqslant C^{\prime}_{\rho}N^{\rho},\quad\forall\rho>0

with
C^{\prime}_{\rho}
independent of
s
. It remains to show that
\Theta_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\eta}
can be bounded independently of
s
. Since
\gamma_{u}\in[0,1],\beta\geqslant 1
, and
\eta\in(0,1-1/(2\alpha+2\lambda))
, according to (
3.16
), we have

\displaystyle\delta^{\eta(\alpha+\lambda)}\Theta_{\alpha,\lambda,\beta,\bm{\gamma},\delta,\eta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\subseteq 1:s}\gamma_{u}^{1-\frac{\eta(\alpha+\lambda)}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}\beta^{\eta(\alpha+\lambda)|u|}D_{\alpha,\lambda,\eta}^{|u|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}\beta^{(\alpha+\lambda-1/2)|u|}D_{\alpha,\lambda,\eta}^{|u|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{j=1}^{s}\left(1+\gamma_{j}^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}\beta^{\alpha+\lambda-1/2}D_{\alpha,\lambda,\eta}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(C_{\infty}\beta^{\alpha+\lambda-1/2}D_{\alpha,\lambda,\eta}\right),

where
D_{\alpha,\lambda,\eta}
is defined in (
3.17
). Therefore, under the tractability condition (
3.22
), the probabilistic
L^{2}
-convergence rate can be made arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{O}(N^{-(\alpha+\lambda)})
with an implied constant independent of
s
.

Finally, we translate the convergence rate with respect to
N
into the total number of evaluations
M=RN
. Under the growth assumption (
3.21
), we have
M=\mathcal{O}((\log N+s\log\log N)N)
for a fixed failure probability by the discussion in Remark
3.10
. Consequently, when condition (
3.22
) is satisfied, the convergence rate with respect to
M
becomes
\mathcal{O}((s\log M/M)^{\alpha+\lambda-\eta})
, where the implied constant is independent of
s
and
\eta>0
is an arbitrary positive number.

4
Implementation of median digital-net-based
L^{2}
-approximation

In this section, we discuss the implementation details of Algorithm

. We first propose two distinct strategies for constructing the universal index set

. Subsequently, we address the computational efficiency of estimating Walsh coefficients through the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform (FWHT). In particular, we develop a specialized acceleration strategy based on Gray code ordering, specifically tailored for index sets designed to cover all possible smoothness parameters and weights.

4.1
Construction of the universal index set

We present two approaches for constructing the universal index set

required by Algorithm
3.2

4.1.1
Universal index set for weights when smoothness parameters are known

We first consider the case where the smoothness parameters
\alpha

\lambda
are given, aiming to construct a universal index set
K
that is independent of the weights
\bm{\gamma}
.

For

4.2
Fast Estimation of Walsh Coefficients

In this subsection, we focus on accelerating the estimation of the Walsh coefficients (
3.3
) in Algorithm

by leveraging the structure of digital nets and the FWHT. To facilitate the analysis, we ignore the effects of rounding error in (

\widehat{f}^{E}_{N,r}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}f(\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E})\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E}),\quad\bm{k}\in K,

(4.7)

where
\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E}
are the digital net points constructed in (

For each fixed

would require

operations. However, due to the structure of digital nets and the aliasing phenomenon, these estimators actually take at most

distinct values. To illustrate this, we define the digit truncation operator

\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k)=(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{E})^{\top}\quad\text{for}\quad k=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}k_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}.

(4.8)

From the construction (
3.2
), we have

\operatorname{wal}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\top}\vec{x}^{E}_{ij,r}}=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k_{j})^{\top}C^{E}_{j,r}\vec{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k_{j})^{\top}\vec{D}^{E}_{j,r}}.

Consequently,

\widehat{f}^{E}_{N,r}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N}(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k_{j})^{\top}\vec{D}^{E}_{j,r}}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}f(\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E})(-1)^{(\sum_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k_{j})^{\top}C^{E}_{j,r})\vec{i}},

which means that
\widehat{f}^{E}_{N,r}(\bm{k})
is determined by the values of
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k_{j})^{\top}C^{E}_{j,r}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{tr}_{E}(k_{j})^{\top}\vec{D}^{E}_{j,r}
. Since these terms belong to
\{0,1\}^{1\times m}
and
\{0,1\}
respectively under modulo 2 arithmetic, we have

\{\widehat{f}^{E}_{N,r}(\bm{k}):\bm{k}\in K\}\subseteq\left\{\pm\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}f(\bm{x}_{i,r}^{E})(-1)^{\vec{\ell}^{\top}\vec{i}}:\ell=0,1,\ldots,N-1\right\}=:B.

(4.9)

Notably, the set

has cardinality at most

1
, which accelerates the computation of Walsh coefficient estimators.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a universal median digital-net-based algorithm for the
L^{2}
-approximation of non-periodic smooth functions on
[0,1]^{s}
. This method operates independently of weight parameters.

For a general class of input candidate index sets
K
, we establish the algorithm’s convergence rate. Specifically, assuming that
f
and its ANOVA components possess dominating mixed partial derivatives up to order
\alpha
, and that these derivatives (along with the components themselves) have finite partial fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
, the algorithm achieves a probabilistic
L^{2}
-error of
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha-\lambda+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
, where
M
denotes the total number of function evaluations. Furthermore, under suitable decay of the ANOVA weights, this bound becomes
\mathcal{O}((s\log M/M)^{\alpha+\lambda-\epsilon})
with the implied constant independent of
s
.

Regarding implementation, we propose two constructions of
K
: one tailored to known parameters
\alpha,\lambda
and another that remains universal across all parameters. To ensure computational efficiency, the FWHT is employed to accelerate the estimation of Walsh coefficients. Additionally, we develop an optimized sorting strategy that further enhances performance, particularly for the more extensive universal construction.

Some challenges remain to be addressed in future research. First, as discussed at the end of Section
5.2
, a potential refinement is to construct more parsimonious index sets
K
that capture the most significant coefficients. Such a reduction in cardinality would not only accelerate the algorithm but also mitigate the selection noise encountered during coefficient estimation. Second, while the target function is assumed to be continuous, our current algorithm produces piecewise constant approximations. Extending this framework to generate smoother approximations is therefore another important direction for future investigation.

v:=\{j:|\kappa_{j}|=\alpha+1\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\in B(\bm{k}_{u},v)}|\widehat{f}_{u}(\bm{k}^{\prime})|-\sum_{v\subseteq u}\sum_{\bm{k}_{u}\in\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T)}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\in B(\bm{k}_{u},v)}|\widehat{f}_{u}(\bm{k}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{|u|}\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}\sum_{\bm{k}_{u}\in\mathbb{N}_{\alpha}^{u}}\bm{1}\left\{\bm{k}_{u}\notin\bigcup_{v\subseteq u}\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T)\right\}\prod_{j\in u}2^{(\frac{3}{2}-\lambda)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}2^{-\ell-1}.

(B.2)

Because
\bm{k}_{u}\notin\bigcup_{v\subseteq u}\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T)
implies

(\lambda-1)\sum_{j\in u}\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}+\sum_{j\in u}\|\kappa_{j}\|>T,

we have for any
\eta\in(0,1-1/(2\alpha+2\lambda))
and
\rho_{\eta}=2^{\eta-1}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}_{u}\in\mathbb{N}_{\alpha}^{u}}\bm{1}\left\{\bm{k}_{u}\notin\bigcup_{v\subseteq u}\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T)\right\}\prod_{j\in u}2^{(\frac{3}{2}-\lambda)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}2^{-\ell-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\bm{k}_{u}\in\mathbb{N}_{\alpha}^{u}}2^{-\eta T+\eta(\lambda-1)\sum_{j\in u}\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}+\eta\sum_{j\in u}\|\kappa_{j}\|}\prod_{j\in u}2^{(\frac{3}{2}-\lambda)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}2^{-\ell-1}

\displaystyle=2^{-\eta T}\left(\sum_{\bm{k}_{u}\in\mathbb{N}_{\alpha}^{u}}\prod_{j\in u}2^{(\eta-1)(\lambda-1)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}+\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}/2}\prod_{l\in\kappa_{j}}2^{(\eta-1)\ell-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{-\eta T}\left(\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=0}^{\alpha}2^{-\alpha^{\prime}}\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}\rho_{\eta}^{\ell}+2^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha+1}}\rho_{\eta}^{\left(\lambda-1+1/(2\eta-2)\right)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}\rho_{\eta}^{\ell}\right)^{|u|},

where
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha^{\prime}}:=\{\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}:|\kappa|=\alpha^{\prime}\}
. According to
[
25
, equation (20)]
, we have

\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}\rho_{\eta}^{\ell}\leqslant\frac{1}{(\alpha^{\prime})!(\rho_{\eta}^{-1}-1)^{\alpha^{\prime}}}.

For
\kappa\in S_{\alpha+1}
, we let
\kappa^{+}=\lceil\kappa\rceil_{1:\alpha}
and
\ell^{\prime}=\lceil\kappa\rceil_{\alpha+1}
. By the one-to-one correspondence between
\kappa^{+}
and
\kappa^{+}-\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha+1}}\rho_{\eta}^{\left(\lambda-1+1/(2\eta-2)\right)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}\rho_{\eta}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}\rho_{\eta}^{(\alpha+\lambda+1/(2\eta-2))\ell^{\prime}}\sum_{\kappa^{+}-\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa^{+}-\ell^{\prime}}\rho_{\eta}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}\rho_{\eta}^{(\alpha+\lambda+1/(2\eta-2))\ell^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\alpha!(\rho_{\eta}^{-1}-1)^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha!(\rho_{\eta}^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/(2\eta-2)}-1)(\rho_{\eta}^{-1}-1)^{\alpha}}.

Our conclusion follows once we put the above bounds into inequality (
B.2
).
∎

Before delving into the analysis of each term, we recall
[

, which characterizes how the median-of-means technique suppresses the failure probability of obtaining inaccurate estimates.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
R
be an odd natural number, and let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{R}
be
R
independent and identically
distributed real random variables. Suppose that for some interval
I\subseteq R
and
p\in(0,1/2)
we have

\operatorname{Pr}\left(X_{1}\notin I\right)\leqslant p.

Then

\operatorname{Pr}\left(\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}X_{r}\notin I\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left(4p(1-p)\right)^{R/2}.

The next lemma shows that the median does not increase the error, which will be utilized in our analysis of
\widehat{f}^{E,\epsilon}_{N,\mathrm{med}}
and
H^{E,\epsilon}_{N}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
R
be an odd natural number, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{R}
be
R
real numbers. Let
y_{1},\ldots,y_{R}
be
R
real numbers satisfying
|x_{r}-y_{r}|\leqslant\epsilon
for all
1\leqslant r\leqslant R
. Then

|\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}x_{r}-\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}y_{r}|\leqslant\epsilon.

Proof.

Denote
z_{r}=x_{r}+\epsilon
for
1\leqslant r\leqslant R
. Then
y_{r}\leqslant z_{r}
. So

\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}y_{r}\leqslant\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}z_{r}=\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}x_{r}+\epsilon.

Similarly, we have

\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}y_{r}\geqslant\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}x_{r}-\epsilon.

Hence,

|\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}x_{r}-\underset{r\in 1:R}{\operatorname{med}}y_{r}|\leqslant\epsilon.\qed

Lemma C.3
.

For

\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}:=\left\{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}:\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{\ell}_{j}^{\top}C_{jr}=\bm{0}\mod 2\right\}.

Lemma C.4
.

For
\bm{k}\in K

\operatorname{Pr}\left(|\widehat{f}_{N,\mathrm{med}}(\bm{k})-\widehat{f}(\bm{k})|^{2}>\frac{\Lambda_{2}}{\delta N}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(8\delta(1-2\delta))^{R/2},

Lemma C.7

For any

\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{D}_{r}}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,r}(\bm{k})|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell})|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{D}_{r}}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,r}(\bm{k})|^{4}\right]

Lemma C.8
.

For any
r\in 1:R
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
, define

\displaystyle t(\bm{k}):=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell})|,\quad S(\bm{k}):=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell})|^{2}.

Then we have

\sigma^{2}(\bm{k}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{D}_{r}}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,r}(\bm{k})|^{4}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{D}_{r}}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,r}(\bm{k})|^{2}\right]\right)^{2}\leqslant 8|\widehat{f}(\bm{k})|^{2}S(\bm{k})+2t(\bm{k})^{2}S(\bm{k}).

Proof.

For
\bm{\ell}=\bm{0}
, we have
\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})=\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})|^{2}
. Hence, by Lemma
C.7

\sigma^{2}(\bm{k})=\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}}\left|\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\right|^{2}.

For any
\bm{\ell}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|2\widehat{f}(\bm{k})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell})+\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{\ell}\}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 8|\widehat{f}(\bm{k})|^{2}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell})|^{2}+2\left|\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{\ell}\}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\right|^{2}.

A further application of Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the second term yields:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{\ell}\}}\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{\ell}\}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})|\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{\ell}\}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})||\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant t(\bm{k})\left(\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\bm{C}_{r}^{\perp}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{\ell}\}}|\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})||\widehat{f}(\bm{k}\oplus\bm{\ell}\oplus\bm{\ell}^{\prime})|^{2}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\bm{k})
