Efficacy and Tolerability of Tegoprazan Compared to Proton Pump Inhibitors in the Treatment of Erosive Esophagitis: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Youssef Diab, Charbel Maalouf, Anthony Junior Al Hakim, Perla Chami, Ali Ghais, Etwal Bou Raad, Louis Chaptini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTegoprazan, a potassium‐competitive acid blocker, is an acid‐suppressing medication under review as a potential alternative treatment in cases of erosive esophagitis. This systematic review and meta‐analysis assesses the efficacy and tolerability of tegoprazan compared to proton pump inhibitors, the current standard of care.MethodsPubmed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and Medline Ovid were systematically searched for articles up to November 25, 2025. Inclusion criteria selected RCTs that included patients with erosive esophagitis and studies of safety and efficacy, while exclusion criteria were defined by nonhuman studies, healthy patient populations, Helicobacter pylori ‐positive populations, and non‐RCT publication types. This review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024602209).ResultsOut of 724 screened studies, 5 RCTs were selected for this review, with a total of 1386 patients. Tegoprazan efficacy outcomes were consistent with non‐inferiority when compared to PPIs in the healing of erosive esophagitis (risk ratio: 1.03, 95% confidence interval: 1.00–1.07), as confirmed endoscopically. Comparable tolerability was also demonstrated (rate ratio: 0.97, 95% confidence interval: 0.68–1.37), with a similar distribution of treatment‐emergent adverse events across treatment groups.ConclusionsTegoprazan was non‐inferior to PPIs in both efficacy and tolerability, in accordance with the definition of non‐inferiority set by the RCTs. This supports its potential use as an alternative treatment in patients with erosive esophagitis.

Introduction and Background

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a gastrointestinal condition that consists of symptoms and/or complications resulting from the backflow of gastric contents into the esophagus. The pathophysiology of GERD includes dysfunction of the anti‐reflux barrier of the gastroesophageal junction, excessive transient lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxations, and impairments in esophageal peristalsis and mucosal defense [1]. GERD can be classified as erosive or nonerosive disease based on the presence or absence of mucosal breaks detected in the esophagus on endoscopy [2].

Heartburn and regurgitation are typical symptoms of GERD, whereas atypical symptoms include pharyngitis, laryngitis, chronic bronchitis, globus sensation, and noncardiac chest pain [3]. In the absence of treatment, GERD can lead to serious complications, such as Barrett's esophagus, esophageal strictures, and an increased risk of developing esophageal adenocarcinoma [4]. The rapid relief of symptoms and the prevention of complications are the main goals of the treatment [5]. Currently, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) remain the drugs of choice in the treatment of GERD [6]. These drugs need to be activated before irreversibly binding and inhibiting the H+/K+ ATPase found on the parietal cells of the stomach, which are responsible for the secretion of gastric acid [7]. However, the short half‐life of PPIs and their need to undergo acidic conversion into their active form diminish their efficacy in acid suppression, particularly during the night [8].

Tegoprazan is a novel potassium‐competitive acid blocker (P‐CAB) that reversibly and competitively binds to the potassium‐binding site of the H+/K+ ATPase. Unlike PPIs, this class of drugs does not require activation and exhibits a more rapid onset of action and longer‐lasting increase in intra‐gastric pH [9]. Tegoprazan has been approved for the treatment of GERD in South Korea and erosive esophagitis (EE) in China [10].

This systematic review aims to assess the efficacy and safety of tegoprazan in comparison with PPIs for the treatment of patients with EE.

Methods

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was carried out after registration with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) and according to protocol (ID: CRD42024602209). It followed the guidelines stated in the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) checklist. This study does not require ethical board approval because data were analyzed from previously published studies, which did not include individual patient data.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in English across six databases: Embase, PubMed, Medline, Cochrane, Web of Science, and Scopus. An extensive search strategy was formulated, including keywords and MeSH terms such as “gastroesophageal‐reflux,” “esophagitis,” “potassium‐competitive acid blocker,” “proton‐pump inhibitors,” and their associated synonyms and related keywords, as identified by MeSH and Emtree terms. The full search strategy is attached in Appendix A.

The search included all the papers published in the databases up to the November 25, 2025. Relevant clinical trials were manually screened to identify additional sources of data. Only RCTs were included. Two reviewers independently screened the title and abstract of identified citations for potential eligibility on Rayyan, which is a standardized and pilot‐tested form to independently screen articles for eligibility. Double blinding was done to reduce bias between the two reviewers. We acquired the full texts of citations judged as potentially eligible by at least one of the two reviewers. Disagreement over eligibility was resolved by discussion or consulting a third party. This process was carried out on the Rayyan platform.

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria included all randomized controlled trials (RCTs), studies comparing safety and efficacy between tegoprazan and PPIs in patients with EE. Only studies comparing tegoprazan monotherapy to PPIs were included.

The exclusion criteria included nonhuman studies, healthy patient population, non‐GERD‐associated diseases, Helicobacter pylori populations, trials with no fully published results, review articles, abstracts, case reports, and expert opinion papers. All included trials administered tegoprazan or the comparator PPI as a monotherapy with no concomitant interactive drugs. The articles were downloaded on Rayyan and initially screened by two reviewers through the title and abstract. The selected articles then underwent full‐text screening. The final selection, as well as any outstanding conflicts, was resolved by the principal investigator.

The efficacy of tegoprazan compared to PPIs was assessed by measuring the endoscopic healing rate at end‐point. Studies that explained and supported their method of measurement adequately were included. The safety profile was assessed based on the incident rate of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) in the patient population.

Data Extraction and Outcomes

Data was extracted by a reviewer on a data extraction form, noting down the study design, clinical trial registration ID, year of publication, population details, interventions, results, adverse effects, as well as extra key findings. Another independent reviewer revised the extracted data to ensure accuracy of reporting. The table was then used to synthesize the results section.

The primary outcome for this review was to assess the non‐inferiority of tegoprazan versus PPIs in EE. All five included trials were non‐inferiority trials that prespecified an identical non‐inferiority margin of 10% on the absolute risk‐difference scale. Non‐inferiority was declared when the lower bound of the two‐sided 95% confidence interval of the treatment difference exceeded −10%. This systematic review and meta‐analysis was not designed to test a prespecified pooled Δ. The secondary outcome was the occurrence of adverse effects. The primary outcome was assessed endoscopically at the end of each study's defined treatment duration, ranging from 4 to 24 weeks, as noted in Table 1.

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB‐2) tool, which evaluates five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain and the overall judgment were rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed via RStudio, using the meta package. Treatment effects were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) for the efficacy outcome, and as rate ratios for the safety outcome, each with 95% confidence intervals. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random‐effects model. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic and results were displayed graphically using forest plots. A two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the three‐arm trial by Lee et al., the shared esomeprazole comparator arm was split into two equal groups of approximately half the sample size to conduct the meta analysis, preserving the variance structure. Both events and totals were halved, so that each tegoprazan dose (50 and 100 mg) was compared against an independent share of the comparator, avoiding double counting. This approach is recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for multi‐arm trials to prevent artificial narrowing of the pooled confidence interval.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by treatment duration and by comparator to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The robustness of the pooled estimate was assessed using a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis, in which the meta‐analysis was repeated with each study omitted in turn. Meta‐regression was not performed, as the small number of included trials provides insufficient power for this method, which is not recommended when fewer than approximately 10 studies are available per covariate.

Results

Participants and Study Designs

The PRISMA flowchart in Figure 1 shows the research selection strategy that was adopted. A total of 1708 studies were identified through a comprehensive search of six databases. Of these, 984 records were excluded due to duplication. At the next stage, 724 records were screened based on their title and abstract only, ultimately extracting 25 studies for full‐text screening. The reviewers were able to exclude 20 studies for various reasons such as wrong publication type and inadequate inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, five studies were included in this systematic review, all involving patients diagnosed with GERD and specifically EE. No studies enrolled healthy volunteers, and all studies were designed as parallel, double‐blind, RCTs. A table of the excluded studies can be found in Table S1.

Risk of Bias

The Cochrane RoB‐2 tool was used to assess the risk of bias across five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from the intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result (Figure 2). While the RCTs by Cho et al., Zhu et al., Lee et al., and Agarwal et al. were judged to have a low risk of bias across all five domains, Min Shin et al. raised some concerns for selection of the reported result. This is a result of trial registration after enrolment commenced, reporting of a superiority comparison within a non‐inferiority design, and incomplete reporting of TEAE counts [12].

Participant Demographics

A total of 1386 participants were enrolled across the five studies, all of which included adult patients over 18 years old, with age inclusion criteria. Gender distribution included predominantly male patients: Cho et al. [10] (74% males), Zhu et al. [13] (73% males), Min Shin et al. [14] (63% males), Lee et al. [15] (60% males), and Agarwal et al. [16] (54% males). All studies reported outcomes that were directly relevant to the systematic review's interest, with all five studies looking into endoscopic healing. Moreover, all five studies focused on non‐inferiority comparisons, clearly assessing whether tegoprazan was non‐inferior to PPIs.

Interventions

All studies compared tegoprazan with FDA‐approved PPIs. Tegoprazan doses ranged from 25 to 100 mg, while comparators included 15 or 30 mg of lansoprazole and 40 mg of esomeprazole. Treatment durations varied, with three studies using 8 weeks and two studies using 4 and 24 weeks.

Efficacy Outcomes

Tegoprazan's efficacy outcomes were consistent with the non‐inferiority margins in the endoscopically confirmed healing of EE compared to PPIs, as seen in Table 2. The overall meta‐analysis (Figure 3) yielded a pooled RR of 1.03 (95% CI 1.00–1.07) under the random effects model, with no significant heterogeneity across studies (I 2 = 0%, τ 2 = 0; p = 0.5179), indicating similar healing outcomes of tegoprazan compared to PPIs. In the 8‐week subgroup, which comprised the greatest number of included studies, the pooled RR was 1.02 (95% CI 0.98–1.06), and with no observed heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%, τ 2 = 0; p = 0.6560). A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of this estimate. The pooled RR remained between 1.02 and 1.04, with the confidence interval consistently bounded at or near 1.00 and heterogeneity remaining negligible (I 2 ≤ 2%), regardless of which study was omitted (Figure 4). These findings indicate a consistent healing outcome with tegoprazan compared to PPIs across the included trials. The only trial that directly compared tegoprazan doses was that of Lee et al., where the cumulative 8‐week healing rates for tegoprazan 50 mg and 100 mg were 96% and 96.1%, respectively, and both were consistent with non‐inferiority to esomeprazole 40 mg, which was 92.9%. This indicates no additional healing benefit from the higher tegoprazan dose.

In the 24‐week trial by Cho et al., endoscopic remission rates in the tegoprazan 25 mg group did not differ significantly across severity grades (LA Grade A = 85.2% [75/88]; B = 89.7% [52/58]; C/D = 75.0% [6/8]; p = 0.4707), whereas in the lansoprazole 15 mg group, the rate decreased significantly with increasing severity (A = 91.9% [79/86]; B = 76.4% [42/55]; C/D = 60.0% [6/10]; p = 0.0048). In the 4‐week trial by Min Shin et al., healing rates in the tegoprazan 50 mg group were 96.7%, 93.9%, and 88.9% for LA grades A, B, and C/D, respectively, compared with 89.1%, 85.7%, and 70.0% in the lansoprazole 30 mg group. Within the LA grade C/D subgroup, healing was 88.9% versus 70.0% up to week 4 (88.9% vs. 60.0% at Week 2), with no statistically significant difference between groups (p > 0.05).

Zhu et al. stratified patients by severity (LA Grade A vs. B/C/D) and reported no significant differences between groups in the cumulative 8‐week healing within each stratum. Lee et al. observed a 100% healing rate with tegoprazan among patients with LA Grade C/D disease, but it should be noted that the arm size was small (50 mg, n = 4; 100 mg, n = 5). No significant differences were found in healing by baseline LA grade between tegoprazan and esomeprazole at Weeks 4 and 8. Agarwal et al. reported the baseline LA grade distribution (LA Grade C/D, approximately 5.5%) but did not present a severity‐stratified analysis of the efficacy.

Owing to the small number of LA Grade C/D patients, the differing endpoints across trials, and the different dosing, these data were summarized narratively rather than pooled.

Safety Outcomes

Safety was measured across all five included studies using clinical assessment of physical exam, vital signs, ECGs, and laboratory tests. Various questionnaires were also used to assess patients' health outcomes. Four studies (Cho et al., Zhu et al., Lee et al., and Agarwal et al.) documented the specific distribution of adverse events experienced by the patients.

TEAEs were recorded for all five studies; however, Min Shin et al. failed to report the number of patients experiencing the adverse events and was therefore not included in the quantitative assessment. For the remaining four studies, TEAEs were experienced by 182/600 (30.3%) patients in the tegoprazan group, and by 151/494 (30.5%) patients in the PPI comparator groups. The most common adverse event experienced, as reported in Figure 5, was gastrointestinal disturbances. The meta‐analysis in Figure 6 yielded a random effects rate ratio of 0.97 (95% CI 0.68–1.37), showing no significant differences in adverse events profiles between tegoprazan and PPIs. Moderate heterogeneity was observed across studies (I 2 = 53.5%, τ 2 = 0.0470).

The gastroesophageal reflux disease health‐related quality of life (GERD‐HRQL) questionnaire was used in four of the included studies (Cho et al., Zhu et al., Lee et al., and Agarwal et al.) to assess heartburn symptoms, dysphagia, effects of medications, and overall health condition related to esophageal acid reflux. Across the four studies, no significant difference was found in GERD‐HRQL scores between interventions. Additionally, the Reflux Disease Questionnaire (RDQ) was used by Zhu et al., Min Shin et al., and Lee et al. to assess the frequency and severity of heartburn, acid regurgitation, and dyspepsia. The change in RDQ score was reported as non‐statistically significant by Zhu et al. and Min Shin et al., whereas Lee et al. detected statistical significance that was attributed to a difference in baseline score.

Three trials assessed for nighttime symptom burden using daily diaries. Zhu et al. reported no significant difference between tegoprazan and esomeprazole in the percentage of symptom‐free days during daytime or nighttime (p > 0.05). Min Shin et al. found that more patients experienced heartburn‐free days with tegoprazan compared to lansoprazole, despite no statistical significance being reached. Finally, Lee et al. found no significant difference in daytime and nighttime heartburn and regurgitation severity between tegoprazan and PPI groups.

Discussion

One of the most prevalent gastrointestinal diseases in the United States is GERD, with an incidence of 5 per 1000 person‐years in adults [17]. It is estimated to be present in 18%–28% of the population in North America, 9%–26% in Europe, and 9%–33.1% in the Middle East [18]. Although mortality from GERD is rare, it is associated with notable morbidity as a result of the bothersome symptoms, as well as a significant economic burden stemming from diagnostic procedures, clinic consultations, therapeutic interventions, and cancer screening protocols [19]. A notable subtype of GERD, EE, is determined endoscopically by observing acid‐induced mucosal damage. It is graded based on the Los Angeles (LA) Classification, with A/B for mild and C/D for severe [20]. The most commonly used treatment of GERD is PPIs, which are H+/K+‐ATPase pump blockers that prevent the buildup of hydrochloric acid (HCl) in the stomach. Recently, P‐CABs have emerged as alternative agents to overcome PPI shortcomings, offering fast onset, prolonged duration, and a more uniform response across different patient populations [21, 22]. This uniform response may be in part attributed to differences in hepatic cytochrome‐mediated metabolism. P‐CABs are metabolized by CYP3A, which shows less variability between individuals compared to the highly variable PPI metabolizer, CYP2C19 [23].

Provided with this background, our systematic review and meta‐analysis of five RCTs aimed to evaluate the comparative and non‐inferior efficacy and safety of tegoprazan versus PPIs in the management of EE. The primary outcomes across a total of 1386 patients from five RCTs showed that tegoprazan was consistent with the non‐inferiority margins when compared to PPIs in mucosal healing (pooled RR = 1.03; 95% CI = 1.00–1.07; I 2 = 0%), as confirmed by endoscopies. Efficacy was consistent across treatment durations, comparator PPIs, tegoprazan doses, and disease severity. As for safety outcomes, the random effects meta‐analysis also demonstrated a comparable rate of TEAEs (pooled rate ratio = 0.97; 95% CI = 0.68–1.37; I 2 = 53.5%). The following sections discuss the mechanistic basis for these findings and their clinical and methodological implications.

The rapid action of tegoprazan is documented in pharmacokinetic studies, with tegoprazan showing a consistent maximal efficacy from Day 1 of treatment, compared to omeprazole, which only reached maximal efficacy after 5 days [24]. This rapid effect may be attributed to tegoprazan's ability to bind to both active and inactive H+/K+‐ATPase, unlike PPIs which are dependent on meals and acid‐mediated conversion [25]. Min Shin et al. compared the treatment outcomes across individuals with different CYP2C19 genotypes (extensive metabolizers vs. intermediate/poor metabolizers) and demonstrated that tegoprazan resulted in greater healing rates, reaching statistical significance in the intermediate/poor metabolizers group. This offers a potential pharmacological advantage for tegoprazan over PPIs, which are known to be dependent on the highly polymorphic CYP2C19 enzyme [26].

Four of the five included trials were conducted in East Asian populations, in which CYP2C19 poor‐metabolizer phenotypes are more common. The trial by Agarwal et al. was the only one to enroll a multinational population (India, Russia, and South Africa). Studies showed that Korean and Chinese populations have a strong genetic disposition for a weak CYP2C19 metabolizer genotype [27, 28], thus, they exhibit an improved symptom response to PPIs [29]. Pan‐ethnic studies showed that on average, there is a significantly higher prevalence of extensive CYP2C19 metabolizer phenotypes in the United States, as well as European ethnicities [30, 31, 32]. Since the efficacy of PPIs, but not of tegoprazan, is influenced by CYP2C19 metabolism, the present findings may not be reproduced in a predominantly extensive‐metabolizer Western population. Adequately powered, multiethnic, multinational RCTs are required to investigate this relationship.

Tegoprazan's efficacy was preserved across the LA‐grade spectrum of disease severity. In Cho et al., tegoprazan 25 mg maintained endoscopic remission at similar rates across mild, moderate, and severe disease (p = 0.4707), whereas lansoprazole 15 mg showed significantly lower maintenance rates as baseline LA‐grade severity worsened (p = 0.0048). In the study by Min Shin et al., tegoprazan achieved higher healing than lansoprazole, including in severe LA grade C/D disease. This pattern is consistent with the rapid, sustained, and CYP2C19‐independent acid suppression described above, which may be especially beneficial in severe disease. However, given the small number of LA grade C/D patients, this potential advantage requires further dedicated study.

Although the pooled estimate indicates that tegoprazan was consistent with non‐inferiority in comparison to PPIs in mucosal healing, the clinical value of tegoprazan lies in providing an effective alternative with potential mechanistic advantages rather than in demonstrably greater symptom relief. Symptom control with tegoprazan was comparable to that of PPIs, as measured by questionnaires and daily‐diary entries, including daytime and nighttime symptoms. No trial showed a significant disadvantage; however, nocturnal symptom relief favored tegoprazan, despite statistical significance not being reached. The scarcity and heterogeneity of symptom outcomes did not permit a quantitative meta‐analysis of such results. As a result, comparative patient‐reported benefit cannot be concluded from these data and requires further trials.

The overall safety profile of tegoprazan across all RCTs was similar to that of PPIs, with no significant differences. While only four studies documented the specific adverse events, the analysis found that the most common TEAEs were gastrointestinal and laboratory‐related events. The missing information from the trial by Min Shin et al. could potentially bias the pooled estimate, despite that trial reporting no new safety signals. The moderate heterogeneity observed for TEAEs (I 2 = 53.5%) likely reflects differences in adverse‐event definitions across trials. These results are supported by early phase pharmacological RCTs conducted on a healthy cohort, where no serious adverse events were noted [33].

Beyond efficacy and short‐term tolerability, several factors should be considered when exploring the clinical adoption of tegoprazan. Tegoprazan was first approved in South Korea in 2018 and has since received marketing authorization in more than 20 countries, predominantly across Asia and Latin America. However, it is not yet approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European Medicines Agency, limiting its regulatory availability [34]. It should be noted that a New Drug Application was submitted to the FDA in January 2026 [35]. Generic PPIs are inexpensive and globally available, whereas tegoprazan is a newer and branded medication whose cost‐effectiveness relative to PPIs has not been formally established [36]. Furthermore, long‐term safety data for tegoprazan are limited. Like other P‐CABs, it produces a moderate rise in serum gastrin without evidence of neoplastic gastric mucosal change to date [36, 37]. These considerations should be weighed alongside the efficacy and tolerability findings of this review when positioning tegoprazan in clinical practice.

Some of the limitations faced in this systematic review were the limited number of RCTs that were selected after the screening phase, as a result of tegoprazan being a new drug. Moreover, not all the studies had the same comparator PPI and dosages of both tegoprazan and PPIs. These factors are critical in influencing the healing effects, as well as the adverse events analysis. Also, the studies used different time frames for outcome assessment, making direct comparisons between studies more challenging. For the same reasons, a dose‐stratified analysis was not feasible, as tegoprazan dose was confounded with the comparator dose and treatment duration. Dose effects could therefore be assessed only within the one trial that compared two tegoprazan doses against a common comparator. Moreover, the patient populations of the RCTs in this meta‐analysis are mostly of South Korean and Chinese backgrounds, limiting the generalizability of the results. The included trials also enrolled a predominantly male population. None reported efficacy stratified by sex, so a differential treatment effect between men and women could not be evaluated. Sex‐disaggregated reporting in future trials would help confirm the generalizability of these findings across sexes. Despite these clinical differences, no statistical heterogeneity was observed for the efficacy outcome (I 2 = 0%), and a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed that the pooled estimate was robust. Despite that, larger and more diverse trials are needed to evaluate and validate these outcomes.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrate that tegoprazan offers a safe, effective, and non‐inferior alternative to PPIs for the treatment of EE, as determined by the non‐inferiority margins set by the RCTs authors. In five RCTs, tegoprazan was found to offer similar endoscopically confirmed mucosal healing rates and tolerability profiles compared to PPIs. These findings support its potential use as a first‐line or substitute therapy in clinical practice. Future research should employ a large‐scale, multicenter, and ethnically diverse approach to validate the efficacy and safety findings across different populations. Additionally, more research should be conducted to evaluate its economic burden on the public health sector and its potential impact on other gastric‐acid related conditions.
