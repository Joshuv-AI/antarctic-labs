The flow of local quantum fluids: Conservation laws and vertex corrections from many-body linear-response theory with local self-energy
D. Valentinis

Abstract
In non-diffusive conduction regimes of strongly correlated quantum electron systems, electromagnetic perturbations simultaneously probe the electronic dynamics in time and space: the exchanged energy ˜ ​ h ​ ! \mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega excites retarded, i.e., frequency-dependent, many-body interactions, while the probing spatial modulation renders the response spatially nonlocal, i.e., dependent on the external wave vector 𝒒 \bm{q} . This work determines the exact nonlocal electrodynamic response of such dynamical quantum fluids under the assumptions of local, frequency-dependent interactions and charge/mass conservation. The latter is ensured by Bethe-Salpeter equations for renormalized interaction vertices, entering the Kubo formalism for two-particle correlation functions (e.g., for density, currents, momentum, stress). Within such framework, it is shown that vertex corrections generally vanish at q = 0 q=0 for single-particle dispersions with inversion symmetry and for bare interaction vertices that are odd with respect to specific point group transformations in momentum space, including inversion for vector vertices, and mirror reflections or two- or higher-fold rotations for tensor vertices. In addition, for quadratic dispersion vertex corrections identically vanish from the current-current correlation function, at any momentum 𝒒 \bm{q} and frequency ! \omega . The robustness of these criteria against further symmetry breaking, multiband effects, and additionally imposing momentum conservation, is discussed, with application to the Hall viscosity of Landau levels. Explicit expressions for generic nonlocal correlation functions are derived for Fermi liquids (with well-defined quasiparticle peaks) and non-Fermi liquids (devoid of quasiparticles), for arbitrary local self-energies.

A central challenge in the analysis of two-particle response functions
\chi_{\hat{\Theta}}(\bm{q},\Omega)
is the construction of a
conserving
theory, which respects fundamental conservation laws for quantities like electric charge, current, energy, and momentum flow. In the many-body setting, these continuity equations for densities and currents translate into constraints on vertex corrections for correlation functions, enforced by Ward-Takahashi identities
Ward 1950
;
Takahashi 1957
;
Schrieffer 1963
;
Vollhardt and Wölfle 1980
;
Nieh et al. 1998
;
Mahan 2000
;

;

;
Narozhny 2023

\Lambda^{R}
; see Sec.

\Sigma^{R}
and vertex corrections have to be consistently chosen, which poses a considerable hindrance to analytical progress, and in multiple cases becomes a formidable problem.
A systematic resolution of these difficulties is provided by deriving both self-energy and vertex corrections from a Luttinger-Ward functional, whenever available

;
Baym 1962
; see Sec.
II

Palle 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
. For these reasons, it is desirable to devise well defined criteria to discern the presence/absence and relative importance of vertex corrections for many-body theories, depending on the set of imposed conservation laws.
In the case of a local self-energy, common wisdom is that vertex corrections vanish from two-particle correlation functions at
q=0

Pakhira and McKenzie 2015
, as it has been explicitly shown for the DMFT conductivity
Khurana 1990
;
Pakhira and McKenzie 2015

;
Kotliar and Vollhardt 2004
; see Sec.
III

In this work, the exact nonlocal electrodynamic response of correlated-electron systems is addressed by assuming a framework of local, frequency-dependent, and charge-conserving interactions. This DMFT-like approximation allows for a rigorous derivation of the conditions under which vertex corrections – often the most computationally intractable part of many-body theory – can be precisely determined, or shown to vanish.
Clear selection rules emerge based on the parity of the electronic dispersion
\xi_{\bm{k}}
and on the bare interaction vertices
\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})
: at
q=0
, a symmetry point-group operator
{g}
should exist that leaves the dispersion invariant,
\epsilon_{{g}\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, and under which the bare vertex is odd,
\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}({g}\bm{k},0)=-\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)
.
Based on these criteria, vertex corrections are shown to be essential for density and bulk stress responses even at
q=0
and for any frequency
\omega
, while they identically vanish for electric, momentum, and thermal currents, and in general for any vector-like vertex that is odd under
\bm{k}\mapsto-\bm{k}
inversion.
Conversely, nonuniversal cases arise for rank-2, tensorial vertices with spatial indexes
\bm{\alpha}=\left\{\alpha,\beta\right\}
for
\alpha\neq\beta
, such as the ones pertaining to shear stresses: these are devoid of vertex corrections under specific symmetry conditions. Namely, the components
\alpha\beta
of shear-stress vertices are nonrenormalized (bare) if one of the following point-group operations exist: a mirror symmetry plane
{M}_{\alpha}
orthogonal to
\alpha
, a two-fold rotation
{C}_{\alpha}
about the axis
\alpha
, or an improper rotation
{S}_{n}
that involves a rotation plus a mirror plane perpendicular to
\alpha

{C}_{3}
,
{C}_{4}
,
{C}_{6}\ldots
suppress all off-diagonal components of shear-stress vertex corrections.

Most notably, for the case of a quadratic isotropic dispersion with conservation of charge but not momentum, a proof that vertex corrections to the current-current correlation function vanish at arbitrary momentum
\bm{q}
and frequency
\omega
is presented; this provides a rare exact result valid for both Fermi and non-Fermi liquids.

Bruus and Flensberg 2004
. This emphasizes that the
\bm{k}
-oddness/null angular average of the bare vertex
\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)
and the
\bm{k}
-even parity of the dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
are the key properties for vertex-correction cancellations.

IV
Vertex corrections from charge conservation at zero and finite momentum for RPA-like two-particle vertex

In the special case when the irreducible two-particle vertex depends only on the exchanged energy
i{}_{n}
, but not on the incoming and outgoing fermionic frequencies
\omega_{m}
,
\omega_{m}^{\prime}
, Eq. (
3
) specializes to the “Random Phase Approximation (RPA)-like” form

\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\equiv\Lambda(i{}_{n})\,\forall\left\{i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}\right\}.

(25)

The case (
25
) arises in approximation schemes where the Luttinger–Ward functional
\Phi[{G}]
is quadratic in the density operator
\hat{n}
, or equivalently, if the interaction is Gaussian in auxiliary bosonic fields. This condition includes: density-density interactions with a dispersionless bosonic mode
\phi_{i}
(e.g., an Einstein phonon or a local collective mode), where the interaction Hamiltonian is
\hat{H}=g\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i}\phi_{i}
, the corresponding fermionic action is
{S}_{i}=1/2\sum_{i}k_{B}T\sum_{i{}_{n}}\hat{n}_{i}(-i{}_{n})g^{2}D_{0}(i{}_{n})\hat{n}_{i}(i{}_{n})
, the self-energy is
\Sigma(i\omega_{n})=g^{2}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}{V}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{k}}G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})D_{0}(i\omega_{n}-i\omega_{m})
, and the two-particle vertex is the bare interaction
\Lambda(i{}_{n})\equiv g^{2}D_{0}(i{}_{n})
; dynamically screened Hubbard-interaction models
U(i{}_{n})
, where the two-particle vertex
\Lambda(i{}_{n})=U(i{}_{n})
; infinite-range density-density interactions, where the Hamiltonian is
\hat{H}=1/(2{N})\sum_{i,j}V(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{n}_{i}(\tau)\hat{n}_{j}(\tau^{\prime})
, and in the large-
{N}
limit the self-averaging interaction produces
\Lambda(i{}_{n})\equiv V(i{}_{n})
; dynamically screeed RPA interactions.

The special case (
25
) allows us not only to retrieve the general conditions for cancellation of vertex corrections of Sec.
III
, but also to obtain explicit forms of vertex corrections, whenever the latter do not vanish. In the following, we formally proceed at finite
\bm{q}
and comment on the
q=0
limit at the end of the derivations.

Under the assumption (
25
), the Bethe-Salpeter equation (
II
) admits a closed-form solution for the renormalized vertex, which is obtained after multiplying the ladder equation (
II
) by
G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})G(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})
and summing over momenta
\bm{k}
and frequencies
i{}_{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})+\Lambda(i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\frac{B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}}{1-\Lambda(i{}_{n})A_{G}},

(26)

where

\displaystyle A_{G}

\displaystyle=A_{G}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n}),

(27a)

\displaystyle B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q}),

(27b)

\displaystyle A_{\Gamma}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=A_{\Gamma}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n}).

(27c)

The correlation function (
1
) then comprises a “renormalized bubble” term stemming from self-energy effects, and a vertex-correction term with scalar but frequency-dependent two-particle vertex function:

\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta},(0)}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})+\delta\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n}),

(28a)

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta},(0)}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=-k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times G(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q}),

(28b)

\displaystyle\delta\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma}\Lambda(i{}_{n})\frac{B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}B_{0}^{\bm{\beta}}}{1-\Lambda(i{}_{n})A_{G}}.

(28c)

From Eqs. (
IV
) and (
27
), we see that the renormalized vertex for the observable
\hat{\Theta}
is proportional to
B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, which involves a sum over momenta
\bm{k}^{\prime}
of the particle-hole bubble multiplied by the
bare vertex

\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})
. Therefore, if the bare vertex is odd with respect to momentum inversion
\left\{\bm{k}^{\prime}\right\}\mapsto\left\{-\bm{k}^{\prime}\right\}
, or if the vertex yields a null sum upon angular average, then
B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is null at
q=0
due to the sum over
\bm{k}^{\prime}
, and vertex corrections vanish identically at zero external momentum,
q=0
, consistently with Sec.
III
. For the same reason,
B_{0}^{\bm{\alpha}}=0
implies that vertex corrections (
28c
) to the two-particle correlation function are also null,
\delta\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(0,i{}_{n})=0\,\forall i{}_{n}
, and the entirety of the linear response is given by the interacting particle-hole bubble
\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta},(0)}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(0,i{}_{n})
in accordance with Eq. (
28
). As derived more generally in Sec.
III
, correlation functions that enjoy absence of vertex corrections include the current-current, momentum-momentum, and heat-heat ones, while the ones pertaining to shear stresses have no vertex corrections under additional rotational/mirror symmetry assumptions, and density-density and bulk stress-stress correlation functions always include nonzero corrections (
28c
).

However, there is one situation in which vertex corrections vanish entirely in the DMFT limit, from both the
q=0
and finite-
\bm{q}
responses, whenever the bare vertex is
\bm{k}
-odd: this is the case of the current response for a quadratic isotropic dispersion, treated in the next section.

V
Absence of vertex corrections for current response with quadratic dispersion and generic two-particle vertex

If we now additionally assume quadratic dispersion, then vertex corrections vanish from response vertices that are linear in momentum
\bm{k}
,
\bm{\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta}}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})\propto\bm{k}
,
even at finite momentum
\bm{q}
for a local self-energy.
We will see that this property holds for the current response,
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{J}
, of a general quadratically dispersing (even if anisotropic) band, of the kind

\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=k_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}k_{i}A_{ij}k_{j},

(29)

where
\left\{k_{0},A_{ij}\right\}\in\mdmathbb{R}
. The bare vertex then reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}}{\partial k_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}\sum_{\beta}A_{\alpha\beta}\left(k_{\beta}+\frac{q_{\beta}}{2}\right).

(30)

Hence, the bare vertex (
V
) is a linear combination of terms which are proportional to momenta components
k_{\beta}
, i.e., current density
\bm{J}
is proportional to momentum density
\bm{\pi}
.
To prove the vanishing of current vertex corrections, we will first assume isotropy for clarity, and then generalize to the anisotropic case.

Explicitly, let us assume a special case of Eq. (
29
), namely electrons with quadratic isotropic dispersion:

\xi_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}k^{2}}{2m}-\mu,

(31)

where
m
is the band mass and
\mu
is the chemical potential. Working at finite
\bm{q}
with the local irreducible two-particle vertex (
3
), the vertex equation (
II
) reads
\Gamma_{\hat{J},\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=\Gamma_{\hat{J},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})+C(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
, where
we have finite-
\bm{q}
generalizations of Eqs. (
13
) and (
III
):

\displaystyle C^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times X^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}),

(32)

and

\displaystyle X^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}).

(33)

Again, if
X(\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
is identically null, then self-consistently
C(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
is also null, and vertex corrections would vanish at finite
\bm{q}
as well. This condition implies that the bare current vertex
\Gamma_{\hat{J},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})
is
\bm{k}
-odd even at finite
\bm{q}
, a property which is generally not obeyed by arbitrary dispersions
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
. In fact, combining Eqs. (
V
) and (
V
), we have

\displaystyle C^{\alpha}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{\hat{J},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})+k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times C^{\alpha}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n}).

(34)

However, for the quadratic dispersion (
31
) the current vertex is linear in momentum,
\Gamma_{\hat{J},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})=\mathord{\mathchar 126h}/m\left(k_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}/2\right)
. Then, a momentum shift
\bm{k}^{\prime}\mapsto\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2
in the first term of Eq. (
V
) produces

\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}F_{G}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}{m}k_{\alpha}^{\prime},

(35)

where

\displaystyle F_{G}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=G(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2,i\omega_{m})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2,i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\left[i\omega_{m}-\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}(k^{\prime})^{2}}{2m}-\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q^{2}}{8m}+\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{q}}{2m}+\mu-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\left[i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n}-\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}(k^{\prime})^{2}}{2m}-\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q^{2}}{8m}-\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{q}}{2m}+\mu\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\Sigma(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})\right]^{-1}.

(36)

Hence, the oddness of the first term in Eq. (
V
) is preserved even at finite
\bm{q}
, and this first term vanishes. The second term in Eq. (
V
) yields, similarly to Eqs. (
17
), a matrix equation:

C^{\alpha}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})[1-K(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})]=0,

(37a)

\displaystyle K(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n}).

(37b)

The condition
1-K(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=0
signals a genuine nonuniform density collective mode at external momentum
\bm{q}
and frequency
i{}_{n}
. Assuming to lie away from such a resonance, the only other solution is
C^{\alpha}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=0\,\forall\left\{\bm{q},\omega_{m},{}_{n}\right\}
.

Then, for the
current response
,
\hat{\Theta}=\hat{J}
and the isotropic dispersion (
31
), the current-current correlation function is devoid of vertex corrections in the DMFT limit, even at finite
\bm{q}
. The conclusion here reached is equivalently consistent with the charge-conservation Ward identity (
II
), which can be shown to be identically satisfied at any momentum
\bm{q}
and frequency
i{}_{n}
, using the bare current vertex and a self-energy renormalized density vertex. Explicitly, the charge-conserving choices for the density and current vertices read

\Gamma_{\hat{n}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=1-\frac{\Sigma(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})}{i{}_{n}},

(38a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\equiv\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}{m}\left(k_{\alpha}+\frac{q_{\alpha}}{2}\right).

(38b)

In turn, vertex corrections to the current-current correlation function
\chi_{\hat{J}\hat{J}}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})
, which enters into the conductivity tensor
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},\omega)
through the Kubo formula (
82
), vanish identically for a local self-energy and quadratic dispersion at any momentum. Practically, since the density response is affected by vertex corrections, while the current response is not, it is advantageous to derive the longitudinal conductivity
\sigma_{L}(\bm{q},\omega)
from the current response, rather than from the density response. The density susceptibility
\chi_{\hat{n}\hat{n}}(\bm{q},\omega)
can then be derived as well, exploiting the continuity equation
Conti and Vignale 1999
;
Dressel and Grüner 2002
;
Giuliani and Vignale 2005

\chi_{\hat{n}\hat{n}}^{R}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{n}{m}\frac{q^{2}}{i\omega}\sigma_{L}(\bm{q},\omega).

(39)

Eq. (
39
) can be straightforwardly verified in the case of an imaginary constant self-energy,
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\equiv-i\Gamma
with
\Gamma>0
, by independently computing
\chi_{\hat{n}\hat{n}}^{R}(\bm{q},\omega)
and
\chi_{\hat{J}\hat{J}}^{\alpha\alpha,R}(\bm{q},\omega)
in the longitudinal channel with
\bm{q}=q\hat{u}_{\alpha}

Chen and Su 1989
;
Kim et al. 2019
.

In principle, the above considerations made for the isotropic quadratic dispersion (
31
) can be generalized to dispersions for which derivatives of order higher than 2 vanish. The most general form of such quadratic dispersions is given by Eq. (
29
).
In particular, we need the quadratic term
k_{i}A_{ij}k_{j}
, even if the mass tensor is anisotropic, to simultaneously guarantee the
\bm{k}
-oddness of the bare current vertex (
15
) and the
\bm{k}
-evenness of
G(\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2,i\omega_{n})G(\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2,i\omega_{n})
, after the shift by
\bm{q}/2
as done in Eq. (
V
).

The vanishing current vertex corrections for the dispersion (
29
) at any momentum
\bm{q}
can be advantageous for current-response calculations in generic locally interacting models at low energies
\xi\rightarrow 0^{+}
. In this low-energy regime, if the dispersion
\xi_{\bm{k}}
can be approximated as quadratic like in Eq. (
29
), then current vertex corrections can be approximately neglected.

VI
Charge-conserving correlation functions and Kubo formulae at zero and finite momentum

The locality of the self-energy and of the irreducible two-particle vertex not only impact the correlation function (
1
) for the observable
\hat{\Theta}
, but also the associated linear-response function
\Theta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\omega)
if the latter follows from a Kubo formula
Kubo 1957
;
Kubo et al. 1957
;
Mahan 2000
;
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
:

\Theta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\omega)=\frac{i\alpha}{\omega+i0^{+}}\left[\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta},R(0)}(0,\omega)-\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta},R(0)}(0,0)\right],

(40)

where
\alpha
is a momentum- and frequency-independent constant. Examples of Eq. (
40
) include the optical conductivity tensor
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
for the current response
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{J}
, and the viscosity (or viscoelasticity) tensor
\eta_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega)
for the stress response
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{\textit{T}}
.

Within the DMFT assumptions of local self-energy and two-particle vertex, the correlation function for the observable
\hat{\Theta}
is decomposed into two parts, following Eqs. (
1
) and (
II
): we have

\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta},(0)}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})+\delta\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n}),

(41)

where the two parts pertain to the “renormalized bubble” with interacting Green’s functions, and to ladder vertex corrections, respectively:

\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta},(0)}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=-k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})G(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\beta}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q}),

(42)

\delta\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=-k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}C^{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})G(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n}).

(43)

In Eq. (
43
) we have made use of the definition (
V
) for the quantity
C^{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
. In the following, our perspective will be to first write explicit expressions for Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
) in terms of the self-energy
\Sigma(i\omega_{m})
, and proceed analytically as far as possible in two opposite regimes: the one with well-defined, sharp quasiparticle peaks (coherent, or “Fermi-liquid”) and the one with broad, featureless spectral functions (incoherent, or “non-Fermi liquid”).

We will utilize the spectral (Lehmann) representation of the renormalized (interacting) Green’s functions, given by Eq. (
80
) with the spectral function (
A
).

VI.1
Vertex correction to the correlation function at finite momentum

Let us first treat the vertex-correction part (
43
), where comparatively less analytical progress can be made without knowing the explicit expression for the two-particle vertex
\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
. Employing the spectral representation (
80
) in Eq. (
43
), and defining the
\hat{\Theta}
-vertex transport function
as

\Upsilon_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},\bm{q})=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})\delta(\xi-\xi_{\bm{k}})\delta(\xi^{\prime}-\xi_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}),

(44)

Eq. (
43
) is recast into

\displaystyle\delta\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\times k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\frac{C^{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})}{(i\omega_{m}-\epsilon_{1})(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n}-\epsilon_{2})}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi^{\prime}\Upsilon_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},\bm{q})

\displaystyle\times A_{G}(\xi,\epsilon_{1})A_{G}(\xi^{\prime},\epsilon_{2}).

(45)

VI.2
Renormalized-bubble correlation function at finite momentum

The “renormalized-bubble” part (
42
) can be brought into more compact and explicit form. This form is the only part that survives at finite
\bm{q}
for a quadratic dispersion, as previously shown in Sec.
V
, and at
q=0
for a
\bm{k}
-even dispersion and a
\bm{k}
-odd bare vertex, as demonstrated in Sec.
III
.

To arrive at the result, we use the spectral representation (
80
) in Eq. (
42
), we define the
\hat{\Theta}
-transport function
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\beta}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})\delta(\xi-\xi_{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle\times\delta(\xi^{\prime}-\xi_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}),

(46)

and we perform the Matsubara sum

\displaystyle k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\frac{1}{(i\omega_{m}-\epsilon_{1})(i\omega_{m}-\epsilon_{2})}=\frac{f_{FD}(\epsilon_{1})-f_{FD}(\epsilon_{2})}{\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}},

(47)

with the invariance property
f_{FD}(\epsilon)=f_{FD}(\epsilon+i{}_{n})
of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function with respect of shifts by a bosonic Matsubara frequency
{}_{n}=2\pi nk_{B}T
. The final outcome transforms Eq. (
42
) into

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta},(0)}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi^{\prime}\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},\bm{q})

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{2}A_{G}(\xi,\epsilon_{1})

\displaystyle\times A_{G}(\xi^{\prime},\epsilon_{2})\frac{f_{FD}(\epsilon_{1})-f_{FD}(\epsilon_{2})}{i{}_{n}+\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}}.

(48)

One can further simplify Eq. (
VI.2
) for the interesting case of the quadratic dispersion (
31
), especially in the small-momentum regime where we can linearize
\xi_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\approx\xi_{\bm{k}}+\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{q}
. This analysis is performed in the companion paper
Valentinis 2026b
, and applied to different types of local self-energies.
Here we stay at the general level of unspecified
\Sigma(i\omega_{n})
, and in the following specialize Eqs. (
VI.2
) and (
VI.2
) to
q=0
.

VI.3
Renormalized-bubble correlation function at zero momentum

At
q=0
, where according to Sec.
III
vertex corrections cancel for any dispersion with inversion center and
\bm{k}
-odd bare vertex, Eqs. (
VI.2
) and (
VI.2
) specialize to the zero-momentum transport function

\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\beta}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)\delta(\xi-\xi_{\bm{k}}),

(49)

and the zero-momentum correlation function

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta},(0)}(0,i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\xi)

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{2}A_{G}(\xi,\epsilon_{1})A_{G}(\xi,\epsilon_{2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{f_{FD}(\epsilon_{1})-f_{FD}(\epsilon_{2})}{i{}_{n}+\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}}.

(50)

We now have two opposite regimes in which analytical progress is possible. The first limit is the one in which the transport function (
49
) is sharply peaked around
\xi=0
in comparison to the broader spectral function
A_{G}(\xi,\epsilon)
: this is the regime of no sharply defined quasiparticle, “non-Fermi liquid”, or incoherent; the second limit is the one in which we neglect all energy dependences of the transport function (
49
) in comparison to the sharper spectral function
A_{G}(\xi,\epsilon)
: this is the regime of well-defined quasiparticle, “Fermi liquid”, or coherent. In the following we separately analyze these two limits, while in the crossovers the more general expression (
VI.3
) applies.

VI.3.1
Coherent regime at zero momentum: Allen formula for the linear-response function

Let us approximate

\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\xi)\approx\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(0)\,\forall\xi,

(51)

which places our system in the coherent, or “Fermi liquid”, regime. Starting from Eq. (
VI.3
) for the correlation function, the derivation reported in App.
E.1
leads us to the following result for the linear-response Kubo formula given by Eq. (
40
):

\displaystyle\Theta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\omega)=\frac{i\alpha\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(0)}{\omega+i0^{+}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon\frac{f_{FD}(\epsilon)-f_{FD}(\epsilon+\omega)}{\omega+i0^{+}+\left[\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon)\right]^{\ast}-\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon+\omega)},

(52)

where
∗
means complex conjugation. Eq. (
VI.3.1
) is the most general expression for the Kubo formula at
q=0
in coherent regime, when vertex corrections (
VI.1
) vanish, which requires inversion symmetry of the dispersion and oddness of the bare vertex. It is a kind of generalized “Allen formula”
Berthod et al. 2013
;
Allen 2015
.

VI.3.2
Incoherent regime at zero momentum

When the transport function (
49
) is strongly peaked in comparison to the spectral functions, we can approximate

\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\xi)\approx\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(0)\delta(\xi),

(53)

which implies that the system lies in the incoherent, or “non-Fermi liquid”, regime. In this case, the derivation sketched in App.
E.2
brings us to another explicit expression for the linear-response Kubo formula (
40
), namely

\displaystyle\Theta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha\Phi_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}(0)}{2\pi(\omega+i0^{+})}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon_{1}\left[f_{FD}(\epsilon_{1})-f_{FD}(\epsilon_{1}+\omega)\right]

\displaystyle\times\left\{\frac{1}{\left[\epsilon-\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon)\right]\left\{\epsilon+\omega-\left[\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon+\omega)\right]^{\ast}\right\}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{\left[\epsilon-\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon)\right]\left[\epsilon+\omega-\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon+\omega)\right]}\right\}.

(54)

Eq. (
VI.3.2
) applies to incoherent, locally interacting non-Fermi liquids, such as strange metals analyzed through DMFT
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Müller-Hartmann 1989
;
Khurana 1990
;
Georges et al. 1996
;
Janiš and Vollhardt 2001
;
Kotliar and Vollhardt 2004
;
Berthod et al. 2013
;
Schäfer et al. 2021
or through Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev large-
{N}
saddle-point equations
Esterlis and Schmalian 2019
;
Cha et al. 2020
;
Esterlis et al. 2021
;
Patel et al. 2023
;
Valentinis et al. 2026
.

VII
Momentum conservation and renormalization of momentum and current vertices

VII.1
Global momentum conservation at
q=0

The above derivations ensure charge/mass conservation at any
\bm{q}
and
i{}_{n}
, but not momentum conservation. In fact, for a local self-energy, momentum conservation in accordance with the Ward identity (
II
) cannot hold locally at generic
\bm{k}
and
\bm{q}
, since the local irreducible vertex function (
3
) cannot possibly carry the required momentum structure to preserve momentum conservation at any
q
. However, at
q=0
one can still impose the
global
conservation of
total momentum
in the DMFT limit, by constraining the renormalized momentum vertex to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\pi},\alpha}(\bm{k},0,i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=\Gamma_{\hat{\pi},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{\Sigma(i{}_{n}+i\omega_{m})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})}{i{}_{n}}\right].

(55)

Eq. (
VII.1
) directly stems from the Ward identity (
II
) at
q=0
and ensures conservation of total momentum in the system, which is equal to the
q=0
component. For a general dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, this constraint on the momentum vertex does not propagate to a constraint on the current vertex, since the latter is in general not proportional to momentum
Raines et al. 2024
.

However, for the isotropic quadratic dispersion (
31
) current is indeed proportional to momentum, so Eq. (
VII.1
) fixes the
q=0
current vertex through the momentum Ward identity:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}(\bm{k},0,i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{\Sigma(i{}_{n}+i\omega_{m})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})}{i{}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}{m}k_{\alpha}\left[1-\frac{\Sigma(i{}_{n}+i\omega_{m})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})}{i{}_{n}}\right].

(56)

Inserting Eq. (
VII.1
) into the Kubo formula (
40
) for the current-current correlation function –
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{J}
–
yields a conductivity consistent with the perfect Drude model,
\sigma(\omega)=ie^{2}n/\left[m(\omega+i0^{+})\right]
, without momentum dissipation apart from a delta function at
\omega=0

Maiti et al. 2017
. In order to ensure the conservation of both total momentum (at
q=0
) and charge/mass (at any
q
), one can formally modify the density vertex (
38a
) such that the Ward identities (
II
) for charge conservation at any
q
and (
II
) for momentum conservation at
q=0
are both simultaneously obeyed:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{n}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\Sigma(i{}_{n}+i\omega_{m})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})}{i{}_{n}}

\displaystyle\times\left[i{}_{n}-\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\bm{q}\cdot\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}{m}\left(\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}\right)\right].

(57)

A many-body theory constructed with the current vertex (
VII.1
) and the density vertex (
VII.1
) for quadratic isotropic dispersion has only finite-
q
(inhomogeneous) processes as sources of momentum dissipation. In this sense,
one could refer to such theory as
emergent viscoelasticity
, or
quasi-hydrodynamics
, meaning that the leading long-wavelength term of momentum dissipation appears at order
q^{2}
, as it occurs for the viscosity coefficient (viscosity-conductivity relation) in Galilean-invariant systems
Taylor and Randeria 2010
;
Bradlyn et al. 2012
.

Nevertheless, the analogy with emergent viscoelasticity is not complete for the theory (
VII.1
), since the coefficient of the
q^{2}
term in the long-wavelength expansion of the conductivity is not the viscosity given by the renormalized bubble (
VI.3
) without vertex corrections. This is because a Galilean-invariant system obeys momentum conservation at any
\bm{q}
and
i{}_{n}
, as prescribed by Eq. (
II
) at
q\neq 0
, while Eq. (
VII.1
) imposes momentum conservation only at
q=0
.
Another due comment is that, by imposing Eqs. (
VII.1
) and (
VII.1
) as the renormalized density and current vertices, these vertices are no longer consistent with the ladder Bethe-Salpeter equations (
II
). This is because the latter force a frequency-dependent but momentum-independent renormalization of the bare vertex, in accordance with Eq. (
13
) for the correction
C^{\alpha}(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
, while Eq. (
VII.1
) for global momentum conservation prescribes a
\bm{k}
-dependent renormalization of the vertex, even at
q=0
. Such discrepancy is linked to the impossibility of imposing momentum conservation with a purely local two-particle vertex (
3
), which would instead require a more general nonlocal vertex
\Lambda(\bm{k},\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
obeying the Bethe-Salpeter equation (
II
). Implicitly, by imposing Eqs. (
VII.1
) and (
VII.1
), we are relaxing the assumption of a purely local two-particle vertex, including nonlocality in an, as yet unspecified, nonlocal two-particle vertex.

VII.2
Momentum conservation at any
\bm{q}
, and relation between nonlocal momentum-linear correlation functions and the viscosity tensor

Along the same lines as in the previous Sec.
VII.1
, one can make the theory obey both momentum conservation and charge conservation at any
\bm{q}
, at the unavoidable cost of distancing the theory from the pure DMFT limit and relaxing the locality assumption in the self-energy and/or the two-particle vertex. In the following, we choose to keep a momentum-independent self-energy, but the ensuing conclusions also hold for a nonlocal
\Sigma(\bm{k},i\omega_{n})
upon substitution.

Technically, when momentum conservation is imposed in general, the system should obey the associated Ward identity (
II
) at any
\bm{q}
and
i{}_{n}
. This identity is a linear relation between the momentum vertex
\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\pi}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{n},i{}_{n})
and the stress vertex
\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}_{\hat{\textit{T}}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{n},i{}_{n})

He and Levin 2014
. As such, it does not determine the momentum (except at
q=0
) and stress vertices individually, but only their relation, in a “gauge freedom” reminiscent of gauge invariance for electromagnetic vector and scalar potentials. Moreover, the Ward identity (
II
) affects only the
longitudinal
part of the stress vertex (parallel to
\bm{q}
), so that we can add to it a transverse part (orthogonal to
\bm{q}
).

Here, instead of reconstructing full forms of the momentum and stress vertices, we can directly impose the Ward identity (
II
), from which the relation (
II
) between the momentum-momentum and stress-stress correlation functions straightforwardly stems
Taylor and Randeria 2010
;
Bradlyn et al. 2012
;
Valentinis 2026a
.

In turn, Eq. (
II
) implies that any nonlocal correlation function of observables proportional to momentum
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{q},\tau)
also enjoys a relation with the double divergence of the stress-stress correlation function
\chi_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})
. In particular, the isotropic quadratic dispersion (
31
) implies proportionality between momentum and current densities as previously argued, and thus the Ward identity (
II
) restores the relation between the leading nonlocal
q^{2}
part of the nonlocal conductivity tensor
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},\omega)
and the
q=0
viscosity tensor
\eta_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega)
, valid for Galilean-invariant systems
Taylor and Randeria 2010
;
Bradlyn et al. 2012
;
He and Levin 2014
. Explicitly, this relation is

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{ine^{2}}{m(\omega+i0^{+})}+\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}(\bm{q},\omega)+{o}(q^{4}),

(58)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=e^{2}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\frac{q_{\mu}q_{\nu}}{m^{2}(\omega+i0^{+})^{2}}\eta_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}(\omega)

\displaystyle+\frac{ie^{2}}{m^{2}(\omega+i0^{+})^{3}}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}\kappa_{T}^{-1},

(59)

where
k_{T}^{-1}
is the inverse compressibility
Bradlyn et al. 2012
, while
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},\omega)
and
\eta_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}(\omega)
formally stem from Eq. (
40
), with
\hat{\Theta}=\hat{J}
at
q\neq 0
and
\hat{\Theta}=\hat{\textit{T}}
at
q=0
, respectively. The Drude term at
q=0
in Eq. (
58
) directly follows from the zero-momentum part of the current vertex, in accordance with Eq. (
VII.1
): this absence of dissipative conductivity at
\omega>0
makes sense, since when momentum is conserved there are no dissipation channels for the current response and the
q=0
conductivity reduces to the pure Drude model
Maiti et al. 2017
. The compressibility term at the right-hand side of Eq. (
VII.2
) emerges from the “contact” terms, specifically the current-stress commutator, in Eq. (
II
) once the latter is expanded to order
q^{2}
.

Conversely, Eq. (

) can be connected to correlation functions other than current for non-quadratic dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
. For graphene-like system with linear dispersion

. However, this connection cannot hold within the DMFT limit, and requires nonlocality of at least one object between self-energy and irreducible two-particle vertex.
For translationally invariant non-Fermi liquids, a recent work analyzes the finite-momentum electrodynamics of critical Fermi surfaces, investigating the spectrum of bosonic fluctuations and their impact on the hydrodynamic and tomographic regimes of nonlocal conduction

VIII
Discussion

VIII.1
Broken symmetries

VIII.1.1
Breaking of parity and time-reversal symmetry

The parity-related conditions for vanishing vertex correction at
q=0
, namely the dispersion (
12
) and the related vertex (
15
) respectively being even and odd under a point-group symmetry operation
{g}
, effectively generalize Khurana’s argument for vanishing vertex corrections to the conductivity in the DMFT limit of the Hubbard model
Khurana 1990
. Within the DMFT-like assumptions of local self-energy and irreducible two-particle vertex (
3
), these criteria can be applied to any (possibly anisotropic) dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
and vertex
\Gamma_{\hat{\Theta},\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{k},0,i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
for observable
\hat{\Theta}
for which momentum
\bm{k}
is a good quantum number. In particular, the even
\bm{k}
-inversion condition
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{-\bm{k}}
is protected by a combination of parity
{P}
and time-reversal
{T}
symmetries:
{P}
specifies the symmetry of the electronic state with respect to space inversion
\bm{r}\mapsto-\bm{r}
(or equivalently
\bm{k}\mapsto-\bm{k}
in reciprocal space of momenta), while
{T}
ensures Kramers’ degeneracy, i.e., the existence of distinct but doubly degenerate fermionic states related by time-reversal symmetry
Kramers 1930
;
Wigner 1993
.

If both
{P}
and
{T}
are broken, the criteria on vertex-correction vanishing do not apply and need to be generalized.
Parity-broken states are realized in non-centrosymmetric crystals, in which electronic bands do not satisfy parity with respect to
\bm{k}
-inversion due to the absence of an inversion symmetry point. As an example, Fig.
4
(a) illustrates the noncentrosymmetric crystal structure of GaAs. The lack of inversion symmetry allows for antisymmetric spin-orbit coupling, which implies a nonzero momentum- and spin-dependent effective internal magnetic field
\bm{B}_{i}(\bm{k})=-\bm{B}_{i}(-\bm{k})
, in accordance with the Rashba-Dresselhaus effect
Dresselhaus 1955
;
Bychkov and Rashba 1984
. Hence, for a given spin species
\sigma=\left\{\uparrow,\downarrow\right\}
, in general we have
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\sigma}\neq\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\sigma}
. This property invalidates the
\bm{k}
-inversion arguments leading to vertex-corrections cancellations at
q=0
as described in Sec.
III
, and the Bethe-Salpeter equation becomes a matrix in spin space
\left\{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\right\}=\left\{\uparrow,\downarrow\right\}
, in a way similar to the multiorbital case of Sec.
VIII.2
. Hence, one expect vertex corrections to not generically vanish anymore for spin-resolved
\bm{k}
-odd vertices in the presence of antisymmetric spin-orbit coupling, leading to phenomena like the spin Hall response
Sinova et al. 2015
.

For reference, Fig.
4
(b) shows an effective two-band tight-binding bandstructure of GaAs in the absence of spin-orbit coupling, so that
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\sigma}=\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\sigma}
; the exemplary path
\Gamma\rightarrow X\rightarrow L\rightarrow\Gamma
in reciprocal space is chosen, with
\Gamma=(0,0,0)
,
X=2\pi/a(1,0,0)
, and
L=2\pi/a(1/2,1/2,1/2)
, where
a
is the lattice parameter.
However, even in the presence of spin-orbit coupling, Kramers’ degeneracy is still preserved through
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\uparrow}=\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\downarrow}
. To break the latter as well, one needs time-reversal symmetry breaking, as realized for instance by the application of an external magnetic field
\bm{B}(\bm{r},t)
.
Fig.
4
(c) shows the two-band tight-binding model for GaAs along the reciprocal-space path
\Gamma\rightarrow X\rightarrow L\rightarrow\Gamma
(solid curves) and its reverse
\Gamma\rightarrow L\rightarrow X\rightarrow\Gamma
(dashed curves), in the presence of both Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling and a Zeeman spin-splitting magnetic field. The Hamiltonian for this model is specified in App.
F
. Here we see that bands along the two inverted paths are not superimposed in the region
X\leftrightarrow L
where the Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling term (
F
) is nonnull, highlighting that
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\sigma}\neq\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\sigma^{\prime}}
, i.e., both
{P}
and
{T}
are simultaneously broken, thus invalidating any symmetry between
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
and
\epsilon_{-\bm{k}}
.

In relation to the above discussion about time-reversal symmetry breaking, Sec.
VIII.1.2
comments on the exemplary special case of Hall viscosity due to Landau diamagnetism in an isotropic 2D electron liquid with a local self-energy.

VIII.1.2
Time-reversal symmetry breaking and Hall viscosity

In the absence of time-reversal symmetry, the Hall viscosity (or odd viscosity) component of the frequency-dependent viscosity tensor
\eta_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega)
emerges
Avron et al. 1995
;
Read 2009
;
Bradlyn et al. 2012
;
Hoyos et al. 2014
;
Hoyos 2014
;
Burmistrov et al. 2019
. Focusing on two spatial dimensions for definiteness, the Hall viscosity stems from the Kubo formula

\displaystyle\eta_{H}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathrm{Im}\left\{\chi^{(xx-yy)xy,R}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,\omega)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathrm{Im}\left\{\chi^{xxxy,R}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,\omega)-\chi^{yyxy,R}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,\omega)\right\}.

(60)

The expression (
VIII.1.2
) corresponds to the real part of the
\omega\rightarrow 0
limit of the following complex-valued generalized Hall viscosity:

\tilde{\eta}_{H}(\omega)=\frac{i}{2\omega}\left[\chi^{(xx-yy)xy,R}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,\omega)-\chi^{(xx-yy)xy,R}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,0)\right].

(61)

Similar to the Hall conductivity, the Hall viscosity (
VIII.1.2
) produces viscous forces that are perpendicular to the velocity gradient, rather than opposite to the flow direction. Because these forces are orthogonal to the flow, they do not produce work and therefore they do not dissipate energy.

The off-diagonal stress-stress correlation function
\chi^{\alpha\alpha xy,R}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,\omega)
in Eqs. (
VIII.1.2
) and (
61
) may be affected by or immune to vertex corrections, depending on the physical mechanism for time-reversal symmetry breaking. If the latter occurs without external magnetic fields, as in magnetically ordered materials like ferromagnets
Blundell 2001
or altermagnets
Song et al. 2025
, then momentum
\bm{k}
is still a good quantum number; then, the
\bm{k}
-inversion criteria (
12
) and (
15
), laid out in Sec.
III
for the absence of vertex corrections at
q=0
with generic single-band dispersion
\xi_{\bm{k}}
, still hold.

Specifically, in the DMFT limit the Bethe-Salpeter equation (
II
) shows that the Hall viscosity amounts to its renormalized-bubble form (
42
), if the system is symmetric under exchange
k_{x}\leftrightarrow k_{y}
. To see this property, recall that at
q=0
the stress vertex satisfies
\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(\bm{k},0,i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(\bm{k},0)+C^{\alpha\beta}(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
, where the vertex renormalization
C^{\alpha\beta}(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
(when nonnull) is only a function of frequency and satisfies Eq. (
13
). Now, for the stress component
\textit{T}_{xy}
the vertex renormalization is null upon angular integration, so vertex corrections vanish for this component. If, in addition, the vertex renormalizations for
\textit{T}_{xx}
and
\textit{T}_{yy}
are equal, i.e.,
C^{xx}(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=C^{yy}(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
, then such renormalizations cancel in the subtraction
\textit{T}_{xx}-\textit{T}_{yy}
. In turn, these conditions require that the sum over momenta
\bm{k}
in
C^{\alpha\alpha}(i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
is invariant under exchange
k_{x}\leftrightarrow k_{y}
, which requires point-group symmetry containing axis exchange, such as
C_{4}
,
C_{4v}
, and
D_{4}
. In other words, in group-theoretical language vertex renormalizations for
\textit{T}_{xx}
and
\textit{T}_{yy}
are equal if these stress-tensor components belong to the same reducible representation
A_{1g}\oplus G
where
G\neq A_{1g}
is another point group. Hence, the Hall viscosity (
VIII.1.2
) is devoid of vertex corrections only under the specific requirement of system symmetry under
k_{x}\leftrightarrow k_{y}
exchange.

On the other hand, if time-reversal symmetry breaking occurs due to the application of an external magnetic field
\bm{B}
, as in the case of quantum Hall states generated by Landau orbital diamagnetism, then
\bm{k}
is not a good quantum number and the criteria (
12
) and (
15
) need to be revised.
For an applied magnetic field
\bm{B}=B\hat{u}_{z}
, in Landau-level basis the Green’s function is

G_{ll^{\prime}}(i\omega_{n})=\frac{\delta_{ll^{\prime}}}{i\omega_{n}+\mu-\epsilon_{n}-\Sigma_{l}(i\omega_{n})},

(62)

where the Landau levels
l\in\mdmathbb{N}
for quadratic isotropic dispersion are

E_{l}=\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}\left(l+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(63)

and
\omega_{c}=eB/m
is the electron cyclotron frequency.
The stress tensor operator connects different Landau levels; using the Green’s functions (
62
), the uniform part of the stress-stress correlation function per spin is then

\displaystyle\chi^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}_{\hat{\textit{T}\hat{\textit{T}}}}(0,i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\sum_{l,p}G_{l}(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{\hat{\mathcal{T}},\alpha\beta}(l,p,i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})G_{p}(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle\times\Gamma^{(0)}_{\hat{\mathcal{T}},\gamma\delta}(m,n).

(64)

The stress-stress correlation function for the Hall viscosity corresponds to

\displaystyle\chi^{\alpha\alpha xy}_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}(0,i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{{A}}\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\sum_{l,p}G_{l}(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})

VIII.3
Generality and applications of vanishing vertex corrections at finite
\bm{q}
for quadratic dispersion

For the quadratic dispersion (
29
), we have shown that vertex corrections vanish in the DMFT limit not only at
q=0
, but at any momentum
\bm{q}
and frequency
\omega
in a charge-conserving (but not momentum-conserving) theory. This result greatly simplifies the calculation of two-particle correlation functions to the renormalized-bubble term (
42
), for any local self-energy. The companion paper
Valentinis 2026b
departs from this result to derive explicit “Allen-like” expressions for the shear viscosity and the small-
\bm{q}
, finite-
\omega
optical conductivity of correlated electrons.

The quadratic-dispersion case may be appropriate to cold-atom traps and degenerate quantum fluids, but may seem of limited applicability to solid-state lattice systems with anisotropic crystal structures. However, notice that for simple Bravais Lattices (e.g., simple cubic, tetragonal, and triangular lattices) the long-wavelength (low-energy) limit of the electronic dispersion is well captured by the quadratic form (
29
). The same quadratic approximation holds for band edges of lattice structures: most lattice systems, including square lattices, display a quadratic, roughly isotropic dispersion near their band edges before warping occurs at higher energies. Hence, one expects that, whenever the quadratic-dispersion approximation is applicable, vertex corrections in the DMFT limit are qualitatively negligible and a renormalized-bubble form (
42
) of the two-particle correlation function might correctly describe the low-energy physics even at finite
\bm{q}
.
These considerations are appealing in view of their potential applications to the finite-momentum, finite-frequency electrodynamics of both Fermi liquids and non-Fermi liquids which are not translationally invariant, i.e., where Galilean invariance does not hold
Merzoni et al. 2024
. The latter case is especially fascinating due to the finite-momentum charge response of strange metals, which is observed to be essentially momentum-independent in disagreement with standard predictions based on Boltzmann quasiparticle transport
Guo et al. 2024a
. I leave this exciting application to future work.

VIII.4
Experimental implications: tuning of shear-stress vertex corrections by mechanical strain

The
\bm{k}
-parity conditions (
12
) and (
15
) in the DMFT limit show that shear stresses are not affected by vertex corrections at
q=0
for shear components
\left\{\alpha,\beta\right\}
involving a coordinate
\alpha
with respect to which the system possesses an orthogonal mirror symmetry plane. For instance, the SrTiO
3
cubic unit cell of Fig.
3
(a) possesses three of such planes, which means that vertex corrections vanish from all shear components of the kinetic stress tensor. However, suppose a mechanical deformation (strain) is applied to the system, so that the unit-cell symmetry is lowered to a fewer number of symmetry planes, with the triclinic configuration being the most extreme, asymmetric one. Then, assuming that reversible strain adiabatically and perturbatively modifies the dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, by the arguments in Sec.
III.2
the system acquires shear-stress vertex corrections for components
\left\{\alpha,\beta\right\}
including the coordinate
\alpha
orthogonal to a strain-induced broken symmetry plane. This way, a tantalizing perspective would arise, namely to controllably tune shear stresses and the ensuing generalized shear viscosity, by acting on the underlying symmetry of the crystalline lattice by mechanical deformations.
However, the measurement of such shear stresses requires the identification of the finite-
\bm{q}
correlation function, for the specific dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, which couples to momentum
\bm{k}
and therefore allows the connection with the stress tensor through Eq. (
II
) or generalizations thereof
Bradlyn et al. 2012
;
Link et al. 2018
;
Valentinis 2026a
. In general, such correlation function will be affected by vertex corrections at
q\neq 0
, unless the dispersion is quadratic as argued in Secs.
V
and
VIII.3
. Nevertheless, the shear components of the inferred stress tensor should be not affected by vertex corrections in the DMFT limit, which simplifies computations and comparison with experimental data.

IX
Conclusions

This work presented parity-based criteria for the presence or absence of vertex corrections to two-point correlation functions for observables
\hat{\Theta}
at finite transferred momentum
\bm{q}
and frequency
\omega
for correlated-electron fluids, endowed with a one-band single-particle dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, and assuming local self-energy
\Sigma(i\omega_{n})
and local irreducible two-particle vertex
\Lambda(\omega_{m},\omega_{m}^{\prime},{}_{n})
(a configuration that we dubbed “DMFT limit”). The results are derived from the ladder Bethe-Salpeter equation (
II
) for the renormalized vertices, and are consistent with the ensuing Ward identity (
II
) for charge conservation (but in the absence of momentum conservation).

For generic dispersion and at
q=0
with arbitrary frequency
i{}_{n}
, the criteria for vanishing vertex corrections are: even parity of the dispersion with respect to momentum inversion
\bm{k}\mapsto-\bm{k}
, according to Eq. (
12
); odd parity of the vertex associated to the observable
\hat{\Theta}
with respect to a point-group symmetry operation
{g}
other than momentum inversion, as in Eq. (
15
). For vertices that transform like polar vectors,
{g}={I}
is identified with momentum inversion,
\bm{k}\mapsto-\bm{k}
. According to these criteria, various electrodynamic quantities are universally devoid of vertex corrections in the DMFT limit: these include particle current, momentum current, and energy current, having corresponding vertices proportional to momentum
\bm{k}
.

Other vertices which transform like rank-2 tensors, with spatial indexes
\left\{\alpha,\beta\right\}
, may enjoy vertex-correction cancellations under additional symmetry transformation constraints. These operations include: a mirror symmetry plane
{M}_{\alpha}
orthogonal to
\alpha
, a two-fold rotation
{C}_{\alpha}
about the axis
\alpha
, or an improper rotation
{S}_{n}
that involves a rotation plus a mirror plane perpendicular to
\alpha
. All these options suppress vertex corrections for the components
\alpha\beta
of kinetic shear-stress vertices
\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})
, involving the spatial direction
\alpha
. Furthermore, higher-order rotations
{C}_{3}
,
{C}_{4}
,
{C}_{6}\ldots
suppress all off-diagonal components of shear-stress vertex corrections.

However, other notable quantities are unavoidably affected by ladder vertex corrections: among those are the charge density and the bulk components of the kinetic stress tensor. Thus, even in the DMFT limit, neglecting vertex corrections at
q=0
in two-particle correlation functions is not always rigorously justified; this work provides specific symmetry criteria for such cancellation to exactly occur.

For density-density interactions, where the two-particle vertex
\Lambda(i{}_{n})
depends just on the exchanged frequency
n
, the ladder resummation in the Bethe-Salpeter equation (
II
) can be explicitly performed, and leads to the RPA-like structures (
IV
) of renormalized vertices.

For the observables
\hat{\Theta}
enjoying aforementioned cancellation of vertex corrections, the renormalized-bubble form (
42
) of the correlation function is exact at
q=0
. This form specializes to “Allen-like” formulae (
VI.3.1
) when the transport function (
49
), encoding all the effects of the dispersion on
\hat{\Theta}
-correlations, is much broader in energy than single-particle spectral functions, i.e., in the coherent/Fermi-liquid scenario. Conversely, the self energy-dependent analytical form (
VI.3.2
) applies to the opposite limit of sharp spectral function and broader spectral functions, which models incoherent/non-Fermi liquid electronic states.

For quadratic (but possibly anisotropic) dispersions (
29
) at arbitrary finite
\bm{q}
,
i{}_{n}
, vertex corrections are shown to identically vanish in a charge-conserving but not momentum-conserving configuration. Hence, the entire nonlocal electrodynamics of quadratically dispersing systems without Galilean invariance can be investigated with the renormalized-bubble form (
VI.3.1
) of two-particle correlation functions. This is the subject of the companion paper
Valentinis 2026b
, where well-defined quasiparticles and various forms of local self-energies are assumed.

Enforcing momentum conservation at each
\bm{q}
and
i{}_{n}
is impossible with a purely local two-particle vertex
\Lambda(\omega_{m},\omega_{m}^{\prime},{}_{n})
, since the latter lacks the required momentum structure to allow for the needed momentum redistribution among one-particle states. However, implicitly relaxing the hypothesis of a local two-particle vertex, one can directly employ the finite-
\bm{q}
momentum-conservation Ward identity (
II
) to impose momentum conservation at any
\bm{q}
, thus deriving a relation between the renormalized momentum and stress vertices, which in turn leads to the relation (
II
) between the momentum-momentum and stress-stress correlation functions. For the isotropic quadratic dispersion (
31
), the latter relation leads to the known Galilean-invariant connection between the conductivity and viscosity tensors at order
q^{2}

Bradlyn et al. 2012
, as quoted in Eqs. (
58
) and (
VII.2
).

The
\bm{k}
-parity criteria outlined in this work need to be generalized if the single-band, non-superconducting, or time-reversal symmetry assumptions are relaxed, in accordance with Sec.
VIII.1
. The case of Landau diamagnetism is considered as an exemplary application, and in Sec.
VIII.1.2
it is shown that vertex corrections vanish from the Hall viscosity in the DMFT limit. However, the
T=0
“topological” value of the Hall viscosity for noninteracting Landau levels is shown to be altered by a local self-energy and by temperature through spectral-functions broadening and Fermi-Dirac occupation functions, respectively.

The present results elucidate general and exact symmetry-based criteria for vanishing vertex corrections in local quantum electronic fluids, encompassing Fermi liquids and non-Fermi liquids, and provide theoretical guidance for nonlocal electrodynamics experiments in strongly interacting systems of relevance for condensed matter, quantum simulators, and high-energy physics. This work offers a suitable platform for the analysis of local dynamical correlations, and allows for future generalizations to vertex corrections generated by topological/multiband systems, broken-symmetry states like Eliashberg superconductors, and time-reversal asymmetric configurations.

\left[G^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)\right]^{-1}=\left[G^{R}_{0}(\bm{k},\omega)\right]^{-1}-\Sigma^{R}(\omega),

(79)

where
G^{R}_{0}(\bm{k},\omega)
is the noninteracting Green’s function (with
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)=0
).

For the local self-energy
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)
, the electronic spectral function stems from the Lehmann representation
Mahan 2000
;
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
;
Berthod 2018

G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon\frac{A_{G}(\bm{k},\epsilon)}{i\omega_{m}-\epsilon},

(80)

corresponding to Eq. (
77
), which leads to the Lorentzian-like form
Berthod et al. 2013
;
Berthod 2018

\displaystyle A_{G}(\bm{k},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Im}\left\{G^{R}(\bm{k},\epsilon)\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\Sigma_{2}(\epsilon)}{\left[\epsilon-\xi_{\bm{k}}-\Sigma_{1}(\epsilon)\right]^{2}+\left[\Sigma_{2}(\epsilon)\right]^{2}},

(81)

where
\Sigma_{1}(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\left\{\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\right\}
and
\Sigma_{2}(\omega)=\mathrm{Im}\left\{\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\right\}
.

Aside from the single-particle momentum
\bm{k}
and fermionic Matsubara frequency
i\omega_{n}
, for two-body correlation functions we consider the exchanged momentum
\bm{q}
and the bosonic Matsubara frequency
i{}_{n}=2\pi nk_{B}T
.

The focus of this work is on Kubo formulae of the kind of Eq. (
40
), for correlation functions as in Eq. (
1
). Here we explicitly write two notable special cases, for the conductivity and the viscosity tensors.
The conductivity tensor in linear response for a translationally invariant system satisfies the Kubo formula

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{ie^{2}}{\omega}\left[\chi_{\hat{J}\hat{J}}^{\alpha\beta,R}(\bm{q},\omega)+\delta_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\left\langle n\right\rangle}{m}\right],

(82)

where
\chi_{\hat{J}\hat{J}}^{\alpha\beta,R}(\bm{q},\omega)=\lim_{i{}_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i0^{+}}\chi_{\hat{J}\hat{J}}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})
is the retarded current-current correlation function, analytically continued to the real axis. Explicitly, the current-current correlation function is a special case of Eq. (
1
) for an observable
\hat{\Theta}=\hat{J}
equal to the current density. Eq. (
82
) also includes the diamagnetic static term with average electron density
\left\langle n\right\rangle
and electron mass
m
.

The generalized viscosity tensor satisfies another linear-response Kubo formula:

\displaystyle\eta_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\omega+i0^{+}}\left[\chi_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta,R}(0,\omega)-\chi_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta,R}(0,0)\right],

(83)

where
\chi_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta,R}(0,0)=k_{T}^{-1}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\gamma\delta}
with
k_{T}^{-1}=-\left.{V}\partial P/\partial{V}\right|_{{N},T}
isothermal compressibility
Bradlyn et al. 2012
;
Link et al. 2018
, and
\chi_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta,R}(\bm{q},\omega)=\lim_{i{}_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i0^{+}}\chi_{\hat{\textit{T}}\hat{\textit{T}}}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\bm{q},i{}_{n})
is the (retarded) analytic continuation of the stress-stress correlation function. Formally, the stress-stress correlation function results from Eq. (
1
) for an observable
\hat{\Theta}=\hat{\textit{T}}
equal to the stress tensor.

Appendix B
Conditions for dominance of frequency dependence over momentum dependence in the self-energy of many-body systems

In general, the many-body self-energy
\Sigma(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})
and its analytic-continued retarded counterpart
\Sigma^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)
depend on both momentum
\bm{k}
and frequency
\omega
. However, the relative importance of momentum and frequency arguments depends on the specific physical problem and regime under consideration, and it varies with temperature, internal degrees of freedom, and nature of microscopic interactions.
In particular, situations where dynamical interactions can be considered (at least approximately) local, i.e., momentum-independent, considerably simplify many-body calculations, such as computations of the correlation functions from Eq. (
1
) and interaction vertices as in passing from Eq. (
II
) to Eq. (
II
). In these situations, the self-energy
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)
depends only on frequency and the irreducible two-particle vertex
\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
is also momentum-independent. In the following, some exemplary cases are enumerated when dynamical many-body effects can be considered at least approximately local.

B.1
Strongly interacting correlated-electron systems and non-Fermi liquids

In strongly correlated solid-state systems, complex experimental phase diagrams are accompanied by low-energy intertwined phases, high interaction energy scales compared to characteristic transition temperatures, and collective electron behaviour
Lee et al. 2006
;
Sachdev 2011
;
Rozenberg 2019b
;
Chowdhury et al. 2022
. There, experiments and computations find extended parameter regimes where the self-energy is dominated by its frequency dependence, essentially because electron-electron interactions lead to dynamical (
\omega
-dependent) effects that are enhanced at specific energies by predominantly local interactions. For instance, in Mott insulators as described by the Hubbard model with onsite Hubbard repulsion
U
and hopping parameter
t

Capone et al. 2004
;
Lee et al. 2006
;
Schäfer et al. 2021
;
Qin et al. 2022
;
Menke et al. 2024
, at large
U/t
charge fluctuations are hindered, and electron motion becomes governed by local temporal fluctuations (doublon-holon processes): in this regime, the self-energy can be strongly frequency-dependent, with features like the formation of a Mott gap, while the momentum dependence can be relatively weaker. This is particularly evident in optical conductivity measurements, where the impact of the frequency dependence of the self-energy can be directly observed
Rozenberg et al. 1995
;
Shinjo et al. 2021
.

Moreover, in the normal-state of high-temperature superconductors frequency-dependent self-energy effects are essential both in the pseudogap regime at underdoping, where a gap appears in the single-particle spectrum, and in the strange-metal
Zaanen 2019
;
Chowdhury et al. 2022
;
Phillips et al. 2022
/bad-metal
Emery and Kivelson 1995
;
Lee et al. 2006
;
Vučičević et al. 2015
regimes near optimal doping characterized by power-law dependence of observables on temperature, as detected through ARPES (Angle-Resolved Photoemission Spectroscopy)
Fedorov et al. 1999
;
Damascelli et al. 2003
;
Cooper et al. 2009
, EELS (Electron Energy-Loss Spectroscopy)
Mitrano et al. 2018
;
Husain et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024a
, resistivity
Giraldo-Gallo et al. 2018
;
Legros et al. 2019
;
Grissonnanche et al. 2021
;
Shi et al. 2025
, and optical conductivity
Marel et al. 2003
;
van Heumen et al. 2022
;
Michon et al. 2023
measurements. The momentum dependence of the extracted self-energy is found to be comparatively weaker than its frequency evolution.

Finally, let us mention quantum
U(1)
and disordered spin liquids
Nave and Lee 2007
;
Wang and Liu 2010
;
Sachdev 2011
;
Broholm et al. 2020
, where the self-energy’s frequency dependence becomes crucial because the system is dominated by fluctuating spin correlations rather than by momentum-dependent quasiparticle excitations. Here, the frequency dependence may reflect the scaling of spin excitations at different energy scales.

B.2
Systems close to a classical or quantum phase transition

In many materials, especially those with strong correlations, the system might be in a regime where temporal fluctuations are dominant, and the dynamics of quasiparticles are primarily controlled by frequency
\omega
(or energy
\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega
) rather than by momentum
\bm{k}
.
For instance, near a Mott transition or quantum critical point (e.g., in systems exhibiting magnetic or charge order), the system can undergo significant changes due to the frequency dependence of interactions, such as in the case of critical slowing down. Near such a transition, the self-energy can become nearly independent of momentum but very sensitive to frequency, particularly at long wavelengths or low momenta. In the proximity of the transition, quantum fluctuations of soft collective modes (e.g., spin, charge, loop-current, lattice fluctuations) are crucial for inelastic electron-boson scattering, so that the electron self-energy can be dominated by frequency-dependent effects due to the dynamics of these fluctuations, while momentum dependence may be secondary or weaker.
A microscopic mechanism to explain this sensitivity is provided by patch theories of critical Fermi surfaces
Polchinski 1994
;
Altshuler et al. 1994
;
Lee 2009
;
Mross et al. 2010
;
Metlitski and Sachdev 2010
;
Sachdev 2011
;
Patel and Sachdev 2017
, which I qualitatively summarize below in one of its most recent incarnations
Esterlis et al. 2021
;
Guo et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2024b
.

Consider a point on a 2D Fermi surface where the dispersion is linearized in
k_{x}
and quadratic in
k_{y}
to account for a finite local curvature
\kappa_{R}
:

\epsilon_{\bm{k}}\approx v_{F}k_{x}+\kappa_{R}(k_{y})^{2},

(84)

where
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity. Let us assume that the electrons interact with a soft critical scalar bosonic mode (e.g., a Pomeranchuk instability, a
U(1)
gauge field, a phonon) with propagator

D(\bm{q},i{}_{n})=\frac{1}{\gamma\frac{\left|{}_{n}\right|}{q}+q^{2}+(m_{b})^{2}},

(85)

where
\left|{}_{n}\right|/q
is the Landau-damping term,
\gamma\in\mdmathbb{R}^{+}
, and the renormalized mass
m_{b}\rightarrow 0
at the quantum critical point (QCP). The Landau-damping term can be derived from the boson self-energy
\Pi(\bm{q},i\Omega)
within the patch, assuming (and then self-consistently verifying) that the fermion self-energy
\Sigma(i\omega)
is momentum-independent
Esterlis et al. 2021
. Within the patch (
84
), the momentum exchange in the boson propagator (
85
) is dominated by small-momentum (
q\rightarrow 0^{+}
) processes and is mainly transverse,
q\approx q_{y}
.

In this limit, the fermion self-energy resulting from boson exchange at
T=0
is

\Sigma(\bm{k},i\omega)\propto\int d\Omega\int dq_{x}\int dq_{y}\frac{D(\bm{q},i\Omega)}{i(\omega+\Omega)-\epsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}}.

(86)

Since
q\approx q_{y}
,
D(\bm{q},i\Omega)
is approximately independent from
q_{x}
, which allows one to perform the integral over
q_{x}
in Eq. (
86
) with the residue theorem, after inserting the dispersion (
84
):

\displaystyle\int dq_{x}\frac{1}{i(\omega+\Omega)-v_{F}(k_{x}+q_{x})-\kappa_{R}(k_{y}+q_{y})^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim-i\pi\mathrm{sign}(\omega+\Omega).

(87)

Thus, the integral (
B.2
) removes the dependence on
k_{x}
and
q_{x}
entirely. The remaining integral over
q_{y}
in Eq. (
86
) reads

\displaystyle\Sigma(\bm{k},i\omega)

\displaystyle\propto\int d\Omega\int dq_{y}\frac{\mathrm{sign}(\omega+\Omega)}{\gamma\frac{\left|\Omega\right|}{q_{y}}+(q_{y})^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\int d\Omega\frac{\mathrm{sign}(\omega+\Omega)}{\left|\Omega\right|^{\frac{1}{3}}}\sim\omega^{\frac{2}{3}}.

(88)

Eq. (
B.2
) shows that the fermionic self-energy due to soft-boson exchange is dynamical (depends on
\omega^{2/3}
) and momentum-independent within the patch theory. The same conclusions, here presented in a simple one-loop argument, hold in the self-consistent calculation of bosonic and fermionic properties, realized, e.g., in the translationally invariant 2D Yukawa-Sachdev-Ye–Kitaev model
Esterlis et al. 2021
.
In the renormalization-group sense, the scaling of tangential momentum is
\left[k_{x}\right]={E}
, the one of transverse momentum is
\left[k_{y}\right]=\sqrt{{E}}
, and the one of the frequency-dependent part (
B.2
) of the self-energy is
\left[\Sigma\right]\propto\omega^{\frac{2}{3}}\sim{E}
.

Furthermore, the locality of the self-energy (
B.2
) from the exchange of bosons, in accordance with Eq. (
86
), implies an approximate locality of the two-particle vertex
\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
from the Kadanoff-Baym conserving condition given by Eq. (
7
), whenever the boson propagator (
85
) is dominated by low-momentum (
q\rightarrow 0
) and finite-frequency dynamics. This occurs for local inelastic fermion-boson scattering as modeled by spatially disordered theories
Guo et al. 2024b
;
Patel et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Valentinis et al. 2026
.

B.3
High-frequency limit and optical excitations

At high frequencies
\omega
(or exchanged energy scales
\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega
) with respect to all other system energy scales, the frequency dependence of
\Sigma^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)
often becomes more important than its momentum dependence. This dominance occurs because at temperatures
k_{B}T\ll\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega
the electron ensemble effectively behaves as a
T\approx 0
system, and conduction states are essentially sampled at the Fermi wave vector
\bm{k}\approx\bm{k}_{F}
, while the sum over
\bm{k}
in the conductivity – cfr. Eq. (
1
) for
\hat{\Theta}=\hat{J}
and
q=0
– averages over Fermi-surface anisotropy, especially in the standard Drude/diffusive conductive regime
Dressel and Grüner 2002
;
Berthod et al. 2013
;
Berthod 2018
. The high-frequency regime is also the one where the energy dependence of transport/single-particle scattering rates and effective masses unavoidably matters, while the causal properties of the self-energy dictate its decay at least as
1/\omega
at high frequencies
Berthod et al. 2013
;
Berthod 2018
. All the above discussion affects high-frequency, low-momentum probes such as optical conductivity and photoemission experiments, where the self-energy’s frequency dependence directly reflects the spectral properties of the system
Fedorov et al. 1999
;
Damascelli et al. 2003
;
Cooper et al. 2009
;
Marel et al. 2003
;
van Heumen et al. 2022
;
Michon et al. 2023
.

Appendix C
Derivation of the Ward identity for charge conservation from the ladder Bethe-Salpeter equation

In this appendix we derive the Ward identity (
II
) relating the density and current vertices, from the general Bethe-Salpeter equation (
II
). Specializing the latter to the density vertex (
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{n}
) and multiplying by
i{}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle i{}_{n}\Gamma_{\hat{n}}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=i{}_{n}\Gamma_{\hat{n}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})+i{}_{n}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{\hat{n}}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}).

(89)

In the same way, from the ladder equation for the current vertex (
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{\bm{J}}
) multiplied by
\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\hat{J},\alpha}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})+\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q_{\alpha}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

(90)

Subtracting Eq. (
C

C

\displaystyle W(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{i{}_{n}\Gamma_{\hat{n}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})-\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{J}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})}_{\boxed{B}}+k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times W(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}),

where we defined the quantity

(92)

The term
\boxed{B}
in Eq. (
C
) is fixed by the noninteracting Ward identity

\displaystyle i{}_{n}\Gamma_{\hat{n}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})-\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{J}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=i{}_{n}-\xi_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}+\xi_{\bm{k}}=G_{0}^{-1}(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})-G_{0}^{-1}(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=X(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})+\Sigma(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m}),

(93)

where

(94)

Summing Eq. (
94

\bm{k}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}}G(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})X(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[G(\bm{k}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})-G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\left[G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})-G(\bm{k},i\omega_{m})\right],

(95)

which is independent from
\bm{q}
by shifting the

Now we utilize the DMFT identity corresponding to the Kadanoff-Baym prescription (

\displaystyle\Sigma(i\omega_{m}+i{}_{n})-\Sigma(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})

\displaystyle W(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})-X(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

Appendix D

This appendix contains the derivation of the momentum-conservation Ward identity (

Specializing the latter to momentum density operator (
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\pi},\alpha}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\hat{\pi}}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})+k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

(98)

In the same way, from the ladder equation for the stress operator (
\hat{\Theta}\equiv\hat{\underline{\underline{\textit{T}}}}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(\bm{k},\bm{q})+k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

(99)

Let us multiply Eq. (
D

i{}_{n}
and Eq. (

\mathord{\mathchar 126h}q_{\beta}

\displaystyle Y_{\alpha}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}(\bm{k},\bm{q},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})+k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}G(\bm{k}^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

(103)

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}r_{1}\int d^{2}r_{3}\int d^{2}r_{4}G(\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{3},i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G(\bm{r}_{4}-\bm{r}_{1},i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n}){}_{l,X}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l^{\prime},X^{\prime}}(\bm{r}_{1})\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(\bm{r}_{3},\bm{r}_{4},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}r_{1}\int d^{2}r_{3}\int d^{2}r_{4}\sum_{r,Y}\sum_{p,Z}\sum_{j,Q}\sum_{k,W}G_{r}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G_{p}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(j,k,Q,W,i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times{}_{r,Y}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{r,Y}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{3}){}_{p,Z}(\bm{r}_{4}){}_{p,Z}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{j,Q}(\bm{r}_{3}){}_{k,W}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{4}){}_{l,X}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l^{\prime},X^{\prime}}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,p,j,k}\sum_{Y,Z,Q,W}\int d^{2}r_{1}{}_{r,Y}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{p,Z}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l,X}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l^{\prime},X^{\prime}}(\bm{r}_{1})\underbrace{\int d^{2}r_{3}{}_{r,Y}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{3}){}_{j,Q}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{3})}_{\delta_{rj}\delta_{YQ}}\underbrace{\int d^{2}r_{4}{}_{p,Z}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{4}){}_{k,W}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{4})}_{\delta_{pk}\delta_{ZW}}

\displaystyle\times G_{r}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G_{p}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(j,k,Q,W,i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,p}\sum_{Y,Z}\int d^{2}r_{1}{}_{r,Y}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{p,Z}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l,X}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l^{\prime},X^{\prime}}(\bm{r}_{1})G_{r}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G_{p}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(r,p,Y,Z,i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}).

(135)

Therefore, the renormalized stress vertex satisfies

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(l,l^{\prime},X,X^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(l,l^{\prime},X,X^{\prime})+k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\sum_{r,p}\sum_{Y,Z}{O}_{rpll^{\prime}}(Y,Z,X,X^{\prime})G_{r}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G_{p}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(r,p,Y,Z,i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}),

(136)

where the Landau-level overlaps are

{O}_{rpll^{\prime}}(Y,Z,X,X^{\prime})=\int d^{2}r_{1}{}_{r,Y}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{p,Z}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l,X}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}){}_{l^{\prime},X^{\prime}}^{\ast}(\bm{r}_{1}).

(137)

We now analyze the 4-orbital overlaps (
137
) in Landau gauge employing the eigenfunctions (
130a
):

{O}_{rpll^{\prime}}(Y,Z,X,X^{\prime})=\frac{1}{l_{B}^{4}}\int dx\int_{0}^{l_{B}}dye^{ik_{y}y}\phi_{r}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-Y\right)e^{-ik_{y}^{\prime}y}\phi_{p}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-Z\right)e^{-ik_{y}^{\prime\prime}y}\phi_{l}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-X\right)e^{-ik_{y}^{\prime\prime\prime}y}\phi_{l^{\prime}}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-X^{\prime}\right).

(138)

The integral over
y
in Eq. (
138
) of
e^{i(k_{y}-k_{y}^{\prime}-k_{y}^{\prime\prime}+k_{y}^{\prime\prime\prime})y}/l_{B}
produces
\delta(k_{y}-k_{y}^{\prime}-k_{y}^{\prime\prime}+k_{y}^{\prime\prime\prime})
, in other words it forces
k_{y}+k_{y}^{\prime\prime\prime}=k_{y}^{\prime}+k_{y}^{\prime\prime}
, which translates in terms of guiding centers as
Y+X^{\prime}=X+Z
. We assume magnetic translational invariance, which implies for the renormalized stress vertex

\Gamma_{\textit{T},\alpha\beta}(l,l^{\prime},X,X^{\prime},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})=\Gamma_{\textit{T},\alpha\beta}(l,l^{\prime},i\omega_{m},i{}_{n})\delta_{XX^{\prime}},

(139)

and in turn entails
X=X^{\prime}
and
Y=Z
. The latter relations, together with
Y+X^{\prime}=X+Z
, yield
X=X^{\prime}=Y=Z
, so that the overlaps (
138
) are

{O}_{rpll^{\prime}}(Y,Z,X,X^{\prime})=\frac{\delta_{XX^{\prime}}\delta_{YZ}}{l_{B}^{3}}\int dx\phi_{r}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-Y\right)\phi_{p}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-Y\right)\phi_{l}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-X\right)\phi_{l^{\prime}}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-X\right).

(140)

Summing over the guiding center
Y
, and applying orthogonality of the Landau-level eigenfunctions (
130b
), we have

\sum_{Y}{O}_{rpll^{\prime}}(Y,Y,X,X)=\frac{1}{l_{B}^{2}}\underbrace{\int d\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}\right)\phi_{l^{\prime}}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-X\right)\phi_{l}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-X\right)}_{\delta_{ll^{\prime}}}\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dY\phi_{p}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-Y\right)\phi_{r}\left(\frac{x}{l_{B}}-Y\right)}_{\delta_{rp}}=\frac{\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{rp}}{2\pi l_{B}^{2}}.

(141)

Inserting Eq. (
141
) into the renormalized vertex (
G.1
), we recover a vertex renormalization
\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(l,l^{\prime},X,X^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})=\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(l,l^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\delta_{XX^{\prime}}
that is purely diagonal in Landau-level indexes:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(l,l^{\prime},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(l,l^{\prime})+\delta_{ll^{\prime}}k_{B}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}^{\prime}}\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})\sum_{r}G_{r}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime})G_{r}(i\omega_{m}^{\prime}+i{}_{n})\Gamma_{\hat{\textit{T}},\alpha\beta}(r,r,i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n}).

(142)

Therefore, the vertex renormalization in Eq. (
142
) is diagonal in the indexes
l
,
l^{\prime}
. However, for the Hall viscosity (
61
) only off-diagonal transitions
l\rightarrow l\pm 2
are allowed, as shown in App.
G.2
; therefore, for the Landau-level Hall viscosity vertex corrections vanish and
\tilde{\eta}_{H}(\omega)
can be calculated with just the renormalized bubble (
42
). This conclusion makes sense in light of the “d-wave”, or “
L=2
” character of the Hall viscosity, which cannot induce a density-like, “s-wave”, or “L=0” deformation if the self-energy and the irreducible two-particle vertex are both local.

G.2
Hall viscosity of Landau levels: Kubo-formula derivation and “topological” ground-state contribution

Since the Hamiltonian in a magnetic field
\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
is given by minimal coupling,

\hat{H}=\frac{(\hat{p}-eA)^{2}}{2m}=\frac{(\hat{\pi}_{A})^{2}}{2m}=\frac{(\hat{\pi}_{A,x})^{2}+(\hat{\pi}_{A,y})^{2}}{2m},

(143)

where we have defined the kinetic momentum

\bm{\pi}_{A}=\bm{p}-e\bm{A},

(144)

the kinetic stress tensor is

\hat{\textit{T}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\pi}_{A,i}\hat{\pi}_{A,j}+\hat{\pi}_{A,j}\hat{\pi}_{A,i}\right).

(145)

We introduce the annihilation and creation operators

\hat{a}=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}\left(\hat{\pi}_{A,x}-i\hat{\pi}_{A,y}\right),

(146a)

\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}\left(\hat{\pi}_{A,x}+i\hat{\pi}_{A,y}\right),

(146b)

with the magnetic length
l_{B}=\sqrt{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}/(eB)}
, so that the Hamiltonian becomes

\hat{H}=\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(147)

Inserting the ladder-operator definitions (
146
) in Eq. (
147
), we have

\hat{\pi}_{A,x}=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}{\sqrt{2}l_{B}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}),

(148a)

\hat{\pi}_{A,y}=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}}{i\sqrt{2}l_{B}}(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}).

(148b)

Therefore, the stress-tensor components are

\displaystyle\hat{\textit{T}}_{xy}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{2i(l_{B})^{2}}2\left[\hat{a}^{2}-(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{2m(l_{B})^{2}}i\left[\hat{a}^{2}-(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\right].

(149)

In the same way, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\textit{T}}_{xx}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{2(l_{B})^{2}}2\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{2m(l_{B})^{2}}\left[\hat{a}^{2}+(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}+\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right]

(150)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\textit{T}}_{yy}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\frac{-\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{2(l_{B})^{2}}2\left(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{2m(l_{B})^{2}}\left[-\hat{a}^{2}-(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}+\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right].

(151)

Therefore, subtracting Eqs. (
G.2
) and (
G.2
), we also have

\hat{\textit{T}}_{xx}-\hat{\textit{T}}_{yy}=\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}^{2}}{m(l_{B})^{2}}\left[\hat{a}^{2}+(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\right].

(152)

Hence, from Eqs. (
G.2
) and (
152
) we see that the stress tensor (
G.2
) links the Landau level
l
with the one
l\pm 2
.

The stress-stress correlation function for the Hall viscosity is given by Eq. (
VIII.1.2
). For a local self-energy
\Sigma(i\omega_{n})
(which we assume to not depend on the Landau-level index
n
) and a local irreducible two-particle vertex
\Lambda(i\omega_{m},i\omega_{m}^{\prime},i{}_{n})
, the vertex corrections vanish, as shown in App.
G.1
.

We now employ the ladder rules
\hat{a}^{2}\left|l\right\rangle=\sqrt{l(l-1)}\left|l-2\right\rangle
,
(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\left|l\right\rangle=\sqrt{(l+1)(l+2)}\left|l+2\right\rangle
. Since
\hat{\textit{T}}_{xy}
and
\hat{\textit{T}}_{xy}
only allow transitions of
p=l\pm 2
, we have the transition matrix elements for
l\rightarrow l+2

\left\langle l+2\right|\hat{\textit{T}}_{xx}-\hat{\textit{T}}_{yy}\left|l\right\rangle=2\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}}{2}\sqrt{(l+1)(l+2)},

(153)

\left\langle l+2\right|\hat{\textit{T}}_{xy}\left|l\right\rangle=\frac{i\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}}{2}\sqrt{(l+1)(l+2)},

(154)

with energy difference
\epsilon_{l+2}-\epsilon_{l}=2\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}
, while for
l\rightarrow l-2

\left\langle l-2\right|\hat{\textit{T}}_{xx}-\hat{\textit{T}}_{yy}\left|l\right\rangle=2\frac{\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}}{2}\sqrt{l(l-1)},

(155)

\left\langle l-2\right|\hat{\textit{T}}_{xy}\left|l\right\rangle=-\frac{i\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}}{2}\sqrt{l(l-1)},

(156)

with energy difference
\epsilon_{l-2}-\epsilon_{l}=-2\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\omega_{c}
.

G.3

47

G.5
Interacting regime at finite frequency and

At finite temperature and frequency, using the spectral representation from Eq. (

) and (
