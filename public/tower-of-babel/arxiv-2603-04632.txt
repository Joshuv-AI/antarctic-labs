Least trimmed squares regression with missing values and cellwise outliers
Jakob Raymaekers, Peter J. Rousseeuw

Abstract
Regression is the workhorse of statistics, and is often faced with real data that contain outliers. When these are casewise outliers, that is, cases that are entirely wrong or belong to a different population, the issue can be remedied by existing casewise robust regression methods. It is another matter when cellwise outliers occur, that is, suspicious individual entries in the data matrix containing the regressors and the response. We propose a new regression method that is robust to both casewise and cellwise outliers, and handles missing values as well. Its construction allows for skewed distributions. We show that it obeys the first breakdown result for cellwise robust regression. It is also the first such method that is geared to making robust out-of-sample predictions. Its performance is studied by simulation, and it is illustrated on a substantial real dataset.



Keywords:

Casewise outliers;
Outlying cells;
Prediction;
Robust regression;
Symmetrization.

1
Introduction

Real data often contains outliers. These outlying
data deviate from the structure suggested by the
majority of the data, thereby raising concern
that they may have been generated by a different
mechanism. Whether these outlying data are errors
or informative pieces of information, it is
essential to detect them.

Our preferred approach for detecting outliers is
that of robust statistics. This approach first
fits a model to the majority of the data, and
then finds outliers by measuring their distance
from the robust fit.
Many proposals for robust regression have been
made, such as the least trimmed squares (LTS)
method
(
Rousseeuw 1984
)
. LTS minimizes
the sum of squared residuals on a subset of the
cases, rather than on all of them as in OLS.
By focusing on a subset, LTS finds the central
part of the data that fits most tightly.
Suspicious points are then detected by looking
at the residuals with respect to this fit.
Alternative approaches are S-estimators

(
Rousseeuw and
Yohai 1984
)
and MM-estimators

(
Yohai 1987
)
. For a book-length
treatment see
Rousseeuw and
Leroy 1987
.

What all these methods have in common, is that
they consider casewise outliers. This assumes
that a case is either entirely clean, or
entirely suspicious. More recently, a different
paradigm has gained traction in the development of
robust statistics. Instead of assuming outlying
cases, it assumes instead that some cells in the
data matrix (containing the predictors and the
response) have been replaced by suspicious
values. This cellwise paradigm was formalized
by
Alqallaf et al. 2009
.
Raymaekers and
Rousseeuw 2025
review
the challenges of this approach, and provide a
summary of statistical settings in which
progress has been made, such as cellwise robust
estimation of covariance matrices.

Cellwise robust regression methods are
substantially less established. The first proposal
was by
Leung
et al. 2016
. They proposed to
first estimate a cellwise robust covariance
matrix on the predictors and response combined.
Next, they inverted the submatrix corresponding
with the predictors and multiplied it with the
column corresponding with the covariance between
predictors and response. This results in
an estimate of the regression coefficients that
performs quite well, but depends strongly on the
assumption that the uncontaminated data are close
to Gaussian.
A second proposal is the Shooting S estimator by
Öllerer
et al. 2016
. It adopts the
coordinate descent algorithm for linear
regression and incorporates robustness in each
of the fits. By working per coordinate, a
sparsity penalty can be integrated

(
Bottmer
et al. 2022
)
.
Finally, a recent proposal is the CR-Lasso
algorithm by
Su
et al. 2024
. It optimizes an
elegant objective function inspired by mean-shift
penalized objectives for casewise robust
regression
(
She and
Owen 2011
)
.

These three existing regression methods robustly
estimate regression coefficients, but only
Shooting S provides robust in-sample predictions,
and none of them provide robust out-of-sample
predictions. One could compute out-of-sample
predictions by treating the new regressor data
as entirely free of cellwise outliers, but that
is a questionable assumption in practice.
We propose a new method consisting of two steps.
In the first step, we clean the regressors of
cellwise outliers and impute missing values.
In the second step, we perform casewise robust
regression to deal with outliers in the response.
To deal with potential skewness in the data, we
symmetrize it by working on pairwise differences.
Out-of-sample, we provide a natural prediction
for a new case by cleaning it first, and only
afterward multiplying by the robustly estimated
coefficients.

Figure 1:
A toy example to illustrate the basic
idea of the method.

The basic idea of this approach is illustrated
on a small toy example in Figure
1
,
with two regressors
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
and the
response
y
. The majority of the data points
(in black) nicely follows the linear model, but
on the right we also see an outlier with a high

x_{1}
value. If we were running OLS, it would
exert heavy leverage and cause the fitted plane
to become almost horizontal. So the first job
is to obtain a robust fit that is unaffected
by the outlier. The method does that, yielding
the light green plane.
The second job is to make a prediction in the
outlier. Just plugging in the regression
coefficients would follow the vertical dashed
red line upward, yielding a huge
\widehat{y}
. Instead
the method flags the first cell of the outlier,
and imputes it along the horizontal dashed
blue line, in the way the EM algorithm would.
And finally it makes a prediction from this
clean datapoint, following the vertical
dashed blue line upward, yielding a reasonable
prediction.
This mechanism is the same whether we make
the prediction in an in-sample point

(x_{1i},x_{2i})
or an out-of-sample
(new) point
(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*})
.

follows. Section
2
describes
preliminaries including the exact setup and
a cellwise robust covariance estimator.
Section
3
introduces the
proposed two-step estimator and proves its
good breakdown behavior.
Section
4
provides more
information on its algorithm.
Section
5
contains an
empirical study, followed by a real data
application in Section
6
.
Section
7
concludes.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the setup and some
preliminary tools needed to construct our new
method.

2.1
Data symmetrization

We assume that the clean data is generated by the
linear model

Y=\alpha+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}+E

(1)

where the random variable
Y
is the response,

\alpha
is the unknown intercept,

\bm{\beta}
is the vector of unknown slope parameters,

\bm{X}
is the random vector of the regressors with
location
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}}
and scatter matrix

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}}
, and
E
is an i.i.d. error term.

For reasons that will be explained later,
it may be useful to consider a
transformation of the regression
model (
1
). We can consider
an independent copy
(\bm{X}^{\prime},E^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
of

(\bm{X},E,Y)
, from which it follows that

Y-Y^{\prime}=\bm{\beta}^{\top}(\bm{X}-\bm{X}^{\prime})+E-E^{\prime}\;.

If we denote the variables
\widetilde{Y}:=Y-Y^{\prime}
,

\bm{\widetilde{X}}:=\bm{X}-\bm{X}^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{E}:=E-E^{\prime}

we thus obtain

\widetilde{Y}=\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\widetilde{X}}+\widetilde{E}

(2)

in which all variables are symmetric. Note that
the slope parameter vector remains the same, and
that the intercept
\alpha
vanishes (but it
can easily be estimated afterward).
Because the symmetrized variables
\widetilde{Y}
,
\bm{\widetilde{X}}

and
\widetilde{E}
are defined as convolutions, they tend
to be a bit closer to Gaussian than the raw
variables.

If the second moments exist, we can see
that the transformed covariates
\bm{\widetilde{X}}
now
have the property that
\E[\bm{\widetilde{X}}]=\bm{0}

and
\E[\bm{\widetilde{X}}\,\bm{\widetilde{X}}^{\top}]=2\E[XX^{\top}]
.
In other words, the covariance matrix of
the symmetrized data is twice the
covariance matrix of the original
\bm{X}
.
On the other hand, the information of the
location of
\bm{X}
is lost in the
symmetrization.

The above reasoning is for populations.
When we have a finite univariate sample

\bm{y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
of
Y
we
construct the symmetrized dataset

{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{y})=\{y_{j}-y_{i}\,;\,i\neq j\}

that contains
n(n-1)
entries.
It is well-known that the empirical
distribution of
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{y})
is a good
approximation of the distribution of

Y-Y^{\prime}
. We also symmetrize

\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\}
.

2.2
The cellwise robust MCD method

The proposed regression estimator will make use
of the cellwise minimum covariance determinant
(cellMCD) estimator by
Raymaekers and
Rousseeuw 2024a
. The
cellMCD method estimates the location and the
covariance matrix of a multivariate dataset, and
flags outlying cells. It explicitly uses the
linear relationships between the variables to
find the suspicious cells, and works best when
the underlying distribution of the data before
contamination is roughly elliptical. We proceed
by a more detailed description of the method.

Suppose we observe
n
cases

\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
from a
d
-variate
population with unknown center
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}}
and
covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}}
. Denote by

\bm{W}
an unknown
n\times d
binary matrix
in which
\bm{W}_{ij}=1
indicates the clean
cells, and
\bm{W}_{ij}=0
the contaminated
cells. The cellMCD method then estimates

\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}}
, and
\bm{W}
by
maximizing the observed likelihood of the
clean cells, under a penalty ensuring
\bm{W}

does not contain too many zeros.
More specifically, cellMCD minimizes the
objective

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\big(\ln|\bm{\Sigma}^{(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})}|+d(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})\ln(2\pi)+\MD^{2}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}}\,,\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})\,\big)}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}||\bm{1}_{d}-\bm{W}_{.j}||_{0}

(3)

\displaystyle\mbox{ under the constraints }\lambda_{d}(\bm{\Sigma})\geqslant a\mbox{ and }||\bm{W}_{.j}||_{0}\geqslant h\mbox{ for all }j=1,\ldots,d

with respect to
\bm{\mu}
,
\bm{\Sigma}
, and
\bm{W}
.
The first sum of this objective function is
minus twice the log likelihood of the clean
cells, in which the row
\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}}

indicates that the log-determinant, the
dimension and the Mahalanobis distances are
calculated only on the variables
j
with

\bm{W}_{ij}=1
. The second sum ensures that
not too many cells are flagged as outlying.
Indeed, the notation

||\bm{1}_{d}-\bm{W}_{.j}||_{0}

stands for the number of nonzero entries in
the vector
\bm{1}_{d}-\bm{W}_{.j}
which is
the number of zero weights in column
j
of

\bm{W}
, so the number of flagged cells in
column
j
of
\bm{X}
. The constants

q_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
are computed
from the desired percentage (by default 1%)
of flagged cells in the absence of
contamination. At the same time we keep the
robustness constraints that

||\bm{W}_{.j}||_{0}\geqslant h\approx 0.75n

and
\lambda_{d}(\bm{\Sigma})\geqslant a>0
, that
is, the smallest eigenvalue of the estimated
covariance matrix is bounded from below.

The cellMCD estimator minimizes the objective
of (
3
) through block-coordinate
descent. More precisely, we iterate between
updating
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
and
\bm{W}
while
keeping the other quantity fixed. Updating

\bm{W}
for fixed
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
boils down
to a thresholding rule based on the
contribution of each cell to the observed
likelihood. Updating
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
for
fixed
\bm{W}
works by optimizing the observed
likelihood as is done in missing value
problems. It has been proved that each step
of the algorithm lowers the objective, so
the algorithm always converges.

2.3
Least trimmed squares regression

In addition the the cellMCD method, we will
also apply Least Trimmed Squares (LTS)
regression
(
Rousseeuw 1984
)
.
Suppose we want to fit a regression model

y_{i}=\alpha+\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\mbox{noise}_{i}

(4)

to
n
data points

(\bm{x}_{1}\,,y_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{n}\,,y_{n})

where the
\bm{x}_{i}
are
d
-variate.
The classical least squares (OLS) estimator
estimates
\alpha
and
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
by minimizing
the sum of all squared residuals
r_{i}^{2}
,
where
r_{i}=y_{i}-\widehat{\alpha}-\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}
.
OLS is not robust because the effect of a case

(\bm{x}_{i}\,,y_{i})
on the objective is unbounded.
LTS regression instead minimizes a trimmed
sum of squared residuals

(\widehat{\alpha},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})_{\mbox{\tiny LTS}}=\argmin_{\alpha,\bm{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{h}r_{(i)}^{2}

where
r_{(1)}^{2}\leqslant r_{(2)}^{2}\leqslant\ldots\leq r_{(n)}^{2}
denote the ordered
squared residuals. A typical choice of
h

is
0.75n
, so that the LTS fit can withstand
up to 25% of outlying cases. Also this
algorithm works by alternating between
updating the
h
-subset and the regression
coefficients.

3
Methodology

3.1
Estimating the regression coefficients

The proposed regression estimator consists
of two steps.
In the first step we clean the data matrix of
the regressors, by imputing missing values as
well as outlying cells. This is done without
using information about the response variable.
In the second step we regress the response on
this cleaned data matrix to obtain estimates
of
\alpha
and
\bm{\beta}
. This regression is
carried out by the robust LTS method, to
mitigate the effect of potentially outlying
response values. The resulting cellwise robust
version of LTS we call

cellLTS regression
.

We rewrite the regression
model (
4
) in matrix form as

\bm{y}=\alpha+\bm{X}\bm{\beta}+\mbox{noise}

(5)

where the
n\times 1
column vector

\bm{y}
contains the response,
and the
n\times d
matrix
\bm{X}

has the regressors. The
d\times 1

column vector
\bm{\beta}
contains the
regression slopes, and
\alpha
is the
intercept. In this formulation the
matrix
\bm{X}
does not contain a
column of 1’s because the intercept is
a separate term.
We proceed by describing the steps of
the proposed cellLTS method.

Step 1.
Cleaning the
data matrix of regressors.

(a)

Run the cellMCD algorithm described
in Section
2.2
to obtain a
cellwise robust estimate
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{X}
of the
scatter matrix of the predictors. Instead of
running cellMCD on
\bm{X}
, however, we apply
it to the symmetrized data
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{X})

obtained by symmetrizing each of its columns,
and afterward we divide the resulting scatter
matrix by 2. For this estimation we run
cellMCD with
\widetilde{h}:=h(h-1)
and center fixed
at the origin, since we know that
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{X})

is symmetric about
\bm{0}
. We use

{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{X})
because cellMCD was originally
developed for Gaussian (or at least roughly
elliptical) data before contamination, and
symmetrization generally brings the
distribution closer to Gaussianity.

(b)

Obtain
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{X}
by applying a robust
univariate location estimate to each
column of
\bm{X}
.

(c)

Obtain
\bm{W}_{\bm{X}}
. We first compute
the matrix

\bm{Z}\coloneqq(\diag(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{X}))^{-1/2}(\bm{X}-\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{X}\bm{1}_{n})
. Its cells can
be seen as robust
z
-scores of the cells
in
\bm{X}
. Next we initialize
\bm{W}_{\bm{X}}

by setting
(\bm{W}^{(0)}_{\bm{X}})_{ij}
to
zero whenever
|z_{ij}|>\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{1,0.99}}\approx 2.57
,
and to 1 otherwise. Starting from

\bm{W}^{(0)}_{\bm{X}}
we obtain the final

\bm{W}_{\bm{X}}
by iterations that optimize
the cellMCD objective with respect to

\bm{W}_{\bm{X}}
, keeping
\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{\bm{X}}
and

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{X}}
fixed.

(d)

Obtain
\bm{\widehat{X}}
by imputing the cells
with
(\bm{W}_{\bm{X}})_{ij}=0
with their
best linear prediction according to

(\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{X}
,
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{X})
. More precisely,

\widehat{x}_{ij}=\begin{cases}x_{ij}\qquad\mbox{ if }\;\;\bm{W}_{ij}=1\\
\widehat{\mu}_{j}+\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{j,(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})}\left(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}^{(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})}\right)^{-1}(\bm{x}_{i}^{(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})}-\bm{\widehat{\mu}}^{(\bm{W}_{i\bm{\cdot}})})\;\;\mbox{ if }\;\;\bm{W}_{ij}=0\;.\\
\end{cases}

(6)

Some remarks on this approach are in order:

•

We could refine the robust location estimate of
step (b) by using it as a starting value for cellMCD
iteration steps with fixed
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{X}
. This requires
more computation, but in our experiments it did not
improve the final estimator.

•

The justification for step (c)
is twofold. One, we need an imputation for
\bm{X}

and not
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{X})
, so we cannot use the output
of step (b) directly. Two, we want to flag outliers
in-sample and out-of-sample in a uniform way, so
we use a start that is the same in both situations.

Step 2.
To obtain robust estimates
of the regression coefficients
\alpha
and
\bm{\beta}
,
we do the following.

(e)

We symmetrize the response variable
\bm{y}

to
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{y})
, which helps when the response
is skewed. We then standardize
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{y})
by
dividing it by the scale estimate of its
univariate MCD with the same
\widetilde{h}=h(h-1)

as before, and denote the result as
\bm{\widetilde{y}}
.

We also symmetrize the imputed

\bm{\widehat{X}}
of (
6
) to
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{\widehat{X}})
.
We do not include a column of 1’s because the
symmetrized model has no intercept.
We standardize
{\mbox{Sym}}(\bm{\widehat{X}})
by dividing
each column
j
by

\sqrt{(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{X}})_{jj}}
, and denote
the resulting regressor matrix as
\bm{\widetilde{X}}
.

(f)

Both
\bm{\widetilde{X}}
and
\bm{\widetilde{y}}
have

\widetilde{n}:=n(n-1)

rows, and we will consider
\widetilde{h}
-subsets

\widetilde{H}
of
\{1,\ldots,\widetilde{n}\}
with

\widetilde{h}=h(h-1)
elements.
The cellLTS estimator of
\bm{\beta}
is defined as

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}(\widetilde{H})}\Big(\sum_{\ell\in\widetilde{H}}r^{2}(\widetilde{y}_{\ell},\bm{\widetilde{x}}_{\ell},\bm{\beta})+\lambda||\bm{\beta}||_{2}^{2}\Big)

(7)

with residuals

r(\widetilde{y}_{\ell},\bm{\widetilde{x}}_{\ell},\bm{\beta}):=\widetilde{y}_{\ell}-\bm{\widetilde{x}}_{\ell}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
.
The
\lambda
in the penalty term is a
small positive number to avoid
collinearity problems.
Note that penalizing
||\bm{\beta}||_{2}
makes
sense because the components of

\bm{\beta}
are comparable to each other.
This is due to the scaling of the variables
in step (e), which made them unitless and
gave them similar scales. This also makes
it possible to set a default value for

\lambda
, like
10^{-4}
.

(g)

The intercept of the regression of
the unsymmetrized
\bm{y}
on
\bm{\widehat{X}}

is estimated as follows. First
compute the pseudo residuals

\bm{r}^{*}=\bm{y}-\bm{\widehat{X}}\,\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
.
Then apply the univariate robust MCD
to them, and set the intercept
\widehat{\alpha}

equal to its location estimate.

(h)

Transform the regression coefficients
to undo the standardization in step (e).

3.2
Computing out-of-sample predictions

In the previous section we have explained the
two-step estimator to obtain robust estimates
for the regression coefficients. We now discuss
how to obtain in-sample and out-of-sample
predictions.

The in-sample predictions are straightforward
once the algorithm has run. For the
i
-th
observation, the predicted
\widehat{y}_{i}
is given by

\widehat{y}_{i}=\widehat{\alpha}+\bm{\widehat{x}}_{i}\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
where the
imputed
\bm{\widehat{x}}_{i}
was computed earlier in
step (d).

Now suppose we want to obtain the out-of-sample
prediction at a new
\bm{x}
. Note that this new

\bm{x}
may itself contain missing values and/or
outlying cells! Therefore we cannot just
use
\widehat{\alpha}+\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
, as we saw
in Figure
1
.
Instead we first need to compute the
corresponding cleaned case
\bm{\widehat{x}}
.
We begin by flagging cells of
\bm{x}

by optimizing the cellMCD objective
of (
3
) in which we keep the
estimates
(\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{\bm{X}})

fixed. We initiate this iterative
optimization by applying the marginal
detection rule to

\bm{z}\coloneqq(\diag(\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{X}))^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{\widehat{\mu}}_{X})
. Then we
obtain
\bm{\widehat{x}}
by applying the imputation
rule (
6
) to
\bm{x}
instead of

\bm{x}_{i}
. Finally, the out-of-sample
prediction is given by

\widehat{y}=\widehat{\alpha}+\bm{\widehat{x}}^{\top}\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
. The
ability to compute reasonable predictions
for partly corrupted out-of-sample inputs is
a major advantage of the cellLTS approach.

Note that, thanks to step (c) of the algorithm
which starts from the same marginal detection
rule that we used for
\bm{W}_{\bm{X}}
, we treat
in-sample
prediction and out-of-sample prediction uniformly.
In other words, if we input one of the in-sample

\bm{x}_{i}
as a new
\bm{x}
, the prediction will be
the same as before.

3.3
Breakdown

For the breakdown value we use
model (
1
) but write it
a little differently as
\bm{y}=[\bm{1}_{n}\,;\bm{X}]\,\bm{\gamma}+\mbox{noise}

where
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is the

n\times 1
column vector of responses.
The
(d+1)\times 1
vector

\bm{\gamma}=(\alpha,\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\bm{\top}}
combines
the intercept and the slopes.
We also combine the actual data
in the
n\times(d+1)
matrix

\bm{Z}=[\bm{X}\,;\,\bm{y}]
whose
i
-th row
is
(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ip},y_{i})
.

We now contaminate the dataset
\bm{Z}
.
Replacing at most
m
cells in each
column of
\bm{Z}
by arbitrary values
yields a contaminated matrix
denoted as
\bm{Z}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}}
.
We define the
cellwise
finite-sample breakdown value
of the
regression estimator
\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}
at
the dataset
\bm{Z}
as

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\gamma}},\bm{Z}):=\min\left\{\frac{m}{n}:\;\sup_{\bm{Z}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}}}{\left|\left|\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{Z}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}})-\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}(\bm{Z})\right|\right|}=\infty\right\}.

(8)

That is, it is the smallest fraction

m/n
that can move the vector of
regression coefficients
\bm{\widehat{\gamma}}

arbitrarily far away.

The
casewise
breakdown value is
defined similarly, but instead of
replacing at most
m
cells in each
column, it replaces at most
m
entire
rows of
\bm{Z}
.
Casewise breakdown values of regression
methods have been studied extensively
in the past, see e.g. the book by

Rousseeuw and
Leroy 1987
.

It turns out that the use of
symmetrization comes at the cost
of a reduced breakdown value.
If
m
out of
n
cells are contaminated
in a given variable, among the

n(n-1)
pairs,
n(n-1)-(n-m)(n-m-1)

are contaminated.
From Proposition 2 in
(
Raymaekers and
Rousseeuw 2024a
)

we know that the breakdown value of
cellMCD is at most

(\widetilde{n}-\lfloor\widetilde{n}/2\rfloor)/\widetilde{n}

so we have breakdown when the number of
contaminated cells per column is at least

\widetilde{n}-\lfloor\widetilde{n}/2\rfloor=n(n-1)-\lfloor n(n-1)/2\rfloor=n-n(n-1)/2=n(n-1)/2
. The
corresponding
m
is given by the
quadratic equation

\displaystyle n(n-1)-(n-m)(n-m-1)=n(n-1)/2

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow m=\frac{2n-1\pm\sqrt{2n^{2}-2n+1}}{2}\;.

Because
m<n
the valid root is the
smaller one, yielding
m^{*}=\lceil(2n-1-\sqrt{2n^{2}-2n+1})/2\rceil.

Note that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(m^{*}/n)=1-1/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.2929
,
like the breakdown value of the Theil-Sen
simple regression. So whenever the
fraction of contaminated cells per
variable is below
29\%
, the
symmetrized data has fewer than 50%
of contaminated cells in each variable.

Proposition.

If
\bm{X}
and
\bm{y}
are in general
position and
h>n-m^{*}
,
then the breakdown value of cellLTS is

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{\widehat{\gamma}},\bm{Z})=(n-h+1)/n
.

The highest attainable breakdown value of
cellLTS is thus
m^{*}/n
which is
roughly
1-1/\sqrt{2}\approx 29\%
.
This is the first time a breakdown result
was obtained for a
cellwise
robust
regression method. We also have

Corollary.

The casewise
breakdown value of cellLTS equals
its cellwise breakdown value.

Both proofs can be found in
Section
A
of the Supplementary
Material.

The proposed method could easily be
extended by replacing the LTS in step (f) by
another regression estimator with high casewise
breakdown value, provided the penalty term is
preserved. This would change the algorithm but
not the cellwise breakdown value. Likewise we
could replace the
||\bm{\beta}||_{2}^{2}
in
(
7
) by
||\bm{\beta}||_{1}
.

4
More about the algorithm

4.1
Optimizing the regression objective
function

Note that (
7
) can be rewritten
by extending the dataset
[\bm{\widetilde{X}}\,;\,\bm{\widetilde{y}}]

by adding
d
rows of length
d+1
, namely

(\lambda,0,\ldots,0,0)

up to
(0,0,\ldots,\lambda,0)
.
The regressor columns of this extra

d\times(d+1)
matrix are the basis vectors
multiplied by
\lambda
, and
their responses are all zero.
If we now denote

\widetilde{H}^{*}=\{\widetilde{n}+1,\ldots,\widetilde{n}+d\}

we can write the optimization problem in
(
7
) as

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}(\widetilde{H})}\sum_{\ell\in\widetilde{H}\cup\widetilde{H}^{*}}r^{2}(\widetilde{y}_{\ell},\bm{\widetilde{x}}_{\ell},\bm{\beta})

(9)

where
\widetilde{H}
is any
\widetilde{h}
-subset of

\{1,\ldots,\widetilde{n}\}
and
\widetilde{H}^{*}
stays fixed.
In this way the penalty term becomes part
of an LTS objective function, with the
constraint that the last
d
cases must
belong to the subset. This allows us to
carry out the optimization by a
modification of the FastLTS
algorithm of
Rousseeuw and
Van Driessen 2006

in which the C-step is constrained to
contain the last
d
cases, so it is only
allowed to replace the other
\widetilde{h}
cases.
This C-step still lowers the objective
in every step, until it converges.
Afterward we assign weights to the actual

n
cases just as in FastLTS, and then
carry out a weighted least squares fit
with the same ridge penalty as
in (
7
).

4.2
Faster symmetrization

The symmetrization strategy laid out previously
renders the data more symmetric, which improves
the performance of estimators such as cellMCD.
In principle we can compute all
n(n-1)
pairwise
differences and work with that large set.
Alternatively, we can consider the smaller set
of differences

\widetilde{Y}_{N}=\{y_{\ell}-y_{i}\,;\,1\leqslant i<\ell\leqslant n\}

(10)

of size
N=n(n-1)/2
, so the set of all
differences equals
\widetilde{Y}_{N}\,\cup\,-\widetilde{Y}_{N}
.
We can define
\bm{\widetilde{X}}_{N}
analogously, so the
symmetrized regression dataset becomes

(\bm{\widetilde{X}}_{N},\widetilde{Y}_{N})\cup(-\bm{\widetilde{X}}_{N},-\widetilde{Y}_{N})
. Most
estimators
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
of the slope vector of
a regression without intercept, including
LTS, satisfy the natural condition that

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}((\bm{X},Y)\cup(-\bm{X},-Y))=\bm{\widehat{\beta}}(\bm{X},Y)

so that it suffices to compute

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{N},Y_{N})
on
N
datapoints instead of

2N
. Analogously, most estimators
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}
of a
covariance matrix with center
\bm{0}
, including
cellMCD, satisfy

\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{X}\cup-\bm{X})=\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{X})

so it suffices to compute
\bm{\widehat{\Sigma}}(\bm{\widetilde{X}}_{N})
.

Still,
N
is of the order
O(n^{2})
, which creates
issues for large sample sizes
n
. It would be
better if
N
were less than quadratic in
n
.
In the setting of casewise robust M-estimation
of covariance,
Dümbgen and
Nordhausen 2024
studied a
smaller set of pairwise differences.
In our notation it is

\widetilde{Y}_{n,k}=\{y_{i+j}-y_{i}\,;\,1\leqslant i\leqslant n\,,\,1\leqslant j\leqslant k\}

(11)

for some
1\leqslant k<n
. Here
y_{i+j}
is
interpreted in a cyclic way,
meaning that
y_{n+s}:=y_{s}
for

1\leqslant s\leqslant n
. To avoid
systematic effects, they first randomly permute
the indices
1,\ldots,n
. A major advantage of
the set
\widetilde{Y}_{n,k}
is that each case
i

occurs in the same number (
2k
) of pairwise
differences, so each case gets the same weight.
The set
\widetilde{Y}_{n,k}
has
nk
elements, so
for fixed
k
it is
O(n)
.

Instead of
\widetilde{Y}_{n,k}
we propose to draw

k
random permutations
\widetilde{Y}^{j}
and each
time generate the
n
pairs in
\widetilde{Y}_{n,1}^{j}
,
and then use the combined set of pairs

\widetilde{Y}_{n,1}^{1}\cup\ldots\cup\widetilde{Y}_{n,1}^{k}\;.

(12)

This set has the same number of pairs and
each case still has the same weight, but it
is likely to yield more stable results
because an unfortunate permutation will only
have a small effect.

Dümbgen and
Nordhausen 2024
found empirically that when
replacing
\bm{\widetilde{X}}_{N}
by
\bm{\widetilde{X}}_{n,k}
the result
of their estimator was sufficiently close already
for
k=20
. The next section will confirm
that this is also true for (
12
) in
cellLTS.

Simulation study

As the cellwise regression setup does not
allow for affine equivariance, and the
performance of regression methods depends
on the distribution of the regressors, we
have to consider a variety of simulation
setups.

For the predictors, we consider the pairs

(n,d)=(100,10)
and
(400,20)
of
sample size and dimension, in line with the
simulation study of
Raymaekers and
Rousseeuw 2024a
for
covariance estimators. For the distribution
of the clean data we consider the multivariate
Gaussian distribution centered at the origin
with covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mbox{\scriptsize A09}}
given
by
(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mbox{\scriptsize A09}})_{ij}=(-0.9)^{|i-j|}
.
We also use the random covariance matrix

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mbox{\scriptsize ALYZ}}
constructed by

Agostinelli et al. 2015
. In addition, we
generate two setups with skewed predictors
by first sampling independent
centered exponential variables with rate 1,
or standard lognormal variables. Then we
linearly transform these data so that their
classical covariance matrix becomes

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mbox{\scriptsize A09}}
or
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mbox{\scriptsize ALYZ}}
.

To generate a response, we set the true
coefficients equal to
\bm{\beta}=[d\;\;\;d\!-\!1\;\;\;\cdots\;\;\;2\;\;\;1]
and
set the variance of the error terms to be

where we choose

by default.
Then we generate

e_{i}
follows a Gaussian distribution with

\sigma^{2}
.

To generate cellwise outliers, we start by
drawing a fraction
\varepsilon=20\%
of cells
at random in each column of the clean data

Each contaminated cell is then put at a
value
\gamma=1,\ldots,10

for
\varepsilon=10\%
are shown in
Section

for

7
Conclusion

In this paper we have proposed the cellLTS
regression method for handling data that may
contain casewise outliers, cellwise outliers,
and missing values. It is a two-step procedure
that first applies a cellwise robust estimator
of multivariate location and covariance to the
regressor data, which allows it to clean away
cellwise outliers and to impute missing values.
In the second step it regresses the response on
the cleaned regressors by the casewise robust
least trimmed squares method. In both steps a
symmetrization is applied, to help the method
deal with skewed data.

An additional feature is that cellLTS contains
an algorithm for robust out-of-sample
prediction. When presented with a new set of
regressor values, it does not automatically
assume that all of its entries are
well-behaved. Instead it investigates whether
some of its cells should be flagged and
imputed, and it also imputes missing values.

Simulations indicate that cellLTS outperforms
the three existing methods for cellwise
robust regression, both in terms of
accuracy of the regression coefficients and
in terms of prediction performance as
measured by mean squared error. Finally, the
method is illustrated on a substantial real
data set that has some rough edges. In the
main text the data is analyzed with only
five regressors, and in the Supplementary
Material a longer analysis is performed
that takes all 27 regressors into account.

by
\widehat{\sigma}_{y}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}}
, so it multiplies

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*}
by
1/\widehat{\sigma}_{y}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}}\leqslant 1/a_{y}
.
Moreover, transforming back the standardization
of the
j
-regressor in step (e) multiplies it
by
\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{\,{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}}}
, which
multiplies the slope
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}_{j}
by

1/\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{\,{\mbox{\scriptsize{con}}}}\leqslant 1/a_{j}
.
Doing this for all slopes multiplies the
norm of
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*}
by at most
1/a_{\min}

where
a_{\min}:=\min(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})>0
.
Combining these effects yields

||\bm{\widehat{\beta}}||_{2}\leqslant\frac{c_{y}\sqrt{M/\lambda}}{a_{\min}a_{y}}\leqslant\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{y}\sqrt{M/\lambda}}{a_{\min}a_{y}^{2}}\,,

in which the finite upper bound only depends
on the uncontaminated data.
Therefore
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
does not break down,
hence
\varepsilon^{*}_{n}\geqslant(n-h+1)/n
.
Since we saw before that

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}\leqslant(n-h+1)/n
, the breakdown
value of cellLTS equals
(n-h+1)/n
.

Proof of the Corollary.

This is because the casewise breakdown value
is defined by replacing
m
entire rows of
the data, which is a special case of
replacing
m
cells in each column. So the
casewise breakdown value is always at least
as high as the cellwise one. The casewise
breakdown value of cellLTS is also not
higher than this, because the reasoning in
the above proof showing that

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}\leqslant(n-h+1)/n

still holds when replacing cases.

Esmeralda County, Nevada has fewer than 1000
inhabitants, making all census data very noisy.
Forest County in Pennsylvania has a 2-to-1
ratio of men to women. The reason is that about
a third of its population are inmates at the

relatively low education levels.
Edwards County in Texas is another tiny
rural county, where the data may not be very
reliable. We see that the education levels are

rates and death rates are low. This may be a
statistical anomaly, as the more recent
incidence rate for the county is 545.6 according

Bent County in Colorado is similar to Forest
County in that it also has a large correctional
facility with about 1500 inmates. Given its
total population of about 6000, this is a large
fraction. The Alaskan counties in this cellmap
have been discussed before, and show deviating
behavior due to remoteness, high fraction of
native population, and overall high incomes
with low unemployment. Finally, Pitkin county
is also an interesting case. It has the lowest
death rate due to cancer in the dataset. Even in
recent years, it ranks among the lowest in the
whole of the US. There are several explanations,
including the facts that Pitkin is generally
wealthy with a high median income, low poverty,
and high private insurance coverage. Moreover,
Pitkin has a lot of seasonal homes for
skiing, and such counties tend to have low
cancer death rates. The reason may be that
seriously ill people often prefer to receive
treatment in or near their main home.
