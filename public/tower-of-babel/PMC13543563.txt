A study on the effects of CCRT on cognitive function and blood biomarkers in community-dwelling older adults with MCI
Authors: Zhenkun Tan, Junjiao Ping, Haibo Zhang, Ying Zhang, Chuijia Kong, Yingyu Lu, Baofan Liu, Tingyun Jiang, Xinxia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the effects of Computerized Cognitive Remediation Therapy (CCRT) on cognitive function and blood biomarker levels in community-dwelling older adults with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), and to provide objective evidence for evaluating the effectiveness of CCRT.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial included 68 participants who completed the intervention and were included in the final analysis (37 in the intervention group and 31 in the control group). The control group received health education, while the intervention group received both health education and a 12-week CCRT intervention. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) before and after the intervention. Plasma levels of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and phosphorylated Tau181 (P-tau181) were also measured at baseline and post-intervention. Linear mixed-effects models and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were used to examine the effects of CCRT on cognitive function and biomarker levels, as well as the predictive value of biomarkers for cognitive improvement.ResultsAfter the 12-week intervention, the intervention group showed significantly greater improvements in MMSE and MoCA total scores compared to the control group (MMSE: P = 0.007; MoCA: P = 0.001). Significant improvements were also observed in memory (MMSE: P = 0.027), language (MMSE: P = 0.018; MoCA: P < 0.001), and attention (MoCA: P < 0.001). Plasma GFAP levels significantly decreased in the intervention group compared to the control group (P < 0.001), while no significant difference was found in P-tau181 levels (P = 0.501). ROC curve analysis showed that a reduction in GFAP levels significantly predicted cognitive improvement in MCI patients (AUC = 0.805, P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that a 12-week CCRT intervention can significantly improve cognitive function and reduce GFAP levels in community-dwelling older adults with MCI. The change in GFAP is associated with cognitive improvement, suggesting its potential as an objective biomarker for evaluating intervention effectiveness.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry，Trial Registration Number: ChiCTR2500107961，Registration Date: August 21, 2025.Keywords: Mild Cognitive Impairment, Cognitive Function, Biomarkers, Computerized Cognitive Remediation Therapy

Introduction

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by subjective and objective declines in cognitive function that exceed what is expected for an individual’s age and educational background, but do not meet the diagnostic criteria for dementia [1–3]. MCI is commonly regarded as a transitional stage between normal aging and dementia and is associated with a higher risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [1, 4, 5]. With the global aging population, the disease burden of dementia and MCI is rapidly increasing [6]. According to a 2022 report, the prevalence of MCI among community-dwelling individuals aged 50 years and older worldwide has reached 15.56% [7]. Alzheimer’s disease is now the fifth leading cause of death among people aged 65 years and older in the United States [8]. The global economic cost of dementia is projected to reach USD 2.54 trillion by 2030 and increase to USD 9.12 trillion by 2050 [9]. Both MCI and AD already impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems and society [10–12]. Despite the elevated risk of progression to dementia, a proportion of individuals with MCI remain cognitively stable or may even revert to normal cognition over time [4, 13]. Therefore, early identification and timely intervention in MCI are of critical importance for the prevention and delay of dementia onset.

Current research and clinical guidelines indicate that there are no FDA-approved medications specifically for the treatment of Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). Although some drugs are used in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), their efficacy in MCI patients has not been fully established. Moreover, the high cost of pharmacological treatments limits their widespread application in community-dwelling individuals with MCI [14]. As a result, non-pharmacological interventions remain a key area of focus in MCI management.Non-pharmacological approaches for MCI include dietary interventions, physical exercise, traditional Chinese medicine therapies, conventional cognitive training, and computerized cognitive interventions. Among these, Computerized Cognitive Remediation Therapy (CCRT) has emerged in recent years as a promising non-pharmacological strategy for the early intervention of MCI and dementia [15]. A growing body of evidence supports the efficacy of CCRT in improving cognitive function among individuals with MCI [16–21]. CCRT utilizes computer-assisted training programs to provide targeted and repetitive practice in specific cognitive domains, aiming to improve or slow the decline of cognitive function in individuals with neurodegenerative conditions [22–25]. Compared with traditional cognitive interventions, CCRT offers several advantages: it delivers standardized content, provides real-time feedback, ensures high reproducibility, and facilitates consistent intervention quality. It is also user-friendly, particularly suitable for older adults, and has low implementation costs, making it feasible for broad use in community and home-based settings. In addition, CCRT can be administered remotely, aligning with the dual trends of population aging and digital health development.

Currently, the evaluation of CCRT efficacy primarily relies on cognitive assessments and neuroimaging data [26–29]. However, cognitive scales may lack objectivity in capturing subtle treatment effects, and neuroimaging assessments are often complex and expensive. Therefore, there is a need to identify more economical, accessible, and objective biomarkers to evaluate the effects of CCRT in individuals with MCI. With the rapid advancement of blood biomarker detection technologies, it has become increasingly feasible to detect neurodegeneration-related markers in the blood with high sensitivity and specificity [30–32].

Among candidate biomarkers, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker of astrocytic activation, has shown promise across the AD continuum. Circulating GFAP is elevated in MCI and AD and has been reported to correlate with cognitive impairment [33], and it is also associated with amyloid pathology and may change early in the disease process [34]. In contrast, plasma phosphorylated tau 181 (p-Tau181) is a robust marker of tau pathology with established diagnostic and prognostic performance for AD and related disorders [35–38]. Although these biomarkers are increasingly used for disease characterization and risk stratification, evidence on whether GFAP and p-Tau181 change in response to CCRT in community-dwelling MCI populations is scarce. Accordingly, this randomized controlled trial investigated the effects of a 12-week community-based CCRT program on cognitive performance (MMSE and MoCA) and plasma biomarkers (GFAP and p-Tau181) in older adults with MCI. We further examined whether changes in GFAP and p-Tau181 were associated with cognitive improvement and explored the predictive value of biomarker changes for post-intervention cognitive recovery. Clarifying blood biomarker correlates of response may help establish more objective endpoints for CCRT and support future implementation in community settings.

Participants and methods

Participants

Participants with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) were primarily recruited from community-dwelling older adults in Zhongshan City through telephone outreach and in-person announcements at local community centers between May and August 2024. A total of 497 elderly individuals were screened using the DSM-5 criteria and cognitive screening tools, resulting in 91 individuals who met the diagnostic criteria for MCI. These participants had not previously received any hospital-based diagnosis, pharmacological treatment for neurodegenerative diseases, or other cognitive interventions. Among them, 11 individuals declined to participate. The remaining 80 participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention group (n = 40) or the control group (n = 40). During the 12-week intervention period, 12 participants were excluded from the per-protocol analysis. 2 relocated out of the community (1 in each group), 2 were hospitalized due to illness (1 in each group), and 8 discontinued participation (1 in the intervention group and 7 in the control group). Consequently, 68 participants completed the full study protocol and were included in the per-protocol analysis (Intervention: n = 37; Control: n = 31) (Fig. 1). All participants underwent baseline cognitive assessments prior to the intervention, using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Post-intervention cognitive function was reassessed in both groups. Blood biomarker levels, including plasma phosphorylated tau protein 181 (P-tau181) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), were measured at baseline and after the intervention. The diagnosis of MCI was based on the DSM-5 criteria for mild neurocognitive disorder in conjunction with MMSE scores [39].

Diagnostic criteria for MCI

MCI was defined according to the criteria for mild neurocognitive disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [39]: (1) Subjective cognitive decline reported by the individual or an informant; (2) Objective cognitive impairment measured by cognitive scales: MMSE score ≤ 17 for illiterate individuals, ≤ 20 for those with 1–6 years of education (primary school), and ≤ 24 for those with ≥ 7 years of education (junior high school or above); (3) Preserved independence in daily functioning: total score ≤ 22 on the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale; (4) Does not meet the diagnostic criteria for dementia (according to DSM-IV).

Study design and principles

Study design

This study was designed as a randomized controlled trial (RCT), strictly adhering to the diagnostic criteria, inclusion criteria, and exclusion criteria.

Study procedure (Fig. 1)

Randomization and allocation concealment

Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio using simple randomization. The allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician in R (version 4.1.3) using computer-generated random numbers (set.seed(12345) followed by sample() with equal allocation). No blocking or stratification was applied. The statistician programmed and executed the random assignment and produced the final allocation list.

All the randomization number will be placed in opaque, sealed envelopes. After eligible participants sign the informed consent form and complete baseline assessments, they will receive a sealed envelope with the assigned number. Allocation concealment will be strictly implemented through an independent procedure conducted by an independent researcher at the clinical site, maintaining strict separation between randomization and outcome evaluation processes.

Blinding principle

Intervention implementers were not blinded to group allocation due to the nature of the intervention. Outcome assessors, data collectors, and data analysts/statisticians were blinded to group allocation. To minimize contamination and expectation effects, participants in different groups were scheduled in separate time slots and were instructed not to discuss intervention-related details with others. Assessors were not involved in intervention delivery, and intervention staff had no role in outcome assessments.

Control method

A parallel-group design was used to compare the intervention and control groups.

Intervention method

Control group

The control group received health education. The sessions were delivered by trained professionals, including one psychiatrist or psychotherapist and one nurse. All personnel involved in the intervention received standardized training organized by the research team prior to implementation. The training content included:

Education on disease knowledge: Participants were provided with systematic information about the basic concepts, progression, and possible outcomes of Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), as well as its relationship to Alzheimer’s disease. This aimed to enhance participants’ understanding of the condition, increase awareness of early signs, and promote self-monitoring.

Frequency and duration of health education in the control group: Each session lasted 30 min and was delivered once every two weeks, for a total of six sessions over a 12-week intervention period.

Intervention group

In addition to the measures received by the control group, participants in the intervention group underwent Computerized Cognitive Remediation Therapy (CCRT).

Intervention content

CCRT is a computerized cognitive training program based on the principles of cognitive remediation therapy. It is guided by the theories of synaptic plasticity, neural network plasticity, functional reorganization, and cortical functional compensation. The program aims to reduce cognitive impairment, restore cognitive function, and slow cognitive decline in individuals with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) through cognitive ability assessment and targeted, adaptive cognitive training.In this study, participants in the intervention group received cognitive training via the CCRT system, which includes a variety of task-based games designed to target key cognitive domains typically impaired in MCI, such as attention, memory, executive function, processing speed, and abstract categorization. All tasks were embedded with an adaptive difficulty mechanism—task difficulty increased with better performance and decreased with poorer performance. The specific tasks included:

Mahjong matching

Mahjong tiles with various suits and characters are randomly displayed on the screen. Participants are required to quickly and accurately identify and match two identical tiles. The number of distractors and the similarity between tiles increase progressively.

Robot Identification

Multiple moving robot figures are displayed on the screen. After presenting a target robot, participants must select the identical one from the moving robots.

Whack-a-mole

Participants are instructed to click quickly when a mole appears, while refraining from clicking when a cat or bomb appears. These stimuli appear randomly and at increasing speed.

Flash memory task

A single image flashes on the screen for one second. A second image is then presented, and participants must determine whether it matches the previously shown image. As the task progresses, the image variety and flash speed increase.

Category matching task

An image representing a certain category (e.g., a watermelon representing “fruit”) is shown, followed by several options. Participants must choose the image that belongs to the same category.

Intervention frequency and duration: Each CCRT session consisted of all five modules, with each module lasting 5 min, totaling 25 min per session. The training was conducted twice a week for 12 weeks, totaling 24 sessions [42].

No other concomitant cognitive training programs or psychotherapeutic interventions were during the trial.

Assessment tools

General information

A general information questionnaire was designed for this study, covering the following main components: (1) Sociodemographic information: including gender, age, education level, marital status, living arrangements, interactions with neighbors, and participation in social activities. (2) Health-related information: including blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, and history of diabetes. (3) Lifestyle and behavioral habits: including smoking, alcohol consumption, tea drinking, physical exercise, reading, playing cards or mahjong, and the use of electronic devices such as computers or smartphones for internet access.

Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is a widely used screening tool for mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Its Chinese version has been extensively applied in Chinese populations and has demonstrated good reliability, validity, and clinical applicability [43]. The MMSE assesses five cognitive domains: orientation, memory, attention and calculation, recall, and language. Each correct response is scored as 1 point, and incorrect responses are scored as 0, with a maximum total score of 30. The cutoff scores for cognitive impairment are adjusted based on education level: ≤17 points for illiterate individuals (no formal education), ≤ 20 points for those with primary education (1–6 years), and ≤ 24 points for those with junior high school education or above (≥ 7 years).

During the study, a floor effect was observed in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) among some older adults in the community, with many participants scoring near the lower limit of the scale. This limited the sensitivity of MoCA in detecting variations in cognitive function recovery. Therefore, the MMSE was adopted as the primary criterion for defining cognitive recovery. Cognitive recovery was defined as an MMSE score exceeding the cutoff for cognitive impairment after the intervention (i.e., > 17 for illiterate individuals, > 20 for those with primary education, and > 24 for those with junior high school education or above), indicating a return to normal cognitive function.

Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a screening tool for mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Its Chinese version has been widely used in Chinese populations and has demonstrated good reliability, validity, and clinical applicability [44]. The MoCA assesses multiple cognitive domains, including short-term memory, visuospatial ability, executive function, attention, language, abstract thinking, and orientation. It consists of 30 items, with a total score of 30 points. Each item is scored based on the participant’s response, with correct answers awarded the corresponding points and incorrect answers scored as zero. A MoCA score below 26 is generally considered indicative of cognitive impairment. For individuals with fewer than 12 years of education, 1 additional point is added to the total score as an education adjustment.

Activities of Daily Living Scale, ADLs

The Activities of Daily Living (ADL) Scale is used to assess the daily functioning of community-dwelling older adults. Its Chinese version has been widely applied in Chinese populations and has demonstrated good reliability, validity, and clinical applicability [45]. The scale consists of 14 items, including toileting, eating, dressing, grooming, walking, bathing, making phone calls, shopping, meal preparation, housework, laundry, using transportation, taking medications, and managing finances. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale: 1 (completely independent), 2 (some difficulty), 3 (needs assistance), and 4 (completely dependent). The total score ranges from 14 to 56, with scores greater than 22 indicating significant functional impairment.

Biological sample collection and processing

Fasting venous blood samples were collected at two time points: baseline (prior to intervention) and post-intervention (week 12). Participants were instructed to fast for at least 8 h prior to blood draw. On the following morning, after completing the community-based questionnaire assessment, two tubes of fasting elbow venous blood were collected into pre-labeled vacuum tubes: one plain tube without additives and one tube containing EDTA-K2. Tubes were gently inverted to mix. Immediately after collection, samples were placed in universal transport boxes and stored at 4 °C. All samples were transported to the laboratory within 4 h. For each time point, a 5 mL blood sample in an EDTA-K2 anticoagulated tube was centrifuged using a refrigerated centrifuge at 3,000 rpm for 15 min. Plasma was separated, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. All biomarker testing was performed collectively after completion of sample collection from all participants. GFAP concentrations were determined using a fully automated fluorescence immunoassay analyzer (AST-Dx90), and calculated based on fluorescence intensity. The lower limit of detection (LLOD) was 3 pg/mL. Plasma p-Tau181 levels were measured using the Simoa single molecule array technology on the Simoa HD-X Analyzer™ (Quanterix Corp., Billerica, MA, USA), with the Simoa® pTau-181 Advantage V2 assay kit (Catalog No. 103714).

Data collection

Data collection in this study included general information surveys, neuropsychological assessments, physical examinations, blood biomarker testing, and CCRT training data records. All data were collected by trained research staff following standardized protocols, with strict quality control measures in place. The general information survey was conducted through face-to-face interviews using standardized questionnaires, which captured participants’ demographic characteristics (e.g., sex, age, education level, marital status, living arrangement, and social engagement), health status (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, reading habits, and use of electronic devices). Cognitive assessments were administered by trained professionals using validated scales. Physical examinations included measurements of blood pressure, height, and weight, from which body mass index (BMI) was calculated. For biomarker testing, participants were required to fast for at least 8 h prior to sample collection. A 5 mL fasting venous blood sample was collected in the morning, stored at 4 °C immediately after collection, and transported to the laboratory within 4 h. Plasma was separated by refrigerated centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 15 min), aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C until batch analysis. All samples were tested using the same batch of reagents to minimize inter-assay variability. All data were entered using a double-entry method, with two researchers independently entering and cross-checking the data to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Quality control

Quality control in study design

During the study design phase, an extensive literature review was conducted, combined with insights from previous research experience, to develop a questionnaire aligned with the study objectives. The questionnaire was refined based on feedback from experts in relevant fields and clinical practitioners. A pilot study was conducted prior to the formal investigation to assess the clarity, feasibility, and potential biases of the questionnaire. Revisions were made accordingly to ensure its scientific rigor and applicability.

Quality control in field implementation

This study was supported by the Third People’s Hospital of Zhongshan, the Zhongshan Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and local Community Service Stations. To enhance participant compliance, the following measures were adopted: (1) During recruitment, participants were fully informed of the study purpose, procedures, and potential benefits to ensure informed and voluntary participation; (2) Research staff maintained a professional and friendly attitude throughout interviews and assessments to build trust; (3) Data collection procedures were optimized to minimize participant burden and improve compliance; (4) To encourage full participation in assessments and follow-ups, appropriate material incentives were provided under the premise of maintaining research integrity.

Quality control in sample storage

All blood samples were labeled with unique participant codes to ensure traceability and avoid misplacement. Samples were stored in a − 80 °C freezer and regularly monitored to ensure integrity. Blood collection, transportation, storage, and testing were conducted by trained personnel following standardized procedures to minimize variability and experimental error.

Quality control in data entry

All data were independently entered by two researchers and cross-checked to ensure accuracy. Data were managed using EpiData software, and all records were de-identified and accessible only to the research team. To reduce human error, routine quality checks were conducted. In cases of abnormal or illogical values, source verification and correction were performed.

Safety evaluation

No serious adverse events occurred during the 12-week intervention period. Participants in the intervention group demonstrated good adherence, and none withdrew due to discomfort. A few individuals reported mild visual fatigue and distraction during the initial phase, which resolved spontaneously and did not affect the continuation of the intervention. No adverse reactions were reported in the control group.

Compliance evaluation

Participant adherence was assessed using the treatment attendance method. In both the intervention and control groups, the attendance rate exceeded 90%, indicating good compliance and suggesting that the intervention was well-accepted among individuals with MCI.

Ethical considerations

This study complied with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third People’s Hospital of Zhongshan (Ethics Approval Number: SSYLL20220401). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All personal data were de-identified and stored in encrypted databases, accessible only to the research team and used solely for research purposes. The intervention was non-invasive and low risk. Any discomfort experienced during the study was promptly addressed to safeguard participants’ rights and well-being.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 and R version 4.1.3. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to examine the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Participants who withdrew from the study were excluded from the final analysis.

Changes in cognitive outcomes from baseline to post-intervention were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) for repeated measures. Time (baseline vs. post-intervention), group (intervention vs. control), and the time × group interaction were included as fixed effects. Age, sex, education level, and BMI were included as covariates. Covariates were prespecified a priori based on clinical relevance and prior evidence as potential prognostic factors for cognitive outcomes. To maintain model stability given the sample size, we limited adjustment to a small set of key baseline factors. As baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, no additional covariates were included to address baseline imbalance. A participant-specific random intercept was included to account for within-subject correlation. A random-intercept-only model was specified (random intercept for participant ID), and no random slope for time was included. Results are reported for both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses. GFAP and p-Tau181 concentrations were log-transformed and standardized prior to inclusion in the LMMs. To control for multiplicity in cognitive subdomain analyses, Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds were applied. Model diagnostics were performed for all LMMs. All models converged without singular fits; residual Q–Q plots suggested approximate normality with no major deviations, and multicollinearity was low (maximum VIF < 3).

Effect sizes were calculated by computing the change in cognitive scores before and after the intervention for each participant, then dividing the change by the baseline standard deviation within each group to obtain standardized change values.

To assess the predictive value of biomarker changes for post-intervention cognitive recovery, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using predicted probabilities from multivariable logistic regression models, and the area under the curve (AUC) was reported. Differences between AUCs were evaluated using the DeLong method. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling (B = 2000), and bootstrap-corrected AUCs were reported.

Effect of CCRT intervention on cognitive function

After the 12-week intervention, the linear mixed-effects model showed a significant between-group difference in standardized change in MMSE total score in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis (effect size: 0.519 SD, 95% CI: 0.191 to 0.848, P = 0.002), with the intervention group demonstrating a mean standardized improvement of 0.390 SD and the control group a mean standardized decline of − 0.130 SD (Fig. 2; Table 2). Results were consistent in the per-protocol (PP) analysis (effect size: 0.494 SD, 95% CI: 0.138 to 0.851, P = 0.006). Discussion

This study included 68 patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) who completed a 12-week follow-up, aiming to evaluate the effects of computerized cognitive remediation therapy (CCRT) on cognitive function and associated plasma biomarkers. The results demonstrated that overall cognitive function significantly improved in the intervention group after 12 weeks of CCRT. A significant reduction in plasma GFAP levels was observed in the intervention group, while changes in p-Tau181 levels showed no statistically significant difference compared to the control group. Furthermore, changes in GFAP levels showed good predictive value for post-intervention cognitive recovery among MCI patients. Overall, the findings confirm the beneficial effects of CCRT on cognitive function in individuals with MCI and highlight its impact on blood-based biomarkers. The study also reveals an association between blood biomarkers and cognitive improvement, suggesting their potential utility as objective indicators for evaluating intervention outcomes. These results provide supporting evidence for the clinical application and mechanistic evaluation of CCRT interventions.

In this study, the intervention group that received 12 weeks of CCRT showed significantly higher MMSE and MoCA total scores compared to the control group. Previous studies have systematically evaluated the efficacy of computerized cognitive training (CCT) in individuals with MCI, confirming its effectiveness in improving both global cognition and specific cognitive domains [21, 29, 46–49]. Research generally indicates that cognitive training leads to significant improvements in overall cognitive function as well as in various cognitive subdomains [21, 40, 46, 47, 50–53].

However, some studies have reported that while CCT produces notable gains in memory and delayed recall, it does not consistently enhance working memory, visuospatial processing, attention, language, or executive function [54], and has limited effects on language fluency [49]. Meta-analyses have further shown that both supervised and unsupervised CCT can improve various types of memory functions in MCI patients, although differences in efficacy between the two approaches have been observed [40]. Additionally, some evidence suggests that unsupervised home-based training and training frequencies exceeding three times per week may not be effective for cognitive improvement [55].

It is worth noting that this study adopted a 12-week CCRT intervention protocol with a frequency of twice per week. While longer intervention durations may result in broader cognitive improvements, they are often associated with reduced adherence and increased resource demands [56]. Additionally, some studies have shown that conducting computerized cognitive training more than three times per week may lead to diminished cognitive outcomes and training fatigue [57, 58]. Therefore, the current protocol aimed to strike a balance between sufficient intervention intensity and practical feasibility, while maintaining participant engagement.

Nonetheless, the duration and frequency of the intervention may influence improvement in specific cognitive domains. In this study, no significant gains were observed in certain domains, such as orientation, attention, and calculation in the MMSE, as well as visuospatial/executive function, naming, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation in the MoCA. This may be partially attributed to the limited overall intervention dose. For example, one study suggested that the optimal daily training dose for individuals over 60 years old is 50–55 min per day, six days per week [59]. Similarly, a meta-analysis reported that while a total training time of 4–36 h could improve performance in verbal episodic memory tasks, significant improvements in visual episodic memory were only observed when the total training time exceeded 36 h. No consistent effects were found for working memory across all training durations [40].

These findings suggest that training duration is closely associated with the effectiveness of CCT in specific cognitive domains, especially in complex functions, where shorter intervention doses may be insufficient. In this study, a modular training design was adopted, targeting key functions such as attention, memory, and language, in order to enhance specificity and practicality. However, due to time constraints per session and the limited total intervention period, compromises had to be made in the selection and repetition of modules, which may have limited the depth of training in certain domains and, consequently, their improvement.

Moreover, cognitive subdomain outcomes in this study were derived from global cognitive screening tools (MMSE and MoCA), which have limited sensitivity in detecting subtle domain-specific changes. Therefore, future studies should incorporate more detailed neuropsychological assessments to better capture the effects of CCRT on specific cognitive domains [60].

In summary, although the present study demonstrated overall cognitive improvement in MCI patients following CCRT, the effects on certain cognitive domains were not significant. To further enhance intervention efficacy, future research should consider longer intervention durations, higher training frequencies, and more sensitive cognitive outcome measures to better detect potential improvements.

This study found that plasma GFAP levels significantly decreased in community-dwelling MCI patients who received CCRT, and changes in GFAP levels were predictive of post-training cognitive recovery. GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) is a biomarker of astrocyte activation in the central nervous system [61]. In response to pathological changes in surrounding tissues, astrocytes undergo activation, proliferation, and morphological as well as functional changes—collectively referred to as reactive astrocytosis [61–63]. These reactive astrocytes are involved in the expression of proinflammatory molecules, contributing to neuroinflammatory processes and exacerbating neurodegenerative pathology [63–65].

Plasma GFAP is considered a sensitive biomarker for the early detection and progression monitoring of the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum. Elevated GFAP levels have been reported in individuals with MCI and AD, and have been shown to effectively differentiate MCI from cognitively normal individuals, as well as to predict the risk of conversion from MCI to AD [65–71]. Moreover, GFAP is closely associated with β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition and is considered an early and relatively specific marker of amyloid pathology [34, 62]. Higher baseline GFAP levels have also been linked to greater cognitive decline over subsequent years, particularly in domains such as memory, attention, and executive function [30, 70–72]. In addition, GFAP elevation has been associated with increased neuroinflammation and reduced volume in AD-vulnerable brain regions, further underscoring its role in neurodegenerative pathology [72–75].

The observed reduction in GFAP levels among MCI patients following CCRT may reflect an anti-inflammatory mechanism underlying cognitive improvement. Specifically, CCRT may suppress excessive astrocyte activation and reduce the release of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and slowing neurodegenerative progression [61]. These findings suggest that CCRT may exert not only cognitive benefits but also neuroprotective effects. Future studies should further explore how CCRT modulates astrocyte function and related inflammatory signaling pathways to deepen our understanding of its neurobiological mechanisms.

This study did not observe a significant effect of CCRT on plasma p-Tau181 levels. Abnormal phosphorylation of tau protein is a key feature of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology and is typically associated with neuronal dysfunction and neurofibrillary tangles. Among tau-related biomarkers, plasma p-Tau181 has demonstrated strong predictive value in AD research and is considered a core pathological marker associated with both MCI and AD, with elevated levels observed in both conditions [35–38].

p-Tau181 has been shown to predict cerebral Aβ burden as accurately—or even more so—than other biomarkers, especially when combined with markers such as Aβ42/Aβ40 and GFAP [30]. It correlates well with PET-detected tau and Aβ pathology and is closely associated with short-term cognitive decline and hippocampal atrophy. Importantly, p-Tau181 can identify AD across the entire clinical continuum [35], making it a promising biomarker for early diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative disease progression.

Furthermore, tau pathology has been shown to mediate the relationship between Aβ plaques and synaptic density, and is implicated in synaptic loss [76]. Therefore, although no significant changes in p-Tau181 were detected in the present study, this marker remains a critical indicator of AD-related neuronal injury. It is possible that p-Tau181 may reflect more dynamic neurodegenerative processes over longer intervention durations or at different stages of disease progression. Continued monitoring of p-Tau181 in future studies is warranted to better understand its longitudinal behavior and response to cognitive interventions.

In summary, this study applied computerized cognitive remediation therapy (CCRT) to community-dwelling older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and found that CCRT significantly improved cognitive function and reduced plasma GFAP levels. The study population consisted of first-diagnosed, medication-naive older adults with MCI in a community setting, enhancing the representativeness of the findings. CCRT was shown to be feasible and well accepted in real-world community environments due to its ease of use and high adherence. The inclusion of objective biomarkers provided an additional reference for evaluating intervention efficacy. Moreover, this study explored the longitudinal predictive value of baseline biomarkers, highlighting their potential in forecasting cognitive recovery and supporting early detection and intervention strategies for MCI. These findings contribute to advancing cognitive health management practices at the primary care level.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability and stability of the findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these results. Second, the 12-week intervention period did not allow for assessment of long-term effects or post-intervention maintenance. Longitudinal follow-up studies are needed to evaluate the sustained benefits of CCRT in community-dwelling MCI populations. Additionally, although MMSE and MoCA are widely used cognitive assessment tools, they may lack the sensitivity to detect changes in specific cognitive domains. Future studies should consider incorporating more comprehensive and domain-specific neuropsychological assessments.

Furthermore, due to practical constraints and based on prior research, only a limited set of blood biomarkers was measured post-intervention. Future research should explore longitudinal changes in a broader panel of biomarkers to better elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying cognitive improvement.
