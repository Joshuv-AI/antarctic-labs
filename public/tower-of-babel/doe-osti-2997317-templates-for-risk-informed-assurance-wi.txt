ABSTRACT
We investigate recovery of geometric structure from networks embedded in manifolds with spatially
varying curvature, extending the constant-curvature framework of Lubold et al. (2023). Our work
supports cascade risk assessment in critical infrastructure through the Templates for Risk-informed
Assurance with Curvature Embeddings (TRACE) framework.
Simulations on a bi-modal Gaussian surface show that constant-curvature methods yield weighted
averages shaped by clique patterns, while hierarchical clustering identifies distinct regimes. Lo-
calized estimation, however, reveals boundary contamination in transitional regions. To address
heterogeneity, we develop distance metrics for graphs with edge and node features, proving their
metric validity, and validate them via deterministic graph generation from canonical tilings.
We further propose a diffusion-based anomaly detection approach that treats networks as glued
manifolds, using curvature discontinuities to detect structural anomalies. Employing the carré-du-
champ operator and scalar curvature, we achieve robust anomaly discrimination, demonstrated on
the Singapore Water Treatment (SWaT) dataset with joint network-traffic and sensor features.
Integration with TRACE reveals how curvature shapes cascade dynamics: positive curvature
impedes, while negative curvature accelerates propagation. This geometric perspective provides
interpretable risk metrics and visualization tools for critical infrastructure managers. While full
validation remains ongoing, our contributions establish a rigorous foundation for geometric analysis
of network resilience and cascade vulnerability.

1. INTRODUCTION

The identification of latent geometric structure in network data has emerged as a fundamental
challenge in network science, with profound implications for understanding system behavior, pre-
dicting cascading failures, and designing resilient infrastructures. The seminal work of Lubold
et al. (2023) established a rigorous framework for recovering the latent space geometry including
manifold type, dimension, and curvature, from observed network data under the assumption of
constant sectional curvature. However, real-world systems often exhibit spatially heterogeneous
geometric properties that violate this constant curvature assumption, necessitating extensions to
accommodate non-constant curvature manifolds.
This report presents a comprehensive investigation into the behavior of curvature recovery methods
when applied to manifolds with spatially varying curvature. Through carefully designed simula-
tions on a constructed bi-modal Gaussian surface, we characterize the relationship between local
geometric properties and global curvature estimates, revealing that the standard methodology yields
weighted averages influenced by clique formation patterns. Furthermore, we develop and evaluate
a hierarchical clustering approach for localized curvature estimation, uncovering both promising
capabilities and fundamental challenges in geometric decomposition.
The integration of these geometric insights with the Templates for Risk-informed Assurance with
Curvature Embeddings (TRACE) framework provides a mathematically rigorous foundation for
assessing cascade vulnerability in high-consequence systems. By establishing the relationship be-
tween manifold curvature and failure propagation dynamics, we enable quantitative risk assessment
that captures the fundamental geometric constraints governing cascade spread.
“Information Geometry and Its Applications”(Amari, 2016) introduces a geometric perspective on
statistical models, treating probability distributions as points on curved manifolds. For readers
exploring Latent Geometry Graphs, the book offers useful insights into how curvature and distance
can influence structure in data-driven graphs. It is foundational background for “The Many Faces
of Information Geometry”(Nielsen, 2022), which gives an overview of geometrically modeling a
family of probability distributions, including geodesics and measures of information. This in turn is
foundational for the Latent Geometry Graph (LGG) model defined in Léo et al. (2022). “Identifying
the Latent Space Geometry of Network Models through Analysis of Curvature” ? gives algorithms
for the discovery of the (curvature of) the manifold the graph lives on. It is the discovery of this
curvature of the LGG’s manifold that we explored in this LDRD.
Fundamental to the geometric model is the notion that these spaces are manifolds of constant
curvature. This curvature is equivalent to the behavior of observed geodesic distances, exemplified
by whether nodes are farther away (hyperbolic manifolds), equidistant, (planar manifolds), or closer
(spherical manifolds) geodesically than would be expected for a planar domain. Graphs coming
from hyperbolic manifolds appear tree like, with nodes far from a given node not well connected

 13

to each other. Graphs coming from spherical manifolds appear ball-like, with nodes antipodal to a
given node being close and well connected to each other. Planar graphs are in between. In order
to use this model, one must have a predefined separation between these cases, that is, the curvature
distributions should be trimodal, so that the three manifold types are well separated and can be
recognized by our algorithms and software.
The chain of hypotheses were that were explored are the following:
 1. Control networks of interest are consistent with probabilistic sampling of geometric latent
 spaces.
 2. Subgraphs coming from each of these types of latent spaces with constant curvature can be
 identified.
 3. Subgraph manifolds can be consistently glued together to define a global space of non-constant
 curvature that the entire global graph comes from.
 4. The curvature of the graphs can be computed. (Encumbant on the graphs being large and
 dense enough to distinguish the latent spaces.)
 5. Curvature correlates to graph vulnerability.
 6. Curvature changes during an attack on the graph (nodes become more well connected, as the
 attack attempts to spread itself throughout the network in a cascade).
 7. Displaying graph curvature to operational managers is an intuitive and helpful way to alert
 them to their network being under attack, and can guide responses to defend the network.
While we were not able to definitively ascertain the truth or usefulness of these hypotheses, we
have tools, models, and designs of experiments that could be used to further pursue them.

defined as the graph of the function

2.5. Hierarchical Clustering Approach for Localized Curvature Recovery

2.5.1. Methodology

The limitations of constant curvature estimation for heterogeneous manifolds motivate our proposed
hierarchical approach for localized curvature recovery. The methodology proceeds through four
stages:

First, given an adjacency matrix A, we perform graph clustering to obtain distinct clusters
𝐶1 , . . . , 𝐶 𝑀 that potentially correspond to regions of homogeneous curvature. Second, we ap-
ply the standard LCM method (Lubold et al., 2023) globally to obtain baseline estimates 𝜅ˆ𝑠 and 𝜅ˆℎ .
For nodes in different clusters, we interpret these as estimates of the inter-cluster manifold curva-
ture. Third, for each cluster 𝐶𝑚 , we extract the induced subgraph and apply the LCM methodology
locally to obtain cluster-specific curvature estimates 𝜅ˆ𝑠,𝑚 and 𝜅ˆℎ,𝑚 . Fourth, we iterate this process
as needed, under the hypothesis that clusters lie on manifolds of approximately constant curvature
even when the global manifold exhibits heterogeneity.

2.6. Implications and Proposed Refinements

2.6.1. Key Findings

Our experimental investigation yields several critical insights into curvature recovery from hetero-
geneous manifolds. First, constant curvature estimates from the LCM method represent weighted
averages of local curvatures, with weights determined by clique formation probability rather than
uniform spatial sampling. Regions of high positive curvature contribute disproportionately to
global estimates due to enhanced clustering. Second, standard clustering algorithms can suc-
cessfully identify geometric regimes in networks, but boundary effects significantly contaminate
localized curvature estimates. Third, the distribution of points within clusters affects curvature
estimates as much as the underlying geometry, with concentrated distributions yielding higher
estimates than dispersed configurations.

 33

2.6.2. Methodological Refinements

These findings motivate several methodological refinements to address the identified limitations.
The boundary contamination problem suggests restricting clique selection to those whose edges
lie predominantly within a single cluster. Specifically, we propose considering only cliques where
at least (1 − 𝜖)ℓ 2 edges remain within the cluster, for some small tolerance 𝜖. This ensures that
curvature estimates reflect the geometry of cluster interiors rather than transitional regions.
The hard clustering approach creates artificial boundaries that may not align with smooth curvature
transitions. Soft clustering methods that assign probabilistic membership could provide smoother
transitions and reduce boundary artifacts. Alternatively, we could weight cliques based on their
centrality within clusters, giving higher weight to cliques whose neighborhoods lie entirely within
a single cluster.
The observation that different clusters exhibit similar curvature estimates despite distinct geometric
properties suggests potential for statistical testing. An F-test framework could formally assess
whether observed curvature differences across clusters exceed expected variability, providing a
principled approach for determining the number of distinct geometric regimes.

2.6.3. Alternative Approaches

Beyond refining the clustering-based methodology, several alternative approaches warrant investi-
gation. A nearest-neighbor framework could estimate local curvature at each node by applying the
LCM method to subgraphs induced by local neighborhoods. While this increases computational
cost and may suffer from high variance, it could provide fine-grained curvature maps without
imposing artificial cluster boundaries. This approach relates to methods for estimating intrinsic
dimension in local neighborhoods (Facco et al., 2017).
The restriction to cliques, while theoretically motivated, may be overly conservative. Alternative
subgraph structures that indicate geometric proximity—such as dense subgraphs with high edge
density or nodes with many common neighbors—could provide more flexible distance estima-
tion. For instance, if nodes 𝑝 1 , . . . , 𝑝 𝑘 have few connections to node 𝑞, this indicates that 𝑞 is
geometrically distant from the group, providing distance information without requiring complete
subgraphs.
Graph embedding methods that explicitly model curvature, such as those based on hyperbolic or
product space embeddings, could provide alternative approaches to curvature estimation. Recent
work on learnable curvature parameters in neural network embeddings suggests potential for end-
to-end learning of geometric parameters, though incorporating appropriate constraints remains
challenging.

3.1. Recommended experiments

First, it must be demonstrated that the algorithm can correctly recover the curvature-type of the
manifold from the graph. Once that is done, some key open questions include how robust and
sensitive the algorithm is to changes in the graph. For example, if we lose one edge, does the
curvature type change? Of particular concern is when cliques that share only one inter-clique
edge are perturbed to have either zero or two edges. The assumed random process of connecting
edges from the manifold would do this with reasonable probability. It appears that having zero
edges would skyrocket the assumed geodesic distance to infinity. It may be possible to circumvent

 36

this limitation by assuming/computing an inter-clique distance at most the sum of the computed
geodesic distances on the shortest clique-to-clique path. (The triangle inequality holds on all
manifold types.) Conversely, if there are more than the expected number of edges, the geodesic
distance would be lower than reality. This is less likely to be an issue.
There is an assumption that the manifolds are well-separated, an empty interval in the curvature
calculations, and the algorithm is not presented with graphs whose curvature is intermediate.
However, it is unclear how this separation distance, the size of the empty interval, could be known
a priori. Having large graphs would appear to accentuate the separation between hyperboloids and
sphere or plane, making the manifold structure more certain. On the other hand larger graphs on
the sphere attenuate the appearance of curvature and might shrink the empty interval between those
two types. This is especially a concern as we imagine that the curvature is changing locally as
the attack cascades, and that the curvature transitions from one type to another, hence this empty
interval must be traversed, at least discretely, and the curvature separation may come down to the
existence of a single edge.
The algorithm description implies that it is insensitive to the clique size chosen, as long as it is
small enough that there enough inter-clique edges to distinguish the curvature types. This should be
tested. In particular, although we generate cliques of size 𝑘, we could tell the curvature-discovery
algorithm to use cliques of size 𝑘 −1. We may remove intra-clique edges to make certain cliques fall
below 𝑘 vertices, and hence not be included by the curvature-discover algorithm. The insensitivity
of the algorithm to this effect should be verified.

4.1. Theoretical Foundation

The geometric heterogeneity revealed by our localized curvature analysis naturally extends to the
problem of anomaly detection in graphs. We now present a complementary methodology that
leverages diffusion geometry (Coifman and Lafon, 2006; Jones et al., 2024) to identify structural
anomalies through curvature discontinuities. This approach treats anomalies as “seams” between
glued manifold patches, where abrupt changes in local curvature indicate boundaries between
distinct geometric regimes.

The fundamental insight underlying this methodology is that real-world graphs often arise from
multiple underlying geometric structures that have been connected or “glued” together. These
connection points manifest as discontinuities in intrinsic geometric quantities, particularly scalar
curvature. By computing curvature directly from the graph’s diffusion operator and identifying
regions of high curvature variation, we can detect these structural anomalies without requiring prior
knowledge of the underlying manifold structure.

4.2.3. Implications for Curvature Recovery

The success of tangent space recovery with known coordinates suggests that the graph Laplacian
preserves sufficient geometric information for curvature estimation. When combined with appropri-
ate coordinate functions—whether obtained through diffusion maps, spectral embeddings, or other
manifold learning techniques—the carré-du-champ framework provides a principled approach to
local curvature estimation.

The challenge of unknown coordinates motivates several research directions. First, the development
of embedding methods that explicitly preserve geometric properties could improve tangent space
estimation. Second, iterative refinement procedures that alternate between coordinate optimization

 43

and geometric estimation may converge to consistent geometric representations. Third, the incor-
poration of additional constraints, such as preservation of local distances or angles, could guide the
embedding toward geometrically meaningful coordinates.

properties. Additionally, the optimization must balance fitting the observed edges with maintaining
geometric consistency, potentially requiring regularization terms that penalize deviations from the
estimated manifold structure.
The connection to hyperbolic space optimization methods suggests potential strategies. Recent work
on gradient descent in hyperbolic neural networks demonstrates that careful parameterization and
numerical techniques can enable stable optimization on curved spaces. Extending these methods
to manifolds with varying curvature requires addressing the additional complexity of spatially
heterogeneous geometry.

4.3.1.7. Performance Metrics and Validation

The experimental results on glued manifolds demonstrate several key performance characteristics of
the diffusion-based anomaly detection methodology. The method successfully identifies geometric
seams with high precision, as evidenced by the clear correspondence between designed patch
boundaries and detected high-seam edges. The detection sensitivity, measured by the ratio of
seam scores at boundaries versus interior edges, exceeds 10:1 in most cases, providing robust
discrimination between anomalous and regular structures.
Temporal anomaly detection shows similarly strong performance, with the method identifying all
regions where curvature changes exceed twice the standard deviation of background variation. The

false positive rate remains below 5% when using adaptive thresholds based on local curvature
statistics, while maintaining a true positive rate above 90% for significant geometric changes.

The computational efficiency of the method scales favorably with network size. For the test
manifold with approximately 500 nodes, the complete analysis including eigendecomposition,
curvature computation, and anomaly scoring requires less than 2 seconds on standard hardware.
This performance suggests feasibility for near-real-time monitoring of infrastructure networks with
thousands of nodes.

4.3.2. Quantitative Performance Analysis

To quantitatively assess the anomaly detection performance, we analyze the distribution of seam
scores across different graph regions. On a 12 × 12 grid with a single shortcut edge of weight 4.0
(compared to regular edge weights of 1.0), we observe the following characteristics.

The scalar curvature values range from approximately −0.0009 to 0.0002, with the majority of
nodes exhibiting curvature within 10−5 of zero. Nodes directly adjacent to the shortcut show

The node seam scores exhibit a bimodal distribution, with regular nodes showing scores below

threshold-based anomaly detection.

Edge seam scores provide even stronger discrimination, with regular edges showing scores below

exceeds 20:1, demonstrating the method’s sensitivity to structural anomalies.

4.3.4. Extensions to Complex Graph Structures

The methodology naturally extends to graphs with rich attribute structures. Node features can be
incorporated by augmenting the diffusion coordinates 𝑋 with normalized feature vectors, allowing
the curvature computation to reflect both topological and attribute-based geometry. Edge features
influence the analysis through the weighted adjacency matrix 𝑊, where edge weights can encode
similarity, capacity, or other domain-specific metrics.
For directed graphs, the symmetrization 𝑊sym = (𝑊 + 𝑊 𝑇 )/2 preserves the underlying geometry
while enabling the application of spectral methods. Alternatively, the magnetic Laplacian can be
employed to preserve directional information in the curvature computation.
Temporal graphs present opportunities for detecting change points through time-varying curvature
analysis. By computing scalar curvature at regular time intervals and tracking the evolution of
seam scores, the method can identify moments when the graph’s geometric structure undergoes
significant reorganization.

4.4. Integration with the TRACE Framework

4.4.1. Curvature and Risk Assessment

The Templates for Risk-informed Assurance with Curvature Embeddings (TRACE) framework
leverages our geometric insights for cascade risk assessment in high-consequence systems. The
framework establishes a fundamental relationship between manifold curvature and failure propaga-
tion propensity, with positive curvature creating natural barriers to cascade spread while negative
curvature facilitates rapid propagation.
For networks with spatially varying curvature, our localized analysis enables region-specific risk
assessment. High-curvature regions identified through our clustering approach represent zones
of enhanced resilience where redundant paths limit cascade extent. Conversely, flat or negatively
curved regions require additional monitoring and potential structural reinforcement to prevent
catastrophic failures.
The integration of graph Laplacian-based curvature estimation with the diffusion geometry frame-
work provides multiple complementary perspectives on network vulnerability. While the LCM
method estimates average curvature over clique-defined regions, the diffusion-based approach pro-
vides pointwise curvature estimates that can identify fine-grained geometric features. The synthesis
of these approaches enables multi-scale risk assessment that captures both global geometric trends
and local structural anomalies.

5. APPLICATIONS

The heterogeneous geometric structure revealed by our analysis has direct implications for criti-
cal infrastructure protection. Power grids, for instance, may exhibit positive curvature in urban
distribution networks with redundant connections, transitioning to near-zero curvature in rural
transmission lines, and potentially negative curvature in hierarchical generation structures. Un-
derstanding this geometric heterogeneity enables targeted hardening strategies that account for
region-specific vulnerabilities.
On the other hand, satellite constellation networks present unique challenges due to their dynamic
topology as satellites orbit. The time-varying geometry requires continuous monitoring to identify
periods when the constellation configuration induces vulnerable geometric structures. Our hier-
archical methodology could track geometric evolution by performing localized analysis at regular
intervals.
Nuclear safety systems demand the highest reliability standards, making geometric analysis partic-
ularly valuable. The identification of regions with reduced curvature could guide the placement of
additional safety mechanisms or redundant control systems. The quantitative risk metrics provided
by integrating local curvature estimates with the TRACE framework enable rigorous safety case
development.

5.2.3. SWaT Preprocessing

The aim of preprocessing is to produce a graph representation of the SWaT dataset to which a
Graph Neural Network can be applied to construct node embeddings which capture the structure of
the graph and simultaneously both the node and edge features (Xu, 2021). A graph representation

connectivity between them is vaguely summarized with a block diagram with directed edges, and
we refer to this as the physical edge set 𝐸 𝑃 .

However, the dataset lacks explicit information to align the network traffic captures with this

traffic captures, only one address appeared per node, associating each node with a single address
and yielding an additional edge set 𝐸𝐶 , corresponding to the network traffic. In addition to a
node set, this allows for the inclusion of network traffic statistics as edge attributes. Rather than
maintaining a directed multigraph, we instead simplify it into a directed graph by the creation of

5.3. Visualization and Embedding of Discovered Geometric Structure

Once we have identified the (sub)manifold curvature of a (sub)graph, what would we do with it?
We seek a visualization of all of the submanifold patches, ideally time varying.

The computer graphics literature has algorithms that may be helpful for generating embeddings
of local manifolds with prescribed curvature and visualizing them (Corman, 2024; Chuang and
Kazhdan, 2011). Free software is readily available online for performing many of the necessary
steps (Brakke, 1992; Jacobson et al., 2018). In particular, we may start with a “flat” manifold of the
entire graph’s manifold, then locally refine triangles and evolve them. These evolution algorithms
would be particularly efficient at revising the curvature as the graph changes as an event unfolds.

The building blocks for such an algorithmic pipeline include:

 • Energy functionals that involve curvature — e.g. Gaussian curvature, mean curvature,
 bending energy, or curvature defect (angle deficits)

 • Mesh refinement / subdivision so that you have enough resolution in regions of high curvature.

 • Vertex relocation / smoothing under constraints (you choose some target curvature / metric,
 and optimize vertex positions subject to that).

 • Discrete differential geometry operators: Laplace-Beltrami, cotangent weights, angle deficit,
 curvature estimation on triangle meshes.

 • Constraint enforcement / singularities: if exact prescription is impossible everywhere (e.g.
 Gauss-Bonnet constraints), you allow some singular points or relaxations.

5.3.1. Extension: Gluing Manifolds

The topological “gluing” literature is mostly concerned with already-embedded triangular patches
and surfaces, and providing how to define consistent parameterizations of local patches, such that
where local patches overlap, either patch’s parameterization may be used, and one can map back
and forth between them. “Parametric pseudo-manifolds” (Gallier et al., 2012) describes sufficient
conditions and concrete constructive rules for rectifying parameterizations and building gluing
data. This may be helpful if, for example, we develop prescribed-curvature embedded manifolds of
different parts of a graph, and want to visualize them together. They may be needed, for example,
if the graphs are extremely large. The surface evolution algorithms scale well with triangulation
(manifold) size, but it is unclear how well the curvature discovery algorithms scale. Additionally,
the discover algorithm’s requires that there is at least one edge between every pair of cliques. If
this condition does not hold globally over a graph, we may desire to run it locally over overlapping
subdomains where the condition does hold, then gluing the result together. Even so, it is likely that
a better approach than embedding submanifolds then gluing, would be to attach (glue) then embed.
That is, we may compute the curvature of subgraphs, attach these data together (perhaps with an
averaging over overlapping regions), and evolve a flat surface to obtain the locally desired curvature
everywhere.

6. CONCLUSION

This report has presented a comprehensive investigation into curvature recovery from networks
embedded in non-constant curvature manifolds, revealing both the capabilities and limitations of
current methodologies. Our experimental analysis on a carefully constructed bi-modal Gaussian
surface demonstrates that the method of Lubold et al. (2023) yields weighted averages of local
curvatures, with the weighting influenced by clique formation patterns that favor high-curvature
regions.
The hierarchical clustering approach successfully identifies distinct geometric regimes, recovering
the four primary curvature zones in our test manifold. However, localized curvature estimation
reveals unexpected challenges, particularly boundary effects that contaminate estimates in transi-
tional regions. The observation that flat regions show spurious positive curvature due to boundary
point inclusion highlights the need for refined clique selection criteria and potentially soft clustering
approaches.
The integration of these geometric insights with the TRACE framework provides a mathematically
rigorous foundation for cascade risk assessment in high-consequence systems. By establishing
the relationship between local curvature and failure propagation propensity, we enable targeted
vulnerability assessment and mitigation strategies that account for geometric heterogeneity.
Future work should focus on addressing the identified limitations through methodological re-
finements, particularly in boundary treatment and continuous curvature field estimation. The
development of adaptive algorithms that automatically determine appropriate analysis scales and
the validation on diverse real-world networks represent critical next steps. As networks continue to
play increasingly central roles in critical infrastructure and national security, the ability to charac-
terize their geometric properties and predict cascade vulnerabilities becomes essential for ensuring
system resilience.
