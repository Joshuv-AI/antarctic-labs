ABSTRACT
Angiosarcoma is a poorly understood sarcoma due to its high heterogeneity and rarity. Here we show a comprehensive clinical and molecular analysis of a large cohort of 254 angiosarcoma patients through the patient-partnered Angiosarcoma Project. By integrating transcriptomic, somatic, and germline variant data, we find that subcutaneous angiosarcomas frequently exhibit TGF-β and receptor tyrosine kinase signaling upregulation, with driver mutations in KDR, PLCG1, and POT1. Meanwhile, cutaneous angiosarcomas are enriched for MYC-driven programs, UV mutational signatures, immune checkpoint gene expression, and TP53, FLT4, and BRAF mutations. Germline POT1 pathogenic variant carriers have a 92.7-fold higher risk of developing angiosarcoma, with ‘double-hit’ germline and somatic variant carriers developing disease decades earlier. Additionally, POT1-mutated tumors underexpress TERT and overexpress CHAMP1. Altogether, these findings elucidate the molecular framework of angiosarcoma, nominate therapeutic targets, and highlight the power of direct patient engagement in rare cancers.

Introduction

Angiosarcoma is a rare malignant tumor of endothelial cell origin that accounts for 1–2% of all soft-tissue sarcomas1. While most commonly presenting as cutaneous diseases in the head and neck region or on the chest wall after receiving radiation therapy for breast cancer, primary angiosarcomas can also present in the subcutaneous breast, liver, bone, spleen, extremities, trunk, and other visceral organs1. Because of its low incidence and high heterogeneity across multiple primary sites2,3, angiosarcoma remains poorly understood and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges to clinicians, with a median overall survival of 16 months and a 5-year overall survival rate of 60% for localized disease and 0% for metastatic disease4,5. To address the challenges inherent to rare disease research, the Count Me In Angiosarcoma Project6 (ASCProject) leveraged a patient-driven research model, where angiosarcoma patient samples are collected by direct outreach to angiosarcoma patients and their physicians on social media platforms. Since its original launch in 2017, the ASCProject has collected survey responses from more than 250 patients and served as a valuable reference for many angiosarcoma-focused as well as pan-sarcoma studies, enabling the discoveries of novel mechanistic insights and the exploration of new therapeutics7–9.

The initial report by Painter et al. about the ASCProject was instrumental in identifying key recurrent mutations in angiosarcoma6. However, the number of available processed samples (n = 47) at that time was still limited, and much of the genomic heterogeneity underlying angiosarcoma arising from diverse primary sites remains uncharacterized. For example, while the transcriptional landscape of head and neck angiosarcomas and breast angiosarcomas has been explored in case studies and small cohorts8,10–12, studies of the transcriptional profile of angiosarcomas arising from other primary sites are lacking. Given the known mutational heterogeneity13,14, prognostic differences3,5, and treatment outcomes4,15 of angiosarcomas by different primary sites, a systematic comparison of angiosarcoma transcriptional profiles could reveal distinct pathological pathways and inform therapy development as well as clinical management. Moreover, a germline missense variant in POT1 (p.R117C) that disrupts shelterin binding and promotes abnormal telomere elongation has previously been identified in a TP53-negative Li-Fraumeni-like family to be responsible for cardiac angiosarcoma in a dominant-negative manner16. Whether other germline variants in POT1 or other genes could play a role in angiosarcoma predisposition remains largely unknown. Altogether, these standing questions highlighted the need for more comprehensive profiling of angiosarcoma patients to elucidate the molecular landscape of angiosarcomas and identify potential molecular targets for therapeutic interventions.

In this study, we took advantage of the large cohort size of the ASCProject and analyzed survey responses or extracted medical record data from 254 patients and expanded the molecular profiling of patients in the latest release of the ASCProject to include a total of 122 tumor bulk RNA sequencing samples, 94 tumor exome sequencing samples, and 229 germline exome sequencing samples [Fig. 1a]. We found significant transcriptional heterogeneity between angiosarcoma samples arising from different primary sites and characterized the dominant oncogenic pathways from each site, as well as identifying potential therapeutic targets. The increased number of samples with characterized tumor mutational profiles also allowed for the refinement of the mutational characteristics of cutaneous and non-cutaneous angiosarcomas. In addition, the large number of germline samples available also allowed us to perform population-level gene burden analysis to identify key genetic factors involved in angiosarcoma predisposition. Taking advantage of the integrative nature of the dataset, we additionally identified transcriptional programs associated with recurrent mutations. Results

The Angiosarcoma Project cohort contains patients with diverse clinical presentations and rare primary sites

The study of angiosarcoma heterogeneity has long been constrained by insufficient sampling across primary sites. In this expanded ASCProject cohort, we assembled a more diverse representation of tumor origins to enable site-specific analyses. Based on the self-reported survey data from 254 angiosarcoma patients, the most common primary site was breast (n = 113, 44.5%), followed by the combined category of head, neck, face, and scalp (HNFS) (n = 61, 24.0%), extremities (n = 26, 10.2%), heart (n = 16, 6.3%), musculoskeletal (n = 12, 4.7%), hepatobiliary (n = 10, 3.9%), other visceral organs (n = 10, 3.9%) including lung (n = 4), bowel (n = 2), kidney (n = 2), ovary (n = 1), urinary (n = 1), and other rare sites (n = 3, 1.2%) including penis (n = 1), brain (n = 1), and nerve (n = 1). [Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Data 1]. Among these patients, 106 (41.7%) have had prior cancer diagnoses, the majority of which were primary breast cancers (n = 43/106, 40.6%) and skin cancers (n = 28/106, 26.4%) [Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2, and Supplementary Data 2]. Among breast angiosarcoma patients with extracted medical records (n = 80), 51.3% (n = 41/80) were not radiation-associated, 90.2% (n = 37/41) of whom had never received prior cancer diagnoses. Age at diagnosis also varied by primary site. For example, cutaneous breast angiosarcomas had a median age at diagnosis of 64, while parenchymal breast angiosarcomas had a median age at diagnosis of 39 (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.001) [Fig. 1d].

Metastatic patterns similarly varied by primary sites [Supplementary Fig. 3A]. Among patients with extracted medical records (n = 158), metastatic events were reported in 67 patients (42.4%, 95% CI: 34.6–50.5%, Exact Binomial Proportion Test). Among all metastatic events (n = 140), the most common site of metastasis was the lung (n = 28/140, 20%, 95% CI: 13.7–27.6%), followed by bone (n = 27/140, 19.3%, 95% CI: 13.1–26.8%) and liver (n = 22/140, 15.7%, 95% CI: 10.1–22.8%) [Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3A]. Bone and liver metastasis co-occurrence (n = 11) was the most common, the majority of which had heart (n = 4), spleen (n = 3), or parenchymal breast (n = 3) as the originating primary site [Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3A]. The primary site most frequently associated with metastasis was the heart (n = 8/8, 100%, 95% CI: 63.1%-100%), where all patients had metastatic events [Supplementary Fig. 3A].

A total of 577 treatment entries were extracted from patient electronic health records with an average of 4.27 records per patient [Supplementary Fig. 4A]. Among the medical records, the most commonly prescribed drugs across treatment modality were paclitaxel (n = 101/577, 17.5%, 95% CI: 14.5–20.9%), followed by gemcitabine (n = 79, 13.7%, 95% CI: 11.0–16.8%), doxorubicin (n = 69, 12.0%, 95% CI: 9.42–14.9%), docetaxel (n = 64, 11.1%, 95% CI: 8.65–13.9%), and drugs in a clinical trial (n = 38, 6.59%, 95% CI: 4.70–8.93%) [Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 3]. These five drug categories in combination account for 56.1% of all treatment records in the metastatic setting (n = 156/278, 95% CI: 50.0–62.0%), among which olaratumab (n = 18/278, 6.47%, 95% CI: 3.88−10.0%), pembrolizumab (n = 13, 4.68%, 95% CI: 2.51–7.86%), pazopanib (n = 12, 4.32%, 95% CI: 2.25−7.42%), and ifosfamide (n = 10, 3.60%, 95% CI: 1.74−6.52%) were also prescribed at least 10 times.

Transcriptomic heterogeneity of angiosarcoma reveals primary site-specific programs and therapeutic vulnerabilities

To characterize the transcriptional and mutational landscape of angiosarcomas, we conducted bulk RNA-sequencing and whole-exome sequencing of 159 angiosarcoma tumors from FFPE samples, with 122 tumors passing RNA quality filters and 94 passing exome quality filters with no significant bias of tumor purity by primary sites [Methods, Supplementary Fig. 5]. After harmonizing the gene expression across samples, we performed principal component analysis based on the top 2000 most highly variable genes across samples, which revealed qualitative sample separation by primary sites, cutaneous status, morphology, and other clinical attributes [Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Figs. 6A–I, Methods]. To group samples by transcriptional similarities, unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on the principal components is performed, which reveals 5 major clusters [Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 7a]. Among these clusters, Cluster 1 is mostly homogenous, with most of the samples being parenchymal breast samples and these samples were reciprocally mostly assigned to Cluster 1 [Supplementary Fig. 7B]. To identify characteristics associated with each angiosarcoma subtype, differentially upregulated genes and pathways are then identified in a one-versus-rest manner while adjusting for sample purity and inferred sex [Fig. 2d and Supplementary Data 4–6, Methods]. Additionally, to identify potential therapeutic vulnerability associated with each subtype, the expression of genes targeted by a curated set of therapies is also highlighted [Supplementary Fig. 8A, B]. To ensure the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are not entirely driven by tissue-specific signals, we compared the proportion of tissue-specific genes between above and below the nominal significance threshold and observed no significant bias [Method, Supplementary Fig. 9A, B]. Similarly, we observed similar pathway enrichment signal with or without removing tissue-specific genes prior to enrichment analysis [Method, Supplementary Fig. 9C, D].

Parenchymal breast angiosarcomas are characterized by TGF-Beta and receptor tyrosine kinase upregulation

In parenchymal breast angiosarcoma, the most significantly upregulated genes include the well-established angiogenic receptor FLT1 (encodes VEGFR-1, log2FC = 1.95, padj = 2.41e-14) and markers of cancer-associated fibroblasts such as NID2 (log2FC = 1.63, padj = 2.54e-17) [Supplementary Fig. 10A]. Indeed, the most enriched pathway for parenchymal breast samples includes epithelial mesenchymal transition (Odds Ratio = 4.38, padj = 4.62e-11) and angiogenesis (Odds Ratio = 4.75, padj = 0.010) [Supplementary Fig. 10B], and TGF-beta upregulation (Odds Ratio = 3.49, padj = 4.53e-06) [Supplementary Fig. 10C]. The most frequently mutated genes include PLCG1 (35.7%, n = 10/28), KDR (encodes VEGFR2, 32.1%, n = 9/28), PTPRB (25.0%, n = 7/28), POT1 (21.4%, n = 6/28), and PIK3CA (14.3%, n = 4/28). Among these mutations, the hotspot constitutive activating mutation KDR p.T771R was the most common (17.9%, n = 5). Notably, VEGFA, which encodes VEGF, was not significantly overexpressed (log2FC = -0.31, padj = 0.46). Instead, other members of the receptor tyrosine kinase family such as PDGFB (log2FC = 1.59, padj = 2.88e-06), PDGFRB (log2FC = 1.05, padj = 2.48e-06) and NRP1 (log2FC = 1.55, padj = 1.12e-12), a known co-receptor that promotes VEGFR2 dimerization17, were overexpressed [Supplementary Fig. 8A]. The overexpression of non-VEGF receptor tyrosine kinases and the presence of constitutive activating mutations in KDR suggest parenchymal breast angiosarcomas may be more reliant on direct increases of pro-angiogenic cell signaling independent of VEGF expression, may provide context for the lack of efficacy of some anti-VEGF therapies18 and provide a molecular rationale for targeting signaling downstream of VEGF in future studies.

Cutaneous breast angiosarcomas exhibit MYC activation

In cutaneous breast angiosarcoma samples which were all radiation-associated, MYC is among the most highly expressed genes (log2FC = 1.64, padj = 6.14e-18) [Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 11A] and its regulated genes dominate both the hallmark (Odds Ratio = 12.93, padj = 1.11e-33) and oncogenic pathway (Odds Ratio = 7.35, padj = 3.02e-12) enrichment signal as the most upregulated pathway [Supplementary Fig. 11B, C]. Similarly, MYC amplification is also the most common genetic alteration in cutaneous breast samples (50.0%, n = 7/14), followed by AMER1 (21.4%, n = 3/14), HRAS (21.4%, n = 3/14), SETBP1 (21.4%, n = 3/14), TP53 (21.4%, n = 3/14), and BRAF mutations (14.3%, n = 2/14). [Supplementary Fig. 11D]. Outside of MYC, tumor-associated proteases such as MMP17 (log2FC = 2.42, padj = 2.03e-08) and growth factor FGFR4 (log2FC = 2.05, padj = 1.72e-04) were also overexpressed, as well as the immunotherapy target CTLA4 (log2FC = 1.70, padj = 0.00062) [Supplementary Fig. 8A], which provides a molecular rationale to investigate anti-CTLA4 immunotherapy in cutaneous breast angiosarcoma.

HNFS angiosarcomas show metabolic rewiring and upregulation of immunotherapy targets

Head, neck, face and scalp (HNFS) angiosarcomas are enriched for genes related to lipid metabolism such as FADS2 (log2FC = 2.98, padj = 1.91e-15) and the melanoma oncogenic driver GRM1 (log2FC = 5.21,padj = 1.28e-14) [Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 12A]. In addition, the enrichment of genes involved in cholesterol homeostasis (Odds Ratio = 6.76, padj = 4.33e-10) [Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 12B] may reflect metabolic reprogramming to support endothelial cell proliferation19. Furthermore, HNFS angiosarcomas also overexpress genes upregulated in PGF-treated human umbilical cord vein endothelial cells (log2FC = 2.66, padj = 5.03e-04) [Supplementary Fig. 12C], which has been associated with invasion and angiogenesis in melanoma cells20. The mutational landscape of HNFS angiosarcoma consists mostly of TP53 mutations (56.0%, n = 14/25), where mutations in POT1 (32.0%, n = 8/25), FLT4 (20.0%, n = 5/25) were also seen in TP53 wildtype samples [Supplementary Fig. 12D]. Moreover, PDCD1 (encodes PD-1) was also upregulated in HNFS samples (log2FC = 1.13, padj = 0.04) [Supplementary Fig. 8A]. Taken together, this suggests therapies that target cholesterol biosynthesis and immunotherapy may be potential strategies to treat the highly UV-driven HNFS angiosarcomas.

Cardiac angiosarcomas overexpress HEY2 and carry mutations in chromatin remodelers

One of the top overexpressed genes in cardiac angiosarcoma was HEY2 (log2FC = 4.58, padj = 5.18e-12), which is a critical regulator of cardiac chamber development and whose overexpression has been shown to be able to induce endothelial-mesenchymal transition in vitro21 [Supplementary Fig. 13A]. While none of the hallmark pathways were significantly enriched, likely due to low samples size [Supplementary Fig. 13B], the ERRB2 oncogenic program (Odds Ratio = 2.81, padj = 0.028) was enriched [Supplementary Fig. 13C], which was shown to regulate HEY2 expression in conjunction with FGF signaling22. KDR mutation is present in 3 out of 4 cardiac angiosarcoma patients, where 2 patients have a co-occurring mutation in ARID1A and 1 in KMT2C. The fourth patient had mutations in TP53 and ASXL1 [Supplementary Fig. 13D]. These findings highlight the importance of oncogenic drivers and mutations in chromatin remodeling genes in the pathogenesis of cardiac angiosarcoma.

Extremities angiosarcomas exhibit myogenic and lymphangiogenic phenotypes

Extremities angiosarcomas were highly enriched for skeletal muscle structural genes such as ACTN3 (log2FC = 6.38, padj = 4.77e-11) [Supplementary Fig. 14A]. While these associations may be partly influenced by the stroma, the enrichment of myogenesis (Odds Ratio = 9.11, padj = 2.60e-13) and interferon alpha (Odds Ratio = 6.35, padj = 0.0010) and interferon gamma signaling (Odds Ratio = 3.25, padj = 0.024) suggests a highly plastic and immune-active microenvironment [Supplementary Fig. 14B]. While the cancer gene set lacked enrichment [Supplementary Fig. 14C], the presence of MYC amplification (25.0%, n = 2/8), mutations in MYC co-factor TRRAP (37.5%, n = 3/8), and mutations in the lymphangiogenic receptor FLT4 (encodes VEGFR3, 25.0%, n = 2/8) in lymphedema-associated angiosarcoma patients suggests these tumors may harbor druggable vulnerabilities within the MYC signaling and lymphangiogenic pathways.

Cluster-based pathway enrichments reveal YAP signature in rare sites

In other rare site angiosarcoma samples that were not classified into one of the previous sites, genes related to red blood cell identity were overexpressed, including the erythroid markers CA1 (log2FC = 7.56, padj = 2.11e-23) and GYPA (log2FC = 10.03, padj = 6.47e-16) [Supplementary Fig. 15A], likely a reflection of high blood content in these samples. Consistent with this, heme metabolism is by far the most enriched hallmark pathway (Odds Ratio = 39.04, padj = 10.0e-48) [Supplementary Fig. 15B], where the ATF2 overexpression (Odds Ratio = 5.50, padj = 0.026) was the only significant oncogenic gene set [Supplementary Fig. 15C]. The mutational landscape is heterogeneous, including mutations in TP53 (16.7%, n = 2/12) and ATRX (8.3%, n = 1/12), as well as deletion of ING1 (16.7%, n = 2/12) [Supplementary Fig. 15D].

While overexpression analysis of angiosarcoma subtypes by anatomical sites may capture the strongest site-specific signal, the degree of transcriptomic and mutation overlap between sites suggests site-specific classification may be insufficient in capturing underlying biological states. To address this, we regrouped the samples by their unsupervised cluster [Supplementary Fig. 16] and performed differential expression analysis to identify cluster-specific markers [Supplementary Figs. 17–21] and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities [Supplementary Fig. 8B]. In particular, Cluster 2, which contained the most samples from rare primary sites, showed upregulation of the oncogenic YAP signature (Odds Ratio = 5.67, padj = 0.0010) [Supplementary Fig. 18C], suggesting potential convergence of oncogenic programs across primary sites.

Cutaneous angiosarcomas are high in TMB, while primary angiosarcomas are more often defined by driver mutations

Next, we identified the most frequently mutated genes in the entire cohort. Overall, TP53 mutations were the most common (n = 25.5%, n = 24/94) with statistically significant higher mutation rates than expected as inferred by MutSig2 (q = 5.28e-11), followed by other significant genes such as POT1 (17.0%, n = 16/94), KDR (16.0%, n = 15/94), PLCG1 (14.9%, n = 14/94), ASXL1 (8.51%, n = 8/94) and BRAF (4.3%, n = 4/94) [Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 7]. Other frequently recurrently altered genes include PTPRB (11.7%, n = 11/94), MYC (11.7%, n = 11/94), ING1 (11.7%, n = 11/94), FLT4 (9.6%, n = 9/94), and ARID1A (7.4%, n = 7/94) [Fig. 3c]. Among these genes, some have mutation bias in cutaneous angiosarcoma, such as MYC (padj = 0.0061) and TP53 (padj = 0.021), whereas KDR has mutation bias in non-cutaneous angiosarcoma (padj = 0.021) [Fig. 3d]. There are also several mutations that are shared among patients, such as the highly recurrent KDR p.T771R mutation (n = 7, median VAF = 0.26), CGREF1 p.E249K (n = 3, median VAF = 0.25), and POT1 p.R117C (n = 3, median VAF = 0.25) [Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 23]. Altogether, the top 20 most mutated genes account for 83.0% (n = 78/94) of the mutations found in the angiosarcoma cohort.

Characterization of germline pathogenic variants in angiosarcoma patients

To complement somatic genomic and transcriptional analysis, we also comprehensively profiled the germline contributions to angiosarcoma. Blood and saliva samples from 232 patients were profiled with WES with an average coverage of 301.1X (IQR: 127.6X-424.82X). After quality control, a germline variant filtering pipeline based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines was applied to identify putative germline pathogenic variants from 229 unrelated angiosarcoma patients [Methods, Supplementary Fig. 24]. Germline POT1 pathogenic variant carriers have a significantly increased risk for angiosarcoma on a population level

To determine whether any germline pathogenic variants are occurring at a higher rate in angiosarcoma patients compared to the population background, we next performed gene-burden analysis against an internally curated control of 21,639 cancer-free individuals adjusting for genetic ancestries and sex [Methods]. Among the 58 genes for which putative pathogenic variants were found in at least 2 patients, POT1 was by far the most significantly enriched gene after adjusting for multiple hypothesis testing (p = 1.02e-14, corrected for 1183 tests), where carriers of POT1 germline pathogenic variants are 92.7-fold (95% CI: 31.5–273.1) more likely to develop angiosarcoma than cancer-free controls [Fig. 4b]. Compared to somatic POT1 mutations, which were all missense mutations occurring in the DNA-binding region of POT1, germline POT1 pathogenic variants were generally loss-of-function frameshift variants occurring near the TPP1 binding domains of POT1 [Fig. 4c], suggesting distinct selection pressure between POT1 mutation types.

POT1 “double-hit” is associated with a 20-year earlier development of angiosarcoma

Given the strong association between germline POT1 pathogenic variants and angiosarcoma risk, as well as the prevalence of POT1 somatic mutations among angiosarcoma tumor samples [Fig. 5a], we next asked whether these mutations could be co-occurring in the same individual and whether these individuals could have different clinical presentations than the rest of the cohort. To systematically assess this ‘double-hit’ concept, we scanned 5772 genes with at least one germline pathogenic variant or somatic mutation across the cohort to identify putative ‘double-hit’ carriers. Across the entire cohort, 8 instances of “second-hit” were found, among which 1 occurred in MYO15A, 1 occurred in ALMS1, 1 occurred in ASPM, and 5 occurred in POT1 [Fig. 4c]. Notably, while patients with either only germline pathogenic variants in POT1 or having POT1 somatic mutations are not significantly younger than patients with no detected POT1 mutations (mean age: 68.3 vs 54.2 [p = 0.091, MWU] and 66.7 vs 54.2 [p = 0.043], respectively), patients with both germline pathogenic and somatic POT1 non-synonymous mutations developed angiosarcomas ~20 years earlier compared patients with no POT1 mutations (mean age: 35.2 vs 54.2, MWU: p = 0.011). Angiosarcoma tumors with POT1 mutation underexpress TERT and overexpress CHAMP1

To understand whether recurrent mutations in angiosarcoma tumors are associated with different transcriptional programs, we subset to pairs with both tumor WES and RNA available (n = 57) [Fig. 5d] and performed differential expression analysis between mutated and wildtype samples in the top mutated genes in angiosarcoma with sufficient representation in the subset (≥5 mutated samples) [Supplementary Fig. 25A–J and Supplementary Data 10 and 11]. We were able to recover known associations, such as the upregulation of NPR1 (log2FC = 1.73, padj = 0.023), a surface interactor of VEGFR2, in KDR mutated samples [Supplementary Fig. 25B], as well as the upregulation of MYC expression (p = 0.0082) [Supplementary Fig. 25E] and its transcription targets (Odds Ratio = 25.9, padj = 8.83e-68) [Supplementary Fig. 25J] in MYC amplified samples. Furthermore, we found TERT to have significantly lower expression in POT1 mutated tumors (log2FC = −6.45, padj = 0.0038), whereas CHAMP1, which promotes homology-directed repair at telomeres in a telomerase-independent manner23, is significantly overexpressed (log2FC = 0.72, padj = 0.014). Additionally, POT1 mutated tumors are enriched for oxidative phosphorylation genes (Odds Ratio = 5.83, padj = 2.18e-10), which is a signature of telomerase-depleted tumors24. While further experimental validation is required, these transcriptional signatures raise the possibility that POT1 mutated angiosarcoma tumors may utilize the alternative-lengthening of telomere pathway as a telomere maintenance mechanism, which could render them sensitive to therapeutic intervention in the form of ATR and PARP inhibition25.

Discussion

Overcoming the challenges associated with rare cancers such as angiosarcoma requires coordinated efforts from physicians, researchers, and, critically, patients themselves. Through direct patient engagement, the ASCProject generated the most comprehensive clinical and molecular characterization of angiosarcoma to date. By integrating clinical, transcriptomic, somatic tumor, and germline genetic data from over 250 angiosarcoma patients, we provide insights into the heterogeneity between the subtypes of angiosarcomas by primary sites, cutaneous status, and molecular features. Through transcriptional profiling, we highlighted the role of TGF-beta signaling, EMT, MYC signaling, and cholesterol synthesis across subtypes. In particular, the upregulation of pro-angiogenic signaling via constitutively activating mutations or direct overexpression of tyrosine kinase receptors, rather than the direct upregulation of VEGF, offers molecular context for investigating therapies that target signaling downstream of VEGF. Additionally, cutaneous tumors displayed higher tumor mutational burden, UV-associated mutational signatures, and increased expression of immune checkpoint genes, underscoring key differences in immune regulation between cutaneous and non-cutaneous angiosarcomas and highlighting the need to tailor immunotherapies to specific angiosarcoma subtypes. The large number of exome-sequenced patient saliva samples also enabled population-scale gene burden analysis, which revealed POT1 as a pan-angiosarcoma predisposition gene and highlighted the key role of POT1 “double-hit” in the onset of angiosarcoma. Furthermore, the identification of alternate lengthening of telomeres (ALT) programs upregulation in POT1-mutated angiosarcomas may reveal therapeutic vulnerability in tumors beyond angiosarcoma.

While this study provides an initial molecular overview of the different subtypes of angiosarcomas, our observations raise several questions that require further functional follow-up. For example, the enrichment of TGF-beta signaling in parenchymal breast angiosarcoma could be of interest, especially considering exposure to ionizing radiation, one of the biggest risk factors for angiosarcoma, is known to promote phenotypic transformation of endothelial cells into fibroblast-like cells via increasing TGF-beta signaling26. Furthermore, the prevalence of ALT pathways in sarcomas has been long recognized27, and angiosarcoma samples with POT1 mutations have been shown to be associated with higher telomeric contents16,28. Pathogenic germline variants in telomere pathways have also been shown to predispose to other types of sarcoma29. Our observation of POT1 “double-hit” in early-onset angiosarcoma patients, marked by the co-occurrence of germline POT1 loss-of-function variant and somatic POT1 missense mutation, raises the possibility that sarcomas may acquire aberrant telomere activity in a “dominant-negative” manner, where cells with mutations in a telomere regulation gene may have increased selection advantage due to the lack of functional wild-type in hosts with loss-of-function germline variants.

Although our study represents a substantial angiosarcoma cohort, the sample size is still modest compared to other conventional cancer studies, which highlights the need for more community-driven research projects like the Angiosarcoma Project to enable patient participation in research studies, especially for rare diseases where sample size is a key challenge. Due to the nature of the study design, the composition of the cohort is enriched for angiosarcoma survivors, which could lead to biases in under-sampling more aggressive forms of angiosarcoma with poorer prognosis. Additionally, as the cohort was assembled over years across different hospital systems, only limited number of clinical attributes could be extracted. Other limitations of the study include the use of exome-sequencing, which is limited in its capability to capture large structural alterations and gene fusions that are known to exist in angiosarcoma, and alternative approaches such as whole-genome or long-read sequencing could offer a more comprehensive picture of the genomic alteration landscape of angiosarcoma. In addition, while the total number of bulk RNA sequenced tumors is large, not all angiosarcoma primary sites are represented equally, and their inherent heterogeneity as well as microenvironment may confound the site-specific pathway enrichment analysis. This is partially addressed by comparing enriched pathways before and after the removal of tissue-specific genes; however, it does not rule out the possibility that the stromal signal still outweighs tumor-specific signals. Due to the lack of angiosarcoma sequencing data at single-cell resolution, the ability to interpret specific cell types and programs involved in each site also remains limited. Despite these challenges, our study still consolidated a valuable resource for angiosarcoma research and overall offered insights into the genomic landscape as well as the different drivers of angiosarcoma heterogeneity.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All individuals in this study consented to institutional review board-approved protocols (15-057B) that allowed for comprehensive genetic and genomic analysis. This study conforms to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample collection

Protocols for participant recruitment, sample collection, medical record acquisition, angiosarcoma subclassification, and exome sequencing were described extensively in the previous study6. Briefly, for patients who registered for the Angiosarcoma Project and elected to share samples, saliva and blood sample kits were mailed to enrolled patients and returned to the Broad Institute Genomics Platform; for tumor samples, study staff called pathology department associated with each tissue sample and requested 1 hematoxylin and eosin stained (H&E) slide as well as either 5-micron unstained slides or 1 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue block. Tissue samples were received at the Broad Institute by mail and sent to Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center Specialized Histopathology Services - Longwood (SHL) Core for expert pathology re-review (JLH) to confirm the diagnosis of angiosarcoma in each sample. In particular, a H&E slide and three unstained slides from each tumor sample received were reviewed to confirm the presence of angiosarcoma in each sample. In cases without a vasoformative component (i.e., composed entirely of cytologically malignant epithelioid and/or spindle cells without vascular channel formation), immunohistochemistry for ERG was performed to confirm endothelial differentiation. If a given tumor sample was not confirmed to be angiosarcoma, the sample is not included for genomic analysis, but the associated patient information was retained and included in the patient-reported dataset of the ASCProject.

Deparaffinization and sample preparation

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples (sections, scrolls, or 2 mm cores) were deparaffinized using one of two protocols. For the majority of the samples, deparaffinization was achieved using Citrisolv. Briefly, 1 mL of Citrisolv was added to each tube and incubated at room temperature with gentle agitation (250 rpm); incubation times were 10 min for sections/scrolls and 30 min for cores. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged at 21,130 × g (10 min for sections/scrolls; 2 min for cores). This process was repeated with 800 µL of Citrisolv. To remove residual solvent, two sequential washes were performed using 100% ethanol (400 µL and 1 mL, respectively), followed by a 30 s vortex and centrifugation at 21,130 × g. For a subset of samples (n = 5), a modified deparaffinization protocol was employed using Deparaffinization Solution (DPS). Samples were incubated in 640 µL of DPS at 56 °C for 5 min with shaking (700 rpm), followed by centrifugation (21,130 × g for 3 min) and removal of the supernatant. These samples were then washed once with 500 µL of 100% ethanol and centrifuged at 21,130 × g for 3 min. All samples, regardless of the deparaffinization protocol, were air-dried at 37 °C for 20–30 min prior to lysis.

Total nucleic acid extraction

RNA and DNA were extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen, #80234) following the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. Approximately dried pellets were resuspended in 150 µL Buffer PKD and 10 µL Proteinase K, then incubated at 56 °C for 15 min while shaking (700 rpm). Following a 3-min incubation on wet ice, lysates were centrifuged at 21,130 × g for 15 min to separate nucleic acids. The RNA-containing supernatant was transferred to a fresh 2 mL tube for purification, while the DNA-containing pellet was retained in the original tube for parallel processing.

RNA purification

The RNA lysate was incubated at 80 °C for 15 min, mixed with 320 µL Buffer RLT and 1120 µL 100% ethanol, and loaded onto an RNeasy MinElute spin column (21,130 × g, 30 s). On-column DNA digestion was performed using 80 µL of DNase I mix for 15 min, preceded and followed by Buffer FRN washes. After two washes with 500 µL Buffer RPE and a 5 min dry spin at 21,130 × g, RNA was eluted in 30 µL RNase-free water. Eluted RNA was denatured at 65 °C for 5 min and flash-chilled on ice before storage in matrix tubes.

DNA Purification

The DNA pellet was resuspended in 180 µL Buffer ATL and 40 µL Proteinase K, followed by sequential incubations at 56 °C (1 h, 700 rpm) and 90 °C (2 h, stationary). Following a brief centrifugation, 4 µL of RNase A was added (2 min at room temperature). Lysates were mixed with 200 µL Buffer AL and 200 µL 100% ethanol and transferred to a QIAamp MinElute column. The column was washed with 700 µL each of Buffer AW1, Buffer AW2, and 100% ethanol (30 s spins). After a 5 min dry spin at 21,130 × g, DNA was eluted in 50 µL Buffer ATE following a 5 min room-temperature incubation and stored in matrix tubes.

Library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA quality was assessed by using the Caliper LabChip GX2 software to calculate percentage of fragments greater than 200 nt. For each sample, total RNA was used as input for first-strand cDNA synthesis via the Illumina TruSeq RNA Access Library Prep Kit. In version 1.0 and 1.1, 200 ng of total RNA was used for initial library construction, and in version 1.2, 850 ng of total RNA was used. After second-strand synthesis, indexed adapter ligation and PCR amplification were performed libraries to enrich for adapted fragments. An automated PicoGreen assay was then used for library quantification.

Quantified cDNA libraries were then combined into 4-plex pools (200 ng each) for exome-targeted hybridization. In version 1.0 and 1.2 of the capture protocol, probes were hybridized for the recommended duration followed streptavidin magnetic bead capture. In version 1.1, the protocol was optimized by the addition of IDT’s xGen Blocking Oligos during the hybridization step to further reduce off-target binding. In all versions, two subsequent wash steps were performed to remove non-specifically bound products, followed by a second round of amplification to enrich the captured libraries. The enriched libraries were then quantified via qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms and pooled equimolarly. The entire process was executed in a 96-well format using Agilent Bravo or Hamilton Starlet automated liquid handling systems.

Pooled libraries were normalized to 2 nM and denatured with 0.1 N NaOH prior to sequencing. Flowcell cluster amplification and sequencing were performed on Illumina HiSeq 2000 or 2500 platforms according to manufacture protocols using 76 bp paired-end reads and an eight-base index barcode read. Data processing including de-multiplexing and aggregation was conducted using the Broad Picard Pipeline.

Sequencing data processing

All sequencing data were downloaded to the cloud computing platform Terra30, and exome sequencing coverage was calculated using GATK’s (version 3.7) tool “DepthofCoverage”. For the analysis of patient clinical data, extracted medical records are prioritized if available, or else patient-reported data is used. RNA sequencing alignment and quantification were performed using a standard pipeline previously described31. Briefly, STAR was used to align reads to the “hg19” reference genome, and RSEM was used to quantify the abundance of each transcript. For quality control, samples with estimated contamination level ≥ 5%, or having less than 25 million mapped reads with mRNA bases less than 60%, or having more than 5% chimera reads, or having less than 8000 mapped genes were removed. On the transcript level, transcripts with less than a total of 10 reads across all samples were discarded. Quantifications of gene isoforms were then collapsed into the same gene for downstream analysis.

RNA data integration and clustering

To account for gene length bias and inter-sample heterogeneity when analyzing gene expression data, we applied the variance-stabilizing transform (VST) as implemented in DESeq232 to the gene expression matrix to obtain normalized gene expression across the cohort. The top 2000 most highly variable genes across all samples were then selected to perform principal component analysis. To identify sample clusters in an unsupervised manner, hierarchical clustering “hclust” with Ward's clustering criterion “ward.D2” is applied on the Euclidean distance between the normalized expression of the most highly variable genes, where a dendrogram is constructed with tree height corresponds to dissimilarity between merged clusters. To obtain clusters with sufficient samples within each cluster, the dendrogram is visually inspected and cut at height of 200 units of Euclidean distance, yielding clusters each containing minimum of 10 samples [Supplementary Fig. 7a].

Differential gene expression analysis

To account for inter-sample heterogeneity in expression analysis, we first applied the ESTIMATE algorithm33 to infer the purity of each RNA sample and used the expression of the highly sex-specific X chromosome inactivation gene XIST34 to infer the sex of the patient sample, where samples with more than 1000 mapped reads to XIST are classified as female, and those below are classified as male. To identify differentially upregulated genes in clusters either defined by clinically annotated primary sites or unsupervised clustering, we carried out one-sided differential gene expression analysis adjusted for purity and sex in a one-versus-rest manner between sample groups via DESeq2. To ensure the differentially expressed genes are robustly expressed, genes with “baseMean” <100 are removed. Volcano plots are then generated with the “EnhancedVolcano” package35.

To ensure the enrichment signal is not significantly influenced by sample quality, we compared the inferred tumor purity by sample primary site and found no significant bias (Kruskall–Wallis, p = 0.57). Additionally, we define the specificity of a gene’s expression relative to a tissue by its tissue nTPM divided by the gene’s total nTPM across all tissues profiled by the Human Protein Atlas36 and found that while some statistically significant overexpressed genes by tissue are also tissue-specific (defined as above the median of the tissue-specific score for all genes in that tissue), the overall proportion of statistically significant overexpressed genes that are tissue-specific are not systematically significantly higher than those that are not statistically significant [Supplementary Fig. 9]. Furthermore, many of the site-enriched pathways remained significant after the removal of tissue-specific genes, showing that the pathway enrichments are not entirely driven by genes expected in each tissue of origin.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To identify upregulated pathways associated with each primary site and unsupervised sample clusters, we conducted overrepresentation analysis of the significantly overexpressed genes in each primary site/cluster against the hallmark gene sets and the oncogenic gene sets from MSigDB37 using the fast over-representation analysis (FORA) function from the fgsea library38. Except for the RNA-WES integration analysis, where DEGs are defined as overexpressed genes with nominal p-value < 0.05, all overexpressed genes provided as input for pathway enrichment analysis have adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene set odds ratio is quantified as the ratio of the odds of a gene being in the significantly expressed list given it is in the gene set, compared to the odds of it being in the list if it is not in the gene set.

Somatic mutation calling

To identify somatic mutations, samples were profiled with the cancer genome analysis whole-exome characterization pipeline (CGA pipeline) (https://app.terra.bio/#workflows/getzlab/CGA_WES_Characterization_Pipeline_v0.1_Dec2018/2). Briefly, the tumor FASTQ files were aligned to the “b37” reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner39 as implemented in GATK. The ContEST tool40 was then used to estimate the amount of cross-sample contamination, upon which all tumor-normal pairs had a satisfactory contamination fraction (fracContam <0.04). The MuTect tool41 was then applied to identify somatic mutations for each normal-tumor pair, and a panel of normals (PoN) generated from the TCGA dataset, oxoG filter, and FFPE filter was used to reduce false-positive somatic mutation calls. In addition, reads mapped to putative somatic variants were locally realigned with NovoAlign (www.novocraft.com), and an additional round of MuTect inference was performed on the newly aligned BAM files. The Strelka42 tool was then used to detect small somatic insertions and deletions, and all somatic mutations were then annotated using Oncotator43. To ensure sample quality, oxoG and FFPE orientation bias filters (q-value > 30) were used. Additionally, variants without at least one supporting read on both forward and backward strands are filtered out. To obtain copy number alterations, CNVKit was applied on the paired normal and tumor BAM, and copy number events with log2ratio of <−1.5 are called as deep deletion and log2ratio > 1.5 are called as amplification.

Germline variant calling

Germline variants were called using DeepVariant v0.8.044,45 (docker image: “gcr.io/deepvariant-docker/deepvariant:0.8.0”) with the default recommended parameters for whole-exome sequencing. Only variants annotated as “PASS” in the filter column were kept. Variants passing quality filtering were then merged into one VCF file using GATK’s (version 3.7) tool “CombineVariants,” and the “vt” tool (version 3.13) was used on the cohort VCF file to normalize and decompose multiallelic variants. In addition, variants with a read depth of less than 10X or variant allele fraction (VAF) of less than 20% were removed.

Relatedness inference

To account for related individuals in cases and controls prior to subsequent gene enrichment analysis, the tool “PC-AiR” from the GENESIS (version 2.12.0) package was used to perform a principal components analysis (PCA) on the detected germline variants from each cohort, respectively. The tool “PCRelate” as implemented in “Hail” (version 0.2.11, https://github.com/hail-is/hail) was then used to calculate the kinship coefficients between all pairs of individuals within each cohort. Individuals with high kinship coefficients were then removed from each cohort such that no pair had a kinship coefficient above 0.1.

Genetic sex inference

To infer the genetic sex of the cases and controls, the “guess-ploidy” plug-in for bcftools46 was used to derive a log-likelihood sex score. A two-component Gaussian mixture model was then fitted on the scores where the cluster with a higher mean score was assigned as male, and the cluster with a lower mean score was assigned as female. Internal validation using self-reported sex from cases showed high concordance (accuracy: 99.38%) with inferred genetic sex.

Population stratification

To account for population stratification, the genetic principal components (PCs) were calculated for every sample. The merged VCF of cases and controls was first loaded using “Hail” v0.244, and variants with allele frequency > 0.05 or p-value less than 1e-6 from the Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium test were removed from the joint variant set. LD-pruning was then performed on the passing variants with greater than 0.1 correlation within a 1 million base pair window. The function “hwe_normalized_pca” was then used on the resulting set of common variants, and the top 10 PCs were kept for subsequent analysis.

Selection of cancer predisposition genes

To facilitate downstream pathogenic variant filtering, genes with validated evidence of Mendelian cancer susceptibility in humans as well as evidence relating to sarcoma predisposition were curated from three separate sarcoma studies47–49. Among these genes (n = 249), those that were considered in at least two of the three studies, as well as being classified as a “germline tier 1” gene according to COSMIC50 are considered sarcoma-related genes (n = 107). All genes that were considered during the filtering process are available in [Supplementary Data 7].

Germline pathogenic variant filtering

An overview of the filtering pipeline is outlined in [Supplementary Fig. 23]. To annotate the functional impact of germline variants, Variant Effect Predictor (version 108)51 was used with the “ClinVar” plugin (2022-08-29 release). To determine putative germline pathogenic variants, variants passing quality filters were further stratified into two groups based on the genes they impact. In Group 1 are variants impacting sarcoma-related genes. These variants are then further stratified into Groups 1A and 1B depending on whether a “ClinVar” entry exists for these variants. In Group 1A are variants with ClinVar annotations; these variants are considered pathogenic if the number of ClinVar pathogenic submissions is no less than either benign or VUS submissions. In Group 1B are variants without ClinVar annotations, and these variants are considered pathogenic if they have severe consequences, which are defined as having a VEP-inferred primary consequence of one of the following terms: “splice_donor_variant”, “frameshift_variant”, “stop_gained”, “splice_acceptor_variant”, “transcript_ablation”, “stop_lost”, or “start_lost”.

Variants not impacting one of the known sarcoma-related genes are classified into Group 2, and a much more stringent filtering criterion is employed to reduce the false positive rate. For variants in this group, only those with multiple pathogenic or likely pathogenic ClinVar submissions with no conflicts are considered.

After the filtering process described above, variants with gnomAD Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) above 1% are discarded. To avoid including variants detected from systematic sequencing artifacts, variants with >5% allele frequency in either cases or controls are removed. Variants satisfying all of the above filtering criteria are collectively referred to as putative germline pathogenic variants.

Gene burden analysis

After applying the germline pathogenic variants filtering pipeline to both cases and controls, a table containing both angiosarcoma patients and cancer-free controls is constructed, with columns being indicator variables of whether a putative germline pathogenic variant is found in the corresponding gene. For every gene that has at least 2 pathogenic variants found in angiosarcoma patients, a generalized linear model with a binomial family is fitted with angiosarcoma as the outcome and the first 10 genetic principal components and inferred sex of the samples as covariates.

Where PV carrier stands for pathogenic variant carrier. This procedure is repeated for every gene to be tested, and the p-value output from the R “glm” function is reported. Multiple testing corrections and q-values are then computed for each gene based on the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with a false discovery rate of 0.05.
