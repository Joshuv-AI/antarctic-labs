On a new theory of models for formal mathematical systems
Matthias Kunik

Abstract
. We study a new model theory for formal mathematical systems that we developed in a previous paper. We introduce isomorphic and homomorphic structures for formal languages, present some results and examples and conclude our paper with a discussion about the reduced set theory RST adapted to our new theory.



Keywords:
Formal mathematical systems, isomorphisms and homomorphisms, new model theory.

Mathematics Subject Classification: 03C07, 03F03, 03C55

1.
Introduction

In
[
3
]
we have presented a unified theory for formal systems
including recursive systems closely related to formal grammars, including
the predicate calculus as well as a formal induction principle.
In
[
3
, (3.13) Induction Rule (e)]
we use an own rule of inference for formal induction and special predicate symbols for the underlying recursive systems. The remaining rules of inference
[
3
, (3.13) (a)-(d)]
without induction and recursive systems provide an own
Hilbert-style predicate calculus which is similar
to that of Shoenfield in his textbook
[
7
]
.
We will shortly summarize this theory in Section
2
.

In
[
6
]
we have presented a a new definition of a model for a formal mathematical system and derived some basic results,
namely the Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem for elementary submodels and related results like the correctness of the logic calculus.
We have seen in
[
6
]
that it is sufficient to present
the theory of models only for formal mathematical systems without
using the Induction Rule from
[
3
, (3.13)(e)]
.
In Section
3
we introduce homomorphic and isomorphic structures of formal languages, where formal languages are defined
as formal mathematical systems without basis axioms.
Our theory is more general and flexible for applications as compared to the concepts in
[
1
]
, and it is also better suited to a formal approach.
This is supported by the general syntax we use in our formal systems.
We show how the new concepts fit into the existing theory and illustrate this with some examples.
We conclude our study with a discussion of the reduced axiomatic set theory RST from
[
5
]
, which we developed for our model theory.

2.
Formal mathematical systems

For the formal mathematical systems from
[
3
, Sections 3,4]

we use five rules of inference, namely rules (a)-(e) from

[
3
, (3.13)]
. Rule (e) enables formal induction with respect to the recursively enumerable relations generated by an underlying recursive system
S
.
For the theory of models we put
S=S_{\emptyset}=[\,[\,];[\,];[\,]\,]

in order to avoid the use of rule (e).
Then we can shortly write
M=[A;P;B]

instead of
M=[S_{\emptyset};A;P;B]

for our formal systems.
We denote the countably infinite alphabet of variables by

X=\{\,{\bf x_{1}}\,,\,{\bf x_{2}}\,,\,{\bf x_{3}}\,,\,\ldots\,\}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Formal mathematical systems

Let
M=[A;P;B]
be a formal mathematical system and
{\mathcal{L}}

a given subset of
A
-lists from
[
3
, Section 3]
with the properties

(i)

X\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}
,

(ii)

\lambda\frac{\mu}{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}

for all
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathcal{L}}
,
x\in X
,

(iii)

all formulas in
B
contain only argument lists in
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Then
[M;{\mathcal{L}}]
is also called a formal
mathematical system (with restricted argument lists
{\mathcal{L}}
).
A formula in
[M;{\mathcal{L}}]
is a formula in
M

which has only argument lists in
{\mathcal{L}}
.
A proof
[\Lambda]
in
[M;{\mathcal{L}}]
is a proof in
M
,
using rules (a)-(d) with the restrictions

(iv)

the formulas in
[\Lambda]
and
the formulas
F
and
G
in
[
3
, (3.13)(a)-(d)]

contain only argument lists in
{\mathcal{L}}
,

(v)

there holds
\lambda\in{\mathcal{L}}

for the list
\lambda
in
[
3
, (3.13)(c)]
.

Let
\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})
be the set of formulas provable
in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
by using only the rules of inference (a),(b),(c),(d)
from
[
3
, (3.13)]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
[M;\mathcal{L}]
be a formal mathematical system.

(a)

[M;\mathcal{L}]
is called
contradictory
if

F\in\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})
for every formula
F
in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
,
otherwise we say that
[M;\mathcal{L}]
is
consistent
.

(b)

[M;\mathcal{L}]
is called a
Henkin system

if for every variable
x\in X
and for every formula
F
in
[M;\mathcal{L}]

with
\mbox{free}(F)\subseteq\{x\}
there is a constant
c\in\mathcal{L}

such that

\displaystyle\to\exists xF\,F\frac{c}{x}\,\in\,\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})\,.

We obtain from
[
3
, (3.19)]
that the latter condition may be replaced with

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\exists xF\,F\frac{c}{x}\,\in\,\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})\,.

(c)

[M;\mathcal{L}]
is called
complete
if

F\notin\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})\Leftrightarrow\neg F\in\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})

for every closed formula
F
in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Given are two formal mathematical systems
[M;\mathcal{L}]
and

[M^{\prime};\mathcal{L}^{\prime}]
with
M=[A;P;B]
and
M^{\prime}=[A^{\prime};P^{\prime};B^{\prime}]
.

(a)

We say that
[M^{\prime};\mathcal{L}^{\prime}]
is an
extension

of
[M;\mathcal{L}]
if

A\subseteq A^{\prime}\,,\quad P\subseteq P^{\prime}\,,\quad\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}~\textrm{~and~}~\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Pi(M^{\prime};\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\,.

(b)

Let
[M^{\prime};\mathcal{L}^{\prime}]
be an extension of

[M;\mathcal{L}]
. If we have in addition

F\in\Pi(M^{\prime};\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\implies F\in\Pi(M;\mathcal{L})

for all formulas
F
in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
, then

[M^{\prime};\mathcal{L}^{\prime}]
is called a
conservative extension
of

[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

3.
Homomorphic and isomorphic structures

of formal mathematical languages

Let
[M;\mathcal{L}]
be a formal mathematical system with
M=[A;P;B]
.

A model

\mathcal{D}
of
[M;\mathcal{L}]

consists of the following ingredients:

(1)

We have a nonempty set
\mathcal{D}_{*}
, also called the universe of the model. The members
d\in\mathcal{D}_{*}
are called the individuals of the universe.

(2)

For each individual
d\in\mathcal{D}_{*}

we have exactly one name
\alpha_{d}
and the set of names

\mathcal{N}=\{\alpha_{d}\,:\,d\in\mathcal{D}_{*}\,\}\,.

It is understood that different individuals have different names and that the names in
\mathcal{N}
are different from the symbols in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

(3)

Put
\hat{A}=A\cup\mathcal{N}
,

\hat{M}=[\hat{A};P;B]
and

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathcal{L}}}=\{\,

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{\kappa_{1}}{x_{1}}...\frac{\kappa_{m}}{x_{m}}\,\,:\,\,\lambda\in{\mathcal{L}},\,x_{1},...,x_{m}\in X,\,

\displaystyle\kappa_{1},...,\kappa_{m}\in\mathcal{N}\,,\,m\geq 0\}\,.

There results an extended mathematical system
[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
.
Let
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
be the set of all lists
\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}

without variables, i.e. we have
\mbox{var}(\lambda)=\emptyset
.

(4)

We have a
surjective
function

\mathcal{D}:\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}\to\mathcal{D}_{*}
with

\mathcal{D}\left(\lambda\frac{\mu}{x}\right)=\mathcal{D}\left(\lambda\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{D}(\mu)}}{x}\right)

for all
\mu\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
, for all variables
x\in X

and for all
\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
with

\mbox{var}(\lambda)\subseteq\{x\}
.

(5)

To each predicate symbol
p\in P

and each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
we assign an
n
-ary predicate
p_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{*}^{n}
. Especially for
n=0
we either have a truth value
p_{0}=\top
or
p_{0}=\bot
.

(6)

We have an extension of the function
\mathcal{D}
, which assigns a truth value to each closed formula of
[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
.
This extension is also denoted by
\mathcal{D}
and is given by

6.1

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\sim\lambda,\mu)=\top\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{D}(\lambda)=\mathcal{D}(\mu)

for all
\lambda,\mu\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
,

6.2

\mathcal{D}(p)=p_{0}\in\{\top,\bot\}
for all
p\in P
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(p\,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\top\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{D}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{D}(\lambda_{n}))\in p_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and all

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
.

6.3

We have for all closed formulas
F,G\mbox{~of~}[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\neg F)=\top\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{D}(F)=\bot\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\to FG)=\top\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{D}(F)\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}(G))\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\leftrightarrow FG)=\top\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{D}(F)\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{D}(G))\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\&\,FG)=\top\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{D}(F)~\mbox{and}~\mathcal{D}(G))\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\vee\,FG)=\top\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{D}(F)~\mbox{or}~\mathcal{D}(G))\,.

6.4

We have for all
x\in X
and all formulas

F\mbox{~of~}[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
with
\mbox{free}(F)\subseteq\{x\}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\forall x\,F)=\top\Leftrightarrow\left(\mathcal{D}\left(F\frac{\lambda}{x}\right)=\top~\mbox{for all}~\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\exists x\,F)=\top\Leftrightarrow\left(~\mbox{there exists}~\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}~\mbox{with}~\mathcal{D}\left(F\frac{\lambda}{x}\right)=\top\right)\,.

(7)

Let
F
be a formula in

[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
with

\mbox{free}(F)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in X

and
m\geq 0
. We say that
F
is valid in
\mathcal{D}
iff

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(F\frac{\lambda_{1}}{x_{1}}\ldots\frac{\lambda_{m}}{x_{m}}\right)=\top

for all
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
.
Note that this simply means

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\forall x_{1}\ldots\forall x_{m}F\right)=\top
.

(8)

We require that every formula
F\in B
is valid in

\mathcal{D}
. Then we say that
\mathcal{D}
is a model
for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

Remark 3.1
.

(a) We say that an extended mapping
\mathcal{D}
which satisfies
only Conditions (1)-(7) is a
structure
for the formal mathematical system

[M;\mathcal{L}]
. Finally, Condition (8) makes it a
model
for

[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

(b) We use a Hilbert-style calculus for our formal mathematical systems. Hence we can use the substitution rule (c) and formulas with free variables in our axioms.
Let
F
be a formula in
[\hat{\mbox{M}};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
and
x\in X
.
Then
F\in\Pi(\hat{\mbox{M}};\hat{\mathcal{L}})
iff

\forall x\,F\in\Pi(\hat{\mbox{M}};\hat{\mathcal{L}})

from
[
3
, (3.11)(a),(3.13)(b)(d)]
. This matches well with Condition (7) in our definition of the models.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
6
, Lemma 3.3]

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a structure for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

Then we have:

(a)

d=\mathcal{D}\left(\alpha_{d}\right)
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}_{*}
.

(b)

For
x\in X
and every formula
H
in
[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]

with
\mbox{free}(H)\subseteq\{x\}
and for all
\mu\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}

we have

\mathcal{D}\left(H\frac{\mu}{x}\right)=\mathcal{D}\left(H\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{D}(\mu)}}{x}\right)\,.

Lemma
3.2
is used to prove the correctness of the logical calculus, as formulated in the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
6
, Lemma 3.5]

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a model for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.

Using the notation from Condition (3) we obtain for every formula
F
in

[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
:

F\in\Pi(\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}})\Rightarrow F\mbox{~is~valid~in~}\mathcal{D}\,.

Here
\Pi(\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}})
denotes the set of provable formulas in
[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a structure of a general formal mathematical system
[M;\mathcal{L}]
with
M=[A;P;B]
. Then we can just drop the basis axioms in
B
:

Definition 3.4
.

If
[M;\mathcal{L}]
is a formal mathematical system without basis axioms, i.e. we have
M=[A;P;\emptyset]
, then we will say that
[M;\mathcal{L}]
is a
formal mathematical language
or a
formal language
for short.
In this case we will also write
M=[A;P]
instead of

M=[A;P;\emptyset]
.

Remark 3.5
.

Every formal language
[M;\mathcal{L}]
is also a formal system
with logical axioms given in
[
3
, (3.9),(3.10),(3.11)]
, namely the axioms of propositional calculus, the axioms of equality and the quantifier axioms.
Now let
\mathcal{D}
be a structure for the formal language

[M;\mathcal{L}]
. Then
\mathcal{D}
is also a model for

[M;\mathcal{L}]
, and we obtain from Lemma
3.3

that every
provable formula
in
[M;\mathcal{L}]

is valid in
\mathcal{D}
. Therefore, it is very convenient to identify the original concept of a formal language with a specific type of formal systems.

Example 3.6
.

Here we consider a special kind of structures for formal mathematical languages, but one that is mainly considered in model theory up to now:

To each constant or function symbol
a\in A
we assign
a fixed arity
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
For
n=0
we say that
a
is a constant symbol, and for
n\geq 1
we say that
a
is an
n
-ary function symbol.
Then
\mathcal{L}
consists only on terms which are generated by the following rules.

1.

We have
x\in\mathcal{L}

for all variables
x\in X=\{\,{\bf x_{1}}\,,\,{\bf x_{2}}\,,\,{\bf x_{3}}\,,\,\ldots\,\}
.

2.

We have
a\in\mathcal{L}
for all constant symbols
a\in A
.

3.

Let
n>0
and let
f
be an
n
-ary function symbol in
A
.

Then
f(\lambda_{1}\ldots\lambda_{n})\in\mathcal{L}
for all terms

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathcal{L}
.

To obtain a structure
\mathcal{D}

for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
we are following the textbook
of Shoenfield
[
7
, Section 2.5]
.

Given is a nonempty set
\mathcal{D}_{*}
. Its members are called individuals.
By induction with respect to the terms we shall now define an individual

\mathcal{D}(t)
for each variable-free term
t
as follows:

i)

Each individual
d\in\mathcal{D}_{*}
has its own name
\alpha_{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of all these names.
We suppose that the symbols in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
are different
from the symbols in
\mathcal{N}
and that

d_{1}=d_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha_{d_{1}}=\alpha_{d_{2}}

for all
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{*}
.
Define
[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}

as in Condition (3).

ii)

To each constant symbol
a\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
we assign a value
\mathcal{D}(a)\in\mathcal{D}_{*}

with
d=\mathcal{D}(\alpha_{d})
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}_{*}
.
To each
n
-ary function symbol
f
we assign an
n
-ary function

f_{\mathcal{D}}:\mathcal{D}_{*}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}_{*}
.
Let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
be variable-free terms and let

\mathcal{D}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{D}(t_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{*}

be defined previously. Then we put

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(f(t_{1}\ldots t_{n}))=f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{D}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{D}(t_{n}))
.

The remaining steps of the construction are due to Conditions (5)-(7).
Then
\mathcal{D}
is a structure for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
, and
Shoenfield’s first lemma in
[
7
, Section 2.5]

is just a variant of our Lemma
3.2
.

The kind of structures and models we study is more general,
because we don’t just model functions and relations.
This is supported by the general syntax we use in our formal systems,
as the following example shows:

Example 3.7
.

We define
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
with
n\geq 1
distinct symbols

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and the set
\mathcal{L}
of strings
generated by the following rules:

•

We have
X\subseteq\mathcal{L}
with
X\cap A=\emptyset
,

X
the set of variables.

•

We have
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{L}
.

•

If
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{L}
, then

\lambda\mu\in\mathcal{L}
.

We obtain a formal language

[M;\mathcal{L}]
with
M=[A;P]
and a given set
P
of predicate symbols. Let
\mathcal{L}_{*}
be the set of all strings

\kappa\in\mathcal{L}
without variables,
i.e. we have
\mbox{var}(\kappa)=\emptyset
. We put

\mathcal{D}_{*}=\mathcal{L}_{*}
and define the set of names

\mathcal{N}=\{\alpha_{\kappa}\,:\,\kappa\in\mathcal{D}_{*}\}\,.

We may assume without loss of generality that these names are not part of the formal language
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.
Let
[\hat{M};\hat{\mathcal{L}}]
result from
[M;\mathcal{L}]

by adding the names in
\mathcal{N}
to
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.
Let
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
be the set of all lists

\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
without variables.
We prescribe
\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
.
Let
\alpha_{\kappa_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{\kappa_{m}}
with
m\geq 0

and
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{*}

be the complete list of distinct names occurring in
\lambda
,
ordered according to their first occurrence in
\lambda
.
Let
x_{1},\ldots x_{m}
be distinct variables and let
\tilde{\lambda}
result from
\lambda

if we replace
\alpha_{\kappa_{1}},...,\alpha_{\kappa_{m}}
everywhere in
\lambda

by
x_{1},...,x_{m}
, respectively. Since the names in
\mathcal{N}

are not involved in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
,
we have
\tilde{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}
and can define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\lambda)=\tilde{\lambda}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{x_{1}}\ldots\frac{\kappa_{m}}{x_{m}}\in\mathcal{L}_{*}
for

\displaystyle\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}\frac{\alpha_{\kappa_{1}}}{x_{1}}\ldots\frac{\alpha_{\kappa_{m}}}{x_{m}}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
.

The resulting function
\mathcal{D}:\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{*}\to\mathcal{D}_{*}
replaces every name

\alpha_{\kappa}
occurring in
\lambda
by its original string

\kappa\in\mathcal{L}_{*}
and hence satisfies Condition (4).

Now we can directly follow Conditions (5),(6) and (7) in order to obtain any structure for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
with prescribed relations between strings over the alphabet
A
. We can also study the recursively enumerable relations in
[
3
, (1.12)]
, which is of interest because results like
[
4
, Theorem 3.5,Theorem 3.6]
provide us with natural models of formal systems including structural induction. But this requires more effort because we must also
provide names for equivalence classes of strings.

Now we return to the general situation.
In the sequel we consider a formal language

[M;\mathcal{L}]
with
M=[A;P]
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{j}
be a structure for
[M;\mathcal{L}]

with universe
\mathcal{D}_{j,*}
and the set of names

\mathcal{N}_{j}
for
j=1,2,3
, respectively. We put

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1}=\{\alpha_{d}:d\in\mathcal{D}_{1,*}\}\,,\mathcal{N}_{2}=\{\beta_{d^{\prime}}:d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{2,*}\}\,,\mathcal{N}_{3}=\{\gamma_{d^{\prime\prime}}:d^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{3,*}\}\,.

For each
j\in\{1,2,3\}
we have to require
that the names in
\mathcal{N}_{j}
are new symbols,
not already present in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
, and that the
restricted mappings

\mathcal{D}_{j}|_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}:\mathcal{N}_{j}\to\mathcal{D}_{j,*}
with
\mathcal{D}_{1}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}(\alpha_{d})=d
,

\mathcal{D}_{2}|_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}(\beta_{d^{\prime}})=d^{\prime}
and

\mathcal{D}_{3}|_{\mathcal{N}_{3}}(\gamma_{d^{\prime\prime}})=d^{\prime\prime}

are bijective, respectively, see Lemma
3.2
(a).
Apart from these restrictions we can freely choose the names separately for each structure.
Due to Condition (3) we have to add the names in
\mathcal{N}_{j}
of each structure
\mathcal{D}_{j}
to
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.
For
\mathcal{D}_{j}
with
j=1,2,3
we form

\hat{A_{j}}=A\cup\mathcal{N}_{j}

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathcal{L}}_{j}}=\{\,

\displaystyle\kappa_{1},...,\kappa_{m}\in\mathcal{N}_{j}\,,\,m\geq 0\}\,.

Since we have not added basis axioms, we obtain an extended mathematical language

[\hat{M_{j}};\hat{\mathcal{L}_{j}}]

for
j=1,2,3
, respectively. Let
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j,*}
be the set of all lists
\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}_{j}}
without variables, i.e. we have
\mbox{var}(\lambda)=\emptyset
.

In order to let each structure
\mathcal{D}_{j}
of

assign a truth value to variable-free prime formulas

of
[\hat{M_{j}};\hat{\mathcal{L}_{j}}]
with

p\in P
we have to prescribe predicates
p_{n,j}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{j,*}^{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
p_{0,j}\in\{\top,\bot\}

and
j=1,2,3
, respectively.
We want to find suitable conditions in order to define

between the structures
\mathcal{D}_{1}
,
\mathcal{D}_{2}

for
[M;\mathcal{L}]
.
First we have a mapping

\psi:\mathcal{D}_{1,*}\to\mathcal{D}_{2,*}
.
Next we prescribe
\lambda\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1,*}
and consider for
m\geq 0
the complete list of
distinct names

\alpha_{d_{1}},...,\alpha_{d_{m}}
in
\lambda
,
ordered according to their first occurrence in
\lambda
.
Let
x_{1},...,x_{m}\in X
be
distinct variables

and let
\tilde{\lambda}
result from
\lambda

if we replace
\alpha_{d_{1}},...,\alpha_{d_{m}}
everywhere in
\lambda

by
x_{1},...,x_{m}
, respectively.
Since the names in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
are different
from the symbols in
[M;\mathcal{L}]
, we conclude that

\tilde{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}
with

\mbox{var}(\tilde{\lambda})=\{x_{1},...,x_{m}\}

We have seen that we can extend the most important basic concepts of classical model theory to our new, generalized model theory.
The mathematical structures we have defined in our model theory can, in principle, all be obtained using set theory.
Axiomatic set theory provides some generally accepted rules for dealing with sets, and it intends to lay a foundation of mathematics by using only the primitive terms “set” and “membership”.
More general mathematical structures are defined using these primitive terms.
Such a commonly accepted foundation of mathematics is the Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory ZFC with the axiom of choice,
see Jech
[
2
]
and Shoenfield
[
7
, Chapter 9]
.
ZFC is only dealing with sets whose members are sets again.

In
[
5
]
we have presented a generalization of ZFC,
starting with a fragment of axiomatic set theory
called RST, for reduced set theory.
As in ZFC we are only dealing with sets whose members are sets again.
However, unlike ZFC, RST is a simpler set theory that is better suited to our model theory.

Next we will describe the basic principles of RST and its benefits:

A set
U
is called
transitive
iff
Y\subseteq U

for all
Y\in U
.

By
\mathcal{P}(Y)=\{V:V\subseteq Y\}

we denote the power set of
Y
.

Due to
[
5
, Definition 2.1]

we say a set
U
is
subset-friendly
iff

1.

\emptyset\in U
.

2.

U
is transitive .

3.

For all
Y\in U
we have
\mathcal{P}(Y)\in U
.

4.

For all
Y,Z\in U
we have a transitive set

V\in U
with
\{Y,Z\}\subseteq V
.

Now we are listing the six principles
according to which we are dealing with sets in RST.
For sets
A
,
B
,
U
,
V
,
Y
these are given by

P1.

Principle of extensionality.

If
A
and
B
have the same elements, then
A=B
.

P2.

Subset principle.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a property which may depend on previously given sets, then we can form the subset of
A
given by

U=\{Y:\,\mbox{there~holds~}Y\in A\mbox{~and~}Y\mbox{~has~property~}\mathcal{F}\}\,.

Especially the empty set
\emptyset
can be obtained from this principle.

P3.

Principle of regularity.

If
U
is not the empty set,
then we have
Y\in U
with
U\cap Y=\emptyset
.

P4.

Principle for pairing of sets.

If
A
and
B
are given, then we can find a set
U

with
A\in U
and
B\in U
.
We can combine this with (P2)
to form
U=\{A,B\}
.

P5.

Principle for subset-friendly sets.

If
A
is given, then we have a subset-friendly set
U

with
A\in U
.

P6.

Principle of choice.

If
U
has only nonempty and pairwise disjoint elements
then we can find a set
Y
with the following property:
For every member
A\in U
there exists exactly one set
V
with

Y\cap A=\{V\}
.

The novel feature of (P5) is that it contains the set
A
as
parameter. Hence we can use it step by step.
We will first provide a subset-friendly set
U
with
A=\emptyset\in U
.
Then we can apply (P5) to
A=U
again, and so on.
The correctness of (P5) is guaranteed by
[
5
, Theorem 2.5]
.
In this way we have a sufficiently large set as background available.
Within this set we can perform the required set operations, and we apply the subset axioms directly instead of the replacement axioms.

A subset-friendly set satisfies the following closure properties:

•

If
A\in U
, then
\cup(A)\in U
,

\mathcal{TC}(A)\in U
and
\mathcal{P}(A)\in U
,

•

If
A\subseteq V
and
V\in U
, then
A\in U
,

•

If
A,B\in U
, then
A\cup B\in U
,
A\cap B\in U

and
A\setminus B\in U
,

•

If
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in U
, then
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}\in U
,

•

If
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in U
,
then
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}\in U
.

Here
\cup(A)
denotes the union of a set
A
and

\mathcal{TC}(A)
its transitive closure.
We have shown in
[
5
]
that all these closure properties of the
subset-friendly sets are formally provable in RST.

The new set theory RST now offers us the following advantages:

1.

RST is designed for pure and well-founded sets according to (P1) and (P3). It follows
from
[
5
, Theorem 5.1]
that RST admits transitive
well-founded models which can be extended by adding step by step appropriate new axioms to RST. Then the former transitive model just becomes a transitive set and a member of the extended model. In this way we can extend RST and its transitive models, whenever this is needed.

2.

On the other hand, we have seen in
[
5
]
that even the simplest
models of RST are large and rich enough in order to formalize
most parts of classical mathematics. Hence we can also study
axiomatic set theory within the theory of models,
using universal sets instead of proper classes.

3.

Axiomatic set theory cannot provide an absolutely valid description for large and especially uncountable ordinal numbers,
see
[
6
, Theorem 5.1, Theorem 5.3]
.
By using the reduced set theory RST, we do not make existence statements for such ordinals.
In
[
5
, Theorem 5.1]
we obtained transitive well-founded models for RST whose ordinals are countable and definable in an absolute sense, i.e. they have the same meaning in all these models.

4.

The subset-friendly theories in

[
5
, Definition 4.2]
are extensions of RST with new axioms and new symbols in the formulas. They are well suited for formulating general axiomatic theories in which set theory is available in the background.
A subset-friendly theory is an extension of RST
in the sense of Definition
2.3
, such that the subset axioms that correspond to (P2) remain valid,
but with new symbols in the formulas.

In the paper
[
5
]
, we also presented some
fundamental results to obtain conservative extensions
of RST according to Definition
2.3
(b).
