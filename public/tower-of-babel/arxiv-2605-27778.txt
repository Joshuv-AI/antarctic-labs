Dimensions of Mycielskians of cycles
Brian Chung, Mike Krebs

Abstract
. The Mycielskian is a standard construction studied in many an introductory graph theory course. It is natural to consider Mycielskians of cycles, some of the simplest of all graphs. This paper deals with the so-called “dimension” of such graphs. The dimension of a graph G G is the smallest positive integer n n such that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the vertices of G G and some collection of points in n n -dimensional Euclidean space such that if two vertices in G G are adjacent, then the distance between the corresponding points is 1 1 . In previous works, it had been proven that the dimension of the Mycielskian of a k k -cycle is 3 when k k is 3 3 , 4 4 , or 5 5 , and 2 when k = 10 k=10 . In this paper, we answer the question completely. Namely, we show that the dimension is 3 3 when k ≠ 10 k\neq 10 , and 2 2 when k = 10 k=10 .

Theorem 1.1
.

\dim(M(C_{n}))=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }n=10\\

The remainder of this paper is primarily devoted to making precise all preliminary details in the run-up to this theorem, and then a proof of the theorem. 1.1.
Elementary notions from graph theory

In this subsection we provide some graph theory basics; readers familiar with them may want to skip ahead to the next subsection.

A (simple)
graph
consists of two sets
V
and
E
, where every element of
E
is a two-element set of
V
. Elements of
V
(the
vertex set
) are called
vertices
, and elements of
E
(the
edge set
) are called
edges
. We denote the vertex set of a graph
X
by
V(X)
, and its edge set by
E(X)
. We visualize vertices as dots, and we visualize an edge
\{a,b\}
as a line segment or curve with
a
and
b
as endpoints. We say that
a
and
b
are
adjacent
, denoted
a\sim b
, if
\{a,b\}\in E
.

The so-called
cycle graphs

C_{n}
comprise one standard family of graphs. The vertex set of
C_{n}
is
\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, where
a
and
b
are adjacent if and only if
a-b\equiv\pm 1\;(\text{mod }n)
.

Two graphs
G
and
H
are
isomorphic
if there is a bijective function
f\colon G\to H
from the vertex set of one to the vertex set of another such that
a
and
b
are adjacent in
G
if and only if
f(a)
and
f(b)
are adjacent in
H
. We say that a graph
H
is a
subgraph
of a graph
G
if
V(H)\subset V(G)
and
E(H)\subset E(G)
.

1.2.
(Unit-distance) dimension of a graph

We define
U_{n}
to be the graph with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as its vertex set, where two vertices are adjacent if and only if the Euclidean distance between them is
1
. We say that a graph is a
unit-distance graph
if it is isomorphic to a subgraph of
U_{2}
.

In
[
2
]
, Erdős, Harary, and Tutte introduce the notion of the “dimension” of a graph. That is, they define the
(unit-distance) dimension
of a graph
G
, denoted
\dim(G)
, to be the minimum positive integer
n
such that
G
is a subgraph of
U_{n}
. (Although
[
2
]
simply calls this the “dimension” as do many other publications that followed suit, we include the parenthetical “unit-distance” here to disambiguate this definition from other competing notions of dimension for graphs.)

Example 1.2
.

For an integer
n\geq 3
, we define the
cycle graph

C_{n}
to be the graph with vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
, where two vertices are adjacent if and only if their difference is
\pm 1
modulo
n
. It is straightforward to show that no subgraph of
U_{1}
is isomorphic to a cycle graph. However, one obtains a subgraph of
U_{2}
isomorphic to
C_{n}
by considering a regular
n
-gon of side length
1
. In this way
C_{3}
“looks like” an equilateral triangle,
C_{4}
a square, and so on. It follows that
\dim(C_{n})=2
.

Example 1.3
.

For an integer
n\geq 2
, we define the
complete graph

K_{n}
to be the graph with vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
, where every vertex is adjacent to every other vertex. It is relatively straightforward to prove that
\dim(K_{n})=n-1
. The embedding in
U_{n-1}
is achieved by taking the vertices of a regular
n
-simplex with side length
1
. So
K_{2}
“looks like” a line segment,
K_{3}
an equilateral triangle,
K_{4}
a regular tetrahedron, and so on.

We remark that there is a definition related to that of (unit-distance) dimension, namely,
Euclidean dimension
. Here, one insists that the subgraph of
U_{n}
be an induced subgraph. But Euclidean dimension is not the subject of the present paper, so we shall say nothing more of it.

.

3.
A generalization

The method of proof used in Theorem
1.1
can be generalized, as we now discuss. Given a graph
G
, we define an auxiliary graph
\text{VecNeg}(G)
as follows. We regard
G
as a directed graph, with a directed edge from
A
to
B
whenever
A
and
B
are adjacent in
G
. We denote the edge from
A
to
B
by
\overrightarrow{AB}
. The vertex set of
\text{VecNeg}(G)
is the set of directed edges of
G
. Recall that a
walk
in a graph is a sequence
(v_{0},v_{1},\dots v_{n})
of vertices, where
v_{i-1}
is adjacent to
v_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. We sometimes omit the parentheses and commas when writing a walk. The
length
of the walk
(v_{0},v_{1},\dots v_{n})
is
n
, and this walk is said to be
closed
if
v_{0}=v_{n}
. The vertices
\overrightarrow{AB}
and
\overrightarrow{CD}
of
\text{VecNeg}(G)
are adjacent if and only if (i)
C=B
and
D=A
, or (ii) the vertices
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
are distinct, and
ABCDA
is a closed walk of length
4
in
G
. Recall that a finite graph is
nonbipartite
if and only if it contains a closed walk of odd length.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite graph. Then
\dim G\geq 3
if either

(1)

The graph
\text{VecNeg}(G)
is nonbipartite, or

(2)

For some distinct vertices
F,A,B
of
G
, where
A
and
B
are adjacent to
F
, there exists in
\text{VecNeg}(G)
a walk of even length from
\overrightarrow{FA}
to
\overrightarrow{FB}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is isomorphic to a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
U_{2}
. By a slight abuse of notation, we identify
G
with
G^{\prime}
, and every directed edge in
G
with the corresponding unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We first observe that if
\overrightarrow{AB}
and
\overrightarrow{CD}
are adjacent in
\text{VecNeg}(G)
, then
\overrightarrow{AB}=-\overrightarrow{CD}
. (Hence the notation “VecNeg.”) Here’s why. If
C=B
and
D=A
, then the result is immediate. Otherwise, we have that the vertices
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
are distinct, and
ABCDA
is a closed walk of length
4
in
G
. This implies that
ABCD
forms a rhombus of side length
1
, and the result follows.

If (
1
) holds, then there is a closed walk
(e_{0},\dots,e_{n})
of odd length in
\text{VecNeg}(G)
. Here each
e_{i}
is a directed edge in
G
. Using the result of the previous paragraph, we get that
e_{i-1}=-e_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Hence
e_{0}=(-1)^{n}e_{n}=(-1)^{n}e_{0}
. This is a contradiction, because
n
is odd, and
e_{0}
is a unit vector.

If (
2
) holds, then by similar reasoning we obtain
\overrightarrow{FA}=\overrightarrow{FB}
. But then
A=B
, a contradiction.∎

The line of reasoning formalized in the proof of this theorem can be found in
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[

]
.

The reader who wishes to do so can recast the proof of Theorem
1.1
in terms of Theorem

. The central idea is to convert chains of vector equations into walks in the graph
\text{VecNeg}(G)
.
