Executive Summary
This work focuses on the resilience of Pennsylvania’s electric distribution systems, where high-
wind events and severe storms frequently cause widespread power outages. Resilience planning
for these utilities requires understanding how outage patterns vary across service territories and
how system recovery unfolds over time. Pennsylvania’s utilities serve diverse environments
ranging from dense urban areas to sparsely populated rural regions, each facing distinct
operational and meteorological challenges. It is difficult to prioritize and evaluate resilience
upgrades when outage impacts differ across utilities—and when quantifying the benefits of these
investments remains uncertain.

This report presents new data-driven metrics to characterize the resilience performance of
Pennsylvania’s distribution systems during major wind events. We analyze outage records from
multiple utilities to identify patterns in outage initiation, restoration dynamics, and event
severity. By separating outage and restoration processes and tracking customer interruptions over
time, the analysis establishes benchmark metrics that quantify both the intensity and duration of
disruptions. These temporal metrics enable a more consistent evaluation of system performance
under extreme weather conditions.

The findings highlight that wind-driven outages are shaped by a complex interplay between
exposure and network configuration. From a planning perspective, three major insights emerge:

 • Regional heterogeneity requires context-specific standards.
 • Normalization enables cross-system comparison.
 • Data granularity shapes analytical precision.
Overall, the analysis highlights meaningful differences in wind sensitivity and outage behavior
across Pennsylvania utilities, which reinforces the importance of localized resilience planning
and hazard-conditioned performance metrics. By integrating outage processes, restoration
trajectories, and cumulative impact dynamics, this report provides a robust foundation for
evaluating grid resilience to wind and for prioritizing investments in system hardening,
vegetation management, and operational preparedness.

This study provides a direct bridge between observed and projected wind hazard and the
performance metrics commonly used in the PUC’s oversight. By translating wind speed and gust
conditions into customer-impact measures, the analysis enables climate-informed risks to be
evaluated within existing reliability and resilience assessment frameworks.

The results identify actionable wind-speed thresholds at which outage rates and customer
impacts increase nonlinearly. These thresholds provide empirical triggers for planning attention,
which enables utilities and regulators to distinguish routine operating conditions from high-
impact wind regimes that drive disproportionate outage burden and restoration workload.

Overall, this work offers a forward-looking, risk-informed complement to traditional reliability
metrics and enables utilities and regulators to evaluate not only historical performance but also
future system vulnerability and mitigation effectiveness under projected midcentury wind
conditions.

1 Review of Current Requirements and Practices
1.1 Reliability to Resilience
Many definitions of resilience exist; the following definition is used in this report:

  The ability to anticipate, prepare for, and adapt to changing conditions and to withstand,
 respond to, and rapidly recover from disruptions through sustainable, adaptable, and holistic
 planning and technical solutions.

The resilience trapezoid pictured in Figure 1 is helpful to visualize this. It shows a system in
normal operation and then experiencing a disruptive event that progresses through many discrete
outage and restoration activities, until finally the event is over—including longer-term
restoration.

 Figure 1. Resilience trapezoid from Keen et al. (2024)
For several decades, regulators and utilities have included reliability in their policy and planning
practices. Increasingly there is a trend across the industry to advance these activities toward
resilience. As such, it is helpful to consider a continuum that has reliability-focus at one end and
resilience-focus at the other. Reliability is focused on individual outages that occur during
normal operating conditions; resilience is focused on the evolution of a complete major event.
See Figure 2.

1.2 Resilience Frameworks
The purpose of any resilience framework is ultimately to connect risk to appropriate investments
that can alleviate it. Resilience planning workflows commonly begin with identifying hazards—
historical hazards as well as potential high-impact hazards that can occur in the future—while
capturing system characteristics using attribute metrics. The next step of the framework is
analysis, which uses these attribute metrics to connect system characteristics to specific hazards.
Such a categorization can offer insights into specific investments that can be made to mitigate
specific vulnerabilities. This analysis occurs alongside tracking of performance metrics, which
can communicate both how well the system is performing and how effective investments are.

A recent National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) publication (Keen et al. 2024) offers six
resilience components that support a rubric useful for assessing regulatory requirements and
utility resilience plans (Table 1).

 Table 1. Resilience Components, Adapted From Keen et al. (2024)

 Resilience Components

 Preliminary hazard Threat risk analysis
 characterization

 Attribute metrics Investments

 Performance Investment
 metrics prioritization

Brief explanations of these six resilience components, adapted from this work, are given here:

 • Preliminary hazard characterization is a qualitative process used by utilities to determine
 the relative risk of various hazards along with where to focus resilience investments.
 • Attribute metrics measure system characteristics that may be beneficial to resilience and
 can also help utilities determine options to improve their performance metrics.
 • Performance metrics measure a utility’s status in achieving its core objectives (e.g.,
 affordability, safety, reliability, resilience, equity). Major event day System Average
 Interruption Duration Index (SAIDI) is an example of a resilience performance metric.
 • Threat risk analysis is used to quantify the probability, consequence, and vulnerability
 (i.e., risk) of a threat. It can be performed using historical data or forward-looking
 simulations and can be used to determine how system changes (e.g., a new investment)
 affect risk.
 • Investments are discrete considerations or categories of these considerations that a utility
 makes to improve resilience in its service territory.
 • An investment prioritization process identifies cost-effective investments to minimize
 risk. Ideally, this prioritization process will demonstrate the cost and effectiveness of
 investments with respect to specific performance metrics. Cost-benefit analysis is one
 form of investment prioritization.

 2

We used this rubric as a lens through which to assess various resilience frameworks as well as
other states’ resilience planning requirements and their utilities’ resilience plans. To establish an
image of resilience planning activity among the Pennsylvania electric distribution companies
(EDCs), we distributed a set of questions based on these six components of resilience to the 11
utilities and to the TUS. We sought to identify broad aspects of resilience planning practices
across the state among EDCs of varying sizes, and this approach fostered consistency between
EDCs in the aspects of resilience planning they responded to.

For consistent evaluation of these essay-type responses from nearly a dozen different parties, we
identified three resilience content markers for each question and then looked for this content in
each of the 10 responding EDCs’ and the TUS’ responses. Hence, a maximum score of 3 was
possible for each question. Consistent with our goal to emphasize ranges of practices associated
with each question and not the practices of any specific operating company, the results for each
question are shown in Table 2 as the average of all the EDCs’ composite scores, which are
compared to the corresponding composite score for the TUS. For both summary statistics (sum
and average), lower numbers indicate tendency toward a reliability focus, and higher numbers
indicate tendency toward a resilience focus.

This approach does have some acknowledged limitations, such as it being a one-way discourse
rather than a two-way dialogue, that presented responders with a possibly unfamiliar resilience
framework. In addition, responses are not comprehensive descriptions of processes but simply
brief summaries and are based on subjective interpretation of both the questions and the
responses.

Possible next or first steps that regulators in Pennsylvania might consider to move the region’s

 • Develop a hazard identification approach: single hazard or all hazards. The 11 EDCs
 under the PUC’s jurisdiction encompass a wide variety of size, urban to rural customer
 bases, network characteristics, topographical features, and sheer area served—all of
 which results in a uniquely diverse set of hazards and restoration challenges each is

 parties on emerging threats in the region and in specific EDC territories while affording

 the utility must analyze, some hazards may get little attention in the plan.” Two resources
 surfaced during our project that could offer guidance in this area. First, the Pennsylvania

 Association of Pennsylvania, EDCs have been in contact with a Millersville University
 climatologist.
 • Indications from the Pennsylvania TUS are that the region’s most pressing concerns are
 storm related. It is aware of at least the two resources mentioned in the previous bullet

 guidance about specific emerging hazards that Pennsylvania EDCs might want to
 consider in resilience planning.
 • A vulnerability matrix, such as the example shown in Appendix B, can provide consistent

2 Impacts of Wind on Distribution System
  Performance in Pennsylvania
Analysis of utility outage data shows that wind remains one of the most influential drivers of
distribution system outages across Pennsylvania. Even moderate wind conditions can trigger
vegetation contact, conductor galloping, and equipment fatigue. This results in customer
interruptions that accumulate rapidly during regional storm systems. Using multi-year multi-
utility outage records linked with bias-corrected, high-resolution wind data, this section
synthesizes how outage behavior evolves with wind intensity and how restoration unfolds across
major events. The analysis emphasizes event-based and resilience-oriented metrics that reflect
the operational realities utilities face during wind-driven disruptions.

This work draws on multi-year outage records from three Pennsylvania EDCs, each operating
within distinct geographic and vegetation environments. These differences—ranging from
forested ridgelines to river valleys—shape how outages accumulate during wind events and how
restoration unfolds. Table 3 summarizes the key characteristics of the participating utilities most
relevant to wind-driven outage performance. Understanding this system context provides
essential grounding for interpreting the outage-wind sensitivity and restoration dynamics
presented in the reminder of this section.

2.1 Wind as a Primary Driver of Long-Duration Outages
Across Pennsylvania utilities, wind-related events consistently represent the most common cause
of multi-hour interruptions. Outage cause codes show wind, wind-related tree damage, and
associated debris account for most events exceeding 4 hours in duration. Although other causes
such as equipment failures and loss of supply also contribute, these typically result in shorter and
more localized interruptions. This aligns with broader findings in Haakana et al. (2024).

Figure 3 shows the causes of long-duration outages (greater than 4.6 hours) in Utility A. Cause
attribution data reveal vegetation-related outages are the primary drivers of long-duration
interruptions. The combined vegetation categories account for most outage events, which
indicates strong sensitivity to wind-driven tree interactions. This pattern also aligns with Utility
A’s predominantly rural and heavily forested territory, where even moderate increases in wind
speed can produce significant outage clustering. Equipment-related and miscellaneous causes
appear far less frequently, which reinforces the importance of vegetation exposure in shaping
Utility A’s wind-outage behavior.

To assess how outage mechanisms shift as a function of wind exposure, each outage was
assigned to one of three sustained-wind categories and classified according to its underlying
cause. Causes were consolidated into two groups: wind-sensitive mechanisms and non-wind-
sensitive mechanisms. Wind-sensitive mechanisms include vegetation-related contacts and wind-
induced equipment failures, whereas non-wind-sensitive mechanisms include lightning, wildfire,
or scheduled outages. Table 4 reports the percentage of interruptions attributed to wind-sensitive
mechanisms within each wind category.

Under low-wind conditions, most outages arise from non-wind-related causes, which reflects the
background rate of equipment faults and routine service interruptions. In the 20–40 mph
category, the fraction of wind-sensitive outages increases substantially. This transition is
consistent with the onset of branch shedding, tree failures, and mechanical interactions between
conductors and vegetation. Under 40–60 mph conditions, wind-sensitive mechanisms dominate
with vegetation impacts and wind-driven equipment failures accounting for most interruptions.

This monotonic progression across wind categories indicates a threshold regime shift in outage
causation. Once sustained winds exceed approximately 30–35 mph, the outage landscape

becomes overwhelmingly governed by wind-driven processes, which is consistent with the
analysis results in the following section.

2.2 Utility-Level and Systemwide Wind Event Analysis
Wind-driven storm events exhibit threshold behavior across all three participating utilities.
Outage activity generally remains low under modest wind conditions but increases sharply once
gusts exceed the 30–40 mph range. The precise magnitude and timing of these increases differ by
utility because of vegetation exposure, terrain, and rural-suburban land composition.

2.2.1 Wind Sensitivity of Outage Rates
We first characterize how outage occurrence responds to wind conditions. For each utility,
aggregated outage records were related to maximum sustained wind speed and wind gusts using
an exponential response function. Figure 4 summarizes these wind-outage response curves. The
exponential response curves capture the nonlinear increase in outage activity as wind intensity
rises. The shaded regions represent cross-utility variation attributable to differences in vegetation
exposure, terrain, system hardening, and asset condition. Despite these differences, all three
utilities exhibit a similar pattern: Outage incidence remains relatively stable at lower wind speeds
and increases rapidly when wind exceeds approximately 25–30 mph. This behavior indicates a
shared threshold beyond which system vulnerability escalates rapidly, which provides a regional
baseline for wind sensitivity that is largely independent of utility-specific characteristics.

 Figure 4. Comparison of system-level outage response to daily maximum wind speed (left) and
 daily maximum wind gust (right) conditions across three utilities. The shaded areas represent
 range across utilities.

2.2.2 System-Level Outage Burden
To evaluate how systemwide outage impacts scale with wind conditions, we constructed an
hourly metric of duration-weighted customer-minutes of interruptions (CMI) by aggregating all
active outages during each hourly interval. These hourly CMI values were then stratified into
three sustained-wind categories based on the bias-corrected wind fields linked to each utility’s

 9

service territory. Figure 5 summarizes the resulting distributions and demonstrates a strongly
nonlinear relationship between wind intensity and system-level outage impact. Hours with winds
below 20 mph rarely produce substantial customer-minute loss. In contrast, the 20–40 mph range
is associated with a noticeable rise in both median and upper-quartile impacts. The 40–60 mph
category exhibits an order-of-magnitude increase in typical and extreme outage levels, which
reflects sharply elevated system stress. These results show small increments in sustained wind
speed can generate disproportionately large increases in cumulative customer interruption burden
because of the compounding effect of multiple overlapping outages.

2.3 Forward-Looking Wind-Driven Outage Risk and Planning
 Applications
Although the preceding subsections characterize historical outage behavior under observed wind
conditions, forward-looking resilience planning requires understanding how outage risk may
evolve under changing storm patterns, vegetation conditions, and long-term climatic trends. The
exponential response functions estimated for each utility provide a foundation for projecting
outage likelihood under a range of future wind scenarios.

To explore how wind-driven outage risk may evolve under future conditions, we applied the
empirical wind-outage response functions to midcentury (2050) wind projections from Sup3rCC.
Figure 6 compares historical outage response curves, which indicate higher outage rates than
those observed historically with divergence most pronounced when winds exceed the 25–30 mph
sensitivity threshold. This shift reflects increases in the frequency of moderate-to-high wind days
as well as potential changes in storm characteristics that amplify treefall and vegetation-related
damage. The results suggest even small changes in the distribution of wind speeds can lead to
large increases in outage activity, particularly in vegetation-dense service territories.

 10

Figure 6. Historical and midcentury projected wind-outage response curves across three utilities
One key metric from the projected curve is the expected number of outages per day at high wind
intensities. At 60 mph sustained wind speed, the projected future outage rate increases from 510
to 715 outages/day, which represents a 40% increase under midcentury conditions. This result
reflects a combination of both more frequent exceedance of damaging wind thresholds and shifts
in storm characteristics that amplify vegetation-driven failures.

These forward-looking patterns are consistent with recent major-event data. Table 5 summarizes
the percentage of CMI attributable to weather-related causes during major events from 2019 and
2024. Across all years, wind and associated vegetation-driven events consistently account for
more than 90% of total CMI, which underscore the dominant role of wind across Pennsylvania
utilities.

These statistics reinforce two important insights: 1) Major outage impacts in Pennsylvania are
overwhelmingly driven by wind and vegetation exposure and 2) future conditions are likely to

includes vegetation management strategies, hazard-informed resource allocation, and targeted
investments in system hardening.

The forward-looking wind-driven outage framework developed in this study has direct
applications for both utility planning and regulatory review, as follows:

 • Identification of actionable wind thresholds. The analysis identifies sustained wind-
 speed ranges at which outage rates and restoration burden increase sharply. These
 empirical thresholds can inform scenarios analysis, operational preparedness, and risk
 screening.
 • Prioritization of resilience investments. By linking wind exposure to customer-level
 impacts, the framework supports targeted investment decisions such as vegetation
 management strategies, asset hardening, and restoration resource planning—particularly
 in areas prone to high-wind exposure.
 • Complement to traditional reliability metrics. The approach augments backward-
 looking reliability indicators by incorporating projected hazard conditions, which allows
 planners and regulators to evaluate future vulnerability and mitigation effectiveness
 rather than relying solely on historical averages.
Overall, this framework enables a more transparent and evidence-based connection between
climate-driven wind risk and distribution system resilience, which supports planning and
oversight decisions focused on reducing future customer impacts.

3 Event-Based Outage and Restoration Performance
  in Pennsylvania
Wind-driven events affect not only the number of outages a utility experiences but also the
timing and trajectory of outage accumulation and restoration. Traditional reliability metrics such
as SAIDI and SAIFI provide valuable annual summaries but do not capture the internal dynamics
of storms—for example, when outages surge, how rapidly restoration begins, or how cumulative
customer impact grows over the duration of an event. To address this, we evaluate performance
based on metrics from Matsuda-Dunn et al. (2025). These metrics offer a more complete picture
of how utilities respond to wind events.

3.1 Outage Metrics
For each event, the outage process 𝑂𝑂𝑡𝑡 represents the number of active outages at each hour,

Event-based metrics allow us to examine how outage accumulation, restoration progress, and
cumulative customer impact unfold across various Pennsylvania utilities under comparable wind-
driven conditions. Because Utilities A, B, and C operate in distinct geographic and vegetation
environments, their event trajectories reveal meaningful contrasts in system sensitivity,
restoration pace, and overall customer impact.

Figure 7 compares the outage process, restoration process, and cumulative customer impact
across four representative wind-driven events. Each panel displays the hourly evolution of active
outages, completed restorations, and cumulative customer impact normalized by customer count
to ensure statistical comparability. These trajectories highlight several consistent cross-utility
patterns.

 Figure 7. Standardized outage, restoration, and cumulative impact processes for four significant

Across all four events, outage accumulation begins rapidly when wind speed exceeds threshold
levels, which is consistent with the response function in Section 2. However, the pace and
magnitude of outage escalation vary significantly across these events.

Four key patterns were observed:

 • Long tail of residual outages. Even after major restoration progress, a small number of
 customers remain out for extended periods.
 • Plateau behavior after the first operational day. Outages escalate quickly on Day 1
 and then level off. This shows most of the damage happens early, whereas restoration
 extends over several days.
 • Variation in restoration efficiency across events. Differences in restore rate and time-
 to-restore metrics indicate varying workload, access conditions, or resource mobilization.

 • Coupling between outage progression and restoration workload. Events with faster
 outage growth tend to require more aggressive restoration rates. This coupling highlights
 operational challenges during high-wind periods.

4 Conclusion
The outage process, restoration process, and cumulative impact metrics presented in Section 3.1
were calculated using outage data that are presently collected by Pennsylvania EDCs.
Introducing more sophisticated metrics than are required today does not appear to pose a
significant burden to Pennsylvania utilities and could offer opportunities to advance state
planning practices toward resilience. If the frequency of high-wind events increases and EDCs’
distribution systems are not enhanced, the impact to Pennsylvania’s electric customers could be
significant. Of course, the expectation is that Pennsylvania EDCs’ distribution systems will be
enhanced over time, specifically to improve reliability and resilience and by incorporating
resilience into other grid improvement projects. As TUS contemplates the considerations found
in Section 1.5 along with other insights offered throughout this executive summary, we are
confident it will continue to be successful as it guides and supports the state EDCs in advancing
toward more resilience-focused planning.

As reflected in both historical outage records and projected changes in wind exposure, climate-
driven wind risk is increasing across Pennsylvania. However, the customer impacts associated
with high-wind events are not fixed or inevitable. The results of this report demonstrate outage
process, restoration process, and cumulative customer impact vary substantially across utilities
and events, which indicates system vulnerability is shaped not only by hazard intensity but also
by vegetation management practices and restoration strategies. By translating wind exposure into
outage and customer-impact metrics already used in reliability and resilience assessment, this
analysis provides evidence-based tools to identify where mitigation efforts are most likely to
reduce future customer impacts and to support risk-informed planning and investment decisions.
