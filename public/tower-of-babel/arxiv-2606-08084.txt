Assessing model calibration with boosting trees
Selim Gatti

Abstract
The main goal in regression modelling consists in approximating the conditional mean of a response given a set of features. A regression function is said to be calibrated if the resulting mean estimates match the true conditional means for almost every set of features. Aiming for calibration seems not achievable in practice as one typically deals with finite samples of noisy observations. A weaker notion of calibration is auto-calibration, and it means that the expectation of responses being given the same mean estimate matches this estimate. This notion is important, e.g., in insurance pricing as it ensures no cross-subsidization between different price cohorts. In this paper, we show that boosting trees can be used to test necessary conditions for calibration and auto-calibration, respectively. The practical relevance of our approach is supported by a numerical example, in which the proposed tests prove to be very powerful on a large insurance dataset. Keywords. calibration, auto-calibration, regression modelling, mean estimation, insurance pricing.



1
Introduction

In regression modelling, the primary objective is to approximate the true conditional mean of a response given a set of features. To this end, various statistical models are used to fit a regression function that provides a mean estimate for each single set of features. This function is said to be
calibrated
if the resulting mean estimates match the true conditional means for almost all features. Aiming for calibration seems not achievable in practice as models are fitted on finite samples of noisy observations. A weaker notion of calibration is
auto-calibration
(sometimes also called
mean-calibration
or
well-calibration
); see, for example, Krüger–Ziegel
[
22
]
and Denuit et al.
[
7
]
. This notion goes back to earlier works on the reliability of probabilistic forecasts in meteorology; we refer to Bross
[
2
]
, Sanders
[
26
]
and Murphy–Winkler
[
23
]
.
It means that when responses are grouped according to their mean estimates, the average of the responses within each group matches this estimate. This property is important in various applications where sums of mean estimates have to match sums of responses at a global and local level. This is, for example, the case in insurance pricing as an auto-calibrated pricing system avoids systematic cross-subsidy between different price cohorts; we refer the reader to Pohle
[
24
]
, Denuit et al.
[
6
]
, Fissler et al.
[
9
]
and Wüthrich–Merz
[
30
]
.

Testing for calibration has long been studied in the literature. For example, Bierens
[
1
]
considers integrated conditional moment tests, whereas Härdle–Mammen
[
15
]
introduce a test based on the squared distance between the estimated regression function and a kernel-based approximation of the true conditional means. These approaches typically involve a complex construction and require several tuning choices. More recently, Delong et al.
[
3
]
propose a considerably simpler approach based on calibration bands for the special case of responses belonging to the exponential dispersion family.

In contrast to testing for calibration, the development of statistical tests for assessing auto-calibration has, surprisingly, received comparatively limited attention and has only emerged recently. Denuit et al.
[
7
]
propose a test for auto-calibration using Lorenz and concentration curves that requires the evaluation of a non-explicit asymptotic distribution using Monte-Carlo simulations. Wüthrich
[
28
]
provides simpler versions of this test for discrete and finite regression functions. Additionally, Delong–Wüthrich
[
4
]
consider the use of bootstrap techniques. For the special case of binary responses, Hosmer–Lemeshow
[
18
]
derive a
\chi^{2}
-test by binning observations over disjoint intervals, whereas Gneiting–Resin
[
14
]
propose a bootstrap approach to test for auto-calibration in this binary setup. Moreover, Delong–Wüthrich
[
5
]
consider the use of universal inference for responses belonging to the exponential dispersion family.

We take a different approach in this paper. Our goal is to construct statistical tests for calibration and auto-calibration by only considering necessary conditions, which arise from the orthogonal projection property of conditional expectation. In fact, such an approach has already been followed by Fissler et al.
[
9
]
, who propose to perform joint Wald tests in order to test for a finite number of necessary conditions for calibration and auto-calibration, respectively. In practice, the use of their tests generally leads to low power as on the one hand, one needs multiple necessary conditions in order to be able to detect various kinds of violations of these properties, and on the other hand, the power of their tests is decreasing with an increasing amount of necessary conditions.

Our contribution is to show that boosting can be employed to test for calibration and auto-calibration by only assessing a single necessary condition, respectively, while being able to detect multiple kinds of violations of these two properties. Boosting is a class of ensemble methods that iteratively combines
weak learners
, i.e., simple models, to decrease the size of the residuals at each iteration. Its theoretical foundations were originally established by Valiant
[
27
]
and Kearns–Valiant
[
20
]
. It was then popularized by Freund–Schapire
[
10
]
who came up with the AdaBoost algorithm for classification problems, and Friedman
[
11
]
later extended that framework to regression modelling using gradient-based methods. In the present work, we use trees as weak learners to detect violations of calibration and auto-calibration for a given regression function. These violations then enable us to derive a suitable necessary condition for each of the two properties above, which we assess through statistical tests with fully tractable asymptotic distributions. Through a numerical example, we show that although only a single necessary condition for calibration and auto-calibration, respectively, is assessed, the proposed testing procedures achieve a high power on a large motor third party liability insurance dataset.

Organization.
The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows. In the next section, we formally introduce the notions of calibration and auto-calibration, and present equivalent characterizations for both properties. Then, in Section
3
, we propose new testing procedures to assess these properties using boosting trees. Finally, in Section
4
, we show that the proposed testing procedures achieve a high power on a motor third party liability insurance dataset. The last section concludes this work.

2
Calibration and auto-calibration

2.1
Mean estimation in regression modelling

Denote by
Y
the response variable and by
\boldsymbol{X}
the vector of features whose support is given by a feature space
{\mathcal{X}}
. Moreover, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
be the underlying probability space and assume that the response
Y
is square integrable. The mean estimation task in regression modelling consists in approximating the (unknown)
true conditional mean

\mu^{*}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}].

For this, statistical models are used to fit a regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
on some learning dataset. In practice, this fitting procedure is typically done by minimizing strictly consistent loss functions for mean estimation, which are functions whose minimum is only attained by the true conditional mean; we refer the reader to Gneiting
[
12
]
and Gneiting–Raftery
[
13
]
. The goal of this section is to introduce two different notions of calibration for the fitted regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and present equivalent formulations of these properties.

2.2
The calibration property

A regression function is calibrated if it matches the true conditional mean for almost every (a.e.) set of features, i.e.,

\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\textrm{for a.e. }\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Using the response
Y
and the vector of features
\boldsymbol{X}
, we define this property as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

A regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is calibrated for
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
if

\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})={\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}],\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.}

It is important to note that the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}]
is defined up to
{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-}
nullsets, and that the measure theoretical definition of conditional expectation, see Definition 23.4 in Jacod–Protter
[
19
]
, allows us to rewrite the above definition as follows. A calibrated regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,\mathds{1}_{S}]={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\,\mathds{1}_{S}],

(2.1)

for all measurable sets
S\in\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})
. The following proposition can then be derived from this characterization.

Proposition 2.2
.

A regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is calibrated for
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
if and only if

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(Y-\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\right)g(\boldsymbol{X})\right]=0,

(2.2)

for all measurable functions
g
such that
g(\boldsymbol{X})\in L^{2}({\mathbb{P}}).

The proof of this result follows from the property that the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}]
minimizes the
L^{2}
-distance to
Y
over the space of
\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})
-measurable functions. We refer the interested reader to Chapter 23 of Jacod–Protter
[
19
]
.

2.3
The auto-calibration property

Calibration is a strong property that requires the fitted regression function to coincide with the true conditional mean for a.e. set of features
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. A related notion is
auto-calibration
. It is defined as follows, see, e.g., Krüger–Ziegel
[
22
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is auto-calibrated for
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
if

\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})={\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})],\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textnormal{-a.s.}

The auto-calibration property means that by conditioning on a given mean estimate, the conditional expectation of the response matches this estimate. While this definition seems to be close to Definition
2.1
, the difference lies in the conditioning that now takes place with respect to the mean estimate
\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})
instead of the vector of features
\boldsymbol{X}
, i.e., with respect to a coarser
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))\subseteq\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})
. Moreover, note that auto-calibration is a weaker property than calibration as for any calibrated regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})]={\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}]\,|\,\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})]={\mathbb{E}}[\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{X})\,|\,\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})]=\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}),\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.},

where we used in the first equality that
\sigma(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))\subseteq\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})
and in the last equality, that the regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is calibrated. As above, Definition
2.3
can equivalently be expressed using the measure theoretical definition of conditional expectation as follows. An auto-calibrated regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,\mathds{1}_{S}]={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\,\mathds{1}_{S}],

(2.3)

for all measurable sets
S\in\sigma(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))
. The latter
\sigma
-algebra contains all measurable sets that can be uniquely defined by the values of the regression function. In particular, an auto-calibrated regression function has to satisfy

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,\mathds{1}_{\{a\leq\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\leq b\}}]={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\,\mathds{1}_{\{a\leq\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\leq b\}}],

(2.4)

for any values
a\leq b\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
. Interestingly, by setting
a=-\infty
and
b=\infty
, we retrieve the
global unbiasedness
property

{\mathbb{E}}[Y]={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})],

which shows that auto-calibration is a stronger property than global unbiasedness.

The characterization in (
2.3
) allows us to better interpret Definition
2.3
, because it means that the mean estimates provided by an auto-calibrated regression function are unbiased both globally and locally, where the latter term refers to subparts of the feature space that can solely be delimited by the values of the regression function. This property is of particular interest in applications where mean estimates within given specific groups have to be unbiased. This is for example the case in insurance pricing, where auto-calibration of a pricing system is a minimal requirement, as it ensures that each cohort of individuals paying a certain price is on average self-financing.
Similar to Proposition
2.2
, the next result provides an equivalent formulation for auto-calibration.

Proposition 2.4
.

A regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is auto-calibrated for
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
if and only if

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(Y-\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\right)h(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))\right]=0,

(2.5)

for all measurable functions
h
such that
h(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))\in L^{2}({\mathbb{P}}).

We conclude this section by considering a toy example illustrating the difference between calibration and auto-calibration. This example shows that the set of auto-calibrated regression functions is substantially larger than the set of calibrated ones. Moreover, it highlights that the granularity of auto-calibrated regression functions, given by
\sigma(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))
, can vary considerably.

Example 2.5
.

Consider a feature space of only three elements
{\mathcal{X}}=\{A,B,C\}
and let the true conditional mean of
Y\,|\boldsymbol{X}
be given by

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}]=\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}2,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=A,\\
6,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=B,\\
10,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=C.\\
\end{cases}

Moreover, assume that
\boldsymbol{X}
satisfies

{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{X}=A)=1/2,\quad{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{X}=B)=1/4\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{X}=C)=1/4.

The goal of this example is to evaluate the calibration and auto-calibration of the regression functions

\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}2,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=A,\\
8,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=B,\\
8,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=C,\\
\end{cases}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}5,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=A,\\
5,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=B,\\
5,\quad\textnormal{for }\boldsymbol{x}=C.\\
\end{cases}

To this end, we first use (
2.1
) to assess calibration. That is, we check whether mean estimates are unbiased for all sets
S\in\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})
, which is given in this example by

\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})=\Big\{\emptyset,\{\boldsymbol{X}=A\},\{\boldsymbol{X}=B\},\{\boldsymbol{X}=C\},\\
\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\cup B\},\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\cup C\},\{\boldsymbol{X}\in B\cup C\},\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\cup B\cup C\}\Big\}.

For both regression functions, this property fails to hold for
S=\{\boldsymbol{X}=B\}
as we have

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\mathds{1}_{S}]={\mathbb{E}}[\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{X})\mathds{1}_{S}]=1.5\neq 2={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X})\mathds{1}_{S}],

and

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\mathds{1}_{S}]={\mathbb{E}}[\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{X})\mathds{1}_{S}]=1.5\neq 1.25={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X})\mathds{1}_{S}].

Thus, neither of the above regression functions are calibrated and, in fact, as the random variable
\boldsymbol{X}
is discrete and its support is given by
{\mathcal{X}}
, the only calibrated regression function in this example is given by
\mu^{*}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Next, we follow the same procedure to test for auto-calibration by using (
2.3
). For this, note that

\sigma(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}))=\Big\{\emptyset,\{\boldsymbol{X}=A\},\{\boldsymbol{X}\in B\cup C\},\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\cup B\cup C\}\Big\},

and

\sigma(\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}))=\Big\{\emptyset,\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\cup B\cup C\}\Big\}.

As these
\sigma
-algebras are strictly smaller than
\sigma(\boldsymbol{X})
, assessing the auto-calibration of both regression functions requires checking unbiasedness of mean estimates on fewer subparts of the covariate space and, in fact, it turns out that (
2.3
) holds for all sets in
\sigma(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}))
and
\sigma(\widehat{\mu}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}))
, respectively. This allows us to conclude that
\widehat{\mu}_{1}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{2}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are both auto-calibrated, but not calibrated.

\blacksquare

3
Assessing calibration and auto-calibration using boosting trees

3.1
Necessary conditions for calibration and auto-calibration

Assessing calibration is a challenging problem in practice as the true conditional mean is unknown. At first glance, the problem seems easier when considering auto-calibration because this property only involves the response
Y
and the fitted regression function
\widehat{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. For instance, the expected values in (
2.3
) could in principle be replaced by empirical means for a large amount of observations. However, a problem still remains as the equality in (
2.3
) has to be checked for all sets
S\in\sigma(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))
whose amount is typically uncountable and for which the number of available observations could be small. In practice, graphical methods are sometimes used to assess auto-calibration by plotting the empirical mean of binned responses against their corresponding mean estimates; see for example the
actual vs. predicted
plot in Section 4.1.3 in Wüthrich et al.
[
31
]
. The problem of such approaches is that auto-calibration is only assessed for a few sets
S\in\sigma(\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}))
and that the conclusion of this assessment largely depends on the chosen bins; we refer, for example, to Henzi et al.
[
17
]
. This shows the need to have statistical tests to assess both calibration and auto-calibration. The goal of this section is to introduce new testing procedures to this end.

We start our discussion from Propositions
2.2
and
2.4
, which provide equivalent conditions for calibration and auto-calibration, respectively. These conditions are difficult to evaluate in practice as the set of functions
g
in (
2.2
) and
h
in (
2.5
) are typically uncountable. Fissler et al.
[
9
]
suggest to use a finite number
K
of
test functions

g_{1},\dots,g_{K}
, respectively
h_{1},\dots,h_{K}
, in order to simultaneously check the
K
underlying necessary conditions using joint Wald tests. For example, in the case of calibration, the null-hypothesis of their test reads as

\mathbb{H}_{0}:{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(Y-\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\right)g_{k}(\boldsymbol{X})\right]=0,\quad\textrm{for }1\leq k\leq K.

As each of the
K
above equalities represents a necessary condition, their test allows them to reject calibration whenever the null-hypothesis
\mathbb{H}_{0}
is rejected. The same procedure applies to auto-calibration as well. In practice, unfortunately, this approach has some limitations as pointed out by Fissler et al.
[
9
]
. On the one hand, one needs a high number
K
of test functions to be able to detect various kinds of violations of calibration and auto-calibration, respectively. On the other hand, the power of their test decreases with increasing
K
. Moreover, the choice of the test functions is also unclear; some examples are provided in Fissler et al.
[
9
]
.

In this section, we aim at introducing new procedures to test for calibration and auto-calibration by only considering a single necessary condition, respectively, while being able to detect various kinds of violations of the above properties. To this end, we assume that an i.i.d. sample of data
{\mathcal{D}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{D}}}
following the law of a pair
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
is at our disposal, and that the regression function of interest is fitted on the realizations of some part of the data
\mathcal{L}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{L}}\subset{\mathcal{D}}
. We refer to these realizations
\ell=(y_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{L}}
as the
learning set
and denote the fitted regression function by
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, emphasizing its dependency on
\ell
. Under this framework, the calibration property reads as

\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})={\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}],\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.},

and the auto-calibration property is given by

\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})={\mathbb{E}}[Y\,|\,\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})],\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.}

For both of the above properties, note that the randomness only lies in the pair
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
because we only assess the calibration and auto-calibration of the fitted regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
for the single set of realizations
\ell=(y_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{L}}}
.

A standard approach for these assessments is to use the remaining data
{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathcal{L}
for evaluating a test statistics; we refer for example to Denuit et al.
[
7
]
and Wüthrich
[
28
]
. In our case, however, as we want to test for the necessary condition in (
2.2
) and (
2.5
) by solely using a single test function, respectively, we take a slightly different approach here. Indeed, we further divide the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathcal{L}
into a
boosting set

{\mathcal{B}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{B}}
and a
test set

{\mathcal{T}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{T}}}
. The former set
{\mathcal{B}}
, along with the learning set
{\ell}
, will be used to construct square integrable test functions
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}
and
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}
with the help of boosting trees.
The test set
{\mathcal{T}}
, for its part, will be used to evaluate a test statistics allowing us to possibly reject the null-hypothesis of calibration and auto-calibration, respectively.

and

3.2
Construction of the single test function using boosting trees

Using a single test function in (
2.2
) and (
2.5
) leads to the assessment of a necessary condition for calibration and auto-calibration, respectively. In general, this results in low statistical power as a regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
might, for example, satisfy (
2.5
) for some test function
h
while not being auto-calibrated. The choice of the test function thus plays a crucial role for the power of our tests and we consider the use of boosting trees in this regard.

Boosting is a class of ensemble methods that aims to improve an already fitted model by iteratively combining weak learners with the aim of reducing the size of the residuals at each step. In order to assess calibration and auto-calibration, we propose to use regression trees as weak learners for fitting a competitive model to the regression model
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
on both the learning and boosting sets. For this, we start from an initial regression function
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(0)}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},

e.g., the homogeneous mean on
{\ell}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, and define successively

\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(m)}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(m)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(m-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})+\beta_{m}t_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}),

for
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. Above, the parameters
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
correspond to pre-defined
learning rates
and the maps
t_{m}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
belong to the class of regression trees with some pre-defined hyper-parameters that we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{tree}
. At each iteration
m
, these trees are selected by solving

t_{m}\in\argmin\limits_{t\in\mathcal{M}_{tree}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathfrak{L}},{\mathfrak{B}}}L(Y_{i},\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(m-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})+\beta_{m}t_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\right),

for a pre-selected strictly consistent loss function
L
. It is well known that for large values of iteration steps
M
, the resulting regression function
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(M)}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
tends to overfit, i.e., it starts modelling the noisy part of the learning set
{\ell}
and the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
instead of its systematic part. A possible solution to mitigate this issue is to carefully select the learning rates
\left(\beta_{m}\right)_{m}
and to apply early stopping; we refer to Friedman
[
11
]
and to Chapter 10 of Hastie et al.
[
16
]
. We do not further elaborate on these aspects here. A practical implementation of the boosting algorithm that addresses overfitting will be provided in Section
4
, below.

Throughout this paper, we call
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{(M)}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
the
benchmark gradient boosting model (GBM)
and denote it by
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, because it is fitted on both the learning and boosting sets. Its goal is to primarily capture the systematic part of the sets
\ell
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, i.e., we assume that appropriate measures have been taken during the fitting stage to mitigate overfitting. Under this assumption, boosting trees are known to achieve strong out-of-sample predictive performance; see, e.g., Friedman
[
11
]
. As the fitting procedure of this new model makes use of an additional set, the boosting set
\mathcal{B}
, we expect the benchmark GBM
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to achieve a higher predictive power than the originally fitted regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Our proposal is to detect violations of calibration and auto-calibration for the originally fitted regression function by comparing its mean estimates with those produced by the benchmark GBM. For instance, on the one hand, the inequality

\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})>\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})

(3.1)

indicates that the initial regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
might exhibit a systematic negative bias for the sets of features
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
satisfying (
3.1
), whereas, on the other hand, the inequality

\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})

(3.2)

hints to a possible systematic positive bias. Using the originally fitted regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}~:~{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as well as the benchmark GBM
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}~:~{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, test functions
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}
and
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}
can then be constructed with the aim of pushing the expected values in (
2.2
) and (
2.5
) away from
0
whenever the null-hypotheses of calibration and auto-calibration are violated. This can be done, for example, by multiplying the residuals
Y-\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})
by a positive value when (
3.1
) holds, and by a negative value when (
3.2
) holds. We make this choice below, where we provide general procedures to assess calibration and auto-calibration.

3.3
Assessing calibration

In this section, our aim is to assess calibration by testing (
2.2
) for a single test function
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}~:~{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is constructed using the learning set
{\ell}
and the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
. For this, we rely on the following proposition, whose proof is provided in the appendix.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{T}}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random vectors following the law of some independent pair
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
. Moreover, let
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function and
g_{\ell,{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\sigma({\mathcal{B}})
-measurable function for some set of i.i.d random vectors
{\mathcal{B}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{B}}}
that is independent of
{\mathcal{T}}
. Then, the random variables

Z_{i}=(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}))g_{\ell,{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}),\quad i\in{\mathfrak{T}},

(3.3)

are conditionally i.i.d. given
{\mathcal{B}}
. If these random variables further satisfy

0<\textnormal{Var}(Z_{i}\,|\,{\mathcal{B}})<\infty,\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.,}

we have, under the null-hypothesis of calibration of
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, that

T_{n}^{\textrm{cal}}=\frac{\bar{Z}}{\sqrt{S_{Z}^{2}/n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(3.4)

as
n=|{\mathcal{T}}|\to\infty
, and where

\bar{Z}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\quad\textrm{and}\quad S_{Z}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-\bar{Z})^{2}.

Remark 3.2
.

The randomness in (
3.4
) lies simultaneously in the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
and the test set
{\mathcal{T}}
. Moreover, the above result holds for any fixed size of the boosting set, as long as the size of the test set
|{\mathcal{T}}|
goes to infinity. However, although the size
|{\mathcal{B}}|
does not affect the convergence of the test statistics
T_{n}^{\textrm{cal}}
under the null-hypothesis of calibration of
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we emphasize that it plays a role when this null-hypothesis is violated. Indeed, the larger the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
is, the more the benchmark GBM should be able to detect violations of calibration and lead to the rejection of the null-hypothesis in the latter case.

Following the discussion in Section
3.2
, Proposition
3.1
naturally motivates the general procedure for constructing calibration tests, below. The idea of this procedure is to introduce a test statistics based on weighted residuals, where the weights are given by a test function
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}~:~{\mathcal{X}}~\to~{\mathbb{R}}
, see (
3.3
) and (
3.4
). This test function will be constructed using the benchmark GBM
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in such a way that it takes positive values whenever positive biases are detected in the originally fitted regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{{\ell}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and negative values whenever negative biases are detected, see Section
3.2
. The following procedure provides a general framework; an explicit implementation of the resulting test is presented in Section
4.3
.

Procedure to construct a calibration test using boosting trees

1.

Fit a benchmark GBM
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
on the learning set
{\ell}
and the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
.

2.

Select a test function
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

•

g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})>0
, whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})>\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
,

•

g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=c\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
,

•

g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<0
, whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

3.

Compute the test statistics
T_{n}^{\textrm{cal}}
in (
3.4
) on the test set
{\mathcal{T}}
, i.e., for the random variables

Z_{i}=(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}))g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}),\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

where
n=|{\mathcal{T}}|
.

4.

Reject the calibration of the regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
at the pre-specified confidence level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
whenever

|T_{n}^{\textrm{cal}}|>\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
denotes the standard Gaussian distribution.

Remark 3.3
.

The conditions on the test function
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in the second step aim at making the test statistics
T_{n}
positive whenever the calibration assumption is violated, see Section
3.2
. Such a statement assumes that the boosted regression function
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is able to successfully detect those violations, and we emphasize that even if this is not the case, the proposed testing procedure remains valid. It might, however, exhibit a low power. Moreover, note that, above, we impose that the test function differs from
0
whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
. The reason for this requirement is to avoid the degenerate case
T_{n}^{\textrm{cal}}=0,\,{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.}
, which occurs when
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})=\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{\ell,\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{X}),\,{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.}
For most pairs
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
, the latter event has probability zero and in the other cases where this could happen, the corresponding test function would satisfy
g_{\ell,\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv c
for a.e.
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, so that we only assess global unbiasedness. Interestingly, in this situation, the value of the test statistic
T_{n}^{\textrm{cal}}
would not depend on the choice of the constant
c
.

3.4
Assessing auto-calibration

A similar result to Proposition
3.1
can be derived to assess auto-calibration. The only difference is that, this time, the test function is not defined on the feature space
{\mathcal{X}}
, but on the
mean estimate space

{\widehat{\mu}}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})=\left\{{\widehat{\mu}}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}):\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{T}}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random vectors following the law of some independent pair
(Y,\boldsymbol{X})
. Moreover, let
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function and
h_{\ell,{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\sigma({\mathcal{B}})
-measurable function for some set of i.i.d random vectors
{\mathcal{B}}=(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{B}}}
that is independent of
{\mathcal{T}}
. Then, the random variables

Z_{i}=(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}))h_{\ell,{\mathcal{B}}}(\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})),\quad i\in{\mathfrak{T}},

(3.5)

are conditionally i.i.d. given
{\mathcal{B}}
. Morever, if these random variables satisfy

0<\textnormal{Var}(Z_{i}\,|\,{\mathcal{B}})<\infty,\,\quad{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.},

we have, under the null-hypothesis of auto-calibration of
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
that

T_{n}^{\textrm{ac}}=\frac{\bar{Z}}{\sqrt{S_{Z}^{2}/n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

(3.6)

as
n=|{\mathcal{T}}|\to\infty
, and where

\bar{Z}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\quad\textrm{and}\quad S_{Z}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-\bar{Z})^{2}.

The proof of this result is similar to the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Therefore, Remark
3.2
similarly applies here. As above, we provide a general procedure for testing auto-calibration, below. The main difference with respect to the previous section is that the test function
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
does not directly depend on individual sets of features
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, but it is a map defined on the mean estimate space.
The second step of the procedure in Section
3.3
is thus modified as follows. In order to define the test function, we first introduce a map
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\implies\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\quad\textrm{for all }\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}.

(3.7)

This condition ensures that all sets of features having the same mean estimate are assigned the same value under the map
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which allows us to uniquely define a test function
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
through

\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}=h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}\circ\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}.

The following procedure provides a general framework for assessing auto-calibration; an explicit implementation of the resulting test is presented in Section
4.4
.

Procedure to construct an auto-calibration test using boosting trees

1.

Fit a benchmark GBM
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
on the learning set
{\ell}
and the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
.

2.

Select a function
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying (
3.7
) and such that, on average, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{\ell}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}
,

•

\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})>0
, whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})>\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
,

•

\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=c\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
,

•

\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<0
, whenever
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

3.

Compute the test statistics
T_{n}^{\textrm{ac}}
in (
3.6
) on the test set
{\mathcal{T}}
, i.e., for

Z_{i}=(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}))h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\widehat{\mu}_{{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})),\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

(3.8)

where
n=|{\mathcal{T}}|
, and
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{{\ell}}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the unique map satisfying
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}=h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}\circ\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}
.

4.

Reject the auto-calibration of the regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
at the pre-specified confidence level
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
whenever

\left|T_{n}^{\textrm{ac}}\right|>\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
denotes the standard Gaussian distribution.

Remark 3.5
.

We emphasize again that the requirement in (
3.7
) is needed to ensure that the test function
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
in (
3.8
) exists and is unique. Moreover, as in the case of calibration, the conditions on
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in the second step aim to make the test statistics
T_{n}^{\textrm{ac}}
positive. The difference, here, is that one might find two sets of features
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
for which we have

\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})<\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

Therefore, in view of (
3.7
), one can only impose those conditions to hold, on average, for the available features
\boldsymbol{x}~\in~{\mathcal{X}}_{{\ell}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}
as the true distribution of
\boldsymbol{X}
is unknown; we come back to this in Section
4.4
.

We conclude this section by pointing out that the auto-calibration test of Denuit et al.
[
7
]
is also based on the measure theoretical definition of conditional expectation as it seeks to verify whether

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})\leq t\}}]={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X})\,\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{l}(\boldsymbol{X})\leq t\}}],

holds for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which is an equivalent condition to (
2.3
). To this end, these authors consider the curve

\alpha\in(0,1)\longmapsto A_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\mathcal{T}}|}}\sum_{i\in{\mathfrak{T}}}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}))\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\leq\widehat{F}^{-1}_{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}}(\alpha)\}},

(3.9)

where
\widehat{F}^{-1}_{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}}(\cdot)
denotes the empirical quantile of the distribution of
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})
. This curve is then used to compute the test statistics

T_{n}=\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}|A_{n}(\alpha)|,

whose distribution is unknown. Therefore, they propose to compute the critical level of rejection for their test statistics using non-parametric Monte Carlo simulation approximations.

In principle, their test could be modified such that the accumulation of responses and mean estimates in (
3.9
) takes place in the opposite direction as

{\mathbb{E}}[Y\,\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq t\}}]={\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})\,\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq t\}}],

for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, is equivalent to (
2.3
) too. In fact, any other arbitrary (and pre-defined) order could be chosen as well. Although their test assesses an equivalent condition for auto-calibration, whereas we only test for a necessary condition, we show in the next section that our auto-calibration test achieves a higher power for a large insurance dataset. This may seem surprising at first glance. The reason is that the test of Denuit et al.
[
7
]
is based on a Kolmogorov-Smirnov type statistics that considers the entire support of the mean estimates
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X})
simultaneously instead of focusing on parts of the support where violations are most likely to happen. Interestingly, our idea of first learning violations of auto-calibration before defining the test statistics could similarly be used in their setup, leading, for example, to compute the above test statistics for only a subpart of the mean estimation space.

The dataset under consideration

4.4
Testing for auto-calibration

As auto-calibration is a weaker property than calibration, we might expect that some of the models under consideration are auto-calibrated, but not calibrated. In fact, we know that the true conditional mean is auto-calibrated as it is calibrated, whereas the homogeneous mean
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}^{\textrm{hom}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to(0,\infty)
is empirically auto-calibrated by construction (this property is only satisfied on the learning set
{\ell}
), see (
4.1
). As above, we adapt the general testing procedure for auto-calibration in Section
3.4
to the Poisson case.

The main difference with respect to the previous section will lie in the second step of our implementation, where we construct the test function
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
. For instance, a first map
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
taking values in the set
\{-1,0.01,1\}
will be introduced, as for the test function
g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in the previous section. However, as this map should satisfy (
3.7
), see Section
3.4
, its values will not be directly determined by the biases detected by the benchmark GBM. The reason is that one might have

\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})<\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

for two different sets of features
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, leading to
-1=g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq g_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=1
. Therefore, an approximation of the benchmark GBM fulfilling (
3.7
) will be used instead for the construction of the map
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. This approximation will be denoted by
\tilde{\mu}^{\textrm{b}oost}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to(0,\infty)
and be constructed as follows. First, the sets of features
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}
will be grouped according to their mean estimates and weighted averages of the values of the benchmark GBM will be taken, i.e.,

\widetilde{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\sum_{i\in{{\mathfrak{L}}\cup{\mathfrak{B}}}}v_{i}\widehat{\mu}^{boost}_{\ell,{\mathcal{V}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\}}}{\sum_{i\in{{\mathfrak{L}}\cup{\mathfrak{B}}}}v_{i}\mathds{1}_{\{\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\}}},

(4.4)

for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{\ell}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}
. This leads to pairs

\left(\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}),\widetilde{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}},

that provide the average value of the benchmark GBM for each mean estimate appearing in the learning and boosting sets. Then, a linear interpolation will be taken between these pairs in order to extend the domain of definition of the approximation of the benchmark GBM
\widetilde{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}~:~{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}~\cup~{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}~\to~(0,\infty)
to the whole feature space
{\mathcal{X}}
. This approximation will be used to construct a map
\tilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that detects positive and negative biases of the originally fitted regression function, while satisfying (
3.7
). The test function
h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, for its part, will finally be obtained by setting

\widetilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}=h_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}\circ\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}.

The resulting test is provided below and we emphasize that other methods could have been used for the approximation of the benchmark GBM as, for example, cubic interpolation or splines.

Construction of an auto-calibration test using Poisson boosting trees

1.

Fit a benchmark GBM
\widehat{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to(0,\infty)

2.

4.4

\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{\ell}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}

\left(\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}),\widetilde{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{B}}}.

This linear interpolation leads to the extension of the images in (
4.4
) to the whole feature space, which defines an approximation of the benchmark GBM

\widetilde{\mu}^{\textrm{boost}}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to(0,\infty).

Then, introduce the function
\widetilde{g}_{{\ell},{\mathcal{B}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

Isotonic recalibration
is a method used in Wüthrich–Ziegel
[
32

in order to restore the auto-calibration of a given regression function
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

{\ell}=(y_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},v_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{L}}}

(4.5)

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\ell}^{\textrm{Iso}}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}(y_{i}-\mu_{i})^{2}:\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}\Big\}.

(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{L}|}
.

{\mathcal{X}}

[
32

4.5
Power of the tests

In the two previous sections, we assessed the calibration and auto-calibration of various statistical models for the Swiss motor third liability insurance dataset in Wüthrich–Buser
[
29
]
. As this dataset was synthetically generated and the true conditional mean is available for all policies, one can in principle simulate different claims histories from the same true model. We use this to evaluate the power of our tests by generating
100
different datasets. To this end, we keep the triplets of the learning set
{\ell}=(y_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},v_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{L}}}
fixed, while randomly reallocating the remaining policies between the boosting set
{\mathcal{B}}
and the test set
{\mathcal{T}}
at each simulation. In addition, the numbers of claims associated with the policies in
{\mathcal{B}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
are resimulated each time. This results in
100
new datasets of

4.6
Sensitivity of the tests

We conclude this section by looking at the sensitivity of both tests for calibration and auto-calibration. For this, we consider five different regression functions defined through the convex linear combinations

\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}^{\delta}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}^{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\delta\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}^{GLM}(\boldsymbol{x})+(1-\delta)\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(4.6)

where
\delta\in\{0,1/4,1/2,3/4,1\}
. We then assess the calibration and auto-calibration of these regression functions on the same
100
simulated datasets as above and provide the number of rejections in Figure
9
. There, we see that the calibration of regression functions that are different, but close, to the true conditional mean is not systematically rejected and the same holds for auto-calibration. Moreover, the amount of rejections seems to be monotonically increasing in
\delta
as one would expect from (
4.6
).

Number of rejections of the null-hypothesis of calibration and auto-calibration, respectively, for

60\%
of the data, whereas the red curves correspond to the other case. These are only evaluated at
\delta\in\{0,1/4,1/2,3/4,1\}
.

This section, along with the previous one, shows that although we only assess a single necessary condition for calibration and auto-calibration, respectively, the proposed testing procedures are very powerful for the considered Swiss motor third party liability insurance. For instance, the calibration of a regression function was systematically rejected whenever it was not close enough to the true conditional mean, whereas the auto-calibration of a regression function did not always get rejected for the true conditional mean, empirically auto-calibrated regression functions or regression functions being close to the true conditional mean. Moreover, the rejection rate for models that did not violate calibration and auto-calibration, respectively, was close to the significance level of
5\%
in this example and the convergence results in Propositions
3.1
and
3.4
were proved to hold in Figures
7
and
8
.

5
Conclusion

This paper introduces new testing procedures to assess the calibration and auto-calibration of fitted regression functions. To this end, equivalent characterizations of these properties arising from the orthogonal projection of conditional expectation are used to derive necessary conditions. Although testing for necessary conditions generally leads to low statistical power, we show in this paper that boosting trees enable us to construct statistical tests based on a single necessary condition that are able to detect various kinds of violations of calibration and auto-calibration, respectively. This is supported by a numerical example, in which the proposed tests prove to be very powerful. In particular, we show that for calibration, our test outperforms the test of Delong et al.
[
3
]
, and for auto-calibration, it outperforms the test of Denuit et al.
[
7
]
.

The selection of the single necessary conditions to be assessed plays an important role in the power of our tests and our approach consists in fitting a benchmark gradient boosting model on the learning set and an additional set, that we call boosting set, in order to learn violations of calibration and auto-calibration for the originally fitted regression function. The use of boosting is motivated by the strong out-of-sample predictive performance achieved by such models. Going forward, it might be interesting to consider the use of other statistical models for this purpose as, for example, deep neural networks. Moreover, the selection of the necessary conditions to be assessed could in principle be done in other ways, but as pointed out in this paper, it seems important to use a method that adapts to the regression function under consideration in order to obtain powerful tests.

Finally, we emphasize that the idea of using a subpart of the dataset that was not used in the fitting stage to first identify violations of calibration or auto-calibration and, then, select a suitable test statistics could be used for other testing procedures as well. This is for example the case for the auto-calibration test of Denuit et al.
[
7
]
. which is based on a Kolmogorov-Smirnov type test statistics. Focusing on part of the portfolio where violations of the null-hypothesis are most likely to happen could significantly improve the power of such tests.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.1

The proof of Proposition
3.1
is given in this appendix. As the proof of Proposition
3.4
is perfectly similar, it is omitted.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

The random variables
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
in (
3.3
) are conditionally i.i.d. given
{\mathcal{B}}
because the pairs
(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\in{\mathfrak{T}}}
were assumed to be i.i.d. and
{\mathcal{B}}
is independent of
{\mathcal{T}}
. Moreover, under the null-hypothesis of calibration of
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[(Y-\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{X}))g_{\ell,{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{X})\,|\,{\mathcal{B}}]=0,\hskip 8.50012pt{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.}

Define

\sigma^{2}({\mathcal{B}})=\textrm{Var}\left[(Y-\mu(\boldsymbol{X}))g_{\ell,{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{X})\,|\,{\mathcal{B}}\right]>0,\hskip 8.50012pt{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{-a.s.}

Then, for almost every realization
{\mathcal{B}}=\boldsymbol{b}
, we obtain using the central limit theorem (CLT),

\left.\sqrt{n}\bar{Z}\,\right|\,{\mathcal{B}}=\boldsymbol{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{b})),

(A.1)

as
|{\mathcal{T}}|\to\infty
. Since
S_{Z}^{2}
is a consistent estimator of the variance
\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{b})
, we further have

S_{Z}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{b})>0,

(A.2)

as
|{\mathcal{T}}|\to\infty
by the law of large numbers. Finally, the continuous mapping theorem and Slutsky’s theorem allow us to conclude from (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) that

T_{n}=\left.\frac{\bar{Z}}{\sqrt{S_{Z}^{2}/n}}\,\right|\,{\mathcal{B}}=\boldsymbol{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(A.3)

as
|{\mathcal{T}}|\to\infty
. As the above result holds for almost every realization
{\mathcal{B}}=\boldsymbol{b}
and the limiting distribution in (
A.3
) does not depend on
\boldsymbol{b}
, we finally conclude that

T_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

This shows the claim.
