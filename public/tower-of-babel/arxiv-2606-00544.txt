Escaping the Mode Lottery: Multi-Response Training Improves Language Model Generalization
Hasan Amin, Kian Ahrabian, Ming Yin, Rajiv Khanna

Abstract
Modern language-model fine-tuning typically pairs each prompt with a single response, even though many prompts admit multiple valid completions. This effectively reduces a multi-modal conditional distribution to a one-sample view, a phenomenon we call the “mode lottery,” where training emphasizes a subset of plausible modes while leaving others statistically underrepresented. We study multi-response training (MRT), which retains multiple responses per prompt, and develop a principled account of when and why it helps. Our key insight is that prompts and responses are distinct statistical resources: additional prompts reduce uncertainty about the input distribution, while additional responses reduce uncertainty about the conditional output distribution. This yields a simple variance–budget tradeoff that predicts when retaining multiple responses is worthwhile, shows diminishing returns as prompt-level uncertainty dominates, and explains why large redundant corpora can exhibit an implicit multi-response effect. We further analyze response selection, and show that Random- K K -of- N N is the unbiased default for distributional fine-tuning, reward-based selection can induce persistent mode collapse, and a submodular quality--diversity objective provides an efficient alternative with theoretical guarantees. Controlled simulations validate the predicted variance and selection effects, including a striking failure mode where reward-only selection produces gradients misaligned with the true objective. Across structured and real-world datasets, including a new multi-prompt, multi-response benchmark, MRT consistently improves distributional generalization, with the largest gains in high response-diversity, low prompt-redundancy regimes. Overall, MRT reframes response multiplicity as a data-allocation problem with clear guidance: when responses are cheap and diverse, keeping more than one is not a heuristic, but a statistically grounded choice. 1 1 1 Code and data available at https://github.com/shasanamin/multi-response-training .

Introduction

Modern language model training is largely organized around a quiet convention of accompanying one prompt with one response.
This convention is natural for classification-style supervision, but it is a poor fit for open-ended generation.
Prompts such as “
write a function that sorts a list
" admit many correct implementations, and “
summarize this article
" allow multiple valid framings.
Training on only one response therefore exposes the model to a narrow slice of a multi-modal conditional distribution.
We call this the
mode lottery
: the training set may repeatedly show one plausible mode—often the most rewarded, most common, or easiest-to-generate one—while leaving other valid and valuable modes statistically invisible.

This paper studies
multi-response training
(MRT), i.e., training on
K>1
responses per prompt, with standard single-response training (SRT) recovered as the special case
K=1
.
MRT is already present as a successful heuristic in several modern post-training pipelines, where multiple responses per prompt are generated, filtered, ranked, or reused during supervised and reinforcement-learning stages
[

Yet the field lacks a clear account of when retaining multiple responses should help, how large the gain can be, and which responses should be kept.
Our goal is to turn this heuristic into a data-allocation principle.

The central observation is that prompts and responses are different statistical resources.
Additional prompts reduce uncertainty about the marginal prompt distribution, while additional responses to the same prompt reduce uncertainty about the conditional response distribution.
These two uncertainties have different costs and different limits.
If prompt-level variation dominates, collecting new prompts is more valuable.
If within-prompt response variation is large and responses are cheap, MRT can substantially improve distributional generalization.
This also explains why very large, redundant corpora can sometimes work well with one response per prompt, as near-duplicate or task-equivalent prompts can provide what we call an
implicit MRT
effect.

We answer three practical questions.
First,
when
should one keep more than one response?
A variance-budget analysis separates prompt-level uncertainty from response-level uncertainty and yields a closed-form rule for the optimal response multiplicity
K^{\star}
, depending only on the ratio of within-prompt to across-prompt variance and the relative cost of prompts versus responses.
Second,
by how much
can MRT help?
We give complementary Bernstein and hierarchical Rademacher generalization bounds, with the former giving a variance-sensitive view of diminishing returns and the latter handling the non-i.i.d. structure of repeated prompts plus showing that the response-level complexity can scale with the total number of prompt-response pairs rather than only with responses per prompt.
Third,
which
responses should be kept?
We distinguish distributional, reward-tilted, and quality-coverage objectives. Random-
K
-of-
N
(RKoN) is the unbiased default for distributional fine-tuning—which is the focus of this work—while reward-only best-of-
N
can induce persistent mode collapse and proposed GRADES provides an efficient submodular recipe for retaining a balance of high-quality and diverse responses.

We summarize our contributions below:

1.

A variance-budget principle for response multiplicity.

We show that MRT separates prompt-level uncertainty from response-level uncertainty, yielding a simple allocation law for
K^{\star}
and a direct test for when keeping multiple responses is worthwhile.

2.

Generalization guarantees for hierarchical response data.

We give complementary Bernstein and hierarchical Rademacher bounds. The Bernstein view gives a variance-sensitive dependence on
K
, while the Rademacher view shows that, despite repeated prompts and non-i.i.d. structure, the response-level complexity can scale with the full prompt-response sample size when conditional symmetrization is handled correctly.

3.

A target-aware theory of response selection.

We extend our analysis from distributional to reward-tilted and quality-coverage targets, establishing how RKoN is unbiased for distributional fine-tuning, reward-only best-of-
N
can collapse valid modes, and GRADES gives a greedy quality-diversity selector with a
(1-1/e)
submodular approximation guarantee.

4.

Controlled and large-scale validation.

Exact-ground-truth simulations validate the variance law, budget optimum, and selector predictions. LLM fine-tuning on multiple datasets across domains shows the same structure in practical training.

The resulting message is simple yet not unconditional.
MRT is not a claim that every dataset needs many responses per prompt, nor that reward-selected responses are always harmful.
Rather, it provides a principled way to decide when response multiplicity is worth the cost and how to use it.
When prompts are expensive, responses are cheap, and valid completions are diverse, single-response fine-tuning leaves useful supervision on the table.
MRT escapes the mode lottery by training on more of the conditional diversity that frontier language models can already generate.

2
Multi-Response Training

We formalize multi-response training (MRT) as a minimal extension of standard supervised learning or fine-tuning.
Let
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
be an autoregressive model with parameters
\theta
, trained with token-level cross-entropy loss
\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
The population objective is
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}[\ell]
.

Standard single-response training (SRT) observes one response per prompt and minimizes

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{SRT}}=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}).

In contrast, MRT retains
K>1
responses per prompt and minimizes

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{MRT}}=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij}).

SRT is thus the special case
K=1
, while MRT explicitly trains on multiple samples from the conditional response distribution.

Overgeneration and selection.

In modern post-training pipelines, it is common to generate multiple candidate responses per prompt using a base model or policy.
Formally, for each prompt
\mathbf{x}
, we obtain a candidate set
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\}
, from which a subset
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
of size
K
is selected for training.
MRT changes the downstream use of this candidate pool: instead of selecting a single response, it trains on multiple retained responses.

This also raises an important design question of which responses should be kept?
While we focus on
Random-
K
-of-
N
(RKoN)
, which samples
K
responses uniformly
at random
from the candidate pool of
N
responses
for each prompt
, and serves as the unbiased baseline, we also consider two other prominent settings:

Reward-based selection
(e.g., best-of-
N
or top-
K
), which prioritizes high-scoring responses but may concentrate on a narrow subset of modes; and

diversity-aware selection
, which aims to retain responses that are both high-quality and distinct, capturing broader coverage of valid outputs.

Statistical structure.

MRT induces a hierarchical data structure.
Prompts
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}
are sampled i.i.d. from
P(\mathbf{x})
, while responses
\mathbf{y}_{ij}
are sampled conditionally from
P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
This distinction is critical since additional prompts and additional responses play fundamentally different statistical roles.
Increasing
N_{p}
reduces uncertainty about the prompt distribution, while increasing
K
reduces uncertainty about the conditional response distribution.
The benefits and limitations of MRT arise from this asymmetry.

Scope and evaluation.

Our focus is on distributional fine-tuning, where the objective is to model the conditional distribution
P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
rather than to optimize a reward concentrated on a single preferred output.
This setting naturally arises in multi-solution tasks, robustness evaluation, and pass@
K
-style metrics.
Under this perspective, retaining multiple valid responses is not redundant supervision, but additional signal about the conditional structure.

3
When Does MRT Help? A Variance–Budget Law

MRT is not universally beneficial.
Keeping more responses per prompt improves some aspects of learning while leaving others unchanged.
The key question is therefore not whether
K>1
can help, but
when response multiplicity is the right use of data budget
.

A structural decomposition.

The answer follows from a single structural observation that prompts and responses contribute different kinds of information.
New prompts expand coverage of the input distribution, while additional responses refine the model’s estimate of the conditional output distribution.
This asymmetry induces a two-term decomposition.

Lemma 3.1

(Prompt–response variance decomposition)
.

For any fixed
\theta
, the variance of multi-response empirical loss decomposes exactly as,

\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\right]=\frac{V_{\mathbf{x}}(\theta)}{N_{p}}+\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}(\theta)}{N_{p}K},

where

V_{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\ell\right]

is the across-prompt variance and

V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\left[\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\ell\right]

is the expected within-prompt variance.

This decomposition makes the role of MRT explicit.
The first term is a
prompt floor
that depends only on the number of distinct prompts
N_{p}
and cannot be reduced by additional responses.
The second term is the
response variance
, which shrinks linearly with number of responses per prompt
K
.

MRT helps to the extent that within-prompt response diversity is large relative to across-prompt variation.

If most uncertainty comes from variation across prompts, collecting more responses is ineffective.
If uncertainty is dominated by variation within prompts, increasing
K
is highly beneficial.

From variance to allocation.

If all examples had equal cost, the optimal strategy would be trivial: maximize the number of independent prompt-response pairs.
MRT becomes nontrivial because prompts and responses have very different acquisition costs.
In modern pipelines, prompts are often human-authored, curated, filtered, or licensed, while additional responses can be generated cheaply from an existing model.
At scale, this asymmetry can be extreme: the marginal cost of new prompts can dominate, while the cost of additional responses is negligible.
Let
C_{p}
denote the cost of obtaining a prompt and
C_{r}
the cost of obtaining an additional response.
Under a fixed budget
\mathcal{B}=N_{p}(C_{p}+KC_{r})
, we obtain:

Proposition 3.2

(Cost-aware optimal multiplicity)
.

The variance-minimizing response multiplicity is

K^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}}{V_{\mathbf{x}}}\cdot\frac{C_{p}}{C_{r}}}.

Moreover, MRT is preferred over single-response training when

\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}}{V_{\mathbf{x}}}>\frac{C_{r}}{C_{p}}.

The optimal
K^{\star}
thus depends on two ratios:
(i) a
statistical ratio

V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/V_{\mathbf{x}}
, capturing how diverse responses are relative to prompts, and
(ii) a
cost ratio

C_{p}/C_{r}
, capturing how expensive prompts are relative to responses.
The square-root form is crucial, and it implies diminishing returns: increasing
K
yields rapid gains initially but quickly saturates as the prompt floor dominates.

MRT is most valuable when responses are cheap and diverse, and prompts are expensive and non-redundant.

Practical estimation.

The quantities in Proposition
3.2
can be estimated from a small validation pool.
Given prompts with multiple sampled responses, one computes variance across prompt means and variance within prompts.
In practice, this requires only tens of prompts and a modest number of responses per prompt, and serves as a reliable diagnostic of whether MRT is likely to help.

Implicit MRT through redundancy.

The same analysis explains an important empirical phenomenon.
Large corpora can exhibit substantial prompt redundancy, as many practical prompts are paraphrases or near-equivalent tasks (e.g., “
how hot is the weather today?
” vs. “
does it feel really warm today?
”).
In such settings, single-response training can still observe multiple valid responses for the same underlying task, spread across different prompts.
This can induce an
implicit MRT
effect, where prompt redundancy partially substitutes for explicit response multiplicity.

Explicit MRT is most beneficial in low-redundancy regimes, where each prompt covers a distinct region of the task distribution.

In contrast, highly redundant corpora already approximate this effect and therefore see smaller gains from increasing
K
.
We also design experiments to probe this distinction directly.

4
How Much Can MRT Help? Two Generalization Views

The variance–budget law identifies
when
MRT is useful.
We now quantify
how much
it can help.
The answer is governed by the same structure as before: prompt-level uncertainty and response-level uncertainty behave differently.
The key question is how this structure manifests in generalization.

We present two complementary views.
A Bernstein view is variance-adaptive and gives the sharpest fixed-model characterization.
A hierarchical Rademacher view is uniform and shows that the same gains persist under model selection despite the non-i.i.d. structure of repeated prompts.

Variance-adaptive generalization.

For a fixed model, MRT averages
N_{p}
independent prompt-level quantities, each itself an average over
K
responses.
Thus the effective variance is exactly the prompt–response decomposition from Lemma
3.1
.

Theorem 4.1

(Bernstein generalization bound)
.

Assume
0\leq\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq B
.
For any fixed
\theta
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\left(V_{\mathbf{x}}(\theta)+\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}(\theta)}{K}\right)\log(2/\delta)}{N_{p}}}+\frac{2B\log(2/\delta)}{3N_{p}}.

This bound gives a clean quantitative picture.
Increasing
K
reduces only the within-prompt term
V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/K
, while the prompt-level term
V_{\mathbf{x}}
remains unchanged.
MRT converts within-prompt diversity into reducible error but cannot reduce the prompt floor.
This helps explain our empirical observation of gains from increasing
K
being large initially but saturating as the prompt-level uncertainty dominates.

Uniform generalization under hierarchy.

The Bernstein bound is tight but require fixed
\theta
.
Yet training selects
\theta
from data, requiring a uniform guarantee.
We need to carefully handle the MRT data as it involves subtle yet significant hierarchy: there are
N_{p}K
samples but only
N_{p}
independent units.

Two naive analyses fail in opposite ways
:
treating all
N_{p}K
pairs as i.i.d. ignores the prompt bottleneck, while analyzing each prompt separately loses the dependence on
N_{p}
and yields only
O(1/\sqrt{K})
scaling.
The correct analysis must respect both levels simultaneously.

Let

\mathcal{F}=\bigl\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\theta\in\Theta\bigr\},

and

\mathcal{G}=\bigl\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\theta\in\Theta\bigr\}.

For a fixed prompt set
X
, define the
conditional
response-level Rademacher complexity

\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,Y\mid X}\left[\sup_{\theta}\frac{1}{N_{p}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sigma_{ij}\,\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij})\right].

Theorem 4.2

(Hierarchical uniform generalization)
.

Assume
0\leq\ell\leq B
.
With probability at least
1-\delta
, uniformly for all
\theta\in\Theta
,

\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\leq\;2\,\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}}(\mathcal{G})+2\,\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)]+3B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}}}+B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}K}}.

The structure mirrors the Bernstein bound.
Prompt-level terms depend only on
N_{p}
, while response-level terms benefit from the full
N_{p}K
sample size.
Furthermore, under standard capacity control

\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)]=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{p}K}}\right),

so increasing
K
genuinely improves the uniform bound.

The key technical step is keeping
\sup_{\theta}
outside the per-prompt average so that symmetrization acts jointly over all
N_{p}K
responses.
This preserves the
1/\sqrt{N_{p}K}
rate that naive per-prompt analyses lose.

Bernstein explains the gain; hierarchical Rademacher certifies that the gain survives model selection.

Putting the views together.

Both bounds expose the same mechanism.
MRT reduces only the response-level component of generalization error, and all improvements are mediated through this term.
The prompt-level floor remains unchanged.

MRT improves generalization by averaging within prompts, although prompt diversity must still come from more prompts. This unified picture explains why MRT is complementary to prompt collection rather than a substitute for it, and why its gains are largest in high response-diversity, low prompt-redundancy regimes.

Remark 4.3

(Bounded-loss interpretation)
.

The bounds are stated for bounded loss.
For cross-entropy, this can be interpreted via a clipped surrogate or per-token truncation.
Empirically, the results
capture the concentration behavior of the loss, with the approximation gap governed by the upper tail.

5
Which Responses to Keep? Target-Aware Selection

Sections
3
–
4
show that increasing the retained
multiplicity
K
reduces response-level estimation error. We now separate this from a
second design choice:
which

K
responses to keep from an overgenerated pool

\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\}
. This choice is not merely
an implementation detail. The selector determines the training target: it may preserve the
candidate distribution, tilt it toward high reward, or bias training toward broader coverage
of response modes.

Distributional target: Random-
K
-of-
N
.

For distributional fine-tuning, the selector should preserve the conditional response
distribution. Random-
K
-of-
N
(RKoN) does exactly this.

Proposition 5.1

(RKoN preserves the candidate distribution)
.

Let

\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})
,
and let RKoN choose
K
candidates uniformly at random, independent of their values.
For
g(\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}[g(\mathbf{y})],
and

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})=\frac{1}{K}\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbf{y}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}(g).

In particular, if
q=P
, RKoN is unbiased for the distributional target.

Thus RKoN is the assumption-light default for studying MRT itself, because increasing
K
reduces gradient-estimator variance at the predicted
1/K
rate without changing the target
distribution.

Reward target: Best-
K
-of-
N
.

Best-
K
-of-
N
(BKoN) instead selects the highest-reward responses. This can be the
right selector when the goal is reward optimization, but it is generally biased for
distributional fine-tuning. The bias does not vanish by increasing the candidate pool; in
fact, a larger pool can sharpen the reward-tail tilt.

Proposition 5.2

(Persistent upper-tail bias)
.

Suppose responses decompose into modes with probabilities
p_{m}
and distinct gradient
means
\{\mu_{m}\}
. If one mode
m^{\star}
dominates the reward upper tail, then as

n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}]\to\mu_{m^{\star}},\qquad\mu_{m^{\star}}\neq\sum_{m}p_{m}\mu_{m}=\bar{g}

whenever the reward-dominant mode is not distributionally representative.

The limit is stated under bounded gradients and a tail-stable gradient mean for the dominant mode (see Appendix
B.7.2
for proof and the formal sufficient conditions).

This formalizes a target mismatch rather than a failure of optimization: BKoN trains on a
reward-tilted conditional distribution. Empirically, this appears as higher reward but lower
diversity and worse reference likelihood.

Coverage-aware selection.

RKoN is unbiased, but for small
K
it may spend multiple samples on the same response
mode. The ideal variance-reducing alternative is stratified sampling across modes, which
removes between-mode variance while preserving the distributional target. Since true modes
are unknown, we use diversity-aware selectors as practical approximations. In particular,
Diverse-Best-
K
-of-
N
(DBKoN) balances quality and coverage, while DKoN denotes the
pure-diversity special case. Our implementation, GRADES, greedily optimizes a monotone
facility-location surrogate over nonnegative quality scores and response similarities,
giving the standard
(1-1/e)
approximation guarantee for that surrogate. The guarantee is
not a direct population-risk guarantee; it is useful only insofar as the similarity kernel
captures task-relevant response modes. Formal definitions, the stratification result, and
the GRADES proof are given in Appendix
B.7
.

Key Takeaway.

The multiplicity
K
controls how much conditional information is retained, while the
selector controls
which distribution
that information represents. RKoN tests the
variance–budget mechanism for distributional MRT; BKoN exposes reward-induced target
shift; and DBKoN/GRADES probes whether quality–coverage selection can recover some of the
variance reduction of stratification without collapsing valid modes.

6
Experiments

Our theory makes three concrete predictions.
First, increasing response multiplicity
K
reduces only the within-prompt component of uncertainty, yielding diminishing returns once the prompt-level floor dominates.
Second, the benefit of MRT should be largest on datasets with high response-level variability relative to prompt-level variability.
Third, selectors change the
training target
.

We test these predictions through four progressively less controlled validations.
A controlled synthetic setting first verifies the variance law, the cost-aware optimum, and selector bias under exact ground truth.
We then study real LLM fine-tuning across four multi-response benchmarks spanning instruction following, dialogue, preference, and code generation.
Next, we test whether dataset-level variance ratios predict where MRT helps most.
Finally, we examine selector behavior and implicit MRT through prompt redundancy.

Real LLM fine-tuning cannot directly observe
V_{\mathbf{x}}

A reward model favors the central mode without making the others invalid, so reward optimization genuinely competes with distributional coverage.
We train mixture-density networks by NLL and evaluate population loss on fresh draws from the true conditional distribution

7
Conclusion

This paper argues that the value of post-training supervision depends not only on scale, but on how supervision is allocated across sources of uncertainty. Prompts and responses play different statistical roles: new prompts reduce uncertainty about the input distribution, while additional responses reduce uncertainty about the conditional output distribution. This distinction yields a variance–budget law for response multiplicity, and offers guidance on how MRT is most useful when within-prompt response variation is large, prompts are costly or non-redundant, and additional responses are cheap.

The resulting prescription is simple. Estimate the variance ratio
V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/V_{\mathbf{x}}
on a proxy model, choose the response multiplicity according to the resulting operating regime, and use a selector whose target matches the downstream objective. RKoN preserves the candidate distribution and isolates the distributional benefit of MRT; BKoN optimizes a reward-tilted target and can collapse toward upper-tail modes; coverage-aware selectors trade reward concentration against mode coverage. Thus the number of retained responses controls how much conditional information is used, while the selector controls which distribution that information represents.

Across controlled simulations and LLM fine-tuning experiments, we find the same pattern: MRT gives the largest gains in high response-diversity, low prompt-redundancy regimes, with diminishing returns once the prompt-level floor dominates. The same framework also explains implicit MRT, where redundant prompts can substitute for explicit response multiplicity when they expose distinct conditional modes. Overall, MRT is valuable not because it adds more tokens, but because it exposes more of the conditional structure the model is trying to learn.

B.1
Preliminaries and Concentration Tools

Notation recap.

Throughout, prompts
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N_{p}}
are drawn i.i.d. from a marginal

P(\mathbf{x})
, and for each prompt
\mathbf{x}_{i}
we draw
K
responses

\mathbf{y}_{i,1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{i,K}
i.i.d. from the conditional

P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
The per-example loss
\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is assumed bounded,

\ell\in[0,B]
(Remark
4.3
; see also
Remark
B.3
).
The MRT empirical risk is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij}),

and we write
g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

for the prompt-level conditional risk, so that

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
.
Recall the variance components

V_{\mathbf{x}}(\theta)=\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{x}}[g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})]
and

V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\bigl[\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\ell\bigr]
.
Note that
g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\in[0,B]
as a conditional expectation of
\ell\in[0,B]
.
We write
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}):\theta\in\Theta\}
for the
prompt-level loss class and define the prompt-level Rademacher complexity

\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}}(\mathcal{G})=\mathbb{E}_{X,\sigma}\!\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\sigma_{i}\,g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right],

where
\sigma_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
are i.i.d. Rademacher signs independent of
X
. The
conditional response-level complexity
\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)
is as
defined in the main text (Section
4
).

Lemma B.1

(McDiarmid’s inequality
[
25
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be independent random variables, and let

f:\mathcal{X}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the bounded-differences property

\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m},x_{i}^{\prime}}\bigl|f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{m})-f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i}^{\prime},\dots,x_{m})\bigr|\leq c_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

Then for every
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})-\mathbb{E}[f]\geq\epsilon\bigr)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

Lemma B.2

(Lifting conditional tail bounds)
.

Let
E
be an event and
X
a random variable. If

\mathbb{P}(E\mid X)\geq 1-\delta
almost surely, then
\mathbb{P}(E)\geq 1-\delta
.

Proof.

\mathbb{P}(E^{c})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(E^{c}\mid X)]\leq\delta
.
∎

Remark B.3

(Bounded-loss surrogate, formalizing Remark
4.3
)
.

Cross-entropy is unbounded, so the bounded-loss assumption is enforced through a
truncation. Fix a per-token clip level
B_{\mathrm{tok}}>0
and define

\tilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta;\mathbf{x},y_{<t})\coloneqq\min\!\bigl(-\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid\mathbf{x},y_{<t}),\;B_{\mathrm{tok}}\bigr),\qquad\tilde{\ell}(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\ell}_{t},

so that
\tilde{\ell}\in[0,TB_{\mathrm{tok}}]
with

The associated approximation gap is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\,T\,(\ell_{t}-B_{\mathrm{tok}})^{+}\bigr]
, which decays whenever the
token negative log-likelihood has a sufficiently light upper tail. For typical
fine-tuning checkpoints this gap is empirically negligible at

in the
high single digits, and our proofs below are stated for
\tilde{\ell}
but read
identically for
\ell
once truncation is applied.

Assumption B.4

(Loss-class capacity)
.

The loss class
\mathcal{F}=\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\theta\in\Theta\}

satisfies one of the following standard capacity-control conditions:

(i)

Finite VC dimension.

\mathcal{F}
has VC subgraph dimension

d_{\mathrm{VC}}<\infty
.

(ii)

Lipschitz parametrization.

\theta\mapsto\ell(\theta;\cdot)
is

L

with bounded
diameter (e.g., LoRA adapters of rank

(iii)

The covering numbers

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\hat{P}))

\int_{0}^{B}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\hat{P}))}\,d\varepsilon<\infty

Any of (i)–(iii) is sufficient for the conditional Rademacher complexity to satisfy

\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)=O\!\bigl(\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/(N_{p}K)}\bigr)

B.2
Proof of Lemma
3.1
(Variance Decomposition)

Proof.

By the law of total variance applied to
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)
with
respect to the prompt randomness
X=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N_{p}})
,

\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\bigr]=\operatorname{Var}_{X}\!\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}_{X}\!\bigl[\operatorname{Var}_{Y\mid X}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}\bigr].

First term.
Conditional on
X
, the inner average over the
K
responses is

g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
in expectation, so

\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
Since the prompts are i.i.d.,

\operatorname{Var}_{X}\!\left[\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right]=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{x}}[g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})]=\frac{V_{\mathbf{x}}}{N_{p}}.

Second term.
Conditional on
X
, the responses
\{\mathbf{y}_{ij}\}
are independent
across
(i,j)
, so

\operatorname{Var}_{Y\mid X}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}=\frac{1}{N_{p}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\operatorname{Var}_{Y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij})\right]=\frac{1}{N_{p}^{2}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}}\ell.

Taking the expectation over
X
,

\mathbb{E}_{X}\!\bigl[\operatorname{Var}_{Y\mid X}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}\bigr]=\frac{1}{N_{p}K}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\bigl[\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}\ell\bigr]=\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}}{N_{p}K}.

Summing the two contributions yields the claim.
∎

Why a
V_{\mathbf{x}}/N_{p}
floor is irreducible.

Prompts are sampled marginally i.i.d. (giving the
1/N_{p}
factor on
V_{\mathbf{x}}
),
while responses are only
conditionally
i.i.d. given prompts (giving the additional

1/K
factor on
V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}
). Had the responses been marginally i.i.d.,
both terms would scale as
1/(N_{p}K)
and the floor would disappear; it is precisely the
hierarchical structure of MRT data that creates the irreducible prompt-level term.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
3.2
(Cost-Aware Optimal Multiplicity)

Proof.

The budget constraint
\mathcal{B}=N_{p}(C_{p}+KC_{r})
gives
N_{p}=\mathcal{B}/(C_{p}+KC_{r})
.
Substituting into the variance from Lemma
3.1
,

V(K)\coloneqq\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}\bigr]=\frac{V_{\mathbf{x}}+V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/K}{N_{p}}=\frac{(V_{\mathbf{x}}+V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/K)(C_{p}+KC_{r})}{\mathcal{B}}.

Expanding,

V(K)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}}\!\left(V_{\mathbf{x}}C_{p}+V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}C_{r}+V_{\mathbf{x}}C_{r}\,K+\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}C_{p}}{K}\right).

The first two terms are constant in
K
; the remaining sum

V_{\mathbf{x}}C_{r}\,K+V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}C_{p}/K
is strictly convex in
K>0
, since it
is a positive linear combination of
K
and
1/K
.
Setting
dV/dK=0
,

V_{\mathbf{x}}C_{r}-\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}C_{p}}{K^{2}}=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;K^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}}{V_{\mathbf{x}}}\cdot\frac{C_{p}}{C_{r}}}.

By strict convexity,
K^{\star}
is the unique global minimizer on
(0,\infty)
.
Comparing with
K=1
, MRT strictly improves over single-response training in the
continuous relaxation iff
K^{\star}>1
, equivalently

V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/V_{\mathbf{x}}>C_{r}/C_{p}
.
∎

Integer-
K
remark.

The continuous threshold above is necessary but not sufficient for the integer choice

K{=}2
to strictly improve over
K{=}1
. Only the
K
-dependent terms differ between the
two, so

V(2)-V(1)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}}\!\left(V_{\mathbf{x}}C_{r}-\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}C_{p}}{2}\right),

which gives

V(2)<V(1)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}}{V_{\mathbf{x}}}>\frac{2C_{r}}{C_{p}}.

The factor

is sharp: at the boundary,

.

Estimating
K^{\star}
in practice.

The two variance components can be estimated from a small validation pool. Take

prompts and generate
K_{\mathrm{val}}\in[8,16]

\hat{s}_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{K_{\mathrm{val}}-1}\sum_{j}(\ell-\hat{g}_{i})^{2}

\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{val}}}\sum_{i}\hat{s}_{i}^{2},\qquad\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{x}}=\max\!\left(0,\;\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{val}}-1}\sum_{i}\bigl(\hat{g}_{i}-\bar{\hat{g}}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{\widehat{V}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}}{K_{\mathrm{val}}}\right),

where the bias correction subtracts the within-prompt contribution that leaks into the
empirical between-prompt variance. Plugging into

gives a simple plug-in rule
that is robust enough to be used as a budget diagnostic.

(Bernstein Bound)

Proof.

Define per-prompt deviations

so that

The

are independent: their randomness comes from the disjoint blocks

(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i,1:K})
, which are i.i.d. across

|Z_{i}|\leq B

Bernstein’s inequality applied to

yields, for every

\mathbb{P}(|\bar{Z}|\geq\epsilon)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{N_{p}\epsilon^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+\tfrac{2}{3}B\epsilon}\right).

Setting the right-hand side equal to

N_{p}\epsilon^{2}-\tfrac{2B\ln(2/\delta)}{3}\epsilon-2\sigma^{2}\ln(2/\delta)=0
, whose

\epsilon=\frac{\tfrac{2B\ln(2/\delta)}{3}+\sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{2B\ln(2/\delta)}{3}\bigr)^{2}+8N_{p}\sigma^{2}\ln(2/\delta)}}{2N_{p}}.

Using

\epsilon\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}\ln(2/\delta)}{N_{p}}}+\frac{2B\ln(2/\delta)}{3N_{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\bigl(V_{\mathbf{x}}+V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}/K\bigr)\ln(2/\delta)}{N_{p}}}+\frac{2B\ln(2/\delta)}{3N_{p}},

proving the claim.

Clipped-loss corollary.

Applying the same proof to the clipped surrogate

Tightening factor.

Single-response training corresponds to

In practice, responses are drawn from a generator

P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
. We quantify the resulting bias.

Proposition B.5

(Generator mismatch)
.

Suppose responses are drawn from
q
with

\mathrm{TV}\!\bigl(q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}),P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\bigr)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{TV}}

uniformly in
\mathbf{x}
, and let
\mathcal{L}_{P}
denote the population risk under the
target
P
. Then

\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{q}[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}]-\mathcal{L}_{P}\bigr|\leq B\,\delta_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Proof.

For any fixed
\theta
and prompt
\mathbf{x}
, the bound
\ell\in[0,B]
and the
variational definition of total variation give

\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{q}\ell-\mathbb{E}_{P}\ell\bigr|\leq B\cdot\mathrm{TV}\!\bigl(q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}),P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\bigr)\leq B\,\delta_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Marginalizing over
\mathbf{x}
and observing that

\mathbb{E}_{q}[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\ell

yields the claim.
∎

The same Bernstein concentration around
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}]

goes through unchanged with
q
-distributed responses; converting to the target
P
only
adds the additive bias above.

B.5
Proof of Theorem
4.2
(Hierarchical Uniform Bound)

Proof strategy.

The challenge is to handle two layers of randomness—
N_{p}
i.i.d. prompts and
K

conditionally i.i.d. responses per prompt—while keeping
\sup_{\theta}
outside both
sums so the response-level Rademacher term benefits from all
N_{p}K
samples jointly. The
proof decomposes the gap into a prompt-level term (controlled by McDiarmid over prompts)
and a response-level term (conditional symmetrization, then McDiarmid unconditionally),
with a third McDiarmid step to control fluctuations of the conditional Rademacher
complexity itself.

Proof.

Define the prompt-level empirical risk

\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
Decompose

\sup_{\theta}\!\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\bigr)\leq\underbrace{\sup_{\theta}\!\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\bigr)}_{A(X)}+\underbrace{\sup_{\theta}\!\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\bigr)}_{C(X,Y)}.

Step 1: Prompt-level term
A(X)
.

A(X)
is a standard empirical process over
N_{p}
i.i.d. prompts with

g_{\theta}\in[0,B]
. Changing one prompt changes
A(X)
by at most
B/N_{p}
, so by
McDiarmid (Lemma
B.1
) with probability
\geq 1-\delta/4
,

A(X)\leq\mathbb{E}[A(X)]+B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}}}.

Standard symmetrization over
\mathcal{G}

[
3
]
gives

\mathbb{E}[A(X)]\leq 2\,\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}}(\mathcal{G})
.

Step 2: Response-level term
C(X,Y)
.

Conditioning on
X
,
C(X,Y)
depends on the
N_{p}K
independent responses

\mathbf{y}_{ij}
. Changing a single
\mathbf{y}_{ij}
alters
C
by at most
B/(N_{p}K)
.
McDiarmid conditionally on
X
gives, with probability
\geq 1-\delta/4
(lifted to
unconditional probability via Lemma
B.2
),

C(X,Y)\leq\mathbb{E}[C\mid X]+B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}K}}.

The conditional expectation is bounded by symmetrization with ghost responses

\mathbf{y}_{ij}^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{i})
drawn independently of
\mathbf{y}_{ij}
.
Writing
\Phi_{\theta}(Y)=\frac{1}{N_{p}K}\sum_{i,j}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij})

and using
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}\mid X}\Phi_{\theta}(Y^{\prime})
,
Jensen and the standard ghost-sample trick give

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[C\mid X]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X}\!\left[\sup_{\theta}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}\mid X}\Phi_{\theta}(Y^{\prime})-\Phi_{\theta}(Y)\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\mid X}\!\left[\sup_{\theta}\bigl(\Phi_{\theta}(Y^{\prime})-\Phi_{\theta}(Y)\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,Y,Y^{\prime}\mid X}\!\left[\sup_{\theta}\frac{1}{N_{p}K}\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{ij}\bigl(\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij}^{\prime})-\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij})\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,Y\mid X}\!\left[\sup_{\theta}\frac{1}{N_{p}K}\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{ij}\,\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{ij})\right]=2\,\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X),

where the third line uses the symmetry of
(\mathbf{y}_{ij},\mathbf{y}_{ij}^{\prime})
exchanged
under
\sigma_{ij}=\pm 1
(the
\mathbf{y}_{ij}
are conditionally independent given

X
, which is all that the standard symmetrization argument requires;
exchangeability
across
prompts is not needed).
Crucially, the supremum is
not
pushed inside the prompt sum, so the Rademacher
variables
\sigma_{ij}
symmetrize all
N_{p}K
samples jointly.

Step 3: Concentration of the conditional Rademacher complexity.

Let
\Psi(X)=2\,\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)
. Use the natural coupling that, on
replacing
\mathbf{x}_{i}
by
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}
, resamples

\mathbf{y}_{i,1:K}\sim P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime})
while keeping all other responses
fixed. This replaces
K
terms (each in
[0,B]
) inside the Rademacher sum of
N_{p}K

terms, so
|\Psi(X)-\Psi(X^{\prime})|\leq 2K\cdot B/(N_{p}K)=2B/N_{p}
pointwise.
McDiarmid unconditionally over the
N_{p}
prompts gives, with probability

\geq 1-\delta/4
,

\Psi(X)\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}[\Psi(X)]+2B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}}}.

Combining.

A union bound over the three events (
\leq 3\delta/4<\delta
) yields

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta}\!\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{MRT}}(\theta)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}}(\mathcal{G})+2\,\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)]

\displaystyle\quad+B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}}}+2B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}}}+B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}K}}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}}(\mathcal{G})+2\,\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)]+3B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}}}+B\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2N_{p}K}},

which is the stated bound (with
\ln(4/\delta)
rewritten as
\log(4/\delta)
).
∎

Why not push
\sup_{\theta}
inside the prompt sum?

A simpler argument would push
\sup_{\theta}
inside
\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i}
and bound each
per-prompt term by a conditional Rademacher complexity that depends only on
K
. This
would yield a response-level term
\mathfrak{R}_{K}(\mathcal{F})=O(1/\sqrt{K})
with

no
dependence on
N_{p}
, so the bound would not vanish as
N_{p}\to\infty
with
K

fixed. Our three-step argument keeps the supremum outside, yielding

\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)=O(1/\sqrt{N_{p}K})
under
Assumption
B.4
. The price is the third McDiarmid step and a slightly
larger constant (
3B
vs.
B
) on the prompt-level concentration.

Dudley-integral scaling.

Under Assumption
B.4
, Dudley’s entropy integral gives

\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)\leq\inf_{\alpha>0}\!\left(4\alpha+\frac{12}{\sqrt{N_{p}K}}\int_{\alpha}^{B}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\hat{P}))}\,d\varepsilon\right).

For VC subgraph dimension
d_{\mathrm{VC}}
or Lipschitz parametrization with effective
dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the integral is
O(\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}})
, giving

\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}K}(\mathcal{F}\mid X)=O\!\bigl(\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/(N_{p}K)}\bigr)
;
likewise
\mathfrak{R}_{N_{p}}(\mathcal{G})=O\!\bigl(\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/N_{p}}\bigr)
.

B.6
Implicit MRT through Prompt Redundancy

Remark B.6

(Implicit MRT)
.

Suppose the prompt corpus partitions into
C\ll N_{p}
semantic clusters with roughly

N_{p}/C
near-equivalent prompts each, all sharing the same conditional

P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
up to small variation. Then a single-response training (SRT)
sample of
N_{p}
prompts is, within each cluster, statistically indistinguishable from an
MRT sample with effective response multiplicity

K_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx N_{p}/C
collected from a single representative prompt.

Informal calculation.

Treat the cluster-level prompt as a latent variable
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
with
C

realizations. For SRT,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})

with
\mathbf{x}_{i}
uniform over the
C
clusters. Conditioning on the cluster
assignment, this is a hierarchical estimator with
C
“prompts” and

\sim N_{p}/C
“responses” each, so by Lemma
3.1
,

\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{SRT}}\bigr]\approx\frac{V_{\mathbf{x}^{\star}}}{C}+\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}^{\star}}}{C\cdot(N_{p}/C)}=\frac{V_{\mathbf{x}^{\star}}}{C}+\frac{V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}^{\star}}}{N_{p}}.

This interpolates between the two extremes: if clusters are “tight” (
C
small,

N_{p}/C
large), redundancy already realizes the
1/K
benefit of MRT, and explicit
multiplicity adds little; if clusters are “wide” (
C\approx N_{p}
), every prompt is its
own cluster and explicit MRT is the only way to reduce within-cluster variance. This is
the precise sense in which highly redundant corpora exhibit
implicit MRT
.

B.7
Target-Aware Selection: Details and Proofs

We provide the formal selector analysis used in
Section
5
here. Throughout this subsection we fix a prompt

\mathbf{x}
. Let

g(\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\qquad\bar{g}_{P}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim P(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}[g(\mathbf{y})].

An overgenerated candidate pool is denoted

\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\},

where
n
is the number of generated candidates and
K\leq n
is the number retained
for training. A selector
A
returns a subset

\mathcal{S}_{A}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
with

|\mathcal{S}_{A}|=K
, inducing the gradient estimator

\hat{g}_{A}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}(\mathbf{x})}g(\mathbf{y}).

Relative to the distributional target, the selector error decomposes as

\mathrm{EMSE}_{A}=\left\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{A}]-\bar{g}_{P}\right\|^{2}+\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{A}).

The first term is target mismatch; the second is estimator variance. This decomposition
makes the roles of RKoN, BKoN, and coverage-aware selection explicit: RKoN is unbiased
for the candidate distribution, BKoN changes the target toward reward upper tails, and
diversity-aware selectors attempt to reduce mode-undercoverage variance.

B.7.1
Random-
K
-of-
N

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Let

\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}),

and let RKoN choose a subset of
K
indices uniformly at random without replacement,
independently of the candidate values. Write

\bar{g}_{q}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}[g(\mathbf{y})],\qquad\Sigma_{q}=\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbf{y}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}(g(\mathbf{y})).

Condition on any selected index set

\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{K}\}
. Since the index set is independent of the
candidate values and the candidates are i.i.d.,

g(\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}),\ldots,g(\mathbf{y}_{i_{K}})

are independent draws from the same marginal distribution. Therefore

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}}\mid\mathcal{I}]=\bar{g}_{q},\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}}\mid\mathcal{I})=\frac{1}{K}\Sigma_{q}.

The conditional mean does not depend on
\mathcal{I}
, so the laws of total expectation
and total covariance give

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}}]=\bar{g}_{q},\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})=\frac{1}{K}\Sigma_{q}.

When
q=P
, this is unbiased for the distributional target

\bar{g}_{P}
. If
q\neq P
, RKoN remains unbiased for the candidate generator
q
;
any remaining bias is generator mismatch, not selector bias.
∎

Conditional-on-pool interpretation.

The proposition averages over both candidate generation and the random subset. If the
candidate pool is treated as fixed, RKoN instead estimates the empirical pool mean

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{y}_{i})
. Conditional on the pool, sampling without
replacement gives the usual finite-population correction. The unconditional statement
above is the relevant one for comparing selectors as stochastic data-retention rules.

B.7.2
Best-
K
-of-
N
and upper-tail target shift

We next give a sufficient condition under which Best-
K
-of-
n
converges in

expected gradient
to a reward-tail mode. The statement is deliberately an
expectation-level target-shift result: for fixed
K
, the selected gradient average
need not converge in probability to a constant because residual within-mode variance
can remain.

Let
Z\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
denote the latent response mode, with

\mathbb{P}(Z=m)=p_{m}>0
. Let
R(\mathbf{y})
be the reward used for BKoN, and define
the mode-conditional survival functions

S_{m}(t)=\mathbb{P}(R(\mathbf{y})\geq t\mid Z=m).

Proposition B.7

(A sufficient condition for persistent BKoN bias)
.

Fix
K\geq 1
. Suppose there is a reward-dominant mode
m^{\star}
and constants

\gamma>0
,
t_{0}
such that for every
m\neq m^{\star}
and all
t\geq t_{0}
,

S_{m}(t)\leq S_{m^{\star}}(t)^{1+\gamma}.

Let
t_{n}
be a threshold satisfying

S_{m^{\star}}(t_{n})=\log n/n
. Assume
\|g(\mathbf{y})\|\leq G
and the
reward-dominant mode has a tail-stable gradient mean, in the sense that if the
top-
K
selected responses all lie in mode
m^{\star}
with rewards at least
t_{n}
,
then

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}\mid\text{that event}]\to\mu_{m^{\star}}.

A sufficient special case is conditional independence between gradients and rewards in
the upper tail of mode
m^{\star}
, with

\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{y})\mid Z=m^{\star}]=\mu_{m^{\star}}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}]\to\mu_{m^{\star}}.

Consequently, whenever

\mu_{m^{\star}}\neq\bar{g}:=\sum_{m}p_{m}\mu_{m},

BKoN has a non-vanishing asymptotic bias relative to the distributional gradient.

Proof.

Let
N_{m}
be the number of candidates from mode
m
among the
n
generated
responses. By Chernoff bounds,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(N_{m}\notin[p_{m}n/2,2p_{m}n]\right)\leq 2\exp(-p_{m}n/8),

so the event

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{p_{m}n/2\leq N_{m}\leq 2p_{m}n\ \text{for all }m\}

has probability tending to one.

On
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, the number of reward-dominant candidates above
t_{n}
is

T_{m^{\star}}=\sum_{i:Z_{i}=m^{\star}}\mathbf{1}\{R(\mathbf{y}_{i})\geq t_{n}\}.

Conditionally on
N_{m^{\star}}\geq p_{m^{\star}}n/2
,

\mathbb{E}[T_{m^{\star}}\mid N_{m^{\star}}]=N_{m^{\star}}S_{m^{\star}}(t_{n})\geq\frac{p_{m^{\star}}}{2}\log n.

A lower-tail Chernoff bound therefore gives

\mathbb{P}(T_{m^{\star}}<K\mid N_{m^{\star}})\to 0

for fixed
K
.

For any non-dominant mode
m\neq m^{\star}
, again on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{i:Z_{i}=m}R(\mathbf{y}_{i})\geq t_{n}\,\middle|\,N_{m}\right)\leq N_{m}S_{m}(t_{n})\leq 2p_{m}n\,S_{m^{\star}}(t_{n})^{1+\gamma}=O\!\left(\frac{(\log n)^{1+\gamma}}{n^{\gamma}}\right)\to 0.

A union bound over the
M-1
non-dominant modes shows that, with probability tending
to one, at least
K
candidates from mode
m^{\star}
exceed
t_{n}
, while no
candidate from any other mode exceeds
t_{n}
. Hence the top-
K
reward candidates all
come from mode
m^{\star}
. Let
A_{n}
denote this event. We have

\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to 1.

By boundedness,
\|\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}\|\leq G
. Therefore

\left\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}\mid A_{n}]\right\|=\left\|\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{c})\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}\mid A_{n}^{c}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}\mid A_{n}]\right)\right\|\leq 2G\,\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{c})\to 0.

The tail-stable-gradient assumption gives

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}\mid A_{n}]\to\mu_{m^{\star}}
, and hence

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{BKoN}}]\to\mu_{m^{\star}}.

The claimed persistent bias follows whenever
\mu_{m^{\star}}\neq\bar{g}
.
∎

Interpretation.

The proposition is not a novelty claim about reward overoptimization itself. Its role is
to identify the target mismatch induced by using BKoN as a
data-retention rule

inside distributional supervised fine-tuning. Increasing
n
does not average this
bias away; under upper-tail dominance, it concentrates the retained data on the
reward-dominant mode. Mean reward ordering alone is insufficient for the result: a
lower-mean mode with a heavier upper tail can dominate the top-
K
set.

B.7.3
Coverage, stratification, and variance reduction

RKoN preserves the candidate distribution, but with small
K
it may miss modes.
The ideal coverage-aware baseline is oracle stratification over latent modes.

Assumption B.8

(Clusterable responses)
.

For a fixed prompt, responses decompose into modes

\{\mathcal{Y}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, with mode probabilities
p_{m}
, gradient means

\mu_{m}=\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{y})\mid\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{m}],

and within-mode covariance matrices

\Sigma_{m}=\operatorname{Cov}(g(\mathbf{y})\mid\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{m}).

Theorem B.9

(Stratification removes between-mode variance)
.

Under Assumption
B.8
, suppose an oracle stratified selector draws

K_{m}=Kp_{m}
responses from mode
m
, with
K_{m}
integral and

\sum_{m}K_{m}=K
. Then

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m}p_{m}\Sigma_{m}\preceq\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}}),

and

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})-\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m}p_{m}(\mu_{m}-\bar{g})(\mu_{m}-\bar{g})^{\top},\qquad\bar{g}=\sum_{m}p_{m}\mu_{m}.

The gap is exactly the between-mode covariance.

Proof.

For RKoN,
K
independent draws from the conditional distribution give

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})=\frac{1}{K}\operatorname{Cov}(g(\mathbf{y})).

By the law of total covariance over the latent mode,

\operatorname{Cov}(g(\mathbf{y}))=\sum_{m}p_{m}\Sigma_{m}+\sum_{m}p_{m}(\mu_{m}-\bar{g})(\mu_{m}-\bar{g})^{\top}.

For proportional stratification, write

\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}}=\sum_{m}p_{m}\left(\frac{1}{K_{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{m}}g_{mj}\right),

where
g_{mj}
are independent draws from mode
m
. The strata are sampled
independently, so

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}})=\sum_{m}p_{m}^{2}\frac{\Sigma_{m}}{K_{m}}=\sum_{m}p_{m}^{2}\frac{\Sigma_{m}}{Kp_{m}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m}p_{m}\Sigma_{m}.

Subtracting yields

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})-\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m}p_{m}(\mu_{m}-\bar{g})(\mu_{m}-\bar{g})^{\top}\succeq 0,

since it is a nonnegative sum of outer products.
∎

Integer allocation.

The theorem states the clean continuous-allocation result. If
K_{m}
is obtained by
largest-remainder rounding, write

K_{m}=Kp_{m}+\delta_{m},\qquad|\delta_{m}|\leq 1,\qquad\sum_{m}\delta_{m}=0.

For any matrix norm
\|\cdot\|
, if
Kp_{m}\geq 2
for all active modes, then

\left\|\frac{p_{m}^{2}}{K_{m}}\Sigma_{m}-\frac{p_{m}}{K}\Sigma_{m}\right\|=\left|\frac{p_{m}^{2}}{Kp_{m}+\delta_{m}}-\frac{p_{m}}{K}\right|\|\Sigma_{m}\|\leq\frac{2}{K^{2}}\|\Sigma_{m}\|.

Thus the total rounding slack is

O\!\left(\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{m}\|\Sigma_{m}\|\right)

whenever all active modes receive at least a constant expected allocation. If some

Kp_{m}<1
, exact proportional stratification is impossible; the PSD comparison should
then be interpreted as the continuous-allocation idealization.

B.7.4
GRADES: a greedy quality–coverage selector

True response modes are unobserved, so oracle stratification is unavailable. GRADES
implements a facility-location surrogate that trades off quality and coverage within a
candidate pool. We first convert raw reward scores into nonnegative quality weights.
Given raw scores
r_{j}=r(\mathbf{y}_{j})
, define

q_{j}=\varepsilon+\frac{r_{j}-\min_{i}r_{i}}{\max_{i}r_{i}-\min_{i}r_{i}+\varepsilon},\qquad q_{j}>0,

where
\varepsilon>0
is small. This normalization is important: GRADES uses
fractional powers
q_{j}^{\alpha}
, so the quality scores must be nonnegative. If all
raw scores are equal, all candidates receive the same quality up to
\varepsilon
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
denote the pool on which GRADES is run. In the
experiments
\mathcal{U}
may be the top-
K_{\max}
candidates by quality; setting

K_{\max}=n
recovers the full candidate pool. The guarantee below is always with
respect to the chosen pool
\mathcal{U}
. Let

S_{ij}=\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\in[0,1]

be a nonnegative similarity kernel, and let
\alpha\geq 0
control the quality–coverage
tradeoff. GRADES maximizes

F(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}q_{i}^{\alpha}\max_{\mathbf{y}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[q_{j}^{\alpha}S_{ij}\right],\qquad|\mathcal{S}|\leq K,

with the convention that the maximum over the empty set is
0
. The case
\alpha=0

is pure diversity selection; we refer to this special case as DKoN. Positive

\alpha
gives Diverse-Best-
K
-of-
n
selection, implemented by GRADES.

Proposition B.10

(Submodularity of GRADES)
.

If
q_{j}\geq 0
and
S_{ij}\geq 0
, then
F
is normalized, monotone, and submodular.
Therefore greedy selection returns a set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{greedy}}
satisfying

F(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{greedy}})\geq(1-1/e)\max_{|\mathcal{S}|\leq K}F(\mathcal{S}).

Proof.

For each fixed candidate
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, define

F_{i}(\mathcal{S})=q_{i}^{\alpha}\max_{\mathbf{y}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[q_{j}^{\alpha}S_{ij}\right].

This function is normalized because the maximum over the empty set is defined as zero.
It is monotone because adding elements can only increase a maximum. To verify
submodularity, let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and

\mathbf{y}_{e}\notin\mathcal{B}
. Write

m_{i}(\mathcal{S})=\max_{\mathbf{y}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}}q_{j}^{\alpha}S_{ij}.

Since
m_{i}(\mathcal{A})\leq m_{i}(\mathcal{B})
, the marginal gain from adding

\mathbf{y}_{e}
satisfies

F_{i}(\mathcal{A}\cup\{\mathbf{y}_{e}\})-F_{i}(\mathcal{A})=q_{i}^{\alpha}\left[q_{e}^{\alpha}S_{ie}-m_{i}(\mathcal{A})\right]_{+}\geq q_{i}^{\alpha}\left[q_{e}^{\alpha}S_{ie}-m_{i}(\mathcal{B})\right]_{+}=F_{i}(\mathcal{B}\cup\{\mathbf{y}_{e}\})-F_{i}(\mathcal{B}).

Thus each
F_{i}
is submodular. Nonnegative sums of monotone submodular functions are
monotone submodular, so
F=\sum_{i}F_{i}

B.7.5
Mode coverage under block similarity

The following stylized result shows why facility-location selection approximates
stratification when similarities reveal the latent modes.

Proposition B.11

(Block-similarity mode coverage)
.

Under Assumption
B.8
, suppose the candidate pool partitions into
nonempty mode blocks
\{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, and suppose the similarity kernel is
block-constant:

\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\begin{cases}1,&\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\text{ are in the same mode},\\
\tau,&\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\text{ are in different modes},\end{cases}\qquad 0\leq\tau<1.

For
\alpha=0
, if
K\geq M
, greedy GRADES selects at least one response from every
mode before selecting a duplicate from any already covered mode.

Proof.

When
\alpha=0
, all quality weights are one and

F(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\max_{\mathbf{y}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}}S_{ij}.

If no selected response lies in mode
m
, then every response in mode
m
has current
coverage at most
\tau
. Adding any response from mode
m
raises the coverage of
all responses in that mode to
1
, giving marginal gain at least

|\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{m}|(1-\tau)>0.

By contrast, adding another response from a mode already represented in
\mathcal{S}

gives zero marginal gain: responses in that mode already have coverage
1
, while
responses in other modes have similarity only
\tau
, which cannot improve their
current coverage once at least one response has already been selected. Therefore any
uncovered mode has strictly positive marginal gain and any covered mode has zero
marginal gain. Greedy must cover all
M
modes before duplicating a mode.
∎

This result is intentionally idealized. For
\alpha>0
, high-quality modes may be
selected more than once before all low-quality modes are covered. This is the intended
quality–coverage tradeoff of DBKoN/GRADES.

B.7.6
Similarity, gradient covariance, and effective sample size

The submodular guarantee above concerns the surrogate objective
F
. To connect
coverage to gradient variance, one needs an additional modeling assumption relating the
similarity kernel to gradient covariance.

Assumption B.12

(Similarity controls positive cross-covariance)
.

For responses to the same prompt, let

g_{j}=g(\mathbf{y}_{j})
. There exist constants
C_{0},C_{1}\geq 0
such that the
positive trace contribution of the cross-covariance satisfies

\left[\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(g_{j},g_{j^{\prime}})\right]_{+}\leq C_{0}+C_{1}\,\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{y}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j^{\prime}}).

The constant
C_{0}
represents an irreducible covariance floor, such as the shared
prompt contribution. The
C_{1}
term is the component that diversity-aware selection
can reduce by selecting dissimilar responses.

To see the implication, suppose selected gradients have common trace variance

\sigma_{g}^{2}
, and let
\bar{c}
denote the average off-diagonal trace covariance
among selected responses. Then

\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}g_{j}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{g}^{2}}{K}+\frac{K-1}{K}\bar{c}=\frac{\sigma_{g}^{2}-\bar{c}}{K}+\bar{c}.

If
\bar{c}=\rho_{g}\sigma_{g}^{2}
, this is equivalent to an effective sample size

K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{K}{1+(K-1)\rho_{g}}.

Thus diversity-aware selection can increase
K_{\mathrm{eff}}
at fixed
K
by
reducing average positive cross-covariance. When
C_{0}>0
, the variance saturates at an
irreducible floor rather than vanishing as
K\to\infty
.

Limitation.

Assumption
B.12
is not automatic. Generic sentence embeddings may fail to
capture task-relevant modes in domains such as mathematics or code, where two responses
can be lexically dissimilar but algorithmically identical, or lexically similar but
semantically different. In such domains, task-aware kernels based on execution traces,
AST structure, verifier outcomes, proof structure, or unit-test behavior may be more
appropriate. When the similarity model is unreliable, RKoN remains the assumption-light
default.

B.7.7
A stylized model for the size of the coverage gain

We close with a concrete model illustrating the variance reduction from mode coverage.
Let
P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
be a uniform mixture of
M
modes. In mode
m
,
suppose the gradient has the form

g_{m}=g_{0}+\alpha\mathbf{u}_{m}+\xi,\qquad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}I_{d}),

where
g_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\alpha>0
, and

\{\mathbf{u}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are orthonormal vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume

d\geq M
, and suppose
K
is divisible by
M
.

For
K
i.i.d. random responses,

\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})=\frac{d\sigma_{w}^{2}}{K}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{K}\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right).

The first term is within-mode noise, while the second is between-mode variance. Under
proportional stratification, with
K/M
samples per mode, the between-mode component
is fixed by construction and only within-mode noise remains:

\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}})=\frac{d\sigma_{w}^{2}}{K}.

Proposition B.13

(Variance reduction in the orthogonal-modes model)
.

In the model above,

\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{RKoN}})}{\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{g}_{\mathrm{strat}})}=1+\frac{\alpha^{2}(1-1/M)}{d\sigma_{w}^{2}}.

Thus the value of coverage-aware selection grows with the separation of gradient means
across modes.

Proof.

For a random mode
Z\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{1,\ldots,M\})
,

\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\alpha\mathbf{u}_{Z})=\alpha^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{u}_{Z}\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{u}_{Z}\right\|^{2}\right).

Since the
\mathbf{u}_{m}
’s are orthonormal,

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{u}_{Z}\|^{2}=1,\qquad\left\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{u}_{Z}\right\|^{2}=\left\|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{u}_{m}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{M}.

Hence the between-mode trace covariance is

\alpha^{2}(1-1/M)
. Averaging
K
independent random responses divides both the
within-mode trace
d\sigma_{w}^{2}
and the between-mode trace by
K
, giving the RKoN
expression. Under proportional stratification, each mode contributes exactly its
population weight, so the between-mode term is removed and only the averaged within-mode
noise
d\sigma_{w}^{2}/K
remains. Taking the ratio gives the claim.
∎

C.1
Controlled Simulation Details

The controlled validation in §
6.1
uses a continuous multi-modal regression problem in which
V_{\mathbf{x}}
,
V_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}}
, and selector-induced gradient bias are all directly measurable.

Data-generating process.

Prompts are sampled as
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{5})
. Conditional responses follow

y=\sin(2x_{1})+0.5x_{2}+\mathrm{offset}_{m}+\varepsilon,\qquad\mathrm{offset}_{m}\in\{-1.5,0,+1.5\},\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.01),

with
m\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,2,3\}
. The reward model is

R(\mathbf{x},y)=-(y-f(\mathbf{x}))^{2},\qquad f(\mathbf{x})=\sin(2x_{1})+0.5x_{2},

so reward favors the central mode while the outer modes remain valid under the target distribution. This creates the intended tension between reward optimization and distributional coverage.

Model and training.

We train a two-layer mixture-density network with
64
hidden units and
5
Gaussian components using Adam with learning rate
5\times 10^{-3}
,
400
epochs, and gradient clipping at
5
. Population NLL is estimated using fresh draws from
P(y\mid\mathbf{x})
. Unless otherwise stated, each prompt has
n=32
candidate responses.

Variance decomposition.

For Fig.
1
(a), we fix a randomly initialized model and estimate
V_{\mathbf{x}}
and

from
300

200

200

For Fig.

Metrics.

is the mean token-level cross-entropy on held-out dataset references, with prompt tokens masked and only the response span scored. This is our primary distributional metric: lower reference loss means better fit to the curated conditional response distribution represented by the benchmark.

16

is one minus the average pairwise cosine similarity among the model’s
16
generations.
Best-of-
16
reward
is the maximum Skywork-V2 reward among the same
16
generations. Reward is used as a target-shift diagnostic rather than as the primary success metric, because reward-based selectors directly optimize it. For CodeContests, we additionally execute generated programs against benchmark tests and report Pass@
K

D.1
Motivation and Positioning

Two empirical realities motivated us to assemble a new benchmark alongside the four established corpora used in our primary evaluation. First, almost every public “multi-response” resource is in fact a
single
-prompt, multi-completion dataset: it lets one stress-test response-side variability but offers no probe for
prompt-side
redundancy—the everyday situation in which users ask the same thing four different ways and a robust model should respond consistently well to all four. Second, the reference completions in widely-used corpora (notably the “gold” completions in
[
9
]
) were produced by small open-source language models (e.g.,
Pythia
variants); using such completions as ground truth implicitly upper-bounds an evaluation by the capability of a several-orders-of-magnitude-weaker generator than today’s frontier systems.

We therefore release
MOSAIC-1K
(
Multi-Output Structured Alternatives In Context
) as a structured prompt-paraphrase
\times
response-sample benchmark, generated end-to-end with a frontier model. We position it as both (i) a complementary evaluation surface for the present paper and (ii) a stand-alone resource for the broader community working on diversity-aware fine-tuning, reward-model evaluation, prompt-robustness studies, and preference-pair construction.

D.2
Schema and Construction

Structure.

Each of the
1{,}000

MOSAIC-1K
items is a
4{\times}4
grid built around a single
base theme
. The item provides:

•

Four prompt variants

\{x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(4)}\}
: semantically equivalent but stylistically and structurally distinct reformulations of the base theme—not surface paraphrases. Variants differ in framing (imperative vs. declarative), specificity (general request vs. named-function spec), and rhetorical posture (constraints first vs. context first).

•

Four sample responses per variant

\{y_{1}^{(j)},\ldots,y_{4}^{(j)}\}
: four substantively different completions for each
x^{(j)}
. Within a cell, responses take genuinely different approaches—distinct algorithms, distinct narrative choices, distinct advisory frameworks—rather than re-wordings of one answer.

A single item therefore contributes
4\times 4=16
prompt-response pairs, and the full benchmark contributes
16{,}000
such pairs.

Domains and difficulty.

Items are evenly partitioned across four domains,
250
items each:

code
(algorithmic implementation, data structures, debugging),

reasoning
(logical puzzles, mathematical proofs, analytic deduction),

creative
(flash fiction, poetry, dialogue, world-building), and

advisory
(health, career, interpersonal, financial guidance).
Every item carries explicit metadata:
subdomain
,
difficulty

\in\{
introductory, intermediate, advanced
\}
,
response_type

\in\{
procedural, analytical, constrained-creative, open-ended
\}
, and
expected_diversity
(kept as “high”).

Generation protocol.

The dataset was generated by Claude Opus 4.7 dispatched in multiple parallel batches via a tool-augmented orchestrator, with each batch responsible for a contiguous slice of
\{
domain
\times
subdomain
\times
difficulty
\}
tuples and required to emit JSONL records conforming to a strict schema. Generation was followed by a programmatic validator enforcing: exactly four prompt variants, exactly four responses per variant, all string-typed fields non-empty, response length within
[80,250]
words, and base-theme consistency across variants. Items failing any check were re-queued; the released corpus has
1000/1000
items passing all checks. A four-item random spot audit (one per domain) confirmed that prompt variants are non-trivial reformulations rather than surface paraphrases and that within-cell responses are substantively different rather than re-worded.

D.3
Intended Uses

MOSAIC-1K
is designed for any setting in which a researcher wants to disentangle
prompt-side
and
response-side
variability:

•

Prompt-redundancy robustness.
Hold
y
fixed across the four
x^{(j)}
’s and measure how a model’s likelihood, reward, or behavior moves with prompt rephrasing.

•

Multi-mode coverage and pass@
k
-style metrics.
Hold
x^{(j)}
fixed and use the four responses as a built-in “ground-truth diversity set” against which to score generated samples.

•

Reward-model variance estimation.
The
4{\times}4
grid yields a clean nested-ANOVA structure for decomposing reward-model output variance into prompt-paraphrase, response-sample, and residual components.

•

Preference-pair construction at scale.
Within-cell pairs
(y_{a}^{(j)},y_{b}^{(j)})
are natural same-prompt comparisons; cross-cell pairs
(y_{a}^{(j)},y_{a}^{(j^{\prime})})
are paraphrase-controlled comparisons.

•

Multi-response training research.
The structure mirrors exactly the regime our theory studies, involving multiple prompts, each with multiple responses.

reasoning_143
: Reasoning Prompt Axis

Base theme.
Explain how Goodhart’s Law manifests when a metric used for ranking becomes a target that agents optimize for.

Four prompt variants
—each requests a different rhetorical mode (mechanistic, advisory, illustrative, formal).

P1

Explain Goodhart’s Law in the context of a metric used to rank performers: how does the act of optimizing the metric degrade its informativeness?

P2

A manager proposes ranking employees by a single quantitative KPI. Walk them through how Goodhart’s Law will likely play out and what they should expect.

P3

Using a concrete example such as standardized test scores or web search ranking, illustrate how Goodhart’s Law transforms a useful measure into a target that loses correlation with the underlying goal.

P4

Formalize Goodhart’s Law: under what assumptions does optimizing a proxy metric guarantee divergence from the latent goal, and when can it be safe?

One response per variant
.

Response to
P1
—mechanistic. “Goodhart’s Law states that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Mechanistically, a metric is informative because it correlates with a latent quality of interest under the distribution of behaviors observed when the metric was not being optimized. Once agents are ranked by the metric, they shift their behavior toward the cheapest interventions that move the metric, which often differ from the interventions that move the underlying quality. The correlation that justified the metric was a property of the unoptimized regime, not a causal law, so optimization breaks it. Examples include teaching to the test, citation gaming, click-through manipulation, and surgeons declining risky cases to protect mortality rates. The deeper point is that metrics are compressions of multidimensional quality, and optimization exploits the compression, finding behaviors that score well without being good. Mitigations include using multiple metrics, randomizing audit dimensions, and treating metrics as diagnostics rather than incentives.”

Response to
P2
—advisory. “A single-KPI ranking is a textbook setup for Goodhart’s Law. Employees will rapidly identify the cheapest behaviors that move the KPI and reallocate effort toward them, away from valuable activities the KPI does not capture. Initial gains in the headline number are often real but shallow, after which the metric continues rising while the underlying goal stagnates or declines. Side effects include neglected teamwork, hostility toward colleagues whose work indirectly helps the KPI, and creative reinterpretation of definitions. Over time the manager faces a choice: tighten definitions, in which case employees find the next loophole; or broaden the scorecard, in which case the simplicity that made the KPI attractive is lost. The honest expectation is that any high-stakes single metric will be partially gamed, that the gaming will accelerate as employees share tactics, and that the metric’s signal-to-noise will degrade roughly in proportion to the stakes attached.”

Response to
P3
—illustrative. “Standardized testing is the canonical illustration. Test scores were initially designed to estimate broad academic ability and predict later achievement. Once high-stakes ranking attached to scores, schools reallocated curriculum toward tested skills, dropped untested subjects, drilled answer-elimination strategies, and in some cases manipulated who took the test. Scores rose nationally while independent measures of literacy, numeracy, and college readiness moved less or not at all. The metric was still a number on a page but it no longer measured what it had measured. The example shows the four-step pattern: a useful proxy, attachment of stakes, behavioral substitution toward the metric, and decoupling from the underlying construct. The remedy explored in education—audit testing, multiple measures, value-added models—each opens new gaming surfaces, illustrating that the law is not a single failure but a moving target.”

Response to
P4
—formal. “Let
G
be the latent goal and
M
the proxy metric. Under the unoptimized distribution
P_{0}
of behaviors,
M=f(G)+\varepsilon
, where
f
is monotone and
\varepsilon
is noise correlated with
G
in a way that depends on
P_{0}
. Optimization induces a new distribution
P^{\star}
concentrated on behaviors that maximize
M
. If
P^{\star}
lies within the support of
P_{0}
and
f
is monotone there, optimizing
M
still optimizes
G
. The divergence guarantee arises when
P^{\star}
exits the support of
P_{0}
, where
f
’s monotonicity is no longer guaranteed and
\varepsilon
may be systematically large. Formally, divergence is guaranteed under bounded support of
f
’s faithful regime, sufficient optimization pressure, and the existence of high-
M
behaviors outside the faithful regime. Safety obtains when optimization pressure is bounded below the threshold that pushes
P^{\star}
off-distribution, or when
M
is causally rather than correlationally tied to
G
across all behaviors agents can reach.”

The four responses inhabit four different registers (mechanistic explanation, managerial advice, concrete illustration, formal definition) while remaining mutually consistent on the underlying claim.

Deciding between streaming services: how to choose the right combination for your household.

Four prompt variants
—each requests a slightly different framework lens.

P1

How should a household decide which combination of streaming services to subscribe to given the overwhelming number of options?

What practical framework helps families evaluate and select the right mix of streaming platforms without overspending?

One response per variant
(full text).

Response to
P1
—
audit-first.
“Audit your current viewing habits before adding or keeping any service. Check watch history on each platform to see what you actually watched in the past three months. If a service provided fewer than four hours of content your household genuinely enjoyed monthly, it is a candidate for cancellation. Many households pay for five or six services while actively using only two or three, wasting thirty to sixty dollars monthly on inertia.”

Response to
P2
—
rotation strategy.
“Use a rotation strategy instead of maintaining all subscriptions simultaneously. Subscribe to one or two services at a time, binge the content you want over one to two months, then cancel and switch to another platform. Most services allow easy cancellation and resubscription with no penalty. This approach lets a household access every major platform’s best content annually while paying for only two to three services at any given time.”

Response to
P3
—
cost per actual hour.
“Calculate cost per hour of actual entertainment. If you pay fifteen dollars monthly for a service and watch twenty hours, your cost is seventy-five cents per hour, excellent value. If another service costs ten dollars but you watch only two hours monthly, that is five dollars per hour and probably not worth keeping. This metric cuts through marketing and reveals which services genuinely earn their place in your budget through actual usage rather than theoretical access.”

Response to
P4
—
paradox of choice.
“Challenge the assumption that more services equal better entertainment. Subscription fatigue is real: with too many options, households spend more time scrolling through catalogs than watching content. Psychologically, three services provide an optimal selection window. Beyond that, the paradox of choice kicks in and satisfaction per platform drops. Constraining options can paradoxically increase enjoyment by reducing decision overhead.”

Each response selects a substantively different decision framework—behavioral audit, time-shifting, unit economics, choice psychology—rather than rephrasing a single recommendation list.

Strategies for gradually reducing screen time before bed to improve sleep onset.

Prompt variant
P1

“I spend hours on my phone and laptop right up until I try to sleep, and it takes me forever to fall asleep. How can I gradually cut back on screen time before bed?”

Four sample responses for
P1
—four orthogonal behavioral mechanisms.

R1
—
phased curfew.
“Implement a phased screen curfew that becomes stricter over three weeks. Week one, stop all screens
15
minutes before your target bedtime. Week two, extend the buffer to
30
minutes. Week three, aim for
60
minutes. Use a phone alarm to signal the start of your screen-free window. During the curfew, replace screen time with a low-stimulation activity: reading a physical book, light stretching, journaling, or preparing items for the next day. The gradual ramp prevents the shock of suddenly losing your primary evening activity and allows you to discover which offline alternatives you actually enjoy before the window gets large.”

R2
—
content gradient.
“Shift the content you consume rather than eliminating screens all at once. In the first phase, switch from stimulating content—social media, news, video games, work email—to passive, low-arousal content like calm nature documentaries or ambient music playlists. In the second phase, move from backlit screens to e-ink devices like a Kindle, which emit no blue light and don’t suppress melatonin. In the final phase, transition to fully offline activities. This gradient approach acknowledges that screen content varies enormously in its sleep-disrupting potential, and a calm e-reader is categorically different from doomscrolling Twitter at midnight.”

R3
—
environmental design.
“Use environmental design to make screens less accessible in the evening. At a set time each night, physically relocate your phone to a charging station in another room. Switch your laptop to a designated shelf. Replace the phone on your nightstand with an analog alarm clock. Remove social media apps from your phone’s home screen so accessing them requires deliberate effort. The friction you add to screen access matters enormously—research on choice architecture shows that even small barriers dramatically reduce default behaviors. You’re not relying on willpower; you’re making the undesired behavior structurally harder to perform.”

R4
—
competing ritual.
“Create a competing bedtime ritual that is genuinely more appealing than screens. This requires experimentation. Try a warm bath or shower
90
minutes before bed—the subsequent body temperature drop triggers drowsiness. Pair it with a specific herbal tea you only drink at night, creating a sensory anchor for wind-down mode. Add five minutes of guided breathing or a body scan meditation using a non-screen audio player. The ritual needs to feel like a reward, not a punishment. If your screen-free routine feels like deprivation, you’ll revert within days. Design it around pleasure, comfort, and sensory richness so it competes with screens on their own terms.”

The four responses target four different behavioral-change levers (time-budget tapering, content substitution, choice-architecture friction, reward-conditioning ritual)—a within-cell sample of substantive strategy diversity.
