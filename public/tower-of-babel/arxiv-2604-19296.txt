Debiased neural operators for estimating functionals
Konstantin Hess, Dennis Frauen, Niki Kilbertus, Stefan Feuerriegel, [0.8em] LMU Munich

Abstract
Neural operators are widely used to approximate solution maps of complex physical systems. In many applications, however, the goal is not to recover the full solution trajectory, but to summarize the solution trajectory via a scalar target quantity (e.g., a functional such as time spent in a target range, time above a threshold, accumulated cost, or total energy). In this paper, we introduce DOPE ( debiased neural operator ): a semiparametric estimator for such target quantities of solution trajectories obtained from neural operators. DOPE is broadly applicable to settings with both partial and irregular observations and can be combined with arbitrary neural operator architectures. We make three main contributions. (1) We show that, in contrast to DOPE , naïve plug-in estimation can suffer from first-order bias. (2) To address this, we derive a novel one-step, Neyman-orthogonal estimator that treats the neural operator as a high-dimensional nuisance mapping between function spaces, and removes the leading bias term. For this, DOPE uses a weighting mechanism that simultaneously accounts for irregular observation designs and for how sensitive the target quantity is to perturbations of the underlying trajectory. (3) To learn the weights, we extend automatic debiased machine learning to operator-valued nuisances via Riesz regression. We demonstrate the benefits of DOPE across various numerical experiments.

Related Work

\bullet

Plug-in bias:

In many statistical problems, estimating the target parameter of interest requires first estimating nuisance functions (i.e., auxiliary functions that are not themselves of direct interest). Orthogonal estimation
19
,
44
constructs estimators so that small errors in these nuisance functions affect the target only at second order. A common issue is that errors in these nuisance estimates propagate into the final estimate, often leading to systematic bias—commonly referred to as
plug-in bias

14
,
30
.

Orthogonal estimation
19
,
44
addresses the problem of plug-in bias by constructing estimators that are insensitive to small errors in the nuisance functions. Intuitively, this means that approximation errors do not propagate as first-order biases, but only at second order in the target quantity. In semiparametric theory, this is typically achieved by deriving influence-function-based moment conditions
26
,
28
. To remove the first-order bias, one then aims to derive a one-step estimator
30
, which “corrects” (i.e., debiases) the plug-in estimate with the sample average of a debiasing score, and thereby removes the first-order bias term.

However, the problem of plug-in bias has been studied in several settings, such as for causal machine learning
14
,
30
, but
not
for neural operators. Importantly, existing works typically focus on settings that have
function-valued
nuisances
[
23
,
24
,
42
, e.g.,]
.
\Rightarrow

In contrast, our setting involves
operator-valued
nuisances, which introduces new challenges and requires tailored estimation techniques.

\bullet

Automatic debiasing:

Orthogonal estimation has attractive second-order bias properties, but applying it often requires deriving the
debiasing score
analytically. A remedy is automatic debiasing, which aims to construct this correction directly from the target functional
7
,
41
.

This idea can be implemented via automatic debiased machine learning, which reduces debiasing to estimation of an auxiliary function through Riesz regression rather than requiring a closed-form derivation of the correction term
10
,
11
. Prior work studies this idea for
function-valued
nuisances using random forests and multi-layer perceptrons
12
, for regularized Riesz regression
9
, under covariate shift
13
, and for smooth functionals of M-estimands
53
.
\Rightarrow

In contrast, we extend automatic debiasing to
operator-valued
nuisance functions.

Problem Formulation

\bullet~
PDE setup:

We consider the standard setting of operator learning
35
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain.
3
3

3

We make no formal distinction between time and spatial domain, and w.l.o.g.
\Omega
can refer to both.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote a Banach space of admissible input functions (e.g., coefficient functions, boundary conditions, or forcing terms).
For each admissible coefficient function
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we consider a PDE of the generic form

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{a}u=f,

(1)

with homogeneous boundary conditions. We assume that, for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the PDE admits a unique weak solution

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{a}^{-1}f=u\in\mathcal{U},

(2)

where, throughout, we take
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, which is the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on
\Omega
with inner product induced by Lebesgue measure
\mu
. We denote by

\displaystyle S_{0}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{U},\qquad S_{0}(a)=u(a,\cdot),

(3)

the
unknown solution operator
, which maps input functions to solution trajectories.

\bullet~
Neural operators:
Neural operators are learned approximations of the solution operator
S_{0}

34
,
39
,
40
. Given training data consisting of input functions and partial solution observations, neural operators learn a mapping

\hat{S}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{U},

such that
\hat{S}(a)\approx S_{0}(a)
. They operate directly on functional inputs and outputs defined on the continuous domain
\Omega
, which can be evaluated at arbitrary locations
x\in\Omega
. We provide an extended related work on neural operators in Supplement
A
.

\bullet~
Partial and irregular measurements:

In many scientific workflows,
a central challenge
is that the evaluation locations depend on the input through measurement protocols, adaptive solvers, or experimental constraints
4
,
59
. Hence, solution trajectories are often observed only
partially and irregularly

8
,
22
,
25
,
27
.
Accordingly, let
A
denote a random coefficient function with associated solution trajectory
S_{0}(A)\in\mathcal{U}
. Rather than observing the full trajectory, we observe noisy point evaluations

\displaystyle Y_{k}=S_{0}(A)(X_{k})+\varepsilon_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K,

(4)

where
K\in\mathbb{N}
may be random and depend on
A
, where
X_{k}\in\Omega
is an evaluation location, and where the noise satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}\mid A,X_{k}]=0

and

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\mid A,X_{k}]<\infty
.
We model the sampling mechanism through a joint law
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathcal{A}\times\Omega
with factorization

\mathbb{P}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)=\mathbb{P}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mid a)\,\mathbb{P}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a)
,
where
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid a)
is an input-dependent design distribution on
\Omega
. Given
(A,K)
, we assume that the pairs
\{(X_{k},Y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
are conditionally i.i.d. draws from a generic pair
(X,Y)
with

X\mid A=a\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid a)
.
We observe
n
i.i.d. realizations

\displaystyle O_{i}=\Big(A_{i},K_{i},\{(X_{ik},Y_{ik})\}_{k=1}^{K_{i}}\Big),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

(5)

and write

\mathcal{D}=\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Throughout, all
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
norms are taken with respect to the joint law of
(A,X)
. This setting captures the common regime where generating new inputs is relatively cheap, while dense evaluations of the corresponding solution trajectory are costly
43
,
31
.

\bullet

Identifiability:

In order to ensure identifiability from observations sampled under
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}

22

Assumption 3.1

(Design overlap)
.

Let
\mu

\Omega

\displaystyle\xi_{0}(a)(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid a)}(x).

(6)

Then, for
\mathbb{P}

\mu

\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid a)

\displaystyle 0<\xi_{0}(a)(x)\leq C\qquad\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid a)\text{-a.e. }x\in\Omega.

(7)

Finally, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
a\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{a}\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid a))

(8)

\langle f,h\rangle_{a}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}f(x)h(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid a)

Under Assumption
3.1
,
\mathcal{H}_{a}
embeds continuously into

\bullet~
Objective:

Let

\displaystyle g(u+h)=g(u)+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)+R(u,h)

with
|R(u,h)|\lesssim\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}
for all

.

4

\displaystyle\theta\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\!\left[g(S_{0}(A))\right],

4.1
Why naïve plug-in estimation is problematic

A naïve estimation strategy for Eq.
10
is to estimate
S_{0}(A)
with a neural operator
\hat{S}(A)
and treat the surrogate as the ground-truth operator. However, such plug-in estimation leads to severe plug-in bias:

Proposition 4.1

(First-order bias of plug-in estimators)
.

Let
\Delta(\cdot)=\hat{S}(\cdot)-S_{0}(\cdot)
.
Then

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{S}(A))]-\theta\big|\;\lesssim\;\big|\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right]\big|\;+\;\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2},

(12)

i.e., the neural operator error propagates linearly through the Fréchet differential, and induces
first-order bias
.

Proof.

See Supplement
B.1
.
∎

Proposition
4.1
shows that the estimation error of the plug-in estimator is, in general, linear in
\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))]
and thus does not vanish. As a result, root-
n
inference from the plug-in estimator requires this first-order bias to be negligible, i.e., of order
o(n^{-1/2})
. To understand what this implies, suppose the Fréchet derivative admits a uniformly bounded Riesz representer, so that

|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))|\lesssim\|\Delta(A)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
. Then, by overlap, this implies that the linear term can be controlled by

\big|\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))]\big|\lesssim\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}.

Hence, a sufficient condition for root-
n
inference is that the neural operator error satisfies

\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}=o(n^{-1/2}).

However, such rates are typically
highly unrealistic
in practice.
Intuitively, the problem of the plug-in estimator is that nonlinear functionals propagate operator errors
linearly
through the Fréchet derivative, so even small approximation errors accumulate.

4.2
Debiased estimation via
DOPE

As a remedy, we now derive a
one-step Neyman-orthogonal estimator
(
DOPE
).
In contrast to the naïve plug-in estimator from Proposition
4.1
,
DOPE
achieves root-
n
convergence under substantially weaker accuracy requirements on the neural operator.

The starting point is the leading bias term of the naïve plug-in estimator,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}\big(\Delta(A)\big)\right]
,
where
\Delta(\cdot)=S_{0}(\cdot)-\hat{S}(\cdot)
denotes the operator error.
To eliminate the plug-in bias, we seek a correction term that
recovers the value of the functional derivative

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))
using observable quantities.

Figure 2:
Overview of
DOPE
(our debiased neural operator).

However, this is
non-trivial
: the perturbation
\Delta(A)
is
not
observed as a function, but only through noisy point evaluations
Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})
at locations
X_{k}
drawn from the sampling design. Hence, we can
not
directly evaluate the functional derivative.

Instead, we leverage that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\cdot)
is a linear functional and can therefore be represented via a Riesz representer. That is, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
a\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a function
\beta_{0}(a)(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{a}
which is the unique Riesz representer satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\,h(X)\mid A=a\right]=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(a)}(h),\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{a}.

(13)

This representation is crucial: it expresses the functional derivative as an expectation
over pointwise evaluations under the sampling design
. Applying Eq.
13
with generic
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
h=\Delta(A)
shows that the weighted residual
\beta_{0}(A)(X)\big(Y-\hat{S}(A)(X)\big)
recovers the leading plug-in bias in expectation. This suggests the
debiasing score

\displaystyle\psi(O)=g(S_{0}(A))-\theta+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big),

(14)

which we formally derive in Lemma
B.2
. Intuitively, the weighted residual term reconstructs the first-order bias
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))
and thus provides the required correction.
In Supplement
B.2
, we show that the debiasing score
\psi(O)
is Neyman-orthogonal at the truth.
Motivated by this, we now define
DOPE
.

Definition 4.2

(
DOPE
)
.

Given i.i.d. observations
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, consider the debiasing score in Eq.
14
.
We replace the unknown quantities
(S_{0},\beta_{0})
by estimators
(\hat{S},\hat{\beta})
. We further define the pseudo-outcome

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{i}=g(\hat{S}(A_{i}))+\frac{1}{K_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}\hat{\beta}(A_{i})(X_{ik})\big(Y_{ik}-\hat{S}(A_{i})(X_{ik})\big).

(15)

In
DOPE
, the
one-step estimator
of the target functional in Eq.
10
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\psi}_{i}.

(16)

4.3
Theoretical properties

The key property of
DOPE
is
Neyman-orthogonality
: first-order errors
in the neural operator
\hat{S}
and in the correction term
\hat{\beta}
do
not
affect the leading asymptotics. As a result, nuisance errors enter the remainder only at
second-order
. We show this formally in Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem 4.3

(Second-order remainder)
.

Let
\hat{\theta}
be the
DOPE
estimate in Eq.
16
. Let
\hat{S}
and
\hat{\beta}
be estimated via cross-fitting. Then, we have

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]-\theta\big|\;\lesssim\;\|\hat{S}-S_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}+\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}.

(17)

In particular, there is
no first-order bias
from either nuisance component.

Proof.

See Supplement
B.2
.
∎

As a result, by construction,
DOPE

removes the linear bias term
and leaves only a second-order remainder. This is
in contrast
to the plug-in estimator, for which the bias is generally linear in

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right]
(cf. Proposition
4.1
).
Consequently, root-
n
inference for
DOPE
is possible under
substantially weaker nuisance rates
. For example, it suffices that

\displaystyle\|\hat{S}-S_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})\quad\text{and}\quad\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(18)

which corresponds to
n^{-1/4}
-type rates in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
root-mean-squared error.

Remark 4.4

(Input-specific targets via second-stage regression)
.

Our main focus is the population-average estimand
\theta=\mathbb{E}[g(S_{0}(A))]
. In some applications, however, the object of interest is the input-specific functional

\displaystyle g(S_{0}(a))=\mathbb{E}[g(S_{0}(A))\mid A=a].

(19)

In this case, our debiased pseudo-outcomes
\tilde{\psi}_{i}
may be used in a second-stage regression on
A_{i}
to estimate the conditional map
a\mapsto g(S_{0}(a))
. This type of second-stage regression maintains Neyman-orthogonality w.r.t.
\hat{S}
and
\hat{\beta}
.

Remark 4.5

(Asymptotic normality)
.

Under additional regularity conditions controlling the discrepancy between the estimated pseudo-outcomes and the oracle debiasing score, one can further establish asymptotic normality of
DOPE
. We state a formal result in Supplement
B.2
, and provide experimental results for empirical coverage in Supplement
C
.

Remark 4.6

(PPI with neural operators)
.

Our framework easily integrates prediction-powered inference (PPI)
1
,
2
when additional unlabeled inputs are available. Unlike standard PPI, which uses only
whether
a label is observed, our setting also exploits
where
labels are observed through the location-dependent correction term. Thus, with
n_{1}
partially observed and
n_{2}
fully unlabeled inputs, a PPI-enhanced version of
DOPE
is

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PPI}}=\frac{1}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}g\!\big(\hat{S}(A_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{1}{K_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}\hat{\beta}(A_{i})(X_{ik})\big(Y_{ik}-\hat{S}(A_{i})(X_{ik})\big).

(20)

This preserves the orthogonal correction while allowing large-scale unlabeled input data to improve efficiency through the plug-in component.

4.4
Automatic DML with
DOPE

We now turn to the practical question of how to estimate the debiasing weight
\beta_{0}
from data. The key insight is that
\beta_{0}
admits a structured decomposition that separates properties of the functional from the sampling design.

Proposition 4.7

(Decomposition of the debiasing weight)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, and let
w_{g}(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
denote the Riesz representer of
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}
with respect to
\mu
. Then, the unique solution
\beta_{0}(A)(x)
satisfying Eq.
13
admits,
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
-a.e., the decomposition

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(A)(x)=\xi_{0}(A)(x)\,w_{g}(S_{0}(A))(x).

(21)

Proof.

See Supplement
B.3
.
∎

This proposition shows that the debiasing weight
\beta_{0}
factorizes into two components: (i) a
functional sensitivity term

w_{g}
, which captures how the target functional reacts to local perturbations of the trajectory, and (ii) a
design correction

\xi_{0}
, which accounts for irregular and input-dependent sampling. Hence, a naïve way to estimate
\beta_{0}
would be to try to derive
w_{g}
analytically, and to obtain
\xi_{0}
by inverting an estimated sampling design
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
. However, this requires an inversion of the estimated sampling design, which can be
highly unstable
due to division close to
0
.

As a remedy, we show how to estimate the correction term
\beta_{0}
in the debiasing score
without
explicitly estimating the sampling distribution
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
and performing density inversion. Further, we do not require an analytical derivation of
w_{g}
. Instead, our approach directly targets the Riesz representer via a
primal variational characterization
, and thus extends automatic DML to operator-valued nuisances and autodiff-based moments.

Recall from Eq.
13
that the correction term
\beta_{0}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\,h(X)\mid A=a]=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(a)}(h),\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{a}.

(22)

Rather than enforcing this equality over a class of test functions
h
, we exploit the variational characterization of the Riesz representer. For each
A
, we define the linear functional

\mathcal{L}_{A}(h)\coloneqq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h)
.
Then,
\beta_{0}(A)(\cdot)
is the unique minimizer of the quadratic functional

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(A)(\cdot)=\arg\min_{\beta(A)(\cdot)}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\beta(A)(X)^{2}\mid A]-2\,\mathcal{L}_{A}\big(\beta(A)(\cdot)\big)\right\}.

(23)

Averaging over
A
yields the population risk

\displaystyle\beta_{0}=\arg\min_{\beta}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[\beta(A)(X)^{2}\mid A]-2\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}\big(\beta(A)(\cdot)\big)\right].

(24)

Theorem 4.8

(Primal characterization of the Riesz representer)
.

The unique minimizer of Eq.
24
is the Riesz representer
\beta_{0}
satisfying
Eq.
22
.

Proof.

See Supplement
B.3
.
∎

The objective of Eq.
24
directly targets the correction weight

\beta_{0}
required for bias cancellation, without introducing auxiliary test functions
h
or attempting to estimate the sampling density. In particular, the Riesz identity is enforced implicitly through the optimality conditions of Eq.
24
, which avoids the need for numerically unstable density inversion.

Remark 4.9

(Structured parameterization when
w_{g}
is known)
.

In some cases, the decomposition
\beta_{0}(A)(x)=\xi_{0}(A)(x)\,w_{g}(S_{0}(A))(x)
from Eq.
21
in Proposition
4.7
admits a closed-form expression for the Riesz representer
w_{g}(u)
of the functional derivative
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}
(see Supplement
H
).
In this case, one may restrict the parameterization of the debiasing weight to

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}(A)(x)=\hat{\xi}(A)(x)\,w_{g}(\hat{S}(A))(x),

(25)

which reduces the complexity of the estimation problem, and can lead to improved numerical stability and variance reduction.

Conclusion:

In this work, we propose

, a debiased estimator for averaged statistical functionals of neural operator outputs under partial and input-dependent observations. Our approach extends automated DML from function-valued to operator-valued nuisance estimation, and thereby enables bias correction and valid inference for nonlinear functionals of learned solution operators.

Appendix B
Proofs

B.1
Plug-in bias

Proposition B.1

(First-order bias of plug-in estimators)
.

Let
\Delta(\cdot)=\hat{S}(\cdot)-S_{0}(\cdot)
.
Then

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{S}(A))]-\theta\big|\;\lesssim\;\big|\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right]\big|\;+\;\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2},

(26)

i.e., the neural operator error propagates linearly through the Fréchet differential and induces first-order bias.

Proof.

We apply the second-order expansion in Eq.
9
with
h=\Delta(A)
:

\displaystyle g(\hat{S}(A))=g(S_{0}(A)+\Delta(A))=g(S_{0}(A))+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))+R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A)).

(27)

Taking expectations and using
\theta=\mathbb{E}[g(S_{0}(A))]
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{S}(A))]-\theta=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right]+\mathbb{E}[R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A))].

(28)

In the Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, the remainder bound in Eq.
9
gives

\displaystyle|R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A))|\lesssim\|\Delta(A)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2},

(29)

which depends on
A
only.
In order to express this bound in the sampling geometry, we use Assumption
3.1
.
That is, since
\mu\ll\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
with Radon–Nikodym derivative

\xi_{0}(A)(\cdot)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)}\leq C
, we have, for each realization of
A
,

\displaystyle\|\Delta(A)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Delta(A)(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{\Omega}\Delta(A)(x)^{2}\,\xi_{0}(A)(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid A)

(30)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\Delta(A)(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid A)=C\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}\mid A].

(31)

Taking expectation over
A
and using the definition of the joint law

\mathbb{P}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)=\mathbb{P}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathbb{P}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mid a)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\Delta(A)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\leq C\,\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}]=C\,\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2},\qquad\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}].

(32)

Therefore, applying Jensen’s inequality and Eq.
32
, yields

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A))]\big|\leq\mathbb{E}\big[|R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A))|\big]\lesssim\mathbb{E}\|\Delta(A)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\lesssim\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}.

(33)

Finally, combining Eq.
28
and Eq.
33
and applying the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{S}(A))]-\theta\big|\;\lesssim\;\big|\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right]\big|+\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}.

(34)

Unless the linear term vanishes (which it need not for nonlinear
g
),
\sqrt{n}
-inference requires this bias to be
o(n^{-1/2})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right]=o(n^{-1/2}),

(35)

which is a first-order (linear) accuracy requirement on the operator error
\Delta(A)
.

∎

B.2
One-step estimator

Lemma B.2

(Debiasing identity for the score)
.

Let
\Delta
be any measurable perturbation such that
\Delta(A,\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}

almost surely and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}]<\infty.

(36)

If
\beta_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)h(X)\mid A]=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h),\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{A},

(37)

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\Delta(A)(X_{k})\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right].

(38)

Moreover, if
\hat{S}
is any estimator independent of the evaluation fold
(for instance, by cross-fitting), and
\Delta(A)=\hat{S}(A)-S_{0}(A)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right]=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right].

(39)

Hence, the weighted residual term in Eq.
14
cancels the linear plug-in
bias.

Proof.

For the first claim, conditional on
(A,K)
the pairs

\{(X_{k},Y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
are i.i.d., so applying exchangeability yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\Delta(A)(X_{k})\,\middle|\,A,K\right]=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid A].

(40)

Taking expectations and applying the Riesz identity from Eq.
37
with
h=\Delta(A)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\Delta(A)(X_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid A]\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\right],

(42)

which proves Eq.
38
.

For the second claim, write

\displaystyle Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})=\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big)-\Delta(A)(X_{k}),\qquad\Delta(A)=\hat{S}(A)-S_{0}(A).

(43)

Conditioning on
\hat{S}
treats
\Delta
as fixed, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right]-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\Delta(A)(X_{k})\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right].

(44)

For the first term, iterated expectations together with

\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\mid A,X_{k}]=0
give

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right]=0.

(45)

Applying Eq.
38
conditionally on
\hat{S}
to the
second term in Eq.
44
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right]=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\,\middle|\,\hat{S}\right],

(46)

which proves Eq.
39
.
∎

Proposition B.3

(Neyman orthogonality of the debiasing score)
.

Define the score map

\displaystyle m(O;\theta,S,\beta)\coloneqq g(S(A))-\theta+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S(A)(X_{k})\big).

(47)

Let
(\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0})
denote the truth, where

\displaystyle\theta_{0}=\mathbb{E}[g(S_{0}(A))]

(48)

and
\beta_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)h(X)\mid A]=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h)\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{A}.

(49)

Then, the score is Neyman-orthogonal at
(\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0})
in the sense that, for any admissible perturbations

h
and
b
such that
h(A,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
almost surely,
b\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, and
S_{0}+th
remains in the neighborhood from Eq.
9
for sufficiently small
t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0}+th,\beta_{0})\right]\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=0,

(50)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0}+tb)\right]\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=0.

(51)

Proof.

We first consider perturbations of
S
. By definition of the score map,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0}+th,\beta_{0})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[g(S_{0}(A)+th(A))]-\theta_{0}

(52)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})-th(A)(X_{k})\big)\right].

(53)

Differentiating at
t=0
using Fréchet differentiability of
g
gives

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0}+th,\beta_{0})]\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h(A))]-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})h(A)(X_{k})\right].

(54)

Conditional on
(A,K)
, the pairs
\{(X_{k},Y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
are i.i.d., so by exchangeability, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})h(A)(X_{k})\,\middle|\,A,K\right]=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)h(A)(X)\mid A].

(55)

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0}(A)(X_{k})h(A)(X_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)h(A)(X)\mid A]\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h(A))],

(57)

where the last step uses the Riesz identity from Eq.
49
with

h=h(A,\cdot)
.
Substituting into Eq.
54
proves Eq.
50
.

Next, consider perturbations of
\beta
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0}+tb)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0})]+t\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}b(A,X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big)\right].

(58)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[m(O;\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0}+tb)]\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}b(A,X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big)\right].

(59)

Using iterated expectations and the regression property

\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\mid A,X_{k}]=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[b(A,X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[b(A,X_{k})\,\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k})\mid A,X_{k}]\right]=0.

(60)

Averaging over
k
yields Eq.
51
.
Thus, the score is Neyman-orthogonal at
(\theta_{0},S_{0},\beta_{0})
.
∎

Theorem B.4

(Second-order remainder)
.

Let
\hat{\theta}
be the one-step estimator in Eq.
16
. If
\hat{S}
and
\hat{\beta}
are cross-fitted, then

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]-\theta\big|\;\lesssim\;\|\hat{S}-S_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}+\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}.

(61)

In particular, there is
no first-order bias
in either nuisance component.

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\psi}_{i},\qquad\tilde{\psi}=g(\hat{S}(A))+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\beta}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})\big),

(62)

where
(A,K,\{(X_{k},Y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K})
denotes a generic observation in the evaluation fold.

Step 0: Cross-fitting.

By cross-fitting,
(\hat{S},\hat{\beta})
are measurable with respect to the training sample

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
, and independent of the evaluation fold. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}-\theta]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\psi}-\theta\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\right],

(63)

and it therefore suffices to bound the conditional bias
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\psi}-\theta\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]
.

Step 1: Expansion of functional.

Let
\Delta(A)=\hat{S}(A)-S_{0}(A)
. By Eq.
9
, we have that

\displaystyle g(\hat{S}(A))=g(S_{0}(A))+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))+R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A)),\qquad|R(S_{0}(A),\Delta(A))|\lesssim\|\Delta(A)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

(64)

Hence, taking conditional expectation given
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{S}(A))-g(S_{0}(A))\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]+O\!\left(\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta(A)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\right).

(65)

Step 2: Expansion of the residual correction.

Next, we decompose

\displaystyle Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})=(Y_{k}-S_{0}(A)(X_{k}))-\Delta(A)(X_{k})=:\varepsilon_{k}-\Delta(A)(X_{k}).

(66)

Since
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}\mid A,X_{k}]=0
and
\hat{\beta}
is measurable w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
,
iterated expectations give

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(A)(X_{k})\varepsilon_{k}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=0

(67)

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\beta}(A)(X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}\right]=-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}],

(68)

where
X\mid A\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
denotes a generic draw from the sampling design.

With
\hat{\beta}=\beta_{0}+(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]-\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(70)

Step 3: First-order cancellation.

By the Riesz identity in Eq.
13
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid A]=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A)).

(71)

Hence, we take expectations conditionally on
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(72)

Substituting Eq.
72
into Eq.
70
and Eq.
68
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\beta}(A,X_{k})\big(Y_{k}-\hat{S}(A)(X_{k})\big)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})(A,X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(73)

Hence, the
\beta_{0}
-part of the correction term exactly cancels the first-order term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(\Delta(A))\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]

(74)

from the Taylor expansion in Eq.
65
, while the additional cross-term involving

(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})\Delta
remains and is controlled in the next step.

Step 4: Bounding the remaining second-order terms.

Combining Eq.
65
with Eq.
73
, after cancellation of the linear term, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\psi}-\theta\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=O\!\left(\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta(A)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\right)-\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})(A,X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(75)

For the cross-term, conditional Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\Big|\leq\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}})}\cdot\|\Delta(A)(X)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}})}.

(76)

Hence, using
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
yields

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})(A)(X)\Delta(A)(X)\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\Big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}})}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(77)

Finally, by Assumption
3.1
(i.e., bounded
\xi_{0}(A)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
) and
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta(A)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\Omega}\Delta(A)(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}\right]

(78)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\Omega}\Delta(A)(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid A)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}\right]=C\,\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(79)

Substituting Eq.
77
and Eq.
79
into Eq.
75
then gives

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\psi}-\theta\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\big|\;\lesssim\;\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}})}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(80)

Finally, taking expectation over
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
together with the tower property yields

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]-\theta\big|\;\lesssim\;\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Delta(A)(X)^{2}]=\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}+\|\hat{S}-S_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2},

(81)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition B.5

(Asymptotic normality)
.

If
\hat{S}
and
\hat{\beta}
are cross-fitted,
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}^{2}]<\infty
, and the nuisance estimators satisfy

\displaystyle\|\hat{S}-S_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),\qquad\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(82)

and, in addition, the estimated score is conditionally mean-square consistent:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\psi_{i})^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}\right]=o_{p}(1),

(83)

then

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}]\big).

(84)

Moreover, a consistent estimator of the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\theta}
is

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\hat{\theta})^{2}.

(85)

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\delta_{i}\coloneqq\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\psi_{i}.

(86)

Then

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}-\theta=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}.

(87)

Since
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}]=0
, we may further decompose

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\delta_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\Big)+\sqrt{n}\,\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(88)

Step 1: Bias term.

By definition,
\delta_{i}=\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\psi_{i}
, and since
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}]=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\theta\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(89)

Hence, by the second-order remainder result (Theorem
B.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=O_{p}\!\left(\|\hat{S}-S_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}+\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}\right).

(90)

Under Eq.
82
, this yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=o_{p}(1).

(91)

Step 2: Stochastic remainder.

Conditional on
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
, the variables

\{\delta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\delta_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\big)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}\right)=\mathbb{V}(\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}})\leq\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}].

(92)

By Eq.
83
, the right-hand side is
o_{p}(1)
, so conditional Chebyshev implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\delta_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]\big)=o_{p}(1).

(93)

Step 3: Central limit theorem (CLT).

Combining Eq.
88
, Eq.
91
, and
Eq.
93
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}+o_{p}(1).

(94)

Since
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d., mean zero, and have finite variance, the classical CLT gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}]\big).

(95)

Slutsky’s theorem then yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}]\big).

(96)

Step 4: Variance estimation.

We first show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\hat{\theta})^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}].

(97)

Write again
\tilde{\psi}_{i}=\psi_{i}+\delta_{i}
. Then, conditional on

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}
, the variables
\delta_{i}
are i.i.d., so by the weak law of large numbers and Eq.
83
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}]=o_{p}(1),

(98)

hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}=o_{p}(1).

(99)

Moreover, by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}\delta_{i}\right|\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=o_{p}(1),

(100)

since
n^{-1}\sum_{i}\psi_{i}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}^{2}]
and
Eq.
99
holds.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\psi}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}\delta_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}

(101)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{2}+o_{p}(1)\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}^{2}].

(102)

Also,
\hat{\theta}\xrightarrow{p}\theta
, so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\hat{\theta})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\psi}_{i}^{2}-\hat{\theta}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}^{2}]-\theta^{2}=\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}],

(103)

which proves Eq.
97
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\hat{\theta})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\hat{\theta})^{2}\right)

(104)

satisfies

\displaystyle n\,\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(\hat{\theta})\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}].

(105)

Therefore,
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(\hat{\theta})
is a consistent estimator of the asymptotic variance
\mathbb{V}[\psi_{i}]/n
of
\hat{\theta}
.
∎

Automatic DML for operator-valued nuisances

Proposition B.6

(Decomposition of the debiasing weight)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
and let
w_{g}(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
denote the Riesz
representer of
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}
with respect to
\mu
. Then the unique solution
\beta_{0}(A)(x)
satisfying Eq.
13
admits (
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)
-a.e.) the decomposition

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(A)(x)=\xi_{0}(A)(x)\,w_{g}(S_{0}(A))(x).

(106)

Proof.

By definition of the Riesz representer, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h)=\int_{\Omega}w_{g}(S_{0}(A))(x)\,h(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(107)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\beta_{0}(A)(X)\,h(X)\mid A\right]=\int_{\Omega}\beta_{0}(A)(x)\,h(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid A).

(108)

Equating the two expressions for all
h\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
implies

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(A)(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid A)=w_{g}(S_{0}(A))(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(109)

which yields the decomposition in Eq.
106
by Radon–Nikodym differentiation.
∎

Theorem B.7

(Primal characterization of the Riesz representer)
.

The unique minimizer of Eq.
24
is the Riesz representer
\beta_{0}
satisfying
Eq.
22
.

Proof.

We prove that the unique minimizer of the population risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\beta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[\beta(A)(X)^{2}\mid A]-2\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}\big(\beta(A)(\cdot)\big)\right]

(110)

is the Riesz representer
\beta_{0}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)h(X)\mid A]=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}(h),\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{A}.

(111)

Step 1: Conditional Hilbert space structure.

For each
A=a
, define the Hilbert space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{a}\coloneqq L^{2}(\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A=a))

(112)

with inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,h\rangle_{a}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[f(X)h(X)\mid A=a].

(113)

By Assumption
3.1
, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{a}
, we have

\displaystyle\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}h(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{\Omega}h(x)^{2}\,\xi_{0}(a)(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x\mid a)\leq C\,\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{a}}^{2}.

(114)

Hence,
\mathcal{H}_{a}
embeds continuously into
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
.
Therefore, the Fréchet derivative

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}(h)\coloneqq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(a)}(h)

(115)

defines a continuous linear functional on
\mathcal{H}_{a}
by restriction.

Step 2: Existence of the Riesz representer.

By the Riesz representation theorem, for each
a
, there exists a unique

\beta_{0}(a)(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{a}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}(h)=\langle\beta_{0}(a)(\cdot),h\rangle_{a}=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{0}(A)(X)h(X)\mid A=a],\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{a}.

(116)

This establishes the conditional Riesz identity on
\mathcal{H}_{a}
.

Step 3: Variational characterization.

We now use the standard variational characterization of the Riesz representer
in a Hilbert space
10
. For any Hilbert space
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)

and continuous linear functional
L(h)=\langle r,h\rangle
, the representer
r

satisfies

\displaystyle r=\arg\min_{b\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\|b\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-2L(b)\right\}.

(117)

Indeed, for any
b\in\mathcal{H}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|b\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-2L(b)=\|b\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-2\langle r,b\rangle=\|b-r\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\|r\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

(118)

which is uniquely minimized at
b=r
. This variational formulation is the basis of automatic Riesz regression in debiased machine learning
10
,
12
. Our construction extends this idea to a conditional, operator-valued setting.

Step 4: Conditional minimization.

Applying Eq.
117
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{a},\quad L=\mathcal{L}_{a},\quad r=\beta_{0}(a)(\cdot),

(119)

we obtain, for each fixed
a
,

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(a)(\cdot)=\arg\min_{\beta(a)(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{a}}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\beta(A)(X)^{2}\mid A=a]-2\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(a)}\big(\beta(a)(\cdot)\big)\right\}.

(120)

Step 5: Averaging over
A
.

Finally, observe that the population risk Eq.
110
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathcal{R}_{A}\big(\beta(A)(\cdot)\big)\Big],

(121)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{A}\big(\beta(A)(\cdot)\big)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\beta(A)(X)^{2}\mid A]-2\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{S_{0}(A)}\big(\beta(A)(\cdot)\big).

(122)

Since the objective separates across values of
A
, minimizing
\mathcal{R}(\beta)

is equivalent to minimizing
\mathcal{R}_{A}(\beta(A)(\cdot))
for almost every
A
.
By Eq.
120
, the unique minimizer is therefore

\beta_{0}(A)(\cdot)
.

This means that
\beta_{0}
is the unique minimizer of Eq.
110
, which completes the proof.

∎

\displaystyle u_{i}\coloneqq\big(\hat{S}(A_{i})(x_{\ell})\big)_{\ell=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\qquad b_{i}\coloneqq\big(\hat{\beta}(A_{i})(x_{\ell})\big)_{\ell=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

g

5
5

5

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\beta}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{K_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}\hat{\beta}(A_{i})(X_{ik})^{2}-2\,\mathrm{JVP}(g_{m},u_{i};b_{i})\right]+\lambda\|\hat{\beta}\|^{2},

G.1
Pharmacokinetic model

We consider a pharmacokinetics benchmark in which each subject is characterized by a function-valued input
A_{i}
consisting of a subject-specific dosing-rate profile together with subject-level clearance and volume parameters. The resulting latent concentration trajectory
u(A_{i},\cdot)
follows a standard one-compartment pharmacokinetic model
37
,
52
, where clearance governs elimination, volume determines how the administered drug is translated into concentration, and the time-varying dosing profile allows for heterogeneous infusion- or pulse-like regimens across subjects.

The corresponding scientific targets are not the full concentration trajectories themselves, but rather low-dimensional summaries such as exposure, peak burden, or time above a clinically relevant threshold.
We observe each trajectory only at sparse time points and vary the degree of input-dependent sampling through an irregularity parameter
\rho
, where
\rho=0
corresponds to approximately uniform monitoring and larger values of
\rho
increasingly concentrate observations near the subject-specific peak region.

standard for benchmarking neural operators

\bullet

Governing model:

We consider the elliptic Darcy problem on
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
,

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\bigl(a_{i}(x)\nabla u_{i}(x)\bigr)=1,\qquad x\in(0,1)^{2},

(155)

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

\displaystyle u_{i}(x)=0,\qquad x\in\partial(0,1)^{2}.

(156)

Thus, the forcing term is constant and equal to
1
, and all sample-specific heterogeneity enters through the random coefficient field
a_{i}
.

At the operator-learning level, each sample is represented by a coefficient field on a tensor-product grid with

\displaystyle x_{p}=\frac{p-1}{H-1},\qquad p=1,\dots,H,\qquad y_{q}=\frac{q-1}{W-1},\qquad q=1,\dots,W,

(157)

where
H=W=17
, and grid locations

\displaystyle X_{pq}=(x_{p},y_{q})\in[0,1]^{2}.

(158)

The coefficient field is sampled from the family

piecewise_constant_transformed_gaussian_field
. First, define a coarse
5\times 5
grid by

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{r}=\frac{r-1}{5-1},\qquad r=1,\dots,5,\qquad\bar{y}_{s}=\frac{s-1}{5-1},\qquad s=1,\dots,5.

(159)

For each pair of modes
(m_{x},m_{y})\in\{1,2,3\}^{2}
, we use the cosine basis

\displaystyle\phi_{m_{x},m_{y}}(\bar{x}_{r},\bar{y}_{s})=\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi m_{x}\bar{x}_{r})\,\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi m_{y}\bar{y}_{s}).

(160)

Independent Gaussian coefficients
\xi_{m_{x},m_{y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are drawn, and the latent coarse field is

\displaystyle Z_{rs}=\frac{0.65}{3}\sum_{m_{x}=1}^{3}\sum_{m_{y}=1}^{3}\xi_{m_{x},m_{y}}\,\phi_{m_{x},m_{y}}(\bar{x}_{r},\bar{y}_{s}).

(161)

The coarse coefficient field is then obtained by exponentiation and lower clipping,

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{rs}=\exp(Z_{rs}),\qquad\bar{a}_{rs}\leftarrow\max\{\bar{a}_{rs},0.2\}.

(162)

Finally, the coarse field is mapped to the
17\times 17
grid by nearest-cell replication in both spatial directions, yielding a piecewise-constant coefficient field on the fine grid.

\bullet

Implementation level:

The PDE is solved on the
17\times 17
grid by a finite-difference discretization of the divergence-form operator. Let

\displaystyle h_{x}=h_{y}=\frac{1}{17-1}.

(163)

On interior grid points, the code assembles the linear system corresponding to

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}(a\,\partial_{x}u)-\partial_{y}(a\,\partial_{y}u)=1.

(164)

To preserve flux continuity across coefficient jumps, harmonic means are used at cell interfaces. For example, at the west interface,

\displaystyle a_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}^{(h)}=\frac{2a_{ij}a_{i-1,j}}{a_{ij}+a_{i-1,j}},

(165)

with analogous formulas for east, north, and south interfaces.

The resulting interior system has dimensions

\displaystyle(17-2)\times(17-2)=225,

(166)

is assembled explicitly as a dense matrix, and is solved using
numpy.linalg.solve
. Boundary values are then set to zero to obtain the full
17\times 17
solution field.

Quadrature uses a normalized two-dimensional trapezoid rule. If
w^{(x)}
and
w^{(y)}
denote the one-dimensional trapezoid weights,

\displaystyle w^{(x)}_{1}=w^{(x)}_{H}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad w^{(x)}_{p}=1\ \text{for }p=2,\dots,H-1,

(167)

and analogously in the
y
-direction, then the unnormalized two-dimensional weights are

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{pq}=w^{(x)}_{p}w^{(y)}_{q}.

(168)

These are normalized to sum to one, i.e.,

\displaystyle w_{pq}=\frac{\widetilde{w}_{pq}}{\sum_{r=1}^{H}\sum_{s=1}^{W}\widetilde{w}_{rs}}.

(169)

The corresponding discrete inner product is

\displaystyle\langle f,h\rangle_{w}=\sum_{p=1}^{H}\sum_{q=1}^{W}w_{pq}f_{pq}h_{pq}.

(170)

\bullet

Sampling mechanism and irregularity parameter:

We observe each latent solution field only at a sparse set of spatial locations. In all reported experiments, each sample contributes

\displaystyle K=24

(171)

observations.

The observation design is
inverse_energy_mixture
. Let
a_{pq}
denote the coefficient field and
u_{pq}
the latent solution. The code first constructs the inverse-coefficient factor

\displaystyle a^{-1}_{pq}=\frac{1}{\max\{a_{pq},10^{-8}\}}.

(172)

It then computes a discrete gradient magnitude using centered finite differences,

\displaystyle G_{pq}=\sqrt{\bigl(u_{p+1,q}-u_{p-1,q}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(u_{p,q+1}-u_{p,q-1}\bigr)^{2}}.

(173)

The resulting saliency map is

\displaystyle S_{pq}=a^{-1}_{pq}\bigl(0.5+|u_{pq}|+G_{pq}\bigr).

(174)

After centering and normalizing this field by its maximum absolute magnitude, writing the normalized saliency as
\widetilde{S}
, the adaptive design distribution is

\displaystyle q_{pq}\propto\exp\!\bigl(\lambda|\widetilde{S}_{pq}|\bigr)+\varepsilon,\qquad\lambda=7.0,\qquad\varepsilon=10^{-3}.

(175)

Hence, the observation design concentrates entirely according to the adaptive saliency map.

Given
p_{pq}(\rho)
, we sample
K=24
distinct grid locations without replacement, sort the flattened indices, and observe

\displaystyle Y_{ik}=u_{i}(X_{J_{ik}})+\varepsilon_{ik},\qquad\varepsilon_{ik}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),\qquad\sigma_{\varepsilon}=0.01.

(176)

Appendix H
Examples of functional derivatives and their Riesz representers

In some cases, the functional derivatives
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)
and their Riesz representers
w_{g}(u)
can be computed analytically. In the following, we summarize the functionals used in our experiments (Section
5
), and provide closed-form solutions for the functional derivatives and their Riesz representers, which allows us to properly validate our results.

Note that these closed-form solutions are not always easily tractable but can involve complex derivations. Yet,
DOPE
is applicable even when these closed-form solutions are
not
available (see Section
4.2
and Section
4.4
). In the following, all integrals are w.r.t. Lebesgue-measure
\mu
.

\bullet

Normalized AUC:

The normalized area under the (concentration) curve is given by

\displaystyle g(u)=(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(179)

The corresponding Fréchet derivative in direction
h
is

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)=(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(180)

Then, its Riesz representer under
\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t/T
is

\displaystyle w(u)(t)=1.

(181)

For our experiments, this functional is especially interesting as it isolates the inverse-design component;
w_{g}
is constant, and therefore
\beta_{0}(\cdot)(\cdot)=\xi_{0}(\cdot)(\cdot)
.

\bullet

Time-above-threshold:

We define a smooth version of the time-above-threshold (TAT) as

\displaystyle g(u)=(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}\sigma(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(182)

where
c^{\star}
is a clinically meaningful concentration threshold,
\kappa>0
controls the sharpness of the smooth threshold, and
\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))
. This is a smooth version of the time-above-threshold / time-in-range functional, since

\displaystyle\sigma(\kappa x)\;\xrightarrow[\kappa\to\infty]{}\;\mathbf{1}\{x\geq 0\},

(183)

so, pointwise in
t
,

\displaystyle\sigma\big(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star})\big)\;\to\;\mathbf{1}\{u(t)\geq c^{\star}\}.

(184)

Therefore,

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{tat}}(u)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma\big(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star})\big)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\;\to\;\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{u(t)\geq c^{\star}\}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(185)

The corresponding Fréchet derivative in direction h is

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)=(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}\kappa\sigma(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star}))(1-\sigma(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star})))h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(186)

Its Riesz representer under
\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t/T
is

\displaystyle w_{g}(u)(t)=\kappa\sigma(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star}))(1-\sigma(\kappa(u(t)-c^{\star}))).

(187)

This functional is PK-motivated and gives a nontrivial, threshold-localized sensitivity profile.

\bullet

Soft cmax:

We define the soft cmax functional as

\displaystyle g(u)=(1/\lambda)\log\big[(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}\exp(\lambda u(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\big],

(188)

where the temperature parameter
\lambda>0
controls how sharply this approximates peak concentration.
This is a standard smooth proxy for peak concentration “cmax” (continuous maximum functional)

\displaystyle\text{cmax}(u)\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}u(t),

(189)

since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\lambda u(t)}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)\;\longrightarrow\;\sup_{t\in[0,T]}u(t)\quad\text{as }\lambda\to+\infty.

(190)

Its Fréchet derivative in direction
h
is

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)=(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}[\exp(\lambda u(t))/((1/T)\int_{0}^{T}\exp(\lambda u(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)]h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(191)

Equivalently, under
\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t/T
,

\displaystyle D_{g}u(h)=\int_{0}^{T}w_{g}(u)(t)h(t)\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t),

(192)

with Riesz representer

\displaystyle w_{g}(u)(t)=\exp(\lambda u(t))/[(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}\exp(\lambda u(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s].

(193)

This functional is peak-sensitive, nonlinear, and temporally varying, while still having a closed-form derivative.

\bullet

Smooth excess-above-threshold:

We define the smooth excess-above-threshold functional as

\displaystyle g(u)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\kappa}\log\!\bigl(1+\exp(\kappa^{\star}(u(x)-c))\bigr)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(194)

where the sharpness parameter
\kappa^{\star}>0
controls how rapidly the functional transitions around the threshold
c
.
This is a standard smooth proxy for the excess-above-threshold functional

\displaystyle\mathrm{excess}_{c}(u)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-c)_{+}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(195)

since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa^{\star}}\log\!\bigl(1+\exp(\kappa^{\star}(u(x)-c))\bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;(u(x)-c)_{+}\quad\text{as }\kappa^{\star}\to+\infty

(196)

pointwise in
x
, and hence

\displaystyle g(u)\;\longrightarrow\;\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-c)_{+}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\quad\text{as }\kappa^{\star}\to+\infty.

(197)

Its Fréchet derivative in direction
h
is

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)=\int_{\Omega}\sigma\!\bigl(\kappa^{\star}(u(x)-c)\bigr)\,h(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\qquad\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.

(198)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}g_{u}(h)=\int_{\Omega}w_{g}(u)(x)\,h(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(199)

with Riesz representer

\displaystyle w_{g}(u)(x)=\sigma\!\bigl(\kappa^{\star}(u(x)-c)\bigr).

(200)

This functional is nonlinear and threshold-sensitive, while still admitting a closed-form derivative and a simple Riesz representer. Note that in our experiments, we use a linear transformation
\kappa^{*}\coloneqq f(\kappa)
(see Supplement
G.2
).
