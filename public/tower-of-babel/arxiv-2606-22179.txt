The Score Granularity Gap in Black-Box LLM Classification: A Comparative Study of Confidence Constructions
Ao Sun, Tian Sun, Jiaxing Geng

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as black-box classifiers in pipelines that automate confident decisions and route uncertain ones to human review. Such selective prediction needs a confidence score that an operator can threshold at a chosen risk level. Prior work asks whether LLM confidence is well calibrated or well ranked; we ask a complementary, deployment-oriented question that has been largely overlooked: at what resolution can the score be thresholded? We call the answer the score granularity gap . Through a controlled comparison of seven ways to build a confidence score—from a single verbalized number, to token probabilities, to querying the model many times and combining the answers—across 25 model–dataset pairs (9 LLMs, 3 benchmarks), we find that single-shot verbalized confidence, once correctly converted to a class probability, ranks cases surprisingly well, yet takes only a handful of distinct values. It therefore offers an operator only a few coarse thresholds, no matter how well it ranks. We show which constructions widen this gap, at what inference cost, and with what effect on ranking—notably that multi-query aggregation helps weak models but can degrade already-strong ones. We translate these trade-offs into concrete deployment guidance.



1
Introduction

Large language models are increasingly used as black-box classifiers across high-stakes domains, from medical triage to content moderation. When such a system runs without per-call access to model internals, practical deployment hinges on one capability: the model must attach to each prediction a
confidence score
that an operator can compare against a threshold, automating the cases above the threshold and escalating the rest to a human reviewer. This is the classical
selective prediction
setting, and its central control is the threshold: moving it trades
coverage
(the fraction of cases handled automatically) against
risk
(the error rate among them).

A long line of work asks whether LLM confidence is trustworthy in this role—whether it is
calibrated
(does a stated
80\%
mean
80\%
correct?) and whether it
ranks
cases correctly—and recurrently finds verbalized confidence overconfident. We argue that a prior, largely overlooked question matters just as much: not
whether
the score is reliable, but at what
resolution
it can be acted upon. A score may order cases almost perfectly yet be useless for fine-grained control if it takes only a few distinct values—an operator who wants the most confident
70\%
has no threshold that selects exactly
70\%
. The reachable operating points form a coarse staircase, not a smooth dial.

We call this the
score granularity gap
, and a deployment-oriented study of LLM confidence must measure it directly. Doing so requires looking at a confidence score along three distinct axes, which we state here in plain terms and formalize in Section
3
: (i)
ranking quality
—does the score put correct predictions above incorrect ones? (ii)
score resolution
—how many distinct, well-spread thresholds does the score actually offer? and (iii)
cost and interpretability
—how many model calls does the score cost, and can a human read why it was assigned? A score is deployment-ready only when it scores well on the axes the deployment cares about; no single axis suffices.

Against these axes we compare the natural ways to build such a score—asking the model directly, reading its token probabilities, or querying it several times (with paraphrases or interpretable sub-questions) and combining the answers. Our central finding: direct verbalized confidence, once converted to a class probability, ranks cases surprisingly well but is far too coarse for threshold control; token probabilities with calibration widen the resolution cheaply where available; and multi-query aggregation widens it most, at several calls per input and—counter-intuitively—sometimes
degrading
already-strong models.

Contributions.

1.

We identify and operationalize the
score granularity gap
—why a well-ranking LLM confidence score can still be unusable for threshold-based deployment—with metrics for both how many distinct values a score takes and how they are distributed (Section

2.

We present a

across 25 model–dataset pairs, isolating each ingredient—conversion, token probabilities, calibration, multiple queries, and learned vs. naive combination (Sections

3.

We show that the popular “LLM confidence is unreliable” narrative is partly an artifact of measurement (conflating confidence-in-the-answer with class probability) and of resolution, and that

—a trade-off we make explicit rather than averaging away (Section

).

4.

We give

, including an inference-cost accounting and the case for interpretable sub-task scores as a deployment property, not a ranking advantage (Sections
5.5
and
6
).

3
Problem Setup

We scope the study to
binary
classification, the simplest setting in which the granularity gap appears: a single scalar score suffices and a threshold reduces to one operating point. Constraining every signal to the shared interval
[0,1]
also makes learned combination and threshold selection well defined; open-ended generation provides no such common coordinate system. We return to this choice in Section
7
.

3.1
Confidence and Its Conversion to a Class Probability

For an input
x
, a black-box LLM returns a predicted label
\hat{y}\in\{0,1\}
and a
verbalized confidence

c\in[0,1]
. Crucially,
c
is the model’s confidence
in the label it chose
, not the probability of the positive class. These two quantities differ whenever the model predicts the negative class: a confident “no” (
\hat{y}{=}0,\,c{=}0.95
) and a hesitant “yes” (
\hat{y}{=}1,\,c{=}0.55
) both report high-versus-low confidence, but as
class
probabilities the first is low and the second is moderate. Threshold-based deployment ranks by class probability, so we first convert

s\;=\;P(\mathrm{pos})\;=\;\begin{cases}c&\text{if }\hat{y}=1,\\[2.0pt]
1-c&\text{if }\hat{y}=0,\end{cases}

(1)

and call the converted single-shot score
Verb
. Without this step, a confident negative outscores a less confident positive and the ranking is distorted. In our data
c\geq 0.5
for over
97\%
of examples, so the map is well behaved. Prior work often evaluates confidence in the chosen answer
[
20
,
14
]
—appropriate for answer-level uncertainty, but overstating “unreliability” when a class probability is what the task needs.

3.2
Thresholded Deployment: Operating Points and Deployment Properties

Given scores
s
on a test set and a threshold
\tau
, the system
accepts
(predicts automatically) every case with
s
on the confident side of
\tau
and
abstains
on the rest. Sweeping
\tau
traces the
risk–coverage curve
: each
\tau
yields a
coverage
(fraction accepted) and a
risk
(error rate among accepted). An
operating point
is one achievable (coverage, risk) pair. Operators usually fix an
error budget
—a maximum tolerable risk, e.g.
5\%
—and want the highest coverage achievable within it; we report this as
\mathrm{cov}@\tau
(coverage at error rate
\leq\tau
). We summarize the whole curve by the
AURC
(area under the risk–coverage curve; lower is better) and the ranking itself by
PR-AUC
.

We use the term
deployment property
for any attribute of a confidence score,
beyond
expected accuracy, that determines whether it can be operated in practice. Three such properties drive this paper:
ranking quality
(Section
3.3
),
score resolution
(next), and
interpretability
—whether a human can read why a given score was assigned, which is a requirement, not a luxury, in regulated domains (Section
5.5
).

3.3
Measuring Score Resolution: Count, Spread, and the Operating-Point Bound

A score that ranks well is not yet thresholdable. Suppose a scoring function
f
takes only a few distinct values on the test set. Under deterministic thresholding (no randomized tie-breaking),
every
test case sharing a value must be accepted or abstained together, so a function with
V
distinct values induces at most
V
non-trivial operating points on the empirical risk–coverage curve.
Operational controllability is therefore upper-bounded by the number of distinct score values, independently of how well the score ranks.
This simple observation is the crux of the granularity gap.

We measure resolution with three complementary quantities, all computed on the test set:

•

Granularity

G(f)=\bigl|\{f(x):x\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\}\bigr|
: the number of distinct values
f
takes. This is exactly the operating-point bound above.

•

Score entropy

H(f)=-\sum_{b=1}^{20}p_{b}\log p_{b}
: bin the scores into
20
equal-width bins over
[0,1]
, let
p_{b}
be the fraction of test scores in bin
b
, and take the Shannon entropy of this histogram, in
nats
(natural-log units;
0
means all mass in one bin,
\log 20\approx 3.0
means perfectly uniform).
H
captures
how evenly
the values are used, which
G
alone does not.

•

Interior mass

M(f)=\Pr_{x}\!\left[0.1<f(x)<0.9\right]
: the fraction of scores away from the saturated extremes. A score can have many distinct values yet pile them up near
0
and
1
, leaving the interior—where most useful thresholds live—empty.

Reporting all three makes resolution a property of the
distribution
of scores:
H
and
M
guard against the obvious objection that the count
G
alone could be inflated by values packed into a narrow band.

4.2
Multi-Signal Scores: Building a Feature Vector

To obtain richer information, we query the LLM
m{=}10
times per input and collect one scalar in
[0,1]
from each query, forming a
feature vector

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
, where
x_{j}
is the score returned by the
j
-th query. We obtain the
m
queries in two ways.

Interpretable sub-task decomposition.

We hand-design, per dataset,
m{=}10
named yes/no
sub-questions
, each probing one capability relevant to the decision—e.g.
lexical_overlap
and
semantic_entail
for natural-language inference, or
explicit_support
and
relevance
for question answering (full definitions in the supplementary material). Each sub-question is answered by an independent LLM call that returns a score
x_{j}\in[0,1]

and
a short natural-language rationale that we log. We call these features
interpretable
in an operational sense: each
x_{j}
is tied to a human-readable named criterion with a logged rationale, so the score can be explained and audited per example (Section
5.5
)—a deployment property, not a ranking claim.

Multi-prompt paraphrase.

As a strong black-box alternative that requires no hand design, we instead generate
m{=}10
paraphrases of the original classification prompt; each returns a direct classification confidence, converted via Eq. (
1
). These features carry no per-criterion meaning—hence no interpretability—but, as we will see, often rank as well as or better than sub-tasks.

4.3
Aggregating the Feature Vector

Given
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{m}
we compare two ways to collapse it to a single score.

Simple averaging.

\bar{s}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}
. This implicitly assumes every feature points the same way (higher
\Rightarrow
more positive) and deserves equal weight—an assumption that fails badly when features have heterogeneous meaning.

Learned combination (logistic regression).

We fit an
L_{2}
-regularized logistic regression

P(y{=}1\mid\mathbf{x})=\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b\right),

(2)

with
\sigma
the sigmoid and regularization strength
C
chosen by grid search over five log-spaced values from
0.01
to
100
.
The regression is fit on a labeled training split, disjoint from the test set; the exact partition into fitting, calibration, and test sets is given in Section
5.1
(anticipated here because the split is part of the method, not just the protocol). We name the learned sub-task score
Sub-LR
and the learned paraphrase score
MP-LR
. Learned weights matter because features differ in meaning and polarity—on NLI a high
lexical_overlap
indicates
entailment
, our negative class—so averaging cancels signal that the regression recovers by giving each feature its correct sign and weight (Section
5.4
quantifies how unreliable the raw probes are alone).

Diagnostic oracle.

To ask how much signal lives in a few features, we also evaluate, as an
oracle upper bound only
, the best of all
\binom{10}{3}{=}120
three-feature subsets selected by cross-validated PR-AUC (
Oracle-3
). It is not a deployable recipe: choosing the wrong three features can be far worse.

A systematic look at the sub-tasks explains both the averaging collapse and why learning is essential. Treated as standalone classifiers (threshold their own score at

Noise is not enough

not
better ranking. It is the deployment property of Section

(which capability drove a mistake); and (iii) an

(each call logs a rationale, required in regulated triage).

6
Deployment Recommendations

No construction dominates; each occupies a distinct point in the trade-off of ranking, resolution, inference cost, and interpretability. We distill the evidence into a decision procedure (cost in calls per input: Verb and logprob
1{\times}
, Oracle-3
3{\times}
, multi-query aggregation
10{\times}
).

1.

Always
convert verbalized confidence to a class probability (Eq. (
1
)). It is free and removes a systematic distortion.

2.

If log-probabilities are available
, use them with post-hoc temperature scaling: in our data this bought enough resolution to avoid the worst staircase effects at
1{\times}
cost.

3.

If the model is weak
(here,
{\lesssim}85\%
accuracy), multi-query aggregation improves both ranking and resolution, at
10{\times}
cost.

4.

If the model is strong
(
{\gtrsim}90\%
), use Verb for risk–coverage and add aggregation
only
when fine threshold control is specifically needed, since aggregation can worsen AURC here. These cut-offs describe our experiments and may shift across tasks.

5.

If interpretability is required
, prefer the sub-task variant: at ranking comparable to MP-LR it adds weight transparency, error attribution, and audit trails.

Cost, and how to evaluate.

Aggregation’s
10{\times}
calls are a real hurdle at scale, and the gap does not close by scaling the model—even frontier-class models give only
4
–
21
distinct verbalized values—so calibrated log-probabilities are the better value where exposed. Finally, our results argue for reporting
two
axes, not one—ranking (PR-AUC, AURC)
and
resolution (
G
,
H
,
M
), with the normalized
\mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC}_{N}
: a score that ranks perfectly but offers nine thresholds is not deployment-ready, and reporting ranking alone hides this.

7
Limitations

We study
binary
classification on three English benchmarks with
62
–
66\%
positive rates; the granularity gap under severe class imbalance, multi-class, or generative settings, and in other languages
[
21
]
, remains open, and we expect the conversion of Eq. (
1
) and the operating-point bound to need restating there. Our study is comparative, not exhaustive: semantic-entropy families
[
8
]
, conformal wrappers
[
1
]
, and deep ensembles are not benchmarked. The interpretable variant needs per-domain manual sub-task design and labeled data (
{\geq}155
examples); MP-LR avoids the manual design but forfeits interpretability; both cost
10{\times}
inference. Finally, calibrated log-probabilities—the best cost/resolution trade-off we found—could be evaluated on only
2
of
9
models, so that conclusion is correspondingly narrower.

8
Conclusion

A confidence score can rank cases almost perfectly and still be unfit for deployment if it offers an operator only a handful of thresholds. We made this
score granularity gap
precise—bounding operating points by the number of distinct values and measuring spread with entropy and interior mass—and used it to compare seven confidence constructions across
25
model–dataset pairs. Single-shot verbalized confidence ranks surprisingly well but is far too coarse; token log-probabilities with calibration widen the gap cheaply where available; and multi-query aggregation widens it the most but at
10{\times}
cost, helping weak models while sometimes degrading strong ones. Interpretability, we argued, is best seen as a deployment property, earned by sub-task scores at no ranking cost. We hope future evaluations report resolution alongside ranking, so confidence scores are judged by whether they can actually be operated.
