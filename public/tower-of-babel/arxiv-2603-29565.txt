D ⁡ ( − 1 ) D(-1) -triples of triangular numbers
Marija Bliznac Trebješanin

Abstract
. We study pairs and triples consisting of triangular numbers such that the product of any two distinct elements decreased by 1 is a perfect square. For a positive integer n n , we establish a necessary condition for the n n -th triangular number T n T_{n} to be a member of such a pair, and we prove that any such T n T_{n} is also a member of infinitely many D ⁡ ( − 1 ) D(-1) -triples.

Keywords: triangular numbers, recurrences, Diophantine m-tuples

1.
Introduction

Let
n\neq 0
be an integer. We call a set of
m
distinct positive integers a
D(n)
-
m
-tuple (or a Diophantine
m
-tuple with the property
D(n)
) if the product of any two of its distinct elements increased by
n
is a perfect square.
When
n=1
, the classical case introduced by Diophantus, such a set is simply referred to as a Diophantine
m
-tuple.

The study of such sets traces back to Fermat, who discovered the first Diophantine quadruple
\{1,3,8,120\}
. A central question in this area of research is determining the maximum possible size of these sets. It is now established that
D(1)
-quintuples
[
8
]
,
D(4)
-quintuples
[
2
]
, and
D(-1)
-quadruples
[
3
]
do not exist. Another prominent direction of research involves restricting the elements of
D(n)
-
m
-tuples to specific integer sequences, such as Fibonacci numbers (see, for instance,
[
5
,
7
]
). For a comprehensive overview of various generalizations and related problems, we refer the reader to
[
6
, Section 1.5]
.

In the remainder of this paper, to avoid notation ambiguity, we will use the letters
n
and
m
specifically as indices for triangular numbers, and we will focus our attention primarily on the property
D(-1)
.

For a positive integer
n
, the
n
-th triangular number, denoted by
T_{n}
, is defined as the sum of the first
n
positive integers, given by the explicit formula

T_{n}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.

From the main result of
[
4
]
, it follows that

\{T_{n},T_{n+4},T_{4n^{2}+20n+8}\}

is a Diophantine triple of triangular numbers. In
[
1
]
, the author presented a construction showing that for any positive integers
k
and
n
, the triangular number
T_{n}
is a member of infinitely many
D(k^{2})
-triples consisting of triangular numbers.

Let
n
be a positive integer. We are looking for positive integers
m
and
r
satisfying

T_{n}T_{m}-1=r^{2}.

This equation can be rewritten as a generalized Pellian equation

(1)

x^{2}-n(n+1)y^{2}=-(n(n+1)+16),

where the unknowns are
x=4r
and
y=2m+1
. An analysis of the properties of this equation will provide the main results concerning
D(-1)
-pairs and
D(-1)
-triples of triangular numbers.

For some values of
n
, no
D(-1)
-pairs exist. For example,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
cannot belong to any
D(-1)
-pair. Furthermore, we will establish a necessary condition that
n
must satisfy for
T_{n}
to be a member of a
D(-1)
-pair.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer such that there exists a positive integer
m
for which
\{T_{n},T_{m}\}
is a
D(-1)
-pair. Then both
n
and
n+1
are numbers of the form
2^{a}\cdot p_{1}^{l_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{l_{k}}
, where
p_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
l_{1},\dots,l_{k}
are positive integers and
a\in\{0,2\}
for
n
and
a\in\{0,1\}
for
n+1
.

Note that this condition implies that both
n
and
n+1
can be expressed as the sum of two squares.

In general, the converse is not true. For instance,
n=100
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1
, but we will demonstrate that
\{T_{100},T_{m}\}
is not a
D(-1)
-pair for any positive integer
m
.

By analyzing the sequences of associated solutions
(m_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to equation (
1
), we will prove that for any two successive solutions, the set
\{T_{m_{k}},T_{m_{k+1}}\}
forms a
D(-1)
-pair. This naturally leads to our next result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer such that there exists a positive integer
m
for which
\{T_{n},T_{m}\}
is a
D(-1)
-pair. Then
T_{n}
is an element of infinitely many
D(-1)
-triples containing only triangular numbers.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 covers the basic properties of the associated generalized Pellian equation and investigates the conditions on
n
for which
T_{n}
belongs to a
D(-1)
-pair of triangular numbers, concluding with the proof of Theorem
1
. In Section 3, we further examine these equations to show how such pairs can be extended to
D(-1)
-triples, thereby proving Theorem
2
. We also describe an algorithm to generate all numbers
n
up to a given bound such that
T_{n}
forms a
D(-1)
-pair. Finally, Section 4 provides insights into broader generalizations and potential directions for future research.

2.
Generalized Pellian equation and proof of Theorem
1

Let
n
be a positive integer. We are searching for positive integers
m
and
r
such that
T_{n}\cdot T_{m}-1=r^{2}
, or more precisely,

\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\cdot\frac{m(m+1)}{2}-1=r^{2}.

After some algebraic manipulation, this yields the generalized Pellian equation

(2)

(4r)^{2}-n(n+1)(2m+1)^{2}=-(n(n+1)+16),

which can be written in the simpler form of the equation (
1
) in terms of the variables
x
and
y
.

First, let us prove that equations (
2
) and (
1
) are equivalent, meaning that the positive integer solutions
(x,y)
of equation (
1
) are of the form
(4r,2m+1)
for some positive integers
r
and
m
.

Lemma 1
.

If
(x,y)
is a solution to the equation (
1
) then
y
is an odd number and
x
is divisible by
4
. Moreover, if
y
is a positive integer, then
y\geq 3
.

Proof.

Equation (
1
) can be rewritten as
x^{2}+16=n(n+1)(y^{2}-1)
.

It is obvious that
x
is an even number; denote
x=2k
for an integer
k
.
Suppose that
y
is even and denote
y=2c
for an integer
c
. Substituting these into the equation yields

4(k^{2}+4)=n(n+1)(4c^{2}-1).

Since
4c^{2}-1\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, it must have at least one prime factor
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. As
q
is odd, we get
q\mid(k^{2}+4)
, which implies
k^{2}\equiv-4\pmod{q}
. However, this contradicts the fact that
-4
is a quadratic non-residue modulo any prime
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Thus,
y
must be odd.

Since
y
is odd,
y^{2}-1
is a multiple of
8
and
n(n+1)(y^{2}-1)
is divisible by
16
, implying that
x
is divisible by
4
.

Finally, the assumption
y=1
leads to
x^{2}=-16
, a contradiction.
∎

Next, we examine the conditions that
n
must satisfy for equation (
1
) to have a solution. By reducing equation (
1
) modulo
n
and modulo
n+1
, we obtain

x^{2}\equiv-16\pmod{n}\quad\text{and}\quad x^{2}\equiv-16\pmod{n+1}.

This means that
-16
is a quadratic residue modulo both
n
and
n+1
. Consequently, any odd prime factor
p
dividing either
n
or
n+1
must be congruent to
1\pmod{4}
.

Next, suppose that
2^{a}
divides either
n
or
n+1
. For
a\geq 6
, we consider the congruence

x^{2}\equiv-16\pmod{2^{a}}.

Since
2^{4}\mid-16
, it must also divide
x^{2}
, but since
2^{6}\nmid-16
, it follows that
2^{6}\nmid x^{2}
. Hence,
x=4r
, where
r
is an odd integer. Dividing the congruence by
16
yields

r^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{2^{a-4}}.

If
a\geq 6
, we would have
r^{2}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, which is impossible. This implies that
a\leq 5
.

Furthermore, if either
n
or
n+1
is divisible by
2^{5}
, then
n(n+1)/16\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. As before,
r
must be odd in this case, meaning
r^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Dividing equation (
2
) by
16
gives

r^{2}-\frac{n(n+1)}{16}(2m+1)^{2}=-\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{16}+1\right).

Taking this modulo
4
implies
-1\equiv-3\pmod{4}
, a contradiction. Thus,
a\leq 4
.

Similar arguments eliminate the possibility that
a=4
. If
a=3
for either
n
or
n+1
, then
n(n+1)/8\equiv 1\pmod{8}
if
8\mid n
, and
n(n+1)/8\equiv-1\pmod{8}
if
8\mid(n+1)
. Dividing equation (
2
) by
8
yields

2r^{2}-\frac{n(n+1)}{8}(2m+1)^{2}=-\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{8}+2\right).

Notice that
2r^{2}
can only be congruent to
0
or
2
modulo
8
. After examining all possibilities, we see that the left-hand side of the equation is never congruent to the right-hand side modulo
8
. Consequently,
a\leq 2
.

Therefore, both
n
and
n+1
must be of the form
2^{a}\cdot p_{1}^{l_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{l_{k}}
, where
p_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
l_{1},\dots,l_{k}
are positive integers and
a\in\{0,1,2\}
.

However, if
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, this would imply
n+1\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, which contradicts the established condition that all odd prime factors must be congruent to
1
modulo
4
. Thus,
a\neq 1
for
n
. Similarly,
n+1\equiv 0\pmod{4}
would imply
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, which is again a contradiction. Thus,
a\neq 2
for
n+1
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

3.
D(-1)
-triples containing
T_{n}

Let
n
be a positive integer. We wish to further describe the solutions to the generalized Pellian equation (
1
).

From the theory of generalized Pellian equations (see
[
9
, Theorem 109]
), we know that if equation (
1
) has a solution, it has a finite number of classes of solutions. As described in
[
9
, Theorem 108a]
, each class is generated by a fundamental solution
(x^{*},y^{*})
satisfying the inequalities

(3)

0<y^{*}\leq\frac{Y_{1}}{\sqrt{2(X_{1}-1)}}\sqrt{|N|},\quad 0\leq|x^{*}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(X_{1}-1)\cdot|N|},

where
N=-(n(n+1)+16)
and
(X_{1},Y_{1})
is the fundamental solution of the corresponding Pell equation

(4)

X^{2}-n(n+1)Y^{2}=1.

Notice that
(X_{1},Y_{1})=(2n+1,2)
is the fundamental solution to (
4
) for every positive integer
n
.

Remark
.

Now that we have established the bounds for the fundamental solutions, let us show that the converse of Theorem
1
does not hold. As previously mentioned,
n=100
satisfies the necessary conditions of Theorem
1
. To determine if
T_{100}
can form a
D(-1)
-pair with another triangular number, we search for solutions to the generalized Pellian equation

x^{2}-100\cdot 101y^{2}=-(100\cdot 101+16).

Applying the bounds from (
3
) with
(X_{1},Y_{1})=(201,2)
yields
0<y^{*}\leq 10
and
0\leq|x^{*}|\leq 1005
. By testing all possible integer values for
y^{*}
within this range, we find that there are no integer pairs
(x^{*},y^{*})
solving this equation. Consequently, the triangular number
T_{100}
is not a member of any
D(-1)
-pair consisting solely of triangular numbers. It can, however, form a
D(-1)
-pair if the second element is not restricted to be a triangular number (e.g.,
\{T_{100},5653\}
is a valid
D(-1)
-pair).

We consider a specific class of solutions to equation (
1
) and denote its fundamental solution by
(x^{*},y^{*})
. Then every positive integer solution
(x,y)
belonging to this class can be expressed as

x+y\sqrt{n(n+1)}=(x^{*}+y^{*}\sqrt{n(n+1)})\left(X_{1}+Y_{1}\sqrt{n(n+1)}\right)^{k},

for some integer
k\geq 0
if
x^{*}>0
, and
k\geq 1
if
x^{*}\leq 0
.

For generalized Pellian equation (
1
), the following relation holds:

(5)

y_{k+1}=X_{1}y_{k}+Y_{1}x_{k}=(2n+1)y_{k}+2x_{k},\quad k\geq 0.

The sequence of solutions for the second coordinate,
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, forms the linear recurrence sequence

(6)

y_{0}=y^{*},\quad y_{1}=2x^{*}+(2n+1)y^{*},\quad y_{k+2}=2(2n+1)y_{k+1}-y_{k},\ k\geq 0.

Since
y_{k}=2m_{k}+1
, we can express the sequence of indices
(m_{k})
such that
\{T_{n},T_{m_{k}}\}
is a
D(-1)
-pair as follows:

\displaystyle m_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(y^{*}-1),\quad m_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(x^{*}Y_{1}+y^{*}X_{1}-1),

(7)

\displaystyle m_{k+2}=2(2n+1)m_{k+1}-m_{k}+2n,\quad k\geq 1.

Note that if
x^{*}\leq 0
, the initial value
m_{0}
does not yield a valid pair in positive integers; hence, we only consider the indices
m_{k}
for
k\geq 1
.

Lemma 2
.

For the sequence
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of solutions to the generalized Pellian equation (
1
), the following identity holds:

y_{k+1}^{2}-2(2n+1)y_{k}y_{k+1}+y_{k}^{2}=-4(n(n+1)+16).

Proof.

Using equation (
5
), we can express
2x_{k}
as a sum of the terms containing
y
. Squaring this identity and applying the relation
x_{k}^{2}=n(n+1)y_{k}^{2}-(n(n+1)+16)
yields the desired equality.
∎

Now we are ready to prove that
\{T_{m_{k}},T_{m_{k+1}}\}
forms a
D(-1)
-pair for all
k\geq 1
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(m_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be the sequence defined in (
3
). Then

T_{m_{k}}T_{m_{k+1}}-1

is the square of a positive integer.

Proof.

Notice that
8T_{n}+1=(2n+1)^{2}
holds for every integer
n
. Then

\displaystyle T_{m_{k}}T_{m_{k+1}}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{(2m_{k}+1)^{2}-1}{8}\cdot\frac{(2m_{k+1}+1)^{2}-1}{8}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{k}^{2}-1}{8}\cdot\frac{y_{k+1}^{2}-1}{8}-1=\frac{y_{k}^{2}y_{k+1}^{2}-y_{k}^{2}-y_{k+1}^{2}+1-64}{64}.

By substituting the identity from Lemma
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{m_{k}}T_{m_{k+1}}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{k}^{2}y_{k+1}^{2}+4n(n+1)+64-2(2n+1)y_{k}y_{k+1}+1-64}{64}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{y_{k}y_{k+1}-(2n+1)}{8}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2m_{k}m_{k+1}+m_{k}+m_{k+1}-n}{4}\right)^{2}.

It remains to show that the expression inside the parentheses is an integer. It is easy to prove by mathematical induction that
y_{k}y_{k+1}-(2n+1)
is divisible by
8
for each
k\geq 0
. Properties from Lemma
1
are used in the proof.
∎

This implies that any
T_{n}
for which the associated equation (
2
) has a solution is a member of infinitely many
D(-1)
-triples
\{T_{n},T_{m_{k}},T_{m_{k+1}}\}
for
k\geq 1
. Thus, we conclude the proof of Theorem
2
.

Observe that from (
3
) and the fact that
(X_{1},Y_{1})=(2n+1,2)
, it follows that
y^{*}<n
for
n\geq 4
. Consequently, the fundamental solution always yields an integer
m
strictly smaller than
n
such that
\{T_{m},T_{n}\}
is a
D(-1)
-pair. Since the integers
2
and
3
do not satisfy the necessary conditions of Theorem
1
, we can conclude the following:

Proposition 2
.

If a triangular number
T_{m}
, with
m>1
, is a member of a
D(-1)
-pair, then there exists an integer
n
such that
1\leq n<m
for which
m
is a solution to equation (
2
).

Remark
.

We can exploit this property to systematically find all triangular numbers
T_{n}
belonging to a
D(-1)
-pair of triangular numbers for
n\leq C
. Starting with
n=1
, we generate all corresponding solutions
m\leq C
to equation (
2
). We then iterate this process, using each new solution as the seed for the next step, until all generated values surpass the upper bound
C
.

.

To illustrate the described algorithm, we set the upper bound to
C=1000
and systematically search for indices
n
yielding
D(-1)
-pairs of triangular numbers. Starting with the base case
n=1
, we recursively generate the solutions.

4.
Generalization to
D(a)
-triples and future research

In the previous section, we established that every triangular number, which is a member of a
D(-1)
-pair of triangular numbers, can be extended to infinitely many
D(-1)
-triples. Our proof can be naturally generalized to the property
D(a)
for any non-zero integer
a
.

Let
a
be a non-zero integer. Suppose that
\{T_{n},T_{m}\}
is a
D(a)
-pair. The condition
T_{n}T_{m}+a=r^{2}
translates into the generalized Pellian equation

x^{2}-n(n+1)y^{2}=16a-n(n+1),

where
x=4r
and
y=2m+1
. Denoting
N_{a}=16a-n(n+1)
, the identity from Lemma
2
takes the generalized form

y_{k+1}^{2}-2(2n+1)y_{k}y_{k+1}+y_{k}^{2}=4N_{a}=64a-4n(n+1).

When evaluating whether
\{T_{m_{k}},T_{m_{k+1}}\}
forms a
D(a)
-pair, we examine the expression

T_{m_{k}}T_{m_{k+1}}+a=\frac{y_{k}^{2}y_{k+1}^{2}-(y_{k}^{2}+y_{k+1}^{2})+1+64a}{64}.

Substituting the sum of squares from the generalized identity into this fraction yields

T_{m_{k}}T_{m_{k+1}}+a=\left(\frac{y_{k}y_{k+1}-(2n+1)}{8}\right)^{2}.

Even though the statement of Lemma
1
does not hold for
a
in general, restricting our arguments to
x=4r
and
y=2m+1
guarantees that the necessary parity conditions are met. Thus, the mathematical induction operates identically, leading to a generalized conclusion:

Theorem 3
.

Let
a
be a non-zero integer. If there exist positive integers
n
and
m
such that
\{T_{n},T_{m}\}
is a
D(a)
-pair, then
T_{n}
is a member of infinitely many
D(a)
-triples consisting of triangular numbers.

We have shown that the extension from a
D(a)
-pair to a
D(a)
-triple in triangular numbers is universally applicable. However, the necessary conditions for the existence of the initial pair are heavily tied to the specific integer
a
. Consequently, there is no immediate generalization of Theorem
1
. Investigating these new conditions and searching for patterns for various numbers
a
provides a natural and engaging topic for future research.
