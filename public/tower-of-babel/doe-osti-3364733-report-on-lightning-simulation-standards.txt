 I. Introduction

Lightning strikes pose a significant threat to various forms of infrastructure and electrical

equipment. Aircraft and high explosives are of particular interest due to the massive

ramifications of the device being compromised due to exposure to lightning damage.

However, due to lightning being a varying weather phenomenon, testing a device’s

vulnerability to lightning is generally not as simple as simply exposing it to real world

lightning. To consistently test a device’s ability to survive the worst-case scenario of

lightning exposure, the effects of the most severe lightning strikes must be able to be

reproduced consistently and measurably. One such facility for this purpose is the

Sandia Lightning Simulator (SLS), which is designed to simulate the current waveform

of a severe lightning flash. However, the SLS’s design and age lead to difficulties in its

operation and inconsistencies in its output. The methodology used in other lightning

simulation facilities may offer information on how to improve the SLS’s operation, or at

the very least, expose the SLS’s hidden strengths. This report explores lightning

simulation, lightning testing standards, and lightning simulation facilities.

 II. Lightning Models

The study of lightning is incredibly broad and spans a great number of fields. As

described in [1], there are a great number of models for different components of

lightning with such variety in scale that using them holistically is quite difficult. The paper

proposes an “End-to-End Lightning” (E2EL) model which comprehensively connects

many of these models through four major components: weather, lightning, signals, and

sensors. The weather model focuses on modeling electrification within clouds and has

several mature existing models which could be effectively integrated into the holistic

model. However, links between these models and physics-based models describing

lightning initiation and discharge have not been built. This link is key, as in the lightning

model, data from the cloud component is taken as an input to be used to describe the

physical structure and current discharge of a lightning flash. The lightning model is the

component most in need of development, as due to its complex nature, it has been

mostly modeled in a modular approach, with individual simulation methods of various

phenomena failing to be utilized together to create an accurate output waveform. The

correctness of the currents from the lightning model is paramount to success for the

signal model, as it generates RF and optical signals from the currents of the lightning

model. This stage’s outputs will be compared to observational data to verify the quality

of the whole holistic model up to this point. Finally, the sensor model receives the data

from the signal model to be collected by modeled sensors for further validation.

Despite the lack of a holistic model, lightning poses a great threat to electronic devices

and infrastructure, and has been measured, studied, and protected against using

empirical methods for centuries. From these empirical methods, the general behavior of

a lightning strike has been able to be effectively approximated for testing and standards.

As described in [2], a lightning strike is a transient, high current discharge which can

occur over kilometers. The total discharge of a lightning strike, called a “flash,” involves

several “return strokes,” where charge is transferred via high current pulses which occur

fast enough to create a flickering effect when observed by the human eye. Between

these strokes is a lower amplitude continuing current, where a smaller amount of charge

is transferred but the electrical connection to the cloud remains. Charge can be

transferred either via a cloud-to-ground flash or a cloud flash, which can happen either

within the cloud itself (intracloud), to other clouds (cloud-to-cloud), or to the air (cloud-to-

air).

In the case of cloud-to-ground, the lightning flash is broken up between negative

lightning and positive lightning [2]. A storm cloud can be modeled as an electric dipole,

where typically positive charge accumulates on the top of the cloud while negative

charge accumulates on the bottom. The cloud and earth then behave akin to a parallel

plate capacitor, where the relative negative charge on the bottom of the cloud is

attracted to the relative positive charge of the earth. In the case of negative lightning, as

of that shown in Figure 1, breakdown occurs from the negatively charged bottom of the

cloud down to earth. In positive lightning, the ground accumulates a relatively negative

charge, causing breakdown to occur from the top of the cloud. Positive lightning makes

up a significantly smaller percentage of cloud-to-ground lightning strikes and tends to be

much more severe in their amplitude. In addition, they’ve been observed to have fewer

return strokes, with the vast majority being a single stroke [3].

 III. Lightning Standards

Some of the most prominent lightning standards used for aerospace qualifications

include the RTCA DO-160 [5], the SAE ARP standards [6, 7], and the Department of

Defense military standards, MIL-STD-461 and MIL-STD-464 [8, 9]. Across these

standards, very similar waveforms are characterized and utilized for specific lightning

threats, and the MIL-STD reports contain idealized waveforms directly derived from

SAE standards. The waveforms in [7] are broken up into voltage waveforms and current

waveforms. The voltage waveforms are primarily used to simulate the effects of

lightning either puncturing through or flashing over dielectric shielding. Two main

voltage waveforms are established for this purpose, with Voltage Waveform A having a

linear ramp of 1000kV/µs (± 50%) until breakdown, and Voltage Waveform D having a

double exponential waveform which breaks down on the falling edge. Voltage

Waveform B is designed for tests where the field does not quite reach breakdown and

Voltage Waveform C follows a similar trend to Voltage Waveform A, but specifically

breaks down at 1.2 µs. According to [2], Voltage Waveform A has historically been used

for punch-through and flashover testing of full-scale hardware, while Voltage

Waveforms C and D have been used for attachment point testing, and Voltage

Waveform B has been used for fuel ignition tests.

Current waveforms are described in [7] for multiple stroke and multiple burst testing, but

the most relevant waveform is the multi-component current waveform, which is used to

test the damage caused by a severe lightning flash. The waveform consists of four

components: Component A consists of an initial return stroke, with a fast rise time to a

peak current of approximately 200 kA which then decays exponentially. Component B

describes the extended exponential decay of Component A which eventually becomes a

constant current in Component C, which lasts for hundreds of milliseconds. Finally,

component D describes a second return stroke with a similar rise time to Component A,

but with a much lower peak current of approximately 100 kA. Components A and D can

also be used for indirect effects, such as to test effects from induced magnetic fields

from a nearby lightning strike. It's important to note that the severe lightning flash

current waveform is meant for testing of a “Black Swan” event, and the amount of

lightning strikes that reach these levels make up about 1% of all lightning strikes [10].

The mean lightning strike only reaches about 30 kA with a much shorter decay time.

 IV. Sandia Lightning Simulator

The SLS is designed to test for the lightning waveform specifications outlined in [8] and

[9]. Specifically, the SLS is designed to perform both single- and double-stroke testing,

generating the full ABCD severe current waveform [11]. While the SLS has received

several upgrades since its inception, and more since its upgrade in 2005, its method of

operation remains mostly identical. The method of generating the desired pulse involves

creating an underdamped RLC discharge and using a crowbar switch to short across

the capacitor to create an overdamped exponential decay [12]. The pulse is initially

generated by two parallel Marx banks located in the North tank. Each Marx consists of

eight stages with each stage consisting of two 2.6 µF capacitors in series connected tail

to tail and charged with opposite polarity to ±65 kV. This leads to a fully erected peak

voltage of approximately 1 MV. The Marx generators are triggered using a smaller Marx

generator, a Marx Trigger Generator (MTG), which consists of eight stages of 60 nF

capacitors charged to 70 kV. The MTG is triggered using a TG-70 trigger generator,

which is charged using a separate high voltage power supply.

When connected and erected, the North tank outputs an RLC underdamped current

waveform with a peak of approximately 210 kA with a rise time of approximately 1.2 µs.

A laser-triggered spark gap switch is then used to crowbar the Marx at approximately

87% peak current. Since the voltage and current waveforms are 90° out of phase, at

peak current, the voltage would reach zero. At least some voltage needs to be present

for the crowbar switch to close. At approximately 87% peak current, the voltage across

the crowbar is at 50% peak, leaving enough voltage for the switch to close properly. The

result is an output current waveform with the fast rise time of an underdamped

oscillating waveform and a long exponentially decaying tail. It’s important to note that

non-ideal resistance and inductance within the Marx results in a small underdamped

ringing at the start of the output waveform.

 Figure 3, illustration of the Sandia Lightning Simulator [12].

For double-stroke testing, a Continuing Current Generator (CCG) is used to generate

component C of the waveform for hundreds of milliseconds before the second stroke.

The process of generating components A and B of the waveform is like that of

component D. Contained in the East side of the South tank is another eight stage Marx

of 2.6 µF capacitors which result in a peak output current of 100 kA and is crowbarred

with an identical laser triggered spark gap switch. On the West side of the tank is a 1.3

µF Marx bank which can be used for generating lower current waveforms (around 20

kA).

Voltages and currents are measured and fed back to a LabView program in the screen

room using shielded coax cables. These are displayed alongside the trigger signals for

the crowbar and laser to monitor the timing. Ensuring the crowbar switch timing is

correct is essential to the SLS’s operation. Triggering the crowbar early will result in a

lower output current, and triggering late may result in failure to close, and thus, potential

damage to the Marx banks via the Voltage reversal of the underdamped ringing

waveform. Output current is measured using Current Viewing Resistors (CVR) in line

with the return path, while crowbar voltage is measured with a resistive divider. The

North and South tanks are measured separately.

Despite the LabView program for monitoring the diagnostics and output signals, the

SLS machine is still mostly operated manually. The high voltage power supplies for the

Marxes are manually controlled to obtain a linear ramp, requiring approximately 200

repeated button presses to reach full charge. As such, charging data is not digitally

recorded as each charge cycle may be completely unique, and the charge voltage of

the Marx can vary widely. Component failures, such as damaged capacitors, are usually

observed during charging, as shorted capacitors will fail to reach desired charge

voltage, and open capacitors will charge at a different rate. Trigger signals are sent to

the TG-70 and lasers via a series of delay generators, which is also triggered from the

main console. Current efforts to upgrade the SLS involve replacing the charge power

supplies for the Marx banks and MTG with Glassman power supplies which can be

controlled from the screen room with a LabView program alongside the diagnostics and

signal acquisition.

The SLS can be used to test direct strikes as well as nearby lightning effects. Direct

strikes are typically tested by either directly connecting to the test article or by leaving a

gap to arc across. This method can be used to test how lightning strikes may burn

through the article. Nearby lightning is tested by shorting the output of the facility with a

copper loop and placing the test article near it, simulating the effects of a lightning strike

within meters of the test article.

 V. Other Lightning Simulators

As stated in [13], the method of using a crowbar to convert a fast oscillatory waveform

into a slow exponential decay has been widely used for several decades. [14] describes

a “Mobile Imitator” which generates a two-stroke waveform with almost identical

specifications to SLS. Three separate modules, named LCG1, LCG2, and LCG3, are

used to generate a similar ACD waveform with two strokes and a semi-uniform

continuing current between the two. The peaks are generated using a crowbar switch

method like SLS, to the point of also having the small decaying ringing present

immediately after the rise, as shown in Figure 4. The continuing current generation is

thoroughly described, as the waveform generated is quasi-constant rather than a

constant amplitude like that of SLS.

 Figure 4, 200 kA current output pulse of system described in [14].

[15] describes a prototype for a direct lightning effects simulator to be used at White

Sands Missile Range. The design is directly compared to an alternative to SLS, being

much more compact while still generating a high current pulse due to lower inductance.

The system uses a two-stage Marx with 50 µF capacitors charged to 16 kV and uses

high power diodes in place of the laser-triggered crowbar switch. The diode starts in

reverse bias when the Marx is erected. As the voltage oscillates and becomes negative,

the diode is switched to forward bias, shorting the RL circuit at peak current and zero

voltage. Theoretically, this design can output much higher current more consistently due

to being entirely dependent on the polarity of the voltage across it.

 Figure 5, MATHCAD model used to describe the circuit proposed in [15].

This methodology has been used in several commercial lightning simulators [16]. The

Boeing Lightning Lab has historically used a number of different generators for meeting

a number of lightning specifications, such as the “A” and “D” generators, which used 10-

12 parallel capacitors (125 µF and 37.5 µF respectively) charged to 60 kV to deliver the

A/B and D components. The banks were crowbarred using three parallel strings of 120

kV PIV high power diodes to hold off the voltage during reverse bias, with each string

having around 10 series diodes. However, these pulsers were not setup to perform

double-stroke testing, as the ARP standard they were built for allows for the ABC

components to be tested separately from the D component. The output for the “A” bank

is shown in Figure 6. It’s important to note that there is no low amplitude ringing on the

initial fall of the waveform like that of SLS and the LCG pulsers. Other capacitor banks

using diode crowbarring are used at DNB, Element LTI, and NIAR.

The Boeing Lightning Lab also has nearby lightning testing capabilities, but to a very

different definition. Rather than having a test article within a 1-3 meter proximity from a

lightning strike, the nearby lightning testing conducted is in response to a lightning strike

on a nearby component (mm to cm distance) [17]. For this testing, an electrical

component is mounted on the bottom of a metal plate (usually a panel from the wing or

somewhere else on the aircraft) and connected to ground. The plate itself is also

connected to ground using shorting bars. The electrical connection from the generator is

then made to the plate either by a direct connection or via a diverter wire across a gap

which is vaporized upon firing. The electrical current induced upon the panel is then

measured through the shorting bars and the device connection via Current

Transformers (CT).

Figure 6, current waveform output of Boeing “A” Bank, without the crowbar on top and

 with the crowbar on bottom [16].

The potential consistency of operation offered by this crowbarring method should not be

understated. The laser-triggered spark gaps present on SLS are pressurized with SF6

gas and this pressure must be closely monitored and tuned to ensure the switch closes

reliably, and the external timed trigger required to close them results in vulnerability to

load inductance decreasing the output of the system; additional inductance results in a

slower rise time, resulting in the trigger firing before the signal reaches the 87% mark. In

addition, spark gap switches require disassembly and cleaning as electrical breakdown

causes buildup on the electrodes, and incorrect cleaning procedures can cause the

electrode surfaces to lack their required uniformity. Attempts to clean spark gap

electrode surfaces using bead blasting, in particular, leads to massively reduced ability

to hold off voltage [18].

Diode crowbarring bypasses the need for pressurized gas and timed triggering, allowing

the system to trigger itself more reliably. It’s important to note, though, that these

systems charge to a much lower voltage and either use fewer stages in a Marx, or no

stages at all. As stated in [15], reducing the scale of the system to reduce inductance

allows the system to obtain a high current output with a much lower voltage input.

However, reducing the system inductance rather than designing around it leads to a

system which is more vulnerable to high load impedance. As shown in Figures 7-a and

7-b, adding a 1 µH load to a ringing circuit equivalent to the SLS reduces the output

current by approximately 10 kA, or 5%, whereas adding the same load to a circuit with

the values shown in [15] results in massively increased rise time and amplitude reduced

by nearly 50%. This inductance is representative as a worst-case scenario, as the

typical load inductance range seen on SLS varies from 0.1-1 µH. Thus, despite the

timing challenges introduced by the laser triggered switch, SLS’s output current is much

closer to the short circuit current in the presence of an inductive load. Theoretically, high

power diodes could improve the timing and ease of use on the system, but to be able to

hold off SLS’s 1 MV erected voltage, the diodes strings would need to be hundreds of

diodes long.

 VI. Conclusion

The systems described in this report are primarily used for Black Swan testing where

rare, high power lightning strikes could potentially cause catastrophic damage to the

test article. The basis for the standards used in this testing is based on empirical data

and real-world measurements of lightning behavior. With that said, there is still room for

improvement for creating a holistic model for lightning, which can be used to generate

more educated and accurate simulations. The SLS is due for several improvements and

upgrades to keep up with newer pulsed power systems. Yet, despite the SLS’s age and

operational difficulties, the system manages to have a robustness that allows it to

generate higher currents for more inductive test articles. Diode crowbarring could

theoretically be used to ease the burden of timing the crowbar switch and allow for more

current output, but realistically, it’s unlikely to benefit the system.
