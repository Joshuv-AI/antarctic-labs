Exploring the association between menopause and burning mouth syndrome: an updated review
Authors: Kowsar Qaderi, Farideh Eghdampour, Manthar Ali Mallah, Mehri Kalhor, Hooman Ghasemi, Nima Fallahnia, Zeynab Sohrabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) is a chronic orofacial pain disorder characterized by a burning or dyesthetic sensation in the oral mucosa in the absence of identifiable clinical or laboratory abnormalities, predominantly observed in postmenopausal women. Despite its prevalence, the underlying causes remain poorly understood. This systematic review aims to synthesize current evidence on factors associated with BMS to inform future research and clinical management.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases to identify relevant studies published between January 2000 and June 2025. After removing 108 duplicates from an initial pool of 688 records, 22 studies met the inclusion criteria. Eligible designs included cross-sectional, case-control, cohort, and descriptive studies involving individuals diagnosed with BMS. Methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsBMS was consistently associated with female sex, menopause, hypothyroidism, chronic medication use, and sociodemographic factors such as older age, marital status, unemployment, and lower educational attainment. Some studies found no significant correlations between BMS and salivary biomarkers such as protein, opiorphin, Candida species, or diabetes, while the role of progesterone remains inconsistent across studies. Additional contributing factors included chronic pelvic pain, heightened pain sensitivity, depression, anxiety, stressful life events, poor sleep quality, diminished quality of life, and oral health issues such as denture use, xerostomia, periodontitis, dental procedures, and parafunctional habits. Lifestyle factors particularly smoking, alcohol consumption, and intake of acidic, spicy, or strong-flavored foods were reported to exacerbate BMS symptoms.ConclusionThe findings highlight the multifactorial nature of BMS and identify vulnerable populations at increased risk. While numerous associations have been documented, no single etiological factor can be definitively implicated, underscoring the complexity of BMS pathogenesis and the need for interdisciplinary approaches in diagnosis and management.Keywords: Burning mouth syndrome, Stomatodynia, Glossalgia, Menopause, Oral pain, Oral mucosa, Hormonal factors, Psychological factors

Background

Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) is a chronic orofacial pain disorder characterized by a burning or dysesthetic sensation in the oral cavity without any identifiable biological or clinical abnormalities [1, 2]. The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) has characterized it as “burning oral sensation or pain, unremitting while in the absence of objective clinical changes in the oral mucosa [3]. The patient with BMS also complains of pain in the oral mucosa, allodynia, prickling pain, tingling, electrical discharges, numbness, itching, change in the sense of taste, and dry mouth [4, 5]. The tongue is the most common site of involvement, with symptoms most often centered on the front half of the tongue. Moreover, about half of the primary BMS patients reported the tongue to be the only affected area, followed by the hard palate and gums [6].

In the age range of 30–39 years, the prevalence of BMS was 0.6%, increasing to 12.2% among individuals aged 70 years and older. The prevalence in Asia (1.05%) is lower than in Europe (5.58%) and North America (1.10%). Also, the prevalence of females (1.15%) was higher than males (0.38%) in the general population [7].

Risk factors for BMS are classified as local such as dental procedures, parafunctional habits, and oral irritants and systemic, including hormonal changes, endocrine disorders, nutritional deficiencies, and psychological conditions. This syndrome is probably of multifactorial cause, often idiopathic, and its etiopathogenesis remains enigmatic [2]. Menopause is a physiological life stage associated with hormonal changes (decline in estrogen, progesterone, and androgens) that may affect oral health. BMS is associated with gender, age, and menopause because of hormonal changes that occur and which could have a pathogenic role [5]. Women during menopause undergo biological and endocrine changes, especially in the production of sex steroid hormones, which affect their health. Menopause, and the associated hypo-estrogen state, have implications on the structure and function of the mouth [8]. Studies have identified several factors that may be responsible for the burning sensation in the mouth. They can be physical, biological (some bacteria or fungi) or chemical [9]. Physiological condition associated with hormonal changes, such as menopause, diabetes, thyroid diseases, hypersalivation associated with autoimmune disease, drug use, dysgeusia (altered taste perception), and gastroesophageal reflux disease, can be correlated with BMS [9]. Also, psychological factors, neurological factors and even Circadian Rhythm have been raised to be related to the etiology of the disease [10, 11]. However, the factors associated with BMS are not completely clear. In this study, we reviewed articles published from 2020 that discuss the correlated factors of BMS.

Then, full texts of the remaining 77 studies were inspected carefully and 56 articles were excluded. Two articles which had similar population with two other studies have been removed and finally, 22 articles met inclusion criteria were included in the data extraction (Table 2).

General characteristics of the studies

The total sample size was 1032. The oldest study was performed in 2001 and the most recent study in 2025. The highest number of studies was conducted in Italy with 6 articles, and China and Japan with 2. The maximum and minimum sample size was related to the study of Tarkkila et al. [14] with 3173 subjects and the study of Castillo et al. [21] with 22 subjects, respectively. In the studies that have mentioned the diagnostic criterion of BMS, the most used tool was ICOP (International Classification of Oro-facial Pain) 2020 (five articles) and clinical examinations (three articles).

All the studies had acceptable scores in the risk of bias assessment (Table 1). The highest quality assessment score based on the NOS checklist was related to the study of Tarkkila et al. Factors associated with BMS

Gender

Studies have demonstrated that BMS is strongly associated with female gender [25]. Thus, BMS affected more women than men. Most studies on BMS conducted on females. In the present review, five studies (with the sample size 3469) conducted only on female population; the sample size of other 16 remained studies was 3107. Of them 2132 (68.6%) were females. In general, findings of the current review investigated correlating factors of BMS in the findings of different observational studies conducted on 5763 females and 1005 males.

Menopause

BMS affects in particular post-menopausal women [16]. Menopause women with BMS had significantly higher mean Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores than postmenopausal and menstruating women [15, 23]. The results of logistic regression studies revealed that climacteric [The stage of life around menopause in women] symptoms were predictive of painful mouth (P = 0.000) and dry mouth (P = 0.000) [14]. The use of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) did not prevent the oral symptoms.

Personal factors

The findings show that several personal factors—such as being married) [32], unemployed [25, 32], less educated [24, 32], older [29, 32], with earlier onset of BMS [32], and long-term chronic drug use [19, 29, 31] are correlated with burning mouth syndrome, though results vary across studies. A 2021 cross-sectional study did not find significant associations with age, education, gender, or employment status, and diabetes as a comorbidity did not significantly change the OHIP results in that study [17].

Neurologic factors

In comparison with healthy participants, individuals with BMS exhibited higher pain sensitivity to phasic heat stimuli across most temperatures. The degree of sensitivity varied by body region—greater in the orofacial area and reduced on the forearm—and was more pronounced in the morning, possibly reflecting hypervigilance [18].

Chronic pelvic pain

Compared with healthy individuals, patients with BMS showed statistically significant increases on several urological chronic pelvic pain syndrome (UCPPS) measures, including incomplete emptying, intermittency, quality of life, the pain subscale, and the chronic prostatitis symptom index [26, 31].

Salivary substance concentration

Some studies reported significant correlations between salivary progesterone levels and oral mucosal MUC1 expression. However, other investigations found no significant differences in salivary total protein concentration, salivary flow rate, opiorphin levels, or Candida species between BMS patients and controls. These findings suggest inconsistent evidence regarding the role of salivary biomarkers in BMS pathogenesis [20].

In contrast, another study reported no significant differences in salivary total protein concentration or salivary flow rate between BMS patients and controls, though it noted alterations in salivary proteins related to stress, immunity, and inflammation; [21] additionally, serum estradiol (E2) levels positively correlated with unstimulated salivary flow and negatively with amylase activity [33], while salivary opiorphin levels did not differ between groups and likely do not drive BMS pathogenesis, with elevated systemic opiorphin possibly reflecting broader dysregulation [28].

Thyroid

In one study, patients with BMS exhibited hypothyroidism, elevated thyroid autoantibody levels, and biochemical evidence of thyroid nodules [27].

Psychological factors

Psychological factors show robust associations with burning mouth syndrome (BMS) [25]. Depression (notably when measured by BPI, PD-Q, or BDI-II) [24, 30, 31, 34, 35] or a history of [34], anxiety (based on the scale of STAI) [24, 30, 34], poorer sleep quality (based on the scale of PSQI) [24, 34], daytime sleepiness [24, 34], fear of cancer [30] and psychological distress [19] were all significantly correlated with BMS. Despite these associations, BMS did not confer a significantly increased risk of dementia or Parkinson’s disease compared with individuals without stomatitis, even after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics and comorbidities [35]. Additionally, BMS is linked to reduced attention, working memory, and executive function [34], and patients report impaired quality of life (SF-36) and deficits in cognitive domains relative to controls [34]. Changes in daily activities have been documented as consequences of BMS in some studies [19]. Among male BMS patients, higher exposure to risk factors such as smoking and alcohol was observed [17], and cancer phobia emerged as one of the factors correlated with BMS. Also, cancer phobia was among factors correlated with BMS [19]. Stressful life events are the most frequently reported triggers across studies [31, 32, 34].

Dental factors

Dentures, and dry mouth were significantly associated with BMS [25]. Logistic regression analysis of variables showed that para-functional habits such as tongue thrusting [20, 31], pressing the tongue against teeth [20], diurnal clenching, and nocturnal clenching and/or bruxism, tongue and/or cheek biting [20], lip sucking, periodontitis, and smoking [31] were among potential causative agents of BMS. No association was observed between BMS and the prevalence of Candida species [19]. Some factors considered to be BMS trigger and worsen BMS such as tooth extractions [19], dentures wearing [19], and dental treatment [34].

Food

Smells, citric and spicy foods were the main worsening features reported in a study [19]. In a study, the use of spicy substance (pepper sauce) produced a strong burning to the tongue in 56% of BMS patients, but only in 20% of controls [27].

Discussion

Overall, consistent associations with BMS were observed for female sex, postmenopausal status, psychological factors (including depression, anxiety, and stress), and oral/dental conditions such as denture use and parafunctional habits. In contrast, evidence regarding salivary biomarkers, Candida species, and diabetes remains inconsistent across studies.

This study evaluated the factors affecting the incidence of BMS using a systematic review method on 22 studies. The results indicated that BMS is correlated to female gender, age (old age), age of disease onset, marital status (married), employment status (unemployed), level of education (less than higher education), psychological disorders, cancer phobia, chronic drug use, menopause, hormone replacement therapy (HRT), and other treatments for climacteric symptoms.

BMS pathophysiology is complex and the interaction between different local, systemic, and psychological factors causes this disorder [36, 37]. In addition, BMS is a type of neuropathic pain after reducing the function of the taste system in susceptible people. In this theory, stimulation of taste-related nerves inhibits pain transmission [27, 38].

Studies conducted indicate that various factors, including hormonal changes, are directly related to the incidence of BMS. The prevalence of this disease is significantly higher in women [39–41]. Postmenopausal women may suffer from dry mouth due to decreased estrogen and progesterone levels [10]. Approximately 90% of cases of burning mouth syndrome occur between the ages of 50 and 60, three to twelve years after menopause [42, 43].

The results of past studies indicate that hormonal imbalance in many women or young men is associated with the occurrence of this disease [38]. Also, patients who receive anti-estrogen treatments also face this disorder more than others. Anti-prostate treatments have caused BMS in older men. On the other hand, menopause in women has also been introduced as a dipper factor associated with an increase in BMS. During menopause, the sudden decrease of estradiol, progesterone, and ovarian androgens causes hormonal imbalance [44].

Psychotic disorders, including depression and anxiety, are systemic factors related to the occurrence of BMS [45]. Previous studies have confirmed the relationship between psychogenic disturbances and BMS and stated that the prevalence of this relationship is higher in female than in male [7, 10]. A cohort study in Korea revealed that the prevalence of depression and anxiety increases in women by rising the prevalence of BMS, which is consistent with the present study [10]. Problems such as depression and anxiety can change a person’s perception of feelings. And the pain threshold of patients with BMS (especially in women) is lower [46, 47].

Although the relationship between BMS and many etiological factors is still speculative [44], avoiding detrimental oral habits and local irritants, quitting smoking, and maintaining good mental health may help prevent BMS [31].

A study in America reported the prevalence of this syndrome in people older than 18 years (0.7%) [48]. A study in Finland [40] and Italy [49] reported the prevalence of heartburn syndrome as much as 15 and 13%, respectively. A meta-analysis (2022) stated that stated that the prevalence of BMS in the general population and clinical patients is 1.73% and 7.72%, respectively, indicating the effect of diseases on the incidence of BMS. On the other hand, the prevalence of BMS in 50 years old patients and older is higher than people younger than 50 years old [7]. In addition, the studies have shown that the prevalence of BMS increases with age, and the risk ratio for BMS in the elderly over 70 years old is 2.58 times that of the participants in the age range of 40–49 years [36].

From a clinical perspective, identifying high-risk populations particularly postmenopausal women, individuals with psychological distress, and patients with poor oral health may facilitate early diagnosis, targeted preventive strategies, and multidisciplinary management approaches.

Limitations

There are several limitations that weaken the strength of evidence in this review. Some included articles featured small sample sizes, methodological issues, or insufficient data. The precise magnitude of the association between BMS and the various correlates has not been examined. The inherent limitations of cross-sectional designs, which cannot establish causality, are also evident in this review. Additionally, potential biases may have been introduced at different stages of the study process. Several areas warrant further investigation. In particular, more research is needed to (1) assess the impact of poor mental health on BMS and (2) evaluate potential interventions or solutions to address this issue.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that BMS is influenced by a complex interaction of demographic, hormonal, psychological, neurological, and oral/dental factors. Female sex and postmenopausal status, together with psychological distress and oral health conditions, represent the most consistently reported associations. Recognizing vulnerable individuals may support earlier intervention and improve quality of life.
