Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia: clinical outcomes and predictors of 28-day mortality in a tertiary-care cohort
Authors: Merve Nur Gucluer Kocaoglu, Hasip Kahraman, Saygın Nayman, Nurettin Erben, Elif Doyuk Kartal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB) is associated with substantial short-term mortality, but the relative contributions of methicillin resistance, treatment adequacy, and host-related factors to outcome remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and microbiological characteristics of SAB, compare methicillin-resistant (MRSA) and methicillin-susceptible (MSSA) episodes, and identify independent predictors of 28-day mortality in a Turkish tertiary-care cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult patients hospitalized with monomicrobial S. aureus bacteremia at Eskisehir Osmangazi University Faculty of Medicine Hospital between January 2019 and May 2023. Demographic, clinical, microbiological, and treatment-related data were collected retrospectively. Factors associated with 28-day all-cause mortality were assessed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsA total of 372 patients were included, of whom 293 (78.8%) had MSSA bacteremia and 79 (21.2%) had MRSA bacteremia. The two groups were comparable with respect to age, comorbidity burden, illness severity, and source or presumed focus of infection. Appropriate empirical therapy was significantly more frequent in MSSA than in MRSA bacteremia (90.8% vs. 44.3%, p < 0.001), and time to appropriate therapy was shorter in the MSSA group (median 0 vs. 2 days, p < 0.001). Despite these differences, mortality did not differ significantly between MSSA and MRSA bacteremia at 7 days (14.3% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.848), cumulative 14 days (16.7% vs. 16.5%, p = 0.955), or 28 days (30.7% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.620). Overall, 28-day mortality was 30.1% (112/372). In multivariable analysis, older age (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05; p = 0.014), higher Pitt Bacteremia Score (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.41–2.20; p < 0.001), and intensive care unit (ICU) admission (OR 2.87, 95% CI 1.62–5.09; p < 0.001) were independently associated with 28-day mortality. Methicillin resistance, appropriateness of empirical therapy, and time to appropriate treatment were not independently associated with mortality.ConclusionsIn this cohort, short-term mortality in SAB was more strongly associated with host-related factors and acute illness severity than with methicillin resistance or treatment timing. Early risk stratification at the time of diagnosis may help guide the intensity of monitoring and management in patients with SAB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13847-w.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB) remains one of the most serious bloodstream infections in clinical practice, with substantial short-term mortality despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and source control [1, 2]. In addition to the acute bacteremic episode, SAB may lead to metastatic complications such as infective endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, and other deep-seated infections, all of which may prolong treatment and worsen clinical outcomes [3, 4]. Previous cohort studies have shown that mortality associated with SAB remains considerable not only in the early period but also during longer-term follow-up, with 30-day mortality consistently reported between 15 and 30% across diverse institutional settings [5, 6].

Methicillin resistance is a major challenge in the management of SAB. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) limits empirical treatment options and has been associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and greater antimicrobial complexity [7, 8]. However, the prevalence of MRSA among S. aureus bloodstream isolates varies substantially across regions and institutions, likely reflecting differences in patient populations, infection control practices, and antimicrobial stewardship policies [1, 8]. In Turkey, MRSA remains an important component of the epidemiology of S. aureus bloodstream infections, with WHO surveillance data estimating a national rate of approximately 32.6% among bloodstream isolates [5, 9].

Despite the clinical relevance of methicillin resistance, its independent effect on short-term mortality in SAB remains controversial. Although several meta-analyses have suggested worse outcomes in MRSA bacteremia than in methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) bacteremia, results across institutional cohorts have been inconsistent [10, 11]. Increasing evidence suggests that host-related factors, including age, comorbidity burden, and acute illness severity, may be more important determinants of short-term mortality than methicillin resistance alone [6, 12]. Similarly, the prognostic impact of delayed appropriate therapy has not been uniform across studies after adjustment for confounding factors and immortal-time bias [13, 14].

In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the clinical and microbiological characteristics of patients with SAB in a Turkish tertiary-care center, to compare MSSA and MRSA bacteremia episodes, and to identify independent predictors of 28-day all-cause mortality. By providing single-center data from Turkey, this study may help inform empirical treatment decisions and early risk stratification in similar hospital settings [5].

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted at Eskisehir Osmangazi University Faculty of Medicine Hospital, a 1010-bed tertiary-care referral hospital. During the study period, the adult inpatient capacity was 809 beds, comprising 385 medical ward beds, 341 surgical ward beds, and 83 adult intensive care unit beds. We reviewed the records of patients hospitalized in clinical wards or intensive care units between January 01, 2019, and May 26, 2023, who had S. aureus isolated from blood cultures. A total of 372 patients meeting the inclusion criteria were included. During the study period, no dedicated institutional protocol specifically for MSSA or MRSA bacteremia management was in place. Vancomycin was used for MRSA bacteremia during the study period, while daptomycin was used in a limited number of cases. Vancomycin trough concentration monitoring or routine therapeutic drug monitoring was not available.

Scope and patient selection

The inclusion criteria were being 18 years of age or older, having at least one positive blood culture for S. aureus, and being followed in the hospital for at least 72 hours. Only monomicrobial bacteremia cases were included in the analysis. For patients with multiple bacteremia episodes during the study period, only the first episode was evaluated. Polymicrobial bacteremia cases, patients with missing clinical data, and recurrent episodes from the same patient were excluded from the study. Definitions

Bacteremia was defined as the presence of at least one positive S. aureus blood culture consistent with clinical and/or laboratory findings. Empirical therapy was defined as the treatment initiated before the final report of culture results, and the use of an agent to which the isolate was susceptible during this period was defined as appropriate empirical therapy. Receiving at least one susceptible antibiotic during either the empirical or targeted therapy periods was evaluated as appropriate therapy. Time to appropriate therapy was calculated in days as the interval between the collection date of the first positive blood culture and the initiation date of the first susceptible treatment. Acquisition setting was classified as community-acquired, healthcare-associated, or nosocomial. Nosocomial bacteremia was defined as bacteremia occurring more than 48 hours after hospital admission. Episodes occurring within 48 hours of admission were classified as healthcare-associated if they met the criteria proposed by Friedman et al. [15], and as community-acquired if no healthcare-associated criteria were present. Immunosuppression was defined as the presence of active malignancy, chemotherapy, use of immunosuppressive drugs, or underlying immune deficiency disorders. The source or presumed focus of bacteremia was assigned after review of clinical findings, microbiological results, radiological findings, device history, and relevant specialist notes. Primary bacteremia was defined as SAB without a definite identifiable focus after review of available documentation, and episodes that could not be clearly assigned to another source according to predefined criteria were classified as primary bacteremia. Catheter-related infection was defined as S. aureus bacteremia in the presence of an intravascular catheter, supported by simultaneous S. aureus growth in blood culture and catheter or catheter-tip culture. Organ-specific sources were assigned when supported by compatible clinical, imaging, microbiological, or surgical findings. Cardiovascular infection included infective endocarditis, cardiac device infection, or another documented intravascular cardiovascular focus identified during the SAB episode. Other sources included central nervous system, intraabdominal, and urinary tract infection foci. The day the first positive blood culture was obtained was designated as the index date; the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and Pitt Bacteremia Score (PBS) were calculated based on the clinical and laboratory data obtained on the day of blood culture collection. Seven-day, cumulative 14-day, and 28-day mortality were defined as all-cause death occurring within 7, 14, and 28 days after the index date, respectively.

Microbiological methods

Blood samples were collected under aseptic conditions into BACTEC™ Lytic/10 Anaerobic/F and BACTEC™ Plus Aerobic/F bottles (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA) and incubated in the BACTEC™ FX system for a maximum of 7 days. Gram staining was performed on bottles providing a positive signal. Samples were subcultured onto Sheep Blood Agar, Chocolate Agar, Eosin-Methylene Blue (EMB) Agar, and Inhibitory Mold Agar. Following 24–48 hours of incubation, pathogen identification was performed using a matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry system (MALDI Biotyper; Bruker Daltonics). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using an automated broth microdilution system (VITEK 2). The results were evaluated according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines. Methicillin resistance was determined based on oxacillin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values, and isolates were categorized into two groups as MRSA and MSSA [16].

Data collection

Demographic (age, sex), clinical, and microbiological data, along with information regarding treatment processes, were obtained retrospectively from the electronic hospital records. The recorded variables included comorbid conditions, CCI, PBS, risk factors for healthcare-associated infections, use of invasive devices (urinary catheter, central venous catheter, cardiac devices), source or presumed focus of infection, echocardiographic findings, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, empirical and targeted therapy data, and time to initiation of appropriate treatment. In addition, ICU admission, hospital and ICU length of stay, and 28-day mortality data were recorded. Survival status within 28 days after the index date was determined from hospital electronic medical records, including review of available post-discharge records for patients discharged before day 28. No patients had missing 28-day outcome data.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Eskisehir Osmangazi University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: June 20, 2023; Decision No: 35). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Eskişehir Osmangazi University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as number and percentage. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables Factors associated with 28-day mortality were first evaluated in univariable analyses. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis, together with treatment-related variables considered clinically relevant a priori (appropriate empirical therapy and time to appropriate therapy), were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Multicollinearity among variables included in the multivariable model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and infection sources

A total of 372 patients were included, of whom 293 (78.8%) had MSSA bacteremia and 79 (21.2%) had MRSA bacteremia. The two groups were similar in terms of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, comorbidities, and illness severity as assessed by the CCI and PBS (Table 1). The distribution of acquisition setting did not differ significantly between MSSA and MRSA bacteremia (p = 0.166), although nosocomial episodes were numerically more frequent in the MRSA group (32.9% vs. 25.3%). Healthcare-related and host risk factors were otherwise comparable between the groups. However, the median time from hospital admission to bacteremia onset was significantly longer in the MRSA group than in the MSSA group [3 (IQR: 0–7) vs. 1 (IQR: 0–5) day, p = 0.032]. Treatment characteristics and timing

Treatment-related variables differed markedly between the groups. Appropriate empirical therapy was significantly more common in patients with MSSA bacteremia than in those with MRSA bacteremia (90.8% vs. 44.3%, p < 0.001). Among the 35 patients with MRSA bacteremia who received appropriate empirical anti-MRSA therapy, vancomycin was used in 32 patients and daptomycin in 3 patients. Likewise, when empirical and adjusted regimens were considered together, the overall rate of appropriate therapy remained higher in the MSSA group (97.3% vs. 78.5%, p < 0.001). Among patients who eventually received appropriate therapy (n = 347), initiation occurred significantly earlier in MSSA bacteremia [0 (IQR: 0–1) vs. 2 (IQR: 0–5) days, p < 0.001]. The 25 patients who did not receive appropriate therapy (8 MSSA and 17 MRSA) were excluded from the timing analysis.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical severity at presentation was similar between the groups, with no significant differences in ICU admission rates (43.7% vs. 50.6%, p = 0.330) or PBS [0 (IQR: 0–1) in both groups, p = 0.727]. Echocardiography was performed more frequently in the MSSA group (32.4% vs. 19.0%, p = 0.021), whereas vegetation was detected at similar rates among evaluated patients (12.6% vs. 20.0%, p = 0.422). Although ICU length of stay was comparable, the median hospital stay was significantly longer in the MRSA group [17 (IQR: 9–33) vs. 14 (IQR: 7–28) days, p = 0.041]. Early mortality was also similar between the groups: 7-day mortality was 14.3% in the MSSA group and 15.2% in the MRSA group (p = 0.848), while cumulative 14-day mortality was 16.7% and 16.5%, respectively (p = 0.955). Similarly, 28-day mortality did not differ significantly between the groups (30.7% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.620).

Risk factors for 28-day mortality

Data on factors associated with 28-day mortality in patients with S. aureus bacteremia are presented in Table 3. The 28-day mortality rate for the entire cohort was 30.1% (112/372), with 260 patients surviving and 112 dying within 28 days. Non-survivors were significantly older than survivors [median 73 (IQR: 63–80) vs. 66 (IQR: 56–73) years, p < 0.001]. Although the frequencies of major comorbidities were similar between the groups, non-survivors had a higher comorbidity burden, as reflected by the CCI [4 (IQR: 3–6) vs. 4 (IQR: 2–6), p = 0.003]. No significant difference was observed between the groups regarding gender distribution (56.2% vs. 60.0% male, p = 0.576). Multivariable analysis of risk factors for mortality

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, older age, higher PBS, and ICU admission were identified as independent predictors of 28-day mortality (Table 4). Each one-year increase in age was associated with a 3% increase in the odds of death (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.05; p = 0.014). Similarly, each one-point increase in PBS was associated with a 76% increase in the odds of 28-day mortality (OR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.41–2.20; p < 0.001). ICU admission was also independently associated with higher mortality (OR, 2.87; 95% CI, 1.62–5.09; p < 0.001). In contrast, CCI (p = 0.329), healthcare-associated/nosocomial infection (p = 0.258), appropriate empirical therapy (p = 0.341), and time to appropriate therapy (p = 0.935) were not independently associated with 28-day mortality in the adjusted model. No substantial multicollinearity was detected among variables included in the multivariable model; all VIF values were below 1.40 and tolerance values were above 0.71. Discussion

SAB remains one of the most consequential bloodstream infections in clinical practice, with an estimated incidence of 10–30 cases per 100,000 population annually, a case fatality rate of 15–30%, and approximately 300,000 deaths worldwide each year [1, 2]. In this retrospective cohort of 372 patients from a Turkish tertiary-care center, the 28-day all-cause mortality rate was 30.1%, placing our findings at the upper end of the reported range. The principal finding of this study is that older age, higher PBS, and ICU admission were independently associated with 28-day mortality. In contrast, methicillin resistance, appropriateness of empirical therapy, and time to appropriate treatment were not independently associated with mortality in the multivariable analysis.

MRSA prevalence and baseline comparability of groups

The proportion of MRSA among SAB cases in our cohort was 21.2%, which is somewhat lower than the 29.5% reported by Borcak et al. from another Turkish tertiary-care center [5]. The latter was reported to be consistent with WHO 2023 surveillance data, which estimated a national MRSA rate of approximately 32.6% in Turkey [9]. Such inter-institutional variation is not unexpected, as MRSA prevalence is known to vary substantially across centers depending on local infection control practices, patient populations, and antimicrobial stewardship policies [2, 8]. The MSSA and MRSA groups were well balanced at baseline with respect to age, sex, and comorbidity burden, as reflected by the CCI, as well as illness severity, as assessed by the PBS, thereby strengthening the validity of between-group comparisons. Notably, the interval from hospital admission to bacteremia onset was significantly longer in the MRSA group (median 3 vs. 1 days, p = 0.032), consistent with the predominantly nosocomial epidemiology of MRSA, whereby prolonged hospitalization is a well-established risk factor for healthcare-associated acquisition [2, 17]. The distribution of source or presumed focus was similar between the groups, with primary bacteremia being the most common category in both (57.7% vs. 57.0%). The relatively high proportion of primary bacteremia in our cohort likely reflects this conservative source-attribution approach. Episodes were classified as primary bacteremia when no definite focus could be identified from available clinical, radiological, and microbiological documentation, or when the source could not be clearly assigned according to predefined criteria. In the retrospective design, cardiovascular foci such as infective endocarditis could not always be distinguished with certainty as primary foci rather than complications of SAB.

MRSA bacteremia and clinical outcomes

Although pooled meta-analytic evidence generally supports higher mortality in MRSA than in MSSA bacteremia, our study did not detect a significant difference in 28-day mortality; mortality was 30.7% in MSSA bacteremia and 27.8% in MRSA bacteremia (p = 0.620). Similar findings were observed for earlier outcomes, with no significant difference between MSSA and MRSA bacteremia in either 7-day or cumulative 14-day mortality. This apparent discrepancy should be interpreted in light of the marked heterogeneity across pooled analyses, as Adeiza and Aminul reported a pooled OR of 2.35 and RR of 2.01 but also considerable between-study heterogeneity (I2 : 90–91%) [11]. In our cohort, MSSA and MRSA groups were broadly comparable with respect to age, comorbidity burden, illness severity, and source distribution, which may have attenuated a resistance-related mortality signal. A broadly similar inconsistency has been reported in other settings; for example, Urriago-Osorio et al. found no excess early mortality associated with MRSA in an ICU cohort of S. aureus infections, with adjusted analyses showing lower 7-day mortality in the MRSA group [10]. In addition, despite substantially higher resistance rates among MRSA isolates to several non-β-lactam agents—including moxifloxacin, clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, fusidic acid, and rifampicin—all isolates in both groups remained susceptible to vancomycin, daptomycin, and linezolid, underscoring that effective targeted therapy remained available regardless of methicillin resistance status. Taken together, these findings suggest that methicillin resistance alone may not necessarily translate into worse short-term outcomes when baseline severity is comparable and effective targeted anti-MRSA therapy remains available [11].

Treatment appropriateness and the timing paradox

One of the notable findings of this study was the marked difference in empirical treatment adequacy between MSSA and MRSA bacteremia. Patients with MRSA bacteremia were substantially less likely to receive appropriate empirical therapy and experienced a longer time to active treatment. This disparity is largely expected in routine practice, as standard empirical regimens are often beta-lactam based and therefore active against MSSA but not MRSA; escalation to vancomycin or another anti-MRSA agent generally depends on susceptibility results [2, 7]. Despite these treatment differences, mortality was similar between the two groups. This observation is in line with previous studies suggesting that the timing of appropriate therapy alone may not be the principal determinant of prognosis in SAB [13, 14]. Rather, host-related factors and the severity of acute illness appear to play a more dominant role in shaping outcomes. This interpretation is also broadly consistent with the observation that 25 patients (6.7%) never received appropriate therapy during their clinical course, yet most survived; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of patients and the possibility of residual confounding.

Univariate predictors of 28-day mortality

In the univariate analysis, non-survivors were older, had higher CCI and PBS values, and were more frequently admitted to the ICU. Healthcare-associated/nosocomial infection was more frequent among survivors (72.7% vs. 59.8%, p = 0.019), likely reflecting differences in source or presumed focus distribution. Catheter-related infection was more common among survivors (25.4% vs. 7.1%), whereas primary bacteremia predominated among non-survivors (75.9% vs. 49.6%, p < 0.001). This pattern may be relevant because catheter-related bacteremia often represents a more identifiable and removable focus, whereas primary bacteremia may include unrecognized or uncontrolled foci, consistent with previous reports on the prognostic importance of eradicable focus and catheter removal [18, 19].

Echocardiography was performed in only a subset of patients and was less frequent in non-survivors (16.1% vs. 31.5%, p = 0.003), whereas vegetation rates among evaluated patients were comparable between groups (19.4% vs. 11.4%, p = 0.340). During the study period, echocardiography was not performed systematically for all SAB episodes, and the decision to perform echocardiography was based on clinical judgment. Therefore, the low overall rate of documented infective endocarditis in our cohort should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect underrecognition rather than a truly low prevalence. This is clinically relevant, as endocarditis is one of the most important complications of SAB and was independently associated with higher infection-related mortality in the multicenter prospective cohort reported by van der Vaart et al. [20]. Importantly, methicillin resistance status, appropriateness of empirical therapy, and time to appropriate treatment did not differ significantly between survivors and non-survivors at the univariate level, in line with the null associations observed in the multivariable model.

Independent predictors of 28-day mortality

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, older age, higher PBS, and ICU admission were identified as independent predictors of 28-day mortality. Each additional year of age was associated with a 3% increase in the odds of death (OR: 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.05; p = 0.014), consistent with previous studies identifying advancing age as an important determinant of adverse outcome in SAB [6, 12]. Each one-point increment in PBS was associated with a 76% increase in the odds of 28-day mortality (OR: 1.76; 95% CI, 1.41–2.20; p < 0.001), underscoring the central role of acute illness severity at presentation in determining short-term outcome [19, 20]. ICU admission was independently associated with nearly threefold higher odds of 28-day mortality (OR: 2.87; 95% CI, 1.62–5.09; p < 0.001), likely reflecting both the severity of the index episode and the vulnerability of critically ill patients [6, 12]. These findings are in line with prior reports showing that age and markers of acute clinical deterioration are among the most consistent predictors of mortality in SAB.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It included a relatively large cohort of patients with monomicrobial S. aureus bacteremia from a tertiary-care center over more than four years, enabling detailed comparison of MSSA and MRSA episodes within the same institutional setting. In addition, baseline characteristics, source or presumed focus, treatment-related variables, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and short-term outcomes were evaluated together, allowing a comprehensive assessment of the relationship between methicillin resistance, treatment adequacy, and mortality. The use of both univariable and multivariable analyses further strengthened the evaluation of factors associated with 28-day mortality.

This study also has important limitations. Its retrospective single-center design limits generalizability and introduces the possibility of residual confounding. Source attribution was based on available clinical, radiological, and microbiological documentation, which may have contributed to the relatively high proportion of cases classified as primary bacteremia. Echocardiography was not performed systematically in all patients, raising the possibility of underrecognition of infective endocarditis or other deep-seated foci. Time to appropriate therapy was recorded in days rather than exact hours; therefore, patients classified as receiving appropriate therapy on day 0 may have had different actual delays in treatment initiation, which may have introduced some misclassification and reduced the precision of the timing analysis. Because time to appropriate therapy is a treatment-timing variable, immortal time bias cannot be fully excluded, although a sensitivity analysis excluding this variable yielded similar results. Finally, persistent bacteremia, complicated infection, total antibiotic treatment duration, and management components that may influence prognosis, including infectious diseases consultation, source-control procedures or timing, surgical intervention, and dosing adequacy, were not assessed systematically.

In conclusion, in this cohort of 372 patients with S. aureus bacteremia, 28-day mortality was independently associated with older age, higher PBS, and ICU admission, whereas methicillin resistance, appropriateness of empirical therapy, and time to appropriate treatment were not independently associated with outcome. These findings suggest that short-term prognosis in SAB is driven more by host-related factors and acute clinical severity than by methicillin resistance itself, and underscore the importance of early risk stratification at the time of diagnosis to guide the intensity of monitoring and management.
