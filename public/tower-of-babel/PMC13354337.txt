Superinfected appendiceal mucocele with a giant appendicolith: a rare entity in a common surgical emergency – a case report
Authors: Bhawesh Bhattarai, Bikash K. Shah, Prashant Ghimire, Akriti Dahal, Badal Karki, Abhishek Bhattarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Appendiceal mucocele (AM) is a rare surgical emergency, commonly caused by an appendicolith. An appendicolith larger than 2 cm is termed “giant,” which is rare and carries a high risk of perforation. This case highlights the diagnostic and surgical challenges of a superinfected AM complicated by a giant appendicolith.Case presentation:A 25-year-old woman presented to our emergency department with a 12-hour history of right lower quadrant pain, non-bilious vomiting, and fever with chills and rigor. On examination, her right iliac fossa was tender with rebound tenderness and a positive psoas sign. Ultrasonography showed an AM and an accompanying appendicolith. Computed tomography showed a hyperdense calculus measuring approximately 24 × 22 mm at the base of the appendix, with a maximum appendiceal diameter of 22.6 mm. Open appendectomy was performed, and histopathological examination found no signs of mucosal hyperplasia or neoplasia.Discussion:The coexistence of a superinfected AM with a giant appendicolith is exceptionally rare. Preoperative imaging findings, including an appendiceal diameter of more than 15 mm and peri-appendiceal fat stranding, raised the suspicion of mucinous neoplasm; however, the absence of mural nodularity and wall irregularity ruled out malignancy. Open appendectomy was performed to minimize the risk of mucocele rupture and pseudomyxoma peritonei. Histopathological examination remained the definitive diagnostic modality.Conclusion:A superinfected mucocele presenting with a giant appendicolith is extremely rare and poses a diagnostic challenge. Early surgical intervention using an open approach is recommended to prevent rupture and the catastrophic complication of pseudomyxoma peritonei. This case highlights the importance of vigilant clinical and radiological evaluation in such presentations.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common causes of emergency surgery, with a lifetime incidence of 7–10%[1]. The condition is diagnosed clinically; however, laboratory values and radiologic imaging are often used in clinical practice to increase diagnostic accuracy[2]. In most cases, appendicular lumen obstruction, either by lymphoid hyperplasia, epithelial hyperplasia, appendicoliths, or tumors, explains the pathophysiology of acute appendicitis, causing an increase in pressure within the lumen, leading to the collapse of lymphatic vessels and veins, reduced arterial blood flow, and ultimately resulting in tissue death and perforation[3].HIGHLIGHTSAcute appendicitis is a common surgical emergency.Appendicolith causes early gangrene of the inflamed appendix.Appendicolith is a common cause of mucocele of the appendix.Mucocele of the appendix might mimic a mucinous neoplasm of the appendix, causing a diagnostic dilemma.

Appendicoliths are present in 3% of the general population and in 10% of acute appendicitis[4]. The appendix can develop calcified deposits that form from densely packed stool and sometimes include mineral deposits, presenting a higher risk of perforation or abscess formation. An appendicolith is termed a giant one when it is more than 2 cm, but this is extremely rare[5].

Mucocele of the appendix is another rare clinical entity that may exhibit either benign or malignant behavior. A simple obstructive mucocele can be caused by an appendicolith, stricture, or epithelial hyperplasia, with an incidence rate of up to 29%[6]. However, malignant mucoceles of the appendix can originate from mucinous cystadenoma in about 31%–34% of cases and from mucinous adenocarcinoma in up to 5% of cases[6]. Benign and malignant mucoceles require different surgical approaches. Hence, preoperative radiological diagnosis is of utmost importance. Additionally, intact removal of the mucocele is crucial to prevent pseudomyxoma peritonei[6]. Appendiceal mucocele (AM) might become superinfected, which is defined radiologically as the presence of an air-fluid level or intraluminal air bubbles within the mucocele[7].

Case history

A 25-year-old female presented to the emergency department with complaints of right lower quadrant pain for 12 hours, associated with non-bilious vomiting. She reported fever with a maximum temperature of 101°F (degrees Fahrenheit), along with chills and rigor. Bowel and bladder habits were normal. She is sexually active, with her last menstrual period 8 days ago, and denies any per vaginal discharge or bleeding. On examination, there was tenderness and rebound tenderness over the right iliac fossa, with a positive psoas sign. Laboratory findings showed leukocytosis with a total leucocyte count (TLC) of 16 000/mm3 and a neutrophilic predominance of 82%, with other parameters being within normal limits. Ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis showed a mucocele of the appendix with an associated appendicolith. Given the radiological suspicion of a mucinous neoplasm of the appendix, computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis was subsequently performed, which revealed a 24 × 22 mm-sized hyperdense calculus at the base of the appendix, with a maximum appendiceal diameter of 22.6 mm, without obvious wall calcification (Fig. 1). The patient was subsequently planned for an open appendectomy, with intraoperative findings of an appendicolith measuring 3 × 2 cm at the shaft of the appendix with a healthy base. The appendix was gangrenous with perforation at both the tip and the shaft (Fig. 3). The appendix was sent for histopathological examination. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged with antibiotics: tablet cefixime 200 mg to be taken every 12 hours and tablet metronidazole 500 mg to be taken every 8 hours for 1 week. Histopathological examination showed appendiceal mucosa lined by columnar cells admixed with goblet cells with focal ulceration. The lamina propria showed hyperplastic lymphoid follicles, and the muscularis propria showed neutrophilic infiltration without any evidence of mucosal hyperplasia or neoplasia (Fig. 4). On follow-up at 1 week, the wound was healthy with no signs of surgical site infection. Discussion

Acute appendicitis mainly occurs due to the blockage of the appendix lumen by lymphoid hyperplasia or appendicolith. Lymphoid hyperplasia is generally the case in children, while appendicolith is more common in adults. Moreover, foreign bodies, parasites, and tumors have also been identified as contributing factors[8,9]. A high prevalence of appendicolith in appendicitis is associated with perforated and gangrenous appendicitis[10]. A study by Singh et al found that in perforated appendicitis, the prevalence of appendicoliths was 39.4% (54/137), compared to 14.6% (124/849) in non-perforated cases [odds ratio (OR): 3.79; 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.5–5.6; P < 0.05][10]. Among adults, appendicolith prevalence in perforated appendicitis was 27.5% (22/80) versus 12.0% (77/642) in non-perforated cases (OR: 2.77; 95% CI: 1.6–4.7; P < 0.05)[10]. The largest reported giant appendicolith was 3.5 cm in size[11]. Appendicoliths are usually asymptomatic unless luminal obstruction is present. Patients typically present with classic signs of appendicitis, including crampy pain in the right lower abdomen that is associated with guarding and rebound tenderness. Appendicoliths can also lead to unusual symptoms, causing appendicitis to mimic conditions like ureteral colic[12].

AM is rare, with an estimated incidence of 0.7%, mainly affecting women aged 50–70 years[10], and its presentation in a 25-year-old is atypical. The Peritoneal Surface Oncology Group International (PSOGI) classifies mucinous lesions of the appendix into two types: non-neoplastic mucinous lesions, such as simple mucoceles or retention cysts resulting from inflammation or obstruction, and neoplastic mucinous lesions, which include mucinous appendiceal neoplasms, mucinous adenocarcinomas, and serrated appendicular polyps[13]. The most frequent non-neoplastic cause is a simple mucus retention cyst resulting from luminal obstruction. These cysts typically measure less than 2 cm, while neoplastic mucoceles are generally larger at the time of diagnosis[10].

On ultrasonography, a mucocele characteristically appears as an ovoid cystic mass in the right lower quadrant with variable internal echogenicity[14]. On a CT scan, a mucocele looks like an enlarged appendix containing uniformly low-density material. Malignant mucoceles, caused by adenocarcinoma, are characterized by mural nodularity and irregular wall thickening[14]. A study by Pickhardt et al proposed that an appendix diameter beyond 15 mm is an indication of a potential neoplasm[15]. An infected mucocele may show intraluminal gas or air-fluid levels, wall thickening, and fat stranding, which can occur in both benign and malignant mucoceles[15].

Clinically, appendicitis associated with or without mucocele is indistinguishable. It has been reported that 50% of mucoceles present with symptoms of typical appendicitis[16]. Detecting an AM should include a search for extraluminal mucin in the peri-appendiceal space, peritoneal cavity, and on the surfaces of abdominal and pelvic organs, such as the ovaries and bowel[17].

The treatment for an AM is mainly surgical, but the specific surgical approach varies depending on whether the mucocele is benign or malignant. An open surgical approach is preferred because it has a higher chance of preventing the rupture of a malignant mucocele. Such a rupture could cause mucin to spread into the peritoneal cavity, leading to pseudomyxoma peritonei, a condition with a poor prognosis that may require further treatment with cytoreductive surgery and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy[18]. Two types of surgical resection are appropriate for AM: appendectomy or right hemicolectomy. Right hemicolectomy is performed for patients with confirmed positive appendiceal or distal ileocolic lymph nodes from a biopsy, inadequate resection margins, or histopathology showing non-mucinous neoplasm. This procedure does not provide a survival benefit for patients with mucinous appendiceal tumors that have spread to the peritoneum[18].

Conclusion

Giant appendicoliths are rare and are often associated with acute appendicitis, with an increased risk of gangrene and perforation, for which urgent appendectomy is warranted. AM is itself a rare entity, and an underlying appendicolith might complicate it with superinfection or perforation. AM of malignant etiology, if perforated, might result in peritoneal seeding (pseudomyxoma peritonei) and has a grave prognosis. Thus, vigilant clinical and radiological evaluation is essential for AM with a giant appendicolith.
