Vegetation cover change and classification-based zonation of Fanjing Mountain using google earth engine

Rongrong Wang, Wengang Cui, Suihua Liu, Ting Yu, Fei Ning, Qingsong Xu, Dan Hu

ABSTRACT
This study utilizes the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform, integrating Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery from 1987 to 2022 alongside terrain data to develop a technical system for “vegetation cover inversion–classification–vertical belt spectrum analysis.” The Fractional Vegetation Cover (FVC) is estimated using a pixel dichotomy model, while spatiotemporal changes are evaluated through Theil-Sen trend analysis and Mann-Kendall tests. A multi-feature combination scheme has been designed, employing random forest models to enhance forest classification and analyze altitude zoning characteristics. The findings indicate that: (1) Over nearly 36 years, FVC in Fanjing Mountain has demonstrated a pattern of “fluctuating decline–adjustment–jump increase,” with 2007 serving as a pivotal turning point; notably, 41.65% of the area exhibited significant improvement, particularly in the southern region; (2) The classification model that integrates terrain features, red-edge index, and texture attributes (T9 scheme) achieves an overall accuracy of 77.88%, with terrain factors being the most influential in significantly enhancing the identification of shrub meadows and evergreen broadleaf forests; (3) Distinct vertical zoning of vegetation is observed: evergreen broadleaf forests dominate lower to mid-elevations (750–1550 m), deciduous broadleaf forests and coniferous forests are found at mid-to-high elevations, while shrub meadows are concentrated above 2400 m, forming a gradient succession sequence. This study provides methodological support and scientific evidence for ecological conservation efforts in subtropical mountain regions.

1. Introduction Global climate change, exacerbated by human activities, has resulted in the ongoing degradation of both the structure and function of terrestrial ecosystems [ 1 , 2 ]. As a fundamental component of these ecosystems, vegetation plays a crucial role in influencing regional ecological security through its functions such as carbon sequestration, soil and water conservation, and biodiversity maintenance [ 3 – 6 ]. The current warming climate and the increasing frequency of extreme events have accelerated processes like deforestation and vegetation degradation. Mountain ecosystems, characterized by their vertical gradients, are particularly vulnerable to climate change; phenomena such as shifts in forest belts and changes in species composition have emerged as significant ecological warning signals. In this context, accurately analyzing the spatiotemporal evolution and vertical differentiation patterns of vegetation cover is essential for informing effective ecological management strategies. Fanjing Mountain is recognized as the “ecological gene bank at 27°N latitude” [ 7 – 9 ]. However, in recent years, climate warming and drying, along with increased human disturbances, have resulted in blurred boundaries between forest types and disordered vertical belt spectrum within this region. Traditional monitoring methods are constrained by limitations in temporal and spatial resolution as well as the complexity of the terrain, making precise and dynamic assessments challenging. Conventional vegetation surveys depend on ground plots and manual interpretation; while these approaches yield high data accuracy, they are hindered by elevated costs, lengthy timeframes, and inadequate large-scale continuity [ 10 ]. Remote sensing technology has enabled extensive monitoring of vegetation coverage through spectral indices such as NDVI [ 11 – 15 ], yet the presence of clouds in mountainous environments leads to significant data loss in optical remote sensing. Furthermore, existing algorithms demonstrate insufficient adaptability to complex terrains [ 16 ]. Although multi-source data fusion techniques can partially mitigate the limitations associated with single datasets, challenges regarding spatio-temporal consistency and model robustness persist. The Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform offers a new paradigm for long-term ecological monitoring due to its PB-level remote sensing data capabilities and cloud computing advantages [ 17 – 19 ]. however, its potential application for optimizing mountain vegetation classification models and analyzing vertical belt spectrum remains largely unexplored. Additionally, understanding the coupling mechanisms between changes in vegetation coverage and carbon cycling alongside climate feedbacks—along with elucidating the regulatory role of forest vertical belt spectrum in energy balance—continues to be at the forefront of research endeavors [ 20 , 21 ]. Current studies still exhibit deficiencies related to multi-source data fusion processes, terrain correction methodologies, and quantification efforts concerning vertical belt spectrum within mountain ecosystems. This study focuses on Fanjing Mountain as a representative area, integrating multi-source remote sensing data from platforms such as Landsat and Sentinel-2. A random forest classification model that incorporates terrain factors is developed based on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform, establishing a technical system comprising “coverage inversion - type classification - vertical belt spectrum analysis.” The research aims to overcome the technical challenges associated with vegetation monitoring in mountainous regions characterized by frequent cloud cover. It seeks to elucidate the driving mechanisms behind the vertical differentiation of subtropical mountain forests and provide methodological support for the conservation of ecologically fragile areas located at similar latitudes. 2. Materials and methods 2.1. Overview of the study Area The Fanjing Mountain National Nature Reserve is situated in the northeastern region of Guizhou Province, at the transitional zone between the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau and the Xiangxi Mountainous Region. Its highest peak, Fenghuang Mountain, reaches an elevation of 2,572 meters. The reserve boasts a distinctive geological landscape characterized by stepped and layered terrain with a vertical difference exceeding 2,000 meters, dome-shaped structural mountain ranges, and four distinct geomorphic units. The subtropical monsoon climate has fostered a complete vertical climate zone within the reserve. Additionally, moist evergreen broad-leaved forests cover 76.1% of the area, providing essential habitat for endangered species such as the Guizhou golden snub-nosed monkey. Serving as a refuge for organisms during the Quaternary ice age and as a vital water source for the middle reaches of the Yangtze River, this reserve integrates both karst and metamorphic rock ecosystems. With its 46 vegetation communities and comprehensive soil zonation spectrum, Fanjing Mountain National Nature Reserve stands out as a natural laboratory for studying biodiversity and ecological evolution. Fractional Vegetation Cover (FVC), defined as the ratio of the projected area of vegetation to the total surface area [ 23 ], serves as a vital indicator for quantifying surface vegetation density, assessing growth conditions, and evaluating ecological health through remote sensing techniques. The pixel dichotomy model, a widely recognized classical method in FVC remote sensing inversion, is grounded in the theory of mixed pixel decomposition. This method operates on the core physical assumption that, constrained by the spatial resolution of remote sensing imagery, the spectral signal of an individual mixed pixel is a linear combination of pure vegetation and pure non-vegetation (e.g., bare soil) areas. By constructing a linear spectral mixture equation, the pixel’s reflectance ( R ) can be expressed as a weighted sum of the pure vegetation reflectance ( R v ) and pure non-vegetation reflectance ( R s ), where the weight is the FVC. Equation (1) represents the mathematical expression: R = F V C · R v + ( 1 − F V C ) · R s (1) Solving this equation yields the foundational FVC formula ( Equation 2 ): F V C = R − R s R v − R s (2) To overcome the difficulty of acquiring large-scale measured reflectance data, NDVI is substituted into this model based on the spectral response mechanism of vegetation cover. Theoretically, the extreme NDVI values in endmember pixels are characterized as follows: pixels with full vegetation cover approach 1, whereas bare surfaces or non-vegetated areas approach 0. Accordingly, the FVC estimation model used in this study is formulated as Equation (3) : F V C = N D V I − N D V I s o i l N D V I v e g − N D V I s o i l (3) Considering the interannual climate variability in the Fanjingshan region, residual sensor differences, and the uneven distribution of extreme pixels, the endmember parameters are dynamically calibrated on an annual basis. Specifically, using cumulative frequency statistics for the annual NDVI data from 1987 to 2022, the 5th and 95th percentiles are designated as the yearly NDVI_min and NDVI_max, respectively. This method ensures that the FVC accurately reflects the relative coverage level of the corresponding year, effectively mitigating early vegetation underestimation and spurious long-term trends caused by fixed thresholds. In comparison to regression or machine learning models, the pixel dichotomy model boasts fewer parameters, faster computation times, and reduced dependence on sample data. These attributes render it particularly suitable for large-scale and long-term dynamic monitoring applications. The research is based on a comprehensive analysis of long-term Landsat TM/ETM/OLI series images spanning from 1987 to 2022. Utilizing the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform, we conducted cloud removal, radiometric correction, and other preprocessing techniques to effectively mitigate the impacts of cloud contamination and atmospheric disturbances. Upon obtaining the preprocessed Landsat series imagery, the NDVI is first calculated scene-by-scene using the normalized ratio of the near-infrared and red bands. Subsequently, the Maximum Value Composite (MVC) method is applied to extract the maximum pixel-wise NDVI within each month to generate the monthly NDVI data. These monthly outputs are further composited into annual maximums, ultimately constructing the 1987–2022 annual NDVI dataset for the Fanjingshan Nature Reserve. The NDVI values were subsequently converted into Fractional Vegetation Cover (FVC) using a pixel dichotomy model. This process facilitated the construction of an annual FVC dataset from 1987 to 2022 as well as a spatial distribution map illustrating the average FVC over a span of 36 years, thereby enabling an evaluation of trends in vegetation coverage changes. 2.3.2. Trend analysis method. The Theil-Sen Median trend estimation method is a robust non-parametric statistical algorithm specifically designed for the analysis of trends in time series data. This synergistic analytical approach not only enhances the robustness of trend estimations but also circumvents limitations associated with assumptions about data distribution due to its non-parametric nature. Consequently, it provides a reliable dual verification mechanism for trend analysis in various fields such as ecological environment monitoring and climate change research. Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning algorithm that consists of multiple decision trees, which collectively yield robust predictions through majority voting for classification tasks or averaging for regression tasks. The model construction process involves the following steps: 1 generating training subsets from the original dataset with replacement using the Bootstrap method; 2 randomly selecting a subset of features to determine optimal splits at each node; 3 fully developing decision trees until specified termination conditions are met; and 4 aggregating the results from each tree. Forest vegetation classification 3.2.1. Vegetation classification system and sample construction. Based on the ecological characteristics of the Fanjing Mountain forest, this study established an integrated data system encompassing “ground-air-space.” The surface coverage was categorized into six distinct types: shrub meadow, bamboo forest, evergreen broad-leaved forest, coniferous forest, deciduous broad-leaved forest, and others. This classification system was developed in conjunction with the “Vegetation Map of the People’s Republic of China (1:1,000,000)” and supported by field investigations.Sample data collection utilized second-class surveys of forest resources alongside aerial photography and high-resolution images from Google Earth. A strategy that combined GIS spatially balanced sampling with stratified sampling was employed to generate a total of 3,821 valid samples. These samples were subsequently divided into training and validation sets at an 80:20 ratio to ensure both spatial independence and temporal consistency. In the realm of remote sensing feature extraction, the system integrates a total of 41 indicators, encompassing Sentinel-2 multispectral bands, vegetation indices, red-edge indices, texture features, and terrain factors to construct a comprehensive joint feature set that includes spectral, spatial, and terrain dimensions. Among these indicators, 10 key bands from Sentinel-2 are selected to develop a spectral response model. Classification experiment. Nine feature combination schemes (T1 - T9) were developed in this research, progressively incorporating multispectral, topographic, vegetation index, red-edge index, and texture features to assess the contribution of each feature to classification accuracy. Unlike unsupervised dimensionality reduction techniques such as Factor Analysis (FA), which generate artificial components through linear combinations and obscure the original physical and ecological meanings of remote sensing variables, this progressive evaluation preserves the specific interpretability essential for mountain vegetation analysis. Furthermore, rather than relying solely on a simple classical classification method, this study designed the 9-experiment framework as a systematic ablation study. By establishing a simple multispectral classification as the baseline (T1), this methodology justifies the advantages of the optimized multi-source model (T9) by quantitatively evaluating the independent, synergistic, and redundant effects of incrementally added features. The classification model employed the random forest algorithm on the Google Earth Engine platform, with a training set and validation set divided in an 80:20 ratio. Among these schemes, T1 served as the baseline configuration utilizing only multispectral bands; T2 introduced topographic features that significantly enhanced the identification capability of shrub meadows; T3 added vegetation indices but demonstrated limited effectiveness in complex mountainous regions; T4 incorporated the red-edge index, which improved the identification of evergreen broad-leaved forests; while T5 integrated all features—despite a substantial volume of information—the overall classification performance did not show significant improvement. Schemes T6 through T8 tested the independent effects of vegetation indices, red-edge indices, and texture features respectively. The results indicated that relying on a single feature was insufficient to meet classification requirements. Ultimately, scheme T9 achieved optimal classification performance by eliminating redundant features. Discussion In this study, the spatiotemporal evolution patterns of vegetation cover and the vertical distribution characteristics of forest vegetation in Fanjing Mountain were systematically investigated through the integration of multi-source remote sensing data and refined classification techniques. Nevertheless, certain limitations persist, necessitating further exploration and refinement. 4.1. Limitations of data sources The study employed Landsat series and Sentinel-2 multispectral images. However, the spatial resolution of these datasets may be insufficient to fully capture fine-scale vegetation distribution details in complex mountainous terrains. Although sub-meter-resolution aerial imagery was accessible, its limited spatial coverage precluded a comprehensive depiction of vegetation distribution across the entire reserve, thereby affecting classification accuracy in certain localized areas. Future research should consider integrating higher-resolution remote sensing data with more extensive ground surveys to enhance classification precision further. 4.2. Limitations of classification methods Although effective, the random forest model is fundamentally a statistical algorithm reliant on feature selection and sample quality. Despite optimizing feature combinations, issues such as feature redundancy or information loss may still affect model generalization. Distinguishing fine-grained vegetation subtypes in complex mountainous terrains remains highly challenging. The 77.88% overall accuracy aligns with applications in similar environments, which typically report accuracies ranging from 75% to 85% even when employing advanced algorithms or high-resolution UAV imagery [ 25 – 27 ]. This performance was primarily constrained by high inter-class spectral similarity, severe topographic shadow effects [ 28 ], and the poor recognition of coniferous forests (producer accuracy of 0.44), which is attributed to their low visible-band reflectance and complex structural characteristics. Furthermore, categorizing vegetation into six broad classes may overlook internal heterogeneity, as broadleaf forests contain undelineated subclasses that limit classification precision. Future work could explore deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), to process high-dimensional remote sensing data and capture more complex spectral and spatial features. 5. Conclusion This study presents a quantitative analysis of the spatio-temporal changes in Fractional Vegetation Cover (FVC) on Fanjing Mountain from 1987 to 2022, elucidating the evolutionary stages and spatial heterogeneity patterns of regional vegetation coverage. By integrating multi-source remote sensing data, we conducted a forest vegetation classification experiment and established a high-precision classification system. Building upon this foundation, we performed vertical belt spectrum vertical belt spectrum analysis to systematically uncover the elevation gradient characteristics of forest vegetation on Fanjing Mountain. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) Over the past 36 years, Fanjing Mountain has exhibited a notable improvement in vegetation coverage, characterized by significant ecological recovery. From 1987 to 2022, the average annual growth rate of Forest Vegetation Coverage (FVC) on Fanjing Mountain was recorded at 0.18%, revealing a three-stage pattern of fluctuating decline, oscillating adjustment, and ecological leap. The year 2007 marked a critical turning point from degradation to recovery, driven by the interplay of early ecological engineering and natural climate fluctuations, whereas the subsequent ecological leap since 2013 was directly attributed to strict conservation policies during the World Natural Heritage application. The Theil-Sen trend analysis indicated that 41.65% of the area demonstrated an improving trend in FVC, particularly in the southern region and core areas, underscoring the effectiveness of implemented ecological protection policies. Spatially, while core protected areas maintained relatively stable high coverage, the overall turning characteristics of the region were primarily dominated by significant vegetation changes in lower-altitude edge areas. (2) The integration of remote sensing features—including terrain data, red-edge indices, and texture characteristics (T9 scheme)—significantly enhanced forest classification accuracy. This optimized combination of multi-source remote sensing features markedly improved the identification capabilities for various forest types. The overall accuracy achieved with the T9 scheme reached 77.88%, accompanied by a Kappa coefficient of 0.71; notably, shrub meadows and evergreen broad-leaved forests were identified with exceptional precision. Furthermore, it was observed that terrain factors contributed more significantly than traditional vegetation indices, highlighting the importance of synergistic effects between terrain and structural information within complex mountainous environments. (3) Building upon high-precision classification results, an initial vertical belt spectrum pattern for forest vegetation was established. By integrating T9 classification outcomes with Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data, distinct altitudinal distributions of forest types were identified—reflecting the predominant influence of thermal zones on determining vegetation types. This research provides both a scientific foundation and spatial basis for future studies on vertical belt spectrum patterns and ecological zone delineations.
