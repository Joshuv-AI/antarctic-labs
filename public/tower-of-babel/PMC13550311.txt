Machine learning-based integration develops a novel lysosome-related prognostic signature associated with prognosis and immune infiltration landscape in acute myeloid leukemia

Published: 2026

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological malignancy, which is caused by the premature arrest of abnormal clones of leukemia cells [1, 2]. AML is highly heterogeneous, with at least 24 genetically defined subtypes, contributing to its complex prognosis [3, 4]. Among these, acute promyelocytic leukemia is the most curable subtype of AML, with a cure rate of 90% [5] However, for other subtypes, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate remains poor, approximately 45% for younger patients (≤ 60 years) and less than 10% for older patients (> 60 years) [6]. Leukemia relapse remains the leading cause of treatment failure, as determined by post-remission treatment strategies guided by risk stratification [7]. Consequently, stratification of AML patients and accurate treatment regimen selection remain critical challenges for researchers and clinical experts, highlighting the urgent need for novel biomarkers capable of assessing prognosis and guiding treatment more precisely.

Lysosomes, known as membrane-enclosed cytoplasmic organelles, contain numerous acid hydrolases that degrade various biological macromolecules [8]. Recent investigations have demonstrated that lysosomes participate in numerous biological processes beyond degradation, encompassing cell proliferation, disease occurrence, and cancer advancement [9]. In tumorigenesis, the rapid division and invasion of cancer cells strongly depend on effective lysosomal function [10]. It has been found that the lysosomal mass of AML cells is increased during leukemogenesis [11]. Moreover, AML cells have enhanced lysosomal matrix enzyme activity compared to normal myeloid cells [12]. Similar to solid cancers, lysosomes can induce chemotherapy resistant in AMLby sequestering drugs [13]. Consequently, comprehending the fundamental mechanisms of lysosome-related genes (LRGs) in AML is essential, which provides potential new therapeutic targets for overcoming drug resistance and improving treatment outcomes.

In this research, we developed a novel AML risk model linked to LRGs. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis comprehensively evaluated causal connections between prognostic genes and AML pathogenesis. Additionally, this model was further applied to evaluate the variations in response to immunotherapy and chemotherapy among AML patients. The findings demonstrate that this LRG signature risk model not only accurately forecasts the AML patients’ prognosis, but also provides a new strategy for individualized treatment planning.

Materials and methods

Collection of data of AML patients

Gene expression and clinical data for AML were extracted from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. The training set was the TCGA cohort, containing 130 AML samples with non-zero survival time. GSE9476 (Platform: GPL96), GSE37642 (Platform: GPL570), and GSE12417 (Platform: GPL570) were downloaded from GEO database. GSE9476, including 10 normal bone marrow mononuclear cell (BMMC) samples (as controls) and 7 disease samples with diagnosis: MDS/AML, was used to perform differential expression analysis; notably, AML subtypes (M classification) were not included in the analysis [14]. The validation sets were GSE37642 and GSE12417, which contained 136 and 79 AML samples with survival information, respectively [15, 16]. Besides, 875 LRGs were acquired from AmiGO2 (http://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo) [17].

Differentially expressed analysis

The “limma” R package (version 3.54.2) was adopted to discover differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between AML and control groups in GSE9476, with threshold criteria of |log2foldchange (FC)| > 1 and adj. P-value < 0.05 [18]. The “ggplot2” R package (version 3.3.6) and the “pheatmap” R package (version 0.1.9) were separately applied to generate a volcano diagram and a heatmap to display DEGs.

Screening of differentially expressed LRGs (DE-LRGs)-related to prognosis of AML patients

DEGs between AML and control groups were intersected with LRGs to obtain DE-LRGs by the “ggvenn” package (version 0.1.9). To discover DE-LRGs related to prognosis of AML patients in the TCGA cohort, univariate COX regression analysis was conducted (hazard ratio (HR) ≠ 1, P-value < 0.05, and proportional hazards (PH) > 0.05). To examine the biological functions of DE-LRGs related to the prognosis of AML patients, GO and KEGG were subsequently conducted (adj. P-value < 0.05). Additionally, the STRING database was utilized to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network to investigate the relationships among DE-LRGs associated with the AML patients’ prognosis at the protein level.

Construction and validation of LRG risk score model

Ten distinct machine learning algorithms, including COXBoost, random survival forest (RSF), stepCOX, Ridge, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), SuperPC, elastic network (Enet), gradient boosting machine (GBM), plsRcox, and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM), were used to develop 100 combined predictive models for establishing LRG risk score models in the training and validation sets. After evaluating Harrell’s concordance indexes (C-indexes) in each cohort, the optimal model was selected based on the highest average C-index across two independent validation cohorts (GSE12417 and GSE37462), ensuring robustness and generalizability. Moreover, genes in the optimal model were designated as prognostic genes for AML patients. Subsequently, the predict function was utilized to ascertain the risk score for each patient based on the expression of prognostic genes. The AML patients were categorized into high- and low-risk groups on the basis of risk scores via the surv_cutpoint function of the “survminer” R package (version 0.4.9). Furthermore, using OS as the endpoint, AML patients’ survival was compared between two risk groups by Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis with the log-rank test via the “survminer” R package (version 0.4.9, P < 0.05). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for 1-, 2-, and 3- year survival were generated to evaluate the model’s prognostic efficacy. The effectiveness of the LRG risk signature was further corroborated in GSE37642 and GSE12417.

Clinical features analysis

To enhance clinical adaptability, we examined the correlations between LRG risk score and clinical features, as well as between prognostic genes and clinical features. Firstly, the relevance between the expression of prognostic genes and clinical features was examined, with the results depicted in a Heatmap. Moreover, the Chi-Square test was applied to analyze disparities in the proportions of subgroups exhibiting different clinical characteristics between two risk groups. Furthermore, the disparities in risk score among subgroups with varying clinical characteristics were examined by rank-sum test. The independent predictive values of risk scores and clinical features were assessed by univariate and multivariate COX analyses (HR ≠ 1 and P < 0.05). Subsequently, the “rms” R package (version 6.3-0) was applied to create the nomogram through integrating these independent prognostic factors or the expression of prognostic genes, respectively. In essence, the model allocates a value to each variable and subsequently consolidates the scores to derive a total score, which forecasts the OS probability for AML patients at 1-, 2-, and 3-years. The forecasting potential of the nomogram was determined by calibration curves and ROC curves.

Functional enrichment analysis

The “clusterProfilter” R package (version 4.7.1.001) was applied to conduct gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to examine potential mechanistic pathways in two risk groups on the basis of differential expression results between them (|NES| > 1 and adj. P-value < 0.05) [19]. To elucidate the distinctions in pathways enriched in two risk groups, the GSVA score of each pathway was measured utilizing the “GSVA” R package (version 1.46.0), and the disparities were measured via the “limma” R package (version 3.54.2) [18, 20]. The threshold values were |t| > 2 and P-value < 0.05. The reference set was the KEGG gene set (c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt) extracted from the MSigdb database.

Investigation of the tumor immune microenvironment between two risk groups

The ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT algorithms were utilized to measure the immune cell infiltration levels, and the expression of 38 immune checkpoints was examined in two risk groups. The Wilcoxon test was adopted to analyze variations between two groups. Moreover, the associations between immune cells and risk score were evaluated via the Spearman algorithm.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Semi-inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of drugs were quantified via the “pRRophetoc” R package in accordance with the genomic data in the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database to assess the efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs in AML patients. Moreover, the Wilcoxon test was adopted to measure variations between two risk groups with P-value < 0.01.

The two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

This study followed the Mendelian reporting specifications for randomized studies (STROBE-MR) [21]. A two-sample MR analysis was conducted to determine the causal influence of prognostic genes on AML via the “TwoSampleMR” R package (version 0.5.7). The data of exposure factors were downloaded from the IEU OpenGWAS database, including eqtl-a-ENSG00000064989 (CALCRL) (31,684 samples and 18,020 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)), eqtl-a-ENSG00000100453 (GZMB) (26,395 samples and 19,776 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000101336 (HCK) (31,684 samples and 16,537 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000110719 (TCIRG1) (30,935 samples and 18,351 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000134851 (TMEM165) (31,684 samples and 19,196 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000158473 (CD1D) (31,684 samples and 20,083 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000165029 (ABCA1) (31,684 samples and 19,938 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000171130 (ATP6V0E2) (31,684 samples and 19,979 SNPs), eqtl-a-ENSG00000198932 (GPRASP1) (9,188 samples and 10,125 SNPs), and eqtl-a-ENSG00000198951 (NAGA) (31,470 samples and 18,832 SNPs). Moreover, the data of outcome were obtained from FinnGen R10 (ID: finngen_R10_C3_AML_EXALLC, sample size: 244 AML and 314,192 controls, and SNPs: 19,680,799). The SNPs as instrumental variables (IVs) were screened using the “TwoSampleMR” R package (version 0.5.7) [22]. The screening criteria were: (1) SNPs were markedly relevant to the exposure (P < 5e-08); (2) SNPs with linkage disequilibrium (LD) were removed via the LD clumping function (parameter setting: r2 = 0.01, kb = 100); (3) the SNPs was measured by the F-statistic value, excluding those with F < 10; (4) SNPs that were considerably relevant to the outcome were eliminated by the harmonise_data function in the “TwoSampleMR” R package (version 0.5.7).

MR analysis was conducted through integrating the mr function and five methods (Inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, Weighted median, Simple mode, and Weighted mode). The results from the IVW method were considered as the primary outcome. When heterogeneity excites (P < 0.05), IVW-random effects (IVW-RE) was chosen. In contrast, IVW-fixed effects (IVW-FE) was chosen. The degree of causality was measured using odds radio (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), and P less than 0.05 representing the significant causality between exposure and outcome. The robustness of the results was confirmed by the sensitivity analyses (heterogeneity test, horizontal pleiotropic test, and leave-one-out (LOO) test). Additionally, to detect the possibility of reverse causality between exposure factors and outcomes, the Steiger test was conducted, with. a result of “TRUE”, demonstrating the absence of reverse causation.

Cell culture

The human bone marrow-derived cell line HS-27 A was purchased from Wuhan Shangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The human AML cell line MOLM-13 was obtained from Xiamen Immocell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The roswell park memorial institute 1640 medium (RPMI-1640) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin was utilized for cell cultivation at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The expression of ten prognostic genes was further quantified in MOLM-13 and HS-27 A cells by RT-qPCR. Following the directions, the FastPure Complex Tissue/Cell Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC113-01, Vazyme, Nanjing) was applied to extract total RNA from the cells. Subsequently, RNA reverse transcribed to cDNA was conducted through ABScript III RT Master Mix for RT-qPCR with gDNA Remover (RK20429, Abconal, China), followed by RT-qPCR using Genious 2X SYBR Green Fast RT-qPCR Mix (RK21205, Abconal, China). The 2−∆∆Ct method was adopted to quantify the relative mRNA expression of prognostic genes. The internal reference gene was the GAPDH. The primer sequences were presented in Table 1.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from HS-27 A and MOLM-13 cells and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Following denaturation, the proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a membrane. The membrane was subsequently incubated in a TBST solution at ambient temperature for 30 min to obstruct non-specific binding sites. Following the blocking procedure, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody using a rocking shaker. The secondary antibody was incubated at ambient temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the membrane was exposed to the ECL chemiluminescence substrate, and chemiluminescent signals were detected using a programmed exposure system. After exposure, images were saved and quantitative analysis was performed with ImageJ software. All antibodies used in this study, including their manufacturers, catalog numbers, and optimized working dilutions, are summarized in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The R software and GraphPad Prism were utilized to perform statistical analyses. The disparities in survival between two groups were measured via the log-rank test. The differences were compared by the Wilcoxon test (n = 2) and the Kruskal-Wallis test (n > 2). The chi-square test was employed to measure the variations in percentage of subgroups exhibiting varying clinical features between two risk groups. A statistically significant P-value is less than 0.05.

Results

Identification of DE-LRGs associated with prognosis of AML patients

Differential analysis discovered 1,027 DEGs between AML and control groups, comprising 326 up-regulated and 701 down-regulated genes (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, the expression of the top 20 up-regulated and down-regulated genes in the AML group was displayed. A total of 89 DE-LRGs were then obtained through intersecting between DEGs and LRGs (Fig. 1C). Among these DE-LRGs, 27 genes were linked to the prognosis of AML patients, comprising 8 protective genes (TMEM165, WDR48, GPRASP1, RAB27A, ABCA1, MPO, RETN, and ELANE) and 19 risk factors (IFITM3, NCF2, CD1D, SLC15A3, NAGA, FUCA1, CTSL, HCK, TCIRG1, GZMB, PRKCD, ATP6V0D1, HLA-DOB, CALCRL, MMD, RAB5C, LPCAT1, CHMP7, and ATP6V0E2) (Supplementary Table 1). The enrichment analyses results indicated that 27 DE-LRGs were markedly enriched in 134 GO items and 15 KEGG pathways (Supplementary Table 2), and we presented top 5 GO items and top 10 KEGG pathways (Fig. 1D, E). As illustrated in Fig. 1D, E, DEGs were associated with ‘phagocytosis’ (GO-BP), ‘lysosomal membrane’ (GO-CC), ‘ATPase-coupled ion transmembrane transporter activity’ (GO-MF), as well as ‘phagosome’ (KEGG). The PPI network of 27 proteins comprised with 17 nodes and 22 interaction pairs (Fig. 1F). Among these proteins, MPO showed the most extensive interactions, binding to six other proteins (including ABCA1, CD1D, ELANE, GZMB, RETN, and NCF2).

Integrated development of a LRG signature

Based on the 27 DE-LRGs associated with the prognosis of AML patients, ten machine learning algorithms and 100 predictive models were integrated to further hone the essential gene signatures that have a major impact on the prognosis of AML. Consequently, the optimal model was StepCox[backward] + RSF (C-index = 0.705), which generated a 10-gene signature associated with AML prognosis: ATP6V0E2, CALCRL, TMEM165, GZMB, HCK, TCIRG1, CD1D, GPRASP1, ABCA1, and NAGA (Fig. 2A). Based on their risk score, the patients from the TCGA cohort were then categorized into high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 2B). Additionally, a substantial difference in patient survival between two groups was found by K-M survival analysis (Fig. 2B). The 1-, 2-, and 3-year AUC values were all above 0.6, indicating moderate predictive ability (Fig. 2B). Additionally, we validated the signature in GSE37642 and GSE12417, with results aligning with those in the training set (Fig. 2C, D). These findings illustrated that the LRG signature was robust.

Enrichment analysis in two risk groups

GSEA and GSVA were employed to illustrate the potential functional pathways in two risk groups. GSEA findings demonstrated that the high-risk group exhibited significant enrichment in immune-related pathways, such as ‘autoimmune thyroid disease,’ ‘antigen processing and presentation,’ ‘TOLL like receptor signaling pathway,’ etc. (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 3). Through GSVA, 117 pathways were significant variations between two risk groups (Supplementary Table 4). In the high-risk group, ‘FSHR-GNAS-PKA signaling pathway,’ ‘CD40 NFκB signaling pathway,’ ‘HCMV gH to ITGA/B-RHOA signaling pathway’, ‘LHCGR-GNAS-PKA signaling pathway,’ ‘citrate cycle, first carbon oxidation,’ and other pathways were enriched (Fig. 3B). In the low-risk group, ‘Heparan sulfate biosynthesis,’ ‘BMP9/10 signaling pathway,’ ‘Myostatin signaling pathway,’ ‘EGF-EGFR-RAS-JNK signaling pathway,’ were markedly enriched (Fig. 3B). Collectively, these findings point to clear differences in pathway activity between the two risk groups. The high‑risk group showed activation of immune‑related and pro‑tumorigenic pathways. The low‑risk group, on the other hand, was enriched for pathways involved in extracellular matrix organization, BMP9/10 signaling, myostatin signaling, and—unexpectedly—EGF‑EGFR‑RAS‑JNK signaling. These opposing patterns may at least partly explain why high‑risk AML patients have worse survival.

Immune landscape in two risk groups

Previous studies have revealed that patients with AML have an altered bone marrow immune environment, which in turn contributes to the severity of the disease [23]. Consequently, we assessed immune microenvironment of two risk groups. Compared to the low-risk group, the high-risk group’s ESTIMATE and immunological scores were noticeably higher (Fig. 3C). The proportions of 22 immune cell infiltration in each sample across two groups were displayed in a bar chart (Fig. 3D). Moreover, the infiltration levels of activated mast cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, and resting mast cells exhibited significant variations between two risk groups, and these three immune cells were all significantly negatively relevant to the risk score (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 1). Results about the expression of immune checkpoints suggested that there was a considerably variable in the expression of 11 immune checkpoints across two groups (Fig. 3F). Excluding CD160, the other ten immune checkpoints were highly expressed in the high-risk group, demonstrating that reduced expression of immune checkpoint genes in the low-risk group may be relevant to less malignant behavior and immune evasion of cancer cells.

Predictive value of LRG signature for chemotherapy

To assess the effect of drugs on AML patients in two risk groups, we calculated the IC50 for each patient in two risk groups. Results demonstrated that there were substantial variations in the IC50 values of 20 distinct treatment-sensitive medications between the two risk groups (Fig. 3G). The higher risk score enhanced the sensitivity of 16 anticancer drugs, including 5-Fluorouracil, AZD2014, AZD6738, AZD7762, Camptothecin, Cisplatin, Dabrafenib, Dactolisib, Epirubicin, Gemcitabine, GNE-317, PF-4,708,671, Ribociclib, Temozolomide, Teniposide, and VE821. Patients in the low-risk group exhibited heightened sensitivity to ABT737, Bl-25,369, Entinostat, and IGF1R_3801 compared to patients in the high-risk group.

The clinical significance of the LRG signature and prognostic genes

We investigated the connections between prognostic genes, risk score, and clinical traits to evaluate the clinical relevance of LRG signature. The heatmap displayed the expression of prognostic genes across subgroups with varying clinical features (Fig. 4A). Moreover, there was a substantial variation in the percentages of cytogenetic risk, M stages, and age between two risk groups (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the risk score between different subgroups in age, cytogenetic risk, and M stages (M0 vs. M3) was markedly different (Fig. 4C). Moreover, the risk score was determined as an independent prognostic factors for AML patients (Fig. 5A). The nomogram was developed utilizing independent prognostic factors to forecast 1-, 2-, and 3-years survival rates for AML patients (Fig. 5B). Additionally, we also created a nomogram based on expression of prognostic genes to forecast 1-, 2-, and 3-years survival rate for AML patients to better examine the connections between prognostic genes and clinical features (Fig. 5C). The slopes of the calibration curves for 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival predictions were about equal to 1 (Fig. 5B, C). The AUC values of the nomogram for 1-, 2-, and 3-years were all above 0.6 (Fig. 5B, C). These results indicated acceptable predictive effectiveness for both nomograms.

Exploring the causal relationship between prognostic genes and AML

After screening of IVs, CD1D, GPRASP1, and NAGA were excluded from the MR analysis due to fewer than three SNPs. Among the remaining seven prognostic genes, only HCK showed a causal relationship with AML by the IVW method (P = 0.0381), and was identified as a risk factor for AML (OR = 1.529) (Fig. 6A). Moreover, the scatter plot, forest plot, and funnel plot also showed the consistency and reliability of the results (Fig. 6B, D). Supplementary Figs. 2–4 were negative result graphs. The robustness of the MR findings was further confirmed through sensitivity analysis (Table 3) (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, no evidence of reverse causality was observed when HCK was used as the exposure and AML as the outcome (Table 4). Collectively, these findings supported the reliability and stability of the MR analysis.

Validation of prognostic genes expression

To validate the transcriptional expression of prognostic genes, RT-qPCR was employed to assess the expression levels of ten prognostic genes in MOLM-13 and HS-27 A cells. Results demonstrated that GZMB, HCK, TCIRG1, CD1D, GPRASP1, and NAGA were highly increased in MOLM-13 cells compared to HS-27 A cells, whereas ATP6V0E2, CALCRL, and TMEM165 were dramatically downregulated in MOLM-13 cells (Fig. 7A-I). The expression of ABCA1 between MOLM-13 and HS-27 A cells was not significant (Fig. 7J). Notably, the expression trends of ATP6V0E2, TMEM165, and ABCA1 aligned with the outcomes of bioinformatics analysis. We hypothesize three potential possibilities for the observed discrepancies in the results: (1) inherent differences between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR, such as variations in detection principles and calculation methods; (2) discrepancies in sample sources and culture conditions, which may contribute to conflicting results; and (3) the influence of experimental variations, including differences in time, experimental procedures, and equipment, which may lead to batch effects.

Discussion

Lysosomes are organelles involved in the degradation and recycling of cellular materials [24]. Recent research has increasingly focused on their role in cancer biology. In particular, increased expression levels of lysosomal membrane protein have been associated with tumor grade, poor prognosis, and metastatic potential in many malignancies, such as colon, breast, and metastatic melanomas cancers, as well as high-grade gliomas [25]. Lysosomes are also implicated in other signaling pathways correlated to cancer, including autophagy and apoptosis [26, 27]. Recent investigations have found some lysosome-associated pathways that are crucial in the pathogenesis of AML and are intricately linked to medication resistance and prognosis of the disease [8]. Despite the potential of LRGs as biomarkers for forecasting cancer prognosis [28, 29], limited research has focused on creating predictive models for AML prognosis utilizing LRGs. In our investigation, we established a novel AML prognostic model comprising LRGs, which demonstrates potential for predicting patient prognosis and guiding immunotherapy and chemotherapy.

This study identified a prognostic signature comprising 10 genes: ATP6V0E2, CALCRL, TMEM165, GZMB, HCK, TCIRG1, CD1D, GPRASP1, ABCA1, and NAGA. Among these genes, TMEM165, GPRASP1, and ABCA1 are prognostic protective factors. Transmembrane proteins (TMEM) are proteins that span biological membranes, and their expression can be up- or down-regulated in tumor tissues [30]. TMEM165 is a Golgi membrane protein closely associated with protein glycosylation [31]. As far as we know, this research is the inaugural report of a connection between TMEM165 and AML. Both RT-qPCR and bioassay findings demonstrated that TMEM165 expression was significantly down-regulated in AML, indicating that it was a protective gene for AML occurrence, but this requires more research in the future. GPRASP1 regulates lysosomal sorting, receptor recycling, and the down-regulation of multiple G protein-coupled receptors [32]. It may function as a potential tumor suppressor and has been linked to a positive outcome in multiple cancers [33, 34]. The ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter protein ABCA1 is crucial for facilitating cellular cholesterol efflux to apoA-I [35]. Reduced expression of cholesterol efflux mediators and elevated cellular cholesterol levels have been associated with different tumors [36]. Studies have shown that ABCA1 exerts a tumor suppressor function in myeloproliferative tumors [37]. Among seven risk factors for the AML patients’ prognosis, V-ATPase generates electrochemical proton gradients across eukaryotic cell membranes, which are essential for energizing basic cellular processes [38]. ATP6V0E2 and TCIRG1 encode the e2 and a3 subunits of V-ATPase, respectively. Knockdown of ATP6V0E2 and TCIRG1 has been shown to inhibit cancer progression [39, 40]. CALCRL serves as a G-protein-coupled neuropeptide receptor, contributing to several biological activities including cell proliferation, inflammation, and death [41, 42]. Numerous studies have established a link between CALCRL expression levels and the prognosis of AML patients. For instance, AML/ETO patients exhibiting low CALCRL expression had an extended median survival time, whereas those attaining complete remission post-chemotherapy demonstrate diminished CALCRL expression levels [43]. Moreover, the suppression of CALCRL expression in a specific subset of leukemic stem cells diminished their frequency and increased their susceptibility to chemotherapeutic drugs, aiding in the eradication of relapse-initiating cells in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [44]. These findings align with our studies, in which we similarly identified CALCRL as a prognostic risk factor for AML patients. Granzyme B (GZMB) is a pivotal member of the serine protease family and is considered a cytotoxic pro-apoptotic protease [45]. Research indicates that GZMB facilitates cancer cell invasion across multiple cancer types [46–48]. CD1D is a principal glycoprotein of the major histocompatibility complex class I that delivers phospholipids and glycolipids to invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells [49]. CD1D is present on the surface of several tumor cells, particularly those originating from the hematological system [50]. Research indicates that patients with AML, ALL, and CLL exhibit markedly reduced levels of CD1D compared to healthy controls; yet, elevated CD1D expression is closely linked to unfavorable prognosis in CLL patients [51, 52]. This is because CLL patients exhibiting elevated CD1D expression demonstrate downregulated CD3ζ expression in their iNKT cells, potentially compromising iNKT cell functionality and, in turn, their anti-tumor efficacy [51, 53]. α-N-acetylgalactosaminidase (α-NaGalase) is a lysosomal glycoside hydrolase encoded by NAGA. α-NaGalase can affect the immune system’s ability to fight cancer by interfering with macrophage activation [54]. Silencing of NAGA has been found to increase death and reduce migration of cancer cells [55]. Notably, HCK has been demonstrated to have a causal association with AML by MR analysis. HCK is a member of the Src family of non-receptor tyrosine kinases and is predominantly expressed in B lymphocytes and myeloid lineage cells [56]. HCK is an inherent oncogene in tumor cells, and its abnormal activation induces hematologic malignancies [57]. Research indicates that HCK is significantly expressed in AML, and increased levels of HCK adversely affect overall and disease-free survival [58]. In contrast, the suppression of HCK induces cell cycle arrest, significantly diminishing proliferation and colony formation in human AML cell lines [57]. These results further confirm the reliability of our results and support the role of HCK as a risk factor in AML. Overall, all identified prognostic genes have been reported to be involved in tumorigenesis or AML pathogenesis, supporting the reliability of our prognostic model.

The extent and type of immune cell infiltration in tumor tissue significantly influence tumor development and prognosis [59]. Our investigation identified markedly elevated levels of infiltrating resting mast cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, and active mast cells in the low-risk cohort. This indicates that the invasion of these immune cells may correlate with a positive prognosis in AML. Resting memory CD4 T cells can endure in the body for prolonged durations without antigenic stimulation and react swiftly upon re-exposure to the antigen [60]. Research indicates that elevated infiltration levels of resting memory CD4 T lymphocytes are associated with a positive outcome in cancer [61, 62]. The involvement of mast cells in carcinogenesis is contentious. Mast cells are generally recognized as stimulators of tumor development, neoangiogenesis, and metastasis, all of which correlate with worse prognosis. Numerous studies have verified that mast cells can recruit T cells and dendritic cells to influence adaptive T cell-mediated immunity [63]. In AML, patients with modest mast cell infiltration demonstrated a markedly inferior OS relative to those with high mast cell infiltration, corroborating our findings [64]. The notable efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy highlights the importance of the immune system in treating cancer [65]. Research indicates that lysosomes function as crucial locations for the degradation of immune checkpoint molecules [66]. Our investigation identified 11 immunological checkpoints that exhibited significantly differently expressed between high-risk and low-risk groups. All immune checkpoint genes, save CD160, demonstrated increased expression levels in the high-risk group. Consequently, targeting these immunological checkpoints may prove advantageous for treating high-risk AML patients. Our findings also revealed that the high-risk group exhibited considerable enrichment in immune-related pathways as determined by GSEA. Targeting these pathways may yield novel therapeutic strategies for AML patients. In conclusion, this research may yield significant insights toward AML immunotherapy.

Drug resistance continues to be a significant factor in chemotherapy failure and cancer-related mortality. Lysosomes are crucial in mediating resistance to certain frequently employed anticancer agents and present prospective strategies to surmount this resistance [67]. In our analysis, the IC50 values of 20 distinct therapeutically sensitive medicines exhibited substantial variation between the two risk groups. The mTORC1/2 dual inhibitor AZD2014 significantly decreased lysosomal pH levels in AML cells, hence enhancing the toxic effects of gituzumab ozogamicin, a medication deemed appropriate and clinically relevant for AML [68]. Entinostat is a synthetic histone deacetylase inhibitor that effectively and selectively induces cell cycle arrest and hyperacetylation of histones H3 and H4. In AML, entinostat triggers cell cycle arrest and death in patient samples, downregulates the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins MCL-1 and BCL-2, elevates p21 levels, and enhances H3 acetylation. The in vivo efficacy of entinostat was additionally validated in a mouse model of AML. Drug sensitivity analysis have yielded novel insights into the therapy of AML, however further investigations are required.

This study has certain limitations. First, the differential expression analysis was based on a relatively small sample size, which may not fully capture the high heterogeneity of AML and its various molecular subtypes. Second, owing to the lack of public AML datasets with adequate normal controls and sufficient sample size, further independent validation was not feasible. Third, the data sources utilized are public databases and do not accurately represent the clinical characteristics and genetic backgrounds of all AML patients, thereby limiting the generalizability of the study findings. Therefore, future large‑scale, multi‑center, and subtype‑stratified studies are needed to verify and extend our results. Fourth, although gene expression was validated in cell lines, the biological interpretation is limited by the use of HS-27 A rather than hematopoietic cell lines; further validation will be performed in appropriate hematopoietic models. Finally, this study concentrated on the prognostic significance of LRGs and has not yet explored their specific role in the genesis and progression of AML. The variability of AML is likely associated with several signaling pathways, transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, and intricate processes including immune evasion; hence, more mechanistic investigations are required to comprehensively elucidate the involvement of LRGs in AML.
