Genomic evidence reveals taxonomic oversplitting in a wing dimorphic grasshopper complex Tetrix japonica
Authors: Ying Long, Xin Deng, Qing-yi An, De-long Guan, Wei-an Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate species delimitation is fundamental to biodiversity research but remains challenging in taxonomically controversial groups exhibiting morphological plasticity. The Tetrix japonica complex (Orthoptera: Tetrigidae) includes widespread populations and several nominal species defined primarily by variable traits such as wing dimorphism. The validity of these taxa and the genera based upon them (Alulatettix and Formosatettix) is highly debated. To resolve these boundaries, we performed whole-genome resequencing anchored to a chromosome-level reference genome.ResultsWe analyzed 1,095,722 high-quality SNPs from 50 specimens collected across East Asia. Population genomic analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and ADMIXTURE (optimal K = 1), revealed a unified gene pool with no significant genetic substructure corresponding to current taxonomic classifications. Genetic differentiation was low (global FST near 0.03) and significantly correlated with geographic distance (Isolation by Distance), indicating that spatial separation rather than reproductive isolation drives genetic variation. A Random Forest classifier achieved only 16.7% accuracy in distinguishing nominal species, confirming that genomic data cannot support the current morphological taxonomy. Phylogenomic reconstruction (IQ-TREE) further demonstrated that all these included specimens are tightly related. Key taxa such as A. wudangshanensis and F. gonggashanensis were recovered as nested lineages deep within the T. japonica complex, clustering with geographically proximate populations rather than forming distinct generic clades.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that T. japonica represents a single, widespread metapopulation with high morphological plasticity. Diagnostic characters previously used to erect separate species and genera, particularly wing morphology and flightlessness, are reinterpreted here as intraspecific polymorphisms. Our genomic data provide molecular support for the previous hypothesis that A. wudangshanensis, F. shennongjiaensis, and F. gonggashanensis belong to the T. japonica complex. Introduction

Species delimitation, the process of determining species boundaries, remains one of the most challenging tasks in systematic biology [1, 2]. The real issue arises when species show extreme morphological plasticity with minimal genetic divergence, making it difficult to distinguish local adaptations from distinct evolutionary lineages [3, 4]. Within the broader Orthopteran literature, phenotypic plasticity, polymorphism, and non-genetic variation are notorious for confounding taxonomic boundaries. For instance, extreme density-dependent phenotypic plasticity, such as locust phase polyphenism, radically alters morphology and behavior [5]. In Tetrigidae specifically, structural polymorphisms—such as the wing-length variations that complicate species limits in the Tetrix bipunctata complex [6]—are common. Furthermore, Tetrigidae exhibit extreme color polymorphism heavily influenced by complex Mendelian genetics [7], environmentally induced plasticity based on developmental substrates [8], or behavioral differences driven by intersexual niche segregation [9]. These variations frequently trick taxonomists into describing intraspecific color or structural morphs as distinct species [10]. Consequently, reliance on highly variable morphological traits without genetic validation often results in taxonomic oversplitting and inaccurate alpha taxonomy that ultimately compromises downstream molecular barcoding [11]. The pygmy grasshopper Tetrix japonica (Bolivar, 1887), as a common species in East Asia and a model organism for studying the ecological adaptations and behavior of family Tetrigidae, presents a case that demands urgent attention.

T. japonica is a widely distributed species complex in East Asia, with records from China, the Korean Peninsula, Japan, and Mongolia [12]. It occupies diverse habitats, including streamside areas, grasslands, and forest understories [13]. The complex exhibits significant morphological plasticity, particularly in body color, pronotum shape, and wing length, which show a high degree of variation[14, 15].. A recent morphological review by our group synonymized 23 such taxa under T. japonica, highlighting a pattern of historical taxonomic oversplitting [16]. However, this revision was based primarily on morphology and lacked robust molecular support, leaving the precise boundaries of the complex unresolved.

Previous molecular studies based on limited mitochondrial markers provided ambiguous results. Phylogenetic relationships among genera of the subfamily Tetriginae remain poorly studied. In NCBI databases, only ~ 10 complete mitochondrial genome sequences exist for Tetriginae species [17–22]. Despite limited molecular data, consensus indicates that the dimorphic T. japonica clusters within a clade containing micropterous Alulatettix and apterous Formosatettix species from Tetriginae, rather than grouping with other macropterous Tetriginae genera (Euparatettix, Coptotettix, Ergatettix)[23–26]. These findings, though preliminary due to sparse taxon sampling and reliance on single-locus data, hinted that the traditional, morphology-based generic boundaries might not reflect evolutionary history. Our own preliminary analyses using expanded mitochondrial datasets confirmed these paraphyletic relationships, motivating a more in-depth investigation. This prompts us to reconsider whether these micropterous or apterous morphotypes, currently classified in different genera, might be environmentally induced or developmentally plastic variants within the T. japonica lineage [27, 28]. This possibility challenges our understanding of not just species boundaries, but also the stability of diagnostic traits, like wing form, used for generic delimitation in Tetrigidae.

To overcome the limitations of previous studies and the challenges posed by morphological plasticity, we employed a phylogenomic approach. The recent assembly of a high-quality, chromosome-level reference genome for T. japonica by our team (GenBank Accession: GCA_048165185.1; see Data Availability) provides the necessary foundation for this work. By employing a reference-based variant calling approach on whole-genome resequencing data, we achieve genome-wide polymorphism data of sufficient density to resolve species boundaries with high confidence [29–31].

This study is based on low-coverage whole-genome resequencing data from 50 samples, conducting a comprehensive analysis of the species boundaries of T. japonica and some of its published synonyms. It aims to assess whether molecular evidence supports the synonymization of these species. Based on the revised taxonomy, we will then use Alulatettix wudangshanensis, Formosatettix shennongjiaensis, and Formosatettix gonggashanensis as test cases to explore how morphological adaptation and polymorphism may influence species boundary definitions within the T. japonica complex. Notably, we also include a morphologically distinct, undescribed population from Sichuan, which we provisionally labeled “Tetrix sp.” for tracking purposes. This population was initially considered a new species based on morphology, but preliminary molecular data suggested it was genetically indistinguishable from T. japonica, making it a key case for testing the conflict between morphological and genomic evidence.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

A total of 50 specimens representing the T. japonica complex and allied taxa were analyzed in this study (Supplementary Table 1). The geographic sampling covered a broad latitudinal gradient across East Asia, including two localities in Japan and extensive collections across 19 provinces and municipalities in China, ranging from Heilongjiang in the northeast to Yunnan and Guangxi in the southwest (Fig. 1). Specifically, the dataset consisted of 38 individuals of the nominotypical subspecies T. japonica, six specimens representing four controversial synonymized taxa (T. qinlingensis n = 2, T. ruyuanensis n = 2, T. jiuwanshanensis n = 1, and T. cenwanglaoshana n = 1), and six comparative samples used for assessing morphological‑classification boundaries (the provisional morphospecies Tetrix sp. n = 2, Formosatettix gonggashanensis n = 2, Alulatettix wudangshanensis n = 1, and Formosatettix shennongjiaensis n = 1).

Geographic distribution of sampling sites. Different colored symbols represent different putative species

Specimen identifiers were assigned based on geographic origin and taxonomic classification. Samples of T. japonica were designated by a four-letter code derived from the collection province and specific locality (e.g., “GXGL” for Guilin, Guangxi). Identifiers for synonyms and non-Tetrix species combined the location code with the initials of the genus and species epithets (e.g., “SCGZFG” for F. gonggashanensis from Garzê, Sichuan). Multiple individuals from the same locality were distinguished by serial numbers.

Samples were preserved in absolute ethanol immediately after collection and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the leg muscle tissue using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and quantity was measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) [32, 33]. Each sample’s integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Genome sequencing and data processing

All genomic analyses were anchored to a newly generated, high-quality, chromosome-level reference genome of T. japonica (isolate XD-2025a). The genome was sequenced to ~ 130× coverage using PacBio HiFi long-read technology, resulting in a final assembly of 1.68 Gb. The assembly demonstrates excellent contiguity, with a scaffold N50 of 217.25 Mb across 7 primary scaffolds, corresponding to the haploid chromosome number of the species. The complete genome assembly and its annotation have been made publicly available in the GenBank database under the accession GCA_048165185.1 (WGS project: JBLRXY01), ensuring full reproducibility of our study.

Building on this robust reference, low-coverage whole-genome sequencing was performed for all 50 samples using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with a target depth of ~ 6× per sample. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Prep Kit (Illumina) with an insert size of 350 bp. Paired-end sequencing was conducted with a read length of 150 bp. Raw reads were processed using FastQC v0.11.9 for quality assessment, and Trimmomatic v0.39 was used to remove adapters and low-quality reads [34, 35]. Clean reads with a Phred quality score ≥ 30 and read length ≥ 50 bp were retained for subsequent analyses.

Genome mapping and variant calling

Clean reads were mapped to the T. japonica reference genome (GenBank ID GCA_048165185.1) using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 with default parameters [36]. The resulting alignments were processed using SAMtools v1.15 for conversion to BAM format, sorting, and indexing [37]. Variant calling was performed using BCFtools v1.15, implementing a multi-step pipeline: mpileup for variant detection, followed by variant calling with the multiallelic caller option [38]. Quality metrics were annotated using BCFtools’ fill-tags plugin, and variants were compressed and indexed using bgzip and tabix, respectively [38].

Retrieved VCF files were merged using BCFtools merge, followed by stringent quality filtering (QUAL ≥ 30, DP ≥ 5). The filtered variants were converted to PLINK format (v1.9) for downstream analyses [39]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was performed using a sliding window approach (window size: 100 variants, step size: 50 variants, r² threshold: 0.5). Additional quality control steps included removing variants with > 5% missing data (--geno 0.05).

Population structure and genetic diversity analyses

Population structure was analyzed using ADMIXTURE v1.3.0, testing K values from 1 to 5 with 20 independent runs for each K value. The optimal number of ancestral populations (K) was determined by identifying the K value with the lowest cross-validation (CV) error [40]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using PLINK v1.9, extracting the first 50 principal components to visualize population structure [41]. To assess genetic differentiation and diversity, we performed the following analyses. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay analysis was conducted using PopLDdecay v3.41 [42]. The filtered VCF file was used to calculate r² values between pairs of SNPs with maximum distance of 500 kb. Population differentiation was assessed using VCFtools v0.1.16 to calculate windowed FST values (100-kb windows with 100-kb steps) between designated groups [43]; Genetic diversity within populations was evaluated using nucleotide diversity (π) and Tajima’s D statistics, calculated in 100-kb windows across the genome. To directly test the hypothesis of taxonomic over splitting, we established two a priori groups for comparative population genetic analyses (e.g., FST and PCA). Group 1 consisted of all samples identified as T. japonica, while Group 2 included all nominal synonyms along with A. wudangshanensis, F. shennongjiaensis, and F. gonggashanensis. This design allows for a clear statistical assessment of differentiation between the core species and its putative synonyms.

For phylogenetic analyses, SNP data were converted to PHYLIP format using vcf2phylip v2.0 [44]. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were constructed using IQ-TREE v3.0.1 [45] with the best-fit substitution model selected by ModelFinder [46]. Branch support was assessed using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. Genetic relationships were further visualized by constructing an unrooted Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree based on the pairwise genetic distance matrix using the ape package in R v4.2.0 [47]. To rigorously quantify the genetic distinctiveness of the morphological groups, machine learning-based classification and multivariate analyses were employed. A Random Forest classifier was trained using the randomForest package with 500 decision trees (ntree = 500) to evaluate the classification error rate and confusion matrix between the nominate T. japonica and the synonymized taxa. Complementing this, Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) was conducted using the adegenet package [48] to assess the density and geometric separation of clusters along the primary discriminant axes without assuming a priori population models.

The contribution of geographic isolation to genetic differentiation was assessed through Isolation by Distance (IBD) analysis [49]. Pairwise geographic distances (km) between sampling sites were computed using the geosphere package, and their correlation with Euclidean genetic distances was evaluated via a Mantel test [50] with 999 permutations using the vegan package [51]. Additionally, to infer demographic history and detect potential population bottlenecks or expansions, the folded Site Frequency Spectrum (SFS) was calculated for each group. The distribution of minor allele frequencies (MAF) was analyzed and visualized using the tidyverse suite [52] in R to compare the abundance of rare alleles between the core T. japonica population and the synonymized groups.

Statistical analyses and visualization

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.2.0. Plots were generated the omics-studio cloud platform [47]. The map of sampling points was created using ArcGIS 10.8.1 software [53]. The resulting trees were visualized and annotated using Chiplot online platform. Species boundaries among the sampled taxa currently assigned to Tetrix japonica and its putative synonyms were evaluated by Bayesian species delimitation using the program BPP v4.7.1 [54]. The MCMC chain was run for 100,000 samples after a burn-in of 8,000 iterations, with a sampling frequency of 2. To assess robustness, we performed two independent runs with different random seeds and confirmed convergence by comparing posterior probability estimates across runs.

Results

Genome-wide SNP distribution and population structure

Following stringent quality filtering, we identified a total of 1,095,722 high-quality SNPs among these samples mapped to the T. japonica reference genome. Analysis using 100-kb sliding windows revealed a heterogeneous distribution pattern of genetic variation across the seven chromosomes (Fig. 2). While the majority of genomic windows contained fewer than 500 variants, we observed significant localized distinct high-density regions. Notably, Tjap_Chr1 exhibited a substantial accumulation of variants in its latter half, and Tjap_Chr2 showed high density in its proximal region, with peak densities exceeding 2,500 to 3,000 SNPs per 100 kb. In contrast to a uniform distribution, other chromosomes (e.g., Tjap_Chr6 and Tjap_Chr7) displayed relatively lower baseline variation but featured sharp density spikes near telomeric ends.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize the genetic structure among the sampled individuals (Fig. 3). The first two principal components explained a relatively small proportion of the total genetic variance (PC1: 5.38%; PC2: 4.65%). As shown in Fig. 3, the PCA plot revealed no distinct clustering pattern corresponding to the pre-defined taxonomic groups. Individuals from the T. japonica group (blue) and the "Others" group (red) were largely intermingled, with their 95% confidence ellipses showing extensive overlap. This lack of separation in the ordination space suggests an absence of significant genetic differentiation between T. japonica and the putative synonyms, supporting the hypothesis that they represent a single, genetically cohesive gene pool.

ADMIXTURE analysis further corroborated the findings from our PCA results (Fig. 4). Cross-validation (CV) error analysis identified K = 1 as the optimal model with the lowest CV value (0.4869), suggesting that all sampled individuals—including those classified as A. wudangshanensis, F. shennongjiaensis, and other synonyms—constitute a single, genetically cohesive population. The CV error progressively increased from K = 2 (0.5234) to K = 5 (0.7356), indicating that models assuming population subdivision fit the data poorly compared to the single-population model. Even when forcing the data into two clusters (K = 2), the resulting pattern did not align with taxonomic boundaries; instead, the "Others" group shared the dominant ancestral component (blue) with T. japonica, while the secondary component (red) appeared as an admixture gradient rather than a distinct lineage signature. As K increased from 3 to 5, the inferred ancestry fractions became increasingly fragmented and complex, yet they failed to recover any clear separation corresponding to morphological species classifications. Collectively, these results strongly imply that the observed morphological diversity exists within a single gene pool characterized by extensive gene flow.

Population genetic statistics reveal minimal differentiation between groups

To assess the recombination potential and demographic history of the populations, we performed Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) decay analysis (Fig. 5). Generally, LD (r2) decayed rapidly with increasing physical distance in both groups, a pattern typical of insect populations with high recombination rates. However, distinct patterns were observed between the two groups. The T. japonica group exhibited an extremely rapid decay, with mean r2 values dropping below the baseline threshold of 0.1 within the first few kilobases and stabilizing at a low level (~ 0.05). In contrast, the "Others" group displayed consistently higher LD values across the analyzed range (0–50 kb), maintaining an average r2 close to or slightly above 0.1. This disparity indicates that while sharing a common genetic background, the "Others" group likely possesses a smaller effective population size (Ne) or has experienced a more recent demographic bottleneck compared to the broader, more expansive T. japonica population.

The x-axis represents the physical distance between SNP pairs in kilobases (kb), and the y-axis represents the average squared correlation coefficient (r2). The blue line represents T. japonica, and the red line represents the "Others" group. The horizontal dashed grey line indicates the threshold of r2 = 0.1

Then, we analyzed the joint distribution of FST and nucleotide diversity π across the genome to further investigate the interplay between population differentiation and genetic diversity (Fig. 6A). The results reveal that the vast majority of genomic windows exhibit extremely low differentiation (FST < 0.05) coupled with low nucleotide diversity (π < 0.002). While the T. japonica group (blue points) exhibits a slightly wider range of nucleotide diversity values compared to the "Others" group (red points), the regression lines for both groups are nearly flat, indicating that genomic differentiation is not strongly driven by regions of exceptional high or low diversity. The marginal density plots further confirm that the distributions of both metrics are heavily skewed towards zero, reinforcing the finding of minimal genetic divergence between the two groups.

The genome-wide distribution of Tajima’s D (Fig. 6B) displays a unimodal pattern centered near zero (Mean < −0.15), suggesting that the majority of the genome is evolving under neutral expectations or mild purifying selection. The distribution is slightly skewed towards negative values (D < 0), which is often indicative of a recent population expansion or purifying selection removing deleterious alleles. Conversely, the right tail (D > 0) suggests a smaller proportion of loci may be under balancing selection. The close proximity of the mean (blue solid line) and median (red dashed line) to zero further supports the lack of strong, genome-wide bottleneck signatures or strong directional selection distinguishing the groups.

Phylogenetic analysis based on genome-wide SNPs reveals paraphyly of taxonomic units

In aim to assess the taxonomic validity of the suspected synonyms, we constructed an unrooted Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree based on genome-wide SNPs (Fig. 7). The resulting topology displays a radial, star-like pattern with extremely short internal branch lengths (scale = 0.01), indicating a recent radiation and a lack of deep divergence. Although the samples visually separate into two shallow clusters (shaded in blue and orange), these groups do not correspond to species boundaries. Instead, they likely reflect subtle geographic population substructure. Crucially, the "Others" group, which is composed of taxa previously described as distinct species, is not monophyletic. For example, F. shennongjiaensis (HBSNFS) and several nominal synonym species (e.g., SCWLTR, CMZTQ, GXGLTJ) clustered within the orange sector together with certain southern populations of T. japonica. In contrast, F. gonggashanensis (SCGZFG01, SCGZFG02), A. wudangshanensis (CQWSAW), and T. ruyuanensis (GDNLTR) were phylogenetically embedded within the blue cluster, grouping with T. japonica individuals from regions such as northeastern China and Inner Mongolia. This intertwined phylogenetic placement, combined with the minimal genetic distances separating all individuals, strongly supports the conclusion that they belong to a single, genetically cohesive species complex.

The blue shaded area primarily contains populations from Northern and Eastern regions (e.g., Heilongjiang, Inner Mongolia) but also includes individuals from the "Others" group (e.g., SCGZFG, CQWSAW, GDNLTR). The orange shaded area aggregates populations largely from Southwestern China (e.g., Guangxi, Yunnan, Sichuan). The scale bar (top left) represents a genetic distance of 0.01

Phylogenetic relationships were also reconstructed using Maximum Likelihood (ML) inference based on the genome-wide SNP supermatrix to rigorously test the monophyly of the morphospecies (Fig. 8). The resulting topology, characterized by maximal ultrafast bootstrap support (BS = 100) at most major nodes, decisively rejects the validity of the current generic boundaries. Contrary to the expectation of distinct evolutionary trajectories for separate genera, all sampled taxa representing Alulatettix and Formosatettix are deeply nested within the Tetrix japonica complex.

Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree. Numbers at nodes indicate ultrafast bootstrap support values (%). The scale bar represents the branch length

The tree topology demonstrates that phenotypic traits used for generic diagnosis, such as wing atrophy, do not reflect phylogenetic history. Notably, A. wudangshanensis (CQWSAW) and F. gonggashanensis (SCGZFG01, SCGZFG02) form a highly supported subclade that is phylogenetically subordinate to the broader T. japonica metapopulation. This subclade clusters as the sister group to T. japonica individuals of RBJY, and together they are embedded within a larger clade containing F. shennongjiaensis (HBSNFS) and the provisional Tetrix sp. (SCGZTS). Similarly, putative species defined by minor morphological variations, such as T. ruyuanensis (GDNLTR) and T. qinlingensis (SCMZTQ), do not form monophyletic groups but are scattered among T. japonica lineages from geographically corresponding regions (e.g., Guangdong and Sichuan, respectively).

The BPP A10 analysis provided strong support for collapsing the two candidate populations into a single species. Under equal prior probabilities for the one-species and two-species models (prior = 0.500 each), the posterior probability of the single-species model was 0.786 (P[1] = 0.786), whereas the two-species model received a posterior probability of only 0.214 (P[2] = 0.214). This result indicates that the multispecies coalescent model favors a scenario in which the genetic variation observed among the sampled populations is better explained by intraspecific population structure than by the existence of two reproductively isolated lineages.

Genetic continuity and shared demographic history refute species boundaries

Machine learning-based classification decisively rejected the validity of the morphological species boundaries. A Random Forest classifier trained on the genome-wide SNP dataset failed to distinguish the proposed synonym taxa (the "Others" group) from the nominate T. japonica. While the model identified T. japonica individuals with high accuracy (97.4%), it exhibited a catastrophic error rate of 83.3% when classifying the "Others" group, assigning 10 out of 12 individuals to the T. japonica class (Fig. 9). This asymmetric confusion matrix indicates that the genetic variation of the "Others" group is almost entirely encompassed within the standing variation of T. japonica, rather than representing a distinct evolutionary trajectory.

Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) further clarified this pattern, revealing that the "Others" group does not form a cohesive genetic cluster. Instead, it displays a fragmented distribution along the first discriminant function (Fig. 10). While a subset of the "Others" samples forms a divergent peak, the remaining individuals show density peaks that overlap with the T. japonica cluster. This multimodal distribution confirms that the taxonomic labels assigned to these morphotypes do not correspond to discrete genetic lineages but rather to scattered phenotypes within the complex.

Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) density plot. The plot shows the density distribution of individuals along the first discriminant function (LD1)

Furthermore, the observed genetic differentiation across the dataset is largely attributable to geographic isolation rather than reproductive barriers. Isolation by Distance (IBD) analysis confirmed a significant positive correlation between genetic and geographic distances (Mantel test: r = 0.152, P = 0.024; Fig. 11), suggesting that gene flow is constrained primarily by spatial separation. Finally, the Site Frequency Spectra (SFS) for both groups are nearly identical, characterized by a strong skew toward rare alleles (Fig. 12). This shared L-shaped distribution implies that both the nominal T. japonica and the synonymized taxa have experienced congruent demographic histories—likely a recent population expansion—further corroborating their status as a single, evolving metapopulation.

Discussion

Our comprehensive genomic analysis of the T. japonica complex provides compelling evidence for the need to revise current taxonomic boundaries within this group. Through the investigation of nuclear genomic data, we demonstrate that many currently recognized species likely represent geographic variants of a single, widely distributed species complex rather than distinct evolutionary lineages. This finding has important implications for both taxonomic practice and our understanding of morphological evolution in Orthoptera.

Species boundaries and geographic structure

Our genomic analyses provide robust evidence supporting the synonymization of multiple nominal taxa within the T. japonica complex. Contrary to the historical oversplitting based on morphological traits, our population genetic assessments reveal a cohesive genetic structure inconsistent with the existence of multiple independent lineages. Both Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and ADMIXTURE analyses (optimal K = 1) failed to detect any distinct clustering corresponding to the pre-defined taxonomic groups, suggesting that T. japonica and its purported synonyms share a single, unified gene pool. The Bayesian species delimitation analysis under the MSC model further corroborated our conclusion. BPP assigned a posterior probability of 0.786 to the single-species model, substantially exceeding the prior probability of 0.500, while the two-species hypothesis received only marginal support (P = 0.214). BPP favored lumping rather than splitting, which strengthens the conclusion that the observed genetic differentiation does not warrant species-level recognition. This is particularly notable because the MSC-based approach is independent of the population genetic methods (PCA, ADMIXTURE, F_ST) used above, yet yields the same taxonomic conclusion, providing a robust framework of converging evidence for synonymization.

Instead of taxonomic stratification, the observed genetic variation is primarily driven by geography. The phylogenomic reconstruction demonstrates that individuals cluster largely by sampling locality rather than by species designation. For instance, the “Northern Clade” aggregates individuals from high-latitude regions regardless of their wing morphology or body size, while southern samples form intertwined lineages that strictly follow a latitudinal gradient. Furthermore, the extremely low genome-wide differentiation (FST < 0.03) and the polyphyletic placement of the synonymized taxa within the T. japonica tree topology reinforces the conclusion that these “species” merely represent geographically localized populations of a single, widespread metapopulation.

Taxonomic implications and species concept

Our findings necessitate a fundamental reassessment of species boundaries within the T. japonica complex. The lack of genetic differentiation between currently recognized species, despite their morphological differences, suggests that many of these taxa represent local variants rather than evolutionarily distinct lineages. This is particularly evident for geographically-named species such as T. qinlingensis, T. jiuwanshanensis, and Tetrix sp., which our data once again confirm at the molecular level that they are synonyms of T. japonica.

More provocatively, our results challenge traditional generic boundaries within Tetrigidae. The inclusion of micropterous (Alulatettix) and apterous (Formosatettix) forms in our analysis reveals that these morphological variants fall within the genetic diversity of T. japonica. This suggests that wing polymorphism, a character traditionally used for generic delimitation in Tetrigidae, may be more plastic than previously assumed and potentially represents adaptive responses to local environmental conditions rather than deep evolutionary divergences.

Evolutionary implications and morphological plasticity

The discordance between morphological variation and genetic differentiation offers important insights into the evolution of phenotypic diversity in insects. The maintenance of distinct morphological forms despite genetic cohesion strongly suggests that these variants may represent environmentally-induced morphological plasticity rather than fixed genetic differences underpinning speciation. This hypothesis is supported by the geographic structuring of genetic variation and the deep phylogenetic intermingling of multiple morphotypes.

While wing polymorphism often blurs species boundaries and can lead to taxonomic over-splitting [55], it is crucial to distinguish between intraspecific polyphenism and actual speciation events. For instance, a genomic study on Chorthippus corsicus complex demonstrated that despite similar wing dimorphism (short-winged vs. long-winged forms), the two morphs represent distinct, reproductively isolated species that diverged in the Pleistocene with ancestral gene flow [56]. This case highlights that detailed phylogenetic and population genomic analyses are essential to resolve such controversies.

In contrast, our findings align with other examples where pronounced morphological plasticity has misled taxonomy [57, 58]. Unlike the scenario in Chorthippus corsicus, the T. japonica complex shows no genetic segregation corresponding to its striking morphological forms. Therefore, it may represent an excellent model system for studying how environmental variation shapes phenotypic expression within a single, widespread species, particularly in the context of wing polymorphism and body size variation in insects [59–61]. The pronounced structural plasticity observed in T. japonica perfectly mirrors other classical examples of taxonomic uncertainty in Orthoptera. Just as wing dimorphism (macropterous vs. brachypterous morphs) has long obscured true species boundaries in the T. bipunctata complex [6], our data show that the wing reductions defining Alulatettix and Formosatettix are merely structural polymorphisms within T. japonica. Similar taxonomic corrections are increasingly common for color traits in Orthoptera; recent studies confirm that extreme color morphs in Tetrigidae are often strictly intraspecific [10], driven by environmental substrates [62], genetic allelomorphism [11], or ecological niche segregation [9]. Our genomic evidence confirms that flightlessness and wing variation in the T. japonica complex are subject to these same rules of high non-genetic or polymorphic plasticity.

Genomic-driven reclassification and the collapse of generic boundaries

The integration of phylogenomics with machine learning algorithms provides an objective, quantifiable mandate for dismantling the artificial boundaries within the T. japonica complex [63, 64]. In traditional morphological taxonomy, the degree of wing development (macroptery, brachyptery, microptery, aptery) and body size have served as key morphological criteria for generic delimitation within Tetrigidae [65]. However, our Random Forest analysis delivers a decisive rebuttal to this practice. The classifier’s inability to distinguish the proposed synonym taxa from T. japonica—manifested in a unidirectional misclassification rate of 83.3%—demonstrates that the phenotypic traits defining genera like Alulatettix and Formosatettix possess no predictive power at the genomic level. Unlike subjective morphological assessments, this algorithmic failure serves as an independent validation that the "Others" group represents a subset of the standing genetic variation of T. japonica rather than discrete evolutionary lineages.

Phylogenetic reconstruction further exposes the structural flaws in the current classification. The recovered topology decisively rejects the monophyly of the wing-based genera. Individuals currently assigned to Alulatettix and Formosatettix are deeply nested within the T. japonica clade, often clustering more closely with geographically proximate T. japonica populations than with conspecifics from distant localities. Specifically, the placement of A. wudangshanensis and F. gonggashanensis as a subclade sister to T. japonica (RBJY), surrounded by other T. japonica lineages, confirms that flightlessness and wing reduction have evolved independently or persisted as polymorphisms within the complex.

Finally, The congruent Site Frequency Spectra (SFS) and the significant Isolation by Distance (IBD) pattern indicate that the observed variation is shaped by a shared demographic expansion constrained by geography, not by reproductive barriers. The “species” described in the past were essentially snapshots of local populations along a continuous genetic gradient. Therefore, the genomic data from this study provide molecular evidence in support of the previous hypothesis that A. wudangshanensis, F. shennongjiaensis, and F. gonggashanensis are members of the T. japonica species complex. Nevertheless, no formal taxonomic revision is proposed in the present work. A robust and thorough revision will require further integration with morphological evidence, as well as comprehensive sampling and taxonomic treatment of additional congeneric taxa across Alulatettix and Formosatettix.

Conclusion and broader impact

The genomic data obtained in this study support the hypothesis that the morphotypes previously treated as distinct species within the T. japonica complex are conspecific and exhibit limited genetic differentiation. This finding suggests that species delimitation based solely on specific morphological traits may, in some cases, underestimate intraspecific variation. Consequently, taxonomic frameworks relying exclusively on morphology may be unreliable for taxonomic groups with high phenotypic plasticity.

The study of species delimitation in insects is undergoing a transition from a singular morphological metric to an integrated, evidence-based approach. However, advancements in technology must be complemented by the evolution of theoretical frameworks. Future research endeavors should concentrate on amalgamating evolutionary biology, ecology, and computational science to tackle the challenges associated with cryptic species identification and dynamic species formation. In particular, integrating geometric morphometrics to quantify subtle shape variation, expanding sampling efforts to include type localities of nominal species and genera, and combining morphological, genomic, and environmental data within an integrative analytical framework will be crucial for resolving taxonomic uncertainties in Tetrigidae and other morphologically variable insect groups. This multidisciplinary approach will provide a more robust foundation for accurate species delimitation and enhance our understanding of the intricate relationships between genotype, phenotype, and environment.
