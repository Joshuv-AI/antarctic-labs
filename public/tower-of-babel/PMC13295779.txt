Impact of socioeconomic deprivation on early and long-term outcomes after major aortic surgery: insights from a 20-year single-centre cohort
Authors: Marco Gemelli, Lauren K Dixon, Thanakorn Rojanathagoon, Ettorino Di Tommaso, Anilkumar S Annaiah, Daniel P Fudulu, Gianni D Angelini, Eltayeb Mohamed Ahmed, Cha Rajakaruna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocioeconomic deprivation has been associated with adverse outcomes after aortic surgery, although evidence remains inconsistent. We assessed its impact on postoperative and long-term outcomes after major aortic surgery.MethodsWe included consecutive patients who underwent type A acute aortic dissection (TAAAD) repair or elective/urgent major aortic surgery between 2004 and 2025 at our institution. Socioeconomic status was determined using the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) and patients were further categorised into most deprived (quintiles 1–3) and least deprived (quintiles 4–5). A secondary analysis evaluated outcomes across all five IMD quintiles. Postoperative outcomes and long-term survival were compared using univariable and multivariable models.ResultsThe TAAAD cohort included 526 patients (median age 64 years; 34% female), with 250 (47.5%) classified as most deprived and 276 (52.5%) as least deprived. In-hospital mortality was significantly higher among the most deprived group (20% vs 12%, p=0.008), with deprivation independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.20 to 4.96). 10-year survival was also significantly reduced in the most deprived group (56.0% vs 69.4%, p<0.001), and deprivation remained an independent predictor of long-term mortality (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.22 to 2.28). The elective/urgent cohort comprised 1320 patients (median age 65 years; 32% female), with 696 (52.7%) classified as most deprived and 624 (47.3%) as least deprived. No significant differences were observed between groups in postoperative and long-term outcomes.ConclusionsSocioeconomic deprivation independently predicts higher postoperative and long-term mortality in patients undergoing emergency TAAAD repair, whereas no association was observed in the elective/urgent cohort.

Introduction

Socioeconomic deprivation is a major determinant of health inequalities and has been consistently associated with increased mortality, reduced life expectancy and poorer quality of life.1 Populations living in deprived areas experience substantially shorter survival than those in affluent regions, reflecting the broader global gradient in health outcomes observed between high-income nations and resource-limited settings.2

These disparities extend across a wide spectrum of diseases; for instance, among patients with diabetes mellitus suffering an acute myocardial infarction, those from the most deprived communities demonstrate significantly higher 1-year, 5-year and overall mortality compared with their least deprived counterparts.3 Socioeconomic deprivation also critically influences surgical outcomes. Multiple studies have shown that deprivation is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications and mortality, likely mediated by a combination of delayed presentation, higher baseline comorbidity burden and reduced access to coordinated postoperative care.46 In cardiac surgery specifically, Chan et al demonstrated that patients from deprived backgrounds exhibit greater clinical complexity and a measurable decline in in-hospital survival following coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).7 Furthermore, structural inequities, such as female sex, black ethnicity and socioeconomic disadvantage, have been independently linked to reduced access to cardiac surgical services and inferior postoperative outcomes.8

Emerging data suggest that these disparities may be particularly relevant in aortic surgery, where outcomes depend on operative management and on timely presentation and rigorous long-term surveillance.9 In a private healthcare environment such as the USA, Catalano et al recently reported that socioeconomic deprivation predicted higher long-term mortality after type A acute aortic dissection (TAAAD) repair, despite comparable early postoperative outcomes.10 Nevertheless, evidence from universal, publicly funded healthcare systems remains limited.

The present study aims to evaluate the impact of socioeconomic deprivation on short- and long-term outcomes after emergency TAAAD repair and elective or urgent major aortic surgery within the publicly funded UK National Health Service (NHS), where access to care is intended to be equitable. We hypothesised that increasing socioeconomic deprivation would likely be associated with worse early and long-term outcomes.

Methods

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set out in the Declaration of Helsinki by the World Medical Association.

Registry and data collection

Our study was a single-centre retrospective observational cohort analysis based on data collected for the National Adult Cardiac Surgery Audit (NACSA) registry. The NACSA registry is a prospectively maintained national database that includes detailed preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative information on all adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery in the UK.11 The Bristol Heart Institute (Bristol, UK) data of this registry have been reviewed and all adult (≥18 years old) patients who underwent major aortic surgery from April 2004 to April 2025 were included. Major aortic surgery was defined as any operation involving the replacement of at least a part of the aorta. Exclusion criteria were paediatric age and non-aortic surgery performed. Major aortic surgery was defined as any surgical procedure involving replacement of at least a portion of the aorta. Exclusion criteria were patients aged <18 years and those undergoing cardiac surgery not involving the replacement of a portion of the aorta. Baseline characteristics, preoperative data, operative details, postoperative outcomes and long-term survival were collected, and due to the inherent clinical and procedural heterogeneity between acute and non-acute aortic pathologies, the study population was stratified into two distinct cohorts: patients who underwent elective or urgent major aortic surgery (‘Elective/Urgent cohort’) and patients who underwent emergency TAAAD repair (‘TAAAD cohort’). These cohorts were analysed independently to avoid confounding factors related to surgical priority and disease complexity. For each cohort, patients were further stratified by Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) quintiles to evaluate the specific impact of socioeconomic deprivation within each clinical setting.

Index of Multiple Deprivation

Socioeconomic deprivation was assessed using the English IMD 2019, using publicly available data from the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government. The IMD is a composite, area-level metric widely used in the UK to quantify relative deprivation, and it combines seven weighted domains (income, employment, education and skills, health and disability, crime, barriers to housing and services and living environment) to generate a comprehensive deprivation score for each lower layer super output area (LSOA), which is a small, standardised geographical unit in the UK, typically containing around 1500 residents, used by the Office for National Statistics (ONS) to report population-based indicators.12 For each patient, the residential postcode at the time of surgery was mapped to the corresponding LSOA using official lookup tables from the ONS, and the IMD value for that area was assigned as an area-based proxy for socioeconomic status. The IMD is reported nationally in five quintiles, ranging from 1 (most deprived) to 5 (least deprived). For the primary analysis, we grouped patients from both the cohorts (‘Elective/Urgent’ and ‘TAAAD’) into two categories to facilitate interpretation: a ‘Most deprived group’ (IMD quintiles 1–3) and a ‘Least deprived group’ (IMD quintiles 4–5). To ensure the robustness of our findings and explore potential non-linear trends across the deprivation gradient, we also performed a secondary analysis considering the full five-quintile IMD classification without grouping patients.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were in-hospital and long-term mortality, stratified by IMD status. In-hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause during the index hospital admission. Long-term survival was defined as the time from the index operation to death from any cause or last available follow-up, with survival status obtained from the NACSA registry. Cause-specific mortality was not available. Secondary outcomes included major in-hospital postoperative complications: cerebrovascular accident (CVA), new-onset dialysis, reoperation for bleeding and deep sternal wound infection (DSWI). A CVA was defined as any acute neurological deficit of presumed vascular origin, including both transient ischaemic attack (TIA) and stroke. TIA was defined as a transient episode of focal neurological dysfunction caused by ischaemia without evidence of acute infarction, whereas stroke was defined as a neurological deficit of vascular origin lasting >24 hours, resulting in death or associated with imaging evidence of infarction or haemorrhage. New-onset dialysis was defined as the requirement for at least one session of haemodialysis after the index operation in patients not receiving dialysis preoperatively. Reoperation for bleeding was defined as the need for surgical re-exploration due to excessive postoperative bleeding or evidence of pericardial effusion or cardiac tamponade. DSWI was defined as an infection involving the sternum and/or mediastinum requiring surgical debridement and/or prolonged antibiotic therapy.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as frequency counts and percentages and were compared using the Pearson’s χ2 test. Continuous variables were reported as mean and SD if normally distributed or as median and IQR if not. The normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and if parametric assumptions were satisfied, a Student’s t-test was used to perform the comparison. On the other hand, if parametric assumptions were not met, we used the Mann-Whitney U test. Risk factor analysis for in-hospital mortality was performed with logistic regression, and univariable logistic regression was performed using the IMD as variable. Survival analysis was performed with the Kaplan-Meier methods, and log-rank test was used to compare survival between the groups. Cox regression was used as a proportional hazards model to assess for significant predictors of long-term survival.

For both multivariable logistic and Cox regression analyses, all clinically plausible preoperative and intraoperative variables were initially considered as candidate predictors. Variables with conceptual overlap or limited clinical relevance were removed a priori. The remaining covariates were assessed for multicollinearity using the generalised variance inflation factor (GVIF), applying the GVIF1/(2⋅df) transformation to ensure comparability across variables with differing numbers of levels. All variables demonstrated acceptable collinearity and were therefore retained in the initial full models.

Given the limited number of outcome events, model building followed an events per variable-driven strategy aimed at achieving parsimony. Full multivariable models were first constructed including all eligible covariates. Model reduction was then performed using backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), sequentially removing predictors whose exclusion improved overall model fit. Deprivation level was forced to remain in all multivariable models, as it represented the primary exposure of interest. The final model for each analysis corresponded to the specification with the lowest AIC.

For in-hospital mortality, a multivariable logistic regression model was fitted, and ORs with 95% CIs were derived from the final model. Long-term mortality was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression, with follow-up time measured in years from the index procedure to death or last confirmed status (‘Died’ or ‘Alive’). Backward AIC-based selection was then applied in the same manner as for the logistic model, again retaining deprivation level throughout the process. HRs with 95% CIs were obtained from the final Cox model. All statistical analysis was performed using jamovi V.2.3.21.0 (The jamovi project (2023). jamovi (V.2.3) (Computer Software). Retrieved from https://www.jamovi.org).

Results

Study population

During the study period, 2206 patients were recorded in the NACSA registry in Bristol as having undergone major aortic surgery. After case verification, 196 were excluded because they had been incorrectly classified as major aortic procedures, represented duplicate entries or had insufficient data available. The final study cohort therefore comprised 2010 patients: 536 who underwent emergency TAAAD repair (‘TAAAD cohort’) and 1402 who had elective or urgent major aortic surgery (‘Elective/Urgent cohort’). The remaining 72 patients were excluded because they underwent non-TAAAD emergency aortic procedures. A flow chart of included and excluded patients is provided in online supplemental material n1.

In the TAAAD cohort, the IMD could be calculated for 526 patients (98%). Among them, 250 (47.5%) were classified in the most deprived group and 276 (52.5%) in the least deprived group (table 1). In the elective/urgent cohort, IMD was available for 1320 patients (94%), of whom 696 (52.7%) belonged to the most deprived group and 624 (47.3%) to the least deprived group (table 1). For the remaining 94 patients, IMD could not be determined, and these cases were excluded from the analysis. Further details on IMD are available in online supplemental material n2.

TAAAD surgery

Baseline characteristics, intraoperative details and postoperative outcomes of the TAAAD cohort are summarised in tables2 3. No significant differences were observed in any of the variables between the most and least deprived groups. In-hospital mortality was significantly higher in the most deprived group (20% vs 11.6%, p=0.008) (table 3). Higher deprivation level was an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality after TAAAD repair, both in univariable analysis (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.15 to 3.02, p=0.012) and after multivariable adjustment (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.20 to 4.96, p=0.014). The final multivariable model accounted for age, hypertension, pulmonary disease, prior CVA, preoperative atrial fibrillation, preoperative cardiogenic shock, cardiopulmonary bypass time, circulatory arrest time and root procedure (AIC 66.9). Further details on multivariable model are available in online supplemental material n3. Regarding postoperative outcomes, the rates of reopening for bleeding, DSWI, new CVA and postoperative dialysis did not differ between groups.

In the secondary analysis using the full five-quintile IMD classification without aggregating patients into broader groups, we observed similar findings for postoperative outcomes. In-hospital mortality did not differ significantly across IMD quintiles. However, a numerically higher mortality was noted in the most deprived quintile (25%, 16% and 21% in quintiles 1, 2 and 3, respectively) compared with the least deprived quintiles (11% and 16% in quintiles 4 and 5, respectively) (p=0.06). Full details of the secondary analysis are available in online supplemental material n4.

In terms of long-term outcomes, patients in the least deprived group demonstrated significantly better survival compared with those in the most deprived group at 1 year (84.7% vs 77.6%), 5 years (79.9% vs 67.4%) and 10 years (69.4% vs 56.0%). The corresponding Kaplan-Meier curve is shown in figure 1A. Socioeconomic deprivation was significantly associated with worse long-term survival. In univariable analysis, higher deprivation was associated with increased mortality risk (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.09 to 2.02, p=0.012). This association remained independent in the multivariable Cox model (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.22 to 2.28, p=0.001), adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, peripheral vascular disease, root procedure, total arch replacement and concomitant CABG (AIC 270.8). Further details on multivariable model are available in online supplemental material n5. The hazard regression plot and adjusted survival curve are provided in online supplemental material n6.

A landmark analysis excluding all patients who died in hospital and restricting the cohort to hospital survivors yielded consistent results. Among discharged patients with TAAAD, deprivation continued to be independently associated with long-term mortality (HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.69, p=0.006). The Kaplan-Meier curve, hazard plot and adjusted survival curve for this analysis are presented in online supplemental material n7.

Analysis based on individual IMD quintiles further confirmed these findings. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, patients in the least deprived quintiles exhibited a tendency towards improved survival compared with those in the most deprived quintiles, with quintile 5 showing a significantly reduced risk of long-term mortality (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.89, p=0.018). The corresponding Kaplan-Meier curve is shown in figure 1B, while 1, 5 and 10-year survival by quintile, along with hazard regression plots and adjusted survival curves, are reported in online supplemental material n8.

Elective/urgent major aortic surgery

Urgent major aortic cases accounted for 22.7% (158 patients) of the most deprived subgroup and 25.2% (157 patients) of the least deprived subgroup within the elective/urgent major aortic cohort, with no significant difference between the two (p=0.30). Baseline characteristics, intraoperative details and postoperative outcomes are summarised in tables4 5. Patients in the least deprived group were significantly older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension, whereas the remaining baseline characteristics were broadly comparable. In terms of intraoperative features, patients in the least deprived group underwent a higher number of procedures requiring circulatory arrest, although the duration of circulatory arrest was shorter. Conversely, the most deprived group had a significantly higher proportion of mechanical valve implantations and a lower rate of concomitant CABG. Postoperative outcomes did not differ significantly between the two groups, with similar rates of in-hospital mortality, reopening for bleeding, DSWI, CVA and dialysis.

Discussion

In this 20-year single-centre analysis, socioeconomic deprivation emerged as a key determinant of early and long-term outcomes in patients undergoing emergency TAAAD repair. Patients from the most deprived areas experienced significantly higher in-hospital mortality and markedly worse long-term survival compared with their least deprived counterparts, despite the absence of meaningful differences in baseline characteristics or intraoperative management between groups. Socioeconomic deprivation remained an independent predictor of both early and late mortality across all multivariable models, underscoring the consistency and robustness of this association. Notably, even when the analysis was restricted to patients discharged alive, the most deprived group continued to exhibit significantly worse long-term survival.

These findings were further reinforced by the secondary analyses: when deprivation was assessed across all five IMD quintiles rather than dichotomised groups, the gradient in survival persisted, confirming the stability of the observed relationship and ruling out potential artefacts of the chosen grouping strategy.

Importantly, the detrimental impact of deprivation was observed within the context of a universalistic, publicly funded healthcare system where access to care is theoretically equitable. This indicates that deprivation continues to influence disease presentation, treatment pathways and long-term management even when financial barriers are minimised. The well-described ‘postcode lottery’ further reflects persistent geographical variation in healthcare delivery within the NHS, highlighting place-based inequities that extend beyond access alone.13 Consistent with this, Omoregbee et al reported significant differences in presentation and outcomes after aortic valve replacement across postcode areas, closely aligned with local deprivation levels.14 This highlights that socioeconomic factors continue to influence presentation, disease trajectory and long-term management even when structural barriers to healthcare access are minimised.

In contrast, no association was identified between deprivation and postoperative or long-term outcomes among patients undergoing elective or urgent major aortic surgery. In this cohort, perioperative mortality, complication rates and long-term survival were comparable across deprivation strata. This divergence likely reflects the fundamentally different disease pathways: whereas elective and urgent repairs are typically definitive interventions with limited residual disease burden, TAAAD repair often requires lifelong surveillance and optimal management of comorbidities, processes that may be more susceptible to the influence of socioeconomic disadvantage.

The divergent impact of socioeconomic deprivation on outcomes between the two surgical cohorts represents one of the most striking findings of this study. TAAAD is a time-critical, catastrophic condition in which delays in symptom recognition, access to emergency services and timely referral can have profound consequences.15 Matthews et al demonstrated that the time intervals between symptom onset, diagnosis and surgery significantly influence mortality.16 Operations performed 8–12 hours after diagnosis were associated with the highest mortality, a risk likely amplified by longer delays from initial symptom onset. Socioeconomically deprived patients may present later, with more advanced physiological compromise, or may face barriers in accessing prompt diagnostic pathways, factors that are not fully captured within perioperative registries but nonetheless contribute to poorer outcomes.15 17 18 Moreover, postoperative management after TAAAD repair demands lifelong imaging surveillance, strict blood pressure control, adherence to medical therapy and close monitoring of comorbidities, domains in which socioeconomic disadvantage may profoundly affect long-term outcomes.19 20 Melby et al reported that the risk of reoperation after TAAAD repair was significantly higher in patients not receiving β-blocker therapy, whereas improved systolic blood pressure control was associated with a reduced need for reintervention.21 Similarly, Sharma et al showed that nearly one-third of patients with aortic dissection fail to attend their first recommended follow-up visit, and this early non-attendance strongly predicts subsequent non-compliance.22 Notably, low-compliant patients experience a twofold higher lifetime risk of death compared with those who adhere to follow-up recommendations. In fact, our study showed that, even among patients discharged alive, the survival curves diverged after approximately 3 years, with the most deprived cohort demonstrating progressively worse outcomes. This pattern may reflect suboptimal long-term management of comorbidities and dissection-related complications, including inadequate blood pressure control, which is a key determinant of late prognosis in this population.

Conversely, elective and urgent major aortic procedures are typically performed after structured preoperative assessment and optimisation, within planned pathways that minimise disparities in presentation and acuity. Most such operations are definitive, leaving little or no residual aortic disease, thereby reducing the need for long-term follow-up. Hernandez-Vaquero et al demonstrated, for example, that patients undergoing elective surgery for ascending aortic aneurysm who survive the postoperative period can fully recover a normal life expectancy in the long term.23 Furthermore, Etz et al, in a cohort of more than 200 patients undergoing the Bentall procedure for bicuspid aortic valve disease, demonstrated that those discharged from hospital achieved long-term survival comparable to that of an age and sex-matched general population.24 In this controlled setting, the effect of socioeconomic deprivation on outcomes appears attenuated, and postoperative survival becomes largely determined by the underlying disease process and operative complexity rather than social determinants of health.

Together, these differences suggest that deprivation exerts its strongest influence in conditions where early presentation, emergency access to care and rigorous long-term management are critical, whereas its impact is less pronounced in planned surgical care pathways with standardised perioperative management.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained registry and therefore remains subject to the inherent constraints of observational research, including the possibility of unmeasured confounding. In particular, factors such as disease severity at presentation, referral pathways and prehospital selection may have influenced the observed associations. Since our cohort only includes patients who underwent surgery, there is an inherent selection bias regarding those managed conservatively or those who did not survive to intervention, which limits the ability to draw definitive causal interpretations. Furthermore, we did not have specific data regarding the timing of presentation, transfer delays or the presence of malperfusion syndromes at the time of admission. Given that these factors are critical determinants of clinical outcomes in TAAAD and may significantly influence the relationship between deprivation and surgical results, their omission from our regression analysis is an important limitation that should be considered when interpreting our findings. Second, the investigation was conducted at a single high-volume centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other institutions with different patient demographics, referral patterns or clinical pathways. Furthermore, although the NACSA registry provides detailed perioperative information, certain clinical or socioeconomic variables that could influence presentation, treatment decisions or long-term outcomes may not have been captured. The use of the IMD as an area-level measure may introduce misclassification, as individuals within the same postcode can have substantially different socioeconomic circumstances that are not captured by the IMD. In addition, deprivation was assessed using IMD 2019 data across the entire study period; although IMD rankings are relatively stable over time, this approach may not fully account for temporal changes in area-level deprivation and could have further contributed to misclassification. Finally, long-term mortality data were obtained from national records, but the specific cause of death was not available. As a result, we were unable to determine whether late mortality was attributable to aortic pathology, cardiovascular disease or unrelated causes, which limits the interpretability of long-term outcome differences between deprivation groups.

Conclusions

Socioeconomic deprivation was independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality and significantly worse long-term survival after emergency TAAAD repair. In contrast, deprivation did not influence perioperative outcomes or long-term mortality in patients undergoing elective or urgent major aortic surgery. This disparity likely reflects the fact that TAAAD repair is rarely definitive, and long-term outcomes depend heavily on consistent follow-up, comorbidity management and timely access to care. These are all factors that may be less readily available to socioeconomically deprived patients.
