Making Separation-First Multi-Stream Audio Watermarking Feasible via Joint Training
Houmin, Zi, Linxi, Yechen, Liwei, Ming

Abstract
Modern audio is created by mixing stems from different sources, raising the question: can we independently watermark each stem and recover all watermarks after separation? We study a separation-first, multi-stream watermarking framework –embedding distinct information into stems using unique keys but a shared structure, mixing, separating, and decoding from each output. A naive pipeline (robust watermarking + off-the-shelf separation) yields poor bit recovery, showing robustness to generic distortions does not ensure robustness to separation artifacts. To enable this, we jointly train the watermark system and the separator in an end-to-end manner, encouraging the separator to preserve watermark cues while adapting embedding to separation-specific distortions. Experiments on speech+music and vocal+accompaniment mixtures show substantial gains in post-separation recovery while maintaining perceptual quality.

Introduction

Recent advances in AI-generated speech
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
and neural audio editing have made it increasingly difficult to protect the copyright and provenance of digital audio content.
Audio watermarking has therefore emerged as a key technology for copyright protection and tamper detection
[
5
]
.
Classical audio watermarking methods usually operate directly in the time or frequency domain, such as phase coding, spread-spectrum embedding, and low-bit coding
[
6
]
.

To improve robustness and payload capacity, deep neural audio watermarking methods have been proposed, including WavMark
[
7
]
, MaskMark
[
8
]
, and SilentCipher
[
9
]
.
These approaches demonstrate strong robustness–imperceptibility trade-offs, enabling higher payloads while maintaining perceptual audio quality.

However, modern generative pipelines often combine audio from multiple sources and rights holders.
A single synthetic waveform may contain a watermarked music accompaniment and a separately watermarked or unwatermarked vocal stream.
Existing watermarking systems are typically designed for single-watermark, single-carrier settings and degrade sharply in such multi-source mixtures.

A natural strategy is to apply a music source separator (e.g., Demucs
[
10
]
, TF-GridNet
[
11
]
, Open-Unmix
[
12
]
, or Spleeter
[
13
]
) to decompose the mixture into stems and then detect watermarks per stem.
In practice, separation is not lossless: even state-of-the-art separators introduce artifacts that may corrupt watermark-bearing cues, leading to low detection rates despite acceptable perceptual quality
[
14
]
.

In this paper, we study whether independently watermarking different stems and decoding after source separation is feasible in realistic mixtures.
We show that directly combining a strong watermark with an off-the-shelf separator often fails due to a mismatch between robustness to generic distortions and robustness to separation-induced artifacts.
We then demonstrate that end-to-end joint training of the watermarking system and the separator together makes this separation-first Multi-Stream framework feasible.

Our contribution can be summarized as follows:

•

We formalize a separation-first, multi-stream audio watermarking evaluation setting.

•

We show that strong neural watermarking methods degrade to unacceptable decoding after separation.

•

We demonstrate that joint end-to-end training substantially improves post-separation robustness while maintaining perceptual quality.

Method

2.1
Problem Setup: Separation-First Multi-Stream Watermarking

We consider mixtures formed by multiple stems (e.g., vocal and accompaniment) that may originate from different sources.
Each stem carries an independent watermark embedded with a distinct secret key.
Given stems
\{x_{i}\}
, watermarks are embedded independently to obtain
\{\tilde{x}_{i}\}
.
The watermarked stems are mixed into a single waveform:

y=\sum_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}.

(1)

A source separator
S(\cdot)
is applied to the mixture to obtain separated estimates
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}=S(y)
.
The corresponding watermark is then decoded independently from each separated stem
\hat{x}_{i}
.

2.2.1
Encoder.

Given an
n
-second audio segment and a binary secret key, the encoder produces a bounded watermark perturbation in the time–frequency domain.
The audio is transformed into a magnitude spectrogram, while the key is embedded into a conditioning vector.
A stack of Conformer
[
15
]
blocks, equipped with Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers
[
17
]
immediately after the LayerNorm of each of its four core modules (two FFNs, MHSA, and CONV), predicts a real-valued modification mask applied to the magnitude spectrogram. The modified spectrogram is then inverted to the time domain using the original phase to produce the watermarked waveform.
The modified spectrogram is inverted to wavedomain combined with the original phase to produce the watermarked waveform.

2.2.2
Decoder.

The decoder takes a potentially distorted audio segment and predicts the embedded bits.
It follows a RobustDNN-style
[
18
]
architecture operating directly on the waveform, using multiple 1D convolutional layers to extract watermark-related features before bit prediction.

2.3
Source Separation Network

2.3.1
Demucs

We employ Demucs
[
10
]
as our source separation network. Demucs operates directly in the time domain, which is crucial for our framework as it avoids potential artifacts introduced by time-frequency transformations that could misalign or distort the embedded watermark signals. Its U-Net-like encoder-decoder architecture with strided convolutions enables multi-scale feature extraction while maintaining fine temporal details through skip connections
[
19
]
. The bidirectional LSTM
[
20
]
layers at the bottleneck effectively model long-range dependencies in audio, which is essential for coherently separating overlapping sources and preserving the continuity of watermark patterns. We use the standard Demucs architecture without modification to leverage its proven separation performance and ensure our findings are not biased by architectural changes.

2.4
Impact of Separation on Watermarking

Source separation is inherently lossy
[
21
]
. Audio separators often introduce artifacts such as phase misalignment, spectral smearing
[
22
]
, and cross-source interference (e.g., residual vocals in the separated accompaniment track)-limitations explicitly acknowledged by the authors of Demucs themselves
[
14
]
. These artifacts act as non-stationary, structured noise that is fundamentally different from the generic distortions (e.g., noise addition, compression) against which most watermarking systems are robust. Consequently, a watermark decoder trained only for generic robustness fails to decode correctly from separated stems, as shown in our experiments (Section 4). This mismatch necessitates a co-design approach where the watermarking system learns to be robust to the specific distortion profile introduced by the separator, and the separator is encouraged to preserve features critical for watermark recovery.

2.5
Joint Training Pipeline

To bridge the robustness gap identified in Section 2.4, we propose an end-to-end joint training framework that optimizes the watermarking encoder/decoder and the Demucs separator concurrently. This co-optimization is the core of our contribution. It allows the gradient from the watermark decoding loss to flow back through the separator, guiding it to preserve watermark-relevant features in its separated outputs. Simultaneously, the watermarking encoder learns to embed information in a manner that is more resilient to the specific, non-linear distortions introduced by the separation process.

The pipeline for each training iteration is detailed below and illustrated in Figure
1
:

1.

Segment Sampling and Watermark Embedding.
For each stem (e.g., vocal and accompaniment), we sample a full-length audio clip (approximately 20 seconds). From each clip, a
2-second segment
is cropped to serve as the carrier for watermarking. The 2-second duration represents a standard trade-off in audio watermarking, providing sufficient temporal context (
L=88200
samples at
44.1
kHz) for encoding a payload of
32
bits while maintaining computational efficiency for
AURA*
. Using our key-conditioned encoder
E(\cdot,\mathbf{k}_{i})
, we embed a unique binary watermark
\mathbf{b}_{i}
into each stem’s segment, conditioned on a distinct, randomly generated secret key
\mathbf{k}_{i}
. This yields watermarked segments
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{seg}}=E(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{seg}},\mathbf{k}_{i})
.

2.

Context Reinsertion.
The watermarked 2-second segments
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{seg}}
are spliced back into their original temporal positions within the 20-second, unwatermarked stems
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{full}}
. This produces full-length watermarked stems
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{full}}
. This step is critical because Demucs is trained on and expects full-track mixtures. Processing an isolated, short segment may not engage the separator’s long-range modeling capabilities (e.g., the bidirectional LSTMs) in the same way as processing a segment within its natural auditory context, which includes preceding and subsequent harmonics, transients, and temporal evolution.

3.

Mixture Creation.
The full-length watermarked stems
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{full}}
are summed to create the mixture
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{full}}
. Prior to summation, we
apply loudness normalization to each stem individually, targeting -16 LUFS (Loudness Units Full Scale)
using the
pyloudnorm
library. This simulates standard audio mixing practice, ensuring a balanced mixture where no single source dominates. A balanced mixture simplifies the initial separation task, leading to cleaner estimates and reducing a source of interference for the watermark decoder.

4.

Separation and Decoding.
The mixture
\mathbf{y}
is fed into the Demucs separator
S(\cdot)
, which produces estimates for each stem:
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{full}}=S_{i}(\mathbf{y})
. From these separated full-length tracks, we extract the 2-second slices
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{seg}}
that are temporally aligned with the originally watermarked segments. Finally, each
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{seg}}
is passed to the watermark decoder
D(\cdot)
to recover the predicted bit sequence
\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{i}=D(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\text{seg}})
.

The entire pipeline is fully differentiable, enabling end-to-end gradient propagation from the decoding loss at the final step back through the separator and to the watermark encoder.

2.6
Datasets and Training Setup.

The watermarking model is pre-trained on approximately 150 hours of instrumental music from Free Music Archive (FMA)
[
23
]
and 300 hours of speech from the Emilia
[
24
]
dataset. This pre-training phase is conducted on four NVIDIA RTX 4090D GPUs for 48 hours. Additional training uses MUSDB18
[
25
]
dataset. The end-to-end joint training is performed on four NVIDIA L20 GPUs for 72 hours. All audio is resampled to 44.1 kHz, and two-second segments are used as watermark carriers. We use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-4}
and initialize from pre-trained checkpoints.

2.7
Loss Functions

Training is driven by a multi-task objective for both the Demucs
[
10
]
and
AURA*

[
16
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=100\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bce}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{stft}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stft}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{spec}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{perc}}

(2)

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bce}}=\mathrm{BCE}(\mathbf{b},\hat{\mathbf{b}})
ensures reliable bit recovery.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stft}}
is a multi-resolution log-magnitude STFT loss across
K\!=\!3
resolutions (fft sizes: 1024, 2048, 512):
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stft}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\log(|X_{k}|\!)-\log(|\tilde{X}_{k}|\!)|_{1}
, where
X_{k}
and
\tilde{X}_{k}
are the original and watermarked spectrograms.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spec}}
computes the multi-scale Mel-spectrogram
L_{1}
distance. Pre-training follows a two-phase schedule: we initially prioritize bit recovery with minimal spectral weighting and no
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{perc}}
. After 20k steps, spectral weights are increased significantly and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{perc}}
is introduced, maintaining this balance throughout joint training. For imperceptibility,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{perc}}
combines a BigVGAN discriminator loss
[
26
]
and a Noise-to-Mask Ratio loss
[
27
]
:
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{nmr}}=\frac{1}{CT}\sum_{c,t}(N_{c,t}/M_{c,t})
, where
N_{c,t}
and
M_{c,t}
are the noise and masking patterns across
C
critical bands and
T
frames, explicitly penalizing distortions that exceed local time–frequency auditory masking thresholds. Crucially, to preserve separation integrity without collapsing the learned watermarking space, the separator is updated
exclusively
via an independent waveform-domain
L_{1}
loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sep}}=|\hat{v}-\mathrm{sg}(\tilde{v})|_{1}+|\hat{a}-\mathrm{sg}(\tilde{a})|_{1}

(3)

where
\hat{v},\hat{a}
are the separated vocal and accompaniment estimates, and
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
is a stop-gradient operation (
detach
) on the watermarked targets.

2.9
Perceptual Quality and Separation Integrity

We evaluate two complementary aspects of audio fidelity: the imperceptibility of embedded watermarks and the integrity of separated stems after joint training.

Watermark imperceptibility.
Table
2
presents the perceptual quality of watermarked audio (without separation). The Joint and Finetuned models achieve SNRs of 28.77 dB and 18.56 dB, respectively, with ViSQOL scores above 4.5. As ViSQOL aligns more closely with human auditory perception than waveform reconstruction metrics, these results confirm that the embedded watermarks remain effectively imperceptible. This confirms that our separation-aware training does not compromise the fundamental requirement of audio watermarking: inaudible embedding. The fact that separation-aware models do not outperform single-carrier baselines in this direct encoding/decoding setting is expected, as our joint objective explicitly prioritizes robustness to separation-induced distortions over standalone imperceptibility.

Separation integrity.
A critical question is whether optimizing for watermark robustness degrades the core functionality of the Demucs separator. Table
3
addresses this by comparing separation quality before and after joint training using standard source separation metrics (SDR and SI-SDR). The results demonstrate that separation integrity is well preserved: vocal SDR changes minimally from 9.36 to 8.89, while accompaniment SDR slightly improves from 12.50 to 13.19. We attribute this asymmetry to the interplay between watermark preservation and spectral density. The sparser vocal track forces the separator to retain watermark-bearing perturbations, marginally reducing its SDR. Conversely, the complex accompaniment spectrum allows joint training to effectively fine-tune the separator’s instrumental representations, slightly improving its SDR. These negligible differences confirm that our framework enhances watermark robustness without compromising the separator’s ability to produce high-quality stems. In other words, the joint training preserves the original performance of the separation network while adding watermarking capabilities.

2.10
Robustness against separation

To evaluate whether watermarking methods can preserve decoding accuracy after separation, we adopt a separation-first robustness standard aligned with the training pipeline in Section 2. Watermarks are embedded independently into two-second segments from different sources (speech+music and vocal+accompaniment), then mixed and separated using Demucs. BER is computed by decoding the aligned segments from each separated stem. As shown in Table 1, conventional neural watermarking methods such as WavMark and Audioseal exhibit unacceptable bit recovery after separation, with BER deteriorating to 20-30% on both benchmarks.
AURA*
provides only a minor improvement, suggesting that robustness to average attacks alone is insufficient to guarantee robustness to source separation.

In contrast, the jointly optimized models generalize substantially better to the 32-bit multi-stream separation setting. Both joint and finetuned variants achieve BER of approximately 1% on the speech+music benchmark, while the fine-tuned model further improves performance on the vocal+accompaniment benchmark (1 % BER). This improvement indicates that incorporating genuine vocal and accompaniment data enables the model to resolve the severe harmonic entanglement and spectral overlap that are inherently absent in artificially spliced speech and music mixtures. These results imply that end-to-end coupling enables the separator to preserve watermark-relevant cues and encourages the encoder–decoder to adapt to separation-specific artifacts. Consequently, the interference caused by multiple watermarks embedded across different stems can be reduced to an acceptable level for reliable decoding.

3
Conclusion

We studied the feasibility of separation-first multi-stream audio watermarking and showed that robustness to generic distortions does not guarantee robustness to source separation.
By jointly training the watermarking system and the separator end-to-end, we substantially improve post-separation decoding accuracy while maintaining competitive perceptual quality.
These results suggest that separation-aware training is essential for reliable watermarking in modern multi-source generative audio pipelines. While our current framework is limited to two-stem mixtures, future endeavors will aim to expand this joint optimization to more complex, multi-stem scenarios.
