Cerebral venous blood flow regulates intracerebral pressure and brain clearance via meningeal lymphatic vessels
Authors: Marie-Renee El Kamouh, Myriam Spajer, Ruchith Singhabahu, Anne-Laure Joly Marolany, Kurt A Sailor, Laura Mouton, Diana Doukhi, Sunil Koundal, Tanner Metcalfe, Dominique Langui, Abel Grine, Kevin Boyé, Felipe Saceanu Leser, Justus Ninnemann, Joshua Gottschalk, Cyrus Sadeghi, Han Xu, Ligia Simoes Braga Boisserand, David Akbar, Jerome Van Wassenhove, Anthony Ruze, Amelle Nasri, Marie-Charlotte Bourrienne, Elora Buscher, Young-Kwon Hong, Mikael Mazighi, Pierre-Marie Lledo, Helene Benveniste, Mathieu Santin, Anne Eichmann, Stéphane Lehericy, Jean-Léon Thomas, Stéphanie Lenck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is characterized by elevated intracranial pressure and dural venous sinus stenoses, which can be relieved by venous stenting. Here we investigated whether venous blood flow may play a role in controlling brain pressure. Using magnetic resonance imaging in patients with IIH and healthy controls, we identified that dural venous stenoses in IIH were associated with alterations of the perivenous fluid pattern and brain edema. We developed a mouse model of jugular vein ligation (JVL), which developed transient intracerebral hypertension, brain edema and impaired brain clearance, along with defective meningeal lymphatic vessels (MLVs). MLV depletion increased intracerebral pressure in control and JVL mice, but only MLV-deficient ligated mice failed to restore brain fluid clearance. These findings implicate MLVs in the control of intracerebral pressure and establish the dural venous sinuses as critical platforms where venous flow directs MLVs to ensure brain fluid clearance.

IIH is a neurological disorder characterized by headaches and elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) in the absence of a brain tumor or other identifiable disease1. IIH predominantly affects young women, often in the context of weight gain or obesity, suggesting a role for hormonal factors in its pathogenesis. Patients with IIH exhibit bilateral stenoses of the dural transverse venous sinuses, resulting in impaired cerebral venous outflow. Venous stenting effectively relieves venous obstruction, normalizes ICP and alleviates symptoms, indicating that disrupted venous blood flow contributes to abnormal brain fluid drainage in these patients2. However, the mechanisms by which cerebral venous outflow influences brain fluid clearance remain poorly understood.

The defective clearance of brain waste contributes to toxic protein accumulation and neurodegenerative diseases3. Because the brain is devoid of intrinsic lymphatic vasculature, brain clearance requires a special waste removal system that may implicate the intracerebral glymphatic system and the extracerebral lymphatic vasculature4. The glymphatic system is suggested to drain the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulating within the perivascular spaces and the brain parenchyma under the regulatory control of perivascular astrocytes5,6. The lymphatic circuit that drains the intracerebral glymphatic outflow includes the dural and extracranial lymphatic vessels and their collecting cervical lymph nodes4,7–11. CSF entry into the periarterial spaces is controlled, at least in part, by arterial pulsation12,13. The outflow pathways of intracerebral CSF, their regulatory mechanisms and the specific role of perivenous spaces in the context of brain clearance remain controversial topics14.

Based on this premise, we hypothesized that abnormal blood flow in the dural venous sinuses of patients with IIH may alter the properties of perisinusal spaces and that such alterations may extend to dural MLVs that regulate the clearance of central nervous system (CNS)-derived fluids from the brain. By using contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in patients with IIH and healthy controls, along with a mouse model of altered cerebral venous outflow via bilateral JVL, we determined that brain clearance and homeostatic ICP depend on MLVs and demonstrated that cerebral venous outflow is essential for maintaining the integrity and function of these vessels. Our study offers insights into IIH pathophysiology and reveals the mandatory interplay between the cerebral venous and meningeal lymphatic systems for the maintainance of ICP.

Results

Venous sinus stenoses in patients with IIH correlate with perivenous fluid pattern alterations

To detect perisinusal fluid along dural perivascular spaces, we used contrast-enhanced 3T black-blood MRI with the three-dimensional (3D) T1-SPACE DANTE (Sampling Perfection with Application optimized Contrast using different flip angle Evolution, Delay Alternating with Nutation for Tailored Excitation) sequence10,15–17. We used perisinusal fluid MRI as a proxy measure of meningeal lymphatic drainage, as histological studies have demonstrated the presence of lymphatic vessels within perisinusal spaces in humans, primates and mice10,15.

We imaged the perisinusal fluid of a cohort of 16 female patients with IIH and a cohort of 19 female healthy age-matched volunteers. Participants were imaged with a 3T Prisma Fit MRI scanner (Siemens Healthineers) using a series of sequences administered before and after intravenous injection of the contrast agent gadobutrol (Fig. 1a). Intravenous gadolinium injection does not allow a direct assessment of CSF circulation, unlike MRI with intrathecal gadolinium injection18. However, it is ethically accepted in healthy volunteers and, moreover, provides quantitative, 3D information on perisinusal fluid patterns, which we interpret as a surrogate marker of perisinusal drainage10. After gadobutrol injection, a venous elliptical sequence was used to delineate the dural venous sinuses and major bridging veins, and a 3D T1-SPACE DANTE sequence was used to delineate perisinusal and perivenous fluids. After postprocessing of the MRI scans (Fig. 1b), we analyzed the 3D reconstruction of the venous sinuses (blue) and perisinusal compartments (green) (Fig. 1c,d).

The venous sinuses and perisinusal spaces had similar volumes in both patients with IIH and healthy controls (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). Controls showed cisternae of perisinusal fluid (red arrowheads in Fig. 1b,c) along the superior sagittal sinus (SSS) as well as vessel-like tubular structures along the transverse sinuses (TS), sigmoid sinuses (SS) and straight sinuses (green arrowheads, Fig. 1b,c). Patients with IIH always presented with bilateral TS narrowing (Fig. 1d, asterisks) and consistently displayed alteration of the sinus and perisinus patterns. At the SSS level, the venous sinus and the perisinusal cisternae (red arrows, Fig. 1d) were enlarged compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1c,e,f). In the underlying subarachnoid space, the cortical bridging veins collecting into the SSS were also larger and surrounded by perivenous signal enhancement (Fig. 1h, white arrowheads, and Extended Data Fig. 1c) compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1g). The leptomeningeal perivenous spaces were, thus, continuous with the dural superior sagittal perisinus in patients with IIH. Downstream of the SSS, the TS and the SS showed an opposite pattern with reduced perisinusal spaces compared to controls (Fig. 1c,d,i,j and Extended Data Fig. 1c–e). Patients with IIH always presented with bilateral TS narrowing and a reduction of the surrounding perisinusal fluid (Fig. 1d, asterisks; Fig. 1i; Fig. 1d, green arrowheads; Fig. 1j). Therefore, the perisinusal and perivenous spaces of patients with IIH were expanded along the SSS while they regressed along the TS and SS. This set of meningeal MRI studies led us to conclude that (1) perivascular fluids circulate between the leptomeninges and dural sinuses and (2) the perivascular fluid pattern along dural sinuses depends upon cerebral venous outflow.

We next investigated whether intracranial hypertension was linked to altered brain fluid homeostasis. Volumetric analyses were conducted with the 3D fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence for the brain, cerebral ventricles and choroid plexus and with the 3D T1-MPRAGE (magnetization prepared–rapid gradient echo) sequence for the total intracranial volume. Patients with IIH showed increased brain volume (Fig. 1k) without volumetric changes of the ventricles and choroid plexus (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g) compared to controls. Their intracranial volume was also enlarged (Fig. 1l), which correlated with an increased volume of the skull (Fig. 1m), particularly of the calvaria, which was thicker than in controls (Extended Data Fig. 1h,i). We also found an enlargement of perineural routes in patients with IIH, as shown by the increased volume of the optic nerve and surrounding perineural space compared to controls (Fig. 1n).

In summary, IIH-associated TS stenoses were found to correlate with perivenous space alterations along the dorsal venous sinuses and leptomeningeal bridging veins, brain edema, increased fluid within and around the optic nerve and calvarial remodeling.

Mice with surgical JVL develop transient intracerebral hypertension

The observation of perisinusal fluid pattern alteration in patients with IIH suggested that MLV function may be impaired by abnormal venous sinus outflow, potentially compromising brain fluid drainage. To investigate this, we developed a mouse model of surgical blockade of the cerebral venous outflow.

Adult female mice underwent bilateral ligation of the internal and external jugular veins (JVL mice) (Fig. 2a). The jugular veins are responsible for draining the cerebral blood toward the subclavian veins, and the cervical paravertebral venous plexuses serve as the only alternative route. We first conducted MRI monitoring of venous remodeling using a 11.7T MRI scanner at various timepoints after JVL surgery (2, 4, 7, 14, 28 and 60 days) and after sham surgery (Fig. 2b). Two-dimensional time-of-flight (TOF) magnetic resonance venography revealed significant remodeling of the facial veins located upstream of the ligation points compared to sham-operated mice (Fig. 2c). The diameter of these extracranial veins doubled within 2 days post-surgery (2 dps) and gradually returned to baseline over the course of 4 weeks (Fig. 2d). The volume of dural venous sinuses remained stable (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 2a,b) but showed transient molecular remodeling, as indicated by the upregulated dural expression of Piezo1, which encodes a mechanosensitive ion channel involved in endothelial cell response to shear stress (Fig. 2f)19–21.

To determine whether impaired cerebral venous outflow altered brain fluid homeostasis, we measured intracerebral pressure (ICeP) over time (2, 4, 7, 14 and 28 dps) using a pressure sensor catheter inserted into the brain parenchyma in isoflurane anesthetized mice. A significant, yet transient, increase of ICeP was observed in JVL-2 mice, followed by a rapid return to baseline in JVL-7 mice (Fig. 2g). To assess whether this elevation of ICeP correlated with an increase of brain volume, as observed in humans (Fig. 1k), we measured brain volume using 11.7T MRI and a 3D magnetization-prepared gradient echo (MP-GRE) sequence, which revealed that brain volume progressively increased from the time of surgery until 28 dps and returned to baseline only at 60 dps (Fig. 2h). Brain tissue swelling involved both the gray matter and the white matter (Fig. 2i,j) and occurred at a constant ventricular volume (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Confocal and electron microscopy imaging of the white matter revealed structural defects indicative of fluid infiltration in JVL-2 mice, including enlarged corpus callosum with reduced myelin basic protein (MBP)-labeled area (Extended Data Fig. 2d–g) as well as decreased density of myelinated axons (Extended Data Fig. 2h–j). Hence, the blockage of cerebral venous outflow caused cerebral edema that persisted beyond the initial phase of intracerebral hypertension. Our studies showed that the JVL mice developed an IIH-like phenotype characterized by a transient intracerebral hypertension and brain edema.

Edema and inflammation of dural perisinus after JVL

To determine if leakage of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) may contribute to brain edema, we assessed BBB integrity of JVL mice both postmortem and in real time. JVL-2 mice received intravenous administration of Cadaverine-Alexa (Cad-A555, molecular weight 950 Da) 30 minutes prior to euthanization, and the fluorescent tracer patterns of brain sections were analyzed (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Cad-A555 labeling was exclusively intravascular, inside large radial and small cortical vessels, indicating that BBB integrity was preserved at the peak of intracerebral hypertension after JVL. We next used dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI with intravenous injection of gadobutrol at different timepoints after JVL (2, 7, 14 and 28 dps) (Fig. 2k). T1-weighted perfusion sequence (DCE fast low angle shot (FLASH)) allowed to measure ktrans as an indicator of BBB permeability. ktrans quantifies gadolinium transport between plasma and intracerebral tissues, encompassing both extracellular and extravascular spaces22. Compared to control mice, ktrans was significantly increased upon intravenous administration of anti-Unc5B-3 antibody, a treatment transiently opening the BBB23. By contrast, ktrans value was unchanged in JVL mice, ruling out gadobutrol entry from the blood into brain tissues (Fig. 2k). Therefore, BBB integrity is maintained after JVL.

Dural sinuses are fenestrated vessels, and their endothelium permits solute exchanges with the perisinusal space24. To test for dural venous sinus leakage, JVL-2 and sham mice were intravenously injected with fluorescent dextran (3 kDa) or Cad-A555, 30 minutes prior to euthanization. Confocal imaging of skull caps failed to detect tracer within the sinus or extravasated in the perisinus (Extended Data Fig. 3c). By contrast, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis of whole dura mater RNA extracts revealed a transient molecular remodeling with increased expression of the endothelial transport channel and fenestration marker Plvap in JVL-2 mice (Extended Data Fig. 3d)25. Immunolabeling of skull caps showed that PLVAP expression increased in JVL-2 mice compared to the controls, especially around the rostral–rhinal confluence of the sinuses as well as in the interparietal bone region where trans-osseous channels connect the dura with the skull bone marrow (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f)24,26. Electron microscopy analysis of TS moreover revealed perisinusal edema and inflammation in JVL-7 mice but not in controls (Fig. 2l–n). Characteristic features of TS in JVL-7 mice included enlarged perisinusal space with a loose network of collagen fibers and stromal cells (Fig. 2n); deposition of plasma proteins or cholesterol as well as immune cell adhesion to the endoluminal side of sinus endothelium (Fig. 2o); and accumulation of neutral lipid droplets and caveolae as well as presence of Weibel–Palade bodies in sinus endothelial cells (Fig. 2p). Weibel–Palade bodies are secretory granules enriched in von Willebrand factor and P-selectin that promote platelet adhesion and leukocyte recruitment, respectively27. These data indicate that JVL induces solute exchanges and immune cell trafficking among sinus, perisinus and skull bone solutes.

JVL induces meningeal lymphatic remodeling and CSF drainage alterations

Having established a surgical IIH-like model in mice, we examined if JVL induced remodeling of the meningeal lymphatic circuitry. Whole-mount imaging of MLVs was conducted on skull caps and skull base samples after immunolabeling with anti-Lyve-1 (lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1) antibodies (Fig. 3a–c). A transgenic Prox1–tdTomato reporter mouse28 was also used to detect Prox1 (prospero homeobox 1)- expressing cells and to broadly detect both Lyve-1+ and Lyve-1− lymphatic vessels (Fig. 3d). The lymphatics of the dorsal and ventral regions of the skull both responded synchronously but in an opposite manner to JVL. During the first week, the dorsal lymphatic bed regressed in JVL mice compared to controls, with a decrease expression of Prox1 and Lyve-1 at the confluence of sinus (COS) (Fig. 3c–f) and smaller size lymphatics in the dorsal part of TS (Fig. 3g), whereas no size change was observed along the SSS (Fig. 3h). Conversely, lymphatic vessels were dilated in the ventral part of the transverse sinus (Fig. 3i) and the basal part of the skull (Fig. 3j–l). At 2 weeks after JVL, MLVs were restored in both regions of the skull.

We next measured CSF drainage into the brain-draining lymph nodes after JVL, using intracisterna magna (ICM) injection of a fluorescent Alexa-ovalbumin (OVA) marker and subsequent dosage of marker in the collecting lymph nodes to evaluate the kinetics of CSF drainage29. JVL and sham-operated mice received successive ICM injections of two different markers: a green OVA-A488 and a far-red OVA-A647 conjugate that were delivered at 120 minutes before euthanization for green OVA-A488 and at 30 minutes before euthanization for far-red OVA-A647. OVA conjugates were measured in several CSF-collecting lymph nodes, including deep and superficial cervical lymph nodes as well as lumbo-aortic and sacral lymph nodes (Fig. 3m). From 7 dps to 28 dps, we measured a global reduction in OVA-A488 content 120 minutes after ICM injection, whereas OVA-A647 content was increased at 30 minutes after ICM injection in all collecting lymph nodes (Fig. 3n and Extended Data Fig. 4a–g). After 28 dps, the flow rate gradually decreased in the lymph nodes, normalizing completely by 2 months after surgery. These data indicate that CSF drainage into cervical lymph nodes was accelerated in JVL mice.

Paired remodeling of MLVs and intracerebral CSF circulation

To investigate the cause of MLV and extracranial CSF drainage alterations, we carried out intracerebral imaging of CSF drainage after JVL, both in real time and postmortem. Studies were conducted at the peak of the lymphatic drainage alteration (JVL-7 mice) and when the MLV pattern was restored (JVL-14 mice).

We performed T1 DCE MRI imaging with a 9.4T scanner, 1 hour after ICM injection of gadoteric acid (Gd-DOTA; Fig. 4a)29–31. Quantitative analysis of the T1 maps showed that the transport of Gd-DOTA into the brain parenchyma was decreased in JVL-7 mice compared to sham-operated mice (Fig. 4b,c). Extracranial transport of Gd-DOTA through the cribriform plate was not significantly increased in JVL-7 mice, at 1 hour after tracer injection (Extended Data Fig. 4h). JVL-7 mice also received ICM injection of fluorescent tracer Cad-A555 (molecular weight 950 Da) 30 minutes prior to euthanization to quantify the tracer patterns in brain sections at the level of the somatosensory cortex (SSC) (Fig. 4d). Cad-A555 entry into the brain parenchyma and perivascular spaces was decreased in JVL-7 mice compared to controls (Fig. 4e,f). Intraparenchymal fluid transport was also impaired in JVL-7 mice, as shown by the reduced parenchymal distribution of intraputamen-injected tracer (Cad-A555) compared to controls (Fig. 4g–i). As shown in Fig. 4j,k, the reduction of CSF marker transport was associated with a decreased polarization of aquaporin-4 (AQP4), a water channel required for fluid exchange between perivascular spaces and brain parenchyma13. Therefore, dorsal MLV depletion and accelerated CSF drainage into the cervical lymph nodes correlated with a reduction of intracerebral CSF circulation.

Intracerebral CSF circulation was expected to resume once MLVs were restored at 14 dps. ICM injection of JVL-14 mice with either Cad-A555 or OVA-A488 resulted in a broader distribution of tracers in cortical regions (Fig. 4d–f and Extended Data Fig. 4i–k). Cortical perivascular spaces were enlarged in the SSC of JVL-14 mice compared to controls (Fig. 4l,m). Volumetric imaging of the SSC by light sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) showed that perivascular space enlargment correlated with a reduced diameter of smooth muscle actin (SMA)-positive penetrating arteries in JVL-14 mice (Extended Data Fig. 4l,m). Compared to controls, JVL-14 mice also showed more cortical arterio-arterial anastomoses at the cortical surface (stars), between the middle cerebral and anterior cerebral arteries, demonstrating local remodeling of cerebral arterial circuits upon JVL (Extended Data Fig. 4n,o). In the SSC of JVL-14 mice, AQP4 polarization along blood vessels was reestablished (Fig. 4j,k), and AQP4 expression was increased (Fig. 4n,o).

Perivascular CSF drainage depends on parenchymal border macrophages (PBMs)32. Using LSFM, we assessed the total volume of PBMs in sham-operated and JVL-14 mice (Fig. 4p). Although the total PBM volume did not differ significantly between these two groups (Fig. 4q), PBMs in JVL-14 mice appeared to penetrate deeper into brain tissue. To confirm this, we used confocal microscopy to quantify CD206+ PBMs along cerebral vessels in the SSC of JVL mice. As expected, PBM numbers increased at 14 dps, aligning with enhanced intracerebral CSF drainage (Fig. 4r,s). We further examined perivascular phagocytic cells through LSFM and intracerebral injection of fluorescent OVA-A488 (Extended Data Fig. 4p,q), along with immunolabeling for podocalyxin (PDLX) and SMA to distinguish arteries (PDLX+SMA+) from veins (PDLX+SMA−). In JVL-14 mice, we observed accumulation of OVA-A488+-labeled phagocytic cells within cortical perivenous spaces, whereas these spaces were sparsely labeled in sham-operated mice. These data show that active remodeling of astroglial and PBMs along perivascular spaces accompanies the restoration of intracerebral CSF drainage after JVL. Our studies demonstrated that paired remodeling of MLVs and brain fluid circulation occurred after JVL and blockade of cerebral blood outflow.

MLVs regulate ICeP and intracerebral CSF drainage

To determine whether MLV remodeling contributed to changes in brain fluid clearance after JVL, we examined the effects of enhanced or reduced MLV function on ICeP, perivascular CSF flow and lymphatic drainage in sham-operated and JVL mice. ICM injection was used to deliver either AAV-VEGF-C to promote MLV growth or AAV-VEGF-C trap (VEGFR3[1–4]-Ig) to induce regression of MLVs7,8,33,34. AAV-treated mice underwent sham or JVL surgery 4 weeks after ICM injection, followed by ICeP measurement prior to euthanization. We predicted that impaired lymphatic function would exacerbate the effects of JVL and prolong the elevation of ICeP; therefore, we assessed ICeP in VEGF-C trap-treated mice at 7 dps (Fig. 5a,b). Conversely, we hypothesized that enhancing lymphatic function would facilitate CSF drainage after JVL, so we measured ICeP in AAV-VEGF-C-treated mice at 2 dps (Fig. 5c,d). Both sham-operated and JVL mice with impaired MLVs had increased ICeP (Fig. 5b). Notably, meningeal lymphatic ablation alone by AAV-VEGF-C trap increased ICeP without additional elevation from venous ligation, indicating that JVL raises ICeP primarily through the regression of dorsal lymphatics. JVL did not result in cerebral hypertension at 2 dps in mice with enhanced meningeal lymphatic drainage compared to AAV-VEGF-C-treated sham mice (Fig. 5d). MLV-depleted mice were then evaluated by 11.7T MRI for volumetric analyses and assessment of BBB properties (Fig. 5e). Consistent with previous observations in JVL mice, increased ICeP correlated with brain swelling (Fig. 5f) while BBB integrity was preserved (Fig. 5g). No stenosis was observed in the dural transverse or superior sagittal sinuses as demonstrated by the similar volume of these sinuses compared to control mice (Fig. 5h,i), indicating that lymphatic dysfunction alone does not cause dural venous stenoses. Impaired brain fluid homeostasis in MLV-depleted mice was associated with reduced PBMs in the SSC (Fig. 5j) and faster CSF drainage to lymph nodes (Fig. 5k,l). Finally, the restoration of intracerebral CSF circulation seen in JVL-14 mice was not observed in MLV-depleted JVL-14 mice, as tracer diffusion after ICM OVA-A488 injection and PVS diameter were similar to those in MLV-depleted sham mice (Fig. 5m–o). Together, these findings demonstrate that ICeP regulation and brain clearance depend on MLV drainage.

In summary, meningeal lymphatic drainage is mandatory for brain clearance and ICeP homeostasis, with cerebral venous blood flow indirectly supporting these processes by maintaining meningeal lymphatic function and integrity.

MLV restoration is driven by interactions between dural mesenchyme and endothelial cells

To gain molecular insights into the mechanisms of lymphatic restoration observed in JVL-14 mice, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis on dura mater isolated from JVL-14 and sham-operated mice (Fig. 6a and Extended Data Fig. 5a). Clusters were annotated by comparing gene expression profiles to reference marker datasets (Fig. 6b and Extended Data Fig. 5b,c)35,36. Analysis focused on blood and lymphatic endothelial cells and mesenchymal cells (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated that the Fibroblasts-1 cluster displayed the highest number of signaling pathway modifications after JVL (Extended Data Fig. 5f), whereas the cluster of endothelial cells showed enrichment in signaling pathways regulating the translation machinery, such as eukaryotic translation initiation (n = 112; P = 0.01502) and elongation (n = 86; P = 0.01502), as well as in Slit/Robo signaling genes (n = 147; P = 0.02296), which participate in developmental angiogenesis and vascular injury repair (Fig. 6c)37–39. The Fibroblasts-1 cluster of JVL-14 mice presented anti-inflammatory signatures, as indicated by the downregulation of interferon-gamma (IFNγ) response (n = 150; P = 0.01544), TNF signaling via NF-κB (n = 150; P = 0.04476), signaling by interleukins (n = 283; P = 0.02105) and cytokine signaling by the immune system (n = 441; P = 0.02185), whereas the TGFβ signaling pathway was upregulated (n = 46; P = 0.04476) (Fig. 6d). We next analyzed the differential ligand–receptor (L–R) interactions between clusters (Fig. 6e). The interactions between ligands produced by Fibroblasts-1 and receptors expressed by endothelial cells were increased in JVL-14 mice, with reinforced VEGF-C-VEGFR2/3 and angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) signaling interactions, suggestive of active dural lymphangiogenesis (Fig. 6f, green asterisks). Cdkn1a gene, also known as p21 and a negative regulator of cell cycle acting downstream of growth factor receptors40,41, along with VEGFR2, was highly downregulated in dural endothelial cells of JVL-14 mice (log fold change = −1.76460; P = 4.27999 × 10−6) (Fig. 6g). The possible contribution of VEGF-C in meningeal lymphatic remodeling after JVL was further supported by the increased level of VEGF-C measured in the plasma at 7 dps (Fig. 6h). These findings correlate with the regrowth of MLVs observed in JVL-14 mice and suggest that subsets of fibroblasts of the dura mater may drive lymphangionic repair.

Discussion

Our study of cerebral blood flow and CSF drainage in patients with IIH and JVL mice showed that the dural veno-lymphatic system regulates ICP and fluid homeostasis. In humans and mice, the impairment of cerebral venous outflow correlated with cerebral hypertension, brain edema and an abnormal pattern of dural perisinusal fluid or lymphatics. Brain edema contributed to intracranial hypertension although without ventricular enlargement or BBB alteration. We concluded that cerebral blood venous outflow regulates the maintenance of MLVs and brain fluid drainage. MLV stimulation with lymphangiogenic factors such as VEGF-C—for instance, via coating of stents used to reperfuse the transverse sinus—may thus improve the treatment of IIH.

In patients with IIH, brain edema and intracranial hypertension are consistently associated with the local presence of venous stenoses and absence of perisinusal fluid drainage in the transverse sinuses. 3D T1-SPACE DANTE imaging showed that perivenous fluids accumulate in the dura along the SSS as well as along the bridging veins in the subarachnoid space. In the pathological condition of IIH, the perivenous fluid is, therefore, continuous with the dural perisinusal space4. This observation may suggest that subarachnoid and, by extension, cerebral perivenous fluids collect in the dural perisinus. This result is consistent with previous data in mice, which described specialized channels along bridging veins, referred to as arachnoid cuff exit (ACE) points, that facilitate fluid and immune exchanges between the subarachnoid space and the dural perisinusal space16. On the other hand, the detection of contrast agent in subarachnoid perivenous spaces may indicate a reflux of dural fluids accumulated in excess in the dura mater of patients with IIH, as a result of the blockade of lymphatic drainage in the transverse and sigmoid sinuses. Another explanation for the accumulation of contrast agent in the perisinus is a possible fluid exchange between venous sinus and perisinus due to sinus hypertension, which is supported by electron microscopy evidence of perisinusal edema and venous endothelium alterations in the transverse sinuses of JVL-7 mice. Further investigations employing dynamic imaging of perivenous and lymphatic clearance will be essential to resolve these alternatives.

Perisinusal fluids are likely transported, at least in part, by MLVs in humans. In patients with IIH, TS stenosis may thus correlate with a local loss of MLVs and blockade of lymphatic drainage, which may induce the upstream enlargement of perisinusal space along the SSS. This interpretation supports the model of MLVs as an exit route for brain clearance fluids. The presence of brain edema in patients with IIH agrees with a previous report that glymphatic outflow is blocked in patients with IIH18. Radiological dilation of cranial nerve sheaths42 combined with regression of dorsal lymphatic drainage moreover suggest that CSF clearance may proceed mainly through the perineural routes in these patients. Further studies will be required to confirm fluid transfer from the perisinusal spaces to MLVs. Nonhuman primates, with dorsal lymphatics positioned similarly to those in humans, provide an ideal model species for such investigations because they are amenable to both dural sinuses interventional surgery and MRI analysis of meningeal lymphatics9,15.

The cause of meningeal lymphatic drainage regression can only be speculated but may be directly related to the increase of intravenous pressure occurring in the dural sinuses of patients with IIH43. Within the rigid envelope of the dura mater, the physical proximity of MLVs with venous sinuses under hypertension may cause compression of lymphatic vessels and impair lymphatic drainage along the SSS, leading to regression of downstream lymphatics. In mice, Piezo1 mechanosensor expression was increased in dural cells shortly after JVL, without SSS dilatation, suggesting that intrasinusal hypertension may activate mechanosensing and sinus-perisinus fluid exchanges, leading to MLV compression and regression. In parallel, venous stenting in patients with IIH restores blood flow and normalizes both intravenous and intracranial pressures, further supporting the role of venous hypertension in lymphatic dysfunction2. Future MRI studies will be required to assess whether venous sinus stenting allows restoration of dorsal meningeal lymphatic drainage as predicted.

Mimicking IIH in mice requires surgical ligation of the transverse sinuses, which is impossible to achieve without causing dural injury and inflammation44. Therefore, we ligated the extracranial jugular veins to induce intracerebral venous hypertension. JVL mice showed intracerebral hypertension and abnormal CSF drainage, which were repaired by extensive remodeling of the head and neck blood and lymphatic vasculature and of cerebral perivascular spaces. In the acute phase, occlusion of venous blood flow caused regression of MLVs in the calvaria. Restoration of MLVs at 2 weeks after JVL synchronized with a remodeling of cortical vasculature. This diversity of repair processes makes JVL mice a potential new model for the study of brain vascular remodeling.

MLV depletion in control mice caused cerebral hypertension, brain swelling and accelerated CSF drainage. This finding appears, at first glance, in disagreement with two pioneering reports on MLVs and the effect of MLV ablation on CSF pressure8,45. On the one hand, Aspelund et al.8 reported that interstitial fluid pressure and water content were unaffected in transgenic MLV-deficient K14-VEGFR3-Ig transgenic mice. This transgenic model never developed MLVs, and compensatory mechanisms may have been established to regulate brain pressure. By contrast, after JVL, MLVs regressed transiently at the adult stage, and their fast remodeling did not allow complete compensation. On the other hand, Louveau et al.45 found no difference in ICP among laser (PBS), Visudyne (ICM) and Visudyne (ICM) + laser mice, which locally lack MLVs, at 3 days after treatment. This discrepancy with our results may result from the different models of meningeal lymphatic deletion used. It may also reflect differences in the measurement sites: Louveau et al.45 monitored CSF pressure in the subarachnoid space (intracisternal recording), whereas we recorded directly in the brain parenchyma (intraparietal cortex). The large volume and multiple drainage routes from the subarachnoid space can rapidly compensate for local changes in CSF pressure, reducing the sensitivity of intracisternal measurements compared to direct ICeP recordings.

Evidence for a causal role of MLV in brain clearance is supported by three findings resulting from the depletion of MLVs by ICM injection of VEGF-C trap. First, MLV depletion in control mice caused cerebral hypertension, as discussed above. Second, MLV depletion resulted in prolonged cerebral hypertension after JVL, whereas ICeP was restored at 7 dps in control JVL mice. Finally, MLV depletion blocked the reperfusion of CSF along perivascular spaces observed in JVL-14 mice. Therefore, brain fluid homeostasis is dependent upon MLV drainage. Intracranial hypertension has been shown to be exacerbated by lymphatic dysfunction in pathological conditions46, whereas it can be improved by enhancing lymphatic function47. Here we provide new evidence of MLV depletion causing intracerebral hypertension and brain edema.

The mechanisms regulating the interplay between ICeP and the glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic systems may involve, at least in part, the population of PBMs, previously reported to regulate glymphatic drainage32. PBMs increased during the second week after JVL and regressed upon MLV depletion. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis of the dura mater of JVL-14 mice revealed molecular alterations in dural fibroblasts and endothelial cells, including upregulation of pro-lymphangiogenic regulators Vegf-c/Vegfr-3 and Ang/Tie33,48 that contribute to the regrowth of dorsal MLVs.

Together, our results reveal a functional veno-lymphatic unit beween dural venous sinuses and MLVs within the perisinus spaces that is mandatory for the regulation of brain clearance and pressure. Cerebral venous blood flow preserved the structure and function of this lymphatic network, which controls the homeostasis of brain clearance and ICP.

Further studies will characterize the nature, dynamics and regulatory mechanisms of the interactions between the dural sinus and neighboring lymphatics.

Methods

Study approval

Human subjects: The prospective Lymph-IMAGIIH study was approved by our institutional review board (IDRCB: 2022-A01422–4) and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT05762367), with the Institut National de la Santé et de la Recherche Médicale (INSERM) serving as the study sponsor. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. Participants received financial compensation (€50) for their participation. Mice: All in vivo procedures complied with guidelines and institutional policies of INSERM and the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Paris Brain Institute (APAFIS no. 34974–2022012017455126).

Human subjects

The Lymph-IMAGIIH study included two groups of female participants aged 18–40 years. Participants were allocated to groups according to their clinical status (IIH or healthy control), and no randomization method was used. Eighteen patients with IIH and 20 age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls were included in the study. IIH was diagnosed according to the modified Dandy criteria49; healthy controls were defined as individuals without any neurological disorder, including the absence of symptoms of IIH such as chronic headaches or uncorrected/unexplained visual disturbances. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT05762367). Among the IIH group, 16 had complete MRI scans; one participant was unable to undergo MRI due to claustrophobia, and intravenous gadobutrol injection failed in another one. No significant age difference was observed between groups (healthy controls: 28.0 (4.96) years; patients with IIH: 30.2 (4.88) years). Patients with IIH had a higher body mass index than controls (31.6 (9.49) versus 23.1 (3.98) kg m−2; P < 0.001). In patients with IIH, the mean CSF opening pressure measured by lumbar puncture at diagnosis was 32.4 (4.24) cm H2O. Fourteen patients were on acetazolamide at the time of MRI, with a mean dosage of 1,107 (691.4) mg per day.

Human MRI protocol

All radiological data were pseudonymized, with postprocessing conducted by an experienced neuroradiologist and a neurologist, both blinded to the participants’ condition. MRI acquisitions were performed in a single 45-minute session on a 3.0T MAGNETOM Prisma Fit scanner (Siemens Healthineers) using a 64-channel head/neck coil and VE11E software. A total of 18 patients with IIH and 20 healthy volunteers were included in the study. Analyses of MRI sequences requiring contrast injection were performed in 16 patients with IIH and in 19 healthy volunteers because of technical failure of contrast injection in the remaining participants. No human data were excluded from the analyses.

Postprocessing of human MRI scans

Manual segmentations were performed using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2; https://www.slicer.org), slice by slice in the coronal, axial and sagittal planes following the anatomical landmarks detailed in Extended Data Fig. 7. The total intracranial volume was segmented on high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE images and defined as the volume enclosed by the inner table of the skull, including brain parenchyma, CSF and meninges. The brain parenchyma, ventricular system and choroid plexuses were segmented from the 3D SPACE FLAIR sequence. Choroid plexuses were segmented using previously validated automatic segmentation software50. The dural venous sinuses were segmented from the 3D venous elliptical sequence and defined as the circulating lumen strictly located within the dura mater, with careful exclusion of cortical bridging veins. The SSS, TS and SS were delineated according to anatomical landmarks described in Extended Data Fig. 7. Cortical bridging veins were defined as cortical veins draining into the SSS and were segmented from the 3D venous elliptical sequence. The perisinusal fluid compartments were segmented from the 3D T1-SPACE DANTE sequence by manually delineating contrast-enhanced areas of the dura mater surrounding the previously segmented dural venous sinuses (Fig. 1b) while excluding leptomeningeal enhancement of the cortical bridging veins and non-enhancing dura10. The bone was segmented using the open-source SIAM model (https://github.com/romainVala/SIAM) on high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE images. The resulting segmentations were imported into 3D Slicer for further processing. SIAM produces three segmentation masks: (1) Skull, excluding nasal and auricular regions; (2) Nose skull, covering anterior skull areas around the nasal cavity; and (3) Ears skull, including regions near the ear canals. Due to segmentation inaccuracies in the Ears skull mask caused by air-related artifacts affecting contrast between bone and surrounding spaces, this mask was excluded from analyses. The Skull and Nose skull masks were combined to define the Total skull volume. The calvaria was manually delineated using an anatomical plane from the superior frontal sinus margin to the external occipital crest using 3D Slicer’s Segment Editor. All volumes were calculated with the Labelmap Statistics function in the Segment Statistics module of 3D Slicer.

Statistical analyses of human data

Segmentations were conducted by two independent, blinded readers—a neuroradiologist and a neurologist—and assessed for reproducibility using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with a two-way random model agreement. ICC values were as follows: brain volume (0.79), total intracranial volume (0.83), ventricles volume (0.69), perisinusal fluids volume (0.90) and venous volume (0.77). Data are expressed as mean (s.d.), with error bars representing s.d. All variables met normality assumptions, as confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk tests. Ventricular and brain volumes as well as total intracranial volume were normalized to the control mean, and a two-way ANOVA (group × variable: ventricles, brain, total intracranial volume) confirmed no significant interaction (P = 0.997). Accordingly, two-tailed unpaired Studentʼs t-tests were used to compare groups, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Mice

C57BL/6 mice, aged 2–4 months, were obtained from Janvier Laboratories and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. All experiments were performed in female mice, as the conditions of dural venous stenosis and idiopathic intracranial hypertension predominantly affect women. Transgenic female B6.FVB/N-Tg(Prox1–tdTomato)TA76Gsat/Mmuc mice aged 2–6 months used in this study were kindly provided by S. T. Proulx (University of Bern)28. Animals were assigned to experimental groups according to the experimental design, and no randomization method was used for group allocation. Data analyses for animal experiments were performed blinded to the experimental conditions.

Surgery procedures

JVL.

Adult C57/BL6 female mice underwent bilateral ligation of the internal and external jugular veins. Prior to surgery, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of buprenorphine (0.1 mg kg−1) and carprofen (20 mg kg−1), along with 200 μl of 0.9% sodium chloride for hydration. Anesthesia was induced with isoflurane (3–4% at 400–500 ml min−1 airflow) and maintained at 1.5% (140–250 ml min−1, adjusted to respiratory rate). Mice were positioned supine on a heated pad to maintain body temperature at 37 °C, monitored via rectal probe. The surgical area was disinfected, and aseptic conditions were upheld. A longitudinal incision was made in the ventro-cervical region to expose the jugular veins, which were then ligated with 6.0 silk sutures. Skin incisions were closed using nonabsorbable propylene sutures.

ICeP measurement

After anesthesia induction (3–4% isoflurane, maintenance at 1.5% with adjusted airflow), a 1-cm longitudinal skin incision was made to expose the skull. A 0.5-mm hole was drilled above the parietal lobe, and a pressure sensor catheter (Millar, SPR1000) was inserted 1 mm into the cortex using a stereotaxic frame. ICeP was monitored for 6 minutes, and mean values were recorded over the last 3 minutes after stabilization.

Intravenous injections of fluorescent tracers.

Fluorescent dextran (Invitrogen, D3308; 3 kDa) or cadaverine (Cad-A555) (Invitrogen, A30677; 0.9 kDa) tracers were administered via lateral tail vein injection in sham or JVL mice using insulin syringes. Tracers were injected at a concentration of 100 μg per 20 g of mice diluted in PBS, in a total volume of 100 μl. The tracers were allowed to circulate for 30 minutes prior to mice euthanasia.

ICM injection.

Prior to surgery, mice received intraperitoneal injections of buprenorphine (0.1 mg kg−1) and carprofen (20 mg kg−1) for analgesia and 200 μl of 0.9% sodium chloride for hydration. After anesthesia induction (3–4% isoflurane, maintenance at 1.5% with adjusted airflow), mice were positioned on a stereotaxic frame with the head tilted at a 120° angle. A 1-cm neck incision exposed the cisterna magna, which was punctured with a 27-gauge needle. Ovalbumin conjugated with either Alexa Fluor 488 (OVA-A488) or Alexa Fluor 647 (OVA-A647) (10 μl, 2 mg ml−1, 2 μl min−1)

Cadaverine conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (Cad-A555) (10 μl, 2 mg ml−1, 2 μl min−1)

Cadaverine conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (Cad-A555) (10 μl, 2 mg ml−1, 2 μl min−1)

AAV9 vectors expressing VEGF-C or VEGFR3[1–4]-Ig (VEGF-C trap) were delivered at titers between 3.53 × 1011 and 6.18 × 1012 pv ml−1, in 2 μl per mouse at 0.5 μl min−1.

The neck incision was thereafter sealed with surgical glue.

ICM injection of fluorescent tracers with a delivery catheter.

Prior to surgery, mice received intraperitoneal injections of buprenorphine (0.1 mg kg−1) and carprofen (20 mg kg−1) for analgesia and 200 μl of 0.9% sodium chloride for hydration. After anesthesia induction (3–4% isoflurane, maintenance at 1.5% with adjusted airflow), the surgical site was shaved and disinfected, and the mouse was placed ventrally on a heated pad. A 2-cm neck incision exposed the cisterna magna, where a catheter was implanted at a 30° angle. The incision was sealed with surgical glue. Two injections of different fluorescent tracers (OVA-A647 and OVA-A488, 4 mg ml−1) were administered through the catheter—the first immediately after catheter placement (120 minutes prior to euthanasia) and the second 30 minutes prior to euthanasia. Each injection was delivered in 5 μl at 1 μl min−1. Mice were awakened between injections.

Intracerebral injection.

Prior to surgery, mice received subcutaneous injections of buprenorphine (0.1 mg kg−1) and carprofen (20 mg kg−1) for analgesia and 200 μl of 0.9% sodium chloride for hydration. After anesthesia induction (3–4% isoflurane, maintenance at 1.5% with adjusted airflow), mice were positioned on a stereotaxic frame. A midline scalp incision (approximately 1 cm) exposed the skull over the putamen. A craniotomy was performed using a dental drill at the target coordinates under stereotaxy. Using a Hamilton syringe (10 μl, 30 gauge), cadaverine conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (Cad-A555) (1 μl, 2 mg ml−1) was injected at 17 nl min−1. After a 2-minute pause, the syringe was slowly withdrawn, and the scalp was closed using nonabsorbable propylene sutures. After surgery, animals were allowed to recover in a heated chamber (37 °C) and were monitored for 2 hours prior to euthanization.

MRI procedures and postprocessing of images

MRI after intravenous injection of gadobutrol.

MRI scans were performed on a preclinical 11.7T MRI scanner (BioSpec, Bruker BioSpin) equipped with a 1H surface cryoprobe for mouse brain imaging. A metal-free, MRI-compatible catheter (MTV-33, 28-cm polyurethane, 1 French, 29 gauge) was inserted into the tail vein and connected to a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus, model 70–2130) preloaded with gadobutrol (Gadovist, 1.0 mmol ml−1). The system was purged and flushed with contrast agent prior to use. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction, 1–2% maintenance, 500 ml min−1 oxygen flow) and monitored for respiratory rate while body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 1 °C. Heads were positioned supine and secured using a bite bar and ear pins. Gadobutrol (400 μl at 50 μl s−1) was injected automatically 20 seconds before the T1-weighted perfusion sequence. MRI protocols included two-dimensional TOF: TE = 3.13 ms, TR = 24.4 ms, flip angle = 80°, bandwidth = 125 kHz, 80 slices, slice thickness = 300 μm, FOV = 15.36 × 15.36 mm2, in-plane resolution = 60 × 60 μm2, acquisition time = 16 minutes, 40 seconds; T1-weighted perfusion (DCE FLASH): TE = 1.2 ms, TR = 38 ms, flip angle = 15°, bandwidth = 125 kHz, 10 slices, slice thickness = 800 μm, FOV = 12.8 × 14.8 mm2, spatial resolution = 200 × 200 μm2, acquisition time = 11 minutes, 43 seconds; and 3D multi-gradient echo (MGE): TE1 = 1.75 ms, ΔTE = 1.8 ms, TR = 40 ms, flip angle = 15°, bandwidth = 200 kHz, FOV = 19.2 × 14.4 × 9.6 mm3, isotropic resolution = 100 μm, acquisition time = 9 minutes, 13 seconds.

Postprocessing of DCE MRI scans after intravenous injection of gadobutrol.

Anatomical region segmentations were performed using 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org). The venous system was manually segmented from the two-dimensional TOF sequence, whereas the brain and ventricular structures were manually segmented from the T2*-weighted MGE (T2w MGE) images. Brain segmentation into gray matter and white matter compartments was performed using a standardized atlas-based approach. First, skull stripping was applied to each T2w-MGE image using affine and nonlinear registration to the Turone Mouse Brain Template and Atlas (TMBTA)51, incorporating tissue priors for gray matter, white matter and CSF with SPM8 (ref. 52). The resulting brain-extracted T2*w MRI volumes were then nonlinearly co-registered to the TMBTA using the registration module of the AIDAmri processing pipeline53. Gray matter and white matter masks were derived by merging predefined atlas-based regions corresponding to each tissue class.

Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images from DCE MRI acquisition were compiled into a four-dimensional stack using Fiji ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) and converted to a NIfTI-1 file. To quantify perfusion, signal intensities of the tracer in the tissue and a feeding vessel were analyzed, facilitating the creation of time-dependent concentration curves and the application of a pharmacokinetic model to estimate quantifiable parameters. ImageJ was used to create masks for regions of interest (ROIs) and for arterial input function (AIF) at the SSS. Quantitative analyses utilized a custom Python pipeline based on Python 3.11. The NiBabel library was used to read NIfTI-1 files, apply ROI and AIF masks and extract signal intensities (S(t)) for each voxel at every timepoint (t). Pharmacokinetic modeling for parameter estimation was based on Mittermeier et al.54. As a positive control for BBB opening, C57BL/6 mice were injected intravenously via the retro-orbital vein with the anti-Unc5B-3 antibody (10 mg kg−1) 1 hour before imaging23.

MRI with ICM injection of Gd-DOTA for glymphatic transport imaging.

MRI scans were performed on a Bruker 9.4T/16 MRI scanner, and T1 mapping was used to evaluate glymphatic transport in JVL and sham-operated mice, after ICM injection of gadoteric acid (Gd-DOTA (Guerbet), 10 μl, 1 μl min−1, molecular weight 558 Da)29,31. MRI experiments were conducted at the Magnetic Resonance Research Center at Yale University.

Postprocessing of Gd-DOTA MRI scans.

Postprocessing was performed as described in Xue et al.31. The T1 maps were filtered to exclude values greater than 5,000 ms. The magnetic resonance images, composed of summed low flip angle (2° and 5°) spoiled gradient recalled (SPGR) images, served as anatomical templates for outlining the brain and lymph nodes. For each mouse, glymphatic transport volume was defined as brain tissue voxels with T1 values ranged from 1 ms to 1,700 ms, indicating tissue uptake of Gd-DOTA and the resulting T1 shortening. For each mouse, glymphatic transport volume was extracted using PMOD software (version 4.0)29,31. Similarly, outflow to the nasal conchae was quantified by outlining these structures and extracting voxels with T1 values from 1 ms to 1,700 ms31.

Collection and analysis of biological material

Plasma collection and Bio-Plex analysis.

Blood was collected from mice via a cheek prick (0.2–0.3 ml) and treated with an intraperitoneal saline injection (0.9% NaCl; 10 ml kg−1) for rehydration. Blood samples in EDTA tubes were centrifuged at 12,000 r.p.m. for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and plasma was stored at −20 °C. Bio-Plex assays were performed using the MILLIPLEX Mouse Angiogenesis/Growth Factor Magnetic Bead Panel Cancer Multiplex Assay (MAGPMAG-24K-03; Merck) at the Paris Brain Institute ICV3 platform, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Data analysis was conducted in Belysa Immunoassay Curve Fitting Software (version 1.2.2, MilliporeSigma).

Tissue collection and processing.

After euthanasia, mice were perfused with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains, lymph nodes, skull caps and skull bases were harvested, fixed in PFA for 24 hours and stored in PBS. Portions of the brains and lymph nodes were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in Tissue-Plus O.C.T. and cryosectioned at 16-μm thickness with a Leica cryostat and then stored at −20 °C. The remaining tissues were kept in PBS and sectioned at 100-μm or 200-μm thickness with a Leica VT1200 vibratome.

Immunofluorescence labeling of whole-mount meninges.

Decalcified skull caps and skull bases (Leica, Surgipath; 2 hours) were blocked overnight in 0.3% Tween 20 and 5% donkey serum in PBS. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-LYVE1, anti-PDLX and anti-vWF) at 1/200 for skull caps and 1/100 for skull bases in blocking solution for 3 days, followed by three 1-hour PBS washes. Secondary antibodies (1/500 in 0.3% Tween 20 PBS) were applied for 48 hours, followed by three additional 1-hour PBS washes. Imaging was performed on a Leica SP8 X confocal microscope with a ×4 objective (5-μm z-stack), and images were analyzed in ImageJ. Six lymphatic diameters were measured per ROI to calculate a mean vessel diameter for each mouse and region, with each dot in the figures corresponding to one mouse.

Brain tissue preparation and analysis of OVA-A or Cad-A tracer distribution.

Brains were extracted after perfusion, fixed in 4% PFA and coronally sectioned at 100 μm using a Leica VT1000 S vibratome. Sections were mounted with Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium. Glymphatic tracer distribution was analyzed on a Nikon AX R confocal microscope with NIS-Elements software. ROIs were delineated on DAPI-stained sections using Fiji ImageJ while using the Allen Brain Atlas as a reference. For quantification of tracer in perivascular spaces and brain parenchyma, the OVA signal was binarized with a uniform threshold, and the percentage area of tracer coverage within each ROI was measured over time. Perivascular space diameter in the SSC was determined by averaging width measurements from five to 10 vessels on each ROI.

Extraction and quantification of lymph node OVA-A.

Lymph nodes (deep cervical, superficial, lombo-aortic and sacral) were collected after euthanasia and perfusion, placed in 2-ml tubes with 200 μl of formamide, centrifuged at 10,000g for 5 minutes and incubated at 65 °C for 48 hours. An OVA standard curve was prepared in formamide (20–0.0156 μg ml−1). Samples (100 μl per well) were analyzed using a SpectraMax i3X plate reader (Molecular Devices).

Immunofluorescence labeling on cryosections and free-floating brain sections.

Cryostat brain sections were defrosted for 1 hour, rehydrated in PBS for 15 minutess and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 hour, followed by three 5-minute PBS washes. To prevent nonspecific binding, sections were blocked for 1.5 hours at room temperature in 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% normal donkey serum in PBS. Free-floating sections were blocked overnight in 0.5% Triton X-100 and 5% normal donkey serum in PBS. Both cryostat and free-floating sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. For cryostat sections, antibodies used included anti-GFAP (1/100) and anti-AQP4 (1/100) and, for free-floating sections, anti-MBP (1/100), anti-CD206 (1/200), anti-PDLX (1/300), anti-AQP4 (1/100) and anti-SMA (1/200). The next day, sections were washed in PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 647, 555 and 488, diluted 1/500 for cryostat sections and 1/300 for free-floating sections in 0.1% or 0.5% Tween 20 PBS, respectively) for 2 hours at room temperature for cryostat sections and overnight at 4 °C for free-floating sections. After three PBS washes, sections were stained with DAPI (10 μg ml−1) for 2 minutes and then mounted with F4680 (Sigma-Aldrich) for cryostat sections or Mowiol for free-floating sections and stored at 4 °C in the dark. Imaging was performed on a Nikon AX R confocal microscope. 3D segmentation was performed using NIS-Elements software. For AQP4 polarization quantification, images were acquired with a ×60 objective on a Nikon AX R confocal microscope. As previously described55, the PDLX channel was duplicated and thresholded to delineate blood vessels. This vessel threshold defined ROIs applied to the original AQP4 images to measure perivascular AQP4 coverage. A second threshold captured total AQP4 immunofluorescence within each ROI. AQP4 polarization was calculated as the ratio of perivascular to total AQP4 coverage. For each animal, 10 ROIs were analyzed from the SSC of each mouse.

qPCR.

Mouse brains and dura mater were collected after perfusion with PBS. Dura mater samples were homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, 15596018) at 10 mg ml−1 by pipetting and passing through a 27-gauge needle until uniform. Brain tissue was processed similarly using TRIzol according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was extracted and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For dura mater, 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, 170–8891). For brain samples, 300 ng of RNA was used per reverse transcription reaction. Real-time qPCR was performed in technical duplicates on a CFX-96 system (Bio-Rad) with SYBR Green chemistry. Primers targeted Piezo1 (Qiagen QT01199142 for brain; custom primers for dura mater: forward 5′-CATTATCCTCTTCCTCATCGCC-3′, reverse 5′-ATGGTGAACAGTGGCTCATAG-3′), PLVAP (Qiagen QT00290584) for dura mater and MBP (Qiagen QT00199683) and AQP4 (Qiagen QT00126063) for brain. Mouse actin (Qiagen QT00095242) was used as the housekeeping gene for all reactions. Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Tissue preparation and immunofluorescence labeling for iDISCO+.

In iDISCO+ procedures with SMA–PDLX–CD206 labeling and SMA–PDLX labeling after ICM injection of OVA-A, samples were dehydrated through a methanol series (20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and two 100% baths) at room temperature, followed by a dichloromethane (DCM)/methanol (2:1) bath for at least 4 hours until the samples sank. After bleaching overnight at 4 °C in methanol containing 5% H2O2, samples were rehydrated through a reverse methanol series (80%, 60%, 40% and 20%) to PBS and washed twice in PTx.2 (PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100). Permeabilization was conducted in PTx.2 with DMSO and glycine for 2 days at 37 °C, followed by blocking in a gelatin-based solution for 2 days. For the SMA–CD206 labeling, samples were incubated with primary antibodies, previously conjugated with Alexa Fluor Antibody Labeling Kits, for 10 days at 37 °C with agitation, using rabbit anti-SMA (1:1,000), goat anti-PDLX (1:1,000) and goat anti-CD206 (1:1,000). For the ICM OVA injection samples, primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-SMA (1:1,000), goat anti-PDLX (1:1,000) and goat anti-CD31 (1:500), were applied for 10 days at 37 °C with agitation, followed by secondary antibodies (donkey anti-goat 555, 1:1,000, and donkey anti-rabbit 790, 1:1,000) for an additional 10 days. After PTwH washes (PBS with 0.2% Tween 20 and 10 μg ml−1 heparin), final dehydration was performed in a methanol series, followed by DCM/methanol (2:1) and two DCM baths and then clearing in dibenzyl ether (DBE) until imaging.

LSFM.

Brains were imaged in the sagittal orientation using an LSFM (Ultramicroscope Blaze; Miltenyi Biotec) with a ×4 objective. Imaging was controlled by Imspector Microscope software (version 7), with the chamber filled with DBE as a clearing agent. A one-sided, three-sheet illumination configuration was used with two-step dynamic focusing. LED lasers at 561 nm, 640 nm and 785 nm generated the light sheet, with a numerical aperture of 0.098. Emission filters included 595/40 for Alexa Fluor 594, 680/30 for Alexa Fluor 647 and 805LP for Alexa Fluor 785. Stacks were acquired with 3.5-μm z-steps, a 40-ms exposure time per step for the 561-nm and 640-nm lasers and a 100-ms exposure time per step for the 785-nm laser. Mosaic acquisitions were performed with a 5% overlap on the full frame. SMA+ vessel diameter in JVL-14 mice was quantified in coronal sections at the SSC, with 10 penetrating vessels per mouse and five measurements per vessel. Macrophages in JVL-14 mice were quantified across the whole cleared sample using Imaris (Bitplane). CD206+ signal was segmented using an intensity threshold, and total labeled volume was extracted to reflect global PBM population.

Electron microscopy.

Samples of brain and dura mater, including venous sinuses and perisinus regions, were fixed in a mixture of 2% PFA and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) overnight at 4 °C, washed in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer and postfixed for 1 hour in 1% osmium tetraoxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer. After washing in sodium cacodylate buffer and water, samples were incubated in 2% aqueous uranyl acetate at 4 °C overnight. After rinsing twice in water, samples were dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100%) and a final dehydration step in 100% acetone (twice, 10 minutes each). Samples were infiltrated with 50% acetone/50% Epon overnight in a tight closed container (to avoid humidity). Finally, they were infiltrated twice with pure Epon (1 hour each at room temperature) and then placed in molds with fresh Epon (EMBed 812; Electron Microscopy Sciences, 14 120). Blocks were heated at 56 °C for 48 hours. Semithin (0.5 μm thick) and ultrathin (70 nm thick) sections were cut with a Leica UC7 ultramicrotome. Semithin sections were stained with 1% toluidine blue in 1% borax, and the ROI was selected under the microscope. Ultrathin sections of the selected region were collected on copper grids (200 mesh; Electron Microscopy Sciences) and contrasted with Reynold’s lead citrate56. Ultrathin sections were observed with a Hitachi HT7700 electron microscope (Milexia) operating at 100 kV. Pictures (4,096 × 3,008 pixels) were taken with a CMOS TEM camera microscope (NanoSprint12; Milexia). Pictures were processed with the open-source image processing program ImageJ when needed.

Dural cells isolation for scRNA-seq analysis.

Mice were euthanized and perfused with 20 ml of PBS. Skull caps were dissected using small surgical scissors and immediately placed in ice-cold PBS. Dural meninges were carefully peeled off the skull caps using fine forceps and transferred to tubes for enzymatic digestion in RPMI medium (Gibco, 21875–034) supplemented with 2.5 mg ml−1 Collagenase D (Roche, 11088866001) and 0.1 mg ml−1 DNase I (Roche, 11284932001) for 20 minutes at 37 °C. The tissue was then dissociated by repeated pipetting (25 strokes) and passed through a 35-μm cell strainer. After centrifugation (5 minutes at 400g, 4 °C), cells were resuspended in 150 μl of PBS and pooled from two meninges per well in 96-well V-bottom plates.

Flow cytometry for scRNA-seq analysis.

To minimize nonspecific antibody binding, cell preparations were first incubated for 10 minutes at 4 °C with anti-CD16/32 (Fc block; BD Biosciences; 5 μg ml−1). Subsequently, a fluorescently conjugated antibody cocktail, including Live-or-Dye 405/452 (Biotium, 32003; 0.05 μl per test), was added. Cells were incubated with the antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation (5 minutes at 500g, 4 °C) and a single PBS wash. Finally, cells were resuspended in 600 μl of PBS and sorted using the MoFlo Astrios cell sorter (Beckman Coulter). Dural cells were identified as viable singlets and collected in PBS containing 20% BSA.

scRNA-seq analysis.

Single-cell library preparation was realized with chromium from 10x Genomics. Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ GEM kit and Chip G were used, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. In total, 20,000 cells were loaded on the chromium and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. Using a 100-cycle cartridge from Illumina, calculations are based on 80,000 reads per cell minimum per sample. The datasets from sham and JVL-14 mice were merged and processed together to perform clustering and downstream analyses. Cells were grouped into clusters based on similarities in gene expression profiles using principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. A range of resolutions (0–1.5, step 0.1) was tested to evaluate cluster stability, and a resolution of 0.6 was selected, resulting in 24 clusters. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for visualizing clusters. Cluster-specific violin plots were generated to assess key metrics such as the number of genes detected (nFeature_RNA), RNA counts (nCount_RNA) and the percentage of mitochondrial and ribosomal genes. Clusters were annotated by comparing cluster-specific gene expression profiles to marker gene lists and reference datasets57–60. Preliminary annotations were verified and refined using the Quby platform. Differential expression of genes between conditions within clusters was performed on Quby using DESeq2. GSEA was conducted using the differentially expressed data using Quby. Pathways with a P value less than 0.05 were plotted. L–R interaction analysis was performed with CellChat, an R toolkit designed for the quantitative inference and visualization of intercellular communication networks from scRNA-seq data. All fibroblast clusters and immune cell clusters with at least 50 cells in both conditions were selected for L–R analysis. Data visualization was done on R version 4.3.1 using the packages ggplot2 and Complex-Heatmap and the in-built visualization tools in CellChat. Interactions with significant differences in communication probability between conditions were selected.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1, GraphPad Software). No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications6,17,32. No animals or data points were excluded from analysis unless predefined technical failure occurred. The normality of distributions was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests. Data are presented as mean, with error bars representing standard deviation. For normally distributed data, depending on the number of conditions compared, either unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnettʼs or Tukeyʼs multiple comparisons were used. For data that did not meet normality assumptions, Mann–Whitney U-tests or Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunnʼs multiple comparisons were applied. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Diagram of JVL-driven changes in CSF and brain solutes circulation.

JVL rapidly causes intracerebral hypertension in the absence of ventricular swelling and BBB leakage. JVL impairs perisinus fluid flow in the dura, resulting in the blockade of intracerebral CSF circulation and regression of dorsal MLVs. Adaptive mechanisms to stabilize ICeP and reduce brain edema include, firstly re-routing of CSF circulation to the spine and acceleration of CSF drainage to the cLNs, then regrowth of dorsal MLVs and increased intra-cerebral CSF circulation. JVL-7 : Normalization of ICeP correlates with decreased intracerebral CSF circulation, astroglial AQP4 depolarization and brain edema. MLV remodeling with regression of dorsal MLVs associates with dural perisinus edema. Accelerated drainage to cLNs. JVL-14 : Regrowth of dorsal MLVs correlates with increased intra-cerebral CSF circulation, AQP4 repolarization, and increased PBMs. Slowdown of CSF drainage to cLNs. Persistent brain edema. Green arrows: Intracerebral CSF circulation. Purple arrow: Subarachnoid CSF circulation. Schematic illustrations were created with BioRender.com. Credit URL https://BioRender.com/odr7i5h.

Human MRI segmentation.

Manual and automated segmentations carried out on MRI scans to measure: the total intracranial volume (a), the volumes of the brain and ventricles (b), the volume of the skull, including calvaria (yellow) and skull base (brown) (c), the volumes of dural perisinusal fluid (d) and dural venous sinuses (e), and the volume of the optic nerve (f).
