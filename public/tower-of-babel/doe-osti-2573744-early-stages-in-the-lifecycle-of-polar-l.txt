1 Abstract
 Stratiform liquid‐bearing clouds are ubiquitous over the polar regions, where they are predominantly
mixed‐phase. These polar clouds induce substantial radiative forcing on the surface and continuously modify the
atmospheric thermodynamic budget, with direct implications for the polar ice pack resilience. However, the
physical representation of these clouds is still a major challenge for climate models. A significant part of polar
liquid‐bearing cloud lifecycle is often manifested in a quasi‐steady self‐sustaining, persistent, and turbulent state,
which is driven by longwave cloud radiative cooling and can last for multiple days. This self‐sustaining cloud
lifecycle stage has been thoroughly investigated in numerous studies, though some of its aspects such as
precipitation still lack robust quantification and evaluation. The preceding cloud lifecycle stages, which may last
up to several hours, have nonetheless remained widely overlooked. These preceding stages initiate at cloud
formation, often in a stable and non‐turbulent atmospheric layer and serve as a key junction between cloud
persistence and cloud dissipation. These two contrasting cloud lifecycle trajectories pose the question if general
circulation models (GCMs) can capture the full lifecycle accurately for the real physical reasons, a necessary
condition to improve our confidence in climate projections given the changing polar climate.
 The purpose of this project was to improve the characterization and understanding of these early stages
in the lifecycle of polar stratiform liquid‐bearing cloud and to aid their representation in GCMs. This research
relied on measurements from the Multidisciplinary drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate (MOSAiC)
field campaign, as well as the observations from the ARM West Antarctic Radiation Experiment (AWARE) and the
permanent ARM site at Utqiagvik, North Slope of Alaska (NSA).
 As part of this research we examined sources and formation mechanisms of liquid‐bearing clouds over
the central Arctic using the MOSAiC observations and reanalysis data and found that a hitherto overlooked
formation mechanism is in effect quite commonly. We also quantified bulk ice precipitation properties at cloud
base in Arctic supercooled clouds. Those bulk statistics can serve as strong process constraints for climate models
and improve our understanding of Arctic cloud lifecycles. We devised a method to derive Lagrangian boundary
conditions and forcing for Lagrangian large‐eddy simulations (LES). We also developed the PHAse Cloud Type
(PHACT) satellite data product based on spaceborne lidar measurements, which is calibrated using Arctic ARM
measurements. This dataset informs us on pan‐Arctic occurrence of opaque and tenuous liquid‐bearing clouds,
the latter of which serves as a proxy for the occurrence of early lifecycle stage of those clouds. The dataset was
used to evaluate the representation of those clouds in CMIP6 simulations. We also found a satellite observational
constraint evaluated against ARM NSA observations, which reduces projected warming uncertainty when taken
into account in supercooled cloud feedback calculations. Support by this project, we reported on the impact of
ice particle shape‐dependent growth on the generating supercooled cloud resilience in Arctic clouds. We also
reported that it is crucial to include snow in climate model radiative transfer calculations to reduce biases relative
to observations (also evaluated using AWARE observations). Finally, supported by this project, we worked on the

2 Project Activities
2.1 Sources and Formation Mechanisms of Central Arctic Liquid-Bearing Clouds
 Understanding Arctic stratiform liquid‐bearing cloud life cycles and properly representing these life cycles
in models is crucial for evaluations of cloud feedbacks as well as the faithfulness of climate projections for this
rapidly warming region. Examination of cloud life cycles typically requires analyses of cloud evolution and origins
on short time scales, on the order of hours to several days. Measurements from the recent MOSAiC expedition
provide a unique view of the current state of the central Arctic over an annual cycle. Here, we used the MOSAiC
radiosonde measurements to detect liquid‐bearing cloud layers over full atmospheric columns and to examine
the cloud‐generating air masses’ properties. We performed 5‐day back‐trajectory calculations for every detected
cloud and clustered them using a unique set of variables extracted from these trajectories informed by ERA5
reanalysis data. This clustering method enabled us to separate between the air mass source regions such as ice‐
covered Arctic and midlatitude open water. We found that moisture intrusions into the central Arctic typically
result in multilayer liquid‐bearing cloud structures and that more than half of multilayer profiles include overlying
liquid‐bearing clouds originating in different types of air masses. Finally, we concluded that Arctic cloud formation
via prolonged radiative cooling of elevated stable subsaturated air masses circulating over the Arctic can occur
frequently (up to 20% of detected clouds in the sounding dataset) and may lead to a significant impact of ensuing
clouds on the surface energy budget, including net surface warming in some cases. The results of study are
described in Silber and Shupe (2022) while the liquid cloud base height and cloud back trajectory data were
published as PI datasets on the ARM Data Discovery.

2.2 Arctic Cloud-Base Ice Precipitation Properties
 Cloud‐climate feedbacks are still the greatest source of uncertainty in current climate projections. Arctic
clouds, which are predominantly stratiform and supercooled, often long‐lived, and nearly‐continuously

precipitate ice particles, contribute roughly 10% of the uncertainty attributed to the global cloud feedback. This
Arctic cloud uncertainty is driven by incomplete observational and theoretical knowledge required to estimate
and explain the state and active processes occurring in those clouds. A focus on ice precipitation properties at
Arctic cloud base rather than the surface deconfounds the product of cloud condensate sink processes from the
influence of the atmospheric thermodynamic state below cloud base, rendering cloud‐base properties a more
appealing target for inference and evaluation of model simulations. Here, we developed an inverse model for the
estimation of cloud base ice precipitation properties over the NSA using the synthesis of ground‐based radar and
lidar measurements. By leveraging a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm as the core of the inverse
model, a wide range of particle size distributions were sampled, and different combinations of ice habit models
were examined, both of which are typically fixed in other retrieval methods. Results showed intriguing links
between different cloud base thermodynamic and ice precipitation properties. Apparent ice number
concentration enhancements at temperatures of ‐5 and ‐15 °C suggest possible secondary ice production (SIP).
The analysis alluded to an overestimation of SIP occurrence and intensity, especially in studies relying only on
radar or lidar measurements. Finally, reflectivity‐dependent ice precipitation rate and ice water content
parameterizations were derived. The results of this study are described in Silber (2023).

2.3 Devising a Method to Derive Lagrangian Forcing for Lagrangian LES
 As part of this project, a significant amount of time was invested trying a novel method to derive
Lagrangian forcing (advective tendencies) for an evolving atmospheric column in Lagrangian simulations. The
method incorporates latent heating and radiative transfer effects along trajectories. Lagrangian simulations are
typically nudged to Eulerian reanalysis profiles for simplicity (serving as boundary conditions), so this method
might serve as a significant advancement. However, it was concluded that the reanalysis dataset used to develop
this methodology is confounded by data assimilation during post‐processing, in some resemblance to modeling

2.4 Using the PHACT Dataset to Reduce GCM Evaluation Biases and to Evaluate Arctic Clouds in GCMs
 Guided by recent ARM ground‐based observations, we have developed the PHAse Cloud Type (PHACT)
product to document the topmost liquid‐bearing cloud layers for different cloud types (ice over liquid, liquid only,
liquid seeded by ice above, mixed‐phase, multilayer and single layer etc.) and for thin (optical depth ≲ 3) and
opaque (optical depth ≳ 3) clouds. The PHACT product is partly described in Cesana and Silber (2024), Cesana et
al. (2024a), and will be fully described in a separated paper.
 In a region where surface warming can be up to four times larger than in the rest of the world, it is crucial
to determine how clouds respond to climate change and whether their feedback will enhance or dampen the
warming. Studies using the most recent climate model generation (CMIP6) unveiled a strong impact of
extratropical mixed‐phase clouds on global climate. More surprisingly, on a global scale, CMIP6 models
overestimate the ratio of supercooled liquid‐phase clouds to all clouds rather than underestimate it as previously
thought. Here we focus our analysis on the Arctic region and evaluate liquid‐bearing clouds in six configurations
of GISS‐ModelE3 and eight CMIP6 models using a lidar simulator. Based on PHACT observations, we develop a
method to discriminate optically thin and opaque liquid clouds, which denote different stages of their lifecycle:
optically thin clouds are usually at the early stages of their lifecycle while opaque clouds are at a mature state.
We find that the models overestimate the amount of liquid clouds at most temperatures. More specifically, they
tend to produce too many opaque clouds with respect to all clouds between ~ ‐15˚C and ~ 8˚C, and too few below
‐15˚C. Pinpointing a root cause of these liquid cloud fraction biases is difficult, but likely contributors could be
biases in ice production in mixed‐phase clouds and/or macrophysical schemes. We further use GISS‐ModelE3
calibrated physics ensemble to explore these possibilities. The results of this study are currently being written up
and will be submitted to a journal in the next few months (Cesana et al., in prep).

2.5 Observational Constraint Reduces Projected Warming Uncertainty
 The increase of carbon‐dioxide‐doubling‐induced warming (climate sensitivity) in the latest climate
models is primarily attributed to a larger extratropical cloud feedback. This is thought to be partly driven by a
greater ratio of supercooled liquid‐phase clouds to all clouds, termed liquid phase ratio. We use an instrument
simulator approach, evaluated against ARM case studies, among others, to show that this ratio has increased in
the latest climate models and is overestimated rather than underestimated as previously thought. In our analysis
of multiple models, a greater ratio corresponds to stronger negative cloud feedback, in contradiction with single‐
model‐based studies. We trace this unexpected result to a cloud feedback involving a shift from supercooled to
warm clouds as climate warms, which corresponds to greater cloud amount and optical depth and weakens the
extratropical cloud feedback. Better constraining this ratio in climate models – and thus this supercooled cloud
feedback – impacts their climate sensitivities by up to 1˚C and reduces inter‐model spread. The results of this
study are described in Cesana et al. (2024b).

2.6 The Impact of Ice Particle Shape-Dependent Growth on Supercooled Clouds
 Here we analyzed a long‐term ARM NSA dataset to explore the impacts of cloud processes on the
probability of finding supercooled water given cloud temperature, P(L|T), in the topmost unseeded liquid‐bearing
layers. P(L|T) has local minima at temperatures around −6°C and −15°C. SimulaƟons using habit‐ (shape‐) evolving
ice microphysics models suggested that these minima are the result of efficient vapor growth by non‐isometric
habits found at these temperatures. We concluded that habit‐dependent vapor growth of ice crystals modulates
the macrophysical occurrence of supercooled water in polar clouds, the effect of which should be included in
model parametrizations to avoid biases and/or error compensation. Our methodology is adaptable for spherical
ice treatments implemented in models (example parametrizations provided), amenable for use with satellite
measurements to give global impartial observational targets for model evaluations (a conclusion that led to the
development of PHACT), and may allow empirical characterization of bulk responses to seeding and possibly
secondary ice effects. The results of this study are described in Silber et al. (2021). A follow‐on (Das et al., 2024)
showed that shape‐dependent growth modulates the impact of ice nucleating particles on mixed‐phase cloud
longevity. A summer undergraduate researcher (Zachary Nice, 2024) has extended this work and is currently
exploring the impacts of crystal shape diversity on mixed‐phase cloud properties.

2.7 Snow in GCM Radiative Transfer Calculations Reduces Biases
 The surprising increase of Earth's climate sensitivity in the most recent coupled model intercomparison
project (CMIP) models has been largely attributed to extratropical cloud feedback, which is thought to be driven
by greater supercooled water in present‐day cloud phase partitioning (CPP). Here, we reported that accounting
for precipitation in the Goddard Institute for Space Studies ModelE3 radiation scheme, neglected in more than
60% of CMIP6 and 90% of CMIP5 models, systematically changes its apparent CPP and substantially increases its
cloud feedback, consistent with results using CMIP models. This accounting of ice precipitation in ModelE3’s
radiative schemes was also demonstrated to reduce biases in drizzling case study simulations based on ARM
observations from AWARE (top left panel in Fig. 4). We found that including precipitation in the comparison with
cloud–aerosol lidar and infrared pathfinder satellite observations (CALIPSO) measurements and in model
radiation schemes is essential to faithfully constrain cloud amount and phase partitioning, and simulate cloud
feedbacks. Our findings suggest that making radiation schemes precipitation‐aware (missing in most CMIP6
models) should strengthen their positive cloud feedback and further increase their already high mean climate
sensitivity. The results of this study are described in Cesana et al. (2021).

2.8 The Earth Model Column Collaboratory (EMC2)
 Climate models are essential for our comprehensive understanding of Earth's atmosphere and can
provide critical insights on future changes decades ahead. Because of these critical roles, today's climate models
are continuously being developed and evaluated using constraining observations and measurements obtained by
satellites, airborne, and ground‐based instruments. Instrument simulators can provide a bridge between the
measured or retrieved quantities and their sampling in models and field observations while considering
instrument sensitivity limitations. Here we presented the Earth Model Column Collaboratory (EMC2), an open‐
source ground‐based lidar and radar instrument simulator and subcolumn generator, specifically designed for
large‐scale models, in particular climate models, but also applicable to high‐resolution model output. EMC2
provides a flexible framework enabling direct comparison of model output with ground‐based observations,
including generation of subcolumns that may statistically represent finer model spatial resolutions. In addition,
EMC2 emulates ground‐based (and air‐ or space‐borne) measurements while remaining faithful to large‐scale
models' physical assumptions implemented in their cloud or radiation schemes. The simulator uses either single
particle or bulk particle size distribution lookup tables, depending on the selected scheme approach, to perform
the forward calculations. To facilitate model evaluation, EMC2 also includes three hydrometeor classification
methods, namely, radar‐ and sounding‐based cloud and precipitation detection and classification, lidar‐based
phase classification, and a Cloud Feedback Model Intercomparison Project Observational Simulator Package
(COSP) lidar simulator emulator. The software is written in Python, is easy to use, and can be straightforwardly
customized for different models, radars, and lidars.
 EMC2 was used to examine a case study of highly supercooled mixed‐phase cloud based on ARM
measurements from AWARE; the same case study also examined by Cesana et al. (2021). We compared
observations with the application of EMC2 to outputs from four configurations of ModelE) in single‐column model
(SCM) mode and from an LES model. We showed that two of the four ModelE3 configurations can form and
maintain highly supercooled precipitating cloud for several hours, consistent with observations and LES. While
our focus was on one of these ModelE3 configurations, which performed slightly better in this case study, both
of these configurations and the LES results post‐processed with EMC2 generally provided reasonable agreement
