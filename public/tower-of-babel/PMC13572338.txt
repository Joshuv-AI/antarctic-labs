The effect of spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia on successful aging in older adults
Authors: Fatoş Uncu, Hasan Evci̇men, Metin Yildiz, Muhammed Ali Aydin, Eren Polat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the aging population increasing, it is important to identify effective psychological and spiritual support factors to help individuals experience the aging process in a healthier and more meaningful way. This study aims to examine the effect of spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia on successful aging.MethodsA quantitative cross-sectional descriptive survey method was used in this research. The study was conducted between February and July 2025 with 871 elderly individuals. Hierarchical regression analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0. Machine learning analysis and estimation were performed using the R programming language version 4.1.3.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 70.75 ± 5.40. According to regression analysis, the level of spiritual transcendence explains 51.9% of the level of successful aging (R²=0.519), and this relationship was found to be statistically significant (t = 30.619, p<0.001). When spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia levels were evaluated together, the explained variance ratio reached 67.5% (R²=0.675). An increase in spiritual transcendence (t = 14.122, p<0.001) and a decrease in kinesiophobia (t=–20.418, p<0.001) show a statistically significant relationship with the level of successful aging. SHAP values show the extent to which each variable influences the model’s predictions and the magnitude of this influence. According to these values, kinesiophobia is identified as the most important variable in predicting the successful aging variable.ConclusionsThe findings from our study indicate that supporting the psychological and spiritual resources of older adults plays an important role in strengthening the successful aging process. Longitudinal studies on successful aging are recommended.

Introduction

The proportion of the elderly population in Turkey is steadily increasing. According to data from the Turkish Statistical Institute (TÜİK), the proportion of individuals aged 65 and over, which constituted 9.1% of the total population in 2019, rose to 10.6% in 2024. This increase indicates that the elderly population has reached 9,112,298 people. This demographic change highlights the importance of research and interventions addressing the physical, psychological, and social needs of older adults [1].

The aging process is a complex period during which individuals undergo various biological, psychological, and social changes. During this process, the decline in physical capacity, the prevalence of chronic diseases, and changes in social roles are among the important factors affecting the quality of life of older adults [2]. However, the concept of successful aging emphasizes that aging is not only a biological process but also a multidimensional phenomenon that includes the individual’s psychological and social well-being. The successful aging model proposed by Rowe and Kahn (1997) includes elements such as maintaining physical health, sustaining cognitive functioning, continuing social participation, and ensuring emotional well-being [3]. Despite its widespread influence, this model has been critiqued for its predominantly biomedical orientation and its failure to adequately incorporate spiritual and existential dimensions of aging. Crowther et al. (2002) argued that positive spirituality constitutes a “forgotten fourth factor” in the Rowe–Kahn framework and proposed an enhanced model that includes spirituality as a core component of successful aging [4]. Similarly, Stowe and Cooney (2015) critiqued the model for neglecting social structural forces, historical context, and spiritual resources that shape late-life functioning, noting that its rigid biomedical criteria exclude many older adults who age successfully by their own subjective standards [5]. In Turkey, where strong religious and cultural traditions inform the daily lives of older adults, these omissions are particularly salient [6]. The Turkish elderly population is characterized by high levels of religiosity and family-centered social networks, which may serve as powerful buffers against late-life adversity. Incorporating spiritual and psychosocial dimensions is therefore not merely a theoretical enhancement but a culturally necessary step toward a more inclusive understanding of successful aging in Turkish society.

In this context, spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia are among the important variables that can affect the successful aging process of older adults. Spiritual transcendence, as conceptualized within Reed’s (2008) Theory of Self-Transcendence, refers to the expansion of self-boundaries in intrapersonal, interpersonal, temporal, and transpersonal ways that facilitate well-being, particularly in the face of vulnerability associated with aging [7]. According to Reed’s model, older adults who achieve self-transcendence demonstrate greater ego integrity, openness, and spiritual meaning-making processes that directly support psychological adjustment and resilience in late life [7]. Spiritual transcendence can also be understood through the lens of Tornstam’s gerotranscendence theory, which posits that in old age, individuals naturally shift toward a deeper cosmic and spiritual perspective, moving away from materialistic concerns toward a sense of connectedness with earlier and future generations [8]. This theoretical shift is associated with greater life satisfaction, reduced fear of death, and enhanced psychological well-being. Studies show that the spiritual needs of older adults are effective in increasing life satisfaction and that spirituality is a supportive factor in psychological well-being [9, 10]. Importantly, spiritual transcendence supports successful aging not merely through abstract belief, but through concrete psychological mechanisms: enhanced coping with existential challenges, greater sense of meaning and purpose, increased inner wholeness, and stronger psychological resilience [7, 11].

On the other hand, the concept of kinesiophobia was first defined by Lethem and colleagues (1983) as “an excessive, irrational, and avoidant fear characterized by fear of re-injury or pain, leading to avoidance of physical movement and activity” [12]. Studies on kinesiophobia show that fear of movement reduces the level of physical activity in older adults and negatively affects their quality of life [13, 14]. Importantly, kinesiophobia exerts its harmful effects not only at the physical level but also across cognitive, emotional, and social dimensions all of which are fundamental to successful aging. Fear of movement restricts social participation, undermines psychological well-being, and erodes functional independence in older adults [15, 16]. For example, a scoping review by Alpalhão et al. (2022) found that kinesiophobia consistently limits physical activity more than pain itself in older populations, and that its effects extend to functional skills, socialization, and quality of life [15]. A study conducted by Genç and Bilgili (2023) with elderly individuals living in nursing homes found that the level of kinesiophobia was significantly related to factors such as age, presence of chronic disease, and physical dependency [14]. These findings suggest that kinesiophobia functions as a multidimensional barrier to successful aging, impeding not only mobility but also the social engagement, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction that define aging well.

This research aims to understand how spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia levels in older adults relate to successful aging. To date, these two concepts have mostly been examined separately. However, when considered together, a theoretically meaningful relationship emerges: spiritual transcendence may function as a psychosocial resource that buffers the negative effects of kinesiophobia on successful aging. High levels of spiritual transcendence characterized by inner peace, meaning-making, and resilience may reduce the existential anxiety underlying kinesiophobia, thereby enabling older adults to engage more actively in physical and social activities. Conversely, kinesiophobia may undermine the social participation and embodied engagement that reinforce spiritual well-being. Understanding this interplay is essential for designing holistic interventions targeting both spiritual and physical dimensions of aging. The findings are expected to shed light on improving health services for older adults and structuring preventive interventions. Based on this rationale, the study addresses the following research questions: (1) Does spiritual transcendence significantly predict successful aging in older adults? (2) Does kinesiophobia significantly predict successful aging in older adults after controlling for spiritual transcendence? (3) Which variable (spiritual transcendence or kinesiophobia) is the stronger predictor of successful aging, as identified by machine learning approaches? The study hypothesizes that higher spiritual transcendence will be positively associated with successful aging, while higher kinesiophobia will be negatively associated with successful aging.

Methods

Type of study

This research used a quantitative cross-sectional, correlational (predictive) quantitative survey method. This study, conducted between February and July 2025 with 871 participants, was designed to determine the relationship between spiritual transcendence, kinesiophobia, and successful aging in older adults. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed in reporting the results of this study [17]. Data were collected face-to-face by trained research team members (MAA and FU) following a standardized protocol to minimize interviewer bias.

Population and study sample of study

The study population consists of individuals aged 65 and over living in a province in eastern Turkey. The minimum sample size required for the study was calculated using the formula for an unknown population. Accordingly, n = t(2) .p.q/d(2) (p = 0.5, q = 0.5, d = 0.05, t = 1.96) was found to be 384 individuals. The study was conducted using convenience sampling with 871 individuals residing in this province who met the inclusion criteria. In the post-hoc power analysis conducted using the results of the current study after the research, it was determined that the power of the study was 99% at a 95% confidence interval with a medium effect size. .

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study are as follows: (1) being 65 years of age or older; (2) being able to communicate verbally; (3) having no diagnosis of dementia or severe cognitive impairment (based on clinical records); (4) not having a psychiatric diagnosis that would prevent scale completion; and (5) volunteering to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria for the study are as follows: (1) having a health condition (e.g., active severe illness, recent fracture, or hospitalization) that prevents participation; (2) having a diagnosis of dementia or a neurocognitive disorder documented in clinical records; (3) having a diagnosed psychiatric illness (e.g., severe depression, psychosis) that could affect the reliability of self-reported data; and (4) refusing to provide verbal consent. We acknowledge that the broad eligibility criteria may limit control of confounding variables such as history of falls or fractures, which could influence kinesiophobia scores, and prior severe illness, which may affect spiritual transcendence levels. These limitations are further discussed in the Limitations section.

Data collection

Personal information forms, the Spiritual Transcendence Scale (STS), the Tampa Kinesiophobia Scale (TKS), and the Successful Aging Scale (SAS) were used to collect data. Data were collected from 871 participants through face-to-face interviews.

Data collection tools

Personal information form

The personal information form included questions about participants’ gender, age, marital status, educational status, income status, and whether they lived alone or with others.

Spiritual transcendence scale

Developed by Piedmont (1999) to determine individuals’ spiritual transcendence [18]. The Turkish adaptation was made by İme et al. (2019) [19]. The STS consists of 24 items and three subscales. The subscales are, respectively: universality, worship satisfaction, and connectedness. There are no reverse-scored items. The STS is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). A minimum of 24 and a maximum of 120 points can be obtained. It is assumed that as the score increases, the level of spiritual transcendence increases. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.90 [19]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.92.

Tampa kinesiophobia scale

Developed by Vlaeyen et al. (1995) to measure individuals’ kinesiophobia status [20]. The Turkish adaptation of the TKS was performed by Yılmaz et al. (2011) [21]. The TKS consists of seventeen items and is scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). There are four reverse-coded items. Reversed items were coded in reverse when calculated. A minimum of 17 and a maximum of 68 points can be obtained. It is assumed that as the score increases, the level of kinesiophobia increases. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the TKS was not reported in the Turkish adaptation study (Yilmaz et al., 2011). In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.88.

Successful aging scale

Developed by Reker to measure individuals’ levels of successful aging [22]. The Turkish adaptation was conducted by Hazer & Özsungur (2017) [23]. The SAA consists of ten items and two subscales. These subdimensions are healthy lifestyle and coping with problems. The SAA is scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree, 7 = strongly disagree). The minimum score is 10, and the maximum is 70. It is accepted that as the score increases, successful aging increases. The Cronbach alpha coefficient is reported to be 0.817 [23]. In this study, the Cronbach alpha value was found to be 0.93.

Data analysis

The analysis of the study data was performed using SPSS 25.0 and G*Power 3.1 statistical package software. Percentage, arithmetic mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values were calculated using SPSS 25.0. Necessary normality tests were performed in the process of analyzing the data, and it was understood that the data showed normal distribution (kurtosis and skewness − 1.5 to + 1.5) [24]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Predictive analyses were performed using the R programming language version 4.1.3.

Ethical considerations

Prior to the study, approval was obtained from a university’s scientific research ethics committee (approval number: 13.02.2025/03–22). Participants in the study were informed about the research, and their informed consent was obtained, clearly stating that it was based on voluntary participation. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were adhered to throughout the study.

Discussion

Our research findings reveal that successful aging is more pronounced in older individuals with higher levels of spiritual transcendence; conversely, this process is negatively affected in individuals with high levels of kinesiophobia. These results are consistent with multidimensional aging theories that suggest the aging process is shaped not only by biological factors but also by psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions [3, 25].

Our study found that successful aging levels increased significantly with increasing levels of spiritual transcendence in older adults. Specifically, spiritual transcendence alone explained 51.9% of the variance in successful aging (R²=0.519, t = 30.619, p < 0.001), representing a large effect. This finding suggests that spiritual transcendence operates through several concrete psychological mechanisms: (1) meaning-making the capacity to interpret aging-related challenges within a spiritually coherent framework; (2) ego integrity the acceptance of one’s life as meaningful and complete; (3) inner wholeness a sense of psychological integration beyond physical limitations; and (4) resilience the capacity to recover from adversity through spiritual resources. These mechanisms are consistent with Reed’s (2008) self-transcendence theory, which proposes that expanding one’s self-boundaries inwardly, outwardly, temporally, and transpersonally enhances well-being in the face of aging-related vulnerability [7]. In Turkish cultural context, where religiosity and spiritual practice are deeply embedded in daily life, these mechanisms may be particularly operative: spiritual engagement provides social belonging, routinized meaning-making, and existential comfort that collectively support successful aging. Our findings are consistent with previous studies on this topic. Ardelt and Koenig (2006) stated that spirituality supports quality of life by increasing older adults’ coping abilities with stress factors encountered in the final stages of life [26]. Lavretsky (2010) states that spiritual practices play a protective role in terms of psychological resilience, coping with depression, and neurobiological functioning in older adults [27]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Faraji and colleagues (2023) also showed that religious and spiritually-based interventions significantly improve the quality of life of older adults [28]. Furthermore, Zimmer and colleagues (2016), based on findings from different countries, emphasized that spirituality and religiosity are significantly associated with both physical and psychological health in old age; this relationship is shaped by mechanisms such as social support, healthy behaviors, and coping with stress [29]. In their meta-analysis using data from thirteen studies conducted using a random effects model, Haugan et al. (2022) demonstrated that spiritual transcendence in individuals over the age of sixty-five has a positive relationship with meaning in life, psychological adjustment, and resilience, and a negative relationship with depression [30]. They also emphasized that spiritual transcendence could guide healthcare professionals in supporting well-being in older adults [30]. The self-transcendence theory developed by Reed (2008) conceptually demonstrates that transcendence is intertwined with the processes of ego integrity, openness, and spiritual meaning-making in old age and thus supports successful aging [7]. Reischer and colleagues’ (2020) qualitative analysis of middle-aged individuals’ life stories revealed that themes related to spiritual integration, humanistic development, and the search for meaning are closely linked to self-transcendence [31]. The tendency of individuals to transcend themselves spiritually in later life emerges as an important element supporting the psychological aspect of successful aging [31]. Furthermore, the conceptual study by McCarthy and Bockweg (2013) emphasizes that the concept of transcendence is a holistic component of successful aging. McCarthy and Bockweg state in their study that spiritual transcendence plays a central role in processes that add meaning to an individual’s life, provide existential satisfaction, and ensure social adaptation. This theoretical framework provides a theoretical basis for the findings in our study and is thought to deepen the impact of spiritual transcendence on successful aging [11]. Furthermore, the psychological resilience and competence models proposed by Zabo et al. (2023) demonstrate that spiritual transcendence enhances an individual’s ability to cope with stress and difficulties by strengthening their internal resources. This mechanism provides an important perspective for understanding the psychological dimension of successful aging. Spiritual transcendence can contribute to maintaining quality of life and functionality by increasing the individual’s capacity to overcome life’s challenges [32]. In light of all this literature, our study provides a meaningful contribution to the field by empirically demonstrating the positive relationship between spiritual transcendence and successful aging, and by identifying the specific psychological pathways through which this relationship operates.

On the other hand, the negative relationship between kinesiophobia and successful aging is strikingly consistent with both theoretical and current empirical data. Kori (1990) defined kinesiophobia as an irrational fear stemming from an individual’s feeling of vulnerability, leading to avoidance of movement; he stated that this fear could cause a decline in physical functioning [33]. Naugle and colleagues (2022) found that higher levels of kinesiophobia in older adults without chronic pain were associated with both lower physical performance and reduced physical activity [34]. In their study evaluating the effect of kinesiophobia on functional balance and postural control in older adults with chronic low back pain and osteoporosis, Alshahrani & Reddy (2024) indicate that increased kinesiophobia has direct and indirect negative effects, particularly on postural control and balance skills [35]. This condition is thought to be detrimental to functional independence, one of the fundamental components of successful aging. Hidaka et al. (2023) investigated the relationship between kinesiophobia, catastrophizing pain, and quality of life in adults with advanced hip osteoarthritis [36]. They found that high levels of kinesiophobia were significantly associated with low quality of life, particularly in the domains of physical functioning, social participation, and role performance [36]. Declines in social participation and role performance represent a direct threat to successful aging, as these are core components of the Rowe–Kahn model and related frameworks. Furthermore, Wlażło and colleagues’ (2025) systematic review showed that kinesiophobia is a multidimensional barrier that negatively affects not only physical activity levels but also quality of life [37]. Importantly, kinesiophobia should not be understood merely as a physical barrier; it also carries psychological and existential dimensions. The fear-avoidance cycle underlying kinesiophobia generates catastrophizing cognitions, reduces self-efficacy, and fosters social withdrawal all of which erode the psychological resources needed for successful aging [15]. In this light, the interaction between kinesiophobia and spiritual transcendence becomes theoretically significant: whereas kinesiophobia depletes psychosocial resources through fear, avoidance, and withdrawal, spiritual transcendence replenishes these resources through meaning-making, resilience, and connectedness. This interactive dynamic may explain why, in our hierarchical regression model (Model 2), adding kinesiophobia alongside spiritual transcendence substantially increased explained variance (R²=0.675), with kinesiophobia emerging as the stronger predictor (t = − 20.418) in the multivariate context. The SHAP analysis reinforces this finding, identifying kinesiophobia as the most influential predictor of successful aging, suggesting that its effect is robust across multiple analytical approaches. Clinically, this pattern implies that interventions targeting kinesiophobia including cognitive-behavioral approaches, fear-avoidance reconditioning, and graded physical activity programs may yield the greatest gains in successful aging outcomes. At the same time, combining these approaches with spiritual support interventions may address the psychosocial dimensions of kinesiophobia and amplify therapeutic benefits. This is important for public health nurses and health professionals who should develop holistic approaches that go beyond programs aimed solely at increasing physical activity and also include cognitive-behavioral interventions for kinesiophobia and, when necessary, spiritual support practices.

This study provides a holistic perspective to the literature by addressing the psychological and spiritual dynamics that influence the aging process together. The hierarchical regression analysis (Model 1 and Model 2) was structured to first establish the independent predictive power of spiritual transcendence (Model 1), and then to examine the incremental contribution of kinesiophobia after accounting for spiritual transcendence (Model 2). This sequential approach allowed us to evaluate each variable’s unique contribution and to determine the combined explained variance. The machine learning analyses including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, Elastic-net/Lasso/Ridge Regression, Monotone Multi-Layer Perceptron (MONMLP), Quantile Regression Neural Network (QRNN), Decision Tree (DT), Principal Component Regression (PCR), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) were conducted as exploratory complementary analyses to validate the classical regression findings using non-linear and ensemble methods. Each model independently captures patterns in the data through different algorithmic approaches: for example, RF and GBM excel at capturing non-linear interactions; SVM is robust to outliers; ANN models complex relationships through layered learning. The convergence of findings across these diverse models strengthens the reliability of our conclusions. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis, which distributes each predictor’s contribution to individual model predictions, confirmed that kinesiophobia was the single most important predictor of successful aging across models. This finding aligns with and extends the classical regression result (Model 2: t = − 20.418), indicating that the dominance of kinesiophobia is robust to the choice of analytical method. Clinically, the SHAP findings suggest that reducing kinesiophobia should be a primary target in interventions aiming to improve successful aging outcomes. The evaluation of spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia within the same framework highlights the need for multidimensional approaches to individual and societal interventions targeting this period. In this regard, strengthening approaches that understand aging not only physically but also emotionally and spiritually should form the basis of efforts to improve the quality of life of older individuals.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that spiritual transcendence and kinesiophobia are significant and independent predictors of successful aging in older adults living in eastern Turkey. Spiritual transcendence alone explained 51.9% of the variance in successful aging (R²=0.519), while the combined model with both predictors explained 67.5% (R²=0.675). Kinesiophobia emerged as the stronger predictor in the hierarchical regression model ( t = − 20.418, p < 0.001) and was confirmed as the most influential variable by SHAP analysis across multiple machine learning algorithms. These findings extend the Rowe–Kahn model of successful aging by empirically supporting the inclusion of spiritual resources as a core dimension of aging well, consistent with Crowther et al.’s (2002) theoretical proposition. They further contribute to the growing body of evidence that kinesiophobia constitutes a multidimensional barrier not merely physical but also psychological and social to successful aging. From a theoretical standpoint, the findings are grounded in Reed’s Self-Transcendence Theory, gerotranscendence theory, and the multidimensional successful aging framework. From a clinical and public health perspective, the results suggest that: (1) spiritually-oriented programs (e.g., meaning-centered group interventions, mindfulness practices, religious community engagement) should be integrated into geriatric care to support successful aging; (2) kinesiophobia screening should become a routine component of geriatric assessment, given its strong negative association with successful aging; and (3) combined interventions addressing both spiritual resources and kinesiophobia (e.g., cognitively-integrated physiotherapy with spiritual support components) may yield the greatest benefit. Future research should include longitudinal designs to establish causal directionality, intervention-based studies targeting kinesiophobia reduction and spiritual enhancement, programs aimed at reducing kinesiophobia and providing spiritual support in older adults, and cross-cultural comparative studies examining how cultural context shapes the relationship between spiritual transcendence, kinesiophobia, and successful aging.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; the observed associations between spiritual transcendence, kinesiophobia, and successful aging cannot be interpreted as directional or causal. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to establish the temporal and causal nature of these relationships. Second, convenience sampling was employed, which limits the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to the broader Turkish elderly population. Participants were recruited from a single province in eastern Turkey; findings may not be fully applicable to elderly populations in western Turkey or in other cultural contexts. Third, data collection relied exclusively on self-report scales, which are subject to response bias, social desirability effects, and recall limitations, particularly in older adults. Fourth, the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria were relatively broad. Critical variables such as history of falls or bone fractures which may independently elevate kinesiophobia scores and history of severe illness or near-death experiences which may independently influence spiritual transcendence levels were not systematically assessed or controlled for. Fifth, potential sociodemographic and health-related confounding variables (e.g., comorbidity burden, medication use, functional disability level, social support) were not included in the regression models, which may have inflated the observed predictive effects. Sixth, cognitive status was not formally assessed using a validated screening tool; although participants with clinical dementia diagnoses were excluded based on records, subclinical cognitive decline could have affected the reliability of self-reported data. These limitations suggest caution in generalizing the findings and point to the need for more controlled methodological designs in future research on this topic.
