Craig-Bampton-based Quadratic Manifold for Nonlinear Substructuring Journal: Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering
Alexander Saccani, Paolo Tiso

Abstract
Component Mode Synthesis methods, such as the Craig-Bampton (CB) approach, are widely used in structural dynamics due to their modularity and compatibility with substructuring workflows. While highly effective for linear systems, extending these methods to geometrically nonlinear structures remains a significant challenge. In this work, we propose a nonlinear extension of the CB method tailored to such contexts. The approach is based on the construction of a quadratic reduction manifold, derived via perturbation analysis, in which high-frequency fixed-interface modes are statically condensed onto a reduced set of low-frequency modes and interface coordinates. This formulation enables the representation of geometric nonlinear effects without increasing the number of reduced degrees of freedom. The resulting Nonlinear Craig-Bampton (NL-CB) reduced-order model is obtained through Galerkin projection onto the tangent space of the manifold and admits a polynomial structure that is efficient for time integration. The formulation preserves the Lagrangian structure of the underlying finite element model, ensuring consistent energetic behavior and numerical stability. The proposed method is demonstrated on representative nonlinear structural systems of increasing complexity. The results show that the NL-CB model captures the essential nonlinear dynamic response while retaining the modularity and computational efficiency of classical substructuring approaches.



1
Introduction

The high computational cost associated with the dynamic analysis of structures using the Finite Element (FE) method led to the development of
Component Mode Synthesis
techniques.
These procedures consist of partitioning large dimensional FE assemblies into smaller subdomains, referred to as substructures, computing a Reduced Order Model (ROM) for each substructure, and eventually assembling the substructure ROMs to retrieve a representative model of the global structural behavior.
By doing so, the computational effort for dynamic analysis is significantly reduced.

The first pioneering work on CMS was independently developed by Guyan in
[
12
]
and Irons in
[
19
]
.
The reduction procedure consisted of splitting the degrees of freedom (DoFs) of each substructure into few interface and more internal DOFs and in statically enslaving the internal DoFs to the interface DoFs. Thus, the substructure ROM was obtained by Galerkin projection of the FE substructure equations on a Reduction Basis (RB) of Static Modes (SMs). This reduction technique goes by the name of the
Guyan-Irons
method.
Hurty in
[
17
]
and Craig and Bampton in
[
5
]
improved the accuracy of the Guyan-Irons model by complementing the RB with few low frequency
Fixed Interface Modes
(FIMs).
This substructuring approach is known as the
Craig-Bampton
method and has enjoyed successful application over the past decades.
As of today, the CB method shares its success in terms of popularity with the
Rubin
substructuring method, first presented in
[
39
]
. The Rubin method can be regarded as the dual alternative to the CB method, in that
Free-interface
Vibration Modes (VMs) and static solutions to imposed loads are used for reduction.

Substructuring techniques are well established for the reduction of linear systems and are routinely used in industry. However, their nonlinear counterpart is still in its infancy.
Investigating the response of nonlinear structures plays a pivotal role in the design of a broad array of structures, such as aerospace components
[
7
,
38
]
, energy harvesters
[
8
]
, MEMs
[
23
]
, and friction joints
[
43
]
, to name a few.
Among different nonlinearity types, geometric nonlinearities are extremely relevant in the design of lightweight structures or systems featuring large displacements, as the in-service motion breaks the linearity assumption.
As a result, the FE equations are nonlinear. This implies a substantial increase of the computational cost compared to linear models.
Even more than for linear cases, nonlinear ROMs are usually the only feasible option for design optimization and parametric explorations.

Unlike dynamic substructuring approaches, monolithic model reduction techniques are well consolidated for geometrically nonlinear structures.
In this field, popular approaches are
Implicit Condensation

[
34
]
,
Implicit Condensation and Expansion
(ICE)
[
15
]
, the method of
Static Modal Derivatives
(SMDs)
[
18
,
31
]
, the method of
Dual Modes

[
25
]
, the method of
Quadratic Manifold

[
22
,
40
]
, and the more recent method of
Spectral Submanifolds

[
21
]
[
46
]
.
All of these methods can be viewed as an extension of modal analysis to nonlinear systems.
In fact, the ROM is constructed starting from a truncated set of VMs, either in the physical space or in state space.
The strength of these methods is that, in all cases, full order simulations are avoided, resulting in efficient ROM construction.

In contrast to substructuring reduction, in monolithic approaches, the ROM is constructed starting from the assembled structure.
As a result, these techniques are not modular and therefore harder to integrate within industrial design procedures, where different structural components are designed independently by different teams.
Additionally, in case of repeated re-analysis of a structure with local substructure modifications, monolithic reduction forces a re-computation of the entire ROM.
Instead, if substructuring is used, when one component is modified, only the substructure ROM associated with that component is recomputed, while the other ROMs remain untouched.
In this way, model updating is extremely efficient.
Furthermore, parallel computing can be exploited both to speed-up model construction and model evaluation.
Another valuable aspect of substructuring is the integration of equation-based models with experimental data, allowing for hybrid models that offer more flexibility in presence of modeling uncertainties
[
1
]
.

For the aforementioned reasons, an extension of CMS methods to geometrically nonlinear structures is extremely relevant for accelerating design in an industrial setting.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, two main strategies have been probed so far towards the integration of monolithic ROM techniques with substructuring.
In the first strategy, the substructure ROMs are constructed in the same spirit of the ICE method, as first presented in
[
27
]
.
There, the CB RB was employed for substructure reduction and a third order polynomial restoring forces in both the FIMs and SMs amplitudes were identified from a set of nonlinear static solutions.
This approach was integrated with
Interface Reduction
methods
[
26
]
in
[
28
]
, an essential step to obtain efficient substructuring ROMs.
Subsequently, the method was extended in
[
30
]
to the Rubin RB.
One of the main benefits of the ICE-substructuring approach lies in its non-intrusiveness, as the computation of the substructuring ROM does not require access to the FE source code.
However, as shown in
[
14
]
, the choice of the training load set strongly impacts the accuracy of the ensuing ROM. As a consequence, fine tuning of the imposed static loads is required.
This limitation was also observed in ICE for monolithic reduction, as reported in
[
37
,
44
]
.
Compared to standard ICE, identifying the nonlinear restoring force for the substructuring ROM is even more challenging as loads imposed in the direction of SMs can trigger static buckling
[
14
]
.

A second strategy for nonlinear substructuring consists of enriching the projection space used in CB and Rubin reduction with second order perturbation vectors, derived similarly to the SMDs.
This second approach was first used in
[
48
]
, formalized in
[
50
]
for the linear CB RB, and extended in
[
49
]
to the Rubin RB.
Furthermore, more recent work in
[
33
,
2
]
confirms the robustness of this approach.
Nevertheless, its efficiency is severely undermined by the quadratic growth of the number of complementary vectors with the size of the starting linear RB.
For this reason, heuristic SMDs selection algorithms, as the one presented in
[
45
]
, have been used in
[
50
]
to reduce the total number of companion vectors. Noteworthy, compared to the ICE substructuring approach, this last method is intrusive and requires the access to the FE source code to construct the ROM.

The work presented here can be regarded as modification of the CB substructuring ROM based on second order perturbation vectors in
[
50
]
.
However, differently from
[
50
]
, we use a quadratic manifold, in the same spirit of the one proposed in
[
22
]
, to avoid additional DOFs needed to capture the dynamic effects induced by the geometric nonlinearities.
Specifically, the reduction manifold is derived using perturbation theory, enforcing a static condensation in modal coordinates of high frequency CB FIMs to low frequency CB FIMs and SMs.
The fundamental hypothesis behind the manifold derivation is that the inertia of high frequency FIMs can be neglected.
Starting from this hypothesis, we retrieve at linear level the classic CB ROM. Thus, the ROM presented here can be seen as an extension of the classic CB ROM to geometrically nonlinear structures, and, as such, we refer to it as NL-CB ROM.
The presented NL-CB ROM, derived upon Galerkin projection on the manifold tangent space, is in polynomial form, thus allowing remarkable speed-ups compared to the full FE model.
Additionally, we show that the NL-CB ROM preserves the
Lagrangian-Structure
associated with the FE model
[
10
]
, thus guaranteeing good stability properties for time-integration.

The manuscript is divided into three main sections. After introducing the FE discretized model and briefly recalling the standard CB method in Section
2
, we present in Section
3
the NL-CB ROM. Eventually, in Section
4
, we present numerical experiments where we apply the presented method on test cases of growing complexity.

Full Order Model Equations

2.1.1
Substructures Dynamic Equilibrium

N_{s}

For each of the substructures,

\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\ddot{\mathbf{d}}^{(s)}+\mathbf{D}^{(s)}\dot{\mathbf{d}}^{(s)}+\mathbf{K}^{(s)}\mathbf{d}^{(s)}+\mathbf{f}^{(s)}(\mathbf{d}^{(s)})=\mathbf{f}^{ext(s)}+\mathbf{g}^{(s)},

(1)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{uu}^{(s)}&\mathbf{M}_{uq}^{(s)}\\

\mathbf{D}_{qu}^{(s)}&\mathbf{D}_{qq}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{u}}^{(s)}\\

\mathbf{f}_{q}^{(s)}(\mathbf{u}^{(s)},\mathbf{q}^{(s)})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{u}^{ext(s)}(t)\\
\mathbf{f}_{q}^{ext(s)}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\mathbf{g}_{q}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{M}^{(s)}
,
\mathbf{K}^{(s)}
, and

(3)

or, in partitioned format,

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{u}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q})=

2.1.2
Assembled FE Equations

Compatibility conditions on interface displacements and forces must be imposed to solve the dynamic problem.
Displacement compatibility is written as

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{B}^{(s)}\mathbf{d}^{(s)}=\bm{0}.

(5)

where
\mathbf{B}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n^{(s)}}
.
This condition enforces continuity of the displacement field at the interface between two or more substructures.
Conversely, equilibrium at interfaces writes

\mathbf{g}^{(s)}=\mathbf{B}^{(s)T}\bm{\lambda},

(6)

where
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the generalized interface force vector, is required to prevent tearing of the substructures at the interface.

The equations for the assembled structures are obtained following a
Primal Assembly
procedure, where the interface reaction forces are eliminated by projecting the substructure equations on a basis of compatible displacements.
This operation is similar to the standard assembly procedure in the FE method, where the substructures play the role of elements.
Specifically, we first define a vector
\mathbf{\tilde{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}}
that collects all the displacement variables from the different substructures, including only once interface displacements shared between different substructures.
In this way, the displacement variables of substructure
s
can be extracted as

\mathbf{d}^{(s)}=\mathbf{L}^{(s)}\mathbf{\tilde{d}},

(7)

where
\mathbf{L}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{(s)\times\tilde{n}}}
is a boolean extraction matrix.
By inserting Eq. (
7
) in the substructure dynamic equilibrium in Eq. (
1
), projecting on
\mathbf{L}^{(s)}
, and summing over
s
, we retrieve the assembled dynamic equilibrium

\mathbf{M}\ddot{\tilde{\mathbf{d}}}+\mathbf{D}\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{d}}}+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{\tilde{d}}+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\tilde{d}})=\mathbf{f}^{ext}(t),

(8)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}^{(s)T}\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}^{(s)T}\mathbf{D}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{K}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}^{(s)T}\mathbf{K}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}^{(s)},

(9a)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\tilde{d}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}^{(s)T}\mathbf{f}^{(s)}(\mathbf{L}^{(s)}\mathbf{\tilde{d}}^{(s)}),\quad\mathbf{f}^{ext}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}^{(s)T}\mathbf{f}^{ext(s)}(t).

(9b)

In the previous expression, the projected interface forces vanish in the summation, since
\mathbf{L}^{(s)}
satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{B}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}^{(s)}=\bm{0}.

(10)

2.2
Craig-Bampton Reduction for Linear Systems

The CB model reduction technique, originally presented in the seminal paper in
[
4
]
to reduce linear structural systems, consists of two subsequent steps:
a reduction at substructure level using the Galerkin projection method and a subsequent primal assembly of the reduced substructure ROMs.
Specifically, the DoFs of substructure
s
are approximated as

\mathbf{d}^{(s)}\approx\mathbf{V}^{(s)}\bm{\xi}^{(s)},

(11)

where
\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{m}^{(s)}}
,
{m}^{(s)}\ll{n}^{(s)}
, is the vector of substructure reduced coordinates and
\mathbf{V}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}^{(s)}\times{m}^{(s)}}
is the substructure RB.
Splitting the substructure displacement vector into internal and interface DoFs, the CB approximation in Eq. (
11
) writes

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{(s)}\\
\mathbf{q}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}^{(s)}&\mathbf{S}^{(s)}\\
\bm{0}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\eta}^{(s)}\\
\mathbf{q}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}

(12)

Here,
\bm{\Phi}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{u}^{(s)}\times{n}_{\phi}^{(s)}}
contains few low frequency FIMs, while
\mathbf{S}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{u}^{(s)}\times{n}_{q}^{(s)}}
gathers the so called SMs, equivalently known as
Component Modes
.
The FIMs basis is defined as

\bm{\Phi}^{(s)}=[...,\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(s)},...],\quad\text{with}\quad(-{\omega}_{j}^{(s)2}\mathbf{M}_{uu}^{(s)}+\mathbf{K}_{uu}^{(s)})\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(s)}=\bm{0},

(13)

where
\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{u}^{(s)}}
is the
j
th VM of the substructure when all the substructure interface DoFs are constrained to zero, and
{\omega}_{j}^{(s)}
its associated angular frequency.
Additionally, the SMs basis, defined as

\mathbf{S}^{(s)}=-\mathbf{K}_{uu}^{(s)-1}\mathbf{K}_{uq}^{(s)},

(14)

represents the set of static displacements in the internal DoFs resulting from imposing unitary interface displacements.
The SMs are essential to capture the contributions of high frequency FIMs to the response to the elastic forces arising from interface motion.

In some applications, a further reduction of the substructure DoFs is achieved by resorting to interface reduction techniques
[
26
,
3
,
16
,
29
]
.
Specifically, the interface DoFs are reduced with the approximation

\mathbf{q}^{(s)}\approx\bm{\Psi}^{(s)}\bm{\chi}^{(s)},

(15)

where
\bm{\Psi}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{q}^{(s)}\times{n}_{\chi}^{(s)}}
, with
{n}_{\chi}^{(s)}\ll{n}_{q}^{(s)}
, is a RB of
Interface Modes
and
\bm{\chi}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{\chi}^{(s)}}
is the vector of interface reduced coordinates.
Thus, the CB approximation in Eq. (
12
) combined with interface reduction becomes

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{(s)}\\
\mathbf{q}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}^{(s)}&\mathbf{S}^{(s)}\bm{\Psi}^{(s)}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\Psi}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\eta}^{(s)}\\
\bm{\chi}^{(s)}.\end{bmatrix}

(16)

After defining the RB, the substructure reduced equations are obtained by inserting the approximation in Eq. (
11
) into the substructure dynamic equilibrium in Eq. (
1
) - neglecting nonlinear forces - and by performing a Galerkin projection, thus obtaining

\mathbf{M}_{r}^{(s)}\ddot{\bm{\xi}}^{(s)}+\mathbf{D}_{r}^{(s)}\dot{\bm{\xi}}^{(s)}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{(s)}\bm{\xi}^{(s)}=\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext(s)}(t)+\mathbf{V}^{(s)T}\mathbf{g}^{(s)},

(17)

where

\mathbf{M}_{r}^{(s)}=\mathbf{V}^{(s)T}\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\mathbf{V}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{D}_{r}^{(s)}=\mathbf{V}^{(s)T}\mathbf{D}^{(s)}\mathbf{V}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{K}_{r}^{(s)}=\mathbf{V}^{(s)T}\mathbf{K}^{(s)}\mathbf{V}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext(s)}=\mathbf{V}^{(s)T}\mathbf{f}^{ext(s)}(t).

(18)

Next, similarly to what described in Section
2.1.2
, the substructure ROMs are assembled using a primal assembly approach.
After defining the global vector of reduced displacements,
\bm{\tilde{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}}
, and the localization matrices
\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{(s)}\times\tilde{m}}
, such that

\bm{\xi}^{(s)}=\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}\bm{\tilde{\xi}},

(19)

the assembled CB ROM equations writes

\mathbf{M}_{r}\ddot{\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}+\mathbf{D}_{r}\dot{\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}+\mathbf{K}_{r}\bm{\tilde{\xi}}=\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext}(t),

(20)

where

\mathbf{M}_{r}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{M}_{r}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{D}_{r}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{D}_{r}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{K}_{r}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{K}_{r}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)},\quad\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext(s)}(t).

(21)

3
Nonlinear Craig-Bampton Reduction

In this Section we present our generalization of CB reduction to geometrically nonlinear structures, referred to as NL-CB ROM.
The NL-CB ROM is constructed by reducing the substructure dynamic equilibrium equations using a Galerkin projection on a nonlinear second order manifold.
We illustrate the theory in three different subsections: in Subsection
3.1.1
we derive the theoretical basis underlying the substructure reduction manifold; in Subsection
3.2.1
we show how the substructure manifold and the substructure reduced equations are numerically computed; finally, in Subsection
3.3
we detail the assembly of the NL-CB ROM using the primal approach.

3.1
Substructure Reduction

3.1.1
Substructure Manifold Derivation

The NL-CB ROM reduction subspace is derived assuming an unforced structure.
This choice does not restrict the application of the resulting ROM only to the investigation of the unforced response, allowing for forced predictions.
In fact, the use of reduction subspaces derived assuming unforced dynamics for forced predictions is at the heart of numerous successful model reduction methods, such as Modal Analysis
[
11
]
, ICE
[
15
]
, the methods of Modal Derivatives
[
18
]
, Nonlinear Normal Modes
[
24
]
, and Spectral Submanifolds
[
13
,
21
,
46
]
.

We consider the unforced dynamic equilibrium of the internal DoFs of a generic substructure
s
– see first block row of Eq. (
2
).
Furthermore, we neglect damping forces as well as the coupling inertial terms between interface and internal DoFs, as these terms are usually small compared to cross coupling elastic forces.
Thus, the dynamic equilibrium writes

\mathbf{M}_{uu}\ddot{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{K}_{uu}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{K}_{uq}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{f}_{u}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q})=\bm{0}.

(22)

Here, as in the rest of this section, we omit the superscript
"(s)"
when referring to substructure related quantities and reinstate it only when strictly necessary.
Although derived for zero external forcing, Eq.(
22
) describes the motion of internal DoFs
\mathbf{u}
, forced by the elastic forces
\mathbf{f}_{u}
resulting from the motion of interface DoFs
\mathbf{q}
.

To obtain a minimal dimensional ROM, we derive the manifold equations allowing for interface reduction.
Thus, we use the approximation in Eq. (
15
) for interface DoFs, from which, however, the unreduced interface case can be retrieved using
\bm{\Psi}
equal to the identity matrix.
In this way, Eq. (
22
) becomes

\mathbf{M}_{uu}\ddot{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{K}_{uu}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{K}_{uq}\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi}+\mathbf{f}_{u}(\mathbf{u},\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi})=\bm{0}.

(23)

Next, we perform a change of variables, writing the internal DoFs as a linear combination of FIMs (defined in Eq.(
13
)), divided between low and high frequency modes.
Specifically, we write

\mathbf{u}=[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\hat{\Phi}}]\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\eta}\\
\bm{\hat{\eta}}\end{bmatrix},

(24)

where
\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{u}\times{n}_{\phi}}
collects the first
{n}_{\phi}
FIMs, while
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{u}\times({n}_{u}-{n}_{\phi})}
the remaining
{n}_{\hat{\phi}}={n}_{u}-{n}_{\phi}
high frequency FIMs.
In the new coordinates, Eq. (
23

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{uu}\bm{\Phi}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{uu}\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\bm{\eta}}\\
\ddot{\bm{\hat{\eta}}}\end{bmatrix}+

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uu}\bm{\Phi}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uu}\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\eta}\\
\bm{\hat{\eta}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uq}\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi}\\
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uq}\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi}\end{bmatrix}+

(25)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{u}(\bm{\Phi}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}},\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi})\\
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{u}(\bm{\Phi}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}},\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},

where we use mass and stiffness orthogonality of FIMs
[
11
]
.
Notably, the coupling in the equations stems from two terms: a linear term, which is a function of the interface DoFs only, and a nonlinear term, which depends on both interface DoFs and FIMs coordinates.

In this work, the reduction manifold for the substructure equation of motion is obtained assuming that high frequency FIMs are statically enslaved to low frequency FIMs and SMs.
Specifically, by neglecting the inertia forces in the second equation in (
25
) we obtain

\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uu}\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uq}\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{u}(\bm{\Phi}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}},\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi})=\bm{0}.

(26)

We expect that this simplifying assumption holds, as we assume that the excitation to high frequency FIMs, coming from low frequency FIMs and interface motion, is at low frequency compared to their characteristic frequencies.
A similar assumption lies behind the monolithic reduction strategies underlying ICE
[
44
]
, SMDs
[
22
,
31
,
47
]
, and Static Quadratic Manifold ROMs
[
22
]
.

Equation (
26
) implicitly defines a static manifold:
the amplitudes of high frequency FIMs can be expressed as a function of the amplitudes of low frequency FIMs and of SMs.
Formally, if we write Eq. (
26
) as

\bm{g}(\bm{\hat{\eta}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\chi})=\bm{0},

(27)

we can define the static manifold
\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\eta}}
, in a neighborhood of the equilibrium point, as

\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\eta}}:=\bm{\eta}\times\bm{\chi}\mapsto\bm{\hat{\eta}}\quad:\quad\bm{g}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\eta}}(\bm{\eta},\bm{\chi}),\bm{\eta},\bm{\chi})=\bm{0}.

(28)

This statement can be justified using the Implicit Function Theorem
[
51
]
, as the Jacobian of
\bm{g}
with respect to
\bm{\hat{\eta}}
, evaluated at the origin is
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uu}\bm{\hat{\Phi}}
, which is non singular.
Additionally, since
\bm{g}
is analytic in its arguments - as we work with polynomial nonlinearities in Eq. (
4
) - we can expand the manifold in Eq. (
28
) in a convergent Taylor series of
\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{\chi}
, truncated at second order.
In this way, we can write the manifold approximation as

\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\eta}}(\bm{\eta},\bm{\chi})\approx\mathbf{\hat{B}}\bm{\chi}+\mathbf{\hat{C}}:\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\eta}+\mathbf{\hat{D}}:\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\chi}+\mathbf{\hat{E}}:\bm{\chi}\otimes\bm{\chi},

(29)

where

\mathbf{\hat{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{\hat{\phi}}\times{n}_{\chi}}
,

\mathbf{\hat{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{\hat{\phi}}\times{n}_{\phi}\times{n}_{\phi}}
,
\mathbf{\hat{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{\hat{\phi}}\times{n}_{\phi}\times{n}_{\chi}}
, and

\mathbf{\hat{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{\hat{\phi}}\times{n}_{\chi}\times{n}_{\chi}}
.
The analytical expressions for
\mathbf{\hat{B}},\mathbf{\hat{C}},\mathbf{\hat{D}},\text{and}\ \mathbf{\hat{E}}
are presented in
A
.
The derivation of the manifold coefficients is performed by leveraging the tensorial decomposition of elastic forces in Eq. (
4
).
Interestingly, the Taylor expansion does not contain any linear term in
\bm{\eta}
: these are identically zero as a consequence of linear FIMs decoupling – see
A
.

When we insert Eq. (
29
) into Eq. (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\approx\bm{\Phi}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\eta}}(\bm{\eta},\bm{\chi})

(30a)

\displaystyle\approx\bm{\Phi}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\mathbf{\hat{B}}\bm{\chi}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{C}}:\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{D}}:\bm{\eta}\otimes\bm{\chi}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{E}}:\bm{\chi}\otimes\bm{\chi},

(30b)

and eventually the reduced space of substructures displacements writes

\mathbf{d}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
\mathbf{q}\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\Gamma}_{\hat{\eta}}(\bm{\eta},\bm{\chi})\\
\bm{\Psi}\bm{\chi}\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

Upon defining the vector of substructure reduced coordinates as

\bm{\xi}=[\bm{\eta}^{T},\bm{\chi}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{{m}},

(32)

the reduction manifold approximation is compactly written as

\mathbf{d}\approx\mathbf{\Gamma}_{d}(\bm{\xi}):=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}),

(33)

where the linear manifold operator,
\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, writes

\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}&\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\mathbf{\hat{B}}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\Psi}\end{bmatrix},

(34)

and the quadratic operator,
\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m\times m}
, can be assembled from
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{C}},\ \bm{\hat{\Phi}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{D}},\ \text{and}\ \bm{\hat{\Phi}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{E}}
, as in Eq. (
30b
).

It is interesting to note that if we truncate the manifold at linear level, we retrieve the reduction subspace used in classic CB reduction, as proved in
B
.
This confirms the classic interpretation behind CB reduction, whereby high frequency FIMs are assumed to respond statically.

3.1.2
Substructure Projected Equations

The substructure ROM is obtained by Galerkin projection of the substructure equations onto the space tangent to the manifold.
Specifically, the dynamic residual vector
\bm{r}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}},\ddot{\bm{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{d}}
is defined by substituting Eq. (
33
) and its time derivatives into Eq. (
1
), obtaining

\bm{r}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}},\ddot{\bm{\xi}}):=\mathbf{M}\ddot{\bm{\Gamma}}_{d}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}},\ddot{\bm{\xi}})+\mathbf{D}\dot{\bm{\Gamma}}_{d}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}})+\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}_{d}(\bm{\xi})+\mathbf{f}(\bm{\Gamma}_{d}(\bm{\xi}))-\mathbf{f}_{ext}(t)-\mathbf{g},

(35)

where

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\Gamma}}_{d}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}\dot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,\tau}:\bm{\xi}\otimes\dot{\bm{\xi}},

(36a)

\displaystyle\ddot{\bm{\Gamma}}_{d}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}},\ddot{\bm{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}\ddot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,\tau}:\dot{\bm{\xi}}\otimes\dot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,\tau}:\bm{\xi}\otimes\ddot{\bm{\xi}},

(36b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,\tau}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,132}.

(36c)

Here, tensor
\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,132}
is obtained from
\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}
by permuting the elements in the second and third dimensions.
Note that the first and second time derivatives of the manifold
\bm{\Gamma}_{d}
include some convective terms that depend also on lower order time derivatives.
For example,
\ddot{\bm{\Gamma}}_{d}
depends not only on
\ddot{\bm{\xi}}
but also on
\dot{\bm{\xi}}
and
\bm{\xi}
.
The convective terms arise from the quadratic component of the manifold, as also shown in
[
22
]
.

Next, the residual is enforced to be orthogonal to the manifold tangent space, obtaining

\frac{\partial\bm{\Gamma}_{d}^{T}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{r}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}},\ddot{\bm{\xi}})=\bm{0},

(37)

where the projector is a linear function of
\bm{\xi}
and writes

\frac{\partial\bm{\Gamma}_{d}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,\tau}\cdot\bm{\xi}.

(38)

The reduced equations in (
37
) are still not in the final form used for ROM time integration.
In fact, a direct projection of the equations during time integration results in a computational cost that scales with the size of the FE model.
To solve this computational bottleneck, we perform an
offline-online
decomposition, whereby the computationally intensive operations, required in the projection of HFM equations, are performed prior to time integration.
Specifically, we preassemble the ROM operators that multiply the reduced coordinates, as is usually done in Galerkin projection models that use a linear RB
[
42
,
32
,
35
]
.
In that case, the reduced forces are cubic polynomials in the reduced coordinates, allowing for a tensorial representation which is extremely efficient to evaluate, and where the reduced nonlinear tensors are precomputed offline
[
35
]
.
Here, instead, the projection on the quadratic manifold yields terms up to seventh order in
\bm{\xi}
for the elastic forces, as results from inserting the quadratic manifold expression into the cubic full elastic forces and projecting the equations on the linear projector in Eq. (
38
).
Also in this case, the tensorial form could be adopted.
However, the higher polynomial order would undermine the efficient evaluation of the reduced forces, as well as the precomputation of the nonlinear tensors.

To improve efficiency of the ROM both in evaluation and model construction, we truncate the reduced nonlinear forces to third order.
The validity of this assumption lies in the fact that we use the ROM to predict vibrations around an equilibrium point, so we expect
\bm{\xi}
to be small.
As a result, we also expect that the relative importance of the monomial terms in
\bm{\xi}
in the reduced equation decreases with the monomial order. This justifies truncation of higher order terms.
Additionally, also the quadratic manifold has local validity, as it is computed using perturbation theory.
For this reason, retaining all the monomial terms in the ROM equations is probably unnecessary, as higher order terms would become significant only when the magnitude of the reduced coordinates become large enough to invalidate the quadratic manifold hypothesis. Interestingly, a similar approximation is implicitly made in
[
9
]
, where a reduction quadratic manifold is derived by assuming a quartic order potential function for ROM elastic forces.

The second simplifying assumption in the ROM derivation consists of neglecting the convective inertial terms in Eq. (
36
).
This hypothesis is consistent with neglecting the inertia and damping of high frequency FIMs in the manifold derivation, as detailed in Section
3.1.1
.
In fact, these convective terms are related to the inertia and damping of the quadratic component of the manifold, which has contributions only from fast FIMs, as can be seen from Eq. (
30b
).

Eventually, the third and last simplifying assumption consists of neglecting the projection of the external load
\mathbf{f}^{ext}(t)
on the quadratic part of the manifold,
\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}
.
As for the previous point, this hypothesis is consistent with the manifold derivation, where we assumed that the forcing on high frequency FIMs is negligible (see Section
3.1.1
).
Again, this owes to the fact that the quadratic part of the manifold has contributions only from high frequency FIMs.

With the aforementioned simplifying assumptions, the projected residual equation in (
37
) writes

\mathbf{M}_{r}\ddot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathbf{D}_{r}\dot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathbf{K}_{r}\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi})+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi})-\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext}(\bm{\xi},t)-\mathbf{g}_{r}=\bm{0},

(39)

where the reduced linear operators write

\mathbf{M}_{r}=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma},\ \mathbf{D}_{r}=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma},\ \mathbf{K}_{r}=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma},\ \mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext}(t)=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{ext}(t),\ \mathbf{g}_{r}=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{g},

(40)

while the expressions for the reduced nonlinear stiffness tensors are

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}^{2}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}),

(41a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}^{2}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma})+\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}^{2}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma})+\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}^{T}\cdot(((\mathbf{K}^{3}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}).

(41b)

We refer the reader to
C
for a formal derivation of the nonlinear ROM operators.
Notably, Eq. (
39
) has the same structure of the ROM equations obtained by projecting the nonlinear FE equations on a linear RB, guaranteeing optimal efficiency.

3.1.3
On the Lagrangian Properties of the Substructure ROM

In this section, we show that the substructure ROM equations in (
39
) inherit the
Lagrangian Structure

[
10
]
underlying the starting FE model equations.
In particular, we show that (i) the reduced mass, damping, and stiffness matrices are positive definite, and that (ii) the reduced elastic forces can be derived from a reduced potential function.
If these conditions are satisfied, reduced kinetic and potential energies, as well as a dissipation function, can be defined.
Preservation of the Lagrangian structure is a sought-after feature when devising new model reduction techniques, as it results in ROMs whose time-integration is numerically stable for a proper choice of time-integration schemes
[
10
]
.
The ROMs constructed using the Galerkin Projection on a linear RB are known to be structure preserving
[
10
]
.
In this work, however, we obtain the substructure ROM equations by Galerkin projection onto the tangent space of a quadratic manifold and by applying the simplifying assumptions detailed in Section
3.1.2
.
This procedure yields a structure preserving ROM, as shown in the following.

The reduced mass, damping, and stiffness matrices are positive definite, as they are obtained by left and right projection of their positive definite counterpart on the linear manifold operator
\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}
, as shown in Eq. (
40
).
Thus, the ROM kinetic energy and dissipation function,
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{D}
, can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{r}(\dot{\bm{\xi}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\xi}}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{r}\dot{\bm{\xi}},

(42a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r}(\dot{\bm{\xi}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\xi}}^{T}\mathbf{D}_{r}\dot{\bm{\xi}}.

(42b)

Moreover, the substructure ROM admits an elastic reduced potential energy of the form

\mathcal{V}_{r}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{K}_{r}\cdot\bm{\xi}+\frac{1}{3}\bm{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi})+\frac{1}{4}\bm{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\ldotp$}\kern-0.43057pt\hbox{$\ldotp$}\kern-0.43057pt\hbox{$\ldotp$}}}(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}),

(43)

where
\mathbf{K}_{r},\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}
, and
\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}
are the substructure reduced stiffness matrix and nonlinear stiffness tensors reported in Eq. (
41
).
We refer the reader to
F
for a proof of this statement.

As a consequence, along a solution of the ROM equation,
\bm{\xi}(t)
, the following power balance holds

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{r}(\bm{\xi}(t),\dot{\bm{\xi}}(t))=-\dot{\bm{\xi}}(t)^{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\bm{\xi}}}\mathcal{D}_{r}(\dot{\bm{\xi}}(t))+\dot{\bm{\xi}}(t)^{T}\mathbf{f}^{r}(t)+\dot{\bm{\xi}}(t)^{T}\mathbf{g}_{r}(t),

(44)

where the total energy of the reduced system
\mathcal{E}_{r}(\bm{\xi}(t),\dot{\bm{\xi}}(t))
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{r}(\bm{\xi},\dot{\bm{\xi}})=\mathcal{T}_{r}(\dot{\bm{\xi}})+\mathcal{V}_{r}(\bm{\xi}).

(45)

Note the presence of the energy related to the interface forces.
This term cancels out after assembling the substructures ROM, as the interface forces are pairs of action-reaction forces and compatibility of displacements is strongly satisfied at the interface.

3.2
Computation of the Substructure ROM

3.2.1
Computation of the Manifold

As presented in Section
3.1.1
and detailed in
A
, the substructure manifold is derived using perturbation theory.
The resulting equations for the Taylor series coefficients vectors, stored in
\mathbf{\hat{B}}
,
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
,
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
, and
\mathbf{\hat{E}}
(see Eq. (
29
)), take the general form

\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{uu}\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{*}=\bm{\hat{\Phi}}^{T}\bm{f}^{*},

(46)

where
\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{\hat{\phi}}}
and
\bm{f}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}_{u}}
are, respectively, a generic manifold component vector and right hand side forcing arising from perturbation.
In other words,
\bm{\eta}^{*}
can be any of
\mathbf{\hat{B}}(:,i)
,
\mathbf{\hat{C}}(:,i,j)
,
\mathbf{\hat{D}}(:,i,j)
, and
\mathbf{\hat{E}}(:,i,j)
, where we use Matlab notation to extract the component vectors.
The direct solution of Eq.(
46
) is not a feasible option, as this would imply the computation of all the high frequency FIMs
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}
, which is intractable for large FE models.

A workaround to this problem is found by exploiting stiffness orthogonality of FIMs.
In fact,
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{*}
that satisfies Eq. (
46
) can be computed as

\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{*}=(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\Phi}\bm{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{uu})\mathbf{K}_{uu}^{-1}\bm{f}^{*}.

(47)

Upon substitution of Eq. (
47
) into Eq. (
46
) and using stiffness orthogonality of FIMs, it is easy to see that this is the solution to Eq. (
46
).
We remark that the computation of the different manifold coefficients requires the inversion of the same linear operator
\mathbf{K}_{uu}
, thus its
upfront factorization can yield computational speed-ups.
In this case, the Cholesky factorization can be used, as the stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite.
The reader should note that this alternative computational procedure does not require the computation of all FIMs, but instead of only low frequency FIMs, which are usually few.
Note that by solving Eq. (
47
), we do not compute
\bm{\eta}^{*}
, but only
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{*}
.
However, this is not an issue because the manifold operators
\mathbf{\hat{B}}
,
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
,
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
, and
\mathbf{\hat{E}}
are eventually premultiplied by
\bm{\hat{\Phi}}
, as shown in Eq. (
30b
).

Another procedure that requires particular care in the computation of the manifold is the assembly of the forcing vector
\bm{f}^{*}
on the right hand side of Eqs. (
46
).
To compute the coefficients of the manifold at leading order,
\bm{f}^{*}
can be constructed by projecting the full order stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}_{uu}
on the basis of low frequency FIMs,
\bm{\Phi}
, and of SMs,
\bm{\Psi}
.
Conversely, a different procedure is followed in the computation of the second order coefficients.
In this case, as can be seen from
A
, vector
\bm{f}^{*}
takes the general form

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mathbf{K}_{uuu}^{2}:(\mathbf{v}_{u}^{i}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{u}^{i}+\mathbf{w}_{u}^{i}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{u}^{i})+

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\mathbf{K}_{uqq}^{2}:(\mathbf{v}_{q}^{j}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{q}^{j}+\mathbf{w}_{q}^{j}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{q}^{j})+

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}\mathbf{K}_{uuq}^{2}:\mathbf{v}_{u}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{q}^{k}.

(48)

Here, the tensors associated to second order nonlinear forces are contracted two times on vectors of displacements partitioned in interface and internal DoFs, which we denote with
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
.
The computation of vector
\bm{f}^{*}
by performing tensor-vector contractions is computationally infeasible, as it requires the assembly of the full order tensors,
\mathbf{K}_{uuu}^{2},\mathbf{K}_{uqq}^{2}
, and
\mathbf{K}_{uuq}^{2}
. This operation is extremely memory consuming even for moderately small FE models.

To overcome this limitation, we assemble
\bm{f}^{*}
using the tangent stiffness derivative approach, following a similar procedure to the one used in
[
31
,
22
,
32
]
for the computation of the SMDs.
Specifically, we consider the following relations

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{uuu}^{2}:(\mathbf{v}_{u}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{u}+\mathbf{w}_{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{u},\bm{0})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{u},

(49a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{uqq}^{2}:(\mathbf{v}_{q}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{q}+\mathbf{w}_{q}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}(\bm{0},\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{q})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{q},

(49b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{uuq}^{2}:\mathbf{v}_{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}(\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{u},\bm{0})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{q}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(\bm{0},\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{q})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{u},

(49c)

where

\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{K}_{uu}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{K}_{uq}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{f}_{u}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q})

(50)

are the substructure elastic forces on internal DoFs and

\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q})\text{ and }\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q})
are two partitions of the tangent stiffness matrix.
We refer the reader to

D
for a proof of these expressions.
In this setting, the tangent stiffness derivatives in Eq. (
49a
) can be computed with a specifically written code or using finite differences.
In this second case, for example, we can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(\epsilon\mathbf{v}_{u},\bm{0})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\approx\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(+h\mathbf{v}_{u},\bm{0})-\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{u}^{el}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(-h\mathbf{v}_{u},\bm{0})\right)(2h)^{-1},

(51)

where a first order central finite difference scheme with incremental step
h
is used.

3.2.2
Assembly of Substructure ROM Tensors using Element Level Projection

The assembly of the nonlinear substructure ROM tensors in Eq. (
39
) is performed using an element level projection strategy.
A direct assembly of the substructure full order tensors,
\mathbf{K}_{2}
and
\mathbf{K}_{3}
, and their subsequent contraction with the manifold operators using the formulas in Eq. (
41
) results in large computational memory consumption.
In the element level projection method, however, the assembly of full order tensors is avoided.
Instead, only the element level tensors are computed and directly contracted with the manifold operators restricted to the element variables.
Specifically, for a generic element
e
in the element set
\mathcal{E}
of a generic substructure, we can define the vector of nodal variables,
\mathbf{d}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}}
, and the element localization matrix
\bm{L}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times n}
, such that
\mathbf{d}_{e}=\bm{L}_{e}\mathbf{d}
.
Next, we consider the manifold operators restricted to element variables

\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}=\bm{L}_{e}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times m},\quad\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,e}=\bm{L}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times m\times m},

(52)

and the element stiffness matrix and nonlinear tensors,

\mathbf{K}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times n_{e}}
,
\mathbf{K}_{e}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times n_{e}\times n_{e}}
, and
\mathbf{K}_{e}^{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times n_{e}\times n_{e}\times n_{e}}
.
In the element level projection scheme, the nonlinear tensors are computed using the following formulas, derived from Eq. (
41
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}^{N_{e}}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}^{T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}_{e}^{2}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}),

(53a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}^{T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}_{e}^{2}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,e})+\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}^{T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}_{e}^{2}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma,e})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e})+

(53b)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}^{T}\cdot(((\mathbf{K}_{e}^{3}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{\Gamma,e}).

The element level projection approach relies on specialized FE programs that can return the nonlinear tensors related to nonlinear elastic forces.
Usually, this tensorial decomposition is not an output of commercial FE software.
To overcome this limitation, the method presented here could be potentially integrated with non-intrusive tensor identification methods in the same spirit of the Enforced Displacements Method or the Enhanced Enforced Displacements Method
[
35
]
.
However, such algorithms would need to be adapted to allow the identification of polynomial approximate reduced forces and to accommodate the projection on quadratic manifold components.

3.3
ROM Assembly

The final form of the ROM is obtained by assembling the substructure reduced equations in (
39
).
Since these equations are substructure related, we report them here reinstating the superscript ’
(s)
’:

\mathbf{M}_{r}^{(s)}\ddot{\bm{\xi}}^{(s)}+\mathbf{D}_{r}^{(s)}\dot{\bm{\xi}}^{(s)}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{(s)}\bm{\xi}^{(s)}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2(s)}:(\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\otimes\bm{\xi}^{(s)})+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3(s)}:(\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\otimes\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\otimes\bm{\xi}^{(s)})-\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext(s)}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)},t)-\mathbf{g}_{r}^{(s)}=\bm{0},

(54)

where
\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{(s)}}
is the vector of substructure reduced variables.
A vector of global reduced coordinates,
\bm{\tilde{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, is defined from the vectors of substructure reduced variables by assigning the same variable to interface displacements that are shared across substructures.
If interface reduction is used, we assume that the interface RBs are compatible.
The substructure reduced variables could be extracted using localization matrices from the vector of global reduced coordinates
\bm{\tilde{\xi}}
as

\bm{\xi}^{(s)}=\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}\bm{\tilde{\xi}},

(55)

where
\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{(s)}\times m}
.
The ROM equations are obtained by substituting Eq. (
55
) in Eq. (
54
), projecting the substructure equations on the localization matrices
\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}
, and by summing over the different substructures composing the assembly.
Thus, the final form of the ROM is

\mathbf{M}_{r}\ddot{\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}+\mathbf{D}_{r}\dot{\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}+\mathbf{K}_{r}\bm{\tilde{\xi}}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}:(\bm{\tilde{\xi}}\otimes\bm{\tilde{\xi}})+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}:(\bm{\tilde{\xi}}\otimes\bm{\tilde{\xi}}\otimes\bm{\tilde{\xi}})-\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext}(\bm{\tilde{\xi}},t)=\bm{0},

(56)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{r}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{M}_{r}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)},\ \ \mathbf{D}_{r}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{D}_{r}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)},\ \ \mathbf{K}_{r}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{K}_{r}^{(s)}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)},

(57a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\cdot((\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2(s)}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}),

(57b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\cdot(((\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3(s)}\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)})\cdot_{21}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)}),

(57c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext}(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{f}_{r}^{ext(s)}(t)

(57d)

The reader should note that the ROM assembly procedure eliminates the reaction forces at the interface between different substructures:

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbf{L}_{r}^{(s)T}\mathbf{g}_{r}^{(s)}=0.

(58)

This is because we assume that the interfaces of different connected substructures are reduced with compatible interface modes.

Time integration of Eq. (
56
) entails Newton-Raphson iterations, which rely on the computation of its Jacobian with respect to the reduced variables.
The expression for the ROM Jacobian writes

\frac{\partial\bm{r}}{\partial\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}=\mathbf{K}_{r}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2\text{t}}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\xi}}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3\text{t}}:(\bm{\tilde{\xi}}\otimes\bm{\tilde{\xi}}),\quad\frac{\partial\bm{r}}{\partial\dot{\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}}=\mathbf{D}_{r},\quad\frac{\partial\bm{r}}{\partial\ddot{\bm{\tilde{\xi}}}}=\mathbf{M}_{r}.

(59)

where

\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2\text{t}}=\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}+\mathbf{K}_{r,132}^{2},\quad\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3\text{t}}=\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}+\mathbf{K}_{r,1324}^{3}+\mathbf{K}_{r,1243}^{3}.

(60)

In this way, the ROM equations can be time-integrated using the classic
Newmark-
\beta
time integration scheme
[
11
]
.

4
Applications

To test the validity of the proposed method, we performed four different numerical experiments, benchmarking the nonlinear substructure ROM with full order FE simulations.
The numerical examples considered are flat and curved von Kármán beams, a flat panel, and a MEMS gyroscope.
The FE implementation used in all numerical experiments is based on
YetAnotherFEcode

[
20
]
, our in-house written Matlab code.

[

[

4

4.4
MEMS Gyroscope

As a last test case, we constructed the NL-CB ROM for the MEMS gyroscope presented in
[
32
]
, a device used to sense angular velocities.
The MEMS meshed geometry is shown in Fig.
5

This device consists of an inertial mass connected to the ground with four s-shaped beams.
During operational conditions, it is driven at the first natural frequency by a pair of electrodes that exert a force along the

This motion generates an out-of-plane displacement along the z axis when an external angular velocity is aligned to the

Through a direct measure of the out-of-plane displacement, the angular rate can be inferred.

As shown in Fig.

This domain partition can be used for efficient re-analysis: in case the suspended mass or the supporting beams are modified – in an optimization loop, for instance – only the corresponding substructures need to be updated while the rest remains untouched.
The geometry of the FE substructures was discretized with hexahedral elements with quadratic shape functions, each with
20
DoFs.
We adopted the
Green-Lagrange

\rho=2300\ \text{Kg}/\text{m}^{3}
, Young’s Modulus
E=148\ \text{Gpa}
, and Poisson’s ratio
\nu=0.23
.
We used
1581
elements for each of the four supporting beams and
8202

14\,626
elements.
The bottom face of each supporting beam was encastered by imposing zero displacements along the three axes, as schematically shown in Fig.
5

After imposing the boundary conditions, the total number of free variables in the model was
261\,495
.
The natural frequency of the assembled FE model is
f_{1}=3.3418\times 10^{4}\ \text{Hz}
and the corresponding VM is plotted in Fig.
5

Note that this mode is in the
x-y

Rayleigh damping with
\alpha=105
and
\beta=0
was considered.

The NL-CB ROM was constructed using two FIMs for the center mass and one FIM for each of the supporting beams.
Moreover, the interface between the suspended mass and the beams was reduced using rigid body interface modes as in the

[
26

In this way, each interface was reduced to three translational modes along the

The NL-CB ROM was constructed using the

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a substructuring ROM for geometrically nonlinear structures that leverages the polynomial structure of elastic forces arising from the nonlinear strain-displacement formulation based on the von Karman and Green-Lagrange strain tensor.
Reduction is performed at substructure level and the assembled ROM is obtained using a primal assembly procedure.
Substructure reduction is achieved by approximating internal DoFs using a quadratic displacement manifold.
Specifically, the dynamic equilibrium of internal DoFs is written in a basis of FIMs, divided into low and high frequency modes.
Then, the inertia and damping of high frequency FIMs are neglected, resulting in nonlinear static equations that are solved at second order using Taylor series.
In this way, high frequency FIMs are statically enslaved to low frequency FIMs and interface DoFs.
The latter can also be potentially reduced with interface reduction techniques.
The substructure ROM equations are obtained by forcing to zero the projection of the FE substructure equations onto the manifold tangent space.
In the projection step, three approximations are made: (i) the seventh order polynomial reduced forces, obtained by projecting the FE elastic forces, are truncated at third order; (ii) the convective inertial and damping terms, arising from the quadratic manifold terms, are neglected; (iii) the projection of the external load onto the quadratic part of the manifold is assumed to be negligible.
This results in a structure-preserving ROM that is highly efficient for time integration.

The substructure manifold is computed by repeatedly solving linear systems of equations featuring the inversion of the same linear operator.
Furthermore, the nonlinear reduced tensors are computed using the element level-projection procedure whereby the element nonlinear tensors are projected on the manifold operators restricted to element variables.
In this way, the assembly of full order tensors is avoided, allowing for feasible ROM construction also in the case of large dimensional FE models.

Notably, if the substructure NL-CB ROM is truncated at linear order, the classic CB substructuring ROM
[
5
]
is retrieved. Thus the presented technique can be regarded as an extension of the classic CB method to geometrically nonlinear structures.

The numerical tests presented in the paper show the excellent approximation quality of the NL-CB ROM, both for small dimensional academic benchmarks and for high-dimensional industrial problems.
The ROM delivers large speed-ups in time integration compared to the nonlinear FE model.
Furthermore, ROM construction is feasible with moderate computational resources.

To conclude, two improvement areas can be identified.
Specifically, the ROM construction requires partial intrusiveness in the FE source code for the computation of the reduced tensors, limiting its use to specialized FE programs. A possible remedy can be found in the indirect identification schemes for nonlinear reduced tensors
[
35
]
, which would require some modifications towards their integration with the presented method.
Finally, a second critical aspect is represented by the need for efficient interface reduction. As shown in the panel test case in Section
4.3
, interface reduction might require a full assembly of the structures, thus partially compromising modularity.
Nevertheless, the NL-CB ROM proves to be an accurate and efficient substructuring ROM that could be integrated with fully modular interface reduction techniques that are yet to be devised.

In this Section, we prove that if we truncate the reduction manifold expansion at linear level we retrieve the reduction space used in classical CB reduction.

Appendix F
Reduced Elastic Potential Energy

In this Appendix, we derive the elastic potential function underlying the substructure ROM presented in Section
3.1
.

Let us consider the substructure FE model potential function,
\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\mathbf{d})\in\mathbb{R}
.
If we insert the displacement manifold approximation
\mathbf{d}\approx\bm{\Gamma}_{d}(\bm{\xi})
in
\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\mathbf{d})
and differentiate with respect to
\bm{\xi}
, we get

\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))^{T}=\frac{\partial\bm{\Gamma}_{d}^{T}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{FE}^{T}}{\partial\mathbf{d}}\bigg\rvert_{\bm{\Gamma}_{d}}=\frac{\partial\bm{\Gamma}_{d}^{T}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}_{d}(\bm{\xi})+\mathbf{f}(\bm{\Gamma}_{d}(\bm{\xi}))),

(93)

where we used that the derivative of the FE potential function with respect to the nodal variables yields the nodal forces.
Using Eq. (
85
) we can write

\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))^{T}=\frac{\partial\bm{\Gamma}_{d}^{T}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}(\mathbf{K}\bm{\Gamma}_{d}+\mathbf{f}(\bm{\Gamma}_{d}))=\mathbf{f}_{r}(\bm{\xi})+\mathcal{O}(4),

(94)

where

\mathbf{f}_{r}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{K}_{r}\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi})+\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi})

(95)

are the substructure ROM elastic forces.
The potential function
\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))
is a polynomial of eighth order in
\bm{\xi}
.
If
\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))_{4}
is the truncation of
\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))
to fourth order monomials, we can write

\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))=\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))_{4}+\mathcal{O}(5),

(96)

and by differentiating with respect to
\bm{\xi}
we get

\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))^{T}=\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))_{4}^{T}+\mathcal{O}(4).

(97)

Then, by comparison of this equation with Eq. (
94
) we can infer that

\mathbf{f}_{r}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{\partial\ }{\partial\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))_{4}^{T}

(98)

and, as a result,
\mathcal{V}_{r}(\bm{\xi}):=\mathcal{V}_{FE}(\bm{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{d}}(\bm{\xi}))_{4}
is the ROM elastic potential function.
Additionally,

\mathcal{V}_{r}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\xi}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{r}\bm{\xi}+\frac{1}{3}\bm{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{K}_{r}^{2}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi})+\frac{1}{4}\bm{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{K}_{r}^{3}:(\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}),

(99)

as by differentiating the above expression with respect to
\bm{\xi}
one retrieves Eq. (
95
).
