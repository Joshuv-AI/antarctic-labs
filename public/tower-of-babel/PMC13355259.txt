A contrast-enhanced ultrasound-based nomogram for preoperative prediction of pseudocapsule status in localized clear cell renal cell carcinoma: a retrospective study
Authors: Ji Ma, Zhen Fang Wang, Fang Li, Jing Chen, Rong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pseudocapsule (PC) status of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is closely associated with tumor aggressiveness and surgical strategy, yet accurate preoperative non-invasive assessment remains challenging. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS)-based prediction model for assessing PC status in patients with ccRCC.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 254 patients with pathologically confirmed ccRCC who underwent surgery at Shanghai General Hospital, between August 2021 and December 2024. All patients received preoperative conventional ultrasound and CEUS examinations. Independent risk factors for PC absence or penetration were identified using multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. These factors were then incorporated into a nomogram to construct a predictive model. The model’s discriminatory performance was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling to generate calibration curves and evaluate goodness-of-fit via the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Finally, the clinical utility of the nomogram was determined using decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThree CEUS features—incomplete PC sign, peak hypoenhancement, and rapid washout pattern—were independent predictors in the final model. The nomogram showed acceptable discrimination [training AUC: 0.779, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.692–0.867; validation AUC: 0.749, 95% CI: 0.600–0.898] and calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P>0.05). DCA supported its potential clinical utility.ConclusionsIn conclusion, a CEUS-based nomogram was developed and internally validated to preoperatively predict PC status in ccRCC. This practical, non-invasive tool can guide and optimize surgical planning.

Key findings

• PC status predicts ccRCC aggressiveness and affects surgical strategy. CEUS enables high-resolution visualization of tumor microperfusion.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This radiation-free, easy-to-use tool enables preoperative risk stratification to guide surgical planning.

• We recommend incorporating this CEUS nomogram into preoperative evaluation for patients with localized ccRCC.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the 14th most common malignancy worldwide, with more than 435,000 new cases diagnosed annually (1). Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) accounts for approximately 80% of RCCs and represents the most prevalent solid renal malignancy in adults (2,3). A distinctive histopathologic feature of ccRCC is the pseudocapsule (PC), a fibrous structure formed at the tumor-parenchyma interface. Clinically, an intact PC functions as a biological and anatomic barrier that constrains local tumor spread and provides a natural dissection plane for nephron-sparing surgery (4).

From a clinical perspective, an intact PC serves as a natural anatomic barrier that facilitates nephron-sparing surgery by providing a favorable dissection plane, reducing intraoperative blood loss, and lowering the risk of positive surgical margins. It is also associated with confined tumor growth and more favorable oncologic outcomes. In contrast, disruption or absence of the PC reflects loss of barrier function and is closely associated with aggressive tumor behavior, increased risk of local invasion and metastasis, and shorter progression-free survival (5). Therefore, accurate preoperative, noninvasive assessment of PC status is clinically relevant for surgical planning, particularly for selecting candidates for partial nephrectomy or tumor enucleation, and for postoperative risk stratification.

Computed tomography (CT) is important for assessing renal tumor morphology and T staging (6). However, its ability to reliably evaluate PC invasion remains limited. Diagnostic performance is influenced by tumor size, imaging resolution, and reader experience, and subtle PC disruption is often difficult to detect using conventional cross-sectional imaging (7). Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is a radiation-free, real-time imaging technique that visualizes tumor microvascular perfusion using microbubble contrast agents (8,9). Owing to its high temporal resolution and sensitivity to microcirculatory dynamics, CEUS allows detailed visualization of perfusion patterns and tumor-parenchyma interfaces and therefore has theoretical advantages for evaluating PC integrity (10). These advantages have been further consolidated by recent reviews systematically summarizing the clinical applications and standardized protocols of CEUS for renal masses (11,12). Nevertheless, current CEUS research has primarily focused on tumor detection, differentiation, and grading (13-15), whereas systematic investigations specifically addressing its value for preoperative assessment of PC status in ccRCC remain limited.

Nomograms are widely used clinical prediction tools that integrate multiple independent prognostic or predictive variables to generate individualized risk estimates in a graphical and clinically intuitive manner (10). Although several studies have developed nomogram models based on clinical and CT or magnetic resonance imaging-derived radiological features to predict pathological characteristics of RCC, such as nuclear grade, metastasis, or PC invasion (13,14), CEUS-specific microvascular and boundary features have not yet been fully incorporated into such predictive frameworks, despite their potential relevance to PC assessment.

Accordingly, a reliable and clinically applicable nomogram may be constructed using multidimensional features derived from preoperative CEUS to enable noninvasive prediction of PC status in localized ccRCC. The purpose of this study was to retrospectively develop and internally validate a CEUS-based predictive nomogram for preoperative assessment of PC integrity, with the objective of providing surgeons with an objective decision-support tool to optimize individualized surgical planning and risk stratification. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-0312/rc).

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study enrolled 254 patients with ccRCC who were admitted to Shanghai General Hospital between August 2021 and December 2024. Conventional ultrasound (US) and CEUS examinations were performed within the month prior to surgery. The inclusion criteria were: (I) pathologically confirmed ccRCC and (II) preoperative clinical T-stage I–II localized RCC. Exclusion criteria included: (I) patients without pathological results; (II) benign lesions, non-ccRCC, renal pelvis lesions, and other rare types of renal tumors; (III) missing or poor-quality CEUS images; (IV) multiple ipsilateral or bilateral ccRCCs; and (V) incomplete clinical data. The study design and patient flow are presented in Figure 1. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai General Hospital under expedited review (No. 2026-278), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of this study.

Clinical and laboratory data

The clinical data that were extracted from electronic medical records included: (I) demographic information: age, sex, and body mass index (BMI); (II) medical history: clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, flank pain, and gross hematuria) and hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg); and (III) laboratory indicators: serum creatinine (Scr), albumin levels, C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and microscopic hematuria. All data were obtained within the week before surgery.

Conventional US and CEUS examination

The patient was positioned in the supine, lateral, or prone position, as required. The instruments used to acquire the CEUS images in this study included GE LOGIQ E9 (General Electric Co., USA) and Siemens ACUSON Sequoia (Siemens, Mountain View, CA, USA), equipped with a convex transducer probe (GE C1-6 probe, 1–6 MHz; Siemens 5C1 probe, 1–5 MHz. First, patients were positioned laterally for conventional US imaging. The tumor was scanned longitudinally and cross-sectionally using grayscale US, after which color Doppler imaging was applied to evaluate the tumor. Finally, CEUS was performed with patients in the same position, and a section displaying the maximum tumor area was selected for imaging. A low mechanical index (MI <0.1) was used for CEUS examination. A 1.0 mL dose of contrast agent (SonoVue; Bracco, Milan, Italy) was administered via bolus intravenous injection, followed by a 5 mL saline flush. The transducer was manually stabilized, and patients were instructed to take shallow breaths to minimize variation caused by respiration. Continuous dynamic observation of the masses under CEUS was conducted for 2 min, and the images were preserved.

Imaging analysis of US and CEUS

Two ultrasonologists, each with more than five years of renal CEUS experience, retrospectively reviewed the CEUS images in random order. They independently evaluated the CEUS features of ccRCC without access to clinical data or the pathological PC status. If disagreement occurred between the two ultrasonologists, a third ultrasonologist was invited to make the final determination.

For conventional US, the imaging characteristics of each ccRCC case were evaluated: (I) size; (II) boundary (clear/unclear); (III) shape (regular/irregular); (IV) echogenicity (hyper/iso/hypoechoic compared with the cortex); (V) echo halo; (VI) homogeneity (homogeneous/heterogeneous); (VII) growth pattern (exophytic/endophytic); (VIII) calcification; (IX) cystic degeneration; and (X) blood flow signals in color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) (with or without).

The analyzed CEUS features included: (I) enhancement pattern: based on the arrival time of the contrast agent within the tumor relative to the adjacent renal cortex, wash-in mode (rapid/simultaneous/slow) and wash-out mode (rapid/simultaneous/slow); (II) peak enhancement degree: hyper-enhancement (higher than normal renal parenchyma), iso-enhancement (similar to normal renal parenchyma), or hypo-enhancement (lower than normal renal parenchyma); (III) PC integrity (intact: clear and continuous enhanced rim surrounding the mass during CEUS; incomplete: discontinuous, interrupted, or unclear); and (IV) homogeneity: homogeneous or heterogeneous (a mass completely enhanced without any defects was classified as homogeneous, regardless of variation in enhancement levels, whereas heterogeneous enhancement was defined as a mass with unenhanced areas).

PC status

PC status was assessed and confirmed by a senior genitourinary pathologist. The extent of PC invasion was classified into four grades: grade 1: complete PC, characterized by an intact PC without disconnection or neoplastic infiltration; grade 2: PC involved but not penetrated; grade 3: PC penetration, in which the tumor crosses the PC and invades the peritumoral renal parenchyma; grade 4: PC not visible at any point in all slides. To ensure accurate assessment of the PC, all evaluations were performed with renal parenchyma present on the opposite side. For analysis, patients were stratified into two groups according to PC status: grade 1–2 (defined as the PC-confined group) and grade 3–4 (defined as the PC-breaching group) (16).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and R (version 4.1.0). Continuous variables with normal and non-normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median (interquartile range), respectively, and compared using the independent t-test and Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test.

Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analyses were included in a multivariable binary logistic regression model. A nomogram was constructed based on the final model using the rms package in R.

The model’s discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and internally validated using bootstrap resampling (1,000 repetitions). Calibration was evaluated with calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Clinical utility was quantified using decision curve analysis (DCA). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Sample size was determined by consecutive enrollment of all eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period, rather than by a priori power calculation. Inter-observer agreement between the two primary ultrasonologists for the conventional US and CEUS features was quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (unweighted for dichotomous variables; linear-weighted for ordinal variables), and interpreted using the Landis-Koch criteria (0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; >0.80, almost perfect).

Construction of a nomogram

Based on the independent predictors of PC-breaching (hypo-enhancement, an incomplete PC, and a rapid washout pattern), a nomogram prediction model was developed (Figure 2). In this nomogram, each of the three predictors correspond to a specific point score. By summing the individual points, a total score is obtained, which is then mapped to a predicted probability of capsular absence or penetration. This model enables rapid and individualized estimation of risk probability for each patient.

Evaluation of the nomogram model

The predictive performance and clinical applicability of the nomogram were comprehensively evaluated using ROC curves, calibration plots, and DCA.

Discriminative power of the nomogram model

The ROC curves for the training and validation cohorts are shown in Figure 3A,3B), respectively. The model demonstrated acceptable discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.779 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.692–0.867] in the training cohort and 0.749 (95% CI: 0.600–0.898) in the validation cohort.

ROC curves. (A) Training cohort. (B) Validation cohort. AUC, area under the curve; CI, confidence interval; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

Accuracy of the nomogram model

Calibration plots for both cohorts are presented in Figure 4A,4B. The predicted probabilities showed good agreement with the observed outcomes, as the nomogram calibration curves closely approximated the ideal reference line. This finding was further supported by non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test results (training cohort: χ2=2.741, P=0.60; validation cohort: χ2=1.549, P=0.82), indicating good calibration in both datasets.

Calibration curve for predicting probability of PC status. (A) Training cohort. (B) Validation cohort. PC, pseudocapsule.

Prediction of clinical utility

The DCA curves for the training and validation cohorts are displayed in Figure 5A,5B. In the training cohort, the nomogram provided higher net clinical benefit than the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies across threshold probabilities of approximately 0.13–0.81 and 0.86–0.92. Similarly, in the validation cohort, the nomogram yielded higher net benefit across a wide threshold range of approximately 0.13–0.93. These results suggest that the nomogram may aid clinical decision-making in this setting.

Discussion

PC is an important pathological feature of ccRCC that represents not only a structural interface between the tumor and adjacent renal parenchyma but also an important indicator of tumor progression and biological aggressiveness (4). Increasing evidence has demonstrated a close association between PC status and surgical margin status, microvascular invasion, local recurrence, and long-term oncologic outcomes (5,17). Therefore, accurate preoperative assessment of PC integrity is of substantial clinical importance for both surgical strategy selection and postoperative risk stratification.

Rationale for PC grouping strategy

In this study, PC absence and PC penetration were defined as PC breaching, based on the concept that the functional integrity of the PC as a local anatomic and biological barrier is more clinically relevant than subtle morphologic differences alone. Tumors with an intact PC or only partial involvement were classified into the PC-confined group, as the barrier function is considered at least partially preserved and the tumor remains relatively confined with a more favorable prognosis. In contrast, tumors with complete PC penetration or complete PC absence were classified into the PC-breaching group, as both conditions represent functional failure or loss of the PC barrier (18). These tumors consistently exhibit higher infiltrative capacity, more aggressive biological behavior, higher microvascular invasion rates, and poorer oncologic outcomes (5,17). This grouping strategy translates morphologic PC heterogeneity into clinically meaningful risk stratification with direct implications for postoperative management and surveillance.

Performance of the CEUS-based predictive model

Based on this PC classification, a CEUS-based nomogram was developed and internally validated for preoperative prediction of PC breaching in ccRCC. The model demonstrated acceptable and stable discriminative ability in both the training and validation cohorts, with AUC values of 0.779 and 0.749, respectively. Calibration curves showed close agreement between predicted and observed probabilities after bootstrap correction, and Hosmer-Lemeshow testing confirmed adequate model fit in both cohorts. DCA further established the model’s clinical utility, showing a consistent net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared with default strategies. Importantly, all selected CEUS predictors were biologically interpretable and closely associated with established pathological mechanisms of PC disruption, reinforcing the biological plausibility of the model rather than reflecting purely data-driven associations.

Interpretation of CEUS predictors

Multivariable analysis identified three independent risk factors for PC breaching: an incomplete PC sign on CEUS, hypoenhancement at peak time, and a rapid wash-out pattern.

From a structural perspective, PC integrity serves as the most direct imaging surrogate of PC status. A continuous peritumoral hyperenhancement rim on CEUS generally corresponds to an intact PC (Figure 6), whereas a discontinuous or interrupted rim reflects histologic destruction of the PC (10,19,20). In our study, an incomplete PC sign strongly predicted pathological PC penetration or absence. At the tissue level, progressive tumor expansion induces peritumoral ischemia, microvascular collapse, and proteolytic degradation, leading to thinning, fragmentation, and eventual breaching of the PC. These biological changes translate directly into focal or circumferential loss of peritumoral enhancement on CEUS, as disruption of the microvascular rim precedes gross anatomic discontinuity. Unlike conventional imaging, CEUS enables real-time visualization of microvascular perfusion, allowing detection of functional impairment of the PC before overt structural collapse (20,21). Clinically, assessment of PC integrity on CEUS provides incremental value beyond tumor size or morphologic features alone. Identification of an incomplete PC rim may indicate reduced tumor containment and a higher risk of aggressive behavior, with direct implications for surgical planning, particularly in nephron-sparing surgery where enucleation margins depend on PC preservation (22,23).

From a microvascular and hemodynamic perspective, highly invasive ccRCC is characterized by structurally abnormal and functionally inefficient neovasculature. Histopathologic studies have demonstrated irregular vessel caliber, endothelial damage, arteriovenous shunting, and loss of normal capillary networks (24,25), with a significantly higher proportion of undifferentiated vessels observed in high-grade compared with low-grade ccRCC (14). These microvascular abnormalities result in heterogeneous and inefficient contrast inflow. On CEUS, this is manifested as reduced peak enhancement (Figure 7), which likely reflects microvascular dysfunction rather than true hypovascularity. Consistent with this interpretation, prior CT-based studies have reported an association between hypoenhancement patterns and incomplete PC status (20). Further, rapid contrast wash-out on CEUS may indicate accelerated outflow caused by arteriovenous shunting, elevated interstitial pressure, and increased vascular permeability, which are characteristic features of a disorganized tumor microcirculation in aggressive ccRCC.

Thus, these CEUS features reflect a coherent mechanistic cascade from microvascular failure to perfusion impairment, stromal barrier destruction, and PC breaching. This aligns closely with the established pathology of PC invasion, which is associated with higher World Health Organization/International Society of Urologic Pathologists grade, microvascular invasion, and infiltrative growth (26). Therefore, PC breaching should be regarded not merely as a local anatomic phenomenon but also as an imaging biomarker of aggressive tumor biology. The integration of structural and hemodynamic CEUS features into a unified predictive framework provides a biologically grounded rationale for risk stratification, with direct implications for postoperative management and surveillance.

Comparative advantages and clinical integration

The performance of our model is comparable to that of previously reported CT radiomics models but offers several practical advantages. While CT radiomics can achieve high diagnostic accuracy and has been successfully applied to predict renal capsule invasion in RCC and to integrate clinical parameters for preoperative prediction of microvascular invasion or surrounding tissue invasion, these approaches often rely on complex feature extraction and machine-learning pipelines and lack intuitive biological interpretability (27-30). In contrast, the CEUS-based approach in the present study utilizes routinely assessable imaging features that are biologically interpretable. CEUS itself provides a real-time, radiation-free method for assessing microvascular perfusion that is well suited for dynamic evaluation. This combination of diagnostic accuracy, biological interpretability, and clinical practicality facilitates integration into routine preoperative workflows. Beyond the comparison with CT-based radiomics approaches, CEUS also complements conventional cross-sectional imaging. CT is essential for staging but has limited sensitivity to subtle PC disruption, owing to spatial resolution, partial-volume effects, and reader-dependence (7). MRI provides higher soft-tissue contrast but is constrained by longer acquisition times, higher cost, and gadolinium contraindications in advanced renal impairment. In contrast, CEUS provides real-time, high-temporal-resolution imaging of tumor microvascular perfusion and the tumor-parenchyma interface and may detect focal disruption of the peritumoral enhancement rim before gross anatomic changes become apparent on CT or MRI (10,11,21). Because sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles are not renally excreted and involve no ionizing radiation, CEUS is safe and repeatable even in patients with renal insufficiency, making it a useful complement to CT and MRI (31).

Clinical implications for surgical management

CEUS enables real-time visualization of tumor microvascular perfusion and provides dynamic information on enhancement and wash-out behavior that is closely related to tumor pathological characteristics. Accurate preoperative identification of PC breaching is particularly important for nephron-sparing surgery. Tumor enucleation relies on the presence of an intact PC as a natural surgical plane. In PC-confined tumors, enucleation or minimal-margin partial nephrectomy allows maximal preservation of renal parenchyma without increasing the risk of positive surgical margins (32). Conversely, PC breaching is a recognized risk factor for positive surgical margins and local recurrence, and such patients may require wider surgical margins and closer postoperative surveillance (18,33). By providing individualized preoperative estimation of PC breaching risk, the proposed CEUS-based nomogram offers a practical tool for tailoring surgical strategy and informing margin planning in ccRCC.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively limited sample size, particularly the size of the validation cohort. To limit overfitting given the modest sample size, the final model retained only three predictors, yielding around 14 events per variable—above the recommended threshold of 10. Internal validation by bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) showed only a small drop in AUC between the training and validation sets (0.779 vs. 0.749), suggesting limited overfitting. Even so, these results should be interpreted with caution, and external validation in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts is needed to confirm the model's robustness and generalizability. Second, CEUS features were evaluated qualitatively rather than quantitatively, and inter-observer variability may have influenced feature assessment. Inter-observer agreement was substantial-to-almost-perfect for the majority of features, with the PC sign showing only moderate agreement (kappa, 0.488)—reflecting the inherent subjectivity of identifying focal peritumoral rim interruptions and supporting our use of consensus adjudication. Future studies incorporating quantitative CEUS perfusion analysis, radiomics, and artificial intelligence-based automation may further improve diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility. Finally, integration of clinical, laboratory, and molecular biomarkers may further refine individualized PC breaching risk prediction.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a CEUS-based nomogram was developed and internally validated for preoperative prediction of PC breaching in patients with ccRCC. Incorporating three CEUS imaging signatures—PC integrity, peak enhancement degree, and wash-out pattern—the model demonstrated acceptable and stable discrimination and reliable calibration in both the training and validation cohorts, and DCA confirmed clinical net benefit across a wide threshold range. This practical, noninvasive tool may aid preoperative assessment of tumor aggressiveness and support individualized surgical decision-making in ccRCC.
