Non-standard analysis for coherent risk estimation: hyperfinite representations, discrete Kusuoka formulae, and plug-in asymptotics Thanks: IM CAS (RVO 67985840).
Tomasz Kania

Abstract
. We develop a non-standard analysis framework for coherent risk measures and their finite-sample analogues, coherent risk estimators, building on recent work of Aichele, Cialenco, Jelito, and Pitera. Coherent risk measures on L ∞ L^{\infty} are realised as standard parts of internal support functionals on Loeb probability spaces, and coherent risk estimators arise as finite-grid restrictions. Our main results are: (i) a hyperfinite robust representation theorem that yields, as finite shadows, the robust representation results for coherent risk estimators; (ii) a discrete Kusuoka representation for law-invariant coherent risk estimators as suprema of mixtures of discrete expected shortfalls on { k / n : k = 1 , … , n } \{k/n:k=1,\ldots,n\} ; (iii) uniform almost sure consistency (with an explicit rate) for canonical spectral plug-in estimators over Lipschitz spectral classes; (iv) a Kusuoka-type plug-in consistency theorem under tightness and uniform estimation assumptions; (v) bootstrap validity for spectral plug-in estimators via an NSA reformulation of the functional delta method (under standard smoothness assumptions on F X F_{X} ); and (vi) asymptotic normality obtained through a hyperfinite central limit theorem. The hyperfinite viewpoint provides a transparent probability-to-statistics dictionary: applying a risk measure to a law corresponds to evaluating an internal functional on a hyperfinite empirical measure and taking the standard part. We include a standard self-contained introduction to the required non-standard tools.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

The measurement of financial risk occupies a central position in modern quantitative
finance, insurance, and regulatory frameworks. Since the seminal work of Artzner, Delbaen,
Eber, and Heath
[
2
]
, the axiomatic approach to risk measures has provided both
theoretical foundations and practical guidance for risk management. A
coherent risk
measure
(CRM) is a functional
\rho
acting on random variables (representing profit and
loss distributions) that satisfies four economically motivated axioms: monotonicity, cash
additivity, positive homogeneity, and subadditivity. (Throughout, we adopt the P&L sign
convention:
X
represents profit, so losses are negative and
\rho(X)
evaluates required
capital; hence the
(-X)
in dual representations.)

The fundamental structural result for CRMs is the
robust representation theorem
,
which asserts that under mild regularity conditions, any coherent risk measure can be
written as a supremum of expectations under a family of probability measures:

\rho(X)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{E}_{Q}[-X].

This representation reveals the dual nature of coherent risk: the set
\mathcal{Q}

encodes model uncertainty or stress scenarios, whilst the supremum captures a worst-case
perspective essential to prudent risk management.

Whilst the theory of CRMs is well-developed at the population level—where one has access
to the full distribution of
X
—practical applications invariably require estimation from
finite samples. This raises the statistical question: what is the appropriate analogue of
coherent risk when one observes only a sample
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
?

This question was recently addressed by Aichele, Cialenco, Jelito, and Pitera
[
1
]
,
who introduced the concept of a
coherent risk estimator
(CRE): a functional

\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the same four coherence axioms, now interpreted
coordinatewise on the sample space. Their work establishes that CREs admit finite-dimensional
robust representations as suprema of linear functionals, with law-invariant and comonotonic
variants corresponding to L-estimators based on order statistics.

1.2.
The non-standard analysis perspective

The purpose of this paper is to develop a unified framework for CRMs and CREs using

non-standard analysis
(NSA). This approach, originating in the work of Abraham
Robinson
[
16
]
, extends the real numbers to the
hyperreals

{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
,
which include infinitesimal and infinite elements. Whilst initially developed for logical
and foundational purposes, NSA has proven to be a powerful tool in probability theory,
particularly through the
Loeb measure construction

[
15
]
, which produces
genuine
\sigma
-additive probability spaces from internal hyperfinite structures.

From the NSA perspective, the objects of interest have particularly transparent
interpretations:

•

Hyperfinite probability spaces.
An internal hyperfinite set

I_{N}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
with
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
infinite, equipped with the counting
probability measure
\mu_{N}(A)=|A|/N
, becomes a genuine probability space via the Loeb
construction. This provides a “bridge” between discrete and continuous probability.

•

Coherent risk measures as standard parts.
A CRM
\rho
on
L^{\infty}
can be
expressed as the standard part of a hyperfinite support functional: dual measures
Q

correspond to hyperfinite weight vectors
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
with
\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}=1
,
and expectations under
Q
become hyperfinite sums.

•

CREs as finite shadows.
By taking
N=n
finite, the hyperfinite
representation specialises to the finite-dimensional robust representation of CREs. The
passage from CRMs to CREs is simply the restriction of the hyperfinite picture to a finite
grid.

•

Plug-in estimators as hyperfinite L-statistics.
On an infinite hyperfinite
i.i.d. sample
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
, spectral risk measures become standard parts of
hyperfinite L-statistics. Consistency results emerge from the hyperfinite law of large
numbers and quantile convergence.

This unified viewpoint offers several advantages. First, it provides a single conceptual
framework encompassing both the population-level theory of CRMs and the finite-sample
theory of CREs. Second, it simplifies certain asymptotic arguments by allowing one to work
internally with the entire probability space at once, taking standard parts only at the
end. Third, it suggests natural generalisations and new results, including uniform
consistency over families of risk measures and bootstrap validity.

1.5.
Notation

Throughout,
(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
denotes a probability space. For brevity, we write
L^{0}
for

L^{0}(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
, the space of (equivalence classes of) measurable
functions, and
L^{\infty}
for
L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})

\mathsf{P}

For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

x_{1:n}\leqslant x_{2:n}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant x_{n:n}

s(x)=(x_{1:n},\ldots,x_{n:n})

2.
Coherent risk measures and coherent risk estimators

This section recalls the basic definitions and fundamental representation theorems,
following the exposition in
[
1
]
and the standard reference
[
11
]
.

2.1.
Coherent risk measures on
L^{\infty}

The axiomatic approach to risk measurement begins with economically motivated desiderata
for a ‘good’ risk functional. We interpret
X\in L^{\infty}
as a random profit and loss
(P&L), with positive values representing gains and negative values representing losses.
The quantity
\rho(X)
represents the capital required to make the position
X
acceptable.

Definition 2.1

(Coherent risk measure)
.

A functional
\rho:L^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
coherent risk measure
(CRM) if for all

X,Y\in L^{\infty}
,
m\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\lambda\geqslant 0
:

(R1)

Monotonicity:

X\leqslant Y
implies
\rho(X)\geqslant\rho(Y)
.

(R2)

Cash additivity:

\rho(X+m)=\rho(X)-m
.

(R3)

Positive homogeneity:

\rho(\lambda X)=\lambda\rho(X)
.

(R4)

Subadditivity:

\rho(X+Y)\leqslant\rho(X)+\rho(Y)
.

The axioms have clear economic interpretations. Monotonicity states that a position with
uniformly better outcomes requires less capital. Cash additivity asserts that adding a
deterministic amount
m
to the position reduces the required capital by the same amount.
Positive homogeneity implies that scaling a position scales the risk proportionally.
Subadditivity captures the principle of diversification: the risk of a combined portfolio
does not exceed the sum of individual risks.

The fundamental structural result for CRMs is the robust representation theorem, which
characterises coherent risk measures in terms of their dual sets of probability measures.
We recall one version; see
[
8
,
11
]
for a comprehensive treatment.

Theorem 2.2

(Robust representation on
L^{\infty}
)
.

Let
\rho:L^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
be a coherent risk measure satisfying the
Fatou property
:
if
(X_{n})
is a bounded sequence converging
\mathsf{P}
-almost surely to
X
, then

\rho(X)\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\rho(X_{n})
. Then there exists a non-empty convex set

\mathcal{Q}
of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathscr{G})
, each absolutely
continuous with respect to
\mathsf{P}
, such that

(1)

\rho(X)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{E}_{Q}[-X],\qquad X\in L^{\infty}.

If, in addition, the set of Radon–Nikodym derivatives

\{dQ/d\mathsf{P}:Q\in\mathcal{Q}\}\subset L^{1}
can be chosen
\sigma(L^{1},L^{\infty})
-compact
(equivalently, uniformly integrable and
\sigma(L^{1},L^{\infty})
-closed),
then the supremum is attained for each
X
.

The set
\mathcal{Q}
is often interpreted as a family of “stress scenarios” or
“generalised probability assessments”, and the representation (
1
)
expresses the risk as a worst-case expected loss over these scenarios.

2.2.
Law invariance and spectral representation

A particularly important class of CRMs are those depending only on the distribution of
the random variable.

Definition 2.3

(Law invariance)
.

A CRM
\rho
is
law-invariant
if
\rho(X)=\rho(Y)
whenever
X
and
Y
have the
same distribution under
\mathsf{P}
.

For law-invariant CRMs on atomless probability spaces, Kusuoka
[
14
]
established
a remarkable representation in terms of expected shortfall:

(2)

\ES_{\alpha}(X):=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\alpha}q_{X}(u)\,du,\qquad\alpha\in(0,1],

where
q_{X}:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is the lower quantile function of
X
,

q_{X}(\alpha):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F_{X}(x)\geqslant\alpha\}
.

Remark 2.4

(Parameterisation convention)
.

In our convention, the parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
is a
tail probability
: small
\alpha

corresponds to the worst tail of the profit distribution (largest losses). This differs from
the regulatory convention for
loss
distributions, where ES is often quoted at a
“confidence level” close to
1
(
e.g.
,
\ES_{0.975}
for the
2.5\%
tail of losses).
The two are related by
\ES_{\alpha}^{\text{profit}}(X)=\ES_{1-\alpha}^{\text{loss}}(-X)
.

Theorem 2.5

(Kusuoka representation
[
14
]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
be atomless and
\rho:L^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
a law-invariant
CRM satisfying the Fatou property. Then there exists a non-empty convex set
\mathcal{M}

of probability measures on
(0,1]
such that

(3)

\rho(X)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{(0,1]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha),\qquad X\in L^{\infty}.

A special case arises when the supremum in (
3
) is achieved by a single
measure, leading to spectral risk measures.

Definition 2.6

(Spectral risk measure)
.

Let
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be a function satisfying:

(S1)

\varphi
is non-increasing;

(S2)

\varphi
is bounded;

(S3)

\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha=1
.

The
spectral risk measure
with spectrum
\varphi
is

(4)

\rho_{\varphi}(X):=-\int_{0}^{1}q_{X}(\alpha)\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Spectral risk measures are coherent and law-invariant. The expected shortfall
\ES_{\alpha}

corresponds to the spectrum
\varphi(u)=\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\alpha]}(u)
.

2.3.
Coherent risk estimators

We now turn to the finite-sample setting. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and interpret

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a realised sample of P&L values.

Definition 2.7

(Coherent risk estimator
[
1
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

A mapping
\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
coherent risk estimator
(CRE) if for all

x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
m\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\lambda\geqslant 0
:

(E1)

Monotonicity:

x_{i}\leqslant y_{i}
for all
i
implies

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)\geqslant\hat{\rho}_{n}(y)
.

(E2)

Cash additivity:

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x+m\mathbf{1})=\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)-m
, where

\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)
.

(E3)

Positive homogeneity:

\hat{\rho}_{n}(\lambda x)=\lambda\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)
.

(E4)

Subadditivity:

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x+y)\leqslant\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)+\hat{\rho}_{n}(y)
.

The axioms (E1)–(E4) are the direct translations of (R1)–(R4) to the finite-sample
setting. Note that we follow the sign convention of
[
1
]
: larger losses (smaller

x_{i}
) increase the required capital.

Definition 2.8

(Law invariance and comonotonicity for CREs)
.

A CRE
\hat{\rho}_{n}
is
law-invariant
if
\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\hat{\rho}_{n}(\sigma(x))
for
every permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. It is called
comonotonic
whenever

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x+y)=\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)+\hat{\rho}_{n}(y)
whenever
x
and
y
are comonotonic,
meaning
(x_{i}-x_{j})(y_{i}-y_{j})\geqslant 0
for all
i,j
.

The main representation results for CREs, established in
[
1
]
, are:

Theorem 2.9

(Robust representation of CREs
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

A function
\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a CRE if and only if there exists a non-empty
convex set
M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}\subseteq\Delta_{n}
such that

(5)

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and the supremum is attained for each
x
.

Theorem 2.10

(Law-invariant CREs
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

A function
\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a law-invariant CRE if and only if there exists
a non-empty convex set
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
such that

(6)

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

with the supremum attained for each
x
.

Theorem 2.11

(Comonotonic law-invariant CREs
[
1
, Theorem 4.10]
)
.

A function
\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a comonotonic, law-invariant CRE if and only if
there exists a unique
a\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
such that

(7)

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Our goal in the following sections is to provide a unified hyperfinite perspective on
these results and to establish new consistency and asymptotic theorems.

3.
Non-standard analysis: a self-contained introduction

This section provides the essential non-standard machinery required for our applications.
We aim for a self-contained exposition accessible to readers without prior exposure to
NSA, whilst maintaining sufficient rigour for our proofs. For comprehensive treatments,
see Keisler
[
13
]
, Albeverio et al.
[
3
]
, and Fajardo–Keisler

[
10
]
.

3.1.
Standing conventions and the Loeb model

Convention 3.1

(Saturation assumption)
.

Throughout this paper, we work in a
countably saturated
non-standard enlargement:
every countable collection of internal sets with the finite intersection property has
non-empty intersection. This level of saturation suffices for all constructions in this paper,
including overspill/underspill arguments and the existence of Loeb measures. Countable saturation
can be arranged in standard constructions, e.g., via an ultrapower by a non-principal ultrafilter
on
\mathbb{N}
in a countable superstructure setting; see
[
3
, Ch. 4]
.

Notation 3.2

(Standing Loeb model)
.

Throughout Sections
3
–
10
we work inside a fixed countably
saturated non-standard universe. When we invoke hyperfinite probability, we use the
following canonical model.

We fix an infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
, chosen once and for all by the mechanism described in
Lemma
3.3
below. The hyperfinite set
I_{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
carries the internal
counting measure
\mu_{N}(A)=|A|/N
on the algebra
\mathscr{I}_{N}
of internal subsets.
Let
(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
denote the associated Loeb probability space.

When we state results about i.i.d. samples
(X_{i})
from a random variable
X
on an
ambient probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
, we implicitly pass to the
non-standard extension
({}^{*}\Omega,{}^{*}\mathscr{G},{}^{*}\mathsf{P})
and its Loeb
completion
({}^{*}\Omega,L({}^{*}\mathscr{G}),L({}^{*}\mathsf{P}))
; all “almost sure”
statements in NSA arguments are understood with respect to
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
unless
explicitly stated otherwise.

Star-suppression convention.

When a standard function
f
is evaluated at a hyperreal argument, we mean, by convention, its
non-standard extension
{}^{*}f
;
e.g.

q(\alpha_{k})
abbreviates
{}^{*}q(\alpha_{k})
.

Lemma 3.3

(Simultaneous validity of hyperfinite properties)
.

Let
(P_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a countable family of properties, where each
P_{j}(n)
is a

first-order
statement about sample size
n
expressible with rational parameters
(e.g., “
{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(|\cdots|>\varepsilon)<\delta
” for rational
\varepsilon,\delta>0
).
By the internal definition principle, each set

S_{j}:=\{n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:P_{j}(n)\text{ holds}\}

is internal. Assume each
P_{j}
holds eventually for standard
n
, i.e.,
S_{j}
contains
all sufficiently large standard naturals.

Then there exists an
infinite

N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
such that
P_{j}(N)
holds for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

For each standard
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define the internal set
B_{m}:=\{n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:n>m\}
.
Consider the countable family of internal sets
\{S_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\cup\{B_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
.

This family has the finite intersection property: for any finite
J\subset\mathbb{N}
and
finite
M\subset\mathbb{N}
, the intersection
\bigcap_{j\in J}S_{j}\cap\bigcap_{m\in M}B_{m}

contains all standard
n>\max(\max_{j\in J}n_{0}^{(j)},\max M)
, hence is non-empty.

By countable saturation,
\bigcap_{j\in\mathbb{N}}S_{j}\cap\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}B_{m}\neq\emptyset
.
Any element
N
of this intersection satisfies
P_{j}(N)
for all
j
(since
N\in S_{j}
)
and
N>m
for all standard
m
(since
N\in B_{m}
), hence
N
is infinite.
∎

Remark 3.4

(Properties used in this paper)
.

The properties
P_{j}
we invoke include: the hyperfinite strong law of large numbers
(Theorem
3.18
) for countably many integrands arising from truncations,
the hyperfinite Glivenko–Cantelli theorem (Theorem
3.20
), and the hyperfinite
CLT (Theorem
10.1
). Each is expressed in the first-order form
“
\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\,\exists n_{0}\,\forall n\geqslant n_{0}
: [bound holds]”,
which transfers to give internal sets
S_{j}
. Lemma
3.3
then
guarantees a single infinite
N
for which all hold simultaneously.

Proposition 3.5

(Atomless separable spaces and Loeb spaces)
.

Every atomless
separable
(i.e., countably generated mod null sets) standard probability
space
(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
is measure-algebra isomorphic to a hyperfinite Loeb space

(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
for some infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
.
Precisely, there is a Boolean algebra isomorphism

\Phi:\mathscr{G}/\mathcal{N}_{\mathsf{P}}\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L}/\mathcal{N}_{L(\mu_{N})}

between the measure algebras (quotients by null sets) such that
\mathsf{P}(A)=L(\mu_{N})(\Phi([A]))

for all
A\in\mathscr{G}
. This isomorphism induces isometric lattice isomorphisms

L^{p}(\Omega,\mathsf{P})\cong L^{p}(I_{N},L(\mu_{N}))
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Proof (reference).

This is Keisler’s representation theorem; see
[
10
, Thm. 10.3.1]
or
[
3
, Ch. 5]
.
The key fact is that the hyperfinite Loeb space
(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
with infinite
N
is
atomless and has the same measure algebra (mod null sets) as the Lebesgue unit interval.
Every atomless separable probability space shares this property by Maharam’s theorem.
∎

For distributional/statistical arguments, when the underlying standard probability space
is atomless and separable (which includes all standard Borel probability spaces), we may
replace it by an isomorphic Loeb space via Proposition
3.5
. This convention
avoids repeated measure-theoretic bookkeeping and allows us to treat hyperfinite sums as
Loeb integrals via standard part.

3.2.
The hyperreal numbers

The starting point of non-standard analysis is the construction of an ordered field
extension
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
of
\mathbb{R}
, called the
hyperreals
. There are several approaches
to this construction (ultraproducts, superstructures, axiomatic); we adopt the axiomatic
viewpoint, which suffices for applications.

Definition 3.6

(Hyperreal numbers)
.

The
hyperreal numbers

{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
form an ordered field extension of
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
the following properties:

(H1)

\mathbb{R}\subset{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
is a proper ordered subfield.

(H2)

(
Transfer principle
) Every first-order statement about
\mathbb{R}
that is true
remains true when interpreted in
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
.

(H3)

There exist
infinitesimals

\varepsilon\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
with

|\varepsilon|<1/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(H4)

There exist
infinite
hyperreals
H\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
with
|H|>n
for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(In standard constructions, (H3) and (H4) follow from (H1) and (H2) together with properness.)

The transfer principle is the key tool for moving between standard and non-standard
contexts. It asserts that the hyperreals satisfy the same first-order properties as the
reals. For instance, the statement “for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
with
x<y
, there exists

z\in\mathbb{R}
with
x<z<y
” transfers to “for all
x,y\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
with
x<y
,
there exists
z\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
with
x<z<y
.”

Definition 3.7

(Finite, infinitesimal, infinite)
.

A hyperreal
x\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
is:

•

finite
if
|x|\leqslant n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

infinitesimal
if
|x|<1/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

infinite
if
|x|>n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Two hyperreals
x,y
are
infinitely close
, written
x\approx y
, if
x-y
is
infinitesimal.

The following proposition provides the crucial connection between finite hyperreals and
real numbers.

Proposition 3.8

(Standard part)
.

Every finite hyperreal
x\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
is infinitely close to a unique real number,
denoted
\st(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and called its
standard part
. The map

\st:\{x\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}:x\text{ is finite}\}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

(i)

\st(x+y)=\st(x)+\st(y)
for finite
x,y
;

(ii)

\st(xy)=\st(x)\st(y)
for finite
x,y
;

(iii)

\st(x)\leqslant\st(y)
if
x\leqslant y
for finite
x,y
;

(iv)

\st(r)=r
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

For any finite
x
, the set
\{r\in\mathbb{R}:r\leqslant x\}
is non-empty and bounded above,
hence has a supremum
s:=\sup\{r\in\mathbb{R}:r\leqslant x\}
. We claim
x\approx s
. If

x-s>1/n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
s+1/(2n)<x
and
s+1/(2n)\in\mathbb{R}
,
contradicting the definition of
s
. Similarly, if
s-x>1/n
, then
s-1/(2n)>x

implies
s-1/(2n)
is an upper bound for
\{r\in\mathbb{R}:r\leqslant x\}
, contradicting the
supremum. Thus
|x-s|<1/n
for all
n
,
i.e.
,
x\approx s
.

Uniqueness: if
x\approx s
and
x\approx s^{\prime}
with
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
s-s^{\prime}
is
infinitesimal and real, hence zero.

The algebraic properties follow from the corresponding properties of
\approx
and the
field operations.
∎

3.3.
The hypernaturals and hyperfinite sets

The natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
extend to the
hypernaturals

{}^{*}\mathbb{N}\subset{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
. By
the transfer principle,
{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
satisfies all first-order properties of
\mathbb{N}
. However,

{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
properly contains
\mathbb{N}
: there exist
infinite hypernaturals

N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathbb{N}
satisfying
N>n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.9

(Hyperfinite set)
.

For
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
, the set

I_{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}=\{k\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:1\leqslant k\leqslant N\}

is called a
hyperfinite set
. It is
internal
in the sense that it arises
from the non-standard extension.

The crucial property of hyperfinite sets is that they behave like finite sets from the
internal perspective. In particular:

Proposition 3.10

(Internal finite operations)
.

Let
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
(possibly infinite) and let
f:I_{N}\to{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
be an internal function.
Then:

(i)

The hyperfinite sum
\sum_{k=1}^{N}f(k)\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
is well-defined.

(ii)

The hyperfinite product
\prod_{k=1}^{N}f(k)\in{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
is well-defined.

(iii)

The maximum
\max_{k\in I_{N}}f(k)
and minimum
\min_{k\in I_{N}}f(k)
are
well-defined.

(iv)

Sorting: if
f
takes values in
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
, then there exists an
internal
permutation

\sigma:I_{N}\to I_{N}
such that
f(\sigma(1))\leqslant f(\sigma(2))\leqslant\cdots\leqslant f(\sigma(N))
.

These properties follow from the transfer principle applied to the corresponding statements
about finite sets. In particular, for (iv) we transfer the statement “for every finite set

S
and function
g:S\to\mathbb{R}
there exists a permutation
\sigma
sorting
g
”; this yields
an
internal
permutation
\sigma
, which is essential for later applications to
order statistics.

3.4.
Internal and external sets

A fundamental distinction in NSA is between
internal
and
external
sets.
Internal sets arise from the non-standard extension and satisfy the transfer principle;
external sets do not.

Example 3.11
.

The set
I_{N}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
for
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
is internal. The set
\mathbb{N}
itself,
viewed as a subset of
{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
, is external when
{}^{*}\mathbb{N}\neq\mathbb{N}
.

A convenient way to see this without appealing to second-order quantification is the
following. In the standard universe, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
every non-empty subset of

\{1,\dots,m\}
has a maximum. This
is
a first-order statement in the superstructure.
By transfer, for every
M\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
every non-empty
internal
subset of
I_{M}
has a
maximum.

Now pick an infinite
H\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathbb{N}\subseteq I_{H}
. If
\mathbb{N}
were internal, it would be
an internal non-empty subset of
I_{H}
and hence would have a maximum, contradicting the
fact that
\mathbb{N}
has no largest element. Therefore
\mathbb{N}
is external.

Proposition 3.12

(Internal definition principle)
.

If
P(x)
is a first-order property and
A
is an internal set, then

\{x\in A:P(x)\}
is internal.

3.5.
Loeb measure: from hyperfinite to standard probability

The Loeb construction
[
15
]
is the key tool for producing genuine probability spaces
from internal hyperfinite structures. We describe it in the context most relevant to our
applications.

Fix an infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
and consider the hyperfinite set
I_{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Let
\mathscr{I}_{N}
denote the algebra of all internal subsets of
I_{N}
. Define the

internal counting probability measure

\mu_{N}:\mathscr{I}_{N}\to{}^{*}[0,1]
by

(8)

\mu_{N}(A):=\frac{|A|}{N},\qquad A\in\mathscr{I}_{N},

where
|A|\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
is the internal cardinality of
A
.

The triple
(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N},\mu_{N})
is an internal finitely additive probability
space. The Loeb construction promotes this to a genuine
\sigma
-additive probability
space.

Theorem 3.13

(Loeb measure construction)
.

Define the
Loeb premeasure

\mu_{N}^{0}:\mathscr{I}_{N}\to[0,1]
by

\mu_{N}^{0}(A):=\st(\mu_{N}(A))
for
A\in\mathscr{I}_{N}
.
Define an outer measure
L^{*}(\mu_{N})
on
all
subsets
B\subseteq I_{N}
by

L^{*}(\mu_{N})(B):=\inf\{\mu_{N}^{0}(A):\ A\in\mathscr{I}_{N},\ B\subseteq A\}.

Let
\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L}
be the
\sigma
-algebra of
L^{*}(\mu_{N})
-measurable sets in the
sense of Carathéodory, and define
L(\mu_{N}):=L^{*}(\mu_{N})\!\upharpoonright_{\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L}}
.
Then:

(i)

\mu_{N}^{0}
is a finitely additive probability measure on the internal algebra

\mathscr{I}_{N}
.

(ii)

\mathscr{I}_{N}\subseteq\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L}
, and
L(\mu_{N})
is a complete
\sigma
-additive
probability measure on
\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L}
extending
\mu_{N}^{0}
.

(iii)

In particular,
\sigma(\mathscr{I}_{N})\subseteq\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L}
, and the restriction
of
L(\mu_{N})
to
\sigma(\mathscr{I}_{N})
is a
\sigma
-additive extension of
\mu_{N}^{0}
.

Proof (reference).

The key step is verifying that the Carathéodory construction yields a complete

\sigma
-additive measure. The countable subadditivity of
\mu_{N}^{0}
on

\mathscr{I}_{N}
(which follows from the internal finite additivity and saturation)
is the crucial ingredient. For the full argument, see Loeb
[
15
]
or

[
3
, Ch. 5]
and
[
10
, Chs. 4–5]
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

The Loeb space is rich enough to support continuous distributions, despite being built
from a discrete internal structure. For instance, if
N
is infinite, the Loeb space

(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
is atomless.

3.6.
Hyperfinite integration and the Riemann sum approximation

The Loeb measure allows us to integrate functions on hyperfinite spaces. For our purposes,
the following connection between hyperfinite sums and standard integrals is fundamental.

Proposition 3.15

(Hyperfinite Riemann approximation)
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and Riemann integrable, and let
{}^{*}f:{}^{*}[0,1]\to{}^{*}\mathbb{R}

be its non-standard extension. For any infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
,

(9)

\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt=\st\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{}^{*}f\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)\right).

Proof.

By Riemann integrability, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\right|<\varepsilon.

By transfer, this statement holds for all
n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
with
n\geqslant n_{0}
. In particular,
it holds for the infinite
N
, giving

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{}^{*}f\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)\right|<\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the hyperfinite sum differs from the integral by
an infinitesimal, and taking standard parts yields (
9
).
∎

Proposition 3.16

(Loeb lifting for
L^{1}
-functions on the hyperfinite grid)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([0,1],\lambda)
, where
\lambda
is Lebesgue measure. Fix an infinite

N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
and write
I_{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
. Then there exists an internal function

F:I_{N}\to{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
such that:

(i)

f(\st(k/N))=\st(F(k))
for
L(\mu_{N})
-almost all
k\in I_{N}
;

(ii)

F
is
S
-integrable (equivalently, Loeb integrable) and

\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}F(k)\Big).

If, in addition,
f
is bounded and Riemann integrable, one may choose
F(k)={}^{*}f(k/N)
.

Proof (reference).

This is a standard lifting result in Loeb integration theory: every
L^{1}
function admits
an internal
S
-integrable lifting on the hyperfinite grid, and the Loeb integral equals
the standard part of the internal counting integral. See, for example,

[
10
, Chs. 4–5]
(Loeb liftings and
S
-integrability) or
[
3
, Ch. 5]
.
The last sentence follows from Proposition
3.15
.
∎

Remark 3.17

(A concrete
S
-integrability criterion)
.

An internal function
F:I_{N}\to{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
is called
S
-integrable
if

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|F(k)|
is finite and, for every internal
A\subseteq I_{N}
with

\mu_{N}(A)\approx 0
, one has
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in A}|F(k)|\approx 0
.
In that case
\st(F)
is Loeb integrable and its Loeb integral equals the standard part
of the internal counting integral.

3.7.
The hyperfinite strong law and Glivenko–Cantelli theorem

For statistical applications, we need the non-standard versions of classical limit theorems.

Theorem 3.18

(Hyperfinite strong law of large numbers)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. with
\mathsf{E}[|X_{1}|]<\infty
on
(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
.
Let
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
be infinite and consider the hyperfinite extension

(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
as random variables on
({}^{*}\Omega,L({}^{*}\mathscr{G}),L({}^{*}\mathsf{P}))
.
Then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}\approx\mathsf{E}[X_{1}]\qquad\text{holds $L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})$-almost surely.}

Proof.

By the classical strong law,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\to\mathsf{E}[X_{1}]
almost surely as

n\to\infty
. For each rational
\varepsilon>0
, define

A_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }\forall n\geqslant n_{0},\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\omega)-\mathsf{E}[X_{1}]\right|<\varepsilon\right\}.

This set is
\mathscr{G}
-measurable (it is a countable union/intersection of measurable events),
and the strong law gives
\mathsf{P}(A_{\varepsilon})=1
.

The non-standard extension
{}^{*}A_{\varepsilon}\subseteq{}^{*}\Omega
is internal (it is the star-extension
of a standard set), hence
{}^{*}A_{\varepsilon}\in{}^{*}\mathscr{G}\subseteq L({}^{*}\mathscr{G})
.
Moreover,

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})({}^{*}A_{\varepsilon})=\st({}^{*}\mathsf{P}({}^{*}A_{\varepsilon}))=\st(\mathsf{P}(A_{\varepsilon}))=1,

using the fact that
{}^{*}\mathsf{P}({}^{*}A_{\varepsilon})=\mathsf{P}(A_{\varepsilon})
by transfer of the probability.

For
\omega\in{}^{*}A_{\varepsilon}
, the internal statement
“
\exists n_{0}\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
with
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\omega)-\mathsf{E}[X_{1}]|<\varepsilon
”
holds (by transfer of the defining property of
A_{\varepsilon}
).
In particular, it holds for the infinite
N
, giving

|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}(\omega)-\mathsf{E}[X_{1}]|<\varepsilon
.

Since
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})({}^{*}A_{\varepsilon})=1
for each rational
\varepsilon>0
, intersecting over

\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
(a countable intersection of Loeb-measure-one sets) yields the claim

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost surely.
∎

Remark 3.19

(Two-level almost sure statements)
.

In hyperfinite sampling statements we use two measures simultaneously:

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
governs the randomness of the sample path
\omega\in{}^{*}\Omega
,
while
L(\mu_{N})
governs the fraction of indices
k\in I_{N}
for a fixed sample path.
Thus “for
L(\mu_{N})
-almost all
k
” should be read as: for
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost all
sample outcomes, the exceptional set of indices has Loeb counting measure zero.

Theorem 3.20

(Hyperfinite Glivenko–Cantelli / quantile shadow)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. with distribution function
F
and lower quantile function

q(\alpha)=\inf\{x:\,F(x)\geqslant\alpha\}
. Assume
\mathsf{E}[|X_{1}|]<\infty
, equivalently

\int_{0}^{1}|q(\alpha)|\,d\alpha<\infty
.

Fix an infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
and consider the hyperfinite sample
(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
with order
statistics
X_{1:N}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant X_{N:N}
. Let
\alpha_{k}:=k/N
.

Then
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost surely, the set of indices

G:=\{k\in I_{N}:\ X_{k:N}\approx q(\alpha_{k})\}

has Loeb counting measure
L(\mu_{N})(G)=1
.

Moreover, for every bounded
Riemann integrable

g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
with

\int_{0}^{1}|g(\alpha)q(\alpha)|\,d\alpha<\infty
, we have

(10)

\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}g(\alpha_{k})X_{k:N}\Big)=\int_{0}^{1}g(\alpha)q(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

Internal infinitesimal bound via convergence in probability.

The classical Glivenko–Cantelli theorem states that
\sup_{x}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|\to 0
almost surely.
The following weaker consequence suffices: for every
\eta>0
,

\mathsf{P}\Big(\sup_{x}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|>\eta\Big)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

This is a first-order statement: for every standard
\eta>0
and
\delta>0
, there exists

n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,
\mathsf{P}(\sup_{x}|F_{n}-F|>\eta)<\delta
.

By transfer, for our fixed infinite
N
and any standard
\eta,\delta>0
,

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}\Big(\sup_{x}|F_{N}(x)-F(x)|>\eta\Big)<\delta.

Taking Loeb measures (standard parts),
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\sup_{x}|F_{N}-F|>\eta)\leqslant\delta
. Since

\delta>0
was arbitrary,
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\sup_{x}|F_{N}-F|>\eta)=0
.

Intersecting over a countable sequence
\eta=1/m
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})\Big(\sup_{x}|F_{N}(x)-F(x)|\text{ is infinitesimal}\Big)=1.

Fix a sample path in this Loeb-probability-one event, and let
\varepsilon_{0}:=\sup_{x}|F_{N}(x)-F(x)|
.
Then
\varepsilon_{0}\approx 0
is a positive infinitesimal. Moreover,
\varepsilon_{0}
is
internal

(it is the internal supremum of an internal function), so we may use it in transferred statements.

The standard quantile bracketing implication: for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\alpha\wedge(1-\alpha))
,

(11)

\sup_{x}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(\alpha-\varepsilon)\leqslant q_{n}(\alpha)\leqslant q(\alpha+\varepsilon).

This can be expressed using only bounded quantification over
\mathbb{R}
and the defining formula for
the lower quantile, so it is a first-order statement in
\alpha
,
\varepsilon
, and
F_{n}
.
By transfer, (
11
) holds for all hyperreal
\alpha\in{}^{*}(0,1)
and hyperreal

\varepsilon\in{}^{*}(0,\alpha\wedge(1-\alpha))
with
\|F_{N}-F\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon
.

Since
\|F_{N}-F\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
is infinitesimal,
for any hyperreal
\alpha\in{}^{*}(0,1)
with
\alpha>\varepsilon_{0}
and
\alpha<1-\varepsilon_{0}
,

q(\alpha-\varepsilon_{0})\leqslant q_{N}(\alpha)\leqslant q(\alpha+\varepsilon_{0}).

In particular,
q_{N}(\alpha_{k})\leqslant q(\alpha_{k}+\varepsilon_{0})
and
q_{N}(\alpha_{k})\geqslant q(\alpha_{k}-\varepsilon_{0})
.

Halos of discontinuities and boundary have Loeb measure zero.

Since
q
is monotone, its set of discontinuity points
\mathrm{Disc}(q)\subseteq(0,1)
is at most countable.
For each standard
\alpha_{0}\in\mathrm{Disc}(q)\cup\{0,1\}
, define the
halo

H_{\alpha_{0}}:=\{k\in I_{N}:\ \alpha_{k}\approx\alpha_{0}\}=\{k\in I_{N}:\ |\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{0}|\ \text{is infinitesimal}\}.

We claim
L(\mu_{N})(H_{\alpha_{0}})=0
. For any standard
\delta>0
,

H_{\alpha_{0}}\subseteq\{k\in I_{N}:\ |\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{0}|<\delta\},

and the right-hand side is internal with

\mu_{N}(\{k:|\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{0}|<\delta\})\leqslant 2\delta.

Taking standard parts,
L(\mu_{N})(H_{\alpha_{0}})\leqslant 2\delta
. Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary,

L(\mu_{N})(H_{\alpha_{0}})=0
. (Note:
H_{\alpha_{0}}
has outer Loeb measure zero, hence is Loeb measurable.)

Define

D:=\bigcup_{\alpha_{0}\in\mathrm{Disc}(q)\cup\{0,1\}}H_{\alpha_{0}}.

Since
\mathrm{Disc}(q)\cup\{0,1\}
is countable and each
H_{\alpha_{0}}
has Loeb measure
0
,
and
L(\mu_{N})
is countably additive,
L(\mu_{N})(D)=0
.

The quantile shadow on
G=I_{N}\setminus D
.

Take
k\notin D
, so
\alpha_{k}
is not in the halo of any discontinuity of
q
nor in the halo of

\{0,1\}
. This means
t:=\st(\alpha_{k})\in(0,1)\setminus\mathrm{Disc}(q)
, hence
q
is continuous at
t
.

Since
q
is continuous at
t
and
\alpha_{k}\approx t
, both
\alpha_{k}-\varepsilon_{0}\approx t

and
\alpha_{k}+\varepsilon_{0}\approx t
(because
\varepsilon_{0}
is infinitesimal). Therefore

q(\alpha_{k}-\varepsilon_{0})\approx q(t)\approx q(\alpha_{k}+\varepsilon_{0}).

The transferred bracketing gives

q(\alpha_{k}-\varepsilon_{0})\leqslant q_{N}(\alpha_{k})=X_{k:N}\leqslant q(\alpha_{k}+\varepsilon_{0}),

hence
X_{k:N}\approx q(t)=q(\st(\alpha_{k}))\approx q(\alpha_{k})
(using continuity again).
Thus
k\in G
, and we conclude
G\supseteq I_{N}\setminus D
, hence
L(\mu_{N})(G)=1
.

Convention for the integral identity.

We interpret integrals involving internal functions (such as
q_{N}
and
g_{N}
) as Loeb integrals
on the hyperfinite grid
\{\alpha_{k}:k\in I_{N}\}
, so that

\int_{0}^{1}H(\alpha)\,d\alpha=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}H(\alpha_{k})\Big)

whenever
H
is
S
-integrable on the grid (cf. Remark
3.17

and Lemma
4.1
).

The integral identity via truncation.

Define the step function
g_{N}(\alpha):=g(\alpha_{k})
on
((k-1)/N,k/N]
. Then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}g(\alpha_{k})X_{k:N}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{N}(\alpha)\,q_{N}(\alpha)\,d\alpha

because
q_{N}(\alpha)=X_{k:N}
on
((k-1)/N,k/N]
.

We prove
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q|\,d\alpha\approx 0
directly by truncation (not as a consequence of
the quantile shadow, which only gives pointwise information on a full-Loeb set).
For
M>0
, define
q^{(M)}:=\max(\min(q,M),-M)
and
q_{N}^{(M)}:=\max(\min(q_{N},M),-M)
.
Then

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q|\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}^{(M)}-q^{(M)}|+\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q_{N}^{(M)}|+\int_{0}^{1}|q-q^{(M)}|.

For the first term:
|q_{N}^{(M)}-q^{(M)}|\leqslant 2M
pointwise, and on the full-Loeb set
G
,

q_{N}^{(M)}(\alpha_{k})\approx q^{(M)}(\alpha_{k})
. By the Loeb dominated convergence theorem
(see
[
3
, Thm. 4.3.6]
: for internal functions dominated by an
S
-integrable bound,
pointwise near-equality on a Loeb-measure-one set implies the integrals are infinitely close),

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}^{(M)}-q^{(M)}|\approx 0
for each fixed standard
M
.

For the second term:
|q_{N}-q_{N}^{(M)}|=|q_{N}|\mathbf{1}_{|q_{N}|>M}
. Since
\mathsf{E}|X_{1}|<\infty
, the
hyperfinite SLLN gives
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|X_{k}|\approx\mathsf{E}|X_{1}|<\infty
. By the standard
integration identity for order statistics,
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}|\,d\alpha=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|X_{k:N}|
,
which has finite standard part. Thus
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q_{N}^{(M)}|\to 0
as
M\to\infty
(in the
sense that for any standard
\delta>0
, there exists standard
M
such that this integral
<\delta
).

For the third term:
\int_{0}^{1}|q-q^{(M)}|\to 0
as
M\to\infty
by
\mathsf{E}|X_{1}|<\infty
.

Given
\delta>0
, choose
M
large enough that the second and third terms are each
<\delta/3
.
Then choose the infinitesimal such that the first term is infinitesimal. Taking standard parts,

\st(\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q|)\leqslant 2\delta/3<\delta
. Since
\delta
was arbitrary,
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q|\approx 0
.

Now we can complete the proof of (
10
):

\int_{0}^{1}g_{N}q_{N}-\int_{0}^{1}gq=\int_{0}^{1}g_{N}(q_{N}-q)+\int_{0}^{1}(g_{N}-g)q.

The first term is bounded by
\|g\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{N}-q|\approx 0
.
For the second term, fix

and split

\Big|\int_{0}^{1}(g_{N}-g)q\Big|\leqslant\|q^{(M)}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|g_{N}-g|+2\|g\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|q-q^{(M)}|.

Since

is Riemann integrable,
\int_{0}^{1}|g_{N}-g|\approx 0
(by the hyperfinite Riemann sum

and use

. Taking standard parts
yields (
10
).
∎

3.8.
Overspill and underspill

Two technical principles are frequently used in NSA arguments. We assume throughout that
our non-standard universe is
countably saturated
: every countable collection of
internal sets with the finite intersection property has non-empty intersection.

Proposition 3.21

(Overspill)
.

Let
A\subseteq{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
be internal. If
A
contains all standard natural numbers, then

A
contains some infinite hypernatural.

Proof.

For each standard
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define the internal set

A_{m}:=\{n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:\ n\in A\ \wedge\ n>m\}.

Each
A_{m}
is internal by the internal definition principle (intersection of
A
with
the internal set
\{n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:n>m\}
). Each
A_{m}
is non-empty since
m+1\in A

(as
A
contains all standard naturals) and
m+1>m
.

The collection
\{A_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\}
is countable and has the finite intersection property:
for any
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
A_{m_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{m_{k}}\supseteq A_{\max(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})}\neq\emptyset
.

By countable saturation,
\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}\neq\emptyset
. Pick
H
in this intersection.
Then
H\in A
and
H>m
for every standard
m\in\mathbb{N}
, hence
H
is infinite.
∎

Proposition 3.22

(Underspill / underflow)
.

Let
A\subseteq{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
be internal. If
A
contains every infinite hypernatural, then

A
contains some standard natural number.

Equivalently: if
A\cap\mathbb{N}=\emptyset
, then there exists an infinite
H\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
such that

A\subseteq\{H+1,H+2,\dots\}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
A
contains every infinite hypernatural and set
B:={}^{*}\mathbb{N}\setminus A
.
Then
B
is internal. If
B=\emptyset
, then
A={}^{*}\mathbb{N}
and in particular
A
contains every standard natural,
so the conclusion holds. Hence we may assume
B\neq\emptyset
.

By assumption,
B
contains no infinite hypernaturals, hence every
element of
B
is finite and therefore standard; thus
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.

Fix an infinite
H\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
. Then
B\subseteq I_{H}=\{1,\dots,H\}
. Since
B
is a non-empty
internal subset of the hyperfinite set
I_{H}
, transfer of the finite-maximum principle
implies that
B
has a maximum element
m=\max B
. This
m
cannot be infinite (because
B

contains no infinite hypernaturals), hence
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore
B\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
,
so
B
is finite. Consequently
A
contains all standard naturals
>m
, and in particular

A
contains some standard natural number.

For the equivalent formulation, assume
A\cap\mathbb{N}=\emptyset
, so
\mathbb{N}\subseteq{}^{*}\mathbb{N}\setminus A
.
Consider the internal set

C:=\{m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:\ \{1,\dots,m\}\subseteq{}^{*}\mathbb{N}\setminus A\}.

Then
C
contains every standard
m\in\mathbb{N}
, hence by overspill
C
contains an infinite
H
.
Thus
\{1,\dots,H\}\subseteq{}^{*}\mathbb{N}\setminus A
, which is equivalent to

A\subseteq\{H+1,H+2,\dots\}
.
∎

4.
Hyperfinite representation of coherent risk measures

We now develop the hyperfinite representation of coherent risk measures, which forms the
foundation for our unified treatment of CRMs and CREs.

4.1.
The hyperfinite dictionary

Working on the standing Loeb model
(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
from
Notation
3.2
, we may regard elements of
L^{\infty}
as essentially bounded
Loeb-measurable functions on
I_{N}
. The Loeb integration theory provides a canonical
internal representation of such functions.

Lemma 4.1

(Internal liftings and Loeb expectation)
.

Let
X:I_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be Loeb measurable and essentially bounded. Then there exists a bounded
internal function
\tilde{X}:I_{N}\to{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
such that
X=\st(\tilde{X})

L(\mu_{N})
-almost surely, and

\int_{I_{N}}X\,dL(\mu_{N})=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{X}(k)\Big).

If
Y
is Loeb integrable and
\tilde{Y}
is an
S
-integrable internal lifting, then

\int_{I_{N}}Y\,dL(\mu_{N})=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{Y}(k)\Big)

as well.

Proof (reference).

See
[
10
, Chs. 4–5]
for liftings of Loeb-measurable functions and the identification
of Loeb integrals with standard parts of internal counting integrals.
∎

The following lemma ensures that uniform integrability passes to the non-standard extension,
which is crucial for making our hyperfinite representation
internal
.

Lemma 4.2

(Uniform integrability implies
S
-integrability of
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}\subset L^{1}_{+}
be uniformly integrable with
\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathsf{E}[Z]\leqslant C<\infty
.
Then for every
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
, the internal function
Z:I_{N}\to{}^{*}[0,\infty)
is
S
-integrable
on the Loeb space
(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
, with

\int Z\,dL(\mu_{N})=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z(k)\Big).

In particular, if
\mathsf{E}[Z]=1
for all
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
, then
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z(k)\approx 1

for all
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
.

Moreover, if
X\in L^{\infty}
has a bounded internal lifting
\tilde{X}
with
|\tilde{X}|\leqslant M
,
then for any
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
the product
\tilde{X}\cdot Z
is
S
-integrable and

\mathsf{E}[-XZ]=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z(k)\Big).

Proof.

Uniform integrability of
\mathcal{Z}
means: for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
K>0
such that

\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathsf{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{\{Z>K\}}]<\varepsilon
. This is a first-order statement:

\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\ \exists K\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\ \forall Z\in\mathcal{Z}:\quad\mathsf{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{\{Z>K\}}]<\varepsilon.

By transfer, for every standard
\varepsilon>0
there exists standard
K>0
such that

{}^{*}\mathsf{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{\{Z>K\}}]<\varepsilon
for all
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
.

In terms of the hyperfinite counting measure, this says

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k:Z(k)>K}Z(k)<\varepsilon

for all
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
. This is exactly the criterion for
S
-integrability: the “tail”
of the internal sum is uniformly small for large standard
K
. By the standard characterisation
of
S
-integrability (see
[
3
, Prop. 4.3.5]
), each
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is
S
-integrable.

The Loeb integral identity follows from the definition of
S
-integrability: if
Z
is
S
-integrable,
then
\int Z\,dL(\mu_{N})=\st(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z(k))
.

For the constraint
\mathsf{E}[Z]=1
, transfer gives
\int Z\,dL(\mu_{N})=1
for all
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
.
By the Loeb integral identity,
\st(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z(k))=1
, hence

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z(k)\approx 1
for all
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
.

For the product: since
|\tilde{X}|\leqslant M
and
Z\geqslant 0
is
S
-integrable,
|\tilde{X}\cdot Z|\leqslant M\cdot Z

is dominated by an
S
-integrable function, hence
\tilde{X}\cdot Z
is
S
-integrable.
∎

4.2.
Hyperfinite robust representation

We can now state and prove the hyperfinite version of the robust representation theorem.
The key observation is that standard attainment on a compact set immediately gives us an
internal maximiser via the non-standard extension of the standard maximiser. By
Lemma
4.2
, elements of
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
are automatically
S
-integrable,
so we can work directly with
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
as an
internal
set.

Theorem 4.3

(Hyperfinite robust representation on
L^{\infty}
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
be atomless and separable, and let
\rho:L^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
coherent risk measure with the Fatou property. Assume it admits a robust representation of the form

\rho(X)=\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathsf{E}[-XZ],\qquad\mathcal{Z}\subset L^{1}_{+},\ \mathsf{E}[Z]=1,

where
\mathcal{Z}
is compact in
\sigma(L^{1},L^{\infty})
(equivalently, uniformly integrable and

\sigma(L^{1},L^{\infty})
-closed).

We may (and do) identify the underlying atomless probability space with the standing Loeb model

(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
(Notation
3.2
), and let
\tilde{X}
be a bounded
internal lifting of
X
as in Lemma
4.1
. Then

\rho(X)=\st\Big(\sup_{Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z(k)\Big),\qquad X\in L^{\infty},

where
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is the internal non-standard extension of
\mathcal{Z}
, and elements

Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
are
S
-integrable by Lemma
4.2
.
The supremum over
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is
internal
, and is attained by some
Z^{\sharp}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
.

Equivalently, defining weight vectors
a=\Psi(Z)
via the normalisation

\Psi(Z)_{k}:=Z(k)/\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z(j),

we have
\sum_{k}a_{k}=1
exactly and

\rho(X)=\st\Big(\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\,(-\tilde{X}(k))\Big),

where
\mathcal{A}_{N}:=\{\Psi(Z):Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}\}
is
internal
(as the image of
an internal set under an internal map). For the maximiser
Z^{\sharp}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
,

\Psi(Z^{\sharp})
achieves a value infinitely close to the supremum over
\mathcal{A}_{N}
.

Proof.

Internality of
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
.

Fix once and for all a measurable representative for each element of
\mathcal{Z}\subset L^{1}_{+}

(possible since
\mathcal{Z}
is a set, not a quotient).
With this choice,
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
may be viewed as an internal family of internal functions
I_{N}\to{}^{*}[0,\infty)
.

By Lemma
4.2
,
since
\mathcal{Z}
is uniformly integrable, every
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is automatically
S
-integrable.
Crucially,
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is an
internal
set (as the non-standard extension of a standard set),
so the supremum
\sup_{Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}}(\cdots)
is internal.

Fix
X\in L^{\infty}
and define the functional

f_{X}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
by
f_{X}(Z):=\mathsf{E}[-XZ]
. This is continuous in the
\sigma(L^{1},L^{\infty})
topology
on
\mathcal{Z}
(by definition of that topology). Since
\mathcal{Z}
is

\sigma(L^{1},L^{\infty})
-compact, there exists a standard maximiser
Z^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that

f_{X}(Z^{*})=\rho(X)
.

The non-standard extension
{}^{*}Z^{*}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
(viewing
Z^{*}
as an element of the
standard universe) satisfies
{}^{*}f_{X}({}^{*}Z^{*})={}^{*}\rho(X)=\rho(X)
by transfer. Set
Z^{\sharp}:={}^{*}Z^{*}
.
Alternatively, one may transfer the existence statement directly: the first-order statement
“
\exists Z\in\mathcal{Z}\ (f_{X}(Z)=\rho(X))
” holds, hence by transfer there exists

Z^{\sharp}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
such that
{}^{*}f_{X}(Z^{\sharp})=\rho(X)
.

We may (and do) identify the underlying atomless probability space with the standing Loeb model

(I_{N},\mathscr{I}_{N}^{L},L(\mu_{N}))
, and let
\tilde{X}:I_{N}\to{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
be a bounded internal
lifting of
X
as in Lemma
4.1
. By Lemma
4.2
, since
Z^{\sharp}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}

is
S
-integrable and
\tilde{X}
is bounded, the product
(-\tilde{X})\cdot Z^{\sharp}
is
S
-integrable.
Applying Lemma
4.2
yields

(12)

\rho(X)={}^{*}f_{X}(Z^{\sharp})=\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z^{\sharp}(k)\Big).

Moreover, by transfer of “
f_{X}(Z)\leqslant\rho(X)
for all
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
”, we have

{}^{*}f_{X}(Z)\leqslant\rho(X)
for all
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
. Since the supremum over the internal
set
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is internal, we obtain

\rho(X)=\st\Big(\sup_{Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z(k)\Big).

For the normalised weight formulation, define

H(Z):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z(j),\qquad\Psi(Z)_{k}:=\frac{Z(k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z(j)},\quad k=1,\dots,N.

Then
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi(Z)_{k}=1
exactly (this is an algebraic identity).

For
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
, by Lemma
4.2
we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z(k)\approx 1
, i.e.,
H(Z)\approx 1
.
Since
Z\geqslant 0
and
H(Z)\approx 1>0
,
\Psi(Z)
is well-defined and internal.
The set
\mathcal{A}_{N}:=\{\Psi(Z):Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}\}
is internal (as the image of
an internal set under an internal map).

The connection between the two formulations is:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z(k)=H(Z)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi(Z)_{k}\,(-\tilde{X}(k)).

Since
|\tilde{X}|\leqslant M
for some standard
M
, the sum
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi(Z)_{k}(-\tilde{X}(k))

is bounded (in absolute value by
M
, since
\sum_{k}\Psi(Z)_{k}=1
). Since

,

\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z(k)\Big)=\st\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi(Z)_{k}\,(-\tilde{X}(k))\Big).

The weight-vector supremum satisfies

\st\Big(\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\,(-\tilde{X}(k))\Big)=\st\Big(\sup_{Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))\,Z(k)\Big).

Indeed, for

we have

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi(Z)(k)(-\tilde{X}(k))=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z(j)}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))Z(k)=\frac{1}{H(Z)}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))Z(k).

Since
{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
is internal and
H(Z)\approx 1
for every
Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
, the internal set

\{|1/H(Z)-1|:Z\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}\}
consists only of infinitesimals, hence its supremum is infinitesimal.
Moreover,

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-\tilde{X}(k))Z(k)\right|\leqslant\|\tilde{X}\|_{\infty}\,H(Z),

so taking internal suprema preserves the standard part. Therefore the two internal suprema have the same standard part.

The attainment statement follows from transfer: the standard maximiser
Z^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}

extends to
Z^{\sharp}={}^{*}Z^{*}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
, and
a^{\sharp}:=\Psi(Z^{\sharp})
achieves
a value infinitely close to the supremum over
\mathcal{A}_{N}
.
∎

4.3.
From hyperfinite representation to CRE representation

The finite-sample representation theorems for CREs now emerge as special cases.

Hyperfinite proof of Theorem
2.9
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the finite probability space

(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mathscr{G}},\hat{\mathsf{P}})
with
\hat{\Omega}=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
,

\hat{\mathscr{G}}=2^{\hat{\Omega}}
, and
\hat{\mathsf{P}}(\{\omega_{i}\})=1/n
.

A CRE
\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
induces a CRM
\rho
on this finite space by

\rho(X):=\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)
where
x_{i}=X(\omega_{i})
. The axioms (E1)–(E4) translate
directly to (R1)–(R4).

By finite-dimensional convex duality (the finite space version of the robust representation,
which does not require atomlessness), there exists a convex set
\mathcal{Q}
of probability measures
on
\hat{\Omega}
such that

\rho(X)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{E}_{Q}[-X].

Each
Q
on
\hat{\Omega}
corresponds to a weight vector
a\in\Delta_{n}
via

a_{i}=Q(\{\omega_{i}\})
, in which case
\mathsf{E}_{Q}[-X]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i})=\langle a,-x\rangle
. Setting
M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}:=\{a(Q):Q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
, we obtain

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\langle a,-x\rangle.

Convexity of
M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
follows from convexity of
\mathcal{Q}
, and attainment
follows from compactness of the simplex
\Delta_{n}
.
∎

For the law-invariant representation, we need the rearrangement inequality.

Lemma 4.6

(Rearrangement inequality
[
12
, Theorem 368]
)
.

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with decreasing rearrangements
u^{\downarrow}
and
v^{\downarrow}
. Then
for any permutation
\sigma
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}v_{\sigma(i)}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\downarrow}_{i}v^{\downarrow}_{i},

with equality when
u
and
v
are both sorted in the same order (both increasing or both decreasing).

We may now prove Theorem
2.10
.

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

By Theorem
2.9
there exists a non-empty convex
M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}\subseteq\Delta_{n}

such that
\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\langle a,-x\rangle
.

Define the
symmetrised
set

\widetilde{M}:=\operatorname{conv}\bigl\{\pi(a):\ a\in M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}},\ \pi\ \text{a permutation of }\{1,\dots,n\}\bigr\}\subseteq\Delta_{n},

where
\pi(a)
denotes the permuted vector
(a_{\pi(1)},\dots,a_{\pi(n)})
.
Then
\widetilde{M}
is convex and non-empty. Moreover, law invariance implies that for every
x
,

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\hat{\rho}_{n}(\pi(x))=\sup_{a\in M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\langle a,-\pi(x)\rangle=\sup_{a\in M^{*}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\langle\pi^{-1}(a),-x\rangle,

and taking the supremum over all permutations shows

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{b\in\widetilde{M}}\langle b,-x\rangle.

Now for each
b\in\Delta_{n}
let
b^{\downarrow}\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
be its decreasing rearrangement.
Note that
-s(x)
is the
decreasing
rearrangement of
-x
(since
s(x)=(x_{1:n},\ldots,x_{n:n})

is increasing). By the rearrangement inequality (Lemma
4.6
) applied to
u=b
and
v=-x
,

\langle b,-x\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(-x_{i})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}b^{\downarrow}_{i}(-x)^{\downarrow}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b^{\downarrow}_{i}(-x_{i:n})=\langle b^{\downarrow},-s(x)\rangle.

Hence

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{b\in\widetilde{M}}\langle b,-x\rangle\leqslant\sup_{b\in\widetilde{M}}\langle b^{\downarrow},-s(x)\rangle.

Conversely, for any
b\in\widetilde{M}
, since
\widetilde{M}
is permutation invariant, we have

b^{\downarrow}\in\widetilde{M}
. Moreover,
s(x)
is a permutation of
x
, so there exists a
permutation
\sigma
with
s(x)=\sigma(x)
. Then

\langle b^{\downarrow},-s(x)\rangle=\langle\sigma^{-1}(b^{\downarrow}),-x\rangle\leqslant\sup_{b^{\prime}\in\widetilde{M}}\langle b^{\prime},-x\rangle=\hat{\rho}_{n}(x),

where we used
\sigma^{-1}(b^{\downarrow})\in\widetilde{M}
by permutation invariance.
Hence
\sup_{b\in\widetilde{M}}\langle b^{\downarrow},-s(x)\rangle\leqslant\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)
, establishing equality.

Therefore, with

M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}:=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}\{b^{\downarrow}:\ b\in\widetilde{M}\}\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow},

we obtain

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n}).

Attainment follows because
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
is a closed subset of the compact simplex
\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
,
and the objective is continuous and linear in
a
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.11
(finite Choquet / distortion representation).

Let
\hat{\rho}_{n}
be coherent, law-invariant, and comonotonic additive. By Theorem
2.10

there exists a non-empty convex set
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
such that

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n}).

A standard representation theorem for comonotonic additive, monotone functionals on a finite
lattice states that such a functional is a Choquet integral with respect to a unique capacity
(called the
core
of the functional); see,
e.g.
,
[
9
, Ch. 5]
. In the present finite-dimensional setting, one may
take the capacity
c:2^{\{1,\dots,n\}}\to[0,1]
defined by

c(A):=\hat{\rho}_{n}(-\mathbf{1}_{A}),\qquad A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\},

where
\mathbf{1}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the indicator vector of
A
.

Law invariance implies that
c(A)
depends only on
|A|
, hence
c
is determined by a unique
distortion function on the grid
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
:

g(k):=c(\{1,2,\dots,k\})=\hat{\rho}_{n}(-\mathbf{1}_{\{1,\dots,k\}}),\qquad k=0,\dots,n,

with
g(0)=0
and
g(n)=1
. Coherence (in particular subadditivity) is equivalent in this finite
setting to discrete concavity of
g
(equivalently, to
\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
-monotonicity of the
resulting weights).

By the finite Choquet integral formula (see,
e.g.
,
[
9
, Ch. 5]
or the risk-measure
presentation in
[
11
]
), for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(g(i)-g(i-1)\bigr)\,(-x_{i:n}).

Setting
a_{i}:=g(i)-g(i-1)
yields
a\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
. Uniqueness follows because the values

\hat{\rho}_{n}(-\mathbf{1}_{\{1,\dots,k\}})=g(k)
recover the increments
a_{i}=g(i)-g(i-1)
, hence the weight
vector is uniquely determined.

Consequently
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
must be the singleton
\{a\}
, proving the theorem.
∎

5.
Discrete Kusuoka representation for law-invariant CREs

The Kusuoka representation (Theorem
2.5
) expresses law-invariant CRMs as
suprema over mixtures of expected shortfall. In this section, we establish the
finite-sample analogue for CREs.

5.1.
Discrete expected shortfall

We begin by defining the natural finite-sample version of expected shortfall.

Definition 5.1

(Discrete expected shortfall)
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the
discrete expected shortfall
at
level
k/n
is

(13)

\dES_{k/n}(x):=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i:n}.

This is the average of the
k
smallest (most negative) outcomes, negated to give a
positive quantity for losses. When
k=1
, we recover the minimum:

\dES_{1/n}(x)=-x_{1:n}
. When
k=n
, we get the negative mean:

\dES_{1}(x)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
.

Remark 5.2
.

The discrete expected shortfall
\dES_{k/n}
is a coherent, law-invariant, comonotonic
risk estimator. Its weight vector is
a=(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k},0,\ldots,0)

with
k
entries equal to
1/k
, which lies in
\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
.

5.2.
Decomposition of L-estimators into discrete expected shortfall

The key technical result is that any L-estimator with non-increasing weights can be
written as a mixture of discrete expected shortfalls.

Lemma 5.3

(ES-decomposition lemma)
.

Let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
and set
a_{n+1}:=0
. Define

(14)

\mu_{k}:=k(a_{k}-a_{k+1}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,n.

Then:

(i)

\mu_{k}\geqslant 0
for all
k
;

(ii)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}=1
;

(iii)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(15)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x).

Proof.

(i) Since
a
is non-increasing,
a_{k}\geqslant a_{k+1}
, hence
\mu_{k}=k(a_{k}-a_{k+1})\geqslant 0
.

(ii) We compute:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k(a_{k}-a_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}ka_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}ka_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}ka_{k}-\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}(j-1)a_{j}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}ka_{k}-\sum_{j=2}^{n}(j-1)a_{j}-na_{n+1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}(k-(k-1))a_{k}-0

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}a_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}=1.

(iii) We expand the right-hand side:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i:n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{k}-a_{k+1})\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i:n}.

We exchange the order of summation. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the term
x_{i:n}

appears in the inner sum for all
k\geqslant i
. Thus:

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{k}-a_{k+1})\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i:n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i:n}\sum_{k=i}^{n}(a_{k}-a_{k+1})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i:n}(a_{i}-a_{n+1})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i:n}\cdot a_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n}).\qed

5.3.
The discrete Kusuoka representation theorem

Let
\mathcal{T}:\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}\to\Delta_{n}
denote the linear map
a\mapsto\mu
defined by
(
14
). The lemma shows that
\mathcal{T}
is well-defined and maps
non-increasing probability vectors to probability vectors.

Remark 5.4

(Inverse of
\mathcal{T}
)
.

Given
\mu\in\Delta_{n}
with
\mu_{k}=k(a_{k}-a_{k+1})
and
a_{n+1}=0
, one recovers

a_{i}=\sum_{k=i}^{n}\frac{\mu_{k}}{k},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Thus mixtures of
\dES_{k/n}
are in one-to-one correspondence with weight vectors in
\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
.

Theorem 5.5

(Discrete Kusuoka representation)
.

Let
\hat{\rho}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a law-invariant CRE. Then there exists a non-empty convex
set
\mathcal{M}_{n}\subseteq\Delta_{n}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(16)

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x),

with the supremum attained. Moreover, one may take
\mathcal{M}_{n}=\mathcal{T}(M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}})
,
where
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
is the representing set from Theorem
2.10
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.10
, there exists a convex set
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}\subseteq\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}

such that

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n}).

By Lemma
5.3
, for each
a\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(-x_{i:n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x),

where
\mu=\mathcal{T}(a)
. Thus

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sup_{a\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}(a)_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{T}(M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}})}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x).

Setting
\mathcal{M}_{n}:=\mathcal{T}(M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}})
, we obtain (
16
).

Convexity: since
\mathcal{T}
is linear,
\mathcal{T}(M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}})
is convex
whenever
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
is.

Attainment: the supremum in (
6
) is attained for each
x
at some

a^{*}\in M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}
. Then
\mu^{*}=\mathcal{T}(a^{*})
achieves the supremum in
(
16
).
∎

Corollary 5.6

(Comonotonic case)
.

A CRE
\hat{\rho}_{n}
is comonotonic and law-invariant if and only if there exists a unique

\mu\in\Delta_{n}
such that

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\,\dES_{k/n}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.11
,
M^{s}_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}=\{a\}
is a singleton, hence

\mathcal{M}_{n}=\{\mathcal{T}(a)\}
is a singleton.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

The discrete Kusuoka representation (
16
) is the finite-sample analogue
of the population-level Kusuoka representation (
3
). The probability
measure
\nu
on
(0,1]
is replaced by a probability vector
\mu\in\Delta_{n}
, and the
continuous expected shortfall
\ES_{\alpha}
is replaced by its discrete counterpart

\dES_{k/n}
. This representation makes explicit the sense in which CREs are
“statistical shadows” of CRMs.

6.
Spectral risk measures and hyperfinite L-statistics

We now turn to spectral risk measures and their finite-sample estimators, developing the
hyperfinite perspective that will underpin our consistency results.

6.1.
Spectral risk as a hyperfinite L-statistic

Let
\varphi
be a spectrum (Definition
2.6
) and
X\in L^{1}
a random
variable with lower quantile function
q_{X}
. The spectral risk measure is

\rho_{\varphi}(X)=-\int_{0}^{1}q_{X}(\alpha)\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Now consider a hyperfinite i.i.d. sample
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
with
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
infinite.
Let
X_{1:N}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant X_{N:N}
be the order statistics and
\alpha_{k}:=k/N
.
Define the
hyperfinite L-statistic

(17)

L_{N}^{\varphi}:=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varphi(\alpha_{k})\,X_{k:N}.

Proposition 6.1

(Hyperfinite spectral representation)
.

Let
X\in L^{1}
with quantile function
q_{X}
, and let
\varphi
be a bounded spectrum.
Then, for Loeb-almost all sample paths,

(18)

\rho_{\varphi}(X)=\st(L_{N}^{\varphi}).

Proof.

Apply Theorem
3.20
with
g=\varphi
. Since
\varphi
is bounded and (being
monotone) Riemann integrable, and
X\in L^{1}
implies
\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi(\alpha)q_{X}(\alpha)|\,d\alpha<\infty
,
we obtain

\st\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varphi(\alpha_{k})X_{k:N}\Big)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)q_{X}(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Multiplying by
-1
gives
\st(L_{N}^{\varphi})=\rho_{\varphi}(X)
.
∎

6.2.
Canonical spectral plug-in estimators

Given a spectrum
\varphi
and sample size
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the canonical finite-sample
estimator is constructed as follows.

Definition 6.2

(Canonical spectral plug-in estimator)
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

(19)

\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(x):=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}(\varphi)\,x_{i:n},

where the weights are

(20)

a_{i,n}(\varphi):=\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}\varphi(s)\,ds,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Proposition 6.3
.

The canonical spectral plug-in estimator
\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}
is a comonotonic,
law-invariant CRE. Moreover, its Kusuoka representation (Theorem
5.5
)
has
\mathcal{M}_{n}=\{\mu\}
a singleton with
\mu=\mathcal{T}(a_{n}(\varphi))
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is non-increasing, the weights
a_{i,n}(\varphi)
are non-increasing in

i
(as integrals of a non-increasing function over consecutive intervals). Also,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi=1
, so
a_{n}(\varphi)\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
.

By Theorem
2.11
,
\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}
is a comonotonic, law-invariant CRE.
The Kusuoka representation follows from Corollary
5.6
.
∎

6.3.
Step approximations and the spectral-estimator correspondence

For theoretical analysis, it is useful to associate with each weight vector

a_{n}\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
a step function
\varphi_{n}
on
[0,1]
.

Definition 6.4

(Associated step function)
.

For
a_{n}=(a_{1,n},\ldots,a_{n,n})\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
, define

(21)

\varphi_{n}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}na_{i,n}\,\mathbf{1}_{((i-1)/n,i/n]}(t),\qquad t\in(0,1].

When
a_{n}
arises from a spectrum
\varphi
via (
20
), we have

\varphi_{n}(t)=n\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}\varphi(s)ds\text{ for }t\in((i-1)/n,i/n],

which is the average of
\varphi
on that interval.

Lemma 6.5

(Integral correspondence)
.

For
a_{n}\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
and its associated step function
\varphi_{n}
,

(22)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}\,x_{i:n}=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{n}(\alpha)\,q_{n}(\alpha)\,d\alpha,

where
q_{n}(\alpha):=x_{\lceil n\alpha\rceil:n}
is the empirical quantile function.

Proof.

For
\alpha\in((i-1)/n,i/n]
, we have
\lceil n\alpha\rceil=i
, so

q_{n}(\alpha)=x_{i:n}
. Thus

\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{n}(\alpha)q_{n}(\alpha)d\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}na_{i,n}\cdot x_{i:n}\,d\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}na_{i,n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot x_{i:n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}x_{i:n}.\qed

7.
Spectral plug-in consistency

We now establish the consistency of spectral plug-in estimators, culminating in a
uniform consistency theorem over Lipschitz families of spectra.

7.1.
Pointwise consistency: analytic core

The following lemma isolates the key analytic condition for consistency.

Lemma 7.1

(Primitive convergence implies density convergence)
.

Let
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of step functions on
[0,1]
, each non-increasing,
non-negative, and satisfying
\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{n}=1
. Assume
\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
Let
\varphi
be a bounded spectrum. Define the primitives

\Phi_{n}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}(s)\,ds,\qquad\Phi(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s)\,ds.

If
\Phi_{n}(t)\to\Phi(t)
for all
t\in(0,1)
, then:

(i)

\varphi_{n}(t)\to\varphi(t)
for almost every
t\in(0,1)
;

(ii)

\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
in
L^{1}([0,1])
.

Proof.

(i) Fix
t\in(0,1)
and
h>0
sufficiently small. Since
\varphi_{n}
is non-increasing,
for any
s\in[t,t+h]
we have
\varphi_{n}(s)\leqslant\varphi_{n}(t)
, hence

\frac{\Phi_{n}(t+h)-\Phi_{n}(t)}{h}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\varphi_{n}(s)ds\leqslant\varphi_{n}(t).

Similarly, for
s\in[t-h,t]
,
\varphi_{n}(s)\geqslant\varphi_{n}(t)
, so

\varphi_{n}(t)\leqslant\frac{\Phi_{n}(t)-\Phi_{n}(t-h)}{h}.

Taking
\liminf
and
\limsup
as
n\to\infty
, and using
\Phi_{n}\to\Phi
pointwise:

\frac{\Phi(t+h)-\Phi(t)}{h}\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}(t)\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}(t)\leqslant\frac{\Phi(t)-\Phi(t-h)}{h}.

Now let
h\downarrow 0
. Since
\varphi
is non-increasing,
\Phi
is concave, hence
differentiable almost everywhere. At differentiability points of
\Phi
, both bounds
converge to
\Phi^{\prime}(t)=\varphi(t)
, giving
\varphi_{n}(t)\to\varphi(t)
.

(ii) By (i),
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
pointwise almost everywhere. The uniform bound

\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
allows dominated convergence:

\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|\to 0
.
∎

7.2.
Spectral L-estimator consistency theorem

Theorem 7.2

(Spectral L-estimator consistency: a distribution-free criterion)
.

Let
\varphi
be a bounded spectrum. Let
\hat{\rho}_{n}(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}x_{i:n}
with

a_{n}\in\Delta_{n}^{\downarrow}
, and let
\varphi_{n}
be the associated step function (
21
).
Assume
\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

For every
t\in(0,1)
,
\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}(s)\,ds\to\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s)\,ds
.

(ii)

For every
X\in L^{1}
and every i.i.d. sample
(X_{i})
with law
X
,

\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}\rho_{\varphi}(X).

Remark 7.3
.

The condition
\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
is automatic for the canonical discretisation

a_{i,n}=\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}\varphi
of a bounded spectrum
\varphi
: in this case

\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
since
\varphi_{n}
is a step-function approximation.
For general L-estimator weights
(a_{i,n})
not arising from a fixed spectrum, the uniform
bound is a genuine assumption that must be verified.

Proof.

Assume
(i)
. By Lemma
7.1
, the primitive convergence implies

\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
in
L^{1}([0,1])
. Let
q_{n}(\alpha):=X_{\lceil n\alpha\rceil:n}
be the
empirical quantile function and let
q_{X}
be the population lower quantile. By the
Glivenko–Cantelli theorem,
q_{n}(\alpha)\to q_{X}(\alpha)
almost surely at every continuity
point of
q_{X}
, hence for almost every
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Using Lemma
6.5
,

\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{n}(\alpha)\,q_{n}(\alpha)\,d\alpha,\qquad\rho_{\varphi}(X)=-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,q_{X}(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Therefore,

\left|\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\rho_{\varphi}(X)\right|\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|\,|q_{n}|\,d\alpha+\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi|\,|q_{n}-q_{X}|\,d\alpha.

We handle these two integrals separately.

Fix
M>0
. Since
|q_{n}|=|q_{n}|\mathbf{1}_{\{|q_{n}|\leqslant M\}}+|q_{n}|\mathbf{1}_{\{|q_{n}|>M\}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|\,|q_{n}|\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leqslant M\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|\,d\alpha+\bigl(\sup_{m}\|\varphi_{m}\|_{\infty}+\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\bigr)\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|q_{n}|>M\}}\,d\alpha.

The first term converges to
0
because
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
in
L^{1}
. Moreover,

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|q_{n}(\alpha)|>M\}}\,d\alpha=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{i}|>M\}},

since
q_{n}
is a step function taking the values
X_{i:n}
. By the strong law of large
numbers, the right-hand side converges almost surely to
\mathsf{E}[|X|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M\}}]
.
Letting
n\to\infty
and then
M\to\infty
(using
\mathsf{E}|X|<\infty
) shows

\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi_{n}-\varphi|\,|q_{n}|\,d\alpha\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

For the second term, note first that
q_{n}(\alpha)\to q_{X}(\alpha)
almost everywhere.
Define truncations
q_{n}^{(M)}:=\max(\min(q_{n},M),-M)
and
q_{X}^{(M)}:=\max(\min(q_{X},M),-M)
.
Then
|q_{n}^{(M)}-q_{X}^{(M)}|\leqslant 2M
and
q_{n}^{(M)}\to q_{X}^{(M)}
almost everywhere, so dominated
convergence yields
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}^{(M)}-q_{X}^{(M)}|\,d\alpha\to 0
almost surely for each fixed
M
.
Moreover,

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{n}^{(M)}|+\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}^{(M)}-q_{X}^{(M)}|+\int_{0}^{1}|q_{X}^{(M)}-q_{X}|.

The middle term tends to
0
almost surely for fixed
M
. The last term tends to
0
as

M\to\infty
because
q_{X}\in L^{1}(0,1)
when
X\in L^{1}
. Finally,

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|q_{n}(\alpha)|>M\}}\,d\alpha=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{i}|>M\}}\longrightarrow\mathsf{E}[|X|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M\}}]

almost surely, and the right-hand side vanishes as
M\to\infty
. Hence

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\,d\alpha\to 0
almost surely, and therefore

\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi(\alpha)|\,|q_{n}(\alpha)-q_{X}(\alpha)|\,d\alpha\leqslant\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Combining the two parts proves
(ii)
.

Conversely, assume
(ii)
. Fix
t\in(0,1)
and let
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
.
Set
X:=-\mathbf{1}_{\{U\leqslant t\}}
. Then
X
is bounded and

q_{X}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}-1,&\alpha\in(0,t],\\
0,&\alpha\in(t,1),\end{cases}\qquad\text{so}\qquad\rho_{\varphi}(X)=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

For an i.i.d. sample
X_{i}:=-\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leqslant t\}}
, let
m_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leqslant t\}}
and

t_{n}:=m_{n}/n
. The order statistics satisfy
X_{1:n}=\cdots=X_{m_{n}:n}=-1
and

X_{m_{n}+1:n}=\cdots=X_{n:n}=0
, hence

\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}X_{i:n}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}a_{i,n}=\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\varphi_{n}(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

By the strong law,
t_{n}\to t
almost surely, and the uniform bound
\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty

implies

\left|\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\varphi_{n}-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}\right|\leqslant\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}\,|t_{n}-t|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Using
(ii)
for this bounded
X
gives
\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\varphi_{n}\to\int_{0}^{t}\varphi
almost surely,
and the previous estimate therefore yields
\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}\to\int_{0}^{t}\varphi
,
i.e.

(i)
.
∎

7.3.
Uniform consistency over Lipschitz spectral classes

We now establish uniform consistency over families of spectra satisfying Lipschitz and
boundedness conditions.

Definition 7.4

(Lipschitz spectral class)
.

A family
\mathcal{V}
of spectra is a
Lipschitz spectral class
with constants

(C,L)
if:

(V1)

Each
\varphi\in\mathcal{V}
is non-increasing, bounded, and
\int_{0}^{1}\varphi=1
.

(V2)

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leqslant C
.

(V3)

Each
\varphi\in\mathcal{V}
is
L
-Lipschitz:
|\varphi(s)-\varphi(t)|\leqslant L|s-t|
.

Lemma 7.5

(Uniform discretisation bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a Lipschitz spectral class with constants
(C,L)
. For each

\varphi\in\mathcal{V}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
a_{n}(\varphi)
be the canonical weights
(
20
) and
\varphi_{n}
the associated step function. Then:

(i)

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\sup_{t\in(0,1]}|\varphi_{n}(t)-\varphi(t)|\leqslant L/n
.

(ii)

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}(\varphi)x_{i:n}-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)q_{n}(\alpha)d\alpha\right|\leqslant\frac{L}{n}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)|d\alpha.

Proof.

(i) For
t\in((i-1)/n,i/n]
, we have

\varphi_{n}(t)=n\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}\varphi(s)ds
, the average of
\varphi
on that
interval. For any
s\in((i-1)/n,i/n]
and Lipschitz
\varphi
,

|\varphi(s)-\varphi(t)|\leqslant L|s-t|\leqslant L/n.

Thus
|\varphi_{n}(t)-\varphi(t)|\leqslant L/n
, uniformly in
t
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{V}
.

(ii) By Lemma
6.5
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}(\varphi)x_{i:n}=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{n}(\alpha)q_{n}(\alpha)d\alpha
.
Thus

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n}(\varphi)x_{i:n}-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)q_{n}(\alpha)d\alpha\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}(\varphi_{n}-\varphi)q_{n}\,d\alpha\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\varphi_{n}-\varphi\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{L}{n}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}|.\qed

Theorem 7.6

(Uniform spectral plug-in consistency)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a Lipschitz spectral class with constants
(C,L)
. Let
X\in L^{1}

and
(X_{i})
be i.i.d. with law
X
. Then

(23)

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\rho_{\varphi}(X)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}0.

(The uniformity is over spectra
\varphi\in\mathcal{V}
for a fixed underlying law of
X
.)

Proof.

Let
q_{n}(\alpha):=X_{\lceil n\alpha\rceil:n}
be the empirical quantile function and let
q_{X}
be the
population lower quantile. For each
\varphi\in\mathcal{V}
, write
\varphi_{n}
for the step function
associated with the canonical weights (Definitions
6.2
and
6.4
).
By Lemma
6.5
,

\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{n}(\alpha)\,q_{n}(\alpha)\,d\alpha,\qquad\rho_{\varphi}(X)=-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,q_{X}(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\rho_{\varphi}(X)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}(\varphi_{n}-\varphi)\,q_{n}\,d\alpha\right|+\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\varphi\,(q_{n}-q_{X})\,d\alpha\right|.

For the first term, Lemma
7.5
(i) yields

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}(\varphi_{n}-\varphi)\,q_{n}\,d\alpha\right|\leqslant\frac{L}{n}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)|\,d\alpha=\frac{L}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|.

By the strong law,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\to\mathsf{E}|X|
almost surely, so this term tends to
0

almost surely.

For the second term, boundedness of the class gives

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\varphi\,(q_{n}-q_{X})\,d\alpha\right|\leqslant\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\leqslant C\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|.

It remains to show
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\,d\alpha\to 0
almost surely when
X\in L^{1}
.
By Glivenko–Cantelli,
q_{n}(\alpha)\to q_{X}(\alpha)
at continuity points of
q_{X}
, hence almost everywhere.
Fix
M>0
and define truncations
q_{n}^{(M)}
and
q_{X}^{(M)}
by clamping to
[-M,M]
.
Then
q_{n}^{(M)}\to q_{X}^{(M)}
almost everywhere and
|q_{n}^{(M)}-q_{X}^{(M)}|\leqslant 2M
, so dominated
convergence gives
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}^{(M)}-q_{X}^{(M)}|\,d\alpha\to 0
almost surely for fixed
M
.
Moreover,

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{n}^{(M)}|+\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}^{(M)}-q_{X}^{(M)}|+\int_{0}^{1}|q_{X}^{(M)}-q_{X}|.

The middle term vanishes as
n\to\infty
. Since
X\in L^{1}
, we have
q_{X}\in L^{1}(0,1)
, so

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{X}^{(M)}-q_{X}|\to 0
as
M\to\infty
. Finally,

\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|q_{n}(\alpha)|>M\}}\,d\alpha=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{i}|>M\}}\longrightarrow\mathsf{E}[|X|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M\}}]

almost surely by the strong law, and the limit tends to
0
as
M\to\infty
.
Thus
\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|\to 0
almost surely, and the second term tends to
0
almost surely as well.

Combining the two bounds proves (
23
).
∎

Remark 7.7
.

Theorem
7.6
requires spectra in the
Lipschitz
class
\mathcal{V}
.
This covers distortion risk measures and spectral measures with smooth weights, but does

not
cover the Expected Shortfall spectrum
\varphi_{\alpha}(u)=\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\alpha]}(u)
,
which is bounded but not Lipschitz (it has a jump discontinuity at
u=\alpha
).

For ES specifically, one can prove consistency by a direct argument exploiting the explicit
formula
\ES_{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\alpha}q(u)\,du
and Glivenko–Cantelli, but the

uniform
rate over
\alpha\in[\delta,1]
requires additional care; see
[
7
]

for sharp uniform results on ES estimation.

Corollary 7.8
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
7.6
, assume additionally that

\mathsf{E}[|X|^{2+\eta}]<\infty
for some
\eta>0
. Then

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\rho_{\varphi}(X)\right|=O_{\mathsf{P}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

If, in addition,
X
is almost surely bounded and the distribution function
F
is continuous, then

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\rho_{\varphi}(X)\right|=O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\log n}{n}}\right)\quad\text{almost surely.}

Proof.

From the proof of Theorem
7.6
we have

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{V}}|\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X)|\leqslant A_{n}+B_{n}
with

A_{n}\leqslant\frac{L}{n}\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)|\,d\alpha=\frac{L}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\quad\text{and}\quad B_{n}\leqslant C\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}(\alpha)-q_{X}(\alpha)|\,d\alpha=C\,W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu).

(The quantile formula
W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)=\int_{0}^{1}|q_{n}-q_{X}|
holds whenever both measures have
finite first moments—guaranteed here by
X\in L^{1}
from Theorem
7.6
;
see,
e.g.
,
[
20
, Theorem 2.18]
.)
Thus
A_{n}=O(n^{-1})
almost surely by the strong law.

For the stochastic term, the one-dimensional identity

W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|\,dx
(again
[
20
]
) converts
the quantile integral to a CDF integral.
For each fixed
x
, conditional on
F(x)
we have
F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Bin}(n,F(x))
, hence

\mathsf{E}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|\leqslant\sqrt{\Var(F_{n}(x))}=\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))/n}
.
By the Fubini theorem and Jensen’s inequality,

\mathsf{E}\,W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}\,dx.

The integral is finite under
\mathsf{E}|X|^{2+\eta}<\infty
. Indeed, for
x\geqslant 1
,

1-F(x)=\mathsf{P}(X>x)\leqslant\frac{\mathsf{E}|X|^{2+\eta}}{x^{2+\eta}}

by Markov’s inequality, and hence

\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}\leqslant\sqrt{1-F(x)}\leqslant\sqrt{\mathsf{E}|X|^{2+\eta}}\,x^{-(1+\eta/2)}.

Since
\eta>0
, the function
x\mapsto x^{-(1+\eta/2)}
is integrable on
[1,\infty)
.
A symmetric argument for
x\leqslant-1
(using
\mathsf{P}(X\leqslant x)=\mathsf{P}(-X\geqslant-x)
) yields integrability
on
(-\infty,-1]
, and integrability on
[-1,1]
is trivial because
\sqrt{F(1-F)}\leqslant 1/2
.
Therefore
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}\,dx<\infty
.

Hence
\mathsf{E}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)=O(n^{-1/2})
, and Markov’s inequality yields

W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)=O_{\mathsf{P}}(n^{-1/2})
. (This rate is standard in the 1D empirical Wasserstein
literature; see,
e.g.
,
[
5
]
for optimal moment conditions.)
Hence
B_{n}=O_{\mathsf{P}}(n^{-1/2})
, and the first claim follows.

If
X
is bounded and
F
is continuous, then the probability integral transform and the law of the
iterated logarithm for the uniform empirical process give

\sup_{x}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|=O(\sqrt{\log\log n/n})
almost surely; see,
e.g.
,
[
19
]
.
Since
X
is bounded,
W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F_{n}-F|\,dx
is bounded by

\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{supp}(X))\sup_{x}|F_{n}(x)-F(x)|
, giving the stated almost sure rate.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

From the hyperfinite viewpoint, Theorem
7.6
reflects the fact that
one can work
internally
with the entire class
\mathcal{V}
at once. The Lipschitz
condition provides a deterministic envelope bounding the discretisation error uniformly,
whilst the Loeb-measure quantile convergence handles the stochastic approximation
uniformly over all bounded
\varphi
.

8.
Kusuoka-type plug-in consistency

We now extend the consistency theory to general law-invariant coherent risk measures via
the Kusuoka representation.

8.1.
Setup and assumptions

Let
\rho:L^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
be a law-invariant CRM on an atomless space with Kusuoka
representation (
3
):

\rho(X)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{(0,1]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha).

We construct finite-sample estimators by discretising the Kusuoka integral. Let

(\alpha_{i,n})_{i=0}^{n}
be a grid with
0=\alpha_{0,n}<\alpha_{1,n}<\cdots<\alpha_{n,n}=1
.
For simplicity, take the uniform grid
\alpha_{i,n}=i/n
.

Suppose we have, for each
\alpha\in(0,1]
, an estimator

\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for
\ES_{\alpha}
. The
Kusuoka-type plug-in
estimator
is

(24)

\hat{\rho}_{n}(x):=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha_{i,n},n}(x)\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}]).

8.2.
Consistency theorem

Theorem 8.1

(Kusuoka plug-in consistency)
.

Let
\rho
be a law-invariant CRM with Kusuoka representation (
3
). Assume:

(K1)

(Tightness) For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta\in(0,1)

such that
\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\nu((0,\delta])\leqslant\varepsilon
.

(K2)

(Uniform ES estimation) For every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{\alpha\in[\delta,1]}\left|\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\ES_{\alpha}(X)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}0

for any bounded
X
and i.i.d. sample
(X_{i})
with law
X
.

(K3)

(Uniform envelope) There exists
C<\infty
such that

|\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}(x)|\leqslant C\|x\|_{\infty}
for all
\alpha,n,x
.

(K4)

(Grid refinement) The mesh
\max_{i}(\alpha_{i,n}-\alpha_{i-1,n})\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.

Then for any bounded
X
and i.i.d. sample
(X_{i})
,

(25)

\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}\rho(X).

Assumption (K1) excludes risk measures that put mass arbitrarily close to
\alpha=0
, which is
necessary because
\ES_{\alpha}(X)\to-\infty
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
for unbounded-below
X
,
and discretisation near
0
is unstable. A typical choice for
\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}
is
the discrete ES
\mathrm{dES}_{\lceil n\alpha\rceil/n}
from (
13
), for which (K2)–(K3)
hold; see Theorem
7.2
.

Proof.

Fix a bounded random variable
X
and an i.i.d. sample
(X_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
with law
X
.
For
\nu\in\mathcal{M}
set

I(\nu):=\int_{(0,1]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha),\qquad\hat{I}_{n}(\nu):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha_{i,n},n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}]).

Then
\rho(X)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}I(\nu)
and
\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\hat{I}_{n}(\nu)
.
Since
|\sup_{\nu}a(\nu)-\sup_{\nu}b(\nu)|\leqslant\sup_{\nu}|a(\nu)-b(\nu)|
, it suffices to prove that

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}|\hat{I}_{n}(\nu)-I(\nu)|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\nu((0,\delta])\leqslant\varepsilon
(assumption (K1)).
For each
\nu\in\mathcal{M}
, split

I(\nu)=\int_{(0,\delta]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha)+\int_{(\delta,1]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha)=:I_{0,\delta}(\nu)+I_{\delta,1}(\nu),

and split
\hat{I}_{n}(\nu)
analogously into the terms with
\alpha_{i,n}\leqslant\delta
and
\alpha_{i,n}>\delta
:

\hat{I}_{n}(\nu)=\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}\leqslant\delta}\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha_{i,n},n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}])\\
+\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha_{i,n},n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}])=:\hat{I}_{0,\delta,n}(\nu)+\hat{I}_{\delta,1,n}(\nu).

Because
X
is bounded we have
|\ES_{\alpha}(X)|\leqslant\|X\|_{\infty}
for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Therefore,

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}|I_{0,\delta}(\nu)|\leqslant\|X\|_{\infty}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\nu((0,\delta])\leqslant\|X\|_{\infty}\,\varepsilon.

Moreover, since
X
is bounded,
\|(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|X\|_{\infty}
almost surely, and
assumption (K3) yields
|\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})|\leqslant C\|X\|_{\infty}
for all
\alpha,n

on an almost sure event. Hence,

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}|\hat{I}_{0,\delta,n}(\nu)|\leqslant C\|X\|_{\infty}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\nu((0,\delta])\leqslant C\|X\|_{\infty}\,\varepsilon\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Combining these two bounds gives

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\bigl|\hat{I}_{0,\delta,n}(\nu)-I_{0,\delta}(\nu)\bigr|\leqslant(1+C)\|X\|_{\infty}\,\varepsilon\qquad\text{almost surely.}

It remains to control the contribution from
(\delta,1]
. Write

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{\delta,1,n}(\nu)-I_{\delta,1}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\Big(\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha_{i,n},n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\ES_{\alpha_{i,n}}(X)\Big)\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}])

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\ES_{\alpha_{i,n}}(X)\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}])-\int_{(\delta,1]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha).

For the first term we simply use
\nu((\delta,1])\leqslant 1
and assumption (K2):

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}\left|\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\Big(\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha_{i,n},n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\ES_{\alpha_{i,n}}(X)\Big)\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}])\right|\\
\leqslant\sup_{\alpha\in[\delta,1]}\left|\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\ES_{\alpha}(X)\right|\longrightarrow 0

almost surely.

For the second term, define
g(\alpha):=\ES_{\alpha}(X)
on
[\delta,1]
.
Since
X
is bounded,
q_{X}
is bounded, so
\alpha\mapsto\int_{0}^{\alpha}q_{X}(u)\,du
is continuous
and therefore
g(\alpha)=-(1/\alpha)\int_{0}^{\alpha}q_{X}(u)\,du
is continuous on
[\delta,1]
.
By compactness,
g
is uniformly continuous on
[\delta,1]
. Let
\omega_{g}(h)
denote its modulus
of continuity:

\omega_{g}(h):=\sup\{|g(\alpha)-g(\beta)|:\ \alpha,\beta\in[\delta,1],\ |\alpha-\beta|\leqslant h\}.

Let
\Delta_{n}:=\max_{i}(\alpha_{i,n}-\alpha_{i-1,n})
, which tends to
0
by (K4).
Then, for every
\nu\in\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\ES_{\alpha_{i,n}}(X)\,\nu((\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}])-\int_{(\delta,1]}\ES_{\alpha}(X)\,\nu(d\alpha)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=\left|\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\int_{(\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}]}\bigl(g(\alpha_{i,n})-g(\alpha)\bigr)\,\nu(d\alpha)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{\alpha_{i,n}>\delta}\int_{(\alpha_{i-1,n},\alpha_{i,n}]}\omega_{g}(\Delta_{n})\,\nu(d\alpha)\leqslant\omega_{g}(\Delta_{n}).

This bound is uniform in
\nu
, and
\omega_{g}(\Delta_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Putting everything together, we obtain on an almost sure event:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}|\hat{I}_{n}(\nu)-I(\nu)|\leqslant(1+C)\|X\|_{\infty}\,\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}|\hat{I}_{n}(\nu)-I(\nu)|\to 0
almost surely, we conclude convergence

\hat{\rho}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\to\rho(X)
a.s.
∎

9.
Hyperfinite bootstrap validity

We establish bootstrap validity by exploiting the internal nature of the resampling procedure.
In the hyperfinite framework, the bootstrap distribution is simply the internal distribution
of the statistic conditional on the hyperfinite sample.

9.1.
Internal bootstrap construction

Let
(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
be a hyperfinite sample with infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
.
The empirical measure is
\hat{\mathsf{P}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{k}}
.

Introduce an internal i.i.d. sequence
(U_{1},\dots,U_{N})
, independent of
(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
,
with each
U_{i}
uniformly distributed on
I_{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
(counting law
\mu_{N}
). Define the

hyperfinite bootstrap sample
by

X_{i}^{*}:=X_{U_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

Then
(X_{1}^{*},\dots,X_{N}^{*})
is internal and i.i.d. with law
\hat{\mathsf{P}}_{N}
, and
conditional-on-sample statements are interpreted as statements under the bootstrap-index
randomness
(U_{i})
with
(X_{i})
held fixed. This construction makes the internal conditional
law explicit: it is simply the pushforward of the internal product counting law of
(U_{i})
.

By the star-suppression convention (Notation
3.2
), the mapping

(n,x)\mapsto\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(x)
extends internally to hypernatural
N
, giving
the internal statistic
\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}
.
Let
\hat{\rho}_{N}:=\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
be the estimator on the original sample,
and
\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}:=\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}(X_{1}^{*},\dots,X_{N}^{*})
be the estimator on the bootstrap sample.

We measure closeness of laws using the Kolmogorov distance. For standard probability measures,

d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(Z)):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathsf{P}(Y\leqslant t)-\mathsf{P}(Z\leqslant t)\big|.

In the hyperfinite setting, we must be careful about internality. For
m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
, define the

internal truncated Kolmogorov distance

d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(Z)):=\max_{t\in\{-m,-m+1/m,\dots,m\}}\big|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(Y\leqslant t)-{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(Z\leqslant t)\big|.

This is internal (a maximum over an internal hyperfinite grid).

Lemma 9.1

(Internal Kolmogorov distance approximation)
.

Let
Y
be a finite
{}^{*}\mathbb{R}
-valued random variable on
({}^{*}\Omega,L({}^{*}\mathsf{P}))
, and let
\mu
be a standard probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with continuous CDF
G
.
For
m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
define

d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y),\mu):=\max_{t\in\{-m,-m+1/m,\dots,m\}}\left|\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y)((-\infty,t])-G(t)\right|.

(i)

If there exists an infinite
m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y),\mu)\approx 0
, then

d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(\st(Y)),\mu)=0.

(ii)

The event
\{d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y\mid X),\mu)>\varepsilon\}
is internal for each internal
m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
and each standard
\varepsilon>0
, hence Loeb measurable.

Proof.

(i) Fix an infinite
m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
with
d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y),\mu)\approx 0
and write
\Delta:=1/m
.
Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
be standard and let
\eta>0
be standard.
Since
m
is infinite, we have
t,t+\eta\in(-m,m)
.
Choose gridpoints
s,s^{\prime}\in\{-m,-m+1/m,\dots,m\}
such that

s\leqslant t<s+\Delta,\qquad s^{\prime}\leqslant t+\eta<s^{\prime}+\Delta.

Then

\{Y\leqslant s\}\subseteq\{\st(Y)\leqslant t\}\subseteq\{Y\leqslant t+\eta\}\subseteq\{Y\leqslant s^{\prime}+\Delta\}.

Taking Loeb probabilities and using
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(A)=\st({}^{*}\mathsf{P}(A))
for internal
A
gives

\st({}^{*}\mathsf{P}(Y\leqslant s))\leqslant L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Y)\leqslant t)\leqslant\st({}^{*}\mathsf{P}(Y\leqslant s^{\prime}+\Delta)).

By the definition of
d_{K,m}
,

\big|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(Y\leqslant s)-G(s)\big|\leqslant d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y),\mu)\approx 0,

and

\big|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(Y\leqslant s^{\prime}+\Delta)-G(s^{\prime}+\Delta)\big|\leqslant d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y),\mu)\approx 0.

Hence

\st(G(s))\leqslant L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Y)\leqslant t)\leqslant\st(G(s^{\prime}+\Delta)).

Since
s\approx t
and
s^{\prime}+\Delta\approx t+\eta
and
G
is continuous, we have

\st(G(s))=G(t)
and
\st(G(s^{\prime}+\Delta))=G(t+\eta)
.
Therefore

G(t)\leqslant L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Y)\leqslant t)\leqslant G(t+\eta).

Letting
\eta\downarrow 0
and using continuity of
G
yields

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Y)\leqslant t)=G(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.

\st(Y)\sim\mu
.

(ii) For fixed internal
m
, the grid
\{-m,-m+1/m,\dots,m\}
is hyperfinite and

d_{K,m}
is a hyperfinite maximum of internal conditional probabilities and standard constants.
Thus
\{d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(Y\mid X),\mu)>\varepsilon\}
is internal for standard
\varepsilon>0
.
∎

9.2.
Near-standardness of conditional laws

Bootstrap validity requires that the conditional law of
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}-\hat{\rho}_{N})

is infinitely close to the limiting law of
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}-\rho(X))
.

Assumption 9.2

(Bootstrap regularity)
.

The random variable
X
satisfies:

(B1)

F_{X}
is continuous;

(B2)

X
has a density
f_{X}
that is bounded and bounded away from
0
on the interior
quantile range
\{q_{X}(\alpha):\alpha\in[\delta,1-\delta]\}
for each fixed
\delta\in(0,1/2)
.

Equivalently, (B2) says
f_{X}
is continuous and strictly positive on every compact subinterval
of the interior of the support of
X
.
Under (B1)–(B2), the quantile map
F\mapsto q_{F}
is Hadamard differentiable at
F_{X}
,
and the spectral functional
T(F)=-\int_{0}^{1}q_{F}(\alpha)\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha
inherits
Hadamard differentiability; see
[
18
, Lemma 21.3, Thm. 21.5]
.

Lemma 9.3

(Hadamard derivative of the spectral functional)
.

Under Assumption
9.2
, the spectral functional
T(F)=-\int_{0}^{1}q_{F}(\alpha)\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha

is Hadamard differentiable at
F_{X}
tangentially to
C(\mathbb{R})
, with derivative

T^{\prime}_{F_{X}}(h)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{h(q_{X}(\alpha))}{f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))}\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

The quantile functional
Q:F\mapsto q_{F}(\alpha)
has Hadamard derivative

Q^{\prime}_{F_{X}}(h)(\alpha)=-h(q_{X}(\alpha))/f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))
at continuity points
\alpha
of
q_{X}
;
see
[
18
, Lemma 21.1]
. Since

T(F)=-\int_{0}^{1}Q(F)(\alpha)\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha,

the chain rule gives

T^{\prime}_{F_{X}}(h)=-\int_{0}^{1}Q^{\prime}_{F_{X}}(h)(\alpha)\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{h(q_{X}(\alpha))}{f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))}\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha.\qed

Theorem 9.4

(Hyperfinite Bootstrap Consistency)
.

Let
X\in L^{2}
satisfy Assumption
9.2
, and let
\varphi
be Lipschitz.
Assume moreover that the hypotheses of Theorem
10.5
hold, so that the asymptotic
variance
\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}
in (
28
) is finite.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\cdot|X)
denote the internal conditional law given the sample
(i.e., the law under the bootstrap-index randomness
(U_{i})
with
(X_{i})
fixed).
Then, for
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost all sample paths
\omega
,

d_{K}\left(\mathcal{L}^{*}\left(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}-\hat{\rho}_{N})\;\Big|\;X(\omega)\right),\ N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})\right)\approx 0,

where
\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}
is the asymptotic variance in Theorem
10.5
.

Proof.

The proof combines the standard functional delta method for the bootstrap as explained by

[
18
, Thm. 23.9]
with NSA reasoning, using the internal Kolmogorov distance

d_{K,m}
from Lemma
9.1
to ensure measurability.

Near-standardness of the empirical measure.

Fix a sample path
\omega
such that the empirical CDF
\hat{F}_{N}
satisfies

\|\hat{F}_{N}-F_{X}\|_{\infty}\approx 0
. By the hyperfinite Glivenko–Cantelli theorem
(Theorem
3.20
), this holds for
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost all
\omega
.

Standard bootstrap delta method.

The standard bootstrap delta method
[
18
, Thm. 23.9]
asserts:
under Assumption
9.2
, the conditional distribution of

\sqrt{n}(T(\hat{F}_{n}^{*})-T(\hat{F}_{n}))
given the sample converges to

\mathcal{L}(T^{\prime}_{F_{X}}(\mathbb{G}))

in probability
in the bounded-Lipschitz metric,
where
\mathbb{G}
is an
F_{X}
-Brownian bridge. Since the limit distribution
N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})

has a continuous CDF, this implies convergence in Kolmogorov distance

[
18
, Lemma 21.2]
: for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

\mathsf{P}\Big(d_{K,m}\big(\mathcal{L}(\sqrt{n}(T(\hat{F}_{n}^{*})-T(\hat{F}_{n}))\mid X),\,N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})\big)>\varepsilon\Big)<\delta.

NSA repackaging via internal distance.

Fix standard
\varepsilon,\delta>0
and standard
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The event

E_{m,\varepsilon}:=\{d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}-\hat{\rho}_{N})\mid X),N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}))>\varepsilon\}

is
internal
by Lemma
9.1
(ii), hence Loeb measurable.

The sample size
N
exceeds any standard threshold
n_{0}
(since
N
is infinite), so

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(E_{m,\varepsilon})<\delta
. Taking Loeb measures and using that
\delta>0
is arbitrary,

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(E_{m,\varepsilon})=0
for each standard
\varepsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Intersecting over countably many
\varepsilon=1/k
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain a Loeb-probability-one
set
G
on which
d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}-\hat{\rho}_{N})\mid X),N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}))<1/r

for all standard
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
. To obtain an infinite
M
with
d_{K,M}\approx 0
, we use saturation:
for each standard
r\in\mathbb{N}
, define the internal set

A_{r}:=\{m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:d_{K,m}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}-\hat{\rho}_{N})\mid X),N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}))<1/r\}.

On the set
G
, each
A_{r}
contains all standard
m
. The family
\{A_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\cup\{B_{s}\}_{s\in\mathbb{N}}

(where
B_{s}:=\{m\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:m>s\}
) has the finite intersection property. By countable saturation,
there exists
M\in\bigcap_{r\in\mathbb{N}}A_{r}\cap\bigcap_{s\in\mathbb{N}}B_{s}
, which is infinite (since
M>s
for
all standard
s
) and satisfies
d_{K,M}<1/r
for all standard
r
, hence
d_{K,M}\approx 0
.
By Lemma
9.1
(i), the standard-part distribution equals
N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})
.

Identification of the asymptotic variance.

The random variable
T^{\prime}_{F_{X}}(\mathbb{G})
equals

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathbb{G}(q_{X}(\alpha))}{f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))}\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B(\alpha)}{f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))}\,\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha,

where
B(\alpha):=\mathbb{G}(q_{X}(\alpha))
is a standard Brownian bridge on
[0,1]

(since
\mathbb{G}(x)
at
x=q_{X}(\alpha)
with
F_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))=\alpha
gives

\mathrm{Cov}(B(\alpha),B(\beta))=\min(\alpha,\beta)-\alpha\beta
).

This integral is Gaussian with mean zero. Under the stronger smoothness hypotheses of
Theorem
10.5
(in particular,
q_{X}\in C^{1}
with
q_{X}^{\prime}=1/f_{X}(q_{X})
), the variance equals

\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(\min(\alpha,\beta)-\alpha\beta)\,\frac{\varphi(\alpha)\varphi(\beta)}{f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))f_{X}(q_{X}(\beta))}\,d\alpha\,d\beta.

Since the limit
N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})
has a continuous CDF, weak convergence to it implies
Kolmogorov distance convergence (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Lemma 21.2]
).
Thus
d_{K}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N}^{*}-\hat{\rho}_{N})\mid X),N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}))\approx 0
.
∎

Remark 9.5

(The power of the hyperfinite viewpoint)
.

The advantage of this approach is that we do not need to check “sequences” of conditional
measures converging in a double-limit sense. We fix a single infinite
N
and a typical path;
the bootstrap distribution is then a fixed internal distribution, which we show is near-standard
to the Gaussian. The transfer principle converts the standard Hadamard differentiability
into an internal statement, and near-standardness of the empirical measure (from Theorem

3.20
) provides the bridge between the internal and standard worlds.

10.
Asymptotic normality via the hyperfinite CLT

We derive the asymptotic distribution of spectral plug-in estimators using the hyperfinite
central limit theorem.

10.1.
Hyperfinite central limit theorem

Theorem 10.1

(Hyperfinite CLT)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. on

(\Omega,\mathscr{G},\mathsf{P})
with
\mathsf{E}[X_{1}]=0
and
\Var(X_{1})=\sigma^{2}<\infty
. Let
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
be infinite and consider the hyperfinite
sum
S_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}
on
({}^{*}\Omega,L({}^{*}\mathscr{G}),L({}^{*}\mathsf{P}))
.
Under the finite variance assumption,
S_{N}
is finite
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost surely.
Indeed, by Chebyshev’s inequality (which transfers), for any standard
M>0
,

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(|S_{N}|>M)\leqslant\frac{{}^{*}\mathsf{E}[S_{N}^{2}]}{M^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{M^{2}}.

Taking standard parts,
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(|S_{N}|>M)\leqslant\sigma^{2}/M^{2}
. Letting
M\to\infty
,

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(|S_{N}|\text{ is infinite})=0.

Thus
\st(S_{N})
is well-defined
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost surely.

Then for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})\bigl(\st(S_{N})\leqslant t\bigr)=\Phi(t/\sigma),

where
\Phi
is the standard normal distribution function.

Remark 10.2
.

The hyperfinite CLT is a
standard
result in non-standard probability; see,
e.g.
,

[
3
, Ch. 5]
or
[
4
]
for systematic treatments.

Proof.

By the classical CLT,
S_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^{2})
.
For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

|\mathsf{P}(S_{n}\leqslant t)-\Phi(t/\sigma)|<\varepsilon.

By transfer, this holds for
N
, hence

|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(S_{N}\leqslant t)-\Phi(t/\sigma)|<\varepsilon
.
Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(S_{N}\leqslant t)\approx\Phi(t/\sigma)
.

To pass from
{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(S_{N}\leqslant t)
to the Loeb event
\{\st(S_{N})\leqslant t\}
, note that

\{\st(S_{N})\leqslant t\}=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\{S_{N}\leqslant t+1/m\},

hence by continuity from above of
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
,

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(S_{N})\leqslant t)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\st\bigl({}^{*}\mathsf{P}(S_{N}\leqslant t+1/m)\bigr).

Since
{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(S_{N}\leqslant t+1/m)\approx\Phi((t+1/m)/\sigma)
for each fixed
m
and
\Phi
is continuous,
the limit equals
\Phi(t/\sigma)
.
∎

10.2.
Asymptotic normality of spectral estimators

Before stating the main CLT, we establish two technical lemmas that make the NSA proof rigorous.

Lemma 10.3

(Infinitesimal perturbation preserves standard-part distribution)
.

Let
Y,Z
be finite hyperreal random variables on
({}^{*}\Omega,L({}^{*}\mathsf{P}))
such that

Y\approx Z
holds
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost surely. Then
\st(Y)
and
\st(Z)
have the same
distribution under
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
.

Proof.

Let
A:=\{\omega:Y(\omega)\approx Z(\omega)\}
. By hypothesis,
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(A)=1
.
For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\{\st(Y)\leqslant t\}\cap A=\{\st(Z)\leqslant t\}\cap A

because if
Y\approx Z
and
\st(Y)\leqslant t
, then
\st(Z)=\st(Y)\leqslant t
, and vice versa.
Since
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(A^{c})=0
, we have

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Y)\leqslant t)=L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Y)\leqslant t,A)=L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Z)\leqslant t,A)=L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})(\st(Z)\leqslant t).\qed

Lemma 10.4

(Asymptotic linearity in first-order form)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
10.5
, the asymptotic linearity of the spectral
L-statistic can be expressed as follows: for every
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
,
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

(26)

\mathsf{P}\Big(\Big|\sqrt{n}(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(X_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)<\varepsilon,

where
\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,q_{X}^{\prime}(\alpha)\,(\mathbf{1}_{x\leqslant q_{X}(\alpha)}-\alpha)\,d\alpha
.
This statement is first-order in the sense that it can be formalised using only bounded
quantification over rationals, hence is amenable to transfer and underspill.

Proof.

This is the content of
[
17
, Thm. 8.5]
: under the smoothness hypotheses (bounded

\varphi
,
q_{X}\in C^{1}
with finite
\int\varphi^{2}(q_{X}^{\prime})^{2}
), the L-statistic

\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}
is asymptotically linear with influence function
\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}
.
The “
o_{\mathsf{P}}(1)
” remainder term means precisely that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathsf{P}(|\text{remainder}|>\varepsilon)\to 0
, which can be restated in the
“
\forall\varepsilon\exists n_{0}\forall n\geqslant n_{0}
” form above.
∎

Theorem 10.5

(Asymptotic normality of spectral plug-in CREs)
.

Let
X\in L^{2}
and
\varphi
a bounded spectrum. Assume the quantile function
q_{X}
is
continuously differentiable on
(0,1)
with derivative
q_{X}^{\prime}=1/f_{X}(q_{X})
, where
f_{X}

is the density of
X
. Assume moreover that
\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)^{2}q_{X}^{\prime}(\alpha)^{2}\,d\alpha<\infty
,
so that
\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}
in (
28
) is finite. Then

(27)

\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\rho_{\varphi}(X)\right)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}),

where

(28)

\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(\min(\alpha,\beta)-\alpha\beta)\varphi(\alpha)\varphi(\beta)q_{X}^{\prime}(\alpha)q_{X}^{\prime}(\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta.

Proof.

The result is a classical asymptotic normality theorem for L-statistics; see

[
17
, Ch. 8]
or
[
18
, Ch. 21]
for the functional delta method approach.
For the reader’s convenience, we provide a complete NSA proof using the hyperfinite CLT and the lemmata above.

The influence function representation.

For L-statistics of the form
\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}=-\int_{0}^{1}q_{n}(\alpha)\varphi(\alpha)\,d\alpha
,
the classical theory provides the asymptotic linearity result stated in Lemma
10.4
.
The influence function is

\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,q_{X}^{\prime}(\alpha)\,\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{x\leqslant q_{X}(\alpha)}-\alpha\bigr)\,d\alpha.

Under the smoothness assumptions,
\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}\in L^{2}
with
\mathsf{E}[\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(X)]=0

and
\Var(\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(X))=\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}
.

NSA formulation via hyperfinite CLT.

For infinite
N\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
, consider the internal average

T_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(X_{i}).

By the hyperfinite CLT (Theorem
10.1
),
\st(T_{N})\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})
.

By Lemma
10.4
, the asymptotic linearity statement
(
26
) holds for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
for some standard
n_{0}
.
For the fixed infinite
N
in our standing model,
N>n_{0}
, so

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}\Big(\Big|\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))-T_{N}\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)<\varepsilon.

Intersecting over a sequence
\varepsilon=1/m
(
m\in\mathbb{N}
) and taking Loeb probabilities, we see that

\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\approx T_{N}
holds
L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-almost surely.

Conclusion via standard part.

By Lemma
10.3
, since

\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\approx T_{N}

L({}^{*}\mathsf{P})
-a.s., we have

\st\bigl(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\rho}_{N,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\bigr)\sim\st(T_{N})\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}).

Transfer back to standard sequences.

To obtain the standard convergence in distribution, we use overspill on a complement set.
Fix
t\in\mathbb{Q}
and rational
\varepsilon>0
. Define the internal set

S_{t,\varepsilon}:=\{n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}:|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\leqslant t)-\Phi(t/\sigma_{\varphi})|<\varepsilon\}.

This set is internal because the defining predicate uses
{}^{*}\mathsf{P}
(the non-standard extension
of
\mathsf{P}
) and involves only internal random variables, hypernatural
n
, and standard constants

t,\varepsilon,\sigma_{\varphi},\Phi
. Choose a standard
m\in\mathbb{N}
so large that
1/m<\varepsilon/3
and

\Phi\!\left(\frac{t+1/m}{\sigma_{\varphi}}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{t-1/m}{\sigma_{\varphi}}\right)<\varepsilon/3,

which is possible by continuity of
\Phi
.

Let us define the
influence function
of the spectral functional by

\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,q_{X}^{\prime}(\alpha)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leqslant q_{X}(\alpha)\}}-\alpha\bigr)\,d\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(\alpha)\,\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leqslant q_{X}(\alpha)\}}-\alpha}{f_{X}(q_{X}(\alpha))}\,d\alpha.

For
n\in{}^{*}\mathbb{N}
we define the (internal) hyperfinite sum

T_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(X_{i}).

By transfer of (
26
) with
\varepsilon=1/m
, every infinite
n
satisfies

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}\big(|\sqrt{n}(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))-T_{n}|>1/m\big)<1/m.

Moreover, the classical CLT for the i.i.d. sequence
\mathrm{IF}_{\varphi}(X_{i})
implies (by transfer) that for each standard
u\in\mathbb{R}
and each infinite
n
,

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(T_{n}\leqslant u)\approx\Phi(u/\sigma_{\varphi}).

For such
n
we therefore have the sandwich bounds

{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(T_{n}\leqslant t-1/m)-1/m\leqslant{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\leqslant t)\leqslant{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(T_{n}\leqslant t+1/m)+1/m.

Consequently, for every infinite
n
,

\displaystyle\Big|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\leqslant t)-\Phi(t/\sigma_{\varphi})\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{\pm}\Big|{}^{*}\mathsf{P}(T_{n}\leqslant t\pm 1/m)-\Phi\!\Big(\frac{t\pm 1/m}{\sigma_{\varphi}}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\Big|\Phi\!\Big(\frac{t+1/m}{\sigma_{\varphi}}\Big)-\Phi\!\Big(\frac{t-1/m}{\sigma_{\varphi}}\Big)\Big|+\frac{1}{m}<\varepsilon,

so all infinite
n
lie in
S_{t,\varepsilon}
.

Let
B_{t,\varepsilon}:={}^{*}\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{t,\varepsilon}
be the internal complement.
Since
N\in S_{t,\varepsilon}
for every infinite
N
, the set
B_{t,\varepsilon}
contains
no infinite hypernaturals. If
B_{t,\varepsilon}
contained arbitrarily large standard integers,
then by overspill (Proposition
3.21
) it would contain an infinite hypernatural,
a contradiction. Hence there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{t,\varepsilon}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n_{0}-1\}
,
and therefore every standard
n\geqslant n_{0}
lies in
S_{t,\varepsilon}
.

Since
\varepsilon
and
t
were arbitrary rationals, and CDFs are determined by their
values at rationals (by right-continuity), we obtain

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\rho}_{n,\varphi}-\rho_{\varphi}(X))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma_{\varphi}^{2})
.
∎

11.
Extensions to Orlicz hearts

We briefly discuss how the hyperfinite framework extends to coherent risk measures on
Orlicz hearts.

11.1.
Orlicz spaces and duality

Let
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a Young function (convex, increasing,

\Phi(0)=0
,
\Phi(x)/x\to\infty
). The
Orlicz space

L^{\Phi}
consists of
random variables
X
with
\mathsf{E}[\Phi(|X|/\lambda)]<\infty
for some
\lambda>0
. The

Orlicz heart

H^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
consists of
X
with this condition holding
for all
\lambda>0
.

The dual Orlicz space
L^{\Psi}
, where
\Psi
is the complementary Young function, provides
the dual pairs for robust representation.

11.2.
Hyperfinite representation on Orlicz hearts

Remark 11.1

(Orlicz hearts: what changes)
.

A full Orlicz-heart analogue of Theorem
4.3
requires replacing the
L^{\infty}
–
L^{1}

duality by the
H^{\Phi}
–
L^{\Psi}
duality (where
\Psi
is complementary to
\Phi
) and imposing the usual
lower semicontinuity/Fatou-type condition in the
H^{\Phi}
topology; see
[
6
]
.

Specifically, the key modifications are:

(i)

The representing set
\mathcal{Z}
in Theorem
4.3
must consist of
densities
Z\in L^{\Psi}_{+}
with
\mathsf{E}[Z]=1
, and compactness is taken in
\sigma(L^{\Psi},H^{\Phi})
.

(ii)

The internal lifting
\tilde{X}
of
X\in H^{\Phi}
must satisfy
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Phi(|\tilde{X}(k)|/\lambda)

finite for all standard
\lambda>0
, not merely boundedness.

(iii)

For
Z^{\sharp}\in{}^{*}\mathcal{Z}
,
S
-integrability of the product
\tilde{X}\cdot Z^{\sharp}

follows from the Orlicz–Hölder inequality
|\mathsf{E}[XZ]|\leqslant 2\|X\|_{\Phi}\|Z\|_{\Psi}
(transferred internally).

(iv)

The normalisation
H(Z)\approx 1
still holds since
\mathsf{E}[Z]=1
transfers.

The remainder of the proof of Theorem
4.3
proceeds unchanged once these
modifications are in place.

11.3.
Consistency on Orlicz domains

For plug-in consistency on
H^{\Phi}
, the uniform boundedness assumption (K3) in Theorem

8.1
is replaced by:

(K3’)

There exists
C<\infty
such that

|\widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,n}(x)|\leqslant C\|x\|_{\Phi}
for all
\alpha,n,x
.

Under this and the other assumptions (with
L^{\infty}
replaced by
H^{\Phi}
), the Kusuoka
plug-in consistency theorem extends to Orlicz hearts, though a complete treatment requires
careful attention to the Orlicz-space duality theory.

12.
Concluding remarks

We have developed a systematic hyperfinite framework for coherent risk estimation,
demonstrating that non-standard analysis provides both conceptual clarity and technical
power for this class of problems.

The hyperfinite viewpoint suggests several directions for future work:

•

Sensitivity analysis.
Internal Lipschitz properties of hyperfinite risk
functionals could yield robustness bounds for CREs under model misspecification.

•

High-dimensional extensions.
The hyperfinite framework may extend to
systemic risk measures on high-dimensional portfolios, where the number of assets grows
with the sample size.

•

Dynamic risk.
Time-consistent dynamic risk measures might admit
hyperfinite backward induction representations, simplifying certain asymptotic analyses.

•

Computational aspects.
The hyperfinite picture suggests natural
discretisation schemes for computing coherent risk measures, with explicit error bounds
derived from the standard-part construction.
