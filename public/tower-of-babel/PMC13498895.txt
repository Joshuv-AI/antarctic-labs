Cooperative Suppression of Ovarian Cancer Growth by 1,25‐Dihydroxyvitamin D3 and Sodium Selenite Mediated Through SEPP1 Induction
Authors: Yanmin Zhang, Yaxi Shi, Wenlong Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdvanced epithelial ovarian cancer responds poorly to current treatments, creating an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1,25D3) exerts potent anti‐proliferative effects on ovarian cancer cells in vitro, but the concentrations required for growth suppression in cell culture are not readily achievable in vivo, and higher doses induce hypercalcemia, which hinders its clinical translation. Sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) has been identified as a chemopreventive agent that inhibits the growth of multiple cancer types. In this study, we demonstrated that combined treatment with Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 exerts enhanced anti‐proliferative efficacy against ovarian cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, 1,25D3 upregulated the expression of vitamin D receptor (VDR) and Selenoprotein‐P1 (SEPP1), and this effect was further enhanced by Na2SeO3 co‐treatment. Consistently, Na2SeO3 augmented the suppressive effect of 1,25D3 on ovarian cancer cell proliferation, demonstrating synergistic inhibitory activity. Notably, knockout (KO) of either VDR or SEPP1 abrogated this synergistic effect. Mechanistic investigations confirmed that SEPP1 is a direct target gene of VDR, and functional vitamin D response elements (VDREs) are present within its genomic locus. In vivo, low‐dose 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg) combined with Na2SeO3 outperformed 1,25D3 monotherapy, with no significant hypercalcemia, ascites formation, or metastasis observed throughout the treatment period. These findings indicate that Na2SeO3 acts in combination with 1,25D3 to induce SEPP1 expression, thereby enhancing the antitumor efficacy of low‐dose 1,25D₃ against ovarian tumors. This study provides a promising strategy for the safe and effective application of 1,25D3 in ovarian cancer therapy.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is dubbed the “king of gynecological cancers” and stands as the deadliest among gynecological malignancies [1, 2]. Notably, its mortality rate in China has risen by 3% annually since 2016 [3], with comparable upward trends documented in other countries globally [4]. Owing to the insidious nature of its onset, more than 70% of ovarian cancer cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, and 5‐year survival rates remain below 30% [5]. The limited therapeutic efficacy of current treatments for advanced ovarian cancer underscores the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies [6].

Multiple epidemiological studies have reported an inverse association between ovarian cancer mortality and ultraviolet B exposure [7, 8], which drives approximately 90% of endogenous vitamin D3 production [9]. Consistently, elevated plasma vitamin D3 levels are associated with reduced ovarian cancer risk [10, 11]. Previous work from our laboratory has demonstrated that 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1,25D3) (100 nM), the most biologically active form of vitamin D3 [12], exerts potent inhibitory effects on the proliferation of ovarian cancer cell lines following 6 or 9 days of treatment, via binding to the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and upregulating its protein level [13, 14, 15]. Nevertheless, the anti‐neoplastic efficacy of 1,25D3 in vivo is suboptimal because the concentrations required for growth suppression in vitro exhibit limited efficacy in vivo [16, 17], whereas higher concentrations (375 nM) trigger severe hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria [18, 19, 20], collectively rendering 1,25D3 unsuitable for direct clinical application.

Sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), clinically employed in the treatment of Keshan disease in China [21, 22], is metabolized in vivo to elemental selenium, which binds to Selenoprotein‐P1 (SEPP1), a selenium transporter whose synthesis is selenium dependent [23, 24]. The synthesized SEPP1 is secreted into the blood plasma and induces tumor cell apoptosis by suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [25]. Recently, Na2SeO3 has emerged as a chemopreventive agent in malignancies including pancreatic cancer [26], cervical cancer [27], and gastric cancer [28], owing to its antioxidant properties and favorable safety profile. Nevertheless, the therapeutic role and underlying mechanism of Na2SeO3 in ovarian cancer remain incompletely characterized. Our previous microarray analysis of OVCAR3 cells treated with 1,25D3 (100 nM) has demonstrated that 1,25D3 induces the expression of SEPP1 in a time‐dependent manner. However, whether Na2SeO3 in combination with 1,25D3 can enhance the in vivo inhibitory effect of low‐dose 1,25D3 on ovarian cancer remains unclear, and the underlying molecular mechanism has yet to be elucidated.

In this study, we demonstrated that Na2SeO3 exerted a synergistic effect with 1,25D3 in suppressing the proliferation of OVCAR3 and ID8 cells in vitro, with a synergism index (Q) > 1.15. Furthermore, this synergistic effect was strictly dependent on both VDR and SEPP1. Mechanistically, we confirmed that SEPP1 was a direct target gene of VDR. Additionally, the combination therapy exerted significantly enhanced inhibitory effects on murine ovarian tumor growth in vivo compared with low‐dose 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg) monotherapy. These findings indicate that Na2SeO3 serves as a promising adjuvant anticancer agent capable of enhancing the efficacy of low‐dose 1,25D3, thereby providing a viable strategy to advance the clinical translation of 1,25D3 in ovarian cancer therapy.

Materials and Methods

Gene Expression Analysis in Ovarian Cancer Samples

Differences in SEPP1 mRNA expression between cancerous and adjacent normal tissues were analyzed via GEPIA2.0 (http://gepia2.cancer‐pku.cn/). SEPP1 protein expression variations between cancer and normal tissues or ovarian cancer tissues with different pathological grades were analyzed using data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC), accessed via UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/).

Cell Culture

The human ovarian cancer cell line OVCAR3 and the human embryonic kidney cell line 293 T were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The mouse ovarian cancer cell line ID8 was purchased from Ubigene (China). ID8 and 293 T cells were cultured in high‐glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Procell, #PM150210) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, LONSERA, #S711‐001S) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S, Procell, #PB180120), and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. OVCAR3 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium containing 20% FBS, 1% P/S, and 10 μg/mL insulin (Procell, # PB180432) under the same conditions. Prior to experiments, all cell lines had their identity authenticated through short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination via testing with a PCR‐based detection kit (Vazyme, #D101‐01).

Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA from cell lines was extracted using the FastPure Total RNA Kit (Vazyme, #RC101‐01), and cDNA was synthesized using the PureScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, #PR211‐02). Primer sequences were listed in Table S1. Primers were synthesized by Tsingke (Beijing, China), and specific transcripts were amplified with ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q711‐02) on a Roche LightCycler 480II (Germany, 05015243001). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated as fold changes relative to the control.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, #R0020) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Solarbio, #P1260) for 30 min on ice. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 10 μg of total protein in a volume of 10 μL per lane was resolved by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to 0.22 μm polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, #ISEQ00010) at 300 mA for 80 min. The membrane was blocked in a protein‐free rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme, #PS108P) for 15 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with Tris‐buffered saline (Servicebio, #G0001) containing Tween‐20 (Servicebio, #G5058), detection was performed using Omni‐ECL (Epizyme, #SQ201) on a chemiluminescence imaging system (Cytiva, AMERSHAM ImageQuant 800), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ.

The following primary antibodies were utilized, with their specific dilutions indicated: anti‐SEPP1 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab277526), anti‐VDR (1:500, Santa Cruz, #sc‐13,133), anti‐GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, #60004‐1‐Ig), and anti‐β‐actin (1:5000, Proteintech, #20536‐1‐AP). Secondary antibodies: Goat anti‐rabbit‐IgG (H + L) (1:10000, Proteintech, #SA00001‐2), Goat anti‐mouse‐IgG (H + L) (1:10000, Proteintech, #SA00001‐1).

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96‐well plates at a low density of 200 cells per well and incubated overnight in a cell incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were subsequently treated with Vehicle (0.1% of DMSO), 1,25D3 (MCE, #HY‐10002, dissolved in DMSO to yield a 1 mM stock solution), Na2SeO3 (MCE, #HY‐W686381, dissolved in DMSO to yield a 1 M stock solution), or a combination of both agents at the indicated concentrations for the specified durations. Following drug treatment, the medium was removed, and each well received 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) solution, and plates were incubated for 2 h in the dark. The cell viability was assessed by measuring the optical density (OD) at 450 nm using a multimode microplate reader (TECAN, Infinite M Plex). Inhibition rates of 1,25D3 and Na2SeO3 alone relative to the Vehicle control, as well as the combination index (Q) [29] were calculated as follows: Q=Ea+b/Ea+Eb−Ea×Eb×100% where Ea is the inhibition rate of drug a, Eb is the inhibition rate of drug b, and E(a + b) is the inhibition rate of the combined two drugs. A Q value < 0.85 indicates antagonism, 0.85 ≤ Q < 1.15 indicates an additive effect, and Q ≥ 1.15 indicates synergism.

Plasmid Construction and Stable Transfection

To generate VDR knockout (KO) and SEPP1 KO cell lines, sgRNAs targeting the genomic sequences of SEPP1 or VDR were designed using the CRISPOR platform (http://crispor.tefor.net/). sgRNA sequences were listed in Table S2. sgRNAs targeting VDR or SEPP1 were cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector via Esp3I digestion and T4 DNA ligase mediated ligation. Constructed lentiviral plasmids were packaged in 293 T cells by co‐transfection of the constructed plasmids with psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, #L3000015) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Viral supernatants were harvested at 24 and 48 h post‐transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and supplemented with polybrene (Solarbio, #H8761) to a final concentration of 20 μg/mL. OVCAR3 and ID8 cells were infected with the lentivirus‐polybrene mixture for 48 h, and the stable VDR or SEPP1 KO cell lines were isolated using flow cytometric single‐cell sorting and selected via puromycin (Beyotime, #ST551).

Colony Formation Assay

Cells were seeded into 6‐well plates at a density of 800 cells per well and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. On Day 3 post‐seeding, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with 1,25D3 (100 nM), and cells were cultured for a further 7 days with medium renewal every 3 days. One day before the end of culture, cells were treated with vehicle or Na2SeO3 (10 μM) and incubated for a further 24 h.

Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099) for 15 min at room temperature, then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, G1064) for 10 min. After rinsing off excess stain with running water and air‐drying, colonies were counted under an inverted microscope. Colony formation efficiency was analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Dual‐Luciferase Gene Reporter Assay

The SEPP1 genomic DNA fragment from −2500 to +1500 relative to the transcription start site (TSS) was amplified by PCR and cloned into the PGL3‐enhancer vector to construct wild‐type (WT) luciferase reporter. Site‐specific mutations were introduced using a FAST Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit (TIANGEN, China). Primer sequences were listed in Tables S3 and S4, and all primers were synthesized by Tsingke. WT or mutant (MT) luciferase reporters of SEPP1, renilla control vector, and VDR‐overexpressing vector were transiently co‐transfected into 293 T cells. Subsequently, cells were treated with either vehicle or 1,25D3 (100 nM), and incubated in the cell culture incubator for 4 h, after which cell pellets were collected. The activity of dual‐luciferase reporters was measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime, #RG027) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cleavage Under Targets and Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN)

CUT&RUN was performed with the Hyperactive pG‐MNase CUT&RUN Assay Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, #HD102) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, OVCAR3 cells were harvested and washed with wash buffer, and the cell concentration was adjusted to 5 × 105 cells/mL. The cell suspension (100 μL) was gently mixed with 10 μL of pre‐treated magnetic beads and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After brief centrifugation, the mixture was separated using a magnetic rack and the supernatant was discarded.

Next, 100 μL of prechilled antibody buffer was added to resuspend the bead‐cell complex, followed by the addition of 1 μg/100 μL of VDR antibody. The mixture was gently mixed and incubated overnight at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded using a magnetic rack, and the pellet was washed twice with 800 μL of dig‐wash buffer. Then, 100 μL of pG‐MNase enzyme was added and mixed, followed by rotation and incubation at 4°C for 1.5 h. The supernatant was again discarded via a magnetic rack, and the pellet was washed with dig‐wash buffer. A 100 μL volume of CaCl2 premix was added, and the mixture was immediately incubated on ice for 1.5 h.

Subsequently, 100 μL of stop buffer was added and mixed thoroughly. After brief centrifugation, the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected into a new tube following magnetic separation.

DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (Vazyme, #DC301). Library construction was performed following the manufacturer's protocol. Amplified and purified library products were sequenced by Novogene (Beijing, China).

Murine Intraperitoneal Ovarian Tumor Assay

Murine Intraperitoneal Ovarian Tumor Model

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from the Beijing Charles River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd., China and housed in a standard specific pathogen‐free environment with free access to autoclaved food and water. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Henan University (animal license No. SYXK(Yu)‐2024‐0010) and conducted in strict accordance with ethical guidelines for animal research. After 1 week of acclimatization, ID8‐Luc cells in logarithmic growth phase were resuspended to a concentration of 2.5 × 107 cells/mL. Each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 5 × 106 ID8‐Luc cells in 0.2 mL of culture medium.

Combination therapy of 1,25D3 with Na2SeO3

One week after tumor inoculation, mice were randomly divided into 4 groups with 4 mice per group, designated as vehicle, 1,25D3, Na2SeO3, and combination groups, respectively. 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg, dissolved in corn oil) was administered via oral gavage every other day for a total of 18 administrations, whereas Na2SeO3 (2 mg/kg, dissolved in normal saline) was administered via tail vein injection twice a week for a total of 10 administrations. All mice were maintained on a vitamin D3‐deficient diet throughout the experiment. Tumor growth was monitored using a small animal in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina III), and body weights were recorded once a week. At the end of the experiment, all mice were euthanized using the CO2 inhalation method. Hearts, spleens, lungs, kidneys, omentums, and intraperitoneal tumor tissues were harvested and analyzed. Ascites from intraperitoneal ovarian tumor mice was collected, and its volume was recorded.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

For cell sample preparation: 1 × 106 cells were homogenized in 200 μL double‐distilled water. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 5 min, transferred to a 10 kDa ultrafiltration tube, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 25 min at 4°C. The filtrate from the lower collection tube was harvested for detection. Intracellular selenium content was measured using an ELISA kit following the manufacturer's protocol (E‐BC‐K776‐M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China).

For blood sample preparation: Mouse blood samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g or 3000 × g for 20 min to isolate serum. Serum levels of parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcium, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were determined with corresponding ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (CSB‐E16539m, CSB‐E12649m, COIBO BIO, Shanghai, China; KT21426, MSK, Wuhan, China). For all assays, absorbance at 420–450 nm was measured within 5 min using a multimode microplate reader.

Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) Staining

Mouse omental tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm sections. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (X112051, aladdin), rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (60%, 70%, 75%, 85%, 95%, 100%), stained with hematoxylin (H432819, aladdin), and counterstained with eosin (E774785, aladdin). Sections were then dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam (C0173, Beyotime). Histological images were captured using a bright‐field microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Axio Observer 7).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue sections from mouse intraperitoneal tumors were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval via heat treatment in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and nonspecific binding, sections were incubated with the primary antibody of SEPP1 primary antibody (1:500) overnight at 4°C. Staining analysis was then performed using an IHC Detection Kit (ABclonal, #RK50015), followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibody. Signals were visualized with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. Sections were subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Staining images were observed under an optical microscope.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 10. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using two‐tailed unpaired t‐tests or one‐way ANOVA as appropriate for the experimental design, with significance defined as p < 0.05. All experiments were performed at least in triplicate.

Results

Na2SeO3 Enhances the Inductive Effect of 1,25D3 on SEPP1 and VDR Levels in Ovarian Cancer Cells

To investigate the clinical relevance of SEPP1 in ovarian tumorigenesis and disease progression, we first profiled its expression in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database using GEPIA2.2 and UALCAN platforms, and found that its mRNA (Figure 1A) and protein (Figure 1B) levels were significantly downregulated in tumor tissues relative to normal ovarian controls. We further explored its correlation with tumor pathological grade and observed a stepwise reduction in SEPP1 protein expression with increasing grade (Figure 1C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SEPP1 plays an important role in ovarian cancer oncogenesis.

Previous studies [15] from our laboratory employing microarray analysis of OVCAR3 cells treated with 1,25D3 (100 nM) demonstrated that the SEPP1 gene was induced by 1,25D3 in a time‐dependent manner (Figure 1D). Consistently, both SEPP1 mRNA and protein expression were upregulated following 3 days of 1,25D3 treatment in ID8 and OVCAR3 cells (Figure 1E–G), validating the reliability of the earlier microarray findings.

Given that SEPP1 functions as the primary binding protein for Na2SeO3, we investigated whether Na2SeO3 in combination with 1,25D3 could synergistically upregulate SEPP1 protein expression in ovarian cancer cells. As shown in Figure 1H, ID8 and OVCAR3 cells were treated with gradient concentrations of 1,25D3 (0, 1, 10, and 100 nM) for 3 days, with or without 10 μM Na2SeO3 added during the final 24 h of incubation. 1,25D3 upregulated SEPP1 protein expression in a concentration‐dependent manner in both cell lines, with 100 nM exerting the strongest inductive effect, and this effect was further enhanced by Na2SeO3 co‐treatment. Statistical analysis results were presented in Figure 1I. Notably, the inductive effect of 1,25D3 on its natural receptor‐VDR was similarly augmented by Na2SeO3.

We next performed a time‐course analysis, in which cells were exposed to 1,25D3 (100 nM) for 0, 3, 6, and 9 days, with or without 10 μM Na2SeO3 administered in the last 24 h. Drug‐containing culture medium was refreshed every 3 days throughout the prolonged 1,25D3 incubation. As presented in Figure 1J,K, 1,25D3 at 100 nM upregulated SEPP1 protein levels in a time‐dependent manner in both ID8 and OVCAR3 cells, and this effect was further potentiated by Na2SeO3. Similarly, the time‐dependent induction of VDR by 1,25D3 was also further enhanced by Na2SeO3 co‐treatment.

Na2SeO3 And 1,25D3 Synergistically Inhibit the Proliferation of Ovarian Cancer Cells

In agreement with our previous findings [14, 17], 1,25D3 inhibited the proliferation of OVCAR3 and ID8 cells in a concentration‐ and time‐dependent manner (Figure 2A,B). Building on the synergistic induction of SEPP1 protein expression by Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3, we investigated whether Na2SeO3 could synergize with 1,25D3 to suppress the proliferation of ovarian cancer cells. First, OVCAR3 and ID8 cells were treated with Na2SeO3 (5, 10, 15, 20 and 40 μM) for 1 day. The results revealed that Na2SeO3 alone exerted a modest inhibitory effect on the proliferation of both cell lines (Figure 2C,D). Subsequently, OVCAR3 cells were treated with 1,25D3 (0, 1, 10, and 100 nM) for 3 or 6 days, with a low concentration of Na2SeO3 (10 μM) administered on the final day. Na2SeO3 consistently enhanced the antiproliferative effects of 1,25D3 across all tested concentrations (Figure 2E). A comparable potentiating effect was also observed in ID8 cells (Figure 2F). Further calculation of the combination index (Q) revealed mean Q values ranging from 1.2 to 1.4 in both OVCAR3 and ID8 cells (Figure 2G,H), all of which exceeded the established synergy threshold of Q = 1.15. This finding indicates that the combination exerts a synergistic effect rather than a simple additive effect, with overall slightly higher Q values observed in OVCAR3 cells relative to ID8 cells. Notably, the degree of synergy increased in a dose‐dependent manner with rising 1,25D₃ concentrations in OVCAR3 cells. Taken together, these findings confirm that Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 synergistically suppress ovarian cancer cell proliferation.

Loss of SEPP1 Abrogates the Synergistic Inhibitory Effect of Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 on Ovarian Cancer Cell Proliferation

The antiproliferative effect of 1,25D3 is primarily dependent on VDR [30, 31, 32]. To assess whether the synergistic effect between Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 relies on VDR, VDR‐knockout (KO) OVCAR3 cells were generated using specific sgRNAs via CRISPR‐Cas9 technology (Figure 3A). WT and VDR KO OVCAR3 cells were treated with either 1,25D3 (100 nM) for 3 days or Na2SeO3 (2.5, 5, 10, 15, and 20 μM) for 1 day. The proliferation‐inhibitory effect of Na2SeO3 was significantly attenuated in VDR KO cells (Figure 3B). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed that SEPP1 induction by the combined treatment was markedly diminished in VDR KO OVCAR3 cells relative to WT controls (Figure 3C,D), indicating that Na2SeO3, like 1,25D3, requires VDR to exert its anti‐proliferative effects. To elucidate whether SEPP1 is required for the synergistic effect, SEPP1 was knocked out in ovarian cancer cells (Figure 3E), with a concomitant reduction in intracellular selenium levels (Figure S1). Compared with WT cells, the antiproliferative effects of 1,25D3 at 1, 10, and 100 nM, as well as following 3 and 6 days of treatment, were consistently attenuated in SEPP1 KO OVCAR3 cells. A comparable effect was observed in ID8 SEPP1 KO cells (Figure 3F,G). Similarly, the antiproliferative effect of Na2SeO3 was also diminished in both OVCAR3 SEPP1 KO and ID8 SEPP1 KO cells (Figure 3H,I). Additionally, compared with WT cells (Figure 2E,F), the proliferation‐inhibitory effect of combined Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 treatment was significantly diminished in SEPP1 KO cells (Figure 3J,K). The two agents no longer elicited a synergistic effect in SEPP1 KO cells, with Q < 1.15 (Figure 3L,M). Colony formation assays in OVCAR3 cells showed that both 1,25D3 and Na2SeO3 monotherapies reduced colony numbers to varying degrees, exerting mild inhibition on cell proliferation. The combination treatment markedly reduced colony formation, with a significantly stronger inhibitory effect than either monotherapy. Following SEPP1 knockout, no statistically significant differences in colony numbers were detected across the vehicle, monotherapy, and combination groups (Figure 3N,O). Similar findings were recapitulated in ID8 cells (Figure 3P,Q). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 require both VDR and SEPP1 to mediate their synergistic inhibition of ovarian cancer cell proliferation.

SEPP1 Is a Direct Target Gene of VDR

Since SEPP1 is induced by 1,25D3 and VDR functions as a ligand‐regulated nuclear transcription factor, we investigated whether SEPP1 is a direct target gene of VDR. OVCAR3 cells were treated with vehicle or 1,25D3 (100 nM) for 24 h, then incubated with actinomycin D (ActD, 5 μg/mL), an inhibitor of RNA synthesis, for 0, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 12 h. As shown in Figure S2A, 1,25D3 treatment did not alter the SEPP1 mRNA degradation rate in OVCAR3 cells exposed to Act D, indicating that 1,25D3‐mediated SEPP1 induction occurs via transcriptional activation rather than via mRNA stabilization.

Subsequently, OVCAR3 cells were treated with vehicle or 1,25D3 (100 nM) for 8 or 24 h, in the presence or absence of cycloheximide (CHX, 20 μM), a protein synthesis inhibitor. The results demonstrated that 1,25D3 retained the ability to induce SEPP1 mRNA and protein expression under these conditions (Figure S2B,C). These results confirm that 1,25D3‐mediated SEPP1 induction does not require de novo protein synthesis.

Based on the consensus sequence of vitamin D response elements (VDREs), four putative VDREs were identified in the SEPP1 genomic sequence, with one located in the promoter region and others within 1500 bp downstream of the TSS (Figure 4A). To assess the activity of these four VDREs, dual‐luciferase reporter vectors harboring distinct VDRE fragments were constructed (Left panel, Figure 4B). Subsequently, 293 T cells were transfected with SEPP1 genomic region reporters harboring WT or mutant VDREs, followed by treatment with vehicle control or 100 nM 1,25D3 for 4 h. Results showed that the luciferase activity was induced by 1,25D3, but the magnitude of induction was very low when all four VDREs were present. Similarly, the luciferase activity was maintained following deletion of VDRE‐4, but increased significantly when both VDRE‐3 and VDRE‐4 were deleted. Notably, when VDRE‐2, VDRE‐3, and VDRE‐4 were removed, the activity decreased but residual luciferase activity persisted (Right panel, Figure 4B). These findings preliminarily indicate that VDRE‐3 and VDRE‐4 function as repressive elements, whereas VDRE‐1 and VDRE‐2 act as inductive elements. Subsequent site‐directed mutagenesis showed that mutation of VDRE‐4 failed to restore reporter activity, while mutation of VDRE‐3 reversed the activity repression observed with the WT vector. In contrast, mutation of VDRE‐2 significantly reduced the activity, whereas mutation of VDRE‐1 had no effect on the reporter activity (Figure 4B). Collectively, these results indicate that VDRE‐1 is likely inactive, VDRE‐2 functions as an inductive element, and VDRE‐3 and VDRE‐4 act as repressive elements.

Additional CUT&RUN assays were performed to further characterize VDR‐chromatin binding in OVCAR3 cells treated with 100 nM 1,25D3 for 2 h. Results demonstrated that 1,25D3 significantly enhanced enrichment of peaks within 5 kb upstream and downstream of TSS (Figure 4C). IGV visualizations revealed increased peak abundance across chromosome 5 in the treated group (Figure 4D). Furthermore, treatment with 1,25D3 increased peak abundance within the SEPP1 genomic locus (Figure 4E). Consistently, peaks were detected at the four predicted VDRE sequences within this locus, and the treated group showed specifically enhanced peak enrichment at these four VDRE sites (Figure 4F). Collectively, these observations support the conclusion that SEPP1 is a direct target gene of VDR.

Na2SeO3 In Combination With 1,25D3 Suppresses Ovarian Cancer Tumor Growth and Metastasis

To evaluate the combined therapeutic efficacy of Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 against ovarian tumors in vivo, a murine intraperitoneal ovarian tumor model was established and treated with Na2SeO3 (2 mg/kg), 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg), or their combination (Figure 5A). Specifically, 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg) was administered via oral gavage every other day, whereas Na2SeO3 (2 mg/kg) was administered via tail vein injection twice weekly. After approximately 40 days of treatment, monotherapy with Na2SeO3 or 1,25D3 exerted modest inhibitory effects on tumor growth relative to the vehicle group, reducing tumor burden by 33% and 44%, respectively. In contrast, the combination treatment exhibited significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy, with a 60% reduction in tumor burden compared with the vehicle group. Specifically, the tumor burden in the combination group was only 50% of that in the Na2SeO3 monotherapy group and 70% of that in the 1,25D3 monotherapy group (Figure 5B,C).

Analysis of body weight changes across groups revealed that, at the late stage of tumor growth, body weights in the vehicle group, Na2SeO3 or 1,25D3 group, were significantly higher than in the combination group (Figure 5D). Notably, the weight gain in the former three groups was attributed to ascites accumulation, whereas the combination group showed no abnormal weight gain (Figure 5E).

The vehicle group exhibited the highest incidence of intraperitoneal tumors among all treatment groups. Both Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 monotherapies reduced intraperitoneal tumor burden relative to the vehicle control. Strikingly, the combination treatment achieved the strongest tumor suppressive effect, with an approximately 85% reduction in tumor weight compared with the vehicle group. Specifically, tumor weight in the combination group was only 25% of that in the Na2SeO3 monotherapy group and 40% of that in the 1,25D3 monotherapy group (Figure 5F,G).

Furthermore, omental tissues were harvested to assess tumor metastasis via hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining. Histopathological analysis revealed significantly reduced tumor infiltration in the combination group compared with the vehicle and monotherapy groups (Figure 5H). Safety assessments revealed that serum calcium levels remained within the normal range (below 2.75 mM) across all groups (Figure 5I), and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were not significantly elevated in any of the four groups (Figure S3). Additionally, no significant histopathological lesions were observed in vital organs including the heart, spleen, lung, and kidney across all treatment groups (Figure 5J). Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured to evaluate hepatic function, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was determined to assess renal function. As shown in Figure S4, no significant intergroup differences were detected in these biomarkers, indicating no treatment‐induced hepatic or renal injury.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed weak overall SEPP1 staining in tumor tissues from the vehicle group. In contrast, the combination treatment group exhibited a higher proportion of SEPP1‐positive cells and stronger staining intensity (Figure 5K). Furthermore, we examined whether the combination could upregulate VDR and SEPP1 protein expression in mouse intraperitoneal tumors. As shown in Figure 5L,M, both monotherapies elevated VDR and SEPP1 protein levels, and the combination group exhibited significantly higher expression of both proteins than either monotherapy. Collectively, these in vivo findings demonstrate that at the very least, Na2SeO3 (2 mg/kg) combined with 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg) exerts a combined inhibitory effect on ovarian cancer growth and metastasis via upregulation of VDR and SEPP1 protein expression.

To further explore SEPP1‐associated downstream regulatory mechanisms, we systematically assessed the correlation between SEPP1 expression and pathway‐level somatic alterations in ovarian tumors using an integrated dataset of proteomics, whole‐exome sequencing, and copy number alteration (CNA) via UALCAN. SEPP1 expression was robustly correlated with alterations in the mTOR pathway and SWI/SNF complex (Figure S5). Given their well‐established roles in tumorigenesis and progression, these findings highlight a key direction for our follow‐up mechanistic investigations.

Discussion

Ovarian cancer is the most lethal gynecological malignancy. First‐line clinical treatment centers on surgery, complemented by adjuvant platinum‐based chemotherapy and targeted agents [33, 34]. However, global data indicate that the majority of patients with advanced ovarian cancer exhibit suboptimal responses to current therapies [35]. Notably, unlike the declining mortality trends observed in most other cancers, ovarian cancer mortality has not decreased in recent years but instead shows an annual upward trend [3, 4], underscoring the urgent need to develop effective therapeutic strategies for this disease.

1,25D3, the natural ligand of VDR, exerts potent antiproliferative effects in ovarian cancer cell lines at 100 nM in vitro [13, 14, 15]. However, a major barrier to its clinical translation remains the suboptimal efficacy at low doses and the risk of hypercalcemia at higher doses. EB1089, an analog of 1,25D3 with a weaker calcemic effect than the parent compound [17, 36], nevertheless failed in Phase II clinical trials due to adverse effects [37]. As an endogenous nuclear hormone, 1,25D3 avoids immune rejection and exhibits a more favorable safety profile than EB1089, highlighting the need to advance its clinical translation. Here, we report that Na2SeO3 in combination with low‐dose 1,25D3 inhibited ovarian cancer cell proliferation in vitro and tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, this effect depended on a VDR‐SEPP1 axis. These data provide a translational strategy to realize the clinical potential of 1,25D3.

Our in vitro results demonstrated that Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 synergistically inhibit the proliferation of OVCAR3 and ID8 cells (Figure 2E,H), primarily through induction of SEPP1 expression. As the primary selenium transporter, lower levels of SEPP1 have been associated with increased risks of lung cancer [38] and prostate cancer [39]. Conversely, upregulation of SEPP1 not only supplies selenium for the anti‐tumor activity of Na2SeO3 [22] but also exerts intrinsic anti‐cancer effects, primarily by inhibiting the production of ROS [40, 41].

Na2SeO3 also augments 1,25D3‐induced VDR protein expression (Figure 1H,J). Given that VDR is essential for the biological effects of 1,25D3 [42] and reduced VDR expression correlates with poor ovarian cancer prognosis [43], this dual regulation of SEPP1 and VDR establishes a mechanistic basis for the observed synergy. Our results showed that Na2SeO3 synergized with low concentrations of 1,25D3 (1–100 nM) to inhibit the proliferation of OVCAR3 and ID8 cells (Figure 2E,H), as confirmed by a combination index Q > 1.15. Furthermore, the ovarian tumor experiments in vivo demonstrated that the combination of Na2SeO3 (2 mg/kg) and 1,25D3 (0.3 μg/kg) exerted significantly greater tumor‐suppressive effects than 1,25D3 monotherapy (Figure 5B,C). These findings identify Na2SeO3 as a potential combinatorial agent to overcome the clinical limitations of 1,25D3.

While the combination of 1,25D3 and Na2SeO3 exhibited superior efficacy over 1,25D3 monotherapy, no statistically significant synergistic inhibitory effect was detected in vivo, likely due to the greater complexity of the in vivo microenvironment. To address this limitation, we plan to formulate Na2SeO3 and 1,25D3 as nanocomposites [44, 45]. By leveraging the long circulation, tumor‐targeting ability [46], and high drug loading capacity [47, 48] of nanocomposites, as well as the specific expression of surface receptors such as folate receptor (FR) on ovarian cancer cells [49], we aim to construct folate‐modified, dual‐drug co‐loaded nanocomposites for tumor‐specific targeting. This nanoplatform will enable precise delivery of the two agents to ovarian cancer lesions, further enhancing their combined therapeutic efficacy while mitigating off‐target toxicities of free drugs.

In addition, we note the clinical observation that numerous patients with advanced ovarian cancer acquire resistance to FR inhibitors through the downregulation of FR expression. Preliminary data from our laboratory demonstrated that 1,25D3 upregulated FRα expression in both OVCAR3 and ID8 ovarian cancer cells. Building on this observation, we speculate that nanocomposites loaded with both therapeutic agents, in combination with agents such as anti‐FRα antibody drug conjugates (ADCs), may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ADCs by improving tumor targeting in ovarian cancer patients with low FRα expression. This would expand the clinical applicability of anti‐FRα ADCs and further improve therapeutic outcomes in ovarian cancer.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrated that Na2SeO3 in combination with 1,25D3 induced SEPP1 expression, thereby enhancing the antitumor efficacy of low‐dose 1,25D3 against ovarian cancer. This combinatorial strategy paves the way for the safe and effective clinical translation of 1,25D3 in ovarian cancer therapy.
