A Minimax Theory of Nonparametric Regression Under Covariate Shift
Petr Zamolodtchikov

Abstract
We consider nonparametric regression under covariate shift, where we observe samples from both the target distribution and a related but distinct source distribution. We introduce a novel object, the transfer function, and show that properties of its domain determine our minimax rates. Those exhibit a variety of regimes, including classical rates, governed by the better of source-only and target-only rates, as well as regimes in which the convergence rates exhibit multiplicative interactions between the sample sizes and are faster than the best-of-two benchmark. The rates are shown to be achieved up to logarithmic factors by a design-adaptive estimator. Compared with existing theory, our results cover the case in which covariates have unbounded support.

Introduction

Transfer learning is a widely used approach to improving the performance of a predictive model by incorporating related data into its training set. The central premise is that different datasets may contain information about the task at hand, which the model extracts and leverages, leading to numerous successful implementations over the years. Examples include computer vision
[
17
,
32
]
, healthcare
[
1
,
11
]
, natural language processing
[
23
]
, and experimental particle physics
[
21
,
8
]
. Beyond the natural incentive to improve model performance, transfer learning is particularly interesting when relevant data are scarce.

In the setting of supervised learning, transfer learning aims to design algorithms that perform well on a
target
distribution
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}}
while being fit on a dataset either comprised of
n
samples from a
source
distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}},
or a mix of both
n
samples from the source and
m
samples from the target. Most of classical statistical learning theory had been derived under the assumption of distributional invariance between training and test datasets, that is
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}=\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}},
and, therefore, lacks the ability to explain, predict, and control transfer learning in practice. Hence, transfer learning is an active area of research
[
36
]
. The theoretical literature has largely crystallised around two mathematically distinct, yet complementary, paradigms arising from distributional shifts. The first is called
posterior drift
and refers to the setting in which the covariates’ marginals remain the same,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},
but the conditional distributions of the outputs vary across tasks,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}\neq\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{Y}\!|\mathrm{X}}.
See
[
2
,
7
]
and references therein. This paper addresses
covariate shift
(CS)
[
25
,
27
]
, which is the second setting where only the covariates’ marginals vary,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\neq\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},
while the output’s conditionals remain equal,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{Y}\!|\mathrm{X}}.

The main theoretical goal of analysing CS is two-fold. Firstly, one aims to identify the correct object that describes and quantifies transferability by modulating convergence rates. Secondly, one aims to design algorithms that optimally adapt to distributional shifts. In this article, transferability is quantified by the notion of
transfer function
, a
(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})
-dependent univariate function whose analytic behaviour encodes transferability. More precisely, the latter diverges at the boundary of its domain, and the location of this boundary governs achievable minimax rates. The second goal is achieved, up to logarithmic factors, by a design-adaptive
k
-nearest-neighbours regression estimator.

Quantification of transfer is a central topic in the CS literature. In the linear model, transfer is naturally described by the interactions of the source and target covariance matrices
[
20
,
26
]
. A dominant approach for empirical risk minimisers (ERM) addresses CS by re-weighting the empirical risk, thereby encoding transferability via moment assumptions about the density ratio
[
19
,
13
,
9
,
33
,
18
,
10
,
12
]
.

The line of work we now extend has departed from density-ratio-type assumptions and focused on geometric-type regularity of the source-target pair. Notably, in the classification setting, the pioneering work
[
16
]
introduced the
transfer exponent
, which measures the relative singularity of the source-target pair via the growth of their ball-mass ratios; see also
[
6
]
. In the same spirit, and in the context of nonparametric regression,
\alpha
-families
of source-target pairs were introduced in
[
22
]
and
[
28
]
.

In the nonparametric setting, typical convergence rates have the form
(n^{r_{S}}+m^{r_{T}})^{-1}\asymp(n^{r_{S}}\vee m^{r_{T}})^{-1},
which we call the
wedge rate
and identify as the minimum of a
source rate
and a
target rate
. The wedge rate almost always corresponds to the convergence rate of an estimator that selects the best between two separate estimators trained solely on
n
source samples and
m
target samples, respectively.

In the context of
\alpha
-families, Nadaraya-Watson estimators with fixed bandwidths are minimax optimal. However, in
[
24
]
, it was shown that the unpenalised least squares estimator exhibits much finer transfer properties, comparable to those of a Nadaraya-Watson estimator with pointwise-optimal, or design-adaptive, bandwidth. This led to faster rates for power-to-uniform transfer, where
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
is a power law and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}
is uniform, and motivated the study of design-adaptive estimators in
[
34
]
, where a local
k
-NN was first analysed. Additionally,
[
24
]
identifies a prototypical instance of multiplicative rates. The recent preprint
[
35
]
establishes a lower bound on the integrated convergence rates of power-to-uniform covariate shift under general smoothness, which provides finer insights into the multiplicative regime.

These observations reveal gaps in existing minimax theories pertaining to CS in the nonparametric regression setting. Firstly, the class of source-target pairs in
[
22
]
incorporates very irregular distributions. Those constitute the hard cases and lead to a theory that smooths away finer mechanisms, such as multiplicative rates. Secondly, transfer exponents and
\alpha
-families break down as soon as the design becomes unbounded. Indeed, both quantities then become infinite.

The approach via transfer functions leads to a simple yet expressive minimax theory of nonparametric regression under CS. The result is a single scalar parameter per occurring distribution that governs minimax rates. The analysis is carried out over a restricted class of source-target pairs whose local mass is sufficiently well-behaved, enabling our rates to capture the subtle mechanics of transfer. In particular, our approach yields a rich atlas of clearly identified regimes. As discussed in Section
4.3
, the restriction isolates a clean phenomenon that is structurally robust, and its principled weakening should only broaden the theory. The hard cases are very regular Pareto source-target pairs, a feature of a realistic minimax theory. The transfer function remains meaningful independently of the boundedness, or lack thereof, of the covariate support. Since all the rates are new, our theory also contributes to nonparametric regression with unbounded covariate support.

2
Preliminaries

Nonparametric regression model.
We consider the standard nonparametric regression model. Letting
(X,Y)
be a pair of random variables with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},
we assume the existence of a function
f_{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\displaystyle Y=f_{*}(X)+\varepsilon,

(1)

where
\varepsilon
is a centred random variable, independent from
X.
We additionally assume that
\varepsilon
is sub-exponential with parameters
\alpha,\nu>0.
That is, for all
|\lambda|\leq\alpha^{-1}

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[\exp(\lambda\varepsilon)]\leq\exp\Big(\frac{\nu^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\Big).

Moreover, we will assume that
f_{*}
lies in a Hölder ball
\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(L,\beta):=\bigg\{f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}:\|f\|_{\infty}+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|^{\beta}}\leq L\bigg\},

where
L>0
and
\beta\in(0,1]
are fixed constants.

Covariate shift.
In the setting of Covariate Shift (CS), we are given a sample of
m
observations from the target distribution
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and
n
observations from the source distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
with the constraint that they agree when conditioned on the covariates,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{Y}\!|\mathrm{X}}.

(CS)

Under (
CS
), if
(X,Y)
satisfies Model (
1
) under the source (resp. the target) distribution, then the exact same model is satisfied under the target (resp. the source) distribution. This means that the regression function
f_{*}
and the noise variable’s distribution are the same under
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}}.
The mismatch takes effect on the level of marginal distributions
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}.

Prediction loss.
Given a sample
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{P}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of i.i.d. observations from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and an additional independent sample
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}}=\{(X_{j}^{\prime},Y_{j}^{\prime})\}_{j=1}^{m}
of i.i.d. observations from
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}},
the predictive goal is to construct an estimator
\smash{\widehat{f}}(\cdot,\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}},\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}})
that minimises the Mean Squared Error
\ell(\smash{\widehat{f}}\,):=\operatorname{E}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}}}[(\smash{\widehat{f}}(X^{\prime})-Y^{\prime})^{2}|\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}},\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}}].
Under Model (
1
), this is equivalent to estimating
f_{*}
in the
L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})
loss

\displaystyle\ell(\widehat{f}\,)-\ell(f_{*})=\operatorname{E}_{X^{\prime}\sim\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}\big[(\widehat{f}(X^{\prime})-f_{*}(X^{\prime}))^{2}\big]=\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}.

Under Model (
1
), the distribution of
Y
is entirely determined by the distribution of the covariate
X,
the regression function
f_{*},
and the distribution of
\varepsilon,
meaning that specifying joint distributions of
(X,Y)
is redundant. Consequently, we will call
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
the source distribution and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}
the target distribution in the rest of this article.

Transfer function.
Within (
CS
) and Model (
1
), it is clear that minimax estimation rates depend on the source-target pair
(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}),
and, as mentioned in Section
1
, a key step is to identify and formalise a quantity that captures this dependence. Here, this dependence is quantified through the behaviour of the
transfer function
and its domain. Let us first introduce
\mathcal{M}
as the class of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that admit a Lebesgue density.

Definition 1

(Transfer function)
.

For a pair of distributions
\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}\in\mathcal{M},
and a real number
\gamma\in[0,\infty),
the transfer function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\gamma):=\operatorname{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Q}}[p(X)^{-\gamma}]\in[0,\infty],

where
p
and
q
are the respective densities of
\operatorname{P}
and
\operatorname{Q}.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\cdot)
measures the quantity of mass that
\operatorname{Q}
assigns to low-density regions of
\operatorname{P}.
For fixed
\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},
the transfer function is a
\log
-convex function of
\gamma
that typically blows up to infinity once its argument reaches the boundary of its domain. This leads to the definition of integrability indices.

Definition 2

(Integrability index)
.

For
\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}\in\mathcal{M},
we define the integrability index of
(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q})
as

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}):=\sup\{\gamma\geq 0:\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\gamma)<\infty\}\in[0,\infty].

Further properties of the transfer function and integrability indices are discussed in Section
4.1
.

Regularity.
The derivations in our results rely on additional regularity assumptions that restrict the class of covariates’ distributions in the scope of this paper. First, denote by
\mathcal{M}(D)
the class of measures in
\mathcal{M}
whose Lebesgue density is upper bounded by the constant
D>0,
which is arbitrary but fixed.

Definition 3
.

Let
0<\theta<\infty.
We define
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta)
to be the class of distributions
\operatorname{P}\in\mathcal{M}(D),
with density
p,
such that for all
x\in\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{P})
and all
r\in(0,1],

\displaystyle\theta^{-1}p(x)r^{d}\leq\operatorname{P}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}\leq\theta p(x)r^{d}.

(2)

We will refer to (
2
) as the
local mass
assumption. Definition
3
is satisfied for uniform distributions, or distributions with densities bounded away from zero and infinity, which is one of the most standard settings for nonparametric regression. Standard light-tailed distributions such as Gaussians do not satisfy (
2
), while heavier-tailed distributions such as Pareto and exponential do (see Examples
7
and
8
). If the density
p
of
\operatorname{P}
cancels at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
and
p(x)
is proportional to
\|x-x_{0}\|^{\alpha}
in a neighbourhood of
x_{0}
and some positive
\alpha,
then
\operatorname{P}
does not satisfy (
2
). We further discuss Definition
3
and its impact on our results in Section
4.3

Main Results

In this section, we state our upper and lower bounds before giving an analysis of the resulting rate exponents.
We work under the setting of Model (
1
) under (
CS
). We fix
D,\theta>0.
We will always denote the source distribution by
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and the target distribution by
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}.
As such, we also simplify notations and denote by
\gamma^{*}:=\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})
and by

For readability, we also denote by

3.1
Learnability and impact of covariate shift

In what follows, we abstract away the specific expressions of the multiplicative constants in the upper and lower bounds, retaining only their essential dependencies on the parameters. Explicit expressions can be found in the more precise statements in Appendix
B
, see Theorems
29
, and
32
. We now state our main results. On the one hand, we provide an upper bound for the supremum risk in probability over the class
\mathcal{H}(L,\beta),
which is valid pointwise for any source-target pair. On the other hand, we provide a lower bound on the supremum risk in expectation over
\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)
as well as classes of bounded transferability indices.

Theorem 4

(Upper bound)
.

Let
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(D,\theta).
Then, there exists an estimator
\widehat{f},
an integer
N=N(\theta,D)
and a constant
C(\tau)=C(\tau,d,\beta,\theta,\gamma^{*},s^{*})
, such that for all
\tau>1,
all
m\wedge n\geq N,
all
\gamma\in[0,\gamma^{*})
and all
s\in[0,s^{*}),

\displaystyle\sup_{f_{*}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\geq C(\tau)\mathcal{R}(n,m,\gamma,s)\Bigg\}\leq 3(n^{1-\tau}+m^{1-\tau}),

where the rate
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(n,m,\gamma,s)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\gamma)^{\tfrac{r_{\beta}-s}{\gamma-s}}\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},s)^{\tfrac{\gamma-r_{\beta}}{\gamma-s}}\Big(\frac{\log(nm)}{n}\Big)^{\gamma\tfrac{r_{\beta}-s}{\gamma-s}}\Big(\frac{\log(nm)}{m}\Big)^{s\tfrac{\gamma-r_{\beta}}{\gamma-s}}&\!\!\!\vbox{\hbox{\shortstack[l]{if $(\gamma-r_{\beta})(s-r_{\beta})<0$\\
and $m\in[n,n^{\gamma/s}]$,}}}\\
\Big[\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\gamma\wedge r_{\beta})\Big(\frac{\log(nm)}{n}\Big)^{\gamma\wedge r_{\beta}}\Big]\wedge\Big[\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},s\wedge r_{\beta})\Big(\frac{\log(nm)}{m}\Big)^{s\wedge r_{\beta}}\Big]&\!\!\!\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The pointwise nature of the result is desirable here: conditionally on the regularity imposed by
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta),
the rate depends on the particular source-target pair only through the transfer function. It is important to note that the estimator in Theorem
4
does not depend on the particular choice of source-target pair, nor on any knowledge of the transferability indices
(\gamma^{*},s^{*}).
It depends, however, on the knowledge of
2\beta/(2\beta+d).
In fact, we design a
local

k
-nearest neighbours regressor and discuss its construction in Section
4.2
. Theorem
4
identifies conditions on
(\gamma,s,r_{\beta})
for which a special regime
might
emerge, where the rate becomes multiplicative. This situation occurs when
(\gamma-r_{\beta})(s-r_{\beta})<0
. In this setting, the rates become multiplicative if, in addition,
m\in[n,n^{\gamma/s}].
The latter interval is understood as
[n^{\gamma/s},n]
if
\gamma/s<1
and
[n,n^{\gamma/s}]
when
\gamma/s>1.
As a result, the convergence rates depend on five parameters
(\gamma^{*},s^{*},r_{\beta},n,m).

It is noteworthy that the double role of
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}
as both (part of) the data-generating process, and the testing distribution in Theorem
4
is arbitrary. A careful reading of the proofs indicates that the same result holds if we instead consider that the data consists of
n
samples from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}^{(1)}
and
m
samples from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}^{(2)}
while keeping
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}
as the testing distribution. In this case, the relevant parameters in the rates’ exponents are
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}^{(1)},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})
and
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}^{(2)},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}).
This observation suggests natural extensions to multitask estimation
[
5
]
or domain generalisation
[
4
]
.

We now state a more specialised result that corresponds formally to the case
m=0
in Theorem
4
, in which the training data is sampled from the source distribution only.

Corollary 5

(Upper bound, pure transfer)
.

Let
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(D,\theta).
Then, there exists an estimator
\widehat{f},
an integer
N=N(\theta,D)\geq 2d\vee 3,
and a constant
C(\tau)=C(\tau,d,\beta,\theta,\gamma^{*})
, such that for all
\tau>1,
all
n\geq N,
and all
\gamma\in[0,\gamma^{*}),

\displaystyle\sup_{f_{*}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\mathbb{P}\Big\{\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\geq C(\tau)\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\gamma\wedge r_{\beta})\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{\gamma\wedge r_{\beta}}\Big\}\leq 3n^{1-\tau}.

In view of Corollary
5
, one notices that in configurations of
(\gamma^{*},s^{*},r_{\beta},n,m)
where the rate is not multiplicative, the rates in Theorem
4
are essentially the minimum of the rates obtained from selecting the best among two separate estimators, trained only on the source sample and only the target sample, respectively.

We complement the upper bounds from Theorem
4
with uniform lower bounds over classes of pairs with bounded transferability indices defined as follows

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{P}}(D,\theta,s,\gamma):=\Big\{(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})\in\mathcal{P}(D,\theta)^{2}:\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})\geq\gamma\ \text{ and }\ \gamma^{*}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})\geq s\Big\}.

With this definition, we obtain the following lower bound on the minimax risk over classes
\underline{\mathcal{P}}(D,\theta,s,\gamma).

Theorem 6
.

Work under (
CS
) and within the regression model (
1
) with Gaussian noise
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma^{2}>0.
Let
L,D>0,\ \theta\geq\operatorname{Leb}(\operatorname{B}(0,1)),\beta\in(0,1]
and
(\gamma,s)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1).
Then, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
independent of
n,m
such that for all
n\vee m>1
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{*}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)\\
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}\in\underline{\mathcal{P}}(D,\theta,\gamma,s)\end{subarray}}\operatorname{E}\Big[\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\Big]\geq\begin{cases}C_{0}m^{-s\tfrac{\gamma-r_{\beta}}{\gamma-s}}n^{-\gamma\tfrac{r_{\beta}-s}{\gamma-s}}&\vbox{\hbox{\shortstack[l]{if $(\gamma-r_{\beta})(s-r_{\beta})<0,$\\
and $m\in[n,n^{\gamma/s}]$}}}\\
C_{0}\Big[n^{-(\gamma\wedge r_{\beta})}\wedge m^{-(s\wedge r_{\beta})}\Big]&\text{ else. }\end{cases}

where the
\inf
is taken over all estimators obtained from
n
samples from
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and
m
samples from
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}}.

The same conclusion holds uniformly over classes of source-target pairs with fixed, rather than lower-bounded, transferability indices. The requirement
\theta\geq\operatorname{Leb}(\operatorname{B}(0,1))
can be omitted, as
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta)
is empty for
\theta<\operatorname{Leb}(\operatorname{B}(0,1))
as a consequence of Lebesgue’s differentiation theorem. The lower bound is proved for
s\in(0,1)
using Pareto source-target pairs. Borderline cases such as
\gamma\wedge s=0
or
s=1
can be treated with alternative constructions for the target distribution at the cost of additional technicalities and are not pursued here. The cases
s>1
or
\gamma=\infty
are out of the scope of this article as far as formal lower bounds constructions are concerned.

3.2
Analysis of the rates

As shown in Theorems
4
and
6
, the transferability indices govern the exponents in our convergence rates. Our statements of upper and lower bounds are asymmetric in the sense that the exponents in the upper bound are given for any
0\leq\gamma<\gamma^{*}
and
0\leq s<s^{*},
while the exponents in the lower bounds are expressed in terms of transferability indices. Moreover, the upper bounds also incorporate the transfer function as a multiplicative constant. This is natural, as the existence of lower bounds shows that the multiplicative constants must blow up for exponents that are larger than
\gamma^{*}
and
s^{*}
. The transfer function’s appearance as a multiplicative constant captures this effect. Under certain conditions on the type of singularity at the transferability index, those can be absorbed in the rate, at the expense of a poly-logarithmic factor. While we discuss this more in detail in Section
4.1
, we now proceed to analyse the convergence rates obtained in Section
3.1
.

The goal of the following sections is to describe the structure of the aforementioned convergence rates, their transitions and the different regimes arising from our main results. This leads us to prioritise readability and clarity. As a first step in this direction, we introduce vocabulary to describe the different situations arising from Section
3.1
.

Configurations.
We will refer to a configuration of
(\gamma,s,r_{\beta})
as
supercritical
when
(\gamma-r_{\beta})(s-r_{\beta})<0.
We will call a configuration of the same parameters
(sub)critical
whenever
(\gamma-r_{\beta})(s-r_{\beta})=0
(resp.
(\gamma-r_{\beta})(s-r_{\beta})>0
).

Regimes.
We will call
wedge regime
any configuration of
(\gamma,s,r_{\beta},n,m)
in which the rates are proportional to the
wedge rate

n^{-(\gamma\wedge r_{\beta})}\wedge m^{-(s\wedge r_{\beta})}.
Similarly, the
acceleration regime
refers to any configuration of
(\gamma,s,r_{\beta},n,m)
in which the minimax rates are proportional to the
accelerated rates

n^{-\gamma\tfrac{r_{\beta}-s}{\gamma-s}}m^{-s\tfrac{\gamma-r_{\beta}}{\gamma-s}}.

To further simplify exposition for the remaining Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, we omit all logarithmic factors and set the multiplicative constants to be equal to one. In particular, we omit the multiplicative occurrences of transfer functions. Finally, we will refer to the
n
-dependent part of the convergence rates as the
source rate
and to the
m
-dependent part as the
target rate
.

3.3
Heuristics, related works and examples

We now begin by providing a heuristic explanation of how and why the accelerated rates emerge. We then discuss our results in light of another work that obtained similar regimes, and conclude with examples of the resulting rates for concrete source-target pairs.

Heuristics for the emergence of an accelerated regime.
Let us now heuristically extract the mechanism behind the emergence, or lack thereof, of an accelerated regime. Firstly, we pick an estimator that optimises the bias-variance tradeoff
pointwise
in
x.
The corresponding steps are not relevant here and are detailed in Section
4.2
. This rules out any possibility of improving the
integrated
error rate. The resulting pointwise squared error is of the form

\displaystyle(\widehat{f}(x)-f(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(np(x)+mq(x))^{-r_{\beta}}\asymp(np(x)\vee mq(x))^{-r_{\beta}},

where we see that the bias-variance mechanics impose the exponent
r_{\beta},
and render it unavoidable. In particular, its appearance does not depend on any transferability index of the transfer functions, and this property would still hold, even for a weakening of the regularity assumptions of
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta).
We now introduce a pair of admissible parameters
(\gamma,s).
In order to derive an excess risk bound, the next step is to integrate the latter against
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}.
Writing this integral, and using
a\wedge b=\inf_{\lambda\in[0,1]}a^{\lambda}b^{1-\lambda}
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
and for
a,b>0,
results in

\displaystyle\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{E}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}\bigg[\inf_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\Big\{(np(X))^{-\lambda r_{\beta}}(mq(X))^{-(1-\lambda)r_{\beta}}\Big\}\bigg].

(3)

The minimising
\lambda
is zero if
mq(x)>np(x)
and one if
mq(x)<np(x).
However, in order to make the accelerated regime appear, we instead introduce a parameter
\tau>1
and a subset
E_{\tau}:=\{x:\tau^{-1}mq(x)\leq np(x)\leq\tau mq(x)\},
where
np(x)
is
comparable
with
mq(x).
For
x\in E_{\tau},
it holds that
(np(x))\wedge(nq(x))\asymp(np(x))^{\lambda}(mq(x))^{1-\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in[0,1],
so
\lambda
can be seen a free tuning parameter instead of being imposed by the imbalance between
np(x)
and
mq(x).
Thus, we can separate the integral into three terms. To do so, let us denote by
E_{-}:=\{x:np(x)<\tau^{-1}mq(x)\}
and by
E_{+}:=\{x:\tau mq(x)<np(x)\}.
This, and the previous considerations on the choice of
\lambda,
leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}&\leq\operatorname{E}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}\bigg[(mq(X))^{-r_{\beta}}\mathds{1}_{E_{-}}(X)\bigg]+\operatorname{E}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}\bigg[(np(X))^{-r_{\beta}}\mathds{1}_{E_{+}}(X)\bigg]\\
&\qquad+\operatorname{E}_{\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}}\bigg[(np(X))^{-\lambda r_{\beta}}(mq(X))^{-(1-\lambda)r_{\beta}}\mathds{1}_{E_{\tau}}(X)\bigg].\end{split}

(4)

As a result, the integrated error will be of the order of the dominating term in (
4
). It is crucial to mention that the third term, which is obviously the one leading to an accelerated rate, can not appear when all the samples come from either

\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
or
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}.
Intuitively, the third term
can
dominate under the condition that
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}(E_{\tau})
is large, i.e. when
np(x)
is of the same
“order”
as
mq(x)
on a large enough subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
This explains that the emergence of the accelerated regime depends on
n
and
m
. Strictly speaking, the size of
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}(E_{\tau})
also depends on
\gamma
and
s.
However, their roles are better explained as follows. Since the parameters
\gamma
and
s
are tied to the existence of moments of
p(X)^{-1}
and
q(X)^{-1}
under
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},
they dictate integrability, in terms of
\lambda,
of both terms inside the expectation in (
4
). As a consequence, they fully determine the optimal
\lambda
that leads (or not, depending on the configuration
(\gamma,s)
) to the accelerated rate. This concludes our heuristic explanation of the accelerated rates and their emergence.

Related work.
We now compare our results with the recent work
[
35
]
, which, alongside
[
24
]
, is, to our knowledge, one of the rare works in the literature that reports a multiplicative convergence rate for the covariate shift problem.

The authors show a similar result in the nonparametric regression setting with
d=1
for
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{U}([0,1])
and
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
in a one-parameter class of power laws, that is, for
a>0,
the density of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
is proportional to
x^{a-1}\mathds{1}(x\in[0,1]).
Although their rates are expressed in terms of
a
and
r_{\beta},
it is possible to compute the corresponding transferability indices. We find
\gamma^{*}=1/(a-1)
and
s^{*}=\infty.
With these notations, their minimax rates read

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f_{*}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}\Big[\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{*}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\Big]\gtrsim\begin{cases}n^{-r_{\beta}}\wedge m^{-r_{\beta}}&\text{ if }\gamma\geq r_{\beta}\\
n^{-\tfrac{2\beta+1}{2\beta+a}}\wedge m^{-r_{\beta}}&\text{ if }\gamma^{*}<r_{\beta}\ \text{ and }m\notin\Big[n,n^{\tfrac{2\beta+1}{2\beta+a}}\Big]\\
m^{-(r_{\beta}-\gamma^{*})}n^{-\gamma^{*}}&\text{ if }\gamma^{*}<r_{\beta}\ \text{ and }m\in\Big[n,n^{\tfrac{2\beta+1}{2\beta+a}}\Big].\end{cases}

First, we observe that our notion of (super/sub)critical configurations is consistent with their results. Indeed, since
s^{*}=\infty,
the only supercritical configuration is
\gamma^{*}<r_{\beta}.
Next, our setting does not include power-law behaviour due to the local mass assumption. However, heuristically sending
s
to infinity in our accelerated rates yields the same rate exponents as those in
[
35
]
. These matchings suggest a certain universality in the transferability indices. Supercritical configurations correspond to
r_{\beta}\in[\gamma^{*},s^{*}]
and, under additional conditions on
(n,m)
lead to an accelerated rate of the form
m^{-a}n^{-b}
with
a+b=r_{\beta}.

We now discuss aspects of the results that do not match. Sending
s^{*}
to infinity in our results leads to the acceleration regime being conditional on
m\in[1,n],
which is (typically) much larger than their interval
[n^{(2\beta+1)/(2\beta+a)},n].
In the (sub)critical configuration, our wedge rates do match theirs, but not in the supercritical non-accelerated case. The discrepancy in the rates indicates that the class of distributions itself influences both the rates and the range of
(n,m)
for which acceleration happens. Although tempting, we will not attempt to infer the missing parameter(s) by comparing the two results, especially since convergence rates computed for explicit densities tend to obscure the identification of key parameters, as will be seen in the examples below.

Examples.
We now give illustrative examples of rates obtained with Pareto and exponential source-target pairs. The proofs for those examples can be found in Appendix
C
. We stress again that we omit logarithmic factors and set multiplicative constants to one.

Example 7

(Pareto source-target pairs)
.

We consider the two-parameter family of Pareto distributions on
\mathbb{R}
denoted by
\operatorname{Par}(\alpha,\sigma).
For
\alpha,\sigma>0,
the density
r
of
\operatorname{Par}(\alpha,\sigma)
is defined as

\displaystyle r(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\Big(1+\frac{x}{\sigma}\Big)^{-(\alpha+1)}\mathds{1}(x\geq 0).

Let
\alpha_{\operatorname{P}},\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}},\sigma_{\operatorname{P}},\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}}>0
and consider
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}},\sigma_{\operatorname{P}})
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}},\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}})
with respective densities
p
and
q.
For
D,\theta
chosen as

\displaystyle D=(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}/\sigma_{\operatorname{P}})\vee(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}}),\ \text{ and }\ \theta=2\big(1+1/(\sigma_{\operatorname{P}}\wedge\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}})\big)^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}\wedge\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1},

it holds that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(D,\theta).
Additionally, the transferability indices are

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}=\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})=\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1},\ \text{ and }s^{*}=\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})=\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1}.

To identify the exponents in the rates, we can verify that

\displaystyle s^{*}<r_{\beta}\Leftrightarrow\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}<2\beta/d,\ \text{ and }\ \gamma^{*}<r_{\beta}\Leftrightarrow\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}>\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/r_{\beta}.

If both
\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}<2\beta/d
and
\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}>\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/r_{\beta},
then the wedge rates read

\displaystyle n^{-\tfrac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}}\wedge m^{-\tfrac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1}}.

The first necessary condition for the accelerated rates to occur is

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Big(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}<\frac{2\beta}{d}\ \text{ and }\ \alpha_{\operatorname{P}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{r_{\beta}}\Big)}_{s^{*}<r_{\beta}<\gamma^{*}}\ \text{ or }\ \underbrace{\Big(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}\geq\frac{2\beta}{d}\ \text{ and }\ \alpha_{\operatorname{P}}>\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{r_{\beta}}\Big)}_{s^{*}\geq r_{\beta}>\gamma^{*}}.

(5)

In other words, both distributions must be sufficiently heavy-tailed, or both must be sufficiently light-tailed. The second necessary condition for the accelerated rates to occur is

\displaystyle\underbrace{n\leq m\leq n^{\tfrac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1}{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}}}_{s^{*}<r_{\beta}\leq\gamma^{*}}\ \text{ or }\ \underbrace{n^{\tfrac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1}{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1}}\leq m\leq n}_{s^{*}\geq r_{\beta}>\gamma^{*}}.

In both cases, the accelerated rate is

\displaystyle n^{-\tfrac{(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1)r_{\beta}-\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}-\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}}}m^{-\tfrac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}-(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1)r_{\beta}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}-\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}}.

4
Transfer function, estimator, and local mass

This section provides technical details underlying our results. We begin by studying further properties of the transfer function introduced in Section
2
. We then give details on the estimator that achieves the rates in the upper bounds of Theorem
4
and Corollary
5
, and we end with a discussion on the class of distributions
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta).

4.1
Transfer function

This section exhibits several properties of the transfer function defined in Section
2
as well as its impact on the convergence rates of Theorems
4
and
6
. We describe its behaviour as a function of
\gamma
and explain what it measures. Then, we focus on its transferability indices. There, we link the latter to standard quantities, such as moments and Rényi entropies. Finally, we discuss how the blow-up of
\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\gamma)
close to
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q})
impacts the convergence rates in our upper bounds.

For this section, we consider two arbitrary distributions
\operatorname{P}
and
\operatorname{Q}
with respective densities
p
and
q.
Let us recall that the transfer function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\gamma)=\operatorname{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Q}}\big[p(X)^{-\gamma}\big].

The transfer function satisfies
\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},0)=1,
is
\log
-convex and
\gamma\mapsto\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\gamma)^{\gamma}
is non-decreasing (see Lemma
37
in Appendix
D
). The transferability index
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q})
is the eventual (maybe infinite) boundary point where the transfer function ceases being finite and blows up to infinity. Any
\gamma
for which the transfer function is finite gives an approximation of the amount of
\operatorname{Q}
-mass that lies in low-density regions of
\operatorname{P}.
Indeed, by Markov’s inequality, for any
0\leq\gamma<\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}),
it holds that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Q}\{x:p(x)\leq t\}\leq t^{\gamma}\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q},\gamma).

Similarly, the transfer function controls the truncated moments of
p(X)^{-1}
. This type of control is primarily technical and has been omitted here. It is, however, the type of control that bounds the third term in (
4
) of Section
3.3
and yields the accelerated rate. We now focus on the transfer function’s transferability indices.

First, whenever
\operatorname{P}=\operatorname{Q}
with unbounded support, we immediately have the upper bound
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{P})<1.
Additionally, lower bounds on the transferability index follow from the existence of generalised moments of the distribution.

Lemma 9
.

Let
X\sim\operatorname{P}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\rho,\varepsilon>0.
If
\operatorname{E}[\|X\|^{\rho+\varepsilon}]<\infty,
then
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{P})\geq\rho/(\rho+d).

When
\operatorname{P}\neq\operatorname{Q},
obtaining lower bounds on
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q})
from other known quantities is more involved. Here, we obtain lower bounds in terms of indices of Rényi/Tsallis divergences. For
\alpha>0,\ \alpha\neq 1,
the
\alpha
-Tsallis divergence between
\operatorname{Q}
and
\operatorname{P}
in
\mathcal{M}
is defined as

\displaystyle D^{T}_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Q}\|\operatorname{P}):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\Big(1-\int q(x)^{\alpha}p(x)^{1-\alpha}\,dx\Big)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\operatorname{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Q}}\Big[\Big(\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\Big)^{\alpha}\Big].

The Rényi divergence between
\operatorname{Q}
and
\operatorname{P}
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Q}\|\operatorname{P}):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q(x)^{\alpha}p(x)^{1-\alpha}\,dx=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\big((\alpha-1)(1-D^{T}_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Q}\|\operatorname{P}))\big).

Both divergences are finite for the same parameter
\alpha.
Finiteness of
D_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Q},\operatorname{P})
for some
\alpha
combined with knowledge of
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{P})
is sufficient to obtain a lower bound on
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}).

Lemma 10
.

Let
\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\alpha>0,\alpha\neq 1.
If
D_{\alpha}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q})<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{P})\Big(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\Big)\leq\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}).

The finiteness of Rényi/Tsallis divergences is equivalent to the density ratio
q/p
belonging to
L^{\alpha}(\operatorname{Q}),
therefore, Lemma
10
can be re-interpreted in terms of integrability of the density ratio, combined with integrability of
p^{1-s}.
The lower bound in Lemma
10
is not sharp in the sense that it can become loose for specific pairs
(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}).
For example, we can take
\operatorname{P}=\operatorname{Par}(a,1)
and
\operatorname{Q}=\mathcal{E}(1).
In this case,
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q})
is infinite while
D_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Q}\|\operatorname{P})
is finite for all
\alpha>1
and
\gamma(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{P})=a/(a+1).
The bound of Lemma
10
is not sharp but provides a link between integrability conditions and transferability indices.

\displaystyle\text{if }c\leq 1,\ \text{ then }\ \mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\gamma^{*})=\infty,\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\gamma^{*})<\infty\ \text{ otherwise.}

4.2
Local nearest neighbours regressor

To obtain our rates, we consider a
local

k
-nearest neighbours regressor (

-NN). Let

and
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}}:=\{(X^{\prime}_{i},Y^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be, respectively, two sets of
n
and
m

\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}}
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\operatorname{X},\mathrm{Y}}.
Given two neighbour functions
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and
k_{\operatorname{Q}},
the
local

k
-nearest neighbours estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}Y_{i}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}Y^{\prime}_{j}(x)\Bigg],

(6)

where
Y_{i}(x)
and
Y_{i}^{\prime}(x)
denote the label of the
i
-th nearest neighbour of
x
among
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and
\{X_{1}^{\prime},\dots X_{m}^{\prime}\},
respectively. The functions
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and
k_{\operatorname{Q}}
are chosen to balance bias and variance, pointwise and separately, for each data set
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{P}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}}.
Indeed, let us consider a
k
-NN regressor that depends only on
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{P}}.
Then, it is possible to show that, with high probability and pointwise, the squared error decomposes as

\displaystyle(\widehat{f}(x)-f_{*}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{R_{k}(x)^{2\beta}}_{\text{squared-bias}}+\underbrace{\frac{\log n}{k}}_{\text{variance}},

where the
\log n
factor follows from the high-probability nature of the statement, and
R_{k}(x)=\|x-X_{k}(x)\|_{2}^{2}
is the distance of
x
to its
k
-th neighbour among
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}.
The latter is then related to its population version
\zeta_{k}(x):=\inf\{r\geq 0:\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}\geq k/n\},
and, using the regularity assumption of our distributions, we obtain
\zeta_{k}(x)\asymp(k/(np(x)))^{1/d},
which leads to

\displaystyle(\widehat{f}(x)-f_{*}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\frac{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}{np(x)}\bigg)^{2\beta}+\frac{\log n}{k_{\operatorname{P}}}.

Balancing the squared bias and variance is done by choosing
k_{\operatorname{P}}^{*}(x)\asymp\log(n)^{d/(2\beta+d)}(np(x))^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}.
Now, since the distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
is unknown, we instead use a plug-in approximation. Finally, we need to clip
k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)
to stay within
\log n,
for the high-probability statement, and
n.
This gives us

\displaystyle k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)

\displaystyle\asymp\Big\lceil\log(n)\vee\log(n)^{d/(2\beta+d)}(n\widehat{p}(x))^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}\wedge n\Big\rceil,

where
\widehat{p}
is the
\ell
-nearest neighbours density estimator of
p
. Its expression is given, at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
by

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x):=\frac{\operatorname{P}_{n}\{\operatorname{B}(x,R_{\ell}(x))\}}{R_{\ell}(x)^{d}}=\frac{\ell}{nR_{\ell}(x)^{d}},

(7)

where
\operatorname{P}_{n}
is the empirical distribution of
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}.
The results in Theorem
4
and Corollary
5
are obtained by choosing
\ell\asymp\log((n\vee 1)(m\vee 1)).
This choice of
\ell
leads to a density estimator with high variance and low bias, which is coherent with our approach since all we need is to keep the ratio
p/\widehat{p}
within two positive constants, uniformly over
x
in a large subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The same procedure can be applied to select
k_{\operatorname{Q}}
from
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}}
and obtain the final estimator (
6

\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{P}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}},
the most informative sample. Plugging (
6

bounding, and rearranging leads to

\displaystyle(\widehat{f}(x)-f_{*}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\big(\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{P}}(x)-f_{*}(x)\big)^{2}+\frac{k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}{k_{\operatorname{P}}(x)+k_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)}\big(\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)-f_{*}(x)\big)^{2},

(8)

where
\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{P}}
is a
k_{\operatorname{P}}
-NN estimator trained only on
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{P}}
and
\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{Q}}(x)
is a
k_{\operatorname{Q}}
-NN estimator trained only on
\mathcal{D}_{\operatorname{Q}}.
We see that the pointwise error is a convex combination of the pointwise errors of
\smash{\widehat{f}}_{\operatorname{P}}
and
\smash{\widehat{f}}_{\operatorname{Q}}.
Notice that the neighbour functions
k_{\operatorname{P}}
and
k_{\operatorname{Q}}
are monotone in the underlying densities. Consequently, in regions of large imbalance between
p
and
q,
the weights in (

4.3
Local mass assumptions

In this last part, we discuss the local mass property of the distributions in
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta).
This is arguably the most restrictive assumption in our setting. Nevertheless, our constrained setting puts the proper highlight on the phenomenon.

One feature of our restricted setting is that it prevents minimax rates from being driven by nearly-singular distributions. Indeed, in
[
16
,
22
,
28
]
the lower bounds are derived along sequences (in
n,m
) of measures similar to that of
[
14
, Part III]
, which weakly converge to a limiting measure that is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure. In contrast, our lower bounds are obtained for Pareto distributions.

While our main results and the discussion in Section
4.1
suggest that the transfer function is the natural object to consider, even in broader settings, the local mass condition is not. We now recall the findings of Section
3.3
. We have seen that our results still make sense, to some extent, when we extend them formally to power law distributions. In particular, we captured the structure of the rates in the accelerated regime. However, neither the exponents in the wedge rate nor the thresholds in
n,m
were reproduced by our results. This suggests further interaction between transferability indices and other quantities. The latter would arise from the shapes of distributions that are
not
included in our setting. The derivation of a quantified weakening of (
2
) is out of the scope of this paper, but constitutes the natural continuation of our line of research. Another worthy direction is the case of dimensional mismatch between the supports of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}.
This could be approached within the same general framework of our paper by weakening (
2
) to only require that for some
\theta>0,
and
0<d^{\prime}\leq d,

\displaystyle\theta^{-1}p(x)r^{d}\leq\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}\leq\theta p(x)r^{d},\ \text{ and }\ \theta^{-1}q(x)r^{d^{\prime}}\leq\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}\leq\theta q(x)r^{d^{\prime}},

while keeping the upper bound
p\vee q\leq D.
In principle, it is natural to expect that the case of dimensional mismatch could be encompassed by a more general weakening of (
2
). We leave a precise treatment of this direction for future work.

Remark 20

A.2
Uniform pointwise absolute bias and standard deviation bounds

We now work within Model (
1
), where
f_{*}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)
with
L>0
and
\beta\in(0,1].
Let
\widehat{f}
be a local
k
-NN regression estimator with neighbour function
k\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{1,\dots,n\}
independent from
Y_{i}|X_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n.
That is, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{k(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(x),

(23)

where
Y_{i}(x),\ 1\leq i\leq k(x)
is the label associated to the
k
-th nearest neighbour of
x
among
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}.
We define the associated pointwise average function

\displaystyle\overline{f}\colon x\mapsto\operatorname{E}\big[\widehat{f}(x)|X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big].

(24)

The previous sub-section leads to the following bound on the pointwise bias.

Lemma 22
.

If
n\geq\|k(x)\|_{\infty},
then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle|\overline{f}(x)-f_{*}(x)|\leq 2L\wedge L\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}k(x)^{-1}R_{i}(x)^{\beta},

Additionally, if
\operatorname{P}\in\mathcal{P},
and
k(x)\geq\lceil\log n\rceil
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
then

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i\in k(\mathbb{R}^{d})}U_{n}^{\operatorname{P}}(i)\subseteq\Bigg\{\sup_{x\in\Theta_{n}^{\operatorname{P}}(S_{\tau},k)}\frac{p(x)^{\beta/d}|\overline{f}(x)-f_{*}(x)|}{k(x)^{\beta/d}}\leq\bigg(\frac{S_{\tau}}{n}\bigg)^{\beta/d}\Bigg\}.

Proof.

Since
f_{*}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta),
we get for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
and all
i\in\{1,\dots,k(x)\},

\displaystyle\big|f(X_{i}(x))-f_{*}(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big(f(X_{i}(x))+f_{*}(x)\big)\wedge L\|X_{i}(x)-x\|^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\wedge LR_{i}(x)^{\beta}.

This together with the definition of
\overline{f}(x)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}k(x)^{-1}=1
yields

\displaystyle\big|\overline{f}(x)-f_{*}(x)\big|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}k(x)^{-1}f(X_{i}(x))-f_{*}(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}k(x)^{-1}\big|f_{*}(X_{i}(x))-f_{*}(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}k(x)^{-1}\big(2F\wedge LR_{i}(x)^{\beta}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2L\wedge L\sum_{i=1}^{k(x)}k(x)^{-1}R_{i}(x)^{\beta}.

This proves the first statement. The second statement is obtained by further upper bounding the previously derived inequality by
|\overline{f}(x)-f_{*}(x)|\leq LR_{k}(x)^{\beta}
and applying Lemma
19
.
∎

Proposition 23
.

Let
\widehat{f}
be as in (
23
), with neighbour function
k\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{1,\dots,n\}.
Let
\overline{f}
be defined by (
24
).
Then, for all
n\geq 2d,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x)^{1/2}\big|\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)\big|\geq\eta\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq K_{d}n^{2d+1}e^{-H\eta^{2}},

where
K_{d}=2(25/d)^{d},
and
H=\lambda_{0}^{2}/(8\sigma_{0})
for arbitrary
\lambda_{0},\sigma_{0}>0
such that
\operatorname{E}[\exp(\lambda|\varepsilon|)]\leq\sigma_{0}.

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x)^{-1}[Y_{i}(x)-f(X_{i}(x))]\mathds{1}(i\leq k(x)).

Let
Z_{i}(x):=Y_{i}(x)-f(X_{i}(x))
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
By Proposition 8.1 in
[
3
]
and the regression model (
1
), conditionally on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, the random variables
\{Z_{i}(x)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are mutually independent with zero mean. By assumption, they are sub-exponential, and therefore, they satisfy the uniform noise condition; there exists constants
\lambda_{0},\sigma_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}\operatorname{E}\Big[\exp\big(\lambda_{0}|Z_{i}(x)|\big)\big|X^{n}=x^{n}\Big]\leq\sigma_{0}<\infty,

(25)

where
x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}.

By Lemma 12.1 in
[
3
]
,
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
and any
\eta\leq\min(1,2\sigma_{0})/\lambda_{0}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\big|\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)\big|\geq\eta\Big|X^{n}=x^{n}\Big\}\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{\eta^{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}k(x)}{8\sigma_{0}}\bigg).

(26)

To derive a high-probability bound that is uniform over
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
we introduce the following set of permutations

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x^{n}):=\bigg\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:\exists x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ X_{i}(x)=X_{\sigma(i)},\ \forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\bigg\},

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is the symmetric group over
\{1,\dots,n\}.
Theorem 12.2 in
[
3
]
states that for all
n\geq 2d,
and all
x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
the cardinality of
\mathcal{W}(x^{n})
is bounded by

\displaystyle\ell(x^{n}):=\operatorname{Card}(\mathcal{W}(x^{n}))\leq\Big(\frac{25}{d}\Big)^{d}n^{2d}.

In particular, given
x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
we can define the equivalence relationship
\sim_{x^{n}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as, for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\big(z_{1}\sim_{x^{n}}z_{2}\big)\Longleftrightarrow\Big(\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:x_{i}(z_{1})=x_{i}(z_{2})\Big),

where
x_{i}(z)
is the
i
-th nearest neighbour of
z
among
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}.
Further,
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\sim_{x^{n}}
has cardinality at most
\operatorname{Card}(\mathcal{W}(x^{n})),
and is in bijection with
\mathcal{W}(x^{n}).
Let then
\{A_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{W}(x^{n})}
be the elements of
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\sim_{x^{n}}.
We can now derive a high probability uniform upper bound by partitioning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
according to
\sim_{x^{n}},
using the union bound, and applying (
26
) as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle k(x)^{1/2}\big|\widehat{f}(x)-\overline{f}(x)\big|\geq\eta\Big|X^{n}=x^{n}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{W}(X^{n})}\Bigg\{\sup_{x\in A_{\sigma}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{k(x)^{1/2}}{k(x)}\big(Y_{\sigma(i)}-f_{*}(X_{\sigma(i)})\big)\Big|\geq\eta\bigg|X^{n}=x^{n}\Bigg\}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{W}(x^{n})}\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\ell^{-1/2}\big(Y_{\sigma(i)}-f_{*}(X_{\sigma(i)})\big)\Big|\geq\eta\bigg|X^{n}=x^{n}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{W}(x^{n})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\ell^{-1/2}\big(Y_{\sigma(i)}-f_{*}(X_{\sigma(i)})\big)\Big|\geq\eta\bigg|X^{n}=x^{n}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{W}(x^{n})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}2\exp\bigg(-\frac{\eta^{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}}{8c_{0}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big(\frac{25}{d}\Big)^{d}n^{2d+1}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\eta^{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}}{8c_{0}}\bigg).

We finish the proof by taking the expectation with regards to
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
on both sides.
∎

and obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{a}{h}

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\Big(n^{\gamma/(d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}})}h^{(2\beta+d)\gamma/(d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}})}\vee m^{s/(d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}})}h^{(2\beta+d)s/(d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\Big(n^{\gamma}h^{(2\beta+d)\gamma}\vee m^{s}h^{(2\beta+d)s}\Big)^{1/(d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\big(m^{s\lambda}\vee m^{s\lambda}\big)^{1/(d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}})}

\displaystyle=m^{\tfrac{s\lambda}{d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}+\tfrac{(1-\lambda)}{2\beta+d}},

and the latter is
m
taken to a strictly positive power, which shows that
M
diverges. This, and picking
\mu
large enough ensures that
M\geq 8\vee(32\log(2)),
and
a\geq\sigma
for all
m^{\gamma}<n^{\gamma}<m^{s}.
This proves the lower bound

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}_{f}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{1}C(\gamma,s)}{(4\sigma\mu)^{d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}}n^{-\gamma}\Big(\frac{n^{\gamma}}{m^{s}}\Big)^{\tfrac{r_{\beta}-\gamma}{s-\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{1}C(\gamma,s)}{(4\sigma\mu)^{d\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}}m^{-s\tfrac{r_{\beta}-\gamma}{s-\gamma}}n^{-\gamma\tfrac{s-r_{\beta}}{s-\gamma}}.

This finishes case 4.3, Case 4, and the overall proof.
∎

Lemma 34
.

Let
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{E}(\lambda),
then,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\lambda,e^{\lambda})
and
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})=1.
Additionally, if
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}),\ \operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}),
and
\lambda=\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}\vee\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}},
then,
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}
are both in
\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\exp(\lambda)).
Additionally,
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})=\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}/\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}.

Proof.

We first check the assumptions in Definition
3
. By definition, the density of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
is upper bounded by
\lambda.
Next, for any
x>0
and
0<t\leq 1,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}=\int_{x-t}^{x+t}\lambda\exp(-\lambda u)\mathds{1}(u\geq 0)\,du

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x}^{x+t}\lambda\exp(-\lambda(x+t))\,du

\displaystyle=t\exp(-\lambda t)p(x)\geq e^{-\lambda}tp(x),

This proves that the minimal mass property is satisfied with
\theta=e^{\lambda}.
Next, we prove the maximal mass property. Let
t\in(0,1].
If
0\leq x\leq t,
then
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}=1-\exp(-\lambda(x+t)),
and

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}}{tp(x)}=\frac{1-\exp(-\lambda(x+t))}{t\lambda\exp(-\lambda x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\lambda x)-\exp(-\lambda t)}{t\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp(\lambda t)-\exp(-\lambda t)}{t\lambda}=\frac{2\sinh(\lambda t)}{\lambda t}\leq\exp(\lambda t).

The function
g\colon t\mapsto 2\sinh(\lambda t)/(\lambda t)
continuously increases on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}.
Moreover,
g
admits a limit as
t\to 0,
which is equal to
2.
We have proven that for
0<t\leq 1
and
0\leq x\leq t,
it holds that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}\leq\theta p(x)t.
We now consider
t<x,
in which case we have

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,t)\}}{tp(x)}=\frac{\exp(-\lambda(x-t))-\exp(-\lambda(x+t))}{\lambda t\exp(-\lambda x)}=\frac{2\sinh(\lambda t)}{\lambda t}\leq\exp(\lambda t),

as previously, for all
0<t\leq 1.
We have shown that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\lambda,\exp(\lambda)).
Next, for
s\in[0,1),
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})=\int p(x)^{1-s}\,dx=\int\lambda^{1-s}\exp(-\lambda x(1-s))\,dx<\infty,

while for all
s\geq 1,\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})=\infty.
Hence,
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})=1.
Finally, we show the second claim. Let
\gamma\in[0,\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}/\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}).
Then,

\displaystyle\int q(x)p(x)^{-\gamma}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}^{\gamma}}\int\exp((\gamma\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}-\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}})x)\,dx<\infty,

while
\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\gamma)=\infty
for all
\gamma\geq\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}/\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}.
Hence
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})=\lambda_{\operatorname{Q}}/\lambda_{\operatorname{P}}.

∎

Definition 35

(Pareto distribution)
.

For
\alpha,\sigma>0,
we call Pareto distribution
\operatorname{Par}(\alpha,\sigma)
the distribution on
\mathbb{R}
with density given by

\displaystyle p(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}(1+x/\sigma)^{-(\alpha+1)}\mathds{1}(x\geq 0).

Lemma 36
.

Let
\alpha,\sigma>0
and
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha,\sigma),
then
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\alpha/\sigma,2(1+\sigma^{-1})^{\alpha+1})
and
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})=\alpha/(\alpha+1).
Additionally, if
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}},\sigma_{\operatorname{P}})
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}},\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}})
then
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}
are both in
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta)
with

\displaystyle D=\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}}{\sigma_{\operatorname{P}}}\vee\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}}{\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}}},\ \text{ and }\ \theta=2\Big(1+\frac{1}{\sigma_{\operatorname{P}}\wedge\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}}}\Big)^{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}\wedge\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1}.

Additionally,
\gamma^{*}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}})=\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1).

Proof.

Let
p
be the density of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha,\sigma).
We have, for all
0<r\leq 1,

\displaystyle(rp(x))^{-1}\int_{x-r}^{x+r}p(t)\,dt\leq\frac{2r\sup_{|t-x|\leq r}p(t)}{rp(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2p((x-r)\vee 0)}{p(x)}

\displaystyle=2\Big(1+\frac{x}{\sigma}\Big)^{\alpha+1}\mathds{1}(x\leq r)+2\Big(\frac{\sigma+x}{\sigma+x-r}\Big)^{\alpha+1}\mathds{1}(x>r)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+r/\sigma)^{\alpha+1}\leq 2\Big(1+\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big)^{\alpha+1}.

This implies the maximal mass property since

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}=\int_{(x-r)}^{x+r}p(x)\,dx\leq 2rp((x-r)\vee 0)\leq 2\Big(1+\frac{x}{\sigma}\Big)^{\alpha+1}p(x)r.

On the other hand, we have, for all
0<r\leq 1,

\displaystyle\frac{p(x+r)}{p(x)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\sigma+x}{\sigma+x+r}\Big)^{\alpha+1}\geq\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\sigma+1}\Big)^{\alpha+1},

which shows that the minimal mass property holds with
\theta=2(1+1/\sigma)^{\alpha+1}.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\{\operatorname{B}(x,r)\}=\int_{(x-r)}^{x+r}p(x)\,dx\geq rp(x+r)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}\Big)^{\alpha+1}rp(x).

Obviously,
\|p\|_{\infty}=\alpha/\sigma.
Therefore
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\alpha/\sigma,2(1+1/\sigma)^{\alpha+1}).

Next, we can check that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},s)=\big(\alpha/\sigma\big)^{1-s}\int\Big(1+\frac{x}{\sigma}\Big)^{-(\alpha+1)(1-s)}\mathds{1}(x\geq 0)\,dx

is finite if and only if
s<\tfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}.
Finally, if
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}=\operatorname{Par}(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}})
and
\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}}\in\operatorname{Par}(\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}),
then the inclusion of both into
\mathcal{P}(D,\theta),
with
D,\theta
defined in the claim, is obvious and we can see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}},\operatorname{Q}\mathstrut_{\!\mathrm{X}},\gamma)=\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}\sigma_{\operatorname{P}}^{\gamma}}{\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}^{\gamma}\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}}}\int\frac{(1+\tfrac{x}{\sigma_{\operatorname{P}}})^{\gamma(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1)}}{(1+\tfrac{x}{\sigma_{\operatorname{Q}}})^{\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}+1}}\mathds{1}(x\geq 0)\,dx

is finite if and only if
\gamma<\alpha_{\operatorname{Q}}/(\alpha_{\operatorname{P}}+1).
This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 37
.

Lemma 38

(Lemma 15 in

34

)
.
