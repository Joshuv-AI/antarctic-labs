Degradation of ACSL3 by influenza A virus shifts unfolded protein response from antiviral defense to viral evasion
Authors: Shuoxuan Zhao, Yichao Chen, Tianxin Zhang, Yaoyao Gao, Jing Wu, Fangji Yang, Yongxiang Liu, Xuesong Liu, Nan Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Host restriction factors and viral evasion strategies involved in the virus-host arms races remain to be discovered. Through an initial membrane protein-targeted CRISPR-Cas9 screening, we identified Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3) as a restriction factor against influenza A virus (IAV). Clinical transcriptomic analysis shows that ACSL3 is upregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from mildly ill patients with influenza. Ectopic expression and loss-of-function validation demonstrate the physiological role of ACSL3 in restricting viral proliferation of diverse IAV subtypes (H1N1, H3N2, H3N8) in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, ACSL3 potentiates the antiviral unfolded protein response (UPR) by engaging the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress sensors IRE1α and PERK, thereby enhancing the IRE1α-XBP1-s driven inflammation and inhibiting the PERK-ATF4-CHOP mediated apoptosis. Conversely, IAV triggers the lysosomal degradation of ACSL3 and thus reprograms UPR to promote an immunologically silent apoptosis for viral egress and dissemination. Our findings establish ACSL3 as a molecular switch balancing the UPR-mediated antiviral response, and reveal a targeted viral evasion strategy in the virus-host interplay.

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) remains a formidable public health challenge, responsible for triggering both seasonal flu waves and global pandemics (Xu et al., 2025a, Xu et al., 2025b). It spreads easily via respiratory droplets and causes a massive annual burden of about 1 billion infections worldwide (Jarboe et al., 2025). Most patients experience mild symptoms such as fever, cough, and fatigue (Bodin et al., 2025), while severe cases may progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), a condition associated with a high mortality rate (Verma et al., 2022). Although antiviral drugs targeting IAV proteins are currently in clinical use (Döhrmann et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2022; Saito et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2024), the high mutation rate of IAV compromises their long-term efficacy (Li et al., 2024). Rapid viral evolution drives drug resistance and vaccine escape, and this genetic variability significantly complicates disease-control efforts (Zhou et al., 2021). Unlike viral proteins, host factors hijacked by IAV for viral replication are highly conserved. Targeting these essential host components represents a promising therapeutic strategy. This approach offers potential broad-spectrum antiviral activity against diverse IAV subtypes (Yu et al., 2025), including H1N1, H3N2, and H5N1 (Keown et al., 2022; Haas et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024).

As an enveloped virus, IAV critically relies on host membrane compartments, including the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and protein folding machinery, to assist with viral entry, genome replication, and particle assembly (Alemany et al., 2025; Warfield et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2024). During IAV infection, rapid viral protein synthesis exceeds the ER folding capacity, causing the accumulation of unfolded and misfolded proteins and inducing ER stress. Consequently, this state disrupts ER homeostasis and activates the unfolded protein response (UPR) (Mazel-Sanchez et al., 2021). The ER stress originates from UPR and involves three main signaling factors: inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1) (Xue et al., 2024; Kettel et al., 2024), protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) (Koike et al., 2025), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) (Wilson and McCormick, 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Oka et al., 2022). The UPR exerts antiviral activity by restricting viral replication through activating inflammatory and interferon responses, or by inducing cell death (Fink et al., 2017; Carletti et al., 2019). Conversely, IAV can hijack or downregulate the UPR to facilitate viral protein folding and evade host immune defenses, ultimately accelerating infection progression (Mazel-Sanchez et al., 2021; Heindel et al., 2020; Macauslane et al., 2024).

IAV infection activates the Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1), and subsequently initiates multiple programmed cell death pathways (Zhang et al., 2020), including apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis. Necroptosis proceeds via the ZBP1-RIPK3-MLKL signaling axis (Gautam et al., 2024; Boyd et al., 2025; Verdonck et al., 2022), whereas pyroptosis is executed through NLRP3 inflammasome (Rosli et al., 2023, 2025). Both of them signify typical inflammatory modalities of cell death, and modulate host antiviral immunity by regulating inflammatory responses (Verdonck et al., 2022). Nevertheless, apoptosis represents a caspase-dependent non-inflammatory process. During IAV infection, ZBP1-, RIPK3-, and caspase-8-mediated apoptosis restricts viral propagation (Lei et al., 2023). However, the underlying mechanisms that promote non-inflammatory cell death to expedite immune evasion and viral dissemination need further clarification.

The acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family (ACSL) comprises enzymes that catalyze the conversion of long-chain fatty acids into acyl-CoA. This enzymatic process enables exogenous fatty acids to participate in metabolic pathways such as including β-oxidation, triglyceride synthesis, and phospholipid production (Griffin et al., 2024; Klasson et al., 2022). Accumulating evidence indicates that ACSL3 exerts a regulatory function in the metabolism of exogenous fatty acids and ferroptosis (Puente-Cobacho et al., 2025; He et al., 2025). Although ACSL3 is predominantly localized to the ER membrane (Poppelreuther et al., 2018a), no direct association between ACSL3 and ER stress has been reported to date.

By integrating clinical data with physiological and mechanistic studies, we identified an ER membrane protein, ACSL3, as a host restriction factor. ACSL3 restricts IAV infection by preserving the integrity of UPR signaling. Notably, we demonstrated that IAV exploits a specific form of immune-silent apoptosis to facilitate viral evasion. Based on these findings, we propose a model wherein IAV degrades ACSL3 to modulate distinct UPR pathways. This study reveals a previously unexplored, non-enzymatic function of ACSL3 and provides a promising therapeutic target for the development of antiviral agents against IAV.

Results

CRISPR-based screening identified ACSL3 as a host restriction factor against IAV

To the present, multiple high-throughput genome-wide CRISPR screens have been conducted to identify the host factors involved in restricting IAV infection. Given that membrane proteins play a critical role in the entry, replication, and release of influenza A virus (IAV), we performed a targeted screening of the membrane protein gene library (Fig. 1A). The lentiviral library was established by using 18,988 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting 4497 genes. After transducing into A549 cells, puromycin selection was employed to enrich the cells in which membrane-associated genes were successfully knocked out. Subsequently, the enriched cells were infected with PR8-GFP virus, and then followed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). As shown in Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table S1, differential gene expression analysis was performed between the low-GFP and high-GFP cell populations. Genes symbolized by red dots correspond to host restriction factors that inhibit IAV infection, while those denoted by blue dots represent host factors that facilitate IAV infection. Among these, ST3GAL4 (Zhao et al., 2021), BCL2L1 (Kakkola et al., 2013), and TLR2 (Dai et al., 2018) have been previously reported to promote IAV infection, supporting the reliability of our screening results to some extent. Enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that ER stress, along with ER-associated protein quality control and lipid biosynthesis, are crucial processes that impact IAV infection (Fig. 1C). Among the top-ranked DEGs, ACSL3 was recognized as a candidate of interest. Prior studies have demonstrated that ACSL3 interacts with the M2 protein of multiple IAV subtypes, including H1N1, H3N2, and H5N1 (Haas et al., 2023), which motivated us to investigate whether ACSL3 could serve as a potential broad-spectrum therapeutic target for IAV.

Furthermore, peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) were collected from healthy donors (HD), mild patients with influenza (MI), and severe patients with influenza (SI) (Fig. 1D). The expression level of ACSL3 was upregulated in MI group in comparison to the other groups (Fig. 1E). Besides, the analysis of publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets from patients with influenza (Gao et al., 2021) further revealed that ACSL3 is significantly upregulated in both neutrophils and goblet cells (Fig. 1F and G). Infection of H1N1 in A549 cells and mice also notably upregulated ACSL3 expression both in vitro (Fig. 1H–K) and in the lungs (Fig. 1L and M), indicating a consistent induction of ACSL3 following IAV infection. Previous studies have established that IAV infection triggers a robust secretion of TNF-α (Zhu et al., 2020; Brazee et al., 2020), a well-characterized transcriptional activator of ACSL3 (Jung et al., 2020). Given this, we hypothesized that the IAV-induced upregulation of ACSL3 might be mediated through the TNF-α signaling pathway.

ACSL3 was a broad-spectrum factor that restricted IAV infection

To further investigate the role of ACSL3 in IAV infection, siRNA-mediated knockdown and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of ACSL3 (Fig. 2A, D, and J) were carried out in A549 cells. We observed that either knockdown or knockout of ACSL3 led to a notable enhancement of IAV (PR8) infection (Fig. 2A–E, J and K). Additionally, we uncovered that IAV (PR8) replication was inhibited when ACSL3 was overexpressed in A549 cells (Fig. 2F–I). Furthermore, primary alveolar macrophages (AMs) from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and alveolar epithelial cells (AEC cells) from lungs were isolated for IAV infection in vitro. It is found that knockdown of ACSL3 in these two primary cells also significantly promoted IAV infection (Supplementary Fig. S1). Given that ACSL3 interacts with M2 protein of multiple IAV strains (Haas et al., 2023), we investigated whether ASCL3 could influence the infectivity of other IAV subtypes. ACSL3-deficient mice demonstrate increased susceptibility to IAV infection and more severe disease pathology

To establish a lung tissue-specific ACSL3-deficient mouse model, we delivered recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) with ACSL3 shRNAs to the lungs via intratracheal instillation (Fig. 3A). After instillation, ACSL3 expression in lung tissues was significantly reduced at 15 days (Fig. 3F). Subsequently, mice were infected intranasally with H1N1 (PR8) virus, and samples were collected on day 7 post-infection for downstream analysis (Fig. 3A). In comparison to the control group, ACSL3-deficient mice exhibited markedly greater body weight loss after viral infection (Fig. 3B). ACSL3 influences unfolded protein response (UPR)

To further investigate the underlying molecular mechanism, we carried out immunoprecipitation combined with mass spectrometry (IP-MS) and found that ACSL3 may interact with multiple ER stress-associated proteins (Fig. 4A). Considering that ACSL3 is primarily localized to the ER membrane (Poppelreuther et al., 2018b), we employed qPCR and Western blotting to evaluate the effects of ACSL3 on several crucial ER stress sensors and their downstream signaling pathways. Western blot analysis substantiated that ACSL3 knockdown upregulated PERK phosphorylation during the early phase of viral infection, and suppressed IRE1α phosphorylation at 12 h post-infection (Fig. 4B–E). The qPCR analysis indicated that the disruption of ACSL3 up-regulated the mRNA levels of ATF4 and CHOP, two downstream molecules in PERK pathway, during early infection. We further investigated the inflammatory effector molecules downstream the PERK and IRE1α signaling pathways. Knockdown of ACSL3 not only reduced the phosphorylation of p65 downstream of IRE1α and downregulated nuclear protein levels of XBP1-s and AP-1, but also enhanced eIF2α phosphorylation and upregulated nuclear ATF4 expression, both hallmark downstream events of the PERK signaling pathway (Fig. 4K–N). Besides, ACSL3 knockdown further suppressed the expression of IL6 and TNF-α downstream of the IRE1 pathway (Fig. 4O–R), while simultaneously upregulated the transcription of pro-apoptotic effectors BiK, BAX, and BAK1 (Fig. 4S–U), which are crucial downstream targets of PERK-ATF4-CHOP signaling axis. Furthermore, comparison of virus culture supernatants revealed that ACSL3 knockdown advanced the peak of virus release to 14 h post-infection, compared with 16 h in the control group. Thus, ACSL3 knockdown not only increased the virus titer in the supernatant but also accelerated viral release (Fig. 4V). Taken together, the results indicated that ACSL3 knockdown promotes the activation of PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 signaling pathway during the early phase of viral infection, triggering cell apoptosis and thereby promoting the release of the virus. Conversely, it suppressed the downstream IRE1α signaling pathway during the middle and late phases of infection, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response.

IAV interrupts the interaction between ACSL3 and PERK

Given that ACSL3 was localized to ER membrane and affected ER sensors, we wondered whether ACSL3 could interact with these ER sensors directly. To investigate, we employed confocal microscopy assays and observed that ACSL3 co-localized with both PERK and IRE1, but not ATF6 or BiP (Fig. 5A). Co-immunoprecipitation assays further indicated that increasing IAV viral titer decreased the interaction between ACSL3 and PERK, whereas the interaction between ACSL3 and IRE1α remained unaffected (Fig. 5B–D). To elucidate the mechanism by which ACSL3 regulates the UPR, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using three ACSL3 truncation mutants (Poppelreuther et al., 2012): a mutant lacking the N-terminal region (including the transmembrane domain), one lacking the AMP-binding domain (the catalytic site), and one lacking the C-terminal region. We observed that endogenous IRE1α specifically bound to the AMP-binding region of ACSL3. Given that both IRE1 and PERK possess endoplasmic reticulum (ER) luminal and cytoplasmic domains (Ferri et al., 2020; Ji et al., 2025; Amin-Wetzel et al., 2017), we further investigated the binding sites on the UPR sensors by generating domain-deletion mutants for both proteins. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis demonstrated that ACSL3 binds to the cytoplasmic domain of IRE1α (Fig. 5F). Combined with our previous observation, these data suggest that ACSL3 interacts with the cytoplasmic region of IRE1α through its AMP-binding domain to enhance kinase activity, thereby promoting XBP1 signaling and the antiviral response.

In contrast, deletion of the ER luminal domain abolished the interaction between ACSL3 and PERK (Fig. 5G), indicating that the luminal domain is essential for this association. Furthermore, ACSL3 overexpression increased the interaction between BiP and PERK in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5H and I). Because BiP binding suppresses PERK autophosphorylation and downstream signaling (Kopp et al., 2019), our findings indicate that ACSL3 inhibits the PERK pathway by facilitating the recruitment of BiP to PERK.

We next investigated the stability of ACSL3 during IAV infection and observed that high-dose viral infection reduced ACSL3 protein levels (Fig. 5J and K). To determine the degradation pathway, we treated cells with cycloheximide (CHX) to block de novo protein synthesis. Inhibition of the lysosomal pathway with Bafilomycin A1 significantly rescued ACSL3 expression, whereas the proteasome inhibitor MG132 failed to prevent its degradation (Fig. 5L and M). These data indicate that IAV promotes ACSL3 degradation primarily via the lysosomal pathway. Collectively, our results suggest a viral counter-defense mechanism: while ACSL3 enhances antiviral signaling and suppresses the PERK pathway, IAV neutralizes these host-protective effects by inducing the lysosomal degradation of ACSL3.

The PERK pathway inhibitor ISRIB negated the pro-viral effects of ACSL3 depletion

To determine whether ACSL3 modulated IAV infection was contingent upon UPR, we employed specific pharmacological agents to target IRE1 pathway or PERK pathway (Fig. 6A and H). We observed that the IRE1 agonist IXA4 induced a dose-dependent reduction in IAV NP transcription; however, it was unable to reverse the pro-viral phenotype caused by ACSL3 knockdown (Fig. 6B–D). In contrast, the IRE1 inhibitor 4μ8C abolished the augmenting effect of si-ACSL3 on viral replication, notwithstanding its lack of inherent antiviral activity (Fig. 6E–G). The fact that IRE1 inhibition negated the influence of ACSL3 depletion implied that ACSL3 acts downstream of the IRE1 axis. Conversely, the PERK agonist CCT020312 suppressed IAV NP transcription in a dose-dependent manner and progressively alleviated the pro-viral phenotype triggered by ACSL3 knockdown (Fig. 6I–K). This finding is consistent with our previous results, indicating that ACSL3 depletion activated the PERK pathway (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the PERK inhibitor ISRIB significantly mitigated the viral augmentation induced by ACSL3 knockdown (Fig. 6L–N). ACSL3 serves as a crucial regulator of ferroptosis that promotes monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) phospholipid synthesis to inhibit lipid peroxidation, and its function is contrary to that of ACSL4 (Sae-Fung et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2025). Then we next explored whether ACSL3 modulates IAV infection via ferroptosis, as well as its function in the UPR. By using the ferroptosis inhibitor (Deferoxamine Mesylate), we found that blocking ferroptosis generally led to a reduction in viral load. However, it was unable to eliminate the pro-viral phenotype induced by ACSL3 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S2). This finding effectively dissociates the antiviral activity of ACSL3 from its metabolic regulation of ferroptosis.

Discussion

Infection of IAV initiates multiple modalities of programmed cell death, encompassing apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and the integrated pathway known as PANoptosis (Xu et al., 2025a, Xu et al., 2025b). These processes play a crucial role in host’s antiviral immunity and the spatiotemporal regulation of inflammation. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that ZBP1-mediated sensing of IAV triggers distinct programmed cell death pathways. Among these, apoptosis occurs in a non-inflammatory manner to limit viral spread (Kayagaki et al., 2024); while necroptosis, pyroptosis, and PANoptosis drive robust inflammatory responses and adaptive immune activation (Pandeya and Kanneganti, 2024; Yuan and Ofengeim, 2024; Newton et al., 2021). In the present study, we demonstrate that IAV rewires the UPR pathway through the degradation of ACSL3, thereby shifting the host response from an antiviral state to an immunosuppressive apoptotic state that is conductive to immune evasion.

We identified ACSL3 as a host restriction factor through a membrane protein CRISPR screen. One limitation of our approach is that sorting by GFP intensity cannot cleanly separate uninfected cells from those with very low infection. This may lead to misclassification of sgRNA-positive cells and introduce both false positives and false negatives. We used strict FACS gating and biological replicates to reduce this uncertainty, but some ambiguity is unavoidable in pooled screens that rely on fluorescent reporter viruses. Future studies using complementary methods will help refine the list of candidate hits.

In Fig. 2, Fig. 3, we demonstrated that ACSL3 inhibits WT PR8 replication in vivo and across multiple cell lines, including primary cells. ACSL3 knockdown also reduced the replication of H1N1 (WSN), H3N2 and H3N8 viruses, supporting the conclusion that ACSL3 is a broad-spectrum restriction factor for IAV. However, supplementary experiments (Supplementary Fig. S1E–H) revealed that the effect of ACSL3 is virus-dependent. Knockdown of ACSL3 inhibited HSV replication but promoted CVB3 replication. This divergence likely reflects the reliance of different viruses on distinct host machineries, and the underlying reasons are currently unclear. Importantly, these observations do not weaken the central conclusion that ACSL3 functions as a restriction factor for IAV, which is firmly supported by the data in this study.

ACSL3 is a long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase that plays a central role in the activation of long-chain fatty acids. ACSL3 suppresses ferroptosis through the activation of MUFAs and modulation of arachidonic acid synthesis (Magtanong et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2023). However, we identify ACSL3 as a crucial regulator of the UPR beyond its typical enzymatic activity. Our study identifies ACSL3 as a key orchestrator of the UPR during IAV infection, functioning as a molecular switch that differentially modulates UPR branches. We show that ACSL3 interacts with the cytoplasmic domain of IRE1α to enhance XBP1 signaling, thereby sustaining the host’s adaptive antiviral response. Concurrently, ACSL3 suppresses the PERK pathway by facilitating the recruitment of BiP to PERK, which likely prevents premature translational arrest and apoptosis. Collectively, these findings suggest that ACSL3 fine-tunes the UPR to optimize host cell survival and immune defense during viral stress, positioning this enzyme as a critical node in the integration of lipid metabolism and ER proteostasis. Our findings reveal that IAV triggers the lysosomal degradation of ACSL3, resulting in a functional rewiring of UPR signaling pathway. Specifically, activation of the PERK-ATF4-CHOP axis drives apoptosis to facilitate viral dissemination, while the concurrent inactivation of the IRE1α–XBP1 axis creates an immunosuppressive environment that is favorable for viral proliferation.

We found that ACSL3 is significantly upregulated in mild IAV infections clinically but not in severe cases. We hypothesize that the induction of ACSL3 activates the antiviral UPR immunity in mild cases, while ACSL3 was degraded in severe infections due to excessive expression of viral proteins. This loss of ACSL3 functionally remodels the UPR into an immunosuppressive and apoptotic signaling network that facilitates viral evasion. Additionally, our study establishes that ACSL3 regulates IAV infection independently of the ferroptosis pathway, while the investigation of the metabolic crosstalk between ACSL3 and IAV warrants further attention. Moving forward, our translational efforts will focus on developing targeted therapeutics, including small-molecule stabilizers that prevent ACSL3 degradation.

Conclusions

By integrating clinical, physiological, and mechanistic evidence, our study establishes ACSL3 as a broad-spectrum restriction factor against IAV. We identify ACSL3 as a critical molecular switch orchestrating UPR signaling. Furthermore, we elucidate a mechanism whereby IAV subverts this pathway by triggering ACSL3 degradation, effectively reprogramming the UPR to create an immunosuppressive and pro-apoptotic microenvironment that facilitates viral dissemination.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

A549 (Procell, Cat# CL-0016) and HEK293T (ATCC, Cat# CRL-3216) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (P/S; Solarbio, China). AMs from BALF were cultured in a medium composed of RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 20% FBS, 2% P/S, 50 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; ABclonal, China), and 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME; Invitrogen, USA). AECs from Lung tissues were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 20% FBS, 2% P/S, 50 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Peoprotech, USA), and 55 μM β-ME. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and were tested as mycoplasma free.

Plasmids construction

The plasmids pCMV-ACSL3-3×Flag, pCMV-PERK-3×Myc, pCMV-IRE1-EGFP, and pCMV-EGFP-ATF6-Flag were obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform. The PERK ∆(30–514) and PERK∆ (536–1116) fragments were amplified from the pCMV-PERK-3×Myc plasmid by PCR and subsequently cloned into the pCMV-3×Myc empty vector to generate the mutant plasmids pCMV-PERK∆ (30–514)-3×Myc and pCMV-PERK∆ (536–1116)-3×Myc. The PERK fragment was amplified by PCR from pCMV-PERK-3×Myc and subcloned into pEGFP to generate pEGFP-PERK. The IRE1∆(1–443) and IRE1∆(548–977) fragments were amplified from the pCMV-IRE1-EGFP plasmid by PCR and subsequently cloned into the pCAGGS-HA empty vector to generate the mutant plasmids pCAGGS-HA-IRE1∆(1–443) and pCAGGS-HA-IRE1∆(548–977). The ACSL3 fragment was amplified by PCR from pCMV-ACSL3-3×Flag and subcloned into pmCherry to generate pmCherry-ACSL3. The ACSL3∆(1–135), ACSL3∆(136–612) and ACSL3∆(613–720) fragments were amplified from the pCMV-ACSL3-3×Flag plasmid by PCR and subsequently cloned into the pCMV-3×Flag empty vector to generate the mutant plasmids pCMV-ACSL3∆(1–135)-3×Flag, pCMV-ACSL3∆(136–612)-3×Flag and pCMV-ACSL3∆(613–720)-3×Flag. The full-length BiP gene was amplified from human cDNA by PCR and subcloned into the pEGFP vector to generate the pEGFP-BiP plasmid.

AAV-mediated ACSL3 knockdown mice and virus infection

The recombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAVs) encoding sh-ACSL3 and sh-NC were packaged by PackGene Biotech with a titer of 1 × 1013 genome copies per milliliter (GC/mL). A volume of 20 μL of AAV was administered intratracheally to each mouse for targeted delivery to the lungs. Fourteen days after AAV administration, mice were intranasally inoculated with H1N1 influenza virus (A/Puerto Rico/8/1934) at a dose of 200 plaque-forming units (PFU) per animal. Lung tissues were harvested from mice on day 7 post-H1N1 infection for downstream analysis.

Viral strains and virus infection

The H1N1 (A/Puerto Rico/8/1934) (PR8) was kindly provided by Professor Shaobo Wang (Guangzhou National Laboratory). The H3N2 (A/China/LZP/2017) and H1N1 (A/WSN/1933) were kindly provided by Dr. Wenjun Song (Guangzhou National Laboratory). The H3N8 (A/Henan/4-10/2022) was kindly provided by Professor Shen Yongyi from South China Agricultural University. The PR8-GFP were rescued by our research group using the reverse genetics method. The HSV-1 strain was a kind gift from Dr. Jing Wu. The CVB3 Woodruff strain was a kind gift from Professor Wenran Zhao (Harbin Medical University). These viruses were prepared as viral stocks in DMEM at the specified MOI. Cells were washed with PBS and then inoculated with the virus dilutions, followed by incubation at 35 °C for 1 h in a humidified CO2 incubator. Subsequently, the virus dilutions was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with TPCK at a final concentration of 1:10,000 for continued culture.

CRISPR-Cas9 screening

The membrane protein library plasmid was kindly provided by Professor Qiong Zhang. HEK293T cells were transfected with the plasmids pGMV-dR189, pMD2.G, and Lenti-sgRNA/Lenti-GFP. At 72 h post-transfection, the cell culture supernatant and cells were harvested. After centrifugation, the packaged CRISPR library viral particles were collected.

A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with packaged virus at volumes of 0, 125, 250, 500, 750, and 1000 μL per well, respectively. Polybrene (2 μL, 10 mg/mL) was added to each well simultaneously to enhance viral transduction efficiency. At 48 h post-infection, puromycin was applied at a final concentration of 4 μg/mL for selection. The number of cells required was calculated based on the total number of sgRNAs in the library. Use a library virus to infect A549 cells at a MOI of 0.3, and add 4 μg/mL puromycin to select for 2 more days after 48 h. Then, replace the culture medium containing 2 μg/mL puromycin and infect the cells with PR8-GFP using a MOI of 5. Cells were collected from the infected group and the control group (uninfected cells), resuspended in PBS supplemented with 1% FBS, and adjusted to a final concentration of 107 cells/mL. The cell suspension was then filtered through a 70 μm nylon membrane prior to FACS. Cells exhibiting high GFP fluorescence intensity and those with low GFP fluorescence intensity were separately collected. Genomic DNAs were extracted, followed by PCR amplification using primers (lentiV2-F: CCGAGGGCCTATTTCCCATGATT; lentiV2-R: CCGACTCGGTGCCACTTTTTCAA). The resulting amplicons were purified and recovered, and subsequently submitted to Azenta for next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit. Reverse transcription was subsequently performed with HyperScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR containing gDNA Remover to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. RT-qPCR was conducted using 2 × S6 Universal SYBR qPCR Mix according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The data were normalized to the internal reference gene GAPDH, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical analyses and graphical visualization were performed with GraphPad Prism software. Primers are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Immunoblot

Proteins were extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA (strong) lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. These were mixed with SDS-loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 10 min to achieve complete protein denaturation. Proteins were then separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with appropriate primary and secondary antibodies, and target proteins were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Public single-cell sequencing data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE176269. Individual cells from each sample were aggregated to generate a composite pseudocell, designed to capture the characteristic gene expression patterns of a specific cell type within the sample. The analytical workflow consisted of two main steps: first, cells were clustered based on transcriptional similarity, and gene expression profiles were organized alphabetically by gene name across all selected cells. Subsequently, aggregate gene expression values were calculated and normalized using transcripts per million (TPM) scaling, which involved dividing the total counts by the sum of expression values and multiplying by one million. To assess and correct for batch effects across different datasets, the pseudocells generated in the prior step were processed using Seurat (v5) with default parameters, applying the functions ‘NormalizeData’, ‘FindVariableFeatures’, ‘ScaleData’, ‘RunPCA’, ‘FindNeighbors’, ‘FindClusters’, and ‘RunUMAP’. The resulting UMAP visualization was used to evaluate the presence of batch effects across projects. If significant batch effects were observed, the ‘removeBatchEffect’ function from the ‘limma’ package was applied to adjust for technical variation.

Differentially expressed genes between the healthy control (HC) group and the Influenza A group within each pseudocell were identified using the ‘DESeq2’ package, and subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to characterize the biological functions associated with these genes. The ‘UpSetR’ package was utilized to visualize overlapping gene sets across all cell types, providing a systematic and integrative view of both shared and cell type-specific molecular signatures.

Immunofluorescence and laser confocal microscopy

HEK293T cells were seeded in glass-bottom confocal culture dishes and transfected with corresponding plasmids. After 48 h of transfection, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 and blocked with PBS solution containing 10% FBS. The primary antibody was diluted according to the recommended ratio in the instruction manual and incubated overnight at 4 °C; the secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After DAPI staining of the cell nuclei, imaging was performed using a Zeiss laser confocal microscope (LSM 980).

Immunoprecipitation

Cell lysates were prepared with RIPA (weak) buffer and incubated with ACSL3 antibody at 4 °C overnight. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated for 2 h to capture immune complexes. After isolation using a magnetic rack, the supernatant was removed. The beads were washed with wash buffer and the immunoprecipitated complexes eluted with elution buffer for immunoblotting. Anti-DYKDDDDK (Flag) Tag magnetic beads were directly incubated with the cell lysate for 2 h at 4 °C. The subsequent procedures were the same as those for Protein A/G magnetic beads.

Virus biological safety levels

All experiments involving H1N1 (PR8), H1N1 (WSN), H3N2, H3N8, and AAV were performed in a biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory.

Replicates

All in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. The animal experiments and those involving clinical samples were all assessed for reliability through power analysis.

Statistical analyses

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze gene expression characteristics based on published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. All expression level analyses by Western blotting and qPCR were performed using GraphPad Prism. Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using the unpaired t-test, while those among four groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA.
