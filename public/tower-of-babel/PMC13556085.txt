TREM2 restrains myeloid inflammasome activation to protect against photoreceptor degeneration
Authors: Tianxi Wang, Liyun Su, Manon Szczepan, Xingyan Wang, Austin T. Gregg, Jessica Guo, Lois E. H. Smith, Ye Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myeloid cells, including infiltrating macrophages and resident microglia, are critical regulators of retinal homeostasis and respond rapidly to photoreceptor stress. Dysregulated myeloid responses, however, can exacerbate retinal degeneration. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) modulates phagocytosis, metabolism, and inflammatory signaling, yet its role in retinal degeneration remains incompletely understood. Here, we investigated TREM2 function in the retinal degeneration 10 (rd10) mouse model of inherited retinal degeneration, characterized by progressive photoreceptor loss and robust myeloid cell activation. TREM2 expression was upregulated in degenerating retinas, and global TREM2-deficiency in rd10 mice exhibited accelerated photoreceptor cell death, reduced outer nuclear layer thickness, disrupted retinal pigment epithelium integrity, and altered microglial spatial dynamics. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed that TREM2-positive microglia express APOE-associated and interferon-primed programs. Global TREM2 deficiency was associated with increased inflammasome-related signaling in retinal myeloid cells, including elevated cleaved caspase-1, cleaved gasdermin D, and mature interleukin-1β, linking amplified immune priming to pyroptotic signaling. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of gasdermin D significantly mitigated photoreceptor loss in global TREM2-deficient rd10 retinas, demonstrating a functional contribution of inflammasome-associated responses to disease exacerbation. Together, these findings support a protective role for TREM2-associated immune regulation in the degenerating retina and identify downstream inflammasome pathways as potential therapeutic targets in retinal degenerative diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03903-2.

Introduction

Myeloid cells are central regulators of tissue homeostasis, coordinating immune surveillance, inflammatory responses, and tissue repair following injury. In the retina, resident microglia and recruited macrophages rapidly respond to neuronal stress and degeneration to maintain tissue integrity. However, increasing evidence indicates that dysregulated myeloid responses actively contribute to the progression of retinal degenerative diseases, including retinitis pigmentosa (RP), where excessive or maladaptive inflammation exacerbates neuronal loss and tissue remodeling [1]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms that govern their activation and functional consequences remain incompletely defined [2, 3].

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is an innate immune receptor selectively expressed by myeloid populations [4, 5]. It is a transmembrane receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily that binds lipids, lipoproteins, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released during cellular stress [6, 7]. Through its association with the adaptor protein DNAX-activation protein 12, TREM2 integrates these signals to modulate myeloid cell metabolism, phagocytic capacity, survival, and inflammatory output [8]. In the central nervous system, TREM2 has emerged as a critical regulator of microglial activation, promoting tissue adaptation while restraining inflammatory damage [8–12]. TREM2 has been extensively investigated in the brain [9, 13–15], where it regulates microglial activation and phagocytosis [16–18]. Furthermore, genetic and functional studies have linked TREM2 to the regulation of lipid metabolism, complement activation, and inflammatory restraint [5, 18–21]. In the retina, TREM2 similarly contributes to protective myeloid programs. Its expression is upregulated in human retinal degeneration (RD), and genetic deficiency of TREM2 accelerates disease progression in mouse models, accompanied by altered microglial accumulation and amplified inflammatory signaling [22, 23]. Recent work has identified TREM2-positive microglial subsets that facilitate clearance of degenerating debris [23], highlighting the importance of TREM2-dependent myeloid programs in retinal homeostasis.

The retinal degeneration 10 (rd10) mouse model of inherited RD provides a genetically defined system in which progressive photoreceptor loss elicits robust myeloid activation and redistribution [24–26]. During this process, microglia and macrophages migrate into the outer nuclear layer and adopt transcriptional programs associated with innate immune activation [25, 26]. While these myeloid responses have often been viewed as secondary to neuronal death, accumulating evidence suggests that myeloid cell–intrinsic immune regulation critically shapes disease outcome [27]. Recent work has identified TREM2 as a critical regulator of these responses in rd10 retinas, orchestrating myeloid cell behavior across multiple dimensions [18, 28]. In terms of immune reprogramming, TREM2 deficiency alters microglial activation states in a stage-dependent manner through signal transducer and activator of transcription 2 (STAT2) pathway, amplifying inflammatory signaling at early stages while impairing microglial survival and phagocytic capacity at later stages [29]. Regarding cellular dynamics, independent studies have demonstrated that TREM2 regulates microglial migration and phagocytosis in both rd10 model and chemically induced RD model, in part through modulation of type I interferon (IFN) signaling [28]. At the level of neuroimmune crosstalk, TREM2 deficiency exacerbates photoreceptor degeneration in models of retinal detachment [22] and accelerates retinal neurodegenerative processes through the complement pathway [18]. Together, these findings establish TREM2 as a central modulator of retinal myeloid behavior during degeneration, while also highlighting the complexity of its immunoregulatory functions.

Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies have identified TREM2–Apolipoprotein E (APOE)–associated myeloid programs as conserved regulators of myeloid cell function across inflammatory and neurodegenerative conditions [9, 30]. In contrast, dysregulated IFN signaling and inflammasome activation drive myeloid pathology [31, 32]. Excessive innate immune priming can lower the threshold for inflammasome activation, leading to caspase-1 activation, gasdermin D (GSDMD)–mediated pyroptosis, and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [33]. However, whether TREM2 modulates these innate immune pathways in retinal myeloid populations during degeneration remains largely unknown. In this study, we investigated the role of TREM2 in the rd10 mouse model of inherited RD by integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), global TREM2 deficiency, and genetic and pharmacological inhibition of GSDMD. We found that TREM2 expression was upregulated in degenerating retinas and that global loss of TREM2 was associated with accelerated photoreceptor degeneration, disrupted retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) integrity, and altered retinal myeloid cell responses. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis identified TREM2-associated retinal myeloid populations enriched for APOE-related pathways and further suggested that TREM2-associated APOE-related transcriptional programs were consistent with coupled lipid metabolism and immune response states in the degenerating retina. In addition, global loss of TREM2 was associated with enhanced IFN and complement priming, increased inflammasome-related signaling, and exacerbated photoreceptor degeneration in the rd10 retina. Furthermore, inhibition or genetic deletion of GSDMD partially mitigated retinal degeneration in TREM2-deficient rd10 mice. Together, these findings support a role for inflammasome-associated myeloid pyroptotic pathways in the exacerbation of RD following global TREM2 loss, highlight TREM2-associated immune regulation as a potentially protective mechanism in RD, and identify downstream inflammasome pathways as potential therapeutic targets.

Results

TREM2 deficiency accelerates photoreceptor degeneration and disrupts RPE integrity during RD

In rd10 mice, carrying a missense mutation in the Pde6b gene, there is primary rod photoreceptor degeneration followed by secondary cone loss [24]. In this model, accumulation of IBA1-positive myeloid cells was observed in the retinal outer nuclear layer (ONL) at postnatal day (P) 20, when ONL thickness was comparable to that of wild-type (WT) littermate controls (Fig. 1A), indicating enhanced early myeloid cell infiltration or activation in response to photoreceptor stress. These IBA1-positive cells exhibited marked morphological changes, including enlarged cell bodies, increased circularity, and reduced numbers of process intersections (Fig. 1B and C), indicating a reactive microglial phenotype in rd10 retinas.

We next characterized the temporal dynamics of TREM2 expression during RD by immunostaining, with antibody specificity validated in TREM2 knockout retinas (Fig. S1). Immunostaining of rd10 retinas at P15, P18, and P20 revealed a progressive accumulation of IBA1-positive cells within the ONL, accompanied by increasing TREM2 immunoreactivity (Fig. S2). TREM2 localized to IBA1-positive cells, with IBA1/TREM2 double-positive cells prominent at P18 and P20 in rd10 mice but absent at P15 in both WT and rd10 mice (Fig. S2, A and B). Consistently, TREM2 mRNA and protein levels were elevated in rd10 retinas compared with littermate WT controls (Fig. 1D and E), suggesting that TREM2 induction might serve as an early adaptive response to retinal damage.

To assess the functional impact of TREM2 deficiency during RD, we generated TREM2-deficient rd10 (rd10;Trem2KO) mice (Fig. S3A). At P21, TUNEL staining revealed significantly increased apoptotic photoreceptors in the ONL of rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT littermates (Fig. 1F), while rd10;Trem2HET mice showed no significant difference compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls (Fig. S4A). At P19, IBA1-positive cells in the ONL significantly increased in rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls (Fig. 1G), coinciding with enhanced photoreceptor cell death at P21 and indicating rapid accumulation of myeloid cells in the ONL in the absence of TREM2. These findings suggest that the elevated photoreceptor cell death is unlikely due to impaired myeloid cell recruitment, but rather to altered myeloid cell function, including defective phagocytic clearance of photoreceptor debris.

By P25, ONL thickness was markedly reduced across central, middle, and peripheral retinal regions in rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared to littermate rd10;Trem2WT (Fig. 1H). At P50, a late stage of degeneration, peanut agglutinin (PNA) staining showed significantly reduced cone photoreceptor density throughout the retina in rd10;Trem2KO mice compared with rd10;Trem2WT littermates (Fig. 1I). rd10;Trem2HET mice displayed intermediate effects, with near-normal cone signal in middle and peripheral retinal regions but reduced cone signal in the central retina. Together, these data indicate that TREM2 deficiency not only exacerbates primary rod apoptosis but also accelerates the secondary loss of cone cells.

In addition to photoreceptor loss, RPE structure was disrupted in the absence of TREM2. Phalloidin staining revealed enlarged irregularly shaped RPE cells in rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT, which is comparable with rd10;Trem2HET (Fig. 1J). At the transcriptional level, RPE-related genes (Tjp1, Cdh3, Cldn1, and Otx2) were significantly reduced in both rd10;Trem2HET and rd10;Trem2KO mice compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls (Fig. 1K), with no difference between rd10;Trem2HET and rd10;Trem2KO retinas. These findings indicate that TREM2 is required for maintaining RPE homeostasis during RD, with partial loss sufficient to impair gene expression, while complete deficiency drives overt morphological disruption.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TREM2 deficiency accelerates photoreceptor degeneration and disrupts RPE integrity, supporting a protective role for TREM2 in retinal homeostasis during degeneration.

TREM2 influences myeloid cell accumulation and localization during RD

To define how TREM2 shapes myeloid cell responses during RD, we analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq dataset from rd10 and WT retinas [34] (GSE183206). Both Trem2 expression and the proportion of TREM2-positive microglia (identified using marker genes, e.g., Cx3cr1, Tmem119, and P2ry12) were increased in rd10 retinas compared with WT controls at P21 (Fig. 2A and B), consistent with early IBA1-positive myeloid cell activation in the ONL in Fig. 1A and Fig. S2, suggesting that myeloid cells may undergo early immune priming—a preparatory state of sensitization—to actively respond to the onset of photoreceptor stress.

We next assessed myeloid cell accumulation over time using IBA1 immunostaining on RPE flat mounts. At P23, rd10;Trem2KO retinas exhibited significantly increased IBA1-positive cells compared with rd10;Trem2WT littermates (Fig. 2C), indicating exaggerated early accumulation in the absence of TREM2. rd10;Trem2HET mice showed less IBA1-positive cells compared to rd10;Trem2KO littermates (Fig. S5). By P28, this trajectory rapidly reversed: IBA1-positive cell number declined in rd10;Trem2KO retinas, falling below rd10;Trem2WT control levels, whereas rd10;Trem2WT control retinas maintained elevated IBA1-positive cell number (Fig. 2D). At P50, IBA1-positive cell numbers declined in all genotypes and were largely confined to the peripheral retina, with rd10;Trem2KO retinas showing markedly fewer IBA1-positive cells than rd10;Trem2WT controls (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that TREM2 is required not only to restrain early myeloid activation but also to support myeloid cell survival and maintenance during prolonged retinal stress.

We then assessed Cd68 expression at P50 by qPCR as a marker for lysosomal/phagocytic activity in retinal myeloid cells. Cd68 mRNA levels were significantly decreased in rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT and rd10;Trem2HET controls (Fig. 2F), consistent with reduced phagocytic/lysosomal capacity in the absence of TREM2. This decrease is consistent with reduced myeloid cell abundance and may additionally reflect altered activation states of the remaining retinal myeloid cells during late-stage degeneration in the absence of TREM2.

To determine whether TREM2 deficiency alters the spatial relationship between myeloid cells and photoreceptors at a late stage, we performed co-immunostaining for PNA-labeled cones and IBA1-positive myeloid cells on retinal flat mounts at P50. In rd10;Trem2KO retinas, IBA1-positive cells were frequently positioned near the cone layer. PNA staining showed a disrupted, less organized lattice compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls (Fig. 2G), likely reflecting accelerated photoreceptor degeneration and impaired microglia-associated maintenance of cone outer segment, consistent with the altered microglial positioning in the rd10;Trem2KO condition. The amoeboid morphology of IBA1⁺ myeloid cells and apparent migration toward surviving cones did not indicate enhanced phagocytic clearance but rather a dysregulated pro-inflammatory state associated with inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, consistent with reports linking amoeboid morphology to inflammasome-driven inflammatory responses in neuroinflammatory disease models [35].

Quantitative analysis using Pearson’s correlation analysis and nearest-neighbor distance measurements revealed significantly greater spatial overlap between IBA1 and PNA signals and a reduced shortest distance between myeloid cells and cone photoreceptors in rd10;Trem2KO retinas (Fig. 2H and I). High-magnification imaging, intensity line-scan profiles, and orthogonal 2D-XZ reconstructions further confirmed close apposition of myeloid cells to surviving cones (Fig. 2G), consistent across multiple retinal regions and Z-stacks (Fig. S6). Together, these results demonstrate that TREM2 deficiency enhances the spatial coupling between myeloid cells and cone photoreceptors during RD, suggesting increased myeloid-cone association in the absence of TREM2.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TREM2 regulates both the magnitude and spatial distribution of the retinal myeloid response during RD. The earlier expansion followed by a subsequent decline of IBA1-positive myeloid cells in rd10;Trem2KO retinas suggests dysregulated myeloid activation and impaired maintenance in the absence of TREM2.

TREM2 deficiency impairs APOE signaling and exacerbates lipid accumulation during RD

To define TREM2-associated transcriptional programs in retinal myeloid cells during RD, using the scRNA-seq dataset of P21 rd10 retinas [34], differential expression analysis comparing TREM2-positive and TREM2-negative microglia identified a distinct TREM2-associated transcriptional signature, with Apoe among the most significantly upregulated genes in TREM2-positive microglia (Fig. 3A).

To determine whether increased Apoe expression in TREM2-positive microglia corresponded to altered microglia-rod signaling, we performed cell-cell communication analysis using scRNA-seq dataset [34]. Ligand-receptor interaction scoring revealed that APOE signaling from microglia ranked among the top three predicted microglia-to-rod interactions, with potential binding receptors on rods including very low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), scavenger receptor class B member 1 (SCARB1), and LDL receptor related protein 6 (LRP6) (Fig. 3B), indicating a prominent role for APOE-mediated communication within the degenerating retina.

Comparative cell-cell communication network analysis between rd10 and WT retinas revealed increased APOE signaling activity in rd10 retinas, with microglia predicted as the primary source of APOE release from microglia compared with other retinal cell types (Fig. 3C). Consistently, outgoing signal analysis from rods, cones, bipolar cells, Müller cells, and microglia confirmed that microglia are the dominant contributors to APOE-mediated intercellular communication in the degenerating retina (Fig. S7).

We next validated these findings in vivo. APOE expression was significantly decreased in rd10;Trem2KO compared with littermate controls at both mRNA (Fig. 3D) and protein (Fig. 3E) levels at P21. Similarly, lipoprotein lipase (Lpl), a lipid metabolism gene associated with APOE signaling [36], was also significantly reduced, suggesting impaired lipid processing in the absence of TREM2 in rd10 retinas.

Beyond APOE signaling, scRNA-seq analysis revealed that top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TREM2-positive microglia (e.g., Lrp1, Psap, Ctsb, Ctsa, Lamp2, and Mertk) were enriched for lysosomal function (Fig. 3A). KEGG analysis identified lysosome as the top enriched pathway in TREM2-positive versus TREM2-negative microglia (Fig. S8). These analyses indicate that TREM2 supports both APOE-mediated signaling and lysosomal activity necessary for clearance of degenerating photoreceptor debris.

Given that Apoe and Lpl are key regulators of microglial lipid handling and debris clearance, we next examined whether their reduced expression in TREM2-deficient retinas was associated with accumulation of undigested autofluorescent materials in the subretinal space, which may include lipid-rich debris. Rd10 retinas exhibited a substantial accumulation of autofluorescent particles detectable in both 488-nm and 594-nm channels, which were largely absent in WT retinas (Fig. 3F). This autofluorescence likely reflects a mixture of incompletely degraded lipid-rich and oxidative protein material that accumulates in lysosomal compartments, as previously described for lipofuscin-like autofluorescence in microglia and macrophages [37, 38]. These autofluorescent deposits were predominantly localized within IBA1-positive cells near the RPE layer (Fig. 3G), suggesting active uptake of degenerated photoreceptor debris with impaired downstream processing in rd10 mice. Although autofluorescence may represent a combination of lipid-rich debris, lipofuscin, and other cellular components, the observed distribution is consistent with disrupted phagolysosomal function.

Lipofuscin is an autofluorescent pigment derived from incompletely degraded cellular debris [39]. To further assess defective clearance, we examined lipofuscin-containing IBA1-positive cells. At P30, rd10;Trem2KO retinas show a higher proportion of IBA1-positive cells containing lipofuscin compared with rd10;Trem2WT, indicating increased metabolic stress and impaired phagolysosomal processing in the absence of TREM2 (Fig. 3H). These findings align with prior reports linking accumulation of lipofuscin and other autofluorescent material in microglia to defective debris clearance and lysosomal stress [19].

Collectively, these findings indicate that TREM2 promotes APOE expression and APOE-mediated myeloid cell–photoreceptor interactions, while also maintaining myeloid cell lysosomal function required for efficient clearance of degenerating photoreceptor debris during RD.

TREM2 regulates complement activation and interferon-associated innate immune priming during RD

Beyond its critical role in microglial lipid metabolism, TREM2 serves as a master regulator of inflammatory cascades [5, 40]. To investigate the broader immune programs associated with TREM2 during RD, utilizing the P21 rd10 scRNA-seq dataset [34], we analyzed transcriptional signatures of TREM2-positive microglia in rd10 mice. Pathway enrichment analysis using the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Hallmark gene sets revealed significant upregulation of immune-related pathways, including complement and IFN signaling, indicating that TREM2-expressing microglia are transcriptionally primed to orchestrate innate immune responses during early degeneration (Fig. 4A).

Consistent with activation of innate immune pathways, scRNA-seq analysis revealed that the expressions of C1qa, C1qb, and C3 were all upregulated in TREM2-positive microglia compared to TREM2-negative microglia (Fig. S9A). The qPCR confirmed increased expression of C1qa in rd10 compared with WT retinas at P20 (Fig. 4B). To assess whether TREM2 loss altered complement gene expression during degeneration, we measured C1qb and C3 expression in retinas. Both genes were dysregulated in rd10;Trem2KO retinas relative to rd10;Trem2WT controls at the onset of degeneration (P21) and during degeneration (P25) (Fig. 4C). Together, these results indicate that TREM2 modulates complement-associated immune responses throughout RD.

To determine whether these transcriptional changes were associated with functional complement cascade activation and proteolytic turnover, we examined C3 protein levels and its processing by western blot. Complement component C3 (~ 187 kDa) undergoes stepwise proteolytic cleavage during activation of the complement cascade, generating multiple fragments [18, 41]. Cleavage of C3 produces C3b, consisting of an α′-chain (~ 115 kDa) and β-chain (~ 70 kDa), which can be further processed into smaller fragments such as iC3b, characterized by additional cleavage of the α′-chain into ~ 68 kDa and ~ 43 kDa fragments while retaining the β-chain [18, 41]. Consistent with these known processing events, we detected multiple C3-derived bands spanning ~ 40–115 kDa (Fig. 4D), suggesting active proteolytic turnover. Densitometric quantification revealed differences in full-length C3 and C3-derived fragments in rd10;Trem2HET retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT retinas (Fig. 4D), indicating that TREM2 haploinsufficiency impacts complement activation dynamics in the degenerating retina.

Analysis of cellular sources using the P21 rd10 scRNA-seq dataset revealed that C1qa and C1qb were enriched in microglia, whereas C3 is expressed in both microglia and Müller glia (Fig. S9B). TREM2 expression is largely restricted to myeloid-lineage cells, suggesting that the increased C1qb in rd10;Trem2KO and rd10;Trem2HET whole retinas (Fig. 4C) likely reflects upregulation within myeloid cells during early degeneration (P21–P25). Total C3 protein levels in TREM2-deficient retinas likely include contributions from multiple cellular sources, including microglia and Müller glia. Given the use of global TREM2-deficient mice, these changes cannot be definitively attributed to a single cell type.

Given that type I interferon (IFN) signaling is a key effector pathway downstream of microglial and complement-mediated responses, we assessed components of the IFN pathway during RD. At both P21 and P25, the IFN receptor Ifnar1 was increased in rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls, with rd10;Trem2HET retinas displaying intermediate levels (Fig. 4E). At P50, Ifnar1 was increased in rd10;Trem2HET but not rd10;Trem2KO retinas, while the IFN ligands Ifna4 and Ifnb1 were reduced in rd10;Trem2KO retinas but not rd10;Trem2HET (Fig. 4F), suggesting genotype- and stage-dependent modulation of IFN signaling in the absence of TREM2 at late stage. These findings indicate that TREM2 loss induces a dynamic, stage-dependent reprogramming of type I IFN signaling during RD.

We next examined downstream IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) at P21. Rd10;Trem2KO retinas showed increased expression of early, primary-responsive ISGs, including Isg15, Mx1, and Irf7. In contrast, the expression of late or effector ISGs such as Cxcl10, Mx2, Ifit1, and Oas1a was reduced compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls (Fig. 4G). This distinct ISG pattern indicates that global TREM2 deficiency is associated with altered IFN response dynamics during RD, characterized by enhanced early innate immune activation signals but a relative attenuation of downstream effector ISG programs. These changes likely reflect coordinated transcriptional remodeling across retinal and myeloid cell populations rather than a single cell-type–restricted effect.

TREM2 deficiency promotes myeloid inflammasome activation during RD

Because excessive IFN signaling and complement activation can prime myeloid inflammasome pathways [31, 42–44], we examined whether amplified innate immune signals in TREM2-deficient rd10 retinas were associated with inflammasome activation. Using the P21 rd10 scRNA-seq dataset [34], we analyzed inflammasome-associated transcriptional programs in TREM2-positive versus TREM2-negative microglia in rd10 and WT retinas. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that TREM2-positive microglia were enriched for inflammasome-related transcriptional signatures, including NLRP3- and AIM2-associated gene sets (Fig. 5A), as well as genes encoding key inflammasome complex components (Fig. 5B). In addition, pyroptosis-associated gene signatures, representing downstream outcomes of inflammasome activation, were also enriched in TREM2-positive microglia (Fig. 5C and D). Together, these scRNA-seq analyses indicate that TREM2-expressing microglia are enriched for transcriptional programs associated with inflammasome priming during early RD.

Consistent with these transcriptional changes, immunohistochemistry at P21 showed increased cleaved caspase-1, cleaved GSDMD, and cleaved/mature IL-1β in rd10;Trem2KO retinas compared with rd10;Trem2WT controls, with signals predominantly localized to IBA1-positive cells within the ONL (Fig. 5E). While the degenerating microenvironment drives the upregulation of inflammasome machinery in TREM2-positive microglia subset (Fig. 5A-D), this transcriptional alteration translates into massive proteolytic cleavage, evidenced by active caspase-1, GSDMD, and IL-1β in the absence of TREM2 (Fig. 5E and Fig. S10). Thus, TREM2 is upregulated alongside inflammasome components, functioning as a built-in brake, raising the activation threshold to prevent these highly primed myeloid cells from undergoing terminal pyroptosis.

Together, these findings identify TREM2 as a protective checkpoint that uncouples innate immune priming from terminal pyroptosis in retinal myeloid cells. By removing this restraint, TREM2 deficiency allows amplified complement and IFN signals to cross the activation threshold, directly linking early immune dysregulation to the inflammatory effector pathways that may exacerbate photoreceptor injury.

Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of GSDMD mitigates photoreceptor degeneration in the absence of TREM2

To determine whether inflammasome-driven pyroptosis contributes to photoreceptor degeneration via TREM2, we deleted the pyroptotic effector GSDMD genetically (Fig. S3, B and C) or inhibited pharmacologically in TREM2 deficient rd10 mice. Genetic deletion of GSDMD in rd10;Trem2KO mice significantly preserved ONL thickness at P30 compared with littermate rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdWT controls (Fig. 6A and B), indicating that GSDMD contributes to photoreceptor loss in the absence of TREM2. To further assess the role of myeloid-derived GSDMD, we generated Cx3cr1CreER-driven conditional GSDMD knockout mice in the rd10;Trem2KO background (rd10;Trem2KO;Gsdmdfl/fl;Cx3cr1CreER, or rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdcKO) (Fig. S3C). Conditional deletion of GSDMD in myeloid cells (rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdcKO) similarly resulted in significant preservation of ONL thickness at P35 compared with littermate controls (Fig. 6C and D), supporting a contribution of myeloid GSDMD activity to photoreceptor loss in the absence of TREM2.

Despite reduced photoreceptor degeneration, rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdcKO retinas showed increased accumulation of IBA1-positive cells in retinal flat mounts at P35 compared with control littermates (Fig. S11), suggesting that GSDMD deficiency alters myeloid cell accumulation dynamics, potentially involving changes in clearance or persistence. We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of pharmacologic GSDMD inhibition using disulfiram, a small molecule that covalently blocks GSDMD oligomerization and pyroptotic pore formation [45] (Fig. S12). Mechanistic validation confirmed that disulfiram reduced GSDMD oligomer formation and decreased the oligomer-to-monomer ratio in retinal tissue (Fig. S12). Systemic disulfiram administration from P17 to P26 (Fig. S12) significantly preserved ONL thickness in rd10;Trem2KO mice compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 6E and F), indicating that GSDMD activity contributes to photoreceptor degeneration in this context. These results identify GSDMD inhibition as a potential strategy to enhance photoreceptor survival.

Together, these findings identify TREM2 signaling as central regulator of retinal homeostasis during degeneration (Fig. 6G). In rd10 retinas, TREM2-positive myeloid cells engage APOE-associated lipid metabolic programs and maintain controlled IFN/complement-related immune priming, thereby limiting progression towards inflammasome activation, GSDMD cleavage, and pyroptosis-associated signaling. In contrast, global TREM2 deficiency is associated with disrupted APOE-related transcriptional programs, increased IFN and complement priming, and enhanced inflammasome-related signals, including increased cleavage of caspase-1 and GSDMD. These changes are accompanied by increased inflammatory cell stress and exacerbated photoreceptor degeneration. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of GSDMD interrupts this cascade and preserves photoreceptor survival, suggesting pyroptotic execution as a key therapeutic vulnerability downstream of TREM2 loss.

Discussion

Progressive photoreceptor loss in inherited RD is closely associated with microglial activation [25, 26], yet the mechanisms linking early immune priming to secondary neuronal injury remain incompletely defined. While initial microglial response facilitates clearance of apoptotic photoreceptors, chronic activation drives secondary tissue damage. In this study, we identify TREM2 as a critical metabolic-immune checkpoint that uncouples innate immune priming from terminal inflammasome-mediated myeloid pyroptosis. By defining a protective TREM2–APOE lipid-handling axis that restrains inflammasome activation, our findings provide a mechanistic framework for how disruption of this checkpoint accelerates progressive photoreceptor degeneration.

Myeloid cell accumulation within the ONL is a hallmark of rd10 pathology, and we show that this response is accompanied by increased TREM2 expression at both mRNA and protein levels. Genetic ablation of TREM2 exacerbated photoreceptor apoptosis, cone loss, and RPE dysfunction, demonstrating that TREM2 induction represents a protective myeloid cell adaptation during RD. These findings align with studies in neurodegenerative brain models, in which TREM2 supports microglial survival, lipid metabolism, and disease-associated microglial (DAM) programs [5, 18–21].

A central challenge for myeloid cells during RD is the sustained phagocytosis of dying photoreceptors and the accompanying lipid burden. Single-cell analyses in our study revealed that TREM2-expressing microglia adopt a transcriptionally distinct DAM-like state enriched for APOE-dependent lipid metabolic programs, including upregulation of Apoe and Lpl. APOE is a core component of the TREM2-driven DAM signature [9] and a key regulator of lipid handling and microglia state transitions [30]. Our findings align with recent studies [23, 46] showing that TREM2 and its downstream effectors, such as PPARγ, CD36, and Galectin-3, are required for protective microglial responses and debris clearance in the degenerating retina.

Impaired lipid processing and the consequent accumulation of lipofuscin and undigested lipid cargo have been increasingly recognized as critical drivers of innate immune activation. Lipofuscin, an age-associated pigment composed of oxidized proteins and lipids, can induce cellular stress responses, including lysosomal dysfunction and the generation of reactive oxygen species, both of which are known activators of inflammasomes and pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [37, 47]. Similarly, the buildup of undigested lipid species can trigger Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [48], promoting the secretion of cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. Defects in lipid metabolism, including impaired autophagy-mediated lipid clearance, exacerbate these processes by sustaining intracellular lipid stress, thereby creating a feed-forward loop that amplifies inflammatory responses [49]. In line with this framework, our findings provide a mechanistic framework linking lipid dysregulation to heightened innate immunity, highlighting the interplay between metabolic defects, cellular stress, and inflammatory signaling in disease pathogenesis. Future studies dissecting the precise molecular sensors and pathways involved may identify novel therapeutic targets for conditions characterized by lipid-induced inflammation.

Recent work has also identified the TREM2–galectin-3 axis as an important regulator of subretinal microglial phagocytosis and tissue homeostasis [23]. In line with this, our data support a model in which TREM2 promotes efficient phagolysosomal processing and debris clearance during retinal degeneration. Galectin-3 has also been implicated in lysosomal function and phagolysosomal maturation [50, 51], suggesting that its interaction with TREM2 may directly counteract cellular stress pathways that would otherwise trigger inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. Loss of TREM2 disrupts this pathway, leading to accumulation of undegraded material and increased cellular stress. Under these conditions, microglia may transition from a homeostatic, phagocytic state to a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by inflammasome activation and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. Thus, galectin-3–associated phagocytic activity and pyroptotic cell death likely represent functionally linked outcomes of myeloid cell activation, where effective clearance constrains inflammatory cell death, whereas its failure promotes pyroptotic signaling.

Beyond lipid metabolism, TREM2 loss selectively alters interferon-related transcriptional programs. In rd10;Trem2KO retinas, early ISGs, such as Isg15, Irf7, and Mx1, were markedly upregulated, whereas several downstream effector ISGs were reduced. IFN signaling is a central regulator of microglial activation and neuroinflammatory priming [52, 53], and dysregulated interferon responses have been implicated in RD and sterile inflammatory signaling within the subretinal space. Our data indicate that TREM2 coordinates interferon progression from initial sensing to downstream resolution, restraining excessive innate immune priming. Consistent with this, while moderate IFN signaling utilized by TREM2 can facilitate early microglial migratory responses toward retinal injury [28], our data show that TREM2 deficiency locks myeloid cells into a persistently primed, hyper-sensitized state, amplifying early ISGs and complement activation and predisposing these cells to downstream inflammasome engagement. Together, these findings identify TREM2 as a critical threshold regulator of myeloid cell inflammatory priming.

The observation that rd10;Trem2HET retinas exhibited a stronger induction of the complement- and interferon-related gene signature (e.g., C3, Ifnar1, Mx1, Irf7) (Fig. 4) than rd10;Trem2KO retinas suggests a non-linear gene dosage effect of TREM2. Rather than representing a simple intermediate phenotype, partial TREM2 loss appears to produce a heightened immune sensitive state, whereas complete deficiency results in a comparatively attenuated profile that in some respects more closely resembles WT. Notably, not all structural and molecular readouts followed this pattern, indicating that the effects of TREM2 dosage are context- and modality-dependent. This observation is consistent with prior studies showing that heterozygous and homozygous TREM2 deficiency can produce distinct biological outcomes. TREM2 heterozygous loss has been associated with increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease and microglial dysfunction [18], as well as exacerbated laser-induced choroidal neovascularization and vascular leakage [54], whereas complete TREM2 deficiency causes Nasu-Hakola disease [55] and does not affect laser-induced choroidal neovascularization [56]. Similarly, studies in brain have shown that homozygous TREM2 deficient microglia fail to fully adopt the DAM phenotype [9], suggesting that complete loss of TREM2 alters microglial activation differently from partial loss. One possible explanation is that complete loss of TREM2 induces compensatory adaptation through alternative TAM receptors or DAP12-independent signaling, partially dampening inflammatory transcriptional responses. In contrast, TREM2 heterozygotes may retain sufficient TREM2 signaling to prevent full compensatory rewiring, yet insufficient signaling to maintain proper immune regulation, thereby creating a hyper-reactive state in which immune and interferon responses are exaggerated under stress conditions. Importantly, the divergence between the heightened inflammatory transcriptional signature in rd10;Trem2HET retinas and the more severe structural degeneration observed in rd10;Trem2KO retinas suggests that terminal photoreceptor loss is not determined solely by the magnitude of inflammatory gene expression, but also by specific functional deficits such as impaired lipid clearance and pyroptotic cell death.

A key mechanistic insight of our study concerns the terminal fate of hyper-sensitized myeloid cells during active degeneration. Prior work by Li et al. [29] proposed that in rd10 mice, TREM2 deficiency leaves microglia vulnerable to photoreceptor-derived metabolites (such as arachidonic acid), which trigger NF-κB/STAT2-driven microglial apoptosis. Our findings align with and extend the upstream regulatory network of this model. In the context of innate immunity, the hyperactivation of NF-κB and STAT2 (interferon signaling) represents the canonical “priming” phase (Signal 1) required for inflammasome assembly. Furthermore, lipid-derived metabolites like arachidonic acid are well-documented endogenous triggers for this inflammatory cascade. However, while Li et al. [29] characterized this terminal microglial collapse as apoptosis—relying on classical phenotypic markers such as Caspase-3 cleavage, propidium iodide (PI) uptake, and TUNEL positivity—our targeted molecular dissection redefines the executioner of this cell death. It is increasingly recognized that profound inflammasome activation and GSDMD pore formation intrinsically permeabilize cell membranes (yielding PI and TUNEL positivity) and can induce downstream caspase crosstalk, resulting in phenotypic overlaps frequently misidentified as apoptosis. By combining Cx3cr1-lineage conditional deletion and pharmacological inhibition of GSDMD, we identify GSDMD-mediated pyroptotic signaling as a functional contributor to photoreceptor degeneration under TREM2 deficiency. This distinction is critical: unlike apoptosis, which is generally immunologically silent, GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis is an inherently pro-inflammatory explosive event. Thus, the catastrophic death of TREM2-deficient myeloid cells does not merely halt phagocytosis but actively amplifies local tissue stress and directly drives secondary photoreceptor degeneration.

Importantly, the structural and functional rescue achieved in our study through genetic or pharmacological inhibition of GSDMD supports a causal contribution of pyroptotic signaling to photoreceptor degeneration. Recent efforts utilizing TREM2-activating agonist antibodies have shown promise in elevating soluble TREM2 and promoting microglial phagocytosis in Alzheimer’s and multiple sclerosis models [57]; however, these approaches have produced limited or context-dependent effects on tau pathology and overall neurodegeneration, underscoring the complexity of globally manipulating TREM2 signaling [58]. Because the consequences of TREM2 modulation are highly sensitive to the disease stage, directly intercepting the terminal executioner (GSDMD) provides a precise pharmacological bottleneck. Preventing pore formation with disulfiram traps the hyperactive inflammatory response within myeloid cells, preventing toxic spillage and substantially rescuing photoreceptor survival without fully abolishing early immune sensing.

Disulfiram has been used as a pharmacological inhibitor of GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis by blocking GSDMD pore formation and oligomerization [45]. In our study, we observed reduced high–molecular weight GSDMD oligomers following disulfiram treatment, consistent with suppression of this pathway in the rd10 retina. However, disulfiram is not a specific inhibitor of GSDMD and may affect additional targets, including retinal aldehyde dehydrogenase, as previously reported by Talis et al. [59]. Importantly, differences in experimental paradigms likely contribute to the predominant mechanisms engaged: in our study, disulfiram was administered during the acute peak of photoreceptor degeneration (P17–P26), when myeloid-driven inflammasome activation and pyroptosis are maximal, whereas prior work used chronic administration during later stages (P60–P90) characterized by extensive secondary remodeling. In addition, our acute high-dose systemic delivery may preferentially target rapid GSDMD pore formation in hyperactivated immune cells, whereas chronic low-dose regimens may more strongly engage metabolic pathways such as aldehyde dehydrogenase inhibition. Thus, while off-target effects cannot be excluded, our findings—together with genetic validation using global and Cx3cr1-lineage conditional GSDMD knockout models in the rd10;Trem2KO background—support a primary role for GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis in driving retinal degeneration, and suggest that disulfiram may provide synergistic neuroprotection by acutely suppressing myeloid pyroptosis while also modulating broader metabolic and inflammatory pathways.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Although inflammasome execution was predominantly localized to IBA1-positive cells, we cannot definitively distinguish tissue-resident microglia from infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages, as infiltrating monocytes can rapidly adopt microglia-like transcriptomic and morphological features in the degenerating retina, complicating lineage-specific attributions [60]. In addition, the use of a global TREM2 knockout model limits the ability to attribute observed effects specifically to retinal myeloid cell–intrinsic functions, as developmental effects, systemic immune alterations, and contributions from peripheral myeloid populations cannot be excluded. Similarly, while our data suggest that TREM2 may restrain inflammasome-related transcriptional reprogramming, this inference is currently based on scRNA-seq comparisons of WT versus rd10 retinas and TREM2-positive versus TREM2-negative subpopulations. Direct scRNA-seq profiling of rd10;Trem2KO retinas will therefore be necessary to definitively establish TREM2-dependent transcriptional rewiring in this disease context. Moreover, although our findings support a biologically coherent mechanistic axis linking defective handling to IFN and complement priming, inflammasome activation, and ultimately GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, the causal hierarchy among these processes remains to be fully resolved experimentally. In particular, the present study does not directly demonstrate that correction of upstream lipid or immune dysregulation is sufficient to suppress downstream inflammasome activation, nor does it identify the dominant upstream inflammasome sensor responsible for driving this phenotype. Thus, while the convergent molecular, transcriptional, and functional evidence presented here supports the existence of an integrated pathogenic network, additional mechanistic studies will be required to define the precise pathway relationships and regulatory hierarchy underlying this cascade. In addition, several additional aspects require further investigation. These include identification of the endogenous ligands triggering the cascade in the absence of TREM2 and the relative contributions of specific pyroptosis-released factors (e.g., IL-1β) to photoreceptor toxicity. Furthermore, whether GSDMD deficiency or disulfiram treatment confers protective effects on longer-term outcomes such as RPE integrity at later time points (e.g., P50) remains to be determined. Future studies will be needed to determine whether these interventions provide sustained protection and extend to preservation of RPE structure and function.

In summary, our findings support TREM2 as a master metabolic-immune checkpoint that safeguards the retina by uncoupling intense innate immune priming from terminal pyroptosis. By limiting inflammatory cell death of retinal phagocytes, TREM2 contributes to preserving the structural integrity of the subretinal space, highlighting downstream pyroptotic executioners like GSDMD as stage-specific therapeutic targets for inherited blindness.

Luo (Harvard Medical School and Brigham and Women’s Hospital). rd10 mice were crossed with Trem2KO mice to generate rd10;Trem2 heterozygous offspring, which were subsequently intercrossed to obtain rd10;Trem2KO, rd10;Trem2HET, and rd10;Trem2WT littermates. To generate rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdKO mice or rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdcKO, rd10;Trem2KO mice were crossed with GsdmdKO or GsdmdcKO mice. Offspring were intercrossed to obtain rd10;Trem2KO;GsdmdcKO mice and corresponding control genotypes (Figure S3). Littermate mice were used as control mice. Both sexes were used in all experiments.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data analysis

Publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from GEO (RRID: SCR_005012) (GSE183206) [34] and processed using Scanpy [61]. The dataset includes four conditions: WT-male, WT-female, rd10-male, and rd10-female. Data were pre-filtered by the original author (retaining genes expressed in ≥ 3 cells; 200-6,000 genes per cell; 1,000–20,000 transcripts per cell). Data were normalized to total counts per cell and log-transformed (ln(x + 1)). Dimensionality reduction was performed via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [62], followed by k-nearest neighbor graph construction (k = 20, 50 PCs) and UMAP embedding (min_dist = 0.50, spread = 3.0) [63]. Clustering was conducted using the Leiden algorithm (resolution 2.0) [64]. Cell types were annotated using SCType [65] based on enrichment of canonical markers (C1qa, Aif1, and Rho). Microglial gene expression was imputed using MAGIC [66]. Microglia were classified as TREM2-positive and TREM2-negative using a 75% threshold of imputed TREM2 expression. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test [67], with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons.

Pathway analysis

Pathway activities were estimated using the Decoupler framework [68] with the MSigDB Hallmark gene set collection [69] serving as the reference network. Enrichment scores were computed using the Univariate Linear Model (ULM) algorithm and compared between phenotypes using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test [67]. Additionally, genes associated with inflammasome complex assembly and regulation were obtained from the Gene Ontology database [70, 71], and inflammasome pathway activity was dissected using a custom multi-panel visualization strategy.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication was inferred using LIANA+ [72, 73] to identify robust ligand-receptor interactions. A consensus rank was calculated by aggregating results from multiple established methods (CellPhoneDB [74], NATMI [75], SingleCellSignalR [76], and Connectome [77]) to mitigate individual method bias. Interactions between different cell types were prioritized based on their aggregate rank and specificity to the cell populations of interest.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse eyes were collected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 1 h, then washed in 1X PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) three times for 30 min each. The retina and RPE-choroid complex were dissected and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin, 5% normal goat serum, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS. After washing three times with 1X PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) for 30 min each, tissues were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, followed by three PBS washes. Stained tissues were mounted with anti-fade medium and cover slipped. Imaging was performed using Zeiss 980 confocal microscopes. Antibodies used in this study were included in Table S2.

Image processing and quantitative analysis

Confocal Z-stack images were acquired via a Zeiss laser scanning microscope (LSM 980 Airyscan 2 confocal). To evaluate the physical spatial relationship between PNA-positive photoreceptors and IBA1-positive cells, 3D spatial analysis was performed using ZEN software (Carl Zeiss). Orthogonal cross-sections were generated, and Z-axis fluorescence intensity profiles were extracted and subsequently visualized using OriginPro (OriginLab). Furthermore, the co-expression of inflammatory markers within microglia/macrophages was assessed by analyzing the colocalization of IBA1 with cleaved IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1, and cleaved GSDMD. Quantitative colocalization analysis was conducted utilizing the Coloc 2 plugin in ImageJ (Fiji) to calculate Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse retinas using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12183025) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Equal amounts of total RNA were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, #1708841). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) using SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, #1708886) and gene-specific primers (Table S1). Relative gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to Ppia (Cyclophilin A) as the internal control.

Western blot analysis

Retinas were isolated from enucleated eyes at the indicated time points. Tissues were lysed using an ultrasonic homogenizer in lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, 11836170001). Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227). Equal amounts of protein lysates were denatured in 4X NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer containing 10% 2-mercaptoethanol at 70 °C for 10 min. After SDS-PAGE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) separation and transfer to PVDF membranes, membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies (Table S2). Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 32106) and quantified by densitometry.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism and R (stats package) were used for statistical analyses. Results are presented as means ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM). All experiments were repeated at least three times independently. Normality of datasets was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For datasets that passed the normality test, parametric tests were used: an unpaired t-test for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple-group comparisons. For datasets that failed normality or contained non-informative values (e.g., zeros), a nonparametric test was applied: an unpaired Mann-Whitney test for two-group comparison, and Kruskal-Wallis Dunn’s Multiple Comparisons test for multiple-group comparison. Hypergeometric enrichment analysis was performed with the phyper function in the R stats package. Enrichment was considered significant at the α = 0.05 level with a Bonferroni correction for testing multiple hypotheses. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
