NHS Yellow Cover Document integrity performance assessment of two needle-free closed system transfer devices as terminal closure/access for Luer-Lock syringes and IV bags
Authors: Alan Shaun Wilkinson, Laima Ozolina, Romana Bon, Kate E Walker, Andrew Wallace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the container closure integrity of needle-free Closed System Transfer Device (CSTD) components (ChemoClave and ChemoLock) combined with Luer-Lock (LL) syringes and IV bags as a terminal closure/access device. The UK NHS Yellow Cover Document (YCD) requires containment data for final storage devices of aseptic chemotherapy drugs when prepared in advance in pharmaceutical technical services (PTS). Assessment of both physical and microbial integrity of all combinations was performed: syringe adapter/LL syringes and vented bag adaptor/IV bags. All container combinations were subject to an additional vibrational challenge, with three-axis vibrational data logging representing a typical transportation excursion.MethodsContainer integrity testing was performed according to YCD requirements “Protocols for the Integrity Testing of Syringes” for ChemoClave and ChemoLock syringe adapters (CH2000S/CL2000S) and for ChemoClave and ChemoLock vented bag spikes (CH-14/CL-14) as terminal closure devices. Microbiological integrity was assessed according to Method 1, Part 1.4 using Brevundimonas diminuta at 32°C for 14 days. Physical integrity was assessed using the following dye intrusion methods: YCD Method 3 and European Pharmacopoeia 3.2.9 “Rubber closures for containers”. Readout for dye intrusion was reported visually and using a spectrophotometer. Positive (n=2) and negative (n=1) controls were assessed according to YCD for both arms of the test.ResultsChemoClave and ChemoLock syringe adapters and vented bag spikes/IV bags were shown to be free of microbiological contamination (n=160) and free of dye intrusion (n=80 in total). The data support both ChemoClave and ChemoLock CSTD components as closure for aseptic preparations of pharmaceutical drug products in PTS. 100% closure integrity was demonstrated.ConclusionsChemoClave and ChemoLock CSTD components showed 100% container integrity in accordance with YCD requirements as terminal closure for LL syringes and IV bags in PTS. ChemoClave and ChemoLock syringe adapters and vented bag spike components can be used for the preparation of chemotherapeutic drug products prepared in advance in UK PTS.

Introduction

Various forms of cancer are routinely treated using hazardous drugs such as antineoplastics that are prepared in the hospital pharmacy. Globally, there is an increasing demand for hospital pharmacy services in the preparation of hazardous drug-based medications.1 2 While there is a defined benefit to the patient, there is a risk of accidental exposure of healthcare workers to antineoplastic drugs and other hazardous drugs which can cause serious harm with no defined benefit.3 4

Harm caused to healthcare workers through accidental exposure to hazardous drugs is the subject of a number of publications prompting the development of guidelines for safer handling of hazardous drugs in a number of countries based on anecdotal evidence.47 To address this potential harm, Closed System Transfer Devices (CSTDs) have been designed to minimise the exposure of healthcare workers to hazardous drugs during drug preparation and administration through engineered design.4 5 8 The use of CSTDs provides an additional safety control for healthcare workers that has the potential to reduce occupational exposure to hazardous drugs, and has received increasing attention both in the pharmacy and on the ward.810

UK guidance published in 2018 by the NHS Pharmaceutical Quality Assurance (NHSPQA) as a Yellow Cover Document (YCD) recommends replacement of a sterile syringe cap with a CSTD syringe component on the ward immediately prior to connection to the patient for safe cytotoxic chemotherapy administration, thereby incorporating CSTD components at the administration level.11 The same YCD guidance document states that IV bags should be spiked with CSTDs where needle-free bags are not available for safe handling of cytotoxic drugs in clinical areas, and that bags should not be de-spiked following administration prior to disposal to keep the administration equipment closed after use.11 These combined measures are designed to reduce exposure during administration but do not address contamination that may have its source in compounding activities in the pharmacy. A recent study conducted in an NHS managed hospital has shown that, despite current best practices being implemented within pharmacies, contamination with hazardous drugs remains a persistent threat.12 Furthermore, Marler-Hausen et al showed contamination during administration of doxorubicin from a pre-filled syringe when the syringe cap was replaced by a CSTD syringe cap (Tevadaptor, Simplivia) immediately prior to administration, in line with current UK guidance.10 11

To improve on the current UK clinical practice of adding CSTD syringe components at ward level, the YCD guidelines request manufacturers of CSTDs to provide device integrity data to enable CSTDs to be added to syringes in the pharmacy.11

To allow the adoption of CSTD components in pharmaceutical technical services (PTS) in the UK, the manufacturer of the CSTD components must commission testing in accordance with the NHS YCD guidance for syringe adaptor components, whereby the syringe adaptor component replaces the sterile blind hub as the terminal closure.11 13

The present study aims to report the outcomes from integrity testing of a luer technology CSTD as closure device for Luer-Lock syringes, supporting the adoption of ChemoClave CH2000S syringe adaptor and ChemoLock CL2000S syringe adaptor components as part of the final container system for Luer-Lock syringes in UK clinical practice, as has been previously published for a different closed system transfer device syringe adaptor technology.14 The study also reports the results of applying the European Pharmacopoeia (EP) 3.2.9 method for “Rubber enclosures” as an alternative to the YCD Method 3 for dye intrusion and have applied this methodology to the integrity testing of the ChemoClave CH-14 syringe adaptor in combination with Luer-Lock syringes to demonstrate the applicability of this approach for future UK integrity testing.

In addition, this study reports the required supporting integrity data for the adoption of a CSTD vented bag spike in combination with two IV bag standards equipped with a bag spike port—namely, the ChemoClave CH-14 vented bag spike in combination with a 250 mL IV bag (Viaflo, Baxter) and the ChemoLock CL-14 vented bag spike in combination with a 100 mL IV bag (FreeFlex, Fresenius-Kabi). Current practice is not to allow spiking of IV bags in the pharmacy in the UK based on historical legacy data using administration tubing sets. However, a CSTD bag spike product is much less likely to become physically de-spiked during handling and storage and offers an opportunity to further reduce exposure of nurses on the ward from spiking IV bags containing oncolytic products. The integrity data for combinations of both ChemoClave CH-14 and ChemoLock CL-14 vented bag spike components in combination with IV bags could support a change in practice in UK PTS and support advanced preparation as part of dose banding.11 15

This study aims to provide the necessary YCD integrity data to support a change in UK practice and adoption of two vented bag spike technologies (ChemoClave and ChemoLock) in PTS during compounding and support connectivity on the ward in a closed system without the need to spike IV bags or add syringe adaptor CSTD components by swapping out for blind hubs on the ward, thus further reducing the risk to UK nurses through accidental exposure to hazardous drug materials.11 13

Methods

The NHSPQA 2013 YCD guidance document covers the requirements for container integrity testing, focusing on syringes as the final container system when fitted with a sterile cap as the closure device.13 The YCD requires both microbiological and physical integrity tests to be performed on the container closure system to fully assess container integrity. The guidance requires two assessments to be performed: (1) microbiological sterility testing of media fills with immersion of the container in a culture broth; and (2) test for physical integrity of the closure device by dye intrusion using methylene blue dye, each with appropriate negative and positive controls.13 The study includes some additional integrity testing performed to the current EP 3.2.9 guidance for “Rubber enclosures” as an alternative to the YCD Method 3 for dye intrusion. Both use methylene blue dye as the reporter; however, the EP test allows for the testing of both rigid and non-rigid container systems, so the authors have demonstrated the interoperability of using the EP 3.2.9 as replacement for the YCD Method 3 as part of the study. Specifically, a ChemoLock CL-14 vented bag spike adaptor in combination with an IV bag spike port was assessed for integrity using dye intrusion following EP 3.2.9 as well as YCD Method 3 (table 1). ChemoClave CH2000S syringe adaptors in combination with Luer-Lock syringes at 1 mL and 50 mL volume sizes were also assessed for integrity using the alternative EP 3.2.9 method for dye intrusion.

Method 1: Microbiological integrity using Brevundimonas diminuta

All manipulations required to prepare the test articles, including media draw-up, were performed using an aseptic technique under laminar flow in the biological safety cabinet.

Following media draw-up, the devices were disinfected following Quality Assurance of Aseptic Preparation Services protocols and incubated for 7 days at 25°C, followed by 7 days at 35°C, to confirm the absence of viable bacterial colonies within the Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) fill media of syringes and IV bags, before testing. All test articles shown to be free of growth were released for testing at the end of the 14-day incubation period. Test devices were then subjected to agitation for 2 hours on a rocker-shaker table (set at 70 rpm) to simulate handling and transport of the finished drug-filled devices. The test devices were then partially submerged into a vessel containing single strength TSB inoculated with subcultured Brevundimonas diminuta and incubated for an additional 14 days at 33±2°C. Post-testing, the devices were cleaned and inspected for evidence of microbial growth, as indicated by turbidity in the container.13

Method 1.1: Testing of ChemoLock and ChemoClave syringe adaptors in combination with 1 mL and 50 mL Becton Dickinson syringes

All testing was performed as described in the NHS YCD Method 1, subsection 1.4 for Partial Immersion.13 Becton Dickinson (BD) syringes (1 mL and 50 mL) were connected to ChemoLock or ChemoClave (Spiros Closed Male Luer) syringe adapters (20 units for each syringe size and CSTD type). Single strength TSB was withdrawn into each syringe and syringe adapter combination unit from a media fill vial (50 mL) pre-fitted with a ChemoLock or ChemoClave vented vial spike, respectively. The 1 mL syringes were filled with 0.75 mL TSB and the 50 mL syringes were filled with 37.5 mL TSB. Each of the syringe adaptors was activated three times prior to testing.

Method 1.2: Testing of ChemoLock and ChemoClave vented bag spikes in combination with 100 mL and 250 mL IV bags

Twenty 100 mL Freeflex IV bags were fitted with ChemoLock bag spikes and 20 250 mL Viaflo IV bags were fitted with ChemoClave bag spikes. The bags were emptied of their original contents (0.9% sodium chloride solution) and refilled with 50 mL TSB. The TSB vials were fitted with either ChemoLock or ChemoClave vial adaptors to mate with the appropriate CSTD type. 50 mL syringes fitted with ChemoLock or ChemoClave syringe adaptors were used for transferring the contents of the TSB vials into the test devices (IV bags). Each bag spike was activated three times prior to testing.

Method 2: Physical integrity-dye intrusion testing using methylene blue: YCD Method 3 and EP 3.2.9 Method “Rubber enclosures”

A solution of methylene blue (MB) dye was used to test physical integrity of the devices. The devices were fully immersed for the duration of the test. After the test the devices were thoroughly cleaned and visually inspected for any dye intrusion. The contents of the devices were analysed by a validated visual and spectrophotometric method for quantifying the presence of MB dye solution, using a microplate reader set to a detection wavelength of 664 nm (λmax for MB).14

Two different testing methods were followed: YCD Method 3, full immersion/dye ingress for syringes fitted with ChemoLock syringe adapter devices and for IV bags fitted with ChemoLock bag adapters; and an alternative dye intrusion testing methodology adapted from EP Monograph 3.2.9 “Rubber closures for containers for aqueous parenteral preparations for powders and for freeze-dried powders” self-sealing test for syringes and IV bags fitted with ChemoClave syringe adaptors and bag adapters.13 16

In all cases, each closure device (syringe adapter or bag adapter) was activated three times prior to testing.

Method 2.1: YCD Method 3: Physical integrity-dye intrusion testing using methylene blue 0.4% w/v of ChemoLock syringe adaptors in combination with 1 mL and 50 mL BD syringes

Syringes (1 mL and 50 mL) fitted with ChemoLock syringe adapters (20 units for each syringe size) were filled with 0.75 mL and 10 mL, respectively, of ultrapure water and then internal vacuum was applied by pulling back and securing the plungers a further 0.25 mL and 10 mL, respectively. All test devices were subjected to agitation for 2 hours on a rocker-shaker table (set at 70 rpm) to simulate handling and transport of the drug-filled devices. They were then subjected to a 2-hour dye intrusion test with 0.4% w/v MB solution.13

Method 2.2: EP 3.2.9 Method: Physical integrity-dye intrusion testing using methylene blue 0.1% w/v of ChemoClave syringe adaptors in combination with 1 mL and 50 mL BD syringes

Syringes (1 mL and 50 mL) fitted with ChemoClave syringe adaptors (20 each) were filled with 0.75 mL and 10 mL of ultrapure water. The devices were packed into a custom-built water-tight dye bath container. Additional air-filled syringes were used to secure the devices within the container so that the devices would remain fully submerged in the dye challenge agent during testing and would not float to the dye surface. Sufficient MB dye solution (0.1% w/v) was added to the container to completely submerge all test devices. The container was placed inside a Pyrex vacuum desiccator and the desiccator lid was applied. The vacuum desiccator stopcock was fitted and vacuum tubing was attached to the outlet of the stopcock. Vacuum (reduction of air pressure by 27 kPa) was applied to the contents of the desiccator using a Laboport diaphragm pump. A gas tap was placed immediately in line with the pump inlet, allowing air to be bled into the vacuum line to finetune the vacuum. The level of applied vacuum was monitored via a digital manometer connected to the vacuum line, after the air bleed tap, via a barbed T-piece. For each batch of devices tested, vacuum was applied for a total of 10 min in accordance with EP Method 3.2.9.16 Following the release of vacuum, the devices were kept in situ at atmospheric pressure for a further 30 min prior to removal from the dye container, thorough washing with water and subsequent analysis for evidence of dye ingress.

Method 2.3: YCD Method 3: Physical integrity-dye intrusion testing using methylene blue 0.4% w/v of ChemoLock vented bag spikes in combination with 100 mL IV bags

Twenty 100 mL Freeflex IV bags were fitted with ChemoLock bag spikes. The bags were emptied of their original contents (0.9% sodium chloride solution) and refilled with 50 mL of ultrapure water. All test devices were subjected to agitation for 2 hours on a rocker-shaker table (set at 70 rpm) to simulate handling and transport of the finished drug-filled devices. These were subjected to a 2-hour dye intrusion test with 0.4% w/v MB solution, in accordance with the NHS YCD method.13

Method 2.4: EP 3.2.9 Method: Physical integrity-dye intrusion testing using methylene blue 0.1% w/v of ChemoClave vented bag spikes in combination with 250 mL IV bags

Twenty 250 mL Viaflo IV bags were fitted with ChemoClave bag spikes. The bags were emptied of their original contents (0.9% sodium chloride solution) and refilled with 50 mL of ultrapure water. All test devices were subjected to agitation for 2 hours on a rocker-shaker table (set at 70 rpm) to simulate handling and transport of the finished drug-filled devices. These were subjected to a 2-hour dye intrusion test with 0.1% w/v MB solution, as per EP Method 3.2.9.16

Positive and negative controls

Appropriate positive and negative controls were prepared and tested alongside each batch of test devices for each type of test carried out. Details of how these controls were prepared and tested are shown in online supplemental file 1.

Three-axis inertia measurements of combination products for ChemoLock and ChemoClave closed system transfer devices with Luer-Lock syringes and IV bags

Prior to testing the integrity of the ChemoClave and ChemoLock CSTD components in combination with Luer-Lock syringes and IV bags, all test container combination devices were prepared and subjected to both real-world (~4.5 hours) and laboratory simulated (2 hours) transport challenges. In the UK, most parenteral pharmaceutical products are transported from aseptic production hubs to where they are needed by road transport. One concern is that, during the transport, the integrity of the combination container system may become compromised, which in practice could result in patient harm. Specifically, there is concern within UK PTS around the mechanical robustness of an IV bag spike combination with IV bag when prepared in advance. The authors believe that the bag spikes tested in the study do not pose the same level of risk for ‘de-spiking’ as pre-spiking using an administration set or other tubing set, and there are clear advantages to pre-spiking of IV bags in the pharmacy in terms of reducing the risk of exposure of nurses to oncolytic products. It was therefore deemed important to understand the problem better and to obtain inertial force measurements which would be exerted on finished products during transport by road. This was achieved using a calibrated three-axis inertial measurement data logger connected to the combination devices and used to monitor forces experienced by each device during both a real-world (~4.5-hour) and laboratory simulated (2-hour) transport challenge prior to testing the container integrity. Real-world and simulated mechanical perturbations were designed to represent a worst-case scenario physical challenge from transport on UK motorway and A-roads. The results from both the real-world and simulated (laboratory rocker-shaker) transportation challenges using industry standard power spectral density data plots are shown in figure 1 and figure 2, respectively. Power spectral density data plots are a convenient way to present inertial force measurement data across all the frequency domain versus transport time. An additional explanation of the transport simulation and the use of power spectral density data plots are shown in online supplemental file 1.

Results

Microbiological ingress

The results from testing the microbial integrity of the ChemoLock vented bag spike adaptor (CL-14) in combination with a 100 mL Freeflex IV bag and the integrity results for the ChemoLock syringe adapter (CL2000S) in combination with 1 mL and 50 mL Luer-Lock syringes are shown in table 1. The results from testing the microbiological integrity of the ChemoClave vented bag spike adaptor (CH-14) in combination with a 250 mL Viaflo IV bag and the results for the microbiological integrity testing of the ChemoClave syringe adapter (CH2000S) in combination with 1 mL and 50 mL Luer-Lock syringes are shown in table 2. For all three combinations of devices following partial immersion and 14-day incubation at 33±2°C with the B. diminuta challenge agent, no growth was observed in any (n=20) of the test device containers. All positive controls (n=2) showed positive growth of the organism, and all negative controls were free from growth. When a representative test device was inoculated post-test with the challenge organism and re-incubated at 33±2°C, growth was observed after 3 days of incubation demonstrating the growth-promoting characteristics of the growth media for the challenge organism.

All reported results were consistent with the ChemoLock combination containers demonstrating 100% integrity throughout the testing. No evidence for microbiological ingress was found and no evidence for dye intrusion was observed in any of the devices tested.

Dye intrusion

The results from dye ingress testing of the same components according to the NHS YCD Method 3 showed no evidence of dye ingress in any of the container systems tested.13 All positive control tests showed dye ingress under the conditions of the test. It is important to note that the YCD Method 3 provides instructions for applying a vacuum to the syringe container system which is maintained throughout the test and which is specific to the testing of syringe containers only.13 YCD Method 3 for dye intrusion was applied to IV bags in combination with ChemoLock and ChemoClave vented bag spike adaptors and was shown to be an effective method without the need for internal vacuum. The EP method was also applied to the ChemoClave vented bag spike in combination with Freeflex IV bags as well as Luer-Lock syringes and was able to apply an external vacuum to both types of container system, rigid and non-rigid, demonstrating system suitability.16

Transport power spectral density data

Prior to testing the integrity of the two CSTD technologies in combination with Lock-Luer syringes and IV bags: (1) ChemoClave CH2000S syringe adaptor components in combination with Lock-Luer syringes at 1 mL and 50 mL volume sizes and ChemoClave CH-14 vented bag adaptor in combination with IV bag (Viaflo) and (2) ChemoLock CL2000S syringe adaptor components in combination with Lock-Luer syringes at 1 mL and 50 mL volume sizes and ChemoLock CL-14 vented bag adaptor in combination with IV bag (FreeFlex), all of the combination products were prepared and subjected to two mechanical perturbations. The first was a simulated transport challenge using a laboratory shaker-table set to the maximum agitation for a period of 2 hours. During this time the devices were connected with a three-axis inertial measurement device and data logger that measured all of the mechanical vibration and inertial forces in the X, Y and Z axes versus time. The combination devices were then subjected to a real transport challenge comprising a journey on UK motorways and A-roads lasting approximately 4.5 hours. All combinations for both ChemoClave and ChemoLock combination devices were subjected to the same identical physical transport challenges. The same three-axis inertial measurement sensor and data logger was used for the real transport challenge. The inertial forces and perturbations experienced by the combination devices incorporating both ChemoClave and ChemoLock were designed to represent a worst-case scenario physical challenge representative of UK road transport requirements for the supply of parenteral drug products within the UK. All of the data from monitoring the inertial forces of the combination devices throughout the UK transport challenge was transformed into power spectral density data plots using standard methodological approaches. This is an industry standard and allows easy visualisation of the inertial forces over the full frequency domain to be presented versus transport time. It also allowed for comparison between the laboratory simulated arm of the physical challenge with the real-world transport challenge by presenting the data in the same format. Discussion

In the UK, bag spiking is not recommended in PTS due to the integrity of the bag/device system and concerns during transport and ongoing handling.11 The addition of closed system components as part of the final container system for pre-filled syringes and IV bags is currently limited by a paucity of suitable integrity data resulting in the replacement of syringe caps on the ward for closed system syringe caps in accordance with the latest NHS YCD guidelines.11 13 This practice has the potential to introduce a route of exposure of nurses and other healthcare workers to hazardous drugs during administration.10 11

To allow the adoption of CSTD components in PTS to minimise or eliminate these potential routes of exposure, the manufacturer of the CSTD components must provide container closure integrity data in accordance with the NHS YCD guidance for syringe integrity using the CSTD adaptor components, whereby the syringe adaptor replaces the sterile blind hub as the terminal closure.11 13

This study aimed to evaluate the integrity of the ChemoClave and ChemoLock CSTD components as alternative closures to a sterile blind hub cap for syringes, making them suitable for adoption in UK clinical practice.11 In addition, this study evaluated the required integrity data for the adoption of a CSTD bag spike in combination with two commonly used standard spike port-containing IV bags in PTS to support advanced preparation of ready-to-administer injectable drug products.11

The data generated showed that the container integrity for CSTD vented bag spike components ChemoLock (CL-14) and ChemoClave (CH-14), in combination with Freeflex and Viaflo IV bags, met the latest NHS YCD requirements for container integrity.13 It also demonstrated container integrity data to support the use of ChemoLock (CL2000S) and ChemoClave spinning spiros (CH2000S) syringe adapter components in combination with 1 mL and 50 mL Luer-Lock syringes as terminal closure devices to the latest NHS YCD requirements.13

This study provides an opportunity to improve UK clinical practice, further reducing the potential for accidental exposure to nurses by removing the need to replace syringe hub caps and spike bags on the ward.10 11 The data support the addition of these components in PTS in the UK rather than nurses having to add CSTD components at the ward level, which can expose them to unnecessary risks from chemotherapeutic drugs during administration.10 11 The data reported in this study have the potential to support greater adoption of CSTD components in PTS and to better protect nursing staff in clinical areas when administering ready-to-administer injectable cytotoxic drugs in line with current UK guidelines.11

To address concerns of de-spiking during transit, this study reported the results of a real-world transport and handling challenge with both container systems: the vented IV bag adaptors and syringe adapter combinations with Lock-Luer syringes. The results of this challenge showed no breach of container integrity in either the ChemoLock or ChemoClave CSTD combination products tested. This provides strong evidence in support of the use of these types of CSTD components in PTS including ChemoLock (CL-14) and ChemoClave (CH-14) vented IV bag adaptors, avoiding future need to add these components in clinical areas.11

This study reports on the interoperability of the EP 3.2.9 method for dye ingress testing, as previously validated for reconstitution of injectable drugs for ready-to-administer stock.16 17 This is an important finding as the YCD Method 3 for dye ingress test is only applicable to rigid syringe type devices,13 while the EP 3.2.9 method can accommodate all types of rigid and non-rigid container systems and maintains vacuum throughout testing as this is applied externally.16 The authors recommend the use of the EP 3.2.9 method for future testing of integrity of CSTD components as an alternative to the NHS YCD Method 3.13 16

Finally, the power spectral density data plot in figure 1 shows a typical real-world transport journey lasting 4–5 hours where the test combination devices were transported using a selection of motorway and fast A-roads typical of a UK transport journey from manufacturing hub to patient. The Y-axis shows the inertial G-forces experienced by the finished product devices while the X-axis, plotted on a logarithmic scale, shows the frequency domain with most data in the region of 0–10 Hz. There is a maximum inertial force G2/Hz excursion of 3.63 g during transport by road, the Y-axis readout with the majority of peaks on the respective PSD plot occurring between 0.1 Hz and 5 Hz. The data shown in figure 2 for the laboratory simulated transport journey is quite similar to the real-world data plot in so far as the main frequency data are located within the range of 0–10 Hz. The magnitude of the maximum inertial force experienced by the finished products under laboratory simulated transport conditions was 1.52 g, which is approximately half of the excursion under real-world road transport conditions. The main frequency of vibration shown by the rocker-shaker platform was 1.17 Hz, which is consistent with the fundamental frequency of gyration at 70 rpm. The magnitude of vibration is within a range of 0.001–0.01 G2/Hz. Although the two transport challenges had different PSD profiles, the simulated rocker-shaker table vibrational challenge has sufficient similarity in both frequency and magnitude of vibration to the real-world transport challenge to be a useful inclusion for future YCD testing of device integrity, where using a real transport challenge would not be practicable.

Conclusion

This study shows that the ChemoLock and ChemoClave syringe adaptors and IV bag adaptors maintain container closure integrity including microbial and dye integrity when challenged via recognised standards and following recommendations from the latest NHS YCD guidelines for handling of injectable cytotoxic drugs.11 13 16 These data may be used by UK PTS to incorporate ChemoLock and ChemoClave syringe and IV bag adaptors into their finished injectable product container systems for dose banded products, supporting the reduction of potential exposure of healthcare workers to hazardous drugs.8 11 15
