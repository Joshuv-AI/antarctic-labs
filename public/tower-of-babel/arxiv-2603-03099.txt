Why Adam Can Beat SGD: Second-Moment Normalization Yields Sharper Tails
Ruinan Jin, Yingbin Liang, Shaofeng Zou

Abstract
Despite Adam demonstrating faster empirical convergence than SGD in many applications, much of the existing theory yields guarantees essentially comparable to those of SGD, leaving the empirical performance gap insufficiently explained. In this paper, we uncover a key second-moment normalization in Adam and develop a stopping-time/martingale analysis that provably distinguishes Adam from SGD under the classical bounded variance model (a second moment assumption). In particular, we establish the first theoretical separation between the high-probability convergence behaviors of the two methods: Adam achieves a δ − 1 / 2 \delta^{-1/2} dependence on the confidence parameter δ \delta , whereas corresponding high-probability guarantee for SGD necessarily incurs at least a δ − 1 \delta^{-1} dependence.

What intrinsic differences between Adam and SGD underlie Adam’s empirical performance advantage, and can we develop an analytical framework that rigorously captures the advantage of Adam over SGD?

Main Contributions

We study the stochastic optimization problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x}),

where the algorithm has access only to stochastic gradient estimates and must therefore operate under sampling noise.
We run Adam for
T

Sharper Bound for Adam.
Under the common classical conditions (
L

1-\delta

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}=\widetilde{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta\,T}}\right)\quad\text{(Adam)}.

The above bound improves the existing bound of
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{-2})
in
(
35

16

and bound of
\delta^{-3/2}
in

49

(2)
Lower Bound for SGD and Provable Advantage of Adam.

Under the same assumptions of
L
-smoothness and bounded variance, we establish a hard-instance
(high-probability) lower bound for SGD. Specifically, for any step size
\gamma>0
and any horizon
T\geq 1
,
there exists a hard example such that, with probability at least
\delta
,
the SGD iterates
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
satisfy

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}\;\geq\;\widetilde{\Omega}\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{T}}\right).

Consequently, under the bounded-variance assumption, any high-probability guarantee for SGD on this stationarity
measure must necessarily exhibit (up to logarithmic factors) at least a
\delta^{-1}
dependence, i.e.,
it cannot improve upon
\widetilde{\Omega}\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{T}}\right)
in the worst case.
Comparing this with Adam’s upper bound suggests that Adam improves the confidence dependence by a factor of
\delta^{-1/2}
(up to
\mathrm{polylog}(\delta^{-1})
terms). From a distributional perspective, this comparison implies that across multiple independent runs, Adam’s performance, measured by the average gradient norm, concentrates more tightly around smaller values than SGD’s, indicating distributionally faster convergence. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that provably establishes a performance advantage of Adam over SGD, thereby providing a rigorous explanation for Adam’s empirical acceleration.

(3)
Why Second-Moment Normalization Accelerates Adam.
Our fine-grained analysis identifies second-moment normalization as a key mechanism behind Adam’s advantage over SGD. Specifically, under the bounded variance assumption, this normalization suppresses the accumulation of trajectory noise, so that the
quadratic variation
along the iterate path grows only
polylogarithmically
in
T
. In contrast, vanilla SGD is typically analyzed through
in-expectation
moment bounds, whose corresponding tail guarantees and thus confidence dependence degrade with the highest finite moment available for the stochastic gradients.

Preliminaries

Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x}),

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable. We assume that the function

2.2

To solve the problem, we assume that only gradient estimate of

of iterates
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
At each iteration
t\geq 1
, the method obtains a stochastic gradient estimate of
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}

Throughout the paper, we work on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})

Let

be an
independent

\mathcal{Z}

z_{t}:(\Omega,\mathscr{F})\to(\mathcal{Z},\mathscr{Z})

Define the canonical filtration
\mathscr{F}_{t}:=\sigma(z_{1},\ldots,z_{t})
.
The iterates
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
are assumed to be predictable, namely

\mathbf{x}_{t}
is
\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
-measurable for every
t\geq 1
.
Expectation is denoted by
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
.

At iteration
t\geq 1
, after observing

, the algorithm queries a stochastic first-order oracle

g_{t}:=g(\mathbf{x}_{t};z_{t}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},

and uses

as an estimator of the true gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}).
We assume that such a gradient estimator satisfies the bounded variance condition given in Assumption
2.3
.

2.2
Analytic Assumptions

We adopt the common conditions on the objective
f
and the stochastic oracle in our analysis.
Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
are standard regularity conditions ensuring a well-behaved descent geometry,
while Assumption
2.3
specifies a second moment bounded variance condition.

Assumption 2.1

(Finite lower bound)
.

The objective satisfies

f(x)\geq f^{*}>-\infty,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This condition is necessary for the minimization problem in
eq.

1
to be well-posed: without a finite lower bound, the objective can decrease without limit, and therefore there is no meaningful notion of (approximate) optimality or convergence.

Next, we impose the smoothness, which allows us to relate the one-step progress of the iterates to the gradient norm via the standard descent lemma.

Assumption 2.2

(
L
-smoothness)
.

There exists some constant
L>0
such that

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This
L
-smoothness condition is standard in stochastic optimization
(
30
;
9
;
4
)
: it is commonly adopted in the analysis of both SGD and adaptive methods and holds in a broad range of applications.

Assumption 2.3

(Bounded variance)
.

For each
t\geq 1
, the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
is conditionally unbiased, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[g_{t}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),

and there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|g_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq C.

Assumption
2.3
is a classical noise model in stochastic optimization and stochastic approximation; see, e.g.,

29
;
9
;
4
.
We adopt Assumption
2.3
as a representative “second moment only” condition: unlike assumptions that directly bound the (conditional) second moment of
g_{t},
which typically impose implicit boundedness requirements on the objective/iterates through
\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}],
it only controls the noise variance around
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. At the same time, it avoids the extra bookkeeping introduced by more general affine-variance models (e.g., ABC-type conditions). This choice keeps the focus on the core mechanism behind our Adam–SGD separation rather than on technical artifacts of overly general noise parametrizations.

Asymptotic notation.

Let
a(T)
and
b(T)
be nonnegative functions.
We write
a=\mathcal{O}(b)
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
a(T)\leq c\,b(T)
for all sufficiently large
T
, and we use
a=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(b)
to hide logarithmic terms.
We use
a=\Omega(b)
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
a(T)\geq c\,b(T)
for all sufficiently large
T
, and we use
a=\widetilde{\Omega}(b)
to hide logarithmic terms.

Main Results

In this section, we provide our main results and their implications.
We first highlight the second-moment normalization mechanism behind Adam, and then present our characterization of convergence separation between Adam and SGD.

3.1
Second-Moment Normalization in Adam

Expository reduction (not a restriction).

For expository purposes, we temporarily begin with an RMSProp-style
(
12
)
update to make the second-moment normalization explicit.
1
1

1

This is only a conceptual simplification to isolate the effect of the second-moment scaling; our main results and analysis concern the full Adam algorithm (Algorithm
1
).

Namely, consider

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{v_{t}}+\epsilon}\odot g_{t},\qquad v_{t}=\beta_{2}v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})(g_{t}\odot g_{t}).

Quadratic variation viewpoint.

Stochastic-gradient iterates typically admit an (implicit) semimartingale structure, and a key object that governs their
trajectory behavior is the
quadratic variation
of the iterate increments,

[\mathbf{x}]_{T}\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\|^{2}.

(2)

For the RMSProp-style update above,

[\mathbf{x}]_{T}\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{v_{t}}+\epsilon}\odot g_{t}\right\|^{2}\;\approx\;\gamma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{g_{t,i}^{2}}{v_{t,i}},

(3)

where the approximation hides only the smoothing by
\epsilon
.

To analyze the normalization effect in
eq.

3
, we first provide a closed form for
v_{t}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
v_{t}
in Algorithm
1
and each coordinate
i\in[d]
, one has

v_{t,i}=\beta_{2}^{t}v+(1-\beta_{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_{2}^{\,t-k}g_{k,i}^{2},\qquad\forall\,t\geq 1.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
A.1
.

When
\beta_{2}
is chosen close to
1
and
\gamma
is close to
0
(e.g.,
\beta_{2}=1-O(1/T),\ \gamma=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
which is standard in Adam-type analyses; see, e.g.,
(
49

35

),
the exponential weights in Proposition

make

a smoothed running average of past squares. Approximately, we have that

v_{t,i}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}^{2}.

(4)

Plugging
eq.

4
into
eq.

3
yields the characteristic self-normalized form

\displaystyle[\mathbf{x}]_{T}

\displaystyle\;\approx\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{g_{t,i}^{2}}{\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}^{2}}\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\!\left(1+\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}g_{t,i}^{2}}{v}\right)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;d\,\log\!\left(1+\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{dv}\right),

(5)

where the logarithmic bound is pointwise and follows from the standard inequality

\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{a_{t}}{a_{0}+\sum_{s\leq t}a_{s}}\leq\log\!\left(1+\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{t}}{a_{0}}\right)

for
a_{t}\geq 0
.
Crucially,
section

3.1
requires no moment or tail assumptions on
g_{t}
: the normalization by
v_{t}
transforms the quadratic variation into a logarithmic functional
of
\sum_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}
.
As a result, standard manipulations of this logarithmic form yield a high-probability bound
on the quadratic variation with only
\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)
dependence on the confidence level.

Comparison with SGD.

In comparison, SGD updates

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\gamma\,g_{t}

(6)

accumulate
raw
gradients with a constant step size. Its quadratic variation is therefore given by

[\mathbf{x}]_{T}^{\mathrm{SGD}}\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\|^{2}\;=\;\gamma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|g_{t}\|^{2},

(7)

whose tail behavior is governed directly by the tail of
\|g_{t}\|^{2}
. Under a mere second-moment assumption on the stochastic gradients,
the sum
\sum_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}
does not admit exponential-type concentration, where rare but large realizations of
g_{t}
can dominate
eq.

7
. As a consequence, standard martingale arguments can at best yield polynomial tail bounds for SGD’s trajectory fluctuations, with the dependence on the confidence parameter
\delta
no better than
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)
.

Hence, the second-moment regularization in Adam fundamentally benefits the accumulation structure of the trajectory quadratic variation, upgrading the dependence on the confidence parameter
\delta
in high-probability bounds from the polynomial regime characteristic of SGD under bounded second-moment noise to a markedly sharper
\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)
regime. This mechanism underlies the improved convergence rate characterized in our analysis.

3.2
Convergence Rate Separation

We now formalize a concrete consequence of the second-moment normalization mechanism in Algorithm

A trajectory-wise intermediate bound.

The second-moment normalization first manifests itself through a high-probability control of the

\mathrm{polylog}(\delta^{-1})

From preconditioned energy to convergence rate.

To establish the bound on

A high-probability lower bound for SGD.

To show that the improved confidence dependence is intrinsic to Adam under the same modeling assumptions, we provide a high-probability lower bound for SGD.

Proposition 3.3

Comparison between Adam and SGD.

Comparison between Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
implies that Adam enjoys a convergence rate of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta\,T}}\right)
, whereas SGD cannot be faster than
\widetilde{\Omega}\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{T}}\right)
. This reveals a clear separation between the two algorithms. To our knowledge, this is the first result that rigorously characterizes a convergence-performance gap between Adam and SGD. From a distributional viewpoint (i.e., without fixing
\delta
a priori), this improved tail behavior predicts a
systematic shift over repeated runs. Namely, the performance curve of Adam concentrates on smaller values than that of SGD,
so in typical empirical plots of
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}
across many trials, Adam’s curves lie overall below SGD’s.

Where the separation comes from.

The separation is most transparent at the level of the cumulative preconditioned gradient energy
(comparison between Proposition
3.2
and Proposition
3.3
).
This is exactly where Adam’s second-moment normalization, via the coordinatewise scaling

(\sqrt{v_{t}}+\epsilon)^{-1},
directly moderates the oracle noise. Under the same bounded-variance condition,
such a mechanism improves the
\delta^{-1}
-type high-probability behavior of SGD to a
\mathrm{polylog}(\delta^{-1})

control.

When converting this preconditioned control into a convergence rate, part of the gain is unavoidably lost. The de-preconditioning step requires lower control on the effective preconditioner and, in our argument,
this introduces a
\delta^{-1/2}
degradation. This loss is specific to adaptive methods, as it
comes from handling the random, data-dependent
\{v_{t,i}\}
. In contrast, SGD uses a deterministic step size

\gamma
and does not incur an extra confidence penalty in this conversion.

Overall, even after de-preconditioning, Adam still enjoys a strictly better confidence dependence:
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta T}}\right)
(cf. Theorem
3.1
),
whereas SGD (in general) cannot beat
\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{T}}\right)
under the same classical assumptions
(cf. Theorem
3.2
).

Proof Sketch of Theorem
3.1

In this section, we provide a proof sketch of Theorem
3.1
to highlight the proof strategy and major technical challenges.

Proof sketch in a nutshell.

We remove momentum via the change of variables
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\beta_{1}\mathbf{x}_{t-1})/(1-\beta_{1})
and derive a one-step descent inequality for a Lyapunov potential combining
f(\mathbf{y}_{t})
with a cumulative preconditioned gradient energy term. We then localize the trajectory with a stopping time and exploit Adam’s second-moment normalization
(\sqrt{v_{t}}+\epsilon)^{-1}
to control the martingale and residual terms, yielding a high-probability
\mathrm{polylog}(\delta^{-1})
bound on the preconditioned energy. Finally, de-preconditioning gives the convergence rate, with the only
\delta^{-1/2}
loss coming from controlling the terminal accumulator
\Sigma_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{T,i}
.

Step 1 (Eliminating momentum).

Following the standard variable substitution in
27
, define

\mathbf{y}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\beta_{1}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}}{1-\beta_{1}}\ \ (t\geq 2),\ \ \mathbf{y}_{1}:=\mathbf{x}_{1},

(9)

which transforms the Adam update into a momentum-free recursion in
\mathbf{y}_{t}
(up to the adaptive scaling).

Step 2 (Descent inequality).

Using
L
-smoothness and the update expressed in terms of
\mathbf{y}_{t}
, we derive a one-step descent bound of the following form.

Lemma 4.1

(Descent lemma (informal))
.

Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{v_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be generated by Algorithm
1
, and let
\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be defined by
eq.

9
. Assume Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
hold, and set
\beta_{2}:=1-\frac{1}{T}
.
Then, for every
t\geq 1
, the following descent inequality holds:

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y}_{t+1})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\,-\,\Bigl(f(\mathbf{y}_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\;+\;D_{t}\;+\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left\|\gamma_{t}\odot m_{t}\right\|^{2}\right).

(10)

The leading term on the right-hand side,

-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}
,
is the principal descent component. Moreover,
\{(D_{t},\mathscr{F}_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
forms a martingale difference sequence,
and
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left\|\gamma_{t}\odot m_{t}\right\|^{2}\right)
collects higher-order (residual) contributions. The quantity
D_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle D_{t}

\displaystyle:=\overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\gamma_{t,i}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}g_{t,i}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}g_{t,i}}^{D_{t,1}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\Delta_{t,1,i}|\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]-|\Delta_{t,1,i}|\right)}_{D_{t,2}}.

(11)

The full statement of this lemma is deferred to the appendix (Lemma
A.1
).

Step 3 (Introducing a stopped process).
We first define a nonnegative reference function:
\bar{f}:=f-f^{*}+1.
Then, with the descent lemma in hand, we obtain a trajectory-wise estimate: for any

k\in[1,T]
,

\displaystyle\bar{f}(\mathbf{y}_{k})+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}\,\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}(1)+\underbrace{\left|\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}D_{t}\right|}_{T_{1,k-1}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\left\|\gamma_{t}\odot m_{t}\right\|^{2}}_{T_{2,k-1}}\bigg).

(12)

Based on
eq.

12
, we decompose the cumulative trajectory error into three terms, and the last two of them are
denoted by
T_{1,k-1}
and
T_{2,k-1}
. A key feature is that these terms depend on the loss function value
\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\ (1\leq t\leq k-1),
which calls for a localization argument. We therefore introduce a stopped process to control the
influence of
\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\ (1\leq t\leq k-1)
, and later remove the stopping to recover bounds for the original trajectory. Specifically, we introduce the following stopping time. For any fixed threshold
G>0
, define:

\tau_{G}\;:=\;\min\{\,t:\ \bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\geq G\,\}.

(13)

Then
\tau_{G}
is a predictable (previsible) stopping time with respect to
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, i.e.,
\{\tau_{G}\leq t\}\in\mathscr{F}_{t-1}
for all
t\geq 1
.

We can then derive the following control inequality for the stopped process:

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{\bar{f}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau_{G}\wedge T})+\frac{1}{8}\overbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}\,\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}}^{\mathcal{E}_{\tau_{G}\wedge T}}}_{W_{\tau_{G}\wedge T}}\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}(1)+T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}+T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}.

(14)

We will leverage the second-moment normalization to derive suitable higher-moment bounds for the above stopped process.

Step 4 (Higher-moment bounds for
T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
and
T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
).

We next derive the higher-moment estimates for the stopped remainder terms

T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
and
T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
in
eq.

14
.
Fix any
p\geq 12
and consider moments of order
p/6
.

Moment bound for
T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
.

Recall that
T_{1,k-1}=\bigl|\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}D_{t}\bigr|
, where
\{(D_{t},\mathscr{F}_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
is a martingale difference sequence.
Applying the discrete Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality (Lemma
B.2
) with exponent
p/6
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}^{\,\frac{p}{6}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}D_{t}\right|^{\frac{p}{6}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{p}{6}\right)^{\frac{p}{6}}\Bigg(\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}D_{t,1}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{12}}\right]\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}D_{t,2}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{12}}\right]\right)\Bigg).

(15)

It therefore suffices to bound the
p/12
-moments of the corresponding quadratic variations.
Using the stopping condition
\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\leq G
for all
t<\tau_{G}
together with elementary inequalities, we reduce the first quadratic variation term to a sum:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}D_{t,1}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{12}}\right]\;\leq\;G^{\frac{p}{12}}\left(\frac{p}{12}\right)^{\frac{p}{12}}\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}^{2}\,g_{t,i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{12}}\right]\right).

(16)

The sum above admits a
pathwise
logarithmic bound:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S_{t}:=dv+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|g_{s}\|^{2}
. Then, on every sample path,

\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}^{2}\,g_{t,i}^{2}\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\log\!\left(1+\frac{S_{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}}{vdT}\right)\right).

(17)

A full version of Lemma
4.2
is provided in Lemma
A.2
.
Notably,
eq.

17
holds
pointwisely
(without taking expectations), which is a direct consequence
of the second-moment normalization.

For the second quadratic variation term in
section

4
, a similar (and simpler) argument yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}D_{t,2}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{12}}\right]\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(G^{\frac{p}{12}}\right).

(18)

Combining
section

4
–
eq.

18
and Lemma
4.2
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}^{\,\frac{p}{6}}\right]\;\leq\;\left(\frac{p}{6}\right)^{\frac{p}{6}}\,G^{\frac{p}{12}}\,\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log^{\frac{p}{12}}\!\left(1+\frac{S_{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}}{vdT}\right)\right]\right)\;+\;\left(\frac{p}{6}\right)^{\frac{p}{6}}\,\mathcal{O}\!\left(G^{\frac{p}{12}}\right).

(19)

Moment bound for
T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
.

To control
T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
, we use the following deterministic domination (full statement in Lemma
A.5
):

\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}\|\gamma_{t}\odot m_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}\|\gamma_{t}\odot g_{t}\|^{2}.

(20)

Combining
eq.

20
with the same pathwise logarithmic control in Lemma
4.2

yields the desired
p/6
-moment estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}^{\,\frac{p}{6}}\right]\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}(1)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log^{\frac{p}{6}}\!\left(1+\frac{S_{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}}{vdT}\right)\right].

(21)

With
eq.

19
and
eq.

21
in hand, we proceed to Step 5, where we plug these high-moment bounds
into an exponential-moment argument for the stopped process.

Step 5 (Trajectory-wise preconditioned-gradient control).

Returning to the stopped bound in
eq.

14
, we control the stopped energy process
by an exponential-moment argument. Combining
eq.

14
with the high-moment estimates
from Step 4 (for
T_{1,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
and
T_{2,\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}
) and Taylor series, we obtain a uniform bound of the form

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left\{W_{\tau_{G}\wedge T}^{1/6}\right\}\right]\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}(1)\,\exp\!\left\{G^{1/7}\right\}.

(22)

Removing the stopping time.

On the event
\{\tau_{G}\leq T\}
, we have
W_{\tau_{G}\wedge T}=W_{\tau_{G}}\geq f(\mathbf{y}_{\tau_{G}})\geq G
,
and hence Markov’s inequality applied to
eq.

22
yields

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{G}\leq T)\;=\;\mathbb{P}(W_{\tau_{G}\wedge T}\geq G)\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}(1)\exp\!\left\{G^{1/7}-G^{1/6}\right\}.

(23)

Therefore, for any prescribed
\delta\in(0,1)
, choosing

G_{\delta}\;:=\;\Theta\!\left(\log^{6}\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)

ensures
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{G_{\delta}}\leq T)\leq\delta/2
.

Bounding the preconditioned gradient energy.

By construction of
W_{t}
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{\tau_{G_{\delta}}\wedge T}\;\leq\;W_{\tau_{G_{\delta}}\wedge T}.

(24)

Applying Markov’s inequality to
eq.

22
with
G=G_{\delta}
and using
eq.

24
,
we obtain that, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\mathcal{E}_{\tau_{G_{\delta}}\wedge T}\;\leq\;G_{\delta}.

On the intersection with the event
[\tau_{G_{\delta}}>T]
(which holds with probability at least
1-\delta/2
),
we have
\tau_{G_{\delta}}\wedge T=T
, so the above bound becomes
\mathcal{E}_{T}\leq G_{\delta}
.
By a union bound, it can be shown that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathcal{E}_{T}\;\leq\;\Theta\!\left(\log^{6}\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right),

which is the claimed trajectory-wise preconditioned-gradient control proved in Proposition
3.2
.

Step 6 (Convergence rate from preconditioned-gradient control).

We now explain how the preconditioned-gradient control implies the convergence-rate bound.
By Step 5, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, we have the trajectory-wise estimate

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}\,\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\;\leq\;G_{\delta},\qquad G_{\delta}=\Theta\!\left(\log^{6}\!\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

(25)

Removing the adaptive preconditioner.

Fix any
t\in[T]
and
i\in[d]
. By Lemma
B.7
with
k=t-1
and
h=T
,

v_{t-1,i}\;\leq\;4\,v_{T,i},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\sqrt{v_{t-1,i}}+\epsilon\;\leq\;2\sqrt{v_{T,i}}+\epsilon\;\leq\;2\bigl(\sqrt{v_{T,i}}+\epsilon\bigr).

Therefore,

\gamma_{t-1,i}=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{T}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t-1,i}}+\epsilon}\;\geq\;\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{T}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\bigl(\sqrt{v_{T,i}}+\epsilon\bigr)}.

(26)

Summing
eq.

26
over
i
yields

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}\,\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{\eta}{2\sqrt{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)_{i}^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{T,i}}+\epsilon}\;\geq\;\frac{\eta}{2\sqrt{T}\,(\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}+\epsilon)}\;\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2},

(27)

where
\Sigma_{T}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{T,i}
and we also use
\sqrt{v_{T,i}}+\epsilon\leq\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}+\epsilon
.

Summing
eq.

27
over
t=1,\dots,T
and combining with

eq.

25
, we obtain

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{2\sqrt{T}\,(\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}+\epsilon)}{\eta}\;G_{\delta}.

(28)

Controlling
\Sigma_{T}
.

Under the bounded variance condition, one shows
\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_{T}]\leq F<\infty
, and hence

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}>\sqrt{2F/\delta}\right)\leq\delta/2
by Markov’s inequality.
Combining this with
eq.

25
via a union bound gives that,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{G_{\delta}}{\sqrt{\delta\,T}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta\,T}}\right).

The only
\delta^{-1/2}
factor comes from controlling
\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}
in

eq.

28
, i.e., from eliminating the adaptive preconditioner.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided a provable explanation for a common empirical observation: Adam can outperform vanilla SGD under the classical bounded variance noise model.
Our main insight is that Adam’s diagonal second-moment accumulator yields sharper tail control of the iterates’ quadratic variation.
Using a stopping-time localization and martingale argument, we derive high-probability guarantees.

Concretely, under standard smoothness and bounded variance, we obtain a trajectory-wise high-probability bound on the cumulative preconditioned gradient energy with only
\mathrm{polylog}(\delta^{-1})
dependence, leading to

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta T}}\right)\quad\text{w.h.p.}

In contrast, any comparable high-probability guarantee for SGD must incur at least

\widetilde{\Omega}\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{T}}\right)
dependence on
\delta
, giving the first provable separation between Adam and SGD in a convergent regime.

Future work includes extensions to other adaptive methods, tightening the de-preconditioning step, and studying structured settings with state-dependent heavy-tailed noise.

\displaystyle\quad\left(f(\mathbf{y}_{t+1})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}))_{i}^{2}\right)-\left(f(\mathbf{y}_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))^{2}_{i}+D_{t}+P_{t}.

(33)

The first term on the right-hand side,

-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))_{i}^{2},

constitutes the principal descent component.
The sequence
\{(D_{t},\mathscr{F}_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
is a martingale difference sequence, and the residual term
P_{t}
accounts for higher-order contributions.

The quantities
D_{t},\ P_{t}
and
\Delta_{t}
are given by

\displaystyle D_{t}

\displaystyle:=D_{t,1}+D_{t,2},

\displaystyle P_{t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}L}{2(1-\beta_{1})^{2}}\|\gamma_{t-1}\odot m_{t-1}\|^{2}+C\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\Delta_{t,1,i}||\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]+\left(L+\frac{35dL^{2}\eta}{v}\right)\|\gamma_{t}\odot m_{t}\|^{2},

The auxiliary quantities
\Delta_{t,1,i}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{t,1,i}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{T-1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t-1,i}}+\epsilon}-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t,i}}+\epsilon},

\displaystyle D_{t,1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\gamma_{t,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))_{i}g_{t,i}|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))_{i}g_{t,i},

\displaystyle D_{t,2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta_{t,1,i}||\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]-|\Delta_{t,1,i}|\right).

(35)

The proof of this lemma is deferred to Appendix
C.7
.

In contrast to the descent lemmas used in previous analyses
(e.g.,
35

16

which directly evaluate

This refinement enables the derivation of a high-probability bound

\delta

With the descent lemma established above, we are now prepared
to carry out the subsequent analysis.

We now define the following nonnegative reference function:

\bar{f}:=f-f^{*}+1.

High-probability guarantees can be recast as controlling the large-deviation event

\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>G,

For any

, we set the step size constant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\geq\max\left\{\left(\frac{8d\epsilon}{v^{3/2}}+\frac{8}{\sqrt{v}}\right),\frac{D_{\beta_{1}}\beta_{1}\sqrt{dL}}{1-\beta_{1}},1\right\}.

(36)

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>G\right]=\left[\tau_{G}\leq T\right]=\bigcup_{i=1}^{T}\left[\tau_{G}=i\right],

\tau_{G}

\displaystyle\tau_{G}:=\min\left\{t:\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\geq G\right\}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>G\right)

\displaystyle\mathop{\leq}^{\text{\emph{Markov} }}\frac{1}{e^{{G}^{1/6}}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left\{\frac{1}{6}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{t-1,i}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))^{2}_{i}\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{l}{e^{G^{1/6}}}\left(1+e^{\bar{f}^{1/6}(\mathbf{y}_{1})}+e^{G^{1/7}}+UG^{2}\right).

In addition,
eq.

60
(the second inequality) yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left([\tau_{G}\leq T]\right)\leq\frac{l}{e^{G^{1/6}}}\left(1+e^{\bar{f}^{1/6}(\mathbf{y}_{1})}+e^{G^{1/7}}+UG^{2}\right).

By a simple inclusion of events and an application of
De Morgan’s
laws, we obtain the following inequality:

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq G\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq G\right\}\cap[\tau_{G}>T]\right)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}^{\text{\emph{De~Morgan’s}}}1-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}>G\right\}\cup[\tau_{G}\leq T]\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{G}\wedge T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}>G\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left([\tau_{G}\leq T]\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{2l}{e^{G^{1/6}}}\left(1+e^{\bar{f}^{1/6}(\mathbf{y}_{1})}+e^{G^{1/7}}+UG^{2}\right).

(61)

We next investigate the properties of the key term

\Sigma_{\tau_{G}\wedge T-1}.

We emphasize that the only sublinear factor, namely
\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta}}
, arises entirely from this term.
More precisely, recalling
eq.

30
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{t,i}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)^{t}v_{0,i}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{T}\right)^{t-s}g_{s,i}^{2}\leq v+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}^{2},

which makes

\displaystyle\Sigma_{{t}}\leq dv+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}

We now consider the stopped process
\Sigma_{T}
. For this stopped process, we have the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{T}\leq dv+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

(62)

Observe that in the expression above,
dv
is deterministic, and hence all the randomness of

\Sigma_{T}
arises from
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.
We now investigate its concentration properties.
Taking expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\right]\mathop{=}^{\text{{Lemma \ref{sum:expect:ab}}}}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|g_{t}\|^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\mathop{\leq}^{\text{Assumption \ref{ass:abc}}}C+\frac{2L}{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq C+2L\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right].

(63)

To handle the expectation of the maximum, we invoke the following tower (layer-cake) representation, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}x\text{d}\left(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{x}1\text{d}G\text{d}\left(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{G}^{+\infty}\text{d}\left(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>x\right)\right)\text{d}G

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})>G\right)\text{d}G

\displaystyle\mathop{\leq}^{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{addam_0}}}\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{l}{e^{G^{1/6}}}\left(1+e^{\bar{f}^{{1}/{6}}(\mathbf{y}_{1})}+e^{{G}^{1/7}}+UG^{2}\right)\text{d}G

\displaystyle:=S<+\infty.

Substituting the above result into
section

A.3
, and then combining it with
eq.

62
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{T}\right]\leq dv+C+2LS:=F.

(64)

By
Markov’s
inequality, we obtain the following large-deviation probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}>{H}\right)\leq\frac{F}{H^{2}}.

Combining
section

A.3
, for any
H>0,
we readily obtain the following probability bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq HG\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq G\right\}\cap\left\{\sqrt{\Sigma_{T}}\leq H\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{2l}{e^{G^{1/6}}}\left(1+e^{\bar{f}^{1/6}(\mathbf{y}_{1})}+e^{G^{1/7}}+UG^{2}\right)-\frac{F}{H^{2}}.

Then we take

\displaystyle G:=G_{\delta}^{\prime}=\left(l\max\left\{128,\ 2\log\!\left(\frac{12\big(H+U\big)}{\delta}\right)\right\}\right)^{6},\ H=\sqrt{\frac{2F}{\delta}}.

With this choice, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq HG\right)\geq 1-\delta.

This implies that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2F}{\delta}}\left(l\max\left\{128,\ 2\log\!\left(\frac{12\big(H+U\big)}{\delta}\right)\right\}\right)^{6},

which implies

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{2F}{\delta}}\left(l\max\left\{128,\ 2\log\!\left(\frac{12\big(H+U\big)}{\delta}\right)\right\}\right)^{6}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}.

With this, we complete this lemma.
∎

is continuous on

and has a finite limit at
+\infty
, there exists
M_{\beta_{1}}\geq 10

such that
f(T)\leq\frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}+1
for all
T\geq M_{\beta_{1}}
. On the compact interval

[10,M_{\beta_{1}}]
, continuity implies that
f
attains its maximum, say

K_{\beta_{1}}:=\max_{T\in[10,M_{\beta_{1}}]}f(T)<+\infty.

Therefore, for all
T\geq 10
,

f(T)\leq\max\!\left\{K_{\beta_{1}},\ \frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}+1\right\}=:\frac{D_{\beta_{1}}}{1-\beta_{1}},

where

is a finite constant depending only on

. In particular,

\sup_{T\geq 10}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{T}\left(\frac{T\beta_{1}}{T-1}\right)^{k}\right)=\sup_{T\geq 10}f(T)\leq\frac{D_{\beta_{1}}}{4(1-\beta_{1})}<+\infty.

Combining the above with
section

C.6
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{t,i}^{2}m_{t,i}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}D^{2}_{\beta_{1}}

It is evident that
\gamma_{t,i}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{t,i}
, and thus we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{t,i}^{2}m_{t,i}^{2}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{t,i}^{2}m_{t,i}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}D_{\beta_{1}}^{2}.

Then we get that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\|=\|\gamma_{t}\odot m_{t}\|\leq\sqrt{d}\eta D_{\beta_{1}}.

With this, we complete the proof.

We first consider the case
s=1
. By the tower property of conditional expectation,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_{\mu\wedge n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\mu\wedge n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\right]\mathop{=}^{\text{Property \ref{sum:expect:ab}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\mu\wedge n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{k}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!X_{n}.

Hence
\|Y_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{1}}=\|X_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{1}}
, and the assertion follows.

Now assume
1<s<\infty
, and let
r=\frac{s}{s-1}
denote the conjugate exponent of
s
.
By the standard
L^{s}
–
L^{r}
duality, for nonnegative
Y_{\mu\wedge n}
we can write

\|Y_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{s}}=\sup\bigl\{\mathbb{E}[UY_{\mu\wedge n}]:U\geq 0,\ \|U\|_{L^{r}}=1\bigr\}.

Fix any
U\geq 0
with
\|U\|_{L^{r}}=1
, and define the martingale

M_{k}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[U\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right],\qquad k\geq 0.

Then, by the tower property again,

\mathbb{E}\![UY_{\mu\wedge n}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\mu\wedge n}U\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\mu\wedge n}M_{k-1}Z_{k}\right].

Since
Z_{k}\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}M_{k-1}Z_{k}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sup_{0\leq j\leq n}M_{j}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{\mu\wedge n}Z_{k}\right]=\mathbb{E}\![M_{n}^{*}X_{\mu\wedge n}],

where
M_{n}^{*}:=\sup_{0\leq j\leq n}M_{j}
.
Applying
Hölder’s
inequality gives

\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{*}X_{\mu\wedge n}]\leq\|M_{n}^{*}\|_{L^{r}}\|X_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{s}}.

By
Doob’s maximal
inequality for
L^{r}
martingales (
r>1
),

\|M_{n}^{*}\|_{L^{r}}\leq\frac{r}{r-1}\|M_{n}\|_{L^{r}}\leq\frac{r}{r-1}\|U\|_{L^{r}}=\frac{r}{r-1}=s.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[UY_{\mu\wedge n}]\leq s\|X_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{s}}.

Taking the supremum over all nonnegative
U
with
\|U\|_{L^{r}}=1
yields

\|Y_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{s}}\leq s\|X_{\mu\wedge n}\|_{L^{s}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Lower bound of SGD

To establish a lower bound, it is standard to exhibit a single hard instance (or a distribution over instances)—namely, a function and an oracle/noise model satisfying the assumed conditions—on which the target algorithm necessarily incurs the claimed dependence (e.g., in
\delta
or
T
). Such “hard-instance” or oracle-complexity arguments are a classical tool in stochastic optimization and first-order methods

(
1
;
5
)
.
In contrast, upper bounds typically require uniform control over all admissible instances under the same assumptions, which often demands substantially more delicate analyses.
Accordingly, in our SGD lower bound, it suffices to construct a concrete bounded variance noise family and a simple objective for which SGD exhibits the stated
\delta
-dependence.

We demonstrate via a simple counterexample that, under stochastic gradients for which only second moment information is available, SGD cannot enjoy a high-probability trajectory-wise guarantee with better than an
\widetilde{\Omega}(\delta^{-1})
dependence on the confidence parameter
\delta
(up to logarithmic factors). Moreover, its sample complexity measured by the averaged squared gradient norm is bounded below by

\Omega\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{T}}\right)
(again up to logarithmic factors).

To make these statements concrete, consider the one-dimensional objective

f(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2},

so that
\nabla f(x)=x
. Clearly,
f
is
L
-smooth with
L=1
since
\nabla^{2}f(x)\equiv 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we construct a family of noise distributions indexed by a parameter
A>0
.
For each fixed
A
, let
(\xi_{t}^{(A)})_{t=1}^{T}
be an i.i.d. sequence with common law

\xi^{(A)}=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}A,&\text{with probability }\dfrac{1}{2A^{2}},\\[6.0pt]
-A,&\text{with probability }\dfrac{1}{2A^{2}},\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{with probability }1-\dfrac{1}{A^{2}}.\end{cases}

By construction,
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{(A)}]=0
. Moreover, its second moment is uniformly bounded:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\xi^{(A)})^{2}\right]=A^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{A^{2}}=1,

so this family satisfies the bounded variance condition with constant
C=1
.

Under this oracle, SGD with step size
\gamma>0
updates as

x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\gamma\bigl(x_{t}+\xi_{t}^{(A)}\bigr).

We show that on this simple quadratic objective, the corresponding high-probability behavior of SGD must match the lower bound scalings stated above. We first state a few auxiliary lemmas that will be used in the lower bound proof.

D.1
Auxiliary Lemmas for SGD

Lemma D.1
.

For SGD on
f(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
with updates
x_{t+1}=(1-\gamma)x_{t}-\gamma\xi_{t}^{(A)}
with
x_{0}=-1,

the time-averaged second moment satisfies, for any
\gamma\in(0,1]
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]\leq\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}+\frac{2}{T\gamma}.

Optimizing the right-hand side gives

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{T},

so the best achievable rate with a constant step size is
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
.

Proof.

The recursion can be written as

x_{t+1}=(1-\gamma)x_{t}-\gamma\xi_{t},

which unrolls to

x_{t}=(1-\gamma)^{t}x_{0}-\gamma\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1-\gamma)^{t-1-k}\xi_{k}.

Since the noise is independent with zero mean, cross-terms vanish, and we obtain

\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]=(1-\gamma)^{2t}+\gamma^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1-\gamma)^{2(t-1-k)}.

The geometric sum evaluates to

\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1-\gamma)^{2(t-1-k)}=\frac{1-(1-\gamma)^{2t}}{1-(1-\gamma)^{2}}=\frac{1-(1-\gamma)^{2t}}{\gamma(2-\gamma)}.

Substituting yields the exact expression

\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]=(1-\gamma)^{2t}+\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}\bigl(1-(1-\gamma)^{2t}\bigr).

(81)

Averaging
eq.

81
over
t=1,\dots,T
gives

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]=\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)^{3}\bigl(1-(1-\gamma)^{2T}\bigr)}{T\gamma(2-\gamma)^{2}}.

Using the inequalities

(1-\gamma)^{3}\leq 1,\qquad(2-\gamma)^{2}\geq 1,\qquad 1-(1-\gamma)^{2T}\leq 1,

we obtain the upper bound

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]\leq\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}+\frac{2}{T\gamma}.

Define

g(\gamma)=\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}+\frac{2}{T\gamma},\qquad\gamma\in(0,1].

A direct derivative calculation shows that
g
is minimized at

\gamma^{\star}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{T}+1}.

Plugging
\gamma^{\star}
into
g(\gamma)
and simplifying yields

g(\gamma^{\star})=\frac{2}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{T}.

Thus the optimal order achieved by any constant step size is
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
.
∎

D.2
Formal Statement and Proof of Theorem
3.2

Theorem D.1

(Lower bound on convergence rate)
.

Fix any stepsize
\gamma>0
. Define

\delta_{\gamma}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\exp(-\gamma^{-2}),&\gamma<1,\\
+\infty,&\gamma\geq 1.\end{cases}

Then for every
0<\delta<\delta_{\gamma}
and every
T>1
, one can choose an i.i.d. noise sequence

\bigl(\xi_{t}^{(A_{\delta})}\bigr)_{t=1}^{T}
from the family constructed above, with

A_{\delta}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\delta}},

such that the corresponding SGD iterates satisfy the high-probability lower bound

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl|f^{\prime}(x_{t})\bigr|^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{32\delta\sqrt{T}\,\log(1/\delta)}\right)\;\geq\;\delta.

Proof.

By direct computation, for any
1\leq t\leq T,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}_{t+1}}{2}-\frac{x_{t}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=-\gamma x_{t}\left(x_{t}+\xi_{t}\right)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\left(x_{t}+\xi_{t}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\gamma x_{t}^{2}-\gamma x_{t}\xi_{t}+\gamma^{2}x_{t}\xi_{t}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\xi_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\gamma x_{t}^{2}-\gamma\left(1-\gamma\right)x_{t}\xi_{t}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\xi_{t}^{2}.

For any
1\leq s\leq T,
Summing the above inequality over
t
from
s
to
T
yields

\displaystyle\frac{x_{T}^{2}}{2}+\gamma\sum_{t=s}^{T-1}x_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{s}^{2}}{2}-\gamma(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=s}^{T-1}x_{t}\xi_{t}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=s}^{T-1}\xi_{t}^{2}.

In order for the summation notation to be well defined, we additionally adopt the convention that for any test function
b_{t}
,
\sum_{t=T}^{T-1}b_{t}=0.

By rearranging terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{T-1}x_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{2}^{2}-x_{T}^{2}}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=2}^{T-1}x_{t}\xi_{t}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{t=2}^{T-1}\xi_{t}^{2}.

Noting the obvious inequality

\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}x_{t}^{2}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\geq\frac{x_{1}^{2}-x_{T}^{2}}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)\overbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}x_{t}\xi_{t}}^{V_{T,1}}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\xi_{t}^{2}}_{V_{T,2}}.

(82)

We proceed by a case analysis according to the stepsize
\gamma
.

Case
\gamma\in(0,1)
. In this situation, we first now introduce two auxiliary parameters
Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, by applying the inequality above, we obtain the following event inclusion:

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle:=\left\{2\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\geq\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2}-\gamma(1-\gamma)Y+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}Z\right\}

\displaystyle\supset\left\{V_{T,1}\leq Y\right\}\cap\left\{V_{T,2}\geq Z\right\}.

(83)

Then, by
De Morgan’s
law, we readily obtain the following relationship between the complementary events:

\displaystyle A^{c}

\displaystyle\subset\left\{V_{T,1}\leq Y\right\}^{c}\cup\left\{V_{T,2}\geq Z\right\}^{c}

\displaystyle=\left\{V_{T,1}>Y\right\}\cup\left\{V_{T,2}<Z\right\},

which means

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A^{c}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{V_{T,1}>Y\right\}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{V_{T,2}<Z\right\}\right).

For any
0<\delta<1
, we choose

A_{\delta}=\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\delta}}.

Next, we estimate the three probabilities above separately. For
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{V_{T,2}<Z\right\}\right)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{V_{T,2}<Z\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\max_{1\leq t\leq T-1}\xi_{t}^{2}<Z\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}^{(*)}\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{1}^{2}\geq Z\right)\right)^{T-1}.

In step (
*
) of the derivation above, we used the i.i.d. property of the sequence
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
. Setting
Z=\dfrac{T}{2\delta}
in the expression above, we further obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{V_{T,2}<Z\right\}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{2\delta}{T}\right)^{T-1}\leq 1-2\delta.

For the term
\mathbb{P}\left(\{V_{T,1}>Y\}\right),
we apply
Markov’s
inequality directly and obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{T,1}>Y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Y^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{T,1}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{Y^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}x_{t}\xi_{t}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Y^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\,[x_{t}^{2}]\mathbb{E}\,[\xi_{t}^{2}]\mathop{\leq}^{\text{Proposition~\ref{fdasfweafrae}}}\frac{1}{Y^{2}}\bigl(2\sqrt{T}+1\bigr).

Now choose

Y=\frac{\sqrt{2\sqrt{T}+1\,}}{\sqrt{\delta}},

which implies

\mathbb{P}\left(V_{T,1}>\frac{\sqrt{\,2\sqrt{T}+1\,}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\right)\leq\delta.

Combining the choices of
Y
, and
Z
above, we obtain that the event
G
can be written as

G=\left\{2\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}-\gamma(1-\gamma)\frac{\sqrt{2\sqrt{T}+1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{2\delta}\right\}.

From the preceding probabilistic estimates, we have
\mathbb{P}(G)\geq\delta
.
Define
\delta_{\gamma}\coloneqq e^{-\gamma^{-2}}
.
Then, for all
\delta<\delta_{\gamma}
and all
T>1
, the lower bound on the right-hand side satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\gamma(1-\gamma)\frac{\sqrt{2\sqrt{T}+1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{3\delta}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{T}{\delta\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}\right)\;>\;\frac{1}{16}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\delta\log(1/\delta)}.

(84)

Consequently, since
\mathbb{P}(G)\geq\delta
, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{32}\cdot\frac{1}{\delta\log(1/\delta)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\;\geq\;\delta.

We next consider the regime
\gamma\geq 1
.

Case
\gamma\in[1,\infty)
.

Returning to
eq.

82
, when
\gamma\geq 1
we have

\frac{x_{T}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)V_{T,1}+\frac{\gamma}{2}V_{T,2}.

Since
x_{T}^{2}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}
, the left-hand side satisfies

\frac{x_{T}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2},

and hence

\frac{3}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)V_{T,1}+\frac{\gamma}{2}V_{T,2}.

(85)

Analogously to
section

D.2
, define the event
B
by

B\coloneqq\left\{\frac{3}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)Y+\frac{\gamma}{2}Z\right\}.

By
eq.

85
, we have the inclusion

\{V_{T,1}\leq Y\}\cap\{V_{T,2}\geq Z\}\subseteq B,

and therefore, by
De Morgan’s law
and a union bound,

\mathbb{P}(B^{c})\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}(V_{T,1}>Y)+\mathbb{P}(V_{T,2}<Z).

We choose

A_{\delta}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\delta}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z\coloneqq\frac{T}{2\delta}.

By the same argument as before, this yields

\mathbb{P}(V_{T,2}<Z)\leq 1-2\delta.

Next, set

Y\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{2\sqrt{T}+1}}{\sqrt{\delta}},

which implies

\mathbb{P}\!\left(V_{T,1}>Y\right)\leq\delta.

Combining the two bounds, we conclude that with these choices of
Y
and
Z
, the event
B
occurs with probability at least
\delta
.

In particular,
B
can be written explicitly as

\displaystyle B=\left\{\frac{3}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)\frac{\sqrt{2\sqrt{T}+1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{T\gamma}{2\delta}\right\}.

(86)

Since
\gamma\geq 1
, the right-hand side satisfies

\frac{1}{2\gamma}-(1-\gamma)\frac{\sqrt{2\sqrt{T}+1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}+\frac{T\gamma}{2\delta}\;\geq\;\frac{T\gamma}{2\delta}\;\geq\;\frac{T}{2\delta}\;>\;\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2\delta}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\;>\;\delta,

which completes the proof.

∎

D.3
Formal Statement and Proof of Proposition
3.3

Proposition D.1
.

Fix any stepsize
\gamma>0

\delta_{\gamma}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\exp(-\gamma^{-2}),&\gamma<1,\\

Then for every

Proof.

Setup.
