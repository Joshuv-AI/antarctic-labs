 ABSTRACT
The random motion of charge carriers in an electrical conductor is a function of its temperature. This
phenomenon generates an electrical signal, referred to as Johnson noise, that is present in all analog
signals. Conversely, the temperature dependence of the Johnson noise spectrum also enables a direct
measurement of absolute temperature, which can provide a secondary measurement of temperature when
quantified using sensors such as resistive temperature devices (RTDs) in a process called Johnson noise
thermometry (JNT). For nuclear environments, this is of particular interest in correcting for RTDs signal
drift caused by accumulation, transmutation, and displacement damage caused by neutron and gamma
irradiation. However, JNT instruments are challenging to implement in nuclear reactors because the
precision front end electronics required to perform such measurements are susceptible to radiation damage.
Radiation-hardened components developed for space applications could enable practical JNT systems to
be developed, though they are often qualified using surrogate particles and only for ionizing radiation. To
test the feasibility of using commercial off-the-shelf space-rated components to enable JNT measurements
in nuclear reactors, a JNT instrument was developed using radiation-hardened analog-to-digital converters
that could enable their physical placement within a test reactor while maintaining the signal processing
hardware and microcontroller unit within a clean environment. However, due to hardware limitations,
performing practical JNT measurements with this prototype was deemed infeasible. However, more
generally, the utilization of space radiation–rated electronics in terrestrial and space reactors could provide
a viable route to addressing the radiation survival requirements of next-generation reactor designs.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Accurate temperature measurements are critical to the safe and effective production of nuclear energy.
For example, temperature measurements across the reactor core are used in the measurement of thermal
power output of a reactor. Additionally, safety-critical temperature measurements provide on-line data
to ensure that components are maintained within their safe thermal limits. Resistive temperature devices
(RTDs) and thermocouples (TCs) are installed throughout a reactor and the surrounding facility to
acquire these measurements. RTDs are typically fabricated from platinum with a DC resistance of 100 Ω
(Pt-100) or 1000 Ω (Pt-1000) measured at 0 ◦ C, and they exhibit a lower uncertainty, higher reliability,
and less radiation-induced drift than TCs (Leonard and Hunkar 1967; Qian, Hinds, and Tonner 1997;
Hashemian and Jiang 2009; Chauhan and Neelakantan 2016). RTDs often require complex front-end
circuitry to achieve the desired measurement accuracy and precision. Despite these benefits, RTDs suffer
from calibration drift resulting from insulation degradation, corrosion, and radiation damage in both the
sensing element and lead wires (Kisner et al. 2004). To compensate for changes in lead wire impedance,
additional leads can be added. For example, the simplest method of measuring the resistance of an RTD
is a two-wire measurement, in which the voltage across a resistor bridge containing the RTD is compared
with a reference voltage. Three- and four-wire measurements add leads to one or both sides of the RTD
and enable the impedance changes of the leads to be mitigated (Figure 1). Although these leads could be
added to a bridge circuit in a manner similar to that of the two-wire setup, such configurations monitor the
current injected into the RTD and use a differential amplifier to measure the voltage difference across it. In
this way, the high-impedance inputs to the amplifier prevent significant current from flowing through their
RTD-connected leads and thus enable a precise measurement of the RTD while mitigating the effects of
the lead cables.
Despite the high precision and reliability achievable with RTDs, long-term exposure to nuclear reactor
environments nonetheless results in measurement drift arising from transmutations, lattice defects, gamma
heating, and other effects (Ross 1962). To enable reliable measurements using RTDs, they must be
frequently re-calibrated. Conventional re-calibration techniques compare measurements with a known
reference and are challenging to perform, particularly in smaller, next-generation reactor concepts. For
example, terrestrial small modular reactors (SMRs) and space reactors are both intended for 3–5 years
of continuous and autonomous operation without significant manual intervention. Performing RTD
re-calibrations while in space or when reactors are deployed in resource-limited environments imposes a
significant technical and regulatory hurdle. Additionally, installation of Johnson noise thermometry (JNT)
and other electronics more closely to a reactor could generally enable in a more compact, self-contained
instrumentation package for both space and terrestrial SMRs. To overcome this challenge, a method
that is commonly applied in high-energy physics to enable in situ RTDs re-calibration using thermal
electron (Johnson) noise is being adapted for the high-temperature, mixed neutron/gamma radiation
reactor environments. JNT has been proposed as a means to overcome the drift in the correlation between
the measured resistance of an RTD and its temperature by providing a complementary, secondary
measurement of temperature to correct for signal drift caused by accumulation of transmutation and
displacement damage from neutron and gamma irradiation (Britton et al. 2014). These JNT systems
utilize an existing commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) integrated circuit designed for performing 4-wire
measurements. This front-end circuitry has been demonstrated to survive space radiation environments and
has an integrated analog-to-digital converter (ADC), which facilitates integration with read-out circuitry.
This system will be tested in a research reactor to demonstrate JNT measurements in reactor environments
as well as the performance of space-characterized four-wire COTS circuitry within a terrestrial nuclear
environment. This design and the irradiation data will provide insight into the feasibility of utilizing JNT

Johnson noise arises from the random fluctuations and dissipative motion of charge carriers in an electrical
conductor and is a function of its temperature (Johnson 1927, 1928; Nyquist 1928). The energy density
within a conductor can be described in a form similar to Planck’s law of black-body radiation, which
describes the power spectral density (PSD) of the electromagnetic radiation emitted by a black body at
thermal equilibrium (Callen and Welton 1951). The PSD of Johnson noise S n ( f ) can be described as (Qu
et al. 2019)

 exp(h f /kB T ) − 1
where f is the frequency, Z is the electrical impedance of the body, h is Planck’s constant, kB is Boltzmann’s
constant, and T is the absolute temperature. For T > 298 K, f < 1 GHz, and for a purely resistive element
(i.e., R{Z} = R), this expression can be simplified to

                                             S n ( f ) ≈ 4RkB T ∆ f,                                      (2)

where ∆ f is the measurement bandwidth, which is more typically used to represent Johnson noise. In this
form, knowledge of a conductor’s resistance enables the PSD to be used to directly calculate the absolute
temperature T of a conductor. For a device such as an RTD that can be approximated as an ideal resistor
at a constant temperature, measuring the Johnson noise can provide a secondary method to calculate
temperature. This approach to measuring temperature using Johnson noise is called JNT.
The Johnson noise in an RTD is proportional to the measurement bandwidth, the resistance of the Pt-100
or Pt-1000 element, and the absolute temperature of the RTD (Kisner et al. 2004). Using a differential
voltage measurement across a shunt resistor to measure the current injected into the RTD, the impedance
of the RTD can be measured via a second differential voltage measurement. This measurement is the sum

 3

of the Johnson noise, the noise introduced by the amplifier, and the resistance of the RTD. To measure the
Johnson noise, a second amplifier is introduced to perform the same differential voltage measurement
across the RTD. The cross-spectral density (CSD) of these two amplified measurements allows the
Johnson noise to be isolated from the uncorrelated amplifier noise. High-order, high-pass filters are
also introduced to eliminate the DC component of the RTD measurement, which corresponds to the DC
resistance of the RTD; this isolates the Johnson noise. High-order, low-pass filters are used to prevent
aliasing from influencing the measurements.
The electronics used in JNT instruments are challenging to implement in nuclear reactors because of the
susceptibility to radiation-induced damage and drift of the precision front-end electronics required to
perform such a measurement. Radiation-hardened components developed for space applications could
enable practical JNT systems to be developed, though these components are often qualified only for
ionizing radiation and often using surrogate particles. To test the feasibility of using COTS space-rated
components to enable JNT measurements in nuclear reactors, a JNT instrument was developed using
radiation-hardened ADCs that can be physically placed within a test reactor while maintaining the
signal processing hardware and microcontroller unit (MCU) within a clean environment. To perform
these measurements, custom circuit boards were designed to facilitate connections between the RTD,
the four-wire ADC intended for irradiation environments, and the leads for digital communication and
additional measurements intended to be performed during testing. An MCU-based data acquisition and
storage platform was used to facilitate data transfer between the 3-wire ADC and a host computer where
data have been logged, and the CSD-based digital signal processing was performed using a Pt-1000 RTD.
The implementation challenges encountered in the development of this prototype JNT system highlight the
need for the further development and implementation of radiation-tolerant communication protocols and
bus designs for use in terrestrial and space reactors; however, these limitations do not preclude the use of
space-rated components to hasten the development of reactor-rated systems.

2.2 ELECTRICAL DESIGN
To perform a JNT measurement using a differential ADC, perhaps the most consideration should be spent
designing the front-end filtering and gain stages, prior to digitization (Kisner et al. 2004). A third-order
passive bipolar low-pass filter stage with a cutoff frequency of 2.5 kHz was followed by a third-order
passive bipolar high-pass filter stage with a cutoff frequency of 2.5 Hz. The positive terminal of the
differential ADC was connected through the band-pass filter bank to the positive lead of the RTD during
testing. The negative terminal of the ADC was connected through an identical band-pass filter bank to the
negative terminal of the RTD. Using the Magics integrated circuits (ICs), a programmable constant current
source was applied to energize the RTD and enable four-wire impedance measurements.
Data were recorded using a purpose-built data acquisition system based around an STM32 NUCLEO-H753ZI
development board (STMicroelectronics NV; Geneva, Switzerland) with a clock speed of 480 MHz.
Interrupt-driven polling was configured to occur at a rate of 5 kHz, with samples acquired from two
16-bit radiation-hardened ADCs (MAG-SEI00002-NP; Magics Technologies NV; Antwerp, Belgium)
and both channels on a non-radiation-rated accessory ADC (ADS8354; Texas Instruments, Inc.) intended
to be located within a radiologically clean area. The ADCs were configured as shown in Table 1 prior to
data acquisition, immediately before enabling the excitation current through the RTD. Communication
between the MCU and the ADCs was established through a single serial peripheral interface (SPI) bus.
Data acquired from the ADCs in the producer thread were placed in a first-in-first-out buffer and passed
to a consumer thread, where it was unpacked and interleaved into a data stream. Once a pre-determined
number of samples was acquired, data were transferred as four interleaved 16-bit data words via a TCP/IP
connection for further processing on a host computer. The MCU firmware was written using the Zephyr
RTOS (v4.2.99) and the graphical user interface and data logging code was written in Python (v3.11.13)
using PySide6 (v6.9.0).

3.2 CABLE IMPEDANCE
To facilitate experiments in irradiation facilities while isolating the effects of radiation on the Magics
ADCs, the MCU daughter card should ideally be located in a clean area with connections to the irradiated
Magics ADCs breakout boards over cables. To facilitate this connection, custom cabling was fabricated
to connect the pin headers on the Magics ADC breakout boards to the screw terminal blocks on the MCU
daughter cards using 3 m 9-conductor 9/36 stranded cable, 1 m 7-conductor 18 AWG solid core wire, and
0.15 m 28 AWG single-conductor jumper wires.
The SPI signaling over the 3 m cables resulted in significant attenuation of the SPI clock (SCLK) line such
that configuration of the Magics ADCs could not be performed (Figure 6). However, the chip select (CS)
line could be successfully transmitted. The SPI signaling over the 1 m cable was improved over the 3 m
cables: the master-out slave-in (MOSI), CS, and SCLK were all successfully transmitted to the Magics

 4. DISCUSSION
Beyond the specific use-case of MAG-SEI00002-NP ICs to perform JNT measurements, other space-rated
components could be useful in the radiation environments relevant to terrestrial or space reactors.
Space-rated electronic devices are often validated using irradiation with surrogate particles to an equivalent
dose to mimic conditions in space. However, neutron and gamma irradiation conditions seen in terrestrial
reactors are significantly higher in sustained flux and have different effects on the hardware than the
conditions under which the devices were validated. Still, space-rated components properly validated under
reactor-relevant radiation conditions and utilizing appropriate shielding could provide a means of reducing
measurement uncertainty in terrestrial space and power reactors.
The MAG-SEI00002-NP ADCs used in the JNT system developed for the present work have been
validated to a radiation hardness of > 1 MGy (TID) and an SEL/SEU/SET value of 80 MeV cm2 /mg LET.
Given this level of radiation hardness, the reactor instrumentation community is interested in evaluating
the feasibility of using this IC as a drop-in data acquisition solution for terrestrial and space reactor
environments. To evaluate the feasibility of developing instrumentation using radiation-rated ICs, the JNT
system prototype was designed and implemented using an MCU to interface with the two radiation-hard
MAG-SEI00002-NP ADCs. The common SPI bus used to communicate between the ADCs and the
MCU was operated at 4.096 MHz, which enabled both ADCs to be sampled sequentially and processed
at a rate of 20 kHz (40 kHz split between two ADCs). Furthermore, a current of 1.2 mA was used to
drive a Pt-1000 RTD (1 kΩ load), and the positive and negative ADC inputs were band-pass filtered
separately with a pass band from 2.5 to 2,500 Hz. While noise with amplitude on the order expected of
Johnson noise was recorded (Figure 8) using the prototype instrument, several challenges arose when
performing JNT measurements and suggest that the MAG-SEI00002-NP may not be suited as a drop-in
component to perform JNT measurements without extensive outside considerations—namely, on-board
digital communication and data acquisition rate achievable with the present design paradigm.
The impedance of the wires or traces connecting the controller IC (MCU) to the peripheral device (ADC)
resulted in the inability of the ADCs to drive the signals carrying information between them and the MCU
over cables longer than 0.15 m (Figure 6). This limitation is inherent to hardware implementations of the
SPI protocol, which is intended for digital communication between ICs on the same printed circuit board
(PCB) or between PCBs in close proximity. Specifically with regard to the MAG-SEI00002-NP IC, this
limitation on the length of the cables connecting the MCU and the ADCs forces the MCU to be physically
located within 15 cm. This close proximity implies that if the ADCs were intended for use in a radiation
environment, the controller electronics required to interface with them would also need to be adequately
radiation-hard.
While SPI, and other communication protocols intended for local IC-to-IC communication (I2C, CAN,
etc.), may not be suitable to enable front-end electronics to be operated within a radiation field, other
communication protocols and techniques have been developed which would enable such a strategy. For
example, Ethernet and USB are both designed to transfer information at speeds > 1 GB/s over cables
several meters in length, or greater. Optical fibers and the opto-electronics required to communicate across
them are also designed to carry signals over long distances (> 100 km) and have been demonstrated to
survive neutron radiation (Sweeney et al. 2024).
The 20 kHz sampling rate of the present JNT system is also a challenge for practical use. Johnson
noise is on the order of nV to µV and is calculated based on an root mean square (RMS) voltage, which
requires sufficient pre-amplification and a relatively long sampling period over which a measurement
must be performed. The 20 kHz sampling rate at which the JNT system operates is low compared to

 15

other JNT instruments (Figures 9 and 8), which can operate at > 1 MHz. Because JNT measurements
are implemented in the frequency spectrum and are based on spectral averaging over the filter pass band,
these higher bandwidth measurements result in the significantly lower uncertainties required for JNT
measurements to be used as an in situ calibration methodology to compensate for phenomena such as
radiation-induced drift in RTDs. In the absence of these higher sampling rates, a much longer sampling
period would be required to achieve a similarly low uncertainty, which may prove prohibitive for some
applications.
The MAG-SEI00002-NP IC can accommodate high gains: it is designed to operate with a programmable
gain of up to 256 but can operate at gains of up to 1024, though this behavior is invalidated (Figure 4).
However, at its maximum gain, only a few discrete ADC output values are produced over the temperature
range of 0–150 ◦ C. This already low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), combined with the low sampling rate,
leads to large uncertainties in correlating Johnson noise amplitude with temperature and thus makes
re-calibration difficult.

 5. CONCLUSION
The electronics used in JNT instruments are challenging to implement in nuclear reactors because of
the susceptibility to radiation damage of the precision front-end electronics required to perform such
measurements. Radiation-hardened components developed for space applications could enable practical
JNT systems to be developed, though these components are often qualified only for ionizing radiation,
and often using surrogate particles. To test the feasibility of using COTS space-rated components to
enable JNT measurements in nuclear reactors, a JNT instrument was developed using radiation-hardened
ADCs, which enables its physical placement within a test reactor while maintaining the signal processing
hardware and MCU within a clean environment. The digital communication’s signal integrity, gain
limitations, and data acquisition bandwidth limit the practicality of the present implementation in
real-world applications. However, these challenges are not insurmountable, and the utilization of space
radiation–rated electronics in terrestrial and space reactors could yet be leveraged to address the imminent
radiation survival requirements of next-generation reactor designs.
