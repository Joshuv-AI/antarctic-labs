Immune checkpoint inhibitor neurotoxicity: long-term outcomes with focus on 1-year neurological sequelae
Authors: Antonio Farina, Macarena Villagrán-García, Amna Abichou-Klich, Tifanie Alberto, Jérôme Aupy, Marie Benaiteau, Veronique Bourg, Giovanni Corazza, Aurélien Maureille, Géraldine Picard, Dimitri Psimaras, Marie Rafiq, Jerome Honnorat, Bastien Joubert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on the long-term course of neurological immune-related adverse events (n-irAEs) are limited by the size of studies as well as short and heterogeneous follow-up durations. Herein, we assessed the risk and predictors of 1-year neurological sequelae, and the frequency of n-irAE relapses with or without immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) rechallenge.MethodsPatients with Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade ≥2 n-irAEs identified in a national reference center were included (2015–2024). Early neurological recovery was defined as CTCAE grade <3 and an improvement of ≥1 point from baseline at 3 months. Long-term sequelae were defined as CTCAE grade ≥2 at 1 year. Associations between prognostic factors and 1-year neurological sequelae were assessed using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsA total of 164 patients were included (median age 69 years, 63% male, median (IQR) follow-up 13 (4–25) months), of whom 59 (36%) had paraneoplastic phenotypes, and 141 (86%) had CTCAE grade 3 or 4 n-irAE at baseline. Most patients (151/163, 93%) received first-line treatments, and 33/163 patients (20%) also received second-line treatments. Early neurological recovery was observed in 82/164 patients (50%). Among 83 patients still alive at 1 year, 33 (40%) had neurological sequelae, and 35 (42%) were receiving treatments. Improvement in CTCAE grade occurred in 13/83 patients (16%) between 3 and 12 months, 1/43 patients (2%) between 12 and 24 months, and it never occurred (0/23) between 24 and 36 months. N-irAE relapses occurred in 25/164 patients (15%), a median (IQR) of 6.8 (3.9–11.8) months after onset. ICI rechallenge led to relapse (CTCAE grade 2) in 1/21 patients (5%). In multivariable analysis, the probability of 1-year neurological sequelae was higher in patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes (OR 11.09, 95% CI (3.24 to 44.51)) and in those who had a relapse within 9 months from baseline (OR 11.12, 95% CI (2.13 to 89.53)).DiscussionParaneoplastic phenotypes and relapses increase the risk of neurological sequelae among long-term n-irAE survivors, and ICI rechallenge might be safe in n-irAE patients who have fully recovered. The rarity of recovery and low risk of relapse after 1 year suggest that prolonged immunosuppression may be unnecessary, but prospective studies are needed to refine management strategies.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immune related adverse event - irAE, Immunotherapy, Neurotoxicity, Relapse

Graphical Abstract

Background

Neurological immune-related adverse events (n-irAEs) are rare but increasingly recognized complications of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).1,4 The clinical spectrum is broad, with neuromuscular toxicities reported more frequently than central nervous system (CNS) complications.5 6 Some n-irAE phenotypes, such as myositis with myocarditis,2 3 7 8 or paraneoplastic-like neurological syndromes,38,10 are associated with high morbidity and mortality, underscoring the clinical significance of these emerging syndromes. Although treatment response varies according to the phenotype (eg, around two-thirds of patients with myositis recover, while those with paraneoplastic neurological syndromes (PNS)-like encephalitis are often treatment-refractory),2 3 5 6 8 10 11 previous studies suggest that clinical improvement occurs shortly after therapeutic intervention, and most patients stabilize within 3 months.2 10 11 However, data on the neurological course beyond this time frame are limited by the small numbers of patients included in studies as well as short and heterogeneous follow-up durations,35 8 11,13 leaving critical questions unanswered regarding the risk of chronic progression, relapse, and long-term neurological sequelae. Addressing these gaps is crucial given the prolonged cancer responses and long-term survival observed in a subset of ICI-treated patients, as well as the expanding use of ICIs in non-metastatic cancer settings.14 15 Furthermore, because cancer progression remains the leading cause of death in the n-irAE population,4 8 and alternative oncological treatments are not always available, the scarcity of data regarding the safety of ICI rechallenge represents a major clinical concern.16

Herein, we therefore assessed the long-term neurological outcomes of n-irAEs, focusing on 1-year neurological sequelae and their predictors, and the frequency of n-irAE relapses with and without ICI rechallenge.

Methods

Patients, antibodies, and assessment of outcomes

The database of the French Reference Centre on PNS and Autoimmune Encephalitis was screened to identify all patients who had received ICI treatment (July 2015–February 2024). In this center, information on ICI treatments is collected systematically through antibody test request forms, and, additionally, when our opinion is requested on a case (by email, phone, or during the bimonthly national multicenter interdisciplinary meetings). All patients with de novo Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade ≥2 n-irAEs were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were lack of clinical information or follow-up data, established alternative diagnosis, previous neurological autoimmune disorder (whether exacerbated by ICI administration or not), lack of temporal association (ie, syndromes occurring >12 months after the last ICI dose),17 and baseline CTCAE grade 1 (figure 2). Demographic and clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the available medical charts. Treating physicians were contacted in case of missing information. Neurological phenotypes were classified according to clinical syndromes, supported by compatible imaging or nerve conduction study findings.17 Clinical presentations fulfilling the definition of high-risk phenotypes of PNS from the updated 2021 criteria18 were referred to as paraneoplastic phenotypes. Symptom progression was classified as subacute (≤3 months) or chronic (>3 months). The presence of neural antibodies was determined using tissue-based assays (TBA) and a confirmatory test consisting of line-blot analysis on recombinant proteins (Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany, or Ravo Diagnostika, Freiburg, Germany), cell-based assays, and/or Western blots (in-house techniques) as reported elsewhere.19 Neural antibodies were classified as high-risk, intermediate-risk, and low-risk according to the updated PNS criteria18; in addition, anti-tripartite motif-containing protein 9 and 46 (Trim9/46) and anti-Dachshund Family Transcription Factor 1 (Dach1) were defined herein as high-risk.20 21 Antibodies of unknown clinical relevance included antibodies targeting unknown antigens (positive TBA result without any antigenic specificity identified on the available tests) and antibodies targeting antigens with unclear association with neurological disease (ie, anti-NT5 antibodies). N-irAE treatments were classified into first-line (corticosteroids (CCS), intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG), and plasma exchange) and second-line (cyclophosphamide, rituximab, tocilizumab, tofacitinib, natalizumab, and azathioprine) therapies. Disease severity was evaluated at baseline (corresponding to the time of hospitalization or the time of first neurological evaluation), and at 3, 12, 24, and 36 months thereafter. Severity was classified according to the CTCAE grading system adapted for n-irAEs by Guidon et al.17 Based on previous studies,2 3 8 10 early neurological recovery was evaluated at 3 months from baseline and defined as CTCAE grade <3 and an improvement of ≥1 point from baseline; complete recovery was defined as CTCAE grade 1. N-irAE relapse was defined as the recurrence of the neurological symptoms following a sustained period (≥4 weeks) of improvement (according to the treating physician). Long-term sequelae were defined as a CTCAE grade ≥2 at month 12 and classified as severe (CTCAE grade ≥3) or non-severe (CTCAE grade 2). Sequelae were similarly assessed at 24 and 36 months.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as median (IQR) and categorical data as count (percentage). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. For each neurological phenotype, univariate associations with clinical variables were assessed, and ORs with 95% CIs were calculated. P values were adjusted for multiple testing across all comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. The annualized relapse rate (ARR) was calculated by dividing the total number of relapses by the total follow-up time, and 95% CI estimated assuming a Poisson distribution.22 Associations between prognostic factors and mortality were assessed using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Covariates included in the multivariable model were selected based on significant associations in univariable analyses and clinical relevance. The association between n-irAE treatment and relapse-free survival (RFS) was evaluated using a univariable Cox proportional hazards model. For all Cox models, HRs with Wald 95% CI are reported, and proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Associations between prognostic factors and 1-year neurological sequelae were assessed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Covariates for the multivariable model were selected based on significant associations in univariable analyses and clinical relevance. ORs and Wald 95% CIs are reported. All p values were two-tailed, and p values <0.05 were considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio, V.3.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

N-irAE cohort

A total of 164 patients were included (median (IQR) age: 69 (50–74) years, 63% male, median (IQR) follow-up 13 (4–25) months; Figure 2). The main cancers were melanoma (40/164, 24%), non-small cell lung cancer (38/164, 23%), small cell lung cancer (SCLC; 34/164, 21%), and urological cancers (21/164, 13%). ICIs had been administered as a monotherapy (124/164, 76%) or combination therapies (40/164, 24%), and 59/164 (36%) patients had previous or concomitant non-neurological irAEs. Neurological symptoms occurred a median (IQR) of 2 months (1–5) after the first ICI cycle (table 1) and progressed subacutely in 160/164 patients (98%). N-irAEs involved the CNS (75/164, 46%), peripheral nervous system (64/164, 39%), or both (25/164, 15%). The most frequent CNS phenotypes were meningoencephalitis (37/164, 23%) and focal encephalitis syndromes (ie, limbic encephalitis, 29/164, 18%; cerebellar ataxia, 24/164, 15%; and brainstem encephalitis, 15/164, 9%). The most frequent neuromuscular phenotypes were polyradiculoneuropathy (PRN; 31/164, 19%), myositis (24/164, 15%), cranial neuropathy (16/164, 10%), and sensory neuronopathy (SNN, 14/164, 8%, figure 2). Overall, 59/164 patients (36%) had paraneoplastic phenotypes, mostly (57/59, 97%) limbic encephalitis, cerebellar ataxia, and/or SNN. Among 147 tested patients, 54 (37%) had high-risk PNS antibodies (mostly anti-Hu, n=21; Ma2, n=9; Cv2, n=9; Sox1, n=6; and Yo, n=5), 13/147 (9%) had low-risk or intermediate-risk PNS antibodies (mostly anti-GFAP, n=8; Caspr2, n=2; or Gad65, n=2), and 19/147 (13%) had antibodies of unknown clinical relevance (table 1). High-risk PNS antibodies were consistent with the clinical syndrome in 50/54 (93%) patients and with the cancer type in 43/54 (80%) patients; low-risk and intermediate-risk PNS antibodies were consistent with the clinical syndrome in 10/13 (77%) patients.18 Paraneoplastic phenotypes and brainstem encephalitis were significantly associated with high-risk PNS antibodies (online supplemental figure 1).

Therapeutic interventions

At baseline (a median (IQR) 10 (4–33) days after onset), n-irAEs were severe or life-threatening in most patients (CTCAE grade 3–4, 141/164, 86%; Figure 3A). ICI was withdrawn because of the n-irAE in 163/164 patients (99%). Delayed ICI withdrawal (ie, 1 or more ICI cycle after the onset of the neurological symptoms) was more frequent in patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes compared with the rest of the cohort (34/59, 58% vs 25/102, 25%; p<0.001). Most patients (151/163, 93%) received first-line treatments, in 148/151 cases (98%) within 3 months from baseline (median (IQR) of 0.6 (0–2.1) weeks after baseline); the interval from the onset of neurological symptoms to first-line treatment administration was significantly longer in patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes compared with the rest of the cohort (median (IQR): 6.1 weeks (2.6–9.4) vs 2 weeks (1–3.7), p<0.001). Moreover, 33/163 patients (20%), all with baseline CTCAE grade 3–4, also received second-line treatments, a median (IQR) of 9 weeks (6–20) after symptom onset and in 24/33 cases (73%) within 3 months from baseline (median 5.9 weeks (3–13.9) after baseline). Second-line treatments were administered more frequently to patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes compared with the rest of the cohort (23/58, 40% vs 10/105, 9.5%, p<0.001). Early neurological recovery

At 3 months from baseline, 82/164 patients (50%) had early neurological recovery, including 54/164 (33%) with a complete recovery. Compared with the rest of the cohort, rates of early recovery and complete recovery were significantly lower in patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes (13/59, 22% vs 69/105, 66%, and 3/59, 5% vs 51/105, 49%, respectively; both p<0.001) and significantly higher in patients with myositis (18/24, 75% vs 64/140, 46%, p=0.008 and 13/24, 54% vs 41/140, 29%, p=0.017, respectively).

Mortality

Half of the patients (82/164, 50%) died during the study period, most of whom (56/82; 68%) later than 3 months after baseline (median OS: 23 months, 95% CI (15 to 42.8); online supplemental figure 2A). Causes of death were cancer progression (40/82, 49%), n-irAE (23/82, 28%), other (8/82, 10%), or were unknown (11/82, 13%). Neurotoxicity was the leading cause of death during the first 3 months after baseline (18/26, 69%). Multivariable analysis found a significantly higher risk of death in patients with SCLC (vs melanoma; HR 2.29, 95% CI (1.02 to 5.13)) or brainstem encephalitis (HR 2.39, 95% CI (1.24 to 4.62); online supplemental figure 2B; univariable analyses are presented in online supplemental table 2).

Relapses

Relapses occurred in 25/164 patients (15%), including 2 patients who experienced >1 relapse (patients 1 and 18, (online supplemental table 3; ARR 0.10, 95% CI (0.07 to 0.14); median RFS was not reached; Figure 4A). Relapse phenotypes were PRN (6/25, 24%), cerebellar ataxia (5/25, 20%), limbic encephalitis (4/25, 16%), meningoencephalitis (4/25, 16%), myelitis (3/25, 12%), brainstem encephalitis (3/25, 12%), myositis (2/25, 8%), and/or one case each of SNN, opsoclonus-ataxia syndrome, myasthenia, enteric neuropathy, polyneuropathy, and small-fiber neuropathy. The relapse phenotype was identical to the initial presentation in 17/25 patients (68%), or more restricted, involving only a subset of the initial phenotypes, in 5/25 patients (20%). Only 3/25 patients (12%) had neurological symptoms distinct from the initial presentation, which were combined with prior symptoms in one patient (online supplemental table 3). Relapses occurred a median (IQR) of 6.8 (3.9–11.8) months after onset (data missing for one patient). In 18/24 patients (75%), they occurred within 1 year from onset, the latest occurring at 19.3 months. Factors potentially triggering relapses, reported in 20 patients, included CCS (n=7) and/or other n-irAE treatment (n=4) withdrawal (9/20; relapses occurred a median (IQR) of 1 (0.6–1.8) month after withdrawal), CCS tapering (8/20; a median (IQR) oral CCS daily dose of 30 (15–40) mg), cancer progression (3/20), infection (2/20), and/or ICI rechallenge (1/20). Relapses linked to CCS tapering or treatment withdrawal occurred significantly earlier (median (IQR) of 4.3 (3.5–6.8) months after onset) compared with relapses linked to other factors (median (IQR) of 11.8 (10.6–15.9) months after onset, p=0.011). N-irAE treatments (including both first-line and second-line) as time-dependent covariable had no effect on the 2-year relapse risk (HR 0.56, 95% CI (0.23 to 1.32)). Relapses led to administering first-line treatments and/or increasing the CCS dose in 23/25 patients (92%), and to starting (n=8) or reintroducing (n=1) second-line treatments in 9/25 patients (36%). A minority of 9/23 patients (39%) improved to CTCAE grade 1 or 2, and relapses were fatal in 3 patients (12%), including two brainstem encephalitis with high-risk PNS antibodies (anti-Hu in one patient, and anti-Ma2 in the other) and 1 PRN.

Rechallenge with ICI

ICI rechallenge was attempted in 21/164 patients (13%) a median (IQR) of 6 (3–14) months after the last ICI dose. In most cases (15/21, 71%), the initial ICI treatment was a combination of CTLA-4 and PD-1/PDL-1 inhibitors. At rechallenge, PD-1 monotherapy was used in 18/21 (86%) patients, and a different ICI was used in 8/21 (38%). Most patients who were rechallenged had melanoma (14/21, 67%) and/or a neuromuscular n-irAE (13/21, 62%), while 2/21 patients (9.5%) had a paraneoplastic phenotype (online supplemental table 4). At the time of rechallenge, 20/21 patients (95%) were neurologically asymptomatic (CTCAE grade 1; n=18) or had non-severe residual symptoms (CTCAE grade 2; n=2). Among the 20 patients with available data, 11/20 (55%) were on oral CCS (prednisone ≤10 mg daily for 8/9 cases with available data), including one case also receiving monthly IVIG. ICI rechallenge led to n-irAE relapse in 1/21 patients (5%), who was not under CCS at that time, after two cycles. This patient had a myositis phenotype, CTCAE grade 2 at both first event and relapse, which both improved to CTCAE grade 1 after ICI withdrawal and CCS treatment. Six other patients (29%) had non-neurological irAEs after rechallenge. Overall, ICI was withdrawn again in 14/21 patients (67%), after a median (IQR) of 6 cycles (3–15) following rechallenge (median (IQR) 3.3 (1.4–7.9) months after the rechallenge). The main reason for discontinuation was cancer progression, accounting for 10/14 cases (71%; online supplemental table 4). The 7/21 patients (33%) who were still on ICI at last follow-up (median (IQR) 10.5 months (2–18) after rechallenge) had received a median of 14 cycles (IQR 4–19).

Long-term sequelae

Neurological status at 1 year was available for 83/88 (94%) patients still alive at that time. Notably, 13/83 of them (16%) experienced improvement in CTCAE grade between 3 and 12 months, 9/13 (69%) experienced clinical recovery at 3 months.

CTCAE grade improvement between 3 and 12 months was not significantly more frequent in patients treated by second-line treatment (3/15, 20%) compared with those that did not receive these treatments (10/68, 15%, p=0.70). Improvement in CTCAE grade between 12 and 24 months occurred in 1/43 patients (2%), and it never occurred (0/23) between 24 and 36 months. At 1 year, 35/83 patients (42%) were still receiving treatments: oral CCS (31/35, 89%; ≤ 10 mg prednisone daily in 19/35 (54%)), IVIG (4/35, 11%), cyclophosphamide, rituximab, tocilizumab, and/or methotrexate (one patient each). Use of n-irAE treatments further decreased at 2 and 3 year follow-up (figure 4B).

Most 1-year survivors (50/83, 60%) were fully asymptomatic, while 33/83 (40%) had neurological sequelae (CTCAE grade 2, n=10; or grade 3, n=23). Patients without sequelae had experienced both early neurological recovery (47/50, 94% vs 11/33, 33%; p<0.001) and CTCAE grade improvement between 3 and 12 months (12/50, 24% vs 1/33, 3%; p=0.010) more frequently than the rest of 1-year survivors. The most frequent 1-year sequelae were gait (n=21, including 15 patients (71%) wheelchair-bound or requiring technical assistance) and cognitive impairments (n=9). Most gait impairments were related to cerebellar ataxia (n=8) or PRN (n=5), and residual cognitive impairments were all in patients with limbic encephalitis (n=5) or meningoencephalitis (n=4). Similar rates and types of sequelae were observed at 2 and 3-year follow-up (online supplemental table 5).

The frequency of 1-year sequelae varied according to the n-irAE phenotype, with the highest frequency of sequelae in brainstem encephalitis (100%) and in paraneoplastic phenotypes (69%–100%) and lowest in myositis (23%) and PRN (35%; Figure 3B). In multivariable analysis, the probability of 1-year neurological sequelae was higher in patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes (OR 11.09, 95% CI (3.24 to 44.51)) and in those who had a relapse within 9 months from baseline (OR 11.12, 95% CI (2.13 to 89.53)); it was lower in patients with CTCAE grade two at baseline (OR 0.14, 95% CI (0.01 to 0.77), figure 5; univariable analyses are presented in online supplemental table 6).

Discussion

In the present cohort, more than one-third of patients with n-irAEs had persistent neurological sequelae at 1-year follow-up, and neurological relapses, which occurred mostly within the first year, were relatively uncommon. However, their occurrence, along with greater initial severity and paraneoplastic phenotypes, was associated with an increased risk of long-term sequelae.

The present study expands on existing evidence showing that the acute phase of n-irAEs is associated with high morbidity and mortality, and that treatment response is often rapid and dependent on clinical presentation.2,57 8 12 It further provides unique insights into the long-term neurological course; further improvement occurred in only a minority (~10%) of patients and was notably rare in those who did not recover early, highlighting the importance of optimizing n-irAE management during the early phase. For instance, upfront administration of second-line therapies should likely be considered in patients at higher risk of long-term sequelae, such as those with paraneoplastic phenotypes. The poor prognosis of these phenotypes is consistent with previous studies38,10 and suggests a pathogenesis similar to that of spontaneous PNS, in which cytotoxic T-cell mechanisms lead to extensive and irreversible neuronal loss.1 In this regard, the longer time to treatment initiation in paraneoplastic phenotypes could contribute to a poor prognosis, highlighting the need for biomarkers to anticipate their diagnosis and management.23

Importantly, n-irAE relapses, although uncommon, were also associated with an increased risk of long-term sequelae, a finding likely explained by their severity and limited patient recovery after treatment, which may reflect a reduced neuronal reserve following the initial injury. Furthermore, and in line with previous reports,5 12 13 relapses occurred across all n-irAE phenotypes, although among patients who had myositis, one of the most frequent phenotypes, only one experienced relapse after rechallenge. Moreover, as previously reported, most relapses occurred early after the initial event, either during CCS tapering or shortly after withdrawal of n-irAE treatment; beyond 1 year, the risk of relapse was low and further recovery was rare. Although the optimal duration and tapering of immunosuppression in patients with n-irAEs have not been established, the present findings suggest the need to carefully balance immunosuppression by avoiding both premature withdrawal and unnecessary prolongation (beyond 1 year), and to ensure close clinical monitoring during the first year. Importantly, while oncological responses to ICIs do not appear to be affected by CCS use, it remains unknown whether this is affected by second-line immunosuppressants, such as cyclophosphamide and rituximab,24 25 warranting caution against their prolonged use in patients with n-irAEs. However, certain treatments, such as IL-6 receptor antagonists or JAK inhibitors, have been found to potentially have a synergistic effect with ICI-induced antitumor responses,26,28 although further studies are needed to clarify their effects and to identify the specific cytokines and other therapeutically targetable pathways involved in the pathogenesis of different n-irAEs.29 Systematic investigations of inflammatory biomarkers such as cytokines will also be important to identify cases in which ICI-induced autoimmunity becomes self-sustaining (‘chronic active n-irAEs’)3 15 and who may benefit from long-term immunosuppressive therapy.

Strikingly, mortality was high and, consistently with previous studies,2 4 8 mainly related to neurotoxicity in the first trimester and to cancer progression in the longer term. Accordingly, the risk of death was higher in phenotypes causing directly life-threatening symptoms, such as brainstem encephalitis, as well as in aggressive cancers, such as SCLC. This highlights the importance of exploring the safety of ICI rechallenge; in line with most smaller series,1116 30,32 there was a low relapse rate after ICI rechallenge herein, indicating that n-irAEs should not represent an absolute contraindication. However, rechallenge was predominantly attempted in a selected population (mostly melanoma patients with fully resolved n-irAEs), so caution is warranted in other contexts, particularly in patients with paraneoplastic phenotypes, as these safety data cannot be generalized. Moreover, most patients included herein discontinued ICI relatively early after rechallenge. Thus, although n-irAEs are generally expected to occur within the first few cycles, the safety of longer-term ICI exposure remains less established, supporting the need for close clinical monitoring when rechallenge is attempted.

The present study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design occasionally resulted in missing data and limits the strength of evidence of the study findings. Furthermore, the observational nature of the study, combined with the heterogeneity of clinical presentations, severity, and treatment strategies, hindered the assessment of the impact of different treatment strategies on outcomes. Moreover, detailed oncological information was lacking, which precluded a precise assessment of oncological outcomes. Additionally, the number of variables that could be included in the multivariable analysis for the risk of 1-year sequelae was limited by the relatively low number of who survived this long, an inherent limitation due to the high mortality in the n-irAE population. Furthermore, due to referral bias, paraneoplastic phenotypes (observed in more than one-third of patients in the present cohort) were over-represented compared with previous studies (17%–20%).2 3 8 This could limit the generalizability of the present findings to real-world clinical practice, where the relative frequency of paraneoplastic phenotypes among n-irAEs is likely lower, except in cancer types classically associated with PNS, such as SCLC. Despite these constraints, this study offers new insights into the long-term clinical trajectory of a large cohort of patients with n-irAEs, assessed at consistent timepoints up to 3 years after initial evaluation, contrasting with previous studies characterized by shorter and more variable follow-up periods.2 3 11 Therefore, its findings are valuable for informing patient prognosis, guiding treatment decisions, and shaping future research to confirm these results and address the identified limitations.

In conclusion, paraneoplastic phenotypes and relapses increase the risk of neurological sequelae among long-term n-irAE survivors, and ICI rechallenge might be safe in patients who have fully recovered from n-irAEs. The rarity of recovery and the low risk of relapse after 1 year suggest that prolonged immunosuppression may be unnecessary, but prospective studies are needed to confirm this and to refine management strategies.
