A systematic review of neuroprotective therapies for visual acuity preservation in retinitis pigmentosa
Authors: Sam Sherratt-Mayhew, Max Bisp, Sanna Waheed, Charles Page, Wing Sze Vangie Koo, Shashwat Deep
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis systematic review aims to evaluate the efficacy of neuroprotective therapies in improving best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in retinitis pigmentosa (RP). Secondary outcomes investigated included electroretinogram measures (ERGs), adverse events and other relevant ophthalmological outcomes.Methods and analysisA systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered on PROSPERO. MEDLINE, EMBASE, ClinicalTrials.gov and the Cochrane Library were searched without date restrictions. Randomised controlled trials comparing neuroprotective interventions with any relevant comparator in typical RP and reporting BCVA outcomes were included. Risk of bias was assessed using ROB2 and certainty of evidence graded using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE). Due to heterogeneity in interventions and outcome reporting, narrative synthesis was undertaken.Results10 trials involving 1364 participants were included. Interventions included antioxidants, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) modulators, mechanical neurostimulation and omega-3 fatty acids. Across studies, no neuroprotective therapy demonstrated a clinically or statistically significant improvement in BCVA compared with control, with the exception of Lycium barbarum, which was associated with modest BCVA improvement in a single phase II trial with a very low GRADE certainty of evidence. Vitamin A, docosahexaenoic acid and lutein were associated with changes on secondary outcomes, such as ERG amplitudes, in phase III trials, though findings were from secondary analyses. Overall certainty of evidence was moderate to very low.ConclusionsCurrent evidence does not support the use of neuroprotective therapies to preserve visual acuity in RP. While some interventions show promise, including in secondary functional measures, robust conclusions are limited by small sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity. Larger, standardised trials are required to validate the role of neuroprotection in RP.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Neuroprotective therapies have been proposed as a means to slow photoreceptor degeneration in retinitis pigmentosa, with multiple agents showing biological plausibility and preclinical promise. However, clinical trials have produced inconsistent results. Their impact on visual acuity, a clinically relevant endpoint, has remained unclear.

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is the most prevalent form of inherited retinal dystrophies, encompassing a group of genetically diverse disorders that result in progressive degeneration of retinal photoreceptors, affecting approximately 1 in 4000 individuals.1 2 The disease typically manifests with nyctalopia as the earliest symptom, followed by concentric constriction of the visual field and eventual central vision loss, culminating in severe visual impairment in many cases. Electroretinography (ERG) characteristically demonstrates diminished rod responses, often preceding cone dysfunction.3 Although RP can occur in isolation (typical RP), it may also present as part of syndromic disorders, such as Usher or Bardet-Biedl syndromes.4

Given the heterogeneity of the condition and complexity of the issues it can cause, there is currently no consensus on what constitutes standard medical care for the treatment of RP.5 6 Current management strategies remain largely supportive—centred around genetic counselling, low vision rehabilitation and treatment of complications.6

The absence of definitive curative therapies highlights the need for novel disease-modifying strategies. Neuroprotection has emerged as a promising therapeutic paradigm in neurodegenerative diseases such as RP, having already been explored in depth in conditions such as glaucoma.7 The rationale for neuroprotection lies in targeting shared neurodegenerative pathways that lead to apoptosis. Photoreceptor death is mediated primarily via intrinsic apoptosis in RP, controlled by BAX/BAK signalling.8 9 By inhibiting this signalling either directly, or by inhibiting upstream oxidative stress that triggers it, neuroprotective interventions prevent further photoreceptor apoptosis. Common paths include blocking intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production or promoting neurotrophic factors (stimulating pro-survival pathways).10 11 Hence, neuroprotection is defined as therapy that targets these shared pathways and inhibits intrinsic apoptosis. Several agents have demonstrated neuroprotective potential in preclinical and early clinical studies, in phase I trials and are now undergoing further investigation.12 However, despite promising laboratory and early-phase trial data, the clinical efficacy of these neuroprotective therapies remains unclear due to heterogeneous study designs, variable outcome measures and limited long-term follow-up.6

This systematic review aims to evaluate the current evidence on neuroprotective therapies in RP. Specifically, it will assess their impact on visual acuity (VA), as well as safety profiles and other reported ophthalmological outcomes, compared with any relevant comparator. By appraising the available literature, this review seeks to clarify the therapeutic potential of neuroprotection in RP and to identify promising agents.

Materials and methods

Design

This systematic review was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (figure 1, onlinesupplemental files 1 2).13 14

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses; RCT, randomised controlled trial. *See supplementaty table 2 for breakdown by database.

Eligibility criteria

Trials were included for review if they met the criteria detailed in the PROSPERO registration form according to the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Study Type, Timeframe (PICOST) framework (online supplemental table 3). Studies were included if: they were conducted in a population with typical RP, the intervention being studied was any therapy intended to provide neuroprotection against any relevant control, reported best-corrected VA (BCVA) as a primary or secondary outcome and were designed as a randomised controlled trial (RCT). Typical RP is defined as having the following: nyctalopia (worsening night vision), loss of peripheral visual fields and rod dysfunction on ERG (with cone deterioration following).3 15 Typical RP is an umbrella term for many genetic subtypes of RP, which are subclassified by their inheritance pattern, for example, X-linked RP16 (online supplemental table 3).

BCVA was chosen as the primary outcome of this review due to its acceptance as a Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved endpoint for ophthalmology clinical trials, facilitating cross-trial comparability with reduced heterogeneity and clinical interpretability as well as closely correlating with patient quality of life.1719 BCVA is also an important consideration in extensive, late-stage disease both in RP and more broadly, including in electrostimulation and bionic technology.20 However, BCVA should not be interpreted alone in RP. BCVA can fluctuate in this disease, especially in the early stages, as the dysfunction starts peripherally and is rod-mediated, while BCVA reflects foveal function.21 To address this limitation, electrophysiological and other functional measures were analysed to contextualise non-foveal disease effects. ERGs are considered the standard measure both to diagnose RP and monitor disease progression and visual field area assessment using kinetic or static testing is also important.22 23

Absence of BCVA improvement should be interpreted alongside these secondary outcomes, as BCVA alone can underestimate intervention effects. Secondary outcomes included in the review were ERGs, visual field areas/standard automated perimetry results and other relevant ophthalmological outcomes (see online supplemental table 4) to prevent reaching overly negative results relative to the biological aims of neuroprotective therapy. Side effects were also reported. Due to the high prevalence of animal studies and case reports on scoping searches, only RCTs were included in this review for the sake of clarity for clinicians. Many of these RCTs are early phase as neuroprotective therapies remain theoretical for neurodegenerative ocular diseases.24

Search methods

We searched the following databases in October 2025: MEDLINE, EMBASE, ClinicalTrials.gov and The Cochrane Library. We did not use any date restrictions in our search; however, non-English languages were excluded. The search strategy can be found in online supplemental table 2.

Study selection

Two authors independently screened the texts retrieved from the search strategy by screening for publication type, title and abstract. After independent assessment, the authors compared their selections to ensure consensus on the inclusion or exclusion of a study. Comments, case reports, conference abstracts, literature reviews, basic science, case–control and mechanistic studies were excluded. No cohort studies were identified.

Data collection and extraction

For trials that were included, two authors independently extracted data relevant to risk of bias analysis and relevant characteristics of the study using Rayyan software.25 Data were collected on prespecified parameters: study details, methodology, characteristics of the population, interventions given and relevant outcomes. Online supplemental table 4 contains a breakdown of visual parameters collected for each study. All extracted data were collected by two authors and any disagreements resolved by consensus. A 0.1 increase in logarithm of Minimum Angle of Resolution (logMAR) is equivalent to 5 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters, while a change in 0.2 logMAR (10 ETDRS letters) is considered clinically significant. Note, this conversion is not accurate at extremely low acuity.26 Further conversions are provided in online supplemental table 1.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias (ROB) was graded using the Risk of Bias 2 tool 2019 (ROB2), which uses several domains to give an overall risk of bias of ‘low’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high’, by two authors using published guidance.27

Analysis

Results were synthesised into a narrative report with descriptive statistics as appropriate. Quantitative pooling for meta-analysis was attempted as per the PROSPERO protocol; however, it was deemed infeasible due to heterogeneity (Supplementary File 1). Differences in study population outcome reporting (some trials reported BCVA changes as a rate of change, others as a mean difference over a trial and some as covariate estimates in modelling), follow-up lengths and intervention regimes would have limited validity of quantitative pooling and interpretations of differences (see online supplemental table 1). Outcome standardisation was also not feasible for these reasons. A random-effects model was therefore considered infeasible post hoc. Subgroup pooling was also deemed inappropriate, as this same structural heterogeneity was also present within intervention groups. Consequently, results are presented as a narrative synthesis with descriptive statistics (means and 95% CIs), consistent with PRISMA 2020 recommendations for heterogeneous systematic reviews.13 28

Summary of findings

The findings of this review are reported as changes in BCVA between intervention and comparator groups in the Summary of Findings table, using recommendations from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (online supplemental table 1).28 The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to rank the quality of evidence as high, moderate, low or very low and was independently assessed by two authors before a consensus was reached.

Results

The systematic search in October 2025 yielded 358 pieces of literature. Following removal of 46 duplicate articles, title screening excluded 229 articles and abstract screening excluded a further 68 articles. 15 studies underwent full text review, following which 10 were included for the final analysis in this review. Online supplemental table 5 shows the characteristics of the included studies. In total, data from 1364 participants were included across all the studies. Two of the studies specified a genetic subtype of typical RP as the population.29 30

Antioxidants

Five studies investigated antioxidant therapies. One large-scale phase III RCT used a four-arm design, comparing efficacy of 15 000 units of vitamin A, 400 units of vitamin E, both vitamins in combination and a control arm with a mean follow-up of 5.2 years.31 A second large-scale phase III RCT compared efficacy of vitamin A and lutein versus vitamin A and placebo with a mean follow-up of 4 years.32 Further studies included a phase II trial investigating Lycium barbarum (wolfberry) versus placebo in 42 participants, a phase I/II trial crossover trial investigating lutein monotherapy versus placebo in 34 participants, and a phase II crossover investigating carotene-rich powder versus placebo in 34 participants.3335

VA outcomes

BCVA changes were not found to be significantly altered by vitamin A, E, carotene-rich powder or lutein in phase I, II or III trials. L. barbarum was associated with improved rates of BCVA decline in a phase II trial, finding a small improvement in logMAR during the trial compared with baseline, however, was deemed to have a low GRADE certainty.3135

A decline in BCVA was found in all arms of Berson et al’s 1993 RCT investigating vitamin A and E, with no significant differences in the rate of decline found between the arms. The mean BCVAs in all arms deteriorated at approximately 1 ETDRS letter per year (95% CIs not provided).31 In the lutein cohort of Berson’s following phase III trial in 2010, there were no significant differences in the rate of decline compared with the control arm (95% CIs not provided, p=0.80).32 In the smaller phase II crossover RCT investigating a different lutein dosing regime, final BCVA values after treatment were not reported, covariate estimate of treatment was negligible size (see online supplemental table 1) (p=0.98).34 Carotene-rich powder was associated with a change of +0.40 ETDRS letters while taking carotene-rich powder over 90 days as compared with+0.15 letters while taking placebo over the same timeframe.35

Chan et al found L. barbarum was associated with significantly reduced rates of both high contrast VA (HCVA) and low contrast VA (LCVA) decline over 1 year. The control arm declined +0.13 ETDRS letters over the trial and the intervention arm improving by −0.05 letters in HCVA (p=0.001). Similar results were found on LCVA, with the control arm gaining +0.08 letters and the intervention arm losing −0.07 letters (p=0.001). Statistically significant differences were observed but did not reach the threshold for clinical significance (10 letters).33

Secondary outcomes

Vitamin A was associated with a slower rate of decline of 30 Hz ERG amplitude compared with those who did not in both the control and vitamin E alone arms (95% CIs not provided, p=0.01) while there were no significant changes in rates of decline in those taking vitamin E. A higher amplitude baseline post hoc subcohort was also analysed by Berson et al, as changes in ERG are more prominent in higher amplitude participants, which also found the same association.31 Lutein was not found to significantly change declines in either study investigating its efficacy.32 34 No significant changes were noted to either scotopic or photopic ERG wave amplitudes among participants taking L. barbarum compared with placebo.33 Carotene-rich powder was associated with significantly improved dark and light-adapted ERGs (p=0.001 and 0.01, respectively).35

No adverse results were noted due to antioxidant therapy in any of the trials. See online supplemental table 6 for further secondary outcomes.

GABA modulators

One study investigated the effect of valproic acid on BCVA in RP. This study was a phase II RCT and recruited 90 patients, with a follow-up of 1 year (11 participants did not complete the trial but analysis was conducted as intention-to-treat).29

VA outcomes

No significant changes to BCVA as measured with ETDRS letters were found compared with placebo. In the control arm, participants’ change in ETDRS letters ranged from −0.3 to +1.3 letters and in the valproic acid arm changes ranged from −1.4 to +0.5 letters (95% CIs not provided).29

Secondary outcomes

Valproic acid was associated with a statistically significant reduction in visual field area compared with placebo (mean change −150.4 degrees squared, 95% CI 290.5 to −10.0, p=0.035). Minor adverse events not related to the intervention were noted in both arms.29

Neurostimulation

Two phase II RCTs investigated electrical neurostimulation. The first trial randomised 21 participants 1:1:1 between trans-corneal electrostimulation (TES), electro-acupuncture and sham, and followed participants up for 1 month. The second randomised participants into two intervention groups that received TES, one 150% individualised electrical phosphene threshold (EPT) and one 200% EPT, and one sham.36 37

VA outcomes

Neither trial demonstrated significant improvement to BCVA following neurostimulation. In the first trial, TES was found to improve two out of the seven participants’ BCVA as measured by logMAR to a clinically significant level as compared with none of the participants receiving electro-acupuncture or sham intervention.36 The median BCVA change, when excluding anomalies, was approximately −0.06 logMAR units for TES, −0.04 for electro-acupuncture and −0.03 for sham. P values and 95% CIs were not provided to compare statistical significance for any of these findings, neither cumulative acuity changes nor proportions of participants experiencing improvement. In the second, larger trial, no significant differences in Snellen acuity (decimal notation) were found between the TES arms at 150 and 200% EPT or between either intervention arm versus sham, 95% CIs had a range equivalent to 9 to 20 letters.36 37

Secondary outcomes

Significant improvement in some ERG parameters was associated with TES (photopic b-wave specifically) in Schatz et al’s RCT, while Bittner’s phase II trial did not report ERG parameters. No serious adverse events were reported in either trial.36 37

Omega 3 fatty acids

Two RCTs have investigated the role of the omega 3 fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) as therapy in RP and reported BCVA. The first was a phase III trial conducted in 221 participants with typical RP over 4 years, with both arms receiving 15 000 IU Vitamin A and the intervention group receiving DHA as well. The second, a phase II RCT, was conducted in 44 participants with X-linked RP and had a 4-year follow-up.30 38

VA outcomes

Both trials found non-significant differences between intervention and control arms in BCVA changes. In the larger phase III RCT, results were reported in mean decline of ETDRS letters, with the intervention arm (Vitamin A+DHA) declining by 0.71 letters a year and the control arm (Vitamin A+placebo) by 0.68 letters a year (95% CIs not provided, p=0.86). In the phase II RCT investigating X-linked RP, the mean change in BCVA from baseline was reported in logMAR units, with mean changes in the control arm of +0.06 units and +0.05 units in the intervention arm (p=0.88).30 38

Secondary outcomes

Neither trial reported significant improvements in DHA arms to secondary parameters. No serious adverse events related to DHA were reported in either trial.30 38

Discussion

Summary of results

Current literature does not support neuroprotective therapies in improving BCVA, an FDA-approved trial endpoint, in people living with RP. However, BCVA is prone to underestimating the impact of interventions as discussed and requires contextualisation with secondary outcomes. The largest phase III trials conducted by Berson et al did find associations with improvements in ERG amplitudes and Humphrey Field Analyser measures between intervention cohorts and control. However, the recommendations of these studies remain limited by the methods of statistical analysis used, since conclusions were being drawn from secondary outcomes and subgroup analyses. This increases the likelihood of selection bias and confounding factors and can only be considered exploratory.31 32 38 The other trials are too small to draw valid conclusions from and have methodological flaws. This data is not robust enough to be used as an evidence base, but could serve to generate hypotheses for future studies. Only L. barbarum was associated with improved BCVA in people with RP, however this should be interpreted very cautiously and can only serve as hypothesis generating data due to very low GRADE scores.33 Further, larger trials are needed to determine the efficacy of these neuroprotective therapies on BCVA, electrophysiology and visual field parameters.

Berson et al suggested from their large phase III trials a regime of high dose Vitamin A and 12 mg lutein a day with regular meals of oily fish (which contain high levels of DHA) for people with RP.31 32 38 Recommendation of this regime is not merited by the evidence base due to being based on exploratory data.39 DHA and omega-3 fatty acids more broadly have been investigated thoroughly in other ophthalmological conditions in recent research with inconclusive results, further supporting the fact that further research is needed into the efficacy of these dietary supplements.40

Proposed mechanisms of action

Several classes of neuroprotective agents were found in the literature base. These interventions work by inhibiting intrinsic apoptosis, either by directly inhibiting BAX/BAK activation or by inhibiting ROS production upstream. Antioxidant-based interventions (eg, vitamins A and E) mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging ROS and reducing lipid peroxidation, limiting further mitochondrial damage and inhibiting BAX/BAK.41 GABA-modulating agents (such as valproic acid) exert neuroprotective effects through modulation of neuronal excitability, which inhibits excitotoxicity (which contributes to ROS accumulation) and mitochondrial distress.42 Neurostimulation, including TES and electro-acupuncture, is thought to activate endogenous neuroprotective pathways by inducing retinal depolarisation which upregulates neurotrophic factors (which inhibit apoptotic activation via BAX/BAK).43 Omega-3 fatty acids, for example, DHA, play a structural and functional role by maintaining membrane integrity, which modulates BAX/BAK apoptotic signalling and by stabilising mitochondrial membranes reduces ROS production.40 44

Critical appraisal

Online supplemental tables 7 and 8 show the risk of bias of each study and the certainty of synthesised evidence respectively, considering the methodological strengths and weaknesses of each study.27 28

Most of the studies framed a clear research question and provided a direct answer to the question of this review, often as a secondary outcome. Two studies provided limited information on acuity (no p values) and hence the interventions they investigated had the relevant evidence downgraded by one point on GRADE.28 36

Overall, the included studies, and therefore synthesis results, demonstrated ‘some concerns’ of risk of bias. Only three of the trials were considered to have low ROB.3638 The remainder all showed some concerns, primarily due to loss to follow-up (>5%) introducing potential ROB secondary to missing outcome data.28 Reporting for the causes of participants being lost to follow-up was varied, with some trials providing thorough breakdowns of causes and others not reporting these at all. Two trials used a crossover design which introduces the risk of extra confounders, especially as one did not use a washout period.34 35 Some trials also pooled treatment arms and emphasised secondary outcomes. Risk of bias in secondary outcomes may inflate apparent treatment effects. One trial was found to have a risk of publication bias due to being funded by the company manufacturing the intervention, downgrading the certainty of evidence.35

In general, the evidence base had small sample sizes, leading to significant risks of imprecision, with the majority of trials having less than 100 participants in single-centre studies. Furthermore, of the trials that did have sufficiently large sample sizes, none reported CIs of BCVA readings. This weakens the validity of the data by not providing a full description of the range of potential effect sizes and means precise conclusions cannot be drawn from the evidence.

Ongoing and future research

There is currently significant interest in gene therapies for RP, with a gene therapy recently being shown to be both safe and efficacious in a separate inherited retinal disorder, Leber congenital amaurosis. The majority of trials in RP are investigating gene therapy.45 The gene therapy laru-zova (laruparatetigene zovaparvovec) is currently being investigated in two phase II trials for X-linked RP and interim results showed improvements in low luminance VA and safety profile. Further gene therapies are also being investigated in ongoing trials, including phase III trials (NCT06388200, NCT04611503, NCT06492850) and is an area of promising advances in the treatment of RP, inherited retinal disorders and posterior segment eye diseases more broadly as highlighted in a recent review.46 However, the heterogeneous and not fully characterised genetic basis of RP makes creating a singular gene therapy for the condition challenging as well as transduction efficiency and anatomical penetration.46

As the evidence for oral supplementation using vitamins and other antioxidants is low, there is also ongoing work investigating neurotrophic factors.39 Specifically, ciliary neurotrophic factor secreting implants have been shown to improve visual field sensitivity compared with sham but not VA (this study was not included due to the lack of a true sham comparison arm).47 Other neurotrophic factors, such as rod-derived cone viability factor, are also being investigated. The advantage of these neurotrophic factors is the fact that they could potentially be used across multiple types of RP and other optic neuropathies such as glaucoma.48

An anti-oxidant of interest currently is N-acetylcysteine (NAC), which is being investigated in a phase III trial after showing potential benefit to visual function in the FIGHT-RP1 phase I trial.12 The trial, NAC Attack, is comparing NAC against placebo over 36 months (NCT05537220).

This review highlights the need for further large trials occurring over an extended time frame. There was also significant heterogeneity in the way BCVA was reported, making interpreting results difficult when synthesising results from multiple trials. Advice from the FDA suggests that the key endpoint for BCVA is ETDRS letter change. Furthermore, as trials can last differing time periods, BCVA change should be expressed per unit time (eg, per year) in addition to change from baseline if that is the primary endpoint. These are issues often faced in ophthalmological neuroprotective trials, and other reviews highlight the challenges of interpretation of heterogeneous and low power data.49 Using these recommendations, future meta-analyses could be conducted to quantitatively compare intervention efficacy.

Strengths and weaknesses of the review

Due to the current early state of the evidence base investigating this therapeutic approach to a rare retinal disease, the conclusions of this review regarding the safety and efficacy of neuroprotection in RP are not definitive and require further research. However, this does allow the review to succinctly summarise the current, preliminary evidence base, the trends within it and suggest considerations that future trials may wish to consider.

One weakness of this review is the lack of meta-analysis and quantitative pooling due to the significant heterogeneity of outcome measure of BCVA, particularly as some trials reported end-points as differences at end of trials compared with baseline while others reported mean change per unit time, as well as differences in populations and interventions. While a narrative review does allow more detailed qualitative comparison, this review lacks statistical data to guide clinicians and researchers.

Conclusions

Neuroprotection is a new and developing field in the treatment of RP, with significant clinical interest and a large number of trials being conducted recently. Multiple interventions using different mechanisms of action have been shown to not significantly impact BCVA, however, these results need to be interpreted alongside electrophysiological and visual field outcomes. On synthesis and appraisal, the evidence body was found to be preliminary and the conclusion of intervention efficacy limited in its validity. This review finds that there is currently insufficient evidence for neuroprotective therapy in preserving VA in people with RP in practice and highlights the need for further study in this field.
