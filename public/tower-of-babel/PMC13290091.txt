KIF18A inhibitor prevents chromosomally unstable osteosarcoma growth by activating spindle assembly checkpoint
Authors: Qiang Yan, Yan Zhang, Pengfei Wang, Yahui Fu, Chao Ke, Shuang Han, Jian Xing, Haoran Li, Kun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOsteosarcoma survival rates have not improved significantly in decades, as complex gene mutations and intratumoral heterogeneity hinder new therapy development. To exploit the widespread chromosomal instability in osteosarcoma, we investigated targeting the essential mitotic motor protein KIF18A.MaterialsTwo whole-genome-doubling clones derived from the near-diploid SJSA1 osteosarcoma cell line were used to evaluate sensitivity to KIF18A knockout or pharmacological inhibition. The traditional osteosarcoma cell lines U2OS and MG63 were included in the study of the KIF18A inhibitor at both the cellular and xenograft (nude mice) levels.ResultsDepletion of KIF18A significantly reduced cell viability, survival, and proliferation in the SPT cell, but not in the parental SJSA1 cell. Loss of KIF18A expression led to aberrant spindle assembly and mitotic delay through the activation of spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) signaling. Therefore, the deficiency of SAC rescued the SPT cell growth. We then tested one of the latest KIF18A inhibitors, AM-1882, in SPT, U2OS, and MG63 cells. It showed potent anti-cancer activity and disruption of spindle assembly activity, just like the KIF18A knockout. In the mouse xenograft model, AM-1882 also apparently prevented tumor growth and, notably, did not show any toxicity when compared with chemotherapeutic drugs.ConclusionOverall, our study demonstrated that KIF18A is a promising therapeutic target in osteosarcoma. The novel inhibitor AM-1882 offers exceptional anti-tumor efficacy paired with a favorable, low-toxicity safety profile.KEYWORDS: Osteosarcoma, chromosome instability, KIF18A inhibitor, spindle assembly checkpoint, apoptosis

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone cancer in adolescents. 1 The standard treatment of surgery combined with chemotherapy was established in the 1980s and has not improved significantly in the following four decades. The 60% long-term survival rate of the localized disease, the side effects of the traditional chemotherapy, and the early metastasis during rapid progression call for more effective treatments. 2 , 3 Disappointingly, nearly all prevalent genetic alterations can be detected in osteosarcoma, including TP53 mutations, RB1 deletions, MYC amplification, and the loss of key tumor suppressors. 4 Furthermore, according to the predominant differentiation pattern, osteosarcomas are categorized as osteoblastic, chondroblastic, and fibroblastic. However, most osteosarcoma cases are histologically heterogeneous. 5 This complex genetic landscape and extensive tissue heterogeneity severely limit the development and broad application of targeted therapies.

Mitotic spindle-targeted therapy is widely used to treat human cancers. For example, docetaxel, a microtubule-targeting agent, induces cell death by activating the SAC. However, blood and neurotoxicity limit the outcomes. 6 Other therapies targeting essential mitotic kinases and kinesins have failed due to their low efficacy and uncontrolled toxicity. 7 KIF18A and KIF18B are members of the Kinesin 8 family and function in cell division. The KIF18A motor accumulates at the plus-end of the microtubule in metaphase and appears in the midzone during cytokinesis. The physiology of Kif18a -/- mice is almost completely normal, except for the germ cells, 8 suggesting that the mitotic functions of KIF18A are redundant and it is dispensable for normal cell division, although the KIF18A knockout (KO) cell lines show abnormal mitotic spindles and chromosome oscillation. 9 KIF18A overexpression has been reported in many types of cancer and is correlated with malignant progression. 10 Recent studies have shown that genetic knockout or inhibitor administration of KIF18A specifically inhibits chromosome instability (CIN) tumor growth by promoting mitotic delay and spindle aberration. 11–13 Excitingly, the most prominent features of osteosarcoma are karyotypic diversity and CIN. 4 , 12 , 14 In this study, we demonstrated that KIF18A is required for cell division in aneuploid osteosarcoma cell lines. The KIF18A inhibitor AM-1882 effectively reduced tumor cell viability and proliferation. These findings indicate that KIF18A inhibition represents a promising targeted therapeutic strategy for osteosarcoma.

Results

KIF18A is required for the survival of whole genome-doubled osteosarcoma cells

Based on a comprehensive evaluation of cancer cell aneuploidy research, 14 we found that SJSA1 is the only near-diploid among commonly used osteosarcoma cell lines. To confirm that KIF18A is essential for the viability of aneuploid osteosarcoma cells, we performed a whole-genome doubling in SJSA1 cells by inducing cytokinesis failure (Figure 1A). The resulting tetraploid daughter cells were designated as SPT (SJSA1-derived post-tetraploid), from which two independent clones (SPT1 and SPT2) were isolated for further investigation. The G2 peak of SJSA1 cells perfectly matched the G1 peak of SPT1 and SPT2 cells, demonstrating that the genome of SJSA1 was duplicated in SPT cells. In the three cell lines, we established an inducible KIF18A knockout system. The efficiency of knockout was tested by Western blotting (Figure 1B). ATP consumption-based cell viability was examined, and KIF18A KO conferred a vulnerability in SPT cells. The parental SJSA1 cell didn’t show any significant changes after knockout (Figure 1C). These findings indicated that SPT1 and SPT2 cells were more sensitive to KIF18A KO than were SJSA1 parental cells. We then confirmed this using cell survival and proliferation tests (Figure 1D and 1E). Notably, the whole-genome-doubled SPT1 cell showed no variation in cell proliferation compared with its parental cell. SPT2 decreased slightly, but this did not affect the conclusion that cell proliferation slowed down after KIF18A knockout in SPT cells (Figure S1). Since KIF18A depletion induces chromosome alignment defects and mitotic delay, which may eventually lead to mitotic catastrophe and cell apoptosis. We measured the expression of cleaved Caspase-3 in cells to evaluate the apoptotic levels. Not surprisingly, only the KIF18A KO SPT cells had high levels of cleaved Caspase-3, suggesting that, in the absence of KIF18A expression, SPT cells showed an enhanced apoptotic preference (Figure 1F). The above results verified that KIF18A is a potential therapeutic target for aneuploid osteosarcoma cells.

KIF18A is a key regulator in spindle assembly and chromosome alignment of the SPT cell

Normal spindle assembly is the basis of mitosis. Otherwise, prolonged activation of the SAC delays cell division and thus leads to apoptosis. 15 We therefore sought to confirm the function of KIF18A in dividing osteosarcoma cells. We used H2B-GFP-labeled SJSA1 and SPT cells to measure the duration of mitosis with or without KIF18A expression. The time-lapse images showed that in SJSA1 parental cells, depletion of KIF18A didn’t make a significant change. However, in the SPT cells, KIF18A KO prolonged the mitotic duration, suggesting that KIF18A was required for the mitosis of aneuploid cells (Figure 2A and 2B). The remarkable prolongation was mainly in metaphase, indicating that the SAC was not satisfactory without KIF18A expression. Interestingly, depletion of KIF18A induced mitotic death in a subset of SPT cells (Figure 2A and 2C). These findings suggest that prolonged activation of SAC can directly cause cell apoptosis during mitosis. Meanwhile, long division times usually increase the proportion of dividing cells, resulting in a high mitotic index. This was also the case in SPT cells (Figure 2D).

We next interrogated the precise dynamics of spindle assembly, the primary functional domain attributed to KIF18A. Typically, the loss of KIF18A leads to a change in spindle morphology because of the weak attachment between kinetochores and microtubules. 16 , 17 This phenomenon also occurs in SPT cells, in which the spindles become longer and wider, but not in SJSA1 parental cells. Meanwhile, the width of the DNA clearly increased in the SPT cells (Figure 2E). The shape change indicated a lack of tension along the microtubules in these abnormal spindles. This prevented chromosomes from being well compressed and aligned on the midline. Consequently, SAC activation arrested the metaphase-to-anaphase transition, thereby prolonging mitotic duration and triggering apoptosis. When the tension on the microtubules is insufficient, and the SAC is inactivated, chromosome lagging is common, similar to what occurs in the SPT cells (Figure 2F). Owing to the lack of tension caused by slow movement, massive chromosomes appeared from the main DNA groups. Thus, the chromosome instability was aggravated. The micronuclei arise from the lagging chromosome that cannot join the nucleus before envelope formation. The SPT cells showed a high frequency of micronuclei after KIF18A KO (Figure 2G).

The mitotic delay can also be induced by a multipolar spindle, 11 which is derived from aberrant spindle orientation and organization. In SPT cells, the incidence of multipolar spindle was significantly high when KIF18A was absent (Figure 2H). It might be related to the lack of tension caused by KIF18A KO. In conclusion, the KIF18A depletion induced spindle assembly aberration and mitotic delay. This promoted the risk of chromosome lagging and micronuclei formation, which ultimately led to chromosome instability. Conversely, the chromosome instability may confer high sensitivity to KIF18A inhibition in daughter cells.

KIF18A knockout-induced cell growth inhibition is impaired by SAC deficiency

We predicted that the inhibition of cell proliferation caused by KIF18A KO was mainly due to the activation of the SAC by aberrant spindles. Therefore, a defect in SAC signaling should interfere with the effect. To confirm this, we inactivated the SAC in SPT cells by transfecting target-specific siRNAs or treating the cells with the MPS1 inhibitor AZ3146. Cell viability and proliferation were enhanced in all three manipulated SAC-deficient SPT cells (Figure 3A, 3B, S2A, and S2B). The apoptosis induced by KIF18A KO in SPT cells was largely reduced in all three groups, as shown by the cleaved Caspase-3 expression (Figure 3C and S2C). The increased mitotic index in the KIF18A KO SPT cells was also compromised by the inhibition of SAC signaling (Figure 3D and S2D).

All the above results support that KIF18A is required for normal spindle assembly in aneuploid osteosarcoma cells. The absence of KIF18A expression induces aberrant spindles and the activation of SAC signaling, ultimately leading to cell apoptosis. Therefore, KIF18A is a promising target for osteosarcoma therapy.

KIF18A inhibitor AM-1882 promoted osteosarcoma cell death through the prevention of spindle assembly

AM-1882 is one of the most recently discovered KIF18A inhibitors. Unlike conventional kinesin spindle protein (KSP) inhibitors, this compound selectively targets KIF18A without displaying off-target interactions with other mitotic elements or kinases. 13 As a result, side effects can be greatly reduced. It is a promising drug for the treatment of chromosomally unstable cancers. To verify the therapeutic effects of AM-1882 in osteosarcoma, we applied the tests in SJSA1, SPT1, SPT2, and the commonly used osteosarcoma cell lines U2OS and MG63. The two cell lines were classified as having intermediate and high aneuploidy, respectively. 14 In these cell lines, AM-1882 treatment did not cause any changes in KIF18A expression (Figure S3A). Except for the artificial cell line SPT, the amount of KIF18A was correlated with their aneuploid level. This partly explains why CIN cells were more sensitive to KIF18A inhibitors. Furthermore, prolonged AM-1882 treatment did not reduce KIF18A expression (Figure S3B), suggesting that the administration was not tolerated. Cell viability, survival, and proliferation were significantly inhibited in the cell lines (Figure 4A-4C), and the level of inhibition clearly showed a dose-dependent response to the drug. Furthermore, MG63 cells exhibited a higher sensitivity than U2OS cells, suggesting that a higher degree of aneuploidy conferred a better outcome of the KIF18A inhibitor. Consistently, the expression of cleaved Caspase-3 was increased in AM-1882 treated SPT, U2OS, and MG63 cells (Figure 4D and S3C), indicating that apoptosis was significantly enhanced in these cell lines. The results confirmed that the effect of AM-1882 treatment was similar to that of KIF18A KO. Thus, AM-1882 is an effective KIF18A inhibitor in osteosarcoma cells and showed no obvious side effects on the SJSA1 cell.

We then focused on the disruption of spindle assembly with AM-1882 treatment. As in the KIF18A KO cells, the drug significantly prolonged the mitotic duration of the aneuploid cells (Figure 5A) and led to mitotic death in some of the dividing cells (Figure 5B). Predictably, this was accompanied by an increase in the mitotic index (Figure 5C). We examined spindle morphology after treatment, and AM-1882 induced tension insufficiency led to a bigger, wider, and looser spindle (Figure 5D). The immature spindle activated SAC signaling and apoptosis ultimately. In the cells that escaped mitotic arrest, the occurrence of chromosome lagging and the associated micronuclei was significantly increased (Figure 5E and 5F). The risk of chromosome instability in these aneuploid cells was further aggravated. We also observed a high incidence of multipolar spindles in AM-1882 treated SPT, U2OS, and MG63 cells (Figure 5G). The data indicated that AM-1882 inhibited the proliferation of aneuploid osteosarcoma cells by inhibiting KIF18A activity, causing abnormal spindle assembly, and activating SAC signaling.

In vivo, AM-1882 inhibited osteosarcoma xenografts growth

We subcutaneously inoculated U2OS or MG63 cells into mice to establish osteosarcoma xenograft models. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, the treatment was initiated. In addition to AM-1882 monotherapy, we utilized cisplatin – a standard-of-care chemotherapeutic for osteosarcoma – and docetaxel, an established type of spindle poison, as positive control treatments. In both the U2OS and MG63 models, AM-1882 showed excellent inhibitory activity in terms of tumor control. Meanwhile, Cisplatin only controlled tumor growth for a short term in the beginning, followed by a relapse of proliferation. Docetaxel, on the other hand, always had a limited effect (Figure 6A and 6C). This result suggested that compared to traditional chemotherapeutic agents, AM-1882 has significant cancer inhibition advantages. The immunohistochemical (IHC) staining confirmed that AM-1882 significantly inhibited tumor growth and promoted cell apoptosis in U2OS and MG63 xenografts (Figure 6B and 6D). Compared to the staining intensity of Ki67 and Cleaved Caspase3 in Cisplatin and Docetaxel-treated groups, AM-1882 demonstrated greater tumor control capacity.

Moreover, it also offered advantages in terms of low toxicity. The body weight of the mice did not decrease during the administration. AM-1882 also showed no clear hematological toxicity compared to Cisplatin and Docetaxel (Figure 6E and 6F). Taken together, these results support that KIF18A-targeted therapy is a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of osteosarcoma. AM-1882 is a potent and low-toxicity drug candidate that deserves further investigation and development.

AM-1882 sensitized OS cells to radiotherapy

Although AM-1882 therapy significantly suppressed osteosarcoma growth, a small fraction of tumor cells still survived. This strain is a potential seed for recurrence and drug resistance. To further enhance the efficacy of AM-1882 while reducing its dosage, we explored combination therapy for osteosarcoma. Owing to KIF18A inhibition and the resulting aberrant spindles and chromatids segregation, cells inevitably develop chromosome instability (Figure 2). Surviving cells are directly confronted with replication stress induced by CIN. 18 Therefore, we employed a combination of ionizing radiation (IR) and AM-1882 to largely amplify replication stress, thereby killing cancer cells. The results of the cell viability assay showed that a low dose of AM-1882 combined with 2 Gy IR also produced significant growth-inhibitory effects in CIN cells, but not in the SJSA1 parental cells (Figure S4A). These findings suggested that the anticancer effect of AM-1882 was related to replication stress. Low-dose IR reinforced this stress and significantly reduced CIN cell growth, whereas the cells were not sensitive to IR alone. To determine whether there is a synergistic effect between AM-1882 and IR, we assessed the cell viability of SPT1 and U2OS cells following combination therapy. In both cell lines, the combination index (CI) was less than one, indicating that the two therapies synergistically inhibited tumor growth (Figure S4B).

Consistently, the results of the clone formation assay led to the same conclusion: the combination treatment dramatically reduced CIN cell survival (Figure S4C). Replication stress generates single-strand DNA lesions. This leads to RPA complex phosphorylation and activation of the ATR signaling pathway. 19 To confirm that the cells had indeed faced replication stress, we measured the phosphorylation levels of relevant factors (Figure S4D). Both therapies individually increase the phosphorylation of RPA32 and CHK1, demonstrating the presence of replication stress. When used in combination, they significantly enhanced the stress response. Overall, the combined low dose of AM-1882 and IR therapy exhibited strong anti-osteosarcoma activity in CIN cells. This would facilitate the development of less toxic and more effective osteosarcoma treatment strategies in clinical practice.

Discussion

The fundamental treatment strategy for osteosarcoma with surgery combined with chemotherapy has not changed for decades. Since the establishment of standard therapy, changes in dosage, type of chemotherapeutic agent, or timing of use (pre- or postoperative) have failed to significantly promote survival. For the main patient population of this cancer, adolescents, the treatment has tremendous side effects. 20 As optimization, research on targeted therapies never stops. However, the complexity of the osteosarcoma gene mutation situation is far greater than imagined, although 87% of cases can be attributed to any of the 14 driver gene mutations. 21–24 Moreover, the studies of Chen et al. 25 and Perry et al. 26 demonstrated that tumors initiated by a single mutation will rapidly acquire other important mutations during evolution. All of these characteristics make osteosarcoma difficult to target with driver mutations. Interestingly, Kovac et al. 21 demonstrated that numerous mutations involving oncogenic pathways drive aneuploidy and chromosomal instability during the early stages of osteosarcoma, thereby imbuing osteosarcoma with a high degree of clinical chromosomal heterogeneity.

Aneuploidy is a common feature of cancer. These defects are caused by the defects in chromosome segregation, including improper kinetochore‒microtubule attachment, defects in chromosome cohesion, aberrant SAC, and supernumerary centrosomes. 27 In our study, we found that SAC inactivation rescued the loss of KIF18A, indicating that KIF18A depletion-induced mitotic delay and cell death are dependent on SAC signaling. This finding is also consistent with the general understanding that the SAC maintains the fidelity of chromosome segregation. Predictably, low levels of KIF18A are associated with a higher survival in osteosarcoma patients (Figure 6G). However, in many cancers, including osteosarcoma, SAC components are consistently overexpressed and associated with poor prognosis. 28 Janssen et al. 29 reported that in MAD2-depleted leukemia cells, KIF18A deficiency did not induce chromosome missegregation and cell death, although they also found that KIF18A knockdown caused growth inhibition in MAD2 wild-type cells. The current revised theory is that the SAC is a direct sensor of the kinetochore and microtubule attachment, but the sensing of tension may be indirect. Mutant Hec1, which stabilizes the attachment, satisfies the SAC in a state of low tension by recruiting MPS1 from the SAC complex. 30 , 31 Another study shows that Aurora B recruits SAC components to the kinetochore and initiates anaphase. 32 Although these studies have confirmed that the unattached kinetochore is the main source of SAC activation, the role of tension in SAC signaling has never been fully elucidated. As a source of tension, KIF18A does not affect the attachment. 29 In summary, identifying the specific context in which KIF18A inhibition-induced SAC activation triggers apoptosis remains critical to precisely defining the patient population most sensitive to this therapeutic strategy.

Generally, cell fate depends on the balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic pathways. Inhibition of KIF18A leads to extended activation of the SAC, thereby activating the apoptotic pathway in mitosis, but this does not necessarily mean that cells will be directly killed within the next few hours. In a study by Colin Gliech et al. 33 , AM-1882-treated OVCAR-8 and HeLa cells exhibited an extremely high mitotic death rate. However, in our study, mitotic death events in SPT1, U2OS, and MG63 cells accounted for only a small fraction. The majority of cells successfully completed division and entered the subsequent cell cycle, a phenomenon that may be largely mediated by anti-apoptotic factors. These include the expression levels of the BCL2 family members and the protective effects of mitochondrial fusion and fragmentation induced by CDK1 activation. 34 Nevertheless, it is certain that even if the prolonged mitosis of tumor cells does not directly cause cell death, the resulting CIN is unavoidable. 35 Obviously, this will continuously enhance the sensitivity of cells to KIF18A inhibitors; on the other hand, accumulating stresses caused by CIN, such as replication stress and cell cycle checkpoint stress, will eventually drive cells to stop dividing and enter the apoptotic program. 36 Meanwhile, the latest research 37 indicates that inhibiting tumor growth can also be achieved not by inducing cell death but by altering the cell cycle length. Therefore, mitotic death is not a natural standard for evaluating KIF18A inhibitors. It is also necessary to comprehensively observe the inhibitory effects on tumor cell growth. Also, the details of how sustained SAC activation leads to cell death still require further exploration.

In the past few years, breakthroughs have taken place in the KIF18A-targeted therapy. Amgen had developed a series of KIF18A inhibitors (including AM-1882), with AMG650 as the representative compound. 13 Currently, AMG650 has advanced to phase I clinical trials in triple-negative breast cancer and high-grade serous ovarian cancer (NCT04293094). Volastra also synthesized VLS-1272, which shares a similar structure with AM-1882. The pre-clinical research has demonstrated that it inhibited the growth of more than 100 cancer cell lines. 38 This supports its broad application prospects in the future. Another product from Volastra, named VLS-1488, is undergoing a phase I/II clinical trial in solid tumors with CIN (NCT05902988). At the same time, research on expanding the application scenarios of KIF18A inhibitors’ application scenarios is on the way. In this study, we confirmed that KIF18A KO and AM-1882 significantly inhibited CIN osteosarcoma cell growth through traditional mechanisms. Furthermore, we tested the combination of low-dose AM-1882 and radiotherapy to enhance replication stress and limit cancer cell proliferation. These data suggests we can explore combination therapy with other replication stress inducers, such as PARP1 inhibitors, ATR inhibitors, etc. The research will lead to efficient and low-toxicity clinical treatment regimens. Besides, Liu and colleagues extended the AM-1882 treatment to colorectal cancer and found that the combination of AM-1882 and immunotherapy resulted in good outcomes. 39 These studies indicate that targeted therapy for KIF18A has promising prospects, while also necessitating in-depth investigation into its effects on alterations within the tumor cell events and signaling pathways.

In conclusion, we describe here that KIF18A is required for maintaining the viability and cell proliferation of aneuploid osteosarcoma cells. Loss of KIF18A leads to aberrant spindle assembly and cell death through the activation of SAC signaling. One of the latest KIF18A inhibitors, AM-1882, shows potent inhibitory activity on cell growth in the osteosarcoma cell lines and a mouse xenograft model. Owing to its high efficacy and low toxicity, it has the potential to become an entirely new option for osteosarcoma treatment and to change the traditional strategy of clinical care.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, infection, and synchronization

The U2OS (SCSP-5030) cell line was purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, Shanghai, China. The SJSA1 (CRL-2098) and MG63 (CRL-1427) cell lines were purchased from ATCC. HEK-293T cell was maintained in our laboratory. U2OS cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (Thermo Fisher, 16600082) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone, SH31195). SJSA1 cell was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher, 61870036) supplemented with 10% FBS. MG63 and 293T cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Thermo Fisher, 11965092) supplemented with 10% FBS. All media were supplemented with 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, 15070063).

For SPT cell generation (whole genome doubling), SJSA1 cell was treated with 1 µM Cytochalasin D (Sigma, C8273) for 18 h. Then, the cells were washed in RPMI-1640 and subcloned by limiting dilution to obtain single-cell clones after 4 weeks. Individual clones were analyzed for DNA content by flow cytometry. Doubled clones were then cultured for 30 d and screened again to confirm the genomic alteration. 14

The inducible CRISPR-Cas9 system was established in SJSA1 and SPT cells. Briefly, we co-transfected pCW-Cas9 or KIF18A sgRNA (5’-AGUAGUAGUUCGUGUAC GUCCGG-3’) encoding lenti-sgRNA-Blast vectors with psPAX2 and VSVG, respectively, into 293T cells to obtain lentiviruses, respectively. The viruses were applied to SJSA1 and SPT cells, which were then selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma, P9620) and 5 μg/ml blasticidin (Sigma, 203350) for 5 d. Limiting dilution was performed to obtain single-cell clones, and the clones were verified by Western blotting. To induce KIF18A knockout, the cells were treated with 40 ng/mL doxycycline (DOX, Sigma, D3447) for 24 h. Functional assays were typically initiated 120 h after induction.

Similarly, we produced GFP-H2B virus in 293T cells by the transfection of the LVX-ZsGreen-H2B, psPAX2, and VSVG plasmids. The virus was applied to SJSA1, SPT1, U2OS, and MG63 cells, which were then selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin for 5 d. Positive cells were separated by limiting dilution and amplified.

To synchronize the cells at the G1/S phase border, we employed double-TdR blocking. Specifically, 2 mM TdR (Sigma, T1895) was added to the culture medium and incubated for 18 h. Then, cells were washed with ice-cold HBSS (Thermo Fisher, 14175095) and incubated in fresh medium for 10 h. 2 mM TdR was added again for 16 h. To obtain the cell at M phase, 2 mM TdR was added to the culture medium, and the mixture was incubated for 18 h. Then, the cells were washed with ice-cold HBSS and incubated in fresh medium for 3 h. 100 ng/mL nocodazole (Sigma, 487928) was applied to the cells for 10 h. The cells were arrested in prometaphase.

Flow cytometry

The cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and then fixed in cold 70% ethanol for 20 min at 4 °C. The cells were obtained by centrifugation and washed with ice-cold PBS. The fixed cells were incubated with RNase A (100 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher, R1253) for 60 min at RT, and followed by PI staining (50 μg/ml, Sigma, P4864) for 30 min at RT. Finally, the cells were resuspended in PBS supplemented with 0.5% BSA and subjected to flow cytometry (CytekTM Aurora). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Western blotting

The cell samples were harvested and washed with ice-cold PBS, followed by resuspension in ice-cold RIPA buffer for 30 min on ice. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected as the cell lysate. Electrophoresis was performed, and 30 μg of cell lysate was added to each lane of 10%–15% SDS‒PAGE gels. Protein was transferred to a PVDF membrane, blocking and antigen detection were performed sequentially.

Cell viability

We used a CellTiter-Glo (Promega, G7570) Kit to determine cell viability. We seeded 2 × 103 cells per well in an opaque-walled 96-well plate. The cells were treated with DOX and cultured for 96 h, and combined with 72 h of siRNA transfection or inhibitor administration. Then, 100 μL CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well, mixed for 2 min, and incubated for 10 min at RT. The luminescence value was read by a BioTek microplate reader. All values were normalized to the average of the control group. The combination index (CI) was calculated by CompuSyn software, which is based on the cell viability value of a series of fixed-ratio combination therapy groups.

Clone formation

Cells were suspended to single cells and seeded into a 6-well plate at 1 × 103 cells per well. Then, the cells were treated with the indicated compounds. The medium was changed every 3 d. After 12 d of culture, the cells were washed, fixed with methanol for 10 min at RT, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min at RT. The number of colonies was counted using Image J software, and the survival rate was calculated as the number of colonies in each well normalized to the average number in the control group.

MTT

Cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates, then treated cells with DOX, cultured for 96 h and combined with 72 h of siRNA transfection or inhibitor administration. Next, 10 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT solution (Sigma, 475989) was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for 4 h. We then removed the medium, added 150 μL DMSO per well, and mixed for 15 min. The absorbance of each well was read by BioTek microplate reader.

Time-lapse imaging

The cells were seeded in the glass-bottom culture dishes and treated with DOX or AM-1882. After treatment, the cells were arrested at the G1/S border and cultured in fresh medium for 9 h. The dishes were placed on an IX-81 live cell station (Olympus) and heated at 37 °C heating and 5% CO2. Images were taken every 5 min with a 20× objective.

Immunofluorescence staining

Nocodazole-arrested cells were washed by ice-cold HBSS and incubated in fresh medium for 30 min. Followed by fixation of 4% PFA (Santa Cruz, sc-253236) for 10 min and permeabilization by 0.25% Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher, A16046) for 15 min. The cells were blocked by 10% normal goat serum (Thermo Fisher, 50197Z) for 1 h and incubated with diluted α-Tubulin antibody overnight at 4 °C. The Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody was added to the cells, which were then incubated for 30 min at RT. Finally, the cells were stained with DAPI (5 μg/mL, Thermo Fisher, 62248) for 10 min at RT and mounted with Prolong Glass Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, P36984). Three fields of view were randomly selected, and images were captured with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Osteosarcoma xenograft model

All animal experimental manipulations were based on a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The experimental protocol was established according to the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Six weeks old male Nu/Nu nude mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University and housed under pathogen-free conditions. The mice were randomly assigned to groups using a random number generator and then enrolled in sequence. 2 × 106 U2OS or MG63 cells were suspended in PBS and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse (non-anesthesia). After approximately 10 d, when the tumor volume reached 100 mm3 (measured by a Vernier caliper), treatment was initiated. The tumor volume was measured with a digit caliper and calculated as V = π/6 (L × W × H). The mice were treated with vehicle or 100 mg/kg AM-1882 daily for 18 d. AM-1882 was formulated in 2% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (Sigma, H7509) and 1% Tween-80 (Sigma, P1754), pH 2.2, and stirred overnight before i.p. 6 mg/kg cisplatin every 3 d or 20 mg/kg docetaxel weekly administration were used as positive controls. 13 The two medicines were dissolved in saline. Tumor volume and body weight were measured every 3 d. After 18 d of treatment, all the mice were euthanized by inhaled CO2. Tumor tissues were collected and fixed in 10% formalin (Thermo Fisher, R21640). Blood samples were also collected and then delivered to the laboratory of the Experimental Animal Center for counting blood cells. Ultimately, the data from 40 mice (5 mice per group) was included.

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

The tumor tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and processed via dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. The slides were dewaxed, subjected to antigen retrieval, and hydrogen peroxide treatment and then blocked with horse serum. The diluted antibodies (Ki67, Cell Signaling, 9027; Cleaved Caspase 3, Cell Signaling, 9661) were applied to the slides, which were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibody was incubated at RT for 30 min, and the slides were stained with DAB substrate. The slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Pictures taken with a 40X lens. The calculation of the Immunoscore was based on the product of the staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells.

Statistics

All the in vitro studies included 3 representative replicates, and the statistical data are presented as mean ± SD. The significance of differences between two groups was analyzed by a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA for multiple groups. For the nonparametric data, the Mann‒Whitney test was used for analysis. Calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
