Beyond Awareness: The Critical Need for Actionable Strategies to Bridge the Knowledge‐Practice Gap in Hepatitis B Prevention
Authors: Kong Qingfei, Xiao Wen
Published: 2026

Re: Vaccination Coverage and Awareness on Hepatitis B Virus Among Students in Bangladesh: A Call for Translational Intervention Research.

To the Editor: We read with great interest the cross‐sectional study by Khatun and colleagues, published in Health Science Reports, which investigated the knowledge, attitudes, and vaccination status regarding hepatitis B virus (HBV) among students in Bangladesh. We commend the authors for highlighting a critical public health issue. Their findings—particularly the alarmingly low vaccination coverage among university (42.11%) and college (44.44%) students compared to high school students (86.3%), and the persistence of significant knowledge gaps even among those with higher education—resonate deeply with challenges we observe in community health [1]. The study rightly concludes that enhancing awareness and education is paramount to preventing HBV infection.

However, the study also inadvertently underscores a pervasive and critical gap in much of global health research: the leap from identifying a “knowledge deficit” to prescribing “education” as the solution, without delineating the how. While Khatun et al. effectively diagnose the problem of suboptimal knowledge and vaccination rates, the pathway from this diagnosis to a cure remains largely unmapped. The authors note that low coverage among older students may stem from being missed by the national Expanded Program on Immunization and from factors like lack of motivation or fear of side effects. Yet, the research design does not probe the specific, actionable barriers—cost, access, convenience, trusted messenger credibility, or nuanced risk perception—that transform “awareness” into “action.” This is not a critique of the study's validity but an observation of a common research frontier we must collectively advance.

The issue extends far beyond HBV vaccination. In primary care, we confront a parallel, and perhaps even more resource‐intensive, challenge: medication non‐adherence in chronic disease management. Similar to vaccination, patient understanding of their condition and treatment is foundational. Countless studies correlate better knowledge with improved adherence. Yet, simply knowing that “cognitive insufficiency” exists does not equip us with the tools to fix it effectively. We lack robust, context‐specific, and scalable intervention blueprints. What specific educational content, delivered by whom (e.g., peers, pharmacists, community health workers), through which medium (e.g., interactive mobile messages, group counselling) [2, 3, 4], and at what frequency, reliably improves not just test scores but real‐world behaviors like completing a vaccine series or taking antihypertensive medication daily? The “black box” of effective intervention remains too often closed.

Therefore, we propose a shift in research focus, building upon essential foundational studies like that of Khatun et al. We urge the research community and journals like Health Science Reports to prioritize what we might term “Translational Implementation Research” in behavioral health. The next critical step is moving from cross‐sectional assessments to designing, testing, and comparing specific intervention strategies. For the context studied by Khatun et al., subsequent research questions could include.

Strategy Testing: Does a single, intensive educational seminar in university orientation yield better vaccination uptake than a sustained, peer‐led social media campaign? Is on‐campus, subsidized vaccination pop‐up clinics more effective than referrals to external health centers?

Barrier‐Specific Interventions: For students who cite cost or fear of side effects as barriers, does providing clear information on free vaccine availability through NGO partnerships or structured counselling addressing safety concerns significantly alter behavior?

Leveraging Technology: Can validated, gamified mobile health (mHealth) applications tailored for young adults improve knowledge retention and send reminders for multi‐dose vaccine completion more effectively than standard pamphlets?

The authors rightly acknowledge their study's limitations, including the use of self‐reported vaccination data and its regional focus. These limitations highlight the need for the next phase of research to incorporate more objective measures (e.g., serological validation in sub‐samples) and be conducted across diverse settings.

In conclusion, we strongly agree with Khatun and colleagues that enhancing knowledge is crucial. Their work provides the vital epidemiological groundwork. The pressing need now is for research that dismantles the “education” monolith into testable, replicable, and cost‐effective intervention components. By investing in studies that bridge the “know‐do” gap with concrete strategies, we can transform awareness into actual vaccination and medication adherence. This will not only combat HBV but also optimize management of chronic diseases, leading to profound savings in healthcare resources and, more importantly, vastly improved patient outcomes. We hope future contributions will take up this challenge.
