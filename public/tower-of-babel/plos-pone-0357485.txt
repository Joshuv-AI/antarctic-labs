Multiple simulated spaceflight stressors impact cardiac fibrosis, calcium dynamics, immune function, cytokines and gene variants in rat, Rattus norvegicus

Marek Lenarczyk, Amy Kronenberg, Andy Kwok, Alexander Borg, Ammar J Alsheikh, Angela J Mathison, Michael T Zimmermann, Samuel B R Lawton

ABSTRACT
Space radiation and microgravity pose significant health risks for astronauts conducting deep space exploration. The impact of galactic cosmic radiation (GCR) and weightlessness alone, and their combined effects on cardiac disease, the immune system, cytokines and calcium dynamics should be understood before undertaking such missions. Male WAG/RijCmcr rats, Rattus norvegicus, 8–9 months old were hind limb unloaded (HLU) to simulate exposure to microgravity for 5 days before, during, and 25 days after whole-body exposure to a simplified GCR simulation (simGCRsim) dose of 1.5 Gy that was administered rapidly. Other rats maintained normal weight-bearing. Exposure to 1.5 Gy of simGCRsim with or without HLU increased cardiac perivascular collagen content, the primary endpoint, compared to sham-irradiated no HLU rats after 270 days. The extent of cardiac fibrosis present in the two irradiated groups could not be distinguished, indicating no interaction between the two space flight stressors. A series of secondary endpoints were also measured. Sham irradiation with HLU increased blood lymphocytes but not myeloid cells compared with non-HLU, an outcome that was inhibited by simGCRsim with HLU, indicating an interaction between spaceflight stressors at this high dose-rate. HLU alone decreased multiple circulating cytokines and depleted CD56+ cells in the spleen. Notably, simGCRsim and HLU alone and in combination did not disrupt calcium homeostasis. An exposure to 1.5 Gy simGCRsim no HLU revealed distinct genomic alterations in circulating nucleated blood cells as compared with sham-irradiated subjects no HLU, and identified mutations, frameshifts, and deletions in genes that contribute to human disease in the irradiated cohort. A limited follow-on study involved exposure to 0.75 Gy simGCRsim with no HLU with appropriate controls. This radiation dose, a dose relevant to human missions to Mars, caused cardiac perivascular fibrosis at an extended follow up period of after 360 days. Taken together, our findings show exposure to simGCRsim, representing the composition of GCR behind spacecraft shielding, caused cardiac perivascular fibrosis and gene variants. Simulated microgravity (HLU) alone engaged the immune system and decreased cytokine production. We conclude that exposure of rats to simGCRsim and/or HLU results in persistent health effects with limited interactions between the two spaceflight stressors.

Introduction Space radiation and microgravity pose two significant health risks for astronauts conducting deep space exploration missions. Humans have evolved with minimal exposure to galactic cosmic radiation (GCR) and with the constant downward force of gravity. Extended duration exploration missions to the Earth’s Moon and the planet Mars are actively being prepared. During these missions, astronauts will be exposed to GCR once outside the Earth’s magnetosphere, and the force of gravity will decrease as the distance increases from the surface of the Earth to the Moon or Mars. Humans will encounter reduced gravity on lunar and Martian surfaces. The extent to which human health will be adversely affected by these spaceflight stressors during and after the completion of exploration missions is a major unknown and a concern to NASA and other space agencies. Space radiation environments include low to moderately energetic protons and alpha particles from solar particle events (SPE), as well as GCR, which is composed of higher energy electrons and positrons (2%), protons (85%), helium nuclei (12%), and heavier high-energy and high-charge ions (HZE; 1%, [ 1 ]. During exploratory missions, astronauts will be continuously exposed to GCR and less predictably and sporadically to SPE. Our initial studies determined the impact of space radiation on the risks for degenerative long-term cardiovascular disease at doses that are mission-relevant for future human spaceflight beyond low earth orbit [ 2 ]. Exposure of male WAG/RijCmcr rats at 6 months of age at a ground-based charged particle accelerator with three rapidly switched ion beams representative of components of GCR (protons, silicon ions, and iron ions) caused cardiac vascular pathology with an apparent threshold dose for cardiac perivascular fibrosis over the time course studied [ 2 ]. Fibrosis results from excess deposition of the extracellular matrix, primarily collagen types I and III. NASA recently developed the simplified GCR simulator (simGCRsim) at the NASA Space Radiation Laboratory (NSRL) at Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL). The simGCRsim generates a spectrum of six ion beams ( Table 2 ) that approximate the primary and secondary GCR field experienced at human organ locations within a deep space vehicle and low energy protons present in SPE [ 3 ]. This five-ion, six-beam simGCRsim is better at representing the space radiation environment than the three ion beams of protons, silicon, and iron [ 2 ] in our previous study, and can be used as a standard radiation stimulus to allow comparisons of space radiation studies between laboratories. Exposure to microgravity affects human health. In astronauts, bone loss occurs and blood calcium levels are still decreased up to 1 year after return to the Earth [ 4 ]. Microgravity by dry immersion exposure in humans for 21 days results in the expansion of T cells that persists upon re-exposure to normal gravity for 7 days [ 5 ]. During 6 months of spaceflight, microgravity as well as other spaceflight stressors suppress the immune system [ 6 ]. Cytokines are secreted signaling proteins that activate distinct cell signaling and the immune response. These small proteins (~5–25 kDa) typically function by interacting with specific cytokine receptors on target cell surfaces. Studies of cosmonauts and astronauts living in microgravity during low earth orbit showed no change in circulating cytokines 30 days after returning to 1g [ 7 – 9 ]. Hind limb unloading (HLU) in rats causes a cephalad fluid shift and reduced mobility as reported during spaceflight in astronauts [ 10 ]. HLU is accepted by NASA as a rodent model of microgravity. Reloading studies in mice have been conducted up to 49 days after 30 days of HLU [ 10 ], and others have examined responses up to 270 days after 7 days of HLU [ 11 , 12 ]. In humans exposed to microgravity and rodents exposed to hind limb unloading, the cephalad redistribution of blood volume with the accompanying decrease in pressure gradient should decrease kidney perfusion. The kidney is mainly responsible for regulating calcium dynamics. We reasoned that the 270-day period needed for perivascular cardiac fibrosis to develop in WAG/RijCmcr rats after exposure to space-relevant doses of radiation [ 2 ] would be sufficient to detect the engagement of circulating immune cells, changes in cytokines, and biomarkers of altered calcium metabolism caused by HLU. Changes in the sequence of DNA can trigger specific molecular alterations that can influence cellular physiology and may contribute to changes in higher order functions. Low- and high-linear energy transfer (LET) radiation are well known to affect the DNA structure in cells by inducing single or double-strand breaks along with base damages and complex lesions [ 13 ]. DNA in affected cells can either be repaired and function normally, be mis-repaired with permanent changes in the DNA sequence resulting in a gene variant that may function abnormally, or unrepaired or misrepaired changes that can lead to cell death. Aging can result in somatic mutations in DNA that drive clonal expansion of blood cells to cause clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) that contributes to increased risk for cardiovascular disease [ 14 ]. We reasoned that as age and radiation are independent risk factors for the development of cardiovascular disease, whole genome sequencing of nucleated peripheral blood from mature rats of astronaut age exposed to simGCRsim would reveal gene variants that may contribute to CHIP. The interaction between simulated weightlessness and space radiation on cardiovascular disease needs to be understood before undertaking extended deep space exploration missions. Until recently, combining the spaceflight hazard of reduced weight bearing via HLU in rats of any strain with simGCRsim in conscious animals has not been possible. We created a model protocol with rats simultaneously exposed to HLU and simGCRsim followed out for an extended period after the cessation of spaceflight stressors [ 15 , 16 ]. We tested the central hypothesis that exposure to HLU and space relevant doses of simGCRsim potentiate risk for chronic cellular dysfunction in multiple biological systems using a rat model with no pre-existing disease and that is sensitive to radiation-induced cardiac pathology. Our objective was to measure risk for cardiac disease, alterations of the immune system, cytokines, calcium metabolism, and gene variants. Understanding these risks is a priority based on both the likelihood of occurrence and the severity of their impact on human health for exploration missions. Materials and methods Study design This study was a controlled laboratory experiment to evaluate whether the spaceflight stressors GCR and simulated weightlessness act synergistically on the male WAG/RijCmcr rat. This objective was addressed by exposing these rats to representative components of GCR combined with HLU and measuring perivascular cardiac fibrosis, gene variants, immune system dysregulation, cytokines, and calcium metabolism over a stopping point of 270–360 days. The whole genome was sequenced to identify gene variants known to be associated with clonal hematopoiesis. All studies were performed by investigators blinded to the treatment groups at the Medical College of Wisconsin (MCW), WI, USA. The number of subjects in each experimental group was based on our previous experience with similar measurements in similar studies. Cardiac perivascular fibrosis, the primary endpoint in tissue, and biomarkers of calcium metabolism, immune system activation, cytokines, and gene variants, conventional indicators for cardiac disease and kidney injury, the secondary endpoints, were selected prospectively. Stopping points for data collection were determined prospectively. Cardiac perivascular fibrosis was observed with a sample size of three hearts per group for sham irradiated and six hearts per group for irradiated rats based on previous studies using a similar experimental design and effect size [ 17 ]. This aspect of the experimental design allowed the conduct of additional studies on components of the immune system in heart to generate hypotheses for future larger studies [ 18 , 19 ]. To randomize animals in the experiment, a random number generator was used to ensure each animal received an equal chance at inclusion in any group to eliminate bias and was then assigned a unique ID. The identity of the animal in each experimental group was known to the investigator responsible for initiating and continuing with an intervention, for example, irradiation. The identity of the animal under study was not known to the investigator performing the experimental measurement or the data analysis. These investigators were not the same person. The start of the study was defined as the time rats began hind limb unloading. All data collected for primary and secondary endpoints were included in the study. Raw data are provided in supplementary data files. Experimental animals Seventy-two male WAG/RijCmcr rats were bred at the MCW and were transferred at 8–9 months of age (n = 12/group) to BNL by commercial air courier with access to food and gel. This strain of rat was selected for study as exposure to three ion beams (protons, silicon ions, and iron ions) representative of GCR [ 2 ] results in cardiovascular disease in a time- and dose-dependent manner. This rat age corresponds to an age representative of the astronaut corps. Group sizes were based on a power analysis that indicated that for each dose/condition, data from 12 rats would detect the anticipated effect at a significance level of 0.05 with a power level of 0.80 for conventional biochemical indicators of cardiac and kidney injury [ 2 ]. The rats were acclimatized at BNL for 2 weeks before irradiation. The start of the study was defined as the time rats were hind limb unloaded, or sham treated. Animals were pair-housed and maintained on standard food (Teklad 8904 irradiated rodent diet, [ 20 ] and drinking water provided ad libitum throughout the experiments. The rats were provided with nesting material, maintained on a 12-hour light, 12-hour dark cycle at a temperature of 20 ± 1°C, and relative humidity 50–80%. Animals were monitored at least daily for signs of distress, including ruffled fur, lethargy, or reduced food intake. Animals were euthanized at the end of the study or when humane endpoints were reached. Humane endpoints for euthanasia were a decrease of 20% or more from baseline body weight, persistent hypothermia or a rapid decrease in core temperature for several consecutive days, an increase in respiratory rate, lethargy, recumbency, failure to reach food and water, ruffled fur, lack of grooming, or abnormal body posture. Once animals reached endpoint criteria they were euthanized immediately. The Animal Care and Use Committee at MCW (AUA4086) and BNL (482) approved all live animal experiments, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering and distress. Research staff, animal care personnel and veterinarians supervised the welfare of the animals. To minimize handling stress, animals were moved using cupping rather than tail picking. Research staff in the study had previous experience in animal care and handling of rats. Seventy two animals were used and 71 were euthanized. One control rat died during the third week of the study of unknown causes, determined from necropsy. The death was reported to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and investigated. The remaining data for this group is based on n = 11. There were no clinical signs of infection or deaths in WAG/RijCmcr rats from HLU or from radiation during their stay at BNL or at MCW. All living subjects were followed throughout the full 270–360-day duration of the experiment. The HLU procedure for these same rats has previously been published [ 15 , 16 , 22 ]. Briefly, rats were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5–3.0%; 100% oxygen flow rate). Strips (1 cm wide x 30 cm long) of medical traction tape were placed laterally along the lateral sides of the tail (3M, St. Paul, MN, United States), originating approximately 1 cm from the base of the tail and then spanning 3/4ths the length of the tail. The tape was looped through a custom-built plastic ball bearing swivel then adhered to the opposing lateral side of the tail. A 1.56-in carabiner clip (Nite Ize, Boulder, CO, United States) was attached to the plastic swivel, and the other end of the carabiner was attached to a zipper hook that permitted sliding along a perlon cord (STAS group, BA, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The cord was attached to a support separator spreader pulley (Blue Hawk, Gilbert, AZ, United States). The pulley was able to slide the length of a 5/16-in diameter round, solid steel rod (Hillman, Cincinnati, OH, United States). The zipper lock was adjusted to lift the hind limbs off the floor with an angle of the thorax and abdomen 30 o to the floor. The rats maintained under full weight bearing (non-HLU) received isoflurane but were not tail suspended. All HLU and non-HLU rats were sub-grouped to receive a radiation dose (0.75 Gy or 1.5 Gy) or sham exposure (0 Gy); all animals were singly housed. Wet rodent chow and gel packs were provided to ensure hydration. Corrugated cardboard bedding was supplied to permit burrowing for all rats. On Day 5, animals were transferred from HLU or non-HLU housing to another plexiglass box for radiation or sham-radiation exposure. HLU animals remained tail suspended during the entire transfer process by removing the ball bearing from the carabiner and moving the rat to a plastic plexiglass holder (9.5” x 4” x 7.5”), and plastic bead chains were attached to the swivel. These holders were placed into a rigid foam frame, allowing six rats (in six holders) to be placed into the beamline simultaneously. The custom frame permitted plastic bead chains to hang from a top crossbar. Each bead chain was looped through the hole in the swivel, and the free end was affixed to itself using a plastic zip tie. The bead chain was adjusted at the cross bar to ensure the rat’s body was 30 o relative to the plexiglass floor; the hind limbs continued to be lifted from the floor. The holder lid permitted the tail to extend through and to allow movement in the holder. Rats that did not receive HLU were placed into similar plexiglass boxes drilled with airholes and stacked into rigid foam frames but remained fully weight-bearing. Rats were then transported to the NSRL facility on each irradiation day for sham irradiation or radiation exposure. All methods were carried out per relevant guidelines and regulations. The HLU procedure was initiated 5 days before sham-irradiation or irradiation. The rats remained in the HLU condition 25 days after sham-irradiation or irradiation and were reloaded. The animals were group housed. One week following the end of the HLU period, the animals were shipped back from BNL to MCW by commercial air courier with access to food and gel. Histology To evaluate tissue damage at 270 days or 360 days after irradiation, the entire heart ( n = 3–6/group) was isolated from anesthetized irradiated and sham-irradiated (control) rats and fixed in 10% formalin (v/v) using our standard procedures as described elsewhere [ 23 ]. We consistently obtained transverse heart tissue sections from the middle of both ventricles. Before embedding, the heart was oriented between the base and the apex. Fixed tissue samples were embedded in paraffin with heart samples in the transverse plane. Sections 4 µm thick were cut from each block and stained with Masson’s-trichrome blue according to standard methods described elsewhere [ 23 ]. Ten sections from each heart were used for morphometric analysis as described elsewhere in a blinded fashion [ 23 ]. All selected images of the heart microvasculature were acquired using a Nikon E-55i microscope equipped with a Nikon DS-Fi1 camera. The collagen content was measured in the perivascular and wall space areas on calibrated images (25–35/rat/group) by planimetry and quantified using MetaMorph image analysis program version 7.7.0.0. (Molecular Devices, Downingtown, PA, USA). Planimetry determined the true area of the coronary vessel, thereby eliminating errors that may have been caused by vessel compression and collapse. Sections of both ventricles of the heart (n = 3–6/group) were analyzed in a blinded fashion. Randomly selected images of tissue were acquired using a Nikon Plan Fluor 403/0.75 objective lens mounted on a Nikon E-55i microscope fitted with a Nikon DS-Fi1 camera and saved as 24-bit RGB images in TIFF format. Perivascular fibrotic changes in 20–30 fields containing coronary vessels, 2–50 microns in diameter for each heart were quantified using MetaMorph image analysis program version 7.7.0.0. (Molecular Devices, Downington, PA). On calibrated images, the areas of both the vessel lumen and the perivascular space were measured by planimetry. Planimetry determined the true area of the coronary vessel, thereby eliminating errors that may have been caused by compression and collapse. The area of fibrosis, per luminal area, was then calculated for each coronary vessel accessed. The resultant data was then averaged per animal and then grouped by treatment for statistical analysis. The average microvasculature fibrosis was expressed as percentage collagen per lumen area for each image. The collagen content of the coronary vessels was defined as the total area stained blue with trichrome, expressed as a percentage of the lumenal area, and then averaged per animal and grouped by treatment for statistical analysis. Perivascular cardiac fibrosis was defined as an increase in collagen content in irradiated hearts above the average values for sham-irradiated animals [ 2 , 24 , 25 ]. Whole genome sequencing of nucleated cells from peripheral blood We sequenced the whole genome of circulating, nucleated cell populations in the WAG/RijCmcr rat and used bioinformatics to detect alterations in samples from rats previously exposed to 1.5 Gy of simGCRsim, with no HLU or in sham-irradiated rats also with no HLU. The samples were collected at 240 days following the start of the study. With the rat genome covering approximately 2.75 billion bases, we sequenced 6 samples (3 exposed, 3 sham-exposed) with paired-end 115 bp reads on an S2 NovaSeq flowcell that provided 1.4 billion reads for each sample, for an approximate coverage of 60x across the genome. Using the outcomes of this limited study, we based our initial analysis on genes known to be involved in leukemogenesis in humans and in mice. This bioinformatic-based panel focused on 74 genes previously associated with CHIP [ 14 , 29 ]. We determined the observed variant allele numbers in these 74 genes. This data was generated using the same analytical approach as a certified clinical genomics laboratory uses for patients. Thus, this data demonstrates our abilities and the feasibility to conduct the proposed studies to detect genomic changes associated with CHIP. We calculated the VAF of each genetic variation using the ratio of sequencing reads that detect the alternate allele compared to total read depth at the same site. Geneset and pathway term enrichment and depletion used the Gene Ontology (GO, [ 30 , 31 ] and KEGG [ 32 ] resources and hypergeometric statistics, as implemented in a platform from the Ge lab [ 33 ]. Statistical analysis All endpoint values apart from the gene variant analysis were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). The statistical strategy was selected based on the structure of each dataset and the experimental design. For continuous longitudinal endpoints involving repeated measurements within the same animals, linear mixed-effects modeling was used to appropriately account for within-subject correlation, time-dependent effects, and incomplete observations where applicable. For single-timepoint endpoints, analyses were performed using the statistical approach most appropriate to the specific design and distributional characteristics of the data. This study is focused on the biological effects of a combined stressor exposure to simGCRsim and HLU to directly evaluate whether the effects of combined exposure differ from the expected additive effects of the individual stressors. p-values from multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction to control for multiplicity while maintaining sensitivity appropriate for a multidimensional biological dataset [ 34 ] with a q-value cutoff of 0.1 ( S1 Table ). For secondary, non-longitudinal endpoints where mixed models were mathematically inappropriate, two-group comparisons were performed using the t-test. Statistical significance was defined at an adjusted p value of < 0.05. Perivascular cardiac collagen and calcium dynamics data were analyzed using Sigma Plot 11 software. All data were examined for normality using Shapiro-Wilk test [ 35 ]. Perivascular cardiac collagen data were analyzed using the t-test. Testing for statistical significance for the calcium dynamics data was performed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with all pairwise multiple comparison procedures (Holm-Sidak method) or Kruskal-Wallis One Way ANOVA on ranks when normality test failed. Discussion During exploration missions extending beyond low earth orbit, astronauts will be continuously exposed to low levels of GCR as well as weightlessness and the stress of space operations and potential risks. Prior to undertaking such missions, we need to understand as much as possible in the time available about the impact of these spaceflight stressors on genetic, molecular and biochemical mechanisms in biological systems both during the mission as well as following the return to Earth. We utilized a ground-based animal model exposed to two spaceflight stressors – hindlimb suspension as a simulation of weightlessness (HLU) and radiation exposure simulating a 3-year deep space mission (0.75 Gy of simGCRsim) to determine increased risk for cardiovascular disease, disruption of the immune system, cytokines, gene variants, and changes in calcium homeostasis. A higher simGCRsim dose was included to benchmark the current study against our previous work [ 2 ]. Delivery and then discontinuation of simGCRsim exposures resulted in cardiac perivascular fibrosis, the primary endpoint. Exposure to 1.5 Gy simGCRsim no HLU caused marked genomic alterations in circulating cell populations and identified mutations, frameshifts, and deletions in genes, a secondary endpoint, that contribute to human disease. Reloading after HLU disrupted the immune system and cytokine production, secondary end points. These findings indicate a persistent health effect of each spaceflight stressor on multiple systems. Whole-body exposure of rats to a five-ion six-beam simGCRsim suite of beams delivered in close succession, designed by NASA to simulate the composition of GCR behind spacecraft shielding, resulted in cardiac perivascular fibrosis. The simGCRsim findings confirm the same extent of cardiac perivascular fibrosis, measured as increased collagen content, caused by exposure to 1.5 Gy from three ion beams representative of GCR in the same rat strain and over the same study duration [ 2 ]. Here, a longer follow-up period after exposure to 0.75 Gy of simGCRsim, revealed cardiac perivascular fibrosis after 360 days. While exposure to 0.75 Gy from a group of 3 ion beams delivered in close succession in our earlier study led to increased perivascular collagen levels after 270 days, those results did not reach statistical significance in the more limited observation period [ 2 ]. Thus, the more extended follow-up period used here revealed detrimental cardiovascular outcomes at lower doses, demonstrating a lower apparent threshold for this potentially critical health outcome. Because radiation injury is inherently dynamic, evaluating dose without accounting for time fails to capture how biological systems express latent damage. Using our radiation model, where dose rate is intentionally kept constant, we demonstrate the bivariate relationship between absorbed radiation dose and post-exposure follow up time plays a critical role in the presentation of cardiac perivascular fibrosis. The present study confirms our previous findings of perivascular cardiac fibrosis 270 days after a dose of 1.5 Gy [ 2 ]. We now show exposure to a lower dose of 0.75 Gy with the follow up period extended to 360 days results in perivascular cardiac fibrosis. Thus, radiation dose and post-exposure follow up time determine the severity and progression of cardiac injury. Our findings highlight a critical challenge in space radiobiology that GCR induces complex, time-dependent damage that traditional, static dose calculations fail to properly account for. Reloading rats after HLU decreased levels of immune cells, cytokines, and calcium in peripheral blood. Astronauts who complete long-duration exploratory missions face potential chronic health issues that may manifest decades after, including an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, vision impairment and neurodegenerative conditions. As the duration of exploratory missions increases and astronauts are anticipated to live for more than 20 years after returning from exploratory missions, we now propose that the development of cardiac disease and disruption of homeostasis in multiple systems caused by space radiation and weightlessness develops after extended follow-up periods. The stress of microgravity during spaceflight or HLU dysregulates the immune system and may lead to acute and chronic health issues. The present study shows that the deleterious effects of 30 days of HLU persist 240 days after reloading, manifesting as an increase in multiple immune cells in the circulation: total leukocytes, T lymphocytes, helper T lymphocytes, cytotoxic T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes, and one immune system cell type (NK cells) that decreased in the spleen. Exposure to simGCRsim with no HLU did not change the levels of lymphoid and myeloid cells present in the circulation. Sham irradiation with HLU increased lymphocytes but not myeloid cells in blood compared with sham-irradiation without HLU that was inhibited by simGCRsim with HLU, indicating an interaction between spaceflight stressors. The spleen has important roles in hematopoiesis, red blood cell clearance, and immune function. Exposure to simGCRsim with no HLU depleted natural killer cells resident in the spleen by 67% 240 days after reloading with no further decrease with simGCRsim with HLU. NK cell function in astronauts flying 9–10 days aboard the Space Shuttle decreased upon return to 1g [ 39 ]. Exposure of mice to 0.4 Gy 16 O ions over three fractions decreased NK cell function in the spleen by 25% after 365–548 days [ 40 ]. In contrast, combined exposure to 0.5 Gy gamma radiation and hind limb unloading for 30–31 days without reloading in mice did not alter immune cells in the spleen [ 41 ]. A selective suppression of NK cell function is a known effect of spaceflight and constitutes another of the important considerations for astronaut health during deep space missions [ 42 , 43 ]. Our findings show that the extent of immune cell dysregulation in the spleen caused by the spaceflight stressors simGCRsim and HLU followed by unloading is apparent later than reported in previous studies, indicating its importance to astronaut health long after the completion of exploratory missions. Together, simGCRsim and HLU did not exert a late effect on the levels of immune cells present in heart tissue, indicating the response to these spaceflight stressors is tissue specific. The present study showed that extended reloading periods cause significant inhibition of cytokine production following hindlimb unloading. Our results confirm and extend the observation of decreased cytokine levels in the flight subject as part of the NASA Twins study [ 44 ]. HLU for 30 days persistently reduced levels of 20 of 27 (74%) cytokines in the circulation over the 270 days of reloading. In the NASA Twins Study, living on the International Space Station for 340 days decreased levels of 17 of 50 (34%) cytokines in the flight subject 190 days after return to 1g [ 44 ]. Six of the decreased cytokines, EGF, GRO/KC, Il-10, IL-18, IP10, and leptin ( S4 Table ) were common to rats and humans, supporting the utility of the rat as a model for weightlessness in humans. These findings suggest that simulated weightlessness using HLU in rats and actual weightlessness in humans in space each cause prolonged inhibition of cells that produce cytokines at later times after reloading. Humans live far longer than rats [ 45 ], 270 rat days = 9,396 human days = 26 years, so prolonged decreases in cytokine production observed in rats may result in chronic health issues in astronauts many years after returning to 1g. Addressing this issue is critical, given that astronauts are anticipated to live for more than two decades following the completion of exploratory missions [ 2 ] and current knowledge about chronic health outcomes is limited by the relatively small numbers of career astronauts. We identified 7 gene variants induced by simGCRsim: Smc3, Suz12, Brcc3, Ep300, Cblb, Idh2 and Usaf2 [ 46 – 49 ] , each of which may indicate clonal hematopoiesis. While CHIP is not an established disease, it can inform long-term risk for cardiovascular disease and cancer. The presence of indicators of clonal hematopoiesis in rats exposed to simGCRsim suggests the potential for the development of these two diseases following prolonged spaceflight, as noted in the NASA Twin Study [ 44 ]. An increase in the VAF for the TET2 gene was present after an astronaut, 52 years of age, living aboard the International Space Station, returned to Earth [ 50 ]. With only one subject, it is not possible to determine if this VAF increase was due to time in space or changes in age. Some other increases developed over time in VAF in two separate gene variants for DNMT3A in the twin brother living on Earth [ 50 ]. The twin brother had also been an astronaut and may have developed VAF’s due to time in space, or each could be age-related. These findings indicate increases in VAF are not specific to spaceflight and may be induced by age alone. In 14 astronauts with a median age of 42, who flew 12-day Space Shuttle missions, gene variants were present in DNMT3A and TP53 [ 51 ]. Aging is CHIP's strongest known risk factor [ 14 , 29 , 52 ]. Our findings indicate gene variants indicative of clonal hematopoiesis arising after exposure to simGCRsim in rats are distinct from those in astronauts living in low earth orbit partially protected by the Earth’s magnetosphere. Ontological analysis reveals the effects of simGCRsim map to regions of 4 chromosomes mainly containing immune/MHC/RT1 genes, with additional smaller blocks in genes related to cell regulation, extracellular matrix signaling, DNA repair and cytoskeleton that can lead to persistent adverse health effects. We recognize that these data are for a single time point in three subjects and that further study will be needed to ascertain the significance of this observation. Our observations of variant alleles associated with CHIP in this rat study are intriguing, however, a more detailed time-course study with pre-and post-irradiation samples and longitudinal follow up throughout the observation period would provide more rigorous comparisons. CHIP has been linked to an approximately 1.9 times higher risk of coronary heart disease and a fourfold greater risk of early-onset myocardial infarction [ 14 ]. Aging is the strongest known risk factor for CHIP [ 14 , 29 , 52 ]. CHIP carriers have ~ 2x the relative risk of cardiovascular disease as non-CHIP carriers [ 14 , 29 ]. CHIP carriers > 50 years old have an almost 4-fold increase in cardiovascular risk. Thus, middle-aged astronauts exposed to GCR during exploratory missions are at increased risk of developing CHIP. In the present study, exposure to the spaceflight stressors simGCRsim and HLU alone and in combination triggered systemic alterations in serum calcium levels 240 days of reloading after 30 days of HLU. However, feedback systems tightly managed these shifts, keeping calcium homeostasis stable and within the normal physiological range. In an astronaut study, exposure to microgravity has been linked to accelerated bone breakdown and increased urinary calcium loss, leading to a negative calcium balance that progressively decreases bone mineral density [ 53 ]. Even with physical and nutritional countermeasures present in astronauts during microgravity, sustained decreases in blood calcium occurred 360 days after returning to Earth [ 4 ]. In an astronaut study, parathyroid hormone levels decreased 360 days after astronauts returned to Earth. In our rat study, calcium levels decreased after exposure to 1.5 Gy simGCRsim with HLU and 1.5 Gy simGCRsim with no HLU compared with controls intermittently over the 270-day study period. The present study shows serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 decreased after exposure to simGCRsim and HLU despite inclusion of vitamin D 3 in the standard rat chow (2.4 IU/g). Serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 are a primary indicator of vitamin intake, absorption, and stores, reflecting vitamin D 3 produced endogenously and that obtained from foods and supplements [ 54 ]. In serum, 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 has a half-life of 15 days [ 28 ], so the persistent decreases observed in the present study may reflect a chronic defect in vitamin D intake/stores and/or hepatic conversion of vitamin D 3 to 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 . Vitamin D 3 plays a critical role in calcium metabolism. The risk of vitamin D 3 insufficiency or deficiency may become high for astronauts during exploratory missions because they could lack ultraviolet light exposure during transit to and from Mars and in the surface habitat, and may have an insufficient dietary supply and/or absorption of vitamin D. Four- to 6-months of space flight aboard the International Space Station decreased 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 by 25% from the pre-flight level of 63 ± 16 nmol/L to 48 ± 20 nmol/L, despite the provision of supplemental oral vitamin D 3 (400 IU/day, [ 55 ]. Low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 has been suggested as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease [ 56 , 57 ]. Reduced serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 has been identified during HLU in rats [ 58 ], and as with human spaceflight, calcium metabolism is known to be affected by reduced weight bearing in rodents. Further studies of calcium and vitamin D are needed to understand the underlying mechanism and to develop countermeasures to maintain calcium homeostasis during and after deep space exploratory missions. Our study provides insight into whether synergy or antagonism exists between the two spaceflight stressors, simGCRsim and HLU, and how this impacts outcomes. Increased levels of lymphoid cells in the blood in sham-irradiated rats with HLU are abolished by simGCRsim and HLU. simGCRsim no HLU has no effect on levels of the immune cells measured. These findings suggest antagonism between the two spaceflight stressors. Exposure to 1.5 Gy of simGCRsim with or without HLU increased cardiac perivascular collagen content compared to sham-irradiated no HLU rats after 270 days. The extent of cardiac fibrosis present in the two irradiated groups could not be distinguished, indicating no interaction between the two space flight stressors. Thus, the expected interaction between the two spaceflight stressors on cardiac fibrosis was not evident in the dosing conditions employed here. Sham irradiation with HLU decreased cytokines in blood compared with sham-irradiation without HLU that was inhibited by simGCRsim with HLU, indicating an interaction between spaceflight stressors. These findings suggest a limited interaction between these two spaceflight stressors on the immune system, cardiac perivascular fibrosis, and cytokines that require further study. There are limitations to our study and its interpretation. The time course of tissue (heart and spleen) and blood (immune cells) changes after simGCRsim, and HLU was not determined. Future studies are needed to determine how these risks will change (e.g., cyclical, linear or exponential) the immune system over time. The genomic analysis is preliminary and conducted at one time point in a limited number of animals and without baseline samples on individual subjects prior to any exposures. There was no gene sequencing for HLU as this does not alter the fundamental DNA sequence in rats -its effects are epigenetic and transcriptomic [ 59 , 60 ]. In the NASA Twins Study, a specific group of genes—totaling 811 across multiple cell types—failed to revert to their preflight expression levels within six months after return to Earth. Many of these genes are involved in immune responses and DNA repair, suggesting that long-duration spaceflight may induce prolonged alterations in gene regulation [ 44 ]. These findings suggest astronauts conducting exploratory class missions could experience risks for immunological stress [ 44 ]. The five-ion six-beam simGCRsim incompletely represents the GCR spectrum to which astronauts will be exposed during exploratory missions. A 33-ion sequential beam galactic cosmic ray analog is now available for future studies as warranted [ 3 ]. In the present study, simGCRsim was delivered as a single acute exposure at the highest dose rates achievable. This experimental design was chosen for logistical feasibility however, it does not fully replicate the chronic, low-dose rate environment experienced by astronauts during long-duration deep space missions. The biological consequences of radiation are highly dependent on the temporal distribution of the dose. In the laboratory setting, the delivery of the total dose within minutes precludes the influence of real-time DNA repair mechanisms and cellular recovery processes that occur during protracted exposures, as well as the possibility that some DNA repair mechanisms may fail to be engaged at very low dose-rates. Acute models may fail to capture persistent signaling or chronic inflammation that would occur during an extended exploration mission. To make our findings more relevant to astronaut health, future studies on dose fractionation are needed to determine if this would affect cardiovascular outcomes. In our prior study in WAG/RijCmcr rats, we found that whole body irradiation with 1.5 Gy of a three-ion beam grouping did not result in cardiac mechanical function using echocardiography [ 2 ]. Based on these findings, we elected not to conduct a functional cardiac assessment for the current study. Thus, cardiac perivascular fibrosis was the only direct measure of cardiac structure in the current study. Furthermore, the present study did not evaluate other cytokines, immune cells, or organs besides the heart and spleen. Last, the present study did not address the challenge of countermeasure development capable of protecting astronauts from the adverse effects of prolonged exposure to space radiation and microgravity. The findings from the present study provide the rationale for future studies to address these limitations. Of note, the development of cardiac fibrosis, a hallmark of radiation injury, after exposure to components of space radiation, has been observed in the mouse after 22 months [ 61 ], but not in the Long Evans rat after 12 months [ 62 ], mouse after 9 months [ 11 ], and rabbit after 12 months [ 63 ]. In addition to rodent models, a larger higher order animal model is needed to investigate the combined effects of HLU and simGCRsim on the risk for chronic cellular dysfunction in multiple biological systems including cardiovascular, central nervous and immune systems and vision. Minipigs are a well characterized animal model with anatomical, physiological and biochemical similarities to humans. Their size also allows for studies on dose depth relationships and volume effects to help understand radiation exposure in astronauts. The astronaut corps includes both men and women; however, the current investigation evaluated radiation effects only in male rats. According to a policy issued by the ICRP in 2007, the use of sex-specific data is not advised for general radiological protection of occupationally exposed workers [ 64 ]. More recently, NASA requested that the National Academy of Sciences examine its radiation protection framework for deep space exploration [ 65 ]. In 2022, NASA revised its allowable spaceflight radiation exposure limits, applying the same standards across all ages and sexes [ 66 ]. Under these non-cancer risk guidelines, no modifications are made based on an astronaut’s sex or age. To better inform and validate these guidelines, future research should include studies in female rats and directly compare the outcomes with those observed in males. Follow-up studies are needed to explain the increase in immune cells in blood caused by HLU associated with no effect on immune cells in heart and the decrease in NK cells in spleen. Space radiation-induced bystander effects occur when cells directly hit by high-linear energy transfer GCR or SPE transmit distress signals to neighboring or even distant cells and tissues that were not hit with GCR [ 67 ]. This signaling may allow healthy, non-irradiated cells to exhibit DNA damage, mutations, and genomic instability. Bystander effects can significantly increase the total volume of biological tissue affected during long-duration space flight beyond what direct particle hits alone would reflect [ 68 ]. High-LET particles like iron and silicon, present in simGCRsim, trigger significantly more severe, long lasting bystander damage than protons, and may increase health risks for astronauts conducting exploratory missions [ 69 , 70 ]. It is conceivable that bystander effects may transform a “local” hit from simGCRsim into a “systemic” stress response within the heart tissue or other relevant tissues, potentially increasing the overall cardiovascular risk for long-duration missions. Future studies are needed to define the extent that exposure to limiting, low fluences of simGCRsim may cause tissue level responses relevant to exploratory missions. The present study shows the spaceflight stressors HLU and simGCRsim adversely impact multiple biological systems in the rat resulting in chronic cellular dysfunction that persists long after their discontinuation. Radiation results in cardiac perivascular disease at an exploratory mission relevant dose and alters the sequence of the genome. Simulated microgravity results in chronic activation of the immune system, and dysregulation of cytokines. Our findings are relevant to human space exploration as the effects of microgravity and galactic cosmic radiation may persist long after astronauts return to earth.
