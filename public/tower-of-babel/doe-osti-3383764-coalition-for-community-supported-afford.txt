Executive Summary
The Coalition for Community-Supported Affordable Geothermal Energy Systems (C2SAGES)
Budget Period (BP) 1 work focused on performing the technical and economic feasibility
analysis of a community geothermal system for a new affordable housing development in
Hinesburg, Vermont. The housing development is planned in two phases - Phase 1 and Phase 2.
The geothermal system is designed to meet 100% of the heating and cooling loads and domestic
hot water (DHW) loads of the development. The technical feasibility work included building
energy and geothermal network modeling. The energy modeling compared the proposed
geothermal system comprising of water-to-air heat pumps for space conditioning and water-to-

 3

for space conditioning and natural gas fired DHW. Cost analyses were done to estimate the

baseline and proposed geothermal systems for the Phase 1 and Phase 2 developments. While the
geothermal systems have higher installed costs, they showed significant potential for energy

The utility partner, Vermont Gas Systems (VGS), developed and evaluated multiple utility-scale
financial models, based on several ownership models: 1) VGS owning the entire geothermal
system; 2) property developer owns the entire geothermal system; and 3) split ownership
between VGS and property developer. The first and third models were found to be not viable and
the second model was not attractive to the developer. VGS evaluated a fourth model in which
DOE funding covers 90% of the geothermal loop and heat pump equipment, with the ownership
being transferred to VGS or the property developer at the end of the project. The benefits of a
DOE cooperative agreement can demonstrate to property developers, utilities, and
municipalities, the climate, economic, and community benefits of a networked geothermal
system.
The BP1 diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility (DEIA) work engaged communities and
local stakeholders to identify community-informed priorities and recommendations on the design
and deployment of community geothermal systems. Information on community geothermal
systems was shared through a project website, outreach to educational institutions, and
engagement with Community Group members via virtual meetings, in-person workshops, and
one-on-one meetings. Participants included residents, contractors, developers, government
representatives and environmental organizations. Insights from community engagement related
to priorities, concerns, and recommendations were shared with the project team for consideration
in project decisions. The high-level highlights that emerged from community engagement are:
 • A need for educational opportunities and information sharing about community
 geothermal projects, benefits, challenges, successful cases, and how issues are resolved.
 • A clear role for utility-led community geothermal projects, as there is a desire for the
 utility to provide installation and system maintenance.
 • Lack of available, qualified technicians was a key concern. There is trust that utilities will
 develop the workforce needed to maintain and repair the geothermal systems.
 • Combining retrofit geothermal systems with home weatherization, to enable a packaged
 approach where geothermal and weatherization financing are paired.
 • Affordable and clear financing options are a significant factor for home/building owners
 and developers when considering community geothermal systems.

Background and Introduction
Community geothermal systems, also called networked or district geothermal, utilize the earth’s
subsurface for heating and cooling multiple buildings through a shared underground distribution
network. While geothermal systems have higher upfront costs than other heating, ventilation and
air conditioning (HVAC) systems, their high efficiency, longer lifespans and lower maintenance
requirements can reduce energy and operating costs in the long run (Dandelion Energy, 2020). In
2023, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) selected eleven projects under phase one of the
Community Geothermal Heating and Cooling Design and Deployment Funding Opportunity
Announcement DE-FOA-0002632, to help communities design and deploy geothermal district
heating and cooling systems and create related workforce and training development plans.
This report describes the findings of the C2SAGES project under BP1 funding from the DOE
Geothermal Technologies Office (GTO). This project started in October 2023 and is being led by
GTI Energy, with partners Vermont Gas Systems, LN Consulting, Frontier Energy and National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The BP1 project goals were to perform feasibility
analysis and develop a complete engineering plan for a community geothermal system for a new
affordable housing development in Hinesburg, Vermont. The geothermal system was designed to
meet 100% of the heating and cooling loads and the DHW loads of the development. The
feasibility work included energy models of the buildings for hourly load estimates, coupled with
geothermal system sizing and a borehole plan. The work included physics-based simulations of
the geothermal network, including the ground heat exchanger, connection piping, and building
heat pumps. Network analysis investigated aspects of the thermal network design, such as piping
arrangements, ground heat exchanger sizing, pumping requirements and costs. Finally, the
project team developed a schematic design site layout and performed probable cost analysis for
the geothermal system, including operational energy costs.
This project focused on a 77-unit new residential development, called the Windy Ridge
development, in Hinesburg, VT. This development is planned to be built by Champlain Housing
Trust (CHT) along with Sterling Homes and Habitat for Humanity. The residential development
is planned to be a mix of townhomes, duplexes, and single-family units, with a significant
fraction of the homes intended for households with less than the area median income.
The Hinesburg or Windy Ridge development is planned in two phases, Phase 1 or South
Meadow development and Phase 2 or North Meadow development. Figure 1 shows the planned
Phase 1 and Phase 2 developments. Table 1 lists the different building types and square footage
of each building type in the Phase 1 and Phase 2 developments. It is noted that the housing
makeup changed after this report was prepared and submitted.

While community geothermal systems can benefit from diverse building types and load profiles,
VGS chose to focus their initial efforts on residential or mixed-use new construction
developments for several reasons. One key reason is the ordinances around natural gas in new
construction projects, which dictate that: 1) natural gas cannot be used at all, 2) used with a
carbon fee to the customer, or 3) 85% of the energy needs be met with renewable energy.
Geothermal systems provide gas utilities with an alternative to natural gas. Another reason is the
simplicity, with community geothermal systems for new construction projects expected to cost
less and be easier to execute than retrofitting existing buildings and communities with
geothermal systems, especially in the early stages of understanding how to implement
community geothermal projects. The financial model of how to bill customers is also easier to
develop when one is not relying on load diversity, for example, with a large source of waste heat
contribution. Load diversity is certainly desirable, but it does add complexity. A utility would
need to be confident that a heat generating facility will remain in a given location and generate
the same amount of waste heat for, ideally, the life of the system. A utility would also have to

 6

customize how to bill and compensate customers who are adding heat to and/or taking heat from
the system. VGS’ plan is to build knowledge and experience with new construction
developments and ultimately be able to tackle future retrofit projects with greater load diversity.
Regarding the specific Hinesburg development, VGS chose this project because the developer,
CHT, is looking to switch to all-electric systems and is seeking a cost-effective energy solution
for future residents. CHT has developed and maintains other buildings relying on air source heat
pumps (ASHPs) and has found the maintenance to be more expensive than originally thought.
Geothermal systems use less electricity than ASHPs and the cost of electricity to run a ground
source heat pump (GSHP) is expected to be lower. However, a utility will need to charge
customers for access to the geothermal loop to cover maintenance and as payback on the initial
investment. VGS's current goal is for the customer to be paying about the same to electrify with
GSHPs as ASHPs. GSHPs have additional benefits related to the electric grid. Liu et al. (2023)
noted that mass deployment of GSHPs can electrify the building sector without overburdening
the US electric power system and using geothermal heat pumps to electrify space heating in
buildings requires less electricity generation capacity than using ASHPs.

Full Engineering Feasibility and Technical Design
LN Consulting, with support from NREL, performed the technical feasibility and cost analyses
of the proposed community geothermal system and compared the results to a baseline system
consisting of ASHPs for space conditioning and natural gas fueled DHW. The geothermal
system is designed to meet all space conditioning and DHW loads. A detailed report developed
by LN Consulting is included in Appendix C of this report. This section summarizes the key
assumptions and findings of the analyses performed by LN Consulting.
Energy Modeling
The feasibility work started with developing energy models of the planned buildings. The
simulation program used for this project was Design Builder version 7.3, which is a graphical
user interface for the calculation engine EnergyPlus (v9.4). EnergyPlus™ is a whole building
energy simulation program that engineers, architects, and researchers use to model both energy
consumption—for heating, cooling, ventilation, lighting, and plug and process loads—and water
use in buildings. Energy models were created using architectural concept drawings of the
buildings. For Phase 1, actual architectural drawings were available. For Phase 2 buildings, the
exact floor plans were unknown and thus the project team used estimated floor plans, typically
used by one of the partner developers (Sterling Homes).

Proposed geothermal system: The proposed HVAC system utilizes a closed-loop ground heat
exchanger to source heating and cooling from the ground to supply the zone level water source
heat pumps (WSHP). Each residential unit has a zone energy recovery ventilator (ERV), while
the building common areas have a central ERV. Neither ERV has heating or cooling coils, only
heat recovery device. DHW is provided by a water-to-water heat pump (WWHP) that indirectly
heats storage tanks. Vestibules are heated by electric radiators.
Baseline system: The baseline HVAC system for comparison includes zone ASHPs. These are
assumed to be mini split systems or packaged terminal heat pumps. Each residential unit is
ventilated by zone ERV, while the building common areas are ventilated by a central ERV. None
of the ERV’s have heating or cooling coils, only heat recovery devices. DHW is provided by
direct fired natural gas water heaters. Vestibules are heated by electric radiators.

Thermal Conductivity Testing
Because the geology and subsurface conditions are highly variable, and because these subsurface
properties have a large impact on system performance and ultimately feasibility, it is common to
perform thermal response tests (TRTs) to estimate the ground properties needed to support
geothermal design work. To perform a TRT, a borehole is drilled and a borehole heat exchanger
(BHE) is installed at the site. After BHE installation, water is circulated in the BHE, heated at a
constant rate, and the temperature rise of the circulating water is measured and logged. TRTs are
used to estimate the effective conductivity and heat capacity of the subsurface, the borehole
resistance to heat transfer, and the undisturbed soil temperature. Additional details regarding
TRT background and analysis process can be found in Spitler & Gehlin (2015).

Given the climate in Vermont, the calculated cooling loads being higher than heating loads might
be contrary to expectations. There are two main anticipated reasons for this:
 1. Very well insulated and airtight building envelopes, which can dramatically reduce the
 heating loads and increase cooling load during times of high internal gains. The internal
 gains were based on an assumed schedule (occupancy, plug loads, lighting, etc.) and
 served to reduce the heating loads and increase the cooling loads.
 2. Geothermal design loads represent building condenser loads. The loads are from a mixed
 water loop that is connected to every heat pump in the community. Each heat pump has a
 compressor, which generates heat during operation and this heat is always added to the
 heat pump loop, during heating and cooling modes. The added heat from the compressors
 can be 20-30% of the entire loop load at any given time, potentially increasing the
 cooling loads and decreasing the heating loads by that amount.
Simulations were performed to estimate the borehole system size to meet the geothermal loads.
For both Phase 1 and Phase 2 designs, following the recommendation in the TRT reports, a lower
soil conductivity of 2.0 Btu/hr.-ft-°F (3.5 W/m-K) was used. The first set of simulations were
done assuming a 20-year lifespan to satisfy the Phase 1 network thermal loads while ensuring the
heat pump entering fluid temperatures of at least 41°F (5°C) in the heating mode or at most 93°F
(34°C) in the cooling mode. The temperatures were chosen to avoid freezing in winter and to
keep the fluid temperature entering the heat pump below the ambient air temperature in summer.
Figure 5 shows the fluid temperatures entering and exiting the borehole field. It can be seen from
the figure that the borehole field size was controlled by the minimum fluid temperature exiting
the borehole field and entering the heat pump during the first year of operation. The fluid

 11

temperature exiting the borehole system remained well below the maximum allowed temperature
of 93°F (34°C) over the simulation period.

 Figure 5. Phase 1 borehole entering and exiting fluid temperatures for the first set of simulations.

The second set of simulations was performed to determine the borehole field size to meet the
maximum fluid temperature exiting the borehole field and entering the heat pump instead of
meeting the minimum fluid temperature limit exiting the borehole field. These simulations were
conducted without any control on the minimum fluid temperature exiting the borehole field. A
field of sixteen boreholes, each 328.1 ft (100-m) deep, was found adequate to meet the network
thermal loads. Figure 6 shows the modelled fluid temperatures entering and exiting the borehole
field. The figure illustrates that in the first few years of operation, the fluid temperatures entering
and exiting the borehole field could be below or close to freezing in the heating period. The
figure also indicates that the maximum fluid temperature exiting the borehole field and entering
the heat pump in the final year of operation would be just below the 93°F (34°C) design limit.
These simulations helped estimate the range of required borehole field size to meet the Phase 1
geothermal loads. The results suggested potential network configurations with and without
antifreeze as the heat carrier fluid. Similar simulations were performed to estimate the size of the
Phase 2 borehole system for 20-year lifespan. The results are shown in Figure 7. The final design
had the fluid temperature exiting the borehole system just below 39°F (4°C) under peak winter
loads and slightly above 68°F (20°C) under peak summer loads.

 Utility-Scale Geothermal Business Model Concept
 As more natural gas utilities explore community geothermal as a path for decarbonized growth,
 several different business models are expected to emerge based on a multitude of configurations
 and approaches (e.g. new construction vs. retrofits, networked systems vs. stand-alone single
 building systems) and regional variables (e.g. state and local policies, state incentives, regulatory
 jurisdiction and processes, climate commitments). How geothermal ultimately fits into a natural
 gas utility business model is likely to be determined in a multitude of ways depending on these
 factors.

 16

VGS, the state’s only natural gas investor-owned utility (IOU), serves close to 60,000 customers.
VGS’s path to offering utility-scale geothermal is still very much to be determined; however, for
the past couple of years, they are exploring multiple paths to offering utility-scale geothermal.
The DOE C2SAGES cooperative agreement has aided in the acceleration of VGS’ learning.
VGS has chosen to focus on new construction projects with multiple buildings for geothermal
systems. VGS believes new construction is the lowest cost path (vs. adding geothermal systems
to existing communities) as well as the least technically complex approach. Like most gas
utilities currently exploring community geothermal, VGS has not been granted permission
through state regulators to pass along community geothermal costs to their natural gas rate
payers. Hence, a VGS community scale geothermal system must be a cost-effective investment
on its own and must meet the financial criteria of a fully regulated IOU. Most importantly, a
utility-scale geothermal system needs to be desirable to customers and provided at a cost they
deem acceptable.
The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) provides a robust geothermal tax incentive with the goal of
stimulating innovation and overcoming market barriers (e.g. workforce, drilling technologies,
market acceptance) to scaling the adoption of geothermal; a chronically overdue yet time tested
efficient clean energy solution. Under the current IRA and Investor Tax Credit (ITC) rules,
implementing community geothermal presents a combined social and economic challenge,
especially when there are diverse property development and ownership perspectives. An
approach building on a utility-model of providing thermal energy is likely the only way to
overcome the significant market barrier associated with community geothermal solutions. The
Hinesburg development reflects the complexity of new affordable housing developments
nationwide. The 77 new homes are a mix of rentals and sales, with three separate developers
building these units: 1) a nationally recognized non-profit affordable housing developer; 2) a for
profit market-rate housing developer; and 3) Habitat for Humanity, a known entity that creates
housing for those who cannot afford it under current market conditions. In addition, this land has
been permitted as “mixed use” with a childcare company developing a facility on-site.
If a community geothermal system were to be developed today to serve this project, the ITC, as
newly enabled by the IRA, would require a single entity to own the entire geothermal system
(meaning the outside ground loops and inside GSHPs) for a minimum period of 5 years. This is
based on multiple perspectives and their resulting ownership scenarios (multi-family rental
buildings, individually owned town homes, individually owned single family homes, a
commercial property). This is a significant market barrier to scaling geothermal that is
unaddressed under the proposed IRA/ITC rules. Dozens of natural gas utilities, US geothermal
associations, and industry players have petitioned for rules changes, specially related to split
ownership of the two components (outside ground loops and inside equipment) or as it’s referred
to third party ownership of the ground loop infrastructure.
A proposed practical solution is to enable utility-owned and operated community geothermal
projects rests in the ability to: 1) utilize the ITC and 2) allow a single entity to own the outside
ground loops (a geothermal utility) and multiple entities to own the inside equipment. This
model would replicate a utility model proven successful over the past 100 years. Hopefully, the
proposed rules will be adjusted to accommodate the third-party ownership scenario to unlock the
intent of the IRA provisions to effectively scale geothermal.

 17

The C2SAGES Feasibility Study allowed VGS, as an IOU, to explore ownership and financial
models based on the current ITC rules. In each scenario, VGS has a role to assist in overcoming
market barriers (e.g. first cost, comfort, and managing and servicing underground
infrastructure). With each model VGS explored, the following criteria were considered:
 • The monthly access fee VGS would charge residents using the loop should be
 comparable to what the residents would pay to utilize ASHPs (equipment costs,
 maintenance costs, and operating costs).
 • VGS would receive its traditional return on investment for owning, operating, and
 maintaining thermal energy delivery infrastructure.
VGS considered several different financial models, which are described below.
VGS Owns the Entire Geothermal System
For VGS to take on full ownership of the entire system, including ground loops and inside
equipment, in a manner that would be acceptable to shareholders and regulators, there need to be
two financial components of the business model.
 1. Ground Loop Model: VGS would employ a cost-recovery model for the ground loop
 mirroring how it recovers costs for capital investments today. A financial model would
 amortize the customer repayments, through a monthly loop “access fee,” across an
 investment horizon aligned with the measure life of the ground loop (30-50 years).
 2. Equipment Lease Model: The second component, required to recover utility investment
 in the inside equipment, would be an equipment lease model for a period not less than
 five years and not more than ten years. VGS is currently the only gas utility in the
 country with an existing equipment leasing program.
Pros:
 • Solves the ITC question; VGS could utilize the ITC
 • Creates a turnkey worry-free solution for customer
Cons:
 • OpEx is not cost-competitive with alternative HVAC solutions (ASHPs)
 • Equipment lease is prohibitively expensive
Conclusion: Without subsidization, this is currently not a viable pathway. Once loop access fee,
equipment lease, and service and maintenance on both are incorporated into the utility financial
model, the monthly charge per residential unit would total $365.83. The monthly charge was
determined by VGS based on their cost modeling.
Developer/Owner Owns the Entire Geothermal System
The developer/owner would own the ground loops and inside equipment. But to overcome the
property developer’s professed discomfort with servicing and maintaining the ground loops,
VGS would provide the servicing and maintenance through a Master Services Agreement.
Pros:
 • Developer can utilize the IRA non-profit provisions for the ITC which amounts to a 30%
 direct pay rebate
 • Because of the ITC, the developer’s overall CapEx becomes lower than alternative
 HVAC solutions (i.e. ASHPs)

 18

 • Ultimately, lower OpEx, lower energy costs than alternative HVAC solutions
Cons:
 • The developer is unfamiliar with the service and maintenance of geothermal systems.
 • Developer ownership of the inside equipment complicates the relationship with the
 buyers of the units that are to be sold.
Conclusion: This could be a viable pathway if the property developer/owner changes their
current stance and are comfortable taking on the responsibility of owning a community
geothermal system and if they are willing to tolerate the complications of owning the in-home
equipment (5 year minimum) in units they will sell. The ITC would essentially cover the cost of
the geothermal loop. Without utility mark-up, the total cost of equipment, as well as a service
plan on equipment and loop, would total a monthly payment of $59.66 per month which could be
charged independently or incorporated into the rent for rental units.
Split-ownership Model Between VGS and Developer/Owner
This model consists of separate ownership of the two primary components. The ground loop
would be owned and operated by VGS much like it owns and operates its gas distribution
pipes. VGS would employ a cost-recovery model for the ground loop mirroring how it recovers
costs for capital investments today. A financial model would amortize the property developer/
owner repayments, through a monthly loop “access fee,” across an investment horizon aligned
with the measure life of the ground loop (30-50 years). The HVAC GSHP equipment would be
paid for by the developer/owner, exactly as they would with any real estate development.
The resulting annual OpEx for the property developer and customers would look like:
VGS loop connection charge + electricity costs + equipment maintenance = total annual OpEx
Pros:
 • Aligns well with the core competencies of both the utility and developer/customer
 • Removes the uncertainty around care and maintenance of the ground loop; the utility has
 ownership responsibilities
 • Mirrors traditional utility and property owner relationships
Cons:
 • Non-trivial ITC is not leveraged due to current IRA rules regarding third party
 ownership
 • The resulting OpEx for the Developer/Customer is not cost-competitive compared to
 alternative HVAC solutions (ASHPs)
Conclusion: The cost-recovery utility model revenue requirements make this not viable for the
developer/owner, particularly since annual affordability (OpEx) is a prime objective.
Proposed Model with DOE Funding
This scenario assumed DOE funding to be utilized to cover 90% of the cost of both the
community geothermal loop and the in-unit GSHP equipment. During the cooperative agreement
period, VGS operates and maintains the external loop system. This model makes the following
key assumptions:

 19

 • The DOE provides the property to the recipients free and clear at the end of the
 C2SAGES cooperative agreement.
 • At the close of the cooperative agreement period, VGS takes title of the external loop and
 continues in perpetuity to operate and maintain it.
 • During the cooperative agreement period, the developer/owner is responsible for
 maintaining the in-unit equipment; at the close of the cooperative agreement period
 equipment ownership passes to the developer/owner.
 • During and after the cooperative agreement period, the developer/owner can opt to
 contract with VGS as the service and maintenance provider on all internal GSHP
 equipment.
Pros:
 • Overcomes property developer/owner market barrier of owning and maintaining below-
 ground community energy infrastructure (i.e. the external networked ground loop).
 • Provides affordable housing residents with the benefits of a community geothermal
 system at a rate lower than what they would pay for HVAC alternatives like ASHPs.
 • Provides qualitative and quantitative data on the performance and costs of a community
 geothermal system at minimal risk to affordable housing developers and residents and
 with minimal risk to VGS.
Cons:
 • Replicable and scalable business models utilizing third party (utility) ownership of the
 external ground loops are not immediately tested due to higher-than-expected project
 costs (e.g. use of subcontractors for drilling and ground loop installation).
 • There are additional measurement and verification equipment and labor involved with the
 system monitoring and quantitative analysis.
 • Increased developer/owner engagement efforts associated with the qualitative analysis.
Conclusion: The benefits made possible by a DOE cooperative agreement can demonstrate to
property developers, utilities, and municipalities the climate, economic, and community benefits
of a networked geothermal system. Implementing a community geothermal system and ensuring
the project provides an opportunity for the public to learn and grow more comfortable with the
technology and its performance will meet a need that was expressed multiple times by
developers and community members during the community engagement efforts in phase one of
this project. If community scale geothermal is to emerge and scale as an affordable and effective
greenhouse gas emission reducing strategy, the C2SAGES project would represent a national
working model for decarbonizing cold climate housing.

Social, Energy, and Environmental Equity Impacts
Geothermal heating and cooling can contribute to energy equity by reducing peak energy
demand and by having fewer maintenance needs than conventional HVAC systems. Geothermal
systems reduce the reliance on fossil fuels and contribute to lower greenhouse gas emissions,
which helps to achieve emissions reduction and climate targets (USGCRP, 2016). The proposed
community geothermal system at Windy Ridge would provide 100% of the residents’ thermal
energy needs with no reliance on fossil fuel. Electricity at Windy Ridge will be provided through
Green Mountain Power, an electric utility whose energy supply is 100% carbon free and is 68%
renewable. Geothermal energy has the potential for mitigating climate change and reducing the
risk of health impacts from air pollution arising from fossil fuel-sourced heating. Improved air
quality is associated with reduced risks of respiratory health conditions like asthma, acute lower
respiratory infections, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, as well as heart disease,
stroke, pneumonia, and lung cancer (Health Impacts, World Health Organization).
The potential for geothermal energy systems to increase the resiliency of the electrical grid can
reduce outages during extreme heat events, and the associated health impacts (Barreca et al.,
2015). By contrast, traditional air conditioners can exacerbate heat island effects due to cooling
down spaces by releasing the hot air outside (EPA Heat Islands). By using the ground as a heat
sink, geothermal energy systems can mitigate these impacts upon health and well-being (Murage
et al., 2017) and increase the resiliency of the electrical grid (Liu et al., 2023).

Community Engagement Highlights
The following highlights emerged from the engagement of Community Group members and
development stakeholders. The topics raised would guide future implementation and
communications with the public.
Dissemination of information about community geothermal:
• The need to provide education and build the public knowledge base on community
 geothermal energy systems was a predominant and recurring theme.
• Identify local champions among trusted, local partners who can speak to successful cases and
 address how issues were resolved. The history of communities being used as a testing ground
 can lead to skepticism.
• Understanding risk, benefits, and resources to support, as uncertainty about actual impacts
 can lead to a negative bias.
• Transparency in known failures. Community members wanted to know what issues have
 arisen with existing geothermal systems and what solutions are available to resolve them.
• Visibility of community geothermal systems can help to expand deployment. Successful
 cases may not be widely known due to lack of external visibility of homes and buildings with
 geothermal energy systems. Recommendations for signage or placards.
• Education on design to understand the design options based on both ground conditions and
 convenience preferences.
• Information should contain options relevant to different audiences, have interactive
 components, and be tailored to address community and building owner goals, such as
 comfort, cost, convenience, and environmental goals, which can differ between communities
 and for each building owner.
• Identify educational materials relevant for the second tier of potential technology adopters to
 reach those who would consider community geothermal if it was presented as a feasible and
 trusted option.
• Concern about what happens if a building owner wants to opt out. Community expressed
 interest in design options that allow building owners to opt in or opt out of proposed
 geothermal projects and still have a functioning system.
• There is a large need for initial investment and VGS is in the position with the workforce to
 serve that role in a utility-led model. The perception of a gas utility would need to be
 addressed for its evolving role in community geothermal projects.
Financial considerations include:

 25

• Affordable and clear financing options are a significant factor for home/building owners and
 developers when considering community geothermal systems where residents and building
 owners can easily see who is responsible for paying for installation, maintenance, recurring
 billing, and different types of repairs. Preference was for a utility-led model as a trusted
 leader to provide stability in pricing and costs.
• Disseminating information on cost and maintenance and expected return on investment
 profile. Given the longer lifespan of geothermal energy systems compared to traditional
 HVAC or heat pumps, community members were interested in trusted financial planning to
 aid decision-making.
• Noted barriers include start-up costs, which could be addressed by utility-led deployment.
• Building owners may have different objectives for financial responsibility and risk tolerance.
 Different options for cost profiles should be easily understandable.
• Affordable housing has different needs for initial investment costs, maintenance costs, repair,
 and end of equipment life decommissioning that would need to be addressed to provide a
 feasible option.
• When retrofitting existing buildings and communities with a community geothermal system,
 financial models that couple geothermal energy systems with improved weatherization are
 desired.
Workforce needs include:

Workforce Transition or Development and Training Plan
Developing the Evolving Workforce within VGS
In 2018, in advance of Vermont’s adoption of the Global Warming Solutions Act, VGS began
developing its own Climate Plan. In late 2019, the utility announced a commitment to reduce
greenhouse gas emissions for its customers by 30% by 2030, and to be net zero by 2050. VGS is

 27

currently in the process of a complete redesign of its legacy, natural gas business model.
Providing customers with decarbonized energy solutions is integral to the future success of the
utility. Building upon the already strong skillset of current employees will enable VGS to play a
significant role supporting the state of Vermont in achieving its climate goals and will provide
valuable gas utility employees with job security for many years to come.
Expanding the traditional gas utility services to include community geothermal builds on the
current skills of today’s employees and provides VGS with a business model serving customers
with decarbonized thermal energy. As VGS began the process of introducing employees to
geothermal technology a portfolio of job descriptions for roles within geothermal projects was
compared against job descriptions for current roles within the gas utility. Many commonalities
were recognized in the licensing, skills, and work experience required. This analysis provided
VGS with an understanding of where additional licensing and skill building will be needed as the
utility looks to expand its services to include community geothermal systems.
During BP1, VGS began training current employees in the design, construction, installation,
monitoring, and maintenance of community geothermal systems.
 • Three VGS engineers have attended the Certified Residential Geothermal Designer
 course provided by the IGSHPA.
 • Twelve members of the VGS Operations and Maintenance team attended a two-day
 geothermal overview provided by IGSHPA.
 • The VGS heat pump installation team attended a GSHP overview by WaterFurnace.
 • Interested employees toured the Eversource and National Grid community geothermal
 project sites in Massachusetts.
The current intent of these training opportunities is to equip VGS employees with the education
they need to inform the design of a VGS community geothermal program. VGS has researched
current geothermal workforce constraints in Vermont and interviewed educators, trainers,
designers, and well-drillers to discuss paths to engage new and existing workforce in this
growing field.
Workforce Supporting Community Geothermal at Windy Ridge
CHT has identified firms to develop the geothermal and mechanical system designs, and for
borehole drilling, installation of horizontal piping, and GSHP installation. VGS will provide
oversight over installation and maintenance of the geothermal network loop and GSHP, and
perform the following work:
 • VGS will install 10% horizontal piping, as a training exercise.
 • VGS HVAC technicians will install 10% of interior equipment, as a training exercise.
 • VGS will perform long-term system monitoring, service and maintenance.
The Windy Ridge development will provide VGS technicians and operations and maintenance
staff with hands-on opportunities to learn about the design, installation, and maintenance of
geothermal systems. VGS plans to work with WaterFurnace (a GSHP technology provider) and
the IGSHPA to train employees on both the WaterFurnace monitoring software (Symphony) and
service and maintenance of both community geothermal loops and GSHP equipment. VGS will
evaluate the cost of outsourcing community geothermal system installation and maintenance and

will determine where an in-house and more vertically integrated approach could serve to

Growing the Workforce of the Future
VGS recruits from several educational institutions and job training organizations in Vermont.
The programs that provide foundational education and training for the current needs of VGS are
also well positioned to provide add-on education and training to people entering degree programs
from the HVAC, plumbing, and electrical trades, and in engineering and design. By interviewing
this group, value was identified in embedding geothermal system information, training, and
career opportunities in existing education and training programs. There is opportunity to work
with the key organizations that are responsible for bringing new entrants to the workforce or
upskilling existing workers to take advantage of new technologies and opportunities. Initially,
the opening is for basic information in a format that would be of interest to students, both on the
technology of geothermal systems and on the job and career opportunities and requirements.
Similar to programs that incorporated solar and ASHP installation and servicing to curriculum
focused on the basics of construction, plumbing, and electrical skills, geothermal systems can be
added to educational and training programs as the next rising industry opportunity. This is
especially true if geothermal opportunities are presented as a parallel field that overlaps the
existing knowledge, skills, and abilities related to certifications needed in the other fields.
During BP1, VGS also collaborated with trainers and educators across the state to develop a
presentation sharing a high-level overview of job opportunities in the geothermal industry,
developed a one-page handout describing geothermal systems, workforce needs, and training
resources, and committed to presenting on geothermal job opportunities during upcoming
Vermont Works for Women and Vermont Adult Learning employee training programs.
There are a small number of well drillers in Vermont. VGS interviewed three business owners
who have been in operation for decades and have seen geothermal project numbers rise and fall.
All hesitate to invest in geothermal-specific equipment and instead, choose to invest in costlier
equipment that can drill both water and geothermal wells. With current state and federal tax
incentives for geothermal systems, they are seeing a healthy demand for geothermal today, but
they do not trust this demand to last. All expressed a desire to see longer-term, predictable
customer incentives for geothermal. Were a utility, like VGS, to move into owning and operating
geothermal systems, well-drillers would be more confident in expanding their workforce and
investing in equipment. The drilling company owners shared that there is a lack of training
programs and facilities nationally for well-drillers, but were confident in their ability to provide
on-the-job training to the right people. VGS also interviewed geothermal drill vendors and
identified geothermal-specific drilling equipment that is quicker to obtain, smaller, and better
able to navigate residential neighborhoods. Were the demand for geothermal systems to continue
to grow and then remain steady over a comfortable period, investing in this equipment may
become attractive to both the well-drillers operating in Vermont today and to VGS.

Summary and Conclusions
This report describes the findings of the C2SAGES BP1 work related to technical and economic
feasibility, geothermal network design, utility-scale business model development and workforce
development to support community geothermal systems. The BP1 DEIA work engaged
communities and local stakeholders to identify community-informed priorities and
recommendations on the design and deployment of community geothermal systems.
Key learnings from the BP1 work are:
• Soil thermal response tests and site modeling have determined that the Windy Ridge site is
 well-suited to a utility-scale geothermal system.
• While the geothermal systems have higher installed costs compared to baseline ASHP and
 natural gas DHW system, they showed significant potential for energy savings, lower OpEx
 and maintenance costs, and lower life cycle costs.
• The affordable housing developer of this property is enthusiastic about the possibility of
 geothermal as an alternative to their baseline model utilizing ASHPs and values the role a
 utility can play in the coordination and management of a geothermal project.
• The developer is not receptive to owning and operating a community geothermal system
 independent of a utility. The sentiment of this developer aligns with what was learned
 through interviews with a half dozen Vermont developers.
• The developer would like the guarantee that the utility will provide 24/7 maintenance and
 service of all indoor GSHPs.
• Broadening the scope of skills employed by gas utility workers to include owning, operating,
 servicing, and billing for utility-scale geothermal systems is achievable and would increase
 the current workforce while also providing gas utility workers with valuable skills they can
 draw upon as the gas industry declines over the coming years.
• A community geothermal project following the traditional gas utility financial model results
 in customers being charged a monthly rate that is not acceptable to an affordable housing
 resident.
• The community of Hinesburg is enthusiastic about the potential of community geothermal
 systems and are eager to learn from a system that can be deployed and operated.

Appendix D – Maintenance Plan
In interviews with multifamily housing and mixed-use property developers, Vermont Gas
Systems (VGS) learned that, although developers were open and enthusiastic about geothermal
systems, they all agreed they did not have the familiarity with the service and maintenance of
geothermal technology necessary for them to feel confident operating a community geothermal
system. The developers interviewed expressed a strong preference to have VGS be responsible
for the monitoring and performance of a community geothermal system.
VGS currently employs staff who repair natural gas lines, install and maintain heat pump
equipment, and monitor the natural gas infrastructure. These skills can lend themselves to the
monitoring, servicing, and maintenance of geothermal infrastructure and equipment. With no
individual company providing a comparable service in Vermont, VGS researched geothermal
service and maintenance best practices across North America. Drawing from the existing
skillsets within VGS and the design of the VGS equipment service plan for natural gas and
electric heating and cooling equipment currently offered, VGS drafted a service and maintenance
plan best suited to meet the needs of the customer and ensure the performance and long life of
the geothermal system.
VGS shall provide the following services to the Customer: 1) Ground Loop & Pumps Preventive
Maintenance, System Monitoring, and Emergency Response, and 2) Inside Equipment Service
Contract. Additional service details for Phase 1, South Meadow development are listed below.
Repairs and/or replacement of equipment will be billed on a time and material basis.
Preventive Maintenance, System Monitoring, and Emergency Response:
Annual maintenance
  • Complete annual maintenance steps as outlined in the heat pump manufacturer’s O & M
 manual.
  • Test the ground heat exchanger fluid annually for antifreeze concentration, inhibitor
 concentration, and pH level. Compare to the commissioning sheet at startup and the
 minimum requirements in the job specifications.
  • Test the ground heat exchanger every 3 years for the items in step 2, plus testing for
 water hardness, iron, and total dissolved solids. Compare to the commissioning sheet at
 startup and the minimum requirements in the job specifications.
  • Annual on-site inspections of bore field, service entrance to building, and pumps.
Annual Cost: South Meadow is $3500.00
