The impact of leadership, social interaction, and support on subjective well-being among employees in a Malaysian public university: the mediating role of self-efficacy
Authors: Najah Afina Mohd. Harun, Muhammad Idris Bullare, Walton Wider, Changhe Wu, Yong Xu, Manping Qin, Noramin Bin Mohd Nor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined the relationships among leadership, workplace social interaction, perceived support, self-efficacy, and subjective well-being (SWB) among employees in a Malaysian public university. Grounded primarily in Social Cognitive Theory, the study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-method design. Quantitative data were collected from 522 academic and non-academic staff and analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), followed by semi-structured interviews with eight participants. The findings revealed that perceived support positively influenced self-efficacy, whereas leadership demonstrated a significant negative relationship with self-efficacy. Workplace social interaction did not significantly influence self-efficacy. Self-efficacy significantly predicted SWB and mediated the relationships between leadership, perceived support, and SWB. Qualitative findings indicated that supportive leadership enhanced well-being, whereas excessive monitoring, unclear expectations, and workload pressures undermined employees’ confidence and psychological functioning. Overall, the findings suggest that organizational influences on SWB within higher education environments may be context-dependent rather than uniformly positive.

Introduction

Employee well-being is a broad and multidimensional construct encompassing individuals’ emotional, psychological, and social functioning within both work and life domains. Within this broader concept, subjective well-being (SWB) refers specifically to individuals’ cognitive and affective evaluations of their lives, including life satisfaction and emotional experiences. Unlike objective indicators of well-being, SWB emphasizes how individuals perceive, interpret, and experience their life circumstances1,2. This perceptual dimension is particularly relevant in organizational settings because employees exposed to similar workplace conditions may experience and evaluate those conditions differently. Consequently, understanding SWB is essential for explaining how workplace environments influence employees’ psychological functioning, engagement, and overall quality of working life3.

Employee SWB has become an increasingly important concern within higher education institutions (HEIs), where academic and professional staff operate in highly demanding and rapidly changing environments. Globally, universities have undergone substantial structural transformation driven by intensified international competition, research productivity expectations, digitalization of teaching and learning, and heightened institutional accountability4,5. These changes have significantly altered the nature of academic work, resulting in increasing workloads, role ambiguity, emotional exhaustion, technostress, and sustained performance pressure6–8. Academic staff are now frequently expected to simultaneously fulfill teaching, research, publication, grant acquisition, administrative, supervision, and community engagement responsibilities, often under constrained institutional resources and continuous performance monitoring9,10.

Recent evidence indicates that university employees increasingly experience stress, burnout, declining job satisfaction, and psychological strain associated with escalating work demands11,12. Studies have shown that many academics routinely work beyond formal working hours to meet institutional expectations related to publication targets, grant applications, student supervision, and administrative responsibilities13. Emerging research further suggests that digitalization, the demands of online teaching, and blended learning environments have intensified work-related anxiety and adversely affected university employees’ well-being14,15. In addition, psychosocial risks such as job insecurity, insufficient recognition, limited organizational support, and unclear role expectations have been associated with deteriorating well-being and increased turnover intentions among university employees16–18. These challenges reflect not merely temporary occupational stressors but broader structural shifts within contemporary higher education systems, raising concerns about the long-term sustainability of academic careers and workforce well-being9,19.

The issue is particularly salient in Malaysian HEIs, which operate in highly structured, performance-driven environments. Malaysian universities increasingly emphasize key performance indicators (KPIs) related to publication output, research grants, student supervision, teaching effectiveness, internationalization, and institutional rankings. These institutional expectations align closely with broader national higher education transformation agendas outlined in the Malaysia Education Blueprint 2015–2025 (Higher Education)20, and annual higher education statistics reports published by the Ministry of Higher Education Malaysia21. Simultaneously, universities continue to face increasing student enrolments, financial and staffing constraints, hybrid teaching expectations, and ongoing educational reforms following post-pandemic restructuring efforts22,23. These pressures are further intensified by hierarchical organizational cultures, centralized decision-making structures, and expectations for employees to perform multiple academic and administrative roles concurrently4,5. Local studies have identified workload burden, work-family conflict, institutional pressure, and work environment conditions as significant predictors of psychological distress and mental health challenges among educators in Malaysia22,23. Continuing expansion in Malaysian higher education enrolment and institutional demands further increases pressure on university workforces24. Within such environments, employees’ subjective interpretations of organizational conditions become increasingly important in shaping their psychological adjustment and well-being.

Within higher education settings, SWB is especially important because employees’ psychological functioning directly affects teaching quality, organizational engagement, collaboration, productivity, and institutional sustainability. SWB encompasses individuals’ evaluations of life satisfaction, the presence of positive emotional experiences, and the relative absence of negative emotions1,2. In universities, where work is intellectually demanding, socially interactive, and emotionally intensive, employees’ subjective evaluations of their workplace experiences may substantially influence their motivation, commitment, and ability to cope with institutional demands. Studies involving university lecturers have demonstrated that resilience, supportive work environments, and positive workplace experiences are associated with lower burnout and improved SWB9. Consequently, understanding the organizational and psychological conditions that contribute to SWB has become increasingly important for HEIs seeking to maintain healthy and productive work environments3,25.

Among the various organizational conditions that may influence SWB, this study focuses on leadership, workplace social interaction, and perceived support, as these factors represent complementary dimensions of the workplace environment that are particularly salient within HEIs. Leadership refers to formal influence processes through which supervisors and institutional leaders direct, regulate, and shape employees’ work experiences, expectations, and organizational climate26,27. In contrast, perceived support reflects employees’ psychological evaluations regarding the extent to which the organization, supervisors, and colleagues value their contributions and care about their well-being28–30. Workplace social interaction differs from both constructs by emphasizing interpersonal exchange processes among colleagues, including communication, collaboration, trust, and relational engagement31,32. Although these constructs may interact within organizational environments, they represent structurally, perceptually, and relationally distinct dimensions of workplace experiences.

These three organizational factors were selected because they collectively capture the formal, interpersonal, and psychological dimensions of the higher education work environment. Leadership represents formal structural influence; workplace social interaction reflects relational dynamics; and perceived support captures employees’ psychological interpretation of organizational care and resource availability. Collectively, these dimensions shape employees’ daily workplace experiences and psychological responses within HEIs, where work is highly collaborative, socially embedded, and dependent on interpersonal coordination33,34. Contemporary research further suggests that supportive leadership and healthy organizational climates are increasingly important in mitigating burnout, reducing technostress, and strengthening employee well-being within digitally intensive educational environments8,14. Although other variables such as workload, compensation, and job demands are also important, the present study prioritizes interpersonal and organizational climate factors because higher education work environments rely heavily on collegial interaction, leadership processes, and institutional support systems that directly influence employees’ psychological experiences.

Despite substantial research on leadership, social interaction, and perceived support, existing studies have often examined these variables in isolation, yielding a fragmented understanding of how multiple organizational factors jointly influence employees’ SWB. This limitation is particularly evident in the Malaysian higher education context, where research on employee SWB remains comparatively underdeveloped and often fails to integrate organizational and psychological perspectives4,5. Furthermore, prior research frequently assumes that organizational factors exert uniformly positive and linear effects on well-being outcomes, potentially oversimplifying workplace dynamics within contemporary university environments. In reality, organizational influences may operate differently depending on institutional conditions, role expectations, technological demands, and individual psychological processes35–37.

In this regard, self-efficacy represents an important psychological mechanism through which organizational conditions may influence SWB. Self-efficacy refers to individuals’ beliefs in their capability to manage tasks, challenges, and work demands effectively38,39. Employees with stronger self-efficacy are generally better able to cope with stressors, regulate emotions, adapt to challenges, and maintain psychological functioning under demanding conditions40. Recent research in higher education has further highlighted the role of self-efficacy in helping academic employees manage occupational stress, technological adaptation, and emotionally demanding work environments14,41. Within HEIs, where employees are frequently required to navigate multiple responsibilities and evolving performance expectations, self-efficacy may play a critical role in shaping how organizational experiences are cognitively interpreted and psychologically managed.

Although previous studies have demonstrated that self-efficacy mediates the relationships between organizational conditions and employee outcomes42–44, existing evidence is largely drawn from non-educational or corporate settings and often examines isolated relationships rather than integrated organizational frameworks. Some recent studies have shown that SWB itself may mediate the relationship between workplace conditions and employees’ psychological outcomes in university settings15. Consequently, there remains a limited understanding of how self-efficacy functions as a central psychological mechanism linking multiple organizational factors to SWB within HEIs. This limitation is particularly important in Malaysian university settings, where institutional structures, hierarchical cultures, and performance expectations may shape employees’ psychological experiences differently from those observed in Western or corporate environments.

Taken together, several important research gaps emerge. First, although leadership, workplace social interaction, and perceived support have been widely studied, research within Malaysian HEIs has largely examined these factors independently, limiting understanding of how multiple organizational conditions jointly shape employees’ SWB. Second, empirical research examining SWB specifically within Malaysian HEIs remains relatively limited compared with research in Western and corporate contexts, despite the unique structural and psychosocial conditions that characterize Malaysian universities. Third, insufficient empirical attention has been given to the psychological mechanisms through which organizational factors influence SWB, particularly the mediating role of self-efficacy. Finally, prior studies frequently assume linear and uniformly positive organizational influences on well-being, potentially overlooking the possibility that these relationships may be context-dependent and shaped by institutional realities specific to higher education environments, including digitalization pressures, performance monitoring systems, and changing academic work structures3,8.

This study, therefore, examines how leadership, workplace social interaction, and perceived support jointly influence employees’ SWB within a Malaysian public university, with self-efficacy functioning as a mediating mechanism. Using an explanatory sequential mixed-method design, the study integrates quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide both statistical evidence and contextual understanding of workplace well-being processes. Importantly, the study critically re-examines established organizational relationships within a contemporary higher education context, where leadership may simultaneously exert enabling and constraining influences, and where workplace interaction may not necessarily translate into meaningful psychological support. Specifically, this study aims to (1) examine the relationships among leadership, workplace social interaction, perceived support, self-efficacy, and SWB; and (2) investigate the mediating role of self-efficacy in linking organizational factors to employees’ SWB.

Underpinning theories

This study is primarily grounded in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), with the Positive-Activity Model (PAM) serving as a complementary framework to explain how organizational experiences influence employees’ subjective well-being (SWB). Social Cognitive Theory provides the principal explanatory mechanism, explaining how workplace conditions are cognitively internalized through self-efficacy beliefs, whereas the Positive-Activity Model complements this perspective by explaining how positive workplace experiences contribute directly to the emotional and evaluative dimensions of SWB. Together, these theories provide a coherent explanation of both the indirect and direct pathways linking organizational conditions to employees’ subjective well-being within higher education institutions45,46. Recent organizational and educational research has increasingly utilized SCT-based frameworks to explain how workplace support, leadership, and efficacy beliefs shape employees’ psychological functioning and well-being outcomes within demanding institutional environments41,47.

According to Social Cognitive Theory, human behavior and psychological outcomes are shaped through reciprocal interactions among environmental conditions, cognitive processes, and individual responses48. In the present study, leadership, workplace social interaction, and perceived support are conceptualized as environmental inputs shaping employees’ workplace experiences. Leadership represents formal organizational influence, workplace social interaction reflects relational reinforcement and collaborative engagement, and perceived support captures employees’ psychological evaluation of organizational care and resource availability. Self-efficacy functions as the central cognitive mechanism through which these organizational conditions influence subjective well-being, while SWB represents the psychological outcome of these interactions. From this perspective, workplace environments do not directly determine employees’ well-being. Rather, organizational conditions influence how employees cognitively interpret workplace demands, evaluate their capabilities, regulate effort, and cope with stressors. Recent studies grounded in SCT and Social Cognitive Career Theory further support the argument that organizational support, social interaction, and leadership influence employees’ well-being through cognitive appraisal processes and efficacy-related beliefs46,49.

Self-efficacy refers to individuals’ beliefs in their capability to manage tasks, challenges, and workplace demands effectively. Employees with stronger self-efficacy are generally better able to adapt to stressful conditions, regulate emotional responses, maintain motivation, and sustain psychological functioning under demanding circumstances40,50. In contrast, lower self-efficacy is often associated with heightened stress, reduced confidence, and poorer psychological adjustment. Recent evidence from educational and organizational settings consistently demonstrates that occupational self-efficacy contributes significantly to employees’ resilience, work engagement, stress management, and SWB41,45,51. Accordingly, SCT provides a direct theoretical basis for the mediating role of self-efficacy because organizational conditions are expected to influence employees’ SWB through cognitive appraisal and capability beliefs.

Within this framework, leadership represents a formal organizational influence that shapes employees’ efficacy beliefs through guidance, feedback, task structuring, and performance expectations. Supportive leadership behaviors may strengthen employees’ confidence and perceived capability, whereas unclear communication, excessive demands, or highly controlling leadership practices may weaken self-efficacy and increase psychological strain34,52,53. Empirical studies within educational and organizational contexts further suggest that transformational and supportive leadership practices enhance employee well-being by strengthening efficacy beliefs, psychological resources, and adaptive workplace behaviors49,54,55. Leadership is therefore theorized to influence SWB indirectly through self-efficacy, whereby employees’ perceived capability affects their psychological adjustment and workplace well-being.

Workplace social interaction represents the relational dimension of the work environment and contributes through communication, collaboration, feedback exchange, and interpersonal reinforcement. Through workplace interactions, employees may gain informational support, social learning opportunities, and reassurance regarding their capabilities and performance31,56. Such experiences may strengthen self-efficacy by enhancing employees’ confidence in managing workplace responsibilities and navigating organizational demands. Research grounded in SCT further indicates that supportive social environments and collaborative interpersonal relationships positively influence efficacy beliefs, psychological adjustment, and SWB outcomes46,51. Consequently, workplace social interaction is expected to contribute indirectly to SWB by influencing efficacy beliefs and psychological functioning.

Perceived support reflects employees’ beliefs that the organization, supervisors, and colleagues value their contributions and care about their well-being. Supportive organizational environments may enhance employees’ feelings of competence, security, and control over workplace demands, thereby strengthening self-efficacy as an important psychological resource29,57. Employees who perceive stronger workplace support are more likely to feel capable of managing occupational challenges and maintaining positive psychological functioning. Prior studies have similarly demonstrated that perceived organizational support contributes positively to employees’ efficacy beliefs, subjective career outcomes, and workplace well-being across organizational and educational settings47,58,59. Thus, perceived support is theorized to influence SWB indirectly through enhanced self-efficacy.

While SCT primarily explains the indirect pathway linking organizational conditions to SWB through self-efficacy, the Positive-Activity Model complements this explanation by accounting for the direct emotional and experiential effects of workplace conditions on subjective well-being60. The Positive-Activity Model proposes that positive experiences, interpersonal relationships, and supportive environments contribute directly to individuals’ emotional states and life evaluations. Within higher education institutions, leadership, workplace social interaction, and perceived support may foster positive emotions, trust, a sense of belonging, and meaningful workplace experiences that directly enhance employees’ SWB. Recent research further indicates that positive workplace experiences, supportive leadership, and psychologically meaningful interpersonal relationships contribute directly to emotional well-being, engagement, and positive workplace functioning46,49. However, the extent to which these experiences contribute positively to well-being may depend on how employees perceive and experience them within their organizational contexts.

Integrating SCT and PAM, therefore, clarifies two complementary pathways within the research framework. First, Social Cognitive Theory explains the indirect pathway through which leadership, workplace social interaction, and perceived support influence SWB via self-efficacy, a central cognitive mechanism. Second, the Positive-Activity Model explains the direct experiential pathway through which workplace conditions influence employees’ emotional experiences and subjective evaluations of their working lives. This integrated perspective provides a stronger theoretical justification for examining both mediated and direct organizational influences on employees’ SWB within higher education environments. Furthermore, recent studies in higher education and organizational contexts increasingly support integrated theoretical approaches that combine cognitive, relational, and experiential dimensions to explain employee well-being and psychological functioning within complex institutional environments41,49,55.

SWB

SWB is a concept that encompasses an individual’s evaluation of their life satisfaction and emotional experiences61. It involves both the presence of positive emotions and the absence of negative emotions, as well as a general sense of life fulfillment or satisfaction2,62,63. This evaluation plays a crucial role in shaping one’s mental health and overall quality of life. High-strain employment, which often induces anxiety and depression, has been shown to negatively impact subjective well-being, reducing life satisfaction and increasing emotional distress64–66. This highlights the importance of understanding how individuals cope with and evaluate their circumstances to maintain or enhance their well-being.

Busseri67 explores the tripartite structure of subjective well-being, emphasizing its three primary components: life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect. He argues that these related components are distinct and should be considered separately when assessing an individual’s overall well-being. Busseri’s work suggests that subjective well-being is not a static state but a dynamic one that fluctuates with life events and personal characteristics68,69. Furthermore, Suar et al.70 investigated the predictors of subjective well-being among millennials in India. Their research highlights that subjective well-being is influenced by factors such as social support, financial stability, and emotional intelligence. Millennials, in particular, face unique stressors related to career uncertainty, social pressures, and economic instability, all of which can impact their subjective well-being. The authors argue that fostering emotional intelligence and social connections can buffer the negative effects of these stressors, thereby enhancing overall life satisfaction and emotional well-being71,72.

Hypothesis development

Leadership and self-efficacy

Leadership is conceptualized in this study as a set of behaviors through which supervisors provide direction, support, empowerment, and psychological guidance to employees, particularly through transformational and supportive leadership practices52,53. Such leadership behaviors play a crucial role in shaping employees’ psychological resources, motivation, and work-related cognitions. Drawing on Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), self-efficacy is understood as an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully perform tasks and manage workplace challenges, influenced not only by personal accomplishments but also by social and environmental factors48.

Within organizational settings, leaders play important roles in developing employees’ self-efficacy by facilitating mastery experiences, offering verbal persuasion, providing performance feedback, and modeling effective behaviors. Leadership practices that provide clear guidance, constructive feedback, emotional support, and psychological safety contribute positively to employees’ confidence in managing work demands and occupational challenges. Furthermore, leaders who encourage autonomy, participation, and supportive communication enable employees to experiment, learn, and build competence, thereby strengthening efficacy beliefs and adaptive functioning within the workplace.

Within higher education institutions, leadership is particularly important because academic and administrative employees frequently operate within hierarchical organizational structures characterized by performance monitoring, institutional KPIs, workload pressures, and multiple role expectations involving teaching, research, supervision, and administrative responsibilities. In such environments, supportive leadership may help employees maintain confidence, resilience, and psychological adjustment under demanding occupational conditions. Conversely, ineffective or overly controlling leadership practices may weaken employees’ confidence and increase psychological strain.

Empirical research strongly supports this theoretical perspective, indicating that transformational and supportive leadership behaviors significantly enhance employees’ self-efficacy by reinforcing their sense of competence, control, and psychological empowerment34,54,55,73. Studies in educational contexts further suggest that supportive and transformational leadership practices positively influence employees’ occupational well-being and efficacy beliefs, particularly in demanding institutional environments characterized by complex work responsibilities and performance expectations49. In the context of Malaysian higher education institutions, where hierarchical relationships and leader-subordinate interactions are particularly salient, leadership is likely to play a critical role in shaping employees’ efficacy beliefs and psychological adjustment.

Social interaction and self-efficacy

Social interaction refers to the frequency and quality of interpersonal exchanges among employees within the workplace, including communication, collaboration, collegial engagement, feedback sharing, and informal social support. Within higher education institutions, workplace interaction is particularly important because academic and administrative work is highly collaborative and socially embedded, requiring coordination across teaching, research, supervision, and organizational responsibilities. These interactions serve as important channels through which individuals acquire information, receive feedback, exchange experiences, and obtain emotional reassurance.

Grounded in SCT and social interaction perspectives, workplace interactions are viewed as reciprocal processes that facilitate social learning, observational learning, verbal persuasion, and the development of psychological resources31,48. Through supportive interpersonal interaction and collaborative engagement, employees may strengthen their confidence in handling work demands, improve problem-solving capabilities, and enhance their perceived competence in managing occupational challenges. Frequent and meaningful interaction may therefore contribute positively to the development of efficacy by reinforcing employees’ sense of capability and workplace belonging.

Within higher education institutions, collegial interaction is especially important because university employees frequently work in interdependent environments characterized by collaborative teaching, research partnerships, student supervision, committee work, and knowledge-sharing activities. Positive collegial relationships may therefore help employees manage occupational stressors, technological adaptation, and institutional pressures more effectively. In contrast, fragmented communication, weak collegial support, or limited workplace interaction may reduce opportunities for social learning and emotional reinforcement.

Prior research has demonstrated that meaningful workplace interactions contribute significantly to psychological functioning, social connectedness, coping capacity, and subjective well-being74,75. Studies further indicate that collaborative and supportive workplace relationships strengthen employees’ confidence, adaptive functioning, and psychological adjustment within complex organizational environments56,76. Such interactions are particularly important in higher education institutions, where teamwork, collegial collaboration, and knowledge sharing are central to organizational functioning. These interactions provide employees with informational, emotional, and relational resources needed to navigate work challenges effectively and maintain efficacy beliefs.

Perceived support and self-efficacy

Perceived support is defined as employees’ beliefs regarding the extent to which their organization, supervisors, and colleagues value their contributions and care about their well-being77. This construct is grounded in social support theory, which posits that supportive work environments foster positive psychological outcomes by fulfilling socio-emotional needs, reinforcing individuals’ sense of value, and strengthening psychological security. Within higher education institutions, perceived support may emerge through emotional encouragement, informational guidance, collegial cooperation, and access to institutional resources.

According to SCT, supportive workplace environments contribute to the development of efficacy by reducing uncertainty, enhancing perceived competence, and reinforcing individuals’ confidence in their ability to manage workplace demands effectively48. When employees perceive high levels of organizational and interpersonal support, they are more likely to feel encouraged, valued, and psychologically secure, which enhances their confidence in performing work-related tasks and coping with occupational challenges. Supportive environments further reduce stress while increasing access to emotional, informational, and instrumental resources that strengthen efficacy beliefs and adaptive functioning78.

Within higher education institutions, perceived support may be particularly important because university employees frequently face complex work demands that include research productivity, publication expectations, teaching responsibilities, student supervision, administrative tasks, and institutional performance indicators. In such contexts, supportive organizational climates may function as important psychological resources that help employees maintain resilience, confidence, and occupational adjustment under demanding institutional conditions.

Empirical studies consistently demonstrate that perceived organizational and supervisor support positively influence self-efficacy by reinforcing employees’ belief in their ability to succeed and manage workplace demands effectively47,79,80. Recent organizational and educational research further suggests that supportive institutional climates contribute positively to employees’ psychological resources, occupational adjustment, and workplace well-being58. In higher education settings, where job demands are often complex and multifaceted, perceived support plays a vital role in enabling employees to cope effectively, maintain psychological resilience, and build confidence in their capabilities.

Self-efficacy and SWB

Self-efficacy is defined as an individual’s belief in their capability to organize and execute actions required to manage prospective situations effectively48. Subjective well-being (SWB), on the other hand, refers to individuals’ overall evaluation of their quality of life, encompassing both cognitive judgments, such as life satisfaction, and affective experiences involving positive and negative emotions. Drawing on Cognitive Adaptation Theory, individuals who perceive greater control over their environment are generally more likely to maintain positive psychological functioning and emotional well-being81.

Self-efficacy enhances individuals’ sense of competence, confidence, and control, enabling them to cope more effectively with workplace stressors and occupational challenges. Employees with high self-efficacy are more likely to adopt adaptive coping strategies, regulate emotions effectively, maintain motivation, and achieve desired work outcomes, all of which contribute positively to subjective well-being. Conversely, employees with lower self-efficacy may experience greater helplessness, emotional exhaustion, psychological distress, and reduced confidence in their ability to cope with demanding work conditions.

Within higher education institutions, self-efficacy may be particularly important because employees frequently encounter workload complexity, institutional performance expectations, technological adaptation, research pressures, and evolving academic responsibilities. Employees who perceive themselves as capable of managing these demands may be more likely to maintain psychological balance, positive emotional functioning, and adaptive workplace experiences despite challenging institutional environments.

Extensive empirical evidence supports the positive relationship between self-efficacy and subjective well-being across organizational and educational settings46,82. Recent studies in higher education further indicate that occupational self-efficacy contributes positively to resilience, engagement, stress management, and psychological well-being among educators and university employees41,45,51,83. In organizational contexts, employees with stronger efficacy beliefs are generally better equipped to manage job demands, maintain psychological balance, and sustain positive workplace experiences, resulting in improved well-being outcomes.

Mediating role of self-efficacy

Self-efficacy is proposed as a key psychological mechanism through which organizational factors influence subjective well-being. According to SCT, external environmental conditions do not directly determine individuals’ psychological outcomes; rather, their effects are mediated through internal cognitive processes such as efficacy beliefs and self-regulatory mechanisms48. Self-efficacy, therefore, functions as an important psychological resource that shapes how employees interpret workplace demands, regulate emotional responses, and maintain psychological functioning under challenging conditions.

Leadership, social interaction, and perceived support represent critical organizational resources that shape employees’ workplace experiences and psychological adjustment. However, their influence on subjective well-being is expected to be mediated by self-efficacy. Leadership behaviors that provide guidance, empowerment, and psychological support may strengthen employees’ confidence in their capabilities, thereby enabling them to cope more effectively with work demands and maintain positive evaluations of their lives55,73. Similarly, workplace social interactions provide opportunities for collaboration, feedback exchange, observational learning, and encouragement, all of which reinforce efficacy beliefs and, in turn, improve psychological functioning and well-being74,75. Perceived support further enhances employees’ sense of competence, value, and psychological security, thereby strengthening self-efficacy and promoting positive occupational outcomes47,58.

Within contemporary higher education institutions, where employees frequently navigate workload pressures, institutional performance expectations, technological adaptation, and complex role demands, self-efficacy may be particularly important in translating organizational experiences into subjective well-being outcomes. Employees who perceive themselves as capable of managing occupational challenges are more likely to maintain positive emotional functioning and higher levels of SWB despite demanding workplace conditions.

Empirical studies consistently support the mediating role of self-efficacy in linking organizational conditions to employee well-being outcomes34,46,51,73. Studies grounded in SCT further indicate that efficacy beliefs function as critical psychological resources through which workplace environments influence emotional adjustment, coping processes, occupational functioning, and subjective well-being. By functioning as an internal cognitive and psychological mechanism, self-efficacy translates external workplace conditions into individual subjective well-being outcomes within higher education institutions.

Methods

Research procedure and sample

This study adopted an explanatory sequential mixed-method design, in which quantitative data were collected and analyzed in the first phase, followed by qualitative data collection to further explain and interpret the quantitative findings84. This design enabled both statistical examination of the relationships among leadership, workplace social interaction, perceived support, self-efficacy, and SWB and a deeper contextual understanding of the psychosocial mechanisms underlying employees’ workplace well-being. The mixed-method approach was selected because employee subjective well-being within higher education institutions involves complex organizational, interpersonal, and psychological processes that may not be fully captured through quantitative findings alone.

In the quantitative phase, a cross-sectional survey design was employed to examine the proposed relationships among the study variables. Subsequently, in the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with a subset of participants to provide richer contextual explanations for the quantitative findings. The qualitative findings, therefore, served to elaborate, contextualize, and interpret the statistical relationships observed in the quantitative analysis, particularly regarding employees’ experiences of workplace well-being in a higher education environment.

The study was conducted at Universiti Malaysia Sabah (UMS), a public university in Malaysia. The target population comprised both academic and non-academic staff employed full-time during the study period. Staff on extended leave, employees with less than six months of service, and individuals who did not provide informed consent were excluded from participation. The total population consisted of 1,486 employees, including 971 academic staff and 515 non-academic staff.

A non-probability stratified convenience sampling strategy with proportionate allocation was employed. The population was first divided into two strata, namely academic and non-academic staff, to ensure representation across major employee categories within the university. Based on Krejcie and Morgan’s85 sample size table, the minimum required sample size was 504 participants, proportionally allocated to 278 academic staff and 226 non-academic staff. The stratification process ensured representation across employee groups, whereas the convenience component arose from voluntary participation and accessibility constraints during data collection. Due to organizational access limitations and remote working conditions during the study period, survey invitations were distributed electronically via institutional email and WhatsApp using staff lists obtained from the Human Resource Department. While this approach enabled broad institutional coverage across faculties, centers, and administrative units, participation remained voluntary rather than randomly selected. Accordingly, the sampling approach should be interpreted as stratified convenience sampling rather than probability-based random sampling, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings.

Data collection was conducted electronically using Qualtrics due to remote working arrangements during the study period. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential. Participants were informed that there were no right or wrong answers and that responses would be used solely for academic research purposes. To reduce evaluation apprehension and minimize common method bias, the questionnaire was structured into separate sections, anonymity was assured, and respondents were encouraged to answer honestly based on their workplace experiences. A total of 522 valid responses were obtained, exceeding the minimum required sample size and ensuring adequate statistical power for Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) analysis. The final sample included employees from multiple faculties, centers, and administrative divisions, thereby enhancing internal representativeness within the institutional context. However, the findings are primarily generalizable to higher education institutions with organizational structures and working conditions similar to those of UMS.

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the INTI International University Research Ethics Committee. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and adhered to the principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, or comparable ethical standards. Formal permission to conduct the study at Universiti Malaysia Sabah (UMS) was additionally obtained from the relevant university authorities and the Human Resource Department prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential. All participants provided informed consent before participation and were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

To ensure instrument clarity and linguistic equivalence, the questionnaire was prepared in both English and Malay using a back-translation procedure. The original English version was translated into Malay by professional translators, followed by independent back-translation into English. Discrepancies between the original and translated versions were reviewed and refined to ensure semantic equivalence and contextual appropriateness86,87.

A pilot study involving 30 university staff members was conducted prior to the main survey to assess instrument clarity, contextual suitability, and reliability. Results indicated acceptable internal consistency across all constructs, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. Minor wording revisions were subsequently made based on participant feedback to improve item clarity and comprehension.

For the qualitative phase, a purposive criterion-based sampling approach was employed. Participants were selected based on their SWB scores to represent both relatively high and relatively low levels of subjective well-being. Maximum variation sampling was used to ensure representation across both academic and non-academic staff categories. A total of eight participants, comprising four academic and four non-academic staff, took part in the interviews. Interviews were conducted via Google Meet, lasted approximately 30 to 60 min, and were audio-recorded with participants’ informed consent. Open-ended interview questions were used to explore participants’ experiences and perceptions of subjective well-being in the workplace within the university context.

Quantitative data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) via SmartPLS. A two-stage analytical approach was employed, involving assessment of the measurement model followed by assessment of the structural model. The measurement model evaluation included indicator reliability, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity, whereas the structural model assessment examined path coefficients, coefficient of determination (R²), effect sizes (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²). Bootstrapping with bias-corrected confidence intervals was applied for hypothesis testing.

Common method bias was addressed through both procedural and statistical approaches. Procedurally, anonymity and confidentiality were assured, participation was voluntary, questionnaire sections were separated, and respondents were informed that there were no right or wrong answers. Statistically, common method bias was assessed using both Harman’s single-factor test and the full collinearity assessment approach proposed by Kock88. Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first factor accounted for less than 50% of the total variance, suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to substantially affect the findings. Additionally, all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below the recommended threshold of 3.3, further indicating that common method bias was not a serious concern.

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s89 six-step procedure. A deductive coding approach was adopted, informed by the study’s theoretical framework and research objectives. Trustworthiness was enhanced through iterative coding, repeated review of interview transcripts, cross-validation of themes, and careful alignment between qualitative insights and quantitative findings to ensure interpretive consistency and analytical rigor.

Research instruments

Leadership behavior was measured using the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ-5X) developed by Bass and Avolio90, comprising 45 items assessed on a five-point Likert scale. The MLQ-5X assesses leadership dimensions associated with transformational, transactional, and passive leadership behaviors. Avolio et al.52 reported that the instrument demonstrates strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.82 to 0.87. The instrument was considered appropriate for the present study because it captures leadership behaviors commonly associated with organizational guidance, empowerment, and psychological support within knowledge-intensive workplace environments such as higher education institutions.

Social interaction was measured using a seven-item scale adapted from Chen and Huang31, which assesses communication, coordination, trust, and collaborative interaction among organizational members using a seven-point Likert scale. In the original study, the scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.894 for trust, 0.822 for communication, and 0.829 for coordination. The scale was considered suitable for higher education contexts because university work environments are highly collaborative and rely extensively on interpersonal interaction, communication, and collegial engagement across academic and administrative functions.

Perceived support was measured with a nine-item scale developed by Yoon and Thye91, which assessed employees’ perceptions of support from the organization, supervisors, and coworkers on a five-point Likert scale. The original study conceptualized workplace support as a multidimensional construct comprising organizational support, supervisor support, and coworker support, reporting internal consistency separately for supervisor support (α = 0.70), coworker support (α = 0.68), and organizational support (α = 0.60). In the present study, these dimensions were operationalized collectively as a broader perceived support construct reflecting employees’ overall perception of workplace support within the organizational environment. The instrument was considered suitable for the higher education context because university employees frequently rely on organizational, supervisory, and collegial support to manage complex academic and administrative responsibilities.

Subjective well-being was measured using the Subjective Happiness Scale developed by Lyubomirsky and Lepper92, consisting of four items rated on a seven-point Likert scale. The scale has been widely validated across diverse populations and organizational contexts. Lyubomirsky and Lepper92 reported high internal consistency across 14 samples, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 0.94 (mean α = 0.86), supporting its reliability despite its brevity. Although subjective well-being is generally conceptualized as encompassing both cognitive and affective dimensions, the present study employed the Subjective Happiness Scale as a global indicator of employees’ perceived well-being. The instrument was considered appropriate because it captures individuals’ overall subjective evaluation of their psychological well-being and life satisfaction.

Self-efficacy was measured using a 17-item scale adapted from Holcomb et al.93, which assessed individuals’ confidence in managing tasks, challenges, and workplace demands across different situations using a four-point Likert scale. In the original study, the instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.89 for technology self-efficacy, 0.91 for distance education self-efficacy, and 0.82 for self-regulation. The instrument was considered suitable for higher-education contexts because university employees frequently encounter complex occupational demands that require technological adaptation, self-regulation, and confidence in managing multiple professional responsibilities simultaneously.

In addition to the primary study variables, job category (academic versus non-academic staff) was included as a control variable. The job category was prioritized because academic and non-academic employees experience substantially different work structures, role expectations, workload patterns, and institutional performance requirements within Malaysian higher education institutions. Controlling for job category, therefore, helped reduce potential confounding effects and strengthened the robustness of the structural model. A pilot study was also conducted to ensure that all instrument items were clearly understood, contextually appropriate, and suitable for respondents within the higher education environment. The full research instrument, including all measurement items, is provided in the Appendix.

Results

Quantitative results

The quantitative phase of the study included 522 respondents, exceeding the minimum required sample size of 504 participants, as determined using the Krejcie and Morgan85 sample size table for the population of university staff. The additional responses improved statistical power and were therefore retained in the analysis.

Among the respondents, 274 were male (52.5%), and 248 were female (47.5%). Regarding job category, 286 respondents (54.8%) were academic staff, while 236 respondents (45.2%) were non-academic staff.

Participants were drawn from 17 faculties, institutes, and centers within Universiti Malaysia Sabah. The largest proportion of respondents came from the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences (13.6%), followed by the Faculty of Engineering (9.8%), the Faculty of Science and Natural Resources (9.0%), and the Faculty of Sustainable Agriculture (8.8%). Other departments contributed smaller proportions of participants, ensuring that the sample represented a broad cross-section of the university workforce. The Biotechnology Research Institute was excluded from the main study because it had been used for the pilot study.

Measurement model assessment

The reflective measurement model was evaluated using established Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) procedures, including assessments of indicator reliability, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity94. Because the study employed multiple established instruments adapted to the higher education context, particular attention was given to ensuring that statistical refinement did not compromise the constructs’ theoretical meaning and conceptual coverage.

Indicator reliability was assessed using outer loadings. During the initial evaluation, several indicators exhibited loadings below the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating insufficient contribution to their respective constructs. In line with recommended PLS-SEM practices, indicators with persistently low loadings were removed iteratively to improve the psychometric properties of the measurement model. In total, 39 indicators were removed across the constructs during the purification process.

The item purification process was conducted cautiously and guided by both statistical and theoretical considerations rather than purely statistical criteria. Specifically, indicators were removed only when they consistently demonstrated low loadings and when their removal improved composite reliability and average variance extracted (AVE). At the same time, indicators that were conceptually important to preserving the theoretical breadth of the constructs were retained where appropriate, even when their loadings were only marginally above the recommended threshold. This approach ensured that the refinement process maintained acceptable content validity alongside psychometric adequacy.

Following item deletion, the remaining indicators demonstrated satisfactory loadings ranging from 0.705 to 0.989, exceeding the recommended threshold of .7094. Several indicators with loadings slightly above the minimum acceptable threshold, such as 0.705, 0.739, and 0.748, were intentionally retained because they represented conceptually important aspects of the constructs that would otherwise have been insufficiently represented within the higher education context.

For the leadership constructs specifically, although item purification improved psychometric adequacy, some dimensions of the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ-5X) may have become less comprehensively represented following indicator removal. Nevertheless, the retained indicators continued to capture the core dimensions of supportive, transformational, and guidance-oriented leadership behaviors relevant to the present study. Similarly, for perceived support and social interaction, the retained indicators continued to adequately represent employees’ perceptions of organizational support, collegial interaction, communication, and collaborative engagement despite the refinement process.

Although removing a substantial number of indicators improved the reliability and validity of the measurement model, it may also have reduced the breadth of the construct’s representation. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with consideration of the trade-off between statistical robustness and conceptual coverage. Overall, the refined measurement model demonstrated satisfactory psychometric adequacy and provided a sound basis for subsequent structural model analysis.

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α), rho_A, and composite reliability (CR). As presented in Table 2, all constructs demonstrated high reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.849 to 0.979, rho_A values ranging from 0.943 to 1.001, and composite reliability values ranging from 0.903 to 0.982, all exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70.

Convergent validity was evaluated using the average variance extracted (AVE). All constructs achieved AVE values between 0.679 and 0.899, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50 and thereby confirming adequate convergent validity.

Structural model assessment

Following the establishment of a reliable and valid measurement model, the structural model was evaluated by examining collinearity, path coefficients, mediation effects, coefficient of determination (R²), and effect sizes (f²).

Collinearity among predictor constructs was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All VIF values ranged from 1.000 to 2.396, which were well below the conservative threshold of 5.00. These results indicate that collinearity was not a concern and that the structural model estimates were reliable.

The significance of the hypothesized relationships was assessed using bootstrapping with bias-corrected confidence intervals. As presented in Table 5, leadership significantly influenced self-efficacy (β = −0.350, t = 3.495, p < .001), supporting H1, although the relationship was negative. Perceived support significantly and positively influenced self-efficacy (β = 0.850, t = 5.603, p < .001), supporting H3. However, social interaction did not significantly influence self-efficacy (β = 0.036, t = 0.864, p = .388); therefore, H2 was not supported. Furthermore, self-efficacy significantly predicted SWB (β = 0.426, t = 3.029, p = .002), supporting H4.

The mediating role of self-efficacy was examined using bootstrapping procedures with bias-corrected confidence intervals. As presented in Table 5, leadership exerted a significant negative indirect effect on subjective well-being (SWB) through self-efficacy (β = −0.149, t = 3.046, p = .002, 95% BCa CI [− 0.198, − 0.050]), thereby supporting H5. Similarly, perceived support demonstrated a significant positive indirect effect on SWB through self-efficacy (β = 0.362, t = 3.254, p = .001, 95% BCa CI [0.100, 0.527]), supporting H7. In contrast, the indirect effect of social interaction on SWB through self-efficacy was not significant (β = 0.015, t = 0.866, p = .386, 95% BCa CI [− 0.013, 0.058]); therefore, H6 was not supported.

The mediation findings indicate that self-efficacy functioned as an important psychological mechanism linking organizational conditions to employees’ subjective well-being. Specifically, leadership and perceived support influenced SWB indirectly through employees’ efficacy beliefs and cognitive appraisal processes. The negative indirect effect associated with leadership suggests that certain leadership experiences within the institutional context may have weakened employees’ confidence in managing workplace demands, thereby reducing SWB. Conversely, perceived support strengthened employees’ perceived capability and psychological confidence, thereby enhancing SWB. Because the direct relationships between the organizational predictors and SWB were not examined in the present model, the mediation findings should be interpreted as evidence supporting the indirect psychological transmission mechanism proposed by Social Cognitive Theory rather than as evidence of full or partial mediation.

Job category (academic versus non-academic staff) was included as a control variable to account for potential role-related differences in workplace experiences and institutional demands. The findings indicated that job category did not significantly influence SWB (p > .05), suggesting that the structural relationships observed in the model were relatively stable across employee groups. The inclusion of job category helped reduce potential confounding effects associated with differing academic and administrative work structures within the university environment.

The explanatory power of the model was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²). The results showed that the model explained 66.2% of the variance in self-efficacy (R² = 0.662, adjusted R² = 0.661), indicating substantial explanatory power. In contrast, the model explained 18.2% of the variance in SWB (R² = 0.182, adjusted R² = 0.180), reflecting modest explanatory power. Although the variance explained for SWB was comparatively lower, this finding is consistent with the multidimensional nature of subjective well-being, which is likely influenced by broader psychological, interpersonal, contextual, and environmental factors beyond the organizational conditions included in the present study. Nevertheless, the explanatory power observed for SWB remains comparable to prior research on organizational and higher-education well-being, where subjective well-being is typically shaped by multiple interacting determinants.

Effect sizes (f²) were further examined to determine the contribution of each exogenous construct to the endogenous variables. As shown in Table 5, perceived support demonstrated a large effect on self-efficacy (f² = 0.894), whereas leadership demonstrated a medium effect (f² = 0.311). In contrast, social interaction demonstrated a negligible effect on self-efficacy (f² = 0.002). Self-efficacy also demonstrated a medium effect on SWB (f² = 0.222), indicating that efficacy beliefs contributed meaningfully to employees’ subjective well-being within the higher education context.

Overall, the structural model demonstrated substantial explanatory power for self-efficacy and modest explanatory power for SWB. Perceived support emerged as the strongest predictor of self-efficacy, whereas leadership demonstrated a significant but negative relationship with efficacy beliefs. Self-efficacy played a central mediating role in the translation of organizational experiences into subjective well-being outcomes. In contrast, workplace social interaction did not demonstrate significant direct or indirect effects, suggesting comparatively limited explanatory relevance within the present institutional context.

Qualitative results

The qualitative phase involved semi-structured interviews with eight participants, comprising four academicians and four non-academicians. The analysis revealed four overarching themes, namely Leadership, Social Interaction, Perceived Support, and Self-efficacy. These themes were consistently observed across participants, although variations emerged in the extent to which each factor influenced SWB. Importantly, the qualitative findings provide deeper explanatory insight into the quantitative results, particularly the unexpected negative relationship between leadership and self-efficacy, as well as the non-significant effect of social interaction.

Leadership

Leadership emerged as a highly influential yet complex factor shaping employees’ subjective well-being. On the one hand, participants described leadership behaviors characterized by empathy, inclusiveness, and respect for personal boundaries, which positively contributed to their subjective well-being. For example, one academician highlighted how supportive leadership practices reduced work-related stress and enhanced subjective well-being, noting that “we don’t have people disturbing us, especially our dean; he won’t ask us to do things after working hours on the weekend throughout the Movement Control Order (MCO) phase… as I mentioned earlier, our dean is now very supportive, and inclusive; he always asks for our opinions, which is beneficial for our well-being… additionally he is also very cooperative with everything that I seek for.” Similarly, an administrator emphasized the importance of humane and welfare-oriented leadership, stating that “he is very supportive in staff welfare, and his humanity is very high… throughout my service what I do, the dean never objected or disagreed.”

However, despite these positive accounts, a contrasting pattern emerged whereby leadership practices were also associated with increased pressure, role ambiguity, and diminished autonomy. Several participants reported being assigned tasks beyond their formal responsibilities, often without clear guidance, which contributed to stress and reduced confidence in managing their roles. One administrator expressed frustration with unclear communication channels and excessive demands, explaining that “although his responsibilities are extensive and require him to oversee every section and community, he should not limit his feedback to social media, as it often leads to unclear instructions. Additionally, he assigned me tasks beyond my position level, which has been somewhat burdensome.” Similarly, an academician described how excessive expectations and workload negatively affected both well-being and perceived capability, stating that “I was a deputy dean for two years, but it was challenging. I wasn’t happy with the workload, the dean’s leadership style, or the stress involved… my boss kept assigning new tasks non-stop, which only added to my stress.”

These contrasting experiences provide important explanatory insight into the quantitative finding of a negative relationship between leadership and self-efficacy. While supportive leadership enhances subjective well-being, excessive control, unclear expectations, and role overload may undermine employees’ confidence in their ability to manage tasks effectively. This suggests that leadership is a double-edged construct, with its impact on subjective well-being depending on how it is enacted in practice.

Social interaction

Social interaction was widely perceived as necessary to maintain continuity of work during remote work; however, its contribution to subjective well-being appeared limited and largely functional. Participants described interactions as largely task-oriented, focusing on coordination, communication, and problem-solving rather than emotional connection or social bonding.

For instance, one administrator reflected on the shift in interaction patterns, noting that “we prepare a chart like a customer dissatisfaction survey, then bring the topic to the meeting for discussion. When we work online, we no longer discuss with a large community… after COVID, the meetings and interactions still happened, but were not as clear as they were online.” This suggests that although communication channels were maintained, the quality and depth of interaction were diminished.

Similarly, an academician described the challenges associated with remote communication, emphasizing increased workload and fragmented interaction processes: “initially, communication was chaotic because it was the first time everyone worked remotely… the workload increased, and mental health and well-being were affected… when half the staff cannot attend a face-to-face meeting, we use WhatsApp… the meeting can extend over a whole day or even a week.” These accounts highlight that while interaction frequency may have increased, it did not necessarily translate into meaningful or supportive engagement.

In terms of trust, participants indicated that their confidence in colleagues was largely confined to professional competence rather than personal connection. As one academician noted, “In terms of professional relationships, our trust is well established… but there have been some limits… there’s some level of trust.” Similarly, an administrator stated, “Yes, only work matters… we see their skills and capabilities, so we trust that he can complete his task.”

These findings provide a clear explanation for the non-significant effect of social interaction on SWB observed in the quantitative analysis. Although interaction remained frequent, its instrumental and task-oriented nature limited its capacity to enhance emotional well-being. This suggests that not all forms of social interaction contribute equally to subjective well-being, particularly in remote working contexts where relational depth may be compromised.

Perceived support

Perceived support emerged as a consistently positive factor influencing employees’ subjective well-being. Participants described multiple sources of support, including colleagues, supervisors, and the broader organization, all of which contributed to a sense of security, belonging, and emotional stability.

Collegial support was particularly salient, with participants highlighting mutual assistance and encouragement. One academician noted, “I did receive support from my colleagues. They gave moral support and advice not to overthink… if anybody is lacking… we come to see how we can help each other.” Similarly, an administrator described how colleagues provided practical assistance during a period of physical difficulty, explaining that “my staff often asked if I could see the text… they helped me by reading documents aloud… they also assisted me with typing.”

Organizational support also played a critical role in enhancing subjective well-being. Participants reported receiving flexibility regarding leave, role adjustments, and institutional accommodations. For example, one academician shared that “the organization also gave me some time… for me to calm down and support/allow me to quit my position.” Another administrator described how organizational responsiveness facilitated personal needs, stating that “due to family reasons… I requested a transfer… I said I don’t want to be in administration… please let me join the faculty instead.”

Leadership support was similarly evident, particularly in situations involving personal challenges. One participant noted that “recently, I applied for a contract extension… he immediately took action after that,” while another reflected that “during my recovery period… the dean was understanding… he didn’t pressure me… I’m happy because he gave me space to rest.”

Recognition, although less frequently mentioned, was identified as an additional source of support contributing to positive subjective well-being. As one administrator explained, “I received an excellent leadership award… their appreciation for me makes me feel happy.”

Overall, these findings reinforce the strong positive relationship between perceived support and SWB observed in the quantitative analysis. The qualitative data suggest that both emotional and instrumental support are critical in fostering resilience and maintaining subjective well-being, particularly under challenging working conditions.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy emerged as a key internal resource influencing employees’ ability to adapt to remote working conditions. Participants with higher levels of subjective well-being demonstrated confidence in their ability to use technology, manage tasks independently, and navigate new work environments.

For example, one academician highlighted the role of technology in enhancing both capability and subjective well-being, stating that “thanks to technology during COVID… when we are bored or stressed, we can get many things from the internet… it helps you to communicate with students and deliver lectures.” This reflects how technological competence can support both functional performance and subjective well-being.

Similarly, an administrator emphasized the importance of personal initiative and adaptability in developing self-efficacy, noting that “I learned Excel on my own… it depends on our will… I learned everything by myself.” This suggests that self-efficacy is not only shaped by external support but also by individual motivation and prior experiences.

At the same time, the findings indicate that self-efficacy may be undermined when employees face excessive demands or unclear expectations, particularly under certain leadership conditions. This aligns with the quantitative finding that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between organizational factors and SWB and is negatively influenced by certain leadership practices.

Importantly, the qualitative findings not only confirmed the quantitative relationships but also revealed underlying tensions in workplace dynamics, particularly the paradoxical role of leadership and the functional, rather than emotionally supportive, nature of workplace interactions.

Discussion

The present study contributes to the literature on employee SWB in higher education by showing that organizational conditions and psychological mechanisms operate through complex, context-dependent pathways rather than through uniformly positive relationships. Consistent with SCT, organizational experiences appear to influence SWB through cognitive appraisal processes involving self-efficacy48. At the same time, the findings also align with the PAM, which suggests that workplace conditions may directly shape employees’ emotional experiences and subjective evaluations of work life60. These findings are particularly relevant in contemporary higher education contexts characterized by managerialization, KPI-driven performance systems, digitalization, workload intensification, and evolving institutional expectations4,5.

The leadership findings challenge the assumption that leadership is consistently beneficial for employee outcomes. Although leadership was positively associated with SWB, its negative association with self-efficacy suggests that certain leadership practices may simultaneously support employees while weakening their confidence in managing workplace demands. This negative relationship diverges from the hypothesized positive direction and from prior studies that often portray leadership as empowering, supportive, and beneficial for employee functioning49,54,55,95,96. Thus, leadership in higher education may operate through dual and potentially contradictory pathways.

This pattern may reflect the managerialization of academic work, where leaders are expected not only to provide support but also to monitor performance, enforce KPIs, oversee research productivity, and ensure compliance with accountability systems. Academic employees often manage competing responsibilities involving teaching, publication, grant acquisition, supervision, administration, and institutional service. Within Malaysian higher education institutions, which are often structured around centralized decision-making and performance-oriented expectations, leadership may be experienced as both developmental support and institutional control. Therefore, leadership may influence not only external work conditions but also employees’ internal appraisal of their capability to cope with institutional demands. From an SCT perspective, leadership is better understood as a context-dependent organizational influence rather than a uniformly positive predictor of SWB35,37.

In contrast, workplace social interaction was not significantly associated with SWB, which contrasts with studies that emphasize the benefits of collegiality, collaboration, and social connectedness for employee well-being56,97,98. However, this does not suggest that workplace interaction is unimportant. Rather, it indicates that interaction alone may be insufficient to enhance SWB in contemporary university environments. One possible explanation is that academic interaction has become increasingly task-oriented and digitally mediated. Online meetings, messaging platforms, learning management systems, and administrative coordination tools may increase the frequency of communication while reducing relational depth and emotional support.

This interpretation is relevant in post-pandemic and digitally intensive higher education environments, where functional communication may not necessarily foster collegial connection or psychological reassurance8,14. Although universities have traditionally valued collegial culture, intellectual exchange, and peer support, digitally mediated communication may weaken the emotional and relational value of workplace interaction99. Thus, the quality, emotional significance, and relational depth of social interaction may be more important for SWB than interaction frequency alone.

Perceived organizational support emerged as the most consistent organizational factor associated with SWB, aligning with prior organizational and higher education research100,101. Unlike leadership, which may involve both supportive and demanding elements, perceived support reflects employees’ broader evaluation of whether the institution values their contributions and cares about their well-being. In higher education institutions characterized by workload intensification, role complexity, academic precarity, and institutional restructuring, perceived support may serve as a protective psychological resource. Employees who perceive stronger institutional support may feel more valued, secure, and capable of managing complex work demands. This interpretation is consistent with studies linking organizational support to reduced stress and burnout in higher education settings16,18. From a PAM perspective, perceived support may also directly enhance SWB by fostering positive emotional experiences, a sense of belonging, and psychological security.

The mediating role of self-efficacy reinforces the central assumptions of SCT, which holds that environmental conditions influence psychological outcomes through cognitive appraisal and self-regulatory mechanisms rather than direct environmental effects48. The findings suggest that employees’ beliefs about their capability to manage institutional demands are important in translating workplace experiences into SWB outcomes. This extends prior studies showing that employee SWB is shaped by both structural workplace conditions and individual cognitive processes42–44. In higher education settings, where employees face publication pressures, technological adaptation, administrative accountability, and changing academic expectations, self-efficacy may be especially important in sustaining positive emotional functioning.

Overall, the findings support the argument that organizational influences on SWB do not operate through simple, linear, or uniformly positive pathways. Instead, their effects may vary depending on leadership practices, institutional structures, technological demands, relational quality, and employees’ psychological appraisal processes. This highlights the need to examine employee SWB within specific institutional and sociocultural contexts rather than assuming universal organizational effects across occupational settings.

Theoretical implications

This study contributes theoretically by refining the understanding of how organizational factors relate to employee SWB within higher education institutions. Rather than proposing entirely new theoretical relationships, the findings show that organizational influences may operate differently in university environments shaped by managerialization, accountability pressures, collegial structures, digitalization, and evolving work practices.

First, the study advances the integration of SCT and PAM by supporting both cognitive and experiential pathways to SWB. SCT is mediated by self-efficacy, indicating that organizational conditions influence SWB through employees’ cognitive appraisals and efficacy beliefs. PAM is reflected in the direct organizational effects, suggesting that workplace experiences may also contribute directly to employees’ emotional evaluations and psychological functioning.

Second, the study refines leadership theory by showing that leadership is context-dependent within higher education. While prior studies often emphasize positive leadership outcomes26,34, the present findings suggest that leadership may support employees while also weakening efficacy beliefs under demanding institutional conditions. Leadership may therefore be better conceptualized as a conditional organizational influence rather than a consistently positive predictor of SWB.

Third, the findings extend SCT by showing that self-efficacy is a context-sensitive psychological mechanism. Efficacy beliefs may fluctuate depending on leadership expectations, organizational support, workplace pressures, and employees’ interpretation of institutional experiences. Finally, the study contributes to SWB literature by showing that workplace social interaction should be understood as multidimensional. Its effect may depend less on frequency and more on emotional quality, relational depth, and psychological significance.

Practical implications

The findings offer several practical implications for higher education institutions, but these should be interpreted cautiously given the modest explanatory power for SWB (R² = 0.182). Thus, the implications should be viewed as preliminary rather than definitive predictive conclusions.

First, universities may benefit from leadership development initiatives that balance institutional performance expectations with psychological support and employee capability development. Leadership practices that emphasize excessive monitoring, unrealistic expectations, or performance pressure may weaken employees’ confidence in their ability to manage workplace demands. Supportive communication, realistic workload expectations, participative management, and psychological safety may therefore be important.

Second, universities should move beyond increasing communication frequency and focus on the quality of workplace interaction. Given the non-significant relationship between social interaction and SWB, institutions may consider strengthening collegial mentoring, peer engagement, collaborative support systems, and emotionally meaningful team interactions, particularly in hybrid and digitally mediated work environments.

Third, perceived organizational support appears to be a relatively stable and influential resource. Institutions may consider strengthening flexible work arrangements, mental health support, supervisory support, professional development, mentoring systems, and accessible institutional resources. Such support may help employees manage workload complexity and occupational stress.

Fourth, self-efficacy development may be useful. Skill development, mentoring, constructive feedback, manageable task progression, and professional capability-building opportunities may help employees feel more confident in managing workplace demands, especially in digital and performance-oriented academic environments.

Finally, institutions may consider regular SWB monitoring through workload assessments, employee feedback systems, or SWB surveys. Such initiatives may help identify emerging concerns earlier, although longitudinal and intervention-based research is needed to determine the effectiveness of specific well-being strategies in higher education.

Limitations and future research recommendations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted within a single Malaysian public university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other higher education institutions with different organizational cultures, leadership structures, resource conditions, and institutional expectations. Consequently, the findings should not be generalized across all Malaysian higher education institutions. Future studies should include multiple universities, including private and international institutions, as well as cross-cultural comparisons to improve external validity and contextual understanding.

Second, the study employed a stratified convenience sampling approach rather than probability-based sampling due to organizational access constraints and remote working conditions. Although this approach enabled broad participation across academic and non-academic staff groups, self-selection bias may still be present because participation was voluntary. Future research may benefit from using probability sampling techniques to improve representativeness and reduce sampling bias.

Third, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and does not allow examination of how organizational conditions and psychological mechanisms influence SWB over time. Employees’ perceptions, efficacy beliefs, and workplace experiences may change as institutional demands, leadership practices, and digital work conditions evolve. Longitudinal and repeated-measures studies are therefore recommended to better understand the dynamic nature of employee SWB within higher education settings.

Fourth, the study relied primarily on self-reported data, which may introduce common method bias and subjective response tendencies despite procedural and statistical efforts to minimize such concerns. Future studies could incorporate multi-source data, including supervisor evaluations, peer assessments, behavioral indicators, or objective organizational measures to strengthen methodological robustness.

Fifth, a substantial number of indicators were removed during the measurement model assessment to achieve acceptable psychometric properties. Although this improved statistical reliability and validity, item deletion may have reduced the breadth of construct representation and content coverage. Future studies should further validate the measurement instruments within higher education contexts and consider developing context-specific scales that better capture organizational experiences within universities.

Sixth, SWB was assessed primarily as a general subjective evaluation construct and did not fully capture its multidimensional nature, such as emotional, psychological, social, and occupational dimensions of well-being. Future research should consider multidimensional measures of employee well-being to provide a more comprehensive understanding of psychological functioning within higher education environments.

Finally, the present study focused primarily on leadership, workplace social interaction, perceived support, and self-efficacy. Other potentially important organizational and psychological factors, such as workload, work-life balance, technostress, emotional exhaustion, organizational justice, digital work demands, and coping strategies, were not examined. Future research should extend the model by incorporating these variables to better capture the complexity of employee SWB within increasingly digitalized and performance-oriented higher education institutions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that employee subjective well-being in higher education is shaped by both organizational conditions and psychological mechanisms. Leadership and perceived support significantly influence subjective well-being, while self-efficacy partially mediates the relationships between them. The findings highlight that leadership is context-dependent and may both enhance and undermine employees’ psychological resources. In addition, social interaction contributes to subjective well-being only when it is meaningful and emotionally supportive. By integrating Social Cognitive Theory and the Positive-Activity Model, this study provides a dual-path explanation of subjective well-being through cognitive and experiential processes. Overall, the findings emphasize the importance of supportive, well-structured organizational environments for fostering sustainable subjective employee well-being in higher education institutions.
