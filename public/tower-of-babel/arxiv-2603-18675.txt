The Lee-Yang property of isotropic vector ferromagnets and lattice fields
Yuri Kozitsky

Abstract
. The Lee-Yang property of a given spin model means that its partition function has purely imaginary zeros as a function of an external magnetic field. A similar property is also used in the theory of quantum anharmonic crystals and quantum lattice fields. A number of powerful analytic methods of the mathematical theory of such models employ this property. Its suitable generalization is used in the theory of models of isotropic D D -dimensional spins (rotors) or D D -component quantum lattice fields. So far, the (generalized) Lee-Yang property has been established only for two-dimensional isotropic models. In this work, we prove that isotropic spin and field models living on ℤ \mathds{Z} have this property for all even D D .

Introduction

The present research was inspired by an item on the list of open problems in mathematical physics recently published by Barry Simon in
[
19
]
. In Conjecture 9.2.27 on page 570, it is proposed to “Prove a Lee–Yang theorem for isotropic classical
D
-rotors for
D\geq 4
”. Here we prove this statement for all even
D
and a particular models of such ‘rotors’ and Euclidean quantum lattice fields.

In 1952, two physicists, T. D. Lee and C. N. Yang, proved a statement
[
15
]
, which turned out to be seminal for both mathematical physics
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
16
,
17
,
19
]
and pure mathematics
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
17
]
. Their original result describing the Ising spin model can be sketched as follows. Consider the variables
\sigma_{l}
,
l=1,\dots,N
– spins – taking values
\pm 1
, and the function

(1.1)

Z^{\rm Ising}_{N}(h)=\sum_{\dots\sigma_{l}=\pm 1\dots}\exp\left(h\sum_{1\leq l\leq N}\sigma_{l}+\sum_{1\leq l<l^{\prime}\leq N}J_{ll^{\prime}}\sigma_{l}\sigma_{l^{\prime}}\right).

In statistical physics, it is the partition function of a magnet, where
J_{ll^{\prime}}
are interaction intensities and
h
stands for an external magnetic field.
As a finite sum of exponentials, it can be continued to an exponential-type entire function of
h\in\mathds{C}
. According to the Lee-Yang theorem, all zeros of
Z^{\rm Ising}_{N}
lie on the imaginary axis, provided that the interaction is of ferromagnetic type, i.e.,
J_{ll^{\prime}}\geq 0
for all
l,l^{\prime}
. Later, this result was extended in different directions; see
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
16
,
17
]
,
[
1
, page 171]
, and
[
19
, Chapter 3]
, with applications in statistical physics and lattice Euclidean quantum field theory. In particular, the summation in (
1.1
) was replaced by integration, and the variables
\sigma_{l}
were turned into
D
-vectors. For
D=2
, an analog of the Lee-Yang theorem was proved in
[
16
]
, see also
[
19
, Chapter 3]
.

In this article, we study the function

(1.2)

Z_{N}(z)=\int\exp\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}z\cdot\sigma_{l}+J\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\sigma_{l}\cdot\sigma_{l+1}\right)\prod_{l=1}^{N}\chi(d\sigma_{l}).

In the language of statistical physics, the corresponding model lives on the graph
\mathds{Z}
, which means that
J_{ll^{\prime}}=J>0
if
|l-l|=1
, and
J_{ll^{\prime}}=0
otherwise. In (
1.2
), the integral is taken over
(\mathds{R}^{D})^{N}
,
D,N\in\mathds{N}
,

z=x+iy\in\mathds{C}^{D}
,
z\cdot\sigma=x\cdot\sigma+iy\cdot\sigma
is the corresponding scalar product
and
\chi
is a finite positive measure on
\mathds{R}^{D}
– single-spin measure – satisfying certain conditions. In particular, we assume that

(1.3)

\forall a>0\qquad\int_{\mathds{R}^{D}}e^{a\sigma^{2}}\chi(d\sigma)<\infty,\qquad\sigma^{2}:=\sigma\cdot\sigma,

and that
\chi
is
isotropic
, i.e., it satisfies
\chi(UA)=\chi(A)
, holding for all orthogonal transforms
U\in O(D)
and each Borel
A\subset\mathds{R}^{D}
. An example is the measure supported on the sphere
\mathds{S}_{r}\subset\mathds{R}^{D}
of radius
r>0
, the restriction of which to this sphere is a uniform measure. It corresponds to the aforementioned model of isotropic rotors. Our result is the statement that, for all even
D\in\mathds{N}
, the function in (
1.2
) can be written in the form

(1.4)

Z_{N}(z)=Z_{N}(0)\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\gamma_{j,N}z^{2}),\quad z^{2}:=z\cdot z,\quad\gamma_{j,N}>0,\quad\sum_{j}\gamma_{j,N}<\infty,

whenever
\chi
belongs to a subset of the set of isotropic measures on
\mathds{R}^{D}
, for which (
1.3
) and the following condition are verified

(1.5)

\widehat{\chi}(z):=\int_{\mathds{R}^{D}}e^{z\cdot\sigma}\chi(d\sigma)=C_{\chi}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\gamma_{j}z^{2}),\quad\gamma_{j}>0,\quad\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}<\infty.

The function
\widehat{\chi}
will be referred to as the Laplace transform of
\chi
.
The function of
z^{2}
that appears on the right-hand side of (
1.5
) is a Laguerre entire function of the first kind. These are polynomials possessing real non-positive zeros only, or limits of such polynomials, taken in the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
\mathds{C}
; see
[
10
, pages 8–23]
.
In the following, by
\mathcal{L}
we denote the set of all such functions. Each
f\in\mathcal{L}
can be written in the form

(1.6)

f(\zeta)=C\zeta^{m}e^{\alpha\zeta}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\gamma_{j}\zeta),

with
m\in\mathds{N}_{0}
,
\alpha\geq 0
, and the set
\{\gamma_{j}\}
(infinite, finite, or empty) that satisfies the condition in (
1.5
). In particular,
e^{\alpha\zeta}\in\mathcal{L}
, for all
\alpha\geq 0
. Noteworthy, for
D=1
,
\varphi_{\chi}(t):=\widehat{\chi}(it)
is the Fourier transform of
\chi
, which by (
1.5
) has only real zeros. The study of such functions – Riemannschen ganzen Funktionen – goes back to Riemann; see
[
10
, Chapter 3]
. In particular, verifying the famous Riemann hypothesis concerning the zeros of the zeta function amounts to verifying this property for a certain measure; see
[
18
]
.

2.
The Result

In this section, we first introduce the set of single-spin measures
\chi
, which we are going to deal with.
Then we formulate and discuss our result: the validity of the representation (
1.4
) under the condition that the single-spin measure
\chi
in (
1.2
) belongs to this set.

2.1.
Strongly isotropic measures

Let
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{D})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}_{+})
be the Schwartz spaces of real-valued test functions defined on
\mathds{R}^{D}
and
\mathds{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)
, respectively. Let also
O(D)
denote the group of orthogonal transforms of
\mathds{R}^{D}
. For
U\in O(D)
and
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{D})
, we define
f_{U}
by the equality
f_{U}(x)=f(Ux)
. Set
\mathcal{S}_{D}=\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}^{D}):\forall U\in O(D)\ f_{U}=f\}
. It is known, see
[
2
, Theorem 2.1 and Note on page 245]
, that for each

f\in\mathcal{S}_{D}
, there exists a unique
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}_{+})
such that
f(x)=\phi(x^{2})
. Moreover, the map
f\mapsto\phi
is an isomorphism of the corresponding Schwartz spaces. A distribution,
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{D})
, is said to be isotropic if

\forall U\in O(D)\quad T(f_{U})=T(f):=\int_{\mathds{R}^{D}}T(x)f(x)dx.

By
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{D}
we denote the set of all isotropic
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{D})
.
It is known that
\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R}_{+})
is weakly dense in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}_{+})
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the strongest topology on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{D})
, for which
\phi\mapsto f
can be extended to a continuous map from
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}_{+})
to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}^{D})
. By
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{D}}
we denote the closure of
\mathcal{S}_{D}
in
\mathcal{T}
. Then, for each
T\in\overline{\mathcal{S}_{D}}
, one can find
\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}_{+})
such that
T(x)=\tau(x^{2})
. Obviously,
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{D}}\subset\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{D}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A positive measure,
\chi
, on
\mathds{R}^{D}
is said to be strongly isotropic if: (a) it satisfies (
1.3
); (b) there exists
T\in\overline{\mathcal{S}_{D}}
such that

(2.1)

\chi(d\sigma)=T(\sigma)d\sigma=\tau(\sigma^{2})d\sigma.

Furthermore, we say that an isotropic measure has the Lee-Yang property if it satisfies (
1.5
). The latter is related to all
isotropic measures.

The first relevant example is the aforementioned uniform measure on the sphere
\mathds{S}_{r}\subset\mathds{R}^{D}
, for which
T(\sigma)=\delta_{r}(\sigma)=\delta(\sigma^{2}-r)
,
r>0
,
\delta
being Dirac’s function. Its Laplace transform is

(2.2)

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}(z)=\int e^{z\cdot\sigma}\delta(\sigma^{2}-r)d\sigma=\pi^{D/2}w_{D}(z^{2},r),

\displaystyle w_{D}(\zeta,r)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}r^{D/2+n-1}}{2^{2n}n!\Gamma(D/2+n)},

where
\Gamma
is Euler’s
\Gamma
-function. By means of the Pólya-Schur theorem, see
[
10
, pages 16–23]
, it is possible to show that
w_{D}\in\mathcal{L}
for all
D
. Moreover, the Laplace transform of each strongly isotropic measure, see (
2.1
), can be presented in the form

(2.3)

\widehat{\chi}(z)=\pi^{D/2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}w_{D}(z^{2},r)\tau(r)dr.

By (
2.2
), one readily gets

(2.4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial_{\zeta}}w_{D}(\zeta,r)=2^{-2}w_{D+2}(\zeta,r),

which will be used throughout. It is a particular case of the following property

(2.5)

\forall f\in\mathcal{L}\ \ f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L};

i.e., differentiation maps
\mathcal{L}
into itself, see
[
12
, Proposition 2.6]
. In view of this, we define

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}^{(s)}=\{f\in\mathcal{L}:f^{(s)}\in\mathcal{L}\},\qquad s\in\mathds{N}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{L}^{(s+1)}\subset\mathcal{L}^{(s)}\subset\mathcal{L}
.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R}_{+})
be such that the following holds, cf. (
1.3
),

(2.7)

\forall a>0\qquad\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{ar}\tau(r)dr<\infty.

For this
\tau
, we set, cf. (
2.3
),

(2.8)

\displaystyle v_{\tau}(\zeta,D)=\pi^{D/2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}w_{D}(\zeta,r)\tau(r)dr,\qquad\zeta\in\mathds{C},

which is an entire function of order less than one, or of order one and of minimal type.
Let us notice that each
\tau
satisfying (
2.7
) by (
2.1
) and
(
2.8
) defines a family of measures and their Laplace transforms.
For obvious reason, by (
2.4
) we have that

(2.9)

v_{\tau}(\zeta,D+2m)=(4\pi)^{m}v_{\tau}^{(m)}(\zeta,D),\qquad m\in\mathds{N}.

By this formula and (
2.6
), one immediately gets the proof of the following statement.

Lemma 2.2
.

For some
D\in\mathds{N}
, let
v_{\tau}(\cdot,D)\in\mathcal{L}
. Then

v_{\tau}(\cdot,D+2m)\in\mathcal{L}^{(m)}
for all
m\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.

As mentioned above, the set
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{D}}
contains test functions. In the next important example,
T
is such a function.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
11
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
T
be of the following form

(2.10)

T(\sigma)=:\tau(\sigma^{2})=f(\sigma^{2})\exp\left(-g(\sigma^{2}\right)),

where both
f
and
g
are positive. Moreover, assume that

f
and the derivative
g^{\prime}
are in
\mathcal{L}
. If
g
is a polynomial, then its degree should be at least two. Then the measure as in (
2.1
) with this
T
has the Lee-Yang property for all
D\in\mathds{N}
. In particular,
v_{\tau}(\cdot,D)\in\mathcal{L}
with
\tau
as
in (
2.10
).

Taking in (
2.10
)
f(\zeta)=e^{a^{\prime\prime}\zeta}
,
a^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
, see (
1.6
), and
g(\zeta)=a^{\prime}\zeta+b\zeta^{2}
,
a^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
,

b>0
, one concludes that the measure
\chi(d\sigma)=\exp(-a\sigma^{2}-b(\sigma^{2})^{2})d\sigma
,
a=a^{\prime}-a^{\prime\prime}
, has
the Lee-Yang property for all
D
and
a\in\mathds{R}
. This is a generalization to all
D
of the well-known fact concerning the
\phi^{4}
quantum lattice field, see
[
19
, Theorem 3.4.8, page 233]
and
[
7
,
9
]
. Another example of this kind is the measure

\chi(d\sigma)=\exp\left(-a\sigma^{2}-b(\sigma^{2})^{2}-c(\sigma^{2})^{3}\right)d\sigma,\quad b,c>0,\ \ a\leq b^{2}/3c.

According to Proposition
2.3
, it has the Lee-Yang property for all
D
. At the same time, it is known
[
9
, page 71]
, that, for
D=1
, the measure

\chi(d\sigma)=\exp(-f(\sigma^{2}))d\sigma=\exp\left(-a\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{2}(\sigma^{2}-1)^{2}\right)d\sigma,

does not have the Lee-Yang property for certain values of
a
. At the same time, for this
f
, we have
f^{\prime}(\zeta)=3\zeta^{2}-4\zeta+a+1
, both zeros of which do not lie on the imaginary axis for all real
a
.

2.2.
The statement

We are now ready to formulate and discuss our result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let a strongly isotropic measure,
\chi
, possess the Lee-Yang property, see Definition
2.1
. Then, for every
J>0
and even integer
D
, the function (
1.2
) can be written as in (
1.4
).

The result just stated can be interpreted as follows. Let random
D
-dimensional vectors
S_{1},\dots,S_{N}
have the joint probability distribution given by the probability measure

\nu_{N}(d\sigma_{1},\dots,d\sigma_{N})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(0)}\exp\left(J\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\sigma_{l}\cdot\sigma_{l+1}\right)\prod_{l=1}^{N}\chi(d\sigma_{l}),

where
Z_{N}
,
J
, and
\chi
are as in (
1.2
). Let also
\nu
be the probability measure on
\mathds{R}^{D}
which is the law of
S=S_{1}+\cdots+S_{N}
. By Theorem (
2.4
), it follows that its Laplace transform is

\widehat{\nu}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\gamma_{j,N}z^{2}),

where
\gamma_{j,N}
are as in (
1.4
). That is, we state that
\nu
has the Lee-Yang property.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is essentially based on the statement proved by Lieb and Sokal in
[
16
]
.
We present it here in the form adapted to the context. Set

(2.11)

L=\{z=x+iy\in\mathds{C}^{2}:\exists u\in\mathds{R}^{2}\ (x\cdot u)^{2}+(y\cdot u)^{2}>y^{2}u^{2}\},

and

(2.12)

Q_{N}(z,{\sf J})=\int\exp\left(\sum_{1\leq l\leq N}z_{l}\cdot\sigma_{l}+\sum_{1\leq l<l^{\prime}\leq N}J_{ll^{\prime}}\sigma_{l}\cdot\sigma_{l^{\prime}}\right)\mu_{N}(d\sigma_{1},\dots,d\sigma_{N}),

where
{\sf J}=(J_{ll^{\prime}})
and the integral is taken over
(\mathds{R}^{2})^{N}
. Note that the variables
\sigma_{l}
in (
2.12
) are two-dimensional. In the statement below, by writing
{\sf J}=0
we mean
J_{ll^{\prime}}=0
for all
l,l^{\prime}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

[
16
, Theorem 4.3 and Remark 1]

Assume that the positive measure
\mu_{N}
in (
2.12
) is such that: (a) the integral exists for all
J_{ll^{\prime}}\geq 0
; (b)
Q_{N}(z,0)\neq 0
whenever
z\in L^{N}
, i.e., whenever all
z_{l}
are in
L
. Then
Q_{N}(z,{\sf J})\neq 0
for each
z\in L^{N}
provided
J_{ll^{\prime}}\geq 0
for all
l,l^{\prime}
.

First, we mention that this statement is about a function of
N
complex variables. As we show below,
Z_{N}(h)
, obtained from
Q_{N}
by setting all
z_{l}
equal
h
, can be presented in the form (
1.4
).
Then our Theorem
2.4
generalizes the corresponding result of
[
16
]
to all even
D
, but for a particular choice of the matrix
(J_{ll^{\prime}})
. Here, we remark that the numerical results obtained in
[
14
, Section 2]
point to the validity of our statement for
\chi
as in (
2.2
) and all
D
. Another particular choice of the aforementioned matrix
\sf J
is
J_{ll^{\prime}}=(1+d_{ll^{\prime}})^{-(1+a)}
, where
d_{ll^{\prime}}
is a
hierarchical
distance on
\{1,\dots,N\}
; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
. In this case, the validity of the representation (
1.4
) was proved for all
D\in\mathds{N}
and all isotropic measures possessing the Lee-Yang property, see
[
12
,
13
]
.

3.
The Proof

In the sequel, we use the following notations

(3.1)

\mathds{C}^{-}=\mathds{C}\setminus(-\infty,0],\qquad\mathds{C}^{-}_{2}=\mathds{C}^{-}\times\mathds{C}^{-}\times\mathds{C}.

For
z=x+iy\in\mathds{C}^{2}
, we let
\ell(z)=z^{2}
. Then
\ell:\mathds{C}^{2}\to\mathds{C}
is obviously surjective. Moreover,

(3.2)

\ell^{-1}(\mathds{C}^{-})=L,

see (
2.11
). Indeed, take any
\zeta=\xi+i\eta\in\mathds{C}^{-}
and find
z\in\mathds{C}^{2}
such that
\zeta=\ell(z)
, i.e., such that
x^{2}-y^{2}=\xi
and
2x\cdot y=\eta
. If
\eta\neq 0
,
we take any
x,y\neq 0
such that
x\cdot y=\eta/2
, and then set
u=y
, which yields

(x\cdot u)^{2}+(y\cdot u)^{2}=\eta^{2}/4+(y^{2})^{2}>(y^{2})^{2}
; hence
x+iy\in L
. For
\eta=0=x\cdot y
,
\xi=x^{2}-y^{2}
should be strictly positive. Then
we have to take
z
with
x^{2}>y^{2}
. For such
z
, take
u=\alpha x+\beta y
. Then
u^{2}=\alpha^{2}x^{2}+\beta^{2}y^{2}
. At the same time,

(x\cdot u)^{2}+(y\cdot u)^{2}=\alpha^{2}(x^{2})^{2}+\beta^{2}(y^{2})^{2}>\alpha^{2}x^{2}y^{2}+\beta^{2}(y^{2})^{2}=u^{2}y^{2},

which yields
\ell^{-1}(\mathds{C}^{-})\subset L
. Let us prove
\ell(L)\subset\mathds{C}^{-}
. Take any
\zeta=\xi+i\eta\in\ell(L)
, and let
z\in L
be its preimage, i.e.,
\xi=x^{2}-y^{2}
and
\eta=2x\cdot y
. If
x\cdot y\neq 0
, then
\zeta\in\mathds{C}^{-}
. For
x\cdot y=0
, we have the following possibilities: (a)
y=0
; (b)
y\neq 0
. For (a),
x^{2}>0
since it should be
(x\cdot u)^{2}>0
for some
u
. This yields
\xi>0
, and hence
\zeta\in\mathds{C}^{-}
. For
y\neq 0
, let
u
be as in (
2.11
). Write
u=\alpha x+\beta y
. Then
u^{2}=\alpha^{2}x^{2}+\beta^{2}y^{2}
, and

\alpha^{2}(x^{2})^{2}+\beta^{2}(y^{2})^{2}>\alpha^{2}x^{2}y^{2}+\beta^{2}(y^{2})^{2},

which implies
x^{2}>y^{2}
, and hence
\zeta\in\mathds{C}^{-}
.

Now we define
\ell_{2,2}:\mathds{C}^{2}\times\mathds{C}^{2}\to\mathds{C}^{3}
by the formula

(3.3)

\ell_{2,2}(z_{1},z_{2})=(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{1,2}),\quad\zeta_{1}=z_{1}^{2},\quad\zeta_{2}=z_{2}^{2},\quad\zeta_{1,2}=z_{1}\cdot z_{2}.

Let us prove that

(3.4)

\ell_{2,2}(L\times L):=\ell_{2,2}(L^{2})=\mathds{C}^{-}_{2},

see (
3.1
). The inclusion

\ell_{2,2}(L^{2})\subset\mathds{C}^{-}_{2}

follows by (
3.2
) since
\zeta_{k}=\ell(z_{k})
,
k=1,2
. To prove the opposite inclusion, take
\zeta_{k}=\xi_{k}+i\eta_{k}\in\mathds{C}^{-}
,
k=1,2
, and
\zeta_{1,2}=\xi_{1,2}+i\eta_{1,2}\in\mathds{C}
. We aim to find
(z_{1},z_{2})\in L^{2}
which is a
\ell_{2,2}
-preimages of the triplet
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{12})
. If both
\eta_{k}
are nonzero, then
z_{1},z_{2}\in L
, see above. Thus, it remains to consider the case of
\eta_{1}=0
and
\xi_{1}>0
. Then the corresponding
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
should satisfy
x_{1}^{2}=\xi_{1}+y_{1}^{2}
,
x_{1}\cdot y_{1}=0
,
z_{2}=x_{2}+iy_{2}\in L
, and also

(3.5)

x_{2}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}=\xi_{2},\quad x_{2}\cdot y_{2}=\eta_{2}/2,\quad x_{1}\cdot x_{2}-y_{1}\cdot y_{2}=\xi_{1,2},\quad x_{1}\cdot y_{2}+y_{1}\cdot x_{2}=\eta_{1,2}.

Note that
z_{2}=x_{2}+iax_{2}
is in
L
for each
a\in\mathds{R}
. Indeed, take
u=x_{2}
, and get
(u\cdot x_{2})^{2}+(u\cdot y_{2})^{2}=(1+a^{2})(x_{2}^{2})^{2}>a^{2}(x_{2}^{2})^{2}=u^{2}y_{2}^{2}
. For this choice of
y_{2}
, by the first two equations in (
3.5
), we have the following

(3.6)

a=\frac{\eta_{2}}{\xi_{2}+\sqrt{\xi_{2}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}}},\qquad x_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{2}+\sqrt{\xi_{2}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}}),

and also

x_{1}\cdot x_{2}=\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}(\xi_{1,2}+a\eta_{1,2}),\qquad y_{1}\cdot x_{2}=\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}(\eta_{1,2}-a\xi_{1,2}).

Since
x_{1}\cdot y_{1}=0
, we can write
x_{2}=\alpha x_{1}+\beta y_{1}
, and then get from the latter

\alpha=\frac{\xi_{1,2}+a\eta_{1,2}}{(1+a^{2})(\xi_{1}+y_{1}^{2})},\qquad\beta=\frac{\eta_{1,2}-a\xi_{1,2}}{(1+a^{2})y_{1}^{2}},

with
a
given in (
3.6
) and an arbitrary
y_{1}^{2}>0
. This includes the case of
\eta_{2}=0
. Now

x_{2}^{2}=\alpha^{2}x_{1}^{2}+\beta^{2}y_{1}^{2}=\alpha^{2}(\xi_{1}+y_{1}^{2})+\beta^{2}y_{1}^{2},

which should coincide with
x_{2}^{2}
obtained in (
3.6
). This yields an equation for
y_{1}^{2}
. It has the following positive solution

(3.7)
