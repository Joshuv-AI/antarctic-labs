Targeted inhibition of ATR kinase promotes antitumor immunity in HNSCC through enhanced STING activation and T cell function
Authors: Fabio HB da Costa, Mutsuki Kawabe, Tsung-You Tsai, Kento Okamoto, Mason D. Bartels, Sofia Cortes, Ratna Veeramachaneni, Roberto Rangel, Mitchell J. Frederick, Vlad C. Sandulache, Andrew G. Sikora, Jeffrey N. Myers, Abdullah A. Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•AZD6738 potentiates anti-PD-1 efficacy by remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment.•Treatment increases cytotoxic T cells, reduces Tregs and suppressive myeloid cells.•ATR inhibition drives M1 polarization via cGAS/STING and decreases STAT3 activation.•Transcriptomic analyses reveal enhanced interferon signaling and suppression of EMT.•Study supports AZD6738 development with anti-PD-1 therapy for HNSCC.

Introduction

Oral cavity head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) has an incidence of over 40,000 new cases in the US, and over 500,000 worldwide annually, and over half of these patients will ultimately die of their disease [[1], [2], [3]]. The overall 5-yaer survival for patient with oral cavity head and neck squamous cell carcinoma have not improved over the last three decades despite significant progress in treatment modalities consisting of surgery; radiation; chemotherapy; and, more recently, immunotherapy [1,2,4,5]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) immunotherapy prolongs survival for some HNSCC patients, most patients with recurrent/metastatic (R/M) HNSCC showed limited clinical benefit [[6], [7], [8]]. Recently, results from the KEYNOTE-689 clinical trial demonstrated that adding neoadjuvant and adjuvant pembrolizumab to standard care significantly improved event-free survival in participants with locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [9]. Therefore, novel targeted agents that potentiate the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors are urgently needed. Cancer cells are often defective in DNA damage response (DDR) and repair, creating a therapeutic potential for further DNA damage. Growing evidence indicates that alterations in DNA damage response-related genes, including somatic and germline mutations, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and promoter methylation are closely associated with the initiation, progression, and therapeutic response of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [10].

One potential mechanism for sensitizing HNSCC tumor cells to immunotherapy is targeting the DNA damage response/repair and replication stress (RS) pathways. Several studies have demonstrated that DNA damage can alter systemic immune functions in cancer cells [11,12]. Deficiency in the DDR leads to the accumulation of DNA damage and enhances tumor immunogenicity, suggesting that combining DDR inhibitors with immunotherapy may improve therapeutic outcomes. Head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs) frequently show diminished or lost TP53 function, often resulting from genomic alterations or human papillomavirus (HPV) infection [13]. As a consequence, these tumors rely heavily on the S and G2/M cell cycle checkpoints for proper regulation. Both checkpoints are activated by Ataxia Telangiectasia and Rad3-related protein (ATR), which is typically overexpressed in HNSCC compared to surrounding normal tissue [13]. ATR acts as a serine/threonine protein kinase and it is critical to the cell’s response to replication stress. It is normally activated in response to stalled DNA replication forks to promote G2–M cell-cycle checkpoint transition and fork restart [14]. Pharmacological inhibition of ATR has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer treatment that exploits synthetic lethal interactions with proteins involved in DNA damage repair, overcomes resistance to other therapies and enhances antitumor immunity [15]. AZD6738 (ceralasertib) is a selective, oral inhibitor of the ATR kinase currently in late-stage clinical development [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. It is being evaluated in multiple clinical trials for the treatment of solid tumors [17,18]. However, the effect of AZD6738 on the tumor microenvironment (TME) and immune cells have not been thoroughly investigated in HNSCC.

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of AZD6738 both as a monotherapy and in combination with an anti-PD-1 antibody in the syngeneic mouse oral cancer 1 and 2 (MOC1 and MOC2) cells. Our data demonstrated that AZD6738 significantly enhanced the in vivo response to anti-PD-1 therapy. This enhanced efficacy was attributed to substantial improvements in the tumor microenvironment (TME), including increased numbers and functionality of cytotoxic T cells, along with a reduction in immunosuppressive tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid cells. Furthermore, ATR inhibition with AZD6738 promoted M1 macrophage polarization and modulated a range of cytokines that support T cell activation, both in vivo and ex vivo. Mechanistically, the immune response appeared to be partially driven by the induction of DNA damage response and activation of the cGAS/STING signaling pathway, accompanied by decreased STAT3 phosphorylation. Unbiased RNA sequencing coupled with hallmark gene set enrichment analysis revealed that the combination of AZD6738 and anti-PD-1 antibody treatment significantly enhanced interferon signaling while simultaneously suppressing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways in the preclinical MOC1 tumor model. Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence that ATR inhibition represents a bona fide therapeutic vulnerability in HNSCC. Moreover, the concurrent targeting of ATR and immune checkpoints may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for HNSCC. Given that oncogene-induced replication stress is a key driver of genomic instability across multiple cancer types, our findings are likely to have translational relevance beyond HNSCC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Murine oral cancer 1 and 2 (MOC1 and MOC2) cell lines were obtained from our collaborator, Dr. Ravindra Uppaluri (Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, Harvard University, Boston, MA, USA). The Rangel oral cancer 1 (ROC1) murine cell line was developed in our laboratory. These cell lines were cultured and maintained in mycoplasma-free condition to ensure result reproducibility as previously described [19,20]. The ATR inhibitor, AZD6738 (ceralasertib) used in this study was obtained from AstraZeneca through an existing MTA. The STING antagonist, H-151 was obtained from MedChemExpress.

Generation of ATR knockdown stable cell lines

The mouse ATR shRNA Lentiviral Particles, cat# sc-29,764-V and Control shRNA Lentiviral Particles, cat# sc-108,080 were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. The ATR shRNA (m) lentiviral particles is a pool of concentrated, transduction ready viral particles containing 3 target-specific constructs that encode 19–25 nucleotides (plus hairpin) shRNA designed to knock down ATR gene expression. Briefly, the murine MOC1 and MOC2 target cells were infected with the lentiviral ATR shRNA and control non-targeting (NT) particles in the presence of polybrene (polybrene (5 μg/mL) for 48 h. The cells were then grown in fresh media containing puromycin ((1.0 μg/mL) for selection of stably transfected cells. Successful ATR knockdown in the stable cell lines was confirmed by Western blotting.

Clonogenic survival assay

To determine colony survival in vitro, MOC1, MOC2 and ROC1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and exposed to various doses of the ATR inhibitor, AZD6738 (0–5.0 μmol/L). To determine whether the growth-suppressive effect of AZD6738 depends on STING signaling, MOC1 cells were pretreated with 0.1 µmol/L of STING antagonist (H-151) followed by treatment with AZD6738 as indicated. Clonogenic survival was performed as previously described by our group [21].

Western blot analysis

Minimally toxic drug doses of AZD6738 (0.5–2.0 µmol/L) were identified from clonogenic survival assay IC50 values and used for further in vitro experiments. Briefly, MOC1 and MOC2 cells were treated in 10-cm dishes with AZD6738 (DMSO, 0.5 and 1.0 μmol/L) for 12 h. To assess whether the cytotoxic effect of AZD6738 is mediated through STING signaling, MOC1 cells were pretreated with 0.1 µmol/L of H-151 followed by treatment with AZD6738 as previously indicated. For Whole cell lysates were prepared, and Western blot analyses were performed as described previously [21]. Antibodies used for Western blotting were described in Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

Immunofluorescence

MOC1 and MOC2 cells were plated in duplicate onto the culture slides and treated with AZD6738 (2.0 µmol/L) for 24 h. Cells were then fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde for 1 hour, washed, permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, washed, and blocked for 1 hour at room temperature in PBS buffer containing 2% normal goat serum, and 0.3% Triton X-100. The culture slides were incubated with double standard DNA marker antibody (dsDNA Marker from mice (1:100, #sc-58,749, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Histone H2A.X (Ser139) (20E3, 1:200, cat# 9718, Cell Signaling); overnight at 4 °C followed by Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies, respectively. Nuclei were counterstained with antifade with DAPI. Fluorescent foci were imaged and acquired on Olympus IX71 microscope. Foci were captured and counted in 4 different fields, and data were presented as percentage of cells and intensity with double standard foci using ImageJ software. For colocalization analysis of phosphorylated STAT3 and arginase-1 (Arg-1), MOC1 tumors harvested from mice treated with IgG or AZD6738 were stained with an arginase-1 monoclonal antibody (1:200; MA5–31,577, Thermo Fisher Scientific Invitrogen) and a phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) (D3A7) antibody (1:200; #9145, Cell Signaling Technology). Appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 and Alexa Fluor 488 were used, respectively. Immunofluorescence staining was performed as described above.

In vivo syngeneic orthotopic mouse model of oral cancer

All animal experimentation was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and use Committee of the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. To evaluate the combination effect of AZD6738 and anti-PD-1 on tumor growth, 1.5 × 105 cells of MOC1 and MOC2 (7.5 × 104) cells were suspended in 30 μl of medium without supplement and injected into the lateral tongues of 7–8-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice strain because oral cancer is mostly prevalent in male patients. The mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and acclimated for one week prior to experiments. Mice were then randomized into different groups 8 to 10 days after injection. Mice were treated with AZD6738 (50 mg/kg, q. d. 3 days on, 4 days off, given as oral gavage, p.o.) alone, anti-PD-1 antibody, BioXCell # BE0146 (250 μg/body, twice/week, given intraperitoneally, i.p.) alone and in combination with the two drugs after cell inoculation (when tumors were <3 mm3 in size) for 4 weeks. Control mice group received anti-IgG2a antibody, BioXCell #BE0089 (250 μg/mice, twice/week, given intraperitoneally, i.p.). Each treatment group contained 8–10 mice, and 90% of the mice experienced tumor growth under each condition. Mice were monitored twice a week, and their weight and tumor volume were recorded. Tongue tumors were measured in millimeters with digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as A × B2 × (π/6), where A is the longest dimension of the tumor and B is the dimension of the tumor perpendicular to A and reported as± standard error mean (±SEM), and the model assumes that the errors are evenly distributed. Tumor measurement continued until end of study or when tumors reached the ethical limit when they lost >20% of their preinjection body weight. Mice were then euthanized and tongues, tumors were harvested and subjected to immunohistochemical and immunoprofiling analyses. For myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) depletion experiment, a separate group of mice were injected with MOC1 cells and treated with anti-Ly6G/Ly6C (Gr-1) antibody, BioXCell #BE0075 (250 μg/body, twice/week, given intraperitoneally, i.p.) alone, and with the combination of anti-PD-1 and AZD6738 and the experiment was carried out as described above.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Sections were prepared from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens of mouse tumor tissues following treatment with the drugs as indicated. The antigen was retrieved by incubation at 95 °C in citric acid antigen repair solution for 30 minutes. Following blocking in methanol and water, sections were incubated in 2.5% normal goat serum made in TBST for 30 min, washed and stained with anti-CD8 (D4W2Z) rabbit mAb (1:200, #98,941, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-FoxP3 (D6O8R) Rabbit mAb (1:200, #9145, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Phospho-STING (Ser366) polyclonal antibody (1:200, #PA5–105,674, Invitrogen) and anti-Arginase-1 (GT5811) mouse mAb antibody (1:200, #MA5–31,577, Thermo Scientific Invitrogen) at 4 °C overnight. Binding antibodies were detected using conjugated secondary antibodies as appropriate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then developed with DAB solution, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired using Leica DMLA microscope (Leica Microsystems). IHC quantification was defined as the density of cells and average optical density per area (n = 3 stained tumors; every antibody staining had 5 field views).

Ex vivo differentiation of murine bone marrow (BM)-derived cells and full spectrum flow cytometry

For immunoprofiling of tumors, murine tongue tumors (five per group) were excised, minced with scissors, and digested in DMEM containing collagenase type I (1 mg/mL), collagenase type IV (250 U/mL), and DNase I (20 U/mL) (all from EMD Millipore) for 60 min at 37 °C with gentle rocking. Digested tissue was passed through a 70 µm nylon mesh to generate single-cell suspensions. For immunoprofiling of murine bone marrow (BM)-derived cells, cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Bones were flushed with sterile PBS, and single-cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 µm strainer, followed by red cell lysis. BM cells were plated in 35 mm dishes, at 1.0 × 105 cells/dish, in a 1:1 mixture of fresh complete DMEM medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) and MOC1-derived CM, with or without AZD6738 at 1 µM. Cultures were maintained for 4 days at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, with a medium and drug refresh, on Day 2, equal to as done at plating. See illustration in Fig. 3A, step 2. Single cells from either tumors or bone marrow cultures were prepared and extracellular staining was performed by incubation with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 Fc block (BD Biosciences), for 5 min at RT, to prevent nonspecific binding, followed by staining with a fixable viability dye and fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C, protected from light, diluted in BD Horizon™ Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Intracellular Fixation and Permeabilization Buffer Set (eBioscience), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and subsequently stained with intracellular antibodies for 30 min at RT. All the antibodies used are listed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section. Cells were washed two times in permeabilization buffer and a third time in PBS 2% FBS and finally resuspended in PBS 2% FBS for final cell acquisition at the cytometer. Samples were acquired on a 5-laser Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences). Compensation was performed using Cytek’s SpectroFlo software, and data were analyzed using FlowJo v10 (FlowJo LLC).

T cell proliferation assay

After 4 days of conditioning (Fig. 3A, Step 2), BM cells were harvested, washed, and plated in 96-well U-bottom plates for a T cell suppression assay. Murine splenic T cells were isolated from C57BL/6 mice using negative selection (Pan T Cell Isolation Kit II, mouse, Miltenyi Biotec) and labeled with 5 µM CFSE (carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester; Thermo Fisher) for 5 min at RT, in RPMI 10% FBS, according to manufacturer’s recommendations. Labeled T cells were then washed and added to BM cells at a 1:1 ratio (typically 1.0 × 10⁵ cells/well) in the presence of plate-bound anti-CD3 (1 µg/mL) and soluble anti-CD28 (3 µg/mL) to stimulate activation. Cocultures were maintained for 72 h in complete RPMI. At endpoint, cells were stained with anti-CD3 and anti-CD4/CD8 antibodies and analyzed by flow cytometry. T cell proliferation was assessed based on CFSE dilution within the CD3+ T cell population using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). Proliferation index and division index were calculated using the FlowJo proliferation tool.

Cytokine array profiling

Conditioned media (CM) was collected from MOC1 cells transduced with either a pLenti-vector control or (m)ATR shRNA and subjected to multiplex analysis of 44 mouse cytokines, chemokines and growth factors using the Luminex™ 200 system (Luminex, Austin, TX, USA) by Eve Technologies crop. (Calgary, Alberta, Canada). The assay was performed on the CM fractions according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay sensitivities of these biomarkers range from concentration of 0.3–30.6 pg/mL for the 44-plex. The heat map analysis was generated with Cluster 3.0 and TreeView software (Stanford University).

Mouse xenograft bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

MOC1 cells, were injected in oral tongues of male C57BL/6 mice and treated with AZD6738, anti-PD-1 antibody and in combination as described above. Primary tumor tissues from each group were isolated and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted from tumor samples using the RNeasy mini kit reagents (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific). RNA-seq was performed by LC Sciences (Houston, TX) with 2 µg of total RNA. Briefly, RNA quality was verified on Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) and RNA of ≥7.07 was used for library preparation. Libraries were prepared using Illumina TruSeq mRNA Prep Kit which involved poly-A enrichment according to manufacturer’s guidelines. The mRNA was fragmented, reverse-transcribed, and strand-specific sequencing libraries were constructed. The resultant library size was about 300±50 bp and sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 sequence platform at 2 × 1 50 pair-end configuration. The normalized expression values of FPKM were used for the analysis. Weakly expressed and noninformative (non-aligned) genes were filtered out before normalization. The sequencing raw reads were trimmed by Cutadapt tool, filtered, and cleaned before being aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39). Alignment was performed by HISAT2 alignment package. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled, and gene expression was quantified by StringTie and ballgown. Gene differential expression analysis was performed between the samples by DESeq2 software and edgeR package. Data were filtered and then transformed to log2 counts per million and principal component analysis was performed in the R-statistical analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were identified across the samples using the DESeq2 software and edgeR package respectively. Significance of differentially expressed genes was set on the basis of the FDR cutoff below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥2. The filtering criteria include selection of DEG with an adjusted P value of < 0.05, keeping genes that have at least 50 average reads and 2-fold change in the higher of the comparison among the treatment groups and controls, and exclusion of noncoding RNAs. The top 100 significantly expressed genes (i.e., up- or downregulated among the groups) were considered for further analysis based on the above statistical criteria and depicted in the cluster heatmaps generated by a ggplot2 plotting package. Volcano plots were generated to illustrate significantly upregulated and downregulated genes. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the FGSEA (Fast Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) package and Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB (v7.5.1), with parameters set to minSize = 15, maxSize = 5000, and eps = 0. Enrichment significance was assessed using the normalized enrichment score (NES) and FDR-adjusted q-values. Selected Hallmark pathways were further visualized through enrichment plots, bubble plots, and heatmaps, providing a comparative summary of NES values across different treatment conditions.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0) software was used to perform data analysis The Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA tests were carried out to analyze in vitro data separately. For mouse studies, a 2-way ANOVA test was used to compare tumor volumes between control and treatment groups. For immunohistochemical analyses, a one-way ANOVA and a chi-square test were used to compare immunostaining between control and treatment groups. The Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank test was used to analyze mice survival following drug treatment. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error and P values <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Pharmacological inhibition of ATR enhances anti-PD-1 cytotoxicity and leads to tumor regression in vivo in a MOC1 tumor-bearing model

To evaluate the effect of ATR inhibitor and in combination with PD-1 blockade, immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice bearing MOC1 and MOC2 tongue xenograft tumors, were treated with AZD6738, anti-PD-1 antibody, and in combination as indicated (Fig. 1A). No single agent activity with the AZD6738 was observed in vivo in mice bearing MOC1 or MOC2 tumors (Fig. 1B, and supplemental figure S1B). Anti-PD-1 antibody alone had modest effect on tumor growth inhibition that did not reach significance compared to IgG treated control in MOC1 (Fig. 1b). Additionally, anti-PD-1 had no effect on tumor growth in MOC2 tumor xenografts (supplemental figure S1B). However, in MOC1 model, the combination of AZD6738 and anti-PD-1 antibody significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival of mice compared to control and other treatment groups (*P < 0.0001 and *P = 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 1B and C). Reduced tumor growth and increased mice survival were also observed in MOC2 model following treatment with combination compared to other treatment groups (**P = 0.014; supplemental figure S1B and S1C respectively). No weight loss was observed in mice indicating that the combination therapy was well-tolerated (Fig. 1D, and supplemental figure S1D). Additionally, single tumor growth kinetics showed complete tumor response in a large number of mice treated with the combination therapy, compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 1E–H). Since AZD6738 (ceralasertib) activity has been shown to be dependent on the presence of CD8+T cells [22], we next collected tongue tumors from mice following end of treatment and initially analyzed cytotoxic CD8+T cells and immunosuppressive tumor infiltrating T regulatory cells by immunohistochemistry. The analysis revealed that the CD8+ T lymphocytes were significantly increased in murine tumors treated with combination of the drug compared to other treatment groups (P = 0.0001; Fig. 1I and J). Increased levels of CD8+ T lymphocytes were observed in the MOC2 tumor model (**p < 0.0001, supplemental figure S1E- and S1F). Furthermore, the immunosuppressive FoxP3+ Treg cells were significantly reduced (**P = 0007; Fig. 1K and 1 L) in tumors treated with AZD6738 plus anti-PD-1 antibody compared to other treatment groups, indicating the immunostimulatory effect of the combination therapy. Combination of AZD6738 and anti-PD-1 modulates the tumor immune microenvironment and enhances the M1-macrophage polarization in vivo in MOC1 tumor-bearing model

To comprehensively evaluate the antitumor effects of AZD6738 combined with anti-PD-1 antibody on the tumor microenvironment (TME), we conducted an additional in vivo experiment using the MOC1 mouse model, as previously described. Tongue tumors were collected from five mice per group 18–20 days after treatment, and the composition of immune cell populations was analyzed using full-spectrum flow cytometry. In this experiment, combination treatment resulted in tumor growth reduction and was well tolerated in mice (Fig. 2A and 2 B), consistent with data shown in Fig. 1B and 1 D Among all treatment groups, the combination of AZD6738 and anti-PD-1 antibody significantly increased the proportion of activated CD8⁺ T cells, as indicated by the ratio of CD44⁺PD-1⁻ to CD44⁺PD-1⁺ cells (Fig. 2C). Compared to untreated controls, all treatments reduced the frequency of PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, with the most significant decrease observed in the combination treatment group (Fig. 2D, and supplemental figure S2A and S2B). Anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy led to a modest reduction in PD-1 expression on CD4⁺ T cells, whereas the combination treatment resulted in a more pronounced decrease in PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ cells (Fig. 2E, and supplemental figure S2A and S2B). Macrophage polarization shifted toward an anti-tumor state, as evidenced by an increased M1-like/M2-like ratio, with the most pronounced effect observed in the combination treatment group (Fig. 2F, and supplemental figure S2A and S2B). Functionally, treatment led to an increase in Granzyme B⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, with the highest levels observed in the combination group (Fig. 2G, and supplemental figure S2A and S2B), indicating enhanced cytotoxic activity. The CD8⁺/CD4⁺ T cell ratio increased across all treatment groups, with the highest levels observed in the anti-PD-1 antibody and combination treatments (Fig. 2H, and supplemental figure S2A and S2B). Collectively, these findings indicate that combined ATR inhibition and PD-1 blockade reduces T cell exhaustion, enhances the population of cytotoxic CD8⁺ effector T cells, and promotes macrophage polarization toward an M1-like phenotype, features consistent with a more immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment than that induced by either agent alone. This enhanced immune activation is associated with significantly reduced tumor progression and improved survival. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are known to suppress tumor-reactive T cell responses and limit the efficacy of immunotherapies. To investigate whether efficacy of AZD6738 + anti-PD-1 combination therapy is limited by MDSCs, mice bearing MOC1 orthotopic tongue tumors were treated with an anti-Gr-1 antibody to selectively deplete granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs). Gr-1⁺ cell depletion significantly enhanced the therapeutic effect of AZD6738 combined with anti-PD-1. Compared to the AZD6738 plus anti-PD-1 group, the addition of anti-Gr-1 antibody further suppressed tumor growth (P < 0.0011, supplemental figure S3A). This triple combination also significantly improved survival relative to monotherapy and dual treatment groups (log-rank P = 0.031 supplemental figure S3B). Flow cytometry confirmed effective target engagement: CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ G-MDSCs within the CD45⁺ tumor-infiltrating leukocyte population were markedly reduced with anti–Gr-1 treatment but remained elevated in the AZD6738 plus anti–PD-1 group (Supplemental figure S2B and S3C). AZD6738 increases T cell function ex vivo and negatively modulates proliferation of M2 macrophages and MDSC cells in mouse oral tumor cells

We evaluated the effect of AZD6738 monotherapy on three representative syngeneic murine oral cancer cell lines using a clonogenic survival assay, as previously described. All cell lines exhibited sensitivity to AZD6738, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC₅₀) ranging from 0.18 to 0.74 µmol/L. Among them, MOC1 cells were the most sensitive to treatment (Fig. 5A and B). The accumulation of cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) resulting from unrepaired DNA damage can activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway [[23], [24], [25]]. This activation triggers the production of type I interferons and other pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to an immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment [[23], [24], [25]]. As shown in Fig. 4, AZD6738 treatment elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Given its ability to exacerbate DNA damage and promote cytosolic DNA accumulation [[23], [24], [25]], we hypothesize that ATR inhibition may enhance cGAS/STING pathway activation, thereby amplifying anti-tumor immune responses in addition to its direct cytotoxic effects. Treatment of MOC1 and MOC2 cells with the ATR inhibitor, AZD6738 in vitro led to a significant induction of DNA damage and activation of STING, as indicated by a time and dose-dependent increase in phosphorylation of γH2AX and STING, respectively (Fig. 5C, and supplemental figure S1A). Phosphorylated H2AX (known as γ-H2AX) is a well-established and highly sensitive cellular marker for DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). Therefore, rapid phosphorylation of H2AX on serine 139, forming visible nuclear foci at the sites of damaged chromatin was observed, confirming induction of DNA damage response following treatment with AZD6738 in vitro in MOC1 cells (supplemental figure S4A and S4B). Additionally, AZD6738 treatment inhibited CHK1 activity, suggesting the induction of replication stress (Fig. 5C, and supplemental figure S1A). Notably, the drug also suppressed STAT3 activation in MOC1 and MOC2 cells, indicating that ATR inhibition may interfere with immunosuppressive signaling and potentially shift the tumor microenvironment toward a more pro-inflammatory, immune-permissive state. Evidence of cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), detected through immunostaining using a dsDNA-specific antibody, further supports activation of innate immune signaling following AZD6738 treatment (Fig. 5D–F). To determine whether the tumor cell-killing effects of AZD6738 are mediated through the STING-TBK1 signaling axis, we utilized H-151, a potent and selective STING antagonist. H-151 inhibits STING signaling primarily by suppressing STING palmitoylation and preventing TBK1 phosphorylation. MOC1 cells were pretreated with H-151 for 8 h prior to AZD6738 exposure (supplemental figure S5A). Pharmacological inhibition of STING partially rescued colony survival following AZD6738 treatment, as demonstrated by colony formation assays (Supplemental figure S5B and S5C). In addition, western blot analysis revealed that H-151 pretreatment attenuated AZD6738-induced TBK1 phosphorylation, a key downstream mediator of STING signaling, in MOC1 cells in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S5D). Taken together, these results indicate that ATR inhibition induces MOC1 tumor cell death, at least partially through a STING-dependent mechanism, further confirming the importance of STING signaling in regulating antitumor immune responses. This in vivo increase in phospho-STING levels corroborates the enhanced STING phosphorylation observed in vitro by western blotting. Transcriptomic profiling identifies interferon signaling, cell cycle regulation, and EMT suppression as major features of the response to ATR inhibition in vivo in MOC1 tumors

To characterize the transcriptional effects of AZD6738-based treatment regimens in vivo, bulk RNA sequencing was performed on MOC1 orthotopic tongue tumors harvested from C57BL/6 mice following treatment with IgG control, AZD6738, anti-PD-1, or the combination of AZD6738 and anti-PD-1. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the top 100 differentially expressed genes demonstrated distinct transcriptomic profiles across treatment groups, indicating substantial treatment-induced transcriptional remodeling (Fig. 6A; and supplemental Table S1). Notably, tumors treated with AZD6738 alone or in combination with anti-PD-1 segregated from control tumors, suggesting that ATR inhibition profoundly alters the tumor transcriptional landscape. Differential gene expression analysis further revealed extensive transcriptional changes in AZD6738-treated tumors relative to IgG controls (Fig. 6B; and supplemental Table S2). Among the most significantly altered genes were those associated with DNA damage response (DDR), immune signaling, and cell cycle regulation, consistent with the established role of ATR inhibition in modulating replication stress responses and downstream cellular programs. To define the biological pathways affected by treatment, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using Hallmark gene sets. Both AZD6738 monotherapy and combination therapy were associated with significant enrichment of interferon-γ and interferon-α response pathways compared with IgG-treated tumors (Fig. 6C and D; and supplemental Table S2), indicating robust activation of innate and adaptive immune signaling networks. In contrast, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signatures were significantly suppressed in tumors receiving combination therapy, suggesting that ATR inhibition may also modulate tumor cell plasticity and phenotypes associated with invasion and immune evasion. Given the substantial overlap in transcriptional programs induced by AZD6738-containing regimens, we next compared the combination treatment directly with anti-PD-1 monotherapy and IgG controls to identify molecular changes attributable to ATR inhibition. These analyses revealed distinct pathway-level alterations associated with the addition of AZD6738 (Fig. 6E; and supplemental figure S6). Specifically, Hallmark pathway analysis demonstrated enhanced enrichment of immune activation and apoptosis-related programs in the combination group, whereas pathways governing cell cycle progression remained among the most prominently regulated transcriptional networks (Fig. 6E; and supplemental figure S6; and supplemental Table S2). To facilitate a systematic comparison across treatment groups, normalized enrichment scores (NES) for 15 selected Hallmark pathways were summarized in a comparative heatmap (Fig. 6F). Interferon-α and interferon-γ response signatures emerged as the most consistently and strongly enriched pathways following AZD6738 treatment, with the greatest enrichment observed in the combination arm. These findings are consistent with previous reports linking ATR inhibition-induced replication stress to activation of inflammatory and immune signaling pathways [14,22,23]. In parallel, cell cycle-associated programs, including E2F targets and the G2/M checkpoint, were positively enriched in AZD6738-treated tumors, reflecting activation of checkpoint and DDR-related processes. Metabolic pathways such as glycolysis exhibited context-dependent regulation, showing differential enrichment across treatment comparisons. Collectively, these transcriptomic analyses identify interferon signaling, cell cycle regulation, and suppression of EMT-associated programs as major features of the transcriptional response to AZD6738 in MOC1 tumors. Discussion

Although PD-1-directed immunotherapy benefits some patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), most patients do not meaningfully respond, and numerous clinical trials have failed to identify effective targeted therapies. Recent findings from the KEYNOTE-689 clinical trial revealed that incorporating neoadjuvant and adjuvant pembrolizumab into standard therapy significantly enhanced event-free survival in patients with locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [9]. Ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related protein (ATR) is a key component of the cellular response to DNA damage and replication stress. Inhibition of ATR using AZD6738 (ceralasertib) impairs DNA repair in cancer cells, thereby increasing genomic instability and promoting apoptosis [22,23]. Beyond its direct anti-cancer effects, AZD6738 has also been shown to modulate the tumor microenvironment, rendering it more immunogenic. Specifically, it enhances the infiltration and activation of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells, which play a crucial role in tumor cell elimination. In this study, we demonstrate that AZD6738 exerts broad immunomodulatory effects within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) of a preclinical head and neck cancer model. The treatment not only enhances T-cell activation but also alters the number and function of myeloid cells. Recent preclinical studies have shown that the pleiotropic effects of AZD6738 are highly dependent on dose and treatment schedule, with intermittent dosing yielding maximal efficacy through CD8⁺ T-cell and type I interferon (IFN)-dependent mechanisms [22]. In our model, co-administration of AZD6738 with anti–PD-1 antibodies at comparable dosing levels led to optimal anti-tumor activity, characterized by increased T-cell proliferation and activation. Consistent with prior reports [22,23], we found that the combination of ATR inhibition and PD-1 blockade reduces T-cell exhaustion, expands the population of activated cytotoxic CD8⁺ effector T cells, and promotes macrophage polarization toward an M1-like phenotype. These features reflect a shift toward a more immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment compared to monotherapy. This enhanced immune modulation correlated with significantly reduced tumor progression and improved survival. The M1- macrophages, known for their pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor functions, are typically induced by cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) [26,30]. It is plausible that the observed increase in M1 macrophages in our model results from elevated IFN-γ levels following combination treatment. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which suppress tumor-reactive T-cell responses, are major obstacles to effective immunotherapy. Our in vivo and co-culture experiments provide strong evidence that ATR inhibition impairs myeloid cell differentiation and reduces the induction of immunosuppressive phenotypes that hinder T-cell activity. Furthermore, depletion of Gr-1⁺ cells enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of the AZD6738 and anti-PD-1 combination, underscoring the central role of immunosuppressive myeloid cells in limiting treatment response. Although the mechanism by which ATR inhibition diminishes immunosuppressive MDSC populations in the MOC1 model is not fully elucidated, our data suggest involvement of a CHK1-STAT3-dependent inhibitory signaling axis. Inhibition of this pathway is associated with reduced arginase-1 activity and a reciprocal increase in inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression, consistent with previously reported findings [29]. Several studies have reported that ATR inhibition leads to the accumulation of cytosolic DNA due to unresolved DNA damage following S-phase replication stress [23,31,32]. This cytosolic DNA can trigger the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, initiating innate immune responses. In line with these findings, we observed increased cytosolic DNA, increased DNA damage induction following AZD6738 treatment in vitro and activation of the cGAS-STING-IFN-cytokine signaling axis in vivo. This activation likely contributes to the enhanced therapeutic response observed with ATR inhibition and PD-1 blockade in our HNSCC model. In our study, we also detected an increase in MAC CD45 expression, which further supports the idea that AZD6738 treatment facilitates the maturation of NK cells. These findings suggest that the anti-tumor activity of the combination therapy may, in part, be mediated through the activation of both innate and adaptive immune pathways. Collectively, our results, along with other recent studies, highlight the cGAS-STING pathway as a critical downstream mediator for maximizing the immunotherapeutic potential of ATR-targeted therapies. Furthermore, in our study, the partial restoration of colony-forming capacity following STING blockade, together with the reduction in TBK1 phosphorylation, provides functional evidence supporting a role for STING signaling in AZD6738-induced tumor cell death. These results further strengthen our conclusion that activation of the cGAS-STING axis contributes to the immunomodulatory and antitumor effects of ATR inhibition. Bulk RNA-sequencing analysis of in vivo tumors further illuminated the molecular effects of ATR inhibition combined with PD-1 blockade in the MOC1 model. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and hallmark pathway analysis revealed robust induction of IFN-α and IFN-γ signaling, apoptotic pathways, E2F targets, and G2-M checkpoint activation, consistent with ATR inhibition–induced replication stress and enhanced immune responses. These interferon responses may be essential for effective T cell-mediated anti-tumor activity in this context. Interestingly, we also observed a significant downregulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signatures in tumors treated with the combination therapy. This suggests that the dual treatment not only stimulates immune activation but also suppresses transcriptional programs associated with tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential. In contrast, metabolic pathways such as glycolysis displayed variable enrichment patterns, potentially reflecting differences in immune cell infiltration and stromal composition. Together, these findings indicate that ATR inhibition, particularly in combination with PD-1 blockade, induces profound transcriptional remodeling in tumors. Key pathways impacted include interferon signaling and cell cycle regulation, alongside context-dependent changes in metabolism. These transcriptomic alterations offer important mechanistic insights into the therapeutic efficacy of AZD6738 in HNSCC. However, single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to identify the specific cell populations driving the observed transcriptional changes in tumors following treatment. Other ATR inhibitors, including M6620 (berzosertib), BAY 1895,344 (elimusertib), M4344 (gartisertib), and RP-3500 (camonsertib), are available. We selected AZD6738 because of its well-characterized pharmacological profile, extensive preclinical and translational validation, and established use in combination therapy studies, making it an appropriate tool compound to investigate ATR inhibition in our model.

Further studies are needed to dissect the relative contributions of neoantigen load and immunologic memory, such as through tumor rechallenge experiments in mice treated with ATR inhibitor-mediated T-cell activation. In our study, STING activation by AZD6738 is unlikely to result from off-target effects and is more likely a direct consequence of CHK1 inhibition in tumors. Supporting this, Tang and colleagues have recently demonstrated that ATR inhibition enhances STING phosphorylation by disrupting ATR-CHK1 signaling, which normally suppresses the CDK1-SPOP axis and enforces cell cycle arrest [33]. In the absence of ATR activity, CDK1-SPOP signaling is activated, leading to PD-L1 degradation and accumulation of cytoplasmic DNA fragments. These fragments trigger the cGAS-STING pathway, resulting in STING phosphorylation and downstream immune activation [33]. IFN-γ is well known to activate tumor-intrinsic STING signaling by inducing DNA damage and promoting the accumulation of cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) [34]. Therefore, future investigations will examine whether targeted inhibition of IFN-γ and/or cGAS-STING pathways can abrogate the anti-tumor immune responses elicited by AZD6738. A limitation of this study is that the initial in vivo experiments were conducted using only a single murine syngeneic oral cancer model (MOC1). To enhance the robustness and generalizability of our findings, we subsequently evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of AZD6738 in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy in the immunotherapy resistant MOC2 model. In contrast to the MOC1 model, the combination treatment elicited only modest effects on tumor growth inhibition and immune activation in MOC2 tumors (supplemental figure S1). This attenuated response may reflect the intrinsically immunosuppressive or “cold” tumor microenvironment characteristic of MOC2 tumors, which are known to exhibit relative resistance to immunotherapeutic interventions [35].In conclusion, our findings provide a strong molecular and therapeutic rationale for combining ATR inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade in the treatment of previously untreated patients with advanced head and neck cancer.
