Study on the Adsorption Performance of Nitrite Nitrogen by Biomass Ash Filter Media
Authors: Zijun Yu, Jiahang Yu, Zhichao Qu, Hongmei Zhang, Yong Gao, Wenjuan Niu, Ping Ai, Yaoze Feng, Peng Zhang, Nian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores the utilization of biomass ash as a raw material to prepare a novel block-shaped filter material with a well-developed pore structure and stable chemical properties via short-term high-temperature sintering. Its adsorption performance for nitrite nitrogen was investigated, along with the adsorption kinetics and thermodynamic behaviors. The results of the static adsorption experiments show that the biomass ash filter achieved higher nitrite adsorption per unit under conditions of high nitrite nitrogen concentration, moderate adsorption temperatures and adsorption time, and low solid–liquid ratio. The maximum unit adsorption capacity (6.78 mg/g) was achieved under the following conditions: an initial concentration of 65 mg/L, an adsorption temperature of 35 °C, an adsorption time of 80 min, a pH of 3, and a solid–liquid ratio of 1:400 (g:mL). The filter material surface exhibited a heterogeneous structure. Adsorption of nitrite nitrogen was identified as a spontaneous endothermic reaction involving both multilayer physical adsorption and monolayer/multilayer chemical adsorption, with the former predominating. Under dynamic conditions, the unit adsorption capacity was 32.02%, 52.69%, and 58.15% higher than those of coconut shell biochar, ceramic pellets, and natural zeolite, respectively. This indicates excellent adsorption properties, enabling water purification while achieving biomass ash disposal and recycling, making it highly valuable for promotion.

Introduction

As of October 2023, the installed capacity of renewable energy power generation in China reached 1,404 MW, of which biomass power accounted for 44 MW, with an electricity generation of 164 billion kWh. Statistical data indicate that for every 10,000 tons of biomass consumed for combustion-based power generation, approximately 500 tons of biomass ash are produced as a byproduct, which faces significant disposal challenges with the rapid development of clean energy technologies. If not properly disposed of, it can lead to a range of environmental issues, including land resource occupation, soil structure degradation, increased airborne particulate matter, and water pollution.

Biomass ash exhibits low bulk density, a highly developed pore structure, and strong water absorption capacity. When employed as a filter medium, it can effectively remove COD, TN, NH3–N, and TP from wastewater. As a combustion byproduct of agricultural waste, biomass ash can circumvent the limitations of conventional biochemical methods for nitrogen and phosphorus removal, including long operation cycles, high feedstock costs, poor environmental adaptability, and secondary pollution from microplastics released by the feedstock. Particularly, when applied for nitrogen and phosphorus removal in aquaculture wastewater followed by land application, it can improve soil aeration, water-holding capacity, and nutrient retention, enhance nitrogen and phosphorus availability, and thereby enable safe, large-scale utilization of biomass ash. However, due to its powdery form, it is difficult to recover after water purification and cannot be reused, which limits its widespread application.

On the basis of the method for forming ceramsite, a sintering technique of short duration and high temperature was employed to prepare biomass ash filter media. This approach enables low-energy, high-efficiency, and large-scale molding of biomass ash without additives, thereby addressing the challenges of poor recyclability and limited reusability associated with loose biomass ash filter media in water purification. Nevertheless, the physical and chemical properties, pore structures, and pollutant adsorption characteristics of biomass ash filter media remain insufficiently understood. In view of this, this study selects nitrite nitrogen, a common pollutant in aquaculture wastewater, as the target contaminant to investigate the adsorption behavior of biomass ash filter media under different environmental conditions. The thermodynamic and kinetic characteristics of nitrite nitrogen adsorption are analyzed, the underlying adsorption mechanisms are elucidated, and the adsorption performance is evaluated. The results aim to provide a theoretical basis and practical reference for the large-scale utilization of biomass ash and its application as a filter medium.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Instruments

The biomass ash used in the experiment was bottom ash collected from a biomass boiler fueled by a crop straw. Bio-oil, serving as pyrolysis waste tar, was obtained from a carbonization furnace. Both materials were provided by Wuhan Optics Valley Blue Flame New Energy Co., Ltd. All chemical reagents, including sodium nitrite, sodium hydroxide, sulfuric acid, hydrochloric acid, anhydrous ethanol, and alkaline fuchsin, were of analytical reagent (AR) grade and were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of Biomass Ash Filter Media

The biomass ash was dried at 105 °C for 24 h and then sieved through a 100-mesh (0.154 mm) screen. The sieved ash was uniformly mixed with bio-oil at a ratio of 2:1 (g/mL), placed into a mold, and pressed at 83.6 kPa for 15 min to form a green body. The green body was subsequently transferred to a muffle furnace, heated at a rate of 9 °C/min to 1170 °C, and held at this temperature for 40 min to complete the sintering process. After sintering, the material was furnace-cooled and stored in a sealed container in a dry environment for future use.

Experimental Procedure

Static Adsorption Test

A simulated nitrite nitrogen solution was prepared by using sodium nitrite. The pH was adjusted with 10% H2SO4 and 0.1 mol/L of NaOH. The filter material and the solution were added to a conical flask at a specified solid–liquid ratio and subjected to adsorption testing in a constant-temperature water bath shaker.

Dynamic Adsorption Test

The self-assembled dynamic adsorption system is illustrated in Figure . The adsorption column is vertically divided into three zones: a simulated solution storage zone at the top, a glass bead dispersion and flow stabilization zone in the middle, and a filling zone containing block-shaped biomass ash filter material at the bottom. The recirculated tail liquid is introduced through multiple inlet pipes in different directions to generate a synergistic vortex. The combined effects of centrifugal force and turbulent diffusion promote a uniform distribution of the nitrite solution. The nitrite-containing solution to be treated is mixed with the recirculated tail liquid and flows downward by gravity to facilitate denitrification. During the dynamic adsorption test, the total flow rate of the simulated solution is maintained at 115 mL/min.

Testing Methods

Quantification of the Adsorption Capacity for Nitrite Nitrogen

The concentration of nitrite nitrogen was determined using the basic fuchsin ultraviolet spectrophotometric method. A measured volume of the test solution was transferred to a 50 mL colorimetric tube. Following color development, volume adjustment, shaking, and settling, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 260 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer, with deionized water as the reference.

The unit adsorption capacity of nitrite nitrogen was calculated according to eq . 1 qe=(C0−Ce)×Vm

In eq , C0 represents the initial concentration of nitrite nitrogen (mg/L); Ce represents the equilibrium concentration of nitrite nitrogen after adsorption (mg/L); and qe represents the equilibrium adsorption capacity of nitrite nitrogen per unit mass of adsorbent (mg/g).

Characterization

Biomass ash raw materials were collected and processed to prepare the filter media. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using a D8 ADVANCE diffractometer with a Cu target, operating over a 2θ range of 10°–80° at a scanning rate of 5°/min, to determine the phase composition. Raman spectroscopy was performed using a LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer over the spectral range of 400–2000 cm–1 with a 532 nm visible excitation wavelength to characterize the functional group structures of the biomass ash raw materials. The microscopic morphology of the filter media was observed using a Keyence VHX-6000 digital microscope at 200× magnification to analyze the pore structure characteristics. A Micromeritics instrument was employed to determine the specific surface area and pore structure parameters of the biomass ash filter materials. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) equation was applied to calculate the specific surface area (S BET), total pore volume (V tot), and average pore diameter (Dp). The t-plot method was used to determine the specific surface area of micropores (Smic) and micropore volume (Vmic). The Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method was used to calculate the specific surface area of mesopores (Smes) and mesopore volume (Vmes). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a Nicolet IS50 spectrometer to scan the filter materials before and after adsorption, aiming to analyze changes in their molecular structures.

Heavy Metal Testing

Add 0.5 g of biomass ash raw material to 2 mL of H2O2 (30%) and 6 mL of HNO3 and digest the mixture for 30 min using a microwave digestion system. Following digestion, filter the solution through a 0.45 μm aqueous filter membrane, and determine the total heavy metal content using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer.

The leachate of the biomass ash filter material was prepared using the horizontal vibration method, and its heavy metal content was analyzed with an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer.

Results and Discussion

Structure and Surface Properties of Biomass Ash Filter Material

The XRD results of the biomass ash material are presented in Figure . Prominent diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline silicon dioxide were observed at 12.2° and 16.5°. A broad hump appeared in the 10°–20° range, indicating the presence of a glassy phase composed of amorphous silica and amorphous carbon. The crystallinity of the biomass ash was calculated to be 81.0% using the peak-fitting area integration method. The identified crystalline phases included 72.2 wt % silica, 6.0 wt % wollastonite, and 2.8 wt % melilite, with the remaining 19.0 wt % attributed to amorphous components. The composition of the amorphous phase was further investigated by Raman spectroscopy (Figure ). The peak at 461 cm–1 corresponds to the bending vibration of the Si–O bond within the quartz lattice, while the band at 633 cm–1 is attributed to the bending vibration of Si–O–Si linkages. Peaks at 985 cm–1 and 1021 cm–1 are associated with Si–O bond vibrations in [SiO4] tetrahedra with different bridging oxygen configurations, corroborating the XRD results. The peaks at 1094 cm–1 and 719 cm–1 arise from the bending and stretching vibrations of the C–O bond in calcium carbonate, suggesting that some CaO in the raw biomass ash underwent carbonation due to exposure to moisture and atmospheric CO2 during storage. In addition, peaks at 1340 cm–1 and 1590 cm–1 are assigned to the D and G bands of carbonaceous materials, respectively. The intensity ratio (ID/IG) was calculated as 1.06, indicating a low degree of graphitization and the presence of a significant number of structural defects and disordered carbon. These results confirm the presence of a small amount of amorphous carbon in the raw biomass ash, likely resulting from incomplete combustion, and a larger proportion of defective and disordered carbon in the final ash product. XRD analysis of the biomass ash filter media (Figure ) identified it as a mixture of 74.62 wt % wollastonite (CaSiO3), 3.99 wt % pseudowollastonite (CaSiO3), and 21.39 wt % melilite [Ca2(Al,Mg)­[(Si,Al)­SiO7]]. No amorphous phases were detected. These findings indicate that calcium carbonate in the biomass ash decomposed into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide during the sintering process, and the silicon dioxide subsequently reacted with calcium oxide to form wollastonite. Further reactions between calcium oxide and alumina result in the formation of calcium–aluminum melilite. This phase transformation facilitates the fusion and densification of biomass ash particles, producing a stable matrix with a rough surface. Simultaneously, the release of residual gases and gaseous byproducts contributes to the development of a well-formed pore structure in the filter media, as illustrated in Figure . To further investigate the pore types of the filter media, the specific surface area and pore structure parameters were determined by using the BET method, as presented in Table . The results indicate that the microporous and mesoporous volumes were relatively low, suggesting that the filter media primarily rely on ion exchange associated with percolation through abundant macropores for nitrite nitrogen adsorption. Additionally, leaching tests detected no heavy metals (Cr, Ni, Cu, As, Cd, or Pb) in the leachate from the biomass ash filter, demonstrating that the sintering of biomass ash effectively promotes the immobilization of heavy metals. This result suggests that the use of biomass ash filter material poses no risk of heavy-metal leaching during the adsorption process.

The infrared spectra of the filter media before and after adsorption were analyzed by selecting the samples with the highest unit adsorption capacity. The results are presented in Figure . Distinct Si–O–Si characteristic peaks were observed near 1062 cm–1, along with Si–O peaks around 915 cm–1. The band at 3218.57 cm–1 corresponds to the −OH stretching vibration in Si–OH groups. After adsorption, the intensities of both the Si–O–Si and Si–O peaks decreased, likely due to the influence of adsorbed water molecules on their vibrational properties. Meanwhile, a more prominent broad absorption band emerged around 3218.57 cm–1, suggesting the involvement of multiple adsorption mechanisms in this region. These observations indicate that, upon exposure to water, calcium silicate undergoes surface hydrolysis, wherein disrupted Si–O–Si bonds react with water molecules to form silanol groups (Si–OH). In addition, structural defects (e.g., oxygen vacancies) within the calcium silicate lattice facilitate the adsorption and subsequent dissociation of water molecules to generate hydroxyl groups. The presence of surface hydroxyl groups contributes to the chemical adsorption of nitrite nitrogen by the filter media.

Effect of pH

The results of the orthogonal experiments indicated that pH was the primary controlling factor influencing the adsorption rate of nitrite nitrogen, and the unit adsorption capacity was significantly higher at pH 3 than those at other levels. This phenomenon can be attributed to the spontaneous decomposition of nitrite ions via disproportionation under strongly acidic conditions, which enhances the observed unit adsorption. As pH increased, this decomposition process is suppressed, resulting in a sharp decline in the unit adsorption capacity of nitrite nitrogen. However, strongly acidic or alkaline environments are not suitable for freshwater aquaculture applications. Considering the typical pollution characteristics of effluent from aquaculture recirculating systems, the actual implementation environment, and the results of the orthogonal tests, a focused study on the main controlling factor was conducted under the conditions of an initial nitrite nitrogen concentration of 50 mg/L, an adsorption temperature of 25 °C, an adsorption time of 80 min, and a solid–liquid ratio of 1:400 (g:mL), with pH levels adjusted to 6.5, 7.0, 7.5, 8.0, and 8.5, and the he unit adsorption capacity was used as the evaluation index.

As shown in Figure , the unit adsorption capacity of nitrite nitrogen by the filter media first decreased and then increased with an increase in pH. At pH 6.5, nitrite undergoes disproportionation and spontaneous decomposition, resulting in an improved adsorption effect. As the environment gradually becomes alkaline, the silicate content in the filter media undergoes hydrolysis and forms silicate suspension bonds on the filter media surface, thereby enhancing its adsorption performance.

Adsorption effect of nitrite nitrogen by filter materials varies in different pH-value environments.

Effect of Solid–Liquid Ratio

Based on the results of the main controlling factor test, a simulated nitrite nitrogen solution with an initial concentration of 50 mg/L and a pH of 8.5 was prepared. The adsorption test was conducted at 25 °C for 80 min to investigate the adsorption limit of nitrite nitrogen by the filter media.

Figure shows that the unit adsorption capacity of nitrite nitrogen by the filter media gradually increased with the decrease of the solid–liquid ratio, reaching a maximum and stabilizing at a solid–liquid ratio of 1:500 (g:mL). When the solid–liquid ratio is high, the filter media are in an unsaturated adsorption state. The reduction in the solid–liquid ratio at this stage increases the opportunity for the adsorption sites of the filter media to interact with nitrite ions, thereby contributing to the improvement of adsorption performance. Under lower solid-to-liquid ratios, the adsorption sites of the filter material approached saturation, leading to a decrease in available sites and thus a stabilization of the nitrite nitrogen adsorption capacity.

Adsorption Kinetics

Based on the above experimental results, in order to determine the adsorption time required for the filter media to reach saturation and to investigate the kinetic behavior of nitrite nitrogen adsorption on biomass ash filter media, a simulated nitrite nitrogen solution with an initial concentration of 50 mg/L and pH 8.5 was prepared. The adsorption experiments were conducted using a constant-temperature water-bath shaker set at 25 °C. The solid–liquid ratio was maintained at 1:500 (g:mL), and the adsorption times were set to 15, 30, 45, 60, 120, 180, 240, and 300 min.

The fitted parameters for each model is listed in Table . The pseudo-first-order kinetic model exhibited a high correlation coefficient (R 2 = 0.99), and the predicted equilibrium adsorption capacity (0.53 mg/g) was in close agreement with the experimentally measured value (0.52 mg/g). This indicates that the pseudo-first-order model adequately describes the adsorption behavior of nitrite nitrogen on the filter media, suggesting that the process is primarily influenced by physical diffusion driven by a concentration gradient. The pseudo-second-order model showed a slightly lower correlation (R 2 = 0.96), indicating the presence of chemisorption mechanisms in the adsorption process. The Elovich model yielded an R 2 of 0.91, further suggesting that chemisorption is influenced by the heterogeneous surface structure of the filter media. Fitting with the intraparticle diffusion model reveals a three-stage adsorption process, comprising (i) boundary layer diffusion (fast phase), (ii) intraparticle diffusion (moderate rate), and (iii) adsorption–desorption equilibrium (slow phase). The R 2 values for the first and second stages were 0.99 and 0.97, respectively, while the third stage exhibited a lower R 2 of 0.81. These results indicate that the adsorption of nitrite nitrogen on the filter media is well described by the first two stages, involving both surface adsorption and intrapore chemisorption. Given the small ionic radius of nitrite ions (∼1.23 Å), they can readily diffuse into the internal pores of the filter media. None of the three intraparticle diffusion stages passed through the origin, suggesting that intraparticle diffusion, boundary layer mass transfer, and membrane diffusion jointly contribute to the overall adsorption rate. The increasing trend of the intercept parameter C across the three stages reflects a thickening of the adsorption boundary layer, which corresponds to enhanced mass transfer resistance and a decrease in the intraparticle diffusion rate constant K.

In summary, the adsorption of nitrite nitrogen by the filter media involves both surface adsorption and intrapore adsorption and is governed by boundary layer mass transfer and intraparticle diffusion processes. The overall adsorption mechanism comprises two components: physical adsorption, dominated by diffusion driven by concentration gradients, and chemical adsorption, influenced by the microcellular structure of the filter media surface and the availability of active adsorption sites.

Adsorption Isotherm

Based on the previous experimental results, nitrite nitrogen adsorption on the biomass ash filter media reached equilibrium at 60 min. Accordingly, the following conditions were adopted for the adsorption isotherm and thermodynamic experiments: pH = 8.5, adsorption time of 80 min, solid–liquid ratio of 1:500 (g/mL), adsorption temperatures of 15, 25, and 35 °C, and initial nitrite nitrogen concentrations of 5, 20, 35, 50, 65, 80, and 90 mg/L. These conditions ensured that the filter media operated under saturated adsorption. Under this setup, the equilibrium adsorption capacity and adsorption rate were determined, adsorption isotherm models were fitted, and the thermodynamic characteristics of nitrite nitrogen adsorption on the biomass ash filter media were evaluated.

The solid–liquid adsorption mechanism was investigated by using four classical isotherm models: Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, and Temkin. The corresponding equations for each model are given as follows.

The Langmuir model assumes that adsorption occurs as a monolayer on a homogeneous surface with a finite number of identical active sites. Figure presents the adsorption isotherms of nitrite nitrogen on the filter media fitted with different models. At a given temperature, the unit adsorption capacity of nitrite nitrogen increased with the initial concentration and subsequently reached a plateau. Adsorption saturation occurred when the initial concentration exceeded 65 mg/L, with the corresponding equilibrium adsorption capacities of 0.45 mg/g, 0.51 mg/g, and 0.62 mg/g at 15 °C, 25 °C, and 35 °C, respectively. However, as the available active sites on the filter media surface became progressively occupied, the rate of adsorption gradually declined. The observed increase in equilibrium adsorption capacity with rising temperature can be attributed to the enhancement of molecular thermal motion at higher temperatures, which increases the frequency of collisions between nitrite ions and the active adsorption sites on the filter media.

Adsorption Thermodynamics

To further investigate the relationship between the filter media and nitrite nitrogen, and to evaluate the spontaneity and feasibility of the adsorption process, thermodynamic parameters including Gibbs free energy (ΔG), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS) were calculated using the Van’t Hoff and Gibbs free energy equations. The thermodynamic behavior of the adsorption process was then analyzed accordingly. The specific calculation equations are as follows: 10 ΔG=−RTln⁡Kd 11 ΔG=ΔH−TΔS 12 ln⁡Kd=−ΔHRT+ΔSR

In eqs , , , ΔG: standard Gibbs free energy change (kJ/mol); ΔH: standard enthalpy change (kJ/mol); ΔS: standard entropy change (kJ/(mol·K)); R: general gas constant, 8.314 (J/(mol·K)); T: adsorption temperature (K); Kd: distribution coefficient (dimensionless).

The results of the thermodynamic modeling and the corresponding characteristic parameters are presented in Figure and Table . The calculated values of Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) were negative at all tested temperatures, and |ΔG| increased with rising temperature. This indicates that the adsorption of nitrite nitrogen onto biomass ash filter media is spontaneous, and the spontaneity is enhanced at higher temperatures. Additionally, the positive entropy change (ΔS) suggests increased randomness at the solid-solution interface during adsorption, further supporting the spontaneity of the process. The obtained ΔH values range between 5 and 40 kJ·mol–1, indicating that the adsorption behavior of nitrite nitrogen on the filter material tends toward physisorption and belongs to an endothermic process. This result is consistent with the findings derived from the Freundlich model.

Effect of Co-Existing Ions

In practical recirculating aquaculture systems, K+, NH4 +, NO3 –, and PO4 3– are commonly present as coexisting ions. These ions may compete with the target contaminant NO2 – for active adsorption sites, thereby influencing the adsorption efficiency of the filter material for nitrite nitrogen. To evaluate this effect under realistic aquaculture water conditions, a series of competitive adsorption experiments was conducted by separately introducing varying concentrations of K+, NH4 +, NO3 – and PO4 3– into the system. The experiments were carried out under the following conditions: initial nitrite nitrogen concentration of 3 mg/L, temperature of 25 °C, adsorption duration of 60 min, pH 7, and a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:400 (g:mL).

As shown in Figure , the presence of all four ions reduced the adsorption capacity of the filter material for nitrite nitrogen to varying degrees. Specifically, the inhibitory effects of K+, NH4 + and PO4 3– became more pronounced as their concentrations increased. In contrast, the influence of NO3 – on nitrite nitrogen adsorption did not exhibit a clear concentration-dependent trend. The observed behaviors can be explained by the following mechanisms: K+ and NH4 + are likely attracted electrostatically to negatively charged sites on the filter surface, thereby occupying adsorption positions; PO4 3– tends to undergo ligand exchange with surface silanol groups, which also leads to site competition; whereas the effect of NO3 – may be attributed primarily to a nonspecific competitive effect resulting from the initial increase in ionic strength of the system.

Adsorption effect of nitrite nitrogen by filter materials in the presence of coexisting ions.

In summary, the tested coexisting ions generally exerted an inhibitory effect on the adsorption of nitrite by the filter material. Among them, NH4 + showed the most significant impact, indicating that it is a key interfering ion in practical aquaculture water environment.

Evaluation of the Dynamic Adsorption Effect of Nitrite Nitrogen on Filter Media

To meet the practical needs of aquaculture tailwater treatment, and with reference to the results of static adsorption experiments, the effect of circulation time on the dynamic adsorption effect of nitrite nitrogen was investigated under the following conditions: an initial nitrite nitrogen concentration of 50 mg/L, pH 8.5, 1 L of treatment solution, and one piece of filter media (approximately 16 g). As shown in Figure , the unit adsorption capacity of the biomass ash filter media increased progressively with the extension of circulation time, subsequently stabilized, and then rose again. This may be attributed to the filter media reaching a saturated adsorption state, followed by partial resolution of nitrite ions, during which desorption increased the number of active sites on the media surface. Under a circulation time of 1.5 h, the unit adsorption capacity decreased with the increasing number of filter media, as shown in Figure . This phenomenon may be attributed to a decreased concentration gradient, which leads to a reduction in the unit adsorption capacity. As illustrated in Figure , the biomass ash filter media exhibited a unit adsorption capacity of 0.064 mg/g, which was superior to that of coconut shell biochar (0.048 mg/g), ceramic granules (0.042 mg/g), and natural zeolite (0.040 mg/g), indicating a better purification performance. These findings demonstrate that the unique pore structure and heterogeneous surface of the biomass ash filter media contribute to its effective adsorption performance, enabling it to meet the requirements for nitrite nitrogen removal in aquaculture tailwater reuse.

Conclusions

In this study, short-term high-temperature sintering was employed to prepare biomass ash filter materials. The adsorption behavior of nitrite nitrogen on these materials was investigated, the adsorption mechanisms of nitrite by the biomass ash filters were elucidated, and their dynamic adsorption performance was evaluated. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)The biomass ash filter material exhibited favorable adsorption properties. Among the evaluated parameters, pH exerts the most significant influence on the adsorption efficiency of nitrite nitrogen, while the effects of solid–liquid ratio, initial concentration, reaction time, and reaction temperature decrease in that order. The optimal process parameters for nitrite nitrogen adsorption are an initial concentration of 65 mg/L, a reaction temperature of 35 °C, an adsorption time of 80 min, a pH of 3, and a solid–liquid ratio of 1:400 (g:mL). Under these conditions, the unit adsorption capacity reaches 6.78 mg/g.(2)The adsorption of nitrite nitrogen onto the biomass ash filter medium is a spontaneous and endothermic process that involves a combination of multilayer physical adsorption and monolayer/multilayer chemical adsorption, with physical adsorption predominating.(3)Compared with three common commercial filter materials, coconut shell biochar, ceramic pellets, and natural zeolite-the biomass ash filter medium exhibits 32.02%, 52.69%, and 58.15% higher unit adsorption capacities for nitrite nitrogen under identical conditions, indicating superior removal performance.
