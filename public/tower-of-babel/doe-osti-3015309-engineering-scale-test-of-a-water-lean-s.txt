Executive Summary
Researchers at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) have
developed a new water-lean solvent, N-(2-ethoxyethyl)-3-morpholinopropan-
1-amine (EEMPA or 2-EEMPA), for post-combustion capture of CO2 from coal
and natural gas flue gas. EEMPA has multiple characteristics that make it a
promising option for reducing the cost of CO2 capture from these types of
plants, including the potential to achieve specific reboiler duties (SRDs)
below 2.5 GJ/t CO2 (at 12 vol.% CO2) and not requiring a diluent component
even at very low (<10 wt%) water content.

EPRI, PNNL, RTI International, and their project collaborators developed an
engineering-scale test of EEMPA using the Pilot Solvent Test Unit (PSTU) at
the National Carbon Capture Center (NCCC), funded under U.S. Department
of Energy award FE0031945. The primary objective of this test was to collect
long-term data operating EEMPA with both coal- and natural gas-
representative flue gases at the ~0.5 MWe-equivalent scale (5–10 t CO2/day
captured).

Different methods for manufacturing EEMPA were investigated. Multiple
alternative routes were identified, and initial work was completed indicating
the potential for them to be competitive. After evaluation with a range of
candidate toll manufacturers, ultimately the original synthesis route was
used owing to its relative maturity compared to any of the alternatives that
were tested in the limited time available in the project.

The PSTU is a conventional absorption process typically used to test
aqueous amine solvents. To prepare for testing, two key modifications were
made to the PSTU to operate with EEMPA as a water lean sovlent:
 • Addition of a chilled water loop with the pre-scrubber and
 cooler/condenser to help cool the gas feed to the absorber to lower
 temperatures than could be achieved with available plant cooling
 water, and
 • Installation of larger reboiler in a forced circulation configuration.
The chilled water and gas cooling modification was needed to control the
amount of water entering the absorber with the flue gas and maintain target
water levels. The larger reboiler was needed to accommodate EEMPA’s lower
thermal conductivity compared to an aqueous solvent. Figure 1 below

 1 Introduction
1.1 Background information
 Water-lean amine solvents have been developed as an alternative to
 aqueous amine solvents that represent the most mature technology for post-
 combustion CO2 capture (PCCC) from flue gas streams. They are termed
 water lean due to the relatively low amount of water in them (generally less
 than 20 wt.%) compared to conventional formulations that contain over 50
 wt.% water. Avoidance of water in the mixture can help reduce the heat
 (steam) demand for regenerating the solvent as there is less water, with its
 relatively high latent heat of vaporization, that will be vaporized in the solvent
 reboiler per unit of CO2 captured.

 Researchers at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) have
 developed a new diamine compound, N-(2-ethoxyethyl)-3-
 morpholinopropan-1-amine (EEMPA or 2-EEMPA) (see Figure 1). Unlike many
 other water-lean solvents, EEMPA does not require a diluent component to
 manage an inevitable increase in the viscosity of the solvent as it absorbs
 CO2; the solvent need only consist of EEMPA with a small amount of water,
      generally 2–10 wt.% (Zheng et al., 2020).

 Figure 1
 Chemical structure of N-(2-ethoxyethyl)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine (EEMPA).

 EEMPA exhibits several characteristics that recommend it for further
 investigation as a solvent for a post-combustion CO2 capture process,
 including:

 • Fast kinetics for reaction with CO2, potentially leading to reduced
 absorber packing heights,
 • A low vapor pressure to minimize evaporative losses,

 ENGINEERING-SCALE TEST OF EEMPA: FINAL REPORT PAGE 1

 • Good oxidative and thermal stability,
 • Good compatibility with typical amine process materials of
 construction, including engineering polymers, and
 • The potential to be regenerated using flashing unit operations instead
 of conventional stripping designs, which could lower overall capital
 costs.
 These potential advantages have led to a series of projects that have aimed
 to verify EEMPA’s properties and quantify its performance as a post-
 combustion CO2 capture solvent.

1.2 History of testing
 PNNL and its collaborators have tested EEMPA at the lab and bench scales,
 accumulating a few hundred hours on stream and demonstrating the
 capability of the solvent to achieve capture rates above 90% with specific
 reboiler duties (SRD) between 2.1–2.4 GJ/t CO2 captured. Beyond its thermal
 and mass transfer performance, EEMPA has a higher thermal and oxidative
 stability compared to monoethanolamine, as shown in dedicated
 degradation studies. EEMPA has been the subject of multiple techno-
 economic studies, which have projected the potential for EEMPA to achieve
 costs to capture CO2 of less than $40 per metric ton CO2 captured (for coal-
 fired power applications) (Jiang et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2025).

1.3 Project objectives and scope
 To further the validation of EEMPA as a new post-combustion capture
 technology, EPRI, PNNL, RTI International and their collaborators developed
 a project to perform an engineering-scale (approximately 0.5 MW-equivalent)
 test of EEMPA with both coal- and natural gas-relevant flue gas streams. EPRI
 was awarded a project (FE003145) by the U.S. Department of Energy’s
 National Energy Technology Laboratory to pursue this objective.

 To conduct this test, a test plan was developed to use the Pilot Solvent Test
 Unit (PSTU) at the National Carbon Capture Center (NCCC). The PSTU is a
 complete solvent absorption process including gas cooling and pre-
 treatment, absorption, regeneration, heat recouperation, and gas washing
 unit operations.

 The scope of this test included manufacturing the required volume of solvent
 for the test, modifying the PSTU to accommodate EEMPA’s characteristics,
 development of a test plan covering both coal and natural gas flue gases,
 and execution of the test.

1.4 Report structure
 This report summarizes the development, data, and outcomes of the work
 performed under FE0031945. Section 2 describes the work performed to
 identify alternative methods for synthesizing EEMPA and identify the method
 that was scaled up to manufacture the volume required for testing at the
 NCCC. Section 3 reports about the preparations made at the NCCC for
 testing, and Section 4 provides a high-level summary of the test campaign we
 conducted from August 2024 to March 2025. Section 5 summarizes some of
 the analyses performed using the data collected during the project, and
 Section 6 summarizes the findings and opportunities for additional work.

 The discussion of the testing at the NCCC in this report is a distillation of the
 key points discussed in a separate report to be published through the NCCC
 on its website (Testing EEMPA at the National Carbon Capture Center, 2025
 submitted). In addition, we refer the reader to a paper presented at GHGT-17
 for a view of the work in progress during October 2024 (Swisher et al., 2024).

 2 Solvent synthesis and manufacturing
2.1 Motivation
 EEMPA was originally synthesized using a method based on coupling two
 compounds: N-(3-Aminopropyl)morpholine (3-APM) and 2-
 bromoethoxyethylether (2-BEEE). This reaction scheme is shown in Figure 2.
 This reaction forms EEMPA in a single step, and development by PNNL and
 RTI International conducted in prior projects provided a protocol leading to
 yields of EEMPA over 80%. While conceptually straightforward, this route was
 expected to lead to high costs for manufacturing owing to the use of the
 brominated 2-BEEE compound, which can be costly to procure due to its
 need to be transported under refrigeration.

 ENGINEERING-SCALE TEST OF EEMPA: FINAL REPORT PAGE 3

 To mitigate the risk of this high cost of synthesis, a systematic effort was
 undertaken to identify and test synthetic routes that could use inexpensive
 commodity materials.

 Figure 2
 Reaction pathway used to synthesize EEMPA for the test from 3-aminopropylmorpholine (3-
 APM) and 2-bromoethoxyethyl ether (2-BEEE).

2.2 Multi-step processes
 Multiple routes were investigated where 2-BEEE was replaced with a different
 reactive intermediate compound synthesized from other, lower-cost
 materials. The substitute intermediates were then reacted with 3-APM to
 yield EEMPA. Table 1 below summarizes the most promising routes tested
 and they are described briefly here.
 Chlorodiacetal route
 This route was based on synthesizing ethoxyacetaldehyde by first reacting
 ethanol with 2-Chloro-1,1-dimethoxyethane forming 2-Ethoxy-1,1-
 dimethoxyethane, which was then reacted with p-toluenesulfonic acid
 (PTSA) to form ethoxyacetaldehyde. Ethoxyacetaldehyde was then reacted
 with 3-APM under a H2 atmosphere in the presence of a palladium catalyst.
 Although yield of the aldehyde reaction was relatively high, the average
 overall yield of EEMPA was around 50%, which, given the need for the
 intermediate purification of the aldehyde, would be expected to take away
 some of the potential cost advantage of this route.
 2-Ethoxyethanol oxidation route
 This route relied on direct oxidation of 2-ethoxyethanol, a major commodity
 chemical, to ethoxyacetaldehyde. Experiments showed that the oxidation
 step could achieve very high yield of ethoxyacetaldehyde, and several
 attempts were made to attempt the second reaction step without
 purification of the intermediate. Unfortunately, overall yields varied widely

 ENGINEERING-SCALE TEST OF EEMPA: FINAL REPORT PAGE 4

 and there was no clear strategy for systematically improving the overall yield
 to EEMPA.
 Tosylate route
 Due its relatively high vapor pressure, ethoxyacetaldehyde was a less-than-
 ideal intermediate. As an alternative, a route was investigated using 2-
 ethoxyethyl 4-methylbenzene-1-sulfonate as the intermediate compound,
 which was formed through reacting 2-ethoxyethanol and 4-toluenesulfonyl
 chloride. Like the prior route, this route could have taken advantage of the
 low cost of 2-ethoxyethanol, but it was observed that an intermediate
 purification step would likely be necessary, and thus the overall cost
 effectiveness of the route was not clear.

2.3 Catalytic hydroamination
 In search of another single-step route to EEMPA, a catalytic hydroamination
 route was tested. In this route (Figure 3), ethyl vinyl ether and 3-APM were
 reacted under a H2 atmosphere in the presence of a supported metal
 catalyst. A handful of conditions and catalysts were tested, but no
 combination provided a quantifiable yield of EEMPA. As no clear direction for
 development was evident, further work on this method was abandoned.

 Figure 3
 Reaction scheme for a catalytic amination route to EEMPA starting with ethyl vinyl ether.

2.4 Identifying and setting up manufacturing process
 With the library of alternatives in hand, RTI lead the effort to identify a
 chemical toll manufacturer capable of manufacturing the quantity of EEMPA
 we planned for use at the NCCC (approximately 2,000 gallons, or over 7
 tonnes of EEMPA). Discussions took place with several candidate
 manufacturers, and each was presented with the original synthesis route and
 the best alternatives under development. With this process we selected
 Richman Chemical of Lower Gwynedd, Pennsylvania to manufacture the
 solvent. Due to the immaturity of the alternative methods, the original route
 to EEMPA was used.

Richman undertook to verify the yield of the original method in their
production laboratory. Small test batches were produced and verified that
sufficiently high yields could be achieved with equipment there. Yields
matched or exceeded those measured at PNNL and RTI. Richman proceeded
in manufacturing the full volume.

 3 Test engineering and preparations
3.1 Motivation
 To prepare for testing and populate the initial test plan, Aspen Plus models of
 the PSTU were developed from piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs)
 and other design documents provided by the NCCC. These models were
 used to identify process conditions that would be expected to achieve at
 least 90% CO2 capture for both concentrated (12 vol.% CO2) and low
 concentration (4 vol.% CO2) flue gas conditions.

 Through this exercise, we determined the target water concentrations in the
 solvent and what cooling duties and temperatures would be required to
 maintain the water balance. We also estimated expected reboiler duties.
 Based on these calculations, we determined that we needed to achieve
 lower absorber gas inlet temperatures (a proxy for gas dew point) than would
 be expected relying on just the cooler/condenser column and typical cooling
 water conditions at the NCCC. This analysis also indicated that the existing
 reboiler might be undersized for the duties needed.

3.2 Gas cooling
 To lower the temperature of the gas entering the absorber, we determined the
 range of cooling duties that would be required to cool the gas and condense
 water based on a range of inlet conditions coming from either Plant Gaston
 Unit 5 or the natural-gas fired package boiler. This cooling would be achieved
 by cooling the circulating liquid streams of the cooler/condenser column
 (using an existing exchanger) and the pre-scrubber column (using a newly
 installed exchanger). These exchangers would be supplied with chilled glycol
 solution from a 40-ton air-cooled package chiller. With this set up, we
 expected to be able to control absorber gas inlet temperatures down to
 around 15 °C (59 °F), if needed, to steer the solvent water content to lower
 levels as needed to maintain the target water concentration.

3.3 Reboiler
 Model analysis indicated that the existing thermosyphon reboiler would not
 be capable of delivering the duty needed to achieve the target lean loadings.
 This was largely due to the lower thermal conductivity of EEMPA as a water-

 ENGINEERING-SCALE TEST OF EEMPA: FINAL REPORT PAGE 8

 lean solvent compared to aqueous solvents, which the existing reboiler was
 designed for.

 We identified the steam heater from the GE amino-silicone process
 equipment as a potential alternative since it was still installed at the PSTU.
 Model calculations based on the exchanger’s datasheet indicated that it
 would be able to supply the required duty, and an initial plan was drafted to
 connect it to the PSTU stripper directly.

3.4 Modification plan & execution
 Plans were developed to modify the equipment at the PSTU to accomplish
 the gas cooling enhancement and reboiler change out. Figure 4 shows a
 high-level process flow diagram of the configuration used for testing EEMPA,
 with major system changes highlighted in orange.

 The modifications to the gas cooling system comprised repurposing of an
 existing cooler (also from the GE amino-silicone system), setting of a rented
 air-cooled chiller, and plumbing of a chilled glycol solution circulation loop
 connecting the chiller to the cooler/condenser and (new) pre-scrubber heat
 exchangers.

 As part of the evaluation of the modifications, NCCC staff performed a
 detailed inspection of the GE steam heater, including borescope inspection
 of the tubes. Significant corrosion of the inside surface of the tubes was
 observed. To avoid any risk this might pose to the solvent, a new heat
 exchanger with an identical design to the existing steam heater was procured
 and then installed in place of the damaged one.

 As the steam heater was on an elevated platform above the level of the sump
 of the regenerator column, a circulation pump was also installed to circulate
 solvent from the sump, up through the reboiler, and back into the
 regenerator.

 4 PSTU Test campaign
4.1 Campaign objectives and test plan
 The primary objectives of the test campaign were to demonstrate CO2
 capture rates of at least 90% for both coal-relevant (high CO2 concentration)
 and NGCC-relevant (low CO2 concentration flue gas) and to conduct at least
 two months of testing on each gas. A test plan was organized as a sequence
 of test segments, including parametric tests to determine process conditions
 to deliver the desired capture rates, periods of extended operation at steady
 conditions, and segments employing a sequential design of experiments
 (SDOE) approach to help relate multiple process variables to overall process
 performance. However, due to several unexpected behaviors of the solvent
 and system encountered early in the testing, the test plan was altered
 significantly.

4.2 Campaign summary narrative
 Testing with EEMPA commenced on August 22, 2024. Initial testing was
 conducted with coal flue gas from Gaston Unit 5, and approximately one
 week of testing was accumulated until Unit 5 shutdown. Testing then
 transitioned to using undiluted packaged boiler flue gas.

 Testing on undiluted boiler gas was conducted from September 3rd to
 October 23rd, accumulating approximately 1,200 hours on stream. Capture
 rates varied between 60 and 96%, with 425 hours above 90% capture. The
 water content was successfully controlled between 0.9 wt% and 10.0 wt%
 through variation of absorber gas inlet temperature and regenerator pressure.

 During this period, we discovered that the lean solvent could not be cooled
 to desired temperatures (<110 °F), which limited the CO2 captured in the top
 section of packing and limited capture rates in many cases. Process flows
 were reduced with the aim of trying to lower the temperature of the lean
 solvent entering the absorber.

 Early in this segment of testing, fog was discovered in the regenerator vessel.
 Process conditions were varied to attempt to understand the factors that led
 to its formation, but no combination of factors appears to have stopped it
      (see section 5.6 [pg. 29]).

4.4 Solvent composition
 The purity of the solvent started lower than desired and declined more
 quickly than expected. Initially, purity of the solvent started somewhere
 around 80% EEMPA. A few compounds were quantified, including
 morpholine (a plausible degradation product, 0.8 wt.%), 3-aminopropyl-
 morpholine (a starting material, ~2.8 wt%), and bis-alkyl EEMPA (a side
 reaction of synthesis, 2.0 wt.%). These compounds still did not account for
 the total mass of the solvent, though, and at least 10 wt.% consisted of
 materials that have not been conclusively identified and quantified.

 By the end of testing, the concentration of EEMPA had declined to around
 40 wt.%. The decline in the concentration of EEMPA has been hypothesized
 to be due to a combination of thermal and oxidative degradation pathways
      (see section 5.3 [pg. 24]).

4.5 Fog
 In early September, NCCC staff observed a dense aerosol or fog in the
 regenerator through sight glasses installed at three points along the vessel.
 The fog was first observed while NCCC staff were responding to the solvent
 carryover into the product line (see next subsection). This fog appeared to be
 composed of very fine droplets that moved with the turbulent movement of
 gas within the regenerator.

 In response, we changed the test plan to test different process variables
 hypothesized to contribute to the formation and stability of the fog, including
 reboiler recirculation rate, reboiler output temperature, water concentration,
 regenerator pressure, and the admission of flue gas to the system. Only this
 final factor appeared to have any effect on the fog. Fog was not observed in
 the regenerator when the system was started up and heated but before flue
 gas was admitted to the absorber. The fog would also dissipate within an
 hour once admission of flue gas to the absorber stopped and would return
 within the same time frame once flue gas was readmitted. This suggested a
 link between the presence of CO2 in the regenerator and the fog.

4.6 Solvent carryover
 We also encountered unexpected carryover of solvent out of the process
 when some liquid made its way into the main CO2 product line leaving the
 vessel downstream of the regenerator’s overhead condenser. In the first
 week of September, NCCC staff discovered EEMPA-containing liquid draining
 out of sample lines branching off the main CO2 product line. EEMPA-
 containing liquid was periodically drained out of some of these accessory
 lines over the next weeks while operations were adjusted try to avoid
 conditions that lead to the volumes of carryover. Initially, we hypothesized
 that the carryover was linked to the fog behavior described above, but in the
 end, there was no clear evidence suggesting they arose from the same
 cause. Over time in testing, the amount of liquid decreased substantially.

4.7 Pump seal failures
 In the first week of December 2025, NCCC discovered small leaks in multiple
 pumps. To avoid potentially suddenly worsening leaks that would lead to
 major solvent losses, operation of the PSTU stopped to allow time to
 investigate the leaks and identify more resistant alternatives. Based on a

 ENGINEERING-SCALE TEST OF EEMPA: FINAL REPORT PAGE 17

 brief exposure test of different candidate materials and an evaluation of the
 materials available with reasonable lead times, a Makrez material was
 selected, and operation of the PSTU was restarted in January 2026.

4.8 Heat exchanger plugging
 Over the course of testing, the pressure drop across the rich side of the rich-
 lean cross exchanger increased substantially. Figure 8 shows the pressure
 drops measured on both sides of the exchanger over the whole period of
 testing. While restarting operation in January 2026, NCCC staff noted that
 the pressure drop had increased to such an extent that the desired solvent
 flow rate could not be maintained.

 We performed a set of experiments to attempt to diagnose the issue, varying
 flow rates and temperatures to remove a hypothesized precipitate that could
 be plugging the channels. These experiments didn’t change the trajectory of
 the pressure drop increase, so we returned to the regular testing plan. After
 conclusion of testing, the cross exchanger was disassembled, and a dark,
 insoluble solid material was observed plugging the heat exchanger

 regenerator, a computational study was conducted by PNNL. To understand
 the propensity of EEMPA to condense, the saturation ratio was calculated.

 saturation vapor pressure, expressed in the following equation.

 The most notable feature of these plots is that EEMPA was expected to have
 S > 1 for most of the height of the regenerator in both the dry and the water-
 containing cases; hence, there was a driving force for EEMPA to condense.
 Due to relative relationship between thermal and mass diffusivity for a
 relatively heavy molecule like EEMPA, it was plausible that the solvent could
 cool down faster than it could find a surface to condense on, leading to a
 driving force for homogenous nucleation. Since EEMPA has a relatively low
 vapor pressure, once EEMPA vapor was rapidly cooled by flashing into the
 regenerator vessel, either as rich solvent from the cross exchanger or
 entering the relatively cooler regenerator from the forced circulation reboiler,
 it would be able to form an aerosol. This aerosol could have been further
 stabilized by the absorption of water and CO2 in the droplets, forming
 relatively stable aerosol particles.

6 Findings and perspective for future work
 This project resulted in the largest-scale test of EEMPA as a water-lean
 solvent to date. Over 2,300 hours on stream were accumulated, and capture
 rates above 90% were demonstrated for over 700 of those hours, with 318
 hours over 95% capture.

 During the campaign, water content was successfully controlled over a wide
 range. Water content was controlled by a combination of inlet gas
 temperature (controlled by the direct contact cooler) and the regenerator
 pressure. Water content changes occurred relatively quickly. The system was
 able to operate stably even at high water content (8-10 wt.%) although this
 coincided with high viscosity (>25 cP).

 The efficiency of the heat exchangers was a key factor in the overall
 performance results. The sensitivity of the capacity of the solvent to
 temperature means that even a few degrees higher than expected
 temperature could have a significant effect on the achievable rich loading,
 and recouperation of heat in the cross exchanger directly affects the
 achievable SRD. Heat exchangers should be modeled in detail with
 consideration of increased viscosity, off-design flow rates, and lower thermal
 conductivities of water-lean solvents.

 The results of the campaign were affected by the fact that the concentration
 of EEMPA started below the desired range and declined more quickly than
 anticipated. This underscores the importance of using high purity materials,
 and the original synthesis route for EEMPA is unlikely to be useful at this
 scale of testing if it is not possible to make EEMPA with sufficient purity and
 reasonable cost.

 We encountered a higher level of solvent degradation than anticipated. Part
 of this may be due to higher than desired temperatures in the regenerator.
 Mechanisms of degradation of EEMPA should be investigated further, and
 special attention should be paid to understand the range of temperatures
 that a particular configuration of the regeneration unit operations could
 potentially expose the solvent to.

 We learned that EEMPA has the potential to form an aerosol or fog at
 regenerator conditions. This conclusion is complicated by the fact that the
 solvent was relatively impure, and the potential effect of undesired

 ENGINEERING-SCALE TEST OF EEMPA: FINAL REPORT PAGE 35

components that may not be present if the starting EEMPA solvent had lower
impurities. Any further test of EEMPA should include careful consideration of
the regeneration unit operations and their mechanical design to understand
if there are adverse situations that develop due to sharp temperature
gradients that promote homogenous condensation.

The prevalence of leaks in pumps due to incompatibility between the solvent
and seal materials underscores the need to do systematic testing of
elastomers with the actual material that is expected to go into the system
and not just purified samples. It’s unclear at this point how much the
impurities in the solvent may have contributed to this rapid degradation of
the seals, but if these degradation products are potentially going to be found
in EEMPA used in practice, developing appropriate tests that take this into
account is essential for avoiding downtime that could be mitigated by the use
of proper seals.
