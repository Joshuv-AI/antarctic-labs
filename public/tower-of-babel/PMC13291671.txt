A bacterial symbiont and a plant virus enhance insect fitness by inducing physical defenses against fungal parasites
Authors: Tian-Yu Wang, Ji-Sheng Hong, Shuang-Xiu Song, Kai-Heng Wei, Nai-Fei Chen, Ling-Rui Li, Xi Jiang, Geng-Jun Zhong, Ze-Yu Ma, Yang Yang, Tian Liu, Chengshu Wang, Jun-Bo Luan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceBeneficial microbes enhance the capacity of the host to cope with pathogens, parasites, and predators. These defensive microbes are widespread across species. However, the effects of diverse microbes on host evolution and ecology and their underlying mechanisms are largely unclear. Here, we demonstrate that the plant begomovirus and bacterial symbiont Rickettsia use different mechanisms to jointly facilitate cuticle formation, protecting whiteflies against pathogenic fungi. As multipartite interactions are the rule rather than the exception, we reveal the critical importance of interactions among the host, beneficial microbes, and pathogens in the ecology and evolution of insects in nature. Our study highlights that the microbes residing within insect hosts should be considered when applying natural enemies for pest biological control.

Animals and plants are commonly coinfected by multiple microbes, which are ubiquitous to life on Earth (1, 2). Interactions among the host and multiple microbes determine the health and diseases of animals and plants (3, 4). Both theoretical and empirical studies have shown that beneficial microbes enhance the capacity of the host to cope with natural enemies, such as pathogens, parasites, and predators (5–10). These defensive microbes are widespread across species in nature (11). However, the relationship between defensive symbiont frequency and natural enemy prevalence in the field over time, and the effects of diverse resident microbes on host evolution and ecology are poorly understood (2, 5, 6).

The cuticle is a physical barrier that serves as the first line of defense, preventing pathogens from entering the bodies of animals and plants (12, 13). Previous work suggests that defensive symbiont protection can be mediated by immune responses, but that other potential mechanisms, such as cuticle strengthening, have not been explored (14–18). The insect cuticle primarily consists of a cross-linked matrix of cuticular proteins and chitin (13). Insects can harbor obligate or facultative symbionts, transmit plant pathogens, and be infected with entomopathogenic pathogens (14). Among these microbes, obligate bacterial symbionts have been shown to assist cuticle synthesis by providing tyrosine or its precursors in beetles, weevils, ants, stinkbugs, and whiteflies (e.g., 19–23). However, whether diverse microbes, including facultative symbionts and plant viruses transmitted by vectors, are involved in cuticle synthesis and thereby confer protection to their hosts against a natural enemy is largely unclear.

Whiteflies Bemisia tabaci MED and MEAM1 are two of the most significant and invasive global agricultural pests, causing severe crop losses via the direct feeding and transmission of begomoviruses (24–26). At least 200 begomovirus species can be exclusively transmitted by B. tabaci in a persistent-circulative manner (27, 28). Begomovirus can be transmitted horizontally by viruliferous whiteflies feeding on plants and transovarially at a low frequency (29, 30). These viruses have direct or indirect effect on whitefly fitness (26, 31). Plant-mediated whitefly–begomovirus interactions can be mutualistic, neutral, or negative (26). Bemisia tabaci MED and MEAM1 harbor the obligate symbionts ‘Candidatus Portiera aleyrodidarum’ (hereinafter Portiera) and ‘Candidatus Hamiltonella defensa’ (hereinafter Hamiltonella) in bacteriocytes and facultative symbiont Rickettsia spp. (hereinafter Rickettsia) within cells of their whole bodies (32, 33). Rickettsia spread in B. tabaci MEAM1 populations in the southwestern United States from 2000 to 2008 and in B. tabaci MED populations in the northeastern China from 2018 to 2023 (33, 34). Rickettsia is transmitted maternally and horizontally at relatively low frequency through plants from female B. tabaci MED individuals (33). The fitness of Rickettsia can thereby be tied to the fitness of the host, which can promote mutualism. Rickettsia infection confers fitness benefits and a female bias, leading to the spread of Rickettsia in whitefly populations across the southwestern United States and northeastern China (33, 34). Entomopathogenic fungi are important natural enemies that regulate insect populations in nature (35). Mycoinsecticides have been used to control whiteflies in greenhouse and field crops (36). Thus, B. tabaci–microbe associations are ideal model systems for investigating the effects of multiple microbes on host evolution and ecology and their underlying mechanisms.

A previous study showed that, when fed on Tomato yellow leaf curl China virus-infected tobacco plants, viruliferous B. tabaci MEAM1 exhibited higher BtTres expression and trehalase activity, as well as lower trehalose content, compared to those fed on non-infected tobacco plants (37). Since trehalose serves as a precursor for insect cuticle chitin synthesis (38), begomoviruses may impact the chitin content of whiteflies. Additionally, we previously demonstrated that the whitefly B. tabaci glutamate-oxaloacetate transaminase gene GOT2 compensates for TyrB missing from Portiera for the synthesis of phenylalanine (23, 39). The Rickettsia genome of B. tabaci retained the TyrB and phhA genes (40). Therefore, Rickettsia may cooperate with Portiera to synthesize phenylalanine and tyrosine (18), which can be used to generate cuticular proteins and pigments for promoting cuticle defense. Overall, begomoviruses and Rickettsia could influence cuticle formation of whiteflies, thereby impacting fungal infection of whiteflies. To test this assumption, we tracked the dynamics of begomoviruses, Rickettsia, and entomopathogenic fungi in natural populations of the whitefly B. tabaci in China from 2022 to 2024. We then determined the effects of begomovirus and Rickettsia on fungal infection of whiteflies in the laboratory. Last, we investigated the mechanisms by which begomovirus and Rickettsia regulate the resistance of whiteflies to pathogenic fungi.

Results

Correlative Infection of Begomoviruses, Rickettsia, and Parasitic Fungi in Field Whiteflies.

Begomoviruses, Rickettsia, and parasitic fungi can infect both adults and nymphs of whiteflies. Thus, to investigate the relationships among begomoviruses, bacterial symbionts, and entomopathogenic fungi infecting whitefly populations, we monitored their dynamics in both adults and nymphs of field whitefly populations at three sites in Liaoning Province, China, in 2022; and 13 sites in 10 provinces across China in 2023 and 2024 (Fig. 1A and Dataset S1 A–C). Almost all collected B. tabaci samples were identified as B. tabaci MED, except for a few samples collected from Fuzhou in 2023 and 2024, which were identified as B. tabaci MEAM1 (Dataset S1 A–C). Thus, we only analyzed the B. tabaci MED populations. All B. tabaci samples were positive for Portiera, indicating that the DNA extracted was suitable for microbe analysis. Rickettsia was identified in 87.1% and 82.6% of whitefly adults and nymphs, respectively (Dataset S1 D and E). Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) was identified in 98.5% and 99.6% of begomovirus-infected whitefly adults and nymphs, respectively (Dataset S1 D and E). Six species from four parasitic fungal genera, namely Beauveria bassiana, B. brongniartii, Metarhizium anisopliae, Cordyceps javanica, Cladosporium cladosporioides, and Cl. sphaerospermum, were identified in whitefly adults and nymphs (Dataset S1 A–C). Among them, B. bassiana was found in 83.1% of fungus-infected adults and 81.1% of fungus-infected nymphs (Dataset S1 D and E).

The incidence of begomovirus infection in female adult B. tabaci MED increased from 22.9% in 2022 to 41.5% in 2023 and then declined to 30.1% in 2024. The incidence of Rickettsia infection increased from 74.3% in 2022 to 89.9% in 2024, and the Rickettsia density increased from 2022 to 2024 (Fig. 1 B–D and Dataset S1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The incidence of parasitic fungal infection decreased from 14.6% in 2022 to 7.7% in 2024 (Fig. 1 B–D and Dataset S1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The proportion of fungus-infected whiteflies infected by Rickettsia and/or begomovirus was lower than that not infected by Rickettsia or begomovirus (Fig. 1 B–D). In contrast, the proportion of fungus-non-infected whiteflies infected by Rickettsia and/or begomovirus was higher than that not infected by Rickettsia or begomovirus (Fig. 1 B–D). The proportion of begomovirus-infected whiteflies infected by Rickettsia was higher than that of whiteflies without Rickettsia infection (Fig. 1 B–D). Infection by Rickettsia or begomovirus had a negative correlation with infection by parasitic fungi in adult whiteflies (Fig. 1E, R vs F, ρ = −0.47, P < 0.001, n = 29; Fig. 1F, V vs F, ρ = −0.08, P = 0.001, n = 29). In contrast, infection by Rickettsia had a positive correlation with infection by begomovirus in adult whiteflies (Fig. 1G, R vs V, ρ = 0.09, P < 0.001, n = 29). Whitefly nymph samples collected from these sites showed similar patterns of incidence and correlation of infection by begomoviruses, Rickettsia, and parasitic fungi (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C; SI Appendix, Fig. S2D, R vs F, ρ = −0.48, P < 0.001, n = 29; SI Appendix, Fig. S2E, V vs F, ρ = −0.10, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S2F, R vs V, ρ = 0.06, P = 0.005).

TYLCV and Rickettsia Inhibit Fungal Infection of Whiteflies.

To determine whether begomovirus and Rickettsia impact fungal infection in the laboratory, we monitored the frequencies of parasitic fungi over 2 to 5 generations of whiteflies reared on tomato plants. As TYLCV and B. bassiana represent the main begomovirus and parasitic fungus identified in field whitefly populations in China, they were used for subsequent laboratory experiments. To determine the effect of TYLCV on the dynamics of fungal infection of whiteflies in the laboratory, equal numbers of TYLCV-non-infected and TYLCV-infected whitefly adults without or with Rickettsia infection in a 1:1 sex ratio were released on plants in the same cage and then inoculated with the spore suspension of B. bassiana (F0). In the first population cage assay, in which all whiteflies lacked Rickettsia, whitefly populations only persisted until F1 after exposure to fungi. The proportion of viruliferous whiteflies infected by the fungus was lower than that of non-viruliferous whiteflies across two generations (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the proportion of viruliferous whiteflies without Rickettsia and not infected by fungi increased over two generations (Fig. 2A). Fungal load was lower in viruliferous whiteflies than in non-viruliferous whiteflies across two generations (Fig. 2B; F0, F = 3.69, df = 4, P = 0.007; F1, F = 10.00, df = 4, P = 0.032). In the second population cage assay, in which all whiteflies carried Rickettsia, whitefly populations persisted beyond F4. The proportion of viruliferous whiteflies infected by the fungus was lower than that of non-viruliferous whiteflies over four generations (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the proportion of whiteflies without fungal infection was increased in both viruliferous and non-viruliferous whiteflies housing Rickettsia over four generations (Fig. 2C). Fungal load was lower in viruliferous whiteflies than in non-viruliferous whiteflies over four generations (Fig. 2D; F0, F = 3.89, df = 4, P = 0.042; F1, F = 0.68, df = 4, P = 0.201; F2, F = 0.34, df = 4, P = 0.024; F3, F = 3.06, df = 4, P = 0.005; F4, F = 0.29, df = 4, P = 0.008). Collectively, the viral infection rate remained relatively stable, whereas the fungal infection rate decreased.

To determine the effects of Rickettsia on the dynamics of fungal infection of whiteflies in the laboratory, equal numbers of Rickettsia-non-infected and Rickettsia-infected whitefly adults (without TYLCV infection) in a 1:1 sex ratio were released onto the plants in the same cage and inoculated with B. bassiana. The proportion of Rickettsia-infected whiteflies infected by the fungus was lower than that of Rickettsia-non-infected whiteflies over four generations (Fig. 2E). In contrast, the proportion of the Rickettsia-infected whiteflies without fungal infection increased over four generations (Fig. 2E). The copy number of B. bassiana in Rickettsia-infected whiteflies was lower than in Rickettsia-non-infected insects over four generations (Fig. 2F; F0, F = 5.27, df = 4, P = 0.031; F1, F = 1.00, df = 4, P = 0.162; F2, F = 11.65, df = 4, P = 0.445; F3, F = 0.02, df = 4, P = 0.199; F4, F = 1.17, df = 4, P = 0.022). Taken together, Rickettsia alone confers effective protection against fungi, and the virus provides an additional, albeit smaller, benefit.

TYLCV and Rickettsia Enhance Whitefly’s Resistance Against Pathogenic Fungi.

The field survey and population cage experiments in the laboratory revealed the intimate relationships among whitefly, TYLCV, and Rickettsia. Subsequently, the effects of TYLCV and Rickettsia on whitefly fecundity and mortality upon fungus infection were determined. Infection by TYLCV did not significantly alter the fecundity of whiteflies with or without Rickettsia on TYLCV-host plant tomato (Fig. 3A, P-R-V vs -R+V = 0.335, P+R-V vs +R+V = 0.158). Infection by TYLCV did not significantly change the fecundity of whiteflies with Rickettsia while increased the fecundity by 52.7% in whiteflies without Rickettsia on TYLCV-non-host plant cotton (Fig. 3B, P-R-V vs -R+V = 0.040, P+R-V vs +R+V = 0.062). Infection by Rickettsia increased the fecundity by 142.4% of non-viruliferous and 141.3% of viruliferous whiteflies on tomato (Fig. 3A, P-R-V vs +R-V < 0.001, P-R+V vs +R+V < 0.001); and by 239.2% of non-viruliferous and 172.3% of viruliferous whiteflies on cotton (Fig. 3B, P-R-V vs +R-V < 0.001, P-R+V vs +R+V < 0.001). Thus, Rickettsia infection status has the bigger effect on whitefly fecundity. There was no interaction effect between TYLCV and Rickettsia infection on whitefly fecundity on either plant (Fig. 3A, F = 0.44, P = 0.508; Fig. 3B, F = 0.14, P = 0.711). Infection by Rickettsia increased the rate of TYLCV acquisition (Fig. 3C; 12 h, F = 0.02, df = 6, P = 0.003; 1 d, P = 0.029; 2 d, P = 0.029; 3 d, P = 0.119) and transmission (Fig. 3D, P = 0.029) by whiteflies.

To determine the effect of TYLCV and Rickettsia on the defense of whitefly against fungal infection, we used three parasitic fungal species, namely B. bassiana, Co. javanica, and Cl. cladosporioides that infect whiteflies, as revealed by our field survey, to infect whitefly adults. Compared to non-viruliferous whiteflies, the mortality of viruliferous whiteflies harboring Rickettsia was significantly decreased after exposure to B. bassiana (Fig. 3E, χ2 = 5.97, P = 0.015). In the absence of Rickettsia infection, non-viruliferous whiteflies also succumbed to B. bassiana infection much more rapidly (Fig. 3E, χ2 = 6.50, P = 0.011). Compared to whiteflies without Rickettsia, the mortality of whiteflies with Rickettsia decreased after exposure to B. bassiana regardless of whether they carried (Fig. 3E, χ2 = 13.00, P < 0.001) or did not carry TYLCV (Fig. 3E, χ2 = 13.00, P < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for Co. javanica and Cl. cladosporioides (SI Appendix, Supplementary Results and Fig. S3 A–D). These data indicate that TYLCV and Rickettsia confer protection against pathogenic fungi in whiteflies.

TYLCV Promotes Whitefly Cuticle Formation by Increasing Cuticular Chitin Synthesis.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which TYLCV promotes resistance to pathogenic fungi in whiteflies, we examined the expression of eight genes putatively involved in the chitin synthesis pathway (41, 42) by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) between the TYLCV viruliferous and non-viruliferous whiteflies (Fig. 4A). Compared to non-viruliferous individual, the expression levels of BtTre1 and BtCHS were significantly upregulated in viruliferous whiteflies by 139% and 19%, respectively, at 3 d post virus acquisition, and by 39% and 68%, respectively, at 10 d after virus acquisition (Fig. 4B; for BtTre1, 3 d, F = 10.78, df = 6, P = 0.020 and 10 d, F = 1.20, df = 6, P = 0.275; for BtCHS, 3 d, F = 1.08, df = 6, P = 0.063 and 10 d, F = 2.70, df = 6, P = 0.028). Tre1 and CHS play important roles in chitin synthesis in the insect cuticle (41, 42). Thus, TYLCV may impact chitin synthesis and cuticle formation in whiteflies. We found that the chitin content increased by 56% and 9% at 3 and 5 d after virus acquisition, respectively, in viruliferous whiteflies compared to non-viruliferous whiteflies (Fig. 4C; 3 d, F = 1.10, df = 8, P = 0.004; 5 d, F = 0.54, df = 8, P = 0.024) and remained unchanged at 1 and 7 d (Fig. 4C; 1 d, F = 0.18, df = 8, P = 0.415; 5 d, F = 0.79, df = 8, P = 0.481). In support, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that there were more chitin layers (Fig. 4D), and the cuticle thickness was increased by 38% (Fig. 4E, F = 15.62, df = 18, P < 0.001) in viruliferous whiteflies compared to non-viruliferous whiteflies. The cuticle color of the abdomen was plotted for viruliferous and non-viruliferous whiteflies considering the hue, saturation, and value (HSV) values in the vertical axis and saturation in the horizontal axis. The smaller the vertical value, the darker the color (43, 44). Viruliferous whiteflies exhibited darker-colored cuticles than non-viruliferous whiteflies (Fig. 4F, P = 0.011).

To determine the effect of BtTre1 and BtCHS expressions induced by virus infection on whitefly cuticle formation, BtTre1 and BtCHS were silenced in viruliferous whiteflies harboring Rickettsia using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) (23, 45). Compared to whiteflies fed on 2mDNA1-control plants, the expressions of BtTre1 and BtCHS were significantly reduced in whiteflies fed on 2mDNA1-BtTre1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A, F = 0.15, df = 10, P = 0.013) or 2mDNA1-BtCHS tomato plants for 6 d (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B, P = 0.009). Silencing BtTre1 and BtCHS reduced the chitin content in whiteflies (Fig. 4G, P < 0.001 for VIGS BtTre1, P = 0.077 for VIGS BtCHS). Moreover, TEM analysis revealed that there were less chitin layers (Fig. 4H), and the cuticle thickness decreased by 34% (Fig. 4I, F = 0.18, df = 14, P = 0.012) in BtTre1 RNAi whiteflies. To confirm the role of BtTre1 in cuticle chitin synthesis in whiteflies, we used the Tre1 inhibitor validamycin A (46) to feed whiteflies for 2 d. The chitin content was significantly reduced by 43% after 2 d and by 31% after 5 d in inhibitor-treated whiteflies compared to control whiteflies fed on sucrose (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C; 2 d, F = 0.11, df = 8, P < 0.001; 5 d, F = 24.40, df = 8, P = 0.001). TEM revealed that there were less chitin layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), and the cuticle thickness decreased by 28% (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E, F = 9.74, df = 14, P = 0.008) after whiteflies fed on the inhibitor for 5 d. Therefore, TYLCV reinforces whitefly cuticle by promoting cuticular chitin generation via upregulating the expression of chitin synthesis genes in whiteflies.

Rickettsia Facilitates Whitefly Cuticle Formation by Inducing Phenylalanine Synthesis.

To investigate whether Rickettsia can cooperate with Portiera for the synthesis of phenylalanine, we first used AlphaFold 3 (47) to predict the structures of Rickettsia TyrB and BtGOT2, both of which formed a homodimer (Fig. 5A). Rickettsia TyrB and BtGOT2 exhibited a high degree of overall structural similarity, whereas significant conformational differences were observed in the N-terminal lid helix (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). The active-site residues involved in coenzyme pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP) binding and substrate recognition are conserved in both TyrB and BtGOT2, indicating that they shared catalytic capacity for phenylalanine synthesis (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the N-lid loop of Rickettsia TyrB was shorter than that of BtGOT2. The conformation and amino acid composition of the N-lid loop has a significant influence on substrate preference (48). These residues comprise four different positions (Fig. 5B), contributing to the control of open/closed conformation equilibrium in Rickettsia TyrB and BtGOT2. Rickettsia TyrB had a larger active site (substrate-binding pocket) than BtGOT2, which may accommodate a broader range of reaction substrates, suggesting an enhanced catalytic capacity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). To compare the ability of Rickettsia TyrB and BtGOT2 to catalyze the reaction for phenylalanine generation, the recombinant enzymes of Rickettsia TyrB and BtGOT2 were generated by Escherichia coli (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). The TyrB activity for catalyzing the reaction to generate phenylalanine in vitro was 21% higher than that of BtGOT2 (SI Appendix, Fig. 5C, F = 0.28, df = 10, P = 0.002).

The phenylalanine, tyrosine, and dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) contents were subsequently determined in female adult whiteflies either with or without Rickettsia infection, all of which were free of TYLCV. The phenylalanine, tyrosine, and DOPA contents increased by 35%, 24%, and 51%, respectively, in Rickettsia-infected whiteflies compared to non-infected whiteflies (Fig. 5D, F = 0.66, df = 10, P = 0.033; Fig. 5E, F = 1.11, df = 10, P = 0.040; Fig. 5F, F = 0.22, df = 10, P = 0.010). Expression of three cuticle protein genes was significantly up-regulated in Rickettsia-infected whiteflies compared to non-infected whiteflies within 7 d after emergence (Data is from 33; Dataset S2). TEM analysis revealed that the cuticle thickness increased by 106% (Fig. 5 G and H, F = 6.23, df = 14, P = 0.029) in Rickettsia-infected whiteflies compared to non-infected whiteflies, and the Rickettsia-infected whiteflies had darker-colored cuticles than non-infected whiteflies (Fig. 5I; F = 0.940, df = 52, P = 0.001).

To determine the effect of Rickettsia TyrB on whitefly cuticle formation, we injected adult females with synthetic single-stranded peptide nucleic acids (PNAs), which have previously been applied to silence genes in aphid symbiont Buchnera (49). Anti-TyrB PNAs (PNA_TyrB) was comprised of complementary PNA sequences targeting the TyrB gene and conjugated with an arginine-rich cell-penetrating peptide (CPP). PNAs with a 2-bp mismatch (PNA_mm) served as the negative control. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of Rickettsia TyrB was significantly down-regulated in whiteflies at 1, 3, and 5 d after microinjection with PNAs (Fig. 5J; 1 d, F = 0.10, df = 10, P < 0.001; 3 d, F = 12.10, df = 10, P < 0.001; 5 d, P = 0.002). The phenylalanine content decreased by 38%, 48%, and 6% at 1, 3, and 5 d after microinjection with PNAs (Fig. 5K; 1 d, F = 5.13, df = 10, P = 0.014; 3 d, F = 3.87, df = 10, P = 0.025; 5 d, F = 0.44, df = 10, P = 0.535). The Portiera titer significantly decreased by 34% at 3 d after microinjection with PNAs, but the Hamiltonella and Rickettsia titers remained unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D; for Portiera, F = 6.22, df = 22, P = 0.011; for Hamiltonella, P = 0.887; for Rickettsia, F = 3.84, df = 22, P = 0.103). Further TEM analysis revealed that the cuticle thickness decreased by 47% in PNA_TyrB-injected adult females compared to control whiteflies injected with PNA_mm at 1 d post microinjection (Fig. 5L, P < 0.001). Thus, Rickettsia facilitates whitefly cuticle formation via metabolic cooperation with Portiera for phenylalanine synthesis.

Disruption of B. bassiana Chitinase and Protease Genes Impairs Fungal Infection.

Insect cuticles are rich in chitin and proteins (50). Thus, insect pathogens employ chitinases and proteases to degrade the insect cuticle for successful infection (35). TYLCV enhanced chitin synthesis, and Rickettsia facilitated tanning protein synthesis in the whitefly cuticle. To clarify which chitinases and proteases of B. bassiana are involved in penetrating the whitefly cuticle, we performed RNA-seq analysis of the viruliferous and non-viruliferous female whiteflies, both infected by Rickettsia, at 20 h after fungal challenge. We obtained 114.58 M and 112.84 M clean reads for viruliferous and non-viruliferous female whiteflies, respectively. Among those putative chitinases and proteases, 14 genes showed altered expression levels in viruliferous whiteflies compared to non-viruliferous whiteflies (Dataset S3). Further qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on whiteflies at 20, 24, and 36 h after exposure to B. bassiana. As a result, the gene Bbchi4 (BBA_06317), which encoded a putative chitinase, was differentially expressed in viruliferous whiteflies compared to non-viruliferous whiteflies (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C), while the serine protease gene Bbalp (BBA_00319) was differentially expressed in Rickettsia-infected whiteflies compared to non-infected whiteflies (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–C).

To address the role of these two genes in fungus infection, we performed genetic transformations to generate individual gene deletion (ΔBbchi4; ΔBbalp), complementation (ΔBbchi4::chi4; ΔBbalp::alp), and overexpression (OE::Bbchi4; OE::Bbalp) mutants. In addition, double deletion (ΔBbchi4;alp) and overexpression (OE::Bbchi4;alp) mutants were obtained (SI Appendix, Supplementary Results and Figs. S8 and S9). Subsequent survival assays revealed that ΔBbchi4, ΔBbalp, and especially ΔBbchi4;alp were significantly impaired in infecting whiteflies compared to the wild-type (WT) strain (Fig. 6 A and C; ΔBbchi4, χ2 = 22.14, P < 0.001; ΔBbalp, χ2 = 11.08, P = 0.001; ΔBbchi4;alp, χ2 = 40.03, P < 0.001). There were no significant difference between the WT and complementation mutants (Fig. 6 A, C, and E; ΔBbchi4::chi4, χ2 = 0.01, P = 0.945; ΔBbalp::alp, χ2 = 0.10, P = 0.750). In contrast, challenge with OE::Bbchi4, OE::Bbalp, or OE::Bbchi4;alp significantly increased whitefly mortalities compared to the treatment with the WT strain (Fig. 6 A, C, and E; OE::Bbchi4, χ2 = 19.89, P < 0.001; OE::Bbalp, χ2 = 6.19, P = 0.013; OE::Bbchi4;alp, χ2 = 14.85, P < 0.001). Fungal load assays revealed that the ΔBbchi4, ΔBbalp, and ΔBbchi4;alp mutants exhibited significantly lower copy numbers of a B. bassiana housekeeping gene in whiteflies compared to those infected by the WT strain at 3 d post challenge (Fig. 6 B, D, and F; P = 0.009 for ΔBbchi4; P = 0.019 for ΔBbalp; P = 0.016 for ΔBbchi4;alp). Both the ΔBbchi4::chi4 and ΔBbalp::alp mutants exhibited no significant changes in copy number in whiteflies compared to the WT strain Fig. 6 B and D; P = 0.906 for ΔBbchi4::chi4; P = 0.359 for ΔBbalp::alp). B. bassiana strains overexpressing chi4 and alp showed greater colonization efficiencies than the WT strain (Fig. 6 B, D, and F; P = 0.006 for OE::Bbchi4; P = 0.005 for OE::Bbalp; P = 0.016 for OE::Bbchi4;alp). Taken together, our data confirmed that the core chitinase and protease genes are required in B. bassiana to infect whiteflies.

Repressing Whitefly Cuticle Synthesis Facilitates Fungus Infection.

To further reveal the effects of cuticle changes induced by virus infection on whitefly’s resistance to fungal infection, we generated the BtTre1 RNAi and BtCHS RNAi whiteflies and inhibitor-treated whiteflies for exposures to B. bassiana. The mortality of BtTre1 RNAi or BtCHS RNAi whiteflies was significantly increased following fungal challenge (Fig. 6G, χ2 = 45.64, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S10A, χ2 = 257.14, P < 0.001). Likewise, the mortality of inhibitor-treated whiteflies was significantly enhanced after fungal exposure compared to control whiteflies fed on sucrose (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B, χ2 = 94.64, P < 0.001). To investigate the role of Rickettsia-mediated cuticle formation in the resistance of whitefly to fungal infection, we silenced TyrB in female adults by microinjection with PNAs. After fungal challenge, the mortality of the PNA_TyrB-injected whiteflies was significantly increased compared to control whiteflies injected with PNA_mm (Fig. 6H, χ2 = 4.04, P = 0.045).

Discussion

Although defensive microbes are widespread among animal and plant species in nature, evidence from the field and underlying mechanisms are largely unclear (2, 5, 6, 11, 15, 16). The abundance of whitefly B. tabaci MED surpassed that of B. tabaci MEAM1 in China after its invasion in 2009 (51, 52). Our field survey showed that infection by Rickettsia or begomovirus negatively affected the ability of MED populations to resist fungal infections, whereas Rickettsia infection was positively correlated with begomovirus infection. Population cage experiments over five generations revealed that TYLCV and Rickettsia inhibited fungal infection of whiteflies. We demonstrated that TYLCV and Rickettsia enhanced host resistance against pathogenic fungi. TYLCV and Rickettsia promote cuticle formation by facilitating cuticular chitin and phenylalanine synthesis, respectively (Fig. 7). The pathogenic fungus B. bassiana utilizes chitinase and protease to break down the whitefly cuticle. Repressing whitefly cuticle synthesis thereby facilitated fungal infection (Fig. 7). Thus, the intracellular microbes TYLCV and Rickettsia protect whiteflies from pathogenic fungi by inducing cuticle defense (Fig. 7). As multipartite interactions are the rule rather than the exception in nature (53), our findings reveal the critical importance of interactions among the host, beneficial microbes, and pathogens in shaping the ecology and evolution of insects. As the cuticle is a common and major physical barrier of animals and plants to prevent pathogens from entering the body (12, 13), this study also offers avenues for innovation in agriculture and public health, with far-reaching implications for pest management, plant health, food security, and animal health.

Schematic overview Plant begomovirus and bacterial symbiont Rickettsia conferred host resistance against parasitic fungi. The begomovirus and Rickettsia promoted cuticle formation by facilitating cuticular chitin and phenylalanine synthesis, respectively. Mutation of chitinase and protease in the parasitic fungus B. bassiana disrupted fungal infection. Inhibiting whitefly cuticle formation by repressing chitin and phenylalanine synthesis facilitated fungal infection. Thus, intracellular microbes begomovirus and Rickettsia protects whiteflies from pathogenic fungi by inducing cuticle defense through different strategies.

Few studies have provided evidence for defensive symbiosis in natural populations (6). Thus, field studies are extremely scarce in the functional ecology of defensive microbes (6). A previous work showed that Rickettsia is positively correlated with the frequency and density of entomopathogenic fungus Co. javanica in seven geographic B. tabaci MED populations across China from 2016 to 2020 (54). We found that both Rickettsia and begomoviruses are negatively correlated with the infection of six parasitic fungal species in 13 geographic B. tabaci MED populations across China from 2022 to 2024. Given the spatial and temporal differences between the two studies, variations or differences observed in two studies are not surprising. Correlations observed in the field are more comprehensive when combined with controlled laboratory experiments (6). Our population cage and individual assays jointly demonstrated that TYLCV and Rickettsia enhance resistance in whiteflies against pathogenic fungi, supporting a negative correlation between Rickettsia or begomovirus and parasitic fungi in whiteflies under natural conditions. However, there may be a dosage effect associated with fungal exposure or infection. This could be more consequential in nature, where the infective dose is more variable than in laboratory experiments. Consequently, these interactions may differ or alternatively remain consistent under field conditions.

Theoretical and empirical evidence suggests that vertically and horizontally transmitted symbionts invade a host population via reproductive manipulation or providing beneficial effects to the host (34, 55, 56), or through symbiont-mediated protection against natural enemies (7, 11). Multipartite mutualism is at the forefront of current symbiosis research, and multipartite interactions contribute to host niche expansion (53). Whiteflies contain diverse microbes, including bacterial symbionts and plant viruses within their cells and pathogenic fungi on the cuticle or in the body (33, 35, 57, 58). Rickettsia can be transmitted maternally by B. tabaci MED and horizontally at a relatively low frequency by feeding on plants (33). In contrast, transmission of TYLCV mainly occurs horizontally through viruliferous whiteflies fed on plants and occasionally maternally through whiteflies (29, 57). Rickettsia and TYLCV enhance whitefly fecundity, albeit to different extents. Rickettsia confers whitefly resistance against entomopathogenic bacterium Pseudomonas syringae strains and fungus Co. javanica (54, 59). Rickettsia and TYLCV also protect whiteflies from B. bassiana, a pathogenic fungus widely used for whitefly control (36). Additionally, Rickettsia benefit TYLCV transmission by B. tabaci MEAM1 and MED (60, this study). Thus, Rickettsia and TYLCV can invade whitefly populations by providing a benefit to the host and by mediating protection against natural enemies. The multipartite interactions appear beneficial to whiteflies in the field and could therefore contribute to invasion ability. Multipartite symbiosis is an important component of biocontrol strategies (53). This study also suggests that the microbes residing within insect hosts should be considered when deploying natural enemies for pest biological control. Furthermore, defensive microbes can be engineered and harnessed to manage agricultural and vector-borne diseases, ultimately benefiting plant and animal health.

Insect cuticles are primarily formed from structural cuticular proteins and chitin (13). Tyrosine mediates cuticle tanning (pigmentation and sclerotization) (13). Symbionts play a critical role in cuticle formation in various insects, such as ants (20, 50, 61, 62), beetles (21, 63–66), weevils (19, 66), stinkbugs (22), and whiteflies (23). Obligate symbionts facilitate cuticle synthesis by providing tyrosine directly or collaborating with the host, such as in Oryzaephilus surinamensis, the beetle family Bostrichidae, Pachyrhynchus infernalis, Sitophilus, Cephalotes varians, and whiteflies (23, 50, 62, 65–67). The coexistence of two or more microbial partners within the host is common among insects (68, 69). We found that the facultative symbiont Rickettsia enhanced the cuticle thickness and pigmentation in whiteflies by increasing the phenylalanine, tyrosine, and DOPA contents via metabolic cooperation with the obligate symbiont Portiera. The cooperative mechanism could happen through the transportation of the phenylalanine precursor provided by Portiera and aromatic amino acid aminotransferase encoded by Rickettsia’s tyrB. In contrast, TYLCV promoted the cuticle thickness and pigmentation in whiteflies by increasing chitin synthesis. Therefore, bacterial symbionts and plant viruses use different mechanisms to jointly facilitate cuticle formation, protecting whiteflies against pathogenic fungi.

In conclusion, this study highlights that the host cuticle is an important frontier of the warfare among the host, beneficial microbes, and pathogens. Our results also offer strategies for pest control by disrupting host cuticle formation through engineered microbes or plants.

Materials and Methods

Geographic Survey of Whiteflies and Associated Microorganisms.

Whitefly B. tabaci female adults and nymphs on plant leaves were collected at 13 locations of China from 2022 to 2024 (Dataset S1), with details in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Insect Rearing and Plants.

Rickettsia-infected and uninfected whitefly lines of B. tabaci MED have been established via introgression, following a previously described protocol (33, 34). Whitefly cultures as well as cotton and tomato plants were maintained in separate climate-controlled chambers as described previously (33). TYLCV-infected and VIGS tomato plants were obtained according to the previously described procedure (23, 45, 70). Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Quantitative-PCR (qPCR) and qRT-PCR Analysis.

qPCRs and qRT-PCRs were performed as described previously (71). All primers used are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Pathogenicity Assays.

Entomopathogenic fungi and B. bassiana mutants were tested for their pathogenicity to whiteflies. Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Whitefly Fecundity and Mortality Assays.

The effects of TYLCV and Rickettsia on whitefly fecundity and mortality upon fungus infection as well as Rickettsia on TYLCV acquisition and transmission by whiteflies were determined, with details in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Measurement of Levels of Chitin, Amino Acids, and DOPA in Whiteflies.

Levels of the chitin in whiteflies were determined according to the previously described procedure (72). The level of phenylalanine and tyrosine as well as DOPA was analyzed by UPLC and HPLC-MS, respectively as described before (23). Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Observation of Whitefly Cuticle.

Whitefly cuticle ultrastructure was observed by TEM as previously described (23). The cuticular pigmentation of whiteflies was analyzed using a previously reported method (43). Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Structural Model Predictions.

The predicted structural models for Rickettsia TyrB and B. tabaci GOT2 (BtGOT2) were generated by the AlphaFold 3 webserver (47), with details in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Recombinant Enzyme Activity Detection.

TyrB and BtGOT2 activity were detected following Fe3+ protocol (73), with details in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

RNA-Seq Analysis.

RNA-Seq analysis was conducted as we described previously (33). All of the clean tags were mapped to the reference genome of B. bassiana (74). Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Fungal Transformation.

Mutants were obtained via Agrobacterium-mediated fungal transformation as described before (75), with details in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Fungal Infection of Whiteflies.

Details of the effect of TYLCV and Rickettsia, B. bassiana mutants, and repressing whitefly cuticle synthesis on fungal infection of whiteflies were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Statistical Analyses.

All data analyses were conducted using the SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) as described previously (23). Details were described in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
