LMI Optimization Based Multirate Steady-State Kalman Filter Design
Hiroshi Okajima

Abstract
This paper presents an LMI-based design framework for multirate steady-state Kalman filters in systems with sensors operating at different sampling rates. The multirate system is formulated as a periodic time-varying system, where the Kalman gains converge to periodic steady-state values that repeat every frame period. Cyclic reformulation transforms this into a time-invariant problem; however, the resulting measurement noise covariance becomes semidefinite rather than positive definite, preventing direct application of standard Riccati equation methods. We address this through a dual LQR formulation with LMI optimization that naturally handles semidefinite covariances. The framework enables multi-objective design, supporting pole placement for guaranteed convergence rates and mixed H 2 H_{2} / l 2 l_{2} -induced norm design for balancing average and worst-case performance. Numerical validation using an automotive navigation system with GPS and wheel speed sensors demonstrates that the proposed filter achieves estimation errors well below raw measurement noise levels.



1
Introduction

In many modern control and signal processing applications, state estimation must be performed using measurements from multiple sensors operating at different sampling rates. This multirate sensing environment arises naturally in systems where sensor characteristics, communication bandwidth, or computational constraints lead to varying measurement availability. Examples include industrial process control, robotics (visual and inertial sensor fusion), aerospace systems, autonomous vehicles, and networked control systems. The challenge in such scenarios is to optimally fuse measurements arriving at different rates to achieve accurate state estimation.

The Kalman filter has been extensively used for state estimation in linear systems
[
1
]
. However, the classical Kalman filter formulation assumes that all measurements are available at a uniform sampling rate. When sensors operate at different rates, the system exhibits periodic time-varying dynamics in the measurement equations, even if the underlying plant dynamics are time-invariant. Naive approaches such as zero-order hold or measurement interpolation can lead to suboptimal performance and may not properly account for the uncertainty structure of the multirate measurements.

Multirate state estimation is essential across diverse engineering domains. In autonomous vehicles, GPS (1–10 Hz) and inertial/wheel speed sensors (50–100 Hz) must be fused for navigation
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Similar challenges arise in chemical process control where laboratory analysis and online sensors operate at different rates
[
17
]
, in power systems combining PMUs with SCADA, and in networked control systems with bandwidth-limited or event-triggered sampling
[
18
]
. Mobile robotics applications, including SLAM with laser and encoder fusion
[
28
]
, further demonstrate the widespread need for effective multirate estimation techniques.

Several approaches address multirate Kalman filtering. The lifting technique
[
5
,
6
,
23
,
16
,
7
]
transforms multirate systems into equivalent time-invariant systems at the frame period by augmenting input and output vectors. While preserving state dimension, lifting requires matrix products such as
A^{N}
and operates in open-loop between frame periods, limiting inter-sample accuracy. In contrast, the cyclic reformulation
[
19
]
employed in this paper maintains the original state dimension within each block and explicitly represents periodicity through cyclic shift of state variables, enabling LMI-based optimization that naturally handles semidefinite measurement noise covariances. For systems with measurement delays, pseudo-measurement approaches
[
8
,
9
]
reconstruct current-time observations from delayed measurements and predicted states. Asynchronous filtering
[
10
,
11
]
processes measurements upon arrival but needs careful update handling.

The theoretical foundation of periodic systems and periodic Riccati equations has been extensively studied. Bittanti, Colaneri, and their collaborators established fundamental results on discrete-time periodic Riccati equations (DPRE), including existence, uniqueness, and convergence properties of periodic solutions
[
24
,
25
]
. Varga
[
26
]
developed numerically reliable algorithms for solving both continuous-time and discrete-time periodic Riccati equations using periodic Schur decomposition. De Souza
[
27
]
addressed the periodic Riccati difference equation specifically for optimal filtering of linear periodic discrete-time systems.

This paper proposes a systematic design method for multirate Kalman filters based on cyclic reformulation, building upon the periodic Kalman filtering approach developed by Fujimoto et al.
[
12
,
13
]
. Their method transforms periodic systems into time-invariant dilated systems and enables offline computation of optimal Kalman gains through a discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation (DARE). However, the standard DARE formulation assumes positive definite measurement noise covariance, which does not hold in realistic multirate scenarios where sensors operate at truly different rates.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First, we identify that the cyclic measurement noise covariance
\check{R}
is positive semidefinite but not positive definite in realistic multirate systems, and present an LMI-based approach via dual LQR formulation that handles this naturally. Second, we develop LMI-based extensions for multi-objective design, including pole placement constraints for guaranteed convergence rates and Kalman filter design with
l_{2}
-induced norm constraints
[
22
,
21
]
for balancing average and worst-case performance. Third, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods through automotive state estimation simulations with GPS and wheel speed sensors operating at different rates. The LMI framework also provides additional benefits: stability guarantees through the established connection between cyclic system stability and the monodromy matrix, and straightforward extension to weighted optimal estimation by modifying the objective function.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section

4
develops the LMI-based filter design and weighted estimation. Section
5

l_{2}
-induced norm constraints. Section
6
demonstrates the proposed methods through numerical simulations. Finally, Section
7
concludes the paper.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Standard Kalman Filter

Consider a discrete-time linear time-invariant system:

\displaystyle x(k+1)

\displaystyle=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+w(k)

(1)

\displaystyle y(k)

\displaystyle=Cx(k)+v(k)

(2)

where
x(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector,
u(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the control input,
y(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the measurement vector,
w(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the process noise, and
v(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the measurement noise. The process and measurement noises are assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian white noise with covariance matrices
Q
and
R
, respectively, where
\delta_{kj}
is the Kronecker delta:

\mathbb{E}[w(k)w(j)^{T}]=Q\delta_{kj},\quad\mathbb{E}[v(k)v(j)^{T}]=R\delta_{kj}

(3)

The discrete-time Kalman filter consists of prediction and update steps:

Prediction:

\displaystyle\hat{x}(k+1|k)

\displaystyle=A\hat{x}(k|k)+Bu(k)

(4)

\displaystyle P(k+1|k)

\displaystyle=AP(k|k)A^{T}+Q

(5)

Update:

\displaystyle K(k)

\displaystyle=P(k|k-1)C^{T}(CP(k|k-1)C^{T}+R)^{-1}

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{x}(k|k)

\displaystyle=\hat{x}(k|k-1)+K(k)(y(k)-C\hat{x}(k|k-1))

(7)

\displaystyle P(k|k)

\displaystyle=(I-K(k)C)P(k|k-1)

(8)

where
K(k)
is the Kalman gain matrix,
\hat{x}(k|k)
is the estimated state given measurements up to time
k
, and
P(k|k)
is the estimation error covariance.

For time-invariant systems, the Kalman gain converges to a steady-state value
K_{ss}
as
k\to\infty
, which can be computed by solving the discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation (DARE):

P=APA^{T}-APC^{T}(CPC^{T}+R)^{-1}CPA^{T}+Q

(9)

The steady-state Kalman gain is then:

K_{ss}=PC^{T}(CPC^{T}+R)^{-1}

(10)

In this paper, we adopt the predictor form
[
14
]
, which is standard in observer-based control design. The corresponding gain
L_{ss}=AK_{ss}
yields the estimator:

\hat{x}(k+1)=A\hat{x}(k)+Bu(k)+L_{ss}(y(k)-C\hat{x}(k))

(11)

Defining the estimation error
e(k)=x(k)-\hat{x}(k)
, the error dynamics becomes:

e(k+1)=(A-L_{ss}C)e(k)+w(k)-L_{ss}v(k)

(12)

This formulation facilitates LMI-based stability analysis through the closed-loop matrix
A-L_{ss}C
.

2.2
Periodic Time-Varying Systems

A discrete-time periodic system with period
N
is characterized by:

\displaystyle x(k+1)

\displaystyle=A_{k}x(k)+B_{k}u(k)+w(k)

(13)

\displaystyle y(k)

\displaystyle=C_{k}x(k)+v(k)

(14)

where
A_{k+N}=A_{k}
,
B_{k+N}=B_{k}
, and
C_{k+N}=C_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. The matrices
A_{k}
,
B_{k}
, and
C_{k}
vary periodically with period
N
.

For periodic systems, the Kalman filter gains in the predictor form also become periodic. As
k\to\infty
, the gains converge to
periodic steady-state
values
L_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
, satisfying:

L_{k+N}=L_{k},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,N-1

(15)

This is analogous to the steady-state gain
L_{ss}
in time-invariant systems, but the gains now form a periodic sequence rather than a constant. Computing these periodic gains requires solving coupled Riccati equations, which is computationally intensive for large
N
. Sections
3
and
4
show how cyclic reformulation and LMI optimization transform this into an efficiently solvable convex problem.

3
Multirate Kalman Filter Expression

3.1
Problem Formulation

Consider a linear time-invariant discrete-time system:

x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+Q^{1/2}d_{w}(k)

(16)

where
x(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector,
u(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the control input, and
d_{w}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a normalized disturbance with zero mean and unit covariance. The matrix
Q^{1/2}
is obtained from the Cholesky factorization
Q=Q^{1/2}(Q^{1/2})^{T}
, where
Q\succ 0
is the process noise covariance.

Suppose the system is equipped with multiple sensors operating at different sampling rates. Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
denote the full measurement matrix, where
q
is the total number of scalar measurements. Since not all sensors provide measurements at every sampling instant, we introduce a periodic selection matrix
S_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}

[
21
,
20
]
to represent measurement availability. The measurement equation for the multirate system is:

y(k)=S_{k}Cx(k)+S_{k}R^{1/2}d_{v}(k)

(17)

where
d_{v}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a normalized disturbance with unit covariance, and
R^{1/2}
is obtained from
R=R^{1/2}(R^{1/2})^{T}
with
R\succ 0
being the measurement noise covariance. The matrix
S_{k}
is a diagonal matrix with entries of 0 or 1:

S_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(s_{k,1},s_{k,2},\ldots,s_{k,q})

(18)

where
s_{k,i}=1
if the
i
-th measurement is available at time
k
, and
s_{k,i}=0
otherwise.

If the sampling rates of different sensors have rational ratios, there exists a period
N
such that:

S_{k+N}=S_{k},\quad\forall k\geq 0

(19)

The multirate Kalman filtering problem is to design optimal periodic Kalman gains
L_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
, for the predictor-form estimator:

\hat{x}(k+1)=A\hat{x}(k)+Bu(k)+L_{k}(y(k)-S_{k}C\hat{x}(k))

(20)

where
\hat{x}(k)
denotes
\hat{x}(k|k-1)
as introduced in Section
2
. The objective is to minimize the estimation error
e(k)=x(k)-\hat{x}(k)
in the mean square sense.

3.2
Cyclic Reformulation

To transform the periodic system (
16
)–(
17
) into a time-invariant form, we apply cyclic reformulation
[
19
]
, following its extension to multirate systems in
[
22
,
20
]
. For a signal
\xi(k)
, define its cycled representation
\check{\xi}(k)
by placing the signal in the
(k\bmod N+1)
-th block position with zeros elsewhere. Specifically, for
k\bmod N=j

(j=0,1,\ldots,N-1)
:

\check{\xi}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}O\\
\vdots\\
O\\
\xi(k)\\
O\\
\vdots\\
O\end{bmatrix}\leftarrow(j+1)\text{-th block}

(21)

For example, the cycled input
\check{u}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Np}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\check{u}(0)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}u(0)\\
O\\
\vdots\\
O\end{bmatrix},\quad\check{u}(1)=\begin{bmatrix}O\\
u(1)\\
\vdots\\
O\end{bmatrix},\quad\cdots,

\displaystyle\check{u}(N-1)=\begin{bmatrix}O\\
\vdots\\
O\\
u(N-1)\end{bmatrix},\quad\cdots

(22)

This cycled representation is applied to all signals:
\check{x}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
for the state,
\check{u}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Np}
for the input,
\check{y}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nq}
for the output,
\check{d}_{w}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
for the normalized process disturbance, and
\check{d}_{v}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nq}
for the normalized measurement disturbance. The cycled state dynamics is given by:

\check{x}(k+1)=\check{A}\check{x}(k)+\check{B}\check{u}(k)+\check{Q}^{1/2}\check{d}_{w}(k)

(23)

where the cyclic system matrix
\check{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}
has the structure:

\check{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&A\\
A&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&A&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&A&0\end{bmatrix}

(24)

Similarly,
\check{B}
and
\check{Q}^{1/2}
follow the same cyclic pattern:

\displaystyle\check{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&B\\
B&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&B&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&B&0\end{bmatrix},

(25)

\displaystyle\check{Q}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&Q^{1/2}\\
Q^{1/2}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&Q^{1/2}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&Q^{1/2}&0\end{bmatrix}

(26)

The cycled measurement equation is:

\check{y}(k)=\check{C}\check{x}(k)+\check{R}^{1/2}\check{d}_{v}(k)

(27)

where
\check{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nq\times Nn}
and
\check{R}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nq\times Nq}
are block-diagonal matrices:

\displaystyle\check{C}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(S_{0}C,S_{1}C,\ldots,S_{N-1}C)

(28)

\displaystyle\check{R}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(S_{0}R^{1/2},S_{1}R^{1/2},\ldots,S_{N-1}R^{1/2})

(29)

Proposition 1
(
[
19
]
)

The cyclic reformulation
(\check{A},\check{B},\check{C},

\check{Q}^{1/2},\check{R}^{1/2})
is equivalent to the original periodic system
(A,B,S_{k}C)
with noise covariances
(Q,S_{k}RS_{k}^{T})
in the sense that if
\check{x}(0)=[x(0)^{T},0,\ldots,0]^{T}
, then the non-zero block of
\check{x}(k)
always corresponds to
x(k)
for all
k\geq 0
.

The cyclic noise covariances are given by:

\displaystyle\check{Q}

\displaystyle=\check{Q}^{1/2}(\check{Q}^{1/2})^{T}=\mathrm{diag}(Q,Q,\ldots,Q)

(30)

\displaystyle\check{R}

\displaystyle=\check{R}^{1/2}(\check{R}^{1/2})^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(S_{0}RS_{0}^{T},S_{1}RS_{1}^{T},\ldots,S_{N-1}RS_{N-1}^{T})

(31)

Remark 3.1
(Critical Observation)

The measurement noise covariance
\check{R}
is only positive semidefinite (
\check{R}\succeq 0
), not positive definite. This is because at time instants when certain sensors do not provide measurements (i.e.,
S_{k}
has zero rows), the corresponding blocks
S_{k}RS_{k}^{T}
are singular or have reduced rank.

For example, in the automotive navigation system:

•

When GPS is available (
k\bmod N=0
):
S_{0}=I_{q}

\Rightarrow

S_{0}RS_{0}^{T}=R\succ 0
(full rank)

•

When only wheel speed is available (
k\bmod N\neq 0
):
S_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(0,1)

\Rightarrow

S_{k}RS_{k}^{T}
has reduced rank

Therefore,
\check{R}\succeq 0
but
\check{R}\not\succ 0
. This semidefinite structure prevents the direct application of standard DARE methods and necessitates an LMI-based approach.

3.3
Observability Analysis

The observability of the cyclic system is crucial for the existence of a stable Kalman filter. The observability matrix for the cyclic system is:

\mathcal{O}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{C}\\
\check{C}\check{A}\\
\check{C}\check{A}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\check{C}\check{A}^{Nn-1}\end{bmatrix}

(32)

Proposition 2
(
[
19
,
20
]
)

If the pair
(A,S_{k}C)
is observable over one period (i.e., the periodic observability matrix has full rank), then the cyclic system
(\check{A},\check{C})
is observable.

This result ensures that the cyclic reformulation preserves the observability properties of the original multirate system, which is essential for the well-posedness of the Kalman filtering problem.

3.4
Challenges with Standard DARE Approach

The key advantage of cyclic reformulation is that it transforms the periodic Kalman filtering problem into a time-invariant problem. Following the approach of Fujimoto et al.
[
12
]
, one might attempt to compute the optimal cyclic Kalman gain
\check{L}
by solving the standard DARE:

\check{P}=\check{A}\check{P}\check{A}^{T}-\check{A}\check{P}\check{C}^{T}(\check{C}\check{P}\check{C}^{T}+\check{R})^{-1}\check{C}\check{P}\check{A}^{T}+\check{Q}

(33)

with the Kalman gain:

\check{L}=\check{P}\check{C}^{T}(\check{C}\check{P}\check{C}^{T}+\check{R})^{-1}

(34)

Remark 3.2

The standard DARE formulation (
33
) requires
\check{R}\succ 0
to ensure that the inverse
(\check{C}\check{P}\check{C}^{T}+\check{R})^{-1}
exists and is well-conditioned. In realistic multirate systems where sensors operate at different rates, the cyclic covariance
\check{R}
is only positive semidefinite. When
\check{R}
is singular, standard DARE solvers may fail to converge or produce numerically unstable solutions. Note that Fujimoto et al.
[
12
]
considered systems with
\check{R}\succ 0
; the semidefinite case necessitates the LMI-based approach developed in Section
4
.

3.5
Stability Analysis

The closed-loop estimation error dynamics for the cyclic system is given by:

\check{e}(k+1)=(\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C})\check{e}(k)+\check{Q}^{1/2}\check{d}_{w}(k)-\check{L}\check{R}^{1/2}\check{d}_{v}(k)

(35)

The stability of the multirate Kalman filter is determined by the spectral radius of the closed-loop matrix
\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C}
. A fundamental connection exists between the cyclic system stability and the monodromy matrix of the original periodic system.

Definition 3.1
(Monodromy Matrix)

For the periodic estimation error system with gains
L_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
, the monodromy matrix is defined as:

\Phi_{N}=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}(A-L_{N-1-k}S_{N-1-k}C)

(36)

Theorem 3.1
(Equivalence of Stability Conditions
[
19
,
25
]
)

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

The cyclic closed-loop matrix
\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C}
is Schur stable.

2.

The monodromy matrix
\Phi_{N}
is Schur stable.

3.

The estimation error covariance remains bounded for all time.

Proof 1

The equivalence between (i) and (ii) follows from the spectral mapping property of cyclic systems. Specifically, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\Phi_{N}
, then
\lambda^{1/N}e^{j2\pi m/N}
for
m=0,1,\ldots,N-1
are eigenvalues of
\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C}
. Consequently:

\rho(\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C})=\rho(\Phi_{N})^{1/N}

(37)

where
\rho(\cdot)
denotes the spectral radius. Thus
\rho(\Phi_{N})<1
if and only if
\rho(\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C})<1
. The equivalence between (ii) and (iii) follows from periodic Lyapunov theory: the periodic Lyapunov equation for the error covariance has a bounded positive definite solution if and only if
\Phi_{N}
is Schur stable.

Theorem
3.1
also guarantees the existence of periodic steady-state Kalman gains
[
27
]
: when
\Phi_{N}
is Schur stable, the periodic Riccati difference equation has a unique periodic positive definite solution, and the time-varying Kalman gains converge to periodic steady-state values
L_{k}
as
k\to\infty
. This justifies the offline computation of periodic gains.

Corollary 3.1

When the LMI optimization (
42
) in Section
4
yields a feasible solution with
\check{X}\succ 0
, the resulting filter is guaranteed to be stable, i.e.,
\rho(\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C})<1
.

This stability guarantee is inherent in the LMI formulation, as the constraint (
39
) implicitly enforces Schur stability through the Lyapunov inequality structure. The complete design procedure is presented in Section
4
.

4
LMI-based Filter Design for Multirate Systems

4.1
LMI-based Filter Design

As established in Section
3
, multirate systems with intermittent sensor measurements lead to a semidefinite measurement noise covariance
\check{R}\succeq 0
(not positive definite). The LMI-based approach offers several advantages including natural handling of semidefinite covariances, easier incorporation of robustness constraints, and guaranteed numerical stability.

4.1.1
Dual LQR Formulation

The key insight is to reformulate the Kalman filtering problem as a
dual Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)
problem
[
14
]
. The Kalman filter for the system
(\check{A},\check{C},\check{Q},\check{R})
is equivalent to the LQR problem for the dual system:

\displaystyle\check{\zeta}(k+1)

\displaystyle=\check{A}^{T}\check{\zeta}(k)+\check{C}^{T}\check{u}(k)

(38)

with state weighting
\check{Q}
and input weighting
\check{R}
. The optimal LQR gain directly yields the Kalman gain through this duality, enabling the application of LMI-based methods
[
15
]
to the filtering problem while naturally accommodating the semidefinite
\check{R}
.

4.1.2
LMI Formulation

Define the decision variables:

•

\check{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}
:
\check{X}\succ 0
(Lyapunov matrix)

•

\check{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nq}
:
\check{Y}=-\check{X}\check{L}
where
\check{L}
is the Kalman gain

The LMI formulation consists of the following constraints:

LMI #1: Stability and Performance (DARE-based LMI)

\begin{bmatrix}\check{X}&\check{X}\check{A}+\check{Y}\check{C}&\check{X}\check{Q}^{1/2}&\check{Y}\check{R}^{1/2}\\
(\check{X}\check{A}+\check{Y}\check{C})^{T}&\check{X}&0&0\\
(\check{Q}^{1/2})^{T}\check{X}&0&I_{Nn}&0\\
(\check{R}^{1/2})^{T}\check{Y}^{T}&0&0&I_{Nq}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

(39)

where
\check{Q}^{1/2}
has the same cyclic structure as
\check{A}
with
Q^{1/2}
placed in the cyclic positions, and
\check{R}^{1/2}
is the block-diagonal matrix with
S_{k}R^{1/2}
on the diagonal blocks.

LMI #2: Positive Definiteness

\check{X}\succeq\epsilon I_{Nn},\quad\epsilon>0

(40)

LMI #3: Covariance Upper Bound

To minimize trace
(\check{P}_{e})
where
\check{P}_{e}
is the steady-state error covariance satisfying
\check{P}_{e}\preceq\check{X}^{-1}
, we introduce an auxiliary variable
\check{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}
and impose:

\begin{bmatrix}\check{W}&I_{Nn}\\
I_{Nn}&\check{X}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

(41)

This constraint ensures
\check{W}\succeq\check{X}^{-1}\succeq\check{P}_{e}
. Therefore, minimizing trace
(\check{W})
provides an upper bound minimization for trace
(\check{P}_{e})
.

4.1.3
Optimization Problem

The complete LMI-based filter design problem is:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\check{X},\check{Y},\check{W}}

\displaystyle\text{trace}(\check{W})

(42)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:lmi_main}), (\ref{eq:lmi_stability}), (\ref{eq:lmi_bound})}

This is a standard semidefinite programming (SDP) problem that can be efficiently solved using interior-point methods.

4.1.4
Kalman Gain Extraction

After solving the LMI optimization, the Kalman gain is recovered from the decision variables:

\check{L}=-\check{X}^{-1}\check{Y}

(43)

The cyclic gain
\check{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nq}
must be decomposed into periodic gains
L_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
to be applied in the time-varying estimator (
20
). Due to the cyclic structure of
\check{A}
in (
24
), let
\check{L}_{(i),(j)}
denote the
n\times q
block at block-row
i
and block-column
j
of
\check{L}
. Then:

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle=\check{L}_{(2),(1)},\quad L_{k}=\check{L}_{(k+2),(k+1)}\text{ for }k=1,\ldots,N-2,

\displaystyle L_{N-1}

\displaystyle=\check{L}_{(1),(N)}

(44)

This indexing reflects the cyclic shift structure where the measurement at time
k
(located in block
k+1
of
\check{y}
) affects the state estimate at time
k+1
(located in block
(k+2)\bmod N
of
\check{x}
).

The error covariance upper bound satisfies:

\check{P}_{e}\preceq\check{X}^{-1}\preceq\check{W}

(45)

Key Advantages of LMI Formulation:

1.

Handles semidefinite
\check{R}
: Works seamlessly with
\check{R}\succeq 0

2.

Convex optimization
: Guarantees global optimality via semidefinite programming

3.

Multi-objective design
: Easy to incorporate pole placement and
l_{2}
-induced norm constraints (see Section

)

4.

Direct gain computation
: The Kalman gain is obtained as
\check{L}=-\check{X}^{-1}\check{Y}

Note:
To validate the proposed LMI solution
-\check{X}^{-1}\check{Y}
, we conducted numerical experiments with
\check{R}\succ 0
, where the standard DARE solution exists. Let
\check{K}_{\text{Ric}}
denote the filter gain obtained from the DARE. The predictor-form gain is then
\check{L}_{\text{Ric}}=\check{A}\check{K}_{\text{Ric}}
. Numerical experiments confirm
\|-\check{X}^{-1}\check{Y}-\check{L}_{\text{Ric}}\|_{F}<10^{-6}
.

4.2
Weighted Filter Design

The standard Kalman filter minimizes trace
(\check{X}^{-1})
, treating all state components equally. In practice, specific states may require higher estimation accuracy; for instance, in automotive navigation, position estimation is often more critical than acceleration estimation. This is achieved by introducing a weighting matrix
\check{\Gamma}\succ 0
into the LMI constraint (
41
):

\begin{bmatrix}\check{W}&\check{\Gamma}\\
\check{\Gamma}&\check{X}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

(46)

which ensures
\check{W}\succeq\check{\Gamma}\check{X}^{-1}\check{\Gamma}
via Schur complement. Minimizing trace
(\check{W})
then yields weighted optimal estimation, where the weight
\gamma_{i}^{2}
is applied to each diagonal element
[\check{X}^{-1}]_{ii}
for diagonal
\check{\Gamma}=\mathrm{diag}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{Nn})
.

5
Multi-objective Extensions

A key advantage of the LMI framework is its ability to incorporate multiple design objectives simultaneously. This section presents two important extensions: pole placement constraints for convergence rate guarantees and Kalman filter design with
l_{2}
-induced norm constraints for balancing average and worst-case performance.

5.0.1
Pole Placement Constraints

To guarantee a minimum convergence rate for the estimation error, we can constrain the eigenvalues of the error dynamics matrix
\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C}
to lie within a specified region. For discrete-time systems, a common choice is the disk region:

\mathcal{D}_{\bar{r}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<\bar{r}\},\quad 0<\bar{r}<1

(47)

The parameter
\bar{r}
determines the minimum decay rate: smaller
\bar{r}
implies faster convergence but may require larger gains.

Theorem 5.1
(Pole Placement via LMI
[
15
]
)

The eigenvalues of
\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C}
lie within
\mathcal{D}_{\bar{r}}
if and only if there exists
\check{X}\succ 0
such that:

(\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C})^{T}\check{X}(\check{A}-\check{L}\check{C})-\bar{r}^{2}\check{X}\prec 0

(48)

Using the variable transformation
\check{Y}=-\check{X}\check{L}
, the Schur complement yields the equivalent LMI:

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{r}^{2}\check{X}&\check{X}\check{A}+\check{Y}\check{C}\\
(\check{X}\check{A}+\check{Y}\check{C})^{T}&\check{X}\end{bmatrix}\succ 0

(49)

By Theorem
5.1
, this constraint can be added to the optimization problem (
42
) to ensure the desired convergence rate.

Design with Pole Constraints:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\check{X},\check{Y},\check{W}}

\displaystyle\text{trace}(\check{W})

(50)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:lmi_main}), (\ref{eq:lmi_stability}), (\ref{eq:lmi_bound})}

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:pole_lmi_design}) for pole placement}

5.0.2
Kalman Filter with
l_{2}
-induced Norm Constraint

The standard trace minimization corresponds to optimal Kalman filtering, which minimizes the expected estimation error under Gaussian noise. However, in practice, it is often desirable to also limit the worst-case error amplification. For time-invariant systems, this is characterized by the
H_{\infty}
norm; for periodic systems, the appropriate generalization is the
l_{2}
-induced norm.

Remark 5.1

For the cyclic system (
24
)–(
28
), although the reformulated system is time-invariant, the underlying periodic structure suggests that the performance criterion is more precisely characterized as the
l_{2}
-induced norm rather than the
H_{\infty}
norm. The LMI-based
l_{2}
-induced norm analysis and synthesis for periodically time-varying observers via cyclic reformulation have been established in
[
22
,
20
]
.

Consider the error dynamics with performance output
z(k)=C_{z}e(k)
:

e(k+1)=(A-L_{k}S_{k}C)e(k)+Q^{1/2}d_{w}(k)-L_{k}S_{k}R^{1/2}d_{v}(k)

(51)

where
d_{w}(k)
and
d_{v}(k)
are unit-covariance disturbances. Let
d(k)=[d_{w}(k)^{T},d_{v}(k)^{T}]^{T}
denote the combined disturbance. The
l_{2}
-induced norm from
d
to
z
is defined as:

\|G_{d\to z}\|_{l_{2}/l_{2}}=\sup_{\|d\|_{2}\neq 0}\frac{\|z\|_{2}}{\|d\|_{2}}

(52)

Theorem 5.2
(
l_{2}
-induced Norm via LMI
[
22
]
)

Using the variable transformation
\check{Y}=-\check{X}\check{L}
, the
l_{2}
-induced norm satisfies
\|G_{d\to z}\|_{l_{2}/l_{2}}<\gamma
if there exists
\check{X}\succ 0
such that:

\begin{bmatrix}\check{X}&\check{X}\check{A}+\check{Y}\check{C}&\check{X}\check{Q}^{1/2}&\check{Y}\check{R}^{1/2}\\
(\check{X}\check{A}+\check{Y}\check{C})^{T}&\check{X}-\check{C}_{z}^{T}\check{C}_{z}&0&0\\
(\check{Q}^{1/2})^{T}\check{X}&0&\gamma^{2}I_{Nn}&0\\
(\check{R}^{1/2})^{T}\check{Y}^{T}&0&0&\gamma^{2}I_{Nq}\end{bmatrix}\succ 0

(53)

where
\check{C}_{z}=\mathrm{diag}(C_{z},\ldots,C_{z})
is the cyclic performance output matrix. For full state estimation error (
z=e
), set
\check{C}_{z}=I_{Nn}
.

Remark 5.2
(Scaling for Feasibility)

The LMI (
53
) admits a scaling degree of freedom: replacing
\check{C}_{z}
with
\alpha\check{C}_{z}
and
\gamma^{2}
with
\gamma^{2}/\alpha^{2}
for any
\alpha>0
yields an equivalent
l_{2}
-induced norm condition. This scaling is useful when the (2,2) block
\check{X}-\check{C}_{z}^{T}\check{C}_{z}
causes infeasibility; choosing
\alpha<1
relaxes the constraint
\check{X}\succ\check{C}_{z}^{T}\check{C}_{z}
, enabling a common Lyapunov matrix
\check{X}
to satisfy both the Kalman filter LMI (
39
) and the
l_{2}
-induced norm constraint simultaneously.

Kalman Filter with
l_{2}
-induced Norm Constraint:

Based on Theorem
5.2
, the mixed design problem minimizes the estimation error covariance subject to an
l_{2}
-induced norm constraint:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\check{X},\check{Y},\check{W}}

\displaystyle\text{trace}(\check{W})

(54)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:lmi_main}), (\ref{eq:lmi_stability}), (\ref{eq:lmi_bound})}

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:l2_lmi_design}) with }\|G_{d\to z}\|_{l_{2}/l_{2}}<\bar{\gamma}

where
\bar{\gamma}
is a prescribed upper bound on the
l_{2}
-induced norm.

Remark 5.3

The (2,2) block
\check{X}-\check{C}_{z}^{T}\check{C}_{z}
in (
53
) arises from the bounded real lemma for the
l_{2}
-induced norm. When
\check{C}_{z}=I_{Nn}
, this becomes
\check{X}-I_{Nn}
, which requires
\check{X}\succ I_{Nn}
for feasibility.

5.0.3

All the above extensions can be combined into a single optimization problem:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\check{X},\check{Y},\check{W}}

\displaystyle\text{trace}(\check{W})

(55)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:lmi_main}): Kalman filter performance}

\displaystyle\check{X}\succeq\epsilon I_{Nn}\quad\text{(positive definiteness)}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\check{W}&I_{Nn}\\

\displaystyle\text{LMI (\ref{eq:pole_lmi_design}): Pole placement in }\mathcal{D}_{\bar{r}}

where
\check{\Gamma}\succ 0
is a state weighting matrix.

Conclusion

This paper has presented an LMI-based design framework for multirate steady-state Kalman filters using cyclic reformulation. In such systems, the Kalman gains converge to periodic steady-state values that repeat every frame period. The central finding is that the cyclic measurement noise covariance
\check{R}
becomes positive semidefinite—not positive definite—when sensors operate at different rates, preventing direct application of standard DARE-based methods. The proposed dual LQR formulation with LMI optimization naturally handles this semidefinite structure while enabling optimal estimation error covariance minimization and multi-objective extensions including pole placement and
l_{2}
-induced norm constrained design.

Numerical validation using state estimation in an automotive navigation system with GPS (1 Hz) and wheel speed (10 Hz) sensors confirmed that the proposed filter achieves position RMSE of 0.600 m and velocity RMSE of 0.268 m/s, well below the raw measurement noise levels. The trade-off analysis demonstrated that the LMI framework provides systematic tuning between convergence rate and estimation accuracy.

The framework is applicable to general linear time-invariant systems with periodic measurement patterns common in robotics, aerospace, and autonomous vehicles. Future directions include extensions to nonlinear systems via extended Kalman filtering, adaptive noise covariance estimation, and distributed sensor networks with asynchronous measurements.
