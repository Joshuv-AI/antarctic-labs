Comparative evaluation of the skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue effects of twin block, monoblock, and invisalign mandibular advancement appliances
Authors: Türkan Sezen Erhamza, Zeynep Çevi̇k, Ebru İlhan Koçak, Alaattin Tekeli̇
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue effects of the Twin Block, Monoblock, and Invisalign Mandibular Advancement (Invisalign MA) appliances used for functional treatment in individuals with skeletal Class II malocclusion.Materials and methodsThe study included 45 individuals in the S–MP3cap stage of growth and development who were treated with Monoblock (n = 15), Twin Block (n = 15), or Invisalign MA (n = 15) appliances. Lateral cephalometric radiographs were obtained before functional treatment (T0) and after treatment (T1). A total of 29 measurements were used to assess changes in the dental, skeletal, and soft tissues.ResultsAcross all treatment groups, significant decreases were observed in ANB°, Wits appraisal, overjet, overbite, and skeletal and soft-tissue convexity angles; similarly, significant increases were identified in SNB°, Co–Gn (mm), Pg–Na Perp, FMA°, GoGn–SN°, LFH/TFH, L1–NB°, IMPA°, and the nasolabial angle throughout the treatment period. No statistically significant differences were found among the groups regarding the changes observed in the soft tissues.ConclusionThe Invisalign MA group showed less increase in vertical parameters than the other groups. Maxillary incisor inclinations decreased more significantly in the Invisalign MA group. Mandibular incisor inclination changes were similar between Monoblock and Invisalign MA groups but greater in the Twin Block group. Maxillary molar eruption was comparable between Invisalign MA and Monoblock groups, while Twin Block showed a more pronounced increase. Finally, mandibular advancement angles were similar across all groups.

Introduction

According to McNamara, the most common characteristic of Class II malocclusions is mandibular retrusion [1]. During the active growth period, functional appliances are used to correct this condition [2]. Functional appliances act in growing individuals primarily by inducing changes in the condylar and sutural regions; correcting underlying muscular imbalances; and altering soft-tissue tone and the function of the oro-nasopharyngeal complex [3].

The treatment of Class II malocclusion falls into three main categories: growth modification, camouflage therapy, and surgical intervention. The treatment approach is determined based on the patient’s growth status, skeletal and dental relationships, and esthetic and functional needs [4].

Studies examining the permanent effects of early intervention on skeletal growth, the risk of relapse, and its contribution to the treatment process have yielded differing results, and the literature reflects diverse views on this topic [5]. Some researchers argue that malocclusion tends to worsen rather than self-correct over time and therefore emphasize the need for preventive intervention at an early stage. This approach aims to harness growth potential to control the progression of skeletal and dental discrepancies and to make the treatment process more effective and stable. Some investigators advocate initiating functional treatments ideally one to two years before the peak of the growth spurt [6, 7]. The S stage refers to the period when the sesamoid bone located at the metacarpophalangeal joint of the thumb becomes visible on the hand–wrist radiograph [8]. Various studies have shown that the growth spurt reaches its highest level approximately one year after the adductor sesamoid becomes visible on radiographs [9, 10].

According to some authors, because mandibular growth is most rapid at the peak of the growth spurt (MP3cap stage), the most optimal outcomes are achieved when treatment is carried out during this period [11, 12].

Over the years, various functional appliances have been developed to correct mandibular retrusion and guide the growth process. These appliances aim to support skeletal development and improve dentofacial harmony by positioning the mandible forward [13].

Among removable functional appliances, the Monoblock is widely preferred today due to advantages such as ease of use, more effective maintenance of oral hygiene, and lower cost. Clinical applications have shown that these appliances help guide functional jaw relationships in growing patients and support patient compliance throughout treatment [14].

The Twin Block appliance, like the Monoblock, is one of the removable functional appliances frequently preferred by orthodontists. Its widespread use is attributed not only to its ability to direct sagittal advancement of the mandible but also to its capacity to simultaneously correct transverse discrepancies in the maxilla [15]. Because the appliance consists of two separate components, it significantly improves patient compliance compared with the Monoblock, which encompasses both dental arches together. Owing to this advantage, the Twin Block has been widely adopted in the treatment of Class II malocclusion and has been extensively integrated into orthodontic practice [16].

The bulky design of the Monoblock and Twin Block appliances, their difficulty maintaining intraoral stability, and their restrictive effects on speech are among the principal factors that challenge patient compliance. These issues may reduce a patient’s motivation toward treatment, thereby diminishing the effectiveness of the process and hindering the achievement of the targeted orthopedic and orthodontic outcomes [17]. In recent years, Invisalign Mandibular Advancement (Invisalign MA), developed by Align Technology™, has been increasingly used in clinical practice, offering an esthetic alternative that—beyond aligning the teeth—also advances the mandible. By ensuring high patient satisfaction in terms of esthetics, providing greater comfort, and offering the potential to integrate skeletal and dental treatment in a coordinated manner, the Invisalign MA appliance is considered an effective alternative for functional orthodontic therapy [18].

A review of the literature reveals no study evaluating the skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue effects of the Monoblock, Twin Block, and Invisalign MA removable functional appliances in patients with skeletal Class II malocclusion. The aim of the present study is to compare, via cephalometric analyses, the effects of the Twin Block, Monoblock, and Invisalign MA appliances on skeletal, dental, and soft tissues in individuals with skeletal Class II malocclusion. The null hypothesis (H₀) tested in this study is: “There are no differences in skeletal, dental, or soft-tissue changes among individuals treated with the Twin Block, Monoblock, or Invisalign MA appliances.”

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for this retrospective study was obtained from the X University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (Date: 14.11.2024; Decision No.: 2024.11.04). Informed consent for the use of their data in research was obtained from all participants prior to the initiation of their treatment. For participants under the age of 16, consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians in accordance with ethical guidelines and institutional approval. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Individuals were included if they had mandibular retrognathia (ANB ≥ 4°, SNB < 78°), a normodivergent or hypodivergent growth pattern (GoGn-SN < 37°, FMA < 28°), and were at the S–MP3cap stage of growth and development; individuals with congenital tooth agenesis, craniofacial syndromes, a history of trauma, temporomandibular joint disorders, or systemic diseases were excluded.

A consecutive sampling approach was employed to ensure a systematic inclusion of patients between 2016 and 2024. The selection of the appliances was non-randomized and based on specific clinical indications and patient-centered preferences. The criteria for appliance selection were determined as follows; Invisalign MA was offered to patients with high aesthetic demands who preferred a digital and discreet treatment modality. Monoblock was preferred for patients with high cooperation potential who were deemed more comfortable using a single-piece appliance design rather than a two-piece alternative. Twin Block was selected for cases requiring more significant mandibular advancement or where the two-piece design was prioritized to provide greater patient comfort during functional movements. To ensure technical comparability despite the different appliance designs, all patients were required to be in the same skeletal Class II category and the same growth period (S–MP3cap). Furthermore, to ensure a reliable comparison despite the lack of randomization, we focused on longitudinal delta (Δ) changes, thereby potentially minimizing the influence of initial differences as each patient serves as their own baseline.

Based on a G*Power analysis (80% confidence level, 80% power, effect size = 0.42), a minimum of 15 patients per group was required. From the archives of the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Kırıkkale University, 45 patients who met the inclusion criteria and had been treated with removable functional appliances were identified: Twin Block (n = 15; 3 female, 12 male; mean age 12.47 ± 1.06), Monoblock (n = 15; 5 female, 10 male; mean age 12.15 ± 0.99), and Invisalign MA (n = 15; 8 female, 7 male; mean age 11.98 ± 1.54).

Hand–wrist radiographs were used to determine growth stage. In the Monoblock and Twin Block groups, 10 patients were at the MP3cap stage and 5 at the S stage; in the Invisalign MA group, 9 were at MP3cap and 6 at the S stage.

Construction bite for removable functional appliances

Plaster models were obtained from alginate impressions of the upper and lower arches. A customized wax bite rim was prepared according to the patient’s arch form, and its thickness was adjusted by adding an additional 2–3 mm to the freeway space. After placing the prepared wax rim on the maxillary arch, the construction bite was recorded by positioning the mandible forward to achieve a Class I occlusal relationship.

Appliance designs

Monoblock

The design included a labial bow of 0.7-mm round stainless-steel wire between the canines in the maxillary arch, Adams clasps on the first permanent molars, “drop-shaped” clasp bends (damla kroşe) between the premolars, and an acrylic baseplate covering the lingual, occlusal, and palatal surfaces of the teeth (Fig. 1).

Twin block

Protocol for removable functional appliances

Patients were instructed to wear the appliances approximately 20–22 h per day, except during meals. The expansion screw was activated twice weekly by a quarter-turn each time. After adequate expansion, the screw was locked and the appliance continued to be worn. Following roughly 6 months of appliance use, the posterior acrylic was progressively ground to permit eruption of the molars. The mean functional treatment duration was 12 months, and records were taken at the end of treatment as at baseline. Patients requiring fixed treatment after functional therapy were bonded with brackets.

In some patients, a pre-MA (pre-Mandibular Advancement) phase was carried out before mandibular advancement. This was indicated when one or more of the following were present: deep bite of ≥ 8 mm, retruded maxillary incisors, posterior crossbite, or pronounced rotations of the maxillary molars (≥ 20°). Necessary corrections were completed during the pre-MA phase, after which the advancement phase commenced.

During the mandibular advancement phase, alignment was performed from first premolar to first premolar in both arches. Alignment of the second premolars and first molars was not undertaken because precision wings were present in these regions. No interproximal reduction was performed; arch expansion was used as the primary mechanism during alignment. Flattening of the curve of Spee was achieved by intrusion of the lower anterior teeth.

Patients were instructed to wear the aligners approximately 20–22 h per day, except during meals. Stepwise mandibular advancement of 2 mm was implemented every 8 weeks. The mean duration of the mandibular advancement phase in the Invisalign MA group was 10 months. At the completion of this phase, records were taken as at baseline, and new aligners were prescribed to accomplish required dental refinements.

To assess the reliability of landmark identification and tracings, lateral cephalograms from 5 randomly selected patients in each group (baseline and post-treatment) were re-evaluated one month later. Eight variables were retraced on the lateral cephalometric radiographs (ANB°, FMA°, overjet, Na–Pg, U1–SN°, IMPA°, LL/E, and Gl–Sn–Pg′).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 22 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue changes among the three groups. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Tukey HSD test to identify specific differences between the groups. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate sex distribution across groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare age and treatment duration. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To determine agreement among cephalometric measurements, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used. For intra-observer reliability and repeatability, ICC values were interpreted as follows: < 0.70, poor agreement; 0.70–0.90, good agreement; and > 0.90, excellent agreement [19].

Results

ICC values exceeded 0.95, indicating excellent reliability of the measurements.

There was no significant difference in sex distribution among the groups (p = 0.158; χ² = 3.6). In the Invisalign MA group, 53.3% (n = 8) were female and 46.7% (n = 7) were male; in the Monoblock group, 33.3% (n = 5) were female and 66.7% (n = 10) were male; and in the Twin Block group, 20% (n = 3) were female and 80% (n = 12) were male, for a total of 16 females (35.6%) and 29 males (64.4%).

No significant difference was found among the groups with respect to age (p = 0.624; H = 0.942). The mean age in the Twin Block group was 12.47 ± 1.06 years (median 12.25; min 10.50, max 14.83); in the Monoblock group, 12.15 ± 0.99 years (median 12; min 10.33, max 14); and in the Invisalign MA group, 11.98 ± 1.54 years (median 12; min 9.58, max 15.08).

Treatment duration differed significantly among the groups (p = 0.008): 12.6 ± 2.1 months in the Twin Block group, 12.4 ± 1.6 months in the Monoblock group, and 10.5 ± 2.2 months in the Invisalign MA group.

Baseline (T0) values for the Twin Block, Monoblock, and Invisalign MA groups are presented in Table 1. At T0, five variables differed among groups (SNA°, SNB°, GoGn–SN°, A–Na Perp, soft-tissue convexity angle). In the Twin Block group, SNA°, SNB°, and A–Na Perp were higher, whereas GoGn–SN° was lower. These findings indicate that patients in the Twin Block group had more protrusive maxillae and mandibles and a more horizontal growth pattern. The soft-tissue convexity angle was lower in the Monoblock group.

Discussion

Removable functional appliances, used to prevent the progression of skeletal and dental discrepancies and to render treatment more effective and stable, aim to support skeletal development and improve dentofacial harmony [1]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has evaluated the skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue effects of the Monoblock, Twin Block, and Invisalign MA appliances in patients with skeletal Class II malocclusion; for this reason, the present study was undertaken.

In the existing literature, the effects of functional treatments have not been examined with respect to the S and MP3cap stages of the pubertal growth spurt. In our study, all individuals were classified according to these growth stages. No statistically significant differences were found between the S and MP3cap distributions among the groups; similarly, no significant differences emerged in either within-group or between-group comparisons for treatments delivered during these two stages.

In treatments performed with removable functional appliances, in addition to the sagittal growth of the mandible, vertical growth also occurs as a result of its posterior rotation. Therefore, decreased or normal vertical angles are preferred in removable functional treatments [2]. In our study, FMA and GoGn-SN angles were used to evaluate vertical growth.

There are numerous studies investigating the differences between incremental advancement and single-step advancement in functional [3–5]. These studies have reported that outcomes obtained through incremental advancement are more stable [4, 5]. Another study conducted on this subject found that skeletal effects were more pronounced in treatments involving stepwise advancement [3]. Furthermore, Graber stated that mandibular incisor inclination is better preserved with incremental advancement, and the increase in incisor inclination is less compared to single-step advancement [20]. It has also been reported that patients undergoing incremental advancement experience less discomfort and fewer speech problems, which consequently improves patient compliance compared to single-step advancement [3, 20].

One of the most critical factors in achieving optimal treatment success with functional appliances is the patient’s compliance by wearing the appliance for a sufficient duration during the day [21]. While only nighttime wear was recommended when functional appliances were first introduced, research supporting that this regimen did not induce sufficient mandibular changes led to the adoption of daytime wear as well [2, 22]. Baccetti et al. instructed patients to wear the Twin Block appliance at all times except during meals and sports activities [23]. In our study, the patients included were instructed to wear their appliances for 20–22 h per day, and all three appliances were used continuously except during meals.

Considering the T1–T0 findings, the Twin Block, Monoblock, and Invisalign MA appliances were associated with similar outcomes in the treatment of Class II malocclusion. Across all three groups, increases in SNB°, Co–Gn, Pg–Na Perp, L1–NB°, and IMPA°, together with decreases in the skeletal and soft-tissue convexity angles and in U1–SN°, U1–PP°, U1–FH°, and U1–NA (mm), characterize the correction of Class II malocclusion during the observation period. These findings represent the combined effects of functional therapy and the patients’ natural growth potential.

Consistent with the literature [18, 24], an orthopedic effect was achieved in all three groups in our study, with significant increases in SNB°, Co–Gn, and Pg–Na Perp across all appliances. No differences were detected among the Monoblock, Twin Block, and Invisalign MA appliances for these variables. In studies comparing Twin Block and Invisalign MA, Lombardo et al. [25] and Blackham [26] likewise found no difference between the two appliances in terms of change in SNB angle and reported that both appliances act via similar mechanisms and are effective for treating Class II malocclusion.

Changes in ANB° and Wits were also observed in all three groups. Because the Wits appraisal is influenced by positional changes in the incisors and molars and by consequent alterations in the occlusal plane [27], significant differences were found among groups for ANB°, whereas no significant differences were detected for Wits. In agreement with our findings, Elfouly et al. [28], Lombardo et al. [25], and Blackham [26] also reported no intergroup differences in Wits.

With respect to ANB°, and in line with the results of Wu et al. [29], Talay [30], and Caruso et al. [18], our study found the smallest reduction in ANB in the Invisalign MA group. In our cohort, patients in the Invisalign MA group presented at baseline with more protrusive maxillary incisors; therefore, treatment planning targeted a reduction in their inclination. We believe this may have limited sagittal mandibular advancement, resulting in a smaller change in ANB. Furthermore, the greatest changes in U1–SN°, U1–PP°, U1–FH°, and U1–NA (mm) were observed in the Invisalign MA group, which can be attributed to the treatment plan emphasizing a more pronounced correction of the maxillary incisor angulation and position in this group.

In all three groups, SNA and A–Na Perp showed non-significant increases, and no intergroup differences were detected. Similarly, Cao et al. [31], Yu et al. [32], Wu et al. [29], and Elfouly et al. [28] reported no differences among groups in SNA.

In Blackham’s study comparing the Twin Block and Invisalign MA appliances, no significant intergroup differences were reported for either SNA or A–Na Perp values [26].

In the literature on functional treatments, some studies have reported decreases in SNA [26, 28, 30], while others have reported non-significant increases [29, 31]. Although this might suggest that functional therapy restricts maxillary growth, the Nasion point also changes with normal growth and development. In a study comparing radiographs taken at two time points in 243 growing individuals, Björk reported bony apposition on the frontal surface in the glabellar region [33]. The absence of statistically significant changes in SNA° and A–Na Perp in our study may therefore be related to anterior (sagittal) displacement of the Nasion point due to ongoing growth.

In our study, skeletal vertical measurements (FMA°, GoGn–SN°, P–A Face Height, UFH/TFH, LFH/TFH) and dental vertical measurements (U6–PP [mm], L6–MP [mm]) changed less in the Invisalign MA group. Similarly, Blackham [26] reported smaller changes in FMA°, P–A Face Height, UFH/TFH, LFH/TFH, U6–PP (mm), and L6–MP (mm); Talay [30] reported smaller changes in GoGn–SN°, LFH, TFH, and L6–MP (mm); and Hosseini [34] found the smallest change in LFH in the Invisalign MA group. Tooth movements achievable during functional treatment with Invisalign MA may account for these differences, and posterior acrylic grinding performed in the Monoblock and Twin Block groups may also have contributed to vertical changes.

Regarding vertical changes in the incisors, there was no significant intergroup difference for U1–PP (mm), whereas L1–MP (mm) did differ among groups. The change in L1–MP (mm) was smaller in the Twin Block group than in the other groups. Burstone reported that incisor protrusion is accompanied by relative intrusion [35], and Celebi noted that intrusion increases in proportion to incisor protrusion [36]. In our study, the smaller change in L1–MP (mm) in the Twin Block group is thought to be related to greater mandibular incisor protrusion in that group. Similarly, Cao et al. [31] found a smaller change in L1–MP (mm) in the Twin Block group, along with larger changes in L1–NB° and L1–MP°, reflecting greater alterations in mandibular incisor angulation.

Contrary to many studies in the literature [30–32], we found a significant within-group increase in L1–NB and IMPA angles, but no significant differences between groups. Hosseini et al. [34] and Talay [30] reported decreases in mandibular incisor angles in the Invisalign MA group, whereas Yu et al. [32] and Cao et al. [31] reported smaller increases in mandibular incisor angles in the Invisalign MA group compared with other groups. Although numerous studies [37, 38] suggest that Invisalign MA is more successful than traditional functional appliances in controlling mandibular incisor angulation, studies by Caruso et al. [18], Lombardo et al. [25], and Blackham [26]—which compared Invisalign MA with the Twin Block—found, similar to our results, no differences between the two groups in changes to mandibular incisor angles.

Regarding changes in overjet, all three groups showed a statistically significant reduction, with no between-group differences. However, the Invisalign MA group exhibited the largest changes in U1–SN°, U1–PP°, U1–FH°, and U1–NA (mm) and the smallest change in ANB°, indicating that the reduction in overjet in this group occurred predominantly at the dentoalveolar level. In contrast to our findings, Caruso et al. [18] reported a difference in overjet reduction between the Invisalign MA and Twin Block groups, with a greater decrease in the Twin Block group attributable to more pronounced dental changes. A systematic review and meta-analysis of nine clinical studies involving 283 subjects [32] comparing mandibular advancement using traditional functional appliances versus clear aligners found, consistent with our results, no significant intergroup differences in overjet.

The statistically significant decrease in overbite observed across all groups appears to be associated with increases in U6–PP (mm) and L6–MP (mm) and with increases in vertical skeletal measurements such as FMA and GoGn–SN. In the Monoblock and Twin Block groups, posterior acrylic grinding and the resultant molar extrusion, along with vertical mandibular growth, may have contributed to the reduction in overbite. In the Invisalign MA group, in addition to vertical mandibular growth, the absence of attachments on teeth in the regions of the precision wings may have permitted posterior extrusion, which likely contributed to the decrease in overbite.

Lee et al. [39] reported that Pg is the most reliable reference point for evaluating changes in mandibular position. In our study, variables reflecting the sagittal position of the mandible—SNB and Pg–Na Perp—increased significantly in all groups. A significant decrease in the NAPg angle and a significant increase in Co–Gn, which reflects effective mandibular length, in all three groups further support this finding.

Numerous studies [40, 41] have demonstrated that skeletal changes directly affect soft-tissue morphology and that there is a significant correlation between these two components. In agreement with these reports, we observed that the significant reduction in the skeletal convexity angle was paralleled by a reduction in the soft-tissue convexity angle in all groups. No significant intergroup differences were detected for either angle, leading to the conclusion that the increased convexity angles characteristic of Class II malocclusion can be effectively reduced with the functional appliances used. Similarly, Cao et al. [31] and Blackham [26] reported no significant intergroup differences for either skeletal or soft-tissue convexity angles.

When examining studies comparing Invisalign MA with traditional functional orthodontic appliances [28, 29, 34], soft-tissue changes appear to have been largely neglected. However, the importance of soft-tissue parameters in evaluating the esthetic outcomes of orthodontic treatment has been increasingly recognized. In this context, our study sought to assess not only skeletal and dental changes but also the effects on soft tissues.

Soft-tissue evaluation focused on the convexity angle, nasolabial angle, and the UL/E and LL/E distances (upper and lower lip to the E-line). Although each group exhibited certain changes in these parameters over the course of treatment, between-group comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences.

In all groups, the significant decreases observed in U1–SN°, U1–PP°, U1–FH°, and U1–NA (mm) were paralleled by a significant increase in the nasolabial angle. This increase is consistent with the results of numerous studies in the literature [37, 38]. We also believe that the statistically significant decrease in UL/E values across all groups is related to the reductions in maxillary incisor inclination and position (U1–SN, U1–PP, U1–FH, and U1–NA [mm]). Likewise, the statistically significant increase in LL/E values observed in all three groups is thought to be associated with the significant increases in IMPA, L1–NB, and SNB angles found across the groups. Similar to our UL/E findings, many Twin Block studies have reported upper-lip retraction [39, 42].

Studies conducted with Invisalign MA have likewise reported upper-lip retraction, in line with our results [38, 43]. Our intergroup findings for UL/E and LL/E values are also in agreement with those of Varlık et al. [44] and Blackham [26].

It has been stated that a treatment period of at least 6 months is necessary for the stimulation of mandibular growth with functional appliances [6, 23, 45]. Graber reported that the majority of occlusal effects occurring in functional treatments are observed after the 6th month of treatment [7]. Furthermore, Ruf and Pancherz reported that at least 8 months of treatment is required for changes to occur in the temporomandibular joint [6].

In the present study, significant differences were observed in treatment durations between the groups. While these variations could potentially influence the magnitude of the outcomes, it should be emphasized that treatment was continued in all groups until a successful Class I relationship was established. The shorter duration observed in certain groups might be attributed to the specific biomechanical efficiency of the appliance or higher levels of patient compliance. Nevertheless, the influence of treatment time on the total amount of skeletal versus dental change remains a factor that should be considered when interpreting the results.

In a study by Wu et al., treatment durations were reported as 7.28 ± 2.30 months for the Vanbeek Activator, 10.18 ± 3.06 months for the Herbst appliance, 10.18 ± 3.06 months for the Twin Block appliance, and 22.84 ± 8.98 months for the Invisalign MA appliance [8]. Hosseini et al. reported that treatment duration lasted 14.4 months with Invisalign MA and 18.5 months with Herbst [9]. Ravera et al. stated that Invisalign MA treatments performed during the CVM2 stage lasted 18 months, while those in the CVM3 stage lasted 17 months [46]. Liu et al. reported a treatment duration of 28 months in their study with Invisalign MA [10]. Conversely, An et al. reported an average treatment duration of 11.3 months with Invisalign MA [11].

Based on the studies in the literature, it has been observed that Invisalign MA treatment durations have decreased over the years. This situation is thought to be related to technological advancements as well as the aligner design becoming more efficient. The fact that the treatment duration in the Invisalign MA group was found to be shorter compared to the other groups in our study may also be linked to this progression.

When vertical changes were evaluated, and contrary to many previous studies [25, 31, 37], the Invisalign MA group showed slight increases in vertical measurements, indicating a clockwise rotation of the mandible. In the 1990s, clinicians observed that Invisalign treatment could deepen overbite and suggested that aligners covering all posterior teeth might act like bite-blocks, producing intrusion of the posterior teeth [47]. However, the approximately 0.76-mm thickness of the aligners and, in some patients, a large freeway space may prevent an adequate neuromuscular response; moreover, the contact time of opposing teeth is limited to an average of about 18 min per day, making the generation of clinically meaningful intrusive forces unlikely [48]. This may explain the small increases in vertical values observed in the Invisalign MA group in our study.

In functional treatments, many researchers have stated that proclination occurs in the mandibular incisors due to the effect of mesial forces aimed at achieving mandibular protrusion [12]. Various criticisms have been made regarding the retroclination of maxillary incisors and the proclination of mandibular incisors during the Class II treatment with functional appliances [13, 14]. j In a study by Caruso et al., a comparison was made between clear aligners with precision wings and Twin Block appliances; it was found that overjet correction was twice as high in the Twin Block group (3.3 mm compared to 1.4 mm, respectively). Furthermore, it was determined that the positions of the maxillary incisors and the proclination of the mandibular incisors were more controlled in the clear aligner group with precision wings. Unlike functional appliances such as the Twin Block, clear aligners have a biomechanical effect on controlling the inclination of the incisors due to their design that encompasses the crown surface of the tooth [15]. This finding indicates that lower dentoalveolar compensation does not occur during the skeletal correction of Class II malocclusion [15].

The advantage of simultaneous tooth movement offered by clear aligners, combined with the divergence in biomechanical principles among the appliances, constitutes a limitation regarding the direct comparability of the different appliance types used in our study.

One of the limitations of the present study is its retrospective nature and the lack of randomization in patient allocation. Additionally, the presence of certain baseline variations in skeletal parameters such as SNA, SNB, and GoGn-SN among the treatment groups. In clinical studies involving functional appliances, achieving perfect homogeneity at the baseline can be challenging due to the inherent morphological diversity of Class II malocclusions. To address this potential selection bias and ensure a more reliable comparison, the treatment outcomes were primarily evaluated through delta (Δ) changes (T1-T0). By focusing on the net amount of change rather than absolute post-treatment values, it was aimed for each patient to serve as their own control, thereby potentially minimizing the impact of initial skeletal differences.

The lack of an untreated control group is a significant limitation of this study. Since all patients were in the pubertal growth spurt (S-MP3cap), some of the skeletal changes, particularly the increase in mandibular length, may have occurred due to natural growth. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution.

Another limitation is the lack of long-term follow-up and evaluation of the final orthodontic phase. However, the primary objective of this study was to isolate and compare the immediate skeletal and dental responses to three different functional mechanics during the peak growth period. Future studies evaluating the stability of these changes after the comprehensive fixed appliance phase would provide further insight into the long-term clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Invisalign MA, Monoblock, and Twin Block therapies were associated with a decrease in overjet and an increase in overbite, while the observed soft-tissue changes contributed to favorable effects on the facial profile. Vertical skeletal and dental changes tended to be smaller in the Invisalign MA group compared to the other appliances.

The null hypothesis was rejected for skeletal and dental measurements due to observed intergroup differences; for soft-tissue changes, it was accepted because no intergroup differences were detected.
