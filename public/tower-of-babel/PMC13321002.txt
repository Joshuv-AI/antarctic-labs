Generating drug resistance models in human and murine cancer cell lines and assessing cross-resistance to chemotherapeutics and KRAS inhibitors
Authors: Nicole Martinez, Maria Fumagalli, Ravindra Gujar, Lev Becker, Chang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDrug resistance and cross-resistance limit the durability of cancer therapies, yet reproducible in vitro techniques for generating resistant models remain limited. Here, we present a protocol for generating human and murine cancer cell lines with drug resistance to chemotherapeutics and KRAS inhibitors. We describe steps for generating, expanding, and maintaining resistant cells. We then detail procedures for validating drug resistance and cross-resistance, followed by a quantitative killing assay. This approach facilitates the evaluation of resistance and cross-resistance liabilities of compounds.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Gujar et al.1

Before you begin

Acquired resistance1 to cancer therapies is a major clinical challenge that limits the durability of treatment responses.2,3 Resistance to an initial therapy is often accompanied by the emergence of cross-resistance, wherein resistance to one treatment is accompanied by reduced sensitivity to other therapies, often within the same drug class or targeting related pathways.3,4 Although a variety of in vitro approaches have been used to study drug resistance,5 fewer systems are designed to model drug resistance, and potentially acquired resistance with follow up validation work, in a manner that supports systematic evaluation of cross-resistance and downstream mechanistic studies under standardized conditions.

To address this gap, this protocol was developed and validated using four chemotherapeutics – doxorubicin, oxaliplatin, paclitaxel, and carboplatin – and two KRAS inhibitors, AMG-510 (KRAS G12C) and MRTX1133 (KRAS G12D), across multiple human and murine cancer cell lines. These agents were chosen because of their widespread clinical use,6,7 the predominance of tumor cell-intrinsic resistance mechanisms,8,9,10,11 and the frequent observation of cross-resistance following treatment failure.8,12

The protocol outlines a generalized in vitro framework for inducing drug resistance through five cycles of drug exposure at defined concentrations, coupled with systematic monitoring of cell survival and recovery. Detailed guidance is provided for treatment cycling, dose adjustment, recovery assessment, criteria for successful resistance establishment, and standardized killing assays to quantify both resistance and cross-resistance. Resistant cell populations generated using this approach are immediately suitable for downstream analyses. It should be noted that the model systems described are drug-resistant, encompassing both intrinsic and acquired resistance. Follow-up validation such as clonal analysis or mutational profiling is required to confirm acquired resistance specifically.

Using this framework, we show that chemotherapies or KRAS inhibitors reproducibly induce drug resistance in murine and human cancer cells, typically within five treatment cycles. Moreover, chemotherapeutic- or KRAS inhibitor-resistant cell lines exhibited resistance to other agents within the same drug class, recapitulating clinically observed patterns of cross-resistance1 and validating the use of this system for cross-resistance assessment. In contrast, N17350, a therapeutic elastase, did not induce resistance under the same conditions, and its efficacy was not affected by cross-resistance to other therapeutic modalities.1

Although broadly applicable, the rate and magnitude of resistance development may vary depending on the cell line, therapeutic agent, and dosing strategy. While several observable features during resistance generation may indicate a trajectory toward acquired resistance, appropriate follow-up validation is required for confirmation. Accordingly, critical notes throughout the protocol highlight parameters that may require optimization for specific experimental contexts.

Innovation

This protocol establishes a standardized in vitro framework that directly couples drug resistance generation with cross-resistance testing. Unlike prior approaches that treat resistance as a terminal endpoint or focus on single agents, this method creates a drug-resistant model as a reusable experimental state that can be interrogated across therapies.

A key innovation is the integration of resistance generation and cross-resistance assessment within a unified workflow. By defining reproducible treatment cycles, recovery criteria, and resistance validation benchmarks, the protocol generates stable resistant cell populations that can be directly challenged with secondary agents using standardized killing assays. This enables direct evaluation of whether resistance to a primary therapy confers cross-resistance to related or mechanistically distinct compounds under matched selection pressures.

Importantly, because the R5 populations may encompass both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms, this protocol also serves as a versatile entry point for researchers seeking to study either resistance type with the option to apply downstream clonal or mutational analyses to further dissect the underlying biology.

Prepare drug for resistance induction

Timing: ∼1 h

In some cases, resistance to the selecting agent was accompanied by cross-resistance to additional therapeutics. As shown in Figure 4B, PTX R5 cells exhibited reduced sensitivity to doxorubicin (DOX), a chemotherapeutic agent with a distinct primary mechanism of action. Although these agents differ mechanistically, cross-resistance between paclitaxel and doxorubicin has been reported previously and may arise from shared cell-intrinsic resistance mechanisms, such as adaptive stress-response pathways.11

This protocol is designed to be practical, scalable, and broadly applicable. The five-cycle framework can be completed within 1–2 months, offering a rapid turnaround compared to classical stepwise dose-escalation approaches that may require several months. The protocol has been validated across four standard-of-care chemotherapies and two KRAS inhibitors in multiple human and murine cancer cell lines, demonstrating its versatility across drug classes and cellular contexts. As such, it can serve as a starting point for researchers working with other cytotoxic agents or cell lines not listed here.

Beyond compound screening, the drug-resistant R5 populations generated by this protocol can serve as a resource for downstream mechanistic studies. Observable features during resistance generation, including progressive changes in drug tolerance and morphological shifts consistent with epithelial-mesenchymal transition, may provide preliminary indicators of the resistance trajectory. With appropriate follow-up tools such as clonal analysis or mutational profiling, researchers can further isolate and characterize intrinsic or acquired resistant subpopulations, making this protocol a versatile platform for studying drug resistance biology.

Limitations

This workflow is optimized for adherent cell lines, where drug addition, washout, and medium replacement can be performed without centrifugation, enabling selective removal of dead and dying cells by aspiration. When applied to suspension cell lines, additional centrifugation steps are required to retain viable cells; however, dead or dying cells may co-pellet with live cells, reducing the precision of selection. As a result, selection pressure may be difficult to control in suspension cultures, and additional optimization (e.g., dead-cell removal or viability gating) would be required.

It is important to note that the resistance induction approach described here, which begins at concentrations inducing 70%–90% cell death, differs from classical stepwise dose-escalation protocols that start at approximately sub-IC50 concentrations and gradually increase over several months.5 While stepwise escalation may better distinguish truly acquired resistance from pre-existing resistant subclones (intrinsic), it requires significantly longer timelines and may not be practical for systematic cross-resistance screening across multiple agents and cell lines, which is one important goal for this protocol.

This workflow produces heterogeneous “R5” populations shaped by treatment-induced depletion and subsequent regrowth. In other words, the R5 populations generated by this protocol have not been formally validated as models of purely acquired resistance through clonal analysis, mutational profiling, or long-term resistance stability testing upon drug withdrawal. The R5 populations should therefore be considered drug-resistant models that may encompass both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms. Further validation is required to confirm the nature of resistance in specific experimental contexts.

Additionally, the morphological changes observed during resistance induction, including cell flattening and spindle-like morphology (see Figure 5), are consistent with epithelial-mesenchymal transition, a phenotype associated with acquired drug resistance.14,15 Whether these changes are reversible upon drug withdrawal, and whether resistance is maintained in the absence of drug treatment, are important questions that warrant further investigation. The selection trigger of approximately 70%–90% cell death is determined by brightfield assessment and may vary depending on operator judgment, cell line morphology, and drug-specific death phenotypes (e.g., detachment, rounding, etc). While this approach minimizes cell manipulation and avoids disturbing surviving adherent cells, it is inherently subjective. Reagent-based assays such as alamarBlue may provide a more objective measure; however, alamarBlue reflects metabolic activity and proliferation rather than cell death directly, and its addition introduces an extra manipulation step that may interfere with the recovery phase.

The Calcein-AM 72 hour killing assay is robust for viability but can be influenced by dye loading/efflux differences, metabolic state, and cell density effects. Some resistance phenotypes (slow-cycling, senescent) may appear “alive” but not proliferative; complementary assays (e.g., long-term outgrowth/colony formation) can be informative.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Excessive cell death occurs rapidly (<24 h) following drug exposure (Major Step 4).

Potential solution

The initial drug concentration may exceed the tolerance of the tested cell line, resulting in acute cytotoxicity rather than partial selection. Reduce the starting dose by 2–5-fold, or as indicated in publicly available data, and repeat the first cycle while keeping the exposure duration constant. Performing a preliminary 72 hour dose-response assay (e.g., using Calcein-AM) to estimate the EC50 for the specific cell line can help identify a starting concentration that reproducibly yields ∼70%–90% killing.

Problem 2

Treated cells fail to recover to ∼90% confluency after drug washout (Major Step 4).

Potential solution

Failure to recover typically reflects excessive cumulative stress rather than an inability to develop drug resistance. As illustrated in Figure 3, leverage duplicate treatment wells to test alternative adjustment strategies within the same cycle. In one replicate, reduce the duration of drug exposure while maintaining the same concentration; in the other replicate, decrease the drug concentration by more than two-fold while maintaining the original exposure duration. Allow both conditions to recover and select the strategy that supports reproducible regrowth for the next cycle.

Ensure that recovery medium is fully supplemented and freshly prepared and avoid disturbing surviving cells during the early recovery phase. If recovery is extremely slow or appears stalled, gently lift the cells and replate them into a smaller well format (e.g., 48-well plate) to facilitate recovery and expansion.

Problem 3

During resistance induction, cells may undergo noticeable morphological changes, including alterations in size, shape, and adherence (Figure 5), which can complicate interpretation of cell health and treatment response (Major Step 4).

Potential solution

Morphological changes may reflect alterations in physical appearance such as cell flattening and enlargement or the development of spindle-like shape. Monitor cell morphology daily and document changes across cycles. If pronounced abnormalities persist over multiple cycles, extend the drug-free recovery phase to allow population stabilization before re-treatment. If morphology remains unstable, consider lowering selective pressure or restarting the selection using a reduced starting dose. Resistant populations should exhibit reproducible morphology across passages prior to downstream killing assays.

Problem 4

Loss of resistance phenotype after drug withdrawal or freeze thaw following liquid N2 storage (Major Step 5).

Potential solution

Some resistance states are partially reversible and require continued selective pressure for maintenance. After completion of five treatment cycles, maintain R5 cells under low-dose drug pressure (see Major Step 11), typically ∼1% of the original treatment concentration, during expansion. Avoid prolonged culture in drug-free conditions prior to validation. Freezing resistant populations before validation is discouraged, as freeze-thaw recovery may lead to loss of resistance due to population heterogeneity. If resistance is lost, the selection process must be re-established.

Problem 5

Timing mismatch between passage-matched control (R0) cells and treated cells (Major Step 6).

Potential solution

Differences in recovery times between treatment cycles can cause control cells to reach experimental readiness earlier than treated cells. To maintain synchronization, passage control cells only at the start of each treatment cycle and seed them at reduced density so they remain sub-confluent throughout the cycle. If control cells reach expansion readiness more than two weeks before treated cells, cryopreserve control cells after expansion and thaw them immediately prior to setting up killing assays. This ensures comparable passage number and minimizes confounding effects of prolonged culture.
