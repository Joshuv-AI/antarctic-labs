3. Executive Summary:

 This project aims to advance the manufacturing of high-efficiency perovskite
 photovoltaics.

 To facilitate the processing of tandem devices, and perovskite single-junction and
 multi-junction devices more generally, the project team developed a lamination
 process to integrate the subcells and cells, decoupling the processing from the
 restrictive requirements of serial manufacturing. The lamination approach enables
 independent and precise tailoring of interface properties for stability and
 performance.

 In the first Budget Period, we aimed to validate the promise of a novel low gap
 perovskite by understanding the influence of minor alloying elements on
 optoelectronic quality and evaluating performance in cells, maintaining a bandgap
 below those previously reported. Although infrared photoconductivity was achieved
 beyond 1000 nm, no substantial photovoltage was attainable using the alloyed
 perovskite. This effort was discontinued in favor of allocating greater focus to the
 lamination processing of perovskite devices.

 Throughout the project period, we de-risked the lamination approach to perovskite
 cell manufacturing by examining the dependence of efficiency on process variables.
 After thorough investigation, the project team reported on laminated perovskite cells
 reaching >21% power conversion efficiency. The role of temperature, pressure, and
 time on lamination quality and device outcomes was carefully investigated. Particular
 attention was also paid to the survival of laminated cells after thermal cycling
 accelerated testing given the glass-glass architecture of the laminated device. The
 team demonstrated that even after thermal cycling, laminated perovskite cells retain
 an interfacial toughness comparable to pristine, serial-manufactured cells containing
 common small molecule transport layers.

 Lamination processing of perovskite solar cells is determined to be a promising
 direction to provide flexibility in materials selection for both transport layers, for “self-
 encapsulation” of the environmentally sensitive absorber, and for achieving tough
 packages.

5. Background:
 To reduce manufacturing costs and achieve the high efficiencies required to meet
 the SunShot 2030 goal of 3¢/kWh, tandem perovskite-on-X PVs present a promising
 pathway. Multi-junction solar cells hold promise to dramatically exceed the efficiency
 potential of today’s Si and CdTe. 2-Terminal Perovskite-Perovskite tandem PVs
 (PPTPVs) and perovskite-Si tandems have made substantial progress in the past 5
 years, rising in efficiency from 24% to 30% and 29% to almost 35%, respectively.
 While perovskite-Si tandems show near-term promise, PPTPVs provide a longer-
 term pathway to ultimately breakthrough the asymptotic approach to the price floor
 set by the Si bottom cell. For both tandem technologies, severe challenges have
 arisen in achieving stable top cell perovskite operation when mixing I and Br. State-
 of-the-art triple-halide 1.67 eV top cells achieve t80>1000 hrs by avoiding high Br
 content via partial substitution of bulkier dimethylammonium on the A-site cation,1 or
 introducing not only Br but also Cl on the X-site.2 However, in PPTPVs they sacrifice
 almost 8% absolute PCE due to a sub-optimal spectral match with state-of-the-art
 bottom cells.
 A large increase in tandem PCE (5-10% abs.) would be possible by moving to a
 lower bandgap bottom cell. A lower bandgap perovskite bottom cell shifts the optimal
 top cell bandgap downward and raises the efficiency limits when paired with today’s
 best subcells. A 0.85 eV bandgap cell forms an ideal 1-sun pairing with a 1.61 eV
 top cell – a match for today’s mixed-A-site, low-bromide (x<0.2) chemistries (e.g.,
 CsFAMAPb(I0.83Br0.17)3) that have been heavily engineered worldwide for single-
 junction PV. No substantial chemical alteration to such “top cells” is required to
 unlock efficiencies up to 37% using the 0.85/1.6 eV tandem pairing investigated, if
 the bottom cell were to achieve the 1% EQE EL already achieved by the 1.6 eV
 perovskites.
 Meanwhile, tandem integration beyond research scale (~1 cm2) remains a significant
 engineering and manufacturing challenge, made difficult by the sequential deposition
 and optimization of 10+ device layers. Today, sequential layer-by-layer
 manufacturing requires multiple processing steps after perovskite deposition, which
 results in sub-optimal processing both in terms of expense and performance, in
 order to avoid damage to the perovskite. Strategies to scale perovskite-on-X solar
 cells to large areas are limited by the challenges of: 1) making high-quality, cost-
 effective stacks of multiple thin films; 2) engineering optimized interfaces; 3)
 minimizing degradation mechanisms, especially those due to the diffusion of reactive
 ions and small molecules, and 4) large-area contact metallization and
 interconnection. To overcome these challenges, ideally, a high-quality and low
 defect-density perovskite materials can be fabricated and subsequently “sealed in”
 by high-performance and chemically stable charge transport layers, with few post-
 processing steps.

6. Project Objectives:

 Perovskites present a huge potential for the US PV industry to jump ahead of the
 competition. This project aims to advance the manufacturing of high-efficiency
 perovskite photovoltaics. To facilitate the processing of these tandem devices, and
 perovskite single-junction and multi-junction devices more generally, we developed a
 lamination process to integrate the subcells and cells, decoupling the processing
 from the restrictive requirements of serial manufacturing. The lamination approach
 enabled independent and precise tailoring of interface properties for stability and
 performance.

 The project investigated the development of a new low bandgap perovskite suitable
 for application in a tandem photovoltaic with 1.6 eV top cells. In Budget Period 1,
 project effort evaluated the low-bandgap absorber and cell largely in parallel with
 development of lamination processing for perovskite solar cell. Task 1 focused on
 the ultra-low bandgap absorber and cell development, beginning with composition
 optimization, then cell development. Task 2 developed optimized lamination
 processing, beginning with state-of-the-art perovskite compositions in-hand. Budget
 Period 1 GNG milestones included demonstrating >10% PCE ultra-low Eg cells,
 >15% PCE laminated cells, and submission of a conference manuscript or journal
 abstract. In Budget Period 2, Task 3 merged these two efforts to produce high
 spectral-efficiency tandem perovskite solar cells by lamination. The work effort of
 Task 4 then validated the durability and potential scalability of laminated devices.
 Task 5 addressed durability through chemical modification of perovskite/transport
 layer interfaces. Task 6 focused efforts around understanding the effect of
 lamination process parameter on device durability under accelerated aging tests.
 Budget Period 2 GNG milestones included demonstrating >0.6J/m2 laminated
 interfacial toughness, testing the interfacial toughness after subjecting samples to
 standard thermal cycling tests, submission of ≥2 journal manuscripts. End of project
 (EOP) goals included demonstrating device durability toward 1-sun/85˚C accelerated
 testing (ISOS-L-2), realizing a 22% perovskite-perovskite tandem cell with
 t80>500hrs under 1-sun Voc light soaking, and the submission of a conference
 abstract or journal article offering our perspective on lamination of perovskites.
 Throughout the project, results and milestones were confirmed using a statistically
 meaningful assembly of data from samples equivalent to typically one weeks’
 fabrication effort.

 SETO support was critical to enable retiring of risks in this aggressive project that
 compliments other programmatic activities on single- and multi-junctions. There
 were several unique aspects of this project with strong potential for IP development,
 including development of the low Eg perovskite, process control of the lamination
 parameters, machine design for continuous encapsulation and bonding, and tandem
 device architectures. The interdisciplinary team of materials scientists, mechanical
 engineers, and chemists will bring new perspectives through collaboration that were
 not possible without DOE support. Archival publications and documented
 discussions with industry were key avenues for stakeholder engagement.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
Task 1.0: Ultra-Low Bandgap Perovskite Solar Cells
Subtask 1.1: Absorber Development and Fabrication
Under this subtask, we have executed fabrication and optical screening characterization
within the ternary Pb-Sn-Bi search space as depicted in Figure 1A. This screening was
carried out by dissolving MASn1-xBixI3 or MA[Sn:Pb]1-xBixI3 at 1.25M into 3:1 DMF:DMSO
with chlorobenzene (CB) as antisolvent, then spincasting as thin-films onto glass. To
execute optical characterization in air, the oxygen-sensitive thin films were treated with
two spincast layers of a solution of 30mg/mL 350kDa poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)
in CB. We chose to constrain our search to pure-Sn and 1:1 Pb:Sn B-site with up to 20
molar percent of Bi-alloying. From the Tauc plots shown in Figure 1B and C, it is clear
that increasing concentrations of Bi3+ has a fairly significant effect on the absorption onset,
and thus, the estimated optical bandgap. However, it is also clear from these data that
the total absorption coefficient in the mixed Pb:Sn perovskite is much greater than that of
the pure-Sn samples, leading us to select the mixed Pb:Sn perovskite for further study.
In addition, due to the band bowing effect in the mixed Pb:Sn system, the optical bandgap
is actually lower than that of a pure-Sn system, and further, exhibits superior device
performance due to the added oxidative stability provided by Pb.

Figure 1: A) Ternary plot depicting the compositional space explored, B and C show direct Tauc plots of pure Sn and
Pb:Sn alloyed with varying concentrations of Bi3+.

Representative photoluminescence images are shown in Figure 2A, and it is clear that
the addition of Bi3+ quenches the PL of the material. This effect was consistent across the
Bi3+ loadings reported herein, even down to the lowest nominal concentrations of
Bismuth. One possible reason for this decrease could be the introduction of an indirect
transition by the addition of Bi3+. Figure 2B shows Tauc plots of the absorption onset of
MA[Sn:Pb]0.9Bi0.1I3 modeled as indirect and direct, with the indirect fit suggesting a
transition around 1eV.

Subtask 1.2: Low Gap Perovskite Bulk and Interface Characterization
Under this subtask, we aim to better understand the optoelectronic and morphological
properties of the most promising materials explored under Subtask 1.1. In order to gain a
better understanding of the shift in absorbance onset when Bi3+ is added into the
perovskite, we turned to measuring the photoconductance; an electrical measurement,
that, in principle would allow for much greater sensitivity than an optical measurement. A
schematic of the measurement setup is shown in Figure 4B: The perovskite absorber of
interest is spincast onto an etched FTO/glass substrate. Electrical leads are connected
to either side of the etched channel, and a bias of 50mV is applied to the circuit. A 1MΩ

Page 9 of 77

voltage divider is included in series, and the current across the divider is recorded via
lock-in amplification. Optically chopped, monochromated light irradiates the sample, thus
modulating the resistivity of the perovskite absorber and enabling high signal-to-noise
detection of photoconductance.

Figure 4: A) The normalized current across the voltage divider as a function of incident photon energy. B) A schematic
of the experimental setup.
The photoconductance data is presented on a semi-log scale in Figure 4A, clearly
exhibiting a shift in the onset to less than 1eV. Interestingly, the data suggests that the
extension of carrier generation reaches a maximum at 5% Bi, lining up well with the
absorbance data presented in Figure 1C. With this result, we chose to move forward to
device preparation with 5% Bi alloyed at the B-site. It is worth noting that the
photoconductance measurement relies on lateral conduction across the perovskite
absorber, necessitating a compact, well-formed film morphology to ensure electrical
continuity. Thus, several preparation methods were explored before satisfactory film
morphology and thickness was obtained. Parameters used to tune film morphology were
DMSO:DMF solvent ratio (9:1, 3:1, 2:1), antisolvent timing and type (CB, methylacetate,
ethylacetate, toluene), foramidinium (FA) inclusion in the A-site (0, 0.6, 0.7), and solution
molarity (1.25, 1.8, 2.0). The final set of parameters settled on for the photoconductance
measurement was FA0.7MA0.3[Pb:Sn]1-xBixI3 dissolved at 2.0M in 2:1 DMF:DMSO with
methylacetate as antisolvent, yielding thick, continuous films.

Subtask 1.3: Low Gap Cell Device Fabrication
Under this subtask, we set out to establish a reliable process for the fabrication of low-
bandgap photovoltaic cells. Early in cell process development, it was identified that the
thin-film process conditions optimized for photoconductivity measurements were not
going to translate into a well-functioning absorber in a device. Two main issues were
identified: 1) Film surface was visually rough, appearing as light-grey, and 2) White,
dendritic growth was consistently forming at the glass/perovskite interface, again, easily
visible by eye (Figure 6A). Issue 1 would hinder continuous contact with the top-CTL, and
Issue 2 could inhibit charge transport at the bottom interface. To address these issues,
we implemented an antisolvent-free (ASF) deposition process, taking inspiration from
recently reported high efficiency ASF FAPbI3 cells.3 The process involves dissolving the
perovskite precursors in 2-methoxyethanol (2-ME), an increasingly popular solvent used
in halide perovskite fabrication and once dissolved, adding 7.1% vol. DMSO. The
rationale is that 2-ME, a weakly coordinating solvent, will completely dissolve the
precursors, then DMSO, a highly coordinating solvent, will coordinate the metal centers,
forming complexes known to aid in uniform film formation. During spincasting, the semi-
volatile 2-ME evaporates, leaving behind the DMSO-complexed precursors, which are
driven to form perovskite phase during subsequent annealing.

Figure 6: A) Camera image of the white, dendritic growth frequently observed forming at the perovskite/substrate
interface. This particular sample was prepared with chlorobenzene as antisolvent. B) Camera image of the bottom

Figure 6A shows white, dendritic growth at the glass/perovskite interface in a sample
prepared using 2M in 2:1 DMF:DMSO FA0.7MA0.3Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 + 10% SnF2 (SnF2 being a
common additive used to suppress Sn-vacancies). Chlorobenzene was used as

method described above (1.33M in 2-ME +7.1% vol. DMSO). Figures 6C and D show
representative plane-view and cross-sectional SEM images respectively, clearly showing
the uniformity of films deposited using the ASF method. This ASF method has strong
implications for industrially relevant deposition methods, where the application of an
antisolvent is not possible.

With a process established to repeatably spincast high-quality, low-bandgap halide
perovskite thin-films, we set out to fabricate devices to assess the effect of Bi-alloying on
device performance. JV scans of devices prepared with the ASF method and a
composition of FA0.7MA0.3[Pb:Sn]1-xBixI3 + 10% SnF2 are shown in panels A of Figure 7.
The devices without Bismuth displayed a fairly high Jsc, but had a diminished Voc, likely
due to suboptimal charge transport layers or the presence of Sn(IV) within the absorber
layer. The devices prepared with 5% Bismuth present diminished Jsc and Voc; however,
the devices were rectifying, showing promise for further device optimization. As the
proportion of Bi3+ is increased, the device performance decreases, suggesting that Bi3+ is
especially harmful to device performance. External quantum efficiency (EQE) is shown in
Figure 7B. This data suggests that photon energies below the bandgap of the 0% Bi
device do generate extractable charges in the devices prepared with Bi, aligning with the
photoconductivity result. Overall, although free carriers are generated and collected
below the bandgap of the pristine sample, the device performance is diminished.

Task 2.0: Single-Junction Perovskite Solar Cells by Lamination

Subtask 2.1 Hot Pressing Lamination
Under this subtask, we worked to optimize and standardize the fabrication protocols for
lamination of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with the state-of-the-art mixed-cation-
perovskite composition as absorber layer. We used all-inorganic tin oxide and nickel oxide
thin films as the electron and hole transport layers for this structure of single-junction solar
cells. First, the tin oxide and nickel oxide layers are separately processed on the pre-
patterned ITO and FTO-coated-glass substrates respectively, over which the perovskite
layers are spincoated. The two half-stacks obtained thereafter are then pressed under
elevated pressure and temperature using a hot press. A functional solar cell device is
realized through bonding at the perovskite-perovskite interface. Figure 8A shows the
composition of different layers in the device. Figure 8B shows an isometric view of the
new laminated device architecture. Figure 8C shows an optical image of the device under
transmission mode which is used to structurally characterize the active regions in the
device and accurately measure the device’s active area.

  Figure 8: Schematics showing (A) composition of different layers in the solar cell, (B) a 3-dimensional view of the
laminated device. (C) Opticl image of the device observed under transmission mode to structurally characterize and
 measure the active area.

Figure 9A shows the current density (J) – Voltage (V) responses, in reverse and forward
scans, and the corresponding photovoltaic (PV) performance parameters (inset) of the
                                                  Page 13 of 77

“champion” laminated perovskite solar cell we were able to fabricate in early work in Task
1 with all inorganic transport layers. The device showed a photoconversion efficiency of
13.35% with a small hysteresis. This was among the highest values reported to date for
diffusion-bonded perovskite PVs, providing confidence that with continued optimization,
we would achieve the targeted efficiencies of this project. For example, while the short-
circuit current and open circuit voltage values are on par with those witnessed in the highly
efficient conventional perovskite solar cells, the fill factor value is still lower. The J-V curve
indicates that the low fill factor is arising primarily from a high series resistance of 128 Ω
(10.4 Ω-cm2) which is higher compared to the values observed in conventional perovskite
solar cells (<5 Ω-cm2). A major contributor (~40 Ω) to the series resistance is the
resistance of the ITO finger patterns that we use as one of the electrodes to obtain small
active areas for the devices. Therefore, in future work, we will continue to optimize the
fill factor by reducing series resistance in the device architecture and transport layers.

Continued work in this subtask resulted in a fabrication protocol for lamination of
monojunction perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of
21%. These PSCs feature a state-of-the-art mixed-cation-halide-perovskite (HP)
composition as the absorber layer. By employing this fabrication protocol, we have
successfully laminated PSCs with various transport layers (TLs) and interlayer
combinations, demonstrating the versatility of this approach. Figure 11 illustrates the
composition of the TLs and interlayers we have explored. The process flow for this
approach, shown in Figure 11, involves the parallel processing of TL-HP half stacks on
pre-patterned Au and FTO-coated glass substrates. Subsequently, the two half stacks
are diffusion bonded by hot pressing at a temperature of 150C and a pressure of 8.3 MPa,
resulting in the formation of a complete PSC device.

                                                Page 15 of 77

  Figure 11: Schematic showing lamination of monojunction perovskite solar cells with unique transport layers and interlayer
 combinations.

The lamination approach overcomes the processing constraints that are commonly
encountered in conventional layer-by-layer deposition, which can involve challenging
chemical and thermal processing requirements for the layers on top of HP. The parallel
processing of half-stacks, enabled by this approach, allows for the simultaneous solution
processing of high-quality SnOx and MeO-2PACz SAM as electron transport layers
(ETLs) and hole transport layers (HTLs) respectively. Previous studies have shown that
each of these TLs exhibits high photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) amongst ETLs
and HTLs, respectively. However, they have been restricted to only the bottom transport
layer of the device in the highest performing n-i-p and p-i-n architectures. Furthermore,
the lamination approach provides an additional benefit of allowing the integration of self-
assembled monolayers at the SnOx-HP interface, while also utilizing MeO-2PACz SAM
as the HTL. This would be a challenging task to accomplish with the conventional layer-
by-layer process due to the requirements of hydroxyl group terminated oxide surfaces
and polar solvents, which are not suitable for implementation on the HP layer.

Figure 12: (A) J-V responses in reverse and forward scans of champion PSCs with dual SAMs passivated interfaces, (B) Statistical
analysis of device performance with dual interface passivation using SAMs

Figure 12A shows the current density (J) – voltage (V) responses, in reverse and forward
scans, and the corresponding photovoltaic (PV) performance parameters (inset) of the
“champion” laminated perovskite solar cell we have been able to fabricate so far with
                                                       Page 16 of 77

dual-interface passivation using self-assembled monolayers. The device showed a
photoconversion efficiency (PCE) of 21.03 %, open-circuit voltage of 1.154 V, short-circuit
current density of 23.35 mA/cm2 and fill factor of 0.780 with a small hysteresis of 2.2%.
This is the highest PCE values reported to date for laminated PSCs, and higher than the
target efficiency (15%) of this project. The statistical reproducibility for this configuration
for 10 devices (fabricated across two batches) is presented in Figure 12B. With these
results, we successfully passed the 15th month milestone #GNG-1B of achieving > 15%
PCE champion device and >12% average PCE for 10 devices. This pass indicates there
is small or no optoelectronic loss from lamination process and we were equipped to
replicate the procedure for tandem architectures in the second budget period.

 Figure 13: (A) Optical microscope image of a large area (1.15cm2) laminated PSC. (B) J-V responses in reverse and forward
 scans of the corresponding PSC with dual SAMs passivated interfaces.

We have also successfully demonstrated the scalability of this device fabrication
approach for the first time by laminating PSC on a large area of 1.15 cm2. An optical
microscope image of the device, demonstrating uniform diffusion bonding across the
large active area, is shown in Figure 13A. The corresponding J-V responses in reverse
and forward scans with dual SAMs configuration are shown in Figure 13B, and the
corresponding performance parameters are included in the inset. The device has a PCE
of 17.29%, Jsc of 22.84 mA/cm2, Voc of 1.162 V, FF of 0.652. The high Voc, Jsc, and
Rsh values that were measured on small-area devices in the “Dual SAMs” configuration
(Figure 13B) are retained over the 1 cm2 large area. This demonstrates the promise of
scalability of this approach, by maintaining low shunting and high layer uniformity over
large areas. However, there is a decrease in FF from 0.780 to 0.652, arising from an
increase in the series resistance from 3.46 to 9.84 Ω-cm2. This increase has a major
contribution from the sheet resistance of TCO and linearly increases with the active area,
which can be easily addressed by using appropriate electrode patterns.

To further improve the scalability of diffusion bonded PSCs, thin film deposition
techniques such as atomic layer deposition (ALD) and chemical bath deposition (CBD)

To minimize the PL loss during the interconnect deposition, ALD of Aluminum Zinc Oxide

sputtering and this is expected to minimize any mechanical damage to the perovskite

the deposition temperature for SnOx was set at 80 ˚C.

ALD processes is expected to contribute to decreased shunting and improvement in FF.

Subtask 4.2: Effects of Thermal Cycling on Laminated Interface Toughness
To examine the mechanical properties of the laminated interface after thermal cycling, we
created glass-perovskite laminated samples and a proxy device system composed of
glass-SnOx-perovskite-PMMA-epoxy-glass. This proxy device system was chosen
because it includes a transport layer-perovskite interface present in state-of-the-art
devices. The PMMA-epoxy-glass is needed for the system to be in a proper geometry for
mechanical testing (symmetric substrates on each side). The simplified glass-perovskite
laminated system gives a lower threshold of the interfacial toughness of the lamination
interface because the system consistently fails at the glass-perovskite interface rather
than the perovskite-perovskite interface. In our past work, we have seen that the
toughness of the glass-perovskite interface is greater than the toughness of transport

                                            Page 35 of 77

layer-perovskite interfaces, therefore the simplified glass-perovskite system gives us a
closer lower threshold for the perovskite-perovskite interfacial toughness.
Laminated glass-perovskite samples and non-laminated glass-SnOx-perovskite-PMMA-
epoxy-glass samples were prepared, underwent TC50 testing, and mechanically tested
to quantify interfacial toughness.

Figure 31: Schematic of the laminated and non-laminated sample systems with optical images of initial samples and post TC50
 testing samples for each.

The optically visible delamination regions did not appear to increase throughout TC50
testing, and through mechanical testing the change in interfacial toughness is evaluated.
As expected, the perovskite-perovskite lamination interface is not the weakest in the
system post thermal cycling, the system fails at the perovskite-glass interface.
Even after thermal cycling, the laminated system maintained a comparable toughness to
the best perovskite interface toughnesses in pristine devices from literature.6 The spin
coat comparisons we endeavored to make for toughness testing were not a good
comparison, because it turned out the epoxy needed to apply a cover glass to standard
devices stiffened the system creating an apples-to-oranges comparison. The
nonlaminated test structures made with epoxy were biased to fracture at the epoxy
interface because of the relative flexibility of the perovskite and thin substrate used for
mechanical testing. Therefore, we were unable to make a relevant comparison between

It is important to note that this analysis does not consider residual stresses originating
from earlier manufacturing processes prior to the TC50 tests. Such stresses are likely
present due to the sequence of film depositions and treatments used to fabricate the
perovskite solar cells, and they would vary depending on the specific materials and
processing conditions involved. While incorporating these residual stresses into the
model would be feasible if measured, they are not expected to significantly change the
qualitative conclusions. For this analysis, the presence of the SAM atop the NiOx HTL is
disregarded because NiOx constitutes over 98% of the HTL thickness, and the
mechanical properties of MeO-2PACz SAMs are not well known.
The most extreme thermal strain encountered during TC50 testing corresponds to a
temperature change from 25°C to -40°C, a change of -65°C. At this condition, the
energy-release rate at the failure interface (NiOx/SAMs-HP) is calculated to be 0.3×10⁻³
J/m². This very low value is well below the measured post-TC50 interfacial toughness of
0.20 J/m², suggesting that delamination should not occur—consistent with experimental

                                                   Page 43 of 77

observations. These findings also emphasize one of the major advantages of the
lamination method with glass-glass encapsulation, the use of mechanically symmetric
substrates on both sides of the cell. In contrast, systems using dissimilar substrate
materials (especially those with mismatched coefficients of thermal expansion) can
exhibit much higher energy-release rates, which increases the risk of interfacial failure—
a particularly critical issue given the brittle characteristics of perovskite interfaces, as
demonstrated in our wedge test results.
To further demonstrate the importance of using matched materials on both sides of the
laminated stack, we calculated the energy-release rate for a theoretical device in which
the ETL-side glass was replaced with soda lime glass, a commonly used substrate
material. In this case, the resulting energy release rate increased by two orders of
magnitude to 0.04 J/m², but still remained below the fracture toughness values reported
in this study. A more extreme example involves substituting the ETL-side glass with
aluminum, which has similar elastic modulus to glass but a significantly higher CTE. In
this scenario, the calculated energy-release rate jumps to 1.0 J/m². These examples
illustrate that mismatches in substrate properties, which dominate the device volume,
can greatly increase the likelihood of thermal delamination.
The analytical mechanics framework presented here can be a useful tool to guide the
selection of substrate materials that offer both mechanical protection and adequate
encapsulation, while minimizing the risk of delamination. In particular, flexible PSCs will
benefit from exploring a wider variety of substrate and barrier materials, making it
essential to co-optimize mechanical reliability and protection.

perovskite from the photo-active black phase to the photo-inactive yellow phase.

The materials set chosen for this experiment was as follows: bare nickel oxide (NiOx),
NiOx treated with MeO-2PACz (SAM), NiOx with 5 cycles of ALD alumina (AlOx),
NiOx/AlOx/SAM, tin oxide (SnOx), SnOx/AlOx, and ITO/SAM. This set was motivated

further compare the self-encapsulation effect offered by a laminated stack. Figure 51c is
an example image used to quantify the rate of degradation, while Figure 51d shows how
these raw images are cropped, transformed, and segmented into the individual
substrates. By treating all images taken over >600 hours this way, the film degradation
can be quantified and plotted as a function of time.

at the edges of laminated samples. It was also observed that the NiOx laminated
stacks remained bonded throughout the course of the accelerated test, while the SnOx
and ITO samples debonded early (<100 hours) in the experiment (Figure 53h). We
attribute this to a weaker interface with the perovskite, which can be confirmed with
mechanical testing and potentially be improved with judicious choice of interfacial
modification to promote toughness at that interface.

Subtask 6.1 Accelerated Testing: Stability under light and heat at 85°C
After working to improve the device efficiency to above 18% PCE, we put our device

toward milestone 2.6.1. Devices showed rapid degradation and decline in performance
after 20-40 hours under light and heat, indicating the delamination and interface
reaction between layers happening. 80 to 90% of devices die between 20 and 40 hours.

To understand why MA-free devices show poor performance after elevated light and
temperature testing, though they did not show the visual indication of a phase change by
turning yellow, we dug deeper into characterizing the stacks after light and heat testing.

NiOX/SAM/perovskite stack before and after heat and light soaking of 1-sun and 85˚C for

clear grain boundaries. We also took the X-ray diffraction of all the samples. Both light

  • EOP-A: The durability target of >16.5% PCE after >500hrs of accelerated
 aging at 1-sun/85˚C was not met; however, a significant fraction of this
 durability was achieved using laminated devices, with >1000 hrs of stability
 at 40°C. The durability fell short of the EOP goal because of unoptimized
 perovskite/transport layer interfaces, not because of the lamination process
 itself. In fact, durability studies focused on interfaces alone comparing
 laminated processes to traditional serial processes appear to indicate that
 lamination can be beneficial to overall durability, given the same interfaces.
 Overall, lamination processing appears to offer benefits while being able to
 adopt advances in interface design from the broader, global perovskite
 development effort.

 • EOP-B: Laminated all-perovskite tandem efficiency fell short of the efficiency
 target of >22% largely because an unoptimized recombination layer caused

 devices were successfully demonstrated in the project.
 • EOP-C: All-perovskite tandem cell light soaking t80>500hrs was not achieved
 largely due to the Pb:Sn low-gap cell limiting the durability. Sn2+ is easily

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

This project successfully demonstrated >21% PCE laminated devices, leveraging the
unique process flexibility offered by perovskite lamination.

 • Process Flexibility: Lamination significantly enhances process flexibility by
 decoupling subcell and cell processing from restrictive serial manufacturing
 requirements. This enables independent and precise tailoring of interface
 properties for enhanced stability and performance. This approach also provides a
 pathway for self-encapsulated modules without separate encapsulant sheets.
 • High-Efficiency Devices: Laminated perovskite solar cells achieved a champion
 power conversion efficiency of 21.03%, exceeding the project's target of 18%.
 • Scalability: The scalability of the fabrication approach was demonstrated by
 laminating PSCs on a large area of 1.15 cm2 while maintaining high performance
 metrics.
 • Durability and Mechanical Stability: Laminated perovskite cells successfully
 withstood TC50 thermal cycling tests, retaining interfacial toughness comparable
 to pristine, serial-manufactured cells. Mechanical analysis confirms that the
 lamination process itself does not introduce weaker interfaces and effectively
 mitigates thermal stress due to symmetric substrate use. Device performance
 showed an improvement in power conversion efficiency during thermal cycling
 testing.
 • Improved Stability Pathways: Investigations into MA-free perovskite
 compositions showed successful lamination and high electronic uniformity,
 promising enhanced device stability. Specific interlayers, such as PTAA,
 significantly improved device stability under accelerated aging conditions by
 mitigating interfacial reactions.

                                     Page 71 of 77

  • Low-Bandgap Perovskite Research: Initial work on ultra-low bandgap
 perovskites, though later refocused, achieved infrared photoconductivity beyond
 1000 nm and resulted in a publication.
  • Project Outputs: The project generated several peer-reviewed publications and
 presentations, including awards for research excellence. A provisional patent
 application for "Method for Making Thin Film Electronic Module" was also filed
 based in part upon the funding support of the project.
  • Future Directions: Learnings from this work are informing ongoing efforts to
 eliminate laser scribing in perovskite modules and explore ultrafast lamination for
 inline manufacturing.

9. Path Forward:

  The learnings developed under this award have led to several future prospects. We
  are currently working on developing perovskite lamination for perovskite module
  production under SIPS award 11510. In that work, we aim to eliminate the need for
  laser scribing in the perovskite layer, which would cut off a major degradation
  pathway in today’s SOTA perovskite modules. In other work, we aim to test the
  lower limit of time needed for successful lamination. This has implications for inline
  manufacturing of laminated cells, where the lamination process step should not
  hinder the production rate of the line.
