Clinical relevance of intracranial stenosis as false-positive findings of a deep learning algorithm trained to detect large vessel occlusions: a retrospective cohort study of a supraregional stroke centre
Authors: Michel Jungmann, Sanda Manschikow, Alexander Hönning, Frederik Spohn, Paul Sparenberg, Sven Mutze, Leonie Goelz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to explore the ability to identify high-grade intracranial arterial stenosis (ICAS) by an artificial intelligence (AI) designed to detect large vessel occlusions (LVO) and the clinical relevance of these ‘false positive’ findings.DesignWe are presenting a retrospective cohort study.SettingThe study was conducted at a supraregional stroke centre of an urban tertiary care provider.ParticipantsConsecutive stroke cases treated between January 2023 and December 2023 of patients >18 years of both sexes and any ethnicity were eligible for inclusion. 934 patients (52.7% male) with a mean age of 71.7±13.6 years (25–101 years) were included.InterventionsCT angiographies were analysed by a deep learning algorithm for LVO detection of the anterior circulation. Introduction

Deep learning (DL) based tools with the ability to identify large-vessel occlusions (LVOs) have been commercially available for years. Independent groups have reported varying reliability of the diagnostic performance of such products with sensitivities ranging from 67% to 94% and specificities from 31% to 94%.15 Proof of actual benefit on patient outcome remains somewhat pendant while improvement of stroke workflows has been reported for clinical teams using artificial intelligence (AI).6 7 Part of the reluctance to integrate automated LVO detection into clinical workflows has been attributed to the balancing act of requiring high sensitivities to detect a severely disabling condition and a hereunder increase of potentially disrupting false positive findings.8 9 While the ideal imaging stroke workup may differ among institutions worldwide, CT-angiography (CTA) is most frequently used during the initial, critical phase to detect and differentiate LVO and intracranial arterial stenosis (ICAS).10

ICAS has been identified as a reason for such false positive alerts during LVO detection.1 2 5 11 12 ICAS is also a cause for ischaemic stroke. While the incidence in Caucasians only ranges between 5% and 10%,13 ICAS is one of the leading causes for ischaemic stroke in the Asian population and also cumulates in patients of African and Hispanic descent. From a medical point of view, ICAS identified by an algorithm to detect LVO is therefore not equivalent to false positive alerts caused by image quality or undeterminable origin because symptomatic patients require treatment and observation urgently.14 Accelerated and improved detection rates therefore have the potential to benefit therapeutic management of ICAS.

This analysis aims to examine the number of high-grade ICAS on CTA identified by a commercially available DL algorithm in a real-world stroke collective and to assess its potential as a dedicated diagnostic tool for symptomatic intracranial arteriosclerotic disease.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was registered at the German Register for Clinical Trials (DRKS00034019) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). The institutional review board approved the protocol (Medical Association of Berlin, Germany, Eth-26/24) and waived the need for written informed consent due to the nature of the study. The study comprised seven phases: screening/enrolment; classification of primary radiology report (RR); secondary report; algorithm analysis classification; discrepancy review and endpoint analysis; review of patient records; statistical analysis. The study protocol, summarised in figure 1, is in line with the guidance provided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) initiative.15

Screening and enrolment

Similar previous study designs were unavailable to determine an appropriate sample size. We therefore conducted an exploratory retrospective review of consecutive cases of our supraregional stroke unit between January 2023 and December 2023. To be eligible, patients had to be aged 18 years or over and had to have undergone emergent examination due to neurological deficits resulting in International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10 codes I63.x (cerebral infarction) or G45.x (transient cerebral ischaemic attacks and related syndromes). All primary RRs were composed during clinical routine by a team of neuroradiologists (NR), radiology consultants or experienced residents who had been trained at the study site. Secondary analysis was conducted by a fifth-year resident specially trained to detect and differentiate LVO and stenosis. Discrepancy review was performed by a NR. Endpoint analysis and clinical evaluation were performed by a trained clinical consultant.

Image acquisition and postprocessing

None of the CTAs had previously been used during training of the algorithm. Images were obtained using a 2×192 row CT scanner (Somatom Force, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The tube voltage was 120 kVp, the pitch was 0.9 and the rotation time was 0.25 s. The tube current was modulated automatically (CAREDose4D) (table 1). Images were reconstructed using the iterative reconstruction algorithm, Sinogram Affirmed Iterative Reconstruction (SAFIRE, I26f kernel). Images were reconstructed in axial orientation with 0.75 mm slice thickness.

Report classification

Visual stenosis of the intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) and M1 segment of ≥70% were defined as high-grade stenosis for the primary endpoint analysis. Included cases were reviewed manually and primary reports classified as ‘positive-for-stenosis’, ‘negative-for-stenosis’ or ‘LVO’ by an independent radiologist. The same radiologist also performed a secondary analysis classifying either ‘positive-for stenosis’ or ‘negative-for-stenosis’. In each step, the localisation of potential findings in the ICA or M1 segments of both hemispheres was recorded. Additional data collected for each CTA included the occurrence of artefacts and the experience of the primary examiner (NR, radiology consultant or experienced resident).

AI analysis

At the study centre, all CTAs were routinely analysed by a first-generation, Food and Drug Administration-cleared, Conformité Européenn-marked triage and notification software for LVO detection of the anterior circulation from the intracranial ICA to the M1 segment of the medial cerebral artery (AIDOC, Tel Aviv, Israel). CTAs were automatically processed starting with their export to a virtual machine within the institute. Then, the software’s orchestrator identified the examined body part and study protocol and conducted an initial check for technical issues such as excessive motion or metal artefacts, inadequate field of view or insufficient arterial contrast. After passing of the initial check, CTAs with positive LVO findings were identified in the cloud for prioritisation, and all results were re-injected into the picture archiving and communication system as key images with colour-coded heatmaps or negative summary reports. The independent radiologist reviewed the colour-coded maps of flagged cases and classified results into ‘positive-at-stenosis’ (figure 2), ‘positive-at-LVO’, ‘false positive’ and ‘false negative’ (figure 3). The solution is based on a proprietary two stage algorithm: a fully convolutional region proposal neural network stage and a false positive reduction stage. The first stage is a three-dimensional deep convolutional neural network which was trained on tens of thousands of CTAs acquired on a diverse range of CT scanners with varying acquisition parameters from multiple medical centres around the world. The network is built from a Resnet style feature extractor16 attached to a fully convolutional segmentation head17 that allows the model to segment scans with various sizes. The network is trained on segmented scans using the Adam optimiser.18 From the segmentation map, region proposals are generated in areas with high confidence voxels and passed as input to the second stage of the algorithm. The second stage classifies each region as positive or negative, based on pooled features from the last layer of the first stage network and traditional image processing methods. On detection of suspected positive findings, the solution delivers notifications directly to the radiologist workstation.

Discrepancy review

Comparison of secondary analyses and AI analyses resulted in two discrepancy categories, namely ‘negative-by-report/positive-by-AI’ and ‘positive-by-report/negative-by-AI’. Discrepant cases were transferred to the NR who was blinded to both the secondary and AI results and adjudicated on whether the case in question contained an ICAS, a LVO or neither. ICAS was further described according to location and hemisphere. Cases without AI result were classified as ‘no analysis’.

Endpoint analysis

The independent clinical consultant subsequently determined if the NR agreed with the findings of the AI analysis (‘positive-for-stenosis-by-NR’) or if she disagreed, describing no relevant ICAS (‘negative-for-stenosis-by-NR’). Uncertain cases were recorded and discussed with another NR to determine a consensus result. Accuracy measures for the detection of ICAS (sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV)) were calculated for the AI analysis and primary RRs.

The number of ICAS detected by the LVO algorithm corresponded to the potentially relevant false positive findings.

The clinician evaluated the CTAs for possible confounding reasons, such as beam-hardening, motion or metal artefacts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA for Windows (V.16.1). Missing values were not imputed but presented for all relevant variables. Our reporting adhered to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy statement and recommendations.19 The number of correctly identified ICAS using both the LVO algorithm and the primary RRs represented our primary endpoint. As secondary endpoints, we determined accuracy measures expressed as sensitivity, specificity, NPV and PPV with 95% Clopper Pearson CIs of the LVO algorithm and the primary RRs compared with the reference standard. Clinical parameters and impact were evaluated using descriptive statistics including arithmetic mean, SD, minimum and maximum values (range) and absolute (n) and relative (%) proportions.

Primary analysis

10.1% (n=94) LVO were present in the stroke cohort of 934 patients. 57 of these were identified correctly by the algorithm, 26 cases were false negative and in 11 cases no AI output was generated resulting in a sensitivity of 61% for LVO (95% CI 0.50 to 0.71). During LVO detection, a total of 31 ‘false positive findings for LVO’ (no LVO, including ICAS) occurred (specificity 96% (95% CI 0.95 to 0.97), PPV 65% (95% CI 0.56 to 0.73), NPV 96% (95% CI 0.94 to 0.97)).

4.2% (n=39) high-grade ICAS were detected by secondary analysis and neuroradiological review among 934 stroke patients. 30 primary RR described high-grade ICAS, nine cases remained undetected. False positive reporting for ICAS occurred eight times resulting in a sensitivity of 77%, a specificity of 99%, a NPV of 99% and a PPV of 79% (table 3). Primary reporting on ICAS cases was conducted by experienced residents in 28.2%, by radiology consultants in 56.4% and by NR in 15.4%.

Within the 31 false positive cases for LVO, 13 cases were identified as high-grade ICAS by AI (‘true positive for ICAS’/‘false positive for LVO’). Hence, the DL algorithm flagged 13 of all 39 (33.3%) high-grade ICAS during evaluation of CTAs for LVO. Three of these 13 ICAS had not been reported during clinical routine (primary RR). 26 of 39 high-grade (66.7%) ICAS remained undetected by the algorithm.

18 patients without ICAS nor LVO were flagged falsely by the algorithm (‘false positive for LVO and ICAS’). In three cases of high-grade ICAS and in nine cases without ICAS (and without LVO), no AI output was generated (12 out of 934=1.3% cases without AI result). The sensitivity of the algorithm for detecting high-grade ICAS was 33%, the specificity 98%, the NPV 97% and the PPV 42% (table 3).

Incorrect AI results

97.4% of CTAs displaying high-grade ICAS were free of motion and beam artefacts. Of note, the algorithm displayed false positive results for ICAS (and LVO) only six times in the ICA or M1 segment while two-thirds were localised clearly distinct from these localisations (ie, posterior circulation, table 4).

Table 5 summarises follow-up examinations of the 39 patients with high-grade ICAS on CTA. Non-contrast CT was used in eight of 39 (21%) cases, MRI in 29 of 39 (74%) and TCCS in 35 of 39 (90%) cases during follow-up. In seven out of 13 (53.8%) high-grade ICAS cases detected by the algorithm, follow-up imaging (6×MRI, 1×CT) showed ischaemia associated with the localisation of the stenosis. The same was true for eight out of 26 undetected high-grade ICAS (30.8%). TCCS measurements were pathological in eight out of 13 (61.5%) detected ICAS cases with only five cases (38.5%) showing a duplex spectrum indicating a stenosis ≥70%. In three cases, TCCS showed no stenosis, and two measurements were missing due to technical issues. Eight of the 13 (61.5%) detected stenosis were attributed to arteriosclerosis, one patient was diagnosed with vasculitis and the other cases remained uncertain. Regarding undetected ICAS cases, 13 of 26 (50.0%) were attributed to arteriosclerosis, one was diagnosed with vasculitis and in one case the cause remained undetermined. TCCS measured a stenosis in 14 out of 26 cases (40.0%) in the undetected group, 11 (31.4%) of which were high-grade stenosis. 17 measurements (48.6%) showed no stenosis and four TCCS were missing. Medicinal treatment of ICAS was recorded in all of the 39 patients and consisted of ASA, additional platelet inhibition or OAC and additional stroke risk modifiers (ie, statins, antihypertensive drugs; online supplemental table S2). The clinical course was complicated in eight cases by a progression of neurological symptoms resulting in one death and in one case of fatal intracranial haemorrhage.

Discussion

Analysis of a real-world stroke cohort identified 4.2% high-grade ICAS and 10.1% LVO among 934 patients. The sensitivity of a first-generation DL algorithm in detecting LVO was 61% (95% CI 0.50 to 0.71), while the specificity was 96%. Nearly half of 31 false positive findings for LVO were identified as high-grade ICAS during secondary analysis, but the sensitivity of the DL algorithm in detecting all high-grade ICAS was as low as 33%. These identified cases were declared as ‘false positive for LVO’ and ‘true positive for ICAS’ at the same time. The algorithm was highly reliable in excluding not only LVO but also ICAS (specificity 98%/NPV 97%) and failure to produce an AI output occurred in only 1% of CTA analysis. Even though primary reporting during clinical routine was more accurate in detecting ICAS (sensitivity 77%, specificity 99%, NPV 99%, PPV 79%), three additional high-grade ICAS were detected by the DL algorithm. Neurological symptoms and follow-up examinations showed that 71% of all patients with high-grade ICAS and 77% of patients identified by the DL algorithm exhibited neurological symptoms consistent with the localisation of the stenosis and often required medicinal treatment addressing the underlying ICAS.

First, the diagnostic performance of the tested DL algorithm for its intended use (LVO detection) requires improvement when compared with the literature.1 However, it is not uncommon to measure lower diagnostic accuracies of otherwise potent AI tools when applied in a specific environment, namely a unique patient cohort which inherently differs from the training data. Bankston et al20 also reported lower than expected sensitivities in their retrospective analysis of two algorithms, one of which (RAPIDAI) has been tested with high performance by many other groups. In this study, AIDOC’s algorithm reached a sensitivity of 78% and RAPIDAI of 74% in their smaller thrombectomy cohort. All companies are striving to ensure generalisability of their algorithms and to improve and extend the performance of these products, but centre specific performance differences of AI tools persist inevitably.

Second, reporting on LVO detection generally describes specificities of around 80% and higher and herein concurs with our results.1 2 5 11 More attention should therefore be paid to the infrequent false positive results these algorithms do produce. Other groups already identified ICAS as one cause for false positive alerts during LVO detection in up to 27% of cases.2 5 11 12 The results of our study show that high-grade ICAS can be the cause of nearly half of false positive findings during LVO detection and that these findings have a clinical impact and potential for improved patient care. Hence, ICAS findings during LVO detection should not merely be defined as false positive findings but qualify as significant findings, even if incidental during the acute presentation of a patient. To some extent, it might even be reasonable to define high-grade ICAS as true positive findings during LVO detection as has been proposed by Dehkharghani et al.20

Third, comparable data to our approach, in which we dedicated an algorithm trained for LVO prioritisation to ICAS detection, are lacking. The accuracy measures for ICAS detection in our study do not allow for an enthusiastic appraisal of this method. However, we have shown that careful appreciation of AI results could increase the sensitivity for detection of high-grade ICAS during primary reporting from 77% to 85%. Acknowledging the low sensitivity of the algorithm for ICAS detection and its mediocre sensitivity for LVO, explicit refinement with training data including more ICAS cases and promoting their prioritisation, that is, during supervised learning, would be requisite to fit future AI tools with the ability to detect both LVO and high-grade ICAS reliably. Federated training of algorithms surpassing international borders, for example, exchanging them between Asian, European and American stroke centres, would increase training data for ICAS and reduce bias during training. Supervision by NR appears necessary to aim for accuracy rates equal to or even surpassing general radiology assessment (of 77% in our study). Such performance metrics then promote utility during quick diagnostic processes and generate acceptance by physicians consequently. Additionally, workflows will be enhanced if radiologists are enabled to distinguish instantly between true positive AI results, false positives and potentially significant findings such as high-grade ICAS by comprehensible AI outputs. Transparency is needed to prevent reluctance to accept and deeply implement AI algorithms in the long run.9

Our study has limitations. The retrospective study design has potential for selection bias. Adherence to the STROBE standards increases the level of transparency of the selection and inclusion process of the consecutive patients to minimise these effects. Additionally, given the lack of previous studies on our approach and the exploratory nature of this study, it yields mainly descriptive results. Furthermore, the study describes the performance of one particular AI algorithm in clinical practice and its generalisability on a second stroke pathology. Additional larger studies are warranted to compare the performance of available products and their applicability in detecting LVO and high-grade ICAS prospectively. A broader understanding and access to generalised data may aid the institutional decision-making process involved in the purchase and implementation of suitable AI tools.

Conclusion

Detection of high-grade ICAS by a DL algorithm trained to identify LVO is per definition a ‘false positive finding for LVO’ but highly significant and thus interpretable as ‘true positive for ICAS’. Extended training with ICAS cases might lead to a clinically beneficial tool to identify this pathology in addition to LVO reliably during the diagnostic work-up for ischaemic stroke.

Patient and public involvement

During the development of the study protocol, the internet was researched to determine if ICAS is known within the general public and perceived as a relevant issue for patients and families. Patient forums (ie, Mayoclinic.org and the support network of the American Heart Association) are discussing ICAS because it is affecting patients’ daily lives through medication and impairment; however, the topics about ICAS are scarce. The public was also represented by laymen as an essential part of the ethics committee of Berlin. Hence, the public had influence on the research questions, outcome measures, and the study design. The study protocol was also registered with the German Clinical Trial Registry in plain language to assure accessibility by the public. The study results will be made available after publication of this article in the same registry and in the stroke support network of the AHA (https://supportnetwork.heart.org) to raise awareness and initiate discussion about further research needs.
