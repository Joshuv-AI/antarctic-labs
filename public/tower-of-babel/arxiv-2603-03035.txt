Generalized Bayes for Causal Inference
Emil Javurek, Dennis Frauen, Yuxin Wang, Stefan Feuerriegel

Abstract
Uncertainty quantification is central to many applications of causal machine learning, yet principled Bayesian inference for causal effects remains challenging. Standard Bayesian approaches typically require specifying a probabilistic model for the data-generating process, including high-dimensional nuisance components such as propensity scores and outcome regressions. Standard posteriors are thus vulnerable to strong modeling choices, including complex prior elicitation. In this paper, we propose a generalized Bayesian framework for causal inference . Our framework avoids explicit likelihood modeling; instead, we place priors directly on the causal estimands and update these using an identification-driven loss function, which yields generalized posteriors for causal effects. As a result, our framework turns existing loss-based causal estimators into estimators with full uncertainty quantification. Our framework is flexible and applicable to a broad range of causal estimands (e.g., ATE, CATE). Further, our framework can be applied on top of state-of-the-art causal machine learning pipelines (e.g., Neyman-orthogonal meta-learners). For Neyman-orthogonal losses, we show that the generalized posteriors converge to their oracle counterparts and remain robust to first-stage nuisance estimation error. With calibration, we thus obtain valid frequentist uncertainty even when nuisance estimators converge at slower-than-parametric rates. Empirically, we demonstrate that our proposed framework offers causal effect estimation with calibrated uncertainty across several causal inference settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first flexible framework for constructing generalized Bayesian posteriors for causal machine learning.

Introduction

Causal inference is widely used to assess treatment effects in order to compare interventions and inform decisions
(
9
;
4
;
16
)
. However, conclusions are typically not based on the point estimates of causal effects alone, but rather on considering uncertainty associated with estimating causal effects. For example, in medicine, a treatment that appears beneficial on average may still be clinically unacceptable if the uncertainty is large enough to imply a substantial probability of harm

Bayesian inference provides a natural framework for uncertainty quantification by computing a posterior distribution over the quantity of interest. However, in causal inference, standard Bayesian approaches are subject to fundamental
challenges
: existing approaches typically require specifying a probabilistic model of the observational data-generating process, including nuisance components such as propensity scores and outcome regressions (see Section
2

(
15
)
.

By “generalized”, we mean Bayesian updating based on a loss function rather than a likelihood, following the generalized (or Gibbs) posterior framework of

Conceptually, we follow recent works on generalized Bayes

In our framework, we thus avoid specifying a probabilistic model of the full observational data-generating process; instead, we (i) place priors directly on causal estimands and (ii) update these using identification-driven loss functions for which the minimizers recover the targeted causal effect. This construction yields generalized posteriors for causal effects, without requiring priors or likelihoods for high-dimensional nuisance components. This addresses key challenges of likelihood-based Bayesian approaches, which can be sensitive to nuisance component modeling misspecification. Consequently, our approach brings the following benefits:

\bullet

Flexibility.
Our framework is applicable to a wide range of causal estimands. Throughout our paper, we illustrate our framework using three canonical estimands from the causal inference literature: (i) the average treatment effect (ATE) and (ii) the average treatment effect (CATE) settings. The proposed framework extends naturally to any other identified causal estimand that can be estimated with a loss. Moreover, our framework can be applied on top of state-of-the-art causal ML pipelines, in particular Neyman-orthogonal meta-learners such as the DR-learner
(
17
;
36
)
. Altogether, our framework provides a general recipe for transforming existing loss-based causal estimators into estimators with full uncertainty quantification.

\bullet

Theoretical guarantees.
For Neyman-orthogonal losses, we show that the generalized posteriors have several favorable properties. In particular, the generalized posterior converges to the oracle counterpart and remains robust to first-stage nuisance estimation error (analogous to state-of-the-art Neyman-orthogonal meta-learners for point estimates), even when nuisance estimators converge at slower-than-parametric rates. It follows that the estimated generalized posterior also satisfies a Bernstein-von Mises limit, yielding asymptotically valid frequentist uncertainty quantification. This establishes how orthogonal statistical learning applies to generalized Bayesian causal inference.

\bullet

Practical advantages.

First
, our framework enables the placement of priors directly on the causal estimand of interest, which allows prior information to be specified transparently and avoids the need to encode prior beliefs indirectly through high-dimensional nuisance models, where their effect on the causal estimand is difficult to interpret or control.
Second
, this loss-based formulation makes it possible to characterize and control how nuisance estimation error propagates into posterior uncertainty through properties of the loss such as Neyman-orthogonality.
Third
, the resulting generalized posteriors can be calibrated to match frequentist uncertainty and thus achieve coverage.

Likelihood-based Bayesian causal inference:

A large body of Bayesian causal inference proceeds by specifying a probabilistic model for (parts of) the observed data distribution and performing posterior inference under this model. Many approaches leverage flexible Bayesian nonparametric priors over high-dimensional nuisance components
(
2
;

However, such approaches require strong modeling and prior assumptions that are often difficult to obtain through domain expertise. Further, the nuisance components are typically high-dimensional, so regularization induced by Bayesian priors can inadvertently bias causal effect estimates, known as regularization-induced confounding
(
15

. Hence, likelihood-based Bayesian causal inference can be very fragile.

In parallel, Bayesian approaches have been developed for specific settings but tied to specific model classes
(
41
;
39
;
14
;
8
;
41
;
1

. These methods typically embed causal structure into a chosen probabilistic model (e.g., multi-task Gaussian process models for CATE estimation, which impose Gaussian process priors;
1

However, such approaches are inherently model-specific and depend on strong structural assumptions about the data-generating process. As a result, they do not offer a general framework for uncertainty quantification across causal estimands or learning pipelines, and their performance and uncertainty statements are closely tied to the correctness of the chosen model class. Our work is complementary in scope: rather than proposing another model-specific Bayesian causal method, we develop a general loss-based framework that can be applied on top of existing causal estimators and learning pipelines.

A key challenge for likelihood-based Bayesian causal inference is robustness to nuisance estimation error. In particular, likelihood-based approaches can suffer from a feedback loop in which outcome posterior information influences nuisance posteriors, thereby undermining robustness
(
34
;
32

(

;
6

33

3
)

Yet, this stream does not provide a general framework for uncertainty quantification that preserves orthogonality.
In Section
4

Research gap:
Overall, existing Bayesian causal methods largely rely on model- or estimand-specific constructions and often lack robustness to nuisance misspecification, which limits their applicability as a general framework for uncertainty quantification. A unified framework across estimands and models for (generalized) Bayesian causal inference is still missing.

4
Why standard Bayesian approaches for causal inference are difficult

The naïve approach to Bayesian inference of a causal query
\theta
(in a fixed causal setting
\mathcal{C}
) would be to simply follow the classical Bayes rule
q(\theta|\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}|\theta)\pi(\theta).
5
5

5

By
\propto
we denote proportionality up to a multiplicative constant.

While specifying a prior
\pi(\theta)
directly on
\theta\in\Theta
does not pose any issues—and is in fact desirable for encoding belief into prior—the likelihood
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}|\theta)
is problematic. In causal settings,
\theta
typically does not fully determine the observed data distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, so
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}|\theta)
is not directly defined without further modeling of the data-generating process. For example:

\blacksquare

Example 1 (back-door ATE).

Many different distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
of
(X,A,Y)
induce the
same
ATE
\theta_{\mathrm{ATE}}
. As a result,
\theta_{\mathrm{ATE}}
does
not
uniquely determine a value of the likelihood for
(X,A,Y)
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}\mid\theta_{\mathrm{ATE}})
is
not
well-defined.

Instead, a proper likelihood is defined as
p(\mathcal{D}_{n}\mid\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})
, with a corresponding prior
\pi(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})
over the space of data-generating distributions. This induces the posterior distribution

\displaystyle q(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\int\delta_{\theta}(\bar{Q}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}))p(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\;\rm d\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}},

(5)

which is a marginalization of the joint posterior
p(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}|\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto\pi(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})p(\mathcal{D}|\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})
, and where
\delta_{\theta}(\bar{Q}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}))
denotes a Dirac delta of
\bar{Q}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})
at
\theta
.

To make the prior distribution and likelihood at the level of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
tractable, we restrict the space of possible data-generating distributions to a (semi-) parameterized subset
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{obs}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
. For example:

\blacksquare

Examples 1 & 2 (back-door ATE & CATE):

In the back-door adjustment settings, we may parameterize a model of the data-generating process
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs},\xi}(O)=p_{\xi_{x}}(X)p_{\xi_{a}}(A\mid X)p_{\xi_{y}}(Y\mid A,X)
from a corresponding model class
Q_{\mathrm{obs},\xi}=\bigl\{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs},\xi}(O)\mid\xi=(\xi_{x},\xi_{a},\xi_{y})\in\Xi\bigr\}
, together with some prior
\pi(\xi)\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi)
on the parameters
\xi
. Notice that
\xi_{a}
and
\xi_{y}
actually parametrize the propensity and outcome regression nuisances. The standard Bayes approach thus forces us to specify a prior over nuisances and to compute a posterior for the entire
\xi
, which will typically be higher-dimensional than
\theta
, possibly even infinite-dimensional. The posterior for
\theta
must then finally be computed as a marginalization of the overall posterior over all (model) parameters
\xi
of the data-generating process.

As illustrated by the above example, several practical difficulties arise even in seemingly simple causal settings. In particular:

1.

Model misspecification.
The restricted model class
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{obs}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
may fail to contain the true observational distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{\star}
, especially if the model class is overly restrictive or otherwise misspecified. In this case, the likelihood is misspecified and the correct Bayesian posterior is
not
obtained. Nevertheless, such restrictions are often unavoidable in practice for computational and regularization reasons.

2.

Priors on high-dimensional nuisance functions.
As illustrated in the back-door example, specifying a likelihood requires placing priors on nuisance components such as propensity scores and outcome regressions. Even after restricting
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, these nuisance objects are typically high-dimensional functions, for which posterior convergence and Bernstein–von Mises approximation are
not
guaranteed
unless strong additional assumptions are enforced

(
11
)
.

3.

Sensitivity to nuisance priors, including feedback problems.
The (marginalized) posterior over the causal estimand
\theta
depends on an appropriate prior specification for the nuisance components but correct prior elicitation is difficult. For example, even when the propensity and outcome nuisances are given independent priors, a joint likelihood couples their posteriors; outcome information can then feed back into propensity estimation, which may harm balance and robustness under misspecification (the feedback problem)
(
34
;
28
)
.

4.

Lack of direct control over the induced prior on the causal estimand.

Placing a prior on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
induces also a prior on the causal estimand
\theta
via a non-trivial mapping
\pi(\theta)=\int\delta_{\bar{Q}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})}(\theta)\pi(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})\;\rm d\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
. As a consequence, it is difficult—if not impossible—to directly elicit prior beliefs about
\theta
. This is critical if we want to follow the philosophy of Bayesian updating of beliefs; yet, following the exact Bayes rule formula in causal inference settings may thus make the task of eliciting a prior belief on
\theta
difficult.

With the understanding of how standard Bayesian inference in causal inference is made difficult by nuisance functions, we now turn to generalized Bayes for an alternative solution.

5
Method (Generalized Bayes)

We proceed in four steps. In §
5.1
, we introduce generalized posteriors (i.e., Gibbs posteriors) based on identification-driven losses, which define a principled Bayesian update of belief for causal estimands. In §
5.2
, we turn to the realistic case where nuisances must be estimated and, in §
5.3
, we present an algorithm for computing the corresponding feasible generalized posteriors. In §
5.4
, we provide our main theoretical result: for Neyman-orthogonal losses with cross-fitting, the feasible posterior converges to its oracle counterpart and yields valid uncertainty quantification.

5.1
Gibbs posteriors

To define a Gibbs posterior, we consider a fixed causal setting
\mathcal{C}
with an identified estimand
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
and an estimation strategy
S
with loss
\mathcal{L}^{S}_{n}(\theta;\eta^{S})
. Let
\pi(\theta)\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
denote a prior distribution on the causal estimand, and let
\omega>0
be a calibration parameter. Then, the corresponding
generalized posterior
(i.e., Gibbs posterior)
(
5
)
is defined as

\displaystyle q^{S}_{n}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto\exp\bigl\{-\omega n\mathcal{L}^{S}_{n}(\theta;\eta^{S})\bigr\}\pi(\theta).

(6)

This construction turns any loss-based estimator of a causal estimand
\theta^{\star}
into a full posterior distribution over
\Theta

without
specifying a likelihood or modeling the observational distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
. Equivalently, the same Gibbs posterior can be characterized as the solution to an optimization problem over distributions

\displaystyle q^{S}_{n}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim q}\bigl[\omega n\mathcal{L}^{S}_{n}(\theta;\eta^{S})\bigr]+\mathrm{KL}(q\parallel\pi)\right\}

where
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\parallel\cdot)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence
(
21
)
. This offers a variational formulation, which is particularly useful in practice, as simply replacing
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
by a desired variational family yields a tractable variational objective for approximating the generalized posterior.

5.2
Feasible posteriors

The Gibbs posterior defined above assumes that the nuisance components entering the loss are known. In practice, however, these nuisances must be estimated from data. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
denote the true observational distribution, and write

\eta^{S}_{0}:=\eta^{S}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}})
for the corresponding true (oracle) nuisances of the estimation strategy
S
. We define the
oracle
Gibbs posterior as

\displaystyle q^{S}_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}):=q^{S}_{n}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n};\eta^{S}_{0}).

(7)

In practice,
\eta^{S}_{0}
is unknown and must be estimated, which yields the
feasible
Gibbs posterior

\displaystyle q^{S}_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}):=q^{S}_{n}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n};\hat{\eta}^{S}).

(8)

5.3
Algorithm

To compute the feasible Gibbs posterior in practice, we propose a generic algorithmic procedure (see Algorithm
1
). The algorithm is agnostic to the specific causal estimand (e.g., ATE, CATE) and estimation strategy (e.g., Neyman-orthogonal estimators). The algorithm employs cross-fitting to estimate nuisance components on held-out data, which mitigates overfitting and is needed for Neyman-orthogonality later.

Choosing the calibration parameter
\omega
:

The calibration parameter
\omega
is not specified by either the prior or the loss, and must be chosen separately. To achieve frequentist-valid uncertainty quantification, we follow standard practice in generalized Bayes and tune
\omega
, so that a chosen
(1-\alpha)
posterior credible set has approximate
(1-\alpha)
repeated-sampling coverage. Concretely, we use the bootstrap calibration scheme of
35
: for a candidate
\omega
, we construct the credible region
C_{\omega,\alpha}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
, estimate its coverage by nonparametric bootstrap resampling (by checking containment of the point estimate
\hat{\theta}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{S}(\theta,\hat{\eta}^{S})
), and we update
\omega
via one-dimensional search or stochastic approximation until the empirical coverage matches
1-\alpha
. We refer to
35
for a detailed discussion.

When the object of interest is infinite-dimensional (e.g., a CATE function
\theta(\cdot)
), Bernstein–von Mises results and calibration guarantees only typically apply to finite-dimensional functionals
F(\theta)
(such as finite set of point evaluations, the mean, the median, etc.). In these cases, we calibrate
\omega
for the reported functional
F(\theta)
using the same procedure. For squared-error losses, the Gibbs posterior corresponds to a Gaussian working likelihood with variance
\omega^{-1}
. Hence, a convenient plug-in choice is therefore
\omega=\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{Y}^{S})^{-1}
, where
\hat{Y}^{S}
denotes the pseudo-outcome.

5.4
Theoretical results

Our central methodological question is:
When does the feasible Gibbs posterior
q^{S}_{n,\mathrm{fe}}
behave like the oracle Gibbs

q^{S}_{n,\mathrm{or}}
despite the nuisance estimation?

The answer depends on how the nuisance estimation error enters the loss
\mathcal{L}^{S}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta}^{S})
. State-of-the-art methods for causal ML
(
7
;
27
;
17
, e.g.,)

address this challenge by constructing
Neyman-orthogonal
losses
(
10
)
. For such orthogonal losses, the score
D_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{n}
—i.e., which determines the first-order optimality condition and the corresponding gradient updates—is insensitive to local perturbations in the nuisances
D_{\eta^{S}}
around the true value
\eta^{S}_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\eta^{S}}D_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{n}(\theta^{\star};\eta^{S}_{0})[\theta-\theta^{\star},\eta-\eta^{S}_{0}]=0.

(9)

Neyman-orthogonality makes the leading bias term
\hat{\eta}^{S}-\eta^{S}_{0}
vanish, so that the estimation of
\theta
can remain
\sqrt{n}
-consistent and asymptotically normal, even when the nuisances are learned at slower, nonparametric rates (up to a product-rate condition; such as those of flexible machine learning models)
(
10
)
.

In Theorem
5.1
, we show that leveraging the same orthogonality principle for constructing generalized posteriors yields an analogous result: the feasible posterior converges to the oracle even at less-than-parametric rates of convergence of the nuisances.

Theorem 5.1

(Posterior stability under orthogonal losses (informal))
.

Assume that: (i) the
oracle
Gibbs posterior satisfies a Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) approximation;
(ii) the loss is Neyman orthogonal at
(\theta^{\star},\eta^{S}_{0})
in the sense of Eq.
9
; and
(iii)
\hat{\eta}^{S}
is obtained by sample splitting/cross-fitting and satisfies
\|\hat{\eta}^{S}-\eta^{S}_{0}\|=:r_{n}\to 0
.
Then, the feasible generalized posterior is asymptotically close to the oracle one at the rate

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\left(q^{S}_{n,\mathrm{fe}},\ q^{S}_{n,\mathrm{or}}\right)\;=\;O_{\mathbb{P}}\!\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\bigr)\;+\;o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

(10)

where
\mathrm{TV}
denotes total variation on the target of inference:
either (a) the full parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(finite-dimensional case), or
(b) the induced posterior of any fixed finite-dimensional projection
T(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
when
\theta
is infinite-dimensional (e.g. pointwise CATE values at fixed covariate points).
In particular, if
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/4})
, then
\mathrm{TV}\to 0
in probability and the feasible posterior inherits the corresponding oracle BvM limit.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
for formal statement and proof.
∎

The rate condition
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/4})
matches the familiar threshold from orthogonal learning used in double ML theory
(
7
)
: the nuisance estimators may converge substantially slower than
n^{-1/2}
, while still yielding asymptotically valid uncertainty for low-dimensional targets. We have thus extended the theoretical guarantees of orthogonal statistical learning
(
10
)
from point estimation to their corresponding generalized posteriors.

Remark 5.2

(Why this fails without orthogonality)
.

For non-orthogonal losses, the analogous
\mathrm{TV}
of Eq. (
10
) typically scales as
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n})
rather than
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2})
, which requires nuisance estimators to converge at near-parametric rates in order to keep posterior perturbations small. This explains why generalized posteriors for causal inference built from naïve plug-in losses can be unstable in modern settings with high-dimensional and flexible ML-based nuisance estimators.

Example 1 (back-door ATE).

We illustrate the robustness of Gibbs posteriors by deriving an analytic solution for the back-door adjusted ATE setting using the squared-error loss constructed from the AIPW pseudo-outcome
\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
. Specifically, let us consider the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})=\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}(O_{i};\hat{\eta})-\theta\bigr)^{2}.

(11)

The corresponding loss minimizer is the usual AIPW estimator, i.e.,
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}=\argmin_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{i}
. The oracle pseudo-outcome
\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}(\cdot,\eta)
coincides with the efficient influence function of
\theta^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
of the ATE, implying that the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
is asymptotically unbiased and statistically efficient.

The corresponding feasible Gibbs posterior is given by

\displaystyle q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left\{-\omega n\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\right\}\pi(\theta)

(12)

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left\{-\tfrac{\omega}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{i}-\theta)^{2}\right\}\pi(\theta),

(13)

which allows for a closed-form solution under a normal prior
\pi(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(m_{0},s_{0}^{2})
. In that case, the posterior is exactly normal, i.e.,

\displaystyle q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(m_{p},s_{p}^{2}),

\displaystyle s_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl(s_{0}^{-2}+\omega n\bigr)^{-1},

\displaystyle m_{p}

\displaystyle=s_{p}^{2}\bigl(s_{0}^{-2}m_{0}+\omega n\,\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}\bigr).

In particular, under a diffuse prior
s_{0}^{2}\to\infty
, we have
m_{p}\to\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
and
s_{p}^{2}\to(\omega n)^{-1}
, meaning the generalized posterior centers exactly at the classical
orthogonal point estimate (while
\omega
controls its spread).

Importantly, the only data-dependent term of the analytical solution of the posterior parameters
m_{p},s_{p}^{2}
is the point-estimate
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
, i.e., the minimizer of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})
. As such, we can see how Neyman-orthogonality of the loss directly translates into convergence guarantees for the generalized posterior under nuisance estimation error. Our theorem shows this generally, even when no analytical solution to the posterior is available.

Finally, note that the analytical solution to the posterior was achieved by recognizing that Eq. (
13
) is algebraically equivalent to having a Gaussian “working likelihood” of the pseudo-outcomes
\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{i}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\omega^{-1})
and employing the normal-normal conjugacy. Under this interpretation, the generalized posterior is calibrated by setting
\omega^{-1}=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}})
. Since
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
is the efficient estimator of
\theta_{\mathrm{ATE}}
, it achieves the lowest possible asymptotic variance among all regular, asymptotically linear (RAL) estimators. Consequently,

\displaystyle s_{p}^{2}\to(n\omega)^{-1}=n^{-1}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{AIPW}})=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{ATE}}),

(14)

which implies that, under a diffuse prior, the corresponding frequentist-calibrated generalized posterior achieves the smallest achievable frequentist-calibrated variance and has, in turn, the narrowest faithful credible intervals.

Finite dimensional
\theta

Theorem A.1

(Posterior stability under orthogonal losses (finite-dimensional
\theta
))
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open, and let
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
be a nuisance parameter with truth

\eta_{0}
. For a measurable loss
\ell(O;\theta,\eta)
, define the empirical and population risks

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(O_{i};\theta,\eta),\qquad L(\theta;\eta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\{\ell(O;\theta,\eta)\}.

(15)

Fix
\omega>0
and a prior density
\pi
on
\Theta
. Define the oracle and feasible Gibbs posteriors

q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\ \propto\ \pi(\theta)\exp\{-\omega n\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta_{0})\},\qquad q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\ \propto\ \pi(\theta)\exp\{-\omega n\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\},

(16)

and the corresponding empirical minimizers

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta_{0}),\qquad\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta}).

(17)

Let
\theta^{\star}
denote the target parameter (the population minimizer of
L(\theta;\eta_{0})
), and
set

V_{0}:=\nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta^{\star};\eta_{0}),

(18)

assumed positive definite.

Assume:

(A1)

(BvM approximations for oracle and feasible posteriors).

The oracle and feasible posteriors admit Bernstein–von Mises approximations with common covariance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\Bigl(q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\Bigl(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(20)

(For instance, Eq. ((
19
)) may follow from
25
; and, under sample splitting/cross-fitting
and
\|\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0}\|\to 0

20
).)

(A2)

(Neyman orthogonality).

The loss is Neyman orthogonal at
(\theta^{\star},\eta_{0})
in the sense of Eq. (
9
).

(A3)

(Cross-fitting and nuisance rate).

\hat{\eta}
is obtained by sample splitting/cross-fitting and satisfies

.

(\theta^{\star},\eta_{0})
, so that

\|\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}\|=O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(r_{n}^{2})
.

Then, the total variation distance between feasible and oracle posteriors obeys

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\left(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)\;=\;O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\!\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\bigr)\;+\;o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

In particular, if

\displaystyle L(\theta;\eta)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\{\ell(O;\theta,\eta)\}.

(22)

The
oracle
and

\displaystyle q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\

\displaystyle q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}\ :=\ \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta,\eta_{0}),\qquad\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}\ :=\ \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta,\hat{\eta}).

Assumption (A1) states that the oracle Gibbs posterior satisfies a generalized Bernstein-von-Mises theorem.
In particular, there exists a deterministic positive definite matrix

\displaystyle V_{0}\ :=\ \nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta^{\star},\eta_{0})

(26)

such that the oracle posterior is asymptotically Gaussian with center
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}

and covariance
(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{TV}\!\Bigl(q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(27)

(See the Gibbs-posterior BvM statement as in
25
, here adapted to our notation.)

Next, we argue the same Gaussian approximation holds for the feasible posterior.
Because
\hat{\eta}
is obtained by sample splitting/cross-fitting, conditional on the fold-wise
training samples used to construct
\hat{\eta}
, the validation losses

\ell(Z_{i};\theta,\hat{\eta})
entering

are i.i.d. across
i
within each

Under the same smoothness/curvature conditions as in (a) and using
\|\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0}\|=r_{n}\to 0

we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta\theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}},\hat{\eta})\ =\ V_{0}+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(28)

and a conditional version of the Gibbs-posterior BvM theorem yields

\mathrm{TV}\!\Bigl(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(29)

(If desired, one may treat Eq. (

) as an additional technical assumption; it is standard

) because

from the viewpoint of the validation sample.)

Step 2 (Orthogonality implies
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}=O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(r_{n}^{2})
).

Assumption (A2) is Neyman orthogonality of the loss at
(\theta^{\star},\eta_{0})
(in the sense of
eq.
9
). Under cross-fitting, orthogonality implies that first-order
perturbations in the nuisance do not affect the relevant first-order estimating equation, so the
impact of replacing
\eta_{0}
by
\hat{\eta}
on the empirical minimizer is second order. Concretely,
the standard orthogonal statistical learning argument (stability of Z/M-estimators under
orthogonality) gives

\|\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}\|\ =\ O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(r_{n}^{2}).

(30)

Loosely: write the empirical first-order conditions

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}},\eta_{0})=0
and
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}},\hat{\eta})=0
,
take a Taylor expansion of
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})
around
(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}},\eta_{0})
,
use that the mixed first-order term in
(\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0})
vanishes by orthogonality (and
cross-fitting eliminates empirical-process bias terms), and then solve for the shift

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}
using the local invertibility/strong convexity
of
\nabla_{\theta\theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}
around
\theta^{\star}
. The resulting remainder is of order

\|\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0}\|^{2}=r_{n}^{2}
, yielding Eq. (
30
). See
10
for a rigorous derivation.

Step 3 (Reduce the posterior TV distance to TV between two Gaussians).

Let

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{or}}\ :=\ \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\bigr),\qquad G_{\mathrm{fe}}\ :=\ \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\bigr).

(31)

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\left(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}\!\left(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}},G_{\mathrm{fe}}\right)+\mathrm{TV}\!\left(G_{\mathrm{fe}},G_{\mathrm{or}}\right)+\mathrm{TV}\!\left(G_{\mathrm{or}},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}\right).

(32)

The first and third terms are
o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1)
by Eq. (
29
) and Eq. (
27
). It remains to
bound
\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}},G_{\mathrm{or}})
.

Step 4 (TV bound between Gaussians with common covariance).

Both Gaussians have the same covariance matrix
\Sigma=(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}
but different means

\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}=\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}
,
\mu_{\mathrm{or}}=\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}
.
The KL divergence between multivariate normals with equal covariance is

\mathrm{KL}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}\|G_{\mathrm{or}})=\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}})^{\top}(n\omega V_{0})(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}).

(33)

By Pinsker’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}},G_{\mathrm{or}})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{KL}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}\|G_{\mathrm{or}})}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}})^{\top}(n\omega V_{0})(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n\omega\,\lambda_{\max}(V_{0})}\,\|\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}\|.

(34)

Combining Eq. (
34
) with orthogonality results in Eq. (
30
) yields

\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}},G_{\mathrm{or}})\ =\ O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\!\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\bigr),

(35)

where
\lambda_{\max}(V_{0})
is the largest eigenvalue of
V_{0}
.

Step 5 (Compose the overall rate).

Plugging Eqs. (
35
), (
29
), and (
27
) into
(
32
) gives

\mathrm{TV}\!\left(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\!\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\bigr)+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(36)

which is the rate we set out to prove. In particular, if
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/4})
, then

\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\to 0
and, hence,
\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}},q_{n,\mathrm{or}})\to 0
in probability.
Since total variation convergence implies convergence of all bounded measurable functionals,
the feasible posterior inherits the oracle Bernstein-von-Mises limit (and, therefore, the same
asymptotic Gaussian approximation for credible sets).
∎

A.2
Finite dimensional projections of an infinite dimensional
\theta

Theorem A.2

(Posterior stability under orthogonal losses for infinite-dimensional
\theta
(finite-dimensional projections))
.

Let
\Theta
be a separable Hilbert space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Theta}
.
Let
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
be a nuisance parameter with truth
\eta_{0}
, and define

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(O_{i};\theta,\eta),\qquad L(\theta;\eta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\{\ell(O;\theta,\eta)\},

(37)

where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the true observational distribution. Define the oracle and feasible Gibbs posteriors

q_{n,\mathrm{or}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\ \propto\ \pi(\theta)\exp\{-\omega n\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta_{0})\},\qquad q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\ \propto\ \pi(\theta)\exp\{-\omega n\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\}.

(38)

Let
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta_{0})
and

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and bounded linear functionals
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\in\Theta^{\ast}
, and write

T(\theta):=(T_{1}(\theta),\dots,T_{m}(\theta))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T}
and
q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T}
denote the induced (pushforward) posteriors on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T}:=q_{n,\mathrm{or}}\circ T^{-1},\qquad q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T}:=q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}\circ T^{-1}.

(39)

Assume:

(B1)

(Projection BvM, oracle, and feasible).

There exists a bounded, self-adjoint, strictly positive operator

V_{0}:=\nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta^{\star};\eta_{0})
on
\Theta
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\Bigl(q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ \mathcal{N}_{m}\!\bigl(T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}),\ (n\omega\,TV_{0}T^{\top})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(40)

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\!\Bigl(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ \mathcal{N}_{m}\!\bigl(T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}),\ (n\omega\,TV_{0}T^{\top})^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(41)

where
TV_{0}T^{\top}
is the
m\times m
matrix with entries

(TV_{0}T^{\top})_{jk}:=\langle T_{j}^{\sharp},\ V_{0}T_{k}^{\sharp}\rangle_{\Theta}
,
and
T_{j}^{\sharp}\in\Theta
is the Riesz representer of
T_{j}
.

(B2)

(Orthogonality).
The loss is Neyman orthogonal at
(\theta^{\star},\eta_{0})
in the sense of
Eq. (
9
).

(B3)

(Cross-fitting and nuisance rate).

\hat{\eta}
is obtained by sample splitting/cross-fitting and

\|\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0}\|=:r_{n}\to 0
.

Then

\mathrm{TV}\!\left(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}),\ q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)\;=\;O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\!\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\bigr)\;+\;o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(42)

In particular, if
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/4})
, then

\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T})\to 0
in probability, so the feasible posterior inherits the oracle
projection BvM limit from Eq. (
40
) for
T(\theta)
.

Proof.

We omit the conditioning on
\mathcal{D}_{n}
for readability.

Step 1 (Gaussian approximations in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
).

By Eq. (
40
)–(
41
), define the Gaussian approximations

G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T}:=\mathcal{N}_{m}\!\bigl(T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}),\ (n\omega\,TV_{0}T^{\top})^{-1}\bigr),\qquad G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T}:=\mathcal{N}_{m}\!\bigl(T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}),\ (n\omega\,TV_{0}T^{\top})^{-1}\bigr),

(43)

so that

\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1)
and

\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T})=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1)
.

Step 2 (Orthogonality implies a second-order center shift).

Under Neyman orthogonality and cross-fitting, the usual orthogonal statistical learning stability
argument extends to Hilbert-space M-estimation (Fréchet differentiability and local strong convexity
of
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta;\eta)
):

\|\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}}-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}}\|_{\Theta}\ =\ O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(r_{n}^{2}).

(44)

Since each
T_{j}
is bounded linear,
\|T(\delta)\|_{2}\leq\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\delta\|_{\Theta}
and hence

\|T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}})-T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}})\|_{2}\;=\;O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(r_{n}^{2}).

(45)

Step 3 (Triangle inequality).

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T})+\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})+\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T}).

(46)

The first and third terms are
o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1)
. It remains to bound
\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})
.

Step 4 (TV bound between Gaussians with common covariance).

Both Gaussians have the same covariance

\Sigma_{T}=(n\omega\,TV_{0}T^{\top})^{-1}
and means

\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}=T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{fe}})
,
\mu_{\mathrm{or}}=T(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{or}})
.
For multivariate normals with equal covariance,

\mathrm{KL}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T}\|G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})=\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}})^{\top}\Sigma_{T}^{-1}(\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}})=\frac{n\omega}{2}(\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}})^{\top}(TV_{0}T^{\top})(\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}}).

(47)

By Pinsker’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{KL}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T}\|G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n\omega\,\lambda_{\max}(TV_{0}T^{\top})}\;\|\mu_{\mathrm{fe}}-\mu_{\mathrm{or}}\|_{2}.

(48)

Using Eq. (
45
) and that
\lambda_{\max}(TV_{0}T^{\top})=O(1)
for fixed
T
,
we get

\mathrm{TV}(G_{\mathrm{fe}}^{T},G_{\mathrm{or}}^{T})=O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}).

(49)

Step 5 (Combine).

Plugging into Eq. (
46
) yields

\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T})=O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2})+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1)
.
If
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/4})
, then
\sqrt{n}\,r_{n}^{2}\to 0
and, hence,

\mathrm{TV}(q_{n,\mathrm{fe}}^{T},q_{n,\mathrm{or}}^{T})\to 0
in probability.
∎

Remark A.3

(CATE as a special case)
.

If
\theta=\tau
is the CATE function and
T
is the evaluation map

T(\tau)=(\tau(x_{1}),\dots,\tau(x_{m}))
, the theorem gives stability (and inherited BvM)
for any fixed finite set of evaluation points
x_{1:m}
, provided point evaluation is continuous
in the chosen function space
\Theta
.

Comment on the feasible BvM assumptions in the finite- and infinite-dimensional cases.

Theorem
A.1
is stated under a BvM approximation for the
feasible
Gibbs posterior in the
finite-dimensional setting (Eq. (
20
)) and, in the infinite-dimensional setting, under an analogous BvM condition
for a finite-dimensional projection of the feasible posterior (Eq. (
41
)). These conditions are standard in the
theory of generalized (Gibbs) posteriors based on smooth, locally strongly convex empirical risks, but they are not
automatic here because the feasible empirical risk depends on estimated nuisance functions
\hat{\eta}
.
In particular, one must verify that the cross-fitted empirical risk admits a uniform local quadratic expansion at the
relevant minimizer and that the posterior concentrates on
n^{-1/2}
-neighborhoods where this expansion is valid.

Lemma
A.4
below gives a concrete set of sufficient conditions under which Eq (
20
) holds in the
finite-dimensional case. The proof is a self-contained Laplace/BvM argument for the feasible Gibbs posterior centered at
the feasible minimizer
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}
, based on (i) cross-fitting (to ensure fold-wise conditional i.i.d. structure for
validation losses given the corresponding training sample), (ii) uniform laws of large numbers for the risk and its
derivatives, and (iii) local curvature/separation conditions ensuring posterior concentration.
While Lemma
A.4
is formulated for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the same argument applies verbatim to any

fixed finite-dimensional projection
of an infinite-dimensional parameter (e.g., the coordinate vector of

T(\theta)
for fixed
m
), provided the projected empirical risk satisfies the same local smoothness and curvature conditions. Consequently, Lemma
A.4
can be viewed as a sufficient condition template supporting the
finite-dimensional projection BvM in Eq. (
41
): one applies the lemma to the projected parameter, treating
m
as
fixed, and checks the stated conditions for the corresponding projected risk.

Lemma A.4

(Sufficient conditions for the feasible Gibbs BvM)
.

Consider the finite-dimensional setting of Theorem
A.1
with fixed
\omega>0
and prior density
\pi
on an open

\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose the feasible empirical risk is formed by
K
-fold cross-fitting as follows:
for each
n
, partition
\{1,\dots,n\}
into disjoint folds
I_{1},\dots,I_{K}
with
K
fixed and
|I_{k}|/n\to\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
,
and let
\hat{\eta}^{(-k)}
be constructed using only the training sample
\{O_{i}:i\in I_{k}^{c}\}
. Define

L_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\ :=\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\ell(O_{i};\theta,\hat{\eta}^{(-k)}),\qquad\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}L_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta}),

(50)

and the feasible Gibbs posterior

q_{n,fe}(\theta\mid D_{n})\ \propto\ \pi(\theta)\exp\{-\omega nL_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\}.

(51)

Let
\theta^{\star}
denote the unique minimizer of
L(\theta;\eta_{0}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\{\ell(O;\theta,\eta_{0})\}
,
and let
V_{0}:=\nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta^{\star};\eta_{0})
be positive definite.

Assume there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\overline{B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0})}\subset\Theta
and:

(C1)

(Prior regularity)

\pi
is continuous and strictly positive at
\theta^{\star}
.

(C2)

(Local smoothness in
\theta
)
For
\eta
in a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\eta_{0}
,
the map
\theta\mapsto\ell(O;\theta,\eta)
is three times continuously differentiable on
B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0})
.
Moreover, there exist envelopes
M_{j}(O)
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}[M_{j}(O)]<\infty
such that

\sup_{\theta\in B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0}),\ \eta\in\mathcal{N}}\big\|\nabla_{\theta}^{j}\ell(O;\theta,\eta)\big\|\ \leq\ M_{j}(O),\qquad j=0,2,3.

(52)

(C3)

(Nuisance consistency and containment)
With probability
\to 1
,
\hat{\eta}^{(-k)}\in\mathcal{N}
for all
k
and

r_{n}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\|\hat{\eta}^{(-k)}-\eta_{0}\|\to 0
in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
-probability.

(C4)

(Identification and curvature)

\theta^{\star}
is the unique minimizer of
L(\cdot;\eta_{0})
and there exist constants

c_{\mathrm{loc}},c_{\mathrm{sep}}>0
such that

(local quadratic growth)

\displaystyle L(\theta;\eta_{0})-L(\theta^{\star};\eta_{0})\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{loc}}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\quad\forall\theta\in B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0}),

(separation)

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\notin B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0})}\big\{L(\theta;\eta_{0})-L(\theta^{\star};\eta_{0})\big\}\ \geq\ c_{\mathrm{sep}}.

(C5)

(Continuity in
\eta
)
The maps
\eta\mapsto L(\theta;\eta)
and
\eta\mapsto\nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta;\eta)

are continuous at
\eta_{0}
uniformly over
\theta\in B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0})
.

Then, the feasible posterior admits the BvM approximation with covariance
(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}
:

TV\!\left(q_{n,fe}(\cdot\mid D_{n}),\ N\!\big(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1}\big)\right)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(53)

In particular, Eq. (
20
) in Theorem
A.1
holds under
(C1)–(C5)
.

Proof.

Write
B_{\delta}:=B(\theta^{\star},\delta)
and set
B_{0}:=B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0})
.

Step 1: Uniform LLNs for the cross-fitted risk and its Hessian on
B_{0}
.

For
j\in\{0,2,3\}
, define
\varphi_{j}(O;\theta,\eta):=\nabla_{\theta}^{j}\ell(O;\theta,\eta)
(with
\varphi_{0}=\ell
).
Fix a fold
k
and let
\mathcal{T}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by the training data
\{O_{i}:i\in I_{k}^{c}\}

(and any algorithmic randomness used to compute
\hat{\eta}^{(-k)}
). By cross-fitting, conditional on
\mathcal{T}_{k}

the nuisance
\hat{\eta}^{(-k)}
is fixed and the validation observations
\{O_{i}:i\in I_{k}\}
are i.i.d. and independent of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
Using the envelope bounds in
(C2)
and a standard (conditional) uniform law of large numbers on the compact set

\overline{B_{0}}
, we obtain, for each
j\in\{0,2,3\}
,

\sup_{\theta\in B_{0}}\left\|\frac{1}{|I_{k}|}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\varphi_{j}(O_{i};\theta,\hat{\eta}^{(-k)})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\big[\varphi_{j}(O;\theta,\hat{\eta}^{(-k)})\big]\right\|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(54)

By
(C3)
–
(C5)
and dominated convergence (using the envelopes), uniformly over
\theta\in B_{0}
, we have

\sup_{\theta\in B_{0}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\big[\varphi_{j}(O;\theta,\hat{\eta}^{(-k)})\big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\big[\varphi_{j}(O;\theta,\eta_{0})\big]\right\|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),\qquad j\in\{0,2,3\}.

(55)

Combining this with Eq. (
54
) and summing over folds (noting
K
is fixed and
|I_{k}|/n\to\alpha_{k}
),
yields the uniform convergences on
B_{0}
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in B_{0}}\big|L_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})-L(\theta;\eta_{0})\big|

\displaystyle=\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(56)

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in B_{0}}\big\|\nabla_{\theta\theta}L_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})-\nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta;\eta_{0})\big\|

\displaystyle=\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(57)

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in B_{0}}\big\|\nabla_{\theta\theta\theta}L_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\big\|

\displaystyle=\ O_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(58)

Step 2: Consistency and Hessian stability at
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}
.

By Eq. (
56
) and the identification/separation in
(C4)
, the arg min theorem implies

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\to\theta^{\star}
in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}
-probability and in particular

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\in B_{0})\to 1
.
Moreover, by Eq. (
57
) and continuity of
\theta\mapsto\nabla_{\theta\theta}L(\theta;\eta_{0})
,

\nabla_{\theta\theta}L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})\ =\ V_{0}+o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(59)

Since
V_{0}\succ 0
, Eq. (
59
) also implies that, with probability
\to 1
, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

\nabla_{\theta\theta}L_{n}(\theta;\hat{\eta})\succeq\lambda_{0}I_{d}
for all
\theta\in B_{0}
.

Step 3: Local quadratic expansion around
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}
.

Define the local parameter
h:=\sqrt{n}(\theta-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e})
and write
\theta(h):=\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}+h/\sqrt{n}
.
Let
Q_{n}
denote the distribution of
h
when
\theta\sim q_{n,fe}(\cdot\mid D_{n})
, and let

\Phi:=N(0,(\omega V_{0})^{-1})
. Since total variation is invariant under bijective measurable transformations,

TV\!\left(q_{n,fe}(\cdot\mid D_{n}),\ N(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1})\right)\ =\ TV(Q_{n},\Phi).

(60)

The (unnormalized) density of
Q_{n}
is proportional to

\tilde{q}_{n}(h)\ :=\ \pi(\theta(h))\exp\Big\{-\omega n\big(L_{n}(\theta(h);\hat{\eta})-L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})\big)\Big\}.

(61)

Because
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}
minimizes
L_{n}(\cdot;\hat{\eta})
, we have
\nabla_{\theta}L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})=0
.
By Taylor’s theorem, for each
h
, there exists
t=t(h)\in(0,1)
such that

n\big(L_{n}(\theta(h);\hat{\eta})-L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})\big)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\nabla_{\theta\theta}L_{n}\big(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}+th/\sqrt{n};\hat{\eta}\big)h.

(62)

Fix
M<\infty
. On the event
\{\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\in B_{0}\}
, for all sufficiently large
n
, we have

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}+th/\sqrt{n}\in B_{0}
uniformly over
\|h\|\leq M
.
Thus by Eq. (
57
)–(
59
),

\sup_{\|h\|\leq M}\left|n\big(L_{n}(\theta(h);\hat{\eta})-L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})\big)-\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}V_{0}h\right|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(63)

Moreover, by
(C1)
and
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\to\theta^{\star}
, for each fixed
M
,

\sup_{\|h\|\leq M}\left|\frac{\pi(\theta(h))}{\pi(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e})}-1\right|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(64)

Step 4: Tail control in local coordinates.

Let
B_{M}^{h}:=\{h:\|h\|\leq M\}
. Using Eq. (
62
) and the uniform lower bound on the Hessian on
B_{0}
,
on
\{\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\in B_{0}\}
and for all
\|h\|\leq\delta_{0}\sqrt{n}
,

n\big(L_{n}(\theta(h);\hat{\eta})-L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})\big)\ \geq\ \frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\|h\|^{2}.

(65)

Thus, for large
n
,

\tilde{q}_{n}(h)\ \leq\ \sup_{\theta\in B_{0}}\pi(\theta)\ \exp\!\left(-\omega\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\|h\|^{2}\right)\qquad\text{for all }\|h\|\leq\delta_{0}\sqrt{n}.

(66)

For
\|h\|>\delta_{0}\sqrt{n}
, we have
\theta(h)\notin B(\theta^{\star},\delta_{0})
for large
n
(since
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e}\to\theta^{\star}
),
and, by
(C4)
and Eq. (
56
), with probability
\to 1
,

L_{n}(\theta(h);\hat{\eta})-L_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e};\hat{\eta})\ \geq\ \frac{c_{\mathrm{sep}}}{2},

(67)

so
\tilde{q}_{n}(h)\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\pi(\theta)\exp(-\omega nc_{\mathrm{sep}}/2)
there.
Consequently, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
M<\infty
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \mathbb{P}\!\left(Q_{n}\big((B_{M}^{h})^{c}\big)>\varepsilon\right)\ =\ 0,\qquad\Phi\big((B_{M}^{h})^{c}\big)\ <\ \varepsilon.

(68)

Step 5: Conclude
TV(Q_{n},\Phi)\to 0
.

Let
\phi(h)
denote the density of
\Phi
, i.e.

\phi(h)\propto\exp\{-\frac{\omega}{2}h^{\top}V_{0}h\}
.
By Eq. (
63
)–(
64
), on
B_{M}^{h}
, we have the uniform approximation

\sup_{\|h\|\leq M}\left|\frac{\tilde{q}_{n}(h)}{\pi(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e})\,\phi(h)}-1\right|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1).

(69)

Combining this local uniform approximation with the tail control from Eq. (
68
) yields that the normalized density

f_{n}(h):=\tilde{q}_{n}(h)/\int\tilde{q}_{n}
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|f_{n}(h)-\phi(h)\big|\,dh\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(70)

hence
TV(Q_{n},\Phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int|f_{n}-\phi|=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1)
.
Therefore,

TV\!\left(q_{n,fe}(\cdot\mid D_{n}),\ N(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{f}e},(n\omega V_{0})^{-1})\right)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(1),

(71)

as claimed.
∎

Appendix C
Experiment Details

C.1
Implementation Details

The code for all experiments is available at
https://github.com/EmilJavurek/Generalised-Bayes-for-Causal-Inference
. Here, we provide a short description of the essential details.

Nuisances:

For backdoor experiments, the default nuisance estimator fits a logistic regression for the propensity and two ridge regressions for outcomes (T-learner). All models use a shared, fixed feature map

\Phi(X)=[X,X^{2},\sin(X),x_{\mathrm{extra}},1]
, where
x_{\mathrm{extra}}=X_{1}X_{2}
if
d\geq 2
(or
X_{1}^{3}
if
d=1
).
Propensities are regularized with
\lambda_{\text{prop}}
and clipped to
[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
to stabilize IPW/AIPW.
We use cross-fitting: Nuisances are fit on each training fold and pseudo-outcomes are evaluated on held-out folds.

Prior:

For ATE, the prior is
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
, with default
\mu_{0}=0
and
\sigma_{0}=1
.
For CATE, the prior is a Gaussian process with the kernel specified in the configuration - we mainly use RBF or Matern kernels.

Variational family:

For ATE, the variational family is a univariate Gaussian with parameters
(\mu,\log\sigma)
; optimizing in
\log\sigma
enforces positivity of
\sigma
and yields stable optimization. When optimization is stopped early, the learned posterior variance can be slightly conservative relative to the analytic closed form—this is preferred over an overly confident fitting procedure.
For CATE, the variational family is a sparse inducing-point GP with
M
inducing locations initialized from the training covariates. In the example CATE configuration, we use
M=20
and a Matern-
5/2
kernel with lengthscale 2.0, variance 2.0, and jitter
10^{-4}
.
A constant mean function is fit to the pseudo-outcomes and subtracted before GP fitting, and then added back to predictions.

Variational inference (VI):

The variational inference fit to the generalized posterior is fit with the Adam optimizer. For ATE, we use a batch size 200 (number of
\theta
samples per update), learning rate 0.03, and 2000 epochs. For CATE, the batch size field is ignored because the GP loss is evaluated in full batch.
