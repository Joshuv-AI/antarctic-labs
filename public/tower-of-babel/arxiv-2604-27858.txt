Geometric Complexity in Thermodynamics
Tan Van Vu, Keiji Saito

Abstract
The third law of thermodynamics forbids cooling a physical system to absolute zero in a finite number of operational steps. Although this unattainability principle has been quantified for specific state-to-state transitions, a universal, dynamics-independent bound for implementing a state-agnostic reset map remains elusive. In this work, we unveil the fundamental limits of physical map implementation by deriving a trade-off relation based on geometric complexity. By analyzing continuous paths of maps on a geometric manifold, we prove that the geometric complexity of any classical stochastic map or quantum channel is bounded from below by its execution error. As a consequence, we show that achieving zero error in a state-reset operation requires a divergent geometric complexity—a unified measure that naturally incorporates disparate physical resources, including infinite time, energetic cost, or control bandwidth. This unattainability principle holds universally across both classical and quantum regimes, establishing a strict geometric limit on the physical realization of reset operations in thermodynamic control and quantum computation.



The third law of thermodynamics places a definitive boundary on the manipulation of physical systems: according to the Nernst statement
Nernst 1906
, “
it is impossible for any process, no matter how idealized, to reduce the entropy of a system to its absolute-zero value in a finite number of operations
.” In the context of control and computation, this principle translates into a fundamental prohibition against the perfect initialization, or reset, of a system to a pure state
Di Franco and Paternostro 2013
;
Wu et al. 2013
;
Ticozzi and Viola 2014
;
Freitas et al. 2018
. Because resetting a state inherently requires the extraction of entropy, it is not merely an algorithmic prerequisite for classical and quantum computing, but a thermodynamic process governed by strict physical limits
Landauer 1961
;
Bérut et al. 2012
;
Goold et al. 2015
;
Proesmans et al. 2020
;
Zhen et al. 2021
;
Soldati et al. 2022
;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;
Campbell et al. 2026
.

While the third law is traditionally framed in terms of execution time, recent studies have revealed that time is not the sole decisive factor for microscopic systems. Other physical resources—such as thermokinetic cost, control bandwidth, and the size of heat baths—also play a crucial role
Reeb and Wolf 2014
;
Taranto et al. 2023
;
Masanes and Oppenheim 2017
;
Van Vu and Saito 2025
. Accordingly, quantitative relations for the third law have been derived to characterize the trade-off between these finite resources and the achievable error
Masanes and Oppenheim 2017
;
Wilming and Gallego 2017
;
Scharlau and Mueller 2018
;
Clivaz et al. 2019
;
Van Vu and Saito 2025
;
Allahverdyan et al. 2011
;
Levy and Kosloff 2012
. However, because the extant studies focus on specific, state-to-state dynamical processes where relevant resources are narrowly defined, the resulting bounds are highly dynamics-dependent. A change in the underlying dynamics or control protocol can readily render them inapplicable.

Crucially, a true reset operation cannot rely on bespoke protocols tailored to specific inputs; it must be implemented as a state-agnostic map capable of resetting an arbitrary, potentially unknown initial state. Such maps arise not only in cooling but more generally whenever probability must be removed from a particular subspace, as in state erasure or copying
Van Vu and Saito 2025
, and they can be physically realized through a wide variety of Markovian and non-Markovian pathways. Consequently, the difficulty of approaching a perfect reset manifests differently depending on the implementation—perhaps as a divergent execution time, the demand for infinite wall potentials, or the necessity of infinitely fast transition rates. This fragmentation gives rise to a fundamental question: Can the sheer difficulty of physically implementing reset maps be captured by a single, unified principle?

To answer this, a universal measure that transcends specific underlying dynamics is required. Complexity is the natural notion for characterizing the difficulty of executing a task. Historically, the operational cost of completing specific tasks has been quantified using discrete metrics, such as algorithmic time steps for computation (computational complexity)
Arora and Barak 2009
or primitive gate counts for unitary operations (gate complexity)
Brandão et al. 2021
;
Haferkamp et al. 2022
. However, to capture the continuous nature of physical processes, geometric complexity has recently emerged as a powerful framework
Nielsen 2005
;
Nielsen et al. 2006
;
Dowling and Nielsen 2006
. By measuring the geodesic length between the identity map and the target map within an abstract manifold, geometric complexity distills disparate physical constraints into a single, universal quantity. Despite the centrality of state-reset operations to thermodynamics, computation, and error correction, it has remained unknown whether a rigorous trade-off exists linking the complexity of a physical map to its execution fidelity.

In this work, we establish a complexity unattainability principle that bridges the thermodynamic and operational realms for dissipative maps (see Fig.
1
). We consider the manifold of physical maps and introduce a Riemannian metric that is designed to satisfy three essential properties: physical penalization, protocol scaling, and resource divergence. By employing geometric complexity to quantify the cost of continuously implementing a reset map, we derive a fundamental trade-off relation between the geometric complexity of the map and its execution error [Eq. (
11
)]. This relation indicates that as the error of the reset map approaches zero, the required geometric complexity inevitably diverges, formally mirroring the thermodynamic unattainability of absolute zero. Crucially, this trade-off relation is universal: it holds strictly across the classical-quantum divide, governing both classical stochastic processes and quantum channels. Our results thus establish a fundamental principle independent of the underlying dynamics, imposing strict limits on basic operations in thermodynamics and computation.

Results

We consider a stochastic map
\mathds{T}
on the space of
d
-dimensional probability distributions.
Specifically, the map
\mathds{T}=[T_{mn}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a matrix satisfying
T_{mn}\geq 0
and
\sum_{m=1}^{d}T_{mn}=1
for each
n=1,\dots,d
.
This stochastic map transforms any initial probability distribution
\mathbf{p}
into a final distribution
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
according to
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}=\mathds{T}\mathbf{p}
.
For example, when the underlying dynamics is a Markov jump process governed by a time-dependent transition matrix
\mathds{W}_{t}
over a total duration
\tau
, the map
\mathds{T}
is given by

\mathds{T}=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\mathds{W}_{t}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordering operator.
The stochastic maps described by Eq. (
1
) are important examples, but they are not exhaustive.
Notably, infinitely many stochastic maps cannot be realized by Markov jump processes.
For instance,
\mathds{T}
can represent classical non-Markovian dynamics, such as semi-Markov processes, in which the time-dependent distribution
\mathbf{p}_{t}
evolves according to the generalized master equation
Breuer and Vacchini 2008

\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\mathds{W}_{s}\mathbf{p}_{t-s},

(2)

where
\cdot
denotes the time derivative.
More generally, a stochastic map can also be induced by a quantum channel
\Lambda
by considering transitions between diagonal elements.
That is,
T_{mn}=\matrixelement{m}{\Lambda(\dyad{n})}{m}
for any
m
and
n
, where
\{\ket{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis.
In this study, we do not focus on the underlying dynamics used to realize the map; rather, we focus on the stochastic map itself.
Although we primarily discuss discrete-state cases, the extension to continuous-state cases is straightforward and is presented in the Appendices.

Among all feasible maps, we are particularly interested in those that are designed to suppress the probabilities of specific states to zero.
For example, in information erasure, the target map should reset the probability of the logical state ‘1’ to zero, regardless of the initial distribution.
In cooling, the populations of excited states should be reset to zero so that the system is driven to its ground state.
For the initialization of pure states, the reset map should reduce to zero the probability of finding the system in the subspace orthogonal to the target pure state.
In this work, we characterize the difficulty of implementing such reset maps from the perspective of complexity.

To characterize the complexity of a physical map, one possible quantifier is gate complexity.
In quantum computation, the gate complexity of a unitary circuit
U
is defined as the minimum number of primitive gates (e.g., one- and two-qubit gates) required to implement
U
.
Although stochastic maps and unitary operators have different mathematical structures, it is still natural to ask whether an analogous notion of gate complexity can be defined for stochastic maps.
By analogy with local gates, we define primitive operations that act nontrivially only on two states:

\mathds{T}_{mn}(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}1-\alpha&\beta\\
\alpha&1-\beta\end{pmatrix}\bigoplus\mathds{1}_{\setminus\{m,n\}},

(3)

where
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
and
\mathds{1}
denotes the identity matrix.
This operation exchanges probability between states
m
and
n
according to
p_{m}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)p_{m}+\beta p_{n}
and
p_{n}\leftarrow\alpha p_{m}+(1-\beta)p_{n}
.
Given these primitive operations, a natural question is whether there exists a finite sequence
\{\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
such that the stochastic map
\mathds{T}
can be decomposed as

\mathds{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\prod_{k\geq 1}\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}).

(4)

However, although this decomposition is feasible for certain maps, there exist stochastic maps that cannot be decomposed in this form.
If each primitive operation is implemented by a finite-time Markov jump process, one can verify that the constraint
\alpha+\beta\leq 1
must hold.
Under this constraint, the impossibility of Eq. (
4
) follows from the fact that each local operation has a nonnegative determinant,
\det[\mathds{T}_{mn}(\alpha,\beta)]=1-\alpha-\beta\geq 0
.
Hence, any stochastic map with a negative determinant cannot be decomposed as in Eq. (
4
), because determinants multiply under composition:
\det(\mds{T})=\prod_{k\geq 1}\det[\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})]
. If a stochastic map has a positive determinant, swapping any two rows yields one with a negative determinant.
Even if the condition
\alpha+\beta\leq 1
is relaxed, one can still construct stochastic maps that are not decomposable in this way (see Appendix
A
for the proof).
Therefore, it is not straightforward to define gate complexity for stochastic maps solely in terms of such primitive operations.

An alternative approach to quantifying complexity is grounded in geometry. Initially proposed for unitary operations in quantum computation
Nielsen 2005
;
Nielsen et al. 2006
;
Dowling and Nielsen 2006
, this framework has since been applied across diverse areas, including quantum many-body physics
Balasubramanian et al. 2020
;
Balasubramanian et al. 2021
, quantum field theory
Jefferson and Myers 2017
;
Chapman et al. 2018
, and holographic quantum gravity
Brown et al. 2016
;
Brown and Susskind 2018
(see Ref.
Baiguera et al. 2026
for a review). Intuitively, geometric complexity captures the operational difficulty of a unitary map by evaluating the cost of a smooth path connecting the identity to the target map. It employs a tailored metric on the unitary group that stretches directions associated with resource-intensive operations, thereby placing complex unitaries geometrically farther away. Consequently, continuous path length replaces discrete gate counts, and the complexity of a target unitary is rigorously defined by the length of its geodesic—the shortest, optimal continuous path. In what follows, we move beyond unitary operations, introducing geometric complexity for general classical stochastic maps and quantum channels.

Geometric complexity of stochastic maps

Consider the manifold of stochastic maps equipped with a Riemannian metric
g
.
The geometric complexity of a map
\mathds{T}
is defined as the geodesic length induced by
g
between the identity map
\mathds{1}
and the target map
\mathds{T}
:

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\coloneqq\min_{\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\mathds{T}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{T}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{T}}_{t})},

(5)

where the minimum is taken over all continuous paths
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
connecting the identity map to the given stochastic map (i.e.,
\mathds{T}_{0}=\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}_{1}=\mathds{T}
).
Intuitively,
\mathcal{C}
characterizes the minimum path cost over all continuous implementations of the stochastic map
\mathds{T}
starting from the identity map.
In general, there are many possible choices of metric
g
for defining complexity.
However, an appropriate metric and the corresponding complexity should satisfy three properties:

(i)

Physical penalization
: it should adequately penalize reset maps that are physically difficult to realize;

(ii)

Protocol scaling
: it should provide a linear lower bound on the number of protocols, thereby capturing the cumulative cost of map implementation;

(iii)

Divergent resources
: most importantly, it should capture the divergent physical resources required to achieve perfect reset maps.

For any
d\times d
matrix
\mathds{X}
, let
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{X}}\coloneqq\mathds{X}\mathbf{1}/d
denote its normalized row-sum vector, where
\mathbf{1}
is the all-one vector.
Using this notation and incorporating properties (i)–(iii), we define the following Riemannian metric on the space of stochastic maps:

g_{\mathds{T}}(\mathds{X},\mathds{Y})\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{r_{n}^{\mathds{X}}r_{n}^{\mathds{Y}}}{(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{2}},

(6)

where
\mathds{X}
and
\mathds{Y}
are arbitrary elements of the tangent space.
Evidently,
g
satisfies bilinearity, symmetry, and positive semidefiniteness.
Moreover,
g
is a log-barrier Hessian metric, arising from the Hessian of the potential
\Phi(\mathds{T})=-\sum_{n=1}^{d}\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}
.
Accordingly, this metric assigns a large weight when the map passes near the target region, where
r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}\ll 1
for
n\in\mathfrak{u}
[cf. property (i)], and
\mathfrak{u}\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
denotes the set of undesired states whose probabilities are ideally zero after the transformation.
Although the geodesic distance induced by this metric is analytically intractable under the stochasticity constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}}=1
, we can still derive simple lower and upper bounds on the complexity (Appendix
B
):

\ell\leq\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell,

(7)

where
\ell\coloneqq\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(\ln d+\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{2}}
is the geodesic distance induced by the metric
g
in Eq. (
6
)
without
imposing the constraint.
The lower bound implies that the complexity of perfect reset maps diverges, since
r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}=0
for
n\in\mathfrak{u}
and hence
\ell=\infty
.

To further justify this geometric quantity as a measure of complexity, we show its relation to the number of protocols required to implement a stochastic map [cf. property (ii)].
To this end, consider a stochastic map
\mathds{T}
constructed from
N
sequential protocols, where the
k
th protocol is realized by Markovian dynamics with a time-independent transition matrix
\mathds{W}_{k}
over a unit time interval.
The resulting stochastic map is

\mathds{T}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}e^{\mathds{W}_{k}}
.
Because each protocol is subject to physical implementation constraints, it is natural to require that the transition rates in each matrix
\mathds{W}_{k}
are not arbitrarily large.
Accordingly, we assume that the maximum escape rate is always bounded above by
\gamma
.
Under this assumption, we prove that the geometric complexity provides a lower bound on the number of protocols (Appendix
B
):

N\geq\frac{\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})}{(d+\sqrt{d})\ln(de^\gamma)}.

(8)

This inequality provides an operational interpretation of geometric complexity, thereby clarifying the physical meaning of
\mathcal{C}
as a complexity measure.
In Appendix
B
, we show that, within a parameterized class of Riemannian metrics that includes the classical Fisher information metric, the metric defined in Eq. (
6
) is the unique one satisfying properties (i)–(iii).

In addition, we derive an entropic lower bound on geometric complexity, thereby clarifying its connection to thermodynamics.
Specifically, we prove that the complexity is always lower-bounded by the difference between the Shannon entropy of the uniform distribution before and after resetting by the map (Appendix
B
):

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\geq\quantity{}\ln S(\mathbf{1}/d)-\ln S(\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}/d),

(9)

where
S(\mathbf{p})\coloneqq-\sum_{n=1}^{d}p_{n}\ln p_{n}
is the Shannon entropy of the probability distribution
\mathbf{p}
.
Relations (
8
) and (
9
) thus highlight the operational and thermodynamic aspects of geometric complexity.
Notably, relation (
9
) already sheds light on the unattainability of absolute zero entropy: achieving zero entropy,
S(\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}/d)=0
, necessitates infinite complexity
\mathcal{C}
. Nonetheless, in what follows, we establish a universal principle for reset maps, even in scenarios where the entropy is not reset to zero.

Unattainability principle via geometric complexity

With this continuous geometric framework in hand, we now investigate the fundamental difficulty of implementing state-reset maps.
The error
\epsilon
associated with a reset map
\mathds{T}
is naturally defined as

\epsilon(\mathds{T})\coloneqq\sum_{m\in\mathfrak{u}}\sum_{n=1}^{d}T_{mn}=\sum_{m\in\mathfrak{u}}(\mathds{T}\mathbf{1})_{m}.

(10)

Since
T_{mn}
is the conditional transition probability from state
n
to state
m
,
\epsilon
quantifies the extent to which the system remains in the undesired subspace
\mathfrak{u}
.
As our central result, we establish a rigorous trade-off relation between the geometric complexity of any stochastic map and its execution error:

e^{\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})}\times\epsilon(\mathds{T})\geq 1.

(11)

This inequality dictates that achieving zero error,
\epsilon(\mathds{T})=0
, requires the geometric complexity
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
to diverge, thereby manifesting the third law of thermodynamics in the implementation of stochastic maps.
Therefore, this result can also be regarded as a
geometric
third law of thermodynamics.
Together with relation (
8
), it also implies that infinitely many protocols are required to implement a perfect stochastic map via Markovian dynamics.

We illustrate the unattainability principle using a simple bit-reset map. Consider a reset map
\mathds{T}
implemented by a time-independent transition rate matrix
\mathds{W}
over a duration
\tau
, given by

\mathds{T}=e^{\mathds{W}\tau},~\text{where}~\mathds{W}=\begin{pmatrix}0&w\\
0&-w\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

Since the transition rate
w
from the excited state to the ground state is positive and the reverse jump is forbidden, this map can bring the system close to the ground state regardless of the initial distribution.
From the error expression
\epsilon(\mathds{T})=e^{-w\tau}
, it is evident that the key physical quantity governing reset accuracy is the product of the operation time and the kinetic transition rate.
For this stochastic map, the complexity can be calculated explicitly as

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})=2\atanh[\sqrt{2}f(w\tau)]-\sqrt{2}\atanh[f(w\tau)],

(13)

where
f(u)=(1-e^{-u})/\sqrt{1+(1-e^{-u})^{2}}
.
From the asymptotic relation
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})=w\tau+2\ln 2-\sqrt{2}\ln(1+\sqrt{2})+O(e^{-w\tau})
, we conclude that
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
captures both the operation time and the strength of the transition rates as relevant resources for reset maps.
Using
\epsilon(\mathds{T})=e^{-w\tau}
together with Eq. (
13
), we can confirm, both analytically and numerically, that the complexity satisfies the trade-off relation (
11
).

In what follows, we provide a brief proof of the trade-off relation (
11
).
First, note that the error of the stochastic map
\mathds{T}
can be written as
\epsilon(\mathds{T})=d\sum_{n\in\mathfrak{u}}r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}
.
Since
e^{x}\geq e^{-|x|}
for any real
x
and
e^{-x}
is convex, we obtain the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\epsilon(\mathds{T})=\sum_{n\in\mathfrak{u}}e^{\ln(dr_n^{\mds{T}})}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n\in\mathfrak{u}}e^{-|\ln(dr_n^{\mds{T}})|}

\displaystyle\geq|\mathfrak{u}|\,e^{-|\mathfrak{u}|^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathfrak{u}}|\ln(dr_n^{\mds{T}})|},

(14)

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality.
Next, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
\ell
satisfies

\ell\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathfrak{u}|}}\sum_{n\in\mathfrak{u}}|\ln(dr_n^{\mds{T}})|.

(15)

Combining Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
), we find

\epsilon(\mathds{T})\geq|\mathfrak{u}|e^{-\ell/\sqrt{|\mathfrak{u}|}}\geq e^{-\ell}.

(16)

Finally, since
\ell\leq\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
[Eq. (
7
)], we immediately obtain the desired relation (
11
).

Unified quantum generalization

Next, we derive a quantum generalization of geometric complexity and thereby establish the universality of the trade-off relation (
11
) between complexity and error.
As a quantum analog of classical stochastic maps, we consider a quantum channel
\Lambda
, i.e., a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map.
A relevant example is
\Lambda(\circ)=\tr_{E}[\mathds{U}(\circ\otimes\pi_{E})\mathds{U}^{\dagger}]
, which describes the general reduced dynamics of the system after the environment
E
is traced out. Here,
\pi_{E}
denotes the thermal Gibbs state of the environment, and the unitary operator
\mathds{U}
acts jointly on the system and environment.

We consider a quantum channel that resets arbitrary initial states from an undesired subspace.
Because it is sufficient to evaluate the error for the maximally mixed state, we define the error as the probability that the system remains in the undesired subspace (scaled by a factor of
d
):

\epsilon(\Lambda)\coloneqq\tr[\Pi\Lambda\quantity(\mds{1})],

(17)

where
\Pi
is the projector onto the undesired subspace.
For example, for cooling to the ground state,
\Pi=\sum_{n:\,E_{n}>0}\outerproduct{E_n}{E_n}
, where the sum runs over all excited energy eigenstates; for qubit erasure,
\Pi
is the projector onto the logical-‘1’ subspace.
Since the error for an arbitrary initial state is always upper-bounded by
\epsilon(\Lambda)
, this quantity provides a reliable error measure for quantum channels.

To formalize the geometry of quantum channels, it is convenient to use the Choi-matrix representation
Choi 1975
;
Jamiołkowski 1972
, which provides a channel–state duality. For any continuous path of quantum channels
\{\Lambda_{t}\}
, we consider the corresponding path of bipartite density operators
\{\mathds{M}_{t}\}
, where
\mathds{M}_{t}=(\mathds{1}\otimes\Lambda_{t})(\outerproduct{\Omega}{\Omega})
with
\ket{\Omega}=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\ket{n}\otimes\ket{n}
for an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{n}\}
.
Then we define the geometric complexity of the channel
\Lambda
, associated with a metric
g
, as

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\min_{\{\Lambda_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\mathds{M}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{M}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{M}}_{t})},

(18)

where the minimum is taken over all paths connecting the identity channel to
\Lambda
[i.e.,
\Lambda_{0}=\mathsf{Id}
and
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda
, with
\mathsf{Id}(\circ)=\circ
].
The Riemannian metric
g
on the space of the Choi matrices, which possesses three desired properties (i)–(iii), is defined by

g_{\mathds{M}}(\mathds{X},\mathds{Y})\coloneqq\tr(\phi_{\mds{M}}^{-1}\phi_{\mds{X}}\phi_{\mds{M}}^{-1}\phi_{\mds{Y}}),

(19)

where
\phi_{\mathds{X}}\coloneqq d^{-1}\tr_{1}\mathds{X}
for any bipartite operator
\mathds{X}
.
In the classical limit, this geometric complexity and its associated Riemannian metric reduce exactly to their classical counterparts in Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
).
Notably, all geometric relations derived for the classical case carry over to the quantum case (Appendix
D
).
First, simple lower and upper bounds on the complexity, analogous to Eq. (
7
), can be obtained:
\ell\leq\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell
, where
\ell=\|\ln d+\ln\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)\|_{F}
and
\|\mathds{X}\|_{F}\coloneqq\sqrt{\tr(\mds{X}^\dagger\mds{X})}
denotes the Frobenius norm of an operator
\mathds{X}
.
Second, the relation (
8
) between the complexity and the number of protocols can also be established for both Lindbladian maps and general dissipative maps.
Finally, the entropic bound (
9
) on geometric complexity can be derived analogously, with the Shannon entropy replaced by the von Neumann entropy.

Based on this quantum geometric framework, we arrive at the same complexity-error trade-off relation for quantum channels:

e^{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}\times\epsilon(\Lambda)\geq 1.

(20)

This structural parallelism between the classical and quantum domains highlights the universality of the geometric unattainability principle.

Conclusion

Understanding the fundamental physical limits of thermodynamic and computational processes requires a unified framework that subsumes diverse operational resources—such as execution time, energetic cost, and control bandwidth—into a single measure. In this work, we showed that complexity provides such a unifying measure, extending the third law of thermodynamics from a constraint on state-to-state cooling to a universal limit on physical map implementation. Specifically, by leveraging a geometric framework to quantify operational burden, we developed a comprehensive notion of complexity for both classical stochastic maps and quantum channels. This approach enabled us to establish a universal trade-off relation between the geometric complexity of a state-reset map and its execution error. By subsuming disparate physical ingredients into a single quantity, our central result yields a dynamics-independent unattainability principle: a perfect state-reset operation can be physically implemented only at diverging operational complexity. More broadly, by formalizing the relation between operational complexity and execution error, we showed that the third law of thermodynamics is not merely a state-dependent constraint on entropy extraction, but a fundamental and universal complexity bound of nature.

Appendix A
Proof of the impossibility of decomposing stochastic maps

Lemma 1
.

Consider primitive operations of the following form:

\mathds{T}_{mn}(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}1-\alpha&\beta\\
\alpha&1-\beta\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\setminus\{m,n\}},

(21)

where
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
.
Then there exists a stochastic map that cannot be decomposed into a product of these primitive operations.

Proof.

It suffices to provide a counterexample.
Specifically, we show that the following simple stochastic map cannot be decomposed into the given primitive operations:

\mathds{T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2&1/2\\
1/2&0&1/2\\
1/2&1/2&0\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

To this end, suppose that
\mathds{T}
can be decomposed into a product of primitive operations:

\mathds{T}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}),

(23)

where each
\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})
is a nontrivial primitive operation [i.e.,
\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\neq\mathds{1}
, or equivalently,
(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\neq(0,0)
] that acts only on the
m_{k}
th and
n_{k}
th rows for any
k=1,\dots,N
.
Let
\imath\in[1,N]
be the maximal index such that
(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\neq(1,1)
.
In other words,
\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})
is a swap operation for any
\imath+1\leq k\leq N
.
Note that there always exist permutation matrices
\mathds{P}_{1}
and
\mathds{P}_{2}
(which are trivially stochastic maps) such that
\mathds{T}_{m_{\imath}n_{\imath}}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathds{T}_{m_{\imath}n_{\imath}}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1-\alpha_{\imath}&\beta_{\imath}&0\\
\alpha_{\imath}&1-\beta_{\imath}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathds{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{1}\mathds{T}_{12}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\mathds{P}_{2}.

(24)

Define
\mathds{U}\coloneqq[\prod_{k=\imath+1}^{N}\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})]\mathds{P}_{1}
and
\mathds{V}\coloneqq\mathds{P}_{2}\prod_{k=1}^{\imath-1}\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})
.
Both are valid stochastic maps, and it follows that
\mathds{T}=\mathds{U}\mathds{T}_{12}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\mathds{V}
.
Note that
\mathds{P}^{\top}\mathds{P}=\mathds{P}\mathds{P}^{\top}=\mathds{1}
for any permutation matrix
\mathds{P}
, and that the product of permutation matrices is also a permutation matrix.
Combining this with the fact that
\mathds{T}_{m_{k}n_{k}}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})
is a permutation matrix for any
k\in[\imath+1,N]
implies that
\mathds{U}
is a permutation matrix.
Since
\mathds{U}^{\top}\mathds{T}
is obtained from
\mathds{T}
by swapping rows,
\mathds{U}^{\top}\mathds{T}
can be transformed back to
\mathds{T}
by swapping columns (due to the symmetry of
\mathds{T}
).
Equivalently, there exists a permutation matrix
\mathds{U}^{\prime}
(which is also a stochastic map) such that
\mathds{U}^{\top}\mathds{T}\mathds{U}^{\prime}=\mathds{T}
.
Substituting
\mathds{T}=\mathds{U}\mathds{T}_{12}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\mathds{V}
into the left-hand side of this equality, we obtain
\mathds{T}=\mathds{T}_{12}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\mathds{V}\mathds{U}^{\prime}=\mathds{T}_{12}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\mathds{O}
, where
\mathds{O}\coloneqq\mathds{V}\mathds{U}^{\prime}
is a stochastic map.
The relation
\mathds{T}=\mathds{T}_{12}(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\mathds{O}
can be explicitly expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2&1/2\\
1/2&0&1/2\\
1/2&1/2&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1-\alpha_{\imath}&\beta_{\imath}&0\\
\alpha_{\imath}&1-\beta_{\imath}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}O_{11}&O_{12}&O_{13}\\
O_{21}&O_{22}&O_{23}\\
O_{31}&O_{32}&O_{33}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\alpha_{\imath})O_{11}+\beta_{\imath}O_{21}&(1-\alpha_{\imath})O_{12}+\beta_{\imath}O_{22}&(1-\alpha_{\imath})O_{13}+\beta_{\imath}O_{23}\\
\alpha_{\imath}O_{11}+(1-\beta_{\imath})O_{21}&\alpha_{\imath}O_{12}+(1-\beta_{\imath})O_{22}&\alpha_{\imath}O_{13}+(1-\beta_{\imath})O_{23}\\
O_{31}&O_{32}&O_{33}\end{pmatrix}.

(25)

Note that
(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\neq(1,1)
and
(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\neq(0,0)
.
If
\alpha_{\imath}\in[0,1)
and
\beta_{\imath}\in(0,1]
, then the condition
0=(1-\alpha_{\imath})O_{11}+\beta_{\imath}O_{21}
immediately implies
O_{11}=O_{21}=0
.
As a result,
\alpha_{\imath}O_{11}+(1-\beta_{\imath})O_{21}=0\neq 1/2
, which violates Eq. (
25
).
Therefore, either
\alpha_{\imath}=1
or
\beta_{\imath}=0
.
If
\alpha_{\imath}=1
, the conditions
0=\alpha_{\imath}O_{12}+(1-\beta_{\imath})O_{22}
and
(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\neq(1,1)
imply
O_{12}=O_{22}=0
; thus,
(1-\alpha_{\imath})O_{12}+\beta_{\imath}O_{22}=0\neq 1/2
, which violates Eq. (
25
).
Otherwise, if
\beta_{\imath}=0
, the conditions
0=\alpha_{\imath}O_{12}+(1-\beta_{\imath})O_{22}
and
(\alpha_{\imath},\beta_{\imath})\neq(0,0)
lead to
O_{12}=O_{22}=0
; thus,
(1-\alpha_{\imath})O_{12}+\beta_{\imath}O_{22}=0\neq 1/2
, which also violates Eq. (
25
).
In other words, a contradiction always arises.
Therefore,
\mathds{T}
cannot be decomposed using the given primitive operations, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of results in the classical case

B.1
Proof of the lower and upper bounds (
7
)

We first prove the lower bound.
The geodesic distance under the constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{r}=1
is always larger than or equal to that without the constraint.
Therefore, the geometric complexity can be evaluated as

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\geq\min_{\{\mathbf{r}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}\quantity(\frac{\dot r_n(t)}{r_n(t)})^{2}},

(26)

where the minimum is over all paths
\{\mathbf{r}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
connecting
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{1}}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}}
.
Noting that the geodesic path has constant speed and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the lower bound is derived as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle\geq\quantity[\min_{\{\vb{r}_t\}_{0\le t\le 1}}\int_0^1\dd{t}\sum_{n=1}^d\qty(\frac{\dot r_n(t)}{r_n(t)})^2]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\quantity[\min_{\{\vb{r}_t\}_{0\le t\le 1}}\sum_{n=1}^d\int_0^1\dd{t}\qty(\dv{\ln r_n(t)}{t})^2]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq\quantity[\min_{\{\vb{r}_t\}_{0\le t\le 1}}\sum_{n=1}^d\qty(\int_0^1\dd{t}\dv{\ln r_n(t)}{t})^2]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\quantity[\sum_{n=1}^d\qty(\ln r_n^{\mds{T}}-\ln r_n^{\mds{1}})^2]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\ell.

(27)

Next, we prove the upper bound
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell
.
We consider a specific path
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
connecting
\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}
that satisfies

\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}_{t}}=\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}+(\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})t+c_{t},

(28)

where
c_{t}
is a constant chosen to satisfy the constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}_{t}}=1
.
Specifically,
c_{t}
is defined by

c_{t}\coloneqq-\ln\quantity[\sum_{n=1}^d e^{\ln r_n^{\mds{1}}+(\ln r_n^{\mds{T}} - \ln r_n^{\mds{1}})t}].

(29)

As proved in Theorem
2
(Appendix
C
), such a path of stochastic maps always exists.
The time derivative of
c_{t}
is

\dot{c}_{t}=-\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})e^{\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}+(\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})t}}{\sum_{n=1}^{d}e^{\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}+(\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})t}}.

(30)

For this path, the complexity is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}\quantity(\dv{\ln r_n^{\mds{T}_t}}{t})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}+\dot{c}_{t})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\quantity[ \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^d(\ln r_n^{\mds{T}} - \ln r_n^{\mds{1}})^2} + \sqrt{d(\dot c_t)^2}].

(31)

Here, we use the Minkowski inequality to obtain the last line.
Applying the inequality
|\sum_{n}x_{n}y_{n}/\sum_{n}y_{n}|\leq\max_{n}|x_{n}|
for any real numbers
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
satisfying
y_{n}>0~\forall n
, we obtain

|\dot{c}_{t}|\leq\max_{1\leq n\leq d}|\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}|\leq\ell.

(32)

Combining Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
) yields the desired upper bound:

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell.

(33)

B.2
Proof of the protocol-scaling relation (
8
)

Consider the stochastic map
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq N}
generated by the sequence of transition rate matrices
\{\mathds{W}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
.
That is,
\mathds{T}_{t}=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\mathds{W}_{s}}
, where
\mathds{W}_{t}=\mathds{W}_{k}
for
t\in[k-1,k)
and
1\leq k\leq N

Evidently,
\mathds{T}_{0}=\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}_{N}=\mathds{T}

The time evolution of
\mathbf{r}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}_{t}}
is given by
\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{t}=\mathds{W}_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}
, from which we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{r}_{n}(t)}{r_{n}(t)}

\displaystyle=-W_{nn}(t)-\frac{1}{r_{n}(t)}\sum_{m(\neq n)}W_{nm}(t)r_{m}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-W_{nn}(t).

(34)

Integrating this inequality over time, we obtain

-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}+\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}\leq-\int_{0}^{N}\differential{t}W_{nn}(t)\leq N\gamma.

(35)

On the other hand, since
r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}\in[0,1]
, we have

\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}\leq-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}=\ln d.

(36)

Combining Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
), we obtain

|\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}|\leq\max(N\gamma,\ln d)\leq N\ln(de^\gamma).

(37)

Consequently,

\ell=\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}|\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{T}}-\ln r_{n}^{\mathds{1}}|^{2}}\leq N\sqrt{d}\ln(de^\gamma).

(38)

Combining this with the inequality
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell

7

N\geq\frac{\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})}{(d+\sqrt{d})\ln(de^\gamma)},

(39)

B.3
Proof of the entropic bound (
9
)

Let
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be the geodesic path, and define
\mathbf{r}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}_{t}}
. Then, the geometric complexity is evaluated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}\quantity[\frac{\dot r_n(t)}{r_n(t)}]^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\frac{[\sum_{n=1}^{d}\dot{r}_{n}(t)\ln r_{n}(t)]^{2}}{\sum_{n=1}^{d}[r_{n}(t)\ln r_{n}(t)]^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\quantity|\dv{t}\ln S(\vb{r}_t)|

\displaystyle\geq|\ln S(\mathbf{r}_{0})-\ln S(\mathbf{r}_{1})|

\displaystyle=\ln S(\mathbf{1}/d)-\ln S(\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}/d).

(40)

Here, we use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}/b_{n}\geq(\sum_{n}a_{n})^{2}/\sum_{n}b_{n}
for any nonnegative
\{b_{n}\}
and real
\{a_{n}\}
to obtain the second line, the inequality
\sum_{n=1}^{d}(r_{n}\ln r_{n})^{2}\leq(\sum_{n=1}^{d}r_{n}\ln r_{n})^{2}=S(\mathbf{r})^{2}
to obtain the third line, and the triangle inequality
\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}|f(t)|\geq|\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}f(t)|
for any function
f(t)
to obtain the fourth line.

B.4
Geometric complexity of doubly stochastic maps and unital quantum channels

A direct consequence of our geometric framework is that the complexity of any doubly stochastic classical map
\mathds{T}
, as well as any unital quantum channel
\Lambda
, vanishes (
\mathcal{C}=0
). This is not merely a mathematical convention; it is physically necessary for quantifying the cost of thermodynamic control. To see this, consider the fundamental operational goal of a reset map: initializing an unknown mixed system into a pure state. Such a process necessarily requires entropy extraction from the system. Although the same logic applies to the quantum case, we focus on the classical setting for clarity. A doubly stochastic map
\mathds{T}
satisfies both
\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
(probability preservation) and
\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
(uniform-state preservation). For such maps, entropy is always nondecreasing for any initial probability distribution
\mathbf{p}
:

S(\mathbf{p})\leq S(\mathds{T}\mathbf{p}).

(41)

Because doubly stochastic maps cannot reduce entropy for any state, they are ineffective for state initialization. In the resource theory of purity (nonuniformity)
Gour et al. 2015
, they are classified as free operations, as they cannot increase purity from a free state (i.e., the uniform distribution). Therefore, any rigorous metric quantifying the operational and thermokinetic burden of resetting should assign zero cost to entropy-increasing processes. By assigning zero geometric complexity to doubly stochastic maps, our framework correctly captures their operational equivalence to the identity map in the context of state reset.

B.5
Uniqueness of the metric within a parameterized class of Riemannian metrics

Here, we show that the defined metric is the unique metric satisfying all three properties within a parameterized class of Riemannian metrics that includes the classical Fisher information metric.
Consider a general Riemannian metric parameterized by
\alpha>0
:

g_{\mathds{T},\alpha}(\mathds{X},\mathds{Y})\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{r_{n}^{\mathds{X}}r_{n}^{\mathds{Y}}}{(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{\alpha}}.

(42)

The metric
g_{\mathds{T},2}
corresponds to the metric employed in the definition of complexity, whereas
g_{\mathds{T},1}
is precisely the classical Fisher information metric.
In what follows, we show that only
\alpha=2
yields a metric satisfying all three properties.
Specifically, although the metric
g_{\mathds{T},\alpha}
satisfies property (i) for all
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
, property (ii) cannot be satisfied for
\alpha\in(2,+\infty)
, and property (iii) cannot be satisfied for
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
To this end, we first show that, for any
\alpha\in(0,2)\cup(2,+\infty)
,

\ell_{\alpha}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell_{\alpha},

(43)

where
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
is the complexity associated with the metric
g_{\mathds{T},\alpha}
, and
\ell_{\alpha}
denotes the geodesic distance without the stochasticity constraint, given by

\ell_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\alpha^{\prime}|}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}|(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}-(r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}|^{2}}.

(44)

Here,
\alpha^{\prime}\coloneqq 1-\alpha/2\neq 0
.
The lower bound follows immediately from the following expression for the complexity:

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})=\min_{\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}\quantity[\frac{1}{\alpha'}\dv{t}(r_n^{\mds{T}_t})^{\alpha'}]^{2}}.

(45)

To derive the upper bound, we consider a specific path
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
connecting
\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}
and satisfying

(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}_{t}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}=(r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}+[(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}-(r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}]t+c_{t},

(46)

where
c_{t}
is a constant chosen to satisfy the constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}_{t}}=1
.
Such a path always exists by Theorem
2
.
For this path, the complexity is bounded from above as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\alpha^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}[(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}-(r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}+\dot{c}_{t}]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\alpha^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\quantity[ \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^d[(r_n^{\mds{T}})^{\alpha'} - (r_n^{\mds{1}})^{\alpha'}]^2} + \sqrt{d(\dot c_t)^2}].

(47)

Here, we use the Minkowski inequality to obtain the second line.
From Eq. (
46
), we also have

\displaystyle|\dot{c}_{t}|

\displaystyle=\quantity|\frac{\sum_{n=1}^d[(r_n^{\mds{T}})^{\alpha'} - (r_n^{\mds{1}})^{\alpha'}](r_n^{\mds{T}_t})^{1-\alpha'}}{\sum_{n=1}^d(r_n^{\mds{T}_t})^{1-\alpha'}}|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq n\leq d}|(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}-(r_{n}^{\mathds{1}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha^{\prime}|\ell_{\alpha}.

(48)

Here, we use the inequality
|\sum_{n}x_{n}y_{n}/\sum_{n}y_{n}|\leq\max_{n}|x_{n}|
for any real numbers
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
satisfying
y_{n}>0
for all
n
to obtain the second line.
Combining Eqs. (
47
) and (
48
), we obtain the desired upper bound:

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell_{\alpha}.

(49)

We are now ready to show that property (ii) cannot be satisfied for
\alpha\in(2,+\infty)
.
Consider the map
\mathds{T}=e^{\mathds{W}N}
, which is realized by applying the same protocol
N
times, each generated by the transition rate matrix
\mathds{W}
over a unit time interval.
The transition rate matrix
\mathds{W}
is given by

\mathds{W}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

It is straightforward to verify that

\mathds{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1-e^{-N}\\
0&e^{-N}\end{pmatrix}

(51)

and that
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}}=[1-e^{-N}/2,e^{-N}/2]^{\top}
.
Applying the lower bound on the complexity in Eq. (
43
), we obtain

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})\geq\frac{1}{|\alpha^{\prime}|}|(e^{-N}/2)^{\alpha^{\prime}}-(1/2)^{\alpha^{\prime}}|.

(52)

For
\alpha>2
, we have
\alpha^{\prime}=1-\alpha/2<0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})\geq\frac{2^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|}(e^{N|\alpha^{\prime}|}-1).

(53)

This lower bound on
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})
is exponential in
N
; therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})
cannot yield a linear lower bound on
N
of the form given in Eq. (
8
).
Hence, property (ii) cannot be satisfied for
\alpha>2
.

Next, we show that property (iii) cannot be satisfied for
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
In this case,
\alpha^{\prime}=1-\alpha/2>0
.
From the upper bound in Eq. (
43
), we obtain

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}+1}{\alpha^{\prime}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}|(r_{n}^{\mathds{T}})^{\alpha^{\prime}}-(1/d)^{\alpha^{\prime}}|^{2}}.

(54)

It follows that

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})\leq\frac{d+\sqrt{d}}{\alpha^{\prime}}\max(1-d^{-\alpha^{\prime}},d^{-\alpha^{\prime}}).

(55)

Therefore, the complexity
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\mathds{T})
remains finite even when the error of the map
\mathds{T}
is zero, which violates property (iii).

Appendix C
Detailed derivations for the classical case

For convenience, we denote the spaces of
d
-dimensional probability distributions and stochastic maps by
\mathcal{P}_{d}
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{d}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\,|\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{p}=1\},

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{d}

\displaystyle=\{\mathds{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d\times d}\,|\,\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}\}.

(57)

The subspace of strictly positive probability distributions is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}=\{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\,|\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{p}=1\}.

(58)

C.1
Proof of the existence of a path of stochastic maps

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathds{T}
be a stochastic map such that
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}}=\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}/d\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
, and let
\{\mathbf{r}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be a smooth path in
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
connecting
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{1}}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}}
.
Then there exists a corresponding continuous path
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
in
\mathcal{M}_{d}
connecting
\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}
such that
\mathds{T}_{t}\mathbf{1}/d=\mathbf{r}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

First, consider a smooth path of stochastic maps,
\{\mathds{A}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, defined by

\mathds{A}_{t}=a\sin(\pi t)\frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{d}+[1-a\sin(\pi t)][(1-t)\mathds{1}+t\mathds{T}],

(59)

where
0<a<1
is an arbitrary constant.
Evidently, each
\mathds{A}_{t}
is a valid stochastic map,
\mathds{A}_{0}=\mathds{1}
,
\mathds{A}_{1}=\mathds{T}
, and all elements of
\mathds{A}_{t}
are strictly positive for
t\in(0,1)
.
However, this path of stochastic maps may not satisfy the required condition
\mathds{A}_{t}\mathbf{1}/d=\mathbf{r}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
To construct the desired path, we consider the following path of double-scaled stochastic maps:

\mathds{T}_{t}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}_{t}})\mathds{A}_{t}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}_{t}}),

(60)

where
\mathbf{u}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}_{t}
are
d
-dimensional scaling vectors.
The scaling in Eq. (
60
) is also known as the generalized Sinkhorn transformation
Sinkhorn 1964
.
According to Proposition
3
, there exists a path
\{(\mathbf{u}_{t},\mathbf{v}_{t})\}_{0<t<1}
such that
\mathds{T}_{t}\mathbf{1}/d=\mathbf{r}_{t}
,
\mathds{T}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
, and
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t}=0
.
The gauge condition
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{t}=0
resolves the translational invariance of the log-linear scaling (i.e.,
\mathbf{u}_{t}\leftarrow\mathbf{u}_{t}+c
and
\mathbf{v}_{t}\leftarrow\mathbf{v}_{t}-c
yield the same map
\mathds{T}_{t}
for any real
c
).
By the implicit function theorem, the path
\{(\mathbf{u}_{t},\mathbf{v}_{t})\}_{0<t<1}
is smooth; consequently, the corresponding path of stochastic maps
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0<t<1}
is continuous.

The remaining task is to verify the boundary conditions, namely,

\mathds{T}_{0}=\mathds{1}~\text{and}~\mathds{T}_{1}=\mathds{T},

(61)

where
\mathds{T}_{0/1}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to 0/1}\mathds{T}_{t}
.
Let
\mathbf{u}_{0/1}=\lim_{t\to 0/1}\mathbf{u}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}_{0/1}=\lim_{t\to 0/1}\mathbf{v}_{t}
.
Then
\mathds{T}_{0}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}_{0}})\mathds{A}_{0}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}_{0}})
and
\mathds{T}_{1}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}_{1}})\mathds{A}_{1}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}_{1}})
.
Note that both maps
\mathds{T}_{0}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}_{0}})\mathds{A}_{0}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}_{0}})
and
\mathds{1}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{0}})\mathds{A}_{0}\diag(e^{\mathbf{0}})
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathds{T}_{0}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathds{T}_{t}\mathbf{1}=d\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbf{r}_{t}=d\mathbf{r}_{0}=\mathds{1}\mathbf{1},

(62)

\displaystyle\mathds{T}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathds{T}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}=\mathds{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1}.

(63)

Applying Proposition
4
, we obtain
\mathds{T}_{0}=\mathds{1}
.
Similarly, both maps
\mathds{T}_{1}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}_{1}})\mathds{A}_{1}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}_{1}})
and
\mathds{T}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{0}})\mathds{A}_{1}\diag(e^{\mathbf{0}})
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathds{T}_{1}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 1}\mathds{T}_{t}\mathbf{1}=d\lim_{t\to 1}\mathbf{r}_{t}=d\mathbf{r}_{1}=\mathds{T}\mathbf{1},

(64)

\displaystyle\mathds{T}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 1}\mathds{T}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}=\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}.

(65)

Again, Proposition
4
gives
\mathds{T}_{1}=\mathds{T}
.
Therefore,
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is the desired continuous path connecting
\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}
and satisfying
\mathds{T}_{t}\mathbf{1}/d=\mathbf{r}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathds{A}=[A_{mn}]\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d\times d}
be a stochastic map, and let
\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
be a positive distribution.
Then there exists a unique pair of vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
such that
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}=0
and the matrix
\mathds{T}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}})\mathds{A}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}})
satisfies
\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}/d=\mathbf{r}
and
\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
.

Proof.

To establish the unique existence of
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
, consider the function

F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\sum_{m,n}e^{u_{m}+v_{n}}A_{mn}-d\sum_{m}r_{m}u_{m}-\sum_{n}v_{n}.

(66)

Since

\displaystyle\partialderivative{F(\vb{u},\vb{v})}{u_m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}e^{u_{m}+v_{n}}A_{mn}-dr_{m},

(67)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{F(\vb{u},\vb{v})}{v_n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}e^{u_{m}+v_{n}}A_{mn}-1,

(68)

a global minimum
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
of
F
must satisfy the stationarity conditions
\partialderivative*{F}{u_m}=0
and
\partialderivative*{F}{v_n}=0
, which are exactly equivalent to
\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}/d=\mathbf{r}
and
\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
.
Therefore, it suffices to prove the uniqueness of the global minimum of
F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
.
Since the Hessian matrix of
F
has the form

\mathds{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\diag(\mathds{T}\mathbf{1})&\mathds{T}\\
\mathds{T}^{\top}&\diag(\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1})\end{pmatrix},

(69)

its quadratic form
\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathds{H}\mathbf{z}
reads

\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathds{H}\mathbf{z}=\sum_{m,n}T_{mn}(x_{m}+y_{n})^{2}\geq 0,

(70)

where
\mathbf{z}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots,y_{d}]^{\top}
.
Because
A_{mn}>0
and
T_{mn}>0
for all
m
and
n
,
F
is strictly convex on the subspace orthogonal to
[\mathbf{1}^{\top},-\mathbf{1}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
Moreover,
F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\to\infty
as
|u_{m}|\to\infty
or
|v_{n}|\to\infty
.
Hence,
F(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
is continuous, strictly convex, and coercive on the restricted subspace
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}=0
.
Consequently, it has a unique global minimum
(\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{v}^{*})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathds{A}=[A_{mn}]\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d\times d}
be a stochastic map.
Suppose there exist pairs
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
and
(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}})
such that the two double-scaled matrices
\mathds{T}=\diag(e^{\mathbf{u}})\mathds{A}\diag(e^{\mathbf{v}})
and
\widetilde{\mathds{T}}=\diag(e^{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}})\mathds{A}\diag(e^{\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}})
satisfy
\mathds{T}\mathbf{1}=\widetilde{\mathds{T}}\mathbf{1}
and
\mathds{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\widetilde{\mathds{T}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
.
Then
\mathds{T}=\widetilde{\mathds{T}}
.

Proof.

We consider a bipartite graph
G=(U,V,E)
, where
U=\{1,\dots,d\}
denotes the set of row nodes,
V=\{1,\dots,d\}
denotes the set of column nodes, and
E=\{(m,n)\in U\times V\,|\,A_{mn}>0\}
denotes the set of edges connecting
U
and
V
whenever
A_{mn}
is positive.
This graph can always be decomposed into connected components,
G=\bigcup_{k}G_{k}
, where
G_{k}=(U_{k},V_{k},E_{k})
.
Note that
\{U_{k}\}_{k}
are disjoint subsets, and the same holds for
\{V_{k}\}_{k}
and
\{G_{k}\}_{k}
.
If
(m,n)\notin E
, then
A_{mn}=0
, which immediately implies
T_{mn}=\widetilde{T}_{mn}
.
Thus, to show
\mathds{T}=\widetilde{\mathds{T}}
, it suffices to prove
T_{mn}=\widetilde{T}_{mn}
for
(m,n)\in E
.

To this end, consider each component
G_{k}
.
If
E_{k}=\emptyset
, then
A_{mn}=0
for any
m\in U_{k}
or
n\in V_{k}
, which immediately implies
T_{mn}=\widetilde{T}_{mn}
.
Therefore, we need only consider components
G_{k}
with nonempty
E_{k}
.
Define
x_{m}=\widetilde{u}_{m}-u_{m}
and
y_{n}=\widetilde{v}_{n}-v_{n}
for
m\in U_{k}
and
n\in V_{k}
.
Let
\hat{m}=\argmax_{m\in U_{k}}x_{m}
and
\hat{n}=\argmin_{n\in V_{k}}y_{n}
.
From
\sum_{n}T_{\hat{m}n}=\sum_{n}\widetilde{T}_{\hat{m}n}
and

\widetilde{T}_{mn}=e^{\widetilde{u}_{m}+\widetilde{v}_{n}}A_{mn}=e^{x_{m}+y_{n}}e^{u_{m}+v_{n}}A_{mn}=e^{x_{m}+y_{n}}T_{mn}
for all
m,n
, we have

\sum_{n\in V_{k}}T_{\hat{m}n}=\sum_{n\in V_{k}}T_{\hat{m}n}e^{x_{\hat{m}}+y_{n}}\Leftrightarrow\sum_{n\in V_{k}}T_{\hat{m}n}(e^{x_{\hat{m}}+y_{n}}-1)=0.

(71)

Thus,
x_{\hat{m}}+y_{n}\leq 0
holds for some
n\in V_{k}
(otherwise, the left-hand side of Eq. (
71
) would be strictly positive); hence
x_{\hat{m}}+y_{\hat{n}}\leq 0
.
Similarly, from
\sum_{m}T_{m\hat{n}}=\sum_{m}\widetilde{T}_{m\hat{n}}
, we have

\sum_{m\in U_{k}}T_{m\hat{n}}=\sum_{m\in U_{k}}T_{m\hat{n}}e^{x_{m}+y_{\hat{n}}}\Leftrightarrow\sum_{m\in U_{k}}T_{m\hat{n}}(e^{x_{m}+y_{\hat{n}}}-1)=0.

(72)

Thus,
x_{m}+y_{\hat{n}}\geq 0
holds for some
m\in U_{k}
(otherwise, the left-hand side of Eq. (
72
) would be strictly negative); hence
x_{\hat{m}}+y_{\hat{n}}\geq 0
.
Consequently,
x_{\hat{m}}+y_{\hat{n}}=0
.
It follows that

x_{\hat{m}}+y_{n}\geq 0=x_{\hat{m}}+y_{\hat{n}}\geq x_{m}+y_{\hat{n}}.

(73)

This implies that
x_{\hat{m}}+y_{n}=0
whenever
T_{\hat{m}n}>0
, and
x_{m}+y_{\hat{n}}=0
whenever
T_{m\hat{n}}>0
.
In other words,
y_{n}=-x_{\hat{m}}=y_{\hat{n}}
whenever
(\hat{m},n)\in E_{k}
, and
x_{m}=-y_{\hat{n}}=x_{\hat{m}}
whenever
(m,\hat{n})\in E_{k}
.
By propagating this relation to all nodes in
G_{k}
, we obtain
x_{m}=x_{\hat{m}}
for any
m\in U_{k}
and
y_{n}=y_{\hat{n}}
for any
n\in V_{k}
.
Since
x_{\hat{m}}+y_{\hat{n}}=0
, we have, for any
(m,n)\in E_{k}
,

\widetilde{T}_{mn}=e^{x_{m}+y_{n}}T_{mn}=e^{x_{\hat{m}}+y_{\hat{n}}}T_{mn}=T_{mn}.

(74)

This completes the proof.
∎

C.2
Calculation of the geometric complexity of the two-level reset map

The two-level reset map
\mathds{T}
is explicitly given by

\mathds{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1-e^{-w\tau}\\
0&e^{-w\tau}\end{pmatrix}.

(75)

To evaluate the geometric complexity
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
, it is convenient to consider the curve (C),
e^{x}+e^{y}=1
, on the two-dimensional
xy
plane.
The vectors
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{1}}=[1/2,1/2]^{\top}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\mathds{T}}=[1-e^{-w\tau}/2,e^{-w\tau}/2]^{\top}
are mapped to this plane as the points
P(x_{P},y_{P})
and
Q(x_{Q},y_{Q})
, where
x_{P}=y_{P}=\ln(1/2)
,
x_{Q}=\ln(1-e^{-w\tau}/2)
, and
y_{Q}=\ln(e^{-w\tau}/2)
.
Then
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
is exactly the arc length between points
P
and
Q
along curve (C).
From
y=\ln(1-e^x)
, the arc length between
P
and
Q
is

L=\int_{x_{P}}^{x_{Q}}\differential{x}\sqrt{1+\quantity(\dv{y}{x})^{2}}=\int_{x_{P}}^{x_{Q}}\differential{x}\frac{\sqrt{2e^{2x}-2e^{x}+1}}{1-e^{x}}.

(76)

Setting
z=2e^{x}-1
, the integral becomes

\int\differential{x}\frac{\sqrt{2e^{2x}-2e^{x}+1}}{1-e^{x}}=\sqrt{2}\int\differential{z}\frac{\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}{1-z^{2}}.

(77)

By direct integration, we obtain

\int\differential{z}\frac{\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}{1-z^{2}}=\sqrt{2}\atanh\frac{\sqrt{2}z}{\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}-\atanh\frac{z}{\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}+\text{const}.

(78)

Since
z=0
at
x=x_{P}
and
z=1-e^{-w\tau}
at
x=x_{Q}
, the arc length is

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=2\atanh[\sqrt{2}f(w\tau)]-\sqrt{2}\atanh[f(w\tau)],

(79)

where
f(u)=(1-e^{-u})/\sqrt{1+(1-e^{-u})^{2}}
.
Therefore, the complexity is
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})=L
.

C.2.1
Asymptotic expression of the complexity

We evaluate
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
in the regime
w\tau\gg 1
.
Let
z=e^{-w\tau}\ll 1
. The complexity is then evaluated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle=2\atanh\quantity(\frac{\sqrt{2}(1-z)}{\sqrt{1+(1-z)^2}})-\sqrt{2}\atanh\quantity(\frac{1-z}{\sqrt{1+(1-z)^2}})

\displaystyle=\ln\frac{4-z+O(z^{2})}{z+O(z^{2})}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ln\frac{2+2\sqrt{2}-z+O(z^{2})}{2\sqrt{2}-2+z+O(z^{2})}

\displaystyle=-\ln z+\ln 4-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ln\frac{1+\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1}+O(z)

\displaystyle=w\tau+2\ln 2-\sqrt{2}\ln(\sqrt{2}+1)+O(e^{-w\tau}).

(80)

C.2.2
Analytical proof of
e^{\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})}\times\epsilon(\mathds{T})\geq 1

Since
\epsilon(\mathds{T})=e^{-w\tau}
, it suffices to prove

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\geq w\tau.

(81)

Let
g(u)=2\atanh[\sqrt{2}f(u)]-\sqrt{2}\atanh[f(u)]
, then
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})=g(w\tau)
.
Setting
z=1-e^{-u}\geq 0
, we obtain
f(u)=z/\sqrt{1+z^{2}}
.
Differentiating
g(u)
with respect to
u
gives

\displaystyle\derivative{g}{u}

\displaystyle=\derivative{g}{f}\derivative{f}{z}\derivative{z}{u}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\frac{(1+z^{2})^{2}}{1-z^{2}}\frac{1}{(1+z^{2})^{3/2}}(1-z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2(1+z^{2})}}{1+z}\geq 1.

(82)

Therefore,
g(u)\geq g(0)+u=u
, which immediately proves Eq. (
81
).

C.3
Generalization to the continuous-state case

Here, we generalize the complexity theory to the continuous-variable setting.
For simplicity, we consider stochastic maps acting on probability distributions over the one-dimensional interval
[-1,1]
.
A stochastic map
\mathds{T}
is defined via a transition kernel
\mathcal{K}(x,y)
as

(\mathds{T}p)(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{y}\mathcal{K}(x,y)p(y),

(83)

where
\mathcal{K}(x,y)
satisfies the conditions
\mathcal{K}(x,y)\geq 0
and
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\mathcal{K}(x,y)=1
.
As in the discrete-state case, for each stochastic map
\mathds{T}
, we define
r^{\mathds{T}}\coloneqq\mathds{T}\bar{p}
, where
\bar{p}(x)=1/2
denotes the uniform distribution.
In other words,
r^{\mathds{T}}
is the output distribution obtained by applying the map
\mathds{T}
to the uniform distribution.
For any reset map
\mathds{T}
, we define its geometric complexity as

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\coloneqq\min_{\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{p_{t}}(\dot{p}_{t},\dot{p}_{t})},

(84)

where
p_{t}=r^{\mathds{T}_{t}}
, and the minimum is taken over all continuous paths
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
of maps connecting the identity map
\mathds{1}
to the given map
\mathds{T}
.
Here, the identity map
\mathds{1}
is associated with the kernel
\mathcal{K}(x,y)=\delta(x-y)
.
The Riemannian metric
g_{p}
is defined analogously to that in the discrete-state case:

g_{p}(u,v)=\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\frac{u(x)v(x)}{p(x)^{2}}.

(85)

C.3.1
Protocol-scaling relation

Consider a reset map
\mathds{T}
constructed by sequentially applying
N
protocols, where the
k
th protocol is realized by an overdamped Langevin process with a fixed potential
U_{k}(x)
over a unit time interval.
We impose the physically relevant constraint that the maximum height of the potential
U_{k}(x)
remains finite at all times, namely,
0\leq U_{k}(x)\leq U_{m}
for any
-1<x<1
and
1\leq k\leq N
, where
U_{m}
is a positive constant.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
denote the Fokker–Planck generator corresponding to the
k
th protocol, defined by

\mathcal{F}_{k}(p)=\partialderivative{x}(\pdv{U_k}{x}p + D\pdv{x}p).

(86)

Then, the map
\mathds{T}
is determined by the kernel

\mathcal{K}(x,y)=e^{\mathcal{F}_{N}}\dots e^{\mathcal{F}_{1}}[\delta(x-y)].

(87)

In what follows, we show how the complexity of this map is related to the number of protocols.

First, let
p_{k}(x)=e^{\mathcal{F}_{k}}[p_{k-1}(x)]
, where
p_{0}=\bar{p}
is the uniform distribution.
Define the equilibrium distribution associated with the potential
U_{k}(x)
by
\pi_{k}(x)=e^{-\beta U_{k}(x)}/Z_{k}
.
We prove by induction that

e^{-2\beta kU_{m}}\pi_{k}(x)\leq p_{k}(x)\leq e^{2\beta kU_{m}}\pi_{k}(x),

(88)

where
U_{0}(x)=0
.
This is evident for
k=0
.
Assume that
e^{-2\beta kU_{m}}\pi_{k}(x)\leq p_{k}(x)\leq e^{2\beta kU_{m}}\pi_{k}(x)
holds for some
k\geq 0
.
Since
0\leq U_{k}(x),U_{k+1}(x)\leq U_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta U_{k}(x)}}{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}e^{-\beta U_{k}(x)}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-2\beta U_{m}}\frac{e^{-\beta U_{k+1}(x)}}{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}e^{-\beta U_{k+1}(x)}}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\beta U_{m}}\pi_{k+1}(x)

(89)

and

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\beta U_{m}}\frac{e^{-\beta U_{k+1}(x)}}{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}e^{-\beta U_{k+1}(x)}}

\displaystyle=e^{2\beta U_{m}}\pi_{k+1}(x).

(90)

Therefore,

e^{-2\beta(k+1)U_{m}}\pi_{k+1}(x)\leq p_{k}(x)\leq e^{2\beta(k+1)U_{m}}\pi_{k+1}(x).

(91)

It then follows immediately from Proposition
5
that
p_{k+1}(x)=e^{\mathcal{F}_{k+1}}[p_{k}(x)]
satisfies

e^{-2\beta(k+1)U_{m}}\pi_{k+1}(x)\leq p_{k+1}(x)\leq e^{2\beta(k+1)U_{m}}\pi_{k+1}(x).

(92)

We are now ready to establish the connection between the number of protocols and the geometric complexity by deriving a lower bound on
N
in terms of
\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
.
To this end, consider a smooth path of stochastic maps
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
connecting
\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}
such that

\ln r^{\mathds{T}_{t}}(x)=\ln r^{\mathds{1}}(x)+[\ln r^{\mathds{T}}(x)-\ln r^{\mathds{1}}(x)]t+c_{t},

(93)

where
c_{t}
is a constant chosen to satisfy the normalization condition
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}r^{\mathds{T}_{t}}(x)=1
.
Such a path always exists by Proposition
6
.
Defining
q(x)\coloneqq\ln r^{\mathds{T}}(x)-\ln r^{\mathds{1}}(x)=\ln[2r^{\mathds{T}}(x)]
, we can write
c_{t}
as

c_{t}=-\ln\quantity(\int_{-1}^1\dd{x}e^{-\ln 2 + q(x)t}).

(94)

The time derivative of
c_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}q(x)e^{q(x)t}}{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}e^{q(x)t}}.

(95)

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}q(x)^{2}e^{q(x)t}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}e^{q(x)t}

\displaystyle\geq\quantity[\int_{-1}^1\dd{x}q(x)e^{q(x)t}]^{2},

(96)

which implies that
\ddot{c}_{t}\leq 0
.
Therefore,
|\dot{c}_{t}|\leq\max\quantity(|\dot{c}_0|,|\dot{c}_1|)
.
Since
p_{N}(x)=(\mathds{T}\bar{p})(x)=r^{\mathds{T}}(x)
satisfies

e^{-2\beta NU_{m}}\pi_{N}(x)\leq p_{N}(x)\leq e^{2\beta NU_{m}}\pi_{N}(x),

(97)

it follows that

\frac{1}{2}e^{-\beta(2N+1)U_{m}}\leq r^{\mathds{T}}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\beta(2N+1)U_{m}}.

(98)

Using this inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\dot{c}_{0}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity|\int_{-1}^1\dd{x}[\ln r^{\mds{T}}(x) - \ln r^{\mds{1}}(x)]|

\displaystyle\leq\beta(2N+1)U_{m},

(99)

\displaystyle|\dot{c}_{1}|

\displaystyle=\quantity|\int_{-1}^1\dd{x}[\ln r^{\mds{T}}(x) - \ln r^{\mds{1}}(x)]r^{\mds{T}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\beta(2N+1)U_{m}.

(100)

In other words,
|\dot{c}_{t}|\leq\beta(2N+1)U_{m}
.
Combining this with the bound
|\ln r^{\mathds{T}}(x)-\ln r^{\mathds{1}}(x)|\leq\beta(2N+1)U_{m}
, we obtain the following upper bound on the complexity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\quantity(\dv{\ln r^{\mds{T}_t}(x)}{t})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}(\ln r^{\mathds{T}}(x)-\ln r^{\mathds{1}}(x)+\dot{c}_{t})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\beta(2N+1)U_{m}.

(101)

Consequently, the number of protocols is lower bounded in terms of the complexity as

N\geq\frac{\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})}{4\sqrt{2}\beta U_{m}}-\frac{1}{2}.

(102)

This relation also indicates that the geometric complexity captures the maximum height of the potential, since perfect resetting can be achieved in a finite number of operations provided that
U_{m}
diverges.

C.3.2
The complexity-error trade-off relation

The error of the reset map
\mathds{T}
associated with an undesired region
\mathfrak{u}=[0,1]
is defined as

\epsilon(\mathds{T})\coloneqq\int_{\mathfrak{u}}\differential{x}(\mathds{T}\mathbf{1})(x),

(103)

where
\mathbf{1}(x)=1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
.
This error quantifies how effectively the reset map suppresses the probability weight in the undesired region when acting on the maximally mixed distribution.
First, the geometric complexity is lower bounded by

\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})\geq\sqrt{\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}|\ln r^{\mathds{T}}(x)-\ln r^{\mathds{1}}(x)|^{2}}\eqqcolon\ell.

(104)

Since
e^{x}\geq e^{-|x|}
for any real
x
and
e^{-x}
is convex, we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle\epsilon(\mathds{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{u}}\differential{x}e^{\ln[2r^{\mathds{T}}(x)]}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathfrak{u}}\differential{x}e^{-|\ln[2r^{\mathds{T}}(x)]|}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\int_{\mathfrak{u}}\differential{x}|\ln[2r^{\mathds{T}}(x)]|},

(105)

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality.
Next, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
\ell
is lower bounded as

\ell\geq\int_{\mathfrak{u}}\differential{x}|\ln[2r^{\mathds{T}}(x)]|.

(106)

Combining Eqs. (
105
) and (
106
), we obtain

\epsilon(\mathds{T})\geq e^{-\ell}.

(107)

Finally, since
\ell\leq\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})
, we immediately obtain the desired relation (
11
):

e^{\mathcal{C}(\mathds{T})}\times\epsilon(\mathds{T})\geq 1.

(108)

Proposition 5
.

Consider a particle confined by a time-independent potential
U(x)
and governed by overdamped Langevin dynamics.
Let
p_{0}(x)
be an initial probability distribution satisfying
c_{1}\pi(x)\leq p_{0}(x)\leq c_{2}\pi(x)
, where
\pi(x)=e^{-\beta U(x)}/Z
is the equilibrium distribution and
c_{1},c_{2}
are positive constants.
Then
c_{1}\pi(x)\leq p_{t}(x)\leq c_{2}\pi(x)
for all times.

Proof.

Let
q_{t}(x)\coloneqq p_{t}(x)-c_{1}\pi(x)
and
s_{t}(x)\coloneqq c_{2}\pi(x)-p_{t}(x)
.
Then
q_{0}(x)\geq 0
and
s_{0}(x)\geq 0
for any
x\in[-1,1]
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the Fokker–Planck generator. Since

\displaystyle\derivative{t}q_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(p_{t})=\mathcal{F}(q_{t})-c_{1}\mathcal{F}(\pi)=\mathcal{F}(q_{t}),

(109)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}s_{t}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{F}(p_{t})=\mathcal{F}(s_{t})-c_{2}\mathcal{F}(\pi)=\mathcal{F}(s_{t}),

(110)

and the Fokker–Planck generator
\mathcal{F}
is positivity-preserving, the initial conditions
q_{0}(x)\geq 0
and
s_{0}(x)\geq 0
imply that
q_{t}(x)\geq 0
and
s_{t}(x)\geq 0
for any
t
.
Therefore,
c_{1}\pi(x)\leq p_{t}(x)\leq c_{2}\pi(x)
.
∎

Proposition 6
.

For any smooth path
\{p_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
of strictly positive probability distributions connecting
r^{\mathds{1}}
and
r^{\mathds{T}}
, there exists a corresponding continuous path
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
of stochastic maps connecting
\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}
such that
r^{\mathds{T}_{t}}=p_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

The proof strategy is similar to that in the discrete-state case.
Let
\mathcal{K}
and
\delta
denote the transition kernels associated with the map
\mathds{T}
and the identity map
\mathds{1}
, respectively.
We consider a smooth path of stochastic maps
\{\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
with kernels
\{\mathcal{A}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
defined by

\mathcal{A}_{t,\kappa}=[\kappa+a\sin(\pi t)]\mathcal{I}+[1-\kappa-a\sin(\pi t)][(1-t)\delta+t\mathcal{K}],

(111)

where
\mathcal{I}(x,y)=1/2
for all
x,y\in[-1,1]
, and
0<a<1
and
0<\kappa<1-a
are arbitrary constants.
Clearly, each kernel
\mathcal{A}_{t,\kappa}
defines a valid stochastic map
\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}
,
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathcal{A}_{0,\kappa}=\delta
,
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathcal{A}_{1,\kappa}=\mathcal{K}
, and all transition probabilities
\mathcal{A}_{t,\kappa}(x,y)
are strictly positive for
x,y\in[-1,1]
and
t\in[0,1]
.
However, this path of stochastic maps does not satisfy the required condition
r^{\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}}=p_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
To construct the desired path, we consider a path of stochastic maps determined by the following double-scaled kernels:

\mathcal{K}_{t,\kappa}(x,y)=e^{u_{t,\kappa}(y)+v_{t,\kappa}(x)}\mathcal{A}_{t,\kappa}(x,y),

(112)

where
u_{t,\kappa}(x)
and
v_{t,\kappa}(x)
are real-valued functions on
[-1,1]
.
According to Proposition
7
, there exists a path
\{(u_{t,\kappa},v_{t,\kappa})\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
such that
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\mathcal{K}_{t,\kappa}(x,y)=1
,
r^{\mathds{T}_{t,\kappa}}=\kappa\bar{p}+(1-\kappa)p_{t}
, and
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}v_{t,\kappa}(x)=0
, where
\mathds{T}_{t,\kappa}
denotes the stochastic map associated with the kernel
\mathcal{K}_{t,\kappa}
.
The gauge condition
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}v_{t,\kappa}(x)=0
removes the translational invariance of the log-linear scaling; that is,
u_{t,\kappa}\leftarrow u_{t,\kappa}+c
and
v_{t,\kappa}\leftarrow v_{t,\kappa}-c

\mathds{T}_{t,\kappa}
for any real
c

\{(u_{t,\kappa},v_{t,\kappa})\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is smooth. Consequently, the corresponding path of stochastic maps
\{\mathds{T}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is continuous.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{K}_{0,\kappa}=\mathcal{A}_{0,\kappa}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1,\kappa}=\mathcal{A}_{1,\kappa}
.
Since

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0,\kappa}

\displaystyle=e^{0+0}\mathcal{A}_{0,\kappa},~r^{\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}}=r^{\mathds{T}_{0,\kappa}},

(113)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1,\kappa}

\displaystyle=e^{0+0}\mathcal{A}_{1,\kappa},~r^{\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}}=r^{\mathds{T}_{1,\kappa}},

(114)

the uniqueness of
(u,v)
in Proposition
7
implies that
u_{0,\kappa}=u_{1,\kappa}=0
and
v_{0,\kappa}=v_{1,\kappa}=0
.
Therefore, the path
\{\mathds{T}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
smoothly connects
\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}
and
\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}

We now take the limit

\mathds{T}_{t}\coloneqq\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathds{T}_{t,\kappa}

The resulting path
\{\mathds{T}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}

\mathds{1}
and
\mathds{T}

r^{\mathds{T}_{t}}=p_{t}
for all

.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a strictly positive transition kernel and let
p
be a positive probability distribution.
Then there exists a unique pair of real-valued functions
u(x)
and
v(x)
such that
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}v(x)=0
and
\mathcal{K}(x,y)=e^{u(y)+v(x)}\mathcal{A}(x,y)
is a transition kernel satisfying
r^{\mathds{T}}=p
, where
\mathds{T}
denotes the stochastic map associated with the kernel
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{K}(x,y)=e^{u(y)+v(x)}\mathcal{A}(x,y)
and consider the following functional:

\displaystyle F(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{y}e^{u(y)+v(x)}\mathcal{A}(x,y)

\displaystyle-\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{y}u(y)-2\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}v(x)p(x).

(115)

It is straightforward to verify that
F(u,v)
is a convex functional.
Since

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta u(y)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\mathcal{K}(x,y)-1,

(116)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta v(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{y}\mathcal{K}(x,y)-2p(x),

(117)

the global minimum of
F(u,v)
is achieved only when
{\delta F}/{\delta u(y)}=0
and
{\delta F}/{\delta v(x)}=0
, which are exactly the required conditions
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\mathcal{K}(x,y)=1
and
r^{\mathds{T}}=p
.
Let
f(t)=F(u+tu^{\prime},v+tv^{\prime})
, where
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
are arbitrary real-valued functions.
The second derivative of
f(t)
with respect to
t
is

\displaystyle\evaluated{\dv[2]{f(t)}{t}}_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{y}[u^{\prime}(y)+v^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\mathcal{K}(x,y)\geq 0.

(118)

The equality holds only when
u^{\prime}(y)+v^{\prime}(x)=0
for all
x,y\in[-1,1]
.
If we restrict to the subspace where
\int_{-1}^{1}\differential{x}v(x)=0
, then
u^{\prime}(y)=v^{\prime}(x)=0
.
Therefore,
F
is strictly convex on the restricted subspace.
Furthermore, since
F
is continuous and coercive, it admits a unique global minimum, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Detailed derivations for the quantum case

For convenience, we introduce the following notation.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{d}
denote the set of density operators on the
d
-dimensional complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, and let
\Upsilon_{d}
denote the space of quantum channels
\Lambda:\mathcal{S}_{d}\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{d}
.
Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{d}

\displaystyle=\{\rho\in\mathcal{H}_{d}\,|\,\rho\succeq 0~\text{and}~\tr\rho=1\},

(119)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{d}

\displaystyle=\{\Lambda\,|\,\Lambda(\rho)\succeq 0~\text{and}~\tr[\Lambda(\rho)]=1~\forall\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{d}\}.

(120)

In addition, let
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{+}=\{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{d}\,|\,\rho\succ 0\}
denote the subset of positive-definite density operators.
The identity quantum channel is denoted by
\mathsf{Id}
[i.e.,
\mathsf{Id}(\circ)=\circ
].

We use the Choi-matrix representation of quantum channels.
For each quantum channel
\Lambda
, the corresponding Choi matrix is defined by

\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}\coloneqq(\mathds{1}\otimes\Lambda)(\outerproduct{\Omega}{\Omega}),

(121)

where
\ket{\Omega}=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\ket{n}\otimes\ket{n}
and
\{\ket{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
.
The Choi matrix can be written as
\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}=\sum_{m,n}\outerproduct{m}{n}\otimes\Lambda(\outerproduct{m}{n})
, and the channel
\Lambda
is related to it via

\Lambda(\rho)=\tr_{1}[(\rho^{\top}\otimes\mathds{1})\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}],

(122)

where
\rho^{\top}=\sum_{m,n}\matrixelement{n}{\rho}{m}\outerproduct{m}{n}
.
Since
\Lambda
is CPTP, the Choi matrix is positive semidefinite and satisfies
\tr_{2}\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}=\mathds{1}
.
By the Choi–Jamiolkowski isomorphism, there is a one-to-one correspondence between Choi matrices and quantum channels (CPTP maps)
Choi 1975
;
Jamiołkowski 1972
.
It also follows that
\phi_{\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}}=d^{-1}\tr_{1}\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}=\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)
is a density operator.

D.1
Geodesic distance of the defined Riemannian metric in the unconstrained case

For each Choi matrix
\mathds{M}
representing a quantum channel, the corresponding reduced operator
\phi_{\mathds{M}}=d^{-1}\tr_{1}\mathds{M}
is a density operator.
Therefore, the metric
g_{\mathds{M}}(\mathds{X},\mathds{Y})
on the space of Choi matrices is equivalent to the metric
g_{\phi_{\mathds{M}}}(\phi_{\mathds{X}},\phi_{\mathds{Y}})=\tr(\phi_{\mds{M}}^{-1}\phi_{\mds{X}}\phi_{\mds{M}}^{-1}\phi_{\mds{Y}})
on the space of reduced density operators.
In what follows, we compute the geodesic distance induced by the metric
g_{\phi_{\mathds{M}}}
on the space of positive Hermitian operators.
We show that in the
absence
of the trace constraint (i.e.,
\tr\phi_{\mathds{M}}=1
), this geodesic distance can be obtained analytically.

Let
\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})
denote the geodesic distance between two positive Hermitian operators
\mathds{A}
and
\mathds{B}
induced by the above metric.
Note that, in general, neither
\tr\mathds{A}
nor
\tr\mathds{B}
is required to equal
1
.
We prove that
\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})=\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}

Bhatia and Holbrook 2006
by establishing both
\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})\geq\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})\leq\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}
.

We first prove the lower bound.
Let
\{\mathds{O}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be any smooth path of positive Hermitian operators connecting
\mathds{A}
and
\mathds{B}
.
Defining
\mathds{J}_{t}\coloneqq\mathds{O}_{0}^{-1/2}\mathds{O}_{t}\mathds{O}_{0}^{-1/2}=e^{\mathds{H}_{t}}
, the metric can be written as

\displaystyle g_{\mathds{O}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\tr(\mds{O}_t^{-1}\dot{\mds{O}}_t\mds{O}_t^{-1}\dot{\mds{O}}_t)

\displaystyle=\tr(\mds{J}_t^{-1}\dot{\mds{J}}_t\mds{J}_t^{-1}\dot{\mds{J}}_t)

\displaystyle=\|e^{-\mathds{H}_{t}/2}\dot{\mathds{J}}_{t}e^{-\mathds{H}_{t}/2}\|_{F}^{2}.

(123)

Using the spectral decomposition
\mathds{H}_{t}=\sum_{n}h_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}
, where
\{h_{n}\}
are real eigenvalues, we obtain

\displaystyle\|e^{-\mathds{H}_{t}/2}\dot{\mathds{J}}_{t}e^{-\mathds{H}_{t}/2}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}e^{-\mathds{H}_{t}/2}e^{s\mathds{H}_{t}}\dot{\mathds{H}}_{t}e^{(1-s)\mathds{H}_{t}}e^{-\mathds{H}_{t}/2}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tr\quantity[ \int_0^1\dd{s}\int_0^1\dd{s'} e^{(1/2-s')\mds{H}_t}\dot{\mds{H}}_te^{(s'-1/2)\mds{H}_t} e^{(s-1/2)\mds{H}_t}\dot{\mds{H}}_te^{(1/2-s)\mds{H}_t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}|\matrixelement{m}{\dot{\mds{H}}_t}{n}|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s'}e^{(s+s^{\prime}-1)(h_{n}-h_{m})}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m,n}|\matrixelement{m}{\dot{\mds{H}}_t}{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\dot{\mathds{H}}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

(124)

Here, we use Proposition
8
to derive the fourth line.
Therefore, the geodesic distance is lower bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})

\displaystyle=\min_{\{\mathds{O}_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\mathds{O}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t})}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\{\mathds{H}_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\|\dot{\mathds{H}}_{t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\ln\mathds{1}-\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}.

(125)

Next, we prove the upper bound.
We consider the smooth path
\{\mathds{O}_{t}=\mathds{A}^{1/2}(\mathds{A}^{-1/2}\mathds{B}\mathds{A}^{-1/2})^{t}\mathds{A}^{1/2}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, which connects
\mathds{A}
and
\mathds{B}
.
The time derivative of
\mathds{O}_{t}
is

\displaystyle\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathds{A}^{1/2}(\mathds{A}^{-1/2}\mathds{B}\mathds{A}^{-1/2})^{t}\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\mathds{A}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathds{O}_{t}\mathds{A}^{-1/2}\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\mathds{A}^{1/2}.

(126)

Therefore, the metric can be written as

\displaystyle g_{\mathds{O}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\tr(\mds{O}_t^{-1}\dot{\mds{O}}_t\mds{O}_t^{-1}\dot{\mds{O}}_t)

\displaystyle=\tr[(\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2}))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}^{2},

(127)

which is constant in time.
Consequently, the geodesic distance is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\mathds{O}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{O}}_{t})}

\displaystyle=\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}.

(128)

Combining Eqs. (
125
) and (
128
), we obtain
\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})=\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}
.

Proposition 8
.

The following inequality holds for any real numbers
x
and
y
:

I\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s'}e^{(s+s^{\prime}-1)(x-y)}\geq 1.

(129)

Proof.

If
x=y
, then
I=1
, so the inequality holds trivially.
Otherwise, letting
z=x-y
, we evaluate the integral as follows:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=e^{-z}\quantity(\int_0^1\dd{s}e^{sz})^{2}=e^{-z}\quantity(\frac{e^{z}-1}{z})^{2}=\quantity[\frac{\sinh(|z|/2)}{|z|/2}]^{2}\geq 1.

(130)

Here, we use
\sinh|z|\geq|z|
for all
z
to derive the final inequality.
∎

D.2
Error bound for arbitrary initial states

We show that it suffices to examine the maximally mixed state in order to bound the error of the quantum channel for arbitrary initial states.
Indeed, let
\rho
be an arbitrary initial state.
Using the linearity of quantum channels, we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon(\Lambda)-\tr\quantity[\Pi\Lambda(\rho)]

\displaystyle=\tr\quantity[\Pi\Lambda(\mds{1})]-\tr\quantity[\Pi\Lambda(\rho)]

\displaystyle=\tr\quantity[\Pi\Lambda(\mds{1}-\rho)].

(131)

Since
\Lambda
is a CPTP map and
\mathds{1}-\rho\succeq 0
, we have
\tr\quantity[\Pi\Lambda(\mds{1}-\rho)]\geq 0
.
Therefore,

\tr\quantity[\Pi\Lambda(\rho)]\leq\epsilon(\Lambda).

(132)

This means that the error for any initial state
\rho
is always upper bounded by
\epsilon(\Lambda)
, which is
d
times the error for the maximally mixed state.
Hence, if the maximally mixed state is reset with sufficiently small error, other states can also be reset reliably.

D.3
Proof of the existence of a path of quantum channels

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\Lambda
be a quantum channel such that
\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)
is full-rank, and let
\{\phi_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be a smooth path in
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{+}
connecting
\mathds{1}/d
and
\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)
.
Then there exists a continuous path of quantum channels
\{\Lambda_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
in
\Upsilon_{d}
that connects
\mathsf{Id}
and
\Lambda
, and satisfies
\Lambda_{t}(\mathds{1}/d)=\phi_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

For convenience, we use the Choi-matrix representation of quantum channels.
It suffices to prove the existence of a continuous path
\{\mathds{M}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
in the space of Choi matrices that connects
\mathds{M}^{\mathsf{Id}}
and
\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}
, and satisfies
\tr_{1}\mathds{M}_{t}=d\phi_{t}
and
\tr_{2}\mathds{M}_{t}=\mathds{1}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Note that
\mathds{M}^{\mathsf{Id}}=\outerproduct{\Omega}{\Omega}
.

To this end, we first consider a smooth path
\{\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}

\displaystyle=[\kappa+a\sin(\pi t)]\frac{\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{1}}{d}

\displaystyle+[1-\kappa-a\sin(\pi t)][(1-t)\mathds{M}^{\mathsf{Id}}+t\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}],

(133)

where
0<a<1
and
0<\kappa<1-a
are arbitrary constants.
Evidently,
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}=\mathds{M}^{\mathsf{Id}}
and
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}=\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}
, and each
\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}
is a Choi matrix corresponding to a quantum channel.
Furthermore,
\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}
is strictly positive definite for all
t\in[0,1]
.
However, the condition
\tr_{1}\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}=d\phi_{t}
may not hold.
To construct the desired smooth path, we consider the following scaled operators:

\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}=e^{\ln\mathds{A}_{t,\kappa}+\mathds{U}_{t,\kappa}\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa}},

(134)

where
\mathds{U}_{t,\kappa}
and
\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa}
are Hermitian operators.
According to Proposition
10
, there exists a path
\{(\mathds{U}_{t,\kappa},\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa})\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}

\tr_{1}\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}=\kappa\mathds{1}+(1-\kappa)d\phi_{t}
,
\tr_{2}\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}=\mathds{1}
, and
\tr\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa}=0

The gauge condition
\tr\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa}=0

\mathds{U}_{t,\kappa}\leftarrow\mathds{U}_{t,\kappa}+c\mathds{1}

\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa}\leftarrow\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa}-c\mathds{1}

\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}
for any real

By the implicit function theorem, the path
\{(\mathds{U}_{t,\kappa},\mathds{V}_{t,\kappa})\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}

\{\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is also smooth.

Next, we prove that
\mathds{M}_{0,\kappa}=\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}
and
\mathds{M}_{1,\kappa}=\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}
.
Since

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}

\displaystyle=e^{\ln\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}+0\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes 0},~\tr_{1}\mathds{M}_{0,\kappa}=\tr_{1}\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}=\kappa\mathds{1}+(1-\kappa)d\phi_{0},~\tr_{2}\mathds{M}_{0,\kappa}=\tr_{2}\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}=\mathds{1},

(135)

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}

\displaystyle=e^{\ln\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}+0\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes 0},~\tr_{1}\mathds{M}_{1,\kappa}=\tr_{1}\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}=\kappa\mathds{1}+(1-\kappa)d\phi_{1},~\tr_{2}\mathds{M}_{1,\kappa}=\tr_{2}\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}=\mathds{1},

(136)

the uniqueness of
(\mathds{U},\mathds{V})
in Proposition
10
implies that
\mathds{U}_{0,\kappa}=\mathds{U}_{1,\kappa}=0

\mathds{V}_{0,\kappa}=\mathds{V}_{1,\kappa}=0
.
Therefore, the path
\{\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
smoothly connects
\mathds{A}_{0,\kappa}
and
\mathds{A}_{1,\kappa}

Now, we take the limit

and define
\mathds{M}_{t}\coloneqq\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathds{M}_{t,\kappa}
.
The path
\{\mathds{M}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is then continuous, connects
\mathds{M}^{\mathsf{Id}}
and
\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}
, and satisfies
\tr_{1}\mathds{M}_{t}=d\phi_{t}
and
\tr_{2}\mathds{M}_{t}=\mathds{1}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
By converting each Choi matrix

∎

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathds{A}
be a strictly positive Hermitian operator and let
\phi
be a positive density operator.
Then there exists a unique pair of Hermitian operators,
\mathds{U}
and
\mathds{V}
, such that
\tr\mathds{V}=0
and the operator
\mathds{M}=e^{\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{U}\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{V}}
satisfies
\tr_{1}\mathds{M}=d\phi
and
\tr_{2}\mathds{M}=\mathds{1}
.

Proof.

We consider the following function:

F(\mathds{U},\mathds{V})=\tr(e^{\ln\mds{A} + \mds{U}\otimes\mds{1} + \mds{1}\otimes\mds{V}})-\tr(\mds{U})-d\tr(\mds{V}\phi).

(137)

We show that
F(\mathds{U},\mathds{V})
is a convex function.
To this end, consider
f(t)=F(\mathds{U}+t\mathds{U}^{\prime},\mathds{V}+t\mathds{V}^{\prime})
as a function of
t
, where
\mathds{U}^{\prime}
and
\mathds{V}^{\prime}
are arbitrary Hermitian operators.
It suffices to prove that
\derivative*[2]{f(0)}{t}\geq 0
for any Hermitian operators
\mathds{U}
,
\mathds{U}^{\prime}
,
\mathds{V}
, and
\mathds{V}^{\prime}
.
Using Duhamel’s formula

\derivative{t}e^{\mathds{X}_{t}}=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}e^{s\mathds{X}_{t}}\dot{\mathds{X}}_{t}e^{(1-s)\mathds{X}_{t}},

(138)

we obtain

\displaystyle\derivative{f(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=\derivative{t}\quantity[\tr(e^{\ln\mds{A} + \mds{W} + t\mds{W}'}) - \tr(\mds{U}+t\mds{U}') - d\tr((\mds{V}+t\mds{V}')\phi)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\tr[e^{s(\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}+t\mathds{W}^{\prime})}\mathds{W}^{\prime}e^{(1-s)(\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}+t\mathds{W}^{\prime})}]-\tr(\mds{U}')-d\tr(\mds{V}'\phi)

\displaystyle=\tr(\mds{W}'e^{\ln\mds{A}+\mds{W}+t\mds{W}'})-\tr(\mds{U}')-d\tr(\mds{V}'\phi),

(139)

where
\mathds{W}=\mathds{U}\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{V}
and
\mathds{W}^{\prime}=\mathds{U}^{\prime}\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{V}^{\prime}
.
A global minimum must satisfy
\derivative*{f(0)}{t}=0
for any
\mathds{U}^{\prime}
and
\mathds{V}^{\prime}
.
Since

\displaystyle\evaluated{\dv{f(t)}{t}}_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\tr[\mathds{U}^{\prime}(\tr_{2}(e^{\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}})-\mathds{1})]

\displaystyle+\tr[\mathds{V}^{\prime}(\tr_{1}(e^{\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}})-d\phi)],

(140)

\derivative*{f(0)}{t}=0
is achieved only when
\tr_{1}e^{\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}}=d\phi
and
\tr_{2}e^{\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}}=\mathds{1}
, which are exactly the required conditions
\tr_{1}\mathds{M}=d\phi
and
\tr_{2}\mathds{M}=\mathds{1}
.
Similarly, the second derivative of
f(t)
with respect to
t
is

\displaystyle\evaluated{\dv[2]{f(t)}{t}}_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\tr[\mathds{W}^{\prime}e^{s(\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W})}\mathds{W}^{\prime}e^{(1-s)(\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W})}].

(141)

Since
\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}
is a Hermitian operator, it can be decomposed as
\ln\mathds{A}+\mathds{W}=\sum_{\imath}a_{\imath}\outerproduct{\imath}{\imath}
, where
\{a_{\imath}\}_{\imath}
are real eigenvalues.
Substituting this into the above expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\evaluated{\dv[2]{f(t)}{t}}_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\imath,\jmath}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}|\matrixelement{\imath}{\mds{W}'}{\jmath}|^{2}e^{sa_{\jmath}+(1-s)a_{\imath}}.

(142)

Since

\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}e^{sa_{\jmath}+(1-s)a_{\imath}}=\begin{cases}e^{a_{\imath}}&\text{if}~a_{\imath}=a_{\jmath}\\
\frac{e^{a_{\imath}}-e^{a_{\jmath}}}{a_{\imath}-a_{\jmath}}&\text{if}~a_{\imath}\neq a_{\jmath}\end{cases},

(143)

which is always positive,
\derivative*[2]{f(0)}{t}\geq 0
.
The equality can be achieved only when
\mathds{W}^{\prime}=0
, or
\mathds{U}^{\prime}=c\mathds{1}=-\mathds{V}^{\prime}
.
If we restrict to the subspace where
\tr\mathds{V}=0
, then
c=0
, and
\mathds{U}^{\prime}=\mathds{V}^{\prime}=0
.
Therefore,
F
is strictly convex on the restricted subspace.
Furthermore, since
F
is continuous and coercive, it follows that there is a unique global minimum, which completes the proof.
∎

D.4
Lower and upper bounds on the geometric complexity

We prove the following lower and upper bounds:

\ell\leq\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell,

(144)

where
\ell=\|\ln d+\ln\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)\|_{F}
.

D.4.1
Lower bound

The lower bound follows directly from the properties of the defined Riemannian metric.
Let
\{\mathds{M}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be a smooth path of Choi matrices connecting
\mathds{M}^{\mathsf{Id}}
and
\mathds{M}^{\Lambda}
.
Then
\phi_{\mathds{M}_{0}}=\mathds{1}/d
and
\phi_{\mathds{M}_{1}}=\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)
.
Using the result in Sec.
D.1
that, on the unrestricted space of positive operators, the geodesic distance induced by
g_{\phi_{\mathds{M}}}(\phi_{\mathds{X}},\phi_{\mathds{Y}})=\tr(\phi_{\mds{M}}^{-1}\phi_{\mds{X}}\phi_{\mds{M}}^{-1}\phi_{\mds{Y}})
is
\mathcal{D}(\mathds{A},\mathds{B})=\|\ln(\mds{A}^{-1/2}\mds{B}\mds{A}^{-1/2})\|_{F}
, we obtain the following lower bound on the geometric complexity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\min_{\{\Lambda_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\mathds{M}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{M}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{M}}_{t})}

\displaystyle=\min_{\{\phi_{\mathds{M}_{t}}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\phi_{\mathds{M}_{t}}}(\dot{\phi}_{\mathds{M}_{t}},\dot{\phi}_{\mathds{M}_{t}})}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{D}(\phi_{\mathds{M}_{0}},\phi_{\mathds{M}_{1}})

\displaystyle=\|\ln(d)+\ln\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\ell.

(145)

D.4.2
Upper bound

We consider a specific path
\{\phi_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, defined by

\displaystyle\phi_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{0}^{1/2}(\phi_{0}^{-1/2}\phi_{1}\phi_{0}^{-1/2})^{t}\phi_{0}^{1/2}}{\tr[\phi_{0}^{1/2}(\phi_{0}^{-1/2}\phi_{1}\phi_{0}^{-1/2})^{t}\phi_{0}^{1/2}]},

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}/d,~\text{and}~\phi_{1}=\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d).

(146)

Evidently,
\phi_{t}
is always a density matrix, and the path
\{\phi_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
smoothly connects the initial and final states
\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{1}
.
For notational convenience, we define
\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\coloneqq\phi_{0}^{1/2}(\phi_{0}^{-1/2}\phi_{1}\phi_{0}^{-1/2})^{t}\phi_{0}^{1/2}
,
Z_{t}\coloneqq\tr\widetilde{\phi}_{t}
, and
A\coloneqq\phi_{0}^{-1/2}\phi_{1}\phi_{0}^{-1/2}
.

First, we evaluate the Riemannian metric
g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})
.
To this end, we evaluate the following quantities:

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{\phi}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}^{1/2}A^{t}\ln A\phi_{0}^{1/2}=\widetilde{\phi}_{t}B,

(147)

\displaystyle\dot{Z}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tr(\phi_0^{1/2}A^t\ln A\phi_0^{1/2})=\tr(\widetilde{\phi}_tB),

(148)

where
B\coloneqq\phi_{0}^{-1/2}\ln A\phi_{0}^{1/2}
.
Using these expressions, the time derivative of
\phi_{t}=\widetilde{\phi}_{t}/Z_{t}
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\widetilde{\phi}}_{t}}{Z_{t}}-\phi_{t}\frac{\dot{Z}_{t}}{Z_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\phi}_{t}}{Z_{t}}B-\phi_{t}\tr(\frac{\widetilde{\phi}_t}{Z_t}B)

\displaystyle=\phi_{t}B-\phi_{t}\tr(\phi_tB).

(149)

It follows that
\phi_{t}^{-1}\dot{\phi}_{t}=B-\tr(\phi_tB)
.
Consequently, the metric
g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})
can be written as

\displaystyle g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\tr{[B-\tr(\phi_tB)]^2}

\displaystyle=\tr(B^2)-2\tr(B)\tr(\phi_tB)+d\tr(\phi_tB)^{2}.

(150)

Next, by applying the inequalities
|\tr(X^\dagger Y)|^{2}\leq\tr(X^\dagger X)\tr(Y^\dagger Y)
and
\tr(\phi_t^2)\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\tr(B^2)-2\tr(B)\tr(\phi_tB)+d\tr(\phi_tB)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)^{2}\tr(B^2).

(151)

Since
\phi_{0}=\mathds{1}/d
,
B=\ln A=\ln(d\phi_1)
.
Using these facts, the geometric complexity is upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\min_{\{\Lambda_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\mathds{M}_{t}}(\dot{\mathds{M}}_{t},\dot{\mathds{M}}_{t})}

\displaystyle=\min_{\{\phi_{\mathds{M}_{t}}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\phi_{\mathds{M}_{t}}}(\dot{\phi}_{\mathds{M}_{t}},\dot{\phi}_{\mathds{M}_{t}})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})}

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\sqrt{\tr{[\ln(d\phi_1)]^2}}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{d}+1)\|\ln d+\ln\phi_{1}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell.

(152)

Here, the minimum in the second line is taken over all continuous paths in the space of density operators connecting
\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{1}
, due to the existence of a path of quantum channels proved in Theorem
9
.

D.5
Protocol-scaling relations for quantum maps

D.5.1
Lindbladian maps

Let
\Lambda(\circ)=e^{\mathcal{L}_{N}}\dots e^{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\circ)
be a quantum channel realized by sequentially applying
N
protocols, where each protocol is generated by Lindblad dynamics of the form:

\mathcal{L}_{k}(\circ)\coloneqq-i[\mathds{H}_{k},\circ]+\sum_{c}\quantity[\mds{L}_{k,c}\circ\mds{L}_{k,c}^\dagger- \frac{1}{2}\{\mds{L}_{k,c}^\dagger\mds{L}_{k,c},\circ\}].

(153)

For simplicity, we assume that each protocol is applied over a unit time interval.
As a physically reasonable constraint on the coupling strength between the system and the environment, we impose that
\|\sum_{c}\mathds{L}_{k,c}^{\dagger}\mathds{L}_{k,c}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma
for any
k
.
Let
\phi_{t}
be the density operator at time
t
obtained by applying these protocols to the maximally mixed state, i.e.,
\phi_{t}=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\mathds{1}/d)
, where
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is one of the superoperators
\{\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{N}\}
applied at time
t
. Let
\phi_{t}=\sum_{n}p_{n}(t)\outerproduct{n_t}{n_t}
be the spectral decomposition of
\phi_{t}
. Then the time evolution of
p_{n}(t)
is governed by

\dot{p}_{n}(t)=\sum_{c,m}[w_{nm}^{k,c}(t)p_{m}(t)-w_{mn}^{k,c}(t)p_{n}(t)],

(154)

where
w_{mn}^{k,c}(t)\coloneqq|\matrixelement{m_t}{\mds{L}_{k,c}}{n_t}|^{2}
, and the index
k
depends on time
t
.
Applying Hölder’s inequality,
|\tr(\mds{A}^\dagger\mds{B})|\leq\|\mathds{A}\|_{1}\|\mathds{B}\|_{\infty}
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{p}_{n}(t)}{p_{n}(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c,m}w_{mn}^{k,c}(t)-\frac{1}{p_{n}(t)}\sum_{c,m}w_{nm}^{k,c}(t)p_{m}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c,m}w_{mn}^{k,c}(t)

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{n_t}{\sum_c\mds{L}_{k,c}^\dagger\mds{L}_{k,c}}{n_t}

\displaystyle\leq\|\sum_{c}\mathds{L}_{k,c}^{\dagger}\mathds{L}_{k,c}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma.

(155)

Integrating this inequality over time, we obtain

\ln(1/d)-\ln p_{n}(N)\leq\int_{0}^{N}\differential{t}\gamma=N\gamma.

(156)

On the other hand, since
p_{n}(N)\in[0,1]
, we have

\ln p_{n}(N)-\ln(1/d)\leq\ln d.

(157)

Therefore,

|\ln p_{n}(N)-\ln(1/d)|\leq\max(N\gamma,\ln d)\leq N\ln(de^\gamma).

(158)

Since
\phi_{N}=\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)
, the Frobenius norm is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\|\ln d+\ln\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tr[(\ln d+\ln\phi_{N})^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{n}[\ln d+\ln p_{n}(N)]^{2}}.

(159)

Consequently,

\ell=\|\ln d+\ln\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)\|_{F}\leq N\sqrt{d}\ln(de^\gamma).

(160)

Combining this inequality with the fact
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\ell
yields the following lower bound on the number of protocols:

N\geq\frac{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}{(d+\sqrt{d})\ln(de^\gamma)}.

(161)

D.5.2
General unitary dynamics

We consider the case
\Lambda=\Lambda_{N}\dots\Lambda_{1}
, where each map
\Lambda_{k}
is implemented through general unitary dynamics of the system and an environment
E_{k}
.
That is,
\Lambda_{k}[\circ]=\tr_{E_{k}}[\mathds{U}_{k}(\circ\otimes\pi_{k})\mathds{U}_{k}^{\dagger}]
, where
\pi_{k}=e^{-\beta\mathds{H}_{k}}/Z
is the thermal Gibbs state of the environment
E_{k}
, and
\mathds{U}_{k}
is the unitary operator acting on the joint system composed of the system and the environment.
Under physical constraints, it is natural to assume the following limit on the control bandwidth:
\beta\Delta\mathds{H}_{k}\leq\gamma
.
Here,
\Delta\mathds{H}\coloneqq\max_{n}E_{n}-\min_{n}E_{n}
, where
\{E_{n}\}_{n}
are the eigenvalues of the Hermitian operator
\mathds{H}
.

Defining
\phi_{0}\coloneqq\mathds{1}/d
and
\phi_{k}\coloneqq\Lambda_{k}\dots\Lambda_{1}(\phi_{0})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)\mathcal{D}(\phi_{0},\phi_{N})

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{d}+1)\|\ln(d\phi_N)\|_{F}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{d}+1)\sqrt{\sum_{n}(\ln[d\lambda_{n}(\phi_{N})])^{2}},

(162)

where
0\leq\lambda_{1}(\phi)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{d}(\phi)\leq 1
are the eigenvalues of the density operator
\phi
in increasing order.
Since
|\ln[d\lambda_{n}(\phi)]|\leq\max\!\quantity(|\ln[d\lambda_1(\phi)]|,\ln d)
, we readily obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{d}+1)\max\!\quantity(|\ln[d\lambda_1(\phi_N)]|,\ln d)

\displaystyle=(d+\sqrt{d})\max\!\quantity(\ln\qty[\frac{1}{d\lambda_1(\phi_N)}],\ln d),

(163)

where we used the fact that
d\lambda_{1}(\phi)\leq 1
.
Next, we lower-bound the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\phi_{N})
.
From the relation
\phi_{k}=\Lambda_{k}(\phi_{k-1})=\tr_{E_{k}}[\mathds{U}_{k}(\phi_{k-1}\otimes\pi_{k})\mathds{U}_{k}^{\dagger}]
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\phi_{k})\geq\lambda_{1}(\phi_{k-1})\lambda_{1}(\pi_{k}).

(164)

Therefore,
\lambda_{1}(\phi_{N})\geq\lambda_{1}(\phi_{0})\prod_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{1}(\pi_{k})
.
Since
\lambda_{1}(\pi_{k})\geq e^{-\beta\Delta\mathds{H}_{k}}/d\geq e^{-\gamma}/d
, we obtain the following lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\phi_{N})
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\phi_{N})\geq\frac{1}{d}\quantity(\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{d})^{N}=\frac{1}{d}\quantity(\frac{1}{de^\gamma})^{N},

(165)

which immediately yields

\displaystyle\ln\quantity[\frac{1}{d\lambda_1(\phi_N)}]\leq N\ln(de^{\gamma}).

(166)

Note that
N\ln(de^{\gamma})\geq\ln d
.
Consequently, the geometric complexity is upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\leq N(d+\sqrt{d})\ln(de^{\gamma}).

(167)

This yields the following lower bound on the number of protocols in terms of geometric complexity:

N\geq\frac{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}{(d+\sqrt{d})\ln(de^\gamma)}.

(168)

D.6
Proof of the entropic bound on the geometric complexity

We prove the following lower bound on the geometric complexity:

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)\geq\ln S(\mathds{1}/d)-\ln S(\Lambda[\mathds{1}/d]).

(169)

Here,
S(\rho)=-\tr(\rho\ln\rho)
is the von Neumann entropy of the quantum state
\rho
.
Let
\{\Lambda_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be the geodesic path, and define
\phi_{t}\coloneqq\phi_{\mathds{M}_{t}}=\Lambda_{t}(\mathds{1}/d)
, where
\mathds{M}_{t}
denotes the Choi matrix of the quantum channel
\Lambda_{t}
.
We first show that

g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})\geq\quantity|\dv{t}\ln S(\phi_t)|^{2}.

(170)

Using the spectral decomposition
\phi_{t}=\sum_{n}p_{n}(t)\outerproduct{n_t}{n_t}
, the right-hand side is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\quantity|\dv{t}\ln S(\phi_t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\quantity|\frac{\tr(\dot\phi_t\ln\phi_t)}{\tr(\phi_t\ln\phi_t)} |^{2}

\displaystyle=\quantity|\frac{\sum_n\mel{n_t}{\dot\phi_t}{n_t}\ln p_n(t)}{\sum_np_n(t)\ln p_n(t)}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{[\sum_{n}\matrixelement{n_t}{\dot\phi_t}{n_t}\ln p_{n}(t)]^{2}}{\sum_{n}[p_{n}(t)\ln p_{n}(t)]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n}\quantity[\frac{\mel{n_t}{\dot\phi_t}{n_t}\ln p_n(t)}{p_n(t)\ln p_n(t)}]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\quantity[\frac{\mel{n_t}{\dot\phi_t}{n_t}}{p_n(t)}]^{2}.

(171)

Here, we use
(\sum_{n}p_{n}\ln p_{n})^{2}\geq\sum_{n}(p_{n}\ln p_{n})^{2}
for any distribution
\{p_{n}\}
to obtain the third line, and
\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}/b_{n}\geq(\sum_{n}a_{n})^{2}/\sum_{n}b_{n}
for nonnegative
\{b_{n}\}
and real
\{a_{n}\}
to obtain the fourth line.
On the other hand, the metric can be lower bounded as

\displaystyle g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\tr(\phi_t^{-1}\dot\phi_t\phi_t^{-1}\dot\phi_t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\frac{|\matrixelement{m_t}{\dot\phi_t}{n_t}|^{2}}{p_{m}(t)p_{n}(t)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n}\quantity[\frac{\mel{n_t}{\dot\phi_t}{n_t}}{p_n(t)}]^{2}.

(172)

Combining Eqs. (
171
) and (
172
), we immediately obtain Eq. (
170
).
Consequently, the geometric complexity is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\quantity|\dv{t}\ln S(\phi_t)|

\displaystyle\geq\quantity|\int_0^1\dd{t}\dv{t}\ln S(\phi_t)|

\displaystyle=|\ln S(\phi_{0})-\ln S(\phi_{1})|

\displaystyle=\ln S(\mathds{1}/d)-\ln S(\Lambda[\mathds{1}/d]).

(173)

D.7
Proof of the trade-off relation (
20
)

Let
\phi=\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)
, and let
0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{d}\leq 1
be its eigenvalues.
Since
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=1
, we have
d\lambda_{1}\leq 1
.
Using this fact and the inequality
\sqrt{\sum_{n}x_{n}^{2}}\geq\max_{n}|x_{n}|
,
\ell
can be lower bounded as

\displaystyle\ell

\displaystyle=\|\ln(d\phi)\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}\ln(d\lambda_n)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq|\ln(d\lambda_1)|=-\ln(d\lambda_1).

(174)

Therefore,
e^{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}\geq e^{\ell}\geq 1/(d\lambda_{1})
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\epsilon(\Lambda)=d\tr(\Pi\phi)\geq d\lambda_{1}.

(175)

Consequently, multiplying these two inequalities yields the desired trade-off relation:

e^{\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)}\times\epsilon(\Lambda)\geq 1.

(176)

D.8
Calculation of the geometric complexity for a quantum reset channel

We derive an explicit expression for the geometric complexity of the following quantum channel:

\Lambda(\circ)=\tr_{E}[\mathds{U}(\circ\otimes\rho_{E})\mathds{U}^{\dagger}],

(177)

where the unitary operator
\mathds{U}
swaps the states of the system and the environment, and
\rho_{E}=\kappa\outerproduct{1}{1}+(1-\kappa)\outerproduct{0}{0}
is a quantum state close to the ground state.
In particular,

\mathds{U}\quantity(\mds{1}/2\otimes\rho_E)\mathds{U}^{\dagger}=\rho_{E}\otimes\mathds{1}/2.

(178)

Thus, for arbitrary initial states, the quantum channel
\Lambda
reliably resets the system close to the ground state, with an error bounded from above by
2\kappa
for the projector
\Pi=\outerproduct{1}{1}
.

Given the spectral decomposition
\rho_{E}=\sum_{n}p_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}
, Eq. (
178
) is equivalent to

\sum_{m,n}p_{n}\mathds{U}(\outerproduct{m}{m}\otimes\outerproduct{n}{n})\mathds{U}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m,n}p_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes\outerproduct{m}{m}.

(179)

Hence, the unitary operator is given by
\mathds{U}=\sum_{n,m}\outerproduct{m,n}{n,m}
, where we use
\ket{m,n}=\ket{m}\otimes\ket{n}
for notational convenience.
The quantum channel
\Lambda
can then be written as

\displaystyle\Lambda(\circ)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}p_{m}\outerproduct{m}{n}\circ\outerproduct{n}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\mathds{K}_{mn}\circ\mathds{K}_{mn}^{\dagger},

(180)

where
\mathds{K}_{mn}=\sqrt{p_{m}}\outerproduct{m}{n}
.
Since
\Lambda(\mathds{1}/2)=\rho_{E}
, the geometric complexity of this quantum channel reads

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)=\min_{\{\phi_{t}\}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{t}\sqrt{g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})},

(181)

where the minimum is taken over all smooth paths of quantum states connecting
\mathds{1}/2
and
\rho_{E}
.

Using the Bloch representation, each quantum state
\phi_{t}
can be written as

\phi_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mds{1}+\vb{a}_t\cdot\mbm{\sigma}),

(182)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
are the Pauli matrices,
\mathbf{a}_{t}
is the Bloch vector satisfying
\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}\leq 1
, and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
The inverse matrix
\phi_{t}^{-1}
can also be expressed in terms of
\mathbf{a}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as

\phi_{t}^{-1}=\frac{2}{1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}(\mathds{1}-\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

(183)

Using
\dot{\phi}_{t}=\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}/2
and
(\mathbf{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})(\mathbf{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})=(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})\mathds{1}+i(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{t}^{-1}\dot{\phi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}(\mathds{1}-\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})(\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\quantity[(\dot{\vb{a}}_t\cdot\mbm{\sigma}) - (\vb{a}_t\cdot\dot{\vb{a}}_t)\mds{1} - i(\vb{a}_t\times\dot{\vb{a}}_t)\cdot\mbm{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\quantity[ - (\vb{a}_t\cdot\dot{\vb{a}}_t)\mds{1} + (\dot{\vb{a}}_t - i(\vb{a}_t\times\dot{\vb{a}}_t))\cdot\mbm{\sigma} ].

(184)

Let
\alpha=-(\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})
and
\mathbf{b}_{t}=\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}-i(\mathbf{a}_{t}\times\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})
. Then, the metric is calculated as

\displaystyle g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}}\tr[(\alpha\mathds{1}+\mathbf{b}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}}\tr[\alpha^{2}\mathds{1}+2\alpha(\mathbf{b}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})+(\mathbf{b}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}}\tr[\alpha^{2}\mathds{1}+2\alpha(\mathbf{b}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\|\mathbf{b}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\mathds{1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}}\quantity(\alpha^2+\|\vb{b}_t\|_2^2).

(185)

Since

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{b}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=[\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}-i(\mathbf{a}_{t}\times\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})]\cdot[\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}-i(\mathbf{a}_{t}\times\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})]

\displaystyle=\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\times\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-[\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})+(\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})^{2},

(186)

we obtain

g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})=\frac{2\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}{1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}+\frac{4(\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t})^{2}}{(1-\|\mathbf{a}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}}.

(187)

To proceed, it is convenient to use spherical coordinates,

\mathbf{a}_{t}=[a_{t}\sin\theta_{t}\cos\varphi_{t},a_{t}\sin\theta_{t}\sin\varphi_{t},a_{t}\cos\theta_{t}]^{\top}.

(188)

Then
\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=\dot{a}_{t}^{2}+a_{t}^{2}\dot{\theta}_{t}^{2}+a_{t}^{2}(\sin\theta_{t})^{2}\dot{\varphi}_{t}^{2}
and
\mathbf{a}_{t}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{a}}_{t}=a_{t}\dot{a}_{t}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\dot{a}_{t}^{2}+a_{t}^{2}\dot{\theta}_{t}^{2}+a_{t}^{2}(\sin\theta_{t})^{2}\dot{\varphi}_{t}^{2})}{1-a_{t}^{2}}+\frac{4a_{t}^{2}\dot{a}_{t}^{2}}{(1-a_{t}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1+a_{t}^{2})}{(1-a_{t}^{2})^{2}}\dot{a}_{t}^{2}+\frac{2a_{t}^{2}}{1-a_{t}^{2}}\dot{\theta}_{t}^{2}+\frac{2a_{t}^{2}(\sin\theta_{t})^{2}}{1-a_{t}^{2}}\dot{\varphi}_{t}^{2}.

(189)

Since
a_{t}\in[0,1]
, the geodesic connecting the center of the sphere to any point lies in a fixed plane through the origin, where both
\theta_{t}
and
\varphi_{t}
are constant.
Therefore, the metric simplifies to

g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})=\frac{2(1+a_{t}^{2})}{(1-a_{t}^{2})^{2}}\dot{a}_{t}^{2}.

(190)

Consequently, the geometric complexity can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a_{1}}\differential{a}\frac{\sqrt{2(1+a^{2})}}{1-a^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\atanh\frac{\sqrt{2}a_{1}}{\sqrt{1+a_{1}^{2}}}-\sqrt{2}\atanh\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{1+a_{1}^{2}}}.

(191)

Defining
\wp\coloneqq\sqrt{2\tr(\rho_E^2)-1}=|2\kappa-1|
, we can express the geometric complexity as

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)=2\atanh\frac{\sqrt{2}\wp}{\sqrt{1+\wp^{2}}}-\sqrt{2}\atanh\frac{\wp}{\sqrt{1+\wp^{2}}}.

(192)

As a result,
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
diverges in the limits
\kappa\to 0
and
\kappa\to 1
, that is, when the initial state of the environment becomes pure.
Thus, the geometric complexity captures the purity of the environment, which is one of the crucial resources for cooling.
