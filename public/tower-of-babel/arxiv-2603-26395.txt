Ascending Convex Polyominoes
Nicholas Beaton, Simone Rinaldi

Abstract
Convex polyominoes can be refined according to the number of direction changes in monotone paths connecting pairs of cells, leading to the notion of k k -convexity. In particular, the cases k = 1 k=1 and k = 2 k=2 correspond to L L -convex and Z Z -convex polyominoes, two well-studied subclasses of convex polyominoes, with intermediate families such as centered and 4 4 -stack polyominoes. These families exhibit remarkably different combinatorial behaviours, suggesting that geometric constraints have a strong impact on the nature of the generating function: L L -convex and centered polyominoes possess rational generating functions and growth of order ( 2 + 2 ) n (2+\sqrt{2})^{n} and 4 n 4^{n} , respectively, while Z Z -convex, 4-stack, and convex polyominoes have algebraic functions and asymptotics of order n ​ 4 n n4^{n} , n ​ 4 n \sqrt{n}\,4^{n} , and n ​ 4 n n4^{n} respectively. In this paper we investigate the structure of Z Z -convex polyominoes by introducing a refinement based on the NW- and NE-convexity degrees, which yields a decomposition into three disjoint subclasses 𝒞 ⁡ ( 1 , 2 ) \mathcal{C}(1,2) , 𝒞 ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) \mathcal{C}(2,1) , and 𝒞 ⁡ ( 2 , 2 ) \mathcal{C}(2,2) . To enumerate these families we introduce ascending polyominoes, admitting a simple geometric characterization, and construct a generating tree that leads to functional equations for the corresponding generating functions. By solving these equations we obtain explicit algebraic generating functions and the asymptotic growth for all the subclasses.

Basic definitions and properties

Convexity degree and
k
-convex polyominoes

A path is monotone if it is made of steps in two directions only: North-steps and East-steps (NE-path), North-steps and West-steps (NW-path), South-steps and East-steps (SE-path) or South-steps and West-steps (SW-path). The authors of
[
9
]
proved that a polyomino is convex if and only if any two of its cells are joined by a monotone path internal to the polyomino.
Given
k\geq 0
, a convex polyomino is said to be
k-convex
if any two of its cells can be joined by a monotone path, internal to the polyomino, with at most
k
changes of direction. Obviously, a
k
-convex polyomino is also
h
-convex, for every
h\geq k
. We define the
degree of convexity
of a convex polyomino
P
as the smallest
k
such that
P
is
k
-convex.

In
[
6
]
, the notion of
k
-convexity was refined as follows.
Let
P
be a convex polyomino and let
a,b
be two cells.
We write
a\nwarrow b
(resp.,
a\nearrow b
) if there exists a NW-path (resp., NE-path) joining
a
to
b
.
Whenever
a\nearrow b
, the
NE-distance
from
a
to
b
, denoted by
D_{\mathrm{NE}}(a,b)
, is defined as the minimum number of direction changes among all NE-paths connecting
a
to
b
.
The
NE-degree
of convexity of
P
is then:

D_{\mathrm{NE}}(P)=\max\{D_{\mathrm{NE}}(a,b):a\nearrow b\}.

Equivalently, it is the smallest integer
k
such that
D_{\mathrm{NE}}(a,b)\leq k
for all pairs
a\nearrow b
, and equality holds for at least one such pair.
The quantities
D_{\mathrm{NW}}(a,b)
and
D_{\mathrm{NW}}(P)
are defined analogously.
Finally, the (global) degree of convexity of
P
is

D(P)=\max\{D_{\mathrm{NE}}(P),\,D_{\mathrm{NW}}(P)\}.

The authors of
[
6
]
proved that:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
P
be a convex polyomino such that
D_{\mathrm{NW}}(P)<D_{\mathrm{NE}}(P)
. If
D_{\mathrm{NE}}(P)>2
, then
D_{\mathrm{NW}}(P)=1
.

This property describes how the degrees of convexity in the two directions may behave in a generic convex polyomino. In fact, a convex polyomino having degree of convexity greater than
2
must be 1-convex in one of the two directions. Hence, a convex polyomino may be either
2
-convex in both directions or
1
-convex in one direction and
k
-convex in the other one (

arbitrary). Figure
4

Theorem 3.1
.

Every centered ascending polyomino of size
n+1
is uniquely generated from a centered ascending polyomino of size
n
through the application of one among the operations Left Cell, Right Cell, Row, or Shift.

Proof.

To prove the statement, it suffices to show that for every centered ascending polyomino
P
of size
n+1
, there exists a unique centered ascending polyomino
\phi(P)
of size
n
such that
P
is obtained from
\phi(P)
by one of the four operations defined above.
We proceed by distinguishing the following mutually disjoint cases:

(1)

b>1
.
In this case, the last applied operation must be
Row
, applied to a centered polyomino
\phi(P)
, since only the
Row
operation produces an ascending polyomino satisfying
b>1
.

(2)

b=1
.
We consider the following subcases:

(2.1)

P
has exactly one cell in the leftmost column.
In this case,
P
is obtained by adding a leftmost cell to the centered ascending polyomino
\phi(P)
via
Left Cell
.

(2.2)

P
has more than one cell in the leftmost column, but has exactly one cell in the rightmost column.
In this case,
P
is obtained by adding a rightmost cell to the centered ascending polyomino
\phi(P)
via
Right Cell
, which is possible because
\phi(P)
has more than one cell in the leftmost column.

(2.3)

Neither case (2.1) nor (2.2) applies.
In this case, there is no cell of
P
above the first cell of the base, and there is at least one cell below it. Similarly, there is no cell of
P
below the last cell of the base, and there is at least one above it.
Let
B_{u}
(resp.
B_{d}
) be the row of
P
immediately above (resp. below) the base, and let
\alpha
be the (possibly empty) stack polyomino lying above
B_{u}
.
The situation is sketched in Figure
10
.
Let
\phi(P)
be the polyomino obtained from
P
by removing the row
B_{u}
.
It is a centered ascending polyomino with
b=1
. Moreover,
\phi(P)
is a flipped stack if and only if
\alpha
is empty.
It is now clear that
P
has been obtained through the application of the
Shift
operation to
\phi(P)
,
which is possible since
\phi(P)
satisfies

and fulfills conditions (1) and (2) required for the application of

. ∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Every ascending polyomino of size
n+1
is uniquely generated from an ascending
polyomino of size
n
by applying exactly one of the operations
Left Cell, Right Cell, Row, Shift, Nc, or Nc
∗
.

Proof.

Let
P
be an ascending polyomino of size
n+1
.

Case 1:
P
is non-centered.

(1) Suppose that the topmost cell of the rightmost column of
P
has strictly
greater height than all other cells of
P
. Then
P
is rectangular.
By removing this top cell, we obtain a polyomino
\phi(P)
of size
n
which is still
non-centered and rectangular. Hence
P
is obtained from
\phi(P)
by adding
a cell on top of the last column (operation
Nc
∗
).

(2) Otherwise, the topmost cell of the rightmost column has height less than or
equal to the maximal height of the column immediately to its left.
By removing the entire last column, we obtain a polyomino
\phi(P)
of size
n
.
If
\phi(P)
is centered, then
P
is obtained from
\phi(P)
by applying the

Nc
. Otherwise,
P
is obtained by applying

∗
.

Case 2:

In this case, the same decomposition argument used in
Theorem
3.1
applies. Therefore,
P
is uniquely obtained from

\phi(P)
by applying exactly one of the operations

Left Cell
,
Right Cell
,
Row
, or
Shift
.

In all cases, the construction is unique, which concludes the proof.
∎

(r)&\to&(1)^{r}\,\ldots\,(r-1)^{2}\,(r)\\
\\
(r)^{\prime}&\to&(1)^{\prime}\,\ldots\,(r)^{\prime}\,(r+1)^{\prime}\\
&&(1)^{r-1}\,(2)^{r-2}\ldots\,(r-1)\\
\end{array}\right.

Solving the functional equations

Using standard techniques

,

,

Concerning the centered cases, for any given class

K(x,y,z;t)=K(x,y,z)=\sum_{P\in K}t^{|P|}x^{b(P)}y^{w(P)}z^{r(P)}

the generating function of

and the size

of

Rectangular Case

From the succession rule above we find:

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=txC^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)+txL^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)+txS^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=txyC^{\prime}_{0}(1,y)+tyS^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}txyC^{\prime}(1,y,z)+\frac{txy}{1-z}\left[zC^{\prime}(1,1,z)-C^{\prime}(z,1,z)\right]+\frac{txy}{1-z}\left[zC^{\prime}_{0}(1,1)-C^{\prime}_{0}(z,1)\right]\\
+tyL^{\prime}(x,y,z)+tyS^{\prime}(x,y,z)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{tz}{1-z}\left[C^{\prime}(1,1,1)-C^{\prime}(1,1,z)\right]+\frac{tz}{1-z}\left[L^{\prime}(1,1,1)-L^{\prime}(1,1,z)\right]\\
+\frac{tz}{1-z}\left[S^{\prime}(1,1,1)-S^{\prime}(1,1,z)\right]+\frac{tz}{1-z}\left[N^{\prime}(1)-zN^{\prime}(z)\right]\end{multlined}

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{0}(x,y)

These solutions can then be substituted into (

as the only unknowns in that equation. After rearranging, the standard kernel method can be applied, using the kernel root

(6.13)

Next, (
6.4
) and (

or

and

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{5}x^{2}yz(1-t)(1-ty+t^{2}y-tz-t^{2}z+t^{2}yz)}{(1-tx)(1-t-2ty+t^{2}y^{2})(1-3t+t^{2}-2tz+4t^{2}z+t^{2}z^{2}-t^{3}z^{2})}\,,

(6.14)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{4}xyz(1-2t+t^{2}-ty+2t^{2}y-tz+t^{2}z+t^{2}yz-t^{3}yz)}{(1-t-2ty+t^{2}y^{2})(1-3t+t^{2}-2tz+4t^{2}z+t^{2}z^{2}-t^{3}z^{2})}\,.

(6.15)

Finally we can substitute everything into (

), leaving
N^{\prime}(z)
and
N^{\prime}(1)
. The kernel method can be applied again, with

(6.16)

Non-rectangular case

For brevity we will use the notation:

\partial_{x=1}f(x)=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)\right]_{x=1}

and likewise for
\partial_{z=1}
.
From the succession rules, we have

\displaystyle C(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=txC(x,y,z)+txL(x,y,z)+txS(x,y,z),

(6.17)

\displaystyle C_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=txC_{1}(x)+txR(x),

(6.18)

\displaystyle L(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}txyC(1,y,z)+\frac{txy}{1-z}\left[zC(1,1,z)-C(z,1,z)\right]+\frac{txy}{1-z}\left[C_{1}(1)-C_{1}(z)\right]\\
+tyL(x,y,z)+tyR(x)+tyS(x,y,z),\end{multlined}

\displaystyle R(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}tx\partial_{x=1}C^{\prime}(x,1,1)-txC^{\prime}(1,1,1)+tx\partial_{x=1}C(x,1,1)-txC(1,1,1)\\
+tx\partial_{x=1}C^{\prime}_{0}(x,1)-txC^{\prime}_{0}(1,1)+tx\partial_{x=1}C_{1}(x)+tR(x),\end{multlined}

\displaystyle S(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=txC^{\prime}(1,y,1)+txC(1,y,1)+tS^{\prime}(x,y,1)+tS(x,y,1)+\frac{tyz}{1-y}\left[S(x,1,z)-S(x,y,z)\right],

(6.23)

and one final equation for
N(z)
which we will deal with separately.

Note that
L(x,y,z)=xL(1,y,z)
and similarly
R(x)=xR(1)
and
S(x,y,z)=xS(1,y,z)
, while
C
and
C_{1}
do not factor so easily. So
x
is not really required in (
6
)–(
6.23
), and we can write:

\displaystyle L(1,y,z)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}tyC(1,y,z)+\frac{ty}{1-z}\left[zC(1,1,z)-C(z,1,z)\right]+\frac{ty}{1-z}\left[C_{1}(1)-C_{1}(z)\right]\\
+tyL(1,y,z)+tyR(1)+tyS(1,y,z),\end{multlined}

\displaystyle R(1)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}t\partial_{x=1}C^{\prime}(x,1,1)-tC^{\prime}(1,1,1)+t\partial_{x=1}C(x,1,1)-tC(1,1,1)\\
+t\partial_{x=1}C^{\prime}_{0}(x,1)-tC^{\prime}_{0}(1,1)+t\partial_{x=1}C_{1}(x)+tR(1),\end{multlined}

\displaystyle S(1,y,z)

\displaystyle=tC^{\prime}(1,y,1)+tC(1,y,1)+tS^{\prime}(1,y,1)+tS(1,y,1)+\frac{tyz}{1-y}\left[S(1,1,z)-S(1,y,z)\right].

(6.28)

Using the above we find that:

\displaystyle C(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2}(1-t)}{1-tx}C(1,y,z),

(6.29)

\displaystyle C_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2}(1-t)}{1-tx}C_{1}(1)=\frac{tx^{2}}{1-tx}R(1).

(6.30)

We then get:

\partial_{x=1}C_{1}(x)=\frac{t(2-t)}{(1-t)^{2}}R(1)\,,

(6.31)

\partial_{x=1}C(x,y,z)=\frac{2-t}{1-t}C(1,y,z).

(6.32)

Similar results also apply for
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime}_{0}
.
With these simplifications we no longer need
x
at all, and after eliminating
C_{1}(1)
we get the system:

\displaystyle C(1,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{1-t}L(1,y,z)+\frac{t}{1-t}S(1,y,z),

(6.33)

\displaystyle L(1,y,z)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{tyz}{(1-ty)(1-tz)}C(1,1,z)+\frac{ty}{1-ty}C(1,y,z)+\frac{ty}{(1-t)(1-ty)(1-tz)}R(1)\\
+\frac{ty}{1-ty}S(1,y,z),\end{multlined}

\displaystyle R(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{t(1-t)}{1-3t+t^{2}}C(1,1,1)+\frac{t(1-t)}{1-3t+t^{2}}C^{\prime}_{0}(1,1)+\frac{t(1-t)}{1-3t+t^{2}}C^{\prime}(1,1,1),

(6.36)

\displaystyle S(1,y,z)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{t(1-y)}{1-y+tyz}C(1,y,1)+\frac{t(1-y)}{1-y+tyz}C^{\prime}(1,y,1)+\frac{ty}{1-y+tyz}S(1,1,z)\\
+\frac{t(1-y)}{1-y+tyz}S(1,y,1)+\frac{t(1-y)}{1-y+tyz}S^{\prime}(1,y,1).\end{multlined}

Equations (
6.33
)–(
6

S(1,y,z)
,
S(1,y,1)
and
S(1,1,1)
. The resulting equation looks like:

-\frac{t^{2}(1-y)y(1-3t+t^{2}-ty+2t^{2}y)}{1-5t+6t^{2}-t^{3}}S(1,1,1)=\text{RHS},

(6.39)

where the RHS depends only on
C^{\prime}_{0}(1,1)
,
C^{\prime}(1,1,1)
,

and

(which are all known).

We apply the kernel method twice to (

), first setting

which eliminates the

term, and then with

which eliminates the

term. This gives an algebraic solution to

, which can then be substituted back to give

S(1,1,1)=\frac{t(1-5t+6t^{2}-t^{3})}{2(1-2t)\sqrt{1-4t}}-\frac{t(1-8t+23t^{2}-28t^{3}+14t^{4}-4t^{5}+t^{6})}{2(1-2t)(1-5t+6t^{2}-t^{3})}\,.

(6.40)

We can then set
y=z=1
in (

)–(
6.36
) to get three equations in order to solve

\displaystyle R(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{3}(1-t)}{2(1-2t)\sqrt{1-4t}}-\frac{t^{3}(1-t)}{2(1-2t)}\,,

(6.41)

\displaystyle C_{1}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{4}}{2(1-2t)\sqrt{1-4t}}-\frac{t^{4}}{2(1-2t)}\,.

(6.42)

Then we go back again and set
y=1
to get two equations which give solutions to
C(1,1,z)
and

\displaystyle C(1,1,1)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}(1-3t+t^{2})}{2(1-2t)\sqrt{1-4t}}-\frac{t^{2}(1-6t+12t^{2}-8t^{3}+t^{4}-t^{5})}{2(1-2t)(1-5t+6t^{2}-t^{3})}\,,

(6.43)

\displaystyle L(1,1,1)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}}{2\sqrt{1-4t}}-\frac{t^{2}(1-3t+2t^{2}-t^{3})}{2(1-5t+6t^{2}-t^{3})}\,.

(6.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{tz}{1-z}\partial_{z=1}f(z)-\frac{tz}{(1-z)^{2}}\left[f(1)-f(z)\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{tz}{1-z}\partial_{z=1}f(z)-\frac{tz^{2}}{(1-z)^{2}}\left[f(1)-f(z)\right].

\displaystyle Z_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{t}}{1-t}

\displaystyle=1\pm t^{1/2}+t\pm t^{3/2}+t^{2}\pm t^{5/2}+t^{3}\pm t^{7/2}+t^{4}\pm t^{9/2}+t^{5}\pm t^{11/2}+t^{6}+\dots

6.1
Generating functions of some families of ascending polyominoes

From the generating functions obtained above we can compute generating functions of several families of ascending polyominoes, starting from centered ascending ones.

Theorem 6.1
.

For
n\geq 0
, the number
h(n)
of centered ascending polyominoes is:

h(n)=\frac{3n-5}{n-1}\binom{2n-5}{n-2}\,.

(6.51)

Proof.

Adding up everything except
N
and
N^{\prime}
gives the centered ascending polyominoes:

H(t)=\frac{t(1-t)}{2\sqrt{1-4t}}-\frac{t(1-t)}{2}\\
=t^{2}+2t^{3}+7t^{4}+25t^{5}+91t^{6}+336t^{7}+1254t^{8}+4719t^{9}+17875t^{10}+\dots{}

(6.52)

Hence we have the closed formula. ∎

This is sequence A097613 in the OEIS
[
17
]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

The number of rectangular polyominoes of size
n
is
\binom{2n-4}{n-2}
.

Proof.

Adding up all the rectangular cases gives:

\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{1-4t}}=t^{2}+2t^{3}+6t^{4}+20t^{5}+70t^{6}+252t^{7}+924t^{8}+3432t^{9}+12870t^{10}+\cdots

(6.53)

This has a simple explanation once we observe that rectangular polyominoes are exactly directed-convex polyominoes up to a rotation of
180^{\circ}
counterclockwise. According to
[
3
,
10
,
11
]
, the statement follows. ∎

Adding up everything gives all ascending polyominoes:

Theorem 6.3
.

The generating function of ascending polyominoes is:

A(t)=\frac{t^{2}(2-12t+19t^{2}-4t^{3})}{2(1-4t)^{2}}-\frac{t^{4}(5-8t)}{2(1-2t)(1-4t)^{3/2}}\\
=t^{2}+2t^{3}+7t^{4}+26t^{5}+101t^{6}+404t^{7}+1649t^{8}+6824t^{9}+28498t^{10}+\dots{}

(6.54)

A (not so nice) closed formula for the number
a(n)
can be derived from the generating function. Even without this, we can show that the number of ascending polyominoes grows asymptotically as:

a(n)\sim 4^{n}\,\frac{n}{256}

From the generating function we observe that the dominant asymptotic growth

a(n)\sim K\,n\,4^{n}
is determined by the rational part of the generating function
(the left-hand term), whose double pole at
x=\tfrac{1}{4}
produces the
n4^{n}
factor.
The algebraic part contributes only lower-order terms and, in particular,
yields a negative correction to the leading asymptotics. The same phenomenon occurs for convex polyominoes (see Introduction), where the rational part is responsible for the dominant asymptotic behaviour.

Final Remarks.

Building on known enumerative results, Marc Noy in 2005 (personal communication) pointed out the problem of identifying a class of convex polyominoes lying between the families of
L
-convex and
Z
-convex polyominoes, whose generating function is rational and whose asymptotic growth is
n4^{n}
.
Our results tell us that the family we are seeking must be contained in the set

\mathcal{C}(2,1)\setminus\{\text{4-stacks}\}.

or equivalently, in
\mathcal{C}(1,2)\setminus\{\text{4-stacks}\}.
