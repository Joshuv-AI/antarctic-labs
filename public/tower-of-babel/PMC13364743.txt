Global, Regional, and National Burden of Liver Cancer from 1990 to 2021 and Projections to 2050: A Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Dohwi Yun, Seohyun Hong, Kyeongeun Kim, Sooji Lee, Yesol Yim, Seoyoung Park, Yeona Jo, Seung Ha Hwang, Damiano Pizzol, Junyang Jung, Masoud Rahmati, Selin Woo, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsThis study assessed the burden of liver cancer (LC) and its major risk factors from 1990 to 2021, with projections to 2050.MethodsThe global and regional LC burden from 1990 to 2021 was extracted from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, and stratified by age, sex, Sociodemographic Index (SDI), region, and etiological category. Trends for the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rate (ASDR) were evaluated using estimated annual percentage changes. Attributable risk factors, including alcohol use, drug use, a high body mass index, tobacco use, and high fasting plasma glucose levels, were examined. Projections of the LC burden to 2050 were modeled under baseline and hypothetical risk elimination scenarios.ResultsThe global ASMR of LC remained stable, from 5.86 per 100,000 population (95% uncertainty interval [UI], 5.38 to 6.46) in 1990 to 5.65 (95% UI, 5.13 to 6.30) in 2021. Regions with an initially high ASMR showed increases until the early 2000s, then decreased. The LC burden increased sharply with age and was consistently higher in males, particularly in high-SDI regions. Mortality varied by etiology, with hepatitis B virus as the leading cause, followed by hepatitis C virus and alcohol use. Major risk factors were alcohol and tobacco use among males and drug and alcohol use among females. By 2050, the ASMR and ASDR of LC are projected to reach 6.23 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 4.94 to 8.09) and 158.91 (95% UI, 125.34 to 210.28), respectively, but could decrease to 5.32 (95% UI, 4.21 to 6.95) and 135.33 (95% UI, 106.74 to 179.05) under improved metabolic and behavioral risk scenarios.ConclusionsDespite public health interventions, LC is estimated to maintain a significant global burden.

INTRODUCTION

Liver cancer (LC) is a major global public health concern due to its high mortality rate and limited treatment options.1 In 2021, approximately 529,202 new cases and 483,875 deaths were reported worldwide, making LC the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths.2 The 5-year survival rate for LC remains low globally, ranging from 8.0% to 20.7%.3 This persistently poor survival highlights the limited effectiveness of current diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, especially in resource-limited settings. The World Health Organization (WHO) has implemented the Global Health Sector Strategy to reduce viral hepatitis infections, historically the leading cause of LC, by 2030.4 Additionally, the Sustainable Development Goals have prioritized the reduction of viral hepatitis.5 With the increasing global burden of LC due to non-viral factors such as metabolic and behavioral risks, it has become essential to develop comprehensive, multifaceted strategies that address the shifting epidemiology of LC and its diverse risk factors.6

Despite these global initiatives, recent evidence indicates that the overall LC burden has plateaued, suggesting that existing prevention strategies may be insufficient to address emerging non-viral determinants. Given the multifactorial nature of LC, it is important to conduct a comprehensive assessment of its burden, considering sociodemographic factors such as region, sex, age, and attributable risk factors. By integrating data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021, this study quantifies temporal trends and etiologic contributions to LC mortality and disability, providing updated global projections through 2050 to inform targeted prevention strategies. Through this approach, the study aims to support the development and implementation of effective global and national policies to reduce the burden of LC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study data and case definition

This study analyzed the global, regional, and national burden of LC, utilizing data from the GBD 2021. The GBD 2021 provides a comprehensive assessment of 371 diseases and conditions across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 (Supplementary Table 1). This study is a systematic analysis for the GBD 2021, which was approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board under IRB ID 9060. Cause-specific incidence, prevalence, mortality, years of life lost, years lived with disability, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) were assessed. DALY, a summary measure of health loss, combines years of life lost due to premature mortality and years lived with disability, thereby reflecting both fatal and non-fatal disease burden. Estimates were provided at the country level and further stratified by GBD regions and Sociodemographic Index (SDI) levels. Future projections of the LC burden through 2050 were obtained.7 The GBD 2021 follows the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting.

LC was defined based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. LC was classified under ICD-10 as C22-C22.4 and C22.7-C22.8, and under ICD-9 as 155-155.9 and V10.07.8 Additional detailed information for GBD 2021 is available elsewhere.3 All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.10.4; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

2. Sociodemographic Index

SDI is a composite measure of social and economic factors affecting health outcomes across regions. It is calculated as the geometric mean of three indices, total fertility rate under 25 years of age, average educational attainment, and lagged distributive income per capita.9 Each of these indices has a range of 0 to 1. Based on its value, the SDI is divided into five categories: high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI.

3. Estimating the burden of LC

LC mortality estimates stratified by sex, age, location, and year were produced using the Cause of Death Ensemble Model.1 Data were preprocessed by age-sex splitting and meta-regression-Bayesian, regularized, trimmed model. Crude mortality estimates with additional data from vital registration, verbal autopsy sources, and other relevant covariates were integrated.1 Covariates were selected through a literature review and categorized into three levels based on evidence strength. Level 1 covariates were liters of alcohol consumed per capita, age-standardized prevalence of human immunodeficiency virus, age-standardized seroprevalence of vaccine-adjusted hepatitis B surface antigen, and log transformed summary exposure value (SEV) of LC. Level 2 covariates included hepatitis B 3-dose coverage, hepatitis B vaccine coverage, intravenous drug use, 20 years cumulative cigarettes, mean body mass index (BMI), cigarettes per capita, Healthcare Access Quality index, and diabetes fasting plasma glucose.10 Finally, for level 3, education, SEV for high red meat, lagged distributive income, and SDI was included. The Cause of Death Ensemble Model strategy applied four model classes, including linear mixed effects regression and spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression models. Mortality-to-incidence ratios (MIRs) were derived from registries containing both incidence and mortality data to enhance data availability in the disease-model-Bayesian meta-regression 2.1 modeling framework. LC incidence was then directly estimated from LC mortality estimates using MIRs. Initially, incidence and mortality data were processed and aligned by cancer type, age, sex, year, and location to compute crude MIRs. MIRs were refined using spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression, incorporating the Healthcare Access Quality index, age, and sex as covariates. Subsequently, final mortality estimates were divided by the modeled MIR estimates. The 95% uncertainty interval (UI) was generated with 1,000 draws with replacement. Uncertainty in the MIR was assumed to be independent of uncertainty in the estimated mortality.

To disaggregate the total number of LC cases occurring at ages 10 or more into five etiologic categories (hepatitis B virus [HBV], hepatitis C virus [HCV], alcohol, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis [NASH], and other causes), a systematic search was performed by GBD 2021 with results limited to human studies. Within each study, the proportions of cases attributable to the five etiologic categories were calculated. Cases identified as NASH or nonalcoholic fatty liver disease were classified under the NASH category.7

5. Risk factors

This study examined the burden of LC attributable to high alcohol use, drug use, high BMI, tobacco, and high fasting plasma glucose, all classified as level 2 risk factors in the GBD. The GBD 2021 employed a comparative risk assessment framework and risk-outcome pairs were selected based on robust and persuasive evidence supporting causal relationships.9 Prevalence of exposure and theoretical minimum risk exposure level were assessed by extensive literature analysis. The risk-deleted LC mortality rate is calculated by subtracting the population attributable fraction for a specific risk factor from the observed LC mortality rate. Through this, the contribution of each risk factor and the number of deaths that could be prevented if exposure to these factors were minimized were estimated. Additionally, high BMI for adults (aged ≥20 years) was defined as BMI greater than 20–23 kg/m2. High BMI for children and adolescents (aged 2–19 years) was defined as being overweight or obese based on International Obesity Task Force standards.13

6. Projections to 2050

LC mortality and DALYs were forecasted up to 2050 by integrating independent drivers of health, sociodemographic determinants, and key interventions.14 SDI, vaccine coverages, and anti-retroviral therapy were included. The forecasting framework incorporated historical trends, recency-weighted changes, and external shocks. Future risk factor trajectories were modeled using SEV to assess population attributable fractions and cause-specific mortality scalars. A generalized ensemble modeling approach was utilized, incorporating 12 sub-models based on two primary methodologies: annualized rate of change-based models and a two-stage spline model employing a meta-regression-Bayesian, regularized, trimmed model. An ensemble modeling approach was adopted for mediation in mediator SEV computation to prevent overestimation of risk contributions. Sub-model weights were determined through out-of-sample predictive validity experiments, training models on 1990–2009 data, and validating on 2010–2019 data using root mean squared error. Final forecasts up to 2050 were generated using 500 draws per model.

Cause-specific mortality estimates were obtained using a log-linear model, modeled as a function of the SDI, temporal factors, other cause-specific covariates, and the risk factor scalars. DALYs and mortality forecasts were generated under the reference scenario and an alternative scenario. The reference scenario reflects expected trajectories based on historical and current risk factor trends. The alternative scenario, termed as the improved behavioral and metabolic risks scenario, assumes the complete elimination of smoking, dietary risks, and high BMI in adults by 2050, with a linear decline to 0 starting in 2023.14 In addition, no new smoker initiation is assumed after 2022. While this improved scenario is intended to illustrate the potential upper bound of achievable health gains, its assumptions are optimistic and unlikely to be fully realized in practice. Accordingly, this scenario should be interpreted as a hypothetical maximum impact estimate rather than as a realistic forecast.

RESULTS

1. Global burden of LC

The total number of global mortality and incidence of LC increased from 238,970 (95% UI, 218,720 to 263,040) to 483,880 (440,400 to 540,180) and from 244,700 (224,800 to 268,500) to 529,200 (480,340 to 593,850) between 1990 and 2021 (Supplementary Table 2), respectively. Globally, the ASMR increased before the 2000s (APC, 1990–2000, 1.10%; 95% UI, 0.95 to 1.25), followed by a decline until 2021 (APC, 2000–2010, –0.83%; 95% UI, –1.19 to –0.47; 2010–2021, –0.55%; 95% UI, –0.76 to –0.34) (Supplementary Tables 3-5). A similar trend was observed for ASIR (APC, 1990–2000, 1.33%; 95% UI, 1.22 to 1.44; 2000–2010, –0.43%; 95% UI, –0.73 to –0.12; 2010–2021, –0.54%; 95% UI, –0.75 to –0.32).

Fig. 1 shows the global distribution of age-standardized incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALY rate in 2021. Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania showed high burdens of LC compared to other regions (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). In Sub-Saharan Africa, the ASMR in 2021 was 7.51 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 6.29 to 9.13), and the ASDR was 198.43 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 163.14 to 246.47). Among these regions, Western Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest ASMR and ASDR. In Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania, the ASMR was 7.90 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 6.64 to 9.53) and the ASDR was 221.86 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 185.01 to 271.74). East Asia had notably high burdens (ASMR, 8.36 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 6.85 to 10.22; ASDR, 239.81 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 193.34 to 296.83). High-income regions had values similar to the global ASMR and ASDR (ASMR, 5.38 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 4.93 to 5.68; ASDR, 124.02 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 117.05 to 130.38), while the Asia Pacific regions exhibited remarkably higher rates (ASMR, 9.19 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 8.11 to 10.20; ASDR, 205.85 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 185.25 to 229.31).

2. Variations of LC burden by region, and SDI

Globally, the ASMR of LC was highest in 2000 (6.48 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 6.08 to 6.98) and declined by 2021 (5.65 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 5.13 to 6.30) (Fig. 2). ASMR was consistently higher in males than in females, with male rates ranging from 8.10 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 7.24 to 9.37) to 9.48 (95% UI, 8.81 to 10.23), compared to female rates ranging from 3.46 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 3.11 to 3.80) to 3.77 (95% UI, 3.46 to 4.18). Additionally, the trend for females remained stable across all regions throughout the study period.

Analyzing ASMR by SDI, in males, trends and numerical ranges for high SDI, high-middle SDI, and middle SDI regions were relatively consistent with the global pattern. These regions showed an increase from 1990 to 2000 (APC: high SDI, 2.56%; 95% UI, 1.99 to 3.13; high-middle, 1.25%; 95% UI, 1.04 to 1.46) and decreased from 2000 to 2010 (high SDI, –0.62%; 95% UI, –0.70 to –0.53; high-middle, –1.36%; 95% UI, –1.96 to –0.75; middle, –1.09%; 95% UI, –1.73 to –0.46) and from 2000 to 2021 (high SDI, –1.08%; 95% UI, –1.26 to –0.89; high-middle, –0.77%; 95% UI, –1.05 to –0.50). Low-middle SDI region remained stable with a slightly upward trend throughout the entire period. In contrast, Low SDI region showed a significant decreasing trend. APC of low SDI region was –1.48% (95% UI, –1.63 to –1.32) from 2000 to 2010, and –0.93% (95% UI, –1.05 to –0.80) from 2010 to 2021.

In contrast to the ASMR trend, which generally followed a similar global trend except in the low-middle SDI regions, the ASIR showed distinct patterns in certain regions, diverging from the global trend (Supplementary Figs 1 and 2). While other regions showed an increase before 2000s followed by a decline, ASIR in high SDI region tended to remain relatively high throughout the observed period. APC for ASIR in high SDI was 2.76% (95% UI, 2.22 to 3.29) from 1990 to 2000, 0.10% (95% UI, 0.03 to 0.16) from 2000 to 2010, and –1.26% (95% UI, –1.47 to –1.06) from 2010 to 2021.

3. Subtype of LC and its regional and sex disparity

Globally, the ASMR of LC was higher in males than in females (Fig. 3). HBV constituted the largest etiologic share (ASMR: male, 3.54 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 2.87 to 4.41; female, 0.76; 95% UI, 0.61 to 0.94), followed by HCV (male, 1.99; 95% UI, 1.69 to 2.33; female, 1.54; 95% UI, 1.30 to 1.76) and alcohol use (male, 1.77; 95% UI, 1.45 to 2.13; female, 0.45; 95% UI, 0.36 to 0.56); NASH (male, 0.52; 95% UI, 0.41 to 0.64; female, 0.44; 95% UI, 0.36 to 0.54) and “other causes” contributed smaller proportions, and hepatoblastoma was negligible (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). The highest ASMRs were observed in Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania region, driven predominantly by HBV (male, 7.32 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 5.75 to 9.52; female, 1.30; 95% UI, 1.01 to 1.67) and accompanied by a pronounced male-female gap. Sub-Saharan Africa shows the next-highest burden with a mixed but HBV-leaning profile (male, 3.88 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 2.99 to 5.15; female, 1.46; 95% UI, 1.05 to 1.96) and a clear male excess. North Africa and Middle East display an HCV-dominant pattern (3.14 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 2.33 to 3.93; 1.81 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 1.41 to 2.24). In Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia regions and in high-income regions, alcohol (male, 2.11 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 1.79 to 2.43; female, 0.45; 95% UI, 0.36 to 0.56) and HCV (male, 1.09; 95% UI, 0.86 to 2.33; female, 0.92; 95% UI, 0.77 to 1.07) accounted for substantial fractions, while HBV (male, 1.21; 95% UI, 0.92 to 1.54; female, 0.34; 95% UI, 0.26 to 0.44) contributed comparatively little. NASH was proportionally most prominent in Sub-Saharan Africa. South Asia, Latin America, and Caribbean had lower overall rates with smaller, mixed etiologic contributions.

4. Age and sex-specific differences in LC burden

Fig. 4 shows the mortality and incidence rates of LC by sex, age, and SDI levels. In males, the highest incidence rate was observed in the 85–89 age group (86.33 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 74.84 to 96.74), and the highest mortality rate was observed in the 90–94 age group (95.70; 95% UI, 80.52 to 107.70). In females, the 85–89 age group had the highest incidence rate of 46.23 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 34.50 to 53.74), and those aged ≥95 years had the highest mortality rate of 58.95 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 40.16 to 69.81). The mortality rate exceeded the incidence rate in those aged 90 and older, whereas the rates were similar in both sexes under 90 years of age. Additionally, males consistently exhibited higher mortality and incidence rates across almost all age groups compared to females.

Both mortality and incidence rates generally increased with age, highlighting the increased disease burden in older populations. This trend was particularly evident after the age of 50, where mortality rates began to show a greater divergence across SDI levels (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). In high SDI regions, the mortality rates (90–94 years: 123.56 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 103.2 to 135.5; ≥95 years: 97.49 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 74.51 to 110.86) were notably higher than the incidence rates (90–94 years: 93.77 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 78.09 to 103.4; ≥95 years: 63.72 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 48.74 to 72.49) for males in the 90 and older age group. In contrast, low-middle and low SDI regions showed significantly lower incidence and mortality rates compared to other regions, with minimal differences observed between sexes.

5. Burden of LC attributable to risk factors

Globally, high alcohol use (1.08 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.89 to 1.30) was the leading attributable risk factor for ASMR due to LC in 2021, followed by drug use (0.76 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.58 to 0.97), tobacco use, high BMI, and high fasting plasma glucose (Fig. 5). Compared to 1990, when drug use was the third-leading risk factor (0.41 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.29 to 0.57), its burden increased significantly by 2021. Regionally, drug use emerged as the leading risk factor in Oceania (0.96 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.64 to 1.32), Central Asia (1.80 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 1.12 to 2.64), high-income North America (1.44 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 1.21 to 1.67), and high-income Asia Pacific (1.30 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.89 to 1.79) in 2021 (Supplementary Tables 12-14). In contrast, drug use remained the lowest-ranked risk factor in Sub-Saharan Africa. Tobacco was the leading risk factor in East Asia, and high BMI was the leading risk factor in North Africa and Middle East.

Globally, LC in males was primarily attributable to high alcohol and tobacco use in both 1990 (ASMR: 1.65 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 1.36 to 2.00; 1.49 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.52 to 2.39) and 2021 (1.80 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 1.49 to 2.18; 1.17 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 0.41 to 1.96). The burden of these risk factors remained relatively stable over this period. However, in females, drug use became the leading risk factor, with the ASMR of 0.47 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 0.33 to 0.64), followed by high alcohol use with 0.45 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 0.36 to 0.57) in 2021. Although both alcohol and drug use have consistently been the predominant risk factors, it is notable that the order of their contribution has reversed. In contrast to males, drug and alcohol use have approximately equal influence in females, and drug use emerging as the most significant contributor to LC burden in females, surpassing alcohol use (Supplementary Figs 3 and 4).

6. Projections to 2050

Fig. 6 presents the projected ASMR for LC through 2050 under two scenarios: the reference scenario and the improved metabolic and behavioral risks scenario. Under the reference scenario, the ASMR and ASDR were projected to increase to 6.23 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 4.94 to 8.09) and 158.91 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 125.34 to 210.28) by 2050, respectively (Supplementary Tables 15 and 16). In contrast, under the improved scenario, significant reductions are projected, with the ASMR and ASDR expected to decrease to 5.32 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 4.21 to 6.95) and 135.33 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 106.74 to 179.05), respectively.

In 2022, the projected ASMR for males and females under the reference scenario was 8.14 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 7.23 to 9.49) and 3.48 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 3.12 to 3.83), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). By 2050, the ASMR for males is projected to increase to 9.01 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 6.99 to 12.12) if the current trend continues. However, under the improved behavioral and metabolic risks scenario, the ASMR for males may decrease to 7.53 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 5.84 to 10.18). Among females, the ASMR is projected to be 3.73 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 2.95 to 4.72) under the reference and 3.24 (2.56 to 4.10) under the improved scenario. By 2050, the ASDRs would decrease under an improved scenario (male, 194.83 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 150.99 to 265.71; female, 77.29 per 100,000 population; 95% UI, 62.04 to 97.55), whereas the rates would increase in the reference scenario.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed the global, regional, and national burden of LC from 1990 to 2021, with a focus on sex, age, attributable risk factors, and projected future trends. Between 1990 and 2021, the global number of incident cases and deaths due to LC more than doubled. Although the global ASMR and ASIR remained stable during this period, regions with a higher burden exhibited an increase until the early 2000s, then declined thereafter. Significant differences in the burden of LC were observed across sex, geographic regions, and SDI levels. The burden was consistently higher in males than in females, with marked regional etiologic heterogeneity. Globally, high alcohol use was the leading attributable risk factor for LC. While drug use ranked third in 1990, it became the second leading risk factor by 2021, contributing significantly to Central Asia and high-income regions. Males had a higher attributable burden from alcohol and tobacco use, while drug and alcohol use were the leading risks among females. By 2050, the ASMR for males is projected to increase under the reference scenario and to decrease significantly if behavioral and metabolic risks are eliminated. For females, although there was a comparable trend with males, its degree was less pronounced. Improvements in metabolic and behavioral factors among males will be essential to reduce the burden of LC. These findings underscore the importance of integrated approaches addressing preventable risk factors to mitigate the future LC burden.

One of the major causes of LC is chronic infection with viral hepatitis. Sub-Saharan Africa is an endemic region for viral hepatitis, with 5%–10% of adults being chronically infected, which contributes to the high burden of LC. Through efforts by the WHO and the International Agency for Research on Cancer, viral hepatitis vaccination programs have been introduced in Sub-Saharan Africa since the late 1990s. These initiatives significantly reduced HBV in vaccinated cohorts, but regional LC incidence declines remain limited due to low birth-dose coverage and long latency.15

The greater regional disparity in ASMR of LC among males than females likely reflects interactions between uneven exposures and sex-biased biology.16 Major hepatocarcinogenic exposures—tobacco and alcohol—show higher prevalence and greater geographic heterogeneity in males.17 Chronic viral hepatitis also contributes to sex-differences in risk. HBV is often more prevalent and progresses more severely in males, potentially increasing male mortality.18 HBV and HCV drive LC through chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis, with HBV additionally exerting direct oncogenic effects via viral integration and HBV-encoded X protein-related pathways.19,20 HBV predominates in East and Southeast Asia due to historically high endemicity, early-life acquisition, whereas the substantial HCV burden in North Africa and Middle East reflects legacy iatrogenic transmission and long disease latency.21,22 In high-income regions and Central and Eastern Europe, heavy alcohol use—often interacting with HCV and smoking—accelerates steatohepatitis, cirrhosis, and malignant transformation.23

The mortality rate of LC frequently exceeds the incidence rate among older age groups, regardless of SDI level.24 The overall 5-year survival rate for LC remains low, at approximately 17%, but increases to 31% for localized disease, and drops to 3% for distant metastasis.6 These figures highlight the importance of early detection and timely intervention. Young and middle-aged adults generally experience better treatment outcomes and longer survival, whereas adults aged ≥60 years more often present at advanced stages with limited therapeutic options and shorter survival.25

The spatial concordance between mortality and DALY rates in 2021 indicates that, in high-burden, low SDI regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa and East Asia, limited access to early diagnosis and curative treatment leads to premature death; DALYs are therefore primarily driven by years of life lost. In contrast, high SDI regions have broader diagnostic coverage and more effective therapies, leading to longer survival and a greater contribution of years lived with disability, reflecting a growing survivorship burden.26-28 Etiologic patterns also vary by development level. While HBV and HCV remain predominant globally, high SDI regions exhibit growing contributions from metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and alcohol alongside residual viral hepatitis.29 Unlike hepatitis-related LC, MASLD-associated LC is often detected late, limiting treatment options and worsening prognosis.30

Mortality of LC due to drug use among females is substantial in high-income regions. Drug use includes illicit drugs and non-medical use of prescription medications. Females often show different patterns of use and treatment barriers.31 Although the overall substance-use burden remains male-predominant, the sex gap narrows for non-medical use, reflecting higher female exposure to prescription opioids and sedatives.32 Females may transition to dependence more rapidly and experience heightened vulnerability, including initiation related to internalizing motives, intimate partner violence, or role-related pressures.33 Despite comparable or better treatment outcomes, access to care is often lower due to stigma, childcare needs, service mismatches, and fear of legal or custodial consequences. While females comprise only about one quarter of people who inject drugs, they experience disproportionately greater harms, including about 1.2-fold higher human immunodeficiency virus prevalence than that in males, compounded by unsafe injecting and sexual practices.

Although a specific strategy targeting LC has not yet been established, the WHO has implemented a Global Health Sector Strategy aimed at reducing the incidence of viral hepatitis infections by 90% and deaths by 65% between 2016 and 2030. However, progress toward elimination has been slow, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.34 Challenges include under-resourced health systems, low vaccine coverage, limited access to antiviral treatments, and widespread stigma surrounding hepatitis. Addressing these challenges requires strengthened health infrastructures, increasing public awareness, and expanding access to preventive and therapeutic interventions.

Previously, the Sustainable Development Goals focused primarily on reducing viral hepatitis, reflecting its historically dominant etiology.35 As non-viral factors such as alcohol-related liver disease and MASLD increase, our findings suggest etiology-aligned, region-specific packages that specify (1) risk stratification, (2) surveillance delivery, and (3) referral to definitive care.36 In HBV-hyperendemic regions, such as Southeast and East Asia, governments should ensure timely newborn vaccination, maintain high infant coverage, and expand antiviral access for eligible chronic HBV patients, with routine monitoring of mother-to-child transmission and virologic suppression. In parallel, patients with cirrhosis or advanced fibrosis should receive semiannual hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance by ultrasound, with optional alpha-fetoprotein testing. Abnormal results should trigger multiphasic computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging followed by multidisciplinary evaluation to enable potentially curative therapy at an early stage.37 Where HCV remains substantial, simplified primary-care test-and-treat should incorporate reflex confirmatory testing and rapid initiation of direct-acting antivirals, prioritizing high-risk groups. In alcohol-driven settings, notably in high SDIs, routine clinical screening and brief intervention for alcohol use disorder should be integrated with fibrosis assessment and surveillance eligibility determination, alongside population-level alcohol control measures such as marketing restrictions. To control the LC due to MASLD, care pathways should combine cardiometabolic risk management with stepwise noninvasive fibrosis assessment (e.g., fibrosis-4 index followed by transient elastography where available) to identify patients requiring closer follow-up and surveillance referral, complemented by fiscal measures targeting sugar-sweetened beverages and ultra-processed foods.38

Several previous studies have examined the global burden of LC, with most focusing on the direct etiological factors such as viral hepatitis and alcohol use.39,40 However, these studies were often limited in geographic scope, concentrating primarily on specific countries, such as China or the United States, or were restricted to specific age groups.8 While some studies have analyzed risk factors associated with LC, they have primarily focused on smoking and alcohol use, with comparatively less attention given to other contributors. As a result, the growing impact of drug use as a major risk factor in recent years has often been underestimated or insufficiently addressed.41 Furthermore, although several studies have addressed future projections of LC based on current trends, they have not evaluated the potential impact of eliminating risk factors. These limit our understanding of how targeted prevention strategies could influence the future burden of LC. To address these limitations, this study provides a comprehensive examination of the burden of LC across regions, sex, and age groups. This analysis revealed that, in addition to the traditionally male-dominant risk factors such as smoking and alcohol use, drug use has become a significant contributor to LC, particularly among females.

This study comprehensively analyzed the recent trends of LC burden. While previous studies have focused on males due to their higher burden, this study includes both sexes and highlights the significant contribution of drug use among females. However, this study has several limitations. First, this study shares inherent limitations with GBD 2021, which relies on the accuracy and completeness of national health registry data. In regions with limited data, estimates are based on statistical modeling and historical extrapolation, which can introduce potential uncertainty. Furthermore, in lower SDI regions, underdiagnosis is common due to limited access to healthcare services, which may lead to the underestimation of incident case counts. Second, the data do not provide pathology-based LC subtypes such as hepatocellular carcinoma; therefore, we could not assess subtype-specific burden and instead relied on cause-specific LC by etiologies, which may mask heterogeneity across histological subtypes. Third, the “improved behavioral and metabolic risks” scenario is an idealized upper bound and may therefore overestimate achievable gains. Still, it quantifies the maximum avoidable burden from modifiable risk factors and may provide a transparent benchmark.

In conclusion, effective LC prevention and control require strategies tailored to sociodemographic contexts, emphasizing reductions in metabolic and behavioral risk and guided by etiology-specific and SDI-based approaches.
