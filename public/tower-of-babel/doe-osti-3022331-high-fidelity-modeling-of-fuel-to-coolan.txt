1 Introduction
 This report summarizes the work completed under NEUP project number 21-24006. The objectives of
this project are to advance the high-fidelity modeling capabilities and important phenomena that is
important for high-burnup UO2 and accident tolerant fuels (ATF) during transient conditions. Accurate
modeling of the time-dependent phenomena that impact material performance must be used to determine
the figures of merit and safety margin. Phenomena such as fuel fragmentation, cladding oxidation, pellet-
clad interaction, clad ballooning, and clad rupture are examples that pose challenges to modeling during
these transients. This project focused on the development of high-fidelity tightly coupled multiphysics
models that can capture the time-dependent material response and associated thermal hydraulic conditions
during these events. These models can then be validated against existing separate effects tests and in-pile
integral experiments and will be used to model Transient Reactor Test facility (TREAT) loss-of-coolant
accidents (LOCA) experiments.
 To achieve the project objective, we used a combination of NEAMS and NRC codes to model various
LOCA test sets for the separate effects and in-pile integral experiments. BlueCRAB tool set, which can
accurately predict material response at a sub-fuel pin level, as well as modeling the entire reactor system
response to these events. Fuel performance was modeled using BISON (various versions) and FAST
(version 1.2.1). BISON and FAST can model on a sub-fuel pin level the fuel performance under transient
conditions. Both have simplified thermal hydraulic models that are capable of providing basic coolant
boundary conditions. To better capture the thermomechanical interaction between the fuel, clad, and
coolant, more sophisticated thermal hydraulic models are necessary.
 TRACE is the NRC’s system analysis code that has been validated against years of Light Water Reactor
(LWR) operations and experimental data. It was designed to model LWR transient analysis of design basis
accidents including LOCA. It is capable of accurately predicting the flow evolution in where phase change
is expected. TRACE (version 5 Patch6) was used in this project. This choice was dictated by the version
compatible with the NEAMS code BlueCRAB.
 BlueCRAB couples external codes (such as SCALE, PARCS, TRACE, and FAST) to the NEAMS
codes (such as MAMMOTH, PRONGHORN, Nek5000, BISON, and SAM) using the MOOSE framework
[1]. Using coupled code systems is important when trying to capture the relevant physics of a problem. To
accurately model the fuel-to-coolant thermomechanical transport under transient conditions, it is necessary
to know not only the fuel performance behavior but also the thermal hydraulic response to the transient
condition.
 For this project we used BlueCRAB to couple two sets of codes, first to couple BISON to TRACE and
second to couple FAST to TRACE. While the BISON/TRACE coupling has had some level of testing the
FAST/TRACE coupling has had very little. Adding capabilities and verification of the coupled calculations
is essential for future high-fidelity calculations using external codes coupled to NEAMS codes.
 This report is broken up into 7 sections covering the BISON cladding models, high burnup UO2 and
ATF LOCA database development, TWIST LOCA experiment, modeling and simulation, summary, and
recommendations for future work.

creep, irradiation growth, plasticity, phase transition, oxidation, and failure criteria. Additionally, an

2.1 LWR Cladding Material and Behavior Models for Standard Operations
 During long-term steady-state operations, cladding is exposed to irradiation damage, heating, and
pressure, which leads to deformation. Fuel swelling also diminishes the pellet-clad gap, resulting in
mechanical contact with the cladding and an evolving heat conductance. Total heat transfer is determined
using a modified Ross-Stoute approach combining conductance due to mechanical contact, fill gas
conductance, and radiant heat conductance. Zircaloy cladding is also susceptible to thermal and irradiation
creep, which are modeled using a modified Matsuo implementation and Hoppe’s model, respectively, that
are parameterized according to data from stress relieved annealed (SRA) and recrystallization annealed
(RXA) Zircaloy. BISON also provides the option to implement the ESCORE or Franklin model for axial
elongation due to irradiation growth and axial strain with respect to Zircaloy alloy.
2.1.1 Thermal and Irradiation Creep
 BISON has two models available for creep under normal operations. For primary and secondary
thermal creep, the approach provided by Limback and Andersson is used. The model is a modified Matsuo
implementation, which was derived from experimental data from creep tests analyzed using Dorn’s quasi
theoretical model [3]. The creep tests were conducted on Zry-4 cladding samples subjected to varying
temperatures, hoop strains, internal pressures, and test durations. The creep data were analyzed according
to transient and steady-state conditions, which are treated as primary and secondary creep rates,
respectively. The models are dependent on stress, temperature, and time.

2.2 LWR Cladding Material and Behavior Models for Accident Conditions
 During accident conditions, such as a LOCA, coolant temperature and pressures change drastically,
comprising a transient within the reactor. Disruptions in normal cooling result in high temperatures and
internal rod pressures, which prompt structural evolution and dynamic thermomechanical behavior of the
fuel and cladding, compromising cladding integrity and demanding adequate predictive capabilities. Rod
ballooning and burst have the potential to expel fragmented fuel from the rod. Current model options for
rod burst opening size include Jernkvist’s and ORNL’s. The threshold for cladding burst can be determined
by several different limiting criteria, including using a model from Erbacher establishing a threshold burst
stress dependent upon phases present in the cladding. High temperature creep is simulated using a Norton
power law fitted with experimentally determined parameters and is also influenced by phase. High
temperature oxidation has been approximated using many different correlations specified for different
temperature ranges. Phase evolution of Zircaloy is predicted using Massih’s model for crystallographic
phase transition, which is a function of time and temperature. Finally, dynamic plasticity within the cladding
is modeled according to Hooke’s law and a standard power law.
2.2.1 High Temperature Zr Oxidation for Coolant Accident Conditions
 BISON employs several correlations to capture oxidation under accident conditions with temperatures
up to 2600 K. Consensus among researchers has found that Leistikow correlations and the Cathcart-Pawel
correlations are valued equally for reliability and are often suggested to be used in combination for
temperatures up to 1800 K. For temperatures exceeding 1900 K, the Prater-Courtright correlations are the
industry standard. For temperatures between 1800-1900K, an interpolation method has been used to derive
an additional formulation from the Leistikow/Cathcart-Pawel and Prater-Courtright correlations. The

2.3 FeCrAl Properties
 FeCrAl alloys have garnered recent interest for use as accident tolerant fuel cladding in LWRs. The
alloys have demonstrated improved resistance to many lifetime-limiting phenomena occurring in Zircaloy.
Clad corrosion, oxidation, and swelling are less prominent in FeCrAl cladding under steady-state and
transient conditions in comparison to Zircaloy; however, cladding embrittlement through tritium
permeation poses greater risk. The unique thermomechanical behavior of FeCrAl alloys require novel
models to accurately simulate clad performance throughout normal and accident conditions, advancing
commercial utilization of accident tolerant cladding. BISON incorporates models derived from
experimental data and material property databases for several FeCrAl alloy varieties: Special Metals
MA956, Plansee PM2000, Kanthal APMT, C06M, C35M, C36M, C26M, and Resistalloy International
Fecralloy. Failure criteria for normal and accident conditions have been determined by Gamble et al for a
conservative approach to predicting clad failure. BISON models for thermal and irradiation creep, and
oxidation vary according to the alloy type. Additionally, a growing database of material properties,
including thermal conductivity, Poison’s ratio, specific heat capacity, the coefficient of thermal expansion,
and plastic strain are included in BISON according to the alloy type. Finally, tritium permeability is
predicted using a Crank-Nicolson scheme derived from experimental data on hydrogen permeability.

2.4 BISON Model Summary
 BISON is a key tool in nuclear fuel performance modeling for simulating many fuel types and
geometries under a broad range of conditions. Extreme environment and conditions cladding is subjected
to during reactor operation induce complex fuel and cladding behavior, complicating modeling efforts.
Overall, many models are utilized in BISON for accurate prediction of Zircaloy and FeCrAl cladding
behavior and properties during both normal and accident conditions. Most formulations are empirical and
were derived from existing experimental results and fitted according to the data. However, good agreement
is shown for implementation within a given set of conditions, and interpolations can be sufficient where
conditions are applicable in predicting the behavior outside the bounds of tested parameters. These
predictive capabilities facilitate efforts in advancing LWR fuel-cladding concepts for improved utilization
and safety.

4 TWIST LOCA
 The Transient Water Irradiation System for TREAT (TWIST) is a static-water blowdown capsule
designed to perform LOCA tests on fresh and high burnup fuel rodlets. The TWIST capsule consists of the
test capsule where the test fuel rodlet is placed, the blowdown valve, and expansion tank. The TWIST
capsule is sealed in the outer containment called BUSTER, Figure 10. The capsule is specifically designed
to enable the relevant heat-transfer boundary conditions during LOCA events to study fuel and fuel
fragmentation, relocation, and dispersal (FFRD) behavior during such events. The test capsule is designed
for fuel lengths up to 50 cm, pressures up to 15 MPa, and temperatures up to 3100 K. The working fluids
are Gas, Steam and Water. The capsule is designed to measure, cladding temperature, fuel centerline
temperature, rod ballooning, fission gas release, rod plenum pressure, rod plenum temperature, time of
cladding rupture, fuel motion/relocation capsule internal pressure, and capsule internal temperature [45].

5.1 TRACE TWIST Model
 The TRACE TWIST model developed for this project was based on the RELAP5 model and modified
to mimic the behavior of the planned LOCA tests outline in [46], [47]. The model development included
both the long rod (50 cm) and short rod (25 cm) fresh fuel tests.
 The TRACE long (50 cm) and short (25 cm) rod models are similar in construction and operation
varying only in fuel sample geometry and power history. Therefore, we will provide an overview of the
general model characteristics and operation in this section. We will also provide results of the standalone
models for both the long and short rods. In addition, a study was conducted on the impact of the gas-gap
conductance model to help determine the best modeling approach.
 The TWIST TRACE models include 28 thermal hydraulic components that represent the TWIST
capsule, blowdown valve, expansion tank, and BUSTER containment. There are 6 heat structures that
model heat conduction in the fuel specimen, TWIST capsule wall, and expansion tank, Figure 12. Radiation
heat transfer is modeled between concentric heat structures using appropriate view factors. Four custom
materials are included to model Helium, ZrO2, UO2, and Zr-2.5Nb. The power to the fuel specimen is
provided by a control system driven power component.
 The TWIST capsule test section is represented by TRACE pipe components 100, 110, 111, and 112.
Components 100 and 110 represent the inner capsule that houses the fuel specimen. It is separated from the
outer capsule annulus by a thin wall made of ZrO2. Component 111 represents the outer capsule annulus.

5.3 FAST Fuel Modeling
 The FAST fuel modeling consisted of the development of 4 new standalone models (Halden 4, Halden
9, Studsvik 191, LOC-C-5) intended for use with testing the FAST modeling capabilities and develop
experience with modeling fuel for the TWIST test calculations. Additional Studsvik models were developed
but not discussed here due to code errors encountered with those models as discussed in Section 5.3.3. The
following sections document the FAST model Development and present the results. All models discussed
in this section are provided in the final deliverables folder Models/FAST/.
5.3.1 Halden 4 Model and Results
 The Halden IFA-650.4 test was a single pin LOCA test conducted on pre-irradiated fuel provided by
Framatome ANP, irradiated in a commercial PWR to a peak burnup of 92.3 GWd/MTU[51], [52]. The
experiment aimed to study cladding behavior of rods exceeding current commercial burnup limits under
LOCA conditions, achieving a targeted peak cladding temperature of 800 °C. Notably, this test displayed
cladding ballooning, rupture, and fuel dispersal. When compared to historical LOCA testing on lower

 56

burnup fuel, this test indicated that higher burnups and well-developed rim-structure formation may
exacerbate fuel fragmentation and dispersal during a LOCA transient.
 An as-run model for the test was generated starting from a general LOCA example provided in the
FAST-training materials, adjusting the geometry, steady state irradiation condition, and transient
temperature data that has been collected and digitized from available reports and publications[44], [53].
Reported data for Halden 650.4 was among the most abundant available; thus, it was a logical starting point
for establishing a useful model for BISON comparison and model evaluation. Adjustments were made to
power history steps, coolant, and transient controls before running the model.
 The Halden 4 model now completes with detected rod deformation. Initially, simulated values for
internal rod pressure and cladding deformation differed significantly from actual measurements, prompting
an investigation into the fill gas modeling. The original simulation didn't include refabrication of the fuel
rod into the test rodlet, so it was updated for accurate conditions. Further modifications included updating
linear axial power distribution and time-dependent coolant pressures based on reactor analysis code data.
 Results from the Halden 4 model are plotted in Figure 31through Figure 34. The outer cladding surface
temperature was provided as a boundary condition for the test using the measured temperature response,
and the expected agreement in seen in Figure 31. Figure 32 compares the modeled cladding diameter at the
end of the test with measurements from post-transient examination. The model indicated cladding
deformation, with maximum ballooning occurring at approximately the same axial position as the measured
maximum ballooning. However, the modeled rodlet diameter is significantly lower than the measured
diameter at other axial positions. This discrepancy is due to FAST’s cladding diameter model, which
imposes a threshold value for ballooning, renodalizes once this threshold is met, and confines the ballooning
to a single node. Consequently, the model does not accurately represent the balloon shape and rodlet
diametral strain along the axial length.
 The timing of rodlet burst can be examined using the predicted and measured rodlet pressure, which
are plotted in Figure 33. The FAST model for Halden 4 predicted premature failure in comparison to
reported values, which is denoted by the drop in pressure predicted to occur before 200 seconds compared
to the measured value beyond 300s seconds. The modeled results show that while predicted plenum
temperature (Figure 34) and pressure matched during steady state, they increased sharply and contributed
to early failure, following the temperature boundary condition shape after rodlet failure.

5.3.2 Halden 9 Model and Results
 The Halden IFA-650.9 test also utilized high burnup fuel (90 GWd/MTU) with developed rim structure;
however, this test differs from 650.4 by utilizing low fission power and a targeted peak cladding temperature

conditions[52]. The rupture led to significant fuel relocation, with fuel from the upper part of the stack
dropping into a ballooned region. Notably, post-test examinations also demonstrated that fuel fragmentation
and relocation are extensive and not limited to the rim region[52].
 The model for Halden IFA-650.9 was also developed using data from the digitized database for input
and results comparison and, when run, returned cladding ballooning during the transient[44]. The reported
data for cladding diameter showed two balloons, one in the middle of the rod and another toward the bottom.
However, the model predicted significant ballooning at a single axial location between these two reported
balloons, as shown in Figure 35. This discrepancy can be attributed to FAST’s renodalization and freezing
mechanisms, which limit the axial spread of ballooning and result in increased volume at a singular node,
causing much larger predicted outer cladding deformation.
 Similar to the Halden 4 model, the Halden IFA-650.9 model showed increased plenum pressure and
early predicted failure, Figure 36. However, in this test case, the pressure drop observed followed more
closely with the measured pressure change, unlike the Halden 4 model which showed a re-pressurization.
Adjustments were made to the cladding-to-coolant coefficient of heat transfer, as referenced in FAST’s
integral assessment document, but these changes resulted in even higher predicted values of plenum
pressure compared to the already overpredicted values from the original model. Although the rod still failed
prematurely compared to the measured time of failure, the discrepancy was within 50 seconds, a smaller
gap than observed in the Halden 4 model.

5.3.3 Studsvik 191 Model and Results

 Test 191, part of the LOCA program at Studsvik, aimed to investigate the behavior of high burnup fuel
rods under simulated LOCA conditions. Using a high burnup Westinghouse ZIRLO™-clad rod segment
with an average burnup of 69.3 GWd/MTU, the test involved heating the rod to 1,160 degrees °C and
holding at temperature for 25 seconds while in a steam environment to induce ballooning and rupture[52],
[54]. The primary objectives were to study the cladding's response to high temperatures, the extent of
ballooning and rupture, and the subsequent fuel fragmentation and dispersal. Test 191 represents a high
burnup, high pressure scenario as tests 189 and 191 were initially pressurized higher (110 vs. 82 mbar) to
show the impact of aggravated pressure on rupture. High burnup tests (~71 GWd/MTU in tests 189, 191,
192, and 193) showed more fuel fragmentation than lower burnup tests (~55 GWd/MTU in tests 196 and
198).
 The FAST model development efforts were focused on Test 191 as it is a current benchmarking case
for BISON fuel performance modeling, so direct comparisons between FAST and BISON results could be
evaluated. This required accessing or digitizing available plots for power histories and axially dependent
outer cladding surface temperatures [54], [55], [56]. It should be noted that key areas where estimated
values had to be used include linear power distribution, coolant pressure, and some missing pellet geometry
specifications. We will use one of these models to perform FAST BISON code-to-code comparisons and
determine how the models affect the overall results.
 To standardize comparisons between FAST and BISON models, the cladding outer surface
temperatures as a function of axial position provided in the BISON benchmarking input were loaded into
FAST as a boundary condition. Additionally, the piece-wise function for coolant pressure used in BISON
was also input as a boundary condition for the FAST model. Previous issues with implementing rod
refabrication were addressed by adjusting the beginning axial node, initial pressure, refill pressure, plenum
length, and time step at which refabrication occurs. However, even with these changes, the run returned a
radial error.
 In debugging the radial error, the time steps used during the transient was changed from the suggested
0.2 s to 1.0s, allowing the model to run to completion. The bug was discussed with NRC collaborator Dr.
James Corson and the issue was escalated to PNNL. This finding prompted a time step study, re-running
the model with incremental transient time steps from 0.1s-1.0s. The radial error was encountered with time
steps 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 s. For the other timesteps, no real pattern was discernable, and no convergence was

 Figure 60 through Figure 65 illustrate the time evolution of the maximum fuel and cladding
temperatures, and plenum pressure as well as the cladding profilometry at rupture for both rods. In BISON,
a plastic instability criterion was chosen to determine cladding failure when the inelastic strain rate
(primarily dominated by high temperature creep) exceeds a value of 100 h-1 (2.77778×10-2 s-1). Cladding
rupture was computed to occur at similar times between the large and short rods at 1082.7 s and 1082.8 s.
This corresponds to rupture occurring almost 20 seconds after blowdown initiates. It is unclear on what
differences between the coupling methodology between TRACE/FAST and TRACE/BISON would lead to
the BISON driven coupled simulations not presenting a similar reduction in rupture time compared to the
previously shown studies. Similar rupture pressures between the two rods are also observed at about 5 MPa.
Contrary to the standalone TRACE and coupled TRACE/FAST simulations that the cladding temperature
approaches the fuel temperature much more rapidly, indicating a potential issue occurring the transfer of
TRACE parameters to BISON. Smaller observed cladding radii are observed in the coupled
BISON/TRACE simulations because the assumed failure criteria is based upon the strain rate and indicates
that rapid deformation is imminent. Therefore, the plastic instability criteria is suitable for determining
rupture time rather than an estimate on the cladding profilometry as significant cladding deformation would
occur over the next few seconds.

6 Summary
 This report shows a significant effort towards the advancement of high-fidelity modeling capabilities
and important phenomena that are important for high-burnup UO2 and accident tolerant fuels (ATF) during
transient conditions. It represents a step forward in accurately modeling the time-dependent phenomena
that impact material performance must be used to determine the figures of merit and safety margin for
nuclear reactor fuels. A comprehensive database of digitized open source LOCA was developed and used
to help create the models in this project. Extensive testing of BISON, BlueCRAB, FAST, and TRACE was
carried out to determine their ability to adequately simulate the complex phenomena during LOCA events.
For this project, the underlying goal of determining the readiness of coupled calculations was a specific
priority. This is also the least tested and most difficult part of modeling this phenomenon. This report
demonstrates the model development process and documents the lessons learned during the process. It also
outlines the future need to carry out additional work on coupled code verification and validation.
 To carry out the studies in this project new thermal hydraulic and fuel models needed to be developed
to test the coupled code system. TRACE models of the TWIST test rig and models for the Studsvik were
developed and verified. An uncertainty study was performed on the gap-gas conductance TRACE TWIST
model to determine the sensitivity of the solution to different gas-gap properties and calculation models.
 Five new FAST models were developed, including Halden 650.4, Halden 650.9, Studsvik 191, LOC-
C long, and LOC-C short. The Halden and Studsvik models were verified and validated against
experimental data. The LOC-C tests were verified against the TRACE results. In addition, Studsvik 192
and 196 models were started but the development of them was halted due to code errors encountered during
the development of Studsvik 191. All these models will be provided to the NRC as new test problems for
the FAST code.
 Two new BISON models were developed for the LOC-C long and LOC-C short rod. MOOSE thermal
hydraulics module and TRACE models with necessary BlueCRAB wrappers were created for the Studsvik
rod 191, rod 196, and Halden IFA 650.4 and 650.9 BISON validation cases. A mesh sensitivity study was
performed on the rod 191 channels for depletion and LOCA cases. An SGF fluid comparison was performed
for the 191 depletion cases. LOCA simulations and sensitivity analysis were completed for rod 191 and 196
for the THM SGF and IAPWS97 fluid libraries and attempted for TRACE, with results validated against
experimental outcomes and compared to the existing standalone BISON output. While BISON and
TRACE were able to produce the behavior desired for the LOCA, ensuring that the simulations were able
to transfer values proved challenging. So much so that the Halden IFA development was postponed due to
further complication added from using 1.5D mesh transfers. The 2D Studsvik cases offered better stability
during operation and transfer but suffered from overwriting and build issues. In both versions of
BlueCRAB available to the developers during this process, configurations which were indicated to allow
for sampling of TRACE mesh values did not fulfill that requirement. Identifying this issue was further
hampered by difficulties in building and running BlueCRAB.
 A significant amount of time was spent debugging models and working with INL to improve
BlueCRAB’s robustness for use with BISON, FAST, and TRACE. Issue arose with building BlueCRAB
to support BISON, TRACE, and FAST; time step size selection; and data transfers. Many issues were
identified during this project, some of which remain. For example, a major problem encountered was the
inability to rebuild new versions of the BlueCRAB environments on local systems when new code updates
were released. This significantly hindered the ability to progress as more time was spent trying to figure
out how to get the codes to work over model development. In the last two quarters of the project, this issue
was resolved by the development of BlueCRAB container models that included BISON, FAST, and

 70

TRACE. This made it possible to focus on model development and coupled code debugging rather than
code installation.
 During this project, many issues were encountered and lessons learned. The models developed and
analysis performed during this project identified deficiencies in the coupled codes and a wealth of suggested
improvements. Below is a summary of suggested future improvements.
 • Little to no BlueCRAB documentation is available. It would be helpful to provide future users
 with BlueCRAB wrapper guidance documents.
 • Current BlueCRAB directory contains outdated examples. Having the deployed BlueCRAB
 directory only contain input files which use the new mesh structures and coupling will aid
 developers.
 • Address linpack library issues on linux systems so that larger scale simulations can be
 performed at scale using SLURM scripts.
 • Address BISON convective two-phase boundary condition and temperature transfer issues
 between BlueCRAB.
 • Clean up test cases at build so that 0 errors can be expected as a working version of
 BlueCRAB.
 • Recreate LBLOCA example using new TRACE structures and transfers.
 • Additional debugging of the FAST/BlueCRAB interface. As stated in this report, the
 FAST/BlueCRAB interface is largely untested. Many issues were encountered when trying to
 select the appropriate time step size for running the coupled calculations. In addition,
 overwriting of FAST boundary conditions when coupling to other codes needs to be resolved.
 • Further gas-gap conductance studies should be performed for high-burnup fuels. This will
 affect the timing of clad failure.
 • A more complete plenum gas pressure study should be conducted. This will help inform new
 experiments and help determine refabrication of the fuel rodlets.
 • The current version of TRACE compatible with BlueCRAB is Version 5 Patch 6. The latest
 version of TRACE is Version 5 Patch 9 which continues new component based numerical
 schemes, improve fuel rod models, and other updates that would impact the results of this
 work.
 The team plans to continue work on finalizing the coupled calculations as issues encountered here are
addressed in new versions of BISON and BlueCRAB are released. Two additional publications are planned
involving the BISON studies presented in this report and an in-depth comparison of coupled
BISON/TRACE and FAST/TRACE results.
