Atomistic insights into the magnetic-field modulation of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}$$\end{document} adenosine receptor
Authors: Carmen Pisano, Federico Del Signore, Paolo Marracino, Ilaria Pappa, Davide Cocco, Elena Della Valle, Simona Salati, Ruggero Cadossi, Micaela Liberti, Francesca Apollonio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMFs) are increasingly used in clinical settings for the treatment of inflammatory and degenerative conditions. Among their molecular targets, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}$$\end{document} adenosine receptors (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document}) show increased receptor density on the cell membrane upon PEMF exposure, yet the underlying physical mechanism remains uncertain. In this study, atomistic molecular dynamics simulations were used to investigate how an external static magnetic field, used as a simplified representation of the quasi-static magnetic component of PEMF exposure, influences the structure and electrostatics of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document}. Our simulations revealed that the field predominantly affects the receptor’s extracellular region, resulting in a transition from a closed to a more open conformation of the ligand-entry region. This structural rearrangement was accompanied by widening and increased hydration of the extracellular entrance, indicating enhanced accessibility of the orthosteric binding site. At residue level, Glu169, Ala265, and Pro266 exhibited pronounced dipole reorientation, indicating localized electrostatic adaptation consistent with the structural response. Together, these findings provide an atomistic insight into how magnetic perturbations can influence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} extracellular dynamics and offer a mechanistic framework compatible with experimental observations of altered \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} function under PEMF exposure.

Introduction

Pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMFs) have long attracted interest in biomedical research because of their beneficial effects in inflammation control and tissue repair1,2. Yet, the molecular basis of their biological action remains poorly understood, limiting the rational development of therapeutic protocols.

Among the molecular targets influenced by PEMF exposure, the A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2A}$$\end{document} adenosine receptors (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document}) have emerged as a central mediator of biological responses related to inflammation3, neuroprotection, and vascular regulation. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document} are G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) predominantly coupled to G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_s$$\end{document} proteins, whose activation suppresses immune responses by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release4 and promotes neuronal protection under oxidative or inflammatory stress5. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document} are highly expressed in the central nervous system, particularly in the striatum, where their modulation has been associated with neuroprotective effects and improved motor function in Parkinson’s disease6,7. Beyond the nervous system, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document} are also involved in the regulation of vascular tone and blood flow through their vasodilatory effects8. These combined anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and vasoregulatory effects highlight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document} as a promising therapeutic target not only in neurodegenerative disorders, but also in cardiovascular conditions characterized by vascular dysfunction, including hypertension.

Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document} represent a strategic molecular hub where PEMFs may exert clinically relevant effects, linking cellular biophysics to translational outcomes. Experimental evidence has shown that exposure to PEMFs increases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} density on the cell membrane and their binding availability in several cell types9,10. This biological observation forms the rationale for the present study and motivates the investigation of the atomistic mechanisms that could explain such macroscopic effects.

However, while these data support a functional link between field exposure and the activation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document}-downstream signaling pathways, they do not clarify mechanistic basis by which electromagnetic fields influence receptor structure and dynamics at the molecular scale.Fig. 1Meta-analysis of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}$$\end{document} adenosine receptor modulation under PEMF exposure. To bridge this gap, computational modeling offers a powerful means to connect experimental data with molecular-level mechanisms. Atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations enable the investigation of receptor conformational transitions in realistic membrane environments, revealing how external physical stimuli may alter activation pathways16–18. Previous simulation studies have mapped the allosteric coupling among transmembrane helices (TM3, TM5, TM6, TM7) and the influence of lipids and cholesterol on receptor dynamics and signaling19,20. Similar approaches have been proposed to explore field-dependent effects at the atomic level, indicating that MD can effectively probe the modulation of GPCRs by electromagnetic fields21–25. Within these frameworks, MD resolves dynamic allosteric networks and transient interaction sites, capturing subtle rearrangements in hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic contacts that accompany activation23–25.

Here, we use atomistic MD simulations to investigate how magnetic fields affect the structure and electrostatics of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document}. Because PEMF waveforms modulate on millisecond timescales, many orders of magnitude slower than atomic motion, the magnetic component experienced by the receptor can be treated as quasi-static within the MD temporal window. A time-independent magnetic field is therefore employed as an adiabatic approximation of the instantaneous magnetic perturbation, with the goal of identifying residue-level structural and electrostatic susceptibilities of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {ARs}$$\end{document} rather than reproducing the full temporal complexity of PEMF exposure. By bridging electromagnetic biophysics and the molecular response of GPCR, this study aims to provide a mechanistic rationale for the experimentally observed receptor sensitization under PEMF exposure.

Electrostatic rearrangements and local field response

Based on this global screening, six residues defining the extracellular entrance were selected for detailed statistical analysis. Figure 5b focuses on this restricted set of residues, revealing how magnetic-field exposure modifies dipole-orientation distributions and highlighting the difference between stable and responsive sites. This analysis quantifies how the dipole orientations of the selected residues redistribute under magnetic-field exposure, highlighting differences in their sensitivity to the applied field (see Table S1 in Supplementary Material for the statistical characterization). Functional interpretation of the field effect

The combined geometrical and electrostatic analyses suggest that magnetic-field exposure primarily influences the extracellular gate of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} by modulating residue-level dipoles in regions of enhanced structural flexibility. The field interacts with polar and charged residues, particularly Glu169, Ala265, and Pro266, whose dipole reorientation under exposure suggests a redistribution of the local electrostatic potential. These changes may extend to the surrounding residues, potentially influencing hydrogen-bond networks and intra-loop interactions. This local electrostatic rearrangement is associated with the extracellular loops toward geometries that favour hydration and a more open gate configuration. In this context, the observed bias toward more open loop geometries and increased hydration is consistent with an electrostatic contribution to the modulation of the extracellular gate. Overall, the results support a coherent interpretation in which magnetic interaction with residue dipoles is associated with changes in the local electrostatic environment and with a modulation of extracellular loops conformations, leading to a more hydrated and accessible entrance to the orthosteric binding site. Importantly, this conformational adaptation remains comparable with the overall fold of the receptor and does not perturb the transmembrane core. Discussion

This study provides an atomistic description of the response of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} to an external time-independent magnetic field, exploring a possible link between electromagnetic exposure and receptor conformational dynamics. By combining equilibrium and non-equilibrium molecular dynamics simulations, the results indicate that the magnetic field predominantly affects the extracellular region of the receptor, inducing subtle changes in geometry, hydration, and local electrostatics while preserving the integrity of the transmembrane core.

Analysis of the extracellular entrance revealed the presence of two predominant conformational states of the extracellular entrance, which can be described as “less accessible” and “more accessible” configurations. Under control conditions, the receptor remained mainly in the less accessible state, whereas magnetic-field exposure increased the probability of the more accessible configuration. This shift suggests that the field may bias conformations more open to ligand interaction, in agreement with experimental reports of enhanced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} responsiveness under PEMF exposure8,9,12,27.

The observed increase in water density within the extracellular cavity further supports this interpretation. Enhanced hydration and cavity expansion are recognized features linked to ligand accessibility and early activation processes in GPCR28,29. The effect was limited to the extracellular region, while the cytoplasmic portion and G-protein interface remained stable, suggesting that the magnetic field modifies local electrostatics and hydration rather than producing large-scale structural perturbations. A hydrogen bond analysis further reveals a tendency toward increased protein–water contacts under magnetic-field exposure, while protein–protein hydrogen bonds remain essentially unchanged, consistent with a localized increase in solvent accessibility at the extracellular interface without significant perturbation of the receptor core.

At the residue level, Ala265 and Pro266, located at the extracellular ends of helices VI and VII, showed the strongest field sensitivity, displaying dipole reorientation and local charge redistribution. These residues correspond to sites previously implicated in extracellular gating and TM6–TM7 rearrangements preceding GPCR activation16,17,28. Notably, the meta-binding site defined by these and neighboring residues has been identified as a key determinant of ligand accessibility to the orthosteric pocket28, suggesting that the field-induced perturbation observed here may influence ligand recognition and receptor conformational flexibility in functionally relevant ways. Together, these observations support a model in which magnetic exposure selectively perturbs the electrostatic landscape experienced by flexible extracellular residues, facilitating local conformational adjustments that bias the entrance region toward increased accessibility.

From a methodological perspective, this work employed an extended Velocity–Verlet algorithm capable of integrating the Lorentz force30,31, ensuring accurate particle motion under magnetic perturbations. Validation against the analytical solution for cyclotron motion confirmed the stability and precision of the modified integrator, providing a reliable computational framework for the study of field–protein interactions.

It should be noted that the time-independent magnetic field employed here represents an adiabatic approximation of the full PEMF waveform. While this choice is physically justified by the separation of timescales between field modulation and atomic motions, and by the instantaneous nature of the Lorentz force, it does not capture possible effects associated with the temporal modulation of the field. The present results should therefore be interpreted as identifying residue-level susceptibilities to magnetic perturbation, providing a molecular-level framework that future studies employing time-varying field protocols or multiscale approaches could build upon.

In addition, the field strength employed (1 T) is higher than the millitesla range typically used in PEMF experiments. At the molecular scale, magnetic interactions are typically much smaller than thermal fluctuations, making their direct effects difficult to resolve within the timescales accessible to MD simulations. In this framework, the applied field acts as a sensitivity-enhancing perturbation that enables the identification of residue-level structural and electrostatic responses, following an approach analogous to that commonly adopted in MD studies of electric-field effects on biological membranes and proteins32–35, and consistent with recent MD studies employing field strengths in the range of 1–10 T to probe magnetic field effects on biological systems36. Overall, these results support a scenario in which residue-level electrostatic perturbations represent the earliest detectable signatures of the magnetic-field effect, while increased receptor accessibility emerges as a collective, higher-order consequence of these local responses. Although the induced rearrangements are modest in magnitude, they appear sufficient to bias the extracellular gate toward more accessible conformational states.

In conclusion, static magnetic fields appear to induce limited but measurable rearrangements in the extracellular region of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document}. The combination of geometrical expansion, increased hydration, and residue-specific dipole changes suggests that magnetic exposure may bias the receptor towards configurations with greater solvent accessibility. These atomistic observations offer a plausible physical interpretation of previously reported increases in the activation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {A}_{2A}\hbox {AR}$$\end{document} signaling cascade under PEMF exposure8,9,12,13 delivering a direct mechanistic rationale at the molecular level that complements the macroscopic biological evidence, and provide a basis for further studies on electromagnetic modulation of GPCRs.

Future developments could extend this approach to different field strengths or orientations and employ enhanced-sampling or multiscale models to explore the coupling between electromagnetic fields and receptor dynamics. Overall, the methodology described here may support broader investigations into the molecular mechanisms underlying electromagnetic interactions with biological systems.

Magnetic field implementation and validation

Although GROMACS is a highly reliable tool for MD simulations and includes built-in functionalities for applying uniform electric fields, it does not provide a native routine for introducing an external magnetic field. Therefore, a custom implementation was developed to apply a static magnetic field and the corresponding Lorentz force to each atom. This feature was incorporated into the GROMACS source code following the algorithmic framework proposed by Spreiter and Walter31 and extended by Della Valle et al.30. The static field employed here represents an adiabatic approximation of the magnetic component of PEMF exposure. Because PEMF waveforms typically vary on millisecond timescales (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{-3}$$\end{document} s), several orders of magnitude slower than atomic motions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{-9}$$\end{document} s), the receptor effectively experiences the magnetic perturbation as quasi-static within the temporal window accessible to atomistic MD simulations. Furthermore, the Lorentz force acting on each atom at any given instant depends solely on the instantaneous field value and particle velocity, carrying no memory of the waveform’s temporal profile. The structural and electrostatic observables probed here therefore reflect the receptor’s instantaneous response to the magnetic perturbation, independently of the full temporal structure of PEMF exposure. A uniform induction magnetic field B (specified in Tesla) was passed to the integrator and converted into GROMACS internal units through a constant scaling factor (Eq. 1):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_B = 10464.27 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}according to (Eq. 2):2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{B}_{{T}} = s_B \, \textbf{B}_{\mathrm{internal}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_T$$\end{document} is the field strength in Tesla.

The action of the external magnetic field on charged atoms was introduced by explicitly adding a Lorentz-force term to the Newtonian equation of motion (Eq. 3):3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} m_i \frac{d\textbf{v}_i}{dt} = \textbf{F}_i + q_i \left( \textbf{v}_i \times \textbf{B} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_i$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_i$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{v}_i$$\end{document} denote the mass, charge, and velocity of atom i; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{F}_i$$\end{document} represents the sum of bonded and non-bonded forces acting on the particle; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}$$\end{document} is the magnetic-field vector.

The external magnetic field enters the equations of motion exclusively through the Lorentz force term and does not modify the underlying potential energy function or the parametrization of the force field: bonded and non-bonded interaction terms remain unchanged, as reflected in Eq. (3), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{F}_i$$\end{document} retains its standard form. This formulation is consistent with classical electrodynamics, in which a magnetic field does not perform work on charged particles and therefore does not contribute to the potential energy surface, but rather modifies particle trajectories through velocity-dependent forces. As a consequence, the observed structural and electrostatic effects arise from the direct coupling between the Lorentz force and the dynamics of charged atoms and residues, rather than from modifications of the underlying force field parametrization. The physical consistency of this approach is further supported by the validation described below, and is consistent with previously established approaches for magnetic molecular dynamics simulations of biological systems30,36,39.

Time integration was performed using an extended Velocity–Verlet scheme30,31,36, in which the magnetic contribution is introduced through the Larmor frequency component into the velocity and position update equations. Specifically, we adopted the inversion algorithm described in31, in which the strength of the magnetic field enters through the Larmor frequency Ω = qB/m, where q is the particle charge, B the magnetic field intensity, and m the particle mass. Additional validation and stability checks were performed on the full receptor–membrane system. The verified implementation was then incorporated into the production version of GROMACS used in this study, enabling atomistic computation of both interatomic and Lorentz forces. This extension allowed the direct coupling between the applied magnetic field and molecular dynamics, providing a physically consistent framework for the analysis of the field-induced rearrangements described in the Results.

Simulation setup and data generation

The system preparation followed a standard multi-step protocol. After system construction, an initial energy minimization step was performed to remove steric clashes and optimize local geometry. This was followed by an equilibration phase under NPT conditions (310 K,1 bar), allowing the lipid bilayer and solvent environment to relax around the protein. The equilibration phase was followed by an additional unrestrained pre-production simulation (∼50 ns) to ensure full stabilization of the system prior to data collection. Following this preparation, a series of all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed under both control and field-exposed conditions.

All simulations were carried out using the GROMACS package40 with the CHARMM27 force field41. A modified version of the code, including the Lorentz-force term described in the previous Section, was employed. A total of 11 independent simulations were generated, consisting of 5 trajectories for the 0 T condition and 6 trajectories for the 1 T condition, each with durations up to 270 ns, corresponding to an aggregate sampling of approximately 1.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu \hbox {s}$$\end{document} for each condition.

An external magnetic induction field of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B = 1~\text {T}$$\end{document} was applied along the z-axis, orthogonal to the membrane and parallel to the receptor’s transmembrane bundle. Periodic boundary conditions were used in all three spatial directions. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, and Lennard–Jones interactions were truncated at 1.2 nm.

Temperature was maintained at 310 K using a Berendsen thermostat42 with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps, while pressure was controlled semi-isotropically at 1 bar using a coupling constant of 1 ps. The time step was set to 2 fs. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm.

RMSD analysis, secondary-structure analysis using DSSP43, and pore-radius profiles along the receptor axis44, and hydrogen bond counts for protein–water and protein–protein interactions were used to monitor the global stability of the receptor throughout the simulations and to verify preservation of the overall fold under both conditions. These analyses are reported in the Supplementary Material. Each trajectory was visually inspected to confirm the absence of artifacts related to periodic imaging or boundary effects.

This protocol yielded a statistically consistent ensemble of equilibrated configurations for both the control and field-exposed systems, forming the basis for the structural and electrostatic analyses described in the Results Sections.

Trajectory analysis

Three complementary analyses were performed on the molecular dynamics trajectories to quantify the effects of magnetic-field exposure on the receptor: (i) extracellular entrance area, (ii) water density distribution, and (iii) residue dipole moments. These analyses were performed using custom C++ routines, whose mathematical formulations are described in detail in the following subsections. Post-processing, statistical analyses, and figure generation were carried out in MATLAB (MathWorks).

Extracellular entrance area

To assess the conformational dynamics of the extracellular gate, we defined an entrance region based on six residues (Ser67, Glu169, His264, Ala265, Pro266, and Leu267) which delineate the meta-binding site and the access path to the orthosteric pocket28. For each simulation frame, the positions of these residues were projected onto the xy plane, and the covariance matrix of their coordinates was computed. The area of the best-fitting ellipse was then calculated as (Eq. 7):7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A = \pi \sqrt{\lambda _1 \lambda _2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _2$$\end{document} are the eigenvalues of the positional covariance matrix. This metric captures both the amplitude and orientation of conformational fluctuations at the receptor entrance. The entrance area is therefore used as a quantitative descriptor of the degree of opening of the extracellular gate.

The temporal evolution of the extracellular area was monitored over 270 ns for each trajectory and analyzed independently in order to capture the intrinsic dynamical variability and to highlight transitions that may not be evident in averaged profiles. The corresponding probability density function (PDF) was computed to characterize the distribution of entrance-area values. The distributions reveal two preferential regions centered around approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.7~\textrm{nm}^2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.4~\textrm{nm}^2$$\end{document} corresponding to configurations with smaller and larger entrance areas, respectively.

Control trajectories displayed occasional spontaneous transitions between configurations characterized by smaller and larger entrance areas (around 90 ns and 150 ns), whereas the magnetic-field–exposed systems preferentially sampled configurations with larger entrance areas. The shift of the distribution toward larger entrance-area values under magnetic-field exposure indicates an increased sampling of more open configurations, consistent with enhanced accessibility of the ligand-binding site.

Water density analysis

To evaluate hydration dynamics within the receptor, the number of water molecules was quantified inside a cylindrical volume spanning the extracellular and intracellular regions and centered on the receptor z-axis. The spatial density ρ (z) was computed as Eq. 8:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \rho (z) = \frac{N(z)}{V(z)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N(z) is the number of water molecules in a slice of thickness Δ z, and V(z) is the corresponding cylindrical volume. Mean profiles and standard errors were calculated for both control (0 T) and field-exposed (1 T) conditions.

A marked increase in hydration was observed in the extracellular region (around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z \approx 9~\textrm{nm}$$\end{document}), with mean densities of approximately 7.5 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15~\textrm{molecules}\,\textrm{nm}^{-3}$$\end{document} for 0 T and 1 T, respectively, corresponding to a maximum difference of about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$7~\textrm{molecules}\,\textrm{nm}^{-3}$$\end{document}. In the receptor interior (6–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$7~\textrm{nm}$$\end{document}), the water content approached zero in both conditions, while the intracellular region (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z < 6~\textrm{nm}$$\end{document}) showed no significant difference.

A small variation in water density was also detected around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z \approx 10.5~\textrm{nm}$$\end{document}, likely reflecting the flexibility of the extracellular loops.

To visualize the temporal evolution of hydration, water-density maps ρ (z,t) were constructed over the 270 ns trajectories. The field-exposed system displayed a persistent high-density band near the extracellular gate, confirming a stable hydration enhancement, consistent with the structural expansion described in Results Section.

Dipole moment analysis

Local electrostatic changes were investigated by computing the dipole moment vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_i$$\end{document} for each residue i as Eq. 9:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{M}_i = \sum _{j} q_j\, \textbf{r}_j , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_j$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}_j$$\end{document} denote the partial charge and position of atom j belonging to residue i. The Cartesian components \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{ix}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{iy}$$\end{document} were averaged over the 270 ns trajectories and compared between control (0 T) and exposed (1 T) conditions. The differences \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta M_{ix}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta M_{iy}$$\end{document} were used to identify residues exhibiting the strongest field-induced alterations.

Subsequently, probability density functions (PDFs) of the y-component were computed for the six key residues that define the extracellular gate. Those showing the largest dipole reorientations–Glu169, His264, Pro266, and Leu267–were further analyzed through 3D vector mapping within the receptor structure, highlighting orientation shifts and magnitude variations under magnetic-field exposure.

Together, these analyses quantify the local electrostatic and mechanical contributions to the receptor’s conformational response and support the structural interpretation discussed in Results Section.
