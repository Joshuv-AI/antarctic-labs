An improved surgical technique for penoscrotal transposition: the penile upward migration method
Authors: Haichen Chen, Wenni Li, Zhiwei Wu, Qin Yang, Shoufu Wang, Yang Hu, Bo Xu, Yonglong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPenoscrotal transposition(PST) is a congenital malformation often associated with hypospadias. Conventional techniques, such as the scrotal M-shaped flap method and buttonhole technique, are limited by significant trauma, compromised blood supply, or unsatisfactory cosmetic outcomes. The “penile upward migration method”, derived from the buttonhole technique, has been used clinically since February 2018 in our center to correct PST. It is based on the therapeutic principle of the buttonhole technique. This study aims to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of a novel “penile upward migration method” for correcting PST.MethodsA retrospective case series study was conducted on patients who underwent surgery using the penile upward migration method between January 2018 and June 2023. During the operation, a larger circle is marked around the penile base, while a smaller concentric circle is marked and excised to allow upward penile migration. This technique can be used for isolated PST, residual PST after hypospadias repair, or simultaneous correction of PST and hypospadias. Data on demographics, operative details, complications, and postoperative cosmetic outcomes were collected and analyzed.ResultsA total of 35 patients (29 with partial and 6 with complete PST) were included in the study. The mean age was 51.6 months. Thirty-two patients underwent PST correction alone (average operative time: 70.36 ± 9.76 min), while 3 patients underwent simultaneous correction with hypospadias repair (average operative time: 156.67 ± 21.86 min). Postoperative follow-up averaged 13.6 months. Complete correction was achieved in 33 cases. Complications included one case of partial skin necrosis at the penile root and two cases of hypertrophic scarring, all of which resolved with subsequent treatment. Caregivers reported high satisfaction with the postoperative penile and scrotal appearance based on the modified Pediatric Penile Perception Score (PPPS).ConclusionThe penile upward migration method is a safe and effective way of correcting PST. This surgical procedure is also simpler than the buttonhole method. It offers advantages of shorter operative time, minimal impact on penile blood supply, and aesthetically pleasing results with hidden scars. However, further comparative studies are warranted.

Introduction

Penoscrotal transposition(PST), although generally a rare malformation, is a relatively common congenital malformation of the external genitalia in specialized centers dealing with complex genital malformations, characterized by an abnormal positioning of the penis in relation to the scrotum, where the base of the penis is surrounded by scrotal skin, and in some cases, the penis is located below the scrotum. The degree of transposition can be classified into partial and complete types. This abnormality may be complete, in which case the penis is entirely covered by the scrotum, or it may be partial, in which case the scrotum does not fuse above the penis [1, 2]. The occurrence of isolated PST is uncommon and is most frequently associated with hypospadias [2]. Surgery is the only treatment option. Due to the association with hypospadias, PST corrective surgery is complex and invasive, and accordingly, a two-stage approach is often chosen: repairing the hypospadias in the first stage and correcting the PST in the second stage.

The scrotal M-shaped flap method is a commonly used technique for correcting PST, achieving the corrective effect by transferring the scrotal flap [3]. Our hospital initially adopted this classic surgical approach, but postoperative complications such as scrotal skin necrosis and scar hyperplasia were often pronounced. Based on our experience, the considerable trauma associated with transferring scrotal flaps makes the simultaneous correction of a bifid scrotum challenging, often leading to suboptimal cosmetic and functional results. While some scholars argue that PST indicates abnormal penile displacement rather than scrotal ascent. Based on this assumption, the “buttonhole technique” was developed, which elevates the penis via subcutaneous tunneling. This method is referred to as the “buttonhole technique.” It entails the subcutaneous relocation of the penis for the purpose of PST correction, with the objective of achieving relatively satisfactory therapeutic outcomes. However, this surgical procedure is relatively technically complex [4].

Derived from the therapeutic principle upon which the buttonhole technique is grounded, the “penile upward migration method” has been proposed as an innovative approach, which has been used clinically for PST correction since February 2018 in our center. This surgical procedure is simple to perform and can correct both PST and bifid scrotum, with satisfactory postoperative appearance. In this study, we will describe this improved surgical technique with a view to its clinical application.

Methods

A retrospective case series was conducted to systematically gather data on patients’ demographic and clinical profiles, operative details, and postoperative outcomes.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients undergoing primary surgery for hypospadias with PST, residual PST after hypospadias correction, or isolated PST. (2) Use of the penile upward migration method to correct PST. (3) The time of surgery ranges from January 2018 to June 2023. (4) Participants must be between six months and 18 years of age.

Exclusion criteria: Patients requiring urethral repair due to residual penile curvature, or in conjunction with urethral fistula or urethral stricture.

Surgical procedure

Surgical procedure in residual PST after hypospadias correction or isolated PST

The patients were managed by total intravenous anesthesia combined with caudal block. Thereafter, a 5 − 0 Prolene suture was placed vertically in the glans, thus acting as a holding suture, and a 6 or 8Fr double-lumen silicone catheter was inserted. Initially, a circle is delineated around the base of the penis with a marker pen, its diameter being slightly larger than the base of the penis (referred to as the larger circle). In a similar manner, a circle of comparable diameter, but of a slightly smaller size (referred to as the smaller circle), is delineated. The center of the smaller circle is located at the upper edges of the bifid scrotum. The distance of the upward shift is the vertical distance between the centre of the larger circle and the tangents at the upper edges of the bifid scrotum (Fig. 1). The incision should be made in the shape of the larger circle, with the blade passing only through the epidermis and dermis, without penetrating the Scarpa’s fascia, at which point the penile skin is characterized as an island flap. Make an incision on the dorsal side of the penis and dissect the skin and Camper’s fascia upwards towards the smaller circle. Then, excise the skin tissue along the marked circle, taking care to preserve Scarpa’s fascia intact, and remove an appropriate amount of subcutaneous fat. In the case of a bifid scrotum, an incision should be made along the midline of the scrotum from the ventral aspect of the penis downward. The skin and subcutaneous tissue layers are then to be separated through each scrotum, with both testicles being brought towards the centre to eliminate the split. The excess tissue is then removed from the scrotal midline. The incisions on the penis and scrotum were stitched using 6 − 0 absorbable suture. Finally, the gauze compression was applied following a hydrophobic dressing (Fig. 2).

Postoperative management and follow-up

For patients with isolated PST, a urethra catheter was kept in place for 3 days, with the dressing being changed at the same time. At seven days after the operation, the dressings should be removed to expose the wound. Patients may be discharged by eight days after the operation. For patients undergoing primary surgery for hypospadias with PST, the dressing was removed after five to seven days. A urethral catheter was kept in place for 14 days. The patient may be discharged if urination is normal after the urinary catheter has been removed. Regular follow-ups at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months after discharge were conducted to monitor the appearance of the penis and scrotum, the status of PST correction, and the occurrence of complications. The primary outcome is the postoperative status of PST correction. This was evaluated using the Corrêa-Macedo classification system. Three independent pediatric urologists, blinded to patient grouping and time points, graded residual PST as mild (penile root visible), moderate (scrotum partially covers penile root), or severe (scrotum completely above or enveloping the penis). The final grade was determined by majority consensus (≥ 2 reviewers). Treatment success was defined as mild or no PST residual [1]. The appearance of the penis and scrotum was evaluated by caregivers using the modified Pediatric Penile Perception Score (PPPS). The evaluative standards included the length of the penis, the appearance of the scrotum, scarring, and the overall appearance at six months post-discharge. Satisfaction with each item could be expressed using a 4-point Likert scale, which included the ratings of very dissatisfied (0 points), dissatisfied (1), satisfied (2), and very satisfied (3) [6, 7].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient characteristics, operative details, and outcomes. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median(range). No formal inferential statistical testing was conducted for hypothesis generation due to the single-group case series design.

Results

From January 2018 to June 2023, a total of 35 patients who met the criteria were included in the study. The patients in the study were between 18 and 133 months old, with an average age of 51.6 months. The PST classification was as follows: 29 cases of partial type and six cases of complete type. Three cases presented with isolated PST, while 32 cases involved concurrent hypospadias. In terms of hypospadias classification, we observed 3 cases (8.6%) with no hypospadias, 2 cases (5.7%) of penile type, 5 cases (14.3%) of penoscrotal type, 16 cases (45.7%) of scrotal type, and 9 cases (25.7%) of perineal type. There was a total of 5 instances of disorders of sex development (DSD), which consisted of two cases of gonadal dysplasia (including one case of mixed gonadal dysplasia and one case of 46, XX male), and three cases of 46, XY DSD (consisting of two cases of 5-alpha-reductase deficiency and one case of partial androgen insensitivity). Of these, 29 cases first underwent hypospadias repair surgery, followed by PST correction. The interval between the initial surgery and the current procedure ranged from six to 68 months, with an average of 12 months. Three cases underwent simultaneous correction of hypospadias and PST in a single stage. All surgeries were completed successfully. Following evaluation by three independent urologists using the Corrêa-Macedo classification, treatment success (defined as mild or no PST residual) was achieved in all of the patients (33 cases of no PST and two cases of mild PST). The average surgical duration for 32 patients who underwent PST correction surgery alone was 70.36 ± 9.76 min, with a range between 60 and 85 min. The surgical duration for 3 patients who underwent PST correction and urethroplasty was 156.67 ± 21.86 min, with a range between 140 and 180 min. Postoperative follow-up was conducted for all patients, with an average follow-up period of 13.6 months, ranging from 6 to 33 months. One case of partial necrosis of the penile root skin and two cases of hypertrophic scarring at the incision site occurred. These complications occurred in residual PST after hypospadias correction. The average scores of modified PPPS for each evaluation item were as follows: penile length 2.24 ± 0.82, scrotal appearance 2.59 ± 0.5, scarring 2.38 ± 0.6, and overall appearance 2.79 ± 0.41. Discussion

PST is a congenital structural malformation that is not uncommon in specialized centers dealing with complex genital malformations. The pathogenesis is unclear, but some studies suggest it may be related to chromosomal variations [3, 4]. During the fetal period, in the normal development of males, around the 9th to 10th week of gestation, under the influence of androgens, genital swellings, known as scrotal swellings in males, arise in the inguinal region. With further development, they move caudally and each one makes up half of the scrotum. If this descent is interfered with during the process, the scrotum may be misplaced above or to the sides of the penis, resulting in an abnormal migration of the scrotum known as PST. Consequently, the preliminary conventional surgical approach, rooted in this pathogenesis, entailed the creation of M-incise on both lateral sides of the scrotum, situated above the penile base. Subsequently, the scrotum was moved inward and downward, positioning it beneath the penis and restoring the normal position of the penis and scrotum [3]. However, a counterargument exists among scholars who propose that PST is indicative of abnormal penile displacement rather than scrotal ascent. Therefore, they utilize subcutaneous tunneling as a therapeutic approach to elevate the penis [4]. Derived from the previously mentioned therapeutic principle, the “penile upward migration method” has been proposed as an innovative approach.

PST is often associated with hypospadias, and the timing of surgery remains controversial. Some scholars recommend performing urethroplasty and correction of PST in a single stage [8]. However, urethral reconstruction for proximal hypospadias often requires transection of the urethral plate. And PST correction requires extensive dissection of the scrotum and flap transfer, resulting in significant trauma that can impair the blood supply to the penis and scrotum. Consequently, most scholars advocate staged urethral reconstruction and correction of penile deviation. There are differences within staged correction approaches. Some advocate correcting curvature and reconstructing the urethra first, followed by PST correction later [8]. Others propose initial penile straightening and PST correction, followed by urethroplasty [9].

In this study, the majority of children with PST and hypospadias underwent staged surgery in the earlier years. This consisted of primary correction of curvature and urethroplasty in the first stage, followed by correction of PST in the second stage. As techniques improved, attempts were made to perform a single-stage procedure to correct both the hypospadias and the PST. However, we believe that, whether using staged or single-stage approaches, it is essential to ensure that sufficient penile skin is available to cover the required length of the urethral defect. At the same time, extensive dissection during correction of the PST must be avoided to prevent compromising penile skin perfusion, which could lead to urethral reconstruction failure.

Currently, PST corrective techniques mainly include two types: the first type, the M-flap technique [3, 10]. The procedure of this operation is to make an M-shaped incision on the scrotum on both sides above the penis base, and then move the scrotum inward and downward below the penis to restore the normal position of the penile and scrotum. The advantage of the surgical method lies in preserving the dorsal skin strip, including its vascular and lymphatic supply, which helps prevent postoperative lymphedema. The shortcomings include the large trauma from the separation of the skin flap, with a high risk of postoperative scrotal skin flap necrosis. Additionally, the M-shaped incision is long, leading to a significant postoperative scar and an unsatisfactory appearance. The second approach is represented by the “buttonhole technique” [4], which yields excellent cosmetic outcomes. However, these surgical procedures involved in this process, which include penile skin degloving and flap inversion, contribute to its complexity and, consequently, its limited applicability in clinical practice.

This study uses the penile upward migration method, which requires only a circular skin incision at the base of the penis. Compared to the “buttonhole technique”, it is not only simpler to operate and has a shorter surgery time, but also does not affect the blood supply to the penis. Compared to the M-flap method for the scrotum, it has the following advantages: (1) The scrotal M-flap method involves a long incision and a visible sutured scar. This means postoperative scarring on both sides of the scrotum and the ventral side of the penis-scrotum junction is significant and not easily concealed. Our method involves a ring incision at the root of the penis. The incision is not obvious once the penis has migrated upwards. The postoperative incision is slightly deep and the scar is not obvious. This technique may contribute to favourable early cosmetic outcomes. (2) The penile upward migration method involves only an incision in the skin and removal of part of the fat tissue (Camper’s fascia). The Scarpa’s fascia remains intact, ensuring that the blood supply to the skin and the new urethra is unaffected. The M-flap method for the scrotum, when separating the subcutaneous tissue of the scrotum, it is easy to cause excessive separation near the dorsal side of the penile root, which can affect the blood supply to the penis, and the blood supply to the separated scrotal flaps cannot be guaranteed, and necrosis is likely to occur postoperatively. (3) Correction effect: The “M” shape flap method for the scrotum only involves the transfer of scrotal skin flaps. On one hand, the scrotal skin flap near the base of the penis should not be excessively excised, as the effect of complete correction of PST is often not thorough. On the other hand, due to the failure to fully relax the subcutaneous fascia tissue in the scrotal area, postoperative scrotal shrinkage may occur, resulting in an unsatisfactory appearance. The penile upward migration method not only moves the penis up completely, but also the operation does not involve both sides of the scrotal skin, and it can fully remove the abnormal fascia within the scrotum, thereby lowering both testicles to their normal position, thus achieving the natural state effect of the scrotum. (4) This surgical technique significantly reduces operating time compared to other methods. This is especially important for pediatric patients, who are in a critical stage of growth and development. Since children’s physiological functions are not fully mature, prolonged anesthesia increases the risk of respiratory depression, circulatory fluctuations, and neurological damage. Shortening the duration of surgery effectively reduces exposure to anesthesia, thereby accelerating the recovery process for pediatric patients, and thus holds significant clinical value.

During the surgical treatment of PST by the penile upward migration method, the following aspects should be noted during the operation.

(1) The initial step in the procedure is the selection of the incision site at the base of the penis. The incision should be placed as close as possible to the junction between the penis and scrotum. This ensures adequate foreskin coverage during penile erection, thereby reducing tension on the incision. Furthermore, the re-shaped penoscrotal region will exhibit a slight dorsal concavity, concealing the incision within this contour for an aesthetically pleasing outcome. (2) The neosite circle is designed to be slightly smaller, creating natural tension and a secure fit for the penis above the scrotum. This dimensional difference is essential for preventing the formation of dead space and reducing the risk of postoperative hematoma, thereby promoting better adherence. Additionally, the incision appears sunken, achieving a better cosmetic outcome. In most cases, an elevation of 1–1.5 cm is sufficient. It is recommended that the location of the incision be marked with a marker prior to surgery. (3) Thirdly, subcutaneous tissue management is addressed. It is imperative to meticulously remove subcutaneous fat to prevent a bulky appearance after penile upward migration, while exercising extreme caution to avoid injury to penile vessels. The dissection should be performed bilaterally around the superficial dorsal arteries and veins. In our technique, we keep the fundiform and suspensory ligaments intact to provide support and stability to the penis in the neosite. Additionally, the neosite circle should be smaller than the circumpenile circle to provide the penis with an appropriate degree of support and tension. (4) For patients with combined hypospadias, intraoperative assessment of the skin flap’s blood supply should determine whether primary correction of penile-scrotal transposition is feasible. Sufficient penile skin must be preserved to cover the required length of urethral reconstruction, while avoiding extensive dissection that could compromise correction. It is imperative to refrain from imposing a single-stage operation.

Among the 35 pediatric patients treated with this method, only one case developed partial skin necrosis at the penile root. The evaluation revealed that the patient exhibited obesity and pronounced penile retraction. Additionally, the surgeon’s inexperience with the procedure at an early stage resulted in an excessively deep dissection, which caused damage to the blood vessels. Another two cases exhibited scar hyperplasia because, in the nascent stages, the ‘smaller circle’ was designed to be slightly larger than necessary. This resulted in additional skin being removed, leading to excessive tension at the incision site. Subsequent treatment of these patients resulted in favorable outcomes, with no reported complications.

The present study is not without its limitations. Firstly, given the absence of a comparative group in this study, the reliability of the conclusions drawn is somewhat limited. Secondly, while this study includes a focused cohort of 35 patients, which is consistent with similar single-center reports in this field, the statistical power and generalization of the findings may be influenced by the sample size and limited follow-up period. Consequently, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution, and further validation in larger prospective studies is warranted. Additionally, the PST associated with hypospadias in this study was mostly corrected after first-stage urethroplasty. Whether this procedure is suitable for simultaneous PST correction during a one-stage urethroplasty is still in the preliminary exploration stage, and further clinical research is needed. Despite these shortcomings, the postoperative effect of this procedure is good, and the results are beneficial.

Conclusion

The penile upward migration method is a safe and effective way of correcting PST. This surgical procedure is also simpler than the buttonhole method. It offers advantages of shorter operative time, minimal impact on penile blood supply, and aesthetically pleasing results with hidden scars. However, further comparative studies are warranted.
