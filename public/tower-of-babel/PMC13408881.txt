Electrochemical artificial humification for sustainable waste biomass valorization and soil remediation
Authors: Junzhuo Cai, Luting Li, Zhang Cheng, Xiaoxun Xu, Chengyi Huang, Yucai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWaste biomass valorization is critical for sustainability. In nature, biomass is converted into humic substances via humification, a key component of global biogeochemical cycles. Here, authors develop a rapid electrochemical artificial humification (EAH) method: unlike natural and microbially mediated humification which take days to years, EAH matures in 60 minutes. Mechanistically, biomass degrades, with quinone-like radicals generated at the anode and H* at the cathode driving primary amine formation, and these intermediates undergo polymerization to form macromolecular artificial humic-like substances (AHLS) whose molecular, elemental and physicochemical traits resemble natural humic substances (NHS). AHLS shows potential as slow-release fertilizers and oxidizing agents, with uses in saline-alkali soil remediation, pollutant removal and nutrient slow release. Techno-economic analysis highlights EAH’s advantages in land use efficiency and short-distance transportation which makes it particularly suitable for land-scarce megacities, while life cycle assessment shows it outperforms landfilling and composting in mitigating greenhouse effects and acidification. This work advances waste-to-resource tech, offers soil fertility and environmental strategies, and meets the core need for scalable, circular sustainability solutions.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Electrochemistry, Chemical engineering

Introduction

The burgeoning agro-processing industry, while driving economic growth, simultaneously intensifies the global challenge of managing organic waste1–3. A significant portion of these organic wastes comes from the food processing sector. A prime example is okara, the residue generated from soybean processing. Its production is immense: ~1.2 kg of fresh okara is generated per kilogram of soy milk or tofu produced4, leading to a global annual output estimated at 140 million tonnes5. Fresh okara typically contains over 80 wt% moisture6, rendering it highly perishable and prone to spoilage. However, the dry matter of okara is rich in high-quality organic components, comprising 20–30% protein, 50–60% dietary fiber, and 10–15% lipids7. Direct disposal via landfill not only contributes to severe secondary pollution but also represents a substantial waste of valuable organic resources8. Furthermore, if this moisture-laden, organic-rich residue is not managed appropriately, its annual anaerobic decomposition releases millions of tonnes of CO2-equivalent greenhouse gases9, exacerbating environmental pressures.

Conventional aerobic composting technology achieves resource utilization by converting organic solid wastes, such as agricultural/forestry residues and food waste, into humic substances10,11. Despite its demonstrated utility, this approach faces three critical limitations: Firstly, it exhibits low organic matter conversion efficiency12 and generates substantial greenhouse gas emissions13. Secondly, the process demands stringent conditions and highly specific substrate characteristics. For instance, the high moisture content (>80 wt%) of fresh okara impedes oxygen diffusion and microbial metabolism14, hindering compost initiation. Consequently, composting okara typically necessitates dewatering, amendment with dry bulking agents (e.g., straw) to achieve an optimal C/N ratio, and the maintenance of stable thermophilic conditions (50–70 °C)15. Thirdly, conventional composting requires lengthy treatment periods and substantial spatial footprint16. These constraints generally restrict facility siting to peri-urban areas, substantially increasing transportation costs for both feedstocks and end-products and elevating the overall economic burden. In regions characterized by scarce land resources and stringent environmental regulations, establishing and sustaining composting plants poses grand challenges.

To address these challenges, researchers have pursued technical optimizations through microbial augmentation, chemical conditioning, and physical field assistance. For example, hyperthermophilic composting mediated by superthermophilic consortia shortens the composting cycle via accelerated organic matter degradation and humification17; Fenton-like systems incorporating catalysts (e.g., Fe3O4) enhance decomposition through reactive free radicals18; and electro-assisted composting augments microbial activity under electric fields, promoting humification19. Despite reducing maturation time, these strategies fail to overcome inherent limitations of microbial metabolism. Consequently, while composting duration may decrease from 30~45 to 20~30 days, transformative breakthroughs remain elusive.

The inherent constraints of microbial humification arise from three fundamental limitations: First, key enzymatic reactions exhibit high Michaelis constants (e.g., cellulase Km = 2 ~ 5 mM), compromising substrate affinity and critically impeding the initial depolymerization of biomass macromolecules into precursor substrates20. Second, microbial metabolic efficiency—capped at intrinsic maximum growth rates (about 0.5 h−1 in mesophilic ranges)21,22—is acutely sensitive to environmental fluctuations, restricting humification kinetics21. Third, the transformation of organic matter into stable humus demands protracted microbial succession (bacterial/fungal community succession requiring ~38 d/~48 d)23, spanning thermophilic (40–65 °C), cooling, and maturation phases (10–40 °C)16,24. These inherent properties render the process inherently resistant to optimization via auxiliary techniques. Consequently, addressing the escalating disposal demands for agricultural and food wastes necessitates a paradigm shift beyond incremental improvements.

Hydrothermal humification technology, which has emerged in recent years, achieves a chemically mediated artificial humification process that holds promise for complete carbon retention, allowing the entire reaction to reach completion in just a few hours11,25–27. However, this technology typically requires operation at high temperatures (>160 °C) and high pressures (5~250 bar), and relies on strongly alkaline reaction media (pH > 10). This not only places extremely high demands on the safety, materials and pressure resistance of the reactors, leading to high equipment investment costs, but the high-temperature and high-pressure process conditions also consume large amounts of fossil energy, increasing environmental and ecological risks. In addition, the stability and characteristic of the products from hydrothermal humification technology is extremely sensitive to parameters such as reaction temperature, reaction time, and pH. Although hydrothermal technology has produced humic substances, it does not seem to be as gentle as in nature and requires more extreme and harsh conditions. These insights motivate a paradigm-shifting proposition: how to replacing microbially mediated reactions with targeted abiotic chemistry to bypass biological limitations and overcome inherent microbial constraints, thereby achieving rapid artificial humification. Mechanistic studies reveal that humification involves both biotic/biochemical processes and abiotic reactions28, proceeding through two critical phases: (1) energy-intensive depolymerization of macromolecules (>200 kJ mol−1 activation energy) into precursor substrates29,30, and (2) stabilization via Maillard reactions (80–120 kJ mol−1) and polyphenolic radical polymerization (50–80 kJ mol−1)31,32.

Among emerging technologies, electrochemical technology stands out due to its core advantages. It operates stably under ambient temperature, atmospheric pressure, and neutral conditions without the need for high-temperature, high-pressure, or alkaline environments, obviously reducing equipment requirements for pressure and temperature resistance. Secondly, its reaction thermodynamics and kinetics are precisely tunable via electrochemical parameters such as voltage and current33–35, enabling efficient utilization of surplus electricity from renewable energy sources (wind and photovoltaic power) and reducing fossil energy consumption. Furthermore, it exhibits conversion efficiencies that are orders of magnitude higher than those of traditional biological processes36,37. This capability directly addresses Phase 1 bottlenecks. Furthermore, electrochemical processes can activate radical reactions in situ, potentially accelerating Phase 2 humification. Notably, peroxydisulfate (PDS), a potent radical initiator for polymerization38, can be electrochemically activated to generate reactive species as our previous reported39. This suggests a novel electrochemical humification pathway: leveraging electro-activated PDS to initiate radical chain reactions that overcome barriers and drive macromolecular humic substance formation.

Building upon this foundation, we propose a core scientific hypothesis: Leveraging the tunability and high degradation efficiency of electrochemistry, PDS can be electrochemically activated to initiate radical polymerization—mimicking natural humification in aerobic composting while circumventing inherent microbial limitations—to achieve rapid artificial humification under mild conditions of normal temperature and pressure. This hypothesis underpins our development of an Electrochemical Artificial Humification (EAH) system. Compared with Aerobic composting (AC) technology (Fig. 1a-Ⅰ), the EAH technology (Fig. 1a-Ⅱ) exhibits remarkable advantages in land use and reaction time: its land requirement is 15 times smaller than that of traditional methods, and its reaction efficiency is enhanced by orders of magnitude over conventional methods (completed within tens of minutes under neutral conditions). In addition, the product of the EAH technology is multifunctional, possessing the triple characteristics of a slow-release fertilizer, a restorer, and a sustainable oxidizer. thereby offering a transformative strategy for simultaneous waste valorization and humic substance synthesis. This study aims to: (i) Establish the EAH protocol using fresh okara as a model agricultural waste; (ii) Identify critical operational parameters via in situ spectroscopic monitoring (e.g., fluorescence, ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy); (iii) Systematically characterize the composition, structure, and functional groups of humified products; (iv) Elucidate reaction mechanisms through operando electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, revealing dynamic coupling between PDS activation and humification; (v) Assess environmental applications (e.g., saline-alkali soil remediation) and perform techno-economic analysis (TEA); (vi) Evaluate the technology’s sustainability and applicability by life cycle assessment (LCA). By overcoming fundamental constraints of biological humification, EAH promises unprecedented spatiotemporal efficiency. This work establishes an integrated “waste-to-material-to-remediation” paradigm, providing critical scientific and technological support for sustainable agricultural development.

Results and discussion

EAH performance

Three-dimensional excitation-emission matrix fluorescence (3D-EEM) spectroscopy was initially employed to characterize the changes in okara under different treatment conditions. Consistent with established conventions, the EEM matrix was divided into five distinct regions, corresponding to aromatic protein I (AP I), aromatic protein II (AP II), fulvic-like acid (FLA), soluble microbial by-product-like substances (SMP), and humic-like acid (HLA). These regions are defined as follows: (EX < 250 nm, EM < 330 nm) for AP I, (EX < 250 nm, EM 330–380 nm) for AP II, (EX < 250 nm, EM > 380 nm) for FLA, (EX > 250 nm, EM < 380 nm) for SMP, and (EX > 250 nm, EM > 380 nm) for HLA40,41. As shown in Fig. 1b-I, under voltage-free conditions (0 V), the fluorescence of the products was primarily concentrated in the AP and SMP regions, exhibiting a pattern similar to that of raw okara (Supplementary Fig. S1), with only a slight change in intensity. With increasing applied voltage, the fluorescence signal in the HLA and FLA regions gradually increased, accompanied by a concurrent decrease in fluorescence intensity in the AP and SMP regions. Upon reaching an applied voltage of 25 V (Fig. 1b-V), the fluorescence intensity in the HLA region peaked. When the applied voltage was further increased to 30 V (Fig. 1b-VI), the fluorescence intensity in the HLA and FLA regions showed no distinct difference compared to that at 25 V. In addition, the UV-Vis spectrum at 30 V exhibited a lower intensity than that at 25 V (Supplementary Fig. S2). Fluorescence regional integration (FRI) was used to quantify the relative percentage of each EEM region, and the humification index (HIX) was calculated to evaluate humification extent42,43. Results presented in Fig. 1c, d demonstrate that at applied voltages 25 V, the relative proportions of HLA and FLA 94%, respectively, with corresponding HIX values of 8.70. At a voltage of 20 V, the total relative proportion of HLA and FLA (88%) and HIX (5.63) were both lower than at 25 V. At 30 V, although the total relative proportion of HLA and FLA remained the same as at 25 V, the relative proportion of HLA slightly decreased from 90% at 25 V to 88%, indicating that a higher voltage causes a certain degree of degradation of macromolecular humic substances. This also explains why the proportion of FLA increased by 2% compared with that at 25 V. In addition, the HIX of 30 V is 7.60, which is also lower than the 8.70 at 25 V. The above results demonstrate that either excessively high or low applied voltage can lead to a decrease in humification efficiency. Notably, neither standalone PDS treatment nor standalone 25 V voltage treatment (Supplementary Fig. S3) achieved detectable humification effects. It can be seen that neither single PDS oxidation nor electrochemical action alone can drive the humification process of okara, the combined effect of both is a necessary prerequisite for humification.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to characterize changes in functional groups of okara and its treated products. As shown in Fig. 1e, the absorption band P1 (3421 cm−1) corresponds to the stretching vibration of O-H groups in cellulose and hemicellulose of okara. Band P2 (2930 cm−1) is attributed to C-H bond vibrations of methyl or methylene groups in lipids within okara. Bands P3 (1646 cm−1), P4 (1417 cm−1), and P5 (1247 cm−1) correspond to amide I, amide II, and amide III bonds in okara proteins, respectively. Band P6 (1065 cm−1) arises from the stretching motion of C-O groups in cellulosic polysaccharides, while band P7 (893 cm−1) is a characteristic peak of β-glycosidic bonds in polysaccharides of okara’s cellulose and hemicellulose. Under 25 V voltage treatment alone (without PDS), the intensities of all bands (P1-P7) decreased (Supplementary Fig. S4), suggesting that lignocellulose, proteins, and lipids in okara were degraded into small-molecule substances under electrical stimulation. In contrast, after combined treatment with 25 V and PDS, the intensities of bands P5 and P7 obviously increased and both shifted to lower wave-numbers, indicating that the original components of okara had undergone transformation. Furthermore, carbon conversion and humified carbon content of the products under different conditions were calculated. As shown in Fig. 1f, under the 25 V + PDS treatment, the humic acid carbon (HAC) reached a maximum of 170.2 mgC g−1, with a carbon conversion rate of 81.87%. This performance was superior to that of 30 V, indicating that further increasing the applied voltage was detrimental to the humification process.

Collectively, the above results confirm that a viable technology for converting natural biomass into HLA via EAH has been successfully established. Notably, this technology exhibits exceptional efficiency in humic-like substance production: the carbon conversion rate exceeding 80% outperforms that of microbially mediated artificial humification technologies, such as composting. The artificial humic-like substances derived from waste okara obtained in this study were denoted as artificial humic-like substances (AHLS). For subsequent mechanistic investigations and assessments of the product’s potential applications, a voltage of 25 V was selected as the operational electrical condition unless specifically stated.

Characterization of EAH products

The humification products derived from okara were thoroughly characterized and compared with the original material. The distinctive dark coloration of humic substances has long served as a visual indicator of humification progress. As shown in Fig. 2a, the color of AHLS progressively intensified with increasing voltage. However, when the voltage was further increased to 30 V, the color showed no clear change compared with that at 25 V. Quantitative analysis using the CMYK (Cyan-Magenta-Yellow-Key) subtractive color system revealed that the AHLS sample processed at 25 V exhibited the highest black pigment concentration (32%) among all tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. S5). To further investigate the structural transformation, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the micro- and nano-scale morphological changes. The electron micrographs revealed a distinct evolution from the initially smooth surface of raw okara to a porous architecture with well-defined nanochannels (Fig. 2b). Following electrochemical treatment, AHLS exhibited a highly porous morphology (Supplementary Fig. S6), featuring uniformly distributed nanostructures with diameters ranging from 30 to 100 nm. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 2c) confirmed the persistence of characteristic PDS diffraction peaks (PDF#00-012-0483) in the electrochemically humified product. This observation suggests that PDS may have been encapsulated within the formed macromolecular matrix, resulting in preserved crystallinity. Notably, the entrapped PDS could potentially function as a slow-release oxidant, thereby contributing to the long-term control of organic-contaminated soil44. In addition, for the application scenarios of healthy soil, we provided another alternative in the Supplementary Information, namely systematically conducting optimization experiments to reduce the dosage of PDS based on the original experimental conditions (Supplementary Figs. S7–S11). The 3D‑EEM and UV‑Vis results (Supplementary Figs. S12–S13) demonstrate that, while keeping other experimental conditions constant, reducing the dosage of PDS from 4 g to 0.8 g and appropriately extending the reaction time can achieve a humification effect similar to that under the original conditions. By balancing the amount of PDS added and the reaction time, complete consumption of PDS can be achieved. Under this condition, the EPR results of the product did not detect the signal of sulfate radicals (Supplementary Fig. S14), indicating that there is no residual PDS in the system. It should be clearly stated that, at present, our target product is positioned as a multifunctional soil restorer rather than a traditional organic fertilizer with a single function. The aforementioned optimization plan for complete consumption of PDS is only considered as an alternative solution for healthy soil applications and is not the core research focus of this study. Therefore, our research mainly focuses on application scenarios involving contaminated or degraded soils. In all subsequent mechanistic studies, performance validations, and application-related experiments, we uniformly used the PDS-containing product (AHLS) as a multifunctional soil restorer, concentrating on its role and application value in soil remediation.

The solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (13C SS-NMR) spectra in Fig. 2d and the corresponding integrated bar graph in Fig. 2e demonstrate that the electrochemical humification process substantially alters the chemical environment of carbon in the raw okara feedstock. Specifically, the peak in the R2 chemical shift region (0–46 ppm), which is predominantly attributed to -CH groups from methyl, methylene, and methine moieties in the aliphatic chain structures of okara, exhibits notable changes (Supplementary Table S1). Compared to raw okara, AHLS shows a higher integrated proportion in this chemical shift region, indicating an increased abundance of alkyl groups. Furthermore, AHLS exhibited sharply decreased integrated proportions in the R1 chemical shift region (46–93 ppm) and at the P3 peak (105 ppm); these respectively correspond to signals from cellulose C2/C3/C5 and cellulose C1 in okara. This finding implies cleavage of the cellulose backbone and disruption of its rigid structure during EAH. The P2 peak (130 ppm) is ascribed to contributions from unsaturated C=C bonds, while the P1 peak (173 ppm) originates from C=O groups in amides or esters. Relative to raw okara, AHLS exhibited increased integrated proportions at both the P1 and P2 peaks. These observations collectively suggest that lipids and proteins in okara were degraded into small-molecule precursors, which subsequently underwent condensation and esterification reactions to form more complex products. Notably, the 13C SS-NMR spectra of both AHLS and natural humic substances (NHS) displayed similar characteristic peaks corresponding to alkyl groups (~30 ppm), aromatic methoxy groups (56 ppm), aromatic carbons (130 ppm), and carboxylic acid groups (170 ppm)41,45.

Further analysis was conducted using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 2f, deconvolution of the C 1s peak revealed four characteristic peaks: P1 (284.87 eV, C-C), P2 (286.32 eV, C-O), P3 (287.53 eV, C=O), and P4 (289.08 eV, O-C=O). Compared to raw okara, AHLS showed reduced intensity for P2 (C-O), lower intensity and binding energy for P3 (C=O), enhanced P1 (C-C) intensity, and a new P4 (O-C=O) peak—indicating hydroxyl oxidation, amidation, carboxylation, and carbon chain elongation during electrochemical humification. For the N 1s spectrum (Fig. 2g), AHLS had two peaks: P1 (400.02 eV, amide bonds from okara proteins) and P2 (401.66 eV, likely protonated amines/Schiff bases/pyridines, due to high binding energy). Relative to raw okara, P1 decreased and P2 emerged. Additionally, primary amine concentration in the system first increased then decreased (Supplementary Fig. S15). These results confirm that amides degraded via electricity-PDS synergy, releasing primary amine intermediates: some converted to protonated amines (avoiding over-oxidation under acidic conditions), while others formed Schiff bases (via carbonyl nucleophilic addition-elimination) or pyrrole heterocycles (via condensation-cyclization). In the S 2p spectrum (Fig. 2h), sulfur in raw okara existed as sulfur-containing amino acids (-S-, P1:160.99 eV; P2:162.79 eV) and -S=O (P3:166.19 eV). In AHLS, these amino acids oxidized to sulfoxides (increased content), with two new peaks: P4 (166.95 eV, sulfones, -SO2-) and P5 (167.27 eV, sulfate). Compositional analysis (Supplementary Tables S2–S4) showed higher carbon/nitrogen contents in AHLS than raw okara—indicating successful carbon/nitrogen sequestration. Furthermore, AHLS’s carbon (52.4%) and oxygen (31.5%) contents were similar to those of NHS. NHS typically have aromatic-rich structures (with cycloalkanes, nitrogen heterocycles, and oxygenated functional groups) and carbon/oxygen contents of 50% ~ 66% and 20% ~ 46%, respectively31,46.

Overall, AHLS have been successfully synthesized via EAH. AHLS have a porous nanostructure, abundant oxygen/nitrogen-containing functional groups, and a highly conjugated system, and exhibit a high degree of similarity to NHS in terms of macroscopic physical properties, microscopic molecular structure, and elemental composition. Notably, these unique characteristics of AHLS will inform the subsequent exploration of its potential environmental applications. Furthermore, its properties—such as functional group composition and elemental makeup—also offer insights into investigating its humification mechanism. Comparisons of product characterization results suggest that multiple reactions may occur between the proteins, lipids, and polysaccharides in okara during the EAH process; these reactions collectively drive the EAH of okara.

EAH mechanism

Based on the analysis of the aforementioned electrochemical processes and products, a hypothetical mechanism for the artificial humification of okara into AHLS via electrochemical humification is proposed. As depicted in Fig. 3, the process proceeds in three key stages: Initially, biomass components—including polysaccharides such as cellulose and hemicellulose, as well as lipids—are either directly oxidized at the anode or indirectly oxidized by reactive oxygen species (ROS), generating abundant quinone and phenol intermediates. This step represents the primary rate-limiting stage in conventional lignocellulosic biomass degradation, yet it is drastically accelerated under the present electrochemical system. By contrast, proteins within okara undergo predominantly hydrogenation-driven degradation near the cathode, yielding primary amine intermediates that are progressively released into the bulk solution as the reaction proceeds. Subsequently, the accumulated quinone and phenol intermediates follow two distinct reaction pathways: they either undergo radical polymerization upon activation by PDS, or react with primary amines to produce C‑N bond‑containing intermediates.

A hypothetical mechanism for the conversion of okara into AHLS via EAH.

To validate this hypothesis, the temporal evolution of reaction characteristics and spectroscopic properties was investigated. First, 3D-EEM spectroscopy was used to explore compositional changes during okara’s EAH (Fig. 4a). Over time, AP and SMP regions in okara gradually decrease, while HLA increase; this indicates that organic matter in okara is transformed under the synergistic effect of electricity and PDS. Notably, the accumulated amount of FLA also decreases over time. This suggests that even though FLA are generated, they are mostly consumed via polymerization into higher-molecular-weight HLA. To further verify this “degradation followed by polymerization” process, in situ UV-Vis spectroscopy was performed, and the parameter S275-295 nm (absorbance slope between 275 and 295 nm) was calculated to indicate the molecular weight of dissolved organic matter (DOM); this value is inversely proportional to molecular weight47. As shown in Fig. 4b, under “electricity-only” or “PDS-only” conditions, S275-295 valve increases consistently over 60 min, indicating a decrease in molecular weight. This is attributed to the direct or indirect oxidative degradation of proteins, lipids, and polysaccharides in okara by electricity or PDS, which is consistent with previous observations. Notably, under the synergistic effect of electricity and PDS, S275-295 valve increases apparently in the first 20 min but decreases thereafter. Additionally, the E2/E3 ratio (absorbance ratio at 254 and 365 nm) is also commonly used to characterize DOM molecular weight changes48. The temporal trend of E2/E3 is consistent with that of S275-295 nm (Supplementary Fig. S16). Collectively, the trends of S275-295 nm and E2/E3 confirm that during electrochemical humification, the synergy of electricity and PDS enhances the degradation of native macromolecular substances in okara, while PDS initiates radical polymerization to generate new macromolecular products. This is further supported by time-resolved 3D-EEM and UV-Vis analyses (Supplementary Figs. S17–S22).

As FTIR spectroscopy shown in Fig. 4c, in the early reaction stage (0–10 min), a large amount of unactivated PDS masks the peak signals of the products. As the reaction proceeds, PDS in the system is activated at the electrode surface, allowing the previously masked product peaks to become visible. When the reaction proceeds to 30 min, the characteristic peak of PDS disappears, indicating that most PDS in the system has been activated by this stage. At this time, the intensities of peaks P2 and P4 of the product increase; this is attributed to the abundance of reactive groups in the system, which initiate radical polymerization to form AHLS. Due to the complex structure of AHLS, it is difficult to determine the sequence of functional group changes using one-dimensional FTIR absorption peaks. Therefore, two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2DCOS) of FTIR (Fig. 4d, e) was used to investigate the detailed structural changes of the electrochemical humification products at different time points. In the synchronous 2DCOS spectrum, two positive auto-peaks (at 1197 cm−1 and 869 cm−1) and one positive cross-peak (869 cm−1, 1197 cm−1) were observed, indicating a synchronous response of these functional groups during okara’s EAH. The emergence of the new peak at 1197 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibrations of C-O in aromatic rings and C-O-C, while the new peak at 869 cm−1 corresponds to the out-of-plane bending vibration of C-H in aromatic rings. In the asynchronous 2DCOS spectrum, two negative cross-peaks [(1197 cm−1, 1290 cm−1) and (869 cm−1, 1290 cm−1)] and two positive cross-peaks [(697 cm−1, 1197 cm−1) and (697 cm−1, 869 cm−1)] were observed. According to Noda’s rule49, the sequence of functional group changes during EAH is as follows: (1290 cm−1, 697 cm−1) > (869 cm−1, 1197 cm−1). These results indicate that the emergence of new peaks occurs after the disappearance of the PDS characteristic peak, which strongly suggests that small-molecule precursors (generated via oxidative degradation by electricity and ROS in the early stage) undergo ring fusion and cyclization reactions via PDS-initiated radical polymerization.

To confirm that this process involves radical polymerization, sulfate radical quenching experiments were conducted first, with results shown in Fig. 4f. After quenching treatment, the production of both HLA and FLA was inhibited: negligible HLA production was observed in the first 40 minutes, and FLA production was slightly inhibited without the “increase followed by decrease” trend. Additionally, tert-butanol was used as a quencher for •OH. The addition of tert-butanol reduced the fluorescence intensity of the humic-like substance region compared to the control (without tert-butanol) (Supplementary Fig. S23), suggesting that the degradation or transformation of proteins and polysaccharides in okara is inhibited due to •OH quenching. Furthermore, in-situ EPR spectroscopy was employed to track radical species evolution near the anode, cathode, and in the bulk solution during the reaction, revealing distinct spatial and temporal trends. As depicted in Fig. 4g, the anode region exhibited distinct semiquinone-hydroquinone radical signals, which increased within the first 10–30 min before gradually declining. This result suggests that the oxidation of okara in anode region, and the produced phenolic compounds undergo electron transfer to form semiquinone intermediates, which may be driven directly by anode. Notably, hyperfine splitting patterns associated with ortho- and meta-substituted benzene structures emerged at 20 min but diminished after 30 min, indicating the transfer or transformation of transient hydroquinones. In contrast, the cathode region displayed a strong DMPO-H adduct signal (Fig. 4h), forming a highly reductive environment favoring hydrogenation. As a result, the total primary amine concentration in cathode region was observed initially rose before decreasing (Supplementary Fig. S15). Meanwhile, in the bulk solution, the detection of DMPO•-OSO3H (Fig. 4i) confirmed activation from PDS to sulfate radical anions (SO4−∙). At 20 min, a partially obscured signal (light red region) was deconvoluted as semiquinone-hydroquinone, suggesting radical migration from the anode. By 30 min, a characteristic signal (g = 2.0025, brown region) emerged, attributable to delocalized π-electrons in developing humic structures, aligning with humification processes.

To further elucidate the molecular composition evolution of AHLS during the electrochemical humification process at the molecular level, based on the verification of the above radical polymerization mechanism, we further characterized the raw okara and AHLS using Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS). The van Krevelen (VK) diagrams in Fig. 4j, k show that raw okara residue is mainly distributed in the region with higher H/C and lower O/C ratios, while AHLS is mainly distributed in the region with lower H/C and higher O/C ratios, indicating an overall increase in the unsaturation and oxidation degree of the products. Meanwhile, the relative abundance in the VK diagrams shows that the tannin-like component increased from 1% to 14%, and the lignin/CRAMs component increased from 29% to 44% (Fig. 4l). Their increase reflects the molecular condensation trend under oxidative conditions, and the enrichment of tannin-like compounds provides molecular-level evidence for the oxidative polymerization of polyphenols to form AHLS. In addition, from the distribution characteristics of NOSC and (DBE-O)/C (Supplementary Fig. S24), the abundance of AHLS molecules is increased in the region where NOSC > 0 and (DBE-O)/C > 0. AHLS molecules also exhibit higher double bond equivalents (DBE = 6.90), nominal oxidation state of carbon (NOSC = −0.50 versus −0.80 for raw okara residue) and average molecular mass (Supplementary Table S5). The above results indicate that during the electrochemical humification process, the polymerization and recombination of small molecular fragments occurred, introducing more oxygen-containing groups, and the overall degree of oxidation and aromatization was improved.

The aforementioned spectroscopic results, together with FT-ICR MS, validate the comprehensive mechanism illustrated in Fig. 3. In the anode region, okara undergoes direct electrolysis and indirect oxidation to generate abundant polyphenols and quinones, which diffuse into the bulk solution. Subsequently, these compounds follow one of two pathways: they either initiate radical polymerization under PDS activation, or react with primary amines produced at the cathode to form Schiff bases. Both pathways drive a gradual increase in molecular weight, ultimately yielding humic-like substances. Three primary pathways govern the polymerization of small molecules: (1) Polymerization via coupling of hydroquinone-quinone radicals. Quinone intermediates generated at the anode undergo single-electron transfer at the electrode surface to form semiquinone radicals. These radicals then undergo radical coupling with ortho- or para-positioned carbon atoms, forming C-C bonds. (2) Direct coupling of phenoxy radicals with benzene rings to increase molecular weight. After oxidation at the anode, phenolic compounds lose electrons to form phenoxy radicals. These radicals undergo free radical addition with benzene rings, generating a new C-O-C bond-containing radical intermediate. This intermediate is highly unstable and readily abstracts a hydrogen atom from the system, allowing the benzene ring to regain aromaticity and form stable diphenyl ether compounds. (3) Ring extension via Michael addition reaction. Primary amines diffusing into the bulk solution undergo nucleophilic attack on the β-carbon of quinone compounds, producing C-N-containing intermediates (e.g., aminoquinones). These C-N-containing intermediates are rapidly oxidized by free radicals in the system, forming new C=N bond structures (e.g., quinone imine moieties). The amino groups in these newly formed C=N-containing intermediates can attack either their own carbonyl carbons or quinone ring carbons, triggering intramolecular cyclization and ultimately generating nitrogen- and oxygen-containing heterocyclic structures. Additionally, some free -OH groups may undergo stepwise oxidation, endowing AHLS with -COOH functional groups—this is corroborated by 13C SS-NMR and FTIR results. Ultimately, through a suite of complex mechanisms including anode reactions, cathode reactions, and PDS-initiated radical reactions, okara is converted into humic-like substances with properties analogous to NHS.

Intended environmental implications

In-situ soil amelioration

To explore the application potential of AHLS (the EAH product), targeted experiments were designed based on its physicochemical properties. First, in-situ saline-alkali soil remediation tests were conducted: as shown in Fig. 5a, AHLS effectively reduced soil pH, with a 2% application rate lowering pH from 9.06 to 8.32. The mechanism underlying this regulation lies in the complexation/chelation between acidic functional groups (e.g., -COOH, -OH) in AHLS and alkaline ions in saline-alkali soil, which neutralizes soil alkalinity to achieve precise pH adjustment. Further analysis of soil structure revealed that AHLS significantly (p < 0.05) improved the aggregate structure of saline-alkali soil (Fig. 5b). Results showed that even a low 0.5% AHLS addition increased the proportion of large-sized aggregates (5–10 mm) from 1% to 11%—a change driven by polar groups (e.g., -OH, C=O) in AHLS, which form composite colloids with soil mineral particles to provide critical bonding for stable aggregate formation. This optimized aggregate structure further enhanced the soil’s instantaneous water content and long-term water retention capacity (Supplementary Fig. S25), creating more favorable conditions for soil microbial activity and plant growth. Notably, the relationship between AHLS dosage and large-aggregate proportion was not monotonically positive: when the application rate exceeded 2%, the proportion of large aggregates decreased. This discrepancy can be attributed to interference from excess powdered AHLS during aggregate structure determination—surplus powder may fill gaps between aggregates or be misclassified as fine particles, leading to an underestimation of the statistical proportion of large-sized aggregates50.

Besides the soil physicochemical properties, 16S rRNA gene sequencing further revealed (see Supplementary Fig. S26 for pre-experiments) that AHLS, via correlated chemo-biological mechanisms, fundamentally restructured the microbial community of saline soil and drove the ecosystem to undergo restoration along a three-stage succession trajectory (Fig. 5cI and Supplementary Fig. S27). Under initial saline stress, the community was dominated by halotolerant Halomonadaceae (Pseudomonadota) and filamentous Coleofasciculaceae (Actinomycetota), a community structure adapted to ionic toxicity but with restricted metabolic versatility. The addition of AHLS triggered rapid community reorganization: within 15 days, the relative abundance of Bacillaceae (Bacillota) and Cyclobacteriaceae (Bacteroidota) increased sharply, while Coleofasciculaceae declined significantly, as labile organic acids in AHLS selectively supported the rapid proliferation of these copiotrophic heterotrophs. By the third month, a fresh community equilibrium was established: the initial dominant Halomonadaceae were almost completely replaced by humification-associated Lysobacteraceae (Pseudomonadota) and oligotrophic Saccharimonadaceae (Patescibacteria). Accompanying this core shift, Chitinophagaceae (Bacteroidota) and Oxalobacteraceae (Pseudomonadota) also increased markedly, while Bacillaceae and Cyclobacteriaceae declined to low levels. Concomitant with the successional shift of dominant microbial taxa, AHLS induced metabolic reprogramming of the soil microbiota (Fig. 5c II), specifically driving a shift in the microbiota’s survival strategy from light dependence to organic-matter dependence. This shift enabled a leap in ecological function via the activation of two key metabolic pathways: first, the establishment of a self-sustaining soil carbon cycle. Chemoheterotrophy increased substantially at 15 days and underwent a remarkable expansion by 3 months, with aerobic chemoheterotrophy emerging as the single most dominant functional trait. By the end of the experiment, heterotrophic metabolism had become overwhelmingly predominant, verifying that the microbiota had effectively harnessed AHLS-derived organic matter to construct a robust, self-perpetuating carbon cycling network. Second, the marked enhancement of nitrogen mobilization and transformation: nitrate reduction capacity was greatly elevated within the first 15 days. By 3 months, ureolytic activity had intensified significantly, a change closely linked to the proliferation of Oxalobacteraceae. Collectively, these processes constituted a highly efficient nitrogen turnover pathway, facilitating the mobilization and release of bioavailable nitrogen for subsequent plant uptake. These metabolic transitions and maturation demonstrate that AHLS can sustainably alleviate salt stress by restructuring microbial networks, while establishing a self-sustaining soil carbon cycle and enhancing nitrogen mobilization, providing core support for the stabilization of saline-alkali soil ecosystems. These findings indicate that the addition of AHLS simultaneously improves the physical, chemical, and biological properties of saline-alkali soil, and the synergy of these three aspects drives the remediation of saline-alkali soil. After remediation, the physicochemical properties of the saline-alkali soil meet the growth requirements of some pioneer plants tolerant to saline-alkali conditions51, which fully demonstrates the great application potential of AHLS in the sustainable remediation of saline-alkali land.

Slow-release performance of nano-fertilizer

Both raw okara and AHLS contain substantial amounts of nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen, and previous studies have confirmed the potential of humic-like substances as slow-release fertilizers. As shown in Fig. 5d, e, AHLS gradually releases HLA, FLA, and nitrogen over time. Fitting the slow-release kinetics of HLA and FLA using the Exp Assoc model (Supplementary Fig. S28) revealed a two-stage release process: the first stage is a rapid release (28–29%), attributed to the quick dissociation and dissolution of water-soluble polar groups on the surface of HLA and FLA in aqueous solutions; the second stage is a slow release (71–72%), which arises because, beyond a small amount of surface-soluble polar groups, most of the FLA and HLA molecules aggregate via hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, or are tightly bound to complex molecular skeletons (e.g., aromatic rings, long-chain alkyl groups). Additionally, the contents of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) and total nitrogen (TN) increase gradually over time, consistent with the slow-release trend of HLA and FLA in AHLS. In this release pattern, a small amount of nutrients is rapidly released to avoid initial soil nutrient stress, while most nutrients are slowly released to provide sustained, stable supply and also avoid stress to the soil-plant ecosystem caused by nutrient excess.

The pH buffering capacity of AHLS and the pot experiment

The titration curves of AHLS and the blank group (Fig. 5f) indicate that AHLS exhibits significant (p < 0.05) pH buffering capacity, and the functional groups most likely contributing to pH buffering are acidic groups such as carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups. To further verify the pH buffering properties of AHLS, we calculated the buffering capacity (β) and deprotonation amount (Q) using the formulas provided in Supplementary Text S1. The results of Supplementary Fig. S29 show that the β-pH curve of AHLS exhibits multiple peaks, consistent with the typical feature of stepwise dissociation of multiple functional groups in humic substances52. Two distinct buffer capacity peaks appear at pH = 7.59 and pH = 9.91, corresponding to the concentrated deprotonation processes of carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups, respectively. In addition, the slope of the AHLS Q-pH curve is steeper at pH = 6~8 and pH = 9~10, indicating intense deprotonation reactions within these pH ranges. This corresponds closely with the peak regions of the β-pH curve, further confirming the cooperative buffering effects of different acidic functional groups at specific pH levels. By continuously releasing protons to neutralize alkaline substances in saline-alkaline soils, it can effectively stabilize soil pH, providing an important physicochemical basis for the improvement of saline-alkaline soils.

To verify its actual effect, we conducted a pot experiment with Suaeda glauca. The experimental results (Fig. 5g) show that the addition of AHLS promotes the growth of Suaeda glauca, with its plant height, crown width, and root length being visibly higher than those in the control saline-alkali soil. This indicates that the acidic functional groups in AHLS effectively neutralize soil alkalinity on the one hand and mitigate Na+ toxicity through ion exchange and other mechanisms; on the other hand, the functional groups in AHLS bind to soil particles, promote the formation of aggregates, block the channels for salt migration with capillary water, prevent repeated accumulation of salts in the root zone, and optimize the rhizosphere microenvironment53. Notably, the growth performance of Suaeda glauca in the AHLS treatment group was even better than that in the control group using commercial potting mix. This demonstrates that AHLS not only has high efficiency in improving saline-alkali soil but also possesses a slow-release nutrient supply function, which can sustainably maintain healthy plant growth.

Sustained oxidizer for removing of emerging organic pollutants

XRD results revealed diffraction peaks of PDS in AHLS. Given the environmental significance of PDS slow release and contaminated soil remediation54,55, this study further validated the application potential of AHLS in these aspects. First, di-n-butyl phthalate (DnBP), an emerging phthalate pollutant common in soil, was used as the model contaminant to evaluate the long-term degradation capacity of AHLS. As shown in Fig. 5h, after adding AHLS, the DnBP concentration decreased gradually within 12 h, from approximately (98.51) mg kg−1 to (13.91) mg kg−1, achieving a removal efficiency of (85.88%). Even after three repeated additions of DnBP, it could still be effectively degraded. In contrast, in the control group without AHLS, the DnBP concentration only slightly decreased and remained at (99.48) mg kg−1; notably, repeated DnBP additions within a short period led to a cumulative concentration of (264.81) mg kg−1. To explore the degradation mechanism, in-situ EPR tests were conducted by placing AHLS in a system containing the DMPO paramagnetic trapping agent. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S30 a typical signal of DMPO•-OSO3H was detected at a g-tensor of ~2.0065, with isotropic hyperfine coupling tensorparameters fitted as follows: AN = 1.4 mT, AHβ = 1.0 mT, and AHγ = 0.15 mT. Additionally, a very weak DMPO•-OH signal was observed, presumably derived from the hydrolysis or transformation of SO4−⋅ Our previous studies have shown that natural organic matter can activate PDS via electron transfer39; thus, the humic-like components in AHLS may mediate the slow activation of PDS. Further correlation analysis between the normalized spin count of sulfate radical anions and the mass loss of DnBP (Supplementary Fig. S31) strongly indicated a positive correlation between the release of sulfate radical anions and the decrease in DnBP concentration.

These results confirm that AHLS can oxidize soil pollutants by slowly releasing sulfate radical anions, demonstrating its potential as a “Sustained Oxidizer” and highlighting its value in long-term soil remediation. Meanwhile, this property is expected to address the cumulative risk of persistent pollutants caused by repeated low-concentration exposure, providing a new approach for controlling such pollution.

Adaptability and scalability

To verify the feedstock adaptability of EAH, we tested five representative solid biomass wastes (straw, wood chips, cow manure, refinery sludge, bamboo powder) under identical conditions. 3D-EEM spectroscopy (Fig. 6a) showed consistent fluorescence shifts from Regions I/II/IV to III/V across all samples, confirming conversion of readily biodegradable fractions (proteins, amino acids, SMP) to HLA and FLA. Their relative abundances also increased significantly (Fig. 6b), demonstrating EAH’s broad applicability for biomass waste humification. Notably, the EAH technique exhibits distinct advantages in treating heavy metal-laden solid biomass waste (e.g., sludge), enabling efficient heavy metal removal via electrochemical deposition onto electrode surfaces. Heavy metal concentrations in electrochemically treated oil refinery sludge were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‑OES). The electrochemical treatment not only enhanced the humification degree of the sludge but also significantly (p < 0.05) reduced its heavy metal content (Fig. 6c). Unlike conventional stabilization/immobilization techniques, EAH achieves genuine heavy metal removal from the waste matrix.

To benchmark the sustainable development potential of EAH against conventional waste treatment technologies, we carried out a conceptual order-of-magnitude LCA, comparing EAH with landfilling (LF), AC, and Fenton-assisted composting (FC) for the simulated treatment of 500 tons of wet okara. The results in Fig. 6d, e indicate that electrochemical humification technology is clearly superior to LF and conventional composting processes in terms of global warming potential (GWP) and acidification potential, the a life-cycle carbon emission of EAH was only 89.03 kg CO2 eq t−1, which was 95.67% lower than that of LF (2056.72 kg CO2 eq t−1), and 86.08% and 84.52% lower than that of AC and FC, respectively. In addition, the EAH technology showed advantages in acidification potential. Cause analysis (Supplementary Fig. S32) indicated that the acidification potential impacts of both landfilling and composting processes were mainly attributed to nitrogen loss. The unique advantage of the EAH technology in carbon and nitrogen sequestration can effectively alleviate this issue, expanding more potential for product application. Accordingly, the conceptual order-of-magnitude LCA results for healthy soil application scenarios are presented in Supplementary Fig. S33. In summary, the preliminary, proof-of-concept order-of-magnitude LCA in this study confirms that EAH technology exhibits great potential in mitigating global warming and reducing acidification risks. It should be emphasized that the LCA in this study was conducted based on specific boundaries and assumptions, and only serves as a preliminary reference for the sustainable potential of the technology.

To assess the practical viability of EAH technology, we conducted a preliminary proof-of-concept TEA. Radar chart results in Fig. 6f show differences between the EAH technology and traditional AC: in terms of economic benefits, the operation and maintenance (O&M) costs are higher; however, EAH exhibits overwhelming advantages in key performance indicators, specifically three aspects: (1) the humification carbon conversion efficiency is 2.3~4.1 times higher than that of traditional processes; (2) the land occupation is reduced by 83–92% compared with traditional processes; (3) the maturation time is shortened from days of traditional processes to 60 min, representing a three-order-of-magnitude improvement. This extremely short processing cycle enables further optimization of space utilization through batch rotation strategies. Based on this preliminary order-of-magnitude assessment, under the baseline scenario, the payback period of EAH technology is ~1.5 years, which is obviously shorter than the 3–4 years for conventional composting (Supplementary Fig. S34). Meanwhile, sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. S35) shows that the key factors affecting the techno-economic benefits of EAH technology are mainly electricity price, PDS price, and AHLS product price, with fluctuations in AHLS price having the clearest impact on the payback period. Reducing operational energy consumption through off-peak electricity use and process parameter optimization can effectively improve the economic feasibility of the technology. The stability test further confirmed the potential application of this method as shown in Supplementary Fig. S36.

From the perspective of environmental benefits, the EAH technology improves soil structure and achieves long-term pollutant degradation relying on the slow release of SO4−∙. Critically, the carbon Residue of EAH is 14.6–19.8% higher than that of traditional processes, showing outstanding performance in carbon sequestration. The technology is particularly suitable for three types of scenarios: (1) urban scenarios with limited land space; (2) emergency scenarios requiring rapid organic waste treatment; (3) comprehensive application scenarios with both soil improvement and pollution remediation needs. Its multifunctional characteristics and distinct carbon sequestration capacity jointly provide an innovative technical pathway for sustainable agricultural waste management and resource utilization.

This study developed an EAH method for waste biomass valorization, completing humification in 60 min—three orders of magnitude faster than traditional composting or natural humification (which take days to years). This technique can be implemented under ambient temperature and pressure, which avoids the harsh high-temperature and high-pressure conditions required for hydrothermal humification technologies (Supplementary Table S6). Mechanistically, EAH integrates anode-generated benzoquinone radicals, cathode-derived H* (driving primary amine formation), and electro-activated PDS to initiate radical polymerization, efficiently converting biomass into AHLS. AHLS closely matches NHS in molecular structure, elemental composition (C: 52.4%, O: 31.5%), and microscopic-to-macroscopic structure, with abundant O/N-containing functional groups. It exhibits multifunctional environmental value: reducing saline-alkali soil pH, enhancing soil aggregation and water retention, reshaping microbial communities, acting as a slow-release fertilizer, and enabling sustained organic pollutant degradation via encapsulated PDS. TEA shows EAH cuts land use by 83–92%, while LCA reveals 95.67% and 86.08% lower greenhouse gas emissions than direct landfilling and traditional composting, respectively. By overcoming biological humification bottlenecks, EAH offers an integrated waste-to-material-to-remediation” paradigm, supporting sustainable agricultural waste management, soil health improvement, and climate change mitigation.

Methods

Materials and reagents

Fresh okara was obtained from a traditional tofu production process at a Fushun Tofu Pudding Store in Chengdu City, China (30°42′35″ N, 103°51′13″ E). Okara (Fabae residuum) is also called as douzha (Chinese), tofukasu (Japan) or bejee (Korea)56, a soybean (Glycine max) processing by-product. The freshly acquired okara was aliquoted and cryopreserved at 253 K without prior grinding. Typical saline-sodic soil samples were collected from Yancheng City, Jiangsu Province (120°28′18″ E ~ 120°28′24″ E, 33°49′21″ N ~ 33°49′22″ N). Samples were taken from the 0 to 20 cm depth layer, subsequently air-dried at room temperature and sieved through a 2 mm sieve. K2S2O8 (99%) was purchased from Innochem Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). All the other chemicals were of analytical grade and purchased from Chengdu Cologne Chemical Co., Ltd. Experimental water was purified to double-distilled water grade prior to use.

Electrochemical artificial humification process

EAH of okara was conducted in a standard two-electrode electrochemical cell (80.0 mL capacity) without separator. A titanium mesh (Ti) served as the cathode and a titanium suboxide mesh (predominantly Ti4O7) served as the anode, and the valid geometric area of both electrodes was 5 × 5 cm2. Both electrodes were purchased from Ilka Electrochemical Space Station (China), and directly used after electrochemical cleaning. ADP700 series programmable linear DC power supply (RIGOL, China) was employed to provide the applied voltage. The experiment was carried out under constant voltage mode at pre-set cell voltage. 25 g of okara was mixed with a K2S2O8 solution under continuous stirring (800 rpm) until a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 (w/v) was achieved. The electrochemical treatment was then initiated by applying a preset voltage at 25 ± 2 °C for 60 min to facilitate the formation of AHLS. To investigate the effects of applied voltage and reaction time, aliquots were collected at specific time intervals under varying voltages. Control experiments were performed without applied voltage or without PDS addition. Finally, the reaction products obtained under all conditions were dried directly without prior filtration.

During the EAH, aliquots were collected at predetermined time intervals. The soluble fractions were characterized using ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-Vis; Agilent 8453, Agilent Technologies, USA) and three-dimensional excitation-emission matrix spectroscopy (3D-EEM; F-4500, Hitachi, Japan). To deepen our understanding of the characteristics of artificial HLS, the area of each region (Φi) and the HIX were calculated. The matrix procedure is provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Text S2). HLA and FLA were subsequently isolated according to IHSS recommended procedures, leveraging their differential solubility in acidic and alkaline media. Organic carbon content was quantified using a TOC analyzer (Shimadzu, TOC-L, Japan), The detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Text S3). The total primary amine content was determined by the ninhydrin method, with results expressed as methylamine equivalents (Supplementary Text S4).

Characterizations

The obtained AHLS obtained were characterized following drying and grinding. The morphology of AHLS was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS-Sigma 360, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The structures and compositions were performed using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR, PerkinElmer, SPECTRUM TWO, USA) spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, SHIMADZU-AXIS Supra + , Shimadzu, Japan), and 13C SS-NMR spectroscopy. X-ray diffraction (XRD; Rigaku-Ultima IV, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) was applied to investigate the crystal structure of okara samples before and after treatment. The two-dimensional correlation spectra using were analyzed following Noda’s method49. A pair of maps [the synchronous (Φ) and asynchronous (Ψ) correlation spectra] was generated by the discrete Hilbert−Noda transform. Details are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Text S5). The DOM molecules in raw okara and AHLS were analyzed by Fourier Transform Ion Cyclotron Resonance Mass Spectrometry (FT-ICR MS, Solarix 7T, Bruker, Germany). The solid extraction procedure, instrument parameters and parameter calculations for FT-ICR MS analysis are described in Supplementary Text S6. The heavy metal contents in the oil refinery sludge after electrochemical humification treatment were analyzed by ICP-OES.

Radical identification

An in-situ electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EPR 200 M, CIQTEK. China) was employed to detect the free radicals generated at the cathode, anode, and in the bulk solution during the electrochemical humification process of okara. In trapping experiments, the 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO, 0.22 mol L−1) was employed as the trapping agent. Unless otherwise specified, the modulation amplitude and modulation frequency were set to 0.1 mT and 100 kHz, respectively, with a scan duration of 60 s.

Intended implications for soil improvement

Soil physicochemical properties

A soil incubation experiment was conducted to evaluate the remediation efficacy of AHLS on saline-alkaline soil, with treatments comprising a control group (0% AHLS w/w) and experimental groups amended with 0.5%, 1.0%, or 2.0% AHLSs w/w. Following 15-day incubation, variations in pH, water holding capacity (WH, %), water absorption rate (WR, %), aggregate stability. Soil pH was determined in 1:2.5 (w/v) soil-water suspensions using a calibrated pH meter (SMART SENSOR) with glass electrode57, while WH and WR were measured following established standard protocols58; Aggregate stability was evaluated via dry-sieving analysis, Detailed methodologies are documented in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Text S7).

Soil microbial community

The 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing involved in the experiment was completed by Shanghai Lingen Biotechnology limited company. Microbial DNA was extracted from irrigated soil samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) according to manufacturer’s protocols. The experiment was designed with two groups: a treatment group amended with 2% (w/w) AHLS and a saline-alkaline soil control group without AHLS addition. Soil samples from the treatment group were collected at 15 days and 90 days (with 3 biological replicates set for each treatment). All amplicon sequencing was performed by Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Operational units (OTUs) were clustered using UPARSE (version 10, http://drive5.com/uparse). The phylogenetic affiliation of each 16S rRNA gene sequence was analyzed by uclust algorithm against the Silva (SSU138.1) 16S rRNA database with confidence threshold of 80%.

pH buffer capacity determination of AHLS

The pH was measured with a Lei-ci PHSJ-5 pH meter equipped with an E-201-D composite pH electrode. The pH electrode was calibrated prior to the titrations using three standard solutions (pH 4.01, 6.86, 9.18). Distilled water was used as the blank group, while a suspension containing 50 mg of AHLS served as the experimental group. The titrant was a standardized 0.05 mol L−1 NaOH solution. A change of less than 0.1 pH unit within 3 min was used as the equilibrium criterion.

Life cycle assessments

A preliminary, conceptual order-of-magnitude LCA was adopted to evaluate the potential of EAH technology in carbon emission reduction and acidification mitigation. Specifically, in the goal and scope definition phase, the functional unit of this study was set as “500 tons of wet okara treated”, and the system boundary is shown in Supplementary Figs. S37–S39, covering raw material acquisition and preparation, infrastructure construction, energy consumption, core treatment processes, final product output, and pollutant emissions during the whole process. Based on this framework, we conducted a conceptual order-of-magnitude life cycle inventory (LCI) analysis for the four okara treatment processes (Supplementary Tables S7–S10). Using OpenLCA software, the GWP and acidification potential of the four processes in treating 125 tons of wet okara per day were assessed. It should be specifically noted that, for the convenience of data presentation and comparison, the functional unit for the data finally presented in this study was uniformly standardized as “1 ton of wet okara treated”.

Techno-economic analysis

Based on our laboratory data and referring to pilot-scale and industrial-scale experience, we set a unified assessment boundary with a treatment capacity of 125 t d−1(wet weight), and conducted a preliminary magnitude-assessment TEA analysis. This analysis included key economic parameters such as electricity cost, capital cost, operation and maintenance cost, and product selling price (Supplementary Tables S11–S14). A simplified TEA model for waste biomass waste valorization via EAH is presented in Supplementary Fig. S40.
