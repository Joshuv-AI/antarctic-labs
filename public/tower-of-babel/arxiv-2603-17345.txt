Abstract
This paper studies randomized polynomial kernelization for the weighted d d -matroid intersection problem. While the problem is known to have a kernel of size O ⁡ ( d ( k − 1 ) ​ d ) O(d^{(k-1)d}) where k k is the solution size, the existence of a polynomial kernel is not known, except for the cases when either all the given matroids are partition matroids (i.e., the d d -dimensional matching problem) or all the given matroids are linearly representable. The main contribution of this paper is to develop a new kernelization technique for handling general matroids. We first show that the weighted d d -matroid intersection problem admits a polynomial kernel when one matroid is arbitrary and the other d − 1 d-1 matroids are partition matroids. Interestingly, the obtained kernel has size O ~ ​ ( k d ) \tilde{O}(k^{d}) , which matches the optimal bound (up to logarithmic factors) for the d d -dimensional matching problem. This approach can be adapted to the case when d − 1 d-1 matroids in the input belong to a more general class of matroids, including graphic, cographic, and transversal matroids. We also show that the problem has a kernel of pseudo-polynomial size when given d − 1 d-1 matroids are laminar. Our technique finds a kernel such that any feasible solution of a given instance can reach a better solution in the kernel, which is sufficiently versatile to allow us to design parameterized streaming algorithms and faster EPTASs.

Introduction

In the
weighted
d
-matroid intersection
problem, we are given
d
matroids on a finite ground set
E
and a weight function
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
The goal of the problem is to find a maximum-weight common independent set, that is, we aim to find a subset
X

E

\sum_{e\in X}w(e)
subject to
X

The weighted
d
-matroid intersection problem is one of the most fundamental problems in combinatorial optimization, as it includes the bipartite matching problem, the arborescence problem (when
d=2
), and the
d
-dimensional matching problem as a special case.
The problem can be solved in polynomial time when

,

In this paper, we study kernelization of the weighted

-matroid intersection problem when the solution size is a parameter.
In general,

, with size and parameter bounded by a function of the parameter in the input.
Kernelization plays a central role in parameterized complexity theory
[
23
]
, as kernelization can be used as a preprocessing in fixed-parameter algorithms, and moreover, the existence of a kernel is equivalent to fixed-parameter tractability of a given parameterized problem.

A kernel is
polynomial
if its size is polynomially bounded by the parameter of the input.
There is a line of research in parameterized complexity theory that focuses on classifying fixed-parameter tractable problems on whether they admit polynomial kernels or not.
To show the existence of a polynomial kernel or to reduce the kernel size, a variety of sophisticated techniques have been developed (see e.g.,
[
35
,
39
,
24
,
27
]
).
Also, various complexity-theoretic lower bound tools for kernelization have been established
[

,

,

,
34
]
.
See also the comprehensive textbook by Fomin et al.
[

Recently, Huang and Ward
[

showed that the weighted

-matroid intersection problem admits a kernel of size
O(d^{(k-1)d})
,
where the parameter

is the solution size.
It is, however, open whether it has a polynomial kernel or not when

is a fixed constant.
There are only a few special cases admitting polynomial-size kernels in the literature as below.

One of specific special cases is the
d
-dimensional matching problem, which is the
d
-matroid intersection problem in which all matroids are simple partition matroids.
The problem is a special case of the

-matching problem (also known as the

Thus, the

Another special case is when given matroids are linearly representable.

The algorithm is based on the representative sets technique (see

In recent years, representative sets have become one of the most widely-studied tools in the parameterized algorithms community, which can effectively deal with linear matroids

,

,

,
21

In the above mentioned spacial cases, a polynomial kernel is obtained via well-established kernelization techniques such as the sunflower lemma and representative sets.
However, the existing techniques rely on how given matroids are represented.
In fact, they fail to handle the case when even one matroid is given by an independence oracle.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our main contribution is to develop a new kernelization technique for handling general matroids.
Using this technique, we design kernelization algorithms for a broader class of the weighted
d
-matroid intersection problem,
where one matroid is arbitrary and given by an independence oracle, while the remaining
d-1
matroids are “simple” matroids such as
partition, graphic, cographic, transversal, or laminar matroids.
The matroid classes discussed in this paper are summarized in Appendix
A
.
Throughout the paper,
d
is regarded as a fixed constant.

The kernel that our algorithm finds satisfies an exchange property for matroids, which we call a
reachable kernel
.
Intuitively, a reachable kernel satisfies the condition that any feasible solution of a given instance can reach a better solution in the kernel by exchanging elements one by one with constant probability.

Below is the formal definition of a reachable kernel.

Definition 1
.

Let
E
be a finite set and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{E}
be
a family of feasible sets.
Let

be a non-negative weight function
and let

be a positive integer.
We say that a randomly selected set

is a reachable kernel with respect to
\mathcal{F}
,

k

if the following condition holds:

for any
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}\in\mathcal{F}
with
t\leq k
,
there exist elements
y_{1},\dots,y_{t}\in R
such that

w(y_{i})\geq w(x_{i})
and
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{t}\}
is a set of size
t
in
\mathcal{F}

for any
i\in\{1,2,\dots,t\}
with probability at least
\frac{2}{3}
.
1
1

1

The value
\frac{1}{3}
of the error probability is
not essential. Indeed, by computing a reachable kernel
c

we can reduce the error probability to less than

, increasing the output size by a factor of

.

When

For the problem of finding a set
X\subseteq E
of maximum weight subject to

by the definition, an instance restricted to the reachable kernel

In this sense, the set

can be regarded as a (randomized) kernel under the assumption that the membership oracle for

is available and it does not contribute to the input size

1.2
Advantages of Reachable Kernel

We here describe a few applications of a reachable kernel in different contexts.

The first application is in the design of
parameterized streaming algorithms

[
18
,
9
,
8
,
7
]
.
Suppose that we are given a feasible region
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{E}
over a ground set
E
through oracle access, and the elements of
E
arrive one by one.
The goal is to find a set
X\subseteq E
that maximizes
\sum_{e\in X}w(e)

subject to
X\in\mathcal{F}
and
|X|\leq k
.
We consider the setting where
|E|
is very large and at any moment we can keep at most
f(k)
elements, discarding all the others.
Huang and Ward
[
32
]
introduced a slight generalization of the concept of a reachable kernel, which they call a
representative set
4
4

4

Note that this notion is different from representative sets used for the linear matroid intersection.
.
For the
d
-matroid intersection problem (and, more generally, the
d
-matchoid problem), they proposed a streaming algorithm that always maintains a representative set whose size is bounded by an exponential function of
k
.
In this context, we can confirm that our results can be generalized to adapt the streaming setting, resulting in streaming algorithms that use only polynomial (or quasi-polynomial) space in
k
for several classes of matroids.

Furthermore, our reachable kernel has applications to improving the running time of EPTASs for the budgeted version of matroid intersection
[
13
]
.
Doron-Arad et al.
[
13
]
presented an EPTAS for the budgeted version, using a representative set by Huang and Ward
[
32
]
.
Their EPTAS runs in
q(\varepsilon)^{O(q(\varepsilon))}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(|I|)
time, where
q(\varepsilon)=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right\rceil
and
|I|
denotes the input size.
Although this running time yields an EPTAS, the dependence on
\varepsilon
is extremely high.
By following the argument in
[
13
]
with our reachable kernel, replaced with their representative set, we can improve the running time of the EPTAS to
2^{O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}\cdot\text{poly}(|I|)
for several classes of matroids, where the dependence on
\varepsilon
becomes significantly milder.

The following two lemmas are basic properties of matroids and the greedy algorithm.
We provide proofs for completeness.

Lemma 1

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a matroid. Suppose that
T,X\in\mathcal{I}
and
x\in X

Then, there exists
y\in T\setminus(X-x)

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that

This means that

for all

By taking the span of both sides, we obtain

x\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}\!\big(\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X-x)\big)=\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X-x).

Hence
x\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X-x)
, which implies that

Therefore, some

.
∎

X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}
.
If
|T|=k
, then we have
|T|>|X-x|
and
T,X-x\in\mathcal{I}
, and hence there exists
y\in T\setminus(X-x)
with
X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}
by the definition of a matroid.
Since
y\in T
implies
y\in R
and
w(y)\geq w(x)
, a desired
y
exists in both cases.
∎

For a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
and for
X\in\mathcal{I}
and
x\in X
,
we say that
Y\subseteq E
is an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set with respect to
\mathcal{M}
if

x\in Y
and
X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}
for every
y\in Y
.
In matroid-theoretic terms, an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set is a subset of a fundamental cocircuit of the matroid obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by truncating it to rank
|X|
.
In the later sections, we repeatedly use the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose we are given a matroid
\mathcal{M}_{i}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{i})
for
i\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
, a weight function
w\colon E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and a positive integer
k
.
Let
\mathcal{F}=\bigcap_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathcal{I}_{i}
, and let
X\in\mathcal{F}
be a feasible set with
|X|\leq k
, and
x\in X
.
If
F_{i}\subseteq E
is an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{i}
for
i\in[d-1]
, then
the output of
\texttt{Greedy}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i},k)
contains an element
y

such that

y\notin X-x
,
w(y)\geq w(x)
, and
X-x+y\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Let
R
be the output of
\texttt{Greedy}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i},k)
.
Since
x\in\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i}
, by Lemma
2
, there exists
y\in R\setminus(X-x)
such that
w(y)\geq w(x)
and
X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
.
Such an element
y
satisfies that
X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
for each
i\in[d-1]
, because
y\in F_{i}
and
F_{i}
is an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.
Therefore,
X-x+y\in\bigcap_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathcal{I}_{i}=\mathcal{F}
, which completes the proof.
∎

3
Partition Matroids and Extensions

3.1
Kernelization Algorithm for Simple Partition Matroids

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1
.
Recall that a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is a
simple partition matroid
if
there exists a partition
(E^{1},\ldots,E^{\ell})
of
E
such that

\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq E\mid|I\cap E^{j}|\leq 1\text{ for any }j\in[\ell]\}
.
Suppose that we are given
d
matroids
\mathcal{M}_{0},\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{d-1}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{0}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{0})
is an arbitrary matroid and
\mathcal{M}_{i}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{i})
is a simple partition matroid for
i\in[d-1]
.
We assume that each simple partition matroid
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is represented by a partition
(E_{i}^{1},\ldots,E_{i}^{\ell_{i}})

of
E
.
Define
\mathcal{F}=\bigcap_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathcal{I}_{i}
, which is a family of feasible sets of the problem.
Our goal is to construct a reachable kernel of size
\tilde{O}(k^{d})
.

We construct a reachable kernel by repeating the following procedure
T=\tilde{O}(k^{d-1})
times, where the exact value of
T
will be determined later.
For each
i\in[d-1]
, we sample each element
j\in[\ell_{i}]
independently with probability
1/k
to obtain a random subset
S_{i}\subseteq[\ell_{i}]
, and
let
F_{i}=\bigcup_{j\in S_{i}}E^{j}_{i}
.
Then we apply
\texttt{Greedy}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i},k)
, that is,
we greedily select up to
k
elements from
\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i}
in non-increasing order of weight, so that the selected set is independent in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
We denote the resulting set by
R^{\prime}
and add it to our reachable kernel
R
.
The pseudocode for this algorithm is shown in Algorithm
2
.

R\leftarrow\emptyset
;

repeat

T

times

1

for

i\in[d-1]

do

2

Let
S_{i}\subseteq[\ell_{i}]
be a subset obtained by independently sampling each element
j\in[\ell_{i}]
with probability
\frac{1}{k}
;

F_{i}\leftarrow\bigcup_{j\in S_{i}}E^{j}_{i}
;

R^{\prime}\leftarrow\texttt{Greedy}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i},k)
;

R\leftarrow R\cup R^{\prime}
;

return

R
;

Algorithm 2

PartitionKernel
(\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{d-1})

In what follows, we prove the correctness of the algorithm by showing that the obtained set
R
satisfies (SingleEXC).
Let
X\in\mathcal{F}
be a feasible solution of size at most
k
, and
x
be an arbitrary element in
X
.

Consider one round (lines 3–7) in the algorithm where we generate the sets
F_{i}
’s for
i\in[d-1]
by sampling.
We say that the round is
successful
if

F_{i}
is an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{i}
(i.e.,
x\in F_{i}
and
X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
for any
y\in F_{i}
) for every
i\in[d-1]
.
We now evaluate the probability that one round is successful.

For each
i\in[d-1]
, let
j^{*}_{i}\in[\ell_{i}]
be the index with
x\in E^{j^{*}_{i}}_{i}
.
Then, by the definition of a simple partition matroid,

F_{i}
is an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set with respect to

if and only if
j^{*}_{i}\in S_{i}
and
j\notin S_{i}

j\in[\ell_{i}]

Hence, the probability that

is an
(X,x)

\frac{1}{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{|X-x|}\geq\frac{1}{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k-1}\geq\frac{1}{ek},

where we use the fact that
(1-1/\alpha)^{\alpha-1}\geq 1/e
for
\alpha>1
and the assumption that
k\geq 2
.
Therefore, the probability that one round is successful is at least
\left(\frac{1}{ek}\right)^{d-1}
.
By setting
T=\lceil(ek)^{d-1}\log(3k)\rceil
,
the probability that there exists no successful round among the
T

\left(1-\left(\frac{1}{ek}\right)^{d-1}\right)^{T}\leq\frac{1}{3k},

where we use
(1-1/\alpha)^{\alpha}\leq 1/e
for
\alpha\geq 1
.
Therefore, with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{3k}
, there exists at least one successful round.

For the sets

shows that
the output

.

is complete.

3.2
Kernelization Algorithm for
g(k)
-coverable Matroids

In this subsection, we consider
g(k)
-coverable matroids, and prove Theorem
2
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{0}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{0})
be an arbitrary matroid, and
\mathcal{M}_{i}=(E,\mathcal{I}_{i})
be a
g_{i}(k)
-coverable matroid for
i\in[d-1]
.
Note that
g_{i}(k)\geq k
by the definition of a
g_{i}(k)
-coverable matroid.
We may assume that
|E|=\Omega\left(k\cdot\prod_{i}g_{i}(k)\right)
, since otherwise
E
is trivially a reachable kernel of size
\tilde{O}\left(k\cdot\prod_{i}g_{i}(k)\right)
.

We observe that, for
i\in[d-1]
, the ground set
E
is covered by the spans of singletons, that is, there exists a subset
\tilde{E}_{i}\subseteq E
such that
E=\bigcup_{e\in\tilde{E}_{i}}{\rm span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)
and
{\rm span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)\neq{\rm span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e^{\prime})
for any distinct
e,e^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}_{i}
.
Here,
\tilde{E}_{i}
consists of one representative from each parallel class of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.
We note that
\{{\rm span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)\mid e\in\tilde{E}_{i}\}
forms a partition of
E
such that every independent set
X
contains at most one element from each part of the partition.
We also note that the converse does not hold; even if a set
X
contains at most one element from each part of the partition, it is not necessarily independent.

Our algorithm that computes a reachable kernel for
g(k)
-coverable matroids is based on the same ideas as that for simple partition matroids in Section
3.1
.
We will use the partition
\{{\rm span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)\mid e\in\tilde{E}_{i}\}
to sample a random subset.

R\leftarrow\emptyset
;

repeat

T

times

1

for

i\in[d-1]

do

2

Let
S_{i}\subseteq\tilde{E}_{i}
be a subset obtained by independently sampling each element
e\in\tilde{E}_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}
;

F_{i}\leftarrow\bigcup_{e\in S_{i}}\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)
;

R^{\prime}\leftarrow\texttt{Greedy}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i},k)
;

R\leftarrow R\cup R^{\prime}
;

return

R
;

Algorithm 3

CoverableKernel
(\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{d-1})

In our algorithm, we repeat the following procedure
T=\tilde{O}(\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}g_{i}(k))
times.
For each
i\in[d-1]
, we sample each element
e\in\tilde{E}_{i}
independently with probability
1/g_{i}(k)
to obtain a random subset
S_{i}\subseteq\tilde{E}_{i}
.
Let
F_{i}=\bigcup_{e\in S_{i}}\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)
.
Then, we apply
\texttt{Greedy}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bigcap_{i\in[d-1]}F_{i},k)
.
We denote the resulting set by
R^{\prime}
and add it to our reachable kernel
R
.
The pseudocode for this algorithm is shown in Algorithm
3
.

Similarly to the proof of Theorem
1
, we prove that
R
satisfies (SingleEXC).
Let
X\in\mathcal{F}
be a feasible solution of size at most
k
, and
x
be an arbitrary element in
X
.
Consider one round in the algorithm where we generate the sets
F_{i}
’s for
i\in[d-1]
by sampling.
We say that the round is
successful
if

F_{i}
is an
(X,x)
-exchangeable set with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{i}
(i.e.,
x\in F_{i}
and
X-x+y\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
for any
y\in F_{i}
) for every
i\in[d-1]
.
We now evaluate the probability that one round is successful.

For each
i\in[d-1]
, let
f_{i}\in\tilde{E}_{i}
be the element with
x\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(f_{i})
and
let
Y_{i}\subseteq\tilde{E}_{i}
be the set with
\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(X-x)=\bigcup_{f\in Y_{i}}\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(f)

Note that
f_{i}
and
Y_{i}
are uniquely determined and
f_{i}\notin Y_{i}
.
Note also that
|Y_{i}|\leq g_{i}(k)
by the definition of a
g_{i}(k)

Observe that

F_{i}=\bigcup_{e\in S_{i}}\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(e)

(X,x)

if and only if
f_{i}\in S_{i}
and

for any

.
Hence, the probability that

is an

\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}\left(1-\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}\right)^{|Y_{i}|}\geq\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}\left(1-\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}\right)^{g_{i}(k)}\geq\frac{1}{4\cdot g_{i}(k)},

where the second inequality follows by the fact that

is a monotonically increasing function for

and by the assumption that

.

\frac{1}{4^{d-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}
.

,

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{d-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{g_{i}(k)}\right)^{T}\leq\frac{1}{3k},

for

.
Therefore, with probability at least

, there exists at least one successful round.

.

3.3
Examples of
g(k)
-coverable Matroids

We have already mentioned that simple partition matroids are
k
-coverable,
which implies that Theorem
2
is an extension of Theorem
1
.
In this subsection, we present examples of
g(k)
-coverable matroids.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is called a
graphic matroid
if
there exists a graph
G=(V,E)
such that
F\subseteq E
is an independent set of
\mathcal{M}
if and only if
F
forms a forest in
G
.

Lemma 4
.

Graphic matroids are
O(k^{2})
-coverable.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a graphic matroid represented by a graph
G=(V,E)
.
Fix
X\in\mathcal{I}
with
|X|\leq k
.
In a graphic matroid, an edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E
lies in
\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
if and only if
u
and
v
are in the same connected component of the forest
(V,X)
.
Thus, by removing parallel edges from
\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
, we obtain a set
F\subseteq\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
such that
\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=\bigcup_{f\in F}\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(f)
and
|F|\leq\binom{k}{2}
.
This shows that graphic matroids are
O(k^{2})
-coverable.
∎

The dual of a graphic matroid is called a
cographic matroid
.
In other words,
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is a cographic matroid
if there exists a graph
G=(V,E)
such that
F\subseteq E
belongs to
\mathcal{I}

if and only if
G-F
has the same number of connected components as
G
.

Lemma 5
.

Cographic matroids are
O(k)
-coverable.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a cographic matroid represented by a graph
G=(V,E)
.
If
G
is not connected, we pick two vertices from different components of
G
and identify them,
which results in another graph that also represents
\mathcal{M}
.
By repeating this procedure, we may assume without loss of generality that
G
is connected.

If there exists an element
e\in E
with
\{e\}\notin\mathcal{I}
, then we remove
e
from
\mathcal{M}
,
since such an element does not affect the
O(k)
-coverability.
Note that such an element
e
is a bridge (i.e., an edge cut of size
1
) in
G
, and
removing an element
e
from
\mathcal{M}
corresponds to contracting an edge
e
in
G
.
By repeating this procedure, we may assume without loss of generality that
G
is
2
-edge-connected.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{I}
with
|X|\leq k
. Then,
X\in\mathcal{I}
means that

X
is an edge set of
G
such that
G-X
is connected.
We shrink each
2
-edge-connected component in
G-X
into a single vertex,
which results in a tree
T=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
.
Since
e\in E\setminus X
is in
\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
if and only if

G-X-\{e\}
is disconnected, it holds that
E^{\prime}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\setminus X
.

Let
\varphi\colon V\to V^{\prime}
be the mapping that represents the correspondence between
V
and
V^{\prime}
,
that is, the
2
-edge-connected component containing a vertex
v\in V
in
G
is shrunk into a vertex
\varphi(v)\in V^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
.
Define
U\subseteq V^{\prime}
as

U=\{v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}\mid\mbox{$X$ contains an edge incident to $\varphi^{-1}(v^{\prime})$}\},

where
\varphi^{-1}
denotes the inverse image of
\varphi
.
Then,
|U|\leq 2|X|\leq 2k
.
Note that every leaf of
T
is contained in
U
, because
G
is
2
-edge-connected.
Let
W\subseteq V^{\prime}
be the set of vertices in
T
whose degree is at least
3
.
Since
|W|
is at most the number of leaves in
T
, it holds that
|W|\leq|U|\leq 2k
.

Let
{\cal P}
be the collection of inclusionwise maximal paths in
T
whose internal vertices are not in
U\cup W
.
Since
U\cup W
contains all the leaves and all vertices of degree at least three in
T
,
the family
\{E(P)\mid P\in{\cal P}\}
forms a partition of
E^{\prime}
, where
E(P)
denotes the set of edges of
P
.
Note that
|{\cal P}|=|U|+|W|-1=O(k)
.

For any
P\in{\cal P}
and for any pair of distinct edges
e,e^{\prime}\in E(P)
,

T-\{e,e^{\prime}\}
contains a component whose vertex set
C
consists only of internal vertices of
P
.
Then,
\varphi^{-1}(C)
and
V\setminus\varphi^{-1}(C)
are disconnected in
G-X-\{e,e^{\prime}\}
.
Furthermore, since
C
contains no vertex in
U
, no edge in
X
is incident to
\varphi^{-1}(C)
,
which implies that
\varphi^{-1}(C)
and
V\setminus\varphi^{-1}(C)
are also disconnected in
G-\{e,e^{\prime}\}
.
Therefore,
\{e,e^{\prime}\}\notin\mathcal{I}
, that is,
e
and
e^{\prime}
are parallel in
\mathcal{M}
.
For each
P\in{\cal P}
, we pick one element
e_{P}
from
E(P)
, arbitrarily.
Then, the above argument shows that

E(P)\subseteq\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(e_{P})
for each
P\in{\cal P}
.

Let
F=\{e_{P}\mid P\in{\cal P}\}
.
Then,
|F|=|{\cal P}|=O(k)
, and

\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=X\cup E^{\prime}=X\cup\bigcup_{P\in{\cal P}}E(P)=\bigcup_{f\in X\cup F}\mathrm{span}_{\mathcal{M}}(f).

This shows that cographic matroid is
O(k)
-coverable.
∎

These lemmas show that
Corollary
1
is derived from Theorem
2
.

4
Kernelization Algorithm for Transversal Matroids

Let
G=(U,W;E)
be a bipartite graph with a bipartition
(U,W)
.
The
transversal matroid
represented by
G
is a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(U,\mathcal{I})
over
U
such that

X\subseteq U
is in
\mathcal{I}
if and only if
G
has a matching that covers
X
.
In this subsection, we extend the result for simple partition matroids (Theorem

) to transversal matroids and prove Theorem
3
.

See
3

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of subsets in
U

\mathcal{F}=\bigcap_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathcal{I}_{i}
,

For

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{0}

\mathcal{M}_{0}

For
i\in[d-1]

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i}=(V,\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{i})

do not share a vertex of

.

Since

Suppose we are given a set

Since a (non-simple) partition matroid is a special case of a transversal matroid,
a similar result holds also for partition matroids.
We say that a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is a

if
there exist a partition
(E^{1},\ldots,E^{\ell})
of

and a function

such that

\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq E\mid|I\cap E^{j}|\leq c(j)\text{ for any }j\in[\ell]\}.

A partition matroid is a transversal matroid
represented by a bipartite graph in which all vertices in each

are adjacent
to the same

common neighboring vertices, for every

Therefore, we obtain the following result as a corollary of Theorem

Corollary 2

For the weighted

we can construct a reachable kernel of size

Appendix A
Matroid Classes

In this section, we list the matroid classes dealt with in this paper.

Uniform matroids.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is called a
uniform matroid
if
there exists a positive integer
c
such that
\mathcal{I}
consists of all sets of size at most
c
.

Partition matroids.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is called a
partition matroid
if
there exist a partition
(E^{1},\ldots,E^{\ell})
of
E
and a function
c:[\ell]\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq E\mid|I\cap E^{j}|\leq c(j)\text{ for any }j\in[\ell]\}.

If
c(j)=1
for any
j\in[\ell]
, then it is called a
simple partition matroid
.
Uniform matroids are partition matroids.

Graphic matroids.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is called a
graphic matroid
if
there exists a graph
G=(V,E)
such that
F\subseteq E
is an independent set of
\mathcal{M}
if and only if
F
forms a forest in
G
.
Simple partition matroids are graphic where a graph consists of independent parallel edges.

Cographic matroids.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is called a
cographic matroid

if there exists a graph
G=(V,E)
such that
F\subseteq E
belongs to
\mathcal{I}

if and only if
G-F
has the same number of connected components as
G
.

Transversal matroids.

Let
G=(U,W;E)
be a bipartite graph with a bipartition
(U,W)
.
The
transversal matroid
represented by
G
is a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(U,\mathcal{I})
over
U
such that

X\subseteq U
is in
\mathcal{I}
if and only if
G
has a matching that covers
X
.
Partition matroids are known to be transversal.

Laminar matroids.

Let
E
be a finite set.
A set family
\mathcal{L}\subseteq 2^{E}
is called a
laminar family
over
E
if
for every two sets
A
and
B
in
\mathcal{L}
with
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
,
either
A\subseteq B
or
B\subseteq A
holds.
A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is called a
laminar matroid
if
there exist a laminar family
\mathcal{L}
over
E
and a function
c\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

such that

\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq E\mid|I\cap A|\leq c(A)\mbox{ for any }A\in\mathcal{L}\}.

By definition, partition matroids are laminar.

Linear matroids.

A matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is said to be
linearly representable
or a
linear matroid

if there exists a matrix
A
over some field whose columns are indexed by
E
such that

I\in\mathcal{I}
if and only if the columns indexed by
I
are linearly independent.
It is known that graphic, cographic, transversal and laminar matroids are all linearly representable.
