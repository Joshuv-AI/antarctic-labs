Polyesterification-assisted interface engineering of carboxylate metal-organic frameworks membranes for selective gas separation
Authors: Chao Ma, Ning Li, Lin Xu, Sa Wang, Juan Du, Jiandong Pang, Aibing Chen, Zhihua Qiao, Jingwei Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are promising membrane materials for molecular separations because of their well-defined porosity and tunable chemistry. However, their separation performance is often limited by grain boundaries and interfacial defects that introduce non-selective transport pathways. Here, we proposed an in-situ interfacial polyesterification-assisted bonding strategy for constructing MOF-rich self-standing membranes using triethanolamine (TEOA) in the UiO-66 system. Under thermal treatment, TEOA is proposed to induce localized interfacial reactions that generate anchored polyester species at MOF-MOF contacts, thereby enhancing particle bonding and suppressing non-selective interface defects. The resulting UiO-66 polyester membrane exhibits 92.7 wt% UiO-66 loading, a CO2 permeance of 1310 GPU, and a CO2/N2 selectivity of 38, surpassing the upper bound for this gas pair, with the selectivity arising primarily from solubility-selective transport. In addition, the UiO-66 polyester membrane shows good moisture tolerance and performance regenerability, further suggesting that the polyesterified interface helped mitigate humidity-induced interfacial deterioration and suppress particle-interface defects. A similar strategy is also demonstrated in the MOF-808, UiO-67, and MIL-101 systems, indicating representative applicability within a subset of carboxylate-based MOFs. These results highlight the importance of interfacial chemical engineering in constructing defect-suppressed, highly MOF-rich separation membranes.

Introduction

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) constitute a highly tunable family of materials with diverse chemical and physical properties, presenting extensive prospects for applications in molecular separation, adsorption, catalysis, and biomedicine1–3. However, despite the successful synthesis of over 100,000 types of MOFs, only a very limited number have demonstrated membrane separation performance suitable for industrial applications4. The fundamental bottleneck lies in the inevitable formation of non-selective defects at grain boundaries or phase interfaces during MOF membrane fabrication, which prevents the effective realization of their intrinsic separation properties5,6. Current research on mitigating defects in MOF membranes primarily focuses on two aspects: engineering defects at the microscopic lattice level and regulating interfacial assembly at the mesoscopic scale. At the microscopic level, strategies such as controlled synthesis, ligand modification, and doping have been widely employed to minimize lattice defects and linker deficiencies in MOFs, thereby constructing well-defined pore architectures with ideal molecular sieving capability7–10. However, even if high crystalline, defect-mitigated structures-such as ZIF-8 with pore apertures (3.4 Å) suitable for C3H6/C3H8 separation-can be achieved, interparticle defects and interfacial defects between different phases are inevitably introduced during membrane fabrication11–13. These mesoscale structural issues similarly severely compromise the overall separation performance of MOF membranes.

The presence of intergranular defects (such as micro-cracks and pinholes) in MOF membranes (polycrystalline membranes and mixed-matrix membranes) becomes particularly detrimental when constructing transport channels with Ångström-level precision, as even nanoscale discontinuities can cause a sharp loss of selectivity14–17. These defects typically originate from the inherent size polydispersity of MOF particles and interfacial gaps during their assembly. The interfacial assembly of MOF nanoparticles remain hindered by two fundamental issues: First, for polycrystalline membranes, the MOF particles are bound through physical interactions, resulting in insufficient interfacial cohesion18–20. Second, for MMMs, the polymer phase often forms a discrete matrix that is chemically incompatible with the MOF interface, leading to phase-separation defects. Even at moderate loadings, non-selective defects persist, and at higher loadings, poor interfacial compatibility facilitates the formation of continuous, non-selective diffusion pathways21–25. Therefore, overcoming these challenges requires a revisit of the MOFs interfacial interaction mechanism.

To address this issue, a polyesterification-inspired interfacial engineering strategy for carboxylate-based MOFs was introduced. The design was motivated by the well-established condensation chemistry used in polyester synthesis26–28, but was adapted here to a heterogeneous MOF particle interface by employing triethanolamine (TEOA) as a reactive small-molecule precursor. Upon thermal treatment, TEOA was proposed to interact with accessible MOF surface sites and participate in localized polyesterification with carboxylate-containing linkers, thereby generating interfacial organic bonding between neighboring particles. Distinct from MOF glass membranes, crystal-glass composites, or conventional mixed-matrix membranes, the present approach did not rely on vitrification, bulk polymer filling, or general filler compatibilization. Instead, it aimed at chemical bonding of MOF-MOF contacts in highly MOF-rich membranes, so as to reduce interface defects while preserving a dominant MOF transport phase.

Recent high-loading porous membranes have also been constructed via covalent polymerization of soluble metal-organic cages (MOCs). By contrast, the present strategy targeted insoluble crystalline MOF particles and was based on localized interfacial polyesterification at MOF-MOF grain boundaries. Rather than coupling pre-installed reactive groups in solution, this approach was proposed to use surface coordination perturbation and linker-derived interfacial reactions to integrate adjacent MOF particles. This TEOA, as in-situ polyesterification-assisted interface defect-sealing approach achieved a MOF loading (92.7 wt%) and fundamentally solved the interfacial incompatibility issue, offering an insight for developing defect-free, high-performance separation membranes. In addition, this strategy exhibited universality for carboxylate-based MOF systems including UiO-67(Zr), MIL-101(Cr), and MOF-808(Zr). Meanwhile, gradient-tunable MOF-808 polyester membranes were fabricated via TEOA-mediated polyesterification.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structural properties of MOF polyester

Figure 1 illustrated the formation mechanism of the UiO-66 polyester, wherein the small molecule TEOA exhibited multiple functionality-acting as a molecular “tailor” that linked together UiO-66 particles through competitive coordination, deprotonation, and polymerization. The tertiary amine group (-N-) in TEOA exhibits strong Lewis basicity, enabling it to competitively coordinate with Zr4+ in UiO-66. When -N- of TEOA could coordinated with the Zr cluster, it weakened or even temporarily displaced the originally coordinated BDC carboxylate (COO-) under thermal action. The decoordinated carboxylate anions rapidly capture protons (H+) from water molecules generated by the partially decomposed TEOA, thereby regenerating free carboxylic acid groups (-COOH). As a result of this process, protonated or uncoordinated carboxyl groups were generated in-situ on the UiO-66 particles. These -COOH groups underwent dehydration condensation with the hydroxyl groups (-OH) of TEOA under heating, forming ester linkages (-O-C=O). The multiple hydroxyl groups of a single TEOA molecule could repeatedly participate in this process, reacting with H2BDC linkers from adjacent UiO-66 particles. This enabled TEOA to covalently bond UiO-66 particles, forming a cross-linked network. UiO-66 (Zr6O4(OH)4(C2O4C6H4)5.6) was synthesized via the solvothermal method, fully activated (Figure S1–S3), and then homogenized with TEOA. Also, the model reactions between terephthalic acid (H2BDC, Figure S4) or trimesic acid (H3BTC, Figure S4) and TEOA confirmed that di-/tricarboxylic acid ligands can form ester bonds with the hydroxyl groups of TEOA via dehydration. This model reaction suggested an analogous polymerization mechanism can be extended to UiO-66 or MOF-808. Furthermore, by increasing the molar ratio of TEOA to UiO-66, a greater number of BDC linkers were protonated and participated in the polymerization. The excessive use of preprocessing in TEOA was essentially a saturation treatment strategy for heterogeneous interfaces. UiO-66 particles exhibited limited solid-liquid contact efficiency; excess TEOA was fully adsorbed at interfacial active sites to achieve complete saturation, thereby enabling thorough interfacial polyesterification during membrane fabrication. Different initial TEOA dosages altered the residual interfacial TEOA content, thereby regulating the polyesterification degree and membrane-formation performance. Accordingly, UiO-66 polyester-1 (UiO-66@TEOA6.1), UiO-66 polyester-2 (UiO-66@TEOA10.5), and UiO-66 polyester-3 (UiO-66@TEOA17.2) were prepared (Table S1).Fig. 1Fabrication and formation mechanism of MOF polyester by small-molecule-assisted interfacial bonding strategy.a acting as a molecular tailor, TEOA linked MOF particles through synergistic multiple interactions, forming continuous and highly selective gas transport channels. b possible mechanism of interaction between TEOA and Zr-BDC in UiO-66.

The FT-IR spectra of UiO-66 polyester (Fig. 2b and Figure S10) exhibited a characteristic ester peak at 1717 cm−1, verifying polyesterification in all UiO-66 polyesters32,33. The FT-IR analysis (Figure S10) of H2BDC-TEOA revealed the emergence of a distinct ester carbonyl peak at 1716 cm-1. The introduction of TEOA enabled protonation of BDC ligands in UiO-66, which confirmed that this protonation process created a critical condition for the esterification polymerization between TEOA and H2BDC in UiO-66 (Figure S11).UiO‑66 polyester was an insoluble cross-linked network system, conventional liquid-state NMR was unable to provide valid spectra for quantitative analysis. The solid-state 13C MAS NMR tracked the bonds evolution of polymerization (Fig. 2c, Figure S12). In comparison with pure UiO-66, both UiO-66 polyester and BDTE polyester (polymerization of H2BDC and TEOA) exhibited a distinct signal at 175 ppm in their 13C NMR spectra. This signal corresponded to the ester carbonyl carbon, confirming that UiO-66 can undergo polymerization with TEOA to form ester bonds34. XPS analysis (Fig. 2d and Figure S13–S17) further supported these reaction characteristics, showing a binding energy of 288 eV for C-O-C bonds, indicative of ester linkage formation35,36.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) results (Fig. 2e and Figure S18, Table S2) demonstrated a high degree of polymerization in the UiO-66 polyester, with a molecular weight (Mw) of 35228, which was consistent with the polymerization reaction between UiO-66 and TEOA. The TEOA and BDC linker reaction mechanism could involve competitive interaction, protonation, and esterification. Under thermal activation, dehydration polyesterification occurred between the -OH groups of TEOA and the -COOH groups of H2BDC, leading to the formation of ester linkages (Figure S19). As the reaction proceeds, oligomers interconnect, leading to a continuous increase in molecular weight. This process gradually yields a highly branched, dendritic architecture, ultimately evolving into a three-dimensional cross-linked network. In conclusion, combined TGA, DSC, FT-IR, CP-MAS, and GPC analyses confirmed that the reaction between UiO-66 and TEOA was a polymerization process exhibiting behavior-a hallmark of cross-linked polyester formation.

XRD analysis (Fig. 2f) revealed that the UiO-66 polyester formed with an appropriate amounts of TEOA retained characteristic peaks of crystalline UiO-66, whereas polymerization with excessive TEOA led to UiO-66 exhibiting amorphous peaks. The coordination structure and local ordering of UiO-66 polyester were further analyzed using atomic pair distribution function (PDF) analysis (Fig. 2g and Figure S20–S21). PDF characterization revealed variations in the atomic coordination distances of UiO-66 polyester-1. Combined with the G(r) curves and local coordination structure of UiO-66, the characteristic distances (l, m, n) between Zr6 clusters and BDC ligands decreased. Owing to the competitive coordination of TEOA, its introduction induced missing-linker defects in the framework and further triggered local structural relaxation and framework contraction around Zr6 clusters. The medium-to-long-range characteristic peaks (o, p, q) at high r values, corresponding to inter-cluster distances of adjacent Zr6 nodes, exhibited no obvious shifts. This indicated that TEOA-induced defects and local cross-linking contraction were confined to the short-range coordination scale. Benefiting from the high topological rigidity of the UiO-66 framework, the long-range lattice periodicity and structural integrity of the overall MOF were well preserved. Accordingly, TEOA-mediated polyester crosslinking in UiO-66 polyester-1 did not impair the long-range lattice integrity or macroscopic topological stability of the UiO-66 framework. In addition, the high degree of polyesterification in UiO-66 polyester-3 led to a loss of long-range order (disordering beyond r > 12 Å), while the short-range structure (r < 12 Å)-particularly the connectivity between [Zr6O4(OH)4] clusters via BDC linkers preserved. This phenomenon originated from selective coordination dissociation: the tertiary amine group of TEOA competitively coordinated with the zirconium clusters, weakening the Zr-OC bonds and promoting partial decoordination of the BDC linkers. As the degree of competitive coordination increased, it ultimately led to the disruption of long-range order in UiO-66. Furthermore, the carboxyl groups released from the partially dissociated UiO-66 became protonated and subsequently undergo esterification with the hydroxyl groups of TEOA, forming polyester bonding units.

As shown in Figures S22–S23, ethanolamine (EOA) and diethanolamine (DEOA) were mixed with UiO-66 to investigate the universality of their polyesterification with UiO-66. DSC and FT-IR results confirmed in-situ polyesterification in both systems (evidenced by polyester product signals and characteristic peaks). With one and two hydroxyl groups, respectively, EOA and DEOA had limited reaction sites and only formed local interfacial connections, whereas TEOA (three hydroxyl groups) provided multi-point bridging to form a more complete and stable cross-linked network between UiO-66 particles.

Moreover, the UiO-66 content within the polyester composites was quantitatively evaluated via TGA residual mass analysis (Fig. 2h, i and Figure S24, Table S3). As the TEOA reaction ratio increased, the UiO-66 content in polyesters -1, -2, and -3 was determined to be 92.7%, 65.5%, and 48.5%, respectively. The observed decrease in UiO-66 content was attributed to the increased proportion of TEOA-derived polyester components in the composite, which elevated the total mass of the TEOA@UiO-66. Since the absolute mass of zirconium (from UiO-66) remained constant, the relative mass fraction of UiO-66 decreased accordingly. In addition, based on TGA mass balance analysis (Figure S26 and Table S4), the proportion of BDC linkers involved in covalent polyesterification with TEOA reached approximately 14.8% for UiO-66 polyester-1. The TEOA in UiO-66 had two components: one engaged in interparticle polyesterification and interconnection under thermal treatment to form polyester, while the unreacted small portion decomposed into gas molecules during heating.

Quantitative correlation between pore structure evolution and gas separation performance

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) characterization (Fig. 3a–k and Figure S27–S32) revealed the morphological and structural evolution of UiO-66 induced by polyesterification: at a low-polyesterification degree (UiO-66 polyester), SEM images showed that the UiO-66 particles retained their original morphology. The magnified image of HR-TEM (Fig. 3i–k) directly visualized polyester interfaces between UiO-66 particles without discernible transition zones, corresponding to covalent polyester linkages. These observations confirmed that the polymerization reaction, with UiO-66 as the reactive precursor, enabled TEOA-utilizing its bifunctional nature, to facilitate the self-assembly of UiO-66 nanoparticles. This process effectively eliminated interparticle voids and established close connections among the discrete nanoparticles. With increased polyesterification, UiO-66 particle boundaries disappeared in SEM images (Fig. 3d), and HR-TEM (Fig. 3h and Figure S31) revealed a transformation from crystalline to amorphous continuum. Therefore, the MOF interface polyesterification strategy occurred during the mild and controllable polyesterification stage (polyester 1), which can achieve a synergistic effect between MOF-dominated gas transport and defect elimination. TEOA-derived species mainly functioned as an interfacial structural crosslinker, rather than as a conventional plasticizer. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis at 77 K (Fig. 3l) revealed that UiO-66 polyester-1 retained significant microporosity, with an adsorption capacity of approximately 150 cm3/g in the low-pressure region (p/p0 = 0–0.1). The pore size distribution (Fig. 3m) showed that the characteristic micropores around 0.5 nm were largely maintained. Compared to the pristine UiO-66 crystal (pore volume ~1.5 cm3/g/nm), the micropore volume of UiO-66 polyester-1 decreased to approximately 0.92 cm3/g/nm. This reduction was attributed to two main factors: (1) Framework dissociation effect: The movement of BDC linkers led to partial pore collapse. A higher degree of polymerization corresponds to a greater loss in pore volume. (2) Residual blocking effect: TEOA molecules occupy pore channels via electrostatic interactions, as evidenced by N2 adsorption-desorption analysis. In Fig. 3n, the CO2 adsorption amounts of UiO-66 polyester decreased from 49 cm3/g to 33 cm3/g, while N2 adsorption amounts dropped from 8 cm3/g to 1 cm3/g. The stronger suppression of N2 adsorption compared to CO2 suggested a selective exclusion of larger, weakly interacting gas molecules (N2), indicating promising potential for CO2/N2 separation.

Gas adsorption analysis indicated that effective CO2/N2 separation can be maintained when the micropore volume exceeded 0.25 cm3/g/nm in UiO-66 polyester. The nearly negligible gas uptake (Fig. 3l–n and Figure S33) in the highly polyesterification sample (UiO-66 polyester-3, BDTE polyester, BET < 10 m2/g) further confirmed that the incomplete UiO-66 structure and pore collapse significantly reduced adsorption behavior. Using the same polymerization strategy with TEOA applied to MOF-808, we prepared the MOF-808 polyester (Fig. 3o–q). The MOF-808 polyester exhibited a similar adsorption behavior to the UiO-66 polyester, characterized by a significant reduction in BET and pore volume, while maintaining a higher adsorption capacity for C2H6 compared to C2H4.

The synergistic relationship between the membrane-forming mechanism and microstructure evolution

The thermally induced membrane-formation process of the UiO-66 polyester membrane with TEOA may involve a multi-level synergistic mechanism (Fig. 4a and Figures S34–S35): (1) At the molecular scale, TEOA may competitively coordinate with the [Zr6O4(OH)4] clusters, weakening the Zr-OC bonds and promoting the decoordination of zirconium clusters and COO- groups in partial BDC linkers, thereby enabling in-situ polyesterification with the hydroxyl groups of TEOA. (2) At the mesoscale, as the reaction proceeds, the resulting polyester network may increase molecular weight and enhance dynamic cross-linking density, forming a synergistic covalent/non-covalent dual-network structure. This process gradually yielded a highly branched architecture, ultimately evolving into a three-dimensional cross-linked network. (3) At the macroscale, solvent evaporation and decomposition of small molecules, TEOA may drive the migration of polyester chains toward the gas-liquid interface, where continuous membrane formation was achieved via surface tension gradient-induced spreading. MOF-polyester membranes were fabricated using thermal polymerization, as shown in Fig. 4a and Figure S36 Specifically, the TEOA@UiO-66 powder was first pressed into pellets under a pressure of 3 MPa. The pellets were then heated to 260 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, maintained at this temperature for 50 min to ensure complete esterification, and finally cooled to room temperature at the same rate, yielding the final UiO-66 polyester membrane. AFM and SEM characterization revealed the regulation of membrane morphology by the degree of polymerization (Fig. 4b–m and Figures S37–S40). The presence of distinct gaps and cracks in the UiO-66 crystal particle membrane (Figs. 4b, f) suggested the existence of non-selective pathways, owing to which the separation precision would be adversely affected. In the UiO-66 polyester, the low-polyesterification membrane (UiO-66 polyester 1, Fig. 4c) exhibited a continuous and dense structure formed by filling interparticle gaps (AFM roughness Ra = 103 nm). This optimized structure facilitated selective gas separation dominated by MOF particles. In contrast, the highly polyesterification membrane (UiO-66 polyester 3, Fig. 4e) evolved into a smooth amorphous continuum (Ra = 35 nm), with no UiO-66 particles observable via SEM. This membrane displayed a typical continuous polymer-like amorphous morphology, indicating that the UiO-66 particles were integrated into an amorphous matrix under polymerization. Furthermore, the results of AFM-IR combined analysis (Figure S41) demonstrate that, in the UiO-66 polyester 1 membrane, the infrared peak intensity of polyester groups was the strongest at the edges/contact regions between particles. In contrast, for the high-loading UiO-66/polyester MMM used as a control, the polyester group intensity at the interparticle edges/contact regions was very weak, and no infrared peaks related to polyester groups (1717 cm−1) were observed between particles. These results provided evidence for the polymerization reaction occurring at the interparticle interfaces.

In addition, the improved membrane integrity originated fundamentally from effective connections formed by interfacial covalent anchorage between MOF particles. The polymer did not act as an independent continuous phase to reinforce the membrane, but rather as a chemical connection that provided localized bonding and stress transfer at grain boundaries. Upon DMSO immersion testing (Figure S42), the covalently cross-linked UiO‑66 polyester 1 membrane showed good organic phase retention and stability compared with the UiO‑66/polyester MMM. This was because polyester chains were anchored onto UiO‑66 particle surfaces via interfacial polyesterification, forming robust interparticle connections. In contrast, polyester in the physically mixed membrane merely acted as a filler with only weak grain-boundary interactions. This observation was consistent with the presence of interface-anchored polyester networks, rather than the gap-filling effect of free oligomers.

The polyesterification strategy should also be distinguished from several established routes for membrane formation, as shown in Table S5–S6. Compared with MOF glass/composites, the strategy did not rely on vitrification or melt processing to eliminate grain boundaries. Instead, it used interfacial covalent polyesterification to integrate adjacent crystalline MOF particles, thereby avoiding the need for glass-forming MOFs and minimizing the risk of pore collapse or structural loss associated with high-temperature processing. In polymer-filled or mixed-matrix membranes, defect control was commonly achieved by improving polymer-filler compatibility within a polymer-continuous phase. By contrast, the strategy remained highly MOF-rich loadings and instead relied on localized chemical bonding at the MOF-MOF particle contact. The key feature was therefore not simply the presence of an amorphous organic phase, but its formation and function specifically at interparticle interfaces, where it suppressed non-selective interface-boundary transport while maintaining a dominant crystalline MOF transport pathway.

Furthermore, nanoindentation testing (Figure S43 and Figure S76, Table S7 and Table S10) was employed to evaluate the mechanical properties of both UiO-66 and MOF-808 polyester membranes. The Young’s modulus and hardness of the UiO-66 polyester-1 membrane were measured to be 12.58 GPa and 0.48 GPa, respectively. As the degree of polyesterification increased, both the Young’s modulus and hardness of the UiO-66 polyester membranes decreased accordingly. For TEOA-derived UiO-66 polyester 1 membranes, mechanical enhancement arisen primarily from interfacial bonding of neighboring MOF particles. As the reaction extent increased to UiO-66 polyester-3, the amorphous polyester phase and flexible chain segments generated within the system endowed the membrane with improved flexibility and ductility.

MOF-808 was synthesized via the solvothermal method37, fully activated (Figures S44–S47). The polyesterification strategy was also universally applicable in the MOF-808 (Figures S48–S76). As the degree of polymerization increased, the surface roughness decreased from 125 nm (MOF-808 polyester 1) to 22 nm (MOF-808 polyester 3). SEM images confirmed that the particle packing transitioned from a disordered loose structure to a dense morphology with no significant defects. The consistent evolution in membrane morphology demonstrated that the polyester reaction enhanced the connectivity between MOF particles, confirming the general applicability of this strategy for defect elimination and membrane formation.

Gas separation performance and universal mechanism of tunable MOF-polyester membranes

The separation performance of UiO-66 polyester membranes was evaluated by regulating alkanolamine species and polyesterification degree. Control experiments, gas transport mechanism analysis and universal MOF evaluation further revealed the interfacial regulation mechanism and universal applicability of the interface polyesterification strategy.

The gas separation performance of the UiO-66 polyester membrane was influenced by the distinct alkanolamine molecular structures (Figure S83). Relative to non-cross-linked samples, UiO-66 polyester membranes derived from EOA and DEOA both achieved improved CO2/N2 separation by partially sealing non-selective interparticle defects, yet their overall performance remained inferior to the TEOA-based membrane. The TEOA-based membrane exhibited the optimal balance between CO2 permeance and CO2/N2 selectivity, indicating that it can more effectively seal non-selective defects while maintaining stable selective transport channels. This confirmed that the hydroxyl groups in alkanolamine molecules can serve as reaction sites to participate in the construction of interparticle connection networks, thereby providing a basis for interface polyesterification.

The gas permeation evaluation (Fig. 5 and Figures S77–S80) confirmed that tuning the degree of polymerization enabled targeted optimization of the separation performance of MOF-polyester membranes. As shown in Fig. 5e–g, the actual thicknesses of UiO‑66 polyester‑1, -2, -3 membranes used for gas separation tests were 101 μm, 106 μm, and 97 μm, respectively (Figures S39). In the TEOA-derived UiO-66 system, the UiO-66 polyester-1 membrane exhibited a balance between permeance and selectivity, with a CO2 permeance of 1310 GPU and CO2/N2 selectivity of 38 (Table S11). As shown in Figure S80, as the effective test area increased stepwise to 0.502 cm2 and 1.131 cm2 from 0.2827 cm2, the membrane maintained stable CO2/N2 separation performance, exhibiting negligible variation in CO2 permeance and selectivity. Two non-cross-linked membranes, pure UiO-66 membrane and a UiO-66/polyester mixed-matrix membrane (MMM), were fabricated for gas separation evaluation to exclude interparticle defect leakage as the origin of superior performance. As presented in Figure S81, the pure UiO-66 membrane showed CO2 and N2 permeance of 47913 GPU and 43545 GPU, respectively, with a CO2/N2 selectivity of only 1.1. The 92.7 wt% UiO-66/polyester MMM delivered nearly identical permeance and the same low selectivity of 1.1. These results confirmed that obvious non-selective interfacial defects endowed the membranes with high permeance but negligible separation selectivity. By contrast, the TEOA-covalently cross-linked UiO-66 polyester membrane exhibited a sharply reduced CO2 permeance of 1310 GPU (a drop of over 97%), while its CO2/N2 selectivity was improved to 38. This two-order-of-magnitude permeance decline verified that interface polyesterification effectively sealed non-selective interparticle defects. As a result, gas transport was dominated by the intrinsic micropores of UiO-66 rather than leakage through intergranular cracks and macropores.

According to the solution-diffusion model (Table S12), the CO2/N2 separation mechanism of UiO‑66 polyester membranes was investigated. The solubility selectivity, diffusion selectivity, and permeation selectivity for UiO‑66 polyester-1 membrane were 32.53, 1.17, and 38, respectively. The result verified that the CO2/N2 separation performance of UiO‑66 polyester‑1 mainly stemmed from solubility selectivity rather than diffusion selectivity. Based on the GPC, SEM, solvent stability, and gas separation results, it was considered that the crystalline connection was more consistent with a localized continuous polyester linking layer rather than individual TEOA molecules acting as discrete linkers. Under heating, TEOA was proposed to react with accessible carboxylic acid linker and undergo further polyesterification, and interfacial polyester segments were generated, which were anchored at particle contacts and helped seal interfacial defects.

The gas performance of the UiO-66 polyester-1 membrane originated from: (i) At a high UiO-66 loading of 92.7 wt%, the continuous microporous network assembled by MOF particles dominates CO2/N2 separation, while the polyester phase effectively eliminated interparticle defects. This structure approximated the ideal interfacial sealing state: adjacent MOF particles are interconnected via a thin, localized polyester network, and gas transport remained predominated by the intrinsic micropores of MOFs. (ii) The free-standing membrane design eliminated support-related interfaces and offers greater flexibility in membrane fabrication. (iii) The tertiary amino group (-N-) in TEOA enhanced competitive adsorption of CO2 within the UiO-66 polyester membrane. However, the noticeable reduction in gas permeance in UiO-66 polyester-3 was attributed to its amorphization and decreased microporosity, underscoring the critical role of microporosity in maintaining high permeance.

As shown in Figure S40, the UiO-66 polyester membranes with thicknesses of ~100, ~300 and ~500 μm was fabricated to investigate the thickness effect on gas separation performance. Increasing membrane thickness lowered CO2 permeance yet moderately improved CO2/N2 selectivity. At ~500 μm, the CO2 permeance decreased to 206 GPU (101588 barrer) with a CO2/N2 selectivity of 43, confirming that thickness governed gas permeability. The high CO2 permeance of 1310 GPU achieved was attributed to the ~100 μm membrane. The comparative summary table and relevant analysis in Figure S82 and Table S13 were supplemented to clarify the correlation among membrane thickness, permeance and permeability.

As shown in Figures S83–S84 and Table S14, representative carboxylate MOFs were selected for universality evaluation: UiO-67 (Zr-carboxylate with different BPDC ligands and pores), MIL-101(Cr) (non-Zr-carboxylate MOF with distinct topology), and ZIF-8 (non-carboxylate MOF) as a control. MOF-808, another Zr-carboxylate MOF with trimesic acid ligands, was also supplemented to enrich the comparison. UiO-67 exhibited similar interfacial esterification features and enhanced gas separation performance to UiO-66, proving the strategy adaptable to analogous Zr-carboxylate MOFs. MIL-101(Cr) showed the same evolutionary trend, confirming the method was not limited to Zr nodes but relied on carboxylate ligands and exchangeable interfacial coordination sites. By contrast, TEOA@ZIF-8 showed no interfacial reaction or performance improvement, indicating the strategy was incompatible with non-carboxylate MOF systems. Therefore, this strategy was applicable to carboxylate-based MOFs capable of interfacial esterification. Furthermore, this inter-MOF polymerization strategy was extended to fabricate the MOF-808 polyester membrane. The MOF-808 polyester-1 membrane demonstrated a C2H6 permeance of 610 GPU and a C2H6/C2H4 selectivity of 5.1. Higher polyesterification led to reduced separation performance (MOF-808 polyester 3: 149 GPU, C2H6/C2H4 = 2.3). The membrane exhibited preferential permeation of C2H6, primarily due to the inherent affinity of the MOF-808 framework for C2H6 adsorption. As shown in Fig. 5c, d and Tables S15–S16, both the UiO-66 and MOF-808 polyester membranes exhibited superior separation performance over most reported membranes, demonstrating CO2/N2 selectivity and permeance. All tests were conducted at 25 °C and 2 bar using a 50/50 vol% CO2/N2 mixture for UiO-66 polyester membranes, and a 50/50 vol% C2H4/C2H6 mixture for MOF-808 polyester membranes.

Continuous testing over 120 hours (Fig. 6a, b) confirmed the stability of MOF-polyester membranes: the CO2 permeance of UiO-66 polyester-1 remained around ~1300 GPU (CO2/N2 selectivity ~38), and the C2H6 permeance of MOF-808 polyester-1 stabilized at ~600 GPU (C2H6/C2H4 selectivity ~5). Online humid gas separation tests were carried out, with the procedure depicted in Fig. 6c. Humidified mixed feed gas was generated by a humidifier, and the CO2/N2 separation performance of UiO-66 polyester 1 membrane was monitored continuously under constant conditions. Humid atmosphere reduced both CO2 permeance and CO2/N2 selectivity, which indicated that humid gas did not generate non-selective defects in the membrane. The separation performance decline stemmed from competitive adsorption between water and CO2 as well as temporary pore occupation by H2O molecules, which narrowed gas transport channels. When switching to dry feed gas, membrane performance rebounded slightly and nearly recovered to the initial state after moderate thermal regeneration. The humidity-induced performance change was reversible, and the covalent interface effectively prevented moisture-triggered irreversible swelling and interparticle interfacial defects. Furthermore, variable pressure tests (0.15–0.45 MPa) also confirmed that the MOF-polyester membrane possessed good compactness and separation performance (Fig. 6d and Figure S85). Discussion

We developed an in-situ polyesterification-assisted interface defect-sealing strategy, in which small-molecule TEOA-mediated polycondensation with MOFs to construct UiO-66 and MOF-808 membranes with covalently continuous interfaces. Upon thermal treatment, TEOA was proposed to trigger localized reactions at MOF-MOF contacts, forming anchored polyester species that reinforce particle connectivity and suppress non-selective defects. As a result, the resulting polyester membranes demonstrated highly efficient CO2/N2 and C2H6/C2H4 separations, surpassing the performance of the majority of membranes reported in existing literature. Moreover, the strategy allowed modulation of the reaction degree to finely optimize separation performance, making it suitable for diverse application scenarios. This inter-MOF polymerization approach was applied to other carboxylate MOF systems (MIL-101 and UiO-67), providing a universal interfacial chemical approach between MOFs and polymers. This in-situ polyesterification technique highlighted the critical role of MOF assemblies in key applications such as separation, energy conversion, and storage systems.

Methods

Chemical and materials

The following reagents of analytical grade were utilized without further purification: ZrCl4 (99.9%), ZrOCl2·8H2O (99.9%), terephthalic acid (H2BDC, ≥99%), and trimesic acid (H3BTC, ≥98%) were bought from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). Triethanolamine (TEOA, ≥99%) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. (China). Acetic acid (99.9%), formic acid (98%), and hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP, 99%) were purchased from Innochem Co., Ltd. (China), and dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%), acetone (≥99%) and methanol (99.8%) were bought from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China).

Synthesis of MOFs

UiO-66 was synthesized using solvothermal methods based on the following procedures38. Briefly, ZrCl4 (0.080 g, 0.343 mmol) and terephthalic acid (0.057 g, 0.343 mmol) in equivalent molar ratio were dissolved in 20 ml DMF in a 100 ml Teflon-lined autoclave by using ultrasound for about 1 min. 0.7 ml of acetic acid (acting as a modulator) was then added to the solution and dispersed by ultrasound for about 1 min. The reactors were kept in an oven at 120 °C under static conditions for 24 h. White precipitates were generated and separated by centrifugation (9064 × g, 8 min) after being cooled down to room temperature. The solids were washed with DMF to remove unreacted precursors, and then solvent-exchanged with methanol six times in three days.

MOF-808 was synthesized according to the following procedures37. Briefly, 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (0.21 g) and ZrOCl2·8H2O (0.97 g) were dissolved in a mixture of DMF (30 mL) and formic acid (30 mL) within a 100 mL media bottle. The sealed bottle was heated at 100 °C for 24 h. The resulting white solid was collected by centrifugation (9064 × g, 8 min), followed by sequential washing over 24-hour periods: first with DMF (60 mL × 3) and then with acetone (60 mL × 3). The final product was dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 30 °C.

Fabrication of the MOF-polyester

TEOA@UiO-66 (The mixture of TEOA and UiO-66): The composite was prepared using grinding method at a stoichiometric TEOA. Briefly, the synthesis procedure is as follows: First, grind 0.425 g of TEOA (2.852 mmol) with 0.2 g (0.125 mmol) of UiO-66 uniformly for 20 minutes and dry at 50 °C for 6 hours to facilitate a complete reaction. After TEOA treatment, the resulting TEOA@UiO-66 precursor was subjected to methanol solvent exchange/washing before membrane fabrication in order to remove free or weakly associated excess TEOA. The washed precursor, rather than the original TEOA/UiO-66 mixture, was then used for the subsequent thermal membrane-forming step. The preparation methods for UiO-66 polyester 2 and UiO-66 polyester 3 were identical to that of UiO-66 polyester 1. The only variation was the molar ratio of UiO-66 and TEOA, as specified below, UiO-66 polyester 2: TEOA (0.525 g, 3.523 mmol), UiO-66 (0.2 g, 0.125 mmol); UiO-66 polyester 3: TEOA (0.625 g, 4.194 mmol), UiO-66 (0.2 g, 0.125 mmol).

UiO-66 polyester 1–3: For the fabrication of UiO-66 polyester, the TEOA@UiO-66 was heated with a rate of 10 °C/min to the temperature of 260 °C. Subsequently, the mixture underwent isothermal treatment for 60 minutes, followed by gradual cooling back to room temperature. The entire heating and cooling process was carried out under nitrogen (N2) protection.

BDTE polyester: For the fabrication of BDTE polyester, the TEOA@H2BDC (2 mmol: 1 mmol) was heated with a rate of 10 °C/min to the temperature of 260 °C. Subsequently, the mixture underwent isothermal treatment for 60 minutes, followed by gradual cooling back to room temperature. The entire heating and cooling process was carried out under nitrogen (N2) protection.

TEOA@MOF-808 (The mixture of TEOA and MOF-808): The composite was prepared using the grinding method at a stoichiometric TEOA. Briefly, the synthesis procedure is as follows: First, grind 0.206 g of TEOA (1.38 mmol) with 0.2 g (0.138 mmol) of MOF-808 uniformly for 20 min and dry at 50 °C for 6 h to facilitate a complete reaction. Next, wash the resulting TEOA@MOF-808 product multiple times with methanol via centrifugation (9064 × g, 5 min) to replace and remove unreacted TEOA. Finally, the TEOA@MOF-808 was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for subsequent preparation of MOF-808 polyester 1. The preparation methods for MOF-808 polyester 2 and MOF-808 polyester 3 were identical to those of MOF-808 polyester 1. The only variation was the molar ratio of MOF-808 and TEOA, as specified below: MOF-808 polyester 2: TEOA (0.308 g, 2.07 mmol), MOF-808 (0.2 g, 0.138 mmol); MOF-808 polyester 3: TEOA (0.411 g, 2.76 mmol), MOF-808 (0.2 g, 0.138 mmol).

MOF-808 polyester: For the fabrication of MOF-808 polyester, the TEOA@MOF-808 was heated with a rate of 10 °C/min to a temperature of 260 °C. Subsequently, the mixture underwent isothermal treatment for 60 min, followed by gradual cooling back to room temperature. The entire heating and cooling process was carried out under nitrogen (N2) protection.

BTTE polyester: For the fabrication of BTTE polyester, the TEOA@H3BTC (3 mmol: 1 mmol) was heated with a rate of 10 °C/min to a temperature of 260 °C. Subsequently, the mixture underwent isothermal treatment for 60 minutes, followed by gradual cooling back to room temperature. The entire heating and cooling process was carried out under nitrogen (N2) protection.

MOF-polyester membrane preparation procedure

UiO-66 polyester membranes were fabricated via an isostatic pressing-thermal polymerization synergy technique. 100-300 mg TEOA@UiO-66 powder was first compressed into discs under a pressure of 3 MPa. The discs were then heated to 260 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, held at this temperature for 50 min, and finally cooled to room temperature at a rate of 10 °C/min. The entire heating and cooling process was carried out under nitrogen (N2) protection. The MOF-808 polyester membrane was prepared following the same method as the UiO-66 polyester membrane.

Characterization methods

Powder X-ray diffraction

Powder X-ray diffraction measurements were conducted utilizing a Bruker D2 PHASER XRD instrument equipped with a graphite monochromator and Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å). The beam setup employed a Bragg-Brentano configuration, with operating parameters set at 30 kV and 10 mA. Samples were measured with a 2θ range from 2° to 50°, with a 0.02° step size and a measurement time of 10 s/step.

Thermogravimetric and calorimetric analyses

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a Netzsch 209 F3 Tarsus within a temperature range of 30 to 800 °C. According to the change of thermogravimetric curve, differentiation of thermogravimetry (DTG) curves was obtained. The heating rate was set at 10 °C/min, and a constant N2 flow rate of 100 ml/min was maintained.

The differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) curve was recorded using a Netzsch DSC 204F1 instrument. The sample was situated within a ceramic crucible and subjected to two cycles of heating ramps. In the first scan, the sample was heated at a rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to 260 °C. Following this, the sample was cooled back to 30 °C before initiating the second heating cycle at the same ramping rate. Throughout the measurement, a consistent N2 protection was maintained. Tm was defined as the offset temperature of the melting feature.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Attenuated total reflection (ATR)-FTIR measurements were conducted on a Bruker TENSOR ІІ device in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. For data processing, the extended ATR correction algorithms in the OPUS 8.0 software were used for spectral data correction and peak fitting.

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

The 13C MAS solid-state NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance NEO 600 NMR spectrometer with cross-polarization magic angle spinning (CP-MAS) at a 13C frequency of 120 MHz under 10 kHz spinning rate for UiO-66, UiO-66 polyester, BDTE polyester, MOF-808, MOF-808 polyester, and BTTE polyester samples.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC)

The number-average molecular weight (Mn) and weight-average molecular weight (Mw) were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a polystyrene gel column. Hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) was used as the eluent at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, with samples prepared at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL.

X-ray pair distribution function (XPDF)

X-ray total scattering data for selected MOF materials and their crystalline counterparts were gathered at the 13w beamline at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). All sample data were gathered with the Qmax of 25.0 Å−1 (λ = 0.161669 Å, 76.69 keV). Data were radically integrated, normalized and then the PDFs were calculated with the DiffPy-CMI library with the GudrunX program.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology of the sample was analyzed using a ZEISS CrossBeam 550 instrument featuring a dual beam-focused ion beam (FIB). Prior to imaging, all samples underwent an 8-hour drying process under vacuum conditions and were subsequently surface-coated with gold. A quick plasma surface cleaning procedure was carried out on all samples. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging was conducted at an electron acceleration voltage of 5 kV, utilizing both secondary electron and back-scattering modes.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) analysis was conducted using a Jeol JEM-F200 instrument equipped with a Gatan CMOS CCD sensor. The operating voltage is 200 kV. TEM imaging was performed under dark-field conditions. Sample preparation included ultrathin sectioning. TEM samples were prepared by an FIB sectioning instrument (liquid metal Ga ion source 3000 μAh, the electron acceleration voltage of 30 kV, and the current of approximately 10−100 nA).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to analyse the phase state of MOF-polyester membrane using an AFM5100N (Hitachi, Japan) instrument. The phase state images were captured under tapping mode using SI-DF40P2 probes (Hitachi, Japan). The resonant frequency of the microcantilever was 286 kHz with a nominal spring constant of 28 N/m. Bruker AFM5100N was used to process the AFM images. Localized nanoscale IR spectral measurements were conducted on a NanoIR3 AFM-IR instrument (Bruker, Germany), which integrated atomic force microscopy with a pulsed tunable infrared source.

Gas adsorption-desorption tests

Gas adsorption-desorption isotherms (CO2, N2, C2H6, and C2H4) were investigated using a Micromeritics ASAP-2020 instrument. Gas isotherms were recorded at various temperatures, including 77 K and 298 K. Pore size distribution data are based on the nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) method.

Gas permeation testing

In the gas permeation test system, a constant driving force (i.e., trans-membrane pressure difference) was precisely maintained by the gas flow control system. Specifically, the pressure on the feed side was regulated by a combination of high-precision back-pressure regulators and mass flow controllers (MFCs) to ensure stable pressure throughout the test process, while the permeation side was directly connected to the continuous He sweep stream. Meanwhile, the entire membrane cell was maintained at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), ensuring a constant driving force during testing. The mixed gas permeance (P) (50:50) of the membranes was determined by laboratory-made test equipment at 25 °C and 2 bar. Cover the measured membrane with aluminium tape, seal it with epoxy resins, and install it on the permeation cell (Figures S70–S71). Both the feed gas and sweeping gas flow rates were set at 40 ml/min. For each type of membrane, three different samples were measured, and five different points were selected for each sample to obtain the average permeation data and the corresponding standard deviation (±s.d.).
