Obesity as a risk factor for acute lymphoblastic leukemia in individuals younger than 20 years across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021: a benchmarking global analysis
Authors: Aishwar Dixit, Abdul Rafae Faisal, Asad Zaman, Muhammad Faizan, Ahila Ali, Syed Zaeem Ahmad, Henna A. Qadri, Mohammad Maaz Alam, Pramod Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Childhood obesity has increased substantially over the past three decades and has been implicated in the development and progression of several malignancies, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). This study evaluated global trends in the ALL burden attributable to high body mass index (BMI) from 1990 to 2021 using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data.Methods:We conducted an ecological, population-level, cross-sectional time-trend analysis across 204 countries and territories. Age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), mortality rates, and disability-adjusted life year rates per 100 000 population were extracted. Joinpoint regression was used to assess temporal trends, and Spearman correlation was employed to evaluate associations between national obesity prevalence and ALL incidence. Estimates were derived from GBD comparative risk assessment modeling and are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals.Results:Globally, the ASIR of ALL attributable to high BMI increased from 0.83 to 0.95 per 100 000 between 1990 and 2021 (a 14.2% increase). The highest burden was observed in North America and Western Europe, while middle-income regions demonstrated rising incidence and mortality trends. Country-level obesity prevalence was strongly correlated with ASIR (ρ = 0.76, P < 0.01), indicating a population-level association. Lower reported rates in low-income countries likely reflect underdiagnosis and limited cancer registry coverage. GBD modeling attributed approximately 14.2% of the observed global increase in ALL incidence to high BMI exposure.Conclusion:This ecological analysis demonstrates a population-level association between rising obesity prevalence and increasing global ALL burden. However, causal inference cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design and reliance on modeled estimates. Findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in data-limited settings. Longitudinal and individual-level studies are needed to clarify causality and inform targeted prevention strategies.

Introduction

ALL represents the most widespread hematologic malignancy in children, which occurs in 25% of all pediatric cancers and 75% of childhood leukemia cases globally[1]. ALL continues to be the primary cause of cancer-related deaths in children worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries where children cannot access appropriate diagnoses or effective treatments[2]. The growing number of children who become obese matches the increasing incidence of ALL[3,4]. ALL is presumed to develop from multiple risk factors, including genetic elements alongside environmental factors and lifestyle variables.

Childhood and adolescent obesity rates have increased globally over the past few decades, becoming a critical public health issue that causes various health problems, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and leukemia, among other cancers[5]. The development of leukemia through obesity has been supported by research, which demonstrates that chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and alterations in adipokine signaling create a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment[6]. Studies have found that obesity at the time of ALL diagnosis leads to worse treatment outcomes and elevated rates of relapse and death[5].

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study provides a comprehensive framework for estimating the incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) associated with various diseases and risk factors worldwide. Little research exists on measuring the global acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) disease burden that results from high body mass index (BMI). The analysis of this relationship enables healthcare professionals to create specific public health programs that aim to decrease ALL incidence and enhance treatment success in obese populations.

This article analyzes the worldwide burden of ALL caused by high BMI using the GBD Study data from 1990 to 2021. The analysis will determine ASIR, as well as ASMR and DALY rates, across multiple geographic regions while examining different income classification levels. Our analysis investigates how global obesity levels affect ALL incidence rates to demonstrate how obesity can be controlled to reduce leukemia development.

The GBD study offers a useful approach to estimate the incidence, mortality, and DALYs of various diseases and risk factors across the globe. Nevertheless, there is a lack of studies that have specifically quantified the impact of high BMI on the incidence of ALL worldwide. This is important for the development of specific public health interventions to reduce the incidence of ALL and to improve treatment results among obese individuals. Methods

Study design and data sources

The ecological study investigates how high BMI affects ALL prevalence across 204 countries and territories during the period spanning from 1990 to 2021. The main database used in this research comes from the GBD 2019 and 2021 studies, which were compiled by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)[7–12]. The GBD study enables standardized epidemiological estimates to facilitate consistent comparisons between regions and time points. We analyzed GBD data through 2021 because the Global Burden of Disease 2021 release was the most recent complete, validated global dataset available at the time of our analysis. Subsequent GBD releases (if and when available) can be used for future updates. GBD estimates are derived using Bayesian meta-regression modeling (DisMod-MR) and comparative risk assessment (CRA) frameworks. In regions with sparse primary cancer registry or mortality data, estimates rely more heavily on statistical modeling and covariates, which may increase uncertainty.

Study population

The research subjects consist of people under the age of 20, as most ALL cases emerge during childhood and adolescence. The research examines ALL trends at three levels: global, regional, and national, to evaluate age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR), and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rates (ASDALRs) per 100 000 person-years.

Definition of metrics

ASIR: New ALL cases occur per 100 000 people when the age distribution is normalized.

ASMR: Number of deaths due to ALL per 100 000 individuals.

ASDALR combines the effects of years of life lost due to premature mortality with years lived with disability into a single measurement of disease burden.

Risk factor attribution

The study found obesity to be a principal risk factor that individuals can modify to lower their ALL risk. The CRA framework from the GBD study enabled researchers to calculate the proportion of ALL cases that resulted from high BMI exposure. Using this method, researchers determined the population attributable fraction (PAF) to show how many ALL cases would have been prevented if high BMI exposure had been eliminated. BMI classifications, based on World Health Organization growth standards, used one standard deviation above the median for age and sex to define high BMI. The attribution reflects modeled population-level associations and does not establish individual-level causality.

Literature search

To support our findings, we performed a thorough literature search. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to 31 December 2024, and supplemented the searches with Google Scholar for highly cited reviews. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free text and included permutations of: “acute lymphoblastic leukemia,” “ALL,” “childhood leukemia,” “obesity,” “body mass index,” “BMI,” “adiposity,” “leukemogenesis,” “treatment outcomes,” and “survival.” We screened titles and abstracts for relevance and reviewed full texts when necessary. Inclusion criteria were: (1) original research, reviews, or meta-analyses addressing obesity/BMI and ALL risk, treatment response, or biologic mechanisms; (2) pediatric or adolescent populations when relevant; and (3) English language. Exclusion criteria were non-peer-reviewed articles, conference abstracts without full text, and animal-only studies unless directly informative for mechanisms cited. Key articles supporting mechanistic pathways and treatment-outcome associations were prioritized.

Regional and income-based comparisons

The study grouped countries into four World Bank income levels, which included high-income countries (HICs), upper middle-income countries (UMICs), lower middle-income countries (LMICs), and low-income countries (LICs). Gross national income per capita determines these economic classification categories, which serve as a standard system for evaluating national economic status and healthcare results between different countries. The GBD study divides the world into 21 regions, which group into seven super-regions for better epidemiological precision and comparison. The high-income super-region contains four groups: High-income North America, Western Europe, High-income Asia Pacific, and Australasia. The super-region of Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia contains three separate regions: Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia. The Latin America and Caribbean super-region consists of three sub-regions: Andean Latin America, Central Latin America, and the Caribbean. The region of North Africa and the Middle East exists as one single entity. South Asia exists independently as both a region and a super-region. Southeast Asia, together with East Asia and Oceania, forms the Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania super-region. The Sub-Saharan Africa super-region divides into four distinct regions, including Central, Eastern, Southern, and Western Sub-Saharan Africa. Standardized classification systems allow for uniform health assessment across the globe. Research results demonstrated differing patterns of ALL incidence together with obesity prevalence between income groups and regional areas.

Statistical analysis

Trend analysis

The research analyzed temporal changes in ASIR, ASMR, and ASDALR through Joinpoint regression analysis during the period spanning from 1990 to 2021. The analysis detects inflection points that indicate significant trend changes and uses annual percentage changes (APC) to determine the speed of time-based modifications.

Correlation analysis

The research applied Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to investigate how obesity prevalence affects ALL incidence rates. The research predicted that nations with higher obesity rates would demonstrate higher ALL incidence, which confirms that obesity plays a role in leukemogenesis.

Sensitivity and robustness analysis

To verify the stability of the obtained results, the research used sensitivity tests. Multiple modeling techniques, such as Bayesian hierarchical modeling and Monte Carlo simulations, were applied to model uncertainties in obesity exposure data and leukemia outcomes. The analysis evaluated various confounding elements, which included population variations such as dietary patterns, genetic susceptibility, and healthcare system access across different population groups.

Data processing and software

The statistical analysis for this study used R software version 4.0.3, together with Stata version 16, as its execution platforms. The researchers employed heat maps and time-series plots to visualize how ALL burden from high BMI patterns shifted across regions and time periods.

Reporting of uncertainty and confidence intervals

All point estimates (ASIR, ASMR, ASDALR) are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), as provided by the Global Burden of Disease 2021 dataset. Joinpoint regression results include APC and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Spearman’s correlation coefficients are presented with 95% CIs, estimated using Fisher’s z-transformation. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The GBD 2021 database served as the data source for this research because it contained de-identified information that met ethical research requirements. The study included no human participants; thus, ethical approval was unnecessary. The research presents crucial epidemiological findings about the effect of high BMI on ALL incidence across the globe through its comprehensive methods, thus demonstrating the necessity for specific public health intervention programs to reduce leukemia risks from obesity.

Evidence grading

For key studies cited in the mechanistic and treatment-outcome sections, we recorded the study design and assigned a level of evidence using a modified Oxford Center for Evidence-Based Medicine hierarchy (Level 1 = systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized trials or high-quality cohort studies; Level 2 = cohort studies; Level 3 = case-control studies; Level 4 = case series; Level 5 = mechanistic/expert opinion).

Results

Between 1990 and 2021, the global ASIR of ALL attributable to high BMI increased from 0.83 per 100 000 to 0.95 per 100 000, representing a 14.2% increase over three decades. The highest incidence rates were found in North America and Western Europe, where ASIR values exceeded 1.5 per 100 000 in some countries. Disparities were observed across regions, with middle-income regions such as Latin America and Southeast Asia showing rising trends in ALL incidence. Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia reported lower incidence rates, which may be attributed to underdiagnosis and limited healthcare access rather than a lower true disease burden. Among the countries, the highest incidence was observed in Palau, Mongolia, and Brunei Darussalam, indicating a very high disease burden in these areas. The lowest ASIRs were found in Niger, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Burundi, which may reflect underdiagnosis, limited cancer registry coverage, and incomplete reporting rather than a lower true disease burden (Fig. 1). Therefore, observed lower rates in low-income regions should be interpreted cautiously. Country-level Spearman’s rank correlation between national obesity prevalence and ASIR was ρ = 0.76 (95% CI: 0.70–0.81), P < 0.01 (n = 204 countries/territories). The United States, Mexico, and several Western European countries had the highest obesity rates and, consequently, the highest ALL burdens. On the other hand, countries with low obesity prevalence, such as those in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, had low ALL incidence rates. This could also be influenced by factors such as healthcare accessibility, diagnostic capacity, and reporting accuracy in these regions.

ALL burden was different in each of the income categories. HICs had constant mortality and low DALY rates due to better healthcare systems and treatment. The incidence and mortality rates in UMICs increased because of the ongoing epidemiologic transitions and rising obesity rates. LMICs showed an increase in incidence with minimal survival improvements, which may be attributed to health inequalities and limited leukemia management capabilities. LICs had low incidence and mortality rates, but these are likely to be underestimates due to the challenges of diagnosing and reporting in these regions.

This study demonstrates that obesity produces different effects on ALL throughout various age groups as well as gender groups. Collaboration between researchers has established that ALL incidence rates reach their highest levels within the age range of 5–14 years. The growing number of adolescent obesity cases corresponds to a steady rise in ALL incidence rates within this age group. All age groups showed that boys developed ALL more often than girls, while the male-to-female ALL incidence ratio stood at about 1.3:1, thus confirming scientific knowledge regarding leukemia susceptibility.

The sensitivity analyses demonstrated that these research findings maintained consistency. High confidence intervals from Monte Carlo simulations verified the relationship between obesity and ALL development. Bayes hierarchical modeling, which corrected for healthcare regional variations, supported the observed incidence and mortality trends. The main results were not altered by healthcare access adjustments, along with genetic predisposition and dietary factor modifications, thus confirming obesity’s impact on leukemia risk.

GBD comparative risk assessment modeling attributed approximately 14.2% of the observed increase in global ALL incidence to high BMI exposure at the population level. The death rate from ALL stayed steady in developed countries, yet it rose throughout middle-income nations. The disability burden (DALYs) tracked mortality patterns because regions with fast-growing obesity experienced elevated rates. The correlation between national obesity prevalence and ALL incidence was substantial, with an r value of 0.76 that reached statistical significance (P < 0.01). Among both genders, the disease burden reached its highest point in the 5-14 age bracket among boys. Lower-income regions demonstrated increasing patterns of death and DALY incidence. These findings suggest that high BMI may represent an important contributing factor to the rising global burden of ALL, although causality cannot be inferred from this ecological design.

Discussion

Rising global burden of obesity-related ALL

The growing incidence of ALL in obese patients has become a significant worldwide health issue. The rates of obesity among children and adolescents have increased substantially during the last 30 years, alongside a parallel growth in ALL occurrence. This research indicates that the global ASIR of ALL caused by obesity has increased by 14.2% from 1990 to 2021. The increase in obesity rates is most noticeable in HICs, which include North America and Western Europe, because these regions have the highest childhood obesity rates worldwide. MICs, including Latin America, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East, have experienced rising childhood obesity rates due to rapid urbanization, sedentary habits, and dietary modifications[3–6].

LICs in sub-Saharan Africa, along with South Asian regions, show lower ALL incidence and mortality rates. Underdiagnosis, together with poor cancer surveillance systems and insufficient access to specialized leukemia care, seems to explain the reported lower disease burden rather than actual lower incidence and mortality rates. The expected rise in ALL incidence due to increasing obesity rates worldwide demonstrates the immediate need for preventive strategies and early screening programs, along with global policy interventions to manage this developing crisis[13]. The westernization of diets in MICs, characterized by higher intake of processed foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, and calorie-dense foods, has contributed significantly to the obesity epidemic. Children face higher risks of developing ALL and other obesity-related malignancies because of their consumption of processed foods, reduced physical activity levels, and sedentary behavior. Early-life obesity plays a crucial role in developing hematologic malignancies, according to recent epidemiological studies[14]. Children who are obese face higher chances of leukemia development, along with decreased treatment effectiveness and higher relapse rates compared to their non-obese peers[15]. Public health strategies must focus on obesity prevention among children, as well as enhance medical accessibility and develop combined solutions for leukemia treatment and prevention[16].

Biological mechanisms linking obesity to ALL

Obesity is increasingly recognized as an active metabolic and inflammatory state that may influence leukemogenesis. Chronic low-grade inflammation is a key mechanism, as expanded adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which may disrupt normal hematopoiesis and promote a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment[17,18]. Insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia further elevate circulating IGF-1 levels, enhancing cellular proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis in leukemic precursors[19].

Altered adipokine signaling also contributes to this process. Elevated leptin levels may support leukemic cell survival and chemoresistance, whereas reduced adiponectin levels diminish anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative signaling[20,21]. Obesity-associated metabolic dysfunction has additionally been linked to epigenetic modifications and oxidative stress, potentially promoting genomic instability and malignant transformation[22,23]. Changes in the bone marrow microenvironment in obese individuals may further impair immune surveillance and support leukemic progression[24].

Although these pathways provide biological plausibility, they do not establish causality within an ecological framework.

Impact of obesity on ALL treatment outcomes

The implications of obesity extend beyond disease development and may influence treatment response and long-term outcomes. Several studies report higher minimal residual disease levels and increased relapse risk among obese pediatric ALL patients[25,26]. Obesity-related metabolic alterations may affect chemotherapy pharmacokinetics, influencing drug distribution, metabolism, and toxicity profiles[27,28].

Obese patients may also experience higher rates of treatment-related complications, including infections, thrombosis, corticosteroid-induced hyperglycemia, and metabolic disturbances[29–31]. Additionally, survivors of childhood ALL with persistent obesity face increased risks of long-term cardiometabolic morbidity and secondary health complications[32–34].

These findings highlight the need to consider metabolic status during leukemia management while recognizing that observational associations do not necessarily imply direct causation.

Regional and socioeconomic disparities in obesity-related ALL

Obesity-related ALL affects different populations with various socioeconomic backgrounds due to disparities in healthcare delivery systems and medical facilities. The global epidemiological transition is causing a rise in ALL incidence in MICs throughout Latin America, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East, even though HICs used to have higher obesity-related ALL incidence rates. The reported ALL incidence rates in LICs, including sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, remain low because of insufficient diagnostic capabilities, restricted medical access, and inadequate data collection methods[1,2].

HICs show advanced detection methods but still face various difficulties

The regions that include the United States, Canada, and Western European nations maintain the highest ALL incidence rates linked to obesity among HICs. Improved leukemia surveillance and higher childhood obesity rates, together with better healthcare access, contribute to this observed pattern. Obesity persists as a major obstacle that affects medical treatment results within these specific regions. Children with ALL who have obesity in HICs face more treatment complications and elevated relapse rates because of their metabolic dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and altered pharmacokinetics[3]. HICs benefit from their superior leukemia treatment systems. Genetic analysis, combined with personalized medicine and clinical studies, has produced remarkable improvements in ALL survival rates[35,36]. Obesity serves as a continuing challenge for ALL patients since it increases their risk of treatment side effects, creates metabolic problems following chemotherapy, and affects their chemotherapy response[4].

MICs face an expanding health crisis due to their demographics

Childhood obesity has increased in MICs such as Brazil and Mexico, as well as in parts of India and Southeast Asia, due to the transition to cities, changes in diet, and reduced physical exercise[4,37–39]. Changes toward consuming processed foods with high-calorie content, combined with inactive lifestyles, have likely triggered a rapid increase in obesity-related cancers, including ALL. MICs lack the advantage of early detection that HICs possess to reduce leukemia mortality, as these countries encounter extensive delays in cancer diagnosis and restricted access to proper treatment[5,6]. Advanced diagnostic tools, along with chemotherapy options and specialized oncology centers, remain scarce in numerous MICs due to their limited healthcare infrastructure. Many ALL patients from these regions arrive at medical facilities at a more advanced stage of their disease, resulting in increased mortality and worse therapeutic outcomes. The limited availability of medical services through private health systems creates a widened treatment gap, as wealthy families obtain timely and proper care, whereas poor families receive delayed or insufficient care[13].

LICs: underreported burden and healthcare disparities

The healthcare systems of LICs experience an underreported disease burden because of healthcare inequalities that affect the population. The reported ALL incidence, together with mortality rates, remains lower in LICs, although underdiagnosis likely produces these results. Many LICs face inadequate healthcare systems that prevent children from getting a leukemia diagnosis until they are already in advanced stages of the disease[40]. The healthcare challenges in LICs include insufficient pediatric oncology specialist talent, as well as restricted chemotherapy availability and insufficient supportive care infrastructure. Obesity trends indicate an increasing rate among LIC populations because of the effects of economic development and urbanization, alongside shifts in food patterns, which may lead to higher ALL incidence in these countries in the future. Better healthcare systems in LICs will increase reported leukemia incidence, which demands proactive detection and intervention methods for the future[14]. Although sensitivity analyses were performed, residual confounding remains possible. Genetic susceptibility, early-life infection exposures, environmental carcinogens, perinatal factors, and socioeconomic gradients may partially account for observed geographic variation. These factors were not directly measured in the present ecological analysis.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the ecological cross-sectional design precludes causal inference at the individual level and is subject to ecological fallacy. Second, estimates are derived from GBD-modeled data, which depend on data availability and statistical extrapolation; uncertainty may be greater in low-income regions with sparse primary data. Third, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as genetic, infectious, environmental, and socioeconomic factors were not directly measured. Fourth, attribution of burden to high BMI reflects modeled population-level associations rather than direct causation. Finally, the public health recommendations discussed are extrapolative and were not directly tested within this analysis.

Public health interventions for obesity prevention

The following recommendations are informed by the existing obesity-prevention literature and reflect potential public health implications rather than interventions directly evaluated in this study. Strategies that address obesity prevention need to focus on modifying dietary habits while promoting physical exercise, as well as efforts to reduce socioeconomic inequalities and expand healthcare services.

School-based nutrition and physical activity programs

School-based intervention programs demonstrate their effectiveness through healthy eating programs, physical activity promotion, and regular BMI screening to prevent the development of childhood obesity. HICs have demonstrated through their school meal policies that implementing whole food-based diets and reducing access to processed foods, alongside enhanced physical education, results in lower childhood obesity rates[15]. However, school programs in MICs and LICs encounter multiple barriers when trying to implement similar programs due to insufficient funding for schools, limited access to nutritious food, and cultural dietary resistance. School meal programs supported by government subsidies, combined with child and parent nutritional education and expanded access to recreational spaces, have proven successful in some areas for reducing obesity rates[16].

Community-based interventions and parental education

Community participation serves as an essential factor in fighting childhood obesity alongside the prevention of leukemia. Research shows that parental involvement in nutritional education, combined with meal planning and home-based physical activity, leads to improved weight management among children. Educational efforts about obesity risks, together with their connection to ALL, can motivate people to make healthful lifestyle decisions early in life[41]. Public health campaigns need to focus on early-life nutrition since maternal obesity has been established as a link to an elevated risk of ALL in children[4]. Maternal weight management programs, together with breastfeeding support and infant nutrition initiatives, provide long-term health advantages to children, who may experience a decreased leukemia risk[42].

Policy and government-level interventions

Policy interventions need to be powerful to effectively combat obesity while addressing leukemia connections. Some HICs have implemented successful policies through sugary beverage taxation to decrease consumption, as well as child food marketing regulations, unhealthy snack and fast-food promotion restrictions, food labeling law enhancements, and fresh produce purchase incentives[17]. The implementation of these policies proves difficult for MICs and LICs because of their economic limitations, combined with food scarcity and native eating habits. Nutritional subsidies from government programs, along with public-private collaborations and community-level food policy modifications, have demonstrated success in decreasing childhood obesity in selected developing areas[18].

Future research directions

While significant progress has been made in understanding the link between obesity and ALL, several areas still require further research.

Understanding genetic and epigenetic links

Recent research reveals that obesity modifies gene expression through DNA methylation and histone modifications, thus raising leukemia risk. Further research is needed to determine the specific genetic pathways influenced by obesity that contribute to leukemogenesis. Research into these mechanisms presents the potential to develop focused treatment approaches[19].

Optimizing chemotherapy for obese ALL patients

The metabolic changes of obesity affect chemotherapy delivery, so researchers need to develop individualized chemotherapy dosage protocols for future trials. The use of lean body mass calculations instead of total body weight for dosing purposes will enhance treatment effects while decreasing toxic side effects[20].

Role of gut microbiota in obesity-related leukemogenesis

New scientific studies indicate that gut microbiota composition may influence leukemia risk in obese individuals. Scientists should study how dietary changes and microbiome-focused therapies might minimize obesity-caused leukemia development, as well as enhance ALL treatment results[21].

Longitudinal studies on obesity and ALL risk

Longitudinal birth cohort studies must continue because epidemiological research has already confirmed obesity’s connection to leukemia, but not how early weight management affects leukemia development in adults[22].

Evaluating the effectiveness of prevention strategies in different regions

Public health intervention assessment must focus on various socioeconomic and cultural contexts because further research is required. Studying how various regions adapt obesity prevention programs can help develop region-specific solutions to reduce leukemia risk globally[23].
