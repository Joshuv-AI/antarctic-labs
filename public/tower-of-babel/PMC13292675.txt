Left Atrial Appendage Closure vs Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Yi-Hsin Chan, Wen-Han Cheng, Yung-Hsin Yeh, Ling Kuo, Chih-Min Liu, Jo-Nan Liao, Chung-Lieh Hung, Gregory YH Lip, Tze-Fan Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft atrial appendage closure (LAAC) is a potential alternative to oral anticoagulation for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation.ObjectivesWe performed an updated meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing LAAC with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) for efficacy and safety.MethodsWe systematically searched from January 1, 2012, to April 3, 2026, for randomized controlled trials comparing percutaneous LAAC with DOACs in patients with atrial fibrillation. Using the longest available follow-up from each trial, we pooled efficacy and bleeding outcomes, including stroke, death, and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and pooled relative risks (RRs) with random-effects models.ResultsFour randomized trials involving 5,890 patients were included, with 2,949 assigned to LAAC and 2,941 to DOAC therapy. The median follow-up ranged from 36 to 42 months. There were no significant differences in all stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, systemic embolism, all-cause death, or cardiovascular death. LAAC was associated with a numerically higher risk of ischemic stroke (RR: 1.36; 95% CI: 0.99-1.89; P = 0.06), which became significant (RR: 1.45; 95% CI: 1.03-2.06; P = 0.03) after exclusion of the OPTION trial. LAAC significantly reduced all nonmajor (RR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.40-0.71; P < 0.0001) and nonprocedure-related major (RR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.65-0.97; P = 0.02) and nonmajor bleeding (RR: 0.47; 95% CI: 0.39-0.57; P < 0.0001), whereas risks of all major bleeding (RR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.81-1.17; P = 0.77) and ICH (RR 0.76; 95% CI: 0.45-1.26; P = 0.28) were similar.ConclusionsCompared with DOACs, LAAC reduced nonprocedure-related major and nonmajor bleeding, potentially at the cost of an increased risk of ischemic stroke, without significant differences in major bleeding or ICH.Key words: atrial fibrillation, bleeding, direct oral anticoagulants, left atrial appendage closure, stroke

Atrial fibrillation (AF) remains a global health challenge, significantly increasing the risk of thromboembolic events, most notably ischemic stroke. For decades, the cornerstone of stroke prevention has been systemic anticoagulation.1 Although vitamin K antagonists (VKA) were the historical standard, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have emerged as the preferred pharmacological intervention due to their superior safety profiles, predictable pharmacokinetics, and lack of requirement for routine monitoring.1

However, the clinical utility of DOACs is often limited by long-term challenges, including major bleeding complications, patient nonadherence, and contraindications in high-risk populations.2 Given that more than 90% of thrombi in patients with nonvalvular AF originate within the left atrial appendage (LAA), localized mechanical exclusion has gained prominence as a viable alternative to systemic therapy.3

LAA closure (LAAC), a percutaneous procedure designed to physically occlude the LAA, offers a "one-time" localized solution to stroke prophylaxis.4 Although early landmark trials demonstrated the noninferiority of LAAC compared to warfarin,5,6 the comparative efficacy and safety of LAAC specifically against DOACs remains a subject of intensive investigation. As the prevalence of AF increases and the population of patients at high risk for both stroke and bleeding grows, clarifying the clinical outcomes of LAAC vs DOACs is essential.

Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing LAAC vs DOACs have been conducted. The PRAGUE-17 (Left Atrial Appendage Closure vs Novel Anticoagulation Agents in Atrial Fibrillation) trial investigated DOACs with LAAC in high-risk patients with AF.7,8 The OPTION (Comparison of Anticoagulation with Left Atrial Appendage Closure after AF Ablation) trial compared LAAC with oral anticoagulation (OAC) in patients with elevated stroke risk after catheter ablation for the treatment of AF.9 Notably, 2 RCTs, the CLOSURE-AF (Left Atrial Appendage Closure or Medical Therapy in Atrial Fibrillation) trial and the CHAMPION-AF (Left Atrial Appendage Closure or Anticoagulation for Atrial Fibrillation) trial were recently published.10,11

However, these clinical trials enrolled patients with AF with varying bleeding risks (low vs high) and different rhythm control strategies (eg, catheter ablation in the OPTION trial). Therefore, an updated meta-analysis is warranted to incorporate these data and provide a more comprehensive assessment of clinical outcomes of LAAC vs DOACs. Accordingly, we sought to include RCTs incorporating newly available data from CLOSURE-AF and CHAMPION-AF and to perform a comprehensive meta-analysis evaluating the clinical outcomes of LAAC vs DOACs in patients with AF.

Methods

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

We conducted the meta-analyses in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Supplemental Table 1).12 Two independent reviewers (Y.H.C. and T.F.C.) conducted a comprehensive PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library search between the January 1, 2012, and April 3, 2026. The search items were (left atrial appendage closure OR LAAC OR WATCHMAN OR Amulet OR LAmbre) AND (DOAC OR NOAC OR non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant OR direct oral anticoagulant OR apixaban OR edoxaban OR rivaroxaban OR dabigatran OR oral anticoagulant OR oral anticoagulation) AND (atrial fibrillation). We only included RCTs that compared percutaneous LAAC (e.g., WATCHMAN, WATCHMAN FLX, Amulet) to DOACs (eg, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban), in patients with AF (paroxysmal, persistent, or permanent). We excluded observational and retrospective studies comparing LAAC and DOAC in patients with AF. For duplicate publications, the report with the largest sample size or longest follow-up was chosen. No ethical approval was required because all synthesized data were obtained from previously published RCTs.

Outcome measures

The assessed efficacy outcomes included all stroke, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, systemic embolism, all-cause death, and cardiovascular death. The safety outcome included nonprocedure-related major bleeding, nonprocedure-related nonmajor bleeding, all major bleeding, all nonmajor bleeding, and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). All outcomes were evaluated using the longest reported follow-up from each of the included trials.

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers (Y.H.C. and T.F.C.) used the second version of the Cochrane risk of bias tool to evaluate the risk of bias in randomized trials independently.13 This tool assesses the bias of RCTs in 5 domains: the randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result, resulting in an overall risk of bias for each trial. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” Results were visualized with the Robvis tool.

Quality of evidence

The quality of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system.14 The GRADE assessment took into account elements such as risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, and indirect ness. The results were produced utilizing the GRADE pro Guideline Development Tool (GRADE pro GDT: McMaster University and Evidence Prime, 2024).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by using the Review Manager (5.4; The Cochrane Collaboration) and STATA (version 14.0; Statacorp). To include all 4 trials accurately and consistently, we calculated the pooled relative risks (RRs) using the raw event counts and patient number of follow-up, which standardizes the metric across CHAMPION-AF, CLOSURE-AF, OPTION, and PRAGUE-17 trials, avoiding the conflicting time-to-event statistical models.8, 9, 10, 11,15 The Mantel-Haenszel method was employed to pool RR. In the CLOSURE-AF trial,10 the investigators could not reliably use the HR because the Schoenfeld residual plots showed deviations from the proportional-hazards assumption, particularly for major bleeding. The risk was not constant over time (bleeding risk was much higher early on for the device group due to the LAAC procedure and dual antiplatelet therapy, but decreased later). Because the hazard changed over time, they reported the difference in restricted mean survival time instead. In the PRAGUE-17 trial,8,15 the investigators used Fine-Gray competing risk regression models. They reported the study outcomes expressed as subdistribution HR explicitly adjusted for competing risks. For the OPTION and CHAMPION-AF trials, the investigators reported standard HRs using the proportional hazards models.9,11 As a result, we could not properly pool all 4 trials for a meta-analysis using HRs and the corresponding errors using inverse variance analysis. The random-effect models were used for all analyses. According to the Cochrane Handbook (version 6.2), assessments for publication bias are typically unnecessary when fewer than 10 studies are included. For all comparison in the present study, P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Selection of included trials

Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process. Overall, 611 articles were identified from PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. After removal of duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full-text, 141, 317, 120, and 28 articles were excluded, respectively. Finally, 4 RCTs (CHAMPION-AF, CLOSURE-AF, OPTION, and PRAGUE-17 trials)8, 9, 10, 11 with 5 studies, including 1 additional report providing further details regarding bleeding events from the PRAGUE-17 trial,15 were included. In total, 2,949 and 2,941 patients with AF receiving LAAC and DOAC therapy, respectively, were analyzed in the meta-analysis.8, 9, 10, 11,15 The Cochrane risk of bias assessment is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. All enrolled trials (CHAMPION-AF, CLOSURE-AF, OPTION, and PRAGUE-17 trials) were assessed as high quality, exhibiting low risk of bias in randomization and outcome measurement.

Baseline characteristics of enrolled trials

The baseline characteristics of the 4 enrolled trials are summarized in Table 1. In general, the baseline characteristics were well balanced between treatment groups for each trial. The median follow-up ranged from 36 to 42 months. The mean age of enrolled patients ranged from 69.4 to 78.5 years, and approximately one-third were women. The 4 enrolled trials represented a distinct spectrum of populations: the CHAMPION-AF and OPTION trials primarily enrolled younger patients (mean age of 69-72 year) with moderate thromboembolic and low bleeding risks, as evidenced by mean CHA2DS2-VASc scores of 3.5 and HAS-BLED scores of 1.2 to 1.3. Conversely, the CLOSURE-AF and PRAGUE-17 trials enrolled older patients (mean age of 73-78 year) with substantially greater thromboembolic, bleeding risk, and comorbidities, with the mean CHA2DS2-VASc scores of 4.7 to 5.2 and HAS-BLED scores of 3.0 to 3.1, and a prominent history of previous ischemic strokes (>31%) and major bleeding events (>42%). Notably, the OPTION trial primarily enrolled patients with AF who underwent catheter ablation (100%), whereas the CHAMPION trial enrolled around 48% of patients with AF and prior catheter ablation.

Primary efficacy outcomes for LAAC vs DOAC

For the risk of all stroke, the LAAC group had 103 events compared to 86 events in the DOAC group, resulting in a pooled RR of 1.19 (95% CI: 0.90-1.58; P = 0.23; N = 4). For ischemic stroke, the LAAC group had 85 events compared to 62 events in the DOAC group. Of note, LAAC was associated with a numerically higher risk of ischemic stroke when compared with DOAC therapy (RR: 1.36; 95% CI: 0.99-1.89; P = 0.06; N = 4). There were no significant differences between LAAC and DOACs regarding the risk of hemorrhagic stroke (RR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.45-1.51; P = 0.53; N = 4), systemic embolism (RR: 1.09; 95% CI: 0.29-4.08; P = 0.90, N = 4), all-cause death (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.87-1.16; P = 0.91; N = 4), and cardiovascular death (RR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.80-1.32; P = 0.82; N = 4) (Figure 2).

Primary safety outcomes for LAAC vs DOAC

The meta-analysis showed that LAAC demonstrated a significant benefit in mitigating nonprocedural bleeding complications (Figure 3). For the nonprocedure-related major bleeding, there were 159 events in the LAAC group compared to 200 events in the DOAC group, resulting in a pooled RR of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.65-0.97; P = 0.02; N = 4). For nonprocedure-related nonmajor bleeding, there were 148 events in the LAAC group compared to 313 events in the DOAC group, resulting in a pooled RR of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.39-0.57; P < 0.0001; N = 3). For all nonmajor bleeding, there were 174 events in the LAAC group compared to 319 events in the DOAC group, resulting in a pooled RR of 0.53 (95% CI: 0.40-0.71; P < 0.0001; N = 3). There were no differences between LAAC and DOACs regarding the risk of all major bleeding (RR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.81-1.17; P = 0.77; N = 4) and ICH (RR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.45-1.26; P = 0.28; N = 3).

Primary efficacy and safety outcomes for LAAC vs DOAC after excluding OPTION trial

Because the OPTION trial exclusively enrolled patients undergoing concurrent catheter ablation and rhythm-control management for AF,9 the trial may inherently introduce the confounding procedural and therapeutic effects of ablation. Therefore, we performed further meta-analysis excluding the OPTION trial. After excluding the OPTION trial, LAAC was associated with a significantly higher risk of ischemic stroke (RR: 1.45; 95% CI: 1.03-2.06; P = 0.03; N = 3) and a nonsignificantly lower risk of nonprocedure-related major bleeding (RR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.65-1.01; P = 0.06; N = 3) compared with DOAC therapy. Other efficacy and safety outcomes were consistent with the main analyses after excluding the OPTION trial (Supplemental Figure 2 and 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially removing a single study at one time (leave-one-out analysis). The pooled results were generally in line with the primary effectiveness and safety outcomes (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses comparing LAAC and DOACs demonstrated highly consistent outcomes across major subgroups (Supplemental Figures 4 to 8). For the primary efficacy outcome (composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, or systemic embolism for the CHAMPION trial and composite of death from any cause, stroke, or systemic embolism for the OPTION trial), there were no significant subgroup interactions based on age (<75 vs ≥75 years), sex, CHA2DS2-VASc score (<4 vs ≥4), HAS-BLED score (<3 vs ≥3), or AF type (paroxysmal vs persistent-permanent).

Similarly, the safety outcome (nonprocedure-related major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding for the CHAMPION and OPTION trial) of LAAC vs DOAC were generally consistent across these subgroups. Notably, a significant interaction was observed regarding sex in the safety outcome (P for interaction = 0.03), indicating that the relative magnitude of safety outcome was substantially more pronounced in male patients.

Quality of evidence

The quality of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE system and is reported in Supplemental Table 4. The overall quality of evidence for all efficacy and safety outcomes was assessed as high, due to the RCT design, low risk of bias, and negligible issues related to inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision.

Discussion

In this updated meta-analysis of 4 randomized trials including 5,890 patients with AF, LAAC, and DOACs demonstrated broadly comparable efficacy across major pooled endpoints, except for a numerically higher risk of ischemic stroke with LAAC, while exhibiting distinct nonprocedural safety profiles.

Specifically, there were no significant differences in all stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, systemic embolism, all-cause death, or cardiovascular death. However, ischemic stroke occurred numerically more often after LAAC with the signal becoming significant after exclusion of OPTION. By contrast, LAAC was consistently associated with lower rates of nonprocedure-related major bleeding, nonprocedure-related nonmajor bleeding, and all nonmajor bleeding, whereas all major bleeding and ICH were not significantly different.

An important strength of our analysis is the inclusion of 2 recently reported trials, CHAMPION-AF and CLOSURE-AF, alongside PRAGUE-17 and OPTION. Although earlier meta-analyses included 4 trials, only 2 evaluated DOAC-based strategies, whereas the others reflected VKA-era comparisons.16 The included RCTs in the present study enrolled clinically distinct populations with different inclusion criteria (Supplemental Table 5). CHAMPION-AF and OPTION studied relatively lower-risk patients, whereas CLOSURE-AF and PRAGUE-17 enrolled older patients with substantially higher thromboembolic and bleeding risk, including higher rates of prior ischemic stroke, prior major bleeding, heart failure, and coronary disease.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Accordingly, our pooled estimates should be interpreted as averages across heterogeneous but clinically relevant AF populations and thus offer a more contemporary and comprehensive evaluation of LAAC vs DOAC therapy. The consistency of the reduction in nonprocedural bleeding strengthens the signal for a durable safety advantage with LAAC, whereas an excess risk of ischemic stroke after exclusion of OPTION suggests that patient selection remains critical.

Efficacy of LAAC vs DOACs

The efficacy findings merit careful interpretation. Although overall stroke did not differ significantly, the direction of effect for ischemic stroke consistently favored DOAC therapy, and this signal became significant after exclusion of OPTION trial. Notably, the OPTION trial enrolled a distinct postablation population, in whom thromboembolic risk may be lower than that of general AF cohorts because of rhythm control and maintenance of sinus rhythm. Thus, inclusion of OPTION may have attenuated differences in ischemic stroke risk between the 2 treatment strategies in the overall analysis. Indeed, recent data from ALONE-AF17 and OCEAN18 trials suggest that AF patients with relatively low-to-intermediate stroke risk (mean or median CHA2DS2-VASc scores of around 2) with durable sinus rhythm after successful ablation may not necessarily require lifelong DOAC therapy. Therefore, in the OPTION population, the key clinical question may shift from DOACs vs LAAC to continuation vs discontinuation of DOAC therapy. These patients require not only close monitoring for AF recurrence, but also periodic reassessment of stroke risk, as aging or incident comorbidities may subsequently create a need for anticoagulation therapy.19 Accordingly, the significantly higher risk of ischemic stroke with LAAC compared with DOACs after exclusion of the OPTION trial may raise a “red flag” regarding the efficacy of LAAC for stroke prevention.

The efficacy findings may also reflect the fundamental biological difference between the 2 strategies. DOACs provide systemic antithrombotic protection, whereas LAAC primarily addresses thrombus formation originating from the LAA.20 Residual non-LAA sources of thromboembolism, device-related thrombus, peridevice leak, or patient-level substrate beyond appendage exclusion may therefore contribute to residual ischemic risk after LAAC.21 This may be particularly relevant in older, higher-risk populations such as those enrolled in CLOSURE-AF and PRAGUE-17.7,8,10 Thus, although LAAC appears comparable to DOAC overall, our findings do not support viewing LAAC as a universally equivalent substitute for systemic anticoagulation across all AF patient phenotypes.

Bleeding risk of LAAC vs DOACs

Different from efficacy endpoints, the bleeding results were more consistent and clinically intuitive. The benefit of LAAC was driven primarily by reductions in nonprocedure-related major/nonmajor bleeding and all nonmajor bleeding, rather than by differences in all major bleeding or ICH. This pattern aligns with prior randomized evidence showing lower nonprocedure-related clinically relevant bleeding after LAAC.22 It also fits with the expected time course of treatment-related risk: once the initial procedural period and temporary postimplant antithrombotic therapy have passed, LAAC may reduce cumulative bleeding exposure by avoiding indefinite systemic anticoagulation.22 Similarly, in the prior meta-analysis, LAAC consistently reduced nonprocedure-related clinically relevant bleeding, whereas major bleeding remained comparable overall.16

This point is further reinforced by procedural safety considerations. Prior analysis showed that procedural or device-related complications were highest in the earliest WATCHMAN-era trial and lower in later studies.16 Even so, these complications remain unique to LAAC and have no direct analog with medical therapy. Supplemental Table 6 summarizes the procedural characteristics of the 4 trials, with procedural success rates exceeding 96%. Despite the high implant success rates, key procedural complications occurred in 2.8% of patients in the OPTION and CHAMPION trials and in 5.7% in CLOSURE-AF (Supplemental Table 7). Procedure-related major bleeding occurred in 3.0% and 4.3% of patients in PRAGUE-17 and CLOSURE-AF, respectively.

Postprocedural bleeding risk

Nevertheless, the apparent bleeding advantage of LAAC should not obscure the fact that bleeding remains an important postprocedural problem. In the recent Amulet IDE analysis, major bleeding occurred in 18.1% of patients over 5 years (5.9%/year), with the highest annualized risk during the first 6 months (20.5%/year) after implantation, when antithrombotic intensity is greatest.22 Most bleeding events were nonprocedure-related, predominantly gastrointestinal, and were strongly associated with subsequent stroke, cardiovascular death, and all-cause mortality.22 Notably, nearly half of first major bleeding events occurred in patients who were no longer receiving antithrombotic therapy, emphasizing that bleeding risk after LAAC is not solely medication-related but also reflects underlying patient frailty and comorbidity burden. Most importantly, even beyond 6 months after the procedure, the annual risk of major bleeding remained 3.9% between 6 months and 5 years in the Amulet IDE trial,22 which is not lower than that reported in the 4 DOAC trials (approximately 3%).23 These observations are directly relevant to our findings, revealing that although LAAC may lessen long-term bleeding relative to DOAC therapy, it does not eliminate bleeding risk, particularly during the early postimplant period.

The post-LAAC antithrombotic regimen is important for interpreting postprocedural bleeding risk. Across the 4 trials, postimplant treatment varied substantially, ranging from antiplatelet-only strategies in PRAGUE-17 and CLOSURE-AF to short-term OAC plus aspirin in OPTION (Supplemental Table 8). Despite this variability, the risk of nonprocedural major bleeding was comparable between LAAC and DOACs, with no significant heterogeneity. Data from the Amulet IDE trial further indicate that patient-specific factors, including older age, female sex, diabetes, prior major bleeding, and kidney disease, are the primary determinants of bleeding risk.22 Therefore, the comparison between LAAC and DOAC should not be framed simply as device vs drug class, as outcomes are influenced not only by procedural quality and postimplant antithrombotic strategies but also, importantly, by patient clinical characteristics. Also, the choice of DOAC and dosing regimen (given that off label under/over dosing is common) may impact on bleeding events.

Clinical implications

From a clinical standpoint, our data supported a more selective rather than expansive use of LAAC. Patients with AF who face substantial lifetime bleeding burden, difficulty maintaining long-term DOAC therapy, or a strong preference to avoid chronic anticoagulation may derive meaningful benefit from LAAC, particularly if procedural risk is low and postimplant care is carefully managed. However, more ischemic stroke after exclusion of OPTION cautions against assuming that LAAC could routinely replace DOACs in patients who tolerate anticoagulation well. Importantly, even in the CLOSURE-AF trial, in which patients had a mean HAS-BLED score >3, with more than 40% having a history of major bleeding and approximately one-quarter having stage IV chronic kidney disease, representing a high-bleeding-risk population generally considered unsuitable for long-term DOAC therapy, LAAC did not confer a significantly lower risk of overall major bleeding, likely reflecting the impact of procedural risk. These data again emphasize that LAAC should not be regarded as a treatment capable of eliminating bleeding risk in all patients. The key advantages of LAAC over DOACs were mainly driven by the reduction in nonmajor bleeding, which may still be clinically meaningful to patients, especially when recurrent, visible, anxiety-provoking, or associated with repeated medical visits or treatment interruption. However, this burden must be weighed against the invasive nature of LAAC, the upfront procedural bleeding risk, and the potentially higher risk of ischemic stroke. Our findings therefore align with a shared decision-making approach in which the relative importance of ischemic protection, bleeding avoidance, procedural risk, and patient preference is weighed at the individual level, rather than treating LAAC and DOAC as universally interchangeable strategies (Central Illustration). Indeed, AF management is more than stroke prevention (whether with LAAC or DOAC), requiring a holistic or integrated care management approach in patient care pathways,24,25 that has been associated with improved clinical outcomes.26

Study Limitations

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the present meta-analysis was conducted using a study-level rather than individual patient data, as the individual patient data from each enrolled trial was not available. Although all enrolled RCTs showed high methodological quality, inherent variations in trial design, patient demographics, and postprocedural medication regimens may have introduced clinical heterogeneity. Second, there was methodological variation in the definitions of safety outcomes across the 4 included trials. Although efficacy outcomes (such as stroke and mortality) were generally uniformly defined, the grading systems used to classify major bleeding differed among studies. Specifically, the CHAMPION-AF, OPTION, and PRAGUE-17 trials used the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria to define major bleeding, whereas the CLOSURE-AF trial used the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium criteria, defining major bleeding as BARC type 3 to 5 bleeding. However, the principal findings remained generally consistent in the leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. Third, procedural complications unique to LAAC (eg, pericardial effusion or device embolization requiring intervention) lack direct counterparts in DOAC therapy, which may confound safety interpretations. Fourth, potential treatment contamination occurred in the CLOSURE-AF and OPTION trials,8,9 where a minority of patients (<5%) in the medical (DOAC) arm received VKAs, and a sustained proportion of the device (LAAC) arm continued OAC therapy during long-term follow-up. In addition, 4 trials reported substantial crossover between LAAC and DOAC arms.7, 8, 9, 10 This diversity in antithrombotic management limits the generalizability of the findings and underscores the need for protocol standardization. Moreover, because the PRAGUE-17 and CLOSURE-AF trials did not provide detailed event numbers for specific outcomes within specific subgroups, these studies could not be incorporated into the subgroup analyses of the present study. Given the limited number of trials included in the subgroup analyses and the multiple subgroup comparisons performed, the possibility of inflated type I error (false positive) for significant findings cannot be excluded. Lastly, because all included randomized studies mostly evaluated WATCHMAN-based LAAC, our findings may not be generalizable to other devices such as AMULET.

Conclusions

This updated meta-analysis of 4 RCTs suggests that, compared with DOACs, LAAC reduced nonprocedure-related major and nonmajor bleeding, potentially at the cost of an increased risk of ischemic stroke, without significant differences in major bleeding or ICH.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with AF, data from randomized trials demonstrate that LAAC reduces nonprocedure-related and nonmajor bleeding compared with DOAC therapy, but this benefit may be counterbalanced by a higher risk of ischemic stroke.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future research should refine patient selection, optimize post-LAAC antithrombotic strategies, and identify clinical or imaging markers that predict ischemic and bleeding outcomes with each approach.
