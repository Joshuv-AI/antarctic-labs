Natural Variation in GmSL20 Improves Seed Size and Quality in Soybean
Authors: Yuechuan Luo, Zongbiao Duan, Juxu Li, Zhou Zhu, Tong Wu, Dan Li, Yangping Liu, Liangwei Xu, Hao Wen, Yanting Shen, Shulin Liu, Qingtian Li, Zhixi Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeed size plays an important role in determining crop yield, yet the genetic mechanisms underlying the regulation of this agronomic trait remain incompletely understood in soybean. Here, we perform a genome‐wide association study mapping with seed length variations and identify a major quantitative trait locus (QTL) on chromosome 20, named SL20 (Seed Length 20). GmSL20 encodes a TGA‐type bZIP transcription factor that controls soybean seed size and weight in natural populations. Knockout of GmSL20 suppresses seed enlargement and lipid biosynthesis in soybean, whereas increased expression of GmSL20 improves seed size and oil content. Further functional investigations reveal that GmSL20 directly activates GmST05 and GmZF351, thus promoting carbon partitioning to seeds. A super allele that arose from domestication confers elevated capacity for DNA binding and transcriptional activation to GmSL20. It correlates with larger seed size in haplotype analysis and enhances seed size and weight in elite soybean variety; however, it has not been widely fixed and spread through modern breeding. The role of GmSL20 to promote yield and quality underscores its potential as a valuable resource for soybean breeding programs seeking to improve both traits simultaneously.Keywords: GmSL20, GWAS, seed oil, seed size, soybean

Introduction

Soybean ( Glycine max [L.] Merr.) is a globally significant legume crop, prized for its high protein and oil content. It supplies more than a quarter of the world's protein for human consumption and animal feed, and it plays a critical role in guaranteeing food security and sustainable development worldwide (Rizzo and Baroni 2018; Singer et al. 2023). As global demand for plant‐based protein continues to rise, optimising key agronomic traits such as yield and quality has become an urgent priority for soybean through both conventional breeding and biotechnological improvement strategies (Wilson 2008; Zhang et al. 2022). Among various traits influencing soybean productivity, seed size is of central importance. It directly affects seed weight and seed appearance quality, in a great measure, dictating the yield formation and farmer satisfaction. Moreover, seed size is positively correlated to seedling vigour and nutrient content (Li, Xu, and Li 2019). For its agronomic significance, understanding the genetic mechanisms controlling this trait is critical to improving yield potential and quality of soybean.

Seed size is predominantly determined during early stages of seed development and is tightly controlled by the coordinated growth of maternal integument, endosperm, and embryo (Li, Xu, and Li 2019; Xu et al. 2023). Also, diverse environmental cues and intensive breeding activities have shaped seed size through evolutionary adaptation and artificial selection, respectively. Due to the complexity of seed development, seed size is a quantitative and multigenic trait, governed by intricate transcriptional regulatory networks (Zhang et al. 2023). The IKU‐dependent pathway plays a crucial role in regulating endosperm development. It includes three core genes, that is, IKU1, MINISEED3 (MINI3), and IKU2, that are preferentially expressed during early endosperm proliferation (Garcia et al. 2003; Luo et al. 2005; Wang et al. 2010). Loss of function in any of these genes leads to endosperm growth defects and precocious cellularization, ultimately resulting in smaller seeds. By being recruited on the promoters of MINI3 and IKU2, the transcriptional activator SHORT HYPOCOTYL UNDER BLUE 1 (SHB1) acts in the IKU pathway to promote seed enlargement (Kang et al. 2013; Zhou et al. 2009). Notably, IKU1 interacts with WRKY transcription factor (TF) MINI3, and in coordination with SHB1, to positively regulate MINI3 and IKU2. Hormonal regulation is tightly associated with the determination of seed size, of which transcription factors could function as growth‐promoting and growth‐inhibiting switches in the signal cascade, providing valuable insights into the mechanisms that control seed size. Brassinosteroids (BRs) facilitate seed enlargement by activating the IKU pathway (Chen et al. 2024). The transcription factor BRASSINAZOLE‐RESISTANT1 (BZR1) binds directly to the SHB1, IKU1, and IKU2 promoters upon the perception of BR (Jiang et al. 2013). DELLA proteins, which are negative regulators of GA signalling, are involved in seed size regulation through their interactions with AP2 transcription factor AINTEGUMENTA (ANT) to promote maternal tissue cell proliferation (Gomez et al. 2023). AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 2 (ARF2) links auxin signalling to seed development by limiting cell proliferation in the maternal integuments, where a loss‐of‐function mutation results in larger seed size due to upregulation of ANT and CYCD3;1 (Figueiredo et al. 2016; Schruff et al. 2006). Abscisic acid (ABA) and Jasmonate (JA) suppress seed size via downregulation of SHB1 expression in an ABA INSENSITIVE 5 (ABI5)‐dependent manner and inhibition of integument cell proliferation in a MYC2‐dependent manner, respectively (Cheng et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2021).

Among transcriptional regulators, basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors constitute one of the largest and most conserved TF families in plants. The canonical bZIP domain is a bipartite α‐helical structure of approximately 60–80 amino acids, consisting of an N‐terminal basic region responsible for DNA binding and a C‐terminal leucine zipper that mediates protein dimerization. The final regulatory output of a given bZIP factor is determined by its recognition of specific cis‐regulatory motifs with ACGT as the core in promoters, as well as by its interaction with other regulatory proteins, including additional bZIP factors and diverse transcriptional regulators that are co‐expressed in a particular cell type (Jakoby et al. 2002). Increasing evidence suggests that bZIP transcription factors participate in modulating seed development, such as ABI5, which acts as a key player in seed size control and storage reserve accumulation (Cheng et al. 2014; Zinsmeister et al. 2016). In rice, the TGA‐type bZIP protein OsbZIP47 regulates meristem development and grain size, highlighting the function of the TGA TF family in determining seed morphology and productivity (Hao et al. 2021; Prakash et al. 2022). Despite their importance in plant development, the roles of bZIP TFs in regulating seed size and associated metabolic processes remain largely unexplored, suggesting that additional bZIP‐mediated transcriptional networks may contribute to natural variation in seed traits.

In recent decades, considerable efforts have been made to elucidate the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying seed size variation in soybean, with particular emphasis on identifying and characterizing key regulators and their interactions. Genome‐wide association study (GWAS) and quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping revealed a number of regulatory modules for seed size control, for example, PP2C‐1‐GmBZR1, GmST05/GmMFT‐GmSWEET10a, Dt1‐GmSWEET10a, GmSW17‐GmDP‐E2F‐1, and GmSW14‐GmLEC1a/b (Cai et al. 2023; Duan et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Liang et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2025). It should be noted that, seed size not only directly influences yield, but also shows complex genetic correlations with key quality traits such as seed oil content in soybean (Zhang et al. 2022). In particular, the GmST05/GmMFT‐GmSWEET10a and GmSW14‐GmLEC1a/b modules simultaneously affect seed size and oil content, revealing the genetic basis by which pleiotropy couples yield and quality (Cai et al. 2023; Duan et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2025). Additionally, transcriptome analyses of developing soybean seeds identified GmJAZ3, GmMYB331, GmERF416 and GmERFA as transcriptional regulators influencing pathways that control seed oil accumulation and seed size (Hu et al. 2023; Jin et al. 2025; Liu, Hu, et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025). Nevertheless, the knowledge of genes and mechanisms for synergistic enhancement of seed size and oil content in soybean remains inadequate for molecular design breeding, particularly in natural populations. The identification of more regulatory genes related to seed size and quality will facilitate the improvement of soybean varieties.

In this study, through a GWAS of 698 soybean accessions, we identified a bZIP TF gene, GmSL20, that positively regulates seed length, weight, and oil content. GmSL20 activates the expression of GmST05 and GmZF351 by direct binding to their promoter regions, thereby resulting in larger soybean seeds and enhanced lipid biosynthesis. The superior allele of GmSL20, which has not been widely applied in modern cultivars, correlates with higher seed size and weight among the soybean accessions. Its beneficial effect on seed traits is further reinforced by overexpression in the elite variety harbouring this allele. These findings provide a valuable genetic resource for breeding high‐yield and high‐quality soybean varieties.

Results

Natural Variation in GmSL20 Determines Seed Length in Soybean

To identify key genetic determinants of seed length in soybean, we performed a genome‐wide association study using 698 cultivated accessions from our established resequencing panel (Liu et al. 2020), comprising 357 landraces and 341 improved cultivars. Seed length exhibited an approximately normal distribution across this population (Figure S1), reflecting considerable phenotypic diversity and indicating that variation in this trait is strongly influenced by underlying genetic factors—thus validating the panel's utility for GWAS‐based gene discovery. Using a mixed linear model, we detected a stable and significant signal spanning a 99.89‐kb genomic region on chromosome 20 (Figure 1a–c), which was designated as QTL Seed length 20 (SL20). This locus has not been previously reported and displayed a continuous signal with higher association value compared with loci on other chromosomes, indicating a robust and major contribution to seed length in soybean. Within the interval, 12 protein‐coding genes were found and annotated according to the ZH13 reference genome (Figure 1c) (Shen et al. 2019, 2018), among which two genes (SoyZH13_20G100600 and SoyZH13_20G101000) exhibited clearly higher expression levels during seed development than other tissues as revealed by previous transcriptome data (Figure 1d) (Fan et al. 2025). SoyZH13_20G101000 is an ortholog of PERIANTHIA (At1g68640) in Arabidopsis thaliana or OsbZIP47 (LOC_Os06g15480) in rice ( Oryza sativa ), of which the latter was reported to be involved in grain size regulation (Figure S2) (Chuang et al. 1999; Hao et al. 2021). After removing the polymorphisms with minor allele frequency < 0.01 (MAF < 0.01), 10 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and one insertion/deletion (indel) at SoyZH13_20G101000 locus were found to be closely linked in the natural population. A key nonsynonymous SNP (G953C) in the coding region, together with multiple promoter variations, defines two major haplotypes of SoyZH13_20G101000 (Figure 1e; Figure S3). Notably, haplotype H2 showed significantly greater seed length, width, thickness, and 100‐seed weight than those of haplotype H1 (Figure 1f–i). We thus hypothesized that SoyZH13_20G101000 is the causal gene for soybean seed length regulation at the Seed length 20 locus and named it GmSL20.

To elucidate the function of GmSL20, we developed a set of near isogenic lines (NILs) targeting this locus. Through utilizing a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from a cross between W82 (carrying GmSL20 H1 ) and QH34 (carrying GmSL20 H2 ), NIL‐GmSL20 H1 and NIL‐GmSL20 H2 were obtained from a residual heterozygous line (RHL). Although most of the morphological characteristics of NIL plants were similar, the seed length, width, and thickness were significantly larger in NIL‐GmSL20 H2 (Figure S4), resulting in better performance on 100‐seed weight and yield per plant in comparison with NIL‐GmSL20 H1 . To determine whether promoter‐region variation underlies the seed size difference, we measured GmSL20 expression in developing seeds of NIL‐GmSL20 H1 and NIL‐GmSL20 H2 via RT‐qPCR. Transcript abundance was indistinguishable between the two near isogenic lines (Figure S5a). Likewise, transient promoter activity assays revealed no significant difference in transcriptional output between GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 (Figure S5b). Crucially, the G953C nonsynonymous substitution—yielding an Arg318Pro replacement—is the sole coding‐sequence variant distinguishing GmSL20 H1 in W82 and GmSL20 H2 in QH34. Together, these results indicate that the single nucleotide substitution (G to C) in the coding region, rather than promoter polymorphisms, accounts for the seed size divergence between the two haplotypes.

Functional Validation of GmSL20 in Regulating Seed Size in Soybean

GmSL20 encodes a protein of 455 amino acids with a bZIP domain and a TGACG‐binding (TGA) domain. To validate the function of GmSL20, we introduced a 4365‐bp genomic fragment containing the 3000‐bp promoter and 1365‐bp coding sequence of either GmSL20 H1 or GmSL20 H2 into DN50 (GmSL20 H1 background). Two independent transgenic lines of GmSL20 H1 ‐OE or GmSL20 H2 ‐OE with elevated GmSL20 transcript levels produced larger seeds compared to the wild‐type (Figure 2a,b). Seed length, width, and thickness were dramatically increased in all OE lines (Figure 2c–e), resulting in higher 100‐seed weight and seed weight per plant (Figure 2f,g). Interestingly, the GmSL20 H2 ‐OE lines outperformed the GmSL20 H1 ‐OE lines on seed length, weight, and yield per plant, implying an enhanced regulatory role of the H2 allele (Figure 2c,f,g). Additionally, we tested whether GmSL20 H2 , when restrictively expressed in seeds, could be functional in improving seed size and weight. As expected, the GmSL20 H2 expressed under the control of a seed‐specific promoter (from the β‐conglycinin α subunit gene) significantly promoted seed length, width, thickness, and weight in DN50, suggesting that the expression in seeds is sufficient for GmSL20 to control seed enlargement (Figure S6) (Liang et al. 2024).

We next used a CRISPR/Cas9 system to create knockout mutants in DN50. Two sites in GmSL20 were targeted simultaneously. Two independent alleles, GmSL20 H1 ‐CR1 carried two single‐base insertions and GmSL20 H1 ‐CR2 carried a single‐base insertion and a 24‐bp deletion, are likely to be null, as insertions cause translational frameshift that leads to premature termination of GmSL20 (Figure 3a). In contrast to overexpressing lines, GmSL20 H1 ‐CR lines produced smaller seeds with significant reductions in seed length, width, and thickness compared to DN50 (Figure 3b–e). Accordingly, the 100‐seed weight and seed weight per plant were significantly decreased in mutant lines (Figure 3f,g). Furthermore, because the H2 haplotype likely represents a superior allele of GmSL20, we thus generated two GmSL20 H2 ‐CR lines, in which a single‐base insertion and a deletion occurred in the fourth and ninth exons, respectively (Figure S7a). Consistent with the GmSL20 H1 , knockout of GmSL20 H2 in the QH34 background also reduced seed size (Figure S7b–f), confirming that GmSL20 is essential for seed trait regulation across genetic backgrounds.

It has been reported that a few genes can affect both seed size and quality in soybean (Duan et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2025). We found that GmSL20 was located in several QTL regions related to seed weight and oil content, indicating that it may also control seed size and quality simultaneously (Han et al. 2012; Mao et al. 2013). We thus compared the oil content of GmSL20‐OE and CR lines with wild‐type DN50. The seeds with enhanced GmSL20 expression exhibited significantly higher oil content than that of the DN50. By contrast, the knockout of GmSL20 greatly jeopardized seed oil accumulation (Figures 2h and 3h). Likewise, the seed oil content was significantly increased in NIL‐GmSL20 H2 in comparison with NIL‐GmSL20 H1 (Figure S4g). Taken together, these results demonstrate that GmSL20 predominantly acts as a positive regulator of seed size and oil content in soybean.

GmSL20 Directly Targets GmST05 and GmZF351 to Control Seed Size and Oil Content

We hypothesized that GmSL20 may have a role in activating downstream regulatory genes of seed size and oil content as a bZIP transcription factor. To investigate the molecular mechanism of GmSL20, we first detected its subcellular localization. GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 were located in the nucleus solely while transiently expressed in tobacco epidermal cells, implying that natural variation did not alter the subcellular localization (Figure S8a). Structural prediction of GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 implied that two proteins substantially aligned; however, with an altered fold of the second α‐helix (Figure S8b). Given that the bZIP domain of GmSL20 is located in this α‐helix, this change is unlikely to destruct the TF but may mildly modulate functional properties. This led us to examine whether the DNA binding or transcription activation of GmSL20 was affected by natural variations.

To decipher the direct downstream targets of GmSL20, we employed DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP‐seq) to comprehensively profile the genome‐wide binding events for both GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2. The 12 879 and 23 709 high‐confidence binding peaks were identified for GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2, respectively, with approximately one‐third of peaks located in promoter regions (≤ 2 kb from TSS) and half of peaks located in distal intergenic regions (Figure 4a; Figure S9a,b). A total of 7048 peaks were shared between GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 and the rest were unique (Figure S9c). By searching shared cis‐regulatory elements on binding peaks, we found that both haplotypes identically recognized the canonical bZIP binding motif (AYGTCA) (Figure 4b). To detect whether GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 differed in DNA binding intensity, we conducted a comparative analysis of fold enrichment using shared 2501 binding sites in promoter regions (Figure S9d). Interestingly, aside from more binding events at genome scale, GmSL20H2 also showed significantly stronger binding intensity than GmSL20H1 (Figure 4c). The results indicate that the distinctive binding affinity between the two haplotypes is probably attributable to the protein variation itself, rather than distinct DNA binding specificity. Overall, 3952 and 7699 genes identified in DAP‐seq were grouped into targets of GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2, respectively. To gain insight into the potential biological functions of GmSL20 resulting from DNA binding, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis. The predicted targets of GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 were enriched for biological processes that might be critical to seed development, such as amino acid metabolism and the MAPK signalling pathway (Figure S9e) (Wu et al. 2022).

Two key genes known to positively regulate seed size and/or oil content in soybean, namely GmST05 and GmZF351, were discovered from DAP‐seq profiles (Duan et al. 2022; Li et al. 2017). We found that both GmSL20H2 and GmSL20H1 were able to bind to the promoter regions of GmST05 and GmZF351, of which the latter showed weaker affinity (Figure 4d). We next sought to use yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) to further validate this result. In Y1H assays, GmST05 and GmZF351 promoter fragments interacted with GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 as indicated by yeast growth under higher‐stringency histidine dropout selection in the presence of 3‐AT (Figure 4e). The probes harbouring the core AYGTCA motif from the same promoter fragments of GmST05 and GmZF351 were examined for GmSL20 binding in the EMSA analysis. Compared to the control GST protein, the GST‐GmSL20H1 and GST‐GmSL20H2 proteins were found to specifically bind to the two probes (Figure 4f). Therefore, we conclude that GmSL20H1 and GmSL20H2 directly bind to the promoters of GmST05 and GmZF351 by recognizing the AYGTCA motif.

GmSL20 Activates the Expression of GmST05 and GmZF351

To investigate the genes or pathways that GmSL20 may regulate in the development of soybean seeds, we performed RNA‐seq using wild‐type (DN50) and representative GmSL20 H2 ‐OE lines. A total of 2129 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were upregulated and 603 DEGs were downregulated relative to the DN50 (Figure 5a). KEGG analysis revealed a strong enrichment of genes involved in the MAPK signalling pathway and amino acid metabolism, which is the same as the DAP‐seq data (Figure 5b). In addition, genes associated with vital metabolic pathways, including linoleic acid metabolism and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, were also significantly enriched. These findings implicate a possible way for GmSL20 to facilitate seed enlargement through lipid biosynthesis. Consistent with this observation, we also found direct targets of GmSL20, GmST05 and GmZF351, among upregulated DEGs. Both genes were strongly induced in the GmSL20 overexpression lines (Figure 5c,d; Figure S10a,b). Moreover, GmST05 and GmZF351 exhibited significantly higher expression in NIL‐GmSL20 H2 than in NIL‐GmSL20 H1 (Figure S10c,d), suggesting that the natural variation in GmSL20 leads to differential activation of key downstream genes controlling seed size and oil content.

We then used a transient dual‐luciferase assay to ask whether GmSL20 affects the activity of GmST05 and GmZF351 transcriptional reporters. In Arabidopsis protoplasts, relative luciferase activity from GmST05 and GmZF351 promoters significantly increased more than 2‐fold in the presence of GmSL20H1 or GmSL20H2, with the latter exhibiting a slightly higher transactivation capacity (Figure 5e,f). This difference is consistent with DAP‐seq binding profiles and the observed phenotypic divergence between haplotypes. Together, these results establish GmSL20 as a transcriptional activator that promotes the expression of GmST05 and GmZF351 to facilitate seed size and quality.

Origin, Geographical Distribution and Breeding Potential of GmSL20 H2

The cultivated soybean ( Glycine max [L.] Merr.) was domesticated from its wild relative ( G. soja Sieb. & Zucc.) in China 5000–6000 years ago and subsequently spread to Eastern and Southeast Asia by the mid‐fifteenth century, reaching Europe in 1740, North America in 1765, and South America in the early twentieth century (Zhu et al. 2025). Artificial selection played a crucial role in soybean domestication and improvement, significantly augmenting seed size and oil content during soybean breeding (Zhou et al. 2015). By investigating 4596 previous resequenced soybean accessions, including 825 wild soybeans, 518 landraces, and 3253 improved cultivars, we observed that < 6% of the landraces or improved cultivars characterized by the presence of GmSL20 H2 , suggesting that it is a rare allele despite being advantageous (Figure 6a). Surprisingly, GmSL20 H2 was not found in wild soybeans. Nucleotide diversity analyses also showed that landraces and improved cultivars exhibited higher diversity than wild soybeans (Figure S11a). We then investigated the global geographical distribution of GmSL20 alleles in the landrace and cultivated soybean accessions. GmSL20 H2 is predominantly distributed in Asia, with relatively higher frequency in the Huang‐Huai‐Hai region from China. It should be noted that GmSL20 H2 has not been utilized in the majority of soybean accessions from regions where soybean dissemination occurred later than the eighteenth century, including South America, North America, and Europe (Figure 6b).

The transcription factor GmSL20 directly activates the expression of two key downstream genes, GmST05 and GmZF351, forming a core transcriptional cascade that promotes seed development and lipid biosynthesis. GmSL20H2, which likely emerged during soybean domestication, possesses stronger DNA‐binding affinity and transactivation activity compared to GmSL20H1. This enhanced regulatory capacity enables GmSL20H2 to more efficiently drive the flux of carbon into seeds for enlargement and oil accumulation, ultimately achieving further enhancement of both yield and quality in soybean germplasms of GmSL20 H2 haplotype.

Currently, the high‐yield cultivar QH34 carrying GmSL20 H2, whose 100‐seed weight is approximately 3‐fold larger than DN50, is most widely used in the Huang‐Huai‐Hai region of China. To further evaluate the potential utility of the H2 allele of GmSL20, we developed GmSL20 H2 ‐OE lines using QH34 as the recipient. Overexpression of GmSL20 H2 significantly improved seed length, width, thickness, and weight in the QH34 background, which resulted in an approximately 16% greater seed weight per plant compared with the parental line (Figure S12). Combined with the results from NILs and overexpressing lines, we conclude that the introduction of GmSL20 H2 could further improve soybean yield and quality in elite varieties through crossing or transgenic techniques. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the functionally enhanced GmSL20 H2 , despite its current restricted distribution, has great potential in efforts to breed high‐yield and high‐quality elite varieties worldwide.

Discussion

Seed size acts as a predominant determinant of plant success, with large seeds generally producing more vigorous seedlings due to greater nutritional reserves, increasing their chances of survival, growth, and resource capture (Li, Xu, and Li 2019). The value of seed size in agriculture is mainly due to its contribution to seed weight, one of the favourable agronomic criteria that determines crop yield. How seed size is established has long been a fascinating question for high‐yield breeding in soybean. In this study, we discovered that GmSL20 modulates seed length in soybean through GWAS. Overexpression of GmSL20 facilitated seed size, weight, and oil content, while the CRISPR/Cas9‐edited GmSL20‐CR lines displayed reduced seed size, weight, and oil content compared to the wild‐type. GmSL20 encodes a TGA‐type bZIP transcription factor that shows nuclear localization and the capability to bind activation sequence‐1 (as‐1) motif with AYGTCA as the core recognition sequence on the promoter regions of downstream targets (Li, Liu, et al. 2019). GmST05 and GmZF351 were both identified from the intersection of GmSL20‐upregulated DEGs and target genes in DAP‐seq profiles. Discovered from a major QTL controlling seed thickness and oil content via GWAS, GmST05 positively regulates overall seed traits of soybean, including seed thickness, length, width, weight, oil content, and ultimately seed yield per plant and plot (Cai et al. 2023; Duan et al. 2022). GmZF351 influences seed composition, particularly promoting seed oil accumulation by enhancing acetyl‐CoA supply and activating lipid biosynthesis and storage (Li et al. 2017). Simultaneous induction of two genes may enhance seed size and oil content in a coordinated manner at downstream of GmSL20. Therefore, we characterize a novel genetic and molecular mechanism, by which the GmSL20 acts as a key regulator to promote seed size and lipid biosynthesis via tuning GmST05 and GmZF351 expression. Our study also highlights the emergence of a superior allele from the domestication phase of soybean that further elevates DNA binding and transcriptional activation capacity of GmSL20, leading to greater seed size and quality (Figure 6c).

TGA TFs compose a subset of the larger bZIP protein family with many roles, including the regulation of root growth, flower development, and resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses (Lu et al. 2024). However, their function in soybean is yet to be explored. A previous study has divided 27 GmTGAs into 3 subclades through phylogenetic analysis, with GmSL20/GmTGA26 locating in the subclade II (Li, Liu, et al. 2019). GmSL20 is orthologous to PERIANTHIA (PAN) and OsbZIP47 in Arabidopsis and rice, respectively, albeit relatively low sequence identity. PAN plays a crucial role in the control of floral architecture and the formation of ovules, and defects in PAN function can lead to abnormal floral organ and reduced number of ovules (Chuang et al. 1999; Maier et al. 2009). On the contrary, GmSL20 knockout and overexpressing lines exhibit regular ratio of seed per pod and seed number per plant in comparison with DN50, suggesting that GmSL20 largely does not affect the number of ovules in soybean (Figure S13). Interestingly, overexpressing OsbZIP47 increases grain length in rice, but dampened grain width and weight (Hao et al. 2021). In addition to the dual role of regulating seed size and oil content, GmSL20 is putatively involved in the pleiotropic control of defence to soybean mosaic virus and salt stress (Jiang et al. 2021; Ke et al. 2022). These findings imply functional similarity and divergence of subclade II TGA proteins within angiosperms. Owning to multiple roles of TGA proteins, the potential of this family in improvement of soybean agronomic traits awaits discovery.

There are two major haplotypes of GmSL20 among soybean accessions, namely GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 , of which the latter is closely linked with higher seed length and seed weight. Indeed, we found that NIL‐GmSL20 H2 produced larger seeds and higher oil than NIL‐GmSL20 H1 . Remarkably, overexpression of GmSL20 H2 further enhances the seed size and weight in elite variety QH34 already carrying GmSL20 H2 , exhibiting great potential in improving modern cultivars. GmSL20 H2 represents a superior allele of its locus on controlling seed size; however, it is also a rare allele as indicated by low proportion distributed in major soybean producing regions. The positive Tajima's D and higher genetic diversity in cultivars compared with wild soybean signify the balancing selection at the GmSL20 locus (Figure S11b). Seed size is one of the traits that evolved most dramatically during soybean domestication, resulting in the fixation of advantageous alleles for larger seeds through selective sweeps in modern cultivars. For example, the haplotypes of GmSWEET10a and GmSW17 associated with larger seeds are strongly selected (Liang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2020). This raises the intriguing question about the complexity of allele selection that shapes the genetic makeup controlling seed size in domesticated soybean. First, for the absence of GmSL20 H2 in wild soybeans, it likely emerged during domestication. GmSL20 H2 might have naturally occurred from an incidental mutation event at the Huang‐Huai‐Hai region due to the higher allele frequency compared with other areas, subsequently being fixed and introduced to Eastern and Southeast Asia along with the global dissemination of soybean. Notably, haplotype H2 hardly exists in accessions from North America, South America, and Europe. An attractive hypothesis is that the time for spreading has not been long enough since GmSL20 H2 arose, and the occurrence of GmSL20 H2 is possibly later than the introduction of soybean into Europe and America in the eighteenth century. Supporting this idea, the genetic hitchhiking at the flanking regions around GmSL20 has not been strongly established in comparison with domestication‐targeted GmSW17 (Liang et al. 2024). Second, the “wild‐type” GmSL20 H1 is functional but merely not as efficient as GmSL20 H2 upon alleviating soybean seed size and oil content. In this case, the selective pressure might not be imposed intensely on this locus during the improvement. This is quite plausible given that loci under weak or no selection usually maintain higher levels of genetic polymorphisms because many different alleles can coexist without one having a strong advantage (Akashi et al. 2012; Bitarello et al. 2023). Our finding is consistent with the observation that genes controlling the same trait experience different selection from a recent study (Zhu et al. 2025).

Although numerous SNPs and small indels are present in the promoter region between the two haplotypes, both GmSL20 transcript abundance in NIL‐GmSL20 H1 and NIL‐GmSL20 H2 , as well as the promoter activities of GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 , remain quantitatively equivalent. These observations strongly support the conclusion that coding‐sequence variation o, not regulatory‐region diversity, drives the phenotypic differences in seed size and oil content. Thus, based on the TF nature of GmSL20, one possibility is that DNA binding and/or transcriptional activation is enhanced by polymorphisms on GmSL20 H2 . Indeed, GmSL20H2 exhibits broader binding sites and higher binding intensity at whole‐genome scale compared with GmSL20H1, while mildly higher promoter activity of GmST05 and GmZF351 is observed in the presence of GmSL20H2 compared with GmSL20H1 in a transient dual‐luciferase assay. To speculate further, because the nonsynonymous G to C substitution in the coding region is the sole SNP closely associating with increased seed size, it is highly likely to affect GmSL20 to bind to the promoter regions and/or activate expression of target genes. Notably, the corresponding amino acid change (Arg318Pro) locates in C‐terminal TGA domain (also named DOG1 domain), which might be involved in transcriptional activation and ABA responsiveness (Bentsink et al. 2006; Krüger et al. 2025), but not conserved bZIP domain for DNA binding. It is not yet clear, however, whether this variation impacts GmSL20 function directly via change of conformation or involves association with a partner to modulate its activity. The removal of the positively charged Arg side chain can eliminate critical salt bridge formations and electrostatic interactions needed for binding to other molecules, such as DNA, RNA, or proteins (Fuhrmann et al. 2015; Levintov and Vashisth 2021). Alternatively, the rigid ring‐like structure of proline imposes a specific geometry on the protein backbone, which can disrupt protein folding or introduce kinks into secondary structures and consequently alter the protein's three‐dimensional shape (Ceruso and Weinstein 2002). Regardless, GmSL20 H2 is more active than GmSL20 H1 on improving seed size, suggesting it is hypermorphic rather than amorphic. Plenty of evidence has revealed that the hypermorphic effect is an essential force to drive the crop improvement through fine‐tuning gene expression or function/stability of proteins (An et al. 2023; Liu, Zeng, et al. 2025; Ren et al. 2023). Taken GS2 as an example, a rare mutation disrupting the binding site of OsmiR396c causes elevated GS2 mRNA abundance, which in turn enhances grain weight and yield in rice (Hu et al. 2015). Further investigation is needed to clarify how the specific mutation in GmSL20H2 contributes to its enhanced binding and activation capacity, and to elucidate the deeper‐level mechanisms underlying the hypermorph.

Seed development is profoundly influenced by the well‐coordinated source‐flow‐sink relationship between the photosynthetic tissues and the developing seed (Rosado‐Souza et al. 2023; Smith et al. 2018). The source provides nutrients, such as sugars and amino acids, to support growth of sink, while the flow exerts a critical effect on the transportation of these resources for efficient nutrient allocation. Sucrose is the main carbohydrate transported over long distances through the phloem from source to sink in most plants (Pegler et al. 2023). The flow system in soybean consists of multiple crucial proteins for translocation and conversion of sucrose, including cell wall invertases and sugar transporters as revealed by previous studies (Miao et al. 2020; Song et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2020, 2019; Zhu et al. 2025). Our data implicate that the sink strength enhanced by GmSL20 could be the result of increased transcript levels of GmSWEETs, which are activated by GmST05/GmMFT (Cai et al. 2023; Duan et al. 2022). Additionally, functioning as a central regulator of lipid biosynthesis, GmZF351 may further facilitate carbon allocation toward the seeds in the form of seed oil, synergistically promoting both seed enlargement and oil accumulation with GmST05. In particular, the target genes of the superior allele GmSL20 H2 were significantly enriched in the group of pyruvate metabolism (Figure S9e), which may supply essential carbon precursors for de novo fatty acid synthesis. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that GmSL20 augments the ability of the seed to attract and utilize sugars from the source tissues. This mechanism also aligns with studies in other crops, where enhancement of the flow and sink has been shown to improve seed size and/or yield (Eom et al. 2011; Lou et al. 2025). The role of GmSL20 in regulating the source‐flow‐sink relationship is of particular interest for soybean improvement, as manipulating sink strength and flow activity could provide a strategy for increasing yield and quality without apparent growth penalties (Figure S13).

In addition to directly activating GmST05 and GmZF351, GmSL20 is likely involved in a broader gene regulatory network governing the seed size and quality. The GmNFYA‐GmZF392‐GmZF351 module promotes key metabolic processes spanning acyl‐CoA generation, fatty acid synthesis, triacylglycerol assembly and oil body formation, thereby playing a central role in seed oil accumulation in soybean. GmERFA negatively regulates oil biosynthesis through interaction with GmNFYA and consequent interference with its transcriptional activation ability (Liu, Hu, et al. 2025). Besides, GmZF351 is also a direct target of GmLEC1, a core component of the LAFL regulatory network that controls seed maturation and storage reserve accumulation (Lu et al. 2021). These connections raise the possibility that GmSL20 functionally intersects with both the GmERFA‐GmNFYA‐GmZF392‐GmZF351 and the LAFL networks. Notably, the ABI5 homologue GmbZIP151, together with GmSMS6 and GmUBQ1, forms an integrated molecular module that controls seed size and quality through repressing the transcription of GmST05 (Li et al. 2025). The convergence of these pathways on shared downstream targets suggests that GmSL20 may act within a broader and highly coordinated transcriptional network to fine‐tune seed development and oil accumulation. In conclusion, our findings establish GmSL20 as a positive regulator of seed size and oil content in soybean, operating through induction of GmST05 and GmZF351 to orchestrate carbon partitioning to seeds. Targeted utilization of the rare elite allele GmSL20 H2 in the future may further improve the yield and seed quality of modern soybean varieties.

Methods

Plant Materials and Phenotyping

A total of 698 soybean accessions were used for the genome‐wide association study. Soybean plants of the accessions were grown from May to September at the experimental station of the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China (40°22′ N, 116°23′ E). To generate transgenic soybean plants, the cultivars Dongnong50 (DN50) and Qihuang34 (QH34) were used. CRISPR and overexpression transgenic lines were planted in the field with a row spacing of 0.5 m and plant spacing of 0.15 m. For each transgenic event, 15 plants were grown for further analysis of seed traits. A recombinant inbred line (RIL) population was developed from a cross between soybean cultivars Williams 82 (W82) and QH34. The F1 plants were self‐pollinated and propagated to the F6 generation by single‐seed descent, resulting in 979 RILs. Utilizing the GenoBaits Soy40K array (Liu et al. 2022), a residual heterozygous line (RHL) was identified from the RIL population, carrying a 76.94‐kb heterozygous interval encompassing 7 genes within the QTL SL20. Among these, only GmSL20 (SoyZH13_20G101000/Glyma.20G113600) harbours a nonsynonymous G‐to‐C substitution, while the remaining genes show synonymous mutations or no coding variation. The heterozygosity thus effectively segregated at the GmSL20 locus. This RHL was subsequently self‐pollinated to the F8 generation to obtain a pair of near isogenic lines, designated as NIL‐GmSL20 H1 and NIL‐GmSL20 H2 . Measurements of seed oil content were performed as described previously (Duan et al. 2022).

All seeds were stored for 1 month after harvest before seed trait determination. Seed length, width, and thickness were measured using at least 20 representative dry seeds per accession or line. The 100‐seed weight was assessed with 5–20 replicates, while seed number per plant and seed weight per plant were assessed with 10 replicates. For seed oil content, at least 30 replicates were examined using seeds collected from 10 to 15 soybean plants for each material.

GWAS for Seed Length

A total of 31 870 983 SNPs derived from the previously resequenced soybean accessions were utilized in conducting GWAS for the seed length (Liu et al. 2020). Population structure was assessed using the Bayesian clustering algorithm in fastStructure (Raj et al. 2014). SNPs were filtered to retain those with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05 and a missing data rate < 10%. Association analysis was conducted using a mixed linear model (MLM) in EMMAX software, which was also used to calculate the kinship matrix based on simple matching coefficients from the variance–covariance structure of random effects (Kang et al. 2010). The significance threshold for GWAS was determined by permutation testing, as previously described (Fang et al. 2017). Briefly, phenotypic values were randomly shuffled to disrupt genotype–phenotype associations, and the MLM was reapplied to the permuted data. This process was repeated 1000 times, and the most significant p value from each permutation was recorded. The genome‐wide significance threshold was defined as the p value corresponding to the 5% type I error rate based on the distribution of these minimum p values.

Vector Construction and Transformation

To construct pGmSL20 H1 :GmSL20 H1 and pGmSL20 H2 :GmSL20 H2 overexpression vectors, a 3000‐bp fragment upstream from GmSL20 start codon and a full‐length GmSL20 coding sequence (CDS) without stop codon were amplified from cultivar DN50 or QH34 using KOD FX Neo (Toyobo), with the promoter obtained from genomic DNA and the CDS from cDNA. Corresponding fragments with overlapping sequences were subsequently ligated and cloned into the binary vector pTF101. For seed‐specific overexpression, the GmSL20 H2 coding sequence was cloned into the pBMI666 vector under the control of a soybean seed‐specific promoter from the β‐conglycinin α subunit encoding gene Glyma.20G148300. For CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout of GmSL20, two sgRNAs were designed as previously described (Bai et al. 2022). The editing cassettes were assembled in the binary vector pGES401, which employs a Single Transcript Unit (STU) system under the control of the pM4 promoter to co‐express Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 and a dual sgRNA scaffold. Each resulting construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 for soybean transformation via the Agrobacterium‐mediated method using the corresponding genetic background (DN50 or QH34). Primers are listed in Table S1.

Nucleic Acid Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh young leaves using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Murray and Thompson 1980). Total RNA was isolated from developing seeds at the R5‐3 stage with the Quick RNA Isolation Kit (Huayueyang) (Duan et al. 2022), following the manufacturer's protocol. First‐strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using TransScript One‐Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen). Quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen) on a LightCycler 480 II instrument (Roche) with the following program: 30 s at 95°C and 45 cycles of 10 s at 95°C, 15 s at 60°C, and 5 s at 72°C, followed by melting curve analysis to determine product specificity. The relative transcript level was calculated as previously described (Duan et al. 2022). Primers are listed in Table S1.

Subcellular Localization

The CDS of GmSL20 H1 or GmSL20 H2 was cloned into pGWB541 binary vector to generate 35S:GmSL20 H1 ‐EYFP or 35S:GmSL20 H2 ‐EYFP. Nicotiana benthamiana plants (3‐week‐old) were used to express the resulting constructs by Agrobacterium (GV3101 pMP90)–mediated transient transformation of lower epidermal leaf cells as described previously (Li et al. 2024). HY5‐RFP was used as a nucleus marker. Fluorescence signal was observed after 3 days of infiltration with a confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Zeiss; LSM980), using filters for YFP (excitation: 490–510 nm; emission: 530–550 nm) and RFP (excitation: 550–570 nm; emission: 580–610 nm). Primers are listed in Table S1.

DAP‐Seq Assay

Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of DN50 and QH34, fragmented, and used to construct Illumina sequencing libraries. The coding sequences of GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 were cloned into the pFN19K‐Halo vector to generate HaloTag fusion constructs. The GmSL20H1‐Halo and GmSL20H2‐Halo fusion proteins were expressed in vitro using the SP6 High‐Yield Wheat Germ Protein Expression System (Promega), and incubated with the pooled genomic DNA libraries to form protein‐DNA complexes. Complexes were immunoprecipitated with anti‐HaloTag magnetic beads (Promega). Captured DNA was eluted, amplified by PCR, and subjected to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform at Genedenovo Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Raw sequencing reads were processed with quality control and aligned to the soybean reference genome (Williams 82.a2.v1). Significantly enriched binding peaks were identified using MACS2 software under standard parameters (Zhang et al. 2008). The ChIPseeker R package was employed to annotate peaks to their nearest genes, and de novo motif discovery was performed using the MEME suite (Bailey et al. 2009).

RNA‐Seq

Total RNA was extracted from seeds of DN50 and GmSL20 H2 ‐OE plants at the R3 stage for transcriptome sequencing. Three biological replicates (pools of seeds from different plants) were collected, processed, and analysed for each sample. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq platform by BGI (Shenzhen, China). Raw reads were processed with fastp to remove adapter sequences and low‐quality bases. The clean reads were then aligned to the W82 Refseq v2 genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al. 2019), and gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts (Liao et al. 2014). Differentially expressed genes were assessed using the DESeq2 package (Love et al. 2014) in R with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and |Log2 fold change| > 1. Gene expression was characterized using transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) values for subsequent analyses, including hierarchical clustering.

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay

The 1037‐bp promoter sequence of GmST05 (SoyZH13_05G229200) and the 598‐bp promoter sequence of GmZF351 (SoyZH13_06G263400) were cloned into the pHIS2 vector to generate bait constructs, while the coding sequences of GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 were inserted into the pGADT7 vector to generate prey constructs. The recombinant pHIS2 bait plasmids (pHIS2‐pGmST05 and pHIS2‐pGmZF351), along with the empty pHIS2 vector as a negative control, were first transformed into the Y187 yeast strain. Transformants were screened on SD/−Trp‐His medium supplemented with gradient concentrations of 3‐Amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (3‐AT) to determine the minimal inhibitory concentration required to suppress background activation. Subsequently, the pGADT7‐GmSL20 H1 and pGADT7‐GmSL20 H2 prey plasmids were co‐transformed with the corresponding bait plasmids into the Y187 yeast strain. Yeast cells were plated on SD/−Trp‐Leu‐His dropout medium containing the predetermined concentration of 3‐AT and incubated at 30°C for 3–5 days. Positive colonies were selected, serially diluted, and spotted onto both SD/−Trp‐Leu‐His selective growth medium and the same medium supplemented with 100 mM 3‐AT for the confirmation of protein‐DNA interaction.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay

The coding sequences of GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 were cloned into the pGEX‐4 T vector to generate constructs expressing N‐terminal GST‐tagged fusion proteins. The resulting plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) for expression of GST‐GmSL20H1 and GST‐GmSL20H2 fusion proteins. Recombinant proteins were purified using BeyoGold GST‐tag Purification Resin (Beyotime). Biotin‐labelled DNA probes harbouring putative binding sites in the GmST05 and GmZF351 promoters were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Protein‐DNA binding reactions were performed using the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (ThermoFisher) following the manufacturer's protocol.

Transient Dual‐Luciferase Assay

For transient dual‐luciferase assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts, the 3000‐bp promoter regions of GmST05 and GmZF351 were amplified from Williams 82 genomic DNA and inserted into the pGreen0800‐LUC vector to generate reporter constructs pGmST05:LUC and pGmZF351:LUC. The effector constructs 35S:GmSL20 H1 ‐GFP and 35S:GmSL20 H2 ‐GFP were used for co‐transfection. For transient dual‐luciferase assays in N. benthamiana leaves, the 3000‐bp promoter regions of GmSL20 H1 and GmSL20 H2 were amplified from genomic DNA of W82 and QH34, respectively. Corresponding fragments were inserted into the CP461‐LUC vector to generate pGmSL20 H1 :LUC and pGmSL20 H2 :LUC. N. benthamiana plants (3‐week‐old) were used to express the constructs by Agrobacterium (GV3101 pMP90)–mediated transient transformation of lower epidermal leaf cells as described previously (Li et al. 2024). Leaf discs were excised 3 days post infiltration and ground for bioluminescence measurement. LUC activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) with a GloMax 20–20 Luminometer, following the method described by Ohta (2001). Relative luminescence values were calculated by normalizing firefly LUC activity to Renilla LUC activity.

Genetic Diversity and Selection Analysis

The high‐quality SNP dataset was obtained from a previous study by Zhu et al. (2025), which was based on the Glycine max reference genome ZH13_v2. To evaluate patterns of genetic variation, we calculated nucleotide diversity (π) for wild soybeans ( G. soja ), landraces, and cultivated soybeans using VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011) with a 100‐kb sliding window and a 10‐kb step size. Tajima's D values were also computed using VCFtools with a 100‐kb sliding window to assess selection pressure. Finally, all statistical results were visualized using the R package ggplot2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with WPS Excel. Two‐sided Student's t‐test was conducted for comparisons of means between two groups. Graphs were produced using Prism v9 (GraphPad Software). Box plots show the median, 25th percentile, and 75th percentile. Whiskers indicate the minimum and maximum of the data range, and individual data points are overlaid. For datasets with a sample size of n ≤ 5, individual data points and the mean are shown in bar graphs.
