Spectral coarse spaces based on indefinite operators: the H k H_{k} -GenEO method
Théophile Chaumont-Frelet \ORCID 0000-0002-6210-0774, Victorita Dolean* \ORCID 0000-0002-5885-1903, Mark Fry \ORCID 0009-0003-0543-8714, Ivan G. Graham \ORCID 0000-0002-5730-676X, Matthias Langer \ORCID 0000-0001-8813-7914

Abstract
GenEO (‘Generalised Eigenvalue problems on the Overlap’) is a method for constructing coarse spaces used in the preconditioning of iterative solvers for discrete PDEs. This method combines a (small) number of modes of local PDE eigenproblems to obtain a global coarse space. A coarse solve is then combined with local solves of the global PDE to obtain the preconditioner. A substantial theory for GenEO has been developed for the case when the local elgenproblems are positive semi-definite. This has been applied mostly to positive definite global PDEs, but also recently extended to the case of convection–diffusion–reaction problems, which may be neither self-adjoint, nor positive definite. However, when the global problem is highly indefinite, coarse spaces built from positive semi-definite local eigenproblems fail to be robust in practice. In this paper we consider highly indefinite global PDE problems, characterised by a large parameter k k (allowing also highly variable coefficients), and we develop a new spectral coarse space built from solving eigenvalue problems based on local copies of the global problem . We put no constraint on the diameters of the local domains, thus allowing the local eigenvalue problems to be indefinite. The new method (which we call H k H_{k} -GenEO) is seen to be much more robust as k k increases than methods based on positive semi-definite eigenproblems. We provide sufficient conditions for robustness of the preconditioned GMRES iterative method, in terms of the tolerance of the local eigenproblems and the size of the subdomains for the local PDE solves. In practice the method is observed to be robust with respect to k k under even weaker conditions on the local eigenproblem tolerance. The experiments also suggest the method can be resilient to high variation in PDE coefficients.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with the theory and implementation of two-level domain decomposition
preconditioned iterative methods for solving finite element approximations of the indefinite boundary value problem:

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)-k^{2}nu

\displaystyle=f\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(1.1b)

where
k>0
,
A
is an almost everywhere positive definite matrix with
L^{\infty}
entries and
n
is
an almost everywhere positive
L^{\infty}
function.
The domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
) is assumed to be a Lipschitz polygon (
d=2
) or polyhedron (
d=3
).
We emphasise the highly indefinite case—when
k
is large—since this presents particular challenges for iterative solvers.
While (
1.1a
) is instantly recognisable as a (heterogeneous) Helmholtz equation, the appearance of the Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.1b
)
means that (
1.1
) does not directly model scattering problems (where (
1.1a
) would normally be posed on an infinite domain,
accompanied by a radiation condition instead of (
1.1b
)).
However, (
1.1
) does appear in other physical models of wave phenomena (e.g. a vibrating membrane constrained at its boundary)
and its solution typically oscillates with decreasing period as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus (
1.1
) provides us with a relevant highly indefinite model problem with variable coefficients,
and we use it here to assess the performance of a new class of iterative solvers for such problems.
We will assume throughout that the ‘wavenumber’
k
is such that the problem (
1.1a
), (
1.1b
) is well posed,
in the sense that it has a unique weak solution for all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

The problem (
1.1
) is discretised using conforming finite elements to obtain a linear system (see (
2.7
)).
When this system is very large (especially in 3D), it should be solved iteratively using a good preconditioner
(i.e. a cheap approximate inverse).
Here we propose a novel domain decomposition preconditioner and analyse its performance when used with GMRES as the iterative solver.
In classical one-level overlapping domain decomposition methods, the global domain
\Omega
is covered by a set of
overlapping ‘local’ subdomains
\Omega_{j}^{\ell}
,
j=1,\ldots,Q
,
and the one-level Schwarz preconditioner is built from partial solutions of the global problem on each subdomain.
Since this is, in general, not scalable as the number of subdomains grows, an additional global coarse solve is usually added to enhance scalability,
as well as robustness with respect to coefficient heterogeneity or increasing
k
.

Spectral coarse spaces.

Since classical coarse spaces (piecewise polynomials on a coarse grid), are generally not robust for highly heterogeneous problems, various
operator-dependent coarse spaces have been introduced to better treat the heterogeneity,
e.g. ‘spectral’ coarse spaces, built from well-chosen modes of suitable generalised PDE eigenvalue problems
defined on subdomains. Such spectral information was used for both robust solution approximation (e.g.
[
2
,
12
]
) and for preconditioning in
[
17
,
18
]
.
However, all this work was for positive definite (albeit highly heterogeneous) PDEs.

In this paper we propose and analyse a novel variant of the GenEO spectral coarse space.
GenEO coarse spaces were first introduced in
[
34
]
, where it was proved that,
for self-adjoint positive definite problems, when combined with a one-level method, and using a preconditioned conjugate gradient solver,
the resulting algorithm is not only scalable with respect to the number of subdomains but also robust with respect to coefficient variation.
Related works for positive definite problems include,
for example
[
1
,
22
,
3
,
33
]
;
see also
[
34
]
and
[
16
]
. Here we construct coarse spaces for (
1.1
)
from eigenproblems defined on each subdomain of an overlapping ‘coarse’ cover:
\Omega_{i}^{c}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
.
This
need not be related
to the ‘local’ cover
\Omega_{j}^{\ell}
introduced above;
indeed the
\Omega_{i}^{c}
can be relatively large.
To explain ideas simply, now let
\xi_{i}^{c}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
be a partition of unity subordinate to the coarse cover.
The original GenEO method was aimed at problem (
1.1
) with
k=0
, and built a coarse space
from selected modes of the generalised eigenvalue problem (GEVP):

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla p)=\lambda\bigl[-\xi_{i}\nabla\cdot(A\nabla(\xi_{i}p))\bigr]\qquad\text{on}\;\;\Omega_{i}^{c},

(1.2)

(computed using standard variational finite element approximation). The eigenmodes of (
1.2
) provide
a powerful coarse space for (
1.1
) when
A
is highly heterogeneous and
k=0

[
34
]
.
However, they are less suitable when
k
is very large, as demonstrated numerically in

[
7
,
5
]
where the coarse space based on (
1.2
) (and called
\Delta
-GenEO)
was compared to a coarse space (called
H
-GenEO), based on eigenmodes of the GEVP:

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla p)-k^{2}np=\lambda\bigl[-\xi_{i}\nabla\cdot(A\nabla(\xi_{i}p))\bigr]\qquad\text{on}\;\;\Omega_{i}^{c}.

(1.3)

The indefinite operator from (
1.1a
) now appears on the left-hand side of (
1.3
), ensuring
that some eigenmodes of this problem will be oscillatory (provided the
\Omega_{i}^{c}
are large enough).
In
[
7
,
5
]

H
-GenEO was found to have better robustness properties than
\Delta
-GenEO
at high frequency but no theory was provided.
In the current paper we propose and analyse the ‘
H_{k}
-GenEO’ coarse space based on the following variant of (
1.3
):

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla p)-k^{2}np=\lambda\bigl[-\xi_{i}\nabla\cdot(A\nabla(\xi_{i}p))+k^{2}\xi_{i}n(\xi_{i}p)\bigr]\qquad\text{on}\;\;\Omega_{i}^{c}.

(1.4)

The same indefinite operator appears on the left-hand side, while the right-hand side has the extra positive term
making it close to the norm in which we do the analysis of this paper.
Our actual analysis is for the finite element approximation of this—where the multiplication
\xi_{i}p

is replaced by an algebraically defined operator
\Xi_{i}(p)
; see (
2.49
).

Main results.

Our main results are given in Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2

and we give a simplified summary of these here.
Let
H_{\ell}
(respectively
H_{c}
) denote the maximum diameter of the ‘local’ subdomains
\Omega_{j}^{\ell}

(respectively ‘coarse’ subdomains
\Omega_{i}^{c}
).
On each
\Omega_{i}^{c}
let
\{\lambda_{m}^{i}:m=1,2,\ldots\}
denote the eigenvalues of the numerical approximation of
(
1.4
) (real and ordered in non-decreasing order).
Then, if only the first
m_{i}
eigenfunctions on each
\Omega_{i}^{c}
are used in the coarse space, and
\lambda_{m_{i}+1}^{i}>0
,
we define

\tau\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min_{i=1}^{N}\lambda^{i}_{m_{i}+1}

(1.5)

to be the
eigenvalue tolerance
.
Let
C_{\textup{{stab}}}>0
denote the stability constant arising from the assumption that (
1.1
) has a unique weak solution
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
for each
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
(see Assumption
2.3
).
Without loss of generality, we assume that, on
\Omega
, the eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix
A
are all
\geq 1
,
and all values of
n
are
\leq 1
(Assumption
2.1
).
Then, when the 2-level additive Schwarz preconditioned system is solved by GMRES, our theory proves
its robust convergence under certain conditions.
For this illustration, suppose the finite element mesh is quasiuniform with mesh diameter satisfying
h\sim k^{-1}
.
Then,
k
-robustness holds provided

H_{\ell}\lesssim k^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau\gtrsim(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})^{2}\,k^{2},

(1.6)

where the hidden constants depend on the number of times any subdomain is overlapped by others.
The rate of GMRES convergence depends modestly on
a_{\textup{{max}}}
(the maximum eigenvalue of
A
) and
n_{\textup{{min}}}
(the minimum value of
n
),
although this dependence is minimal in practice. Our results extend to more general meshes, with stricter conditions for robustness.

The first condition in (
1.6
) arises because
H_{\ell}
is required to be small enough to guarantee the solvability of the local problems.

Importantly, our theory imposes no explicit upper constraint on the coarse diameter
H_{c}
,
and we see in the numerical results that it is beneficial to have the coarse subdomains large enough,
making the GEVP (
1.4
) indefinite, and providing oscillatory modes.
The separation of local and coarse subdomain sizes which we employ here contrasts with some earlier work,
which assumed a single subdomain cover for both local and coarse decomposition.

Our analysis of (
1.4
) given here is a substantial generalisation of the theory in
[
6
]
,
which studied the application of
\Delta
-GenEO coarse spaces (using (
1.2
), but applied to systems arising
from general non-self-adjoint and indefinite PDEs of convection-diffusion-reaction type).
In that context only the much more stringent estimate
\tau\gtrsim(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})^{2}\,k^{8}
on the eigenvalue tolerance could be proved,
although the
k^{8}
factor was later reduced in
[
10
]
.
Nevertheless, the eigenvalue tolerance estimate in (
1.6
) still turns out to be rather pessimistic in practice.
Our numerical results (in 2D) seem to indicate that choosing a large enough positive (but constant) value for
\tau

yields
k
-robust convergence of GMRES with very modest coarse space dimensions as
k
increases.
Remarks on this are given in §
5
.

To emphasise the difference between this paper and what went before, we remark that a
substantial task here concerns the study of the indefinite GEVP (
1.4
) and the corresponding projection onto the eigenspace
spanned by all negative (and some positive) eigenvalues. In this respect our analysis is substantially more difficult and different
from earlier studies using the positive definite problem (
1.2
).

Since this spectral theory may be interesting in its own right, it is written in an abstract form in Section
2.2
.
Our proof of (
1.6
) uses the ‘Elman theory’ for GMRES (
[
13
]
or
[
35
, Lemma C.11]
),
and proceeds by proving an upper bound for the norm of the preconditioned matrix and a lower bound on the
distance of its field of values from the origin. We estimate these quantities, explicitly analysing their dependence on,
and independence of, the key parameters of the problem.

Other relevant work.

The first publications on spectral coarse spaces for Helmholtz problems with truncated far-field Sommerfeld radiation condition
include methods based on Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps
[
9
,
4
]
,
while a numerical survey is given in
[
7
]
and then enriched in
[
11
]
.
In
[
27
]
approximation spaces for Helmholtz problems are constructed by solving local positive semi-definite GEVPs
(similar to (
1.2
)) in subspaces of local Helmholtz harmonic functions.
The resulting multiscale approximation space is shown to be quasi-optimal
(i.e. the Galerkin error is bounded by the best approximation in this space times a constant independent of the parameters—in this case
the coefficients of the PDE and
k
).
Subsequently
[
28
]
showed that the corresponding classical additive Schwarz preconditioner yields a Richardson iteration
which is a parameter-independent contraction. However, the subdomains in
[
28
]
are constrained to be
of diameter
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
and therefore the local Helmholtz problems are (close to) coercive.
A similar coarse space was proposed in
[
23
]
, where Helmholtz problems with constant coefficients are analysed.
Most of the theory in
[
23
]
is concerned with the absorptive case where
k
is replaced by
k+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon

with
\varepsilon>0
. In the theory for
\varepsilon
small the subdomain diameter is constrained to be
o(k^{-1})

as
k\rightarrow\infty
meaning the local problems are again coercive.

Related results on the extension of multiscale approximation methods (originally devised
for elliptic problems with oscillatory coefficients), to the Helmholtz case are in
[
30
]
.
The corresponding LOD (localised orthogonal decomposition) is used to construct a coarse space in
[
26
]
,
where corresponding theory (for Helmholtz problems with constant coefficients) is presented.
Related methods are discussed in
[
14
]
.
However, these multiscale approaches are quite different from the spectral methods studied in the present paper.

Additional recent work
[
19
]
has shown that classical additive Schwarz preconditioners using coarse spaces
based on piecewise polynomials are robust for Helmholtz problems as
k\rightarrow\infty
,
provided the approximations on the fine and coarse levels are quasi-optimal (independent of
k
).
Such a prescription can allow the coarse space dimension to be much smaller than the fine space dimension for high wavenumber problems,
although both dimensions can be quite high. Methods based on fixed degree polynomials are considered in
[
15
]

while
hp
versions where
p
increases logarithmically with
k
are included in
[
19
]
.
However, spectral information is not used to construct these coarse spaces and dependence on coefficient variation is not analysed.

Plan of the paper.

In §

the problem is described and a theory for finite element approximation of (

)

2
Background and abstract theoretical results

Here we introduce the variational formulation of problem (
1.1a
), (
1.1b
), and its discretisation.
Then, we present an abstract finite-dimensional theory for variational generalised eigenvalue problems with indefinite forms.
Subsequently, we define our additive Schwarz domain decomposition preconditioner, with the novel feature being the
H_{k}
-GenEO coarse space.

2.1
Problem formulation and finite element discretisation

The weak formulation of (
1.1
) is to find
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

b(u,v)=(f,v)\qquad\text{for all}\ v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.1)

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product and
b:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is defined as

b(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}(A\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-k^{2}{n}uv)\,\mathrm{d}x.

The following weak regularity assumptions are used throughout this work.

Assumption 2.1
.

The coefficients
A
and
n

and the domain
\Omega
in problem (
1.1
) satisfy the following:

(i)

A:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
n:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are measurable functions
with
A
a symmetric tensor field, such that, for some
0<a_{\textup{{min}}}\leq a_{\textup{{max}}}
and
0<n_{\textup{{min}}}\leq n_{\textup{{max}}}
,

\displaystyle a_{\textup{{min}}}|\bm{\xi}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq A(x)\bm{\xi}\cdot{\bm{\xi}}\leq a_{\textup{{max}}}|\bm{\xi}|^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for a.e.}\ x\in\Omega\text{ and all }\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad n_{\textup{{min}}}

\displaystyle\leq n(x)\leq n_{\textup{{max}}}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for a.e.}\ x\in\Omega.

(ii)

Since (
1.1a
) can be equivalently rewritten as

-\nabla\cdot\biggl(\frac{A}{a_{\textup{{min}}}}\biggr)\nabla u-\biggl(\frac{k^{2}n_{\textup{{max}}}}{a_{\textup{{min}}}}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{n}{n_{\textup{{max}}}}\biggr)u=\frac{f}{a_{\textup{{min}}}},

we shall assume, without loss of generality, that
a_{\textup{{min}}}=1=n_{\textup{{max}}}
and that the diameter,
D_{\Omega}
,
of the domain
\Omega
satisfies
D_{\Omega}\leq 1
.

(iii)

We assume that
k\geq k_{0}
for some given
k_{0}>0
.

Notation 2.2
.

For any subdomain
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
we use
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Omega^{\prime}}
to denote the
L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
inner product,

(\cdot,\cdot)_{n,\Omega^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(n\cdot,\cdot)_{\Omega^{\prime}}
to denote the weighted
L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
inner product with weight
n
,
and
\|\cdot\|_{\Omega^{\prime}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{n,\Omega^{\prime}}
to denote the corresponding norms. We also introduce the bilinear forms:

a_{\Omega^{\prime}}(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}A\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad b_{\Omega^{\prime}}(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=a_{\Omega^{\prime}}(u,v)-k^{2}(u,v)_{{n},\Omega^{\prime}}\qquad\text{for}\ u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime}),

(2.2)

and define the semi-norm induced by
a
,
|u|_{a,\Omega^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sqrt{a_{\Omega^{\prime}}(u,u)}
.
We also use the
k
-weighted and
A
- and
n
-dependent inner product:

(u,v)_{1,k,\Omega^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=a_{\Omega^{\prime}}(u,v)+k^{2}(u,v)_{{n},\Omega^{\prime}},

and we denote the induced
k
-norm by
\|u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\prime}}
.
When
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega
, we abandon the subscript
\Omega
in all the notation.

The bilinear forms
a_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{1,k,\Omega^{\prime}}
are symmetric positive definite
on
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})
and
H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})
respectively;
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric, but in general indefinite.
However, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality twice one can easily show that:

\displaystyle|b_{\Omega^{\prime}}(u,v)|\leq\|u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\prime}}\|v\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\prime}}.

(2.3)

We make the following assumption on solvability of (
2.1
).

Assumption 2.3
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, problem (
2.1
) has a unique solution
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
there exists a constant
C_{\textup{{stab}}}>0
such that

\|u\|_{1,k}\leq C_{\textup{{stab}}}\|f\|\qquad\text{for all}\ f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(2.4)

Note that
C_{\textup{{stab}}}
exists if
k^{2}
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of the operator
-\frac{1}{n}\diverg(A\nabla\cdot)
.

Assumption 2.4
.

Throughout,
\mathcal{T}_{h}
will denote a family of simplicial meshes on
\Omega
, parameterised by maximum diameter
h
.
We assume this family is shape regular, i.e. there exists a constant
C_{\textup{{SR}}}
independent of all parameters
such that
\rho_{T}\geq C_{\textup{{SR}}}h_{T}
for each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, where
h_{T}
denotes the diameter of
T

and
\rho_{T}
denotes the diameter of the largest inscribed sphere in
T
.
In the analysis (see Lemma
3.4
) we will require that
h\leq\sqrt{C_{1}}k^{-1}
for some constant
C_{1}
independent of
k
.
We also assume inverse estimates:

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|_{\tau}\leq C_{\textup{{inv}}}k\|v\|_{\tau}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\;\;v\in V^{h},

(2.5)

for some constant
C_{\textup{{inv}}}\geq 1
.

Remark 2.5
.

A sufficient condition to ensure
(
2.5
) with
C_{\textup{{inv}}}
independent of
k
is obtained by requiring
h_{\tau}k

to be bounded below with respect to
k
for all
\tau
, so that
h_{\tau}\sim 1/k

(e.g. a quasiuniform mesh with all elements refined of order
1/k
).
Our strongest results are when
C_{\textup{{inv}}}
is independent of
k
.
However,
C_{\textup{{inv}}}
could still be quite large, allowing local mesh refinement treating corners or edges, for example.
In our estimates below, we will treat

will remain implicit.

Let
V^{h}\subseteq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the
H^{1}
-conforming Lagrange finite element space of polynomials of degree
r\geq 1

with respect to this mesh, with nodal basis functions denoted by
\{\phi_{j}:j=1,\ldots,\dim(V^{h})\}
.
The indices

are also known as

. The Galerkin approximation of (
2.1
) is:

\text{find }u_{h}\in V^{h}\text{ such that }\qquad b(u_{h},v_{h})=(f,v_{h})\qquad\text{for all}\ v_{h}\in V^{h}.

(2.6)

This is equivalent to the linear system

\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f},

(2.7)

where
(\mathbf{B})_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=b(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})
and
(\mathbf{f})_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(f,\phi_{i})
.
Later we also need the matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{S}
that correspond to
a
and
({n}\cdot,\cdot)
,
namely,
(\mathbf{A})_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=a(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})
and
(\mathbf{S})_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=({n}\phi_{j},\phi_{i})

The solvability of (
2.6
) for sufficiently fine meshes and/or high enough polynomial degree
is assured by the following lemma, proved for
r=1
and
h\rightarrow 0
in
[
32
, Theorem 2]
.
Since we could not find this result for general
r\geq 1
in the literature, we give a proof here.

Lemma 2.6

(Solvability for higher polynomial degrees)
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
hold, and set

\beta\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=k\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\\[0.60275pt]
{\|\psi\|_{n}=1}\end{subarray}}\;\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|{z_{\psi}}-v_{h}\|_{1,k},

(2.8)

where
z_{\psi}
is the unique element of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
b(w,{z_{\psi}})=(w,\psi)_{{n}}
for all
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then, there exists a continuous function
\theta:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, solely depending on
\Omega
,
A
,
n
and
k
,
with
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\theta(\tau)=0
and such that

\beta\leq C\,\theta\Bigl(\frac{h}{r}\Bigr),

(2.9)

where
C>0
is a constant which can depend on the shape-regularity constant
C_{\textup{{SR}}}
.
In addition, if
\beta<1/\sqrt{2}
, then the linear system in (
2.6
) uniquely determines
u_{h}
,
and we have, for all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq\frac{1}{{1-2\beta^{2}}}\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|u-v_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{{1-2\beta^{2}}}\biggr)\frac{1}{k}\|f\|_{{n^{-1}}}.

(2.10)

The uniqueness of
u_{h}
and the estimates in (
2.10
) for
\beta<\sqrt{2}/2

constitute a standard result typically referred to as the ‘Schatz argument’
[
31
,
32
]
.
For completeness, we review the proof of (
2.10
) here before proving (
2.9
).

Proof.

Let us first note that, since
b
is symmetric, the definition of
\beta
actually makes sense due to Assumption
2.3
.
Note also that
a priori
we only have a supremum instead of a maximum in (
2.8
);
we will later justify that it is a maximum. It follows from the definition of
\beta
that, for each
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega){\backslash\{0\}}
,

k\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|{z_{\psi}}-v_{h}\|_{1,k}=k\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|(z_{\psi}/\|\psi\|_{n})-v_{h}\|_{1,k}\|\psi\|_{n}=k\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|(z_{(\psi/\|\psi\|_{n})}-v_{h}\|_{1,k}\|\psi\|_{n}\leq\beta\|\psi\|_{n}.

Hence, there exists
v_{h}\in V^{h}
such that

k\|{z_{\psi}}-v_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq\beta\|\psi\|_{n}.

(2.11)

Now let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and let
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
be the unique solution of (
2.1
).
Let us first assume that
u_{h}\in V^{h}
is
any
solution of (
2.6
).
By Assumption
2.3
, there exists a unique
\xi{=z_{k(u-u_{h})}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

such that

b(w,\xi)=k(w,u-u_{h})_{{n}}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.12)

On one hand, (by (
2.11
) with
\psi=k(u-u_{h})
), there exists
\xi_{h}\in V^{h}
such that

\|\xi-\xi_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq\beta\|u-u_{h}\|_{{n}}.

On the other hand, taking the test function
w=u-u_{h}
in (
2.12
)
we see that

k\|u-u_{h}\|_{{n}}^{2}=b(u-u_{h},\xi)=b(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{h})\leq\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,k}\|\xi-\xi_{h}\|_{1,k},

where we used (
2.3
) and the fact that
b(u-u_{h},w_{h})=0
for
w_{h}\in V^{h}
since
u
solves (
2.1
)
and
u_{h}
solves (
2.6
).
Combining these two estimates we obtain

k\|u-u_{h}\|_{{n}}\leq\beta\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,k},

and hence

\displaystyle(1-2\beta^{2})\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,k}^{2}-2k^{2}\|u-u_{h}\|_{{n}}^{2}=b(u-u_{h},u-u_{h})

\displaystyle=b(u-u_{h},u-v_{h})\leq\|u-u_{h}\|_{1,k}\|u-v_{h}\|_{1,k}

for all
v_{h}\in V^{h}
. Assuming that
2\beta^{2}<1
we obtain the first estimate in (
2.10
).
The second estimate follows from the definition of
\beta
; see again (
2.11
).

At that point, we have established (
2.10
) for
any
solution,
but we do not know yet if such a solution exists.
However,
u_{h}
is defined through a finite-dimensional linear system.
In this setting, stability implies uniqueness, which in turns implies existence.
This concludes the proof of (
2.10
).

We now turn our attention to (
2.9
). We first observe that, thanks
to Fredholm alternative (applied in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
), Assumption
2.3
guarantees that the mapping

S:H^{-1}(\Omega)\to H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
defined by

b(S\psi,v)=\langle\psi,v\rangle

for all
\psi\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
and
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a well-defined continuous linear operator
where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
,
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
-pairing.

Let
B_{L^{2}}=\{\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)\;|\;\|{n^{-1}}\phi\|_{n}\leq 1\}

denote the closed unit ball in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Since the injection
L^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)
is compact, we conclude
that
K\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=S(B_{L^{2}})
is a compact set in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Note that for
\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we have

S\phi=z_{\{n^{-1}\phi\}}
(with the notation introduced above).
Hence,

\max_{v\in K}\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|v-v_{h}\|_{1,k}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)\\[0.60275pt]
{\|n^{-1}\phi\|_{n}\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\;\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|z_{(n^{-1}\phi)}-v_{h}\|_{1,k}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\\[0.60275pt]
\|\psi\|_{n}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\;\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|z_{\psi}-v_{h}\|_{1,k}=\frac{\beta}{k}\,.

(2.13)

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since
K
is compact, there exist
N
functions
\{v^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subseteq K

such that, for each
v\in K
, there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
with

k\|v-v^{j}\|_{1,k}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

We then invoke the density of
C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
in

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, which guarantees that, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
there exists
{\widetilde{v}^{j}}\in C_{\rm c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with

k\|v^{j}-\widetilde{v}^{j}\|_{1,k}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Now we invoke a degree-explicit approximation result
(e.g. Melenk and Sauter
[
29
]
) which implies that there
exists a constant
C
(depending on
C_{\textup{{SR}}}
) such that

\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|\widetilde{v}^{j}-v_{h}\|_{H^{i}(\Omega)}\leq C\Bigl(\frac{h}{r}\Bigr)^{2-i}|\widetilde{v}^{j}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for each}\;\;i=0,1,\quad j=1,\ldots,N.

This estimate follows from
[
29
, Theorem B.4]
(given only on a unit element), by applying a standard scaling argument on each finite element
and using the fact that the resulting global approximation operator maps continuous functions into
V^{h}
;
see, e.g. the steps in
[
29
, Theorem 5.5]
.
Hence there exist
v^{1}_{h},\ldots,v^{N}_{h}\in V^{h}
such that

\displaystyle k\|\widetilde{v}^{j}-v^{j}_{h}\|_{1,k}

\displaystyle\leq k\Bigl(a_{\textup{{max}}}\|\widetilde{v}^{j}-v_{h}^{j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+k^{2}\|\widetilde{v}^{j}-v_{h}^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{kh}{r}\biggl(a_{\textup{{max}}}+\Bigl(\frac{kh}{r}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}|\widetilde{v}^{j}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\frac{kh}{r}\biggl(a_{\textup{{max}}}+\Bigl(\frac{kh}{r}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}M(\varepsilon)

(2.14)

for
h/r
small enough, where

M(\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|\widetilde{v}^{i}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

Putting the pieces together and using the triangle inequality we obtain

k\min_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\|v-v_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}+C\frac{kh}{r}\biggl(a_{\textup{{max}}}+\Bigl(\frac{kh}{r}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}M(\varepsilon)

for all
v\in K
.
Let us write
\beta(h,r)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta
to indicate the dependence on
h
and
r
.
We can assume, without loss of generality, that
C\,\geq 1
.
It follows from (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) that

\frac{\beta(h,r)}{C\,}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}+C\frac{kh}{r}\biggl(a_{\textup{{max}}}+\Bigl(\frac{kh}{r}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}M(\varepsilon).

Clearly, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\frac{\beta(h,r)}{C\,}\leq\varepsilon
if
h/r\in(0,\delta)
.
Now choose a decreasing sequence
\varepsilon_{\ell}>0
such that
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\varepsilon_{\ell}=0
.
Then there exist
\delta_{\ell}>0
such that
(\delta_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
is strictly decreasing,
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\delta_{\ell}=0
and

\frac{\beta(h,r)}{C\,}\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\qquad\text{for}\;\;h,r>0\quad\text{with}\;\;\;\frac{h}{r}<\delta_{\ell}.

Defining
\theta(\delta_{\ell})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\varepsilon_{\ell}
and interpolating linearly we obtain
\theta

satisfying (
2.9
).
∎

Before proceeding, we recall Friedrichs’ inequality (e.g.
[
25
, Theorem 13.19]
).

Lemma 2.7

(Friedrichs’ inequality and a consequence)
.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set that lies between two parallel hyperplanes, separated by a distance
L
.
Then, for all
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\prime})
,

\|u\|_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{2}\,}\|\nabla u\|_{\Omega^{\prime}}.

(2.15)

Combining (
2.15
) with Assumption
2.1
(ii) we obtain that, for any subdomain
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
with diameter
H
,
the following estimate holds:

\|u\|_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq\frac{H}{\sqrt{2}\,}\|\nabla u\|_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq\frac{H}{\sqrt{2}\,}\|u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\prime}}

for all
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\prime})
.

2.2
Abstract spectral theory for indefinite generalised eigenvalue problems

We shall build our coarse space by solving
local generalised eigenvalue problems, which may be indefinite (see (
2.49
)).
So in this section we derive, in an abstract setting, the required properties of such problems.
First we collect some properties of a positive semi-definite bilinear form
(such as appears on the right-hand side of (
2.49
)).

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
\widetilde{V}
is a real vector space with
\dim\widetilde{V}=\tilde{n}
.
Let
c
be a symmetric positive semi-definite bilinear form on
\widetilde{V}
,
let
\ker\textup{{c}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{v\in\widetilde{V}:\textup{{c}}(v,w)=0\ \text{for all}\ w\in\widetilde{V}\}

and suppose that
\dim\ker\textup{{c}}=s<\tilde{n}
.
Further, let
\widehat{V}\subseteq\widetilde{V}
be an
(\tilde{n}-s)
-dimensional subspace such that
\widehat{V}\cap\ker\textup{{c}}=\{0\}
.
Then
\widetilde{V}=\widehat{V}\oplus\ker\textup{{c}}
, where
\oplus
denotes a direct sum, and
c
is positive definite on
\widehat{V}
.

Proof.

The relation
\widetilde{V}=\widehat{V}\oplus\ker\textup{{c}}
is clear since
\dim\widehat{V}+\dim\ker\textup{{c}}=\tilde{n}
and
\widehat{V}\cap\ker\textup{{c}}=\{0\}
.
The form
\textup{{c}}|_{\widehat{V}\times\widehat{V}}
is symmetric and satisfies
\textup{{c}}(v,v)>0
for
v\in\widehat{V}\setminus\{0\}
by the assumption on
\widehat{V}
.
Hence
\textup{{c}}|_{\widehat{V}\times\widehat{V}}
is positive definite.
∎

Theorem 2.9

(Abstract theorem for indefinite eigenvalue problems)
.

Let
\widetilde{V}
,
c
and
s
be
as in Lemma
2.8
and suppose also that
b
is a symmetric
(
possibly indefinite
)
bilinear form on
\widetilde{V}
.
Consider the generalised eigenvalue problem: find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in\widetilde{V}\backslash\{0\}
such that

\textup{{b}}(p,v)=\lambda\textup{{c}}(p,v)\qquad\text{for all}\quad v\in\widetilde{V}.

(2.16)

If
s\geq 1
, suppose also that
b
is positive definite on
\ker\textup{{c}}
.
Then
\widetilde{V}
has a basis
\{p_{j}:j=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}\}
, in which
\{p_{j}:j=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\}
are eigenvectors of (
2.16
) which
can be chosen to be orthonormal with respect to
c
and the corresponding finite eigenvalues can be ordered

-\infty<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{\tilde{n}-s}<\infty.

(2.17)

The remaining basis vectors
\{p_{j}:j=\tilde{n}-s+1,\ldots,\tilde{n}\}
form a basis of
\ker\textup{{c}}
.
In this sense we say that they correspond to infinite eigenvalues of (
2.16
).
Moreover,

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(p_{j},p_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\delta_{j,j^{\prime}},\quad

\displaystyle\ j\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\},\ j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}\};

(2.18)

\displaystyle\textup{{c}}(p_{j},p_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}},\quad

\displaystyle\ j\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\},\ j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}\}.

(2.19)

If
s=0
(trivial case) the same conclusion holds but the positive definiteness condition on
b
is not required,
and all eigenvalues are finite.

Proof.

Suppose
s\geq 1
and
b
is positive definite on
\ker\textup{{c}}
.
Let us choose
\widehat{V}\subseteq\widetilde{V}
such that
\widetilde{V}=\widehat{V}\oplus\ker\textup{{c}}
(as in Lemma
2.8
);
then every
p\in\widetilde{V}
has a unique decomposition
p=\widehat{p}+p_{\textup{{c}}}
with
\widehat{p}\in\widehat{V}
and
p_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
.
Thus the GEVP (
2.16
) is equivalent to seeking
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widehat{p}\in\widehat{V}
and
p_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
such
that
\widehat{p}+p_{\textup{{c}}}\neq 0
and

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(\widehat{p}+p_{\textup{{c}}},v)=\lambda\textup{{c}}(\widehat{p},v)\qquad\text{for all}\;\;v\in\widetilde{V}.

(2.20)

This, in turn, is equivalent to solving the coupled system

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(\widehat{p},v_{\textup{{c}}})+\textup{{b}}(p_{\textup{{c}}},v_{\textup{{c}}})

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all}\;\;v_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}},

(2.21)

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(\widehat{p},\widehat{v})+\textup{{b}}(p_{\textup{{c}}},\widehat{v})

\displaystyle=\lambda\textup{{c}}(\widehat{p},\widehat{v})\qquad\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all}\;\;\widehat{v}\in\widehat{V},

(2.22)

for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widehat{p}\in\widehat{V}
and
p_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
such
that
\widehat{p}+p_{\textup{{c}}}\neq 0
.

Now, by assumption on
b
, for each
\widehat{w}\in\widehat{V}
, there exists a unique
w_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
such that

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(w_{\textup{{c}}},v_{\textup{{c}}})=-\textup{{b}}(\widehat{w},v_{\textup{{c}}})\qquad\text{for all}\;\;v_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}.

(2.23)

Denoting the solution to (
2.23
) as
w_{\textup{{c}}}=\mathcal{S}\widehat{w}
, this defines a linear map

\mathcal{S}:\widehat{V}\rightarrow\ker\textup{{c}}
with the property

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(\mathcal{S}\widehat{w},v_{\textup{{c}}})=-\textup{{b}}(\widehat{w},v_{\textup{{c}}})\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\widehat{w}\in\widehat{V}\;\;\text{and}\;\;v_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}},

(2.24)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}\bigl((I+\mathcal{S})\widehat{w},v_{\textup{{c}}}\bigr)=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\widehat{w}\in\widehat{V}\;\;\text{and}\;\;v_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}.

(2.25)

We now consider the GEVP: find
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\widehat{p}\in\widehat{V}\backslash\{0\}
such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textup{{b}}}(\widehat{p},\widehat{v})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\textup{{b}}\bigl((I+\mathcal{S})\widehat{p},\widehat{v}\bigr)=\lambda\textup{{c}}(\widehat{p},\widehat{v})\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\widehat{v}\in\widehat{V}.

(2.26)

(This is the Schur complement of (
2.21
), (
2.22
).)

Note that
\mathcal{S}
is self-adjoint with respect to
b
. This is because, using the symmetry of
b
(at the first and third steps)
and the definition of
\mathcal{S}
(at the second and fourth steps),

\textup{{b}}(\mathcal{S}\widehat{p},\widehat{v})=\textup{{b}}(\widehat{v},\mathcal{S}\widehat{p})=-\textup{{b}}(\mathcal{S}\widehat{v},\mathcal{S}\widehat{p})=-\textup{{b}}(\mathcal{S}\widehat{p},\mathcal{S}\widehat{v})=\textup{{b}}(\widehat{p},\mathcal{S}\widehat{v}).

Thus the bilinear form
\widetilde{\textup{{b}}}
on the left-hand side of (
2.26
) is symmetric on
\widehat{V}
.
Now
c
is symmetric and (by Lemma
2.8
) positive definite on
\widehat{V}
;
so the GEVP (
2.26
) has
\tilde{n}-s
eigenpairs,
which we denote
(\lambda_{j},\widehat{p}_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s
, with real finite eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
,
which can be ordered as in (
2.17
). The
\widehat{p}_{j}\in\widehat{V}
can be chosen orthonormal with respect to
c

and form a basis of
\widehat{V}
.
Now we define

\displaystyle p_{j}=(I+\mathcal{S})\widehat{p}_{j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s.

(2.27)

By (
2.27
), (
2.26
) and since
\mathcal{S}\widehat{p}_{j}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
, we have

\textup{{b}}(p_{j},\widehat{v})={\widetilde{\textup{{b}}}(\widehat{p}_{j},\widehat{v})}=\lambda_{j}\textup{{c}}(\widehat{p}_{j},\widehat{v})=\lambda_{j}\textup{{c}}(p_{j},\widehat{v})\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\widehat{v}\in\widehat{V}.

Also, by (
2.27
) and (
2.25
) we have
\textup{{b}}(p_{j},v_{\textup{{c}}})=0
for all
v_{\textup{{c}}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
, and so
(see (
2.21
) and (
2.22
)),

(\lambda_{j},p_{j}),\,j=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s

are eigenpairs of the original GEVP (
2.16
).
Now, choosing an arbitrary basis,
\{p_{\tilde{n}-s+1},\ldots,p_{\tilde{n}}\}
, of
\ker\textup{{c}}
we obtain a basis of
\widetilde{V}
.
For
j\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\}
, relation (
2.19
) follows for
j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\}

because
\mathcal{S}\widehat{p}_{j},\mathcal{S}\widehat{p}_{j^{\prime}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
, and so
\textup{{c}}(p_{j},p_{j^{\prime}})=\textup{{c}}(\widehat{p}_{j},\widehat{p}_{j^{\prime}})=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}

since the
\{\widehat{p}_{j}:j=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\}
were chosen orthonormal with respect to
c
.
For
j^{\prime}\in\{\tilde{n}-s+1,\ldots,\tilde{n}\}
, (
2.19
) is trivial because then
p_{j^{\prime}}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
.

Relation (
2.18
) then follows directly from (
2.19
) because, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\}

and any
j^{\prime}{\in\{1,\dots,\tilde{n}\}}
,
\textup{{b}}(p_{j},p_{j^{\prime}})=\lambda_{j}\textup{{c}}(p_{j},p_{j^{\prime}})
.
This completes the proof when
s\geq 1
, while the case
s=0
is trivial since (
2.16
) can then be reduced to a standard eigenvalue problem.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

Note that some statements in the previous proof can be made more general:

1.

The requirement that
b
is positive definite on
\ker\textup{{c}}
in part
(i)
of Theorem
2.9

could be weakened to simply requiring that problem (
2.23
) has a unique solution
for all
\widehat{w}\in\widehat{V}
, where
\widehat{V}
is as in Lemma
2.8
.
However, since we shall need the positive definiteness in Proposition
2.12
anyway, we avoid this extra generality.

2.

In general, the eigenvectors
p_{j},\,j=1,\ldots\tilde{n}-s
defined via (
2.27
) are not elements of
\widehat{V}

because
\mathcal{S}
maps into
\ker\textup{{c}}
.

The following corollary then follows directly from Theorem
2.9
.

Corollary 2.11
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.9
, every
v\in V
can be expressed as

v=v_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\textup{{c}}(v,p_{j})p_{j}\quad\text{with}\quad v_{0}\in\ker\textup{{c}},

(2.28)

If
\ker\textup{{c}}=\{0\}
then
s=0
and
v_{0}=0
.

We now define a projection operator mapping
{\widetilde{V}}
onto the span of the first
m

eigenvectors of (
2.16
), where
m
is chosen so that all eigenvectors corresponding to negative eigenvalues are included
(recall the ordering (
2.17
)).
This operator is crucial in the analysis of the proposed preconditioner.

Proposition 2.12

(Projection onto the
m
-dimensional eigenspace)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.9
,
suppose that there exists
m\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s-1\}

such that

\lambda_{m+1}>0
,
and define the projector

\Pi v=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\textup{{c}}(v,p_{j})p_{j}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;v\in\widetilde{V}.

(2.29)

Then

\textup{{c}}(v-\Pi v,v-\Pi v)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{m+1}}\textup{{b}}(v-\Pi v,v-\Pi v).

(2.30)

When applying this proposition we will show that such an
m
exists, under a modest assumption.

Proof.

Let
v\in{\widetilde{V}}
be expressed as in (
2.28
) and set
\alpha_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\textup{{c}}(v,p_{j})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{n}-s\}
.
We use the properties from Corollary
2.11
and Theorem
2.9
to obtain,

\displaystyle\textup{{b}}(v-\Pi v,v-\Pi v)

\displaystyle=\textup{{b}}\Biggl(v_{0}+\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\alpha_{j}p_{j}\,,\,v_{0}+\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\alpha_{j}p_{j}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\textup{{b}}(v_{0},v_{0})+2\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\alpha_{j}\textup{{b}}(p_{j},v_{0})+\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\textup{{b}}(v_{0},v_{0})+2\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\alpha_{j}\lambda_{j}\textup{{c}}(p_{j},v_{0})+\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}.

Then we use the the fact that
v_{0}\in\ker\textup{{c}}
and the positive definiteness of
b
on
\ker\textup{{c}}
to obtain

\textup{{b}}(v-\Pi v,v-\Pi v)=\textup{{b}}(v_{0},v_{0})+\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}\geq\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}\geq\lambda_{m+1}\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\alpha_{j}^{2}.

(2.31)

Using an almost identical argument and the results from Theorem
2.9
we also obtain

\textup{{c}}(v-\Pi v,v-\Pi v)=\sum_{j=m+1}^{\tilde{n}-s}\alpha_{j}^{2}.

(2.32)

Now (
2.30
) follows from (
2.31
) and (
2.32
).
∎

This lemma shows that, although the bilinear form
\textup{{b}}(\cdot,\cdot)
may be indefinite on the full space,
it becomes positive definite on the complement of the subspace spanned by the first
m
eigenvectors,
provided all discarded eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
(for
j>m
) are strictly positive.
In the context of coarse space construction, this implies that if we retain all eigenfunctions associated
with non-positive or small eigenvalues, then the remainder is well-controlled by
\textup{{b}}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
leading to stability estimates essential for robust preconditioning.

2.3
Domain decomposition

Notation 2.13
.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}
be any subdomain of
\Omega
, composed of a union of elements of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
We introduce the finite element spaces:

In order to construct the two-level Schwarz preconditioner, we choose two overlapping covers of

The first consists of ‘local subdomains’

Definition 2.18

\displaystyle\text{for all}\;\;v\in\widetilde{V}_{{\Omega_{i}^{c}}},

(2.51)

\displaystyle\|\Xi_{i}^{{c}}(v)\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\Lambda_{c}^{-1}\|v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}\qquad

Proof.

(2.53)

where

l
.
Then it is easy to see that
\supp v\subseteq\bigcup_{l\in\partial\Omega_{i}^{c}}B(l,h_{l})
.
Moreover, for each
l
,
v
vanishes on a portion of the boundary of
B(l,h_{l})
having
d-1
dimensional measure
\gtrsim h_{l}
.
So, by the generalised Friedrichs’ inequality (see, e.g.
[
35
, Lemma A.15]
) and since
h_{l}\lesssim h
,
we have

\|\nabla v\|_{B(l,2h_{l})}^{2}\gtrsim{h_{l}}^{-2}\|v\|_{B(l,2h_{l})}^{2}\gtrsim{h}^{-2}\|v\|_{B(l,2h_{l})}^{2}.

(3.12)

Also, by shape regularity, each
T\in\supp v
can only be overlapped by a bounded number of balls
B(l,h_{l})
as
h\rightarrow 0
;
so we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(v)}\|\nabla v\|_{T}^{2}\sim\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(v)}\sum_{l\in\partial\Omega_{i}^{c}}\|\nabla v\|_{T\cap B(l,h_{l})}^{2}=\sum_{l\in\partial\Omega_{i}^{c}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(v)}\|\nabla v\|_{T\cap B(l,h_{l})}^{2}=\sum_{l\in\partial\Omega_{i}^{c}}\|\nabla v\|_{B(l,h_{l})}^{2}.

Combining this with (
3.12
) we obtain

\|\nabla v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}\gtrsim h^{-2}\|v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}
.

Hence, from (
3.11
), there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
(which may depend on
C_{\textup{{SR}}}
) such that

b_{i}(v,v)\geq(C^{\prime}h^{-2}-k^{2})\|v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}=(C^{\prime}-(hk)^{2})h^{-2}\|v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}

for all
h
and
v\in\ker c_{i}
. Hence, if
(hk)^{2}\leq C_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C^{\prime}/2
, we have

b_{i}(v,v)\geq C_{1}h^{-2}\|v\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}
.
∎

Remark 3.7

The assumptions of Theorem

.

Let

Further, set

For

\displaystyle b_{i}(\phi_{l_{j}}^{{i}},\phi_{l_{j}}^{{i}})\geq\|\phi_{l_{j}}^{i}\|_{a,\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}-k^{2}\|\phi_{l_{j}}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}\geq\Bigl(\frac{2}{(d_{l_{j}}^{i})^{2}}-k^{2}\Bigr)\|\phi_{l_{j}}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}=\frac{1}{(h_{l_{j}}^{i})^{2}}\biggl(2\Bigl(\frac{h_{l_{j}}^{i}}{d_{l_{j}}^{i}}\Bigr)^{2}-(kh_{l_{j}}^{i})^{2}\biggr)\|\phi_{l}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}.

(3.16)

where

{h_{l_{j}}^{i}/d_{l_{j}}^{i}}

Theorem 3.8

(Estimate of

.

Let

with

Proof.

We recall from equation (
2.26
) that
\lambda_{1}^{i}
is the minimum eigenvalue of the problem:
find
\lambda^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\widehat{p}^{i}\in\widehat{V}_{i}\backslash\{0\}

\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{p}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=b_{i}\bigl((I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{p}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i}\bigr)=\lambda^{i}c_{i}(\widehat{p}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})\qquad\text{for all}\;\;\widehat{v}^{i}\in\widehat{V}_{i},

(3.20)

where
\mathcal{S}_{i}:\widehat{V}_{i}\rightarrow\ker c_{i}
is the operator defined by

b_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i},{w_{c_{i}}})=-b_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},{w_{c_{i}}})\qquad\text{for all}\;\;{w_{c_{i}}}\in\ker c_{i}.

(3.21)

Using (

) and the fact that

maps into

) is equivalent to

(3.22)

Since, by Lemma
2.8
,
c_{i}
is positive definite on
\widehat{V}_{i}
, we obtain from Rayleigh’s principle that

\lambda_{1}^{i}=\min_{0\neq{\widehat{v}^{i}}\in\widehat{V}_{i}}\frac{{b_{i}\bigl((I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{v}^{i},(I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{v}^{i}\bigr)}}{c_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})}.

(3.23)

To estimate (
3.23
) from below, we first derive an estimate from above for
\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}

for any
\widehat{v}^{i}\in\widehat{V}_{i}
. Using (
3.10
), the definition (

) of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
,
the fact that

and (
2.5
), we have,
for any

,

\displaystyle C_{1}h^{-2}\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq b_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i},\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i})=-b_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i})

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i})\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}\|\nabla\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}+k^{2}\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}\|\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}

(3.24)

and thus

\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}\leq(a_{\textup{{max}}}C^{2}_{\textup{{inv}}}+1)C_{1}^{-1}(hk)^{2}\|\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}\leq(a_{\textup{{max}}}C^{2}_{\textup{{inv}}}+1)\|\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}.

(3.25)

As a result, for the numerator in (

), we have

b_{i}\bigl((I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{v}^{i},(I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{v}^{i}\bigr)\geq-k^{2}\|(I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}\geq-k^{2}(a_{\textup{{max}}}C^{2}_{\textup{{inv}}}+2)^{2}\|\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}.

(3.26)

For the denominator note that
\widehat{V}_{i}=V_{i}
by (
3.17
); hence Lemma
2.23
implies that

c_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})\geq k^{2}n_{\textup{{min}}}\|\Xi_{i}^{c}(\widehat{v}^{i})\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}\gtrsim k^{2}n_{\textup{{min}}}\Lambda_{c}^{-2}\|\widehat{v}^{i}\|_{\Omega_{i}^{c}}^{2}.

(3.27)

For the rest of this proof (only) let
\mathbf{min}
denote the minimum of any quantity over all

0\neq{\widehat{w}^{i}}\in\widehat{V}_{i}
. Recall also that
c_{i}
is positive definite on
\widehat{V}_{i}
.
If
\mathbf{min}\,\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{w}^{i},\widehat{w}^{i})\geq 0
, then

and (
3.19
) is trivially true.
Now, assume that
\mathbf{min}\,\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{w}^{i},\widehat{w}^{i})<0

\frac{\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})}{c_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})}\geq\frac{\mathbf{min}\,\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{w}^{i},\widehat{w}^{i})}{c_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})}\geq\frac{\mathbf{min}\,\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{w}^{i},\widehat{w}^{i})}{\mathbf{min}\,{c_{i}(\widehat{w}^{i},\widehat{w}^{i})}}=\frac{\mathbf{min}\,{b}_{i}((I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{w}^{i},(I+\mathcal{S}_{i})\widehat{w}^{i})}{\mathbf{min}\,{c_{i}(\widehat{w}^{i},\widehat{w}^{i})}},

and so, by (
3.26
), (
3.27
), for all

,

\frac{\widetilde{b}_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})}{c_{i}(\widehat{v}^{i},\widehat{v}^{i})}\gtrsim-\Lambda_{c}^{2}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}(a_{\textup{{max}}}C^{2}_{\textup{{inv}}}+2)^{2},

v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|\Phi_{j}^{\ell}v\|_{1,k,\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}^{2}\lesssim\Lambda_{\ell}\left(1+\frac{1}{(k{\delta_{\ell}})^{2}}\right)\|v\|_{1,k,\Omega}^{2}\lesssim\Lambda_{\ell}\|v\|_{1,k,\Omega}^{2},

(3.39)

Now define
z_{j}^{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\Phi_{j}^{\ell}(v-z_{0})\in V_{j}^{\ell}
. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E_{j}^{\ell}z_{j}^{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E_{j}^{\ell}\Phi_{j}^{\ell}(v-z_{0})=(v-z_{0})\sum_{j=1}^{Q}{\Phi_{j}^{\ell}}=v-z_{0}.

Moreover, from Lemma
3.10
we have

\|z_{0}\|_{1,k}^{2}\leq 2\left(\|z_{0}-v\|_{1,k}^{2}+\|v\|_{1,k}^{2}\right)\lesssim(1+\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta)\|v\|_{1,k}^{2}.

Also, using (
3.39
), we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|z_{j}^{\ell}\|_{1,k,\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|\Phi_{j}^{\ell}(v-z_{0})\|_{1,k,\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}^{2}\lesssim\Lambda_{\ell}\|v-z_{0}\|_{1,k}^{2}.

Combining this with Lemma
3.10
and the bound for
z_{0}
we obtain the result.
∎

It is convenient to introduce here the orthogonal projections onto the local spaces
{P_{j}^{\ell}}:V^{h}\rightarrow V^{\ell}_{j}
,
with respect to the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{1,k}
, by requiring that

(P_{j}^{\ell}v,w)_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}=(v,E_{j}^{\ell}w)_{1,k,\Omega}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;v\in V^{h},\;\;w\in V_{\Omega_{j}^{\ell}},\quad\text{and}\;\;j=1,\ldots,Q.

(3.40)

Similarly, we define the orthogonal projection
P_{0}:V^{h}\rightarrow V_{0}
by requiring

(P_{0}v,w)_{1,k}=(v,w)_{1,k}=(v,E_{0}w)_{1,k}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;v\in V^{h},\;\;w\in V_{0}.

(3.41)

Using these we then define the operator
P:V^{h}\rightarrow V^{h}
by

P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=E_{0}P_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}{E_{j}^{\ell}}{P_{j}^{\ell}}.

(3.42)

The operator
P
is an (easier-to-analyse) proxy for the operator
T

(which represents our preconditioned matrix — see (
2.43
) and Proposition
2.15
).
Here, we estimate the field of values of
P
, and then use this to estimate the field of values of
T
,
leading to our main result (Theorem
3.1
).

Proposition 3.12

(Field of values of
P
)
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
3.9
, let
C_{3}
be as in Lemma
3.11
.
Then

\|v\|^{2}_{1,k}\leq C_{3}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)(Pv,v)_{1,k}

(3.43)

for every
v\in V^{h}
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.11
, the definitions of
P_{j}^{\ell}
and
P_{0}
and then Cauchy–Schwarz, we get

\displaystyle\|v\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Biggl(v,z_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}{E_{j}^{\ell}}{z_{j}^{\ell}}\Biggr)_{1,k}=(v,z_{0})_{1,k}+\Biggl(v,\sum_{j=1}^{Q}{E_{j}^{\ell}}{z_{j}^{\ell}}\Biggr)_{1,k}

\displaystyle=(P_{0}v,z_{0})_{1,k}+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}({P_{j}^{\ell}}v,{z_{j}^{\ell}})_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\leq\|P_{0}v\|_{1,k}\|z_{0}\|_{1,k}+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|{P_{j}^{\ell}}v\|_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\|{z_{j}^{\ell}}\|_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}{.}

We now apply Cauchy–Schwarz and Lemma
3.11
again to obtain

\displaystyle\|v\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|P_{0}v\|_{1,k}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|{P_{j}^{\ell}}v\|_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}C_{3}^{1/2}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)^{1/2}\|v\|_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\left((E_{0}P_{0}v,v)_{1,k}+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}({E_{j}^{\ell}}{P_{j}^{\ell}}v,v)_{1,k}\right)^{1/2}C_{3}^{1/2}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)^{1/2}\|v\|_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\left(E_{0}P_{0}v+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}{E_{j}^{\ell}}{P_{j}^{\ell}}v,v\right)_{1,k}^{1/2}C_{3}^{1/2}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)^{1/2}\|v\|_{1,k}

\displaystyle=(Pv,v)_{1,k}^{1/2}\,C_{3}^{1/2}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)^{1/2}\|v\|_{1,k},

and the inequality (
3.43
) follows.
∎

We now study the fundamental properties of the operators
T_{j}^{\ell}
and
T_{0}
.

3.2
Existence and stability of
{T_{j}^{\ell}}
,
j=1,\ldots,Q

Lemma 3.13

(
T_{j}^{\ell}
are well defined)
.

If
{H_{\ell}}k<\sqrt{2}
, then, for each
j=1,\ldots,Q
,
b_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is positive definite
on
V_{\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}
and the operators
{T_{j}^{\ell}}
in (
2.41
) are well defined.

Proof.

Using Friedrichs’ inequality (
2.15
) and the assumption that
a_{\textup{{min}}}=1=n_{\textup{{max}}}
, we obtain, for
u\in V_{\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}

b_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}(u,u)=a_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}(u,u)-k^{2}({n}u,u)_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\geq\frac{2}{{{(H_{\ell})}}^{2}}\|u\|_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}^{2}-k^{2}\|u\|_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}^{2}=\frac{2-{{(H_{\ell})}}^{2}k^{2}}{{{(H_{\ell})}}^{2}}\|u\|_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}^{2},

and hence
b_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is positive definite when
H_{\ell}k<\sqrt{2}
.
The square system (
2.41
) defining
T_{j}^{\ell}v
is then positive definite and the result follows.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

Whilst this is a sufficient condition for the
{T_{j}^{\ell}}
to be well defined, it is
far from necessary since the
{T_{j}^{\ell}}
will be well defined when the linear system corresponding to (
2.41
) is non-singular.
However, it seems hard to guarantee good stability properties for
T_{j}^{\ell}
without the above condition.

Lemma 3.15

(Stability of
{T_{j}^{\ell}}
,
j=1,\ldots,Q
)
.

Suppose that
\sqrt{2}{H_{\ell}}k\leq 1
. Then

\|{T_{j}^{\ell}}v\|_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\leq 2\big\|v|_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\big\|_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;v\in V^{h}.

(3.44)

Proof.

Using (
2.41
), (
2.3
), the fact that
n_{\textup{{max}}}=1=a_{\textup{{min}}}
, and Friedrichs’ inequality (
2.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|{T_{j}^{\ell}}v\|^{2}_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}

\displaystyle=b_{{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}({T_{j}^{\ell}}v,{T_{j}^{\ell}}v)+2k^{2}\|T_{j}^{\ell}v\|_{n,\Omega_{i}^{\ell}}^{2}=b_{\Omega_{i}^{\ell}}\bigl(v|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}},{T_{j}^{\ell}}v\bigr)+2k^{2}\|T_{j}^{\ell}v\|_{n,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|v|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\|T_{j}^{\ell}v\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}+2k^{2}\|T_{j}^{\ell}v\|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|v|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\|{T_{j}^{\ell}}v\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}+k^{2}(H_{\ell})^{2}\|T_{j}^{\ell}v\|^{2}_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}},

which implies

\bigl(1-k^{2}(H_{\ell})^{2}\bigr)\|T_{j}^{\ell}v\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\leq\big\|v|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}
,
and the result follows.
∎

3.3
Existence and stability of
T_{0}

Lemma 3.16

(
T_{0}
is well-defined)
.

Let
C_{2}>0
be as in Theorem
3.9
. If

C_{2}\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}k\Theta^{1/2}(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,},

(3.45)

then there exists
h_{1}>0
such that, for all
h\in(0,h_{1})
, the operator
T_{0}
is well defined.

Proof.

Let
C
and
\Theta
be as in Lemma
2.6
.
Then there exists
h_{1}>0
such that

C\,\Theta\Bigl(\frac{h}{r}\Bigr)\leq\min\biggl\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{k_{0}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{1/2}}{2}\biggr\}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;h\in(0,h_{1}).

Let
h\in(0,h_{1})
and
assume (for a contradiction) that there exists a
w_{0}\in V_{0}\backslash\{0\}{\subseteq V^{h}}
such that

b(w_{0},z)=0\qquad\text{for all}\;\;z\in V_{0}.

(3.46)

Let
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the solution of
b(w,v)=(w_{0},v)
for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
which exists and is unique by Assumption
2.3
.
It follows from Lemma
2.6
that
\beta\leq\min\bigl\{1/2,k_{0}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{1/2}/2\bigr\}<1/\sqrt{2}

(where

2.6

and there exists

Let
z\in V_{0}
be arbitrary.
Inserting
v=w_{0}
in (
3.47
) and combining with (
3.46
) and then (
2.3
), we obtain

\|w_{0}\|^{2}=b(w_{h},w_{0})=b(w_{h}-z,w_{0})\leq\|w_{h}-z\|_{1,k}\|w_{0}\|_{1,k}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;z\in V_{0}.

3.10

\|w_{0}\|^{2}\leq\inf_{z\in V_{0}}\|w_{h}-z\|_{1,k}\|w_{0}\|_{1,k}\leq C_{2}\,\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}\Theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|_{1,k}\,\|w_{0}\|_{1,k}.

(3.48)

Also, equation (
3.46
) with

shows that

0=b(w_{0},w_{0})=\|w_{0}\|_{1,k}^{2}-2k^{2}\|w_{0}\|_{n}^{2}

), implies

\displaystyle\|w_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}\Theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|_{1,k}\sqrt{2}\,k\|w_{0}\|_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\,C_{2}\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}k\Theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|_{1,k}\|w_{0}\|,

and hence

(3.49)

Then we use the triangle inequality, (
3.47
) and (

) to obtain

\|w_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq\|w-w_{h}\|_{1,k}+\|w\|_{1,k}\leq(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})\|w_{0}\|.

(3.50)

With (
3.50
) used in (
3.49
), we arrive at

\|w_{0}\|\leq\sqrt{2}\,C_{2}\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}k\Theta^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})\|w_{0}\|.

Together with (
3.45
) this leads to a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.17

(Stability of
T_{0}
)
.

With
C_{2}
as in Theorem
3.9
, assume that (
3.45
) is satisfied. Then there exists
h_{1}>0
such that, for
h\in(0,h_{1})
,

\|T_{0}u-u\|\leq{C_{2}}\,\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}\Theta^{1/2}(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})\|T_{0}u-u\|_{1,k}\qquad\text{for all}\ u\in V^{h}.

(3.51)

Suppose, in addition, that

C_{2}\,\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}k\Theta^{1/2}(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})\leq\frac{1}{4};

(3.52)

then

\|u-T_{0}u\|_{1,k}\leq 2\|u\|_{1,k}\qquad\text{for all}\ u\in V^{h}.

(3.53)

Proof.

Let
h_{1}>0
be as in Lemma
3.16
.
Further, let
u\in V^{h}
and consider the auxiliary problem:

\text{find }w_{h}\in V^{h}\;\;\text{such that}\quad b(w_{h},v)=\bigl(T_{0}u-u,v\bigr)\quad\text{for all}\;\;v\in V^{h}.

(3.54)

Analogously to (
3.50
), problem (
3.54
) has a unique solution
w_{h}
for

, and

\|w_{h}\|_{1,k}\leq(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})\|T_{0}u-u\|.

(3.55)

Now, by the definition (
3.41
) of
T_{0}
, we have
b(T_{0}u-u,z)=0
for all

.
Inserting
v=T_{0}u-u\in V^{h}
into (

) and using (

), we obtain, for every

\|T_{0}u-u\|^{2}\leq\|T_{0}u-u\|_{1,k}\inf_{z\in V_{0}}\|w_{h}-z\|_{1,k}\leq\|T_{0}u-u\|_{1,k}{C_{2}}\,\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}\Theta^{1/2}\|w_{h}\|_{1,k}.

(3.56)

Together with (

), this proves (
3.51
).

Let us now show (
3.53
). Inserting
P_{0}u-T_{0}u\in V_{0}
in the definition (

) of

we obtain

Using this together with the link between the bilinear forms

and

,
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\|u-T_{0}u\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=(u-T_{0}u,u-T_{0}u)_{1,k}=b(u-T_{0}u,u-T_{0}u)+2k^{2}(u-T_{0}u,u-T_{0}u)_{n}

\displaystyle=b(u-T_{0}u,u-T_{0}u)-b(u-T_{0}u,P_{0}u-T_{0}u)+2k^{2}(u-T_{0}u,u-T_{0}u)_{n}

\displaystyle=(u-T_{0}u,u-P_{0}u)_{1,k}-2k^{2}(u-T_{0}u,u-P_{0}u)_{n}+2k^{2}(u-T_{0}u,u-T_{0}u)_{n}

\displaystyle\|u-T_{0}u\|_{1,k}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{1,k}+2k\|u-T_{0}u\|_{n}\leq\|u\|_{1,k}+2{C_{2}}\,\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}k\Theta^{1/2}(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})\|u-T_{0}u\|_{1,k}

∎

\displaystyle k^{2}\bigl(E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u-u,E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\bigr)_{n}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\bigl({T^{\ell}_{j}}u-u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}},P^{\ell}_{j}u\bigr)_{n,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\leq k^{2}\big\|T^{\ell}_{j}u-u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{n,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\big\|_{n,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq k\bigl\|T^{\ell}_{j}u-u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|{P^{\ell}_{j}}u\big\|_{n,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\leq k\bigl\|{T^{\ell}_{j}}u-u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\frac{H_{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}\,}\big\|{P^{\ell}_{j}}u\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{kH_{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}\,}\Bigl(\|{T^{\ell}_{j}}u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}+\big\|u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}}\Bigr)\big\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}.

(4.6)

Now, note that the hypothesis (
3.3
) implies that
\sqrt{2}k{H_{\ell}}<\sqrt{2}/(3\Lambda_{\ell})<1
;
so we can apply Lemma
3.15
to obtain from (
4.6
) that

k^{2}\bigl(E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u-u,E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\bigr)_{n}\leq\frac{3kH_{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}\,}\big\|u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}<3kH_{\ell}\big\|u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}.

Summing over
j
, applying Cauchy–Schwarz and (
2.34
), we obtain

\displaystyle k^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\bigl(E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u-u,E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\bigr)_{n}\leq 3kH_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\big\|u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq 3kH_{\ell}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\big\|u|_{\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}\leq 3kH_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell}^{1/2}\|u\|_{1,k}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}.

(4.7)

To estimate the sum on the right-hand side of (
4.7
), note that

\|E_{j}^{\ell}P_{j}^{\ell}u\|_{1,k}^{2}=(P_{j}^{\ell}u,P_{j}^{\ell}u)_{1,k,\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}=(P_{j}^{\ell}u,u)_{1,k,\Omega_{j}^{\ell}}=(E_{j}^{\ell}P_{j}^{\ell}u,u)_{1,k},

and so, using (
2.36
), we have

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\Bigg\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\|_{1,k}^{2}=\Lambda_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}(E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u,u)_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\ell}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u,\,u\Biggr)_{1,k}\leq\Lambda_{\ell}\,\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\Bigg\|_{1,k}\|u\|_{1,k}.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|P^{\ell}_{j}u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{Q}(E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u,u)_{1,k}=\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u,\,u\Biggr)_{1,k}\leq\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\Bigg\|_{1,k}\|u\|_{1,k}\leq\Lambda_{\ell}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}.

This, together with (
4.7
), leads to

k^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\bigl(E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u-u,E^{\ell}_{j}P^{\ell}_{j}u\bigr)_{n}\leq 3kH_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}.

(4.8)

Step 4
(
Obtaining (
4.1
)
).
We can now combine (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and (
4.8
) to obtain

\displaystyle|R|

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl(2C_{2}\,\Lambda_{c}(a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast})^{1/2}k\Theta^{1/2}(1+C_{\textup{{stab}}})+3k\Lambda_{\ell}H_{\ell}\Bigr)\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{C_{3}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2},

with
s
and
C_{3}
as defined in Theorem
3.1
.
Hence, using this, (
4.3
) and Proposition
3.12
, we obtain

\displaystyle(Tu,u)_{1,k}

\displaystyle=(Pu,u)_{1,k}+R\geq C_{3}^{-1}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)^{-1}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}-|R|

\displaystyle\geq C_{3}^{-1}\bigl(1+\Lambda_{\ell}\Lambda_{c}^{2}a_{\textup{{max}}}n_{\textup{{min}}}^{-1}C_{\ast}\Theta\bigr)^{-1}(1-s)\|u\|_{1,k}^{2},

as required.

Step 5
(
Obtaining (
4.2
)
).
Since
E_{0}T_{0}u=T_{0}u
, we have

\|Tu\|_{1,k}^{2}=\Bigg\|T_{0}u+\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u\Biggr\|_{1,k}^{2}\leq 2\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}^{2}+2\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u\Bigg\|_{1,k}^{2}.

(4.9)

For the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.9
), Cauchy–Schwarz and (
3.53
) yield

\displaystyle\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=(T_{0}u,T_{0}u)_{1,k}=(T_{0}u-u,T_{0}u)_{1,k}+(u,T_{0}u)_{1,k}

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{0}u-u\|_{1,k}\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}+\|u\|_{1,k}\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|u\|_{1,k}\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}+\|u\|_{1,k}\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}=3\|u\|_{1,k}\|T_{0}u\|_{1,k}.

(4.10)

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
4.9
) we use (
2.36
), Lemma
3.15
and (
2.34
) to obtain

\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{Q}E^{\ell}_{j}T^{\ell}_{j}u\Bigg\|_{1,k}^{2}\leq\Lambda_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\|T^{\ell}_{j}u\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}\leq\Lambda_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}4\big\|u|_{\Omega_{i}}\big\|_{1,k,\Omega^{\ell}_{j}}^{2}\leq 4\Lambda_{\ell}^{2}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}.

(4.11)

Combining (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) with (

) we arrive at

\|Tu\|_{1,k}^{2}\leq 2\times 9\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}+2\times 4\Lambda_{\ell}^{2}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}=(18+8\Lambda_{\ell}^{2})\|u\|^{2}_{1,k},

which proves (
4.2
).
∎

We now complete the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Using (
4.1
) with (
2.44
) we obtain

c_{1}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}\leq(Tu,u)_{1,k}=\bigl\langle\mathbf{M}_{AS,2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\bigr\rangle_{\mathbf{D}_{k}},

which can be written as

c_{1}\leq\frac{\bigl\langle\mathbf{M}_{AS,2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\bigr\rangle_{\mathbf{D}_{k}}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{D}_{k}}^{2}}.

Also, combining (
4.2
) with (

), we get

\|\mathbf{M}_{AS,2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{D}_{k}}^{2}=\|Tu\|_{1,k}^{2}\leq c_{2}\|u\|_{1,k}^{2}=c_{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{D}_{k}},\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad\|\mathbf{M}_{AS,2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{D}_{k}}^{2}\leq c_{2}.

The result on GMRES convergence now follows directly from the Elman theory
[

]

with

.

This section illustrates the effectiveness of the the two-level

-GenEO preconditioner (

),

All computations are performed in

The domain

)),

The local matrices

Behaviour of the method with respect to different parameters

Influence of the threshold
\tau
.

As expected, for both homogeneous and heterogeneous problems,

regulates the size of the coarse space and robustness of the preconditioner.

\bullet

For

This leads to smaller coarse spaces but a clear deterioration in robustness as

increases.
As

increases, the GMRES iterations grow substantially,
especially for larger subdomain counts

.

\bullet

For
\tau=0.4
, a favourable balance is observed.
The coarse space dimension grows moderately with

and

, while the iteration counts mostly remain stable

.
This represents an effective compromise between setup cost and iteration count.

\bullet

For

and

and

compared to
\tau=0.4

but GMRES convergence becomes nearly independent of both
k
and

.
Iteration counts flatten, demonstrating enhanced robustness.

Dependence on frequency
k
and number of subdomains

.

For all values of

tested, the number of negative eigenvalues of (

reflecting the growing indefiniteness of the operator. Nevertheless, when

6
Conclusion

We have developed and analysed a two-level domain decomposition preconditioner for indefinite Helmholtz-type problems,
in which the coarse space (
H_{k}
-GenEO) is constructed directly from local generalised eigenvalue problems
involving the full indefinite operator. This contrasts with
\Delta
-GenEO and
\Delta_{k}
-GenEO approaches, which rely on nearby SPD formulations.

A central contribution of this work is the theoretical framework for the underlying indefinite eigenproblem and the
resulting
k
-explicit sufficient conditions for robustness. Practically, this requires the fine subdomain diameter
to scale with the wavelength and the spectral threshold
\tau
to retain all negative and sufficiently small positive local modes.
Notably, the coarse diameter
H_{c}
does not enter the robustness conditions, allowing decoupling of the one-level and coarse decompositions.

The numerical experiments on homogeneous and layered heterogeneous media confirm the theoretical findings.
Once
\tau
is chosen sufficiently large, iteration counts remain stable with respect to both frequency
k
and subdomain count
N
.
For
\tau=0.4
, GMRES typically converges in approximately
14
–
25
iterations over broad parameter ranges,
while
\tau=0.6
further flattens iteration counts to roughly
11
–
16
at the expense of a larger coarse space.
In contrast, overly aggressive truncation (e.g.
\tau=0.2
) reduces coarse space size but compromises robustness at higher frequencies.

Two-sided thresholds offer limited benefit for moderate-to-large
\tau
, as excluding mildly negative modes has
negligible impact on convergence. However, for small
\tau
, imposing a negative lower bound can remove oscillatory unstable modes
and significantly degrade performance.

Finally, decoupled decompositions prove effective in homogeneous settings, enabling substantial coarse-space reduction
with only minor iteration increases. In heterogeneous media, however, robustness depends on aligning the coarse partition
with the coefficient structure, highlighting the importance of geometric compatibility between decomposition and heterogeneity.

Overall, by constructing the coarse space from the indefinite operator itself and establishing explicit robustness criteria,

H_{k}
-GenEO provides a practical and scalable preconditioner whose convergence is stable with respect to frequency,
heterogeneity, and subdomain count.
