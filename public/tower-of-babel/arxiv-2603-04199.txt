Bayesian Adversarial Privacy
Cameron Bell, Timothy Johnston, Antoine Luciano, Christian P Robert

Abstract
Theoretical and applied research into privacy encompasses an incredibly broad swathe of differing approaches, emphasis and aims. This work introduces a new quantitative notion of privacy that is both contextual and specific. We argue that it provides a more meaningful notion of privacy than the widely utilised framework of differential privacy and a more explicit and rigorous formulation than what is commonly used in statistical disclosure theory. Our definition relies on concepts inherent to standard Bayesian decision theory, while departing from it in several important respects. In particular, the party controlling the release of sensitive information should make disclosure decisions from the prior viewpoint, rather than conditional on the data, even when the data is itself observed. Illuminating toy examples and computational methods are discussed in high detail in order to highlight the specificities of the method.

Introduction

Privacy is an important topic of research in law, philosophy, and data science, and extensive corpora of work are to be found in each of these domains. In legal and philosophical fields, considerable attention has been devoted to clarifying the concept of privacy, e.g., whether or not it depends on malicious intent, whether or not it should be grounded in contract or tort law, and how it relates to broader social norms. However, majority of the scholars in these fields adopt the position that privacy occurs within a rich web of convention and context, see e.g.
Nissenbaum 2004
;
Cofone 2023
;
Rachels 1975
.

Comparatively, in order to be mathematically tractable, research into privacy in data science and mathematics usually considers privacy more formally and therefore abstractly. Let us consider two common frameworks.

1.1
Differential Privacy

Over the past two decades, a substantial portion of the statistical and theoretical computer science literature on data privacy has focused on Differential Privacy (DP)
(
Dwork 2006
)
, a perturbative framework that constrains the sensitivity of an output, released, distribution as a function of its input, private, data.

The original DP definition is given as follows: let
x
and
x^{\prime}
be two ‘neighbouring’ datasets differing in one entry only and let
q(\cdot\mid x)
denote a randomised mechanism releasing public outputs in a measurable space
\mathcal{H}
. The privacy mechanism
q
is said to satisfy
\epsilon
-DP if, for all neighbouring datasets
x,x^{\prime}
and all measurable sets
A\subseteq\mathcal{H}
,

q(A\mid x)\;\leq\;e^{\epsilon}\,q(A\mid x^{\prime}).

(1)

Approximate variants allow, for instance, an additional additive slack parameter
\delta>0
.

This condition ensures that the inclusion or removal of any single individual has a limited effect on the distribution of the released output, even if the rest of the dataset is known. An equivalent formulation in terms of hypothesis testing is given in
Balle et al. 2020
.

The formal and context-independent nature of this framework yields several attractive properties. It admits precise mathematical analysis and composes coherently under natural probabilistic operations
(
Dwork and Roth 2014
)
. A substantial literature has developed comparing alternative definitions
(
Desfontaines and Pejó 2019
)
, establishing privacy guarantees for broad classes of algorithms
(
Chourasia et al. 2021
)
, and studying the trade-off between privacy constraints and statistical accuracy
(
Eigner et al. 2014
)
.

At the same time, differential privacy as defined above is formulated independently of any prior distribution, statistical consideration, adversarial strategies, or task-specific loss function. Its guarantees are therefore uniform and worst-case in nature. While this robustness is perceived as a central strength of the concept, it also implies that privacy protection is not calibrated relative to a specific inferential objective or contextual notion of risk. Several works have highlighted structural tensions between uniform privacy guarantees and highly structured inferential settings
(
Kifer and Machanavajjhala 2011
;
Ghosh and Kleinberg 2016a
)
, and it is unlikely that differential privacy type definitions could ever protect against inferences
(
Near et al. 2025
)
. In addition, adding randomization results in inefficiency for statistical inference without necessarily protecting privacy
(
Kenny et al. 2021
)
.

Another objection
(
Bambauer et al. 2013
)
is that DP is simply impractical, in the sense that the privacy parameters of realistic algorithms end up being extremely large significant, hence enabling losses of privacy. An important parameter
\epsilon
, as given in (
1
), has essentially the same role in all DP definitions. It is intended to take a value close to zero. However, it typically accumulates linearly with each operation performed, and as a result, in applications
\epsilon
is usually above one and sometimes even higher than twenty; see
(
Del Vasto-Terrientes et al. 2025
)
.

For this reason, DP may be more fruitfully considered as a kind of theoretical model, or as a kind of sensitivity analysis. Indeed, ideas developed in the community may be useful for those studying data privacy from other perspectives. However, for more realistic problems, there have been many difficulties in using differential privacy to obtain meaningful privacy guarantees in practice
(
Del Vasto-Terrientes et al. 2025
)
.

1.2
Statistical Disclosure Control

The notion of Statistical Disclosure Control (SDC), developed mainly by national statistics institutes (NSIs), seeks to process statistical data so that it may be disclosed without leaking confidential information. The handbook by
Hundepool et al. 2012
provides an excellent introduction to this subject. It covers census data releases as well as data originating from business and social surveys. Formally, as for DP, an SDC procedure may be viewed as a transformation or randomised mechanism
q(\cdot\mid x)
. The central objective of SDC is to balance
disclosure risk
and
data utility
.

Types of disclosure risk include
identity disclosure
, which occurs when a specific unit can be re-identified from the released data, and
attribute disclosure
, which arises when sensitive characteristics can be inferred, even without explicit identification. Such risks may stem from low-frequency counts or from external information available to an adversary (so-called linkage attacks). In order to achieve a balance with data utility, it may be necessary to process the data, for instance by adding noise, merging cells in tabular data, simply suppressing contentious aspects, or providing synthetic data. These operations can be encoded via different criteria in the software packages
\mu
-ARGUS and
\tau
-ARGUS, widely used by NSIs
(
Hundepool 2004
)
. After transformation, disclosure risk is evaluated using deterministic criteria applied to the released dataset
\eta\sim q(\cdot\mid x)
. For example, a dataset satisfies
k
-anonymity
if there is no record that does not appear at least
k
times, meaning that, even if an adversary can match this record with its appearance in another dataset, there are
k
possible ways of doing so
(
Slavković and Seeman 2023
)
.

In parallel,
information loss measures
are used to assess the degradation in statistical utility induced by the transformation. These may include comparisons between original and released tabulations, preservation of marginal totals, covariance structures, or other summary statistics. The aim is to ensure that
q
reduces disclosure risk without excessively distorting important properties of the data.

For illustrative purposes, consider a town with only two companies from a given sector. Since each company knows their own revenue, publishing a merged statistic for the revenue of both companies enables both companies to learn the revenue data of their competitor. For this reason, it may in this case be necessary to merge this town’s data with another town’s, thereby reducing disclosure risk.

Unlike differential privacy, SDC does not impose a uniform worst-case constraint on the mechanism
q
. Risk is instead evaluated empirically for the realised dataset. Moreover, SDC is typically not formulated within a Bayesian framework: prior information about
x
or about an adversary’s knowledge is usually not explicitly modelled when assessing disclosure risk or information loss. This is a significant drawback since, in most cases, published data follow a history of prior releases, from which informative priors can be constructed.

Furthermore, in order to protect privacy, it is often necessary to hide the exact method of disclosure used. As stated in
Slavković and Seeman 2023
, the implemented method “could not be disclosed transparently without revealing probabilistic information about the individuals whose data were altered due to disclosure limitation”. This can lead to confusion for users, since adding noise may lead to small inconsistencies
(
Eurostat 2024
)
. More importantly, the secrecy about the release mechanism weakens statistical analysis, since the statistical model is partly unknown and the resulting variance due to, say, adding noise is not accounted for. Additionally, this means that an adversary could potentially derive more information from the release than intended, given structural information that could, in itself, be inferred.

1.3
What Should a Quantitative Definition of Privacy Deliver?

To summarise the above discussion, DP is theoretically elegant and illuminating, but does not provide a very reassuring guarantee in practice. On the other hand, SDC is well established, but uses methods of assessment that are somewhat ad hoc and rely on the secrecy of the disclosure method. How then to come up with a privacy framework which is rigorous and explicit, but meaningful in a real-world context?

Furthermore, in order to proceed in a Bayesian fashion, with the goal of delivering optimal inference, we shall have to assume that all actors act rationally, i.e. their decisions are based on some mathematical construct. Privacy is often viewed through a more human lens, though, and it may therefore require context beyond our mathematical framework. As pointed out in
Cofone 2023
, when considered more universally, privacy is not “information”, and information releases that are hidden away on, say, a government data release will be perceived as less privacy-violating than those, say, publicised by mass media.

One may then discuss how much context should be included in the statistical approach to privacy. Adopting a Bayesian model requires setting a prior (or prior predictive) distribution for the data. Conditionally on our chosen release mechanism and the subsequent output, this prior yields a posterior distribution on the data that fully describes what an adversary could extract from a given release.

Alongside the distribution of the data, the goals of the statistical experiment are also important in order to measure the suitability of a release mechanism. Since concealing information from adversaries would also reduce information available to “friendly” statisticians (there is no “free lunch” in privacy
Kifer and Machanavajjhala 2011
), we wish to be as specific as possible about our aims. To this end, our measure of utility and privacy shall be explicitly linked to two
decisions
made by both a statistician and an adversary, respectively, the success of each being measured by a loss function. Within this formalised environment, we can proceed directly and efficiently from what we wish to hide and what we wish to achieve, and how exactly we wish to quantify the success of these goals. This construct is, in a sense, achieving the most general definition possible by positing “privacy as a trade-off” in a rational Bayesian
setting, thus formalised by a utility function
(
Robert 2001
)
.

Since the posterior distribution of the data (and of any parameter we are inferring) given the release depends on the release mechanism over the entire data space, it comes to reason that we cannot choose the method of release based on the observed data only. Intuitively, doing so would itself reveal additional information about this data. Therefore, we will measure and choose the suitability of a given data release mechanism by integrating over the data prior. In this way, we follow the convention of the literature on Bayesian experimental design
(
Chaloner and Verdinelli 1995
;
Rainforth et al. 2024
)
.

1.4
Existing perspectives

Let us conclude by providing an entry on the related statistical literature.

The production on DP is so vast that we shall only survey perspectives somewhat adjacent to our own. Connections with Bayesian statistics have been given in a number of works. The “random mechanism” of posterior sampling is considered in works such as
Dimitrakakis et al. 2017
, although this is essentially just a variant of classical DP. In particular, the data are considered fixed (not sampled from the prior distribution) as in classical DP. More meaningfully Bayesian DP variants were explored in
Ghosh and Kleinberg 2016b
;
Kasiviswanathan and Smith 2008
;
Yang et al. 2015
, amounting to the conditions that, given a prior on the data, the posterior distribution given the released statistic does not change the distribution of any data point too much. However, this does not cover the case when an adversary has additional (specific) information, and there is a mismatch between the specificity given by the prior distribution and the one-size-fits-all style DP analysis.

Bayesian assessments of statistical disclosure have been given in several works. In
Fienberg et al. 1997
, adversarial attempts to infer additional information about individuals from a randomised data release are assessed via simulations. In
Hu 2018
, methods of reconstructing real data from synthetic data are discussed, and in
Reiter et al. 2014
, posterior odds of an adversary matching datasets are considered, albeit with a worst-case analysis. However, whilst the Bayesian frameworks of these works are common to our own, none of these works feature truly systematic ways of measuring privacy and utility.

Additional works have considered other notions of Bayesian privacy. In
Eilat et al. 2021
, mechanisms with a restriction on the KL divergence between the prior and posterior were considered. Furthermore, several works have considered information in an adversarial or minimax sense. The concept of “informational size” related to the extent agents’ beliefs are modulated by information releases (see
McLean and Postlewaite 2001
and related works), and “rational inattention”
(
Sims 2003
)
considers the problem of constructing a random variable
Y
that yields information about another variable
X
subject to an information flow restraint.

Another vast area of research that loosely relates to data release under privacy is what is known as federated learning
(
Kairouz and McMahan 2021
)
, where data in separate silos is processed in such a way as to remain separate. This area is too large to be effectively surveyed here, though it is worth noting that extensive connections have been made with Bayesian analysis
(
Cao et al. 2023
)
and DP
(
Wei et al. 2020
)
. Federated learning has proved extremely effective in practical settings, such as with healthcare data
(
Sheller et al. 2020
)
. In future work, we would be interested in studying the privacy of federated methods using our framework.

Finally, a recent preprint by
Bon et al. 2026
develops a related Bayesian decision-theoretic notion of privacy. Here, one considers a mechanism private if, for every choice in a family of priors, for every
x
in the data-space, and for every choice of a scoring rule within some family, the probability that there is a high difference in optimal inference between prior and posterior (with respect to the information release
q(\cdot|x)
) is low. This alternative perspective therefore adopts a more minimax variant of privacy, if somewhat in line with our framework, with the additional complexity that a worst-case is taken over a family of priors and loss functions. However, we note that the authors’ framework recovers the regular
(\epsilon,\delta)
differential privacy and that several structural features of DP carry over to the wider notion, including post-processing and composition rules.

2
Bayesian Adversarial Privacy

We consider a Bayesian setting in which, given data
x
, inference is to be performed on a parameter
\theta
driving the distribution of
x
. Performing such inference requires releasing information derived from
x
, which may contain sensitive content. Our objective is to evaluate release mechanisms according to both the quality of inference on
\theta
and the privacy leakage regarding
x
.

Although we focus on parameter inference, the framework applies equally when the data themselves are the primary object of interest for both parties. In that case, one may integrate over
\theta
to obtain a marginal formulation.

We will be considering the prior, likelihood and loss functions as fixed throughout. However, it is of course of great practical interest to investigate the behaviour of different release mechanisms under a variety of models or losses.

2.1
Three Agents with Linked Goals

To formalise the trade-off between inference and privacy, we introduce three imaginary agents to represent the competing objectives, named after the famous couple of the RSA foundational paper
(
Rivest et al. 1978
)
, as well as an early privacy paper
(
Bennett et al. 1988
)
.

Alice
is the
mechanism designer
(e.g. a server). Given data
x
, she selects a (possibly randomised) release mechanism
q
, generating a release
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
according to

\eta\sim q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x).

The release induces a posterior distribution on the data:

p(\mathrm{d}x\mid\eta,q)=\frac{q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x)\,p(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x^{\prime})\,p(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})}.

All agents know
q
and observe
\eta
, while
x
remains known only to Alice.

Bob
, the
statistician
, aims to infer the parameter
\theta
using only the released output
\eta
and knowledge of the release mechanism
q
. Let
L_{B}(\theta,\delta)
denote his loss function with decision space
\mathcal{D}_{B}
. Given
(\eta,q)
, Bob’s posterior expected loss is

\rho_{B}((\eta,q),\delta)=\int_{\Theta}L_{B}(\theta,\delta)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid\eta,q),

where

\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid\eta,q)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)\,p(\mathrm{d}x\mid\eta,q).

Bob selects the optimal Bayes decision

\delta_{B}(\eta,q)=\arg\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}_{B}}\rho_{B}((\eta,q),\delta).

The choice of
L_{B}
determines which aspects of the inference are prioritised, such as point estimation, hypothesis testing, prediction, or uncertainty quantification. Through the associated Bayes decision rule
\delta_{B}
, the loss function specifies the feature of the posterior distribution that is operationally relevant. For instance, quadratic loss leads to posterior mean estimation, whereas
0
–
1
loss yields posterior mode decisions in testing problems
(
Robert 2001
)
.

Eve
, the
adversary
or
eavesdropper
, seeks to learn about the data
x
from
(\eta,q)
. With loss
L_{E}(x,\delta)
and decision space
\mathcal{D}_{E}
, her posterior expected loss is

\rho_{E}((\eta,q),\delta)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}L_{E}(x,\delta)\,p(\mathrm{d}x\mid\eta,q),

and she selects

\delta_{E}(\eta,q)=\arg\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}_{E}}\rho_{E}((\eta,q),\delta).

Note that we assume here, for simplicity’s sake, that Eve exploits the same prior distribution on the data
x
as Alice. A generalisation of the current framework to the case of Eve adopting a different prior is straightforward to design, if more heavy notation-wise. This is of particular interest in settings such as linkage attacks, where we may wish to assume Eve is exploiting specific information on the data (e.g. knowledge that a specific individual was part of the survey) that we do not wish to include in the inference. In such a setting, we would need to assume Alice has access to the priors the other agents are using.

Similarly to
L_{B}
, the specification of
L_{E}
determines which features of the dataset are regarded as sensitive. Through the associated Bayes decision rule
\delta_{E}
, the loss function formalises the notion of a privacy breach, whether this concerns the presence of outliers, the value of specific individual observations, or broader structural properties of the data.

We assume throughout that both Bob and Eve solve their respective Bayes decision problems exactly, so that
\delta_{B}
and
\delta_{E}
are true minimisers of their posterior risks. In particular, we treat them as “perfect Bayesians”, endowed with unlimited computational and analytical resources, so that issues of tractability play no role. The explicit dependence of
\delta_{B}
and
\delta_{E}
on the mechanism
q
emphasises that the choice of release simultaneously shapes both statistical utility and privacy.

2.2
Alice’s Objective

We now turn to Alice’s objective, as she is responsible for selecting the release mechanism
q
. For a data–parameter pair
(\theta,x)
, her task is to balance two competing goals: improving Bob’s inference on
\theta
while limiting the information revealed about the realised dataset
x
to Eve.

For example, we could consider:

–

The
full release
where Alice releases the full dataset, so that
\eta=x
a.s. and Bob has access to the full posterior
\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)
for
\theta
. Meanwhile, Eve’s risk reduces to
\min_{\delta}L_{E}(x,\delta)
.

–

The
null release
where Alice releases nothing, which we denote by
\eta=\dagger
, so that Bob and Eve both rely exclusively on prior information. We use
\dagger
to refer to a cemetery state containing no information.

–

A
noisy release
, for example
\eta=x+\epsilon
, for some random
\epsilon
.

–

A
statistic

\eta=T(x)
, such as the sample mean
\bar{x}
or the posterior probability of an event in
\theta
.

–

Different releases on different parts of
\mathcal{X}
, for example
\eta=\bar{x}+\epsilon
when
x\in A
, and
\eta=\dagger
when
x\notin A
.

We define Alice’s loss as the expected difference between Bob’s and Eve’s losses, evaluated at their respective Bayes-optimal decisions under the mechanism
q
:

\displaystyle L_{A}((\theta,x),q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}L_{B}\!\left(\theta,\delta_{B}(\eta,q)\right)\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x)

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{H}}L_{E}\!\left(x,\delta_{E}(\eta,q)\right)\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x),

where
\lambda>0
determines the relative weight placed on statistical utility and privacy protection. The factor
\lambda
can also be interpreted as a Lagrange multiplier appearing in the minimisation of Bob’s loss under a lower constraint on Eve’s loss
(
Bertsekas 1982
)
.

In the special case where the release is deterministic, so that
\eta=\eta(x)
, the expectation with respect to the mechanism disappears, and Alice’s loss reduces to

\displaystyle L_{A}((\theta,x),q)=L_{B}\!\left(\theta,\delta_{B}(\eta(x),q)\right)-\lambda L_{E}\!\left(x,\delta_{E}(\eta(x),q)\right).

We take this linear specification as our reference formulation. It makes explicit that Alice evaluates mechanisms by trading off inference utility against privacy loss. More generally, however, Alice’s objective need not be linear in Eve’s loss. One may instead penalise low values of Eve’s posterior loss more severely through a non-linear transformation. For instance, for some
p>0
, consider

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{A}((\theta,x),q)=L_{B}\!\left(\theta,\delta_{B}(\eta(x),q)\right)+\lambda\bigl[L_{E}\!\left(x,\delta_{E}(\eta(x),q)\right)\bigr]^{-p}.

Such a specification imposes an increasingly strong penalty as Eve’s posterior loss approaches zero, thereby enforcing a stricter notion of privacy protection.

A crucial observation is that
L_{A}((\theta,x),q)
depends not only on the conditional distribution
q(\cdot\mid x)
, but on the entire mechanism
q(\cdot\mid y)
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
. This dependence arises through the induced posterior distribution
p(\mathrm{d}x\mid\eta,q)
, since the decision rules
\delta_{B}
and
\delta_{E}
minimise integrals against this density. In particular, for two mechanisms
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
,

q_{1}(\cdot\mid x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}q_{2}(\cdot\mid x)\quad\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.625pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.875pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}\quad L_{A}((\theta,x),q_{1})=L_{A}((\theta,x),q_{2}).

(2)

That is, two mechanisms may induce identical distributions of the release at the realised dataset while yielding different losses.

To illustrate, let
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}
and consider two deterministic mechanisms:
\eta_{1}(x)=x
(full release) and
\eta_{2}(x)=0
(constant release). Then
q_{1}(\cdot\mid x=0)=q_{2}(\cdot\mid x=0)
, so the releases coincide at
x=0
. However, the induced posterior distributions differ:

p(\mathrm{d}x\mid x=0,q_{1})=\delta_{0}(\cdot)\qquad\text{whereas}\qquad p(\mathrm{d}x\mid x=0,q_{2})\neq\delta_{0}(\cdot).

Since Bob and Eve optimise against these posterior distributions, the resulting decisions
\delta_{B}
and
\delta_{E}
and hence Alice’s loss, generally differ.

In general, Bob and Eve reason globally about the mechanism. Upon observing
\eta
, they consider which values of
x
could have produced it, which are more or less likely to have done so, and which are impossible under the mechanism. In particular, Bob may bypass the data entirely if he has access to
\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid\eta)
directly, without reconstructing
x
.

For this reason, a seemingly protective rule such as “release
x
unless it would help Eve, in which case release
\dagger
” may fail to provide privacy. Since Eve knows the mechanism
q
, observing
\dagger
itself conveys information, namely, that the realised dataset lies in a region deemed sensitive.

This feature distinguishes our problem from classical Bayesian decision theory. One might attempt to determine an optimal release locally at the observed dataset
x
via

q^{\star}(\cdot\mid x)=\argmin_{q}\left(\int_{\Theta}L_{A}((\theta,x),q)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)\right).

(3)

However, this approach is inherently circular. Even if
q^{\star}(\cdot\mid y)
is fixed for all
y\neq x
, modifying
q^{\star}(\cdot\mid x)
changes the induced posterior distributions
p(\mathrm{d}x\mid\eta,q^{\star})
, and therefore alters
L_{A}((\theta,y),q^{\star})
elsewhere in the sample space. Thus, optimality cannot be established pointwise.

Instead, mechanisms must be evaluated globally. Alice therefore seeks to minimise the Bayesian
integrated mechanism risk
, also known as
ex ante risk
, a criterion widely used in Bayesian experimental design and related literatures
(
Chaloner and Verdinelli 1995
;
Fried 2003
;
Rainforth et al. 2024
)
:

\begin{split}R_{A}(\pi,q)&=\iint L_{A}((\theta,x),q)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta,\mathrm{d}x)\\[6.0pt]
&=\iint L_{B}(\theta,\delta_{B}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid\theta)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\\
&\quad-\lambda\iint L_{E}(x,\delta_{E}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x)\,p(\mathrm{d}x)\\[6.0pt]
&=\int\rho_{B}((\eta,q),\delta_{B})\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta)-\lambda\int\rho_{E}((\eta,q),\delta_{E})\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta).\end{split}

(4)

This criterion averages Alice’s loss over the joint model and corresponds to a standard Bayes risk adapted to an adversarial setting with asymmetric objectives. Although computing a global minimiser of
R_{A}(\pi,q)
is typically intractable (we discuss a tractable subclass in Section
3.4
), it provides a principled and coherent basis for comparing mechanisms within any given class of admissible procedures.

Alice’s mechanism risk admits the decomposition

R_{A}(\pi,q)=R_{B}(\pi,q)-\lambda R_{E}(\pi,q),

(5)

where

\begin{split}R_{B}(\pi,q)&=\iiint L_{B}(\theta,\delta_{B}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)\,p(\mathrm{d}x)\\
&=\iint L_{B}(\theta,\delta_{B}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid\theta)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\\
&=\int\rho_{B}((\eta,q),\delta_{B}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta),\end{split}

(6)

and

\begin{split}R_{E}(\pi,q)&=\iint L_{E}(x,\delta_{E}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x)\,p(\mathrm{d}x)\\
&=\int\rho_{E}((\eta,q),\delta_{E}(\eta,q))\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta).\end{split}

(7)

The term
R_{B}(\pi,q)
, which we call the
integrated inference risk
, is the expected loss induced by Bob’s optimal decision, averaged jointly over the prior uncertainty on
\theta
and the randomness of the release
\eta
. Similarly, the term
R_{E}(\pi,q)
, the
integrated privacy risk
, measures the expected success of Eve’s optimal decision, averaged over the prior predictive distribution of the data
x
and the released output
\eta
.

Alice’s objective can therefore be seen as a version of Bob’s integrated risk with a “privacy penalty” on the release, similar to a Lasso or ridge penalty. She aims to use as much of
x
to improve Bob’s inference on
\theta
, without releasing aspects of the data that would allow Eve to make a better decision
\delta_{E}
.

The hyperparameter
\lambda>0
governs the strength of this penalty and encodes the normative balance between statistical utility and privacy protection. When
\lambda=0
, the objective reduces to
R_{B}(\pi,q)
and is therefore minimised by the full release. As
\lambda\to\infty
, privacy considerations dominate, and any mechanism that conveys useful information to Eve is eventually dominated by the null release. The interpretation of
\lambda
is relative to the scales of
L_{B}
and
L_{E}
. If these losses are not at the same scale, the induced trade-off may be distorted. Therefore, meaningful comparison of mechanisms requires explicit specification of
\lambda
.

A reasonable starting point is obtained by choosing
\lambda
such that the full release
q^{\mathrm{full}}
and the null release
q^{\dagger}
yield identical integrated risk. Solving

R_{A}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})=R_{A}(\pi,q^{\dagger})

gives

\lambda=\frac{R_{B}(\pi,q^{\dagger})-R_{B}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})}{R_{E}(\pi,q^{\dagger})-R_{E}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})},

(8)

whenever the denominator is non-zero. This calibration places the two trivial mechanisms at indifference, ensuring that non-trivial releases are evaluated relative to both extremes. Alternative choices of
\lambda
correspond to different priorities between utility and privacy.

Some natural reductions of a mechanism can improve privacy without degrading inference. Suppose that
T(x)
is sufficient for
\theta
, so that

\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)=\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid T(x)).

Bob’s posterior distribution, and hence his optimal decision, are therefore identical whether he observes
x
or
T(x)
. It follows that

R_{B}(\pi,q^{T})=R_{B}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}}).

The effect on Eve is different. Under the full release
\eta^{\mathrm{full}}(x)=x
, her posterior collapses to a point mass,

p(\mathrm{d}x\mid x,q^{\mathrm{full}})=\delta_{x}(\cdot),

so that

\delta_{E}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}})=\arg\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}_{E}}L_{E}(x,\delta).

By contrast, under
\eta^{T}(x)=T(x)
, the posterior remains non-degenerate, and

\delta_{E}(T,q^{T})=\arg\min_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}_{E}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}L_{E}(x,\delta)\,p(\mathrm{d}x\mid T(x),q^{T}),

where the minimised risk is at least
L_{E}(x,\delta_{E}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}}))
.

By comparing the risks, we see that

R_{E}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})\leq R_{E}(\pi,q^{T})\quad\implies\quad R_{A}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})\geq R_{A}(\pi,q^{T}).

Hence, releasing a sufficient statistic
T(x)
weakly dominates releasing the full dataset.

Even if
T(x)
is not sufficient for
\theta
, it may be the case that it leads Bob to make the same decision, i.e.
\delta_{B}(T(x),q^{T})=\delta_{B}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}})
where
q^{T}
is the mechanism that releases
T(x)
. If this property holds, the same argument shows that releasing
T(x)
dominates releasing
x
. In particular, if Alice releases
T(x)=\delta_{B}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}})
, Bob will be able to take his optimal decision, and we may have concealed some information from Eve.

2.3
Comparison with Differential Privacy and Statistical Disclosure Control

We briefly contrast Bayesian adversarial privacy (BAP) with differential privacy (DP) and statistical disclosure control (SDC).

Relative to DP, the BAP framework is explicit about both objectives: what statistical goal is pursued and what aspect of the data is to be protected. The loss functions
L_{B}
and
L_{E}
encode these objectives directly, introducing contextual information into the definition of privacy. Moreover, risk is evaluated by averaging over the prior distribution on
(\theta,x)
, rather than assigning equal weight to arbitrarily unlikely datasets, as in the worst-case formulation of DP.

The framework is also practical and transparent. Since mechanisms are evaluated through explicitly specified priors and loss functions, different designers can tailor the analysis to reflect their normative or practical priorities. In contrast, DP guarantees are often assessed post hoc, primarily to certify that a given mechanism satisfies a formal privacy definition, rather than to optimise a context-specific trade-off between utility and disclosure risk.

Regarding SDC, key limitations arise from inconsistencies in the information available to different parties and from ad hoc disclosure-risk measures. By embedding the problem in a fully Bayesian decision-theoretic setup, BAP ensures coherence: all parties reason from explicitly defined probabilistic models, and privacy and utility are assessed through well-defined risks.

For scenarios outside of an explicitly Bayesian setting, such as releasing census data, one might think that SDC is better equipped to analyse mechanisms. However, if we modify the framework and give Bob a loss function over
x
instead of
\theta
, we can cover these cases. For example, taking

L_{B}(x,\delta)=(\bar{x}-\delta)^{2},\qquad L_{E}(x,\delta)=\ind\!\left[\ind\!\left\{\max_{i}x_{i}>k\right\}\neq\delta\right],

requires Alice to balance accurate estimation of the population mean against protection of the existence of extreme values. This illustrates that the framework extends naturally beyond parameter inference to direct data-release problems.

3
Example 1: A Coin Toss

To illustrate the tension between statistical utility and privacy, and the importance of evaluating mechanisms ex ante, we consider a simple toy example.

Consider a medical study with a binary outcome and a rare sensitive condition, so that inference about treatment effects may conflict with protecting individual-level information. To model the tension between inference and privacy, we consider two coins. The first is a fixed coin that always gives tails, and the second is a fair coin that lands on heads or tails with equal probability. The coin type
\theta
is selected uniformly at random (or more generally according to a known prior), and Alice observes the outcome of a single toss. Bob aims to infer the type of coin used, whereas Eve seeks to infer the realised outcome of the toss itself. Alice must decide what information to release. Revealing the outcome provides valuable information to Bob but allows Eve to perfectly recover the toss, whereas withholding information protects the outcome but limits Bob’s inference.

We formalise our example. The parameter
\theta
takes values in
\{0,1/2\}
with prior probability
1/2
each, where
\theta=0
corresponds to the double-tailed coin and
\theta=1/2
to the fair coin. Therefore, conditional on
\theta
, we consider

X\mid\theta\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\theta).

In particular, observing
X=1
implies
\theta=1/2
, so the data are maximally informative in that case.

Bob and Eve have decision spaces
\mathcal{D}_{B}=\{0,1/2\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{E}=\{0,1\}
, with respective loss functions

\displaystyle L_{B}(\theta,\delta)

\displaystyle=\ind\{\delta\neq\theta\},

\displaystyle L_{E}(x,\delta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\delta=x,\\
1,&\delta=1,\ x=0,\\
10,&\delta=0,\ x=1.\end{cases}

The asymmetric loss
L_{E}
reflects the idea that failing to detect a sensitive event (
x=1
) is substantially more costly than falsely declaring it. Consequently, Eve should declare
\delta=0
only when the posterior odds of
x=0
are at least ten times that of
x=1
.

We now introduce a sequence of release mechanisms and explicitly derive the corresponding evaluation of their integrated risk (
4
).

3.1
The Full Release

We first consider the full release mechanism
q^{\mathrm{full}}
, under which Alice discloses the observed outcome
x
.

Since
\eta=x
, Eve perfectly recovers the data and therefore selects

\delta_{E}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}})=x,

yielding zero loss:

L_{E}\!\left(x,\delta_{E}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}})\right)=0.

Consequently, Alice’s loss is independent of
\lambda
under the full release.

Bob observes
x
directly and forms the posterior

\Prob(\theta=0\mid X=0)=\frac{2}{3},\qquad\Prob(\theta=1/2\mid X=0)=\frac{1}{3}.

Under 0–1 loss, he selects the posterior mode:

\delta_{B}(x,q^{\mathrm{full}})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2},&x=1,\\[6.0pt]
0,&x=0.\end{cases}

Alice’s loss is therefore

L_{A}((\theta,x),q^{\mathrm{full}})=\ind\{x=0,\ \theta=1/2\},

since Bob makes an error only when
\theta=1/2
and
X=0
.

Taking expectation with respect to the joint prior yields

R_{A}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})=\Prob(\theta=1/2,X=0)=\frac{1}{4}.

3.2
The Null Release

Under the null release
q^{\dagger}
, Alice reveals no information about the realised outcome. The mechanism satisfies
\eta=\dagger
almost surely, so the release is independent of
x
and both Bob and Eve must act under the prior distribution alone.

Since

\Prob(\theta=0)=\Prob(\theta=1/2)=\frac{1}{2},

Bob’s posterior coincides with the prior. Under 0–1 loss, both decisions
\delta=0
and
\delta=1/2
yield posterior risk
1/2
, so his optimal decision is not unique. Any such choice leads to the same integrated risk; for definiteness, we may assume he randomises uniformly over
\{0,1/2\}
. Consequently,

\Prob(\theta\neq\delta_{B}(\dagger,q^{\dagger}))=\frac{1}{2}.

Due to the asymmetry in her loss, Eve wishes to avoid the case
\delta=0
and
x=1
. She therefore declares
\delta_{E}(\eta,q^{\dagger})=1
, leading to

L_{E}(x,\delta_{E}(\dagger,q^{\dagger}))=\ind\{x=0\}.

(9)

Alice’s integrated risk is

R_{A}(\pi,q^{\dagger})=\Prob(\theta\neq\delta_{B}(\dagger,q^{\dagger}))-\lambda\Prob(X=0)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{4}\lambda.

(10)

3.3
Randomised Response

Having compared the two extreme mechanisms corresponding to full disclosure and complete non-disclosure, we now consider a parametric family of intermediate mechanisms that introduce randomisation at the release stage.

For
\omega\in[0,1]
, the randomised response mechanism
q^{(\omega)}
is defined by

\eta=\begin{cases}X,&\text{with probability }1-\omega,\\
1-X,&\text{with probability }\omega.\end{cases}

Thus, Alice flips the observed outcome with probability
\omega
before releasing it. Both Bob and Eve are aware of the release mechanism and of the value of
\omega
.

We first observe that
L_{B}
and
L_{E}
, and hence the induced score
R_{A}
, are symmetric around
\omega=1/2
.
Indeed, observing
\eta
under
q^{(\omega)}
is equivalent to observing
1-\eta
under
q^{(1-\omega)}
, so the informational content of the experiment is unchanged when
\omega
is replaced by
1-\omega
.
At an intuitive level, always lying (
\omega=1
) conveys as much information as always telling the truth (
\omega=0
).

Moreover, the family
\{q^{(\omega)}\}_{\omega\in[0,1]}
interpolates continuously between two benchmark mechanisms: the full release, corresponding to
\omega\in\{0,1\}
, and the null release, corresponding to
\omega=1/2
, where
\eta\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)
independently of
X
.
Minimising
R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\omega)})
over
\omega
therefore selects, within this parametric class, a mechanism that performs at least as well as both the null and the full releases.

The optimal decisions for Bob and Eve can be derived in closed form.
For Bob, the posterior over
\theta\in\{0,1/2\}
is obtained directly from Bayes’ rule under the randomised response channel.
Under
0
–
1
loss, the Bayes action is the posterior mode, which yields

\delta_{B}(\eta,\omega)=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2},&\text{if }\{\eta=1,\,\omega\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\}\ \text{or}\ \{\eta=0,\,\omega<\tfrac{1}{2}\},\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(11)

In words, Bob selects
\theta=1/2
whenever the observation
\eta
is more likely to have arisen under
\theta=1/2
than under
\theta=0
.
Equivalently, if Alice is more likely to tell the truth (
\omega<1/2
), Bob follows the signal
\eta
, whereas if she is more likely to lie (
\omega>1/2
), he reverses it.

Eve’s optimal decision is more sensitive to the asymmetry of her loss and to the value of
\omega
.
Minimising posterior expected loss yields

\delta_{E}(\eta,\omega)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\{\eta=1,\,\omega\geq\frac{3}{13}\}\ \text{or}\ \{\eta=0,\,\omega\leq\frac{10}{13}\},\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(12)

The thresholds
3/13
and
10/13
arise from equating the posterior expected losses associated with the two possible actions under the asymmetric loss function. We see that, when
\omega\in\left[3/13,10/13\right]
,
\eta
does not provide enough evidence to impact Eve’s decision, and she acts entirely according to the prior. This leads to a plateau in her term of the risk.

Writing
R_{B}(\omega)
and
R_{E}(\omega)
for Alice’s integrated inference and privacy risks induced by
q^{(\omega)}
, direct enumeration of the joint events
(\theta,X,\eta)
yields

R_{B}(\omega)=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\min(\omega,1-\omega),

and

R_{E}(\omega)=\begin{cases}\frac{13}{4}\,\omega,&0\leq\omega<\frac{3}{13},\\[6.0pt]
\frac{3}{4},&\frac{3}{13}\leq\omega\leq\frac{10}{13},\\[6.0pt]
\frac{13}{4}(1-\omega),&\frac{10}{13}<\omega\leq 1.\end{cases}

Bob’s risk increases linearly in
\omega
when going from the full release at
\omega\in\{0,1\}
to the null release at
\omega=1/2
, so that removing information increases his risk. Eve’s term highlights that any
\omega\in\left[3/13,10/13\right]
yields the same decision, and therefore the same risk.

Consequently, Alice’s integrated risk is

R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\omega)})=R_{B}(\omega)-\lambda R_{E}(\omega),

which is piecewise affine in
\omega
. In Figure
1
, we plot
R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\omega)})
as a function of
\omega
when
\lambda=\frac{1}{3}
is fixed.

Figure 1:
Integrated risk
R_{A}
for the coin-toss example. The black curve represents
R_{A}\!\left(q^{(\omega)}\right)
as a function of
\omega
. The horizontal dotted black line indicates the optimal value obtained from the linear program, while the dashed grey line shows the calibrated reference level
R_{A}(\pi,q^{\dagger})=R_{A}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})
, which coincide here since
\lambda=1/3
.

As
\lambda
varies, and recalling that
q^{(\omega)}
is effectively symmetrical around
\omega=\frac{1}{2}
, the optimal parameter
\omega
under
R_{A}
satisfies

\omega^{\star}(\lambda)\in\begin{cases}\{0,1\},&\lambda<\frac{2}{13},\\[6.0pt]
[0,\frac{3}{13}]\cup[\frac{10}{13},1],&\lambda=\frac{2}{13},\\[6.0pt]
\{\frac{3}{13},\frac{10}{13}\},&\lambda>\frac{2}{13},\end{cases}

with corresponding minimal risk

R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\omega^{\star})})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4},&\lambda<\frac{2}{13},\\[6.0pt]
\frac{19}{52}-\frac{3}{4}\lambda,&\lambda\geq\frac{2}{13}.\end{cases}

In particular, when
\lambda=1/3
from (
8
) (such that
q^{\mathrm{full}}
and
q^{\dagger}
have equal risk), it is optimal to take
\omega=3/13
or
10/13
. In this regime, Alice reveals strictly more information than under the null release, improving Bob’s inference while not improving Eve’s decision. Thus, even when privacy is heavily weighted, complete non-disclosure is suboptimal within this class, and carefully calibrated randomisation strictly dominates both extreme mechanisms.

3.4
Optimal solution via linear programming

In the setting of this example, all the spaces of interest (the parameter space, sample space, and decision space) are finite. We shall see that in such a case, we can rewrite Alice’s problem in terms of a constrained linear optimisation problem, and can therefore solve it using linear programming techniques.

From any observed release
\eta
, Alice can deterministically obtain the Bayes-optimal decisions
\delta_{B}
and
\delta_{E}
of Bob and Eve, respectively. Thus, instead of releasing
\eta
, she can simply release the pair
(\delta_{B},\delta_{E})
. In other words, there is no loss of generality in restricting attention to mechanisms whose output directly encodes these decisions. We therefore take the release to be

\eta=(\delta_{B}^{\star},\delta_{E}^{\star})\in\mathcal{D}_{B}\times\mathcal{D}_{E},

where
\mathcal{D}_{B}=\{0,1/2\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{E}=\{0,1\}
denote the respective decision spaces.

Such mechanisms can be parametrised by the joint probabilities

\rho_{ij}^{k}:=\Prob(\eta=(i,j),\,X=k),\qquad i\in\mathcal{D}_{B},\ j\in\mathcal{D}_{E},\ k\in\{0,1\}.

The induced conditional release is

q^{(\rho)}(\eta=(i,j)\mid X=k)=\frac{\rho_{ij}^{k}}{\Prob(X=k)}.

The variables
\rho_{ij}^{k}
are constrained by non-negativity and the simplex constraints

\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}^{k}=\Prob(X=k),\qquad k\in\{0,1\}.

In addition, the release
(i,j)
must indeed represent Bayes-optimal decisions for Bob and Eve. For Bob, this requires

\E_{\theta}[L_{B}(\theta,i)\mid\eta=(i,j)]\leq\E_{\theta}[L_{B}(\theta,i^{\prime})\mid\eta=(i,j)],

(13)

for all
i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{B}
, and similarly for Eve,

\E_{X}[L_{E}(X,j)\mid\eta=(i,j)]\leq\E_{X}[L_{E}(X,j^{\prime})\mid\eta=(i,j)],

(14)

for all
j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{E}
.

Defining the posterior-averaged loss

\tilde{L}_{B}(x,i)=\int L_{B}(\theta,i)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x),

and noting that

\Prob(\eta=(i,j))=\sum_{k}\rho_{ij}^{k},

multiplying both sides of (
13
) and (
14
) by
\Prob(\eta=(i,j))
yields the linear constraints

\sum_{k}\tilde{L}_{B}(k,i)\rho_{ij}^{k}\leq\sum_{k}\tilde{L}_{B}(k,i^{\prime})\rho_{ij}^{k},

\sum_{k}L_{E}(k,j)\rho_{ij}^{k}\leq\sum_{k}L_{E}(k,j^{\prime})\rho_{ij}^{k},

for any
i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{B}
and
j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{E}
.

Alice’s ex ante Bayesian risk can then be written as

R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\rho)})=\sum_{k}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\Bigl(\tilde{L}_{B}(k,i)-\lambda L_{E}(k,j)\Bigr)\rho_{ij}^{k}.

(15)

This objective is linear in the parameters
\rho_{ij}^{k}
. Minimising
R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\rho)})
subject to the simplex and optimality constraints therefore defines a finite-dimensional linear program.
The number of variables scales as
|\mathcal{D}_{B}\times\mathcal{D}_{E}\times\mathcal{X}|
, and the problem can be solved in time polynomial in this dimension. Doing so will yield an optimal solution
\rho^{\star}
and an associated
globally optimal
mechanism
q^{(\rho^{\star})}
.

In the coin-toss example, all spaces are binary, and the linear program can be solved explicitly. We find that the optimal mechanism is such that

q(\eta=(0,1)\mid X=0)=1,

so that Eve is always misled when
X=0
. When
X=1
, the optimal release is given by

q(\eta=(0,1)\mid X=1)=\frac{3}{10},\qquad q(\eta=(1/2,1)\mid X=1)=\frac{7}{10},

or equivalently

\rho_{0,1}^{1}=\frac{3}{10}\Prob(X=1)=\frac{3}{40},\qquad\rho_{1/2,1}^{1}=\frac{7}{10}\Prob(X=1)=\frac{7}{40}.

In this regime, Eve is always told “1”, while Bob is deliberately misled with probability
\frac{3}{10}
when
X=1
.

The resulting integrated risk is

R_{A}(\pi,q^{(\rho^{\star})})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{4},&\lambda\leq\dfrac{1}{10},\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{13}{40}-\dfrac{3}{4}\lambda,&\lambda>\dfrac{1}{10}.\end{cases}

This risk is strictly smaller than that achieved by our previous best response mechanism. The improvement does not stem from adding further noise, but from enlarging the space of possible mechanisms. This allows Alice to decouple the information provided to Bob and Eve, selectively misleading the adversary without unnecessarily degrading inference.

4
Example 2: Gaussian Hypothesis Testing

As a second example, we consider a Bayesian hypothesis testing problem in a normal–normal model.
The aim is to investigate how inference on the mean parameter
\theta
can be balanced against protecting different structural features of the dataset.

We adopt the conjugate Gaussian model

\begin{gathered}\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}),\\
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,1),\end{gathered}

and write
\bar{X}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
for the sample mean.

Given thresholds
c_{B},c_{E}\in\mathbb{R}
, we first assume Bob is interested in the null and alternative hypotheses

H_{0,B}:\ \theta\leq c_{B},\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad H_{1,B}:\ \theta>c_{B},

respectively. Similarly, we assume Eve is interested in testing the hypotheses

H_{0,E}:\ T(X)\leq c_{E},\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad H_{1,E}:\ T(X)>c_{E},

where we consider two choices for the statistic
T(X)
:
T(x)=\bar{x}
in Section
4.1
, or
T(x)=\max_{i}x_{i}
in Section
4.2
. These two tests correspond to Eve wanting to identify either the centre of the sample or the presence of extreme values in the sample.

We expect that the case where Eve is interested in
\bar{X}
will be more challenging for Alice, since
\bar{X}
is a sufficient statistic for
\theta
. Information about the mean will therefore be directly tied to the posterior in
\theta
that Bob will be investigating. If Eve is interested in
\max_{i}X_{i}
, which is a statistic of the tails of the sample, Alice can potentially find more releases that help Bob without helping Eve.

Both agents use
0
–
1
loss,

L_{B}(\theta,\delta)=\ind\{\delta\neq\ind\{\theta>c_{B}\}\},\qquad L_{E}(x,\delta)=\ind\{\delta\neq\ind\{T(x)>c_{E}\}\}.

Hence, their optimal decisions are posterior majority rules:

\delta_{B}(\eta,q)=\ind\{\pi(H_{1,B}\mid\eta,q)>1/2\},\qquad\delta_{E}(\eta,q)=\ind\{p(H_{1,E}\mid\eta,q)>1/2\}.

We compare the following mechanisms:

(i)

Full release
:

q^{\mathrm{full}}(\cdot\mid x)=\delta_{x}(\cdot)
(so
\eta=x
).

(ii)

Null release
:

q^{\dagger}(\cdot\mid x)=\delta_{\dagger}(\cdot)

(so
\eta=\dagger
is independent of
x
).

(iii)

Noisy full release
:

q^{\mathrm{full}}_{\sigma}
, releasing
Y=x+\varepsilon
with

\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
independent, i.e.

Y_{i}\mid x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2})
.

(iv)

Noisy mean release
:

q^{\mathrm{mean}}_{\sigma}
, releasing

\eta=\bar{x}+\xi
with

\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
independent.

(v)

Noisy median release
:

q^{\mathrm{med}}_{\sigma}
, releasing

\eta=\mathrm{med}(x_{1:n})+\xi
with

\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
independent.

(vi)

One-bit release
:

q^{1\mathrm{bit}}_{\tau}
, releasing

\eta=\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(H_{1,B}\mid x)>\tau\}

for
\tau\in[0,1]
.

This is not an exhaustive list of possible mechanisms.

Additionally, releases (iii)-(vi) outlined above are parameterised releases, with the noisy releases depending on a variance parameter
\sigma
and the one-bit release depending on a threshold
\tau
. We can therefore seek to minimise
R_{A}
over the different releases, then compare these based on their minimised integrated risk.

The class of noisy full releases can be seen to contain both the full release (when
\sigma=0
) and the null release (as the limit case when
\sigma\rightarrow\infty
). Both other parametric releases have a similar inference-privacy trade-off, but do not necessarily contain the full release.

For the noisy mean release, the null release is recovered as
\sigma\rightarrow\infty
, and the case
\sigma=0
gives
\bar{X}
. This is sufficient for
\theta
and therefore is equivalent to releasing
X
from Bob’s perspective, and is also trivially equivalent to
X
if Eve is interested in
\bar{X}
. However, if Eve is interested in
\max_{i}X_{i}
,
\bar{X}
without added noise already gives less information than the full release.

For the one-bit release, the null release is recovered for
\tau=0
. From Bob’s perspective, taking
\tau=1/2
gives a release equivalent to
X
, as in this case we have
\eta(X)=\delta_{B}(X,q^{\mathrm{full}})
. For Eve interested in
\bar{X}
, there is a bijection between events of the form
\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(H_{1,B}\mid X)>\tau\}
and events of the form
\mathbbm{1}\{\bar{X}>c\}
. Thus, there is a value of
\tau
for which the one-bit release gives Eve full disclosure. If Eve is interested in
\max_{i}X_{i}
, however, the one-bit release will conceal a large amount of information.

In both next subsections, the results are reported for
\lambda
chosen as in (
8
),
so that the null and full releases yield the same integrated risk.
For the numerical experiments, we fix
n=5
,
\sigma_{0}=1
, and
c_{B}=c_{E}=0
.
Explicit derivations of the posterior distributions and the corresponding optimal decisions
are provided in Appendix
A
.

4.1
Privacy against the Mean

We first consider the case where Eve targets the sample mean,
T(x)=\bar{x}
.

Since
\bar{X}
is a sufficient statistic for
\theta
, Bob’s and Eve’s testing problems depend on essentially the same information. Consequently, improving Bob’s inference necessarily tends to reduce Eve’s uncertainty, leading to a genuine inference–privacy trade-off in the ex ante risk.

Because of sufficiency and conjugacy, releasing the full vector
X
with additive noise
\sigma
or releasing
\bar{X}
with noise
\sigma/\sqrt{n}
induces identical posterior distributions for
\theta
and therefore identical integrated risks
R_{A}
. This equivalence is illustrated in Figure

R_{A}
over
\sigma

R_{B}
and
R_{E}

\sigma^{\star}

\sigma
,
particularly as
\sigma\to 0
, reflecting its well-known stability properties under perturbations and model deviations.

4.2
Privacy against the Tails

We now consider the case where Eve targets the upper tail of the sample, taking

T(x)=\max_{i}x_{i}

In contrast to the previous setting, Bob and Eve are now interested in structurally different features of the data. Bob’s decision depends only on
\bar{X}

\bar{X}
to improve Alice’s risk, since compressing the data to the sufficient statistic for
\theta

Several qualitative differences from the mean-targeting case emerge. First, there exist mechanisms that strictly improve Bob’s risk without reducing Eve’s risk, and vice versa. Second, the one-bit release dominates all alternatives, achieving the same inference risk as the full release while inducing privacy risk comparable to the null release.

4.3
Notable Differences

The two Gaussian examples reveal structural differences in the privacy–inference relationship.

When Bob and Eve are focused on closely related features of the data, as in the case where Eve targets
\bar{X}
, privacy and inference become intrinsically coupled. Since
\bar{X}
is sufficient for
\theta
, improving Bob’s posterior necessarily reduces Eve’s uncertainty. In this regime, all mechanisms exhibit a genuine trade-off: reductions in
R_{B}
are accompanied by reductions in
R_{E}
. The optimal tuning parameters balance these opposing effects, and no mechanism can substantially improve inference without incurring a privacy cost.

By contrast, when Eve targets the maximum of the sample, Bob’s objective and Eve’s objective depend on structurally distinct aspects of the data. In this case, compressing the dataset to the sufficient statistic
\bar{X}
already eliminates much of the information relevant to Eve while preserving all inferential content for Bob. As a result, Alice can construct releases that strictly improve Bob’s inference without reducing Eve’s risk, demonstrating that privacy and inference need not always be antagonistic.

These examples also emphasise the role of sufficient statistics in privacy design. If the statistic to be protected is not sufficient for the target of the inference, releasing only sufficient statistics may yield substantial privacy gains at no inferential cost. However, when privacy and inference target the same sufficient statistic, more delicate mechanisms are required.

Finally, in both settings, the one-bit release emerges as optimal within the families considered. When Eve targets the mean, this mechanism extracts maximal inferential content about
\theta
, though its performance is sensitive to the choice of threshold
\tau
. When Eve targets the tails, the optimal one-bit release coincides with the mechanism that reveals only Bob’s Bayes-optimal decision under full disclosure, previously discussed at the end of Section
2.2
. In this regime, Bob will make the same decision as if he had observed the full data, while almost all ancillary information about the dataset is concealed from Eve.

Taken together, these examples illustrate a central message of the framework: the feasibility of privacy-preserving inference depends critically on whether the adversary’s objective is aligned with, or orthogonal to, the statistician’s goals. Consequently, the mechanism must be chosen accordingly, as no single release rule could be uniformly optimal across every adversarial objective.

5
Conclusion

As discussed in our introduction, the most commonly used formalisation of privacy, differential privacy, is widely adopted and enforced for its mathematically elegant representation and its ability to validate some randomisation mechanisms,
though it can be seen as disconnected from a genuine and realistic protection of the privacy of data owners, and not directly aligned with the objectives of inference. It also fails to deliver a spelt-out, crystal-clear confidentiality guarantee to such owners. As illustrated in
Dimitrakakis et al. 2017
, the assumptions DP imposes on the likelihood function and on the underlying MCMC
sequence generally impede the statistical aspects. It furthermore fails to account for the inferential aspects of the procedure, and in particular, it vividly lacks both informational and decision-theoretic Bayesian features. By looking at worst-case scenarios and imposing randomisation and insufficiency, DP’s guarantees need not coincide with Bayesian optimality criteria. The same holds for SDC, which is practically verified but also formulated independently of a Bayesian model.

In this paper, we have introduced a privacy framework fully embedded within a Bayesian decision-theoretic perspective. Privacy requirements are formalised through a first loss function, which quantifies breaches according to what is to be protected, at what cost, and for what purposes. By distinguishing privacy from inferential objectives, the framework explicitly captures the inherent trade-off between disclosure protection and statistical utility.

The inferential objective is encoded through a second loss function associated with a statistical model, or more generally with a substitute in the setting of generalised Bayesian inference
(
Dempster 1968
;
Bissiri et al. 2016
;
Matsubara et al. 2022
)
, including score-based methods, variational approximations, or approximate MCMC procedures. Combining both components into a single decision criterion from the perspective of the data handler yields a coherent Bayesian formulation of the privacy–utility trade-off.

For simplicity, we adopt a linear aggregation of the two losses, where the coefficient
\lambda
in Equation (
8
) plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier in a constrained optimisation interpretation. This formulation allows us to evaluate the informational content and privacy leakage of summary statistics (as in ABC,
(
Marin et al. 2011
)
), as well as of perturbed or synthetic data generated from the posterior predictive distribution. It also provides simulation-based tools for quantifying ex ante privacy risk under a given release mechanism.

While the present work establishes a decision-theoretic framework for Bayesian privacy, several directions remain open for further investigation. A first issue concerns the specification of Eve’s prior distribution on
x
and her loss function, whose calibration is central to the resulting privacy assessment. Exploring alternative forms of Alice’s combined loss function, in particular those incorporating stronger penalties for severe disclosure events, is another important direction.

Connections with safe Bayesian procedures
(
Grünwald et al. 2024
)
may provide useful tools for addressing robustness and model misspecification, as well as for analysing the impact of information asymmetries between agents. From a computational perspective, more efficient methods for comparing release mechanisms are needed in order to scale the framework to complex models and to design novel privacy-preserving procedures.

Another direction of interest arises from the computational requirements of finding and evaluating optimal decisions over Bayesian posteriors conditioned on insufficient statistics. Since both Bob and Eve’s posteriors require distributions conditionally on
\eta
, these will not generally be available in closed form. It is therefore vital for Alice to be able to accurately calculate and assess their Bayes rules so that she can correctly calculate the risk of a mechanism. This likely will require the development of problem-specific Monte Carlo methods.
In analogy with posterior predictive model assessment
(
Gelman et al. 2013
)
, the proposed Bayesian privacy construct could also be used to provide ex ante privacy evaluations for stakeholders under specified modelling assumptions. Finally, extending the framework to realistic large-scale settings remains a substantial challenge, including both large datasets and high-dimensional contexts in which multiple variables jointly increase the risk of disclosure through record linkage
(
Domingo-Ferrer et al. 2015
)
.

A
Gaussian Example and Integrated Risk Derivations

A.1
Setup

A.1.1
Generative model

We consider the hierarchical Gaussian model

\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}),\qquad X_{i}\mid\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\theta,1),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
denote the dataset and write

\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}.

A release mechanism is a Markov kernel
q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x)
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{B})
, producing a released variable
\eta
.

Given
q
, the joint prior predictive distribution of
(\theta,X,\eta)
factorises as

\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,p(\mathrm{d}x\mid\theta)\,q(\mathrm{d}\eta\mid x).

(16)

All integrated risks are evaluated with respect to this joint distribution.

Throughout,
\Phi
denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function and
\bar{\Phi}=1-\Phi
its survival function.

A.1.2
Decision problems and Bayes
0
–
1
risk

Bob and Eve each face a binary decision problem under
0
–
1
loss.

Bob (utility objective).

Bob tests the parameter-level hypotheses

H_{0,B}:\theta\leq c_{B},\qquad H_{1,B}:\theta>c_{B},

and we define the latent state indicator

S_{B}=\ind\{\theta>c_{B}\}.

Eve (privacy objective).

Eve tests data-level hypotheses

H_{0,E}:T(X)\leq c_{E},\qquad H_{1,E}:T(X)>c_{E},

where
T(X)
is a data-functional. In this example we consider

T(X)=\bar{X}\quad\text{(mean adversary)},\qquad T(X)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\quad\text{(maximum adversary)}.

The corresponding state indicator is

S_{E}=\ind\{T(X)>c_{E}\}.

Integrated risks.

Both
S_{B}
and
S_{E}
are unobserved at the decision stage and must be inferred from the released variable
\eta
. For each agent
a\in\{B,E\}
, define the posterior success probability

p_{a}(\eta)=\Prob(S_{a}=1\mid\eta).

(17)

Under
0
–
1
loss, each agent adopts the Bayes optimal decision rule,
i.e. chooses the most probable state given
\eta
.
The resulting minimal conditional Bayes risk is therefore

r_{a}(\eta)=r\!\bigl(p_{a}(\eta)\bigr)=\min\{p_{a}(\eta),\,1-p_{a}(\eta)\},

where
r(p)=\min\{p,1-p\}
denotes the optimal
0
–
1
Bayes risk function.

The integrated risks are

R_{a}(\pi,q)=\E[r_{a}(\eta)],

(18)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution defined in Equation (
16
).

In this work, we define Alice’s
ex ante
risk as a linear combination of the two agents’ integrated Bayes risks. The parameter
\lambda>0
governs the privacy–utility trade-off by penalising Eve’s performance relative to Bob’s:

R_{A}(\pi,q)=R_{B}(\pi,q)-\lambda R_{E}(\pi,q).

(19)

The risk is termed
ex ante
because it is evaluated under the joint prior predictive distribution, prior to observing any specific dataset.

In the following sections, we show how the agents can compute their respective risks of interest under the different release mechanisms.
In most cases, closed-form expressions are not available.
Even assuming unlimited computational resources, we therefore describe the numerical procedures used to evaluate these quantities and to produce the tables and figures reported in the paper.

A.2
Case 1: Full release

A.2.1
Mechanism

As a first benchmark, we consider the
full release
mechanism, denoted
q^{\mathrm{full}}
, under which Alice releases the entire data vector. That is,

\eta=X.

A.2.2
Bob: computation of
R_{B}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})

Under full release, Bob observes
X
and therefore works with the standard posterior distribution, which is conjugate in the Gaussian model:

\theta\mid X\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{X},v_{X}),\quad\text{with}\quad v_{X}=(\sigma_{0}^{-2}+n)^{-1}\text{ and }m_{X}=v_{X}n\bar{X}.

(20)

The posterior success probability is then known exactly as

p_{B}(X)=\Prob(\theta>c_{B}\mid X)=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-m_{X}}{\sqrt{v_{X}}}\right).

Importantly,
p_{B}(X)
depends on the data only through the sample mean
\bar{X}
.
Since, under the prior predictive distribution,

\bar{X}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,v_{\bar{X}}\right),\qquad v_{\bar{X}}=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}

(21)

the integrated risk reduces to a one-dimensional expectation:

R_{B}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})=\E_{\bar{X}}\!\left[\min\!\left(\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-v_{X}n\bar{X}}{\sqrt{v_{X}}}\right),\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-v_{X}n\bar{X}}{\sqrt{v_{X}}}\right)\right)\right].

This integral is one-dimensional and Gaussian, and can therefore be evaluated efficiently using standard quadrature methods, such as Gauss–Hermite quadrature.

A.2.3
Eve: computation of
R_{E}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})

Under full release, Eve observes the entire dataset
X
.
In both cases
T(X)=\bar{X}
(mean adversary) and

T(X)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}
(maximum adversary), the target statistic is observed exactly.
The event
\{T(X)>c_{E}\}
is therefore known without uncertainty, implying

r_{E}(X)=0.

Consequently,

R_{E}(\pi,q^{\mathrm{full}})=0.

A.3
Case 2: Null release

A.3.1
Mechanism

Under the
null release
mechanism, denoted
q^{\dagger}
, no information about the data is disclosed. The mechanism outputs a constant, independently of the data
X
,

\eta=\dagger.

Consequently, observing
\eta
provides no information about either
\theta
or
X
, and the posterior distribution coincides with the prior.

A.3.2
Bob: computation of
R_{B}(\pi,q^{\dagger})

Since no information is released, Bob must rely entirely on his prior beliefs, namely

\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}).

His posterior success probability therefore coincides with the prior probability:

p_{B}(\dagger)=\Prob(\theta>c_{B})=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}}{\sigma_{0}}\right).

As this quantity is deterministic and independent of the data, the integrated risk is available in closed form:

R_{B}(\pi,q^{\dagger})=\min\!\left\{\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}}{\sigma_{0}}\right),\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{B}}{\sigma_{0}}\right)\right\}.

A.3.3
Eve: computation of
R_{E}(\pi,q^{\dagger})

Similarly, Eve must rely entirely on the prior predictive distribution of the target statistic
T(X)
.

Mean adversary.

When
T(X)=\bar{X}
, Eve can compute the success probability under the prior predictive distribution defined in Equation (
21
):

p_{E}(\dagger)=\Prob(\bar{X}>c_{E})=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{E}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right),

which again is deterministic. The integrated risk is therefore

R_{E}(\pi,q^{\dagger})=\min\!\left\{\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{E}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right),\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{E}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right)\right\}.

Maximum adversary.

When
T(X)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}
, the
X_{i}
are independent conditionally on
\theta
, with

X_{i}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,1).

Thus, conditionally on
\theta
,

\Prob\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\leq c_{E}\mid\theta\right)=\Phi(c_{E}-\theta)^{n}.

Integrating over the prior
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
yields

p_{E}(\dagger)=1-\E_{\theta}\!\left[\Phi(c_{E}-\theta)^{n}\right].

This is a one-dimensional Gaussian expectation and can be evaluated numerically (e.g. via Gauss–Hermite quadrature). The integrated risk is then

R_{E}(\pi,q^{\dagger})=r\!\left(p_{E}(\dagger)\right).

A.4
Case 3: Noisy full release

A.4.1
Mechanism

After the two extreme mechanisms (full and null release), a natural intermediate mechanism is the
noisy full release
. Under this mechanism, independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation
\sigma\geq 0
is added to each observation. We denote it
q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}
, and the released variable is
\eta=Y
, where

Y_{i}=X_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad\varepsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

independently of
(\theta,X)
. Conditionally on
\theta
, we then have

Y_{i}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,1+\sigma^{2}).

A.4.2
Bob: computation of
R_{B}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}})

Since the augmented model remains Gaussian, conjugacy still holds. Given the release
\eta=Y
, Bob can compute the posterior distribution in closed form:

\begin{gathered}\theta\mid Y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{Y}),v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}),\\[4.0pt]
\text{with}\quad v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}=\left(\sigma_{0}^{-2}+\frac{n}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-1},\quad m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{Y})=v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}\frac{n}{1+\sigma^{2}}\,\bar{Y}.\end{gathered}

(22)

The posterior success probability is therefore

p_{B}(Y)=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{Y})}{\sqrt{v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}}}\right).

As in the full release case,
p_{B}(Y)
depends on the data only through
\bar{Y}
.
Under the prior predictive distribution,

\bar{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,v_{\bar{Y}}\right),\qquad v_{\bar{Y}}=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\frac{1+\sigma^{2}}{n}.

(23)

Bob’s integrated risk therefore reduces to a one-dimensional Gaussian expectation:

R_{B}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}})=\E_{\bar{Y}}\!\left[\min\!\left(\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{Y})}{\sqrt{v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}}}\right),\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{Y})}{\sqrt{v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}}}}\right)\right)\right],

which can be evaluated numerically using standard quadrature methods.

A.4.3
Eve: computation of
R_{E}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}})

Mean adversary.

Eve aims to infer the event
\{\bar{X}>c_{E}\}
. Under the noisy release,

\bar{Y}=\bar{X}+\bar{\varepsilon},\qquad\bar{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right),

with
\bar{\varepsilon}
independent of
\bar{X}
.

Since
(\bar{X},\bar{Y})
are jointly Gaussian, conditioning yields

\bar{X}\mid\bar{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(a\,\bar{Y},\;v_{\bar{X}}(1-a)\right),\qquad a=\frac{v_{\bar{X}}}{v_{\bar{X}}+\sigma^{2}/n},

with
v_{\bar{X}}
defined in Equation (
21
). Hence, the probability of interest is known in closed form

p_{E}(\bar{Y})=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{E}-a\bar{Y}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}(1-a)}}\right).

Therefore, the integrated risk can be evaluated numerically via quadrature, since it reduces to an expectation with respect to the one-dimensional Gaussian prior predictive distribution defined in Equation (
23
), i.e.

R_{E}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}})=\E_{\bar{Y}}\!\left[\min\{p_{E}(\bar{Y}),1-p_{E}(\bar{Y})\}\right].

Maximum adversary.

Eve now seeks to infer the event
\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}>c_{E}\}
.
Applying Bayes’ rule together with the posterior distribution of
\theta\mid Y
given in Equation (
22
), we obtain

p_{E}(Y)=1-\Prob\!\left(\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c_{E}\mid Y\right)=1-\E_{\theta\mid Y}\!\left[\Prob\!\left(\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c_{E}\mid\theta,Y\right)\right].

Conditionally on
(\theta,Y)
, the coordinates
X_{i}
are independent Gaussian variables. More precisely,

X_{i}\mid(\theta,Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{i}(\theta,Y_{i}),s^{2}\right),

where

\mu_{i}(\theta,Y_{i})=Y_{i}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}(\theta-Y_{i}),\qquad s^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}.

It follows that, conditionally on
(\theta,Y)
,

\Prob\!\left(\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c_{E}\mid\theta,Y\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{E}-\mu_{i}(\theta,Y_{i})}{s}\right).

subsubstituting this expression into the previous identity yields

p_{E}(Y)=1-\E_{\theta\mid Y}\!\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{E}-\mu_{i}(\theta,Y_{i})}{s}\right)\right].

Finally, the integrated risk is obtained by averaging over the prior predictive distribution of
Y
:

R_{E}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{full}})=\E_{Y}\!\left[r_{E}(Y)\right].

The inner expectation with respect to
\theta\mid Y
is one-dimensional and is evaluated via Gauss–Hermite quadrature, while the outer expectation over
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is computed by Monte Carlo under the prior predictive
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(O_{n},(\sigma_{0}^{2}+1+\sigma^{2})\,I_{n})
.

A.5
Case 4: Noisy mean release

A.5.1
Mechanism

In the same spirit as the previous section, we consider the
noisy mean release
, denoted
q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}
, under which

\eta=\bar{X}+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

with the noise independent of
(\theta,X)
.

Conditionally on
\theta
, we have

\eta\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,v_{\eta}),\qquad v_{\eta}=\frac{1}{n}+\sigma^{2}.

Under the prior predictive distribution,

\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}+v_{\eta}).

(24)

A.5.2
Bob: computation of
R_{B}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}})

Since the model remains Gaussian, conjugacy holds as in Equation (
20
). Conditioning on the released statistic
\eta
yields

\theta\mid\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}(\eta),v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}),

(25)

where

v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}=\left(\sigma_{0}^{-2}+v_{\eta}^{-1}\right)^{-1},\qquad m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}(\eta)=v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}\,\frac{\eta}{v_{\eta}}.

Bob tests the event
\{\theta>c_{B}\}
. His posterior success probability is therefore

p_{B}(\eta)=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-m_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}(\eta)}{\sqrt{v_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}}}}\right).

Since
\eta
is Gaussian under the prior predictive distribution (Equation (
24
)), the integrated risk reduces to the one-dimensional expectation

R_{B}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}})=\E_{\eta}\!\left[r_{B}(\eta)\right],

which is evaluated numerically via Gauss–Hermite quadrature.

A.5.3
Eve: computation of
R_{E}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}})

Eve considers two possible targets: the sample mean and the sample maximum.

Mean adversary.

Eve seeks to infer the event
\{\bar{X}>c_{E}\}
.
Under the noisy mean release,
(\bar{X},\eta)
are jointly Gaussian.
Using the prior predictive distribution of
\bar{X}
(Equation (
21
)) together with standard Gaussian conditioning, we obtain

\bar{X}\mid\eta\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\tilde{a}\,\eta,\;v_{\bar{X}}(1-\tilde{a})\right),

where

\tilde{a}=\frac{v_{\bar{X}}}{v_{\bar{X}}+\sigma^{2}},

and
v_{\bar{X}}
is defined in Equation (
21
).

It follows that the posterior success probability is

p_{E}(\eta)=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{E}-\tilde{a}\,\eta}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}(1-\tilde{a})}}\right).

Since
\eta
is Gaussian under the prior predictive distribution (Equation (
24
)), the integrated risk reduces to the one-dimensional expectation

R_{E}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}})=\E_{\eta}\!\left[\min\!\{p_{E}(\eta),1-p_{E}(\eta)\}\right],

which is evaluated numerically via Gauss–Hermite quadrature.

Maximum adversary.

Eve now considers the event
\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}>c_{E}\}
.
Conditionally on
\theta
, we have

\Prob\!\left(\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c_{E}\mid\theta\right)=\Phi(c_{E}-\theta)^{n}.

Given the release
\eta
, the posterior distribution
\theta\mid\eta
is Gaussian (see Equation (
25
)), so that

\Prob\!\left(\max_{i}X_{i}\leq c_{E}\mid\eta\right)=\E_{\theta\mid\eta}\left[\Phi(c_{E}-\theta)^{n}\right].

Hence

p_{E}(\eta)=1-\E_{\theta\mid\eta}\left[\Phi(c_{E}-\theta)^{n}\right].

The integrated risk is therefore

R_{E}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{mean}})=\E_{\eta}\!\left[\min\!\{p_{E}(\eta),1-p_{E}(\eta)\}\right].

This computation is nested but remains one-dimensional at each stage: for fixed
\eta
, the inner expectation in
\theta\mid\eta
is evaluated via Gauss–Hermite quadrature, and the outer expectation over
\eta
is also a one-dimensional Gaussian expectation.

A.6
Case 5: Noisy median release

A.6.1
Mechanism

We consider the
noisy median release
mechanism
q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{med}}
, defined by

\eta=\mathrm{med}(X)+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

with the noise independent of
(\theta,X)
.

Under this mechanism, the conditional distributions

\theta\mid\eta,\qquad\bar{X}\mid\eta,\qquad\max_{i}X_{i}\mid\eta

do not admit closed-form expressions. We therefore approximate the integrated risks by Monte Carlo.

A.6.2
Monte Carlo approximation of the risks

As defined in Section
A.1.2
, the posterior success probability
p_{a}(\eta)
and the integrated Bayes risk
R_{a}(\pi,q)
, for
a\in\{B,E\}
, are given in Equations (
17
) and (
18
). Under the noisy median mechanism, closed-form expressions are not available. We therefore resort to Monte Carlo approximation under the joint prior predictive distribution.

Prior predictive simulation.

We generate Monte Carlo samples

(\theta^{(m)},X^{(m)},\eta^{(m)})_{m=1}^{M}

as follows:

\theta^{(m)}\sim\pi(\cdot),\qquad X^{(m)}\sim p(\cdot\mid\theta^{(m)}),\qquad\eta^{(m)}=\mathrm{med}(X^{(m)})+\varepsilon^{(m)},

with
\varepsilon^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
independent.

For each agent, we construct the corresponding indicators

S_{B}^{(m)}=\ind\{\theta^{(m)}>c_{B}\},

and

S_{E}^{(m)}=\ind\{T(X^{(m)})>c_{E}\},

where
T(X)
is either
\bar{X}
(mean adversary) or
\max_{i}X_{i}
(maximum adversary).

Nonparametric estimation of
p_{a}(\eta)
.

We approximate the conditional success probability

p_{a}(\eta)=\Prob(S_{a}=1\mid\eta)

by a histogram-based regression estimator. We construct a uniform grid of
K
bins on the interval

[-L\sqrt{V_{\eta}},\,L\sqrt{V_{\eta}}],\qquad V_{\eta}=\sigma_{0}^{2}+v_{\mathrm{med}}+\sigma^{2},\qquad v_{\mathrm{med}}=\frac{\pi}{2n},

where
v_{\mathrm{med}}
is the standard normal approximation to
\Var(\mathrm{med}(X)\mid\theta)
.
Let
(I_{k})_{k=1}^{K}
denote the resulting bins. For
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, let
I_{k}(\eta)
be the bin containing
\eta
and define

\widehat{p}_{a}(\eta)=\frac{\sum_{m:\,\eta^{(m)}\in I_{k}(\eta)}S_{a}^{(m)}+\alpha}{\sum_{m:\,\eta^{(m)}\in I_{k}(\eta)}1+2\alpha},\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2},

In all experiments, we set
L=6
and use
K\in\{200,500\}
depending on the adversary.

Approximation of the integrated risk.

The integrated risk is then approximated by

\widehat{R}_{a}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}r\!\left(\widehat{p}_{a}(\eta^{(m)})\right),

which provides a Monte Carlo approximation of

R_{a}(\pi,q_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{med}})=\E[r_{a}(\eta)].

This procedure is applied identically for Bob and for Eve, the only difference lies in the definition of the event
S_{a}
.

A.7
Case 6: One-bit release

A.7.1
Mechanism

We now consider a
one-bit release
mechanism, denoted
q_{\tau}^{1\mathrm{bit}}
, defined by

\eta=\ind\{p_{B}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{X})>\tau\},

where
p_{B}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{X})
is the probability of interest in the full release case (see Section
A.2
) that can be computed via the standard posterior in Equation (
20
):

p_{B}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{X})=\Prob(\theta>c_{B}\mid\bar{X})=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{c_{B}-v_{X}n\bar{X}}{\sqrt{v_{X}}}\right).

Since the mapping
\bar{X}\mapsto p_{B}^{\mathrm{full}}(\bar{X})
is monotone, the mechanism is equivalent to thresholding
\bar{X}
:

\eta=\ind\{\bar{X}>t_{\tau}\},

where the deterministic threshold is

t_{\tau}=\frac{c_{B}-\sqrt{v_{X}}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\tau)}{n\,v_{X}}
where
v_{X}=(\sigma_{0}^{-2}+n)^{-1}
as defined in Equation (
20
).

A.7.2
Bob: computation of
R_{B}(\pi,q_{\tau}^{1\mathrm{bit}})

Since
\eta\in\{0,1\}
, the integrated risk decomposes as

R_{B}(\pi,q_{\tau}^{1\mathrm{bit}})=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\Prob(\eta=j)\,r\!\left(\Prob(\theta>c_{B}\mid\eta=j)\right).

All required quantities are expressed as expectations with respect to the prior on
\theta
.

Conditionally on
\theta
, we have

\Prob(\eta=1\mid\theta)=\Prob(\bar{X}>t_{\tau}\mid\theta)=\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,(t_{\tau}-\theta)\right).

Therefore,

\Prob(\eta=1)=\E_{\theta}\!\left[\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,(t_{\tau}-\theta)\right)\right],\qquad\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}).

For
j=1
,

\Prob(\theta>c_{B},\eta=1)=\E_{\theta}\!\left[\ind\{\theta>c_{B}\}\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,(t_{\tau}-\theta)\right)\right],

and for
j=0
,

\Prob(\theta>c_{B},\eta=0)=\E_{\theta}\!\left[\ind\{\theta>c_{B}\}\Phi\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,(t_{\tau}-\theta)\right)\right].

The posterior probabilities are then obtained by Bayes’ rule:

\Prob(\theta>c_{B}\mid\eta=j)=\frac{\Prob(\theta>c_{B},\eta=j)}{\Prob(\eta=j)}.

All expectations above are one-dimensional Gaussian integrals in
\theta
.
Following the implementation, we evaluate them via Gauss–Hermite quadrature after the change of variable
\theta=\sigma_{0}z
.

A.7.3
Eve: computation of
R_{E}(\pi,q_{\tau}^{1\mathrm{bit}})

Mean adversary.

Since
\eta=\ind\{\bar{X}>t_{\tau}\}
and
\bar{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\bar{X}})
under the prior predictive distribution (Equation (
21
)), the conditional law
\bar{X}\mid\eta
is truncated normal. Hence,

\Prob(\bar{X}>c_{E}\mid\eta=1)=\Prob(\bar{X}>c_{E}\mid\bar{X}>t_{\tau})=\frac{\Prob\!\left(\bar{X}>\max\{c_{E},t_{\tau}\}\right)}{\Prob(\bar{X}>t_{\tau})}=\frac{\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{\max\{c_{E},t_{\tau}\}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right)}{\bar{\Phi}\!\left(\frac{t_{\tau}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right)},

and

\Prob(\bar{X}>c_{E}\mid\eta=0)=\Prob(\bar{X}>c_{E}\mid\bar{X}\leq t_{\tau})=\frac{\Prob(c_{E}<\bar{X}\leq t_{\tau})}{\Prob(\bar{X}\leq t_{\tau})}=\frac{\bigl[\Phi\!\left(\frac{t_{\tau}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{c_{E}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right)\bigr]_{+}}{\Phi\!\left(\frac{t_{\tau}}{\sqrt{v_{\bar{X}}}}\right)},

where
[x]_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
.

The integrated risk decomposes as

R_{E}(\pi,q_{\tau}^{1\mathrm{bit}})=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\Prob(\eta=j)\,r\!\left(\Prob(\bar{X}>c_{E}\mid\eta=j)\right),

and all terms are available in closed form.

Maximum adversary.

Conditioning on
\eta
restricts
\bar{X}
but does not yield a tractable expression for the joint distribution of
X
.
We therefore approximate

\Prob(\max_{i}X_{i}>c_{E}\mid\eta=j)

by Monte Carlo under the joint prior predictive distribution.

Specifically, we simulate
M
independent draws
(\theta^{(m)},X^{(m)})
from the prior predictive model, compute
\bar{X}^{(m)}
, define
\eta^{(m)}=\ind\{\bar{X}^{(m)}>t_{\tau}\}
, and evaluate the indicator

S_{E}^{(m)}=\ind\{\max_{i}X_{i}^{(m)}>c_{E}\}.

The conditional probabilities are then estimated as empirical averages within the subsets
\{\eta=j\}
:

\widehat{\Prob}(\max_{i}X_{i}>c_{E}\mid\eta=j)=\frac{\sum_{m:\,\eta^{(m)}=j}S_{E}^{(m)}}{\sum_{m:\,\eta^{(m)}=j}1}.

The integrated risk is then approximated by

\widehat{R}_{E}=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\widehat{\Prob}(\eta=j)\,r\!\left(\widehat{\Prob}(\max_{i}X_{i}>c_{E}\mid\eta=j)\right).
