Associations Between Football-Related Exposures, Head Injury, Tinnitus, and Neuropsychological Health Outcomes Among Professional American-Style Football Players
Authors: Niki A Konstantinides, Michael Leung, Rachel Grashow, Jo Ann Pugh, Elizabeth Nolan, Alicia J Whittington, Frank E Speizer, Herman A Taylor, Aaron L Baggish, Ross D Zafonte, Marc G Weisskopf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAuditory dysfunction such as tinnitus is a common sequelae of traumatic brain injury (TBI), and has been associated with neurobehavioral outcomes, including cognitive decline, depression, and anxiety. Few studies have examined associations between concussion history and tinnitus independent of confounding by blast injury or occupational noise exposure. This study investigated concussion history and tinnitus among former professional American-style football (ASF) players, and evaluated whether tinnitus mediates associations between concussion history and neurobehavioral outcomes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included former ASF players who contracted with a professional league after 1960 and completed self-administered questionnaires between 2019–2025. Surveys assessed football exposure, auditory dysfunction, and mental health. Cumulative head injury exposure was measured using self-reported concussion signs and symptoms during play. Tinnitus was self-reported and assessed concurrently with validated measures of perceived cognition, depression, and anxiety. Logistic regression evaluated associations between concussion symptom history and tinnitus, and linear regression models assessed mediation and interaction effects.ResultsAmong 1085 participants (mean age 57.9 ± 13.5 years; 32.4% Black; 6.1 ± 3.7 seasons), greater concussion symptom history was associated with increased odds of tinnitus (highest vs. lowest quintile: OR = 2.90; 95%CI 1.91–4.43; p < 0.0001). Tinnitus did not mediate associations between concussion symptom history and neurobehavioral outcomes. However, associations with perceived cognition (p-interaction = 0.1), depression (p-interaction < 0.01), and anxiety (p-interaction < 0.01) were larger among participants reporting tinnitus.ConclusionGreater concussion symptom history was associated with increased reporting of tinnitus, and neurobehavioral associations were stronger among those with tinnitus. Clinicians should consider tinnitus when evaluating long-term cognitive and mental health outcomes following repeated head injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01053-6.Keywords: Tinnitus, Concussion, Football, Repeated head injury

Introduction

Tinnitus is an auditory condition characterized by constant or intermittent ringing in the ears, and although many people cope with this perceived sound, tinnitus has been associated with diminished quality of life [1], and long-term health outcomes, such as, cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety, and suicidality [2–8]. Noise exposure [9, 10] and age-related hearing loss [11] are the most common causes of tinnitus, with additional associations reported for cardiometabolic, inflammatory, and lifestyle factors [12–16]. In addition, auditory dysfunction including tinnitus, dizziness, and hearing impairment, may also be a consequence of traumatic brain injury (TBI) [17]. A TBI may result in physical damage to the inner ear, spinal cord, or central auditory system [18], resulting in brain alterations that can result in an increased spontaneous firing rate of auditory neurons, leading to the development of tinnitus [19].

Previous research examining the association of TBI and tinnitus has primarily focused on those at high risk of TBI, such as military veterans [12, 20, 21]. However, among military personnel, head trauma is often accompanied by sudden blast- and noise-related exposures [22–24], making it difficult to determine whether tinnitus is causally linked to the head injury. In contrast, contact sport athletes, such as former professional American-style football (ASF) players, experience repeated head injuries, but these do not occur with blast exposure (e.g., collisions, tackles, head-to-head contact, head-to-body contact, etc.). While ASF players are exposed to high levels of noise during both practices (with simulated crowd noise) [25] and games [26], such exposure occurs to all players regardless of individual experiences with head impacts. ASF players therefore provide an opportunity to consider the effects of head trauma specifically on the risk of tinnitus. To date, the potential association between repeated head injury and tinnitus in athletes remains unexplored, representing a critical knowledge gap. Furthermore, tinnitus is associated with neurobehavioral outcomes like cognitive impairment, depression, and anxiety in the general population [2, 4, 6–8], all of which have been found to be related to concussion symptom history in former ASF players [27, 28]. We therefore examined the association between concussion symptom history and tinnitus in a population of former ASF players, and how this might modulate the association between concussion symptom history and neurobehavioral outcomes.

Methods

Participant Recruitment

The Football Players Health Study (FPHS)[29] at Harvard University enrolled former professional American-style football (ASF) players who were under contract with a professional football league (e.g., the National Football League [NFL], or the American Football League [AFL]) after 1960 when plastic helmets were formally adopted [30]. Using residential mail and email addresses obtained from the NFL Players Association, 22,053 former players were sent either an electronic or hardcopy invitation to complete the enrollment survey. Upon becoming eligible, participants are invited to complete an enrollment survey on a rolling basis. Following enrollment, participants receive follow-up survey invitations every 2 years from the date of their most recently completed survey. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, and participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment (#IRB18-1365).

Head Injury Assessment

Head injury history was measured using a survey instrument that asked about the frequency of ten signs and symptoms associated with concussion that participants experienced while actively playing or practicing football. Called the concussion signs and symptoms (CSS) scale [31], this instrument uses items drawn from the validated post-concussion scale (PCS; Lovell and Collins 1998; Chen et al. 2007) and has been shown to be stable over time and amid changing health status [31]. The CSS scale asks participants “While playing or practicing football, did you experience a blow to the head, neck, or upper body followed by:…” with options as follows: headaches, nausea, dizziness, loss of consciousness, memory problems, disorientation, confusion, seizure, visual problems, weakness on one side of the body, and feeling unsteady on your feet. Participants then selected how many times these symptoms occurred by selecting from the following: no, once, 2–5 times, 6–10 times, 11 or more. Responses of categories that were ranges were assigned specific values for analyses as follows: 3.5 for the 2–5 category, 8 for the 6–10 category, 13 for the 11 or more category. All responses were then summed, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 130. Final CSS scores represent the total number of symptoms of head injury a participant may have experienced after blows to the head, neck, or upper body throughout their professional football career. CSS scores were categorized as quintiles.

Tinnitus

Tinnitus was measured through a self-report survey in which participants were asked, “In the past 12 months, have you had ringing (tinnitus), roaring, or buzzing in your ears or head?” with the option to answer: never, once per week, about once per week, several times per week, almost every day, and every day. This phrasing has been previously used in other large longitudinal studies [34]. For analyses, we created a binary variable of having experienced no tinnitus or having experienced any tinnitus in the past 12 months.

Neuropsychological Measures

Participants completed the Neuro-QOL Applied Cognition v1.0-General Concerns-Short Form, a validated and standardized quality of life assessment applicable across neuropsychological conditions [35, 36]. The Neuro-QOL raw scores are converted into T-scores (mean ± SD: 50 ± 10) based on U.S. general population norms. Scores were inverted so that high scores represent more cognitive difficulties.

Depression and anxiety symptom severity were assessed over the past two weeks using the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [37] and seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) [38], respectively. Both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 response options include not at all, several days, more than half the days, and nearly every day, scored as 0,1,2,3 respectively. Therefore, the PHQ-9 has a total possible score of 27, whereas the GAD-7, has a total possible score of 21. If more than one response was missing on one scale the participant was excluded from analyses with that scale. If only one PHQ-9 or GAD-7 response was missing, but the rest answered, we assigned “0” for that single missing response. In total, 54 (4.97%) participants had one missing PHQ-9 item and 10 participants (0.92%) were excluded because of missing more than one PHQ-9 response. 32 (2.95%) participants had one missing GAD-7 item, and 4 (0.37%) participants were excluded because of missing more than one GAD-7 response.

Covariate Data

Participants provided the first and last calendar year they played professional football, which were used to calculate career duration. This was categorized as 0–7, 8–12, and 13 + seasons of play to account for non-linear relationships with football exposures and health outcomes previously seen in this cohort [39]. Participants were also asked, “During your professional football career, what position(s) did you most often play? (Mark all that apply)” and offered the following categories: offensive line, defensive line, linebacker, defensive back, running back, wide receiver, tight end, quarterback, kicker/punter, and special teams. These positions were classified into a categorical lineman status variable, whereby offensive line and defensive line were considered linemen and linebacker, defensive back, running back, wide receiver, tight end, quarterback, kicker/punter and special teams were considered non-linemen.

The enrollment survey included questions pertaining to birthdate and self-identified race (Black/African American, White/Caucasian, American Indian/Alaskan Native/Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander/ Asian and missing). As previously described [40], race was subsequently categorized into Black, White, and a combined Latino/Asian/Native American/Pacific Islander group due to the small number of participants in these categories.

Other health data also were collected by survey. Professional body mass index (BMI) was computed using reported height and weight (kg/m2) during the participant’s ASF career. Former players were considered to have diabetes if they reported that a medical provider had ever recommended or prescribed medicine for diabetes or high blood sugar. They were considered to have cardiovascular conditions if they reported that a provider had ever recommended or prescribed medication for cardiovascular conditions (e.g., high blood pressure, heart failure, high cholesterol) or had ever told them they had a heart attack or stroke. Headache history was assessed based on whether participants had been recommended or prescribed medication for headaches by a medical provider. Smoking status was categorized as any versus no lifetime smoking history. Alcohol consumption was assessed using standard self-report measures and categorized as none, 1–7, 8–14, or ≥ 15 drinks/week. Participants reporting a diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, dementia, or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis were classified as having a neurodegenerative disease. Arthritis diagnosis was self-reported. Sleep apnea status was categorized as no diagnosis, diagnosis with treatment, or diagnosis without treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Means and standard deviations were used to describe continuous variables and counts and percentages for nominal variables. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for tinnitus by demographics, health characteristics, and CSS score.

In models measuring the association between tinnitus and head injury, we used CSS score quintiles to identify any potential non-linear relationships. These models were adjusted for age and race, but did not include other current health factors that may be consequences of TBI. We used the continuous CSS score in our mediation and interaction analyses. To determine whether we could use a linear term for the continuous CSS score in the mediation and interaction analyses, we tested for any non-linearity between the continuous CSS score and tinnitus by additionally including a quadratic term. We then used the continuous CSS score in our mediation and interaction analyses, where estimates are shown per 1 standard deviation (SD) change in CSS. We conducted a causal mediation analysis using the R package “CMAverse” to assess whether tinnitus acts as a mediator and/or effect modifier for the association of CSS with three neuropsychological outcomes (perceived cognition, depression, and anxiety). Given that we found evidence of interaction but no mediation (eTable 3), we present estimates and 95% CIs from models with and without a multiplicative interaction term between tinnitus and CSS score. First, we fit a model without an interaction term to get the estimate for the association between CSS and mean difference in neuropsychological outcomes in the total population. Then we fit a model with a multiplicative interaction term between CSS score and tinnitus to estimate the association in the tinnitus and no tinnitus groups—the estimate for the tinnitus group is the main effect coefficient when “tinnitus” is set as the reference group, whereas for the no tinnitus group it is the main effect when “no tinnitus” is set as the reference group.

We also examined the association between each neuropsychological outcome and tinnitus status. We first used tinnitus as a binary variable (any tinnitus/ no tinnitus), and then explored whether the association changed by severity of tinnitus, in which we disaggregated the “any tinnitus” group into finer categories (none, once per week, about once per week, several times per week, almost every day, every day). Anxiety was the only outcome with non-normal residuals, and so we accounted for this by fitting linear regressions with robust standard errors using the package “geepack” in R [41]. We considered statistical significance at p < 0.05, and analyses were conducted using R Language for Statistical Computing [42].

Results

A total of 16,473 invitations to complete the enrollment survey were considered to have been received (e.g., residential mail was not returned, or engagement with the invitation email was documented). From this group, 4784 (29.04%) enrolled in the longitudinal cohort study, among whom 1085 (22.68%) completed a follow-up health survey, which included questions about mental health and auditory dysfunction, as of May 2025.

Among 1085 participants, the average ± SD age was 57.9 ± 13.5, and 351 (32.4%) self-identified as Black. Among the participants, 420 (38.67%) reported experiencing tinnitus in the last 12 months (Table 1). Participants reporting tinnitus were slightly older, more likely to be white and linemen, and more frequently reported comorbid health conditions.

We investigated established risk factors for tinnitus (Supplementary Table 1). Black participants showed significantly lower odds of reporting tinnitus as compared to white participants (OR: 0.52; 95% CI 0.38–0.72; p < 0.0001). The diagnoses of arthritis (OR: 1.49; 95% CI 1.07–2.07; p = 0.02), any neurodegenerative disease (OR: 1.92; 95% CI 1.13–3.29; p < 0.0001), and having been prescribed headache medication (OR: 3.03; 95% CI 1.85–5.03; p < 0.0001) were all significantly associated with increased odds of reporting tinnitus. No associations were seen with the other variables, although the OR for taking cardiovascular medicine approached significance (OR: 1.41; 95% CI 0.97–2.06; p = 0.07).

We examined the extent to which football-related exposures excluding head injury may be associated with reported tinnitus in models adjusted for age and race. Career duration, lineman status, and professional BMI were not significantly associated with tinnitus (Supplementary Table 2). In a model adjusted for age and race, the OR for tinnitus increased with increasing quintile of CSS (Fig. 1; OR for quintile 5 compared to quintile 1: 2.90 [95% CI 1.91–4.43; p < 0.0001]).

Discussion

Nearly 39% of the 1085 former ASF players who completed a follow-up survey reported tinnitus in the last 12 months, and greater concussion symptom history during football play was associated with greater odds of reporting tinnitus. We found associations with tinnitus that were both concordant with general population samples and also deviated from results seen in other tinnitus studies. Consistent with general population studies, tinnitus was associated with neurodegenerative disease, arthritis, and headache history [13, 43–45]. In contrast, unlike prior studies [12–14, 16, 20] no associations were observed with cardiometabolic or lifestyle factors. Such differences may be due to healthy worker effects [46] where employed individuals may be healthier than general population cohorts. Therefore, compared to community samples, former athletes may represent a healthier and more homogeneous population due to a history of physical fitness, access to resources and other factors which may attenuate associations. Although tinnitus did not mediate associations between concussion symptom history and perceived cognition, depression, and anxiety, these associations were stronger among former players reporting tinnitus.

The prevalence of tinnitus in this study (38.7%) was substantially higher than that reported in nationally representative samples of men (12.8%) [47], and more similar to what has been reported in military personnel [48]. In a study of military personnel, those with battle blast injury had a tinnitus prevalence of 19.1%, which increased to 31.3% at the time of their second health assessment [48]. Consistent with prior studies, players who self-identified as Black had approximately 50% lower odds of reporting tinnitus [16, 49, 50]. Associations between tinnitus and arthritis, neurodegenerative disease, and headache medication use were also consistent with findings from general population studies [13, 43–45]. Such associations may reflect shared inflammatory mechanisms, as systemic inflammation and autoimmune processes have been implicated in both conditions, potentially affecting the cochlea and auditory pathways of tinnitus are less predictive of the outcomes [51, 52]. Previous studies have highlighted a significant relationship between tinnitus and headache [45], suggesting a possible shared pathophysiological basis although additional research is needed. Given that tinnitus was highly prevalent in this small sample, has been associated with mood disorders [2, 6–8, 45] and suicidality [3, 5, 53, 54], and may represent a novel occupational hazard for ASF players, addressing tinnitus is worthy of further study in this group.

The association between repeated head injury and tinnitus in elite athletes had not been previously examined. Although studies in the general population report 1.5–threefold higher odds of tinnitus among individuals with a history of TBI [12, 13, 20, 55], the injuries captured in those studies differ substantially from the repetitive head impacts experienced by former ASF players. In studies of general populations, participants are asked if they have “any history of head injury, skull fracture, concussion, broken nose, loss of consciousness due to head injury, “whiplash” [12, 13, 20] as well as history of “severe head or neck injuries” which may be more severe and less frequent than head injuries experienced by the ASF player cohort during professional play. Military populations also show elevated tinnitus risk [12, 20, 21], but associations may be confounded by blast and noise exposure [22, 56, 57]. In contrast, blast exposure is absent in professional football, and noise exposure is not specific to players with greater concussion symptom history. Together, these findings provide stronger evidence that head injury itself, as reflected by concussion symptoms, increases the odds of reported of tinnitus.

The mechanical forces that inflict TBI may produce physical damage to the inner ear, a fracture of the temporal bone, and tympanic membrane rupture [58], but between 50–75% of patients with previous TBI report auditory difficulty with no measurable physical damage to the auditory periphery and with normal hearing sensitivity [58]. It has been hypothesized that hearing loss after TBI with no skull fracture (the far more common scenario among ASF players) may be due to injury to the brainstem, such as the inferior colliculus and shearing of axons within the auditory cortex of the temporal lobe [59]. In a study of mice with repeated TBI, where no skull fracture, hemorrhages, or cerebral contusions occurred, the auditory cortex was not directly injured during TBI, but rather was affected by secondary injury spread [18]. Secondary injury processes affecting central auditory pathways may underlie tinnitus following repetitive head injury, even in the absence of overt peripheral damage. However, further pre-clinical and human research would be required to better understand the mechanistic underpinnings of head injury-related tinnitus.

This study has a number of limitations. First, we were not able to directly measure history of TBI, but rather CSS symptoms, which act as a proxy although these are asked in relation to TBI. These are also asked about after the injuries in question, which raises the possibility of biased reporting. However, previous work has found that CSS reporting was a reliable measure over time and was not influenced by changing health conditions [31]. Second, although the noise environment in stadiums would be experienced by all players, some players could have used hearing protection and this could vary by position and therefore head injury risk and CSS score. We adjusted for lineman status, which would have at least partly mitigated this concern, but additionally for such residual confounding by noise to have accounted for the association we found between CSS and tinnitus, higher CSS scores would have had to preferentially have been among those former players who did not use hearing protection, which seems unlikely. Years of play would also lead to more noise exposure, but we adjusted for this and also, although cumulative time of noise exposure would go up steadily with years played, we have actually found that CSS score goes down at the highest levels of years played [39], presumably because players who play that long are more likely to have not experienced as much head injury (and so have a lower CSS score), and therefore confounding would go in the opposite direction to our results at the higher numbers of years played. Third, selection bias may have also played a role in our findings. However, in a previous study we found that players who joined the football player’s health study cohort were similar by age, BMI, and position as those who played professional football but chose not to enroll [29]. Nonetheless, the inverted U-shaped association between tinnitus severity and health outcomes in fact may suggest some selection bias weakened the association at the highest level of tinnitus severity. This is a pattern that often occurs when those at the highest levels of an exposure (here of tinnitus symptoms) are less likely to participate. Fourth, tinnitus is self-reported, and has been found to be underreported in Black participants [47], and in patients with more pressing health-related issues [60]. Therefore, it is possible that participants who are experiencing more symptoms associated with concussion history underreport experiencing tinnitus. Lastly, because participants were asked only whether they had experienced tinnitus within the past year, we are unable to distinguish between acute and chronic tinnitus. Future studies are needed to disentangle the effects of head injury on auditory function separate from noise exposure, physical damage to the ear, and prior ear infection history.

Conclusion

In conclusion, approximately 38% of former ASF players who participated in this study reported experiencing tinnitus in the last 12 months, and concussion symptom history was associated with increased odds of tinnitus in a population without confounding from noise and blast exposure. Individuals with a history of TBI may therefore represent a key group for enhanced tinnitus screening and health campaigns designed to highlight tinnitus and its impact on quality of life, particularly among Black ASF players. Although tinnitus did not mediate associations between concussion symptoms and perceived cognition, depression, and anxiety, these associations were stronger among former players reporting tinnitus. This finding suggests the possibility that if tinnitus symptoms could be avoided or reduced, adverse effects of past concussion history on neurobehavioral outcomes could also be mitigated, providing a potential intervention for those with a past history of head injuries. Identifying individuals affected by tinnitus early on may help protect against its wide-ranging consequences, as well as support timely intervention and improve quality of life among former athletes with a history of head injury.
