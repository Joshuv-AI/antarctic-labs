Nomophobia in mothers of children with ADHD: associations with depressive and anxiety symptoms
Authors: Öznur Adıgüzel Akman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMothers of children with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) may experience increased psychological distress, including anxiety and depressive symptoms. Nomophobia, defined as the fear of being without access to a mobile phone, has been increasingly associated with such distress. However, the relationship between nomophobia and psychological symptoms in this population remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the relationship between nomophobia, anxiety, and depression in mothers of children with ADHD.Materials and methodsThe study included 89 mothers, including 41 mothers of children diagnosed with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) who presented to Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University and 48 mothers whose children had no psychiatric disorders. Participants were recruited on a voluntary basis and completed a sociodemographic data form, the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI).ResultsMothers of children with ADHD had higher nomophobia, anxiety, and depression scores compared to controls; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Nomophobia scores were positively correlated with anxiety (r = 0.29, p = 0.01) and depression (r = 0.48, p < 0.001). In mothers of children with ADHD, significant correlations were found between nomophobia and anxiety (r = 0.34, p = 0.03) and between nomophobia and depression (r = 0.51, p = 0.001). In multiple regression analysis, depression was identified as a significant predictor of nomophobia (R² = 0.35, p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that nomophobia in mothers of children with ADHD is associated with both anxiety and depressive symptoms, with a stronger association observed for depressive symptoms. Although no significant difference in nomophobia levels was found between groups, the association between nomophobia and increased psychological burden is noteworthy. The findings suggest that nomophobia may serve as a potential indicator in the assessment of parental mental health, particularly underlying depressive symptoms; however, larger-sample, longitudinal, and multicenter studies are needed to establish causal relationships and to develop recommendations for clinical practice.Keywords: Mothers of children with ADHD, Nomophobia, Anxiety, Depression

Introduction

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that begins in childhood, is characterized by symptoms such as inattention, excessive activity, and impulsivity, and may persist into adulthood [1]. ADHD is known to have significant psychosocial effects not only on the affected child but also on parents [2–4].

Parents of children with ADHD, particularly mothers, face various psychosocial challenges due to their children’s symptoms and behaviors [2, 3]. Mothers of children with ADHD may experience high levels of stress, depression, and anxiety while coping with their children’s behavioral problems [5, 6]. Hyperactivity and oppositional behaviors, which are especially associated with ADHD, may further increase mothers’ emotional burden. Previous studies have shown that mothers of children with ADHD exhibit higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms compared to mothers of healthy children [4, 5, 7]. In one study, major depressive disorder was identified in 32.5% of mothers of children diagnosed with ADHD, whereas this rate was 12.5% among mothers in the control group [5]. Moreover, studies emphasize that both ADHD and other psychiatric disorders are more prevalent among parents of children with ADHD [8].

Today, the rapid proliferation of mobile technologies has significantly influenced individuals’ daily life habits. Mothers, in particular, have become increasingly dependent on digital devices while caring for their children. Nomophobia, a form of technological dependence, is derived from the term “no-mobile-phone-phobia” and is defined as the anxiety experienced when an individual is unable to access or use their mobile phone [9]. It has also been described as a psychological condition characterized by fear of losing mobile phone connectivity [10]. Recent studies have further examined these relationships in adolescents and parents [11]. The use of technology by parents as a coping mechanism for stress may increase the risk of nomophobia. In this context, mothers of children with ADHD may be at risk for nomophobia due to both the stress experienced while coping with their children’s behavioral difficulties and their own technology use habits.

In recent years, problematic smartphone use has been increasingly conceptualized as a behavioral addiction associated with constant connectivity, fear of missing out, and emotional dysregulation [12]. Individuals may rely on smartphones not only for communication but also as a coping mechanism to manage stress, loneliness, and negative affect. Previous studies have demonstrated that nomophobia is closely associated with psychological distress, including anxiety and depressive symptoms [13, 14].

This pattern is particularly relevant in populations experiencing high psychological burden. Mothers of children with ADHD represent a group at increased risk in this context. Due to elevated levels of parenting stress, emotional exhaustion, and psychiatric vulnerability, these mothers may be more likely to engage in excessive smartphone use as a maladaptive coping strategy. This, in turn, may increase the risk of developing nomophobia. Therefore, examining nomophobia within this specific population may provide important insights into the interaction between digital behaviors and psychological distress.

Studies have highlighted strong associations between nomophobia and depression and have demonstrated the negative effects of this condition on individuals’ mental health [15].

Although the existing literature includes studies on the effects of nomophobia on individuals [14, 16–19] and on psychiatric comorbidities in mothers of children with ADHD [2, 3, 8, 11], no study has specifically examined the relationship between nomophobia, anxiety, and depression in mothers of children with ADHD.

The present study aims to examine nomophobia in mothers of children with ADHD within the context of psychological vulnerability, specifically focusing on its relationship with depression and anxiety, and to compare these findings with mothers of children without psychiatric diagnoses. In addition, it is intended that the findings will contribute significantly to the literature by improving the understanding of family dynamics and supporting the development of psychosocial support programs for mothers.

Materials and methods

Participants and study sample

This study included mothers of children diagnosed with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) who presented to Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University. A total of 89 mothers participated in the study. Participants were recruited voluntarily. Of these, 41 were mothers of children with ADHD and 48 were mothers of children without any psychiatric diagnosis who were included as a control group. All participants completed a sociodemographic data form, the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI).

The required sample size for the study was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.2 software package. For the independent samples t-test, assuming an effect size of 0.67, a 95% confidence level, and 85% statistical power, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 82. The minimum required sample size was calculated as 82; however, a total of 89 mothers were ultimately included in the study. Accordingly, the study group consisted of 41 mothers of children with ADHD, while the control group consisted of 48 mothers of children without any psychiatric disorders. This sample size was considered sufficient for the planned statistical analyses.

Data collection instruments

Sociodemographic data form

The researchers developed this form and includes detailed questions assessing the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and psychiatric histories.

Turkish version of the nomophobia questionnaire (NMP-Q)

The Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q) was developed by Yıldırım and Correia in 2015 and adapted into Turkish in the same year by Yıldırım et al. [9]. The scale consists of 20 items and four dimensions: Not Being Able to Access Information (4 items), Losing Connectedness (6 items), Not Being Able to Communicate (5 items), and Giving Up Convenience (5 items). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale is 0.92, and total scores range from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating greater levels of nomophobia [20]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the Nomophobia Questionnaire was found to be very high, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.953.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

The Beck Anxiety Inventory is a self-report measure used to assess the severity and frequency of anxiety symptoms. It consists of 21 items, each scored from 0 to 3. Total scores range from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity: 0–7 minimal, 8–15 mild, 16–25 moderate, and 26–63 severe anxiety [21]. In this study, the internal consistency of the scale was high, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.936.

Beck depression inventory (BDI)

Developed by Beck et al. in 1961, the Beck Depression Inventory is one of the most widely used psychometric instruments in clinical research [22]. Rather than diagnosing depression, the scale aims to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. It consists of 21 items evaluating negative feelings related to hopelessness, guilt, somatic complaints, and self-esteem. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale, and participants are asked to indicate how they have felt “today and over the past week.” Total scores range from 0 to 63 and are categorized as follows: 0–9 minimal, 10–16 mild, 17–29 moderate, and 30–63 severe depression. The Turkish validity and reliability study has been conducted (Hisli, 1989). In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was found to be high, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.887.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses of the study were performed using the SPSS version 26 software package. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For comparisons between two independent groups, the independent samples t-test was used for continuous variables with a normal distribution, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for variables that did not show a normal distribution. Relationships between continuous variables were examined using Spearman correlation coefficients.

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify variables predicting the total nomophobia score. The dependent variable was the total score of the Nomophobia Questionnaire, and the independent variables were group, age, Beck Anxiety Inventory scores, and Beck Depression Inventory scores. In all statistical analyses, a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Regarding educational level, 32.6% of the mothers were primary school graduates, 34.8% were high school graduates, and 32.6% were university graduates. The mean Nomophobia Questionnaire score of all participating mothers was 69.4 ± 21.8. Of the mothers, 28.1% had low, 46.1% had moderate, and 25.8% had high levels of nomophobia. Although the nomophobia score of mothers of children with ADHD (71.2 ± 22.5) was higher than that of the control group (67.9 ± 21.2), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.113).

The mean Beck Anxiety Inventory score of all participating mothers was 15.8 ± 11.4, and approximately one-third of the mothers (34.8%) exhibited clinically significant levels of anxiety. Although the anxiety score of mothers of children diagnosed with ADHD (16.6 ± 11.9) was higher than that of the control group (15.1 ± 11.0), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.533).

Similarly, the mean Beck Depression Inventory score of all participants was 14.9 ± 9.6; moderate depressive symptoms were observed in 21.3% of the mothers, and severe depressive symptoms in 12.4%. Although the mean depression score of mothers of children with ADHD (15.7 ± 9.9) was higher than that of the control group (14.2 ± 9.3), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.225). Discussion

In this study, levels of nomophobia, anxiety, and depression were compared between mothers of children diagnosed with ADHD and mothers of children without an ADHD diagnosis, and the associations between nomophobia and psychopathological variables were examined. The findings indicate that mothers of children with ADHD had higher nomophobia, anxiety, and depression scores than those in the control group; however, these group differences did not reach statistical significance. Nevertheless, as nomophobia scores increased, anxiety and depression levels showed significant increases, and depression emerged as the strongest predictor of nomophobia.

Findings in the literature support these results. Numerous studies have reported increased levels of stress, anxiety, and depression among parents of children diagnosed with ADHD [2, 23]. Similarly, it has been found that levels of depression and anxiety are significantly higher in mothers of children with ADHD compared to control groups [24]. Another study reported that mothers of children with ADHD have significantly lower quality of life and higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression [25].

In the present study, anxiety and depression scores of mothers of children with ADHD were higher than those of the control group; however, these differences were not statistically significant. This finding suggests that the psychological impact of ADHD on parents may not be homogeneous across all samples. This variability may be related to factors such as socioeconomic support, coping skills, and cultural influences. In addition, it should be noted that Beck Depression Inventory scores reflect levels of depressive symptoms rather than providing a diagnostic evaluation. Our findings indicate that a substantial proportion of participants exhibited clinically significant depressive symptoms.

When the literature on nomophobia is reviewed, it is evident that studies focusing specifically on parental samples are quite limited [11, 26]. In this respect, the present study offers a unique contribution. The majority of nomophobia studies have been conducted with adolescents and young adults, and research examining the relationship between nomophobia and psychological symptoms in mothers is extremely scarce [11, 26]. Therefore, this study fills an important gap in the literature by jointly examining stress, depression, and digital dependency dynamics associated with parenting roles.

An important consideration in interpreting these findings is that ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder with a strong familial component. Previous studies have shown that parents of children with ADHD are more likely to exhibit psychiatric symptoms, including depression and anxiety [2, 8]. This increased psychological vulnerability may contribute to maladaptive coping strategies, such as excessive smartphone use. Given the higher prevalence of psychiatric symptoms among parents of children with ADHD, these individuals may be more vulnerable to such coping strategies, potentially contributing to higher levels of nomophobia, which appears to be a widespread issue across groups.

In this context, nomophobia may not merely reflect technological dependence, but may also be related to underlying emotional distress and a tendency to seek reassurance or distraction through smartphone use. Therefore, the relatively higher levels of nomophobia observed in mothers of children with ADHD in this study may be interpreted within the broader framework of parental psychopathology and stress-related coping behaviors.

A study conducted in Türkiye in 2023 evaluated adolescents and their mothers together and found that high levels of nomophobia in mothers were positively associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms [11]. This finding suggests that parents’ emotional states are directly reflected in digital dependency behaviors and is consistent with the results of the present study. Similarly, a study conducted among pregnant women found strong positive relationships between nomophobia and depression, anxiety, and stress [27]. These results indicate that nomophobia may become more pronounced in women during periods of high emotional burden, such as motherhood, pregnancy, and caregiving roles.

The stronger association between nomophobia and depression compared to anxiety suggests that smartphones may serve not only as communication tools but also as means of emotional escape and regulation. Elhai et al. (2017) proposed that problematic smartphone use is more closely related to depressive affect and that smartphones may be used as a coping strategy for negative emotions [12]. This view is consistent with our finding that depression was an independent predictor of nomophobia. From a clinical perspective, this finding may suggest that elevated levels of nomophobia in mothers of children with ADHD could serve as a potential indicator of underlying depressive symptoms.

In addition, a study conducted in India reported a moderate association between nomophobia and depression and a weaker association with anxiety [28]. Another study from Greece reported that among individuals with severe nomophobia, 73% had high anxiety levels and 40% had high depression levels [13]. These findings support the strong relationship between nomophobia and depression observed in our study.

Although nomophobia levels were higher among mothers of children with ADHD than in the control group, this difference was not statistically significant. However, nomophobia levels in both groups were, on average, at a moderate level. Notably, the identification of high nomophobia in approximately one-quarter of participants indicates that nomophobia is becoming an increasingly prevalent psychosocial problem. Overall, the present study demonstrates that nomophobia is strongly associated with depressive symptoms, while its association with anxiety is relatively weaker. This finding supports the notion that nomophobia may represent a coping mechanism related to emotional vulnerability and psychological stress rather than merely technological dependence. In line with the literature, the association between nomophobia levels and symptoms of anxiety and depression [29, 30]. This finding suggests that this phenomenon may be linked to mothers’ emotional distress independent of their child’s ADHD diagnosis.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of promoting digital awareness and healthy technology use habits within psychoeducational programs for parents. The tendency for digital devices to become a “safety tool” for mothers of children with ADHD should be considered, and the emotional function of technology use should be taken into account in psychological support processes. Awareness-raising interventions for nomophobia, digital detox strategies, and mindfulness-based approaches may strengthen maternal mental health. Furthermore, enhancing social support networks for parents may serve as an important protective factor in reducing the risk of loneliness- and stress-related digital dependency.

This study has several limitations. First, although the final sample size exceeded the minimum required number, studies with larger samples may yield more robust findings, particularly in detecting group differences and examining correlations. Second, data were collected using self-report measures, and participants’ responses may have been influenced by subjective reporting biases. The causal direction between nomophobia, anxiety, and depression cannot be determined due to the study’s design. Therefore, future research using longitudinal designs is needed to examine the bidirectional relationships between nomophobia and psychological symptoms. In addition, only mothers were evaluated in this study. Future research, including fathers, siblings, or other caregivers, would contribute to a better understanding of the impact of family dynamics on digital behaviors. Comparative studies involving families of children with chronic conditions other than ADHD may also help clarify how nomophobia varies across different stressors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study indicates that nomophobia in mothers of children diagnosed with ADHD is not specific to the diagnosis itself, but is associated with depressive symptoms independent of the child’s diagnosis. These findings suggest that nomophobia should be considered not only as a form of technological dependence, but also within the broader context of emotional vulnerability and psychological distress. From a clinical perspective, it may be important to consider nomophobia as part of a comprehensive evaluation of maternal mental health, particularly in mothers of children with ADHD.

Studies focusing specifically on parental populations in relation to nomophobia remain limited. In this regard, the present study contributes to the literature by highlighting the relationship between digital behavior patterns and psychological health in parents. These findings are consistent with recent research indicating that nomophobia in women and caregivers is associated with psychosocial burden [11, 26].

Despite the growing interest in this topic, nomophobia remains relatively underexplored, particularly in parental populations. Future large-scale, cross-cultural, and longitudinal studies are needed to further clarify the nature and direction of these relationships. Such research may contribute to a better understanding of the psychosocial mechanisms underlying nomophobia and support the development of more effective prevention and intervention approaches.
