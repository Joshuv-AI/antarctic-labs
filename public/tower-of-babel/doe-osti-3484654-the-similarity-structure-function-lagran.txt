1 Introduction
In classical gas dynamics, shock waves are often introduced as discontinuities in the flow variables.
This description, however, is a mathematical idealization rather than a physical reality. Experimen-
tal measurements show that real shocks are continuous with finite thickness. The reason they are
commonly treated as discontinuities is that the physical shock thickness is extremely small—typically
only a few molecular mean free paths—making it negligible compared with the characteristic length
scales of most engineering problems.
 Because of this large separation of scales, engineering simulations rarely employ meshes fine
enough to directly resolve the physical shock structure. A shock is considered resolved only when
the computational cell size is smaller than the physical shock thickness. Otherwise, the simulation
is said to be under-resolved, and the shock occupies fewer than one computational cell.
 For under-resolved problems, directly solving the Navier–Stokes equations often produces non-
physical oscillations near the shock. To obtain stable solutions, additional numerical stabilization
techniques are required. One of the most widely used approaches is the shock-capturing method,
where artificial viscosity is introduced into the governing equations. In Lagrangian hydrodynam-
ics, artificial viscosity is the standard shock-capturing technique, and many practical simulations
solve the Euler equations augmented with artificial viscosity instead of the physical Navier–Stokes
equations.
 Besides stabilizing the computation, artificial viscosity possesses another important property:
it intentionally spreads the numerical shock over several computational cells. Consequently, the
numerical shock remains resolved even on coarse meshes, typically containing two to four com-
putational points across the shock depending on the artificial-viscosity parameters. In contrast,
an under-resolved Navier–Stokes simulation without artificial viscosity may represent the entire
shock within a single computational cell, making the internal shock structure impossible to analyze
reliably.
 Now that we have established the challenges associated with shocks and the role of artificial
viscosity, we turn to the methods used to address these issues. We begin by reviewing the neces-
sary fluid dynamics background. We then devote considerable attention to the theory underlying
Lagrangian methods, with a particular focus on the staggered-mesh Lagrangian formulation. In
this section, we also present convergence studies and analyses of the code to assess its numerical
behavior. Next, we introduce the finite-scale theory, which provides a theoretical foundation for
artificial viscosity. We then present the structure function, a post-processing tool that exploits
the self-similarity of shock profiles to reconstruct high-resolution shock data. This reconstruction
enables detailed analyses that would otherwise be impossible on underresolved meshes, including
evaluation of the finite-scale equations. Finally, we present and discuss the results obtained using
these methods. This version has a more formal, report-like tone. If this is for a dissertation or
journal article, it can be made even more concise and technical.

2.2 Lagrangian Formulation
The Lagrangian formulation typically comes about by replacing the partial derivatives by the so-
called “material derivative”, defined as

 ⃗
                                           Dt = ∂t + ⃗u · ∇,                                       (10)
 or equivalently in 1D

                                            Dt = ∂t + u∂x .                                        (11)
 Instead, we will attempt to describe a ground-up approach for Lagrangian fluid dynamics, where
possible. These derivations largely come from [1] and [2].

2.2.1 Coordinates
The coordinate systems describing a Lagrangian fluid are at the core of the method. As such we
shall take care in the treatment. Most of what is described here comes from [1], and as such we will
(mostly) follow their notation. We shall also retain functional dependences of the state variables to
highlight the differences between Eulerian and Lagrangian quantities (though this convention will
be dropped later).
 Lagrangian points (called “particles” in [1]- we are avoiding this nomenclature as we will be
skipping the particle treatment present in that book) are characterized by some set of quantities
at one set point in time. In 3D, three quantities are necessary to uniquely identify the Lagrangian
point, which we shall denote as (asi ), where s is some starting time and i = 1, 2, 3. We have adopted
the superscript time indexing which is often found in CFD, but not present in [1]. Often, the
quantities (asi ) are the Eulerian position (xi ) of the point at the starting time s, but they need not
be.
 The Eulerian position of the point as a function of time is thus given by Xi (asj , t). To quote
directly from [1],

 “An Eulerian observer located at the position xi at time t detects the [point] if and only
 if xi = Xi (asj , t).”

 This quote is used specifically to highlight the importance of the Eulerian observer in interpre-
tation of Lagrangian quantities.
 The Lagrangian velocity is then simply the time derivative of the point’s Eulerian position:

2.2.3 Conservation of Momentum
Conservation of momentum and energy both face a similar problem: their flux depends on spatial
gradients. For momentum, this is the spatial derivative of the stress tensor; for energy, it is the
spatial derivative of work. These spatial derivatives are intrinsically Eulerian, and as such are
difficult to construct in a Lagrangian sense. These difficulties will be remarked on as they are
encountered.
 We begin with an integral form of the conservation, again defined by a box bounded by La-
grangian points (which thus may change in time), V t . The momentum that need be conserved

volume’s mass does not change, particles are not exchanging across the interface, and thus do not

prescribes a momentum flux at the surface of the volume, At .

4 Finite Scale Theory

4.1 History
Large eddy simulation (LES) is a mathematical model used in computational fluid dynamics to bet-
ter model turbulence. LES became popular in the 1980s, followed by implicit large eddy simulation
(ILES) in the 1990s. At the time, ILES was referred to as "nonoscillatory finite-volume schemes."
These numerical schemes were successful in modeling turbulence without explicit subgrid-scale mod-
els, and many researchers did not know why, although some offered speculations. A foundational
paper published in 1993 by Oran and Boris was indeed titled Computing Turbulent Shear Flows—A
Convenient Conspiracy. In 2002, Margolin and Rider published a paper called A Rationale for Im-
plicit Turbulence Modeling, in which they coined the term implicit turbulence modeling and offered
a rationale for the success of "nonoscillatory finite-volume schemes." The term ILES was adopted
in later papers, particularly through the book Implicit Large Eddy Simulation, published in 2007.
 The rationale offered by Margolin laid the foundation for finite-scale theory. He began with
the Multidimensional Positive Definite Advection Transport Algorithm (MPDATA), a widely used
nonoscillatory finite-volume algorithm for atmospheric and geophysical fluid simulations. By deriv-
ing the modified equation for MPDATA, he identified its leading truncation terms to understand
the source of the algorithm’s implicit dissipation. These truncation terms do not appear in the
pointwise governing equations. To provide a comparison, Margolin then analyzed the same govern-
ing equations averaged over a finite control volume. Remarkably, the leading finite-scale terms were
found to have the same mathematical structure as the leading truncation terms of MPDATA. This
agreement provided a theoretical rationale for the success of nonoscillatory finite-volume methods
and established a strong foundation for the development of finite-scale theory.
 2009, Margolin adopted the term finite-scale equations (FSE) in the paper Modeling Turbulent
Flow with Implicit LES, even though the paper was primarily dedicated to providing a framework for
implicit large eddy simulation (ILES). Margolin continued to use this terminology in his subsequent
work.

5 The Similarity Structure Function

5.1 Motivation
In Section 3, we discussed convergence studies on different problem sets—a standard method for
ensuring numerical accuracy in simulations. In the cases examined, we used velocities as the com-
parison variable. However, certain problems require accurate resolution of the shock profile within
the shock itself. For underresolved simulations where the cell size exceeds the shock width, tradi-
tional convergence studies become problematic: the entire shock profile is contained within a single
cell, making it impossible to accurately determine the shock width. In such cases, the shock width
is either incorrectly measured as equal to the cell size or remains undefined. When the cell size is
halved, the apparent shock width also halves, artificially producing first-order convergence regard-
less of the actual numerical scheme. FLAG and similar codes that couple Euler equations with
artificial viscosity exhibit an interesting property: shocks are always resolved, and analytical solu-
tions are available for 1D shock structures. This enables direct comparison of the shock profile and
width against analytical solutions. However, the trade-off is that only a few mesh points typically
lie within the shock region itself, limiting the comparison data. The structure function introduced
here provides an excellent tool for addressing this challenge.

5.2 Construction
The structure function is a way to achieve better spatial resolution from a self-similar numerical
simulation. As opposed to the usual analysis method of studying the entire domain over a single
snapshot in time, we instead study a single point in space over all simulation time. In this way, we
can achieve spatial resolution by simply running the simulation with a smaller timestep.
 Here we consider the piston shock tube problem. The result of such a simulation is a set of
vectors (X ⃗ n, U

⃗ is the collection of fluid velocities Uj at those points. The superscript n corresponds to the
U
computational cycle of the output, running from n ∈ 0, 1, ..., Nt . The subscript j corresponds to
the spatial index, with j ∈ 1, 2, ..., Nx .
 In historical papers by Len Margolin, there are two structure functions corresponding to Eulerian
coordinates and Lagrangian coordinates. This is an artifact of the assumption in many formulations
of artificial viscosity where they cancel ∆x0 . By performing this cancellation, they are implicitly
assuming the derivative is taken in the Lagrangian reference frame. As such, structures which
depend on the artificial viscosity are better represented by transforming out of the Lagrangian
frame. This is the purpose of the Eulerian structure function.
 With the correction to the artificial viscosity, this extra transformation is no longer needed.
As such, the Lagrangian structure function can be used. In the future, it is recommended to use
the unsimplified description of artificial viscosity, and thus use the much more intuitive Lagrangian
structure function.

5.3.2 Post Shock Oscillations
The post shock oscillations (sometimes referred to as “wiggles”) present in the similarity structure
are non physical. They are present in the raw simulation result as well, with each grid point in the
post shock region performing a sort of noisy up and down bouncing. This manifests in the similarity
structure as smooth curves.
 The ODE for the general shock solution (Equation 81) does not admit extra maxima and minima
(where du/dy = 0) regardless of the choice of χ(u). As such, the only way for these oscillations
to manifest in the numerical solution is that the numerical method contains erroneous terms. The
typical method of investigating these errors would be to use a modified equation analysis, where
a Taylor series expansion in substituted into the numerical method and compared to the original
theory. Extra terms are deemed truncation error. However, for staggered mesh schemes, these
modified equations becomes transcendental, and as such cannot be meaningfully solved. Instead,
we use the similarity structure function to probe the form of these terms.
 To admit extra extrema, a term of the form αu(d2 u/dx2 ) must appear in the ODE. Then, an
expansion in the small parameter ϵ = (u − up )/up is used in the post shock region to derive an
exponentially damped sine wave as the form for the oscillations. This small parameter expansion
leads to the dependence on the finite scale parameter A to fall out. [3] covers this derivation in
detail. The main conclusions will be presented here.

7 Further work
Here we will discus future ideas and topics of research. They will only be small remarks, and mostly
speculation, but can serve as a pushing off point for a series of projects. These are in addition to
continuing and finishing the topics investigated already in this paper.

7.1 Sedov
The Sedov blast wave is another self similar solution in fluid dynamics. Specifically, it is a spherically
symmetric blast wave, which has solutions in 1D, 2D, and 3D. Using the similarity structure on
simulations of such a blast wave would allow probing of the implementations of 2D and 3D fluid
codes. This could confirm viability of operator splitting, unsplit methods, multidimensional aritifical
viscosity schemes, and more. Extending the similarity structure to higher dimensions may not be
trivial. We hypothesize a number of potential methods for doing so.
 Due to the symmetry of the problem, a similar method to 1D could be used, where a single node
point is tracked for all time. This would likely give a similar profile as the shock passes. However,
some extra terms may be needed due to the geometry of the mesh and the radial/spherical symmetry.
 Alternatively, a circular cut of the mesh could be used- i.e., all nodes at a constant radius.
This would provide more data points and may elucidate any asymmetry that the code may inad-
vertently cause. This may lead to a 2D structure function, which could warrant some interesting
investigations.
 Finally, a radial slice could be used. This would provide a profile similar to a 1D simulation.
More thought on this is required.

7.2 Turbulence
Turbulence exhibits some self similarity. However, instead of being self similar in time, it is in self
similar across spatial scales. This may also be only in a statistical sense (turbulence is still a new
topic to us). Regardless, a self-similar model for turbulence could be converted into a similarity
structure function. This would allow us to probe the implementation of both a fluid solver itself
and any turbulence sub-models that are used.
 To do the former, it is likely that an Eulerian simulation would need to be used, as Lagrangian
simulations do not admit the vortices necessary for turbulence easily. It is unclear how the structure
function interacts with an ALE method.

7.3 Artificial Heat Conduction
Artificial heat conduction has already been mentioned in the implementation of our own code.
However, it is unclear if that implementation is correct, or what it implies for the rest of the code.
More investigation is necessary. However, it has been considered that it may be a way of removing
the post shock oscillations, as it ensures entropy conservation in way that is otherwise unaccounted
for.

7.4 Experimental Shock Widths
Navier-Stokes does not correctly predict experimental shock widths. This is due to it being an
expansion of the Boltzmann Equation (which does correctly predict shock widths) in terms of

Knudsen number. In shocks, the Knudsen number is relatively high, and as such the expansion
fails.
 Len has written a number of reports on modifying CFD methods in order to correct for this (see
[6]). These methods typically use the finite scale corrections. Investigations into the validity and
usefulness of this could be fruitful.

7.5 Outside of fluid dynamics
The similarity structure can also be extended outside of fluid dynamics. Any model that has a
similarity transform can be used, even if there is no closed form for the solution (such is the case
for viscous finite scale- there is no analytical form for the shock profile, but the similarity transform
still works and can be used with the structure function). We can extend these methods to solid
mechanics, plasmas, and more.
 This tool provides a fantastic way of probing exactly what a code is doing. The better we
understand our computational methods, the more confidence we can have that the answers they
give are useful and accurate.
