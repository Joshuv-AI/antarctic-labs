Anesthetic management and obstetric outcome for open fetal myelomeningocele repair at a Japanese center: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Saya Nagata, Yu Matsumoto, Akira Iura, Takeshi Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpen fetal surgery for myelomeningocele (MMC) is an established treatment internationally, but remains new and rarely performed in Asia. This study aimed to describe our anesthetic management and obstetric outcomes of this procedure in Japanese patients, with a particular focus on preterm birth (PTB).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 10 cases of open fetal MMC repair at our institution between April 2021 and April 2025. Anesthetic management, intraoperative uterine relaxation, maternal hemodynamic control, and perinatal outcome were descriptively analyzed.ResultsPTB occurred in 5 cases (50%): 4 due to preterm labor and 1 due to preterm premature rupture of membranes. One case was delivered before 30 weeks, none between 30 and 34 weeks, and 4 between 34 and 37 weeks. Uterine relaxation was achieved using desflurane at a mean of 2.3 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC). Hypotension was managed with norepinephrine equivalent (NEE) scale (median maximum dose 0.13 µg/kg/min). Magnesium sulfate was reduced from 6 g to 4 g in the latter half of cases after high serum levels (up to 9.1 mg/dL) were observed. The PTB rate decreased from 60% in the first five cases to 40% in the latter five.ConclusionsOpen fetal MMC repair was feasible in this population under a standardized anesthetic approach, with acceptable maternal and obstetric outcomes. Individualized drug dosing and objective assessment of uterine relaxation may further improve safety. Our findings may serve as a reference for institutions introducing fetal surgery in Japan; however, they should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size.Keywords: Myelomeningocele, Open fetal myelomeningocele repair, Anesthetic management, Obstetric outcome, Preterm birth

Background

Myelomeningocele (MMC) occurs in approximately 5.0 per 10,000 live births in Japan [1]. MMC is the most severe type of spina bifida, characterized by the spinal cord and meninges protruding through a defect in the vertebral column. Fewer than 50% of patients affected by MMC can live independently as adults [2] because of clinical manifestations such as hindbrain herniation, hydrocephalus, sensory and motor neurological deficits, bowel dysfunction, and urinary dysfunction [3, 4].

The two-hit hypothesis, in which the failure of neural tube formation is considered the first hit, proposes that secondary neural damage arises in utero owing to the exposure of the neural tissues to amniotic fluid and continuous intrauterine trauma [5]. This hypothesis is supported by animal and human studies. Animal studies have demonstrated that prenatal coverage of a spina bifida-like lesion preserves neurological function and improves hindbrain herniation [6–8]. In 2011, a randomized controlled human trial, known as the Management of Myelomeningocele Study (MOMS), was performed to compare the safety and efficacy of prenatal repair of MMC with those of standard postnatal repair. The findings of MOMS demonstrate the benefits of prenatal treatment, including a reduced rate of actual shunt placement, lower incidence of moderate or severe hind-brain herniation, and greater probability of walking without orthotics or devices [9]. However, obstetric complications including oligohydramnios, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), and preterm labor occur more frequently than in the postnatal repair group, and are major contributors to preterm birth (PTB), the most common complication after fetal surgery. PTB poses a higher risk of admission to the neonatal intensive care unit, respiratory morbidities, and neurological morbidities [10]. Thus, preventing obstetric complications is a major concern in the perioperative care of fetal surgery for MMC.

Open fetal surgery for MMC has become an established therapeutic option in selected countries such as the USA, France, Poland, Brazil, and Germany [11–14]. However, experience in Asian populations remains extremely limited. In Japan, the first open fetal surgery for MMC was performed at our institution in 2021. As of April 2025, 11 procedures had been performed nationwide, with 10 conducted at our center. Based on the accumulated experience, further evaluation of the safety of fetal open surgery for MMC in the Japanese population is warranted. Fetal surgery is highly complex and requires coordinated multidisciplinary perioperative management to prevent complications such as placental abruption and uterine rupture. The anesthesiologist’s role within the team is to prioritize the safety of the mother and fetus while maintaining optimal conditions for the surgical procedure and to prevent pregnancy complications after surgery by controlling maternal cardiopulmonary status and uterine relaxation. Although several anesthetic protocols have been reported internationally, limited data exist on their application and safety in Asian patients.

This study aimed to provide a descriptive analysis of the anesthetic management and obstetric outcomes in Japanese patients undergoing fetal open surgery for MMC, with a particular emphasis on the incidence and characteristics of PTB. This study aimed to inform future strategies for optimizing perioperative care in this population.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was performed in Osaka University Hospital for prenatal repair of spina-bifida. All medical and anesthesia records of women who underwent fetal surgery between April 2021 and April 2025 were analyzed.

The team members involved in the surgery conducted multiple simulations of the entire procedure in the operating room before the surgery. Open fetal MMC repair was performed through close multidisciplinary collaboration among anesthesiologists, obstetricians, pediatric surgeons, neurosurgeons, neonatologists, nursing staff, and other perioperative specialists. Anesthesiologists managed maternal anesthesia, hemodynamic control, and uterine relaxation; obstetricians and pediatric surgeons performed the uterine and fetal exposure procedures; neurosurgeons performed fetal myelomeningocele repair; and neonatologists participated in perioperative planning and postnatal neonatal management. We referred to the anesthetic management protocols of the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia and the University of California, San Francisco, and predetermined the dosages of anesthetic agents to be administered [15].

Preoperative management

Patient selection was based on established inclusion and exclusion criteria, and informed consent was obtained from all participants [9]. Fetal inclusion criteria included a diagnosis of myelomeningocele or spina bifida between T1 and S1, presence of hindbrain herniation, gestational age between 19.0 and 25.9 weeks at the time of surgery, and singleton pregnancy. Exclusion criteria included severe fetal anomalies unrelated to myelomeningocele, significant spinal deformity (kyphosis > 30°), and multiple gestation. Maternal inclusion criteria included age ≥ 20 years. Maternal exclusion criteria included a high risk of preterm birth (including cervical length < 20 mm and previous preterm birth), placenta previa, significant maternal comorbidities (including insulin-dependent diabetes management), prior uterine surgery. Preoperative anesthetic consultation included airway assessment and evaluation of fetal lesion site, placental location, and estimated fetal weight in collaboration with the surgical team. Standard fasting protocols were applied preoperatively [16]. Rectal indomethacin was administered for uterine quiescence. A 50 mg dose of indomethacin was used initially but was reduced to 25 mg after a case of fetal renal dysfunction. A dual maternal-fetal blood preparation system was established for emergency transfusion.

Intraoperative anesthetic management

After the patient entered the operating room, maternal monitors were attached, and the fetal heart rate was checked using ultrasound. An epidural catheter was placed for postoperative pain control, with the patient in the left lateral position. Sequential compression devices were applied to the patient’s lower legs to prevent deep vein thrombosis. After preoxygenation, propofol, rocuronium, and remifentanil were administered under a rapid sequence induction, and maternal blood pressure was carefully monitored. After endotracheal intubation was confirmed, anesthesia was maintained with desflurane (< 1.0 minimum alveolar concentration [MAC]) during the placement of an arterial and a urinary catheter. Fluid administration was restricted to prevent pulmonary edema associated with tocolytic therapy. During the procedure, the fetus was not completely exteriorized from the uterus. Instead, only the lesion site (myelomeningocele) was partially exposed through the uterine incision, while most of the fetal body remained within the uterus. In cases of fetal distress, medications for fetal resuscitation, including atropine and epinephrine, were handed to the sterile field for immediate use by the surgeon. Before skin incision, antibiotics were administered to the mother. To achieve uterine relaxation, the desflurane concentration was increased to > 2.0 MAC before uterine incision. To maintain maternal blood pressure in response to hypotension caused by the increased desflurane concentration, continuous infusions of phenylephrine and/or norepinephrine were administered. Uterine relaxation was verified by manual palpation by the surgeon, and additional volatile anesthetic was administered as necessary. In cases where additional uterine relaxation is necessary, nitroglycerin may be administered as an intravenous bolus (50–200 µg) or a continuous infusion. Following uterine incision, the fetus received intramuscular fentanyl (10–20 µg /kg) and rocuronium (1.0 mg/kg) under direct vision. Following dural closure of the neural placode by the neurosurgeon, the fetal skin was closed. Once uterine closure commenced, an intravenous loading dose of 4–6 g of magnesium sulfate was administered over 20 min to achieve tocolysis, followed by a continuous infusion at 4 g/h. During the study period, the magnesium sulfate loading dose was reduced from 6 g to 4 g following the occurrence of hypermagnesemia and delayed emergence in an early case. Subsequently, the desflurane concentration was gradually reduced, and epidural anesthesia was initiated. Tracheal extubation was performed in the operating room after ensuring full recovery of consciousness and muscle relaxation. Intraoperative blood loss was estimated based on the suction canister volume and surgical sponge weight. Amniotic fluid was aspirated using a dedicated suction system separate from that used for surgical bleeding. In addition, because the operative field was not submerged in amniotic fluid, most surgical bleeding could be directly collected through the blood suction system. Blood loss in these cases was generally limited, and gauze measurements accounted for most of the estimated blood loss.

Postoperative management

Postoperative management was aimed at preventing uterine contractions and obstetric complications through continuous tocolysis and close maternal and fetal monitoring. Magnesium sulfate infusion was continued for 24 h postoperatively, and serum levels were monitored every 4 h to adjust the dosage and avoid toxicity. Additional tocolytics included indomethacin and nicardipine. Ultrasound monitoring was performed every 4 h for 2 days, and cardiotocography was performed for 4 days to monitor maternal and fetal conditions. Particular attention was paid to potential adverse effects of tocolytic therapy, including premature constriction of the fetal ductus arteriosus and maternal metabolic complications. Ritodrine was administered when uterine contractions were suspected. The epidural catheter was removed on postoperative day 2. If the analgesia was inadequate, continuous fentanyl infusion was initiated. Maternal blood and urine examinations and fetal ultrasonography were performed until postoperative day 7. From postoperative day 8 until discharge, fetal ultrasonography was performed. Patients were discharged approximately 30 days after the surgery and were subsequently followed in the outpatient clinic with maternal blood and urine examinations and fetal ultrasonography. Planned cesarean delivery was scheduled after 37 weeks of gestation whenever possible.

Statistical analysis

Only descriptive analyses were performed. Continuous data are expressed as medians (ranges) and categorical data as numbers (percentages). Descriptive analyses and data processing were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). The norepinephrine equivalence (NEE) scale quantifies vasopressor exposure by converting the dosage of each vasopressor into an equivalent dose of norepinephrine. NEE was calculated as previously described [17]. Maternal mean arterial pressure during hysterotomy was extracted every 3 s from the anesthesia records, and the average value was calculated for each case. Preterm labor was defined as regular uterine contractions accompanied by cervical change before 37 weeks of gestation. PPROM was defined as rupture of membranes before the onset of labor and before 37 weeks of gestation. Oligohydramnios was defined as an amniotic fluid index ≤ 5 cm on ultrasonography. Fetal bradycardia was defined as a fetal heart rate of less than 110 beats per minute.

Results

The patient demographics are presented in Table 1. Ten patients were included in this study. The maternal age at the time of surgery was 29.5 years (range, 23–39), and the body mass index (BMI) was 23.2 kg/m² (range, 18.0-32.3). The cervical length was 39.6 mm (range, 30–52), which was longer than the threshold (25 mm) associated with an increased risk of PTB [18]. Anterior placentation, which is associated with an increased risk of intraoperative fetal resuscitation and increasing thinning at the hysterotomy closure site [19], was observed in 6 out of 10 cases (60%). The greatest intervention-related motor function gain was observed in children with MMC lesions bordered cranially at L3 [20]. The level of the MMC was at or below L3 in 8 cases (80%) and above L3 in 2 cases (20%) in our study. The exposed placode area was 6.6 cm² (range, 0.7–12.0).

Intraoperative data are presented in Table 2. The anesthesia, total surgical, and hysterotomy times were 262 min (range, 253–304), 179 min (range, 148–224), and 104.5 min (range, 77–132), respectively. The maximum desflurane concentration during hysterotomy was 14% (2.3 MAC) in all cases. Slight uterine contractions assessed by manual palpation by the surgeon were observed during hysterotomy in one case; however, the condition promptly resolved with a temporary increase in desflurane concentration to 18% (3 MAC), without the need for additional nitroglycerin administration. To manage hypotension associated with the administration of high-concentration desflurane, the maximum norepinephrine-equivalent catecholamine dose administered was 0.13 µg/kg/min (range, 0.03–0.29). The average mean arterial pressure during hysterotomy was 67.2 mmHg (range, 59.9–79.1) Magnesium sulfate administration and physiological changes during pregnancy, such as increased circulating plasma volume and decreased colloid osmotic pressure, are recognized risk factors for pulmonary edema [15, 21]. To prevent pulmonary edema, the intraoperative fluid volume was restricted to 7.5 mL/kg (range, 4.9–16.9), and no cases of pulmonary edema were observed. Magnesium sulfate was administered after uterine closure, and the serum magnesium concentration at the end of surgery was 6.6 mg/dL (range, 4.1–9.1). In one case of transient fetal bradycardia, immediate improvement was observed upon interruption of the surgical procedure, and none required the administration of fetal resuscitation drugs such as atropine.

Discussion

This descriptive case series presents anesthetic management and obstetric outcomes of open fetal myelomeningocele repair in an Asian population, providing preliminary data in a region where such experience remains limited. Of the 11 fetal MMC repair cases performed in Japan, 10 were conducted at our institution. The incidence of PTB and the median gestational age at delivery in our cohort were 50% and 36.5 weeks, respectively. For reference, the MOMS trial reported a PTB rate of 79%, with a median gestational age at delivery of 34 weeks. In terms of prematurity distribution, births before 30 weeks occurred in 10% of our cases and 13% of the MOMS trial, whereas those between 30 and 34 weeks occurred in 0% and 33%, respectively [9]. These values are provided for reference only and are not intended for direct comparison. In our cohort, most PTB cases occurred in the late preterm period.

In our series, the most frequent cause of PTB was preterm labor (80%), followed by PPROM (20%). Although multiple factors have been associated with PTB following fetal MMC repair, including oligohydramnios, chorioamniotic separation, placental abruption, and spontaneous membrane rupture, PPROM has been cited as the most common cause of PTB in prior studies [9, 22]. PPROM is often linked to intraoperative or postoperative damage to the amniotic membrane [22–24]. Strategies such as (1) minimizing the uterine incision, (2) ensuring effective fetal immobilization, and (3) achieving sufficient uterine relaxation are critical to mitigate this risk during fetal surgery [25]. First, an 8 cm uterine incision was uniformly employed in all cases, consistent with the established range of 6.0–8.0 cm reported in previous studies [26]. Second, our fetal anesthesia protocol (fentanyl 20 µg/kg and rocuronium 1.0 mg/kg) also followed standard regimens and effectively achieved fetal immobilization in all cases. Finally, uterine relaxation is the key domain in which anesthesiologists can directly contribute to reducing amniotic membrane injury. In our cases, a median desflurane concentration of 2.3 MAC was used to achieve adequate relaxation. In vitro studies of the pregnant human myometrium have demonstrated a 50% reduction in contractile amplitude and frequency at 1.44 MAC of desflurane, with near-complete inhibition at concentrations between 2 and 3 MAC [27]. The low incidence of PPROM in our study may reflect the effectiveness of these approaches even in Japanese patients. In the general obstetric literature, approximately 40–45% of preterm births follow spontaneous preterm labor, whereas 25–30% are preceded by PPROM [28]. In our study, the proportion of PPROM was not elevated (10%). It is conceivable that our practice of rigorous uterine contraction monitoring and control from the intraoperative period—including adequate analgesia and the use of high-concentration inhalational anesthetics— through the extended postoperative course attenuates contraction-related membrane stress, thereby limiting PPROM and potentially contributing to the comparatively low preterm birth rate observed after fetal surgery for MMC in Japan.

However, high concentrations of volatile anesthetics can lead to maternal hypotension and placental hypoperfusion, potentially causing fetal cardiovascular depression. To prevent these complications, vasopressors, such as norepinephrine and/or phenylephrine, were administered at a maximum norepinephrine equivalent dose of 0.13 µg/kg/min. Consequently, the average mean arterial pressure during hysterotomy was maintained at 67.2 mmHg, and fetal bradycardia occurred in only one case (11%), in contrast to previously reported rates of 18–32% [29, 30]. To further enhance maternal safety, future efforts should focus on clarifying the relationship between fluid volume and pulmonary edema to facilitate more flexible fluid administration as well as implementing objective monitoring of uterine contractility to optimize the use of high-concentration inhalational anesthetics.

Most prior studies on fetal MMC surgery involved predominantly White, Black, or Hispanic populations, with little data available on Asian cohorts [9, 23]. One prominent difference between our study and those conducted internationally is the maternal body habitus. The mean maternal BMI in our cohort was 23 kg/m², whereas international reports often cite averages approximately 26 kg/m² [9, 22–25]. This influenced the dosage of administered uterine relaxants. In other countries, magnesium sulfate bolus doses typically range from 4 to 6 g based on maternal size, although there is no established dosing regimen based on body size [15]. The therapeutic serum magnesium concentration for tocolysis is 5–9 mg/dL [31], while concentrations exceeding 9 mg/dL are associated with a high risk of toxicity, including respiratory depression and loss of deep tendon reflexes [32]. Initially, we administered 6 g of magnesium sulfate based on the Cincinnati protocol; however, one patient developed hypermagnesemia (serum magnesium 9.1 mg/dL) and delayed emergence due to residual neuromuscular blockade. Subsequently, we reduced the bolus dose to 4 g. The median plasma magnesium concentrations at the end of surgery before and after the dose adjustment were 7.9 mg/dL (range, 6.7–9.1) and 5.4 mg/dL (range,4.1–6.7), respectively, suggesting that in Japanese patients, a 4 g loading dose of magnesium sulfate is more suitable than the standard 6 g. Similarly, we initially used a 50 mg dose of indomethacin preoperatively but reduced it to 25 mg after a case of fetal renal dysfunction. Given that Japanese women generally have lower BMIs than Western women, individualized dosing should be considered. (Table 5) Although alternative tocolytic agents such as atosiban and nifedipine may offer favorable maternal safety profiles in certain clinical settings, magnesium sulfate has been widely incorporated into perioperative management protocols for open fetal surgery for MMC at major fetal surgery centers. Unlike some previously reported protocols, the dose adjustments of tocolytic agents were required during the study period in our cohort. These modifications, including the reduction of the magnesium sulfate and indomethacin doses, were introduced reactively based on the maternal and fetal adverse events observed during clinical practice.

Although statistical tests were not conducted for subgroup comparisons between the preterm birth group and the term birth group, the early cases tended to have higher rates of PTB. Among the first five cases, the incidence of PTB was 60%, whereas it decreased to 40% in the latter five cases. (Fig. 1) Although this pattern may reflect improvements in multidisciplinary team proficiency and increasing institutional experience over time, given the very small sample size, this observation should be considered hypothesis-generating only, and no conclusions can be drawn regarding a learning-curve effect or other factors that may have contributed to the observed difference. Further investigations are required to validate this observation.

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective study with a small sample size and no control group. Perioperative management evolved during the study period, and only descriptive analyses were performed, limiting causal interpretation. In addition, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability. However, given the rarity of open fetal MMC repair in Asia, these data provide valuable early clinical experience.

Conclusions

Anesthetic management based on existing protocols was feasible in Japanese patients undergoing open fetal surgery for MMC, based on the observed obstetric outcomes. However, individualized dose adjustment of tocolytic agents was required to accommodate the smaller body habitus commonly observed in Japanese women. Moving forward, the incorporation of objective assessments of uterine relaxation, such as intrauterine pressure measurement, may help reduce reliance on high-dose inhaled anesthetics and uterine relaxants, thereby potentially enhancing maternal and fetal safety. These findings may provide a reference for centers in Asian populations. However, in light of the small sample size and single-center nature of this study, the generalizability of these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further multicenter Asian registries are warranted to establish better perioperative management strategies and obstetric outcomes for open fetal surgery.
