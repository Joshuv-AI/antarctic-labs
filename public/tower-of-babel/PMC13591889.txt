Effects of nutritional supplementation on social impairment and behavioral dysregulation in autism spectrum disorder: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Lianlian Yang, Fan Yang, Qiaoyun Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is characterized by core deficits in social interaction, often accompanied by behavioral challenges such as irritability and hyperactivity. In recent years, various nutritional supplements have been employed to alleviate ASD-related symptoms, yet the relative efficacy of different interventions remains unclear. This study employs network meta-analysis to compare the effects of distinct nutritional supplementation strategies on impaired social functioning and behavioral disorders in ASD.MethodsSystematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from indexing to December 20, 2025. Studies evaluating the effects of nutritional supplements on social functioning, irritability, and hyperactivity symptoms in children with ASD were included. A network meta-analysis using a random-effects model was conducted to calculate mean differences (MD) and 95% credible intervals (CrI). Interventions were ranked using SUCRA values. Data analysis was performed using R version 4.5.0.ResultsA total of 16 RCTs involving 736 children with ASD were included. The results of the network meta-analysis indicate that in terms of social functioning, Omega-3 [MD = − 2.29 (− 3.52, − 1.07)] and Vitamin D (VD) [MD = − 2.89 (− 5.08, − 0.72)] were associated with lower scores compared to UT, and both were superior to probiotics (Omega-3 vs. Probiotic: MD = − 1.74; VD vs. Probiotic: MD = − 2.34). For social functioning, the network estimates suggested that VD and omega-3 may be associated with lower symptom scores compared with usual treatment; however, these estimates were based on sparse and imprecise evidence. SUCRA rankings were calculated as exploratory indicators only and were not used to draw definitive conclusions regarding clinical superiority. For irritability, omega-3 and probiotics showed potentially favorable network estimates, whereas no clear intervention effect was observed for hyperactivity.ConclusionCurrent evidence does not support recommending any nutritional supplement over usual treatment for children with ASD. Although some network estimates suggested possible benefits for social functioning and irritability, these findings were limited by non-significant direct evidence, small sample sizes, sparse networks, heterogeneity, and imprecision. No clear effect was observed for hyperactivity. Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a category of neurodevelopmental disorders with onset in early childhood [1]. Its core characteristics include impairments in social interaction and communication, along with restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior and interests [2, 3]. In recent years, the global prevalence of ASD has shown a persistent upward trend. Epidemiological data indicates its prevalence has exceeded 1% and is even higher in some countries [4]. ASD not only significantly impacts the cognitive, emotional, and social development of affected children but also imposes long-term, substantial economic and caregiving burdens on families and society [5]. Therefore, exploring safe, effective, and sustainable intervention strategies remains a critical focus in ASD research and clinical practice.

Currently, behavioral interventions and educational training remain the cornerstone of ASD treatment. However, in pharmacotherapy, existing approved medications primarily target behavioral issues like irritability, lacking clear efficacy for the core symptom of social dysfunction [6]. While antipsychotic drugs show some effectiveness in controlling severe behavioral disturbances, their potential adverse effects (such as weight gain and metabolic abnormalities) limit long-term use [7]. Furthermore, some children with ASD exhibit limited responsiveness to conventional interventions, with significant individual variation [8]. Consequently, identifying safer, multi-targeted therapeutic approaches that can complement improvements in core and associated behavioral symptoms holds significant clinical importance.

In recent years, increasing research has focused on the potential role of nutritional factors in ASD pathogenesis and symptom expression. On one hand, a proportion of children with ASD exhibit nutritional imbalances or deficiencies in specific micronutrients, such as inadequate vitamin D (VD) and insufficient omega-3 fatty acid intake [9]. On the other hand, basic research suggests ASD may involve multiple biological abnormalities, including heightened neuroinflammation, elevated oxidative stress levels, neurotransmitter system dysregulation, and gut-brain axis dysfunction. These mechanisms provide a theoretical foundation for nutritional supplements as adjunctive interventions [10]. Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly eicosatetraenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), play crucial roles in neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and anti-inflammatory regulation. Studies indicate that plasma omega-3 levels in children with ASD may be lower than in typically developing children [11]. Supplementation with omega-3 may potentially influence social behavior and hyperactivity symptoms by improving neuronal membrane fluidity, modulating inflammatory responses, and regulating neurotransmitter function [12]. However, clinical trial results remain inconsistent, with some studies reporting mild improvements while others observed no significant effects. VD, a fat-soluble vitamin with neuromodulator and immunoregulatory functions, has also garnered extensive attention. VD receptors are widely distributed throughout the central nervous system, participating in processes such as neurogenesis, neuroprotection, and inflammation regulation. Multiple observational studies suggest lower VD levels in children with ASD, with low levels potentially correlating with symptom severity [13]. Whether VD supplementation can improve social functioning or behavioral issues remains inconclusive. Furthermore, synergistic effects between VD and omega-3 in anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective actions have emerged, making combined intervention strategies a growing research focus. Additionally, carnosine—a dipeptide with antioxidant and neuromodulator properties—has been explored as an adjunct therapy for ASD. It may influence cognition and behavior by reducing oxidative stress levels and regulating neurotransmitter function [14]. However, the number of relevant studies remains limited, and conclusions remain inconclusive.

Overall, existing research [15] suggests that multiple nutritional supplements may offer potential benefits for impaired social functioning and behavioral disorders in ASD. However, results exhibit significant heterogeneity, and direct comparisons between different interventions are lacking. Traditional pairwise meta-analyses typically only compare single interventions with placebo, making it difficult to systematically evaluate the relative efficacy and ranking advantages among multiple nutritional strategies. Therefore, this study aims to compare the relative efficacy of different nutritional supplements on impaired social functioning and behavioral disorders (including irritability and hyperactivity symptoms) in ASD through systematic review and network meta-analysis. This approach seeks to provide evidence-based support for selecting clinical adjunctive intervention strategies while guiding the design of future high-quality research.

Methods

The systematic review was supported by the online PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews [16] of the National Institute for Health Research (CRD420251176282).

Literature retrieval

Search PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases from their inception to December 20, 2025. Keyword combinations and MeSH terms related to “autism spectrum disorder,” “nutritional supplementation,” “omega-3,” “VD,” “probiotic,” “prebiotic,” “carnosine,” and “randomized controlled trial (RCTs)” were employed. References were manually searched to identify additional studies. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Material Table S1.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently screened the literature. Initial screening was based on titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review for final inclusion decisions. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third researcher. A pre-designed data extraction form was used to collect information including: first author, publication year, country, sample size, gender, mean age, intervention, and outcome. Missing data were attempted to be obtained by contacting the original authors. When an included trial evaluated a nutritional supplement together with another active co-intervention, such as exercise, oxytocin, applied behavior analysis, or other structured behavioral therapy, the intervention was not considered to represent the independent effect of the nutritional supplement alone. Such studies were classified according to the full intervention strategy or treated as combined-intervention nodes where appropriate. This approach was used to reduce misclassification and avoid attributing the effects of co-interventions solely to nutritional supplementation. For each included study, detailed outcome information was extracted, including the assessment scale used, score direction, extraction time point, and whether endpoint scores or change-from-baseline scores were reported. Outcome directions were standardized before analysis so that lower scores consistently represented symptom improvement across studies. Endpoint scores were preferentially extracted when available. Detailed outcome extraction information is presented in Supplementary Table S6.

Risk of bias

In the Meta-analysis of this study, we used the ROB 2.0 (Risk of Bias 2.0) tool [17] to assess the risk of bias of the included studies. Developed by the Cochrane Collaboration, the ROB 2.0 tool is a standardized tool for assessing the risk of bias in RCTs, which is aimed at systematically identifying and evaluating bias factors that may affect the validity of the results of the study. This improves the accuracy and reliability of Meta-analyses. The ROB 2.0 tool contains five key assessment domains: randomization process, intervention implementation, outcome measures, data reporting, and other sources of bias. Each domain is scored according to the transparency, reasonableness, and potential for bias in the study design and implementation, and is categorized as low risk, high risk, and uncertain risk. In conducting the assessment, two independent reviewers will score each domain based on the specifics of the study.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis first employed traditional pairwise meta-analysis for interventions with direct comparisons, followed by a Bayesian network meta-analysis to simultaneously integrate direct and indirect evidence. All network meta-analyses in this study were performed using R software (version 4.5.2), with model construction and estimation implemented via the gemtc package (version 1.1-0) and Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling executed using JAGS (Just Another Gibbs Sampler, version 4.3.2). A random-effects model was adopted to account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. For continuous outcomes, MD was used because studies contributing to individual pooled comparisons generally employed identical or clinically comparable assessment instruments within each outcome domain. Outcome directions were standardized before analysis to ensure consistent interpretation across studies. All estimates were reported with their 95% credible intervals (CrI). The Bayesian model was run with 4 chains, each completing 50,000 iterations. The first 20,000 iterations served as burn-in to eliminate initialization effects, with the remainder used for posterior estimation. Model convergence was assessed using trace plots and Gelman–Rubin diagnostics. Network consistency was assessed using node split analysis; a P value > 0.05 indicated no significant inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence. The relative efficacy ranking of each intervention was estimated using the area under the cumulative ranking curve, with higher values indicating greater potential effectiveness. Heterogeneity between studies was described using variance parameters and the I² statistic. Stata software (version 15.0, Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) was used to construct traditional funnel plots for assessing publication bias. Additionally, the network command in Stata 15 was employed to construct intervention networks, visually illustrating direct comparative relationships among interventions within included studies. Node size in the network diagram represented the sample size of each intervention study, while line thickness indicated the number of studies comparing two interventions. This clearly presented the network structure and strength of evidence, providing a visual reference for the network meta-analysis. SUCRA values were calculated to provide an exploratory summary of relative ranking probabilities. Given the limited number of included studies, small sample sizes, and sparse network structure, SUCRA rankings were interpreted only as supplementary descriptive results and were not considered definitive evidence of clinical superiority. No universally accepted minimal clinically important difference was available across the included social functioning, irritability, and hyperactivity scales; therefore, statistical estimates were interpreted cautiously in relation to clinical relevance.

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic in pairwise meta-analyses and the between-study variance parameter τ² in network meta-analyses. Where estimable, τ² values were reported to reflect the extent of heterogeneity in the random-effects network model. Given the limited number of studies in several comparisons, prediction intervals were not consistently estimable and were therefore interpreted cautiously when available. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies involving substantial active co-interventions, including aquatic exercise, oxytocin, and applied behavior analysis, to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. The consistency of effect directions and relative ranking patterns after exclusion was qualitatively compared with the primary analysis.

Results

Literature screening results

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 5,894 records were identified through database searching, including PubMed (n = 1,359), Embase (n = 2,338), the Cochrane Library (n = 285), and Web of Science (n = 1,912). After removal of 1,140 duplicate records, 4,754 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 4,734 records were excluded during the initial screening process. The remaining 20 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, among which 4 articles were excluded for predefined reasons. Ultimately, 16 RCTs [18–33] were included in the final analysis.

Basic characteristics of study

This study included 16 RCTs published between 2002 and 2025, involving a total of 736 children with ASD. The studies spanned multiple countries and regions, including Iran (3 studies), China (4 studies), the United States (2 studies), Australia (2 studies), and India, Spain, Canada, New Zealand, and Italy (1 study each), demonstrating reasonable geographical representation. The enrolled population primarily consisted of children, with the average age predominantly ranging from 3 to 12 years. Interventions included omega-3 fatty acids (6 studies), VD (3 studies), VD combined with omega-3 (1 multi-arm study), probiotics (5 studies), prebiotics (1 study), and carnosine (2 studies). Primary outcome measures focused on social functioning, irritability, and hyperactivity. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. During data extraction, several trials were identified as involving nutritional supplementation combined with additional active interventions. Specifically, some studies evaluated VD in combination with aquatic exercise, probiotics in combination with oxytocin, or probiotics in combination with applied behavior analysis. These studies were therefore interpreted as combined-intervention strategies rather than as evidence for the isolated effect of the nutritional supplement alone. This classification was considered when interpreting the network estimates and treatment rankings. Examination of study characteristics across treatment comparisons did not identify major imbalance in participant age, sex distribution, baseline ASD severity, intervention duration, or control group type. However, several potentially important effect modifiers, including baseline nutritional status, gastrointestinal symptoms, and detailed behavioral co-interventions, were incompletely reported across studies, limiting formal quantitative assessment of transitivity.

Risk of bias results

The risk-of-bias assessment is presented in Supplementary Figures S1–S2. Overall, several included trials were judged as having “some concerns” in at least one RoB 2 domain. The most common concerns were incomplete reporting of allocation concealment, unclear blinding procedures, missing outcome data, limited information on intervention adherence, selective reporting concerns, and absence of prospective registration or publicly available protocols. Therefore, the methodological certainty of the included evidence should be interpreted cautiously.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Paired meta-analyses (Table 2) revealed no statistically significant differences between carnosine, omega-3, prebiotics, probiotics, and VD compared with UT across social functioning outcomes. However, omega-3 (4 studies, I²=45.9%) and prebiotics showed a trend toward improvement, while VD exhibited a potentially favorable direction. Overall heterogeneity ranged from low to moderate. For irritability outcomes, with limited comparisons included, no significant differences were observed between omega-3, probiotics, VD, or combined VD and omega-3 versus UT or when compared with each other. Omega-3 versus UT showed moderate heterogeneity (I²=44.7%), while VD versus UT had low heterogeneity (I²=0%), but effect intervals spanned zero. Analysis of hyperactivity outcomes yielded consistent results: neither carnosine, omega-3, probiotics, VD, nor combined interventions demonstrated statistical superiority over UT. Overall heterogeneity in relevant comparisons was low (I² ≤ 21.8%). In summary, no clear significant effects were identified across all pairwise comparisons. While some interventions showed improvement trends, evidence primarily originated from small-scale studies with wide confidence intervals, limiting result stability. The pairwise meta-analysis did not identify statistically significant differences between any nutritional intervention and usual treatment across social functioning, irritability, or hyperactivity outcomes. However, some interventions showed favorable effect directions, particularly omega-3 and VD for social functioning and omega-3 for irritability. Because most direct comparisons were based on a small number of trials with wide confidence intervals, the pairwise estimates were imprecise and may have been underpowered to detect modest effects. Moderate heterogeneity was observed in several pairwise comparisons, including omega-3 versus usual treatment for social functioning (I² = 45.9%) and irritability (I² = 44.7%). These findings suggest that variation in intervention protocols, study populations, outcome measurement, and trial implementation may have contributed to between-study differences. Therefore, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, particularly for comparisons supported by few studies. Sensitivity analyses excluding studies involving substantial co-interventions (including aquatic exercise, oxytocin, and applied behavior analysis) yielded generally similar effect directions for omega-3 and probiotic interventions across social functioning and irritability outcomes, although several estimates became less precise because of the reduced number of available studies. For VD -related comparisons, exclusion of the combined VD plus aquatic exercise trial resulted in insufficient remaining evidence for stable standalone estimates. Overall, the sensitivity analyses suggested that the primary findings were not entirely driven by co-intervention studies, although uncertainty increased substantially in the reduced evidence network.

Results of consistency modeling

The consistency of the evidence network was explored using both global and local approaches. Comparisons between consistency and inconsistency models showed differences in DIC values of less than 5 across outcomes. In addition, node-splitting analyses did not identify statistically significant disagreement between direct and indirect evidence for comparisons in which closed loops were available.

However, several outcome-specific networks were sparse and predominantly star-shaped, with only limited closed-loop structures. Therefore, the ability to reliably detect inconsistency was restricted, and the absence of statistically significant inconsistency should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that the consistency assumption was fully satisfied. In some comparisons, inconsistency could not be adequately assessed because of the limited network structure and small number of contributing studies.

Accordingly, the network estimates and SUCRA rankings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for comparisons supported mainly by indirect evidence or single studies. The τ² estimates are now reported in Supplementary Table S2. Prediction intervals were not emphasized because they were unstable or not reliably estimable in several sparse comparisons. Despite some variability among comparator conditions included within the UT node, the consistency analyses did not identify major disagreement between direct and indirect evidence, supporting the overall coherence of the network structure.

Social scores

14 articles mentioned social scores. The network diagram (Fig. 2A) indicates contrasting relationships between Omega-3, VD, probiotics, prebiotics, and UT. The league table (Supplementary Material Table S3) shows that compared to UT, Omega-3 [MD = -2.29, 95% CrI (-3.52, -1.07)], and VD [MD = -2.89, 95% CrI (-5.08, -0.72)] reduce social scores. Publication bias

Funnel plots (Supplementary Materials Figures S5–S7) were generated to explore potential publication bias and small-study effects. However, because the number of studies was limited for several outcomes and the evidence network was sparse, funnel plots were difficult to interpret reliably. Therefore, publication bias and small-study effects could not be adequately assessed in this review.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies involving substantial active co-interventions, including aquatic exercise, oxytocin, and applied behavior analysis. After exclusion of these studies, the overall direction of the network estimates remained generally consistent with the primary analysis across social functioning, irritability, and hyperactivity outcomes. Omega-3 and probiotic interventions continued to demonstrate potentially favorable effect directions for social functioning and irritability outcomes, although several comparisons became less precise because of the reduced number of available studies and the resulting sparse network structure. For VD -related comparisons, exclusion of the VD plus aquatic exercise trial resulted in insufficient remaining evidence for stable standalone estimates. In addition, the overall SUCRA ranking patterns were broadly similar to those observed in the primary analysis, although ranking uncertainty increased substantially after exclusion of combined-intervention studies. Overall, these findings suggest that the primary conclusions were not entirely driven by co-intervention trials; however, the limited network size and increased imprecision warrant cautious interpretation of the sensitivity analysis results.

Discussion

This study systematically compared the effects of different nutritional supplementation strategies on social functioning and behavioral symptoms in children with ASD through a network meta-analysis. Overall, the results suggest that, in terms of social functioning, omega-3 fatty acid and VD interventions may be associated with more pronounced improvements compared to conventional interventions, and their effects appear to be superior to probiotic interventions. Cumulative probability ranking results indicate that VD has a higher probability of being the optimal intervention, followed by omega-3 and prebiotics. For irritability outcomes, omega-3 and probiotics were associated with lower scores compared to routine care, while no clear differences were observed among other interventions. Ranking results similarly suggested relative advantages for omega-3 and probiotics. Regarding hyperactivity symptoms, network analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between interventions, thus no ranking was performed. An important consideration when interpreting the present findings is the apparent discrepancy between the pairwise and network meta-analysis results. The pairwise meta-analysis did not demonstrate statistically significant superiority for any intervention compared with usual treatment, whereas the network meta-analysis suggested statistically significant effects for several comparisons. This difference likely reflects the methodological characteristics of NMA, which synthesizes both direct and indirect evidence across the treatment network and may therefore improve estimate precision when direct evidence is sparse. Importantly, the node-splitting analyses did not identify statistically significant disagreement between direct and indirect evidence, and the direct, indirect, and network estimates generally showed overlapping credible intervals and similar effect directions. These findings suggest that the statistically significant network estimates were not primarily driven by major inconsistency within the network. However, because many direct comparisons were based on a small number of studies with wide confidence intervals, the overall evidence remains imprecise. Therefore, the statistically significant NMA findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

The interpretation of SUCRA rankings requires caution. Although SUCRA values suggested relatively higher-ranking probabilities for VD and omega-3 in social functioning and for omega-3 and probiotics in irritability, these rankings should not be regarded as definitive evidence of clinical superiority. Ranking probabilities can be unstable when networks include few studies, small sample sizes, wide credible intervals, or comparisons supported mainly by indirect evidence. In this review, only 16 RCTs involving 661 participants were included, and some outcome-specific networks were based on fewer studies. Therefore, the ranking results are best interpreted as exploratory signals that may help guide future research priorities rather than as evidence sufficient to guide clinical decision-making.

Previous evidences [34–36] on nutritional and supplementation-based interventions has emphasized that apparent statistical benefits should be interpreted cautiously when evidence is heterogeneous, indirect, or based on small trials. For example, recent evidence syntheses on VD and other nutritional interventions have highlighted inconsistency across outcomes and the need to consider certainty of evidence before making clinical recommendations. Similarly, trials of combined omega-3 and prebiotic supplementation in other clinical populations suggest biological plausibility but do not justify direct extrapolation to ASD populations. Therefore, the findings of the present NMA should be interpreted as exploratory and should not be used to recommend any supplement over usual care.

For social functioning, VD and omega-3 showed potentially favorable network estimates compared with usual treatment. However, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of superiority. Several comparisons were informed by limited direct evidence, and some estimates were influenced by trials involving combined-intervention strategies [37, 38]. In addition, social functioning was assessed using different instruments, such as SRS and CARS, which may limit direct comparability across studies.

For irritability, omega-3 and probiotics showed potentially favorable effect directions. These findings are biologically plausible, as omega-3 may influence neuroinflammatory and neurotransmitter pathways, while probiotics may act through gut-brain axis mechanisms [39]. However, the clinical interpretation remains uncertain because the number of contributing studies was small, credible intervals were wide, and some probiotic trials included additional active co-interventions.

For hyperactivity, the network meta-analysis did not identify a clear effect of any nutritional supplementation strategy. This may indicate limited efficacy for this symptom domain, but it may also reflect insufficient statistical power, heterogeneity in measurement instruments, and sparse evidence [40]. Therefore, no firm conclusion can be drawn regarding hyperactivity outcomes.

The observed effects may also depend on baseline characteristics that were not consistently reported in the original trials. In particular, baseline nutritional deficiency, VD status, omega-3 status, and gastrointestinal symptoms may modify response to supplementation [41]. For example, children with documented nutrient deficiency or gastrointestinal symptoms may respond differently from unselected ASD populations. However, because these variables were incompletely reported, their influence could not be adequately assessed in this review.

Clinical significance

From a clinical perspective, the current findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting routine use of nutritional supplementation as a primary treatment strategy for ASD-related social or behavioral symptoms. Nutritional supplements may be considered only as potential adjunctive approaches and should not replace evidence-based behavioral, educational, or clinical interventions.

In addition, important practical considerations remain insufficiently addressed in the available literature, including optimal dose, treatment duration, adherence, long-term safety, and monitoring strategies. The clinical response to supplementation may also vary according to baseline nutritional deficiency, gastrointestinal symptoms, dietary patterns, and overall clinical context. Therefore, supplementation strategies should be individualized rather than broadly generalized to all children with ASD.

Although most included trials reported acceptable short-term tolerability, the available evidence remains limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up duration, heterogeneous outcome measures, sparse networks, and potential confounding from co-interventions. Accordingly, the current evidence is insufficient to justify routine clinical recommendation of any specific nutritional supplement for ASD symptom management.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths. First, the use of network meta-analysis allows for the integration of both direct and indirect evidence to systematically compare and rank multiple interventions in the absence of head-to-head comparative trials. Second, by including RCTs and covering various types of nutritional interventions, this study provides a foundation for comprehensively evaluating the evidence in this field. Additionally, the simultaneous focus on multiple dimensions—including core social functioning and behavioral symptoms—enhances the clinical relevance of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the included studies generally had small sample sizes, and the number of trials contributing to each outcome-specific network was limited. As a result, many estimates were imprecise, with wide credible intervals, and the statistical power to detect modest intervention effects was limited.

Second, although the network meta-analysis suggested potential benefits for several interventions, the corresponding pairwise meta-analyses were generally non-significant. This discrepancy may reflect the contribution of indirect evidence and increased statistical precision in the network model, but it also indicates that the findings should be interpreted cautiously rather than as definitive evidence of superiority.

Third, the evidence network was relatively sparse. Several comparisons were informed by single trials or mainly by indirect evidence, which may reduce the robustness of the network estimates. Although global and local inconsistency assessments did not detect statistically significant inconsistency, these tests may have limited power when the number of studies is small.

Fourth, SUCRA rankings should be interpreted with substantial caution. Ranking probabilities may be unstable in sparse networks with small sample sizes, wide credible intervals, and low-precision estimates. Therefore, SUCRA values in this study were considered exploratory indicators only and should not be used as definitive evidence for choosing one nutritional supplementation strategy over another.

Fifth, clinical heterogeneity existed across the included studies, including differences in supplement type, dosage, treatment duration, baseline symptom severity, concomitant behavioral interventions, and outcome measurement scales. These differences may affect comparability across studies and limit the clinical interpretability of pooled estimates.

Sixth, not universally accepted minimal clinically important difference was available across the social functioning, irritability, and hyperactivity scales included in this review. Therefore, statistically significant findings cannot necessarily be interpreted as clinically meaningful improvements.

Another important limitation is that several included trials evaluated nutritional supplementation in combination with other active interventions, such as exercise, oxytocin, or applied behavior analysis. Therefore, the effects observed in these studies may not reflect the independent efficacy of the nutritional supplement itself. Although we reclassified combined interventions where appropriate, residual confounding from co-interventions cannot be fully excluded. The findings should therefore be interpreted as effects of the evaluated intervention strategies rather than definitive evidence for the isolated effects of individual nutritional supplements.

Finally, formal certainty assessment using GRADE or CINeMA was not performed, which limits the strength of evidence interpretation. Future studies should include larger, well-designed RCTs with standardized outcome measures, longer follow-up periods, and predefined clinically meaningful thresholds to confirm the comparative effects of nutritional supplementation strategies in children with ASD.

Conclusion

Nutritional supplementation may represent a potential adjunctive strategy for selected children with ASD; however, current evidence is insufficient to support routine clinical recommendation of any specific supplement for improving social functioning or behavioral symptoms. Nutritional supplementation should not replace evidence-based behavioral, educational, or clinical interventions, and future large, rigorously designed RCTs are needed before definitive clinical recommendations can be made. Given the limited sample size, moderate heterogeneity, risk-of-bias concerns, and absence of formal certainty assessment, current evidence does not support recommending any nutritional supplement over usual treatment. SUCRA rankings should be interpreted only as exploratory signals. Larger, rigorously designed RCTs with standardized outcomes and clinically meaningful thresholds are required before clinical recommendations can be made.
