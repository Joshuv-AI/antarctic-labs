Resource State Distillation via Stabilizer Channels
Christopher Popp, Tobias C. Sutter, Beatrix C. Hiesmayr

Abstract
Quantum technologies rely on high-quality resource states, such as maximally entangled or private states, which are indispensable for quantum communication and cryptography. In practice, however, these states are inevitably degraded by noise. Distillation protocols aim to recover high-resource states from multiple imperfect copies, and while stabilizer-based methods have demonstrated high performance in entanglement purification, they have yet to be established for broader tasks such as secret-key distillation. This work introduces a unified framework for stabilizer-based resource distillation in systems of prime local dimension. By formulating stabilizer routines as quantum channels and deriving closed-form expressions for their output, we enable the application of stabilizer operations to general input states and diverse distillation objectives. We identify key invariances in resource measures—such as coherent and private information—and demonstrate how they can be leveraged to significantly reduce the numerical complexity of channel optimization. To illustrate the framework’s versatility, we introduce several protocols: gF-IMAX for general fidelity optimization, and (S)CI-IMAX and (S)PI-IMAX for optimizing (smooth) coherent and private information in both asymptotic and one-shot regimes. Our numerical results confirm that these protocols effectively tailor stabilizer channels to specific operational tasks, establishing them as a robust and flexible tool for quantum resource distillation.

Quantum information processing relies fundamentally on the availability of suitable quantum resources

Two prominent examples are entanglement distillation (also named entanglement purification)

Distillation protocols are implemented through local operations and classical communication (LOCC) in the bipartite setting, or local operations and public communication (LOPC) in the tripartite setting, including a potentially adversarial party. Their performance can be characterized by information-theoretic quantities that measure a state’s usefulness for a given task (see Ref.

Many of these quantities can be described in terms of generalized divergences and have properties

Among the many entanglement distillation protocols

Moreover, the action of stabilizer operations on Bell-diagonal states admits a compact description of corresponding output states in terms of the input state, the chosen stabilizer, and the applied encoding. This raises a natural question: can these stabilizer methods be generalized beyond fidelity optimization to enable resource distillation guided by arbitrary information measures, thereby producing states that are highly capable for a broad range of quantum processing tasks?

Results

3.1
The stabilizer routine as a quantum channel

In this section, we define the quantum channel corresponding to the stabilizer distillation routine, making it directly available to any bipartite input state and allowing us to extend the distillation setting to a tripartite setting relevant for the distillation of secret-key states.

3.1.1
The stabilizer distillation routine

In Ref.
[
30
]
, a protocol using stabilizer codes for entanglement distillation has been introduced, which has been generalized and analyzed in detail in Ref.
[
31
]
.
If successful, the recurrently applied bipartite
[N,K]
distillation protocol transforms
N
copies of a bipartite input state
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}_{AB})
into
K
copies of a highly entangled bipartite output state
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes K}_{AB})
via LOCC.
For a summary of the formalism for stabilizers and related results, see Sec.
5.1
.

Given a stabilizer
S
with encodings
U(|x\rangle\otimes|k\rangle)=|u_{x,k}\rangle
and
U^{\star}(|y\rangle\otimes|l\rangle)=|u^{\star}_{y,l}\rangle
,
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p}
,
k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N-p}
, and corresponding projective stabilizer measurements
\mathcal{P}(x,y):\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}_{AB}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}(x)\otimes\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(y)
.
One iteration of the protocol includes the following steps:

1.

Alice and Bob perform local stabilizer measurements with outcomes
x,y
.

2.

Bob sends Alice his measurement outcome
y
. Alice may declare failure of the protocol depending on
x
and
y
.

3.

Alice and Bob apply the inverse of stabilizer encodings
U
(Alice) and
U^{\star}
(Bob) on
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
.

The state after steps 1-3 can be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{(U\otimes U^{\star})^{\dagger}~\mathcal{P}(x,y)~\rho_{AB}~\mathcal{P}(x,y)~(U\otimes U^{\star})}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}=|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|\otimes\sigma^{x,y}_{AB},

(3)

where the first
p
qudits contain the measurement outcomes and
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
is the output state to be used in the following iterations.
Here,
\mathbbm{P}(x,y)=\Tr[\mathcal{P}(x,y)~\rho_{AB}]
corresponds to the probability of obtaining the measurement outcomes
x,y
.

3.1.2
The bipartite stabilizer channel

Since, by definition, the codewords
|u_{x,k}\rangle\otimes|u_{y,l}^{\star}\rangle
form a basis of the joint codespaces
\mathcal{Q}(x)\otimes\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(y)
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}}|u_{x,k}\rangle\langle u_{x,k}|

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\star}_{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}}|u^{\star}_{y,l}\rangle\langle u^{\star}_{y,l}|

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}}|u_{x,k}\rangle\langle u_{x,k}|\otimes|u^{\star}_{y,l}\rangle\langle u^{\star}_{y,l}|.

(6)

Now consider the post-measurement state for the input state
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{H}_{AB}^{\otimes N}
and measurement outcomes

, stored in the additional classical registers
X
(belonging to Alice) and

(belonging to Bob), followed by the application of the inverse encoding according to steps 1-3 of the stabilizer distillation routine presented in Sec.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{x,y}_{AB}(\rho_{AB}):=|x,y\rangle\langle x,y|_{X}\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|_{Y}\otimes\frac{(U\overset{AB}{\otimes}U^{\star})^{\dagger}~\mathcal{P}(x,y)~\rho_{AB}~\mathcal{P}(x,y)~(U\overset{AB}{\otimes}U^{\star})}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}.

(7)

Considering all measurement outcomes, we define the bipartite stabilizer routine channel as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{AB}(\rho_{AB}):=\sum_{x,y}|x\rangle\langle x|_{X}\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|_{Y}\otimes(U\overset{AB}{\otimes}U^{\star})^{\dagger}~\mathcal{P}(x,y)~\rho_{AB}~\mathcal{P}(x,y)~(U\overset{AB}{\otimes}U^{\star}).

(8)

Introducing the operators

\displaystyle M^{x}_{A}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k}(|x\rangle\otimes|k\rangle)\langle u_{x,k}|=U^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}_{A}(x),\hskip 130.0002pt(M^{x}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}),

(9)

\displaystyle M^{\star y}_{B}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l}(|y\rangle\otimes|l\rangle)\langle u^{\star}_{y,l}|=(U^{\star})^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}^{\star}_{B}(y),\hskip 122.00015pt(M^{\star y}_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}),

(10)

\displaystyle K_{AB}^{x,y}

\displaystyle:=|x\rangle_{X}\otimes|y\rangle_{Y}\otimes M_{A}^{x}\otimes M_{B}^{\star y},\hskip 145.00021pt(K_{AB}^{x,y}:\mathcal{H}_{AB}^{\otimes N}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{XY}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{AB}^{\otimes N}),

(11)

we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{AB}(\rho_{AB})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}|x\rangle\langle x|_{X}\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|_{Y}\otimes(M_{A}^{x}\otimes M_{B}^{\star y})~\rho_{AB}~(M_{A}^{x\dagger}\otimes M_{B}^{y\dagger})=\sum_{x,y}K_{AB}^{x,y}~\rho_{AB}~K_{AB}^{x,y\dagger}.

(12)

Note that

. Hence, the

are Kraus operators for the quantum channel
\mathcal{L}_{AB}

For fixed measurement outcomes
x,y
, the output state
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}:=\mathcal{L}^{x,y}_{AB}(\rho_{AB})
can be written in terms of

as follows:

\displaystyle\sigma_{AB}^{x,y}=\frac{K_{AB}^{x,y}~\rho_{AB}~K_{AB}^{x,y\dagger}}{\Tr[K_{AB}^{x,y}~\rho_{AB}~K_{AB}^{x,y\dagger}]}=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}K_{AB}^{x,y}~\rho_{AB}~K_{AB}^{x,y\dagger}.

(13)

3.1.3
The tripartite stabilizer channel

In the setting of quantum cryptography, e.g., for the distillation or distribution of secret keys, we consider a tripartite setting by including the adversary party “Eve” holding the system
E

\phi_{ABE}

In the following, we assume that all measurement outcomes are communicated in public. Consider the purification based on the spectral decomposition of

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\lambda_{m}|\Phi_{m}\rangle\langle\Phi_{m}|_{AB}

\displaystyle\phi_{ABE}

\displaystyle=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|_{ABE}=\sum_{m,n}\sqrt{\lambda_{m}\lambda_{n}}|\Phi_{m}\rangle\langle\Phi_{n}|_{AB}\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|_{E}.

(15)

Here

The tripartite channel corresponding to the bipartite stabilizer distillation channel is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{ABE}(\phi_{ABE})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{AB}\otimes\textrm{id}_{E}(\phi_{ABE})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}K_{AB}^{x,y}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E}~\phi_{ABE}~K_{AB}^{x,y\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E},

\displaystyle\phi_{ABE}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=0}^{r-1}\sum_{e,f\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}}\sqrt{\lambda_{m}\lambda_{n}}~\beta_{e,f}^{m,n}~|\Omega(e)\rangle\langle\Omega(f)|_{AB}\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|_{E}

\displaystyle=:\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n,\\
e,f\end{subarray}}\alpha_{e,f}^{m,n}~|\Omega(e)\rangle\langle\Omega(f)|_{AB}\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|_{E}.

For a Bell-diagonal input state

\displaystyle\phi_{ABE}=\sum_{m,n=0}^{r-1}\sqrt{p_{m}p_{n}}~|\Omega(m)\rangle\langle\Omega(n)|_{AB}\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|_{E}.

Here,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{ABE}(\phi_{ABE})

3.2
Principle and relevant objects for stabilizer-based resource distillation

In this section we show how to optimize the stabilizer channels so that their bipartite and tripartite output states are highly potent for operational tasks like entanglement and secret-key distillation.

The capability of general quantum states for the entanglement and secret-key distillation tasks can be characterized or lower-bounded via generalized information quantities. We summarize methods of how to do so in Sec.
5.2
and Sec.
5.3
.
The principle of stabilizer-based resource state distillation is as follows: depending on the distillation task, we choose an information quantity that measures the corresponding capability of a quantum state. Leveraging the precise output form of the stabilizer channels, we optimize the channel regarding the stabilizer, encoding, and measurement outcome such that the information measure for the channel output becomes maximized. This procedure can be iterated, effectively distilling target resource states that are optimal for the operational task as measured by the chosen information quantity. In the following, we determine relevant states and information measures for entanglement and secret-key distillation and leverage their properties to simplify the optimization of the distillation channel.

3.2.1
Relevant states and measures for entanglement distillation

Tracing out the system
E
from the tripartite output state (
26
), the general bipartite output state (
13
) takes the specific form:

\displaystyle\sigma_{AB}^{x,y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}K_{AB}^{x,y}~\rho_{AB}~K_{AB}^{x,y\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y)\end{subarray}}\alpha_{e,f}^{m,m}\frac{1}{d^{p}}~[(T^{e}_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1})~\Omega_{0,0}^{\otimes N-p}~(T_{x}^{f\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1})]_{AB}.

(28)

In the one-shot setting, we leverage Theorem
3
to determine the smooth max-relative entropy
H^{\varepsilon}_{max}(\sigma_{AB}^{x,y})
as an information measure to be optimized for distilling resource states with a high capability for entanglement distillation.

In the asymptotic setting, Theorem
4
implies that the coherent information
I^{A\rangle B}_{c}(\sigma^{x,y}_{AB})
is an achievable rate for entanglement distillation and therefore provides a suitable information measure for optimization.

3.2.2
Relevant states and measures for secret-key distillation

For the task of secret-key distillation, consider the output (

\displaystyle\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}:=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n,\\

(29)

To draw a key from this state, Alice and Bob apply local measurements to their system, whose action is given by the local measurement or dephasing channels
\mathcal{D}_{A/B}(\cdot)
in (

Assume that Alice applies
\mathcal{D}_{A}
to the output state:

\displaystyle\chi^{x,y}_{ABE}:=\mathcal{D}_{A}(\tau^{x,y}_{ABE})=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n,\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y),\\
i,k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N-p}\end{subarray}}\alpha^{m,n}_{e,f}~\langle i|T_{x}^{e}|k\rangle\langle l|T_{x}^{f\dagger}|i\rangle~|i\rangle\langle i|_{A}\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|_{B}\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|_{E}.

(30)

To evaluate this state regarding its usefulness for secret-key generation, we consider the reduced bipartite states:

\displaystyle\chi^{x,y}_{AB}

\displaystyle:=\Tr_{E}[\chi_{ABE}]=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y),\\
i,k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}\end{subarray}}\alpha^{m,m}_{e,f}~\langle i|T_{x}^{e}|k\rangle\langle l|T_{x}^{f\dagger}|i\rangle~|i\rangle\langle i|_{A}\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|_{B},

(31)

\displaystyle\chi^{x,y}_{AE}

\displaystyle:=\Tr_{B}[\chi_{ABE}]=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n,\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y),\\
i\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}\end{subarray}}\alpha^{m,n}_{e,f}~\langle i|T_{x}^{e}T_{x}^{f\dagger}|i\rangle~|i\rangle\langle i|_{A}\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|_{E}.

(32)

In the one-shot setting, Theorem
5
implies that the difference of the hypothesis testing mutual information
I^{\varepsilon}_{H}(\chi^{x,y}_{AB})
and the smooth max-mutual information
I^{\epsilon}_{max}(\chi^{x,y}_{AE})
provides achievable rates for the one-shot secret-key distillation, given certain smoothing parameters
\varepsilon
and
\epsilon
. Optimization of this difference by varying
\varepsilon
,
\epsilon
, and the channel output
\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}
(determining
\chi_{AB}
and
\chi_{AE}
) hence optimizes the resource for one-shot secret key distillation.

Likewise, in the asymptotic setting, the so-called private information
I(\chi_{ABE}):=I(\chi_{AB})-I(\chi_{AE})
provides an achievable rate according to Theorem
6
and serves as a quantity to be optimized via stabilizer channels.

3.3
Simplifications for local isometric and unitary invariance

In Ref.
[
17
]
, it is shown that all considered divergence-based measures, as discussed above and defined in Sec.
5
, are invariant under local isometric or unitary transformations.
We leverage this property in two ways. First, we show that a different choice of encoding for the stabilizer channel corresponds to a local unitary transformation of the output state. Hence, given a certain information measure, the choice of encoding is irrelevant for finding the optimal stabilizer channel if the information measure is invariant. Second, we show that the tripartite purification of the bipartite stabilizer channel output is related to the output of the tripartite channel by a local isometric transformation. Given a local isometric invariant information measure in the tripartite distillation setting, the purification of the bipartite channel output can be considered instead of the tripartite channel output. This effectively reduces the dimension of the states to evaluate and therefore brings significant performance improvements for calculating the information measure for distillation.

3.3.1
Local unitary equivalence of encodings

We now show that a change of stabilizer encoding implies a unitary transformation of the bipartite output state, acting locally on the systems
A
and
B
. Following Proposition 6 of Ref.
[
31
]
, given a stabilizer encoding
U
, for any other stabilizer encoding
V
, there exist unitary matrices
Z_{k}
, such that

V=U~(\sum_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes Z_{k})
and
T^{V,e}_{x}=Z_{x}^{\dagger}T^{U,e}_{x}Z_{y}
. This implies for the output state (
3.2.1
) using the encoding
V
.

\displaystyle\sigma^{x,y,V}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y)\end{subarray}}\alpha_{e,f}^{m,m}\frac{1}{d^{p}}~(Z_{x}^{\dagger}T^{U,e}_{x}Z_{y}\otimes\mathbbm{1})~\Omega^{\otimes N-p}_{0,0}~(Z_{x}^{\dagger}T^{U,f}_{x}Z_{y}\otimes\mathbbm{1})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y)\end{subarray}}\alpha_{e,f}^{m,m}\frac{1}{d^{p}}~(Z_{x}^{\dagger}T^{U,e}_{x}\otimes Z_{y}^{T})~\Omega^{\otimes N-p}_{0,0}~(Z_{x}^{\dagger}T^{U,f}_{x}\otimes Z_{y}^{T})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\\
e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y)\end{subarray}}\alpha_{e,f}^{m,m}\frac{1}{d^{p}}~(Z_{x}^{\dagger}\otimes Z_{y}^{T})(T^{U,e}_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1})~\Omega^{\otimes N-p}_{0,0}~(T^{U,f}_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1})^{\dagger}(Z_{x}\otimes Z_{y}^{\star})

\displaystyle=(Z_{x}^{\dagger}\otimes Z_{y}^{T})~\sigma^{x,y,U}_{AB}~(Z_{x}\otimes Z_{y}^{\star}).

(33)

Note that the coefficients
\alpha
only depend on the input state and that the probabilities
\mathcal{P}(x,y)
only depend on the codespaces
\mathcal{Q}(x)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(y)
and not on the choice of encoding, i.e., their basis.
Also, for the second equation, we used the “transpose (
T
) trick”, holding for any matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}\times d_{B}}
:
M\otimes\mathbbm{1}|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle=\mathbbm{1}\otimes M^{T}|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle
.

Equivalently, one finds for the tripartite channel output:

\displaystyle\tau^{x,y,V}_{ABE}

\displaystyle=(Z_{x}^{\dagger}\otimes Z_{y}^{T})_{AB}~\tau^{x,y,U}_{ABE}~(Z_{x}\otimes Z_{y}^{\star})_{AB}.

(34)

Consequently, any information measure that is invariant under local unitary transformations is invariant under change of encodings for the stabilizer channel when measuring the channel output.

3.3.2
Local isometric equivalence of purifications

We now show that the tripartite purification
\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}
of the bipartite output state
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
of the bipartite channel
\mathcal{L}_{AB}
for a certain measurement outcome
x,y
is related to the post-measurement output state
\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}
of the tripartite channel
\mathcal{L}_{ABE}
applied to the purification
\phi_{ABE}
of the input state
\rho_{AB}
.

By (
8
) and (
16
), the channel
\mathcal{L}_{ABE}
consists of a projective measurement followed by a unitary transformation. Given the purification of the input state
\phi_{ABE}
, applying
\mathcal{L}_{ABE}
and projecting to the measurement outcomes
x,y
, the resulting state
\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}
is also a pure state.
We have for the purification
\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}
of the bipartite output state
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
and the tripartite output state
\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}
:

\displaystyle\Tr_{E^{\prime}}[\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}]=\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}=\Tr_{E}[\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}].

(35)

Hence, both
\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}
and
\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}
are purifications of
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
and as such related by an isometry
V_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}

[
2
]
, mapping one purification system to the other:

\displaystyle\tau^{x,y}_{ABE}=V_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}~\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}~V^{\dagger}_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}.

(36)

As the isometry only acts on the purifying system, also the locally dephased states satisfy this relation:

\displaystyle\chi^{x,y}_{ABE}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{A}(\tau^{x,y}_{ABE})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{A}(V_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}~\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}~V^{\dagger}_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E})

\displaystyle=V_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}~\mathcal{D}_{A}(\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}})~V^{\dagger}_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}

\displaystyle=:V_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}~\zeta^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}~V^{\dagger}_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E},

(37)

where we defined the partially dephased state
\zeta^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}=\mathcal{D}_{A}(\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}})
based on the purification
\psi^{x,y}_{ABE^{\prime}}
.
Consequently, the reduced states
\chi_{AB}
(
31
) and
\chi_{AE}
(
32
) satisfy

\displaystyle\chi^{x,y}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{x,y}_{AB}

(38)

\displaystyle\chi^{x,y}_{AE}

\displaystyle=V_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}~\zeta^{x,y}_{AE^{\prime}}~V^{\dagger}_{E^{\prime}\rightarrow E}.

(39)

Note that because
E^{\prime}
is a purification system for the
d^{N-p}
-dimensional output state
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
,
E^{\prime}
can be chosen to be
d^{2(N-p)}
-dimensional if
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
has full rank. The system
E
used for the purification of the
d^{N}
-dimensional input state
\rho_{AB}
, however, needs to be
d^{2N}
-dimensional if
\rho_{AB}
has full rank. Hence, considering states based on
E^{\prime}
has computational advantages, for the calculation of quantities that are invariant under isometric transformations on the purifying system. Since the Hilbert space dimension grows exponentially with the number of qudits, this reduction lowers the computational complexity of stabilizer-based protocols—such as the SPI-IMAX protocol introduced below—by orders of magnitude, transforming otherwise intractable optimizations into feasible numerical tasks.

3.4
Defining stabilizer-based resource distillation protocols

In this section, we define specific protocols that demonstrate the principle of stabilizer-based resource distillation. We consider the operational tasks of entanglement distillation (cf. Sec.
5.2.1
) and secret-key distillation (cf. Sec.
5.2.2
) and define protocols that optimize corresponding information measures via stabilizer operations.

3.4.1
Entanglement distillation

The first example extends the recently introduced FIMAX protocol
[
31
]
. Using a specific “canonical encoding
U_{c}
”,
FIMAX
requires Bell-diagonal states to increase the fidelity with the maximally entangled state
\mathcal{F}(\rho):=\langle\Omega_{0,0}|~\rho~|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle
. For two copies of the input state and prime dimension, FIMAX is proven to maximize the fidelity increase per iteration for Bell-diagonal states among stabilizer-based distillation protocols. Leveraging the general output of the bipartite stabilizer channel
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
, we define the “generalized Fidelity Increase Maximizing (gF-IMAX)” protocol that is applicable for all two-copy input states in prime dimension.

gF-IMAX protocol:

Let
\rho_{AB}
be any bipartite state shared between Alice and Bob with prime dimensions
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d
and let
U_{c}
be the canonical encoding (cf. Ref.
[
31
]
) for two copies of
\rho_{AB}
. The following steps are recurrently applied.

1.

For each stabilizer
S
, all measurement outcomes
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}
and all
k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}
, Alice computes
\mathcal{F}_{k,l}(\sigma^{x,y}_{AB})=\langle\Omega_{k,l}|~\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}~|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle
for the corresponding post-measurement stabilizer channel outputs
\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}
as in (
3.2.1
).

2.

Alice determines the configuration
(S_{max},x_{max},y_{max},k_{max},l_{max})
that maximizes
\mathcal{F}_{k,l}(\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}))
and communicates it to Bob.

3.

Alice and Bob apply the stabilizer channel corresponding to
S_{max}
with the canonical encoding
U_{c}
and determine their outcomes
x,y
. If
x\neq x_{max}
or
y\neq y_{max}
, they declare failure of the protocol.

4.

Alice applies the unitary Weyl-Heisenberg transformation
W_{k_{max},l_{max}}^{\dagger}~(\cdot)~W_{k_{max},l_{max}}
(cf. Def.
1
) locally to her output.

Although both increase the fidelity by using the stabilizer operations with the canonical encoding, there are relevant differences between gF-IMAX and FIMAX. First, unlike FIMAX for Bell-diagonal states, the canonical encoding does not imply a maximal increase of fidelity in each iteration among all stabilizers, encodings, and measurement outcomes. Depending on the input state, some other encoding may achieve better performance. Second, the condition for failure of FIMAX is given by the difference of measurement outcomes
s:=x-y\neq s_{max}
. This is because for Bell-diagonal states, the channel output only depends on the syndrome, i.e., the difference of measurement outcomes
s=x-y
, which does not hold for general input states. gF-IMAX fails unless precisely
x=x_{max}
an
y=y_{max}
, implying a higher probability of failure due to stricter post-selection, and thus a negative impact on the protocol efficiency. On the other hand, to apply FIMAX to non Bell-diagonal states, a twirl to Bell-diagonal form is required, generally inducing additional noise. As this is not the case for gF-IMAX, it may be more efficient for non Bell-diagonal states. This is demonstrated in Figure
2
, where the efficiency of distilling states is shown in dependence on the input fidelity of random pure states. The performance of gF-IMAX exceeds FIMAX for certain low-fidelity regions, while FIMAX is superior for higher fidelities.

4
Conclusion

In this work, we have formulated the stabilizer distillation routine—originally introduced in Ref.
[
30
]
and further developed in Ref.
[
31
]
—as a quantum channel. This perspective provides a unified framework for stabilizer-based methods, extending their application from bipartite entanglement distillation to tripartite secret-key distillation and general input states.

We derived explicit Kraus representations for both bipartite and tripartite stabilizer channels, parameterized by the choice of stabilizer and encoding. Leveraging the shared group structure of stabilizers and Bell states
[
31
]
, we obtained closed-form expressions for the resulting output states. These expressions establish a general framework for stabilizer-based resource distillation: for any information-processing task characterized by a specific measure, one can evaluate that measure on the channel outputs and optimize the stabilizer channel to maximize the resource increase under iterative application. Key examples demonstrated here include the (smooth) coherent information for (one-shot) entanglement distillation and the (smooth) private information for (one-shot) secret-key distillation.

To ensure this optimization remains computationally tractable, we leveraged local invariances within the relevant information measures. Specifically, we demonstrated invariance under changes of stabilizer encodings and, in the tripartite setting, under the choice of the purification system. These invariances significantly reduce the numerical complexity of the optimization, effectively transforming otherwise intractable tasks into feasible numerical optimizations.

We provided several proofs of concept illustrating the versatility of stabilizer channels for resource-state distillation. First, we generalized the FIMAX protocol
[
31
]
to gF-IMAX, which optimizes fidelity for arbitrary input states. Initial numerical tests on random pure states indicate that gF-IMAX can outperform the standard FIMAX protocol in specific regimes. Second, we introduced CI-IMAX and SCI-IMAX to optimize coherent information in the asymptotic and one-shot regimes, respectively. As coherent information lower-bounds the distillable entanglement, these protocols yield stabilizer channels that efficiently generate highly entangled states. Finally, we extended the framework to tripartite secret-key distillation. Noting that maximizing coherent information via CI-IMAX also maximizes the asymptotic private information, we developed the SPI-IMAX protocol to increase the smooth private information. Since this quantity provides lower bounds for one-shot secret-key rates, SPI-IMAX generates states highly optimized for one-shot cryptographic tasks. Numerical results for isotropic input states confirm that these protocols successfully identify effective operations to tailor resourceful states to specific quantum processing tasks.

Several directions for future research naturally follow. A systematic numerical comparison of the proposed protocols, particularly for highly mixed states, would clarify their relative efficiency and distillation effectiveness. Furthermore, identifying bipartite NPT states that are undistillable via stabilizer-based protocols yet still possess private capacity could provide new insights into the distillability problem and the existence of NPT bound entanglement. Additionally, stabilizer-based methods may help uncover PPT states with nonzero distillable secret-key, which would be instrumental in understanding bound entanglement
[
34
]
and the broader separability problem.

In summary, these contributions establish stabilizer channels as a versatile and optimizable tool for quantum resource distillation. By connecting stabilizer operations to the optimization of general quantum resources, this framework provides practical, high-performance protocols for both entanglement and secret-key distillation in one-shot and asymptotic regimes.

5.1
Stabilizer Distillation Routine

First, we present the background of the stabilizer framework developed in Ref.
[
31
]
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{d}\equiv\mathbbm{Z}/d\mathbbm{Z}
be the quotient ring of integers with addition and multiplication modulo
d
, which is a field for prime
d
and let
\omega\equiv\exp(\frac{2\pi i}{d})
. Complex conjugation
(\star)
is defined in the computational basis.

Definition 1

(Weyl(-Heisenberg) operators, Weyl(-Heisenberg) errors)
.

\displaystyle W_{k,l}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{jk}|j\rangle\langle j+l|,~~k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}

(40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\left\{W(e)~|~e=(\vec{k},\vec{l})\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\right\}:=\left\{\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}W_{k_{n},l_{n}}~|~k_{n},l_{n}\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}\right\}

(41)

The Weyl-Heisenberg operators satisfy the Weyl relations, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{k_{1},l_{1}}W_{k_{2},l_{2}}&=\omega^{l_{1}k_{2}}~W_{k_{1}+k_{2},l_{1}+l_{2}},\\
W_{k,l}^{\dagger}&=\omega^{kl}~W_{-k,-l}=W_{k,l}^{-1},\end{aligned}

(42)

implying that the set of Weyl-errors
\mathcal{E}_{N}
forms a group under multiplication, if we identify errors that are equal up to a phase, i.e.,
W_{k_{1},l_{1}}W_{k_{2},l_{2}}\equiv W_{k_{1}+k_{2},l_{1}+l_{2}}
.
E\in\mathcal{E}_{N}
are called error operators. For
E=W(e)
,
e\equiv(\vec{k},\vec{l})\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}
are called error elements. The group structure of
\mathcal{E}_{N}
induces a group structure via the Weyl relations (
42
) for error elements on
\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}
with addition modulo
d
.

Definition 2

(Bell states)
.

\displaystyle|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle

\displaystyle:=(W_{k,l}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle:=(W_{k,l}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle,~~i,k,l\in\mathcal{Z}_{d},

(43)

\displaystyle|\Omega(e)\rangle

\displaystyle:=(W(e)\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes N})|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle^{\otimes N}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}|\Omega_{k_{n},l_{n}}\rangle,~~e\equiv(\vec{k},\vec{l})\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}.

(44)

Definition 3

(Stabilizer group, generating operators, generating elements)
.

A stabilizer
S
is an abelian subgroup of
\mathcal{E}_{N}
. If
\{W(g_{1}),\dots,W(g_{p})\}
forms a minimal generating set of
S
, each
W(g_{j})
is called a generating operator and we write
S=\langle W(g_{1}),\dots,W(g_{p})\rangle
. Each
g_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}
is called a generating element. Given one choice of generating elements, the corresponding subgroup of
\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}
, i.e.,
G_{S}:=\{g\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}~|~W(g)\in S\}
is also denoted as
G_{S}=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{p}\rangle
.

Let
S
be a stabilizer with generating elements
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{p}\}
. For prime
d
, each generator
W(g)\neq\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes N}
has
d
distinct eigenvalues with equal multiplicity
[
31
]
, allowing us to associate each of the
d
eigenspaces of a generator
W(g_{j})
with a number
x_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}
. We write
\mathcal{Q}(x)
for the joint eigenspace of
\{W(g_{j})\}_{j=1}^{p}
, such that for all
|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{Q}(x)
and
j\in\{1,\dots,p\}
,
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p}
and
\mathcal{Q}(x)\subset\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
, we have
W(g_{j})|\phi\rangle=\omega_{x_{j}}|\phi\rangle
with
x_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}
and
\omega_{x_{j}}\in\mathbbm{C}
.

Decomposing the Hilbert space into eigenspaces of the generators,
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\cong\bigoplus_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p}}\mathcal{Q}(x)
, defines the codespaces
\mathcal{Q}(x)
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
. Given
p\leq N
generators of a stabilizer in
\mathcal{E}_{N}
for prime dimensions
d
, the codespaces have dimension
\dim(\mathcal{Q}(x))=d^{N-p}~\forall x\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p}

[
31
]
.
Let
S^{\star}
be the stabilizer with complex conjugated elements of
S
and generating operators
W^{\star}(g_{j})
.
\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(x)\subset\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
is the joint eigenspace of
\{W^{\star}(g_{j})\}_{j=1}^{p}
, such that for all
|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(x)
and
j\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, we have
W^{\star}(g_{j})|\phi\rangle=\omega^{\star}_{x_{j}}|\phi\rangle
.

Definition 4

(Error coset)
.

Let
S\subset\mathcal{E}_{N}
be a stabilizer and let
G_{S}\subset\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}
be the corresponding subgroup of error elements, such that
S=\{W(g)~|~g\in G_{S}\}
.
Given an error element
e\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}
, the error coset is defined as
C(e):=e+G_{S}=\{e+h~|~h\in G_{S}\}
.

Definition 5

(Codeword)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\cong\bigoplus_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p}}\mathcal{Q}(x)
be decomposed into
d^{N-p}
-dimensional codespaces of a stabilizer
S
.
Let
\{|u_{x,k}\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p},~k\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N-p}}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
with
|u_{x,k}\rangle\in\mathcal{Q}(x)
, i.e.,
W(g_{j})|u_{x,k}\rangle=w_{x_{j}}|u_{x,k}\rangle,~\forall k,j
. The vectors
|u_{x,k}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
are called codewords of
S
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
. The codewords of the stabilizer with complex conjugated elements
S^{\star}
in
\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
are denoted by
|u^{\star}_{x,k}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
.

Definition 6

(Encoding)
.

Let
\{|x\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p}}
be the computational basis of
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes p}
and
\{|k\rangle\}_{k\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N-p}}
be the computational basis of
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N-p}
.
A unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\cong\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes p}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N-p}
is an encoding for a stabilizer
S
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
if
~\forall x\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{p},k\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N-p}:~U(|x\rangle\otimes|k\rangle)
is a codeword of
S
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
.

If
U:|x\rangle\otimes|k\rangle\mapsto|u_{x,k}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
is an encoding for
S
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
, then
U^{\star}:|x\rangle\otimes|k\rangle\mapsto|u^{\star}_{x,k}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
is an encoding for
S^{\star}
in
\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
.

Definition 7

(Stabilizer measurement)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{A}(x):\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}(x)
be the projection to the codespace
\mathcal{Q}(x)
and
\mathcal{P}_{B}^{\star}(y):\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(y)
be the projection to the codespace
\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(y)
.
The measurement corresponding to the PVM
\{\mathcal{P}(x,y):=\mathcal{P}_{A}(x)\otimes\mathcal{P}_{B}^{\star}(y)~|~x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}\}
is called stabilizer measurement.

Definition 8

(Symplectic product decomposition)
.

The symplectic product of two error elements,
e=(\vec{k},\vec{l})
and
f=(\vec{m},\vec{n})
is defined as
\langle e,f\rangle:=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}l_{i}m_{i}-k_{i}n_{i}
.
It induces a decomposition of the set of errors according to its values
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{p})
with respect to a stabilizer with generating elements
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{p}\}:\mathcal{E}_{N}\cong\bigoplus_{s}\mathcal{E}(s)
, with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(s):=\{e\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}\times\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{N}~|~\langle g_{j},e\rangle=s_{j}~\forall j=1,\dots,p\}.

(45)

Ref.
[
31
]
demonstrates that the properties of the stabilizer encodings
U
and
U^{\star}
can be leveraged to show that the state after steps 1 - 3 can be written in terms of certain unitary operators, the so-called error action operators
T^{U,e}_{x}
for the error
e
, the encoding
U
, and the local stabilizer measurement outcome

:

Lemma 1

(
[
31
]
)
.

Let
U
be an encoding of a stabilizer
S
with generating elements
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{p}\}
. For each codespace
\mathcal{Q}(x)\subset\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
and for each

with

satisfying

.

Proposition 2

(
[
31

)

Let

and

Let

be a general input state in the Bell basis.
After performing the stabilizer measurements with results

and discarding the first

\displaystyle\sigma^{x,y}_{AB}=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{P}(x,y)}\sum_{e,f\in\mathcal{E}(x-y)}\rho(e,f)~(T_{x}^{U,e}\otimes\mathbbm{1})~|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle\langle\Omega_{0,0}|^{\otimes N-p}~(T_{x}^{U,f}\otimes\mathbbm{1})^{\dagger}.

5.2
Entanglement and Secret-Key Distillation tasks

In this section, we briefly review the tasks of entanglement distillation and secret-key distillation and define the distillable entanglement and distillable key in the asymptotic and one-shot setting.

5.2.1
Entanglement distillation

The goal of entanglement distillation is to (approximately) create maximally entangled states, which we define as follows:

Definition 9

(Maximally entangled state of Schmidt rank
D
)
.

The state

\displaystyle\Psi_{AB}:=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i,j=0}^{D-1}|i\rangle\langle j|_{A}\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|_{B}

is called the maximally entangled state of Schmidt rank
D
.

Using the shared bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
, Alice and Bob apply a protocol
(D,\mathcal{L}_{AB})
, where the Schmidt-rank
D
is the local dimension of the maximally entangled target state and
\mathcal{L}_{AB}
is a LOCC channel as in (
1
), which they apply to
\rho_{AB}
. The entanglement distillation error is given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{E}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{AB},\rho_{AB}):=1-\mathcal{F}(\Psi_{AB},\mathcal{L}_{AB}(\rho_{AB})),

(47)

where
\Psi_{AB}
is the maximally entangled state and
\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma):=\left(\Tr[\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}]\right)^{2}
is the fidelity of two mixed states
[
35
]
.

The distillable entanglement of a quantum state measures how useful it is for entanglement distillation. Contrary to the asymptotic setting, in which infinitely many copies of the input state can be used for the distillation task, in the one-shot setting the LOCC channel corresponding to the protocol can only be used once. In this setting the output of the channel will generally differ by some error from the maximally entangled target state.

Definition 10

(One-shot distillable entanglement)
.

The one-shot distillable entanglement with distillation error
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
of a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(\rho_{AB}):=\sup\limits_{D,\mathcal{L}_{AB}}\{\log_{2}D~:~p_{\mathcal{E}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{AB},\rho_{AB})\leq\varepsilon\},

where
(D,\mathcal{L}_{AB})
is a protocol creating approximately maximally entangled states of size
D
using the LOCC channel
\mathcal{L}_{AB}
.

In the asymptotic setting, Alice and Bob are allowed to use an infinite number of copies of
\rho_{AB}
to create maximally entangled states. If a protocol uses a finite number
n
of copies of
\rho_{AB}
, it is equivalent to a protocol using the input state
\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}
once. This allows defining the distillable entanglement in the asymptotic setting as the maximum of so-called achievable rates for the creation of target states:

Definition 11

(Rate, distillable entanglement of a quantum state)
.

Given a protocol
(n,D,\mathcal{L}_{AB})
for a LOCC channel
\mathcal{L}_{AB}
acting on
n
copies of a state, having
p_{\mathcal{E}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{AB},\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})\leq\varepsilon
, the rate of the protocol is
R(n,D):=\frac{\log_{2}D}{n}
.
Given a state
\rho_{AB}
, a rate is achievable, if for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1],\delta>0
and sufficiently large
n
, there exists a protocol
(n,2^{n(R-\delta)},\mathcal{L}_{AB})
with
p_{\mathcal{E}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{AB},\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})\leq\varepsilon
. The quantity

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{D}(\rho_{AB}):=\sup\{R~:~R\text{ is an achievable rate for }\rho_{AB}\}

is called the distillable entanglement of
\rho_{AB}
.

5.2.2
Secret-key distillation

The goal of secret-key distillation is to (approximately) create tripartite key states. By definition, those states are a resource to establish a cryptographic key between two parties, which is secret from any malicious third party. For this, consider the local measurement channel corresponding to any local system
F
or
G
and composite system
FG
, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}(\cdot):=\sum_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|_{F}~(\cdot)~|i\rangle\langle i|_{F},~~~\mathcal{D}_{FG}(\cdot):=\mathcal{D}_{F}(\cdot)\otimes\mathcal{D}_{G}(\cdot).

(48)

Definition 12

(Secret-key state)
.

A state
\gamma_{ABE}
is a tripartite secret-key state of size
K
if, after application of the measurement channel

\mathcal{D}_{AB}
, the state becomes a maximally correlated state of size
K
in the systems
A
and
B
,

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{AB}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}|i\rangle\langle i|_{A}\otimes|i\rangle\langle i|_{B},

that is product with the state
\sigma_{E}
of the system
E
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{AB}(\gamma_{ABE})\equiv\mathcal{D}_{AB}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E}(\gamma_{ABE})=\hat{\Psi}_{AB}\otimes\sigma_{E}.

To create such states from a given bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
, Alice and Bob use a protocol
(K,\mathcal{L}_{ABE})
, where
K
is the size of the key system and
\mathcal{L}_{ABE}
is a LOPC channel as in (
2
), which they apply to a purification
\phi_{ABE}
of
\rho_{AB}
. The key distillation error is given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{K}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{ABE},\rho_{AB}):=\inf_{\gamma_{ABE}}\left\{1-\mathcal{F}(\gamma_{ABE},\mathcal{L}_{ABE}(\phi_{ABE}))\right\},

(49)

where the optimization is with respect to all tripartite key states
\gamma_{ABE}
.

Given a quantum state, the distillable key of that state quantifies how useful it is for secret-key distillation. We consider both the asymptotic setting of infinite resources (cf., e.g., Refs.
[
14
,
36
]
) and the resource-limited one-shot setting (cf., e.g., Refs.
[
11
,
15
]
). While the former allows infinitely many copies of an input state to be used simultaneously for the channel, in the one-shot setting, the LOPC channel is only used once with a fixed number of input states. In this setting, secret-key states can generally only be approximately created, so we consider protocols with a finite distillation error (
49
).

Definition 13

(One-shot distillable key)
.

The one-shot distillable key with distillation error
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
of a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(\rho_{AB}):=\sup\limits_{K,\mathcal{L}_{ABE}}\{\log_{2}K~:~p_{\mathcal{K}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{ABE},\rho_{AB})\leq\varepsilon\},

where
(K,\mathcal{L}_{ABE})
is a protocol creating approximately secret-key states of size
K
using the LOPC channel
\mathcal{L}_{ABE}
.

In the asymptotic setting, Alice and Bob may use any number of copies of
\rho_{AB}
to produce secret-key states. If a protocol uses
n
copies of
\rho_{AB}
, it is equivalent to a one-shot protocol using the input state
\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}
and we can define accordingly:

Definition 14

(Rate, distillable key of a quantum state)
.

Given a protocol
(n,K,\mathcal{L}_{ABE})
for a LOPC channel
\mathcal{L}_{ABE}
acting on
n
copies of a state, having
p_{\mathcal{K}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{ABE},\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})\leq\varepsilon
, the rate of the protocol is
R(n,K):=\frac{\log_{2}K}{n}
.
Given a state
\rho_{AB}
, a rate is achievable, if for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1],\delta>0
and sufficiently large
n
, there exists a protocol
(n,2^{n(R-\delta)},\mathcal{L}_{ABE})
with
p_{\mathcal{K}}^{err}(\mathcal{L}_{ABE},\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})\leq\varepsilon
. The quantity

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{D}(\rho_{AB}):=\sup\{R~:~R\text{ is an achievable rate for }\rho_{AB}\}

is called the distillable key of
\rho_{AB}
.

5.3
Distillation bounds based on generalized information measures

Given a certain bipartite input state
\rho_{AB}
, it is generally not clear if there exists a LOCC channel that transforms it to an approximately maximally entangled state. The same holds for LOPC channels to create an approximately secret-key state. However, there exist lower bounds on the (one-shot distillable entanglement and the (one-shot) distillable secret-key. These bounds are based on information measures that can be defined via generalized divergences (see Ref.
[
2
]
for a comprehensive summary). In the one-shot setting, these are “smoothed”, i.e., the environment around a given quantum state for which the measure is to be evaluated is taken into account. Often, the sine distance
[
2
]
is used to define this smoothing environment.

Definition 15

(Smoothing environment)
.

Using the sine distance
P(\rho,\sigma):=\sqrt{1-\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma)}
, the smoothing environment
B^{\varepsilon}(\rho)
around a state
\rho
is defined as

\displaystyle B^{\varepsilon}(\rho):=\left\{\tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):P(\rho,\tilde{\rho})\leq\varepsilon\right\}=\left\{\tilde{\rho}:\mathcal{F}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})\geq 1-\varepsilon^{2}\right\}.

(50)

Specifically, we consider the following generalized divergences and related information quantities that can be used to bound the distillable secret-key from below.

Definition 16

(Quantum relative entropy, quantum mutual information, coherent information
[
37
,
38
]
)
.

\displaystyle D(\rho||\sigma):=\begin{cases}\Tr[\rho(\log_{2}\rho-\log_{2}\sigma)]\text{ if supp}(\rho)\subseteq\text{supp}(\sigma)\\
+\infty~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\text{ else}\end{cases}

(51)

\displaystyle I(\rho_{AB}):=D(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})

(52)

\displaystyle I^{A\rangle B}_{c}(\rho_{AB}):=D(\rho_{AB}||\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})

(53)

Definition 17

(Quantum hypothesis testing relative entropy, hypothesis testing mutual information
[
39
,
40
,
13
]
)
.

\displaystyle D_{H}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma)

\displaystyle:=-\log_{2}\left(\inf_{\Lambda}\left\{\Tr[\Lambda\sigma]:0\leq\Lambda\leq\mathbbm{1},\Tr[\Lambda\rho]\geq 1-\varepsilon\right\}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle I^{\varepsilon}_{H}(\rho_{AB})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\sigma_{B}}D^{\varepsilon}_{H}(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}),

(55)

where the optimization is over all quantum states
\sigma_{B}
.

Definition 18

(Max-relative entropy, smooth max-mutual information
[
41
]
, smooth conditional max-entropy
[
12
]
)
.

\displaystyle D_{max}(\rho||\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\log_{2}\left\|\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\text{ if supp}(\rho)\subseteq\text{supp}(\sigma)\\
+\infty~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\text{ else}\end{cases}

(56)

\displaystyle I_{max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\tilde{\rho}_{AB}\in B^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})}D_{max}(\tilde{\rho}_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\tilde{\rho}_{B}),

(57)

\displaystyle H^{\varepsilon}_{max}(\rho_{AB})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\rho}_{AE}\in B^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AE})\\
\sigma_{E}\end{subarray}}D_{max}(\tilde{\rho}_{AE}||\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{E})

(58)

where
\sigma_{E}
is a quantum state,
\rho_{AE}:=\Tr_{B}[\phi_{ABE}]
is defined via the purification
\phi_{ABE}
of
\rho_{AB}
and
B^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})
is as in Def.
15
.

Note, that the definition of
H^{\varepsilon}_{max}
of Def.
18
is based on the smooth conditional min-entropy
H^{\varepsilon}_{min}
and the duality
H^{\varepsilon}_{max}(\rho_{AB})=-H^{\varepsilon}_{min}(\rho_{AE})
. Consult Refs.
[
2
]
and
[
11
]
for more details.

We are now in a position to state the lower bounds on the one-shot and asymptotic distillable entanglement and distillable key, motivating the information quantities under consideration in this work.

Theorem 3

(Lower bound for the one-shot distillable entanglement
[
2
,
16
]
)
.

Let
\rho_{AB}
be a bipartite state. Let
\mathcal{L}
be any LOCC channel with output
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{AB})=\hat{\rho}_{AB}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, and
\eta\in[0,\sqrt{\varepsilon}]
. It holds that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{D}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})\geq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{L}\end{subarray}}\left(-H^{\sqrt{\varepsilon}-\eta}_{max}(\hat{\rho}_{AB})+4\log_{2}(\eta)\right).

(59)

Theorem 4

(Characterization of the distillable entanglement
[
14
,
2
]
)
.

Let
\rho_{AB}
be a bipartite state. Let
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
be any LOCC channel acting on
n
copies of
\rho_{AB}
with output
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}(\rho^{\otimes n}_{AB})=\hat{\rho}_{AB}
. It holds that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{D}(\rho_{AB})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\mathcal{L}^{(n)}}I^{A\rangle B}_{c}(\hat{\rho}_{AB}).

(60)

For secret-key distillation, the bounds are based on so-called classical-quantum-quantum states, having the form
\chi_{ABE}=\sum_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|_{A}\otimes\chi_{BE}^{i}
.

Theorem 5

(Lower bound for the one-shot distillable key
[
2
,
15
]
)
.

Let
\rho_{AB}
be a bipartite state with purification
\phi_{ABE}
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
\mathcal{L}
be any LOPC channel with classical-quantum-quantum output
\mathcal{L}(\phi_{ABE})=\chi_{ABE}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
. It holds that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{D}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB})\geq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{L},\\
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon^{\prime}),\\
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon^{\prime}-\delta),\\
\zeta\in(0,\delta)\end{subarray}}\left(I_{H}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}-\delta-\eta}(\chi_{AB})-I_{max}^{\delta-\zeta}(\chi_{AE})-\log_{2}(\frac{4(\varepsilon^{\prime}-\delta)}{\eta^{2}})-\log_{2}(\frac{2}{\zeta^{2}})\right).

(61)

Theorem 6

(Characterization of the distillable key
[
14
,
2
]
)
.

Let
\rho_{AB}
be a bipartite state with purification
\phi_{ABE}
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
be any LOPC channel acting on
n
copies of
\phi_{ABE}
with classical-quantum-quantum output
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}(\phi^{\otimes n}_{ABE})=\chi_{ABE}
. It holds that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{D}(\rho_{AB})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\mathcal{L}^{(n)}}\left(I(\chi_{AB})-I(\chi_{AE})\right).

(62)

The quantity
I_{p}(\chi_{ABE}):=I(\chi_{AB})-I(\chi_{AE})
is also called private information.
