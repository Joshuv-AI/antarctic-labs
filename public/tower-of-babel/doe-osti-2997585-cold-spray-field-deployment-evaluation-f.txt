 1.0 INTRODUCTION

 This report highlights the documentation that supported Cold Spray field deployment in double-
 shell tank (DST) 241-AN-105 (AN-105) in July of 2025. This work was a collaborative effort
 between VRC Metal Systems (VRC), Robotic Technologies of Tennessee (RTT), the Hanford
 Tank Waste Operation and Closure (H2C) Chief Technology Office, H2C Tank Farm Projects
 Engineering, H2C Tank and Pipeline Integrity (TAPI) and H2C Ultrasonic Testing (UT)
 Operations & Support.

 2.0 BACKGROUND

 Since 2019, the Chief Technology Office has been developing Cold Spray technology for
 refurbishing Hanford’s DSTs (RPP-RPT-65015). This technology is particularly relevant for
 addressing localized corrosion within the annulus of the DSTs by propelling metal powder
 particles at supersonic speeds to targeted areas. This process results in high-quality coatings
 characterized by high bond strength and low porosity result. Upon the technology’s development
 completion in 2024, the project transitioned from the Chief Technology Office to Tank Farms
 Project Engineering, tasked with field deployment. The Ultrasonic Testing Operations and
 Support group was involved due to their extensive experience in deploying similar robotic
 systems into the DST annuli. Given the technical complexity, VRC and RTT vendor support was
 retained for Cold Spray and robotic operations, respectively.

 Tank AN-105 was selected as the first tank for Cold Spray application due to several key criteria.
 First, the tank integrated with on-going Tank Farm operations, as it was one of three tanks
 chosen for Ultrasonic Testing (UT) examination in Fiscal Year 2025. This providing a cost-
 saving as it leveraged access to the tank annulus. Second, access to Tank AN-105 had no
 projected impact on other mission activities (e.g., there were no planned transfer activities with
 the AN Tank Farm, no impact on the Tank Side Cesium Removal system, and no impact on the
 evaporator). Last, Tank AN-105 had approximately a 30% material loss in a confined region of
 the exterior secondary liner due to localized corrosion. This degradation warranted an action
 level, justifying the Cold Spray application.

 3.0 FIELD ACTIVITIES

 This section provides a comprehensive overview of the activities, events, and challenges that
 took place during the week of Cold Spray deployment at the AN Tank Farm.

         July 14, 2025
 Vendors conducted a dry run of the Cold Spray equipment at the Independent Tank Integrity
 Verification Specialists, LLC (ITIVS) facility prior to site transport. During this time, RTT
 discovered damage to one of the operator control boxes. After switching out the operator control
 box, robotic systems functioned properly. Next, the Cold Spray equipment was attached to the
 floor crawler robot and hoist rigging tests were performed. This testing identified the specific

 rigging steps necessary for eliminating any potential snag. Once these tests were complete, the
 floor crawler was transported to the AN tank farm via H2C personnel.

 While still at ITIVS, RTT further pursued diagnosing the damage to the operator control box.
 Doing so resulted in an electrical short occurring in the control box which rippled through the
 injector box and the wall crawler robot used for testing. RTT was able to replace the wall crawler
 control board, but required a board be expedited overnight for the injector box. Ultimately, this

         July 15, 2025
 RTT changed out the injector box board and verified that the operator control box and wall
 crawler robot were functioning as planned. RTT transported the updated injector box and
 operator control unit to the AN tank farm and connected all Cold Spray system components. A
 pre-witness coupon was generated inside the AN Tank Farm. During the coupon deposition, it
 was identified that the powder feed gas flows was outside of the predefined process parameters.
 This was due to a supply pressure regulator effect, which was quickly/easily corrected for by
 adjusting the pressure regulator on the Raptor Control box. During the pre-witness coupon spray,
 the RTT operator encountered a problem with the video converter in the operator control box.
 The tablet would not connect to the video converter as tested in the dry run at ITIVS’s shop. The
 operator plugged his laptop into the converter directly to obtain video from the cameras onboard
 the crawler during the pre-witness coupon spray.

         July 16, 2025
 A second pre-witness coupon was sprayed inside the AN Tank Farm. During the pre-witness
 spray, the floor crawler’s video converter in the operator control unit was not functioning
 properly. RTT elected not to swap in the spare video converter since it involved opening the
 operator control unit electronics enclosure. The lack of video feedback did not impede the
 crawler’s function. The crawler was functioning properly in the absence of the video feedback.
 After the coupon was surveyed out of the Tank Farm, ITIVS performed UT inspection on it,
 verifying effective bonding between the Cold Spray patch and metal substrate. With the second
 witness coupon complete, the UT Operations & Support group proceeded to deploy the Cold
 Spray floor crawler into the Tank AN-105 annulus with the magnet in the lifted position. The
 crawler was almost transported to the bottom of the annulus when it was attempted to be rotated
 180 degrees to face the correct direction for Cold Spray application. During this rotation, the
 floor crawler inadvertently became magnetically attached to the primary wall, as there was still a
 small amount of attraction to the steel surface. This required the robotic operator to drive the
 crawler as far up the tank wall as possible. At that point, the UT Operations & Support crew
 vigorously pulled the lifting cable and bridle to release the crawler. Once free, the crawler was
 repositioned in the correct orientation prior to being relowered onto the annulus floor.

 The deployment then proceeded to align the Cold Spray crawler into the appropriate position on
 the floor by using visual tank features that corresponded with prior known UT data analysis. An
 H2C video crawler with laser beam capability aided in the alignment of the Cold Spray crawler.
 Once in position, the team initiated the Cold Spray process. Powder flow was immediately
 observed on the overwatch cameras; however, no cleaning was occurring on the annulus floor.

 The process was halted after the second pass to evaluate the issue. It was found that the nozzle
 shutter was nonfunctional. Further investigation revealed that a piece of unreinforced clear
 tubing located within RTT’s injector box had ruptured and air pressure was not being delivered
 to the crawler. This airline was replaced, and the shutter system functioned properly again.

 First, four grit blast cleaning passes were made over a 7-inch by 16-inch area. Next, the Cold
 Spray process was initiated. The crawler proceeded to apply 22 of the 26 anticipated layers of
 410L stainless steel. On the 22nd layer the raster had a brief pause midway through the layer. At
 the end of the 22nd layer, the raster stopped completely. The spray process was halted, and the
 crawler was retrieved out of the annulus. Once out of the tank annulus, the crawler was tested,
 and the raster was functioning properly. It was determined that the microcontroller had reset due
 to an over temperature condition. The crawler was allowed to cool overnight.

         July 17, 2025
 Preparation/pre-deployment included a functional test of the floor crawler and replenishment of
 powder feeders to ensure sufficient material for both patch and post-witness coupon applications.
 The crawler’s deployment into the Tank AN-105 annulus was seamless and built upon
 experience from the day prior’s deployment. The floor crawler was accurately repositioned over
 the existing 7-inch x 16-inch patch utilizing an existing robot equipped with a camera and laser
 beam. An additional 0.02 inches of 410L stainless steel was applied to the existing patch
 following surface preparation with four passes of ceramic blasting media. (Final Cold Spray
 patch thickness was estimated around 0.063.) A post-witness coupon was sprayed immediately
 after retrieval of the unit from the tank annulus. After the coupon was surveyed out of the Tank
 Farm, ITIVS performed UT inspection on it, verifying effective bonding between the Cold Spray
 patch and metal substrate. Application of the Cold Spray patch was completed this morning,
 prior to 11 am.

 Not long after the Cold Spray crawler was released out of the AN Tank Farm, a H2C site-wide
 safety stand was implemented. This resulted in some Cold Spray equipment remaining in the AN
 Tank Farm for a couple additional weeks.

 5.0 SUMMARY

 After extensive development and testing, Cold Spray application was successfully executed
 within the DST annulus of Tank AN-105. With the H2C field crew working alongside RTT and
 VRC vendors, the robotic Cold Spray crawler was deployed onto the annulus floor and navigated
 to a designated location below Riser 25 where localized corrosion had been previously identified.
 A Cold Spray patch was then remotely applied. The patch was qualified through UT examination
 and destructive analysis of pre- and post-witness coupons. These results support the continued
 application of this technology in Hanford’s DSTs.

 1.0 INTRODUCTION

 Testing identified the function of the crawler and the reaction of the substrate in response to
 substrate thickness. Three robotic crawler tests were performed, as outlined in the Robotic
 Technology of Tennessee (RTT) Robotic Cold Spray Field Deployment Test Plan Rev 1 (89760-
 001-SUB-002-001). A VRC Metal Systems (VRC) study with comparable materials has shown
 that cold spray can be applied to very thin materials.

 2.0 ROBOTIC TESTING

 2.1 WHEEL CONTACT

 This test evaluated the minimum substrate thickness allowable for contact force generated by the
 wall crawler. The wall crawler test is bounding for the floor crawler due to its larger magnetic
 pull force and wheelbase. For this, RTT created a test apparatus to fixture various thicknesses of
 carbon steel. First, a wheel from the crawler was pressed on to the fixture to measure the
 deformation (or failure if applicable) of the substrate steel. (See Figure 1.) The force applied to
 the wheel was measured and adjusted to match the maximum force provided by the suspended
 magnet in the crawler chassis. The force applied was 200 pounds on the single wheel contact
 point. The range of thickness substrates varied between 0.187 inches to 0.06 inches. The
 substrate and fixture were constructed to simulate the wheel driving over an 8 inch by 8-inch
 area of thinned material.

 2.2 MINIUM PLATE THICKNESS OVER ENTIRE CRAWLER AREA

 Testing was conducted on A36 steel that had 36 ksi tensile strength, which was more
 conservative than the test plan. Testing measured the deflection/deformation and/or crawler
 operability on the substrate due to the force exerted by the suspended magnet on thinner material.
 In this test, the wall crawler was positioned on a test plate and its function tested as well as the
 deflection/deformation of the test plate monitored. The test plate was swapped for various
 thicknesses to cover the desired ranges. The wall crawler was tested on 0.25-, 0.187- and 0.125-
 inch thickness material.

 3

RPP-RPT-66087 Rev.00                              9/30/2025 - 7:28 AM                                            20 of 199

 For this test, the plate was flat, which is different than the in-field condition of a curved wall in
 the case of the wall crawler. To account for this difference, the magnet standoff to the test
 material was adjusted to match the minimum standoff expected on the curved wall (0.125” –
 0.25”). This tested the substrate thickness more conservatively than field conditions since the
 entirety of the magnet will be at the standoff height from the substrate as opposed to just the
 center as is the case in service.

 2.2.1 Minium Plate Thickness Over Entire Crawler Area Results

 The wall crawler’s maneuverability was measured for the three different plate thicknesses with a
 magnet standoff of 0.125 inch or 0.25 inch. As for the 0.125 inch magnet standoff, the 0.125 inch
 and 0.187 inch plates deflected to the point that contact was made with the magnet, restricting
 the crawler motion. The 0.125 inch magnet standoff did not restrict the crawler’s motion on the
 0.25 inch plate. As for the 0.25 inch magnet standoff, the 0.125 inch plate deflected to the point
 that contact was made with the magnet, restricting the crawler’s motion. The 0.25 inch magnet
 standoff did not restrict the crawler’s motion on the 0.187 inch or 0.25 inch plate. See Figure 5
 and Figure 6.

 2.3 MINIMUM PLATE THICKNESS UNDER THE SUSPENDED MAGNET

 This test simulated the operation of the wall crawler magnet over 0.0625-, 0.125-, 0.1875- and
 0.25-inch thicknesses material to qualify the wall crawler’s capability to remain attached to the
 tank liner when subjected to thinner areas. (Testing was conducted on A36 steel that had 36 ksi
 tensile strength, which is more conservative than the test plan requirement.) The wheels of the
 crawler were supported on a frame as the suspended magnet passes over a test coupon to
 simulate a thinned area underneath the magnet. The fixture had interchangeable test coupons that
 were placed in from underneath of the fixture. The coupon was the primary surface for the
 magnet to attract. The frame was oriented in a vertical position to simulate a tank wall. The
 pulling force of the crawler in this orientation was recorded to determine a profile of
 traction/payload limits based on wall thickness. The test fixture was oriented vertically, and the
 crawler platform was pulled towards the ground. (See Figure 7.) This force was documented for
 the thickness of the substrates chosen for testing.

 Figure 7. Test Fixture for Minium Plate Thickness Under Suspended Magnet.

 2.3.1 Minimum Plate Thickness Under the Suspended Magnet Results

 Maximum pull load was measured for the four different plate thicknesses with a magnet standoff

 3.0 COLD SPRAY TESTING

 VRC has performed studies previously that have shown cold spray can be applied to very thin
 material. These studies were performed on DOE SBIR Award DE-SC0017229. This tested
 nickel-chromium-carbide cold spray coatings on 1018 carbon steel substrate in thin wall
 configuration. The testing setup is shown in Figure 10. The setup consisted of a securement
 frame with a 3 inch x 3 inch window in the center where a sheet of 1018 carbon steel shim stock
 can be freely suspended, creating a large, thin section to perform cold spray coating on. The cold
 spray applicator used was a custom right-angle assembly with a miniaturized nozzle specifically
 designed for steam generator applications in commercial nuclear powerplants. The test consisted
 of applying a surface preparation process using 100% chromium carbide material in 2 passes,
 followed by a coating application process using 50% chromium carbide / 50% nickel powder
 blend in 3 passes. The materials used in the testing are provided in Table 3. The process
 parameters used for surface preparation and cold spray coating are provided in Table 4.
 Evaluation was performed post prep and post spray to determine the extent of substrate damage.

 The thin wall cold spray test results are shown in Figure 11.

 The 0.020 inch thick shim stock (Figure 11a) received 0.017 inch of coating thickness and
 showed no change to the substate from prep and spray processes. The substrate remained flat and
 undeformed, and no perforation was observed.

 The 0.010 inch thick shim stock (Figure 11b) received 0.014 inch of coating thickness and
 showed no change from the prep process, however the spray process induced some deformation
 of the substrate. The cold spray coating applied enough compressive stress to the substrate to
 cause a slight bowing. No wrinkling or perforation was observed.

 The 0.004 inch thick shim stock (Figure 11c) received roughly 0.020 inch of coating, however
 measurement accuracy is questionable due to the presence of wrinkles in the substrate. Substrate
 deformation was noted after surface preparation, indicating plastic deformation of the substrate
 from the particle impact. Surface coating produced significant deformation of the surface,
 including the formation of substrate wrinkles from the compressive stress in the coating that,
 when reacted by the substrate, caused buckling in the thin substrate cross-section.

 The 0.002 inch thick shim stock (Figure 11d) received a coating, however measurement of
 coating thickness was not possible due to extreme substrate deformation. The substrate exhibited
 very extreme deformation after surface preparation from the high velocity particle impact. After
 coating, the substrate formed excessive deformation and wrinkles from substrate buckling, and
 exhibited perforation in one area, indicated by the white arrow in Figure 11d.

 The 0.001 inch thick shim stock (Figure 11e) received only surface preparation and fully
 perforated during the surface preparation process. Perforation extended across the entire window
 area. The shim stock appeared to tear from the high velocity particles and gas impingement.

 Based on the results from VRC’s previous study (DE-SC0017229), the team concluded that the
 minimum thickness for a cold spray coating with no substrate deformation would be 0.020 inch.
 If substrate deformation is acceptable, coatings can be applied down to 0.004 inch, though a
 more conservative limit would be 0.010 inch. Substrate perforation occurs below 0.004 inch, and
 coating on these surfaces should be avoided. A caveat to the previous study is the ideal geometry
 of the thin wall section, being a uniform thickness across a large area, is unlikely to exist in
 practice. It is more likely to encounter corrosion or wear damage that is more severe only in
 small areas, causing highly localized, thin portions. This would provide the thin section much
 more support and may change the outcome of the previous study by reducing deformation in the
 thin sections.

 Another finding from the results is substrate deformation seems to occur when the substrate
 strength is overcome by the coating residual stress. All coatings in the previous study were
 0.014-0.020 inches thick, and even from the limited trials one can observe that when the
 substrate thickness is greater than the coating thickness, no substrate deformation occurs,
 however when it is thinner than the coating, substrate deformation begins to occur and
 progresses with reduction in thickness. Therefore, it is important to balance the relative load-
 bearing capacities of the coating and substrate. Consideration should be given to the maximum
 coating thickness that can be applied while avoiding substrate deformation as a function of
 substrate thickness in the area of concern.

 The previous work described above is a comparable process condition with the planned
 application of 410L/B170 powder on Hanford’s double-shell tank substrate (e.g., A516 or
 TC128). Measured particle velocities of the previous work (~633 m/s) compared to the present
 application on Hanford’s double shell tank (~686 m/s) are similar, with the former being slightly
 lower velocity. The apparent density of the previous powder (Ni/CRC: ~4.26 g/cc) and the
 present application (410L/B170: ~3.08 g/cc) is significantly different, with the former being
 much higher density. This would make the impact momentum (mass x velocity) and energy
 (mass x velocity2) significantly higher for the Ni/CRC material than for the 410L/B170 material.
 Based on this, the team predicts the 410L/B170 used in the present application will cause less
 severe substrate damage than the Ni/CRC material described above. Therefore, the previous
 studies conduced by VRC present a bounding case for substrate damage, and any wall
 thicknesses greater than 0.020 inch will not be deformed or perforated by the cold spray
 application.

 4.0 SUMMARY

 Robotic maneuverability and magnetic deformation of the wall/floor substrate is presumed to be
 the limiting factor to cold spray application within Hanford’s double-shell tanks. When testing
 the minimum substrate thickness allowable for contact force generated by the crawler’s wheel,
 results showed that no permeant deflection is anticipated with an 8 inch by 8 inch substrate area
 thickness of 0.125 inch to 0.187 inch, however, a 0.06 inch substrate thickness showed
 permanent deflection of 0.014 ± 0.002 inch. The method for testing wheel force deflection
 should be considered conservative to that of real-world application, as tank wall/floor thinning is
 localized and not a consistent value over large areas.

 11

RPP-RPT-66087 Rev.00                            9/30/2025 - 7:28 AM                                           28 of 199

 Concerning the wall crawler’s maneuverability on a simulated curved surface, the expected
 magnetic standoff of 0.125 inch to 0.25 inch was tested. As for the 0.125 inch magnetic standoff,
 the substrate thickness of 0.125 inch and 0.187 inch restricted the crawler’s motion, while the
 0.25 inch substrate thickness did not. As for the 0.25 inch magnetic standoff, the substrate
 thickness of 0.125 inch restricted the crawler’s motion, while the 0.187 inch and 0.25 inch
 substrate thickness did not. The method for testing wall crawler maneuverability should be
 considered conservative to that of real-world application, as a) the tank wall/floor thinning is
 localized and not a consistent value over large areas; b) the A36 steel substrate used in testing
 had a lower tensile strength (36 ksi) than the anticipated A537 carbon steel tank substrate (65-90
 ksi); and c) the magnetic standoff tested was consistent across the substrate, which it would not
 be with a curved tank wall surface.

 In testing the minimum substrate thickness allowable for retaining the magnetic crawler to the
 substrate, tests were performed with substrate thicknesses of 0.06 inch to 0.25 inch. The
 maximum pull force was increased to the point of crawler removal. For all substrate thicknesses
 tested, the wall crawler contained enough magnetic force to maneuver with a minimal payload of
 33 pounds (which accounts for the heater and spray nozzle apparatus).

 Last, VRC has performed studies that have shown cold spray can be applied to very thin
 materials. Provided that the nominal Hanford double-shell tank wall/floor thickness is
 conservatively 0.50 inch, 70% wall loss would result in 0.150 inch tank thickness remaining.
 This remaining substrate thickness provides at least an order or magnitude of margin before the
 cold spray process would negatively affect the tank structure via deformation or perforation, with
 deformation anticipated at thicknesses between 0.004 to 0.020 inch and perforation anticipated
         below 0.004 inch.

 The results show that the plate frontside temperature should be within 0.3 K of the backside
 temperature, or about 180.3 °C. This is a result of the convection heat transfer limiting the energy
 flow in the thermal resistance chain. Conduction energy transfer through the plate is at least 1 order

 temperature being very similar to the measured plate backside temperature.

 5.4.3. Cold Spray Coating Generation

 A pre witness coupons was first attempted on the witness coupons cart. During the deposition, low
 powder feed gas flow was observed, stabilizing around 70 slpm for a 90 slpm setpoint. This is
 generally indicative of the powder feeder pressure being too low to drive enough flow to achieve
 the process condition. Further investigation revealed that the powder feed pressure regulator was
 showing approximately 950 psi. To correct the issue, the powder feed pressure regulator was
 adjusted back up to 1100 psi to provide full pressure capability to the system. The result is
 attributed to the supply pressure effect of the regulator, which in this particular case is 14.5%,
 meaning the outlet pressure of the regulator swings 14.5% of the input pressure swing, in the
 opposite direction (i.e. higher inlet pressure causes lower outlet pressure), due to the force balance
 on the regulator poppet. The system was qualified with regulated inlet pressure of roughly 1400
 psi, and in the field we were directly connected to bottles at approximately 2500 psi; a difference
 of 1100 psi, which should cause a reduction in the outlet pressure of approximately 160 psi, which
 explains the observed lower pressure reading of 950 psi (reduced from an 1100 psi setpoint).

 even in the very high curvature region of the substrate. The reason for this result is a very rough
 substrate surface that was present before cold spray application. The very rough surface is a result
 of sample selection and acquisition, which attempted to be as relevant as possible to the existing
 surface condition in the tank annulus. To achieve this, the samples were acquired from DNV,
 having the same composition and surface topography of the expected surfaces in the annulus. Yet
 despite the very rough surface, the coating conformally coated the sample and was fully dense.
 Therefore, the witness coupons meet all set acceptance criteria of the qualified coating system for
 the Hanford tank application. To verify that the coating generated within the annulus matches the
 performance of the witness coupons, post spray data analysis was performed.

 7. Conclusions and Recommendations

 The FY25 work focused on the deployment of cold spray in AN-105. The team established a
 technical evaluation and test plan, executed developments and testing per the testing plan,
 developed and published training manuals and work instructions for field deployment, traveled to
 Hanford for training and field deployment, mobilized cold spray to the Hanford tank farm site,
 worked with the field work crews to stage and deploy cold spray within AN-105, completed testing
 and verification of the cold spray application, and successfully demobilized from site. The work
 was highly successful, with the cold spray team meeting all set technical objectives. The cold spray
 patch applied to AN-105 annulus floor was verified to have increased the wall thickness in the
 areas of concern, and was validated to meet all acceptance criteria for performance according to
 the qualified procedures. The team achieved all of their goals and deployed cold spray for the first
 time in an active tank annulus at the Hanford site; a significant achievement.

 Moving forward, the team makes the following recommendations for future development and
 improvement:

 x Robotic system ruggedization and integration with cold spray systems. The system shall
 have a single-interface control and be able to be more accurately positioned in the annulus.
 The motion system shall be ruggedized to temperature and radiation factors present in the
 annulus.

 System Overview
 The HMMR-T system is a mobile magnetic crawler unit for carrying various tooling associated with performing in-
 field double shell tank repair operations. The primary system is composed of five major components as shown in
 Figure 1. The components are:
 1. HMMR-T Base Crawler Unit
 2. Remote Operator Control Unit (the smaller black case)
 3. Power Injector Unit (the larger black case)
 4. Tether (100 ft base length)
 5. Industrial Ethernet Cable (200 ft extension)

 Figure 1: System Overview

 The HMMR-T prime mover is a four-wheeled, four-wheel drive, skid-steer unit with a suspended permanent
 magnet in the chassis. This design supports the magnet away from the climbing surface and transmits the
 adhering (normal) force to the wheels of the crawler through the chassis of the system. Figure 2 shows a CAD
 representation of the HMMR-T crawler platform with the primary crawler components labeled. The electronics are
 placed in the middle of the crawler. The placement of the electronics provides for shielding from the large magnet
 assembly to minimize any adverse effects on the motor systems. Below the electronics enclosure is the suspended
 magnet assembly.

 be edited after selection by rotating the dial, and confirmed by a long-click. Following is a description of the
 available controls, from top to bottom:

 At the top are feedback icons for the encoder and the heading. The user may interact with these by clicking on
 them. The encoder feedback icon displays the distance driven by the crawler measured in inches, and clicking on
 it will zero the encoder. The heading feedback icon displays the current angular position of the crawler measured
 in degrees; if the user clicks on it, the icon should turn green indicating that the heading lock function has been
 enabled. While heading lock is enabled, the crawler will maintain its angular position. To disable the heading lock
 function, the user shall click the icon again, and it should turn gray.

 Moving down one row, we find the air solenoid control icons. These may be selected and long-clicked. The leftmost
 icon says either “Magnet UP” or “Magnet DOWN,” indicating the current state of the pneumatic magnet lift. On the
 right is a button labeled “Tool Air”. When it is colored green, that indicated the flow of air is enabled, while gray
 indicates the air flow is disabled.

 One row lower, we find 3 numeric entry fields for controlling the weave parameters. The center field is the weave
 center control. When stationary, this field simply sets the location of the weaver mounted tool, but while weaving
 side to side, this field sets the center of the weave. The left and right fields allow the user to set a delay time on
 either side of the weave.

 Moving one row down, we find the main weave control cluster. On the left is the weaver enable button; clicking
 this will toggle the left/right motion of the weaver. In the center is the weave width numeric entry field; this is where
 the user may control the side-to-side travel of the weaver mounted tool. On the right is the weaver speed entry
 field; this is where the user may set the speed in inches/second at which the weaver mounted tool will move side-
 to-side.

 One row further down, we find the raster control cluster. These 3 icons pertain to the forward/backward movement
 of the crawler. On the left is the raster enable button. In conjunction with the autodrive switch (the physical rocker
 switch), this is used to start/stop the crawler from automatically driving forward a set distance, then reversing back
 to where it began. In the center is the raster length numeric control. This is where the user can set the desired
 forward/reverse distance in inches. On the right is a drift compensation entry field, which should be used if the
 crawler is expected to drift (e.g. down a wall due to gravity).

 On the bottom row there is a numeric entry field used to set the crawler travel speed during any kind of autodrive,
 including when rastering is enabled. To the right is an icon that may be clicked to toggle the status of the on-board
 lighting system; this icon should change colors when clicked to indicate the on/off status of the lights. On the right
 is the camera selector button. When this button is clicked, a pop-up menu should appear, which the user may
 scroll through to select (by clicking) one of the available camera feeds.

 Quick-Start Instructions
 STARTUP SEQUENCE:
 1. With E-Stop in the remote operator control unit in the “OFF” position (pressed down), plug the crawler
 tether into the crawler and into the control unit. The connectors on the tether only allow for connection in
 the appropriate locations.

 2. Twist the red E-stop button clockwise until it pops up, closing the power circuit, providing power to the
 crawler and the control box. This will begin the process of the LCD panel booting up.

 4. On the tablet in the remote operator control unit, open the Visual Interface application if it does not auto
 start. This is used to display the camera feedback from the robot.

 5. Press and hold the green button for 1 second to enable driving control of the crawler. Its light should turn
 on. This is the drive enable button for the crawler. The joystick will not function without the green enable
 button being activated.

 RUNNING WITH THE EXTENSION SYSTEM:
 1. With the remote operator control unit E-Stop in the “OFF” position, connect both the injector box and the
 operator control unit to AC power.

 2. Plug the crawler tether into the crawler, and the other end into the injector box. Ensure that the air tubing
 is connected on both ends of the tether.

 3. Plug the extension cable into the “MAIN BOX” port on the injector box, and the other end into the
 “INJECTOR BOX” port on the operator control unit.

 4. Enable power throughout the system by twisting the remote operator control unit E-Stop until it pops up.
 The system may now be further configured as in the regular startup sequence.

 BASIC CRAWLER CONTROL:
 1. Motion of the crawler is controlled by the joystick in the controls case. Use the joystick to manually drive
 the crawler. The joystick is proportional for controlling travel and turning speeds.

 2. To disable crawler motion at any time, without disabling the rest of the system, press the green button. Its
 light should turn off.

 SHUTDOWN SEQUENCE:
 1. Press the green button to disable motion of the crawler.

 2. Press the red E-Stop button on the controls case. This disconnects the battery from the rest of the system.
 The tablet will auto shutdown within 5 minutes of the power being shut off to the control box.

 PNEUMATIC MAGNET CONTROL:
 1. To stow the magnet bar in the “release” position, apply air pressure to the yellow air line in the main tether.
 Approximately 110 psi will be required.

 Special Considerations for Floor Crawler
 Because the floor crawler and wall crawler are very similar, it was considered practical for them to share a manual.
 However, there are a few differences worth pointing out:

 a. The heading feedback and heading lock rely on measuring the pull-vector of the earth’s gravity. Therefore,
 these features will not work when either crawler drives on a horizontal surface. In particular, the floor
 crawler is not intended to be used on any surface other than a horizontal one.
 b. While the wall crawler is intended to be deployed using specialized equipment (a deployment tray), the
 floor crawler is intended to be hoisted up and down directly. At any time when the crawler is raised or
 lowered, the magnet bar must be retracted. Only when the crawler is solidly upright on the intended
 surface should the magnet be deployed. On the other hand, the magnet bar must be re-retracted before
 attempting to hoist the crawler back up.
 c. In the case of a cold spray mission, the crawler should be lowered such that the cold spray heater contacts
 the ground first, not the nozzle. This will protect the delicate nozzle from collision.
 d. The cold spray heater attaches differently than on the wall crawler. It is mounted on an adapter plate on
 top of the floor crawler. This plate has four ¼” shoulder screws, which are received into key slots on the
 heater shroud. The heater shroud has a spring loaded pin with a pull ring on the right hand side; this pin
 drops into a hole in the crawler adapter plate to prevent the heater from sliding off of the shoulder screws.

 Using the Deployment Tray
 The wall crawler is designed to be deployed into the annular space using a special steel tray. The crawler’s magnet
 holds the crawler onto the tray by applying a normal force between the tray and the crawler wheels, creating a
 frictional bond. The tray has a swing arm, which, when lodged tightly between the inner and outer shells of a
 double shell tank, immobilizes the tray. While the tray is so immobilized, the user may drive the crawler on and off
 of the tray.

 DEPLOYING THE WALL CRAWLER WITH TRAY:
 1. Using the pneumatic magnet lift control, raise the magnet bar to its stowed position, then place the crawler
 onto the tray. Ensure that the crawler tether is in front of the tray swing arm so that it will not become
 entangled.

 2. Lower the magnet bar to its active position. The crawler should now have a strong grip on the tray.

 3. The steel loop on the end of the tray arm brake should be hooked onto a crane or hoist; also, a lanyard
 should be hooked to the two eye bolts on the top end of the tray, and a hoist should be connected to the
 lanyard.

 4. Using the hoist on the tray arm, lower the tray/crawler system through the riser tube until the desired
 height is reached. Throughout this process, the hoist on the tray lanyard should either be allowed to
 unspool freely, or must be spun manually to keep up with the primary hoist. Rotate the tray/crawler system
 to keep the back of the tray facing the inner shell tank wall.

 5. Once at the desired height, use the tray lanyard hoist to lift the crawler and tray (leaving the tray arm at
 its current height). Angle the tray arm to contact the outer shell tank wall. The rubber pad on the tray arm
 brake should grip against the surface of the wall. Keep raising the lanyard hoist until the tray is lodged
 tightly against the inner shell tank wall by the tray arm.

 6. The crawler may now be driven off of the tray. Crawler retrieval is a reversal of all of the above steps.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 In support of the Hanford mission Cold Spray was identified as technology for remediation of thinned areas of the
 below ground waste storage tanks. In prior fiscal years, the team of H2C, Orano, VRC and RTT have demonstrated
 technology to remotely deploy the Cold Spray technology into the annulus region of the double shell tanks such as the
 ones contained on the Hanford Tank Farms. This fiscal year the team advanced technology from the development
 stage to field deployment and refurbishment of a thinned area on an in-service double shell tank at Hanford. This
 report summarizes the efforts and results of this work.

 2. TECHNICAL APPROACH

 Development work in fiscal year 2025 was divided into four tasks as defined in the Statement of Work. Task 1
 developed a plan for preparing the wall and floor crawlers for field deployment. Task 2 generated a training or
 operation manual for the High Mobility Manufacturing Robot (HMMR) Cold Spray Crawlers. Task 3 showcased the
 development work on the HMMR crawlers through application of a Cold Spray patch on an in-service tank in the
 Hanford Tank Farms. Task 4 is the final test report which is this document. RTT’s technical approach was documented
 and approved by H2C Chief Technology Office (CTO) engineers and managers at the beginning of this scope.

 3. TASK 1: PREPARE TEST PLAN

 The test plan prepared for this project described a procedure to analyze and test various components of the floor and
 wall crawler Cold Spray robots. The test plan outlined the overall objectives and systems to be tested to characterize
 the crawler and substrate behavior under field conditions. The test plan was executed early in the project timeline to
 bound the expected conditions for the field deployment phase of the project. The test plan was submittal number
 89760-001-002 (1) and is included as Attachment A with this report.

 The test plan set up the outline for evaluating loads applied to the climbing and spray surfaces. The tests outlined were
 minimum thickness material and maximum deflection under wheel loading conditions, the deflection and traction force
 over varying plate thicknesses over the entire crawler area, minimum plate thickness for payload capability,
 deployment tray analysis and floor crawler lifting analysis. The outcomes of the test plan are highlighted here.
 Additionally, the outcomes have been summarized and reported in a white paper (2) and is included as Attachment B
 with this report.

 Wheel Contact
 The first test was the wheel contact on the substrate material under maximum magnetic force conditions. For this test
 RTT constructed a test apparatus to fixture various thickness steel plate material. Once fixtured, a crawler wheel was
 pressed into the center of the plate, and the force and deflection were recorded. The test conducted was bounded by
 the maximum force produced by the wall crawler since its magnetic pull is greater due to its lager magnet and
 wheelbase. The maximum force that the suspended magnet generates per wheel on the climbing substrate is 200
 pounds. The steel plate material varied in thickness from 0.06 inch to 0.187 inch. The fixtured plate was 8 inch by 8
 inch to simulate an area of thinned material the crawler might operate over in the field. Figure 1 shows a test setup
 with press to apply the load, fixture to clamp the material and scale to measure the force applied.

 The tests were conducted by increasing the load up to the maximum 200 pounds. As the load was increased, the
 current deformation of the center of the plate where the force was applied was recorded. After removing the load, the
 resulting permanent deformation of the plate was documented. The datasheet from the tests is presented here in
 Table 1. The observed maximum deflections were from the 0.06 inch plate case. The average maximum deflection
 was 0.086 inch under the 200 pound loading condition. The applied load resulted in a permanent deformation average
 of 0.014 inch for the 0.06 inch plate. Figures 2 - 4 show plots of the load versus deflection recorded for the three
 substrate thicknesses.

 Minimum Plate Thickness Over Crawler Area
 This test was conducted to observe deflection and operability on various thickness plates. The material utilized was
 A36 steel which is more conservative than expected field conditions due to tank farm material being a higher tensile
 strength steel. For this test, the crawler was operated on a sheet of steel 4 feet by 4 feet in size. The testing observed
 for maneuverability of the crawler and excessive deflection of the substrate material. The tests were conducted on
 0.125-, 0.187- and 0.25-inch thickness materials. This test particularly concerns the wall crawler platform. For the test
 material flat plate was utilized for the substrates. In the field, the curvature of the tank shell would decrease the magnet
 standoff distance. Therefore, to make the test more comparable, RTT reduced the magnet standoff of the wall crawler
 to between 0.125-0.25 inch. This test is more conservative than in field conditions since the entirety of the magnet
 surface is at the standoff distance to the substrate versus in the field where only the middle of the magnet is that close
 to the climbing surface.

 The wall crawler’s maneuverability was observed with three different plate thicknesses and two magnet standoff
 distances. For a 0.125 inch magnet standoff, the wall crawler loses maneuverability on the 0.125- and 0.187-inch
 plates. The deflection of the plates is severe enough to contact the magnet of the crawler preventing motion. The
 crawler is operable on the 0.25 inch plate at a 0.125 inch magnet standoff. When the magnet standoff is increased to
 0.25 inch, the crawler is maneuverable on the 0.187- and 0.25-inch plates. Even with the magnet standoff raised to
 0.25 inch, the deflection of the 0.125 inch plate is too great to allow the crawler to operate. Figure 5 shows a
 representative test setup with the wall crawler maneuvering on a plate.

 Figure 5: Wall Crawler Testing on 0.25 inch Plate

 Minimum Plate Thickness Under Magnet
 This test was performed to simulate the operation of the crawler magnet over various thicknesses of plate material and
 determine its operability and capability to remain attached to the tank when the tank wall thickness decreases. The
 wheels of the crawler were supported on a frame that positioned the simulated wall substrate beneath the suspended
 magnet. The frame supports test coupons (varying substrate thickness plates) to be tested. The frame was oriented
 vertically (see Figure 6) with the test coupons installed from the backside (see Figure 7). As the crawler was operated
 on the frame, the deflection of the test coupon and the vertical pull force were captured.

 The tests were conducted over plates varying in thickness from 0.06 inch to 0.25 inch. The 0.06 inch plate resulted in
 the lowest pull load as expected at 68 pounds. The resulting deflection was high at 0.11 inch but with no permanent
 deflection observed after the test was concluded. For all the plate thicknesses tested, the data is presented in Table 2
 with plots shown in Figure 8 and Figure 9 documenting the resulting pull force and deflection as a function of the
 substrate thickness.

 4. TASK 2: WRITE TRAINING MANUAL

 The team prepared an operation manual for the Cold Spray robotic crawlers (HMMR-T). The guidebook covers the
 wall and floor crawlers in the same manual as both crawlers function the same. Differences between the two are called
 out in the manual where applicable. The manual is HMMR-T Vs. 1.0 Operation Guidebook (3) and is included as
 Attachment C with this report.

 The training manual provides a system level overview of the HMMR-T crawler unit, tether connections, operator control
 unit, and remote injector unit for deployment at the tank riser. Beyond the component connections, a breakdown on
 operation of the HMMR-T is provided to get the operator up and running while providing a set of quick-start instructions
 to aid in initial deployment. Following operation, the manual outlines connection of the Cold Spray equipment to the
 HMMR-T. Since the manual targets the HMMR-T for Cold Spray applications, a set of parameters typically useful in
 spray applications and the control sequence for performing a patch are included in the manual. The manual includes
 guidance on the use of the deployment tray for wall crawler operations. The tray is utilized when deploying the wall
 crawler to the primary or secondary shell tank wall in the annulus. Lastly, general parts diagrams and a troubleshooting
 guide are included for field support.

 6. LESSONS LEARNED AND PLANNED IMPROVEMENTS

 The team encountered several obstacles in completion of the field deployment of the Cold Spray robot. This section
 will describe the actions to take prior to the next deployment.

 Magnetic Attachment to Tank Wall
 RTT is investigating several modifications to the floor crawler to greatly reduce or eliminate the magnetic attraction to
 the wall during deployment or retrieval operations. The floor crawler currently requires the magnet to apply a holding
 force to the surface it is operating on. This force stabilizes the crawler during the Cold Spray operation and generates
 the normal force to supply traction to the drive wheels. The team is investigating increasing the standoff distance
 between the magnet and the surface. By increasing the standoff distance, the distance the magnet is held from the
 surface when retracted during deployment or retrieval is increased thereby reducing the potential holding force to the
 tank wall if accidental contact is made. The standoff is adjustable without crawler modification making it a
 straightforward modification. Also, RTT will investigate lowering the magnetic force through the removal of magnet
 bars in the magnet assembly. This will remove significant magnetic force and will be tested if adjustment of the
 standoff distance is inadequate to reduce the holding force on the tank wall. These options will be tested to not reduce
 the tractive force too much for Cold Spray operations.

 Injector Box Airline

 The broken airline highlighted a procedural step missing in RTT’s internal assembly process. Checking that the proper
 components are included in the assemblies has been added into RTT procedures. In discovering this, RTT is also
 examining an upgrade of the airline component that failed in the field. The airline specified in our design was a
 polyurethane flexible hose rated at 170 psi at 72 degrees Fahrenheit. This line could still be an issue with the injector
 box sitting in direct sunlight. RTT will upgrade this airline with a braided high temperature, high pressure rated line to
 eliminate that failure point. RTT will also investigate (and upgrade as warranted) the other internal components in the
 injector box for sensitivity to temperature conditions that may arise in the future.

 Temperature Causing Raster Stop

 Once the crawler was returned to the RTT shop, RTT began trying to recreate the issue. The underlying culprit was
 found to be the overheating of the microcontroller onboard the crawler. Upon testing, RTT could reproduce the exact
 observed behavior of the raster by applying heat to the microcontroller. First the entire crawler assembly was placed in
 an oven and commanded to perform motion consistent with the Cold Spray patch motion (see oven in Figure 23). The
 problem was observed at approximately 150 degrees Fahrenheit. To dive into the problem further, it was consistently
 measured through multiple tests that the behavior observed in the tank annulus was reproduceable at 150 degrees
 Fahrenheit. Shown in Figure 24, heat is directly applied to the microcontroller while simultaneously measuring
 temperature of the chip with an infrared thermometer. The raster motion was commanded to run and when the chip
 temperature measured 150 degrees Fahrenheit the motion would stop. When the heat source was removed, the
 microcontroller would immediately zero the raster (by moving it full left) and then begin the commanded raster motion
 again.

 The microcontroller that was used in the crawler is a Teensy 4.1 (MIMXRT1062DVJ6B) and is specified to have a 203
 degrees Fahrenheit operating temperature capability. In our tests the chip consistently failed at 150 degrees
 Fahrenheit. RTT has identified two replacement chips in the same family but with extended operating temperatures of
 257 degrees Fahrenheit.

 Shipping damage
 During shipment to ITIVS, the shipping box containing the operator control units was slightly damaged. One of the
 operator control units suffered damage to its internal components. On further inspection, two plugs (USB style) were
 broken inside the box, and a wire was also broken. When the control box was powered, the broken wire shorted to the
 CANBUS in the system causing problems in the injector box circuit board and the crawler used to diagnose issues at
 ITIVS during setup. This led to RTT needing to overnight extra supplies to repair the injector box and crawler circuit
 board.

 During shipment, damage also occurred to the tablet in the operator control unit and internal video converter. This
 damage resulted in the monitor display not functioning for the crawler. While the crawler could still be operated through
 the hardware buttons on the controller, the interface between the crawler controls and the VRC Raptor controls was
 not functioning properly. This meant VRC had to control their Raptor directly relying on communication between the
 Raptor operator and the crawler operator for coordination of starting and controlling the spray.

 RTT is presently revamping assembly techniques on the control boxes to further help isolate them during the shipping
 process. While spare components were sent for this deployment, more spares will be made available on future
 deployments. An internal RTT procedural change has been implemented on debugging and diagnosing items in the
 field to limit component damage in the event of a similar event. Lastly, packaging of items for shipment will be
 improved for future deployments. The control boxes are currently rugged pelican cases, but they have connection
 ports that exit the sides of the cases. During shipping these cases are packed inside larger boxes. In the future, the
 packing of the operator control cases will be handled more cautiously with the boxes encased in foam packing inside
 the shipping case.

 7. CONCLUSION

 The project activities have proven the Cold Spray robot is a technology capable of deploying on in-service double shell
 tanks and performing refurbishment of thinned areas on these tanks. Utilizing RTT’s HMMR, the team deployed Cold
 Spray equipment to an in-service below ground double shell tank at the AN tank farm. The system was successfully
 deployed through an existing riser and remotely applied a Cold Spray overlay to a measured thin area on the annulus
 steel floor. During the operation, the team encountered various aspects of the robot system that required attention.
 These issues were effectively resolved, resulting in a successful deployment. Moving forward, modifications will be
 implemented to ensure more efficient deployment in upcoming opportunities.

 As part of the fiscal year 2025 work, Independent Tank Integrity Verification Specialist (ITIVS) was
 contracted to provide deployment field support for the use of the Cold Spray technology with VRC
 Metal Systems and RTT. The Cold Spray technology was made possible in the double shell tanks
 through a CTO contract. The ITIVS support was provided under contract 86598 release 011, Task 1.
 The Statement of Work (SOW) is Requisition #380322 (BMA #86598), Title: Field Support for New
 Non-Destructive Examination Development, Revision Number: 0, Date: 4/17/2025

 Task 1 included ITIVS performing detailed ultrasonic testing (UT) scans of the region of interest (ROI)
 in Tank 241-AN-105(AN-105) Riser 25, prior to the Cold Spray patch being applied. ITIVS performed
 these scans in conjunction with H2C engineering and operators the document to condition and exact
 location for the Cold Spray patch to be applied. ITIVS also provided support for Cold Spray partners,
 VRC Metal Systems and RTT to assemble, test, trouble-shoot and repair equipment at the ITIVS shop
 in Richland WA.

 of the patch be increased and that the location be as close to the refractory pad as possible to avoid the
 edges of the patch interfering with UT of the patch to monitor the corrosion area. ITIVS also
 recommended manual ultrasonic confirmation of the Cold Spray pre-spray and post-spray test coupons

 3.7 Variations in Ultrasonic Thickness Measurements of Cold Spray Patch

 4 CONCLUSION

 ITIVS completed ultrasonic thickness inspections on the Cold Spray patch.
 The ultrasonic data collected prior to the application of the Cold Spray patch and after the application
 of the patch has been evaluated and validated. The scans provided very good data in the patch and the
 region of interest (ROI).
 The ultrasonic data clearly shows that the entire area of the patch is bonded to the base material. The
 location of the patch covers the region of interest. The quality of the Cold Spray patch applied will
 allow for future ultrasonic monitoring of thickness in the region of interest.
 Reference locations have been established that will allow for monitoring of thickness conditions in the
 future.
 The Cold Spray patch provides increased metal thickness above the corrosion area and allows for
 continued monitoring of thickness in this area of the patch. Application of the Cold Spray patch in a
 DST annulus is very impressive. There may be ways to make improvements in the process to provide
 better conditions for ultrasonic thickness monitoring in the future.

 5 RECOMMENDATIONS

 After performing these inspections and evaluating the data, ITIVS makes the following
 recommendations:
 These ultrasonic inspections should be repeated for a few cycles to monitor for any potential changes.
 It appears that some variations in the Cold Spray patch cause some variations in the ultrasonic readings.
 Some of the variations are on the surface which is likely due to minor irregularities on the base material
 surface prior to the Cold Spray being applied. It may be worth determining if the final surface of the
 Cold Spray patch can be made smoother. Possibly by applying a partial patch, then smoothing the
 surface prior to applying the remaining Cold Spray material.
 Evaluate if there is any impact of mill scale remaining on the base metal when the Cold Spray patch
 is applied.
 Further evaluation of Cold Spray patch material as applied in the annulus to minimize any variations
 in ultrasonic measurements.
