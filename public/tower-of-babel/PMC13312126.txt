Protocol for applying a network-enabled gene discovery pipeline to non-model plant species
Authors: Dae Kwan Ko, Federica Brandizzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIdentifying upstream regulators of key genes is essential for understanding gene regulatory mechanisms and translating these insights into functional targets. Here, we present a protocol for applying the network-enabled gene discovery pipeline (NEEDLE) to non-model plant species. We describe steps for environment setup, data preparation, computational analysis, expected outputs, and parameter considerations. NEEDLE integrates RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) processing, weighted gene co-expression analysis (WGCNA), Gene Network Inference with Ensemble of trees (GENIE3), and promoter conservation analysis to prioritize candidate transcriptional regulators.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Ko and Brandizzi.1

Before you begin

This protocol describes the application of NEEDLE to identify transcription factors (TFs) associated with gene expression dynamics during sorghum stem development. The workflow is demonstrated using an RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset comprising 11 developmental samples, as reported in the associated research article.1 Although the protocol uses sorghum as an example, NEEDLE can be applied to other plant species when transcriptome datasets capture sufficient biological variation across time points, developmental stages, tissues, genotypes, or treatment conditions.Note: Based on our testing across diverse transcriptome datasets, six or more biologically meaningful samples are generally recommended for stable network modeling; however, this threshold should be considered a practical guideline rather than a strict requirement, as the optimal sample number may vary depending on dataset complexity and the biological question.

Note: Based on our testing across diverse transcriptome datasets, six or more biologically meaningful samples are generally recommended for stable network modeling; however, this threshold should be considered a practical guideline rather than a strict requirement, as the optimal sample number may vary depending on dataset complexity and the biological question.

NEEDLE consists of three major components: (i) RNA-seq data processing, (ii) WGCNA-based coexpression network construction followed by GENIE3-based gene regulatory network (GRN) inference, and (iii) validation of predicted TF–target relationships through in silico promoter scanning and conservation analysis, followed by optional in planta transient expression assays such as dual-luciferase reporter analysis (Figure 1). Innovation

Most gene discovery pipelines aimed at identifying regulatory drivers of agronomic traits benefit from integrating multiple omics layers, such as epigenomics, proteomics, and chromatin accessibility data. However, these resources are often unavailable for crop and non-model plant species.2,3 NEEDLE addresses this limitation by providing a transcriptome-driven workflow for prioritizing candidate regulators using widely accessible RNA-seq datasets. Rather than introducing a new algorithm, NEEDLE integrates and standardizes established analytical components, including RNA-seq processing, WGCNA-based coexpression network construction, intramodular connectivity analysis, GENIE3-based GRN inference, and promoter conservation analysis, into a coherent pipeline optimized for dynamic transcriptome data.

A key strength of NEEDLE is its structured prioritization strategy, which combines module membership, gene connectivity, and regulatory hierarchy metrics to systematically nominate candidate TFs associated with genes or pathways of interest. The broader NEEDLE workflow also supports downstream experimental validation of selected TF–target relationships using transient expression assays. In the present protocol, we focus on the computational and in silico components of NEEDLE, while experimental validation procedures are described in the associated research article.1 This protocol lowers the barrier to network-guided gene discovery in data-limited systems and expands the practical utility of systems biology approaches for crop improvement.

Computational requirements

For optimal performance, particularly during RNA-seq read mapping and quantification, access to an HPC system is recommended because raw sequencing files can be large and alignment steps are computationally intensive. However, an HPC system is not strictly required for all users or all steps. Small to moderate RNA-seq datasets can be processed on a Linux- or macOS-based workstation if sufficient memory, CPU capacity, and storage are available.

For local execution, we recommend a modern workstation equipped with an 8-core or higher CPU, at least 16 GB of RAM, and a minimum of 100 GB of available storage. Increased RAM of 32 GB or more is recommended for larger transcriptome datasets, especially those containing more than 20 samples or a large number of expressed genes. Downstream analyses, including differential expression analysis, WGCNA-based coexpression network construction, module detection, connectivity analysis, and GENIE3-based GRN inference, can generally be performed on a modern personal workstation for moderate-sized datasets. For large datasets, users may reduce memory demands by prefiltering lowly expressed or low-variance genes, using WGCNA blockwise module detection, or running memory-intensive steps on an HPC node.

In NEEDLE, WGCNA and GENIE3 are used sequentially but they address different analytical questions.3 WGCNA identifies groups of genes with correlated expression patterns across developmental stages, time points, tissues, genotypes, or treatment conditions. These coexpression modules help define biologically coherent gene sets associated with a gene, pathway, or phenotype of interest.3 However, WGCNA does not infer regulatory direction or distinguish transcriptional regulators from downstream target genes. This section describes construction of a GRN for a WGCNA-derived coexpression module using GENIE3, a tree-based regulatory inference method. GENIE3 predicts putative regulatory relationships by using candidate TFs as potential regulators and expression profiles of module genes as potential targets. Expected outcomes

Application of this protocol generates a network-guided set of candidate transcriptional regulators associated with genes or pathways of interest using transcriptome data alone. The workflow begins with RNA-seq data processing and proceeds through WGCNA-based coexpression network construction, GENIE3-based GRN inference, and promoter conservation analysis (Figure 1).

Starting from raw RNA-seq data, users should obtain quality-controlled trimmed FASTQ files, sorted alignment BAM files, and gene-level count matrices suitable for downstream analysis. For the sorghum stem RNA-seq dataset used in this protocol, mapping rates are typically expected to be above 85%, although this value may vary depending on genome quality, read depth, library type, tissue, and species. Differential expression analysis using DESeq2 will produce contrast-specific gene lists with log2 fold changes and adjusted p-values. The number of DEGs will depend on the biological contrast, sample number, sequencing depth, and statistical thresholds.

WGCNA-based coexpression network analysis will yield discrete gene modules, each assigned a unique color label (Figure 2). The number of modules depends on sample size, biological variation, and parameter choices, including soft-thresholding power, minimum module size, and module merging threshold. Each module is represented by a module eigengene summarizing its expression pattern across samples. These eigengenes can be compared across developmental stages, time points, tissues, or treatments to identify biologically relevant modules. Module-specific gene lists and eigengene tables are the key outputs of this stage.

GENIE3-based GRN inference will generate a ranked list of predicted TF–target interactions within selected WGCNA modules (Figure 3). The output includes weighted edges representing inferred regulatory importance. Depending on the selected weight threshold or number of top-ranked edges retained, the resulting network may contain hundreds to thousands of candidate interactions. When integrated with WGCNA-derived module membership and connectivity metrics, this output enables prioritization of candidate upstream regulators associated with genes or pathways of interest.

Promoter conservation analysis using mVISTA is expected to identify conserved CNSs among homologous promoters across related grass species. Conserved regions that overlap predicted CREs or candidate TF-binding motifs provide supportive in silico evidence for predicted regulatory interactions. These results should be interpreted as prioritization evidence rather than proof of direct TF binding or regulatory activity.

Collectively, this protocol enables users to produce quality-controlled RNA-seq count matrices, differential expression gene lists, coexpression modules with eigengenes, ranked TF–target edge lists, and conserved promoter regions supporting predicted regulatory relationships. These outputs provide a reproducible, transcriptome-driven strategy for prioritizing regulatory genes in crop and non-model plant species where multi-omics datasets are limited.

Limitations

Although NEEDLE provides a structured workflow for transcriptome-driven gene regulatory inference, several limitations should be considered when interpreting results. First, the accuracy of coexpression and regulatory network modeling depends strongly on the quality, size, and biological relevance of the input transcriptome dataset. Datasets with too few biologically meaningful samples may reduce the robustness of correlation-based module detection and increase the likelihood of spurious associations. Based on our testing, six or more biologically meaningful samples are generally recommended as a practical starting point, but this should not be interpreted as a strict cutoff. The optimal sample number depends on dataset complexity, biological variation, and the specific question being addressed. Likewise, datasets lacking sufficient biological variation across time points, developmental stages, tissues, genotypes, or treatment may fail to produce well-defined modules or meaningful regulatory hierarchies.

Second, coexpression does not imply causation.3,13 WGCNA identifies genes with correlated expression patterns, and GENIE3 infers putative regulatory relationships based on statistical dependencies, but neither approach directly demonstrates physical TF–DNA binding or regulatory activity in vivo. Network predictions are therefore hypothesis-generating and require experimental validation. In addition, parameter choices, including soft-thresholding power in WGCNA, module merging thresholds, and weight cutoffs in GENIE3, can influence network topology and regulator ranking. Although commonly used parameters are provided in this protocol, users may need to optimize these settings according to dataset characteristics.

Third, transcriptome-only approaches cannot capture regulatory mechanisms mediated through chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, protein–protein interactions, post-translational modifications, translational control, or protein stability. As a result, true regulators with low, transient, or constitutive expression may be missed, and inferred networks may not fully represent the underlying biological complexity. Similarly, promoter conservation analysis provides indirect evidence of regulatory potential but does not confirm direct TF binding or functional activity of CREs.

Finally, technical and annotation-related factors, such as batch effects, sequencing-depth variability, inaccurate genome annotations, inconsistent gene identifiers, or mismatched genome versions, can affect network construction and regulatory inference. Careful preprocessing, metadata verification, and consistent use of genome and annotation versions are critical to minimize these issues.

Taken together, NEEDLE should be viewed as a systematic, transcriptome-based prioritization pipeline that guides downstream functional validation rather than as definitive proof of regulatory relationships.
