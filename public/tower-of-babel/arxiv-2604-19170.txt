Bifurcations in Isoperimetric Problems with Nonlocal Interactions Thanks: Work partially supported by IES/R3/233057 - Royal Society International Exchanges 2023 Round 3. FDR and MG are partially supported by INDAM
Fabio De Regibus, Massimo Grossi, Monica Musso

Abstract
. We study isoperimetric problems modeled on the liquid drop model, with nonlocal interactions under a volume constraint. While balls are natural critical points, we show that, for an unbounded sequence of radii, non-spherical solutions bifurcate from the family of balls. These new solutions lie arbitrarily close to balls and can have arbitrarily large volume. Conversely, at radii outside this sequence, no bifurcation occurs, and nearby solutions are trivial, arising only from rigid motions.



1.
Introduction and main results

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a smooth set, with
N\geq 2
, and consider the energy functional

E(\Omega)=\mathrm{Per}(\Omega)+D(\Omega).

(1.1)

Here the perimeter is defined by

\mathrm{Per}(\Omega)=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\,\Psi\,dx:\,\Psi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N}),\ \|\Psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq 1\right\},

and it coincides with the surface area of
\partial\Omega
when
\Omega
is smooth.
The second term in the energy functional represents a nonlocal interaction. In dimension
N\geq 2
we consider

D(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\lambda}}\,dx\,dy,

(1.2)

for
0<\lambda<N-1
. Note that this coincides with Coulomb interaction when
\lambda=N-2
.
In dimension
N=2
we also consider

D(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right)\,dx\,dy.

(1.3)

We study critical points of the energy functional
E
under a volume constraint. While every ball of radius
R
naturally constitutes a critical point, our focus is on the existence of new, non-spherical solutions. Specifically, we show that for certain distinguished values of
R
, branches of solutions bifurcate from the family of balls, revealing a richer structure of equilibria beyond the classical spherical configurations.

In dimension
N=3
and considering a Coulomb-type interaction corresponding to
\lambda=1
in (
1.2
), the energy functional arises in the classical liquid drop model introduced in
[
2
,
16
]
to describe atomic nuclei. In this model the nucleus is represented by a region
\Omega
of incompressible fluid with nucleons (protons and neutrons) uniformly distributed.
Under this assumption, the number of nucleons is proportional to the volume
|\Omega|
. The perimeter term in (
1.1
) models surface tension, which tends to hold the nucleus together, while the nonlocal term
D(\Omega)
accounts for the Coulomb repulsion between protons.

The basic variational problem for equilibria of this system is the following: given
m>0
, find regions
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary that are critical points of
E
under the volume constraint
|\Omega|=m
. This problem, introduced by Bohr and Wheeler
[
1
]
in connection with nuclear fission, leads to the Euler-Lagrange condition that for some constant
\mu

H_{\partial\Omega}(x)+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,dy=\mu,\qquad\text{for all }x\in\partial\Omega,

(1.4)

where
H_{\partial\Omega}
denotes the mean curvature of the boundary.

As already observed, balls of volume
m
are always solutions: balls minimize the perimeter under the constraint
|\Omega|=m
by the classical isoperimetric inequality (see
[
26
, Theorem 14.1]
), while they maximize the Coulomb interaction term
[
24
, Theorem 3.7]
. The competition between these two effects makes problem (
1.4
) delicate; see
[
6
]
for a survey of results.

In the small-volume regime
m\to 0
, the perimeter term dominates, while the Coulomb interaction becomes dominant for large volume
m\gg 1
. A simple scaling argument clarifies the competition between the two terms in the energy.
Indeed, let
\Omega
be a region with
|\Omega|=m
and write
\Omega=m^{1/3}\mathcal{M}
, so that
|\mathcal{M}|=1
. Then (
1.1
) becomes

E(\Omega)=m^{2/3}\big(\mathrm{Per}(\mathcal{M})+m\,D(\mathcal{M})\big).

Knüpfer and Muratov
[
22
]
proved that for sufficiently small
m>0
the minimizer of (
1.1
) is the ball
B
with
|B|=m
; see also Julin
[
20
]
and Bonacini–Cristoferi
[
3
]
. More recently, Chodosh and Ruohoniemi
[
5
]
showed that this holds for all
m\leq 1
.

For larger volumes the situation changes: balls cease to be global minimizers when
m>m_{*}
, where (see
[
7
,
14
]
)

m_{*}=5\,\frac{2^{1/3}-1}{1-2^{-2/3}}\approx 3.51.

The threshold
m_{*}
is defined as the volume for which a single ball and two equally sized balls at infinite separation have the same energy.
Frank and Nam
[
13
]
showed that no global minimizer exists for
m\geq 8
, improving earlier results by Lu and Otto
[
25
]
, see also
[
12
]
. Choksi and Peletier
[
7
]
conjectured that global minimizers exist precisely for
m\in(0,m_{*}]
, and they are balls. This was confirmed in
[
13
]
for
0<m\leq m_{*}
, assuming no minimizer exists for
m>m_{*}
.

Very few results address the existence of non-spherical solutions to Problem (
1.4
) for
m>m_{*}
. Beyond balls, the only known explicit constructions for large
m
are the rotational tori and double tori in
[
27
,
28
]
. More recently,
[
9
]
constructed a family of compact, embedded solutions with large volume, forming a striking “pearl necklace” along a large circle, with cross-sections closely approximating Delaunay unduloids. These solutions exhibit dihedral symmetry in the plane of the circle, remaining invariant under rotations by angles of
{2\pi\over k}
, for all
k
large.

Balls remain local minimizers well beyond
m_{*}
: they are linearly stable for
0<m\leq 10
and lose stability for
m>10
. At
m=10
a bifurcation occurs, giving rise to non-spherical local minimizers for
m>10
and saddle points for
m<10
. In
[
15
]
, Frank established the existence of a smooth family of nonspherical, volume-constrained critical points near
m=10
, bifurcating from the ball at which it loses stability. These sets are cylindrically symmetric, changing from prolate shapes below
m=10
to oblate shapes above, with energy above that of balls for smaller volumes and below for larger volumes. At
m=10
, an exchange of stability takes place, with balls losing stability while the nonspherical sets become stable.
These configurations were anticipated by Bohr and Wheeler
[
1
]
as intermediate states in the decay of a ball into two. They correspond to volume-preserving deformations of a ball with volume between a critical value and
10
, where the energy along such paths first increases and then decreases.
From a numerical perspective, we would like to mention the work
[
33
]
where different bifurcation branches are traced, uncovering toroidal and Delaunay-like shapes for large volumes and providing illustrative figures.

We also refer to the works
[
11
,
23
]
, which address the cases of dimension
N\not=3
and the Coulomb interaction depends on a parameter
\lambda\not=1
.

In the case of dimension
N=2
, the energy functional
E
does not admit global minimizers, as the logarithmic Coulomb interaction (
1.3
) is unbounded. Nevertheless
one can still consider critical points of
E
under the volume constraint. This type of
logarithmic Coulomb interaction arises, for instance, in the study of pattern formation in some growth and inhibition process in biological systems. We refer to
[
30
]
for several interesting phenomena occurring in this framework.
See also
[
29
]
for a related functional in which the nonlocal term involves a perturbed logarithmic interaction.

The aim of this paper is to establish the existence of an unbounded sequence of radii at which bifurcations from the ball with those radii occur in the liquid drop model, thereby extending Frank’s result
[
15
]
. We prove that the same phenomenon occurs in dimension
N=2
for the Coulomb interaction (
1.3
). More generally, for the nonlocal interaction (
1.2
), we show that in every dimension
N\geq 2
there is an unbounded sequence of radii at which bifurcations from the sphere take place.

More precisely, we prove the existence of a diverging sequence
({\mathfrak{R}}_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that nontrivial branches of critical points bifurcate from the balls centered at the origin and of radius
{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
, denoted by
B_{{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}}
. As a consequence, for any given
m>0
, there exist solutions with volume exceeding
m
that are not balls but lie arbitrarily close to balls. Conversely, we show that if
R\neq{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
, no bifurcation occurs, and therefore no solutions exist in a neighbourhood of the ball of radius
R
, except for the trivial ones obtained by rigid motions of the ball.

The domains we obtain are star-shaped with respect to the origin and have the form

\Omega_{\varphi}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|x|<\varphi\!\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\right)\right\},\quad\varphi(z)=R+u\!\left(z\right)\geq 0,

(1.5)

for some
R>0
and small function
u:\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}
with
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Observe that when
\varphi(z)\equiv{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
, then
\Omega_{\varphi}
is the ball of radius
B_{{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}}
. We consider non-constant functions
u
whose symmetry properties depend on both the dimension
N
and the radius
{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
.

We present our results in the following order: first the liquid drop model (
N=3
and
\lambda=1
); next the case of the logarithmic Coulomb interaction
D
as in (
1.3
) in dimension
N=2
; and finally the general case with
N\geq 2
and
\lambda\in(0,N-1)
in (
1.2
).
We treat these cases separately for two reasons. First, the bifurcation radii take different forms depending on the dimension and on the type of interaction. Second, the nature of the bifurcation results differs across dimensions.

The liquid drop model case:
N=3
and
\lambda=1

{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}=\left(\frac{3(2n+1)(n+2)}{8\pi}\right)^{1/3},\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N},\quad n\geq 2.

Remark 1.1
.

As already mentioned, the case
n=2
reproduces the bifurcation found in
[
15
]
. Our result shows that similar bifurcations occur at all radii
{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
for
n
even, with the domain shape transitioning from prolate to oblate depending on whether
R<{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
or
R>{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
. Our results further extend to odd
n\geq 3
, where the solutions display distinct symmetries from the even-
n
case. Moreover, in this case the number of solutions grows with
n
, revealing an increasingly rich bifurcation structure.

Furthermore we observe that, when
n
is odd and for all
m
as in the statement of the theorem, the domain

\Omega_{R_{n,m}^{t}+v_{n,m}^{t}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|<R_{n}^{t}+v_{n,m}^{t}\left({x\over|x|}\right)\right\},

with

is also a solution to (
1.4
). In fact,
\Omega_{R_{n}^{t}+u_{n}^{t}}
and
\Omega_{R_{n}^{t}+v_{n}^{t}}
differ from each other by a simple rotation. As will be clear from the proof, it is possible to find such a curve of solutions by bifurcating in the appropriate space. In this case, it is easy to see that the first-order expansion is given by
v_{n,m}^{t}=t\mathfrak{L}^{2}_{n,m}+O(t^{2})
in
\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. We refer to Remark
2.7
for the details.

An intriguing open question is whether the solutions constructed in
[
9
]
, which exhibit dihedral symmetry in the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane, lie on the bifurcation branches originating from

{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
for odd
n
, as suggested by Theorem
1
. Addressing this appears to be a highly challenging problem.

Remark 1.2
.

A different behavior of the volume of the bifurcating solutions between even
n
and odd
n
can be observed. Indeed, if
n
is even, we have
\frac{d}{dt}{R_{n}^{t}}_{|t=0}<0
, and this implies that both values above and below
|B_{{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}}|
are attained by
|\Omega_{R_{n}^{t}+u_{n}^{t}}|
already at the linear level. In particular, the bifurcation is transcritical. On the other hand, if
n
is odd,
\frac{d}{dt}{R_{n,m}^{t}}_{|t=0}=0
, and thus this change in volume cannot be observed at the linear level. It seems very plausible that in this case
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}{R_{n,m}^{t}}_{|t=0}\neq 0
, leading to a subcritical or supercritical bifurcation.

Remark 1.3
.

In contrast to the three-dimensional case, the number of solutions at each bifurcation point

remains the same. Indeed, the dimension of the kernel does not increase with

Moreover, note that, as in the odd case in dimension

Remark 1.4
.

It is interesting to note that for

B_{{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}}

1.3.
The case of dimension
N\geq 2
and Coulomb interaction with parameter
0<\lambda<N-1

In this case we consider critical points of the energy functional

E_{\lambda}(\Omega)={\mbox{Per}}(\Omega)+D_{\lambda}(\Omega),

where

D_{\lambda}(\Omega)={1\over 2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}{1\over|x-y|^{\lambda}}\,dx\,dy,

with fixed volume
m
and, of course, any ball of volume
m
is still a critical point of this functional under the prescribed volume constraint. The Euler Lagrange equation is

H_{\partial\Omega}(x)+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\lambda}}\,dy=\mu,\qquad\text{for all }x\in\partial\Omega,

(1.10)

where
H_{\partial\Omega}
still denotes the mean curvature of the boundary and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.

The explicit expression of the sequence of radii where bifurcation occurs depends on the dimension
N
and the exponent
\lambda
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
, it is given by

\mathfrak{R}_{n}={\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\lambda,N)=\left(\frac{n(n+N-2)-N+1}{-(\mu_{n}(\lambda,N)+C({\lambda},N))}\right)^{\frac{1}{N+1-\lambda}},

(1.11)

where

C(\lambda,N)=-\lambda\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(\frac{N-\lambda}{2})}<0,

and

\mu_{1}(\lambda,N)=-C(\lambda,N),\qquad\mu_{n+1}(\lambda,N)=\frac{\frac{\lambda}{2}+n}{N-1-\frac{\lambda}{2}+n}\,\mu_{n}(\lambda,N)<0.

In Lemma
4.6
we show that, for
\lambda
and
N
fixed,
{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
is an increasing sequence of positive numbers that diverges to
+\infty
.

In dimensions
N=2,3
, we obtain essentially the same results as in Theorems
1
and
2
, whereas for
N\geq 4
it is possible to construct new solutions exhibiting additional symmetry types. This illustrates that the situation becomes more involved as the dimension of the ambient space increases. For this reason, here we only consider the case
N\geq 4
.
Our result is not intended to provide a comprehensive description of all possible bifurcations when
N\geq 4
, but rather to offer some insight into the much richer bifurcation structure that may arise in this setting.

Remark 1.5
.

As will become clear from the proof (see Section
4.2
), when

is odd it is not possible to give a general explicit formula describing additional bifurcating branches, as we did in the case

. Nevertheless, the method can be applied to specific cases, and further bifurcations may be obtained by exploiting the symmetries of spherical harmonics. This is not our focus here, since our aim is only to show that bifurcation occurs at every

,

.

Moreover, in this case as well, the domain

If

Sketch of the proof.

The general approach to prove our bifurcation results follows that introduced in
[
15
]
. We first show that any star-shaped domain of the form (
1.5
) is a critical point of the energy
E

under a volume constraint if the function
\varphi
in (
1.5
) is a solution of a certain partial differential equation on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. Trivially,
\varphi=R
is a solution, for any value of
R>0
. The bifurcation problem is then reformulated as finding the non-trivial zeros of a suitable operator equation

u\to\Phi(R,u),\quad\varphi=R+u.

around certain specific values of
R
.
By analyzing the linearized operator
D_{u}\Phi(R,0)
and exploiting the structure of spherical harmonics on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, we can apply the Crandall–Rabinowitz theorem in class of functions satisfying appropriate symmetry conditions. This establishes the occurrence of bifurcation and provides a precise description of the solution branches in a neighborhood of the bifurcation point.
To establish the result in Theorem
3
for
n
even, we directly use a result of Smoller and Wasserman in
[
31
]
, on the symmetry of the spherical harmonics.

To show that for
R\neq{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
there are no solutions close to the ball, we implement a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction argument and prove that any such solutions are necessarily trivial, corresponding merely to translated or rescaled balls.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the spherical harmonics of degree
n
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\theta)=c_{n}P_{n}(\cos(\theta)),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}^{1}_{n,m}(\phi,\theta)=c_{n,m}\sin(m\phi)\sin^{m}(\theta)Q_{n-m}(\cos(\theta)),\quad m=1,\dots,n,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}^{2}_{n,m}(\phi,\theta)=c_{n,m}\cos(m\phi)\sin^{m}(\theta)Q_{n-m}(\cos(\theta)),\quad m=1,\dots,n,

where
c_{n},c_{n,m}\in\mathbb{R}
,

is the Legendre polynomial of degree

and

is given by

Q_{n-m}(t)=\frac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}P_{n}(t).

In particular, the following holds

P_{n}(-t)=(-1)^{n}P_{n}(t),\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{n-m}(-t)=(-1)^{n-m}Q_{n-m}(t).

Proof.

This is well known. Indeed, the spherical harmonics of degree
n
on

can be written as

e^{\pm im\phi}P^{m}_{n}(\cos\theta),\quad m=0,\dots,n,

where

is the associated Legendre polynomial and

is the Legendre polynomial of degree

, that by the Rodrigues’ formula can be written as

P_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}[(t^{2}-1)^{n}].\qed

Lemma 2.5
.

We have

\frac{d}{dt}{R_{n}^{t}}_{|t=0}=-\alpha_{n}<0,

where

\alpha_{n}=c_{n}\frac{(7n^{2}+4n-12)(2n+1)}{12(n+2)(n-1)(3n+1)}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{(n-1)!!}{(n/2)!}\right)^{3}}{\frac{(3n-1)!!}{(3n/2)!}}.

(2.5)

Proof.

Taking into account
[
15
, Lemmas 14, 15]

[
15

to see that

\frac{1}{2}D^{2}_{uu}\Phi(R,0)[v,v]=R^{2}Q_{R}(v),

where

\displaystyle Q_{R}(v)[z]=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{R^{3}}\left(v(z)\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}v(z)+(v(z))^{2}\right)+\frac{\pi}{3}(v(z))^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}v(z)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{v(z^{\prime})}{|z-z^{\prime}|}\,d\sigma(z^{\prime})+\frac{3}{4}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{(v(z^{\prime}))^{2}}{|z-z^{\prime}|}\,d\sigma(z^{\prime}).

By
[

, the square of a Legendre polynomial can be written as

(P_{n})^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}P_{2n-2k},

where

\beta_{k}=\frac{(b_{n-k})^{2}\,b_{k}\,}{b_{2n-k}}\cdot\frac{4n-4k+1}{4n-2k+1},\quad\text{with}\quad b_{\ell}=\frac{(2\ell-1)!!}{\ell!}.

Then

(\mathfrak{I}_{n})^{2}=(c_{n}P_{n})^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n}^{2}\beta_{k}P_{2n-2k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{2k}\mathfrak{I}_{2k},

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathfrak{I}_{\ell}=-\ell(\ell+1)\mathfrak{I}_{\ell},\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{\mathfrak{I}_{\ell}(z^{\prime})}{|z-z^{\prime}|}\,d\sigma(z^{\prime})=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\mathfrak{I}_{\ell},

thus

\begin{split}Q_{{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}}(\mathfrak{I}_{n})&=\left[\frac{2}{{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}^{3}}(1-n(n+1))+\frac{\pi}{3}-\frac{2\pi}{2n+1}\right](\mathfrak{I}_{n})^{2}+3\pi\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{2k}}{4k+1}\mathfrak{I}_{2k}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[-\frac{16\pi(n^{2}+n-1)}{3(2n+1)(n+2)}+\frac{\pi}{3}-\frac{2\pi}{2n+1}+\frac{3\pi}{4k+1}\right]a_{2k}\mathfrak{I}_{2k}.\end{split}

(2.6)

Taking into account (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{R_{n}^{t}}_{|t=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}D^{2}_{uu}\Phi({\mathfrak{R}}_{n},0)[\mathfrak{I}_{n},\mathfrak{I}_{n}]\cdot\mathfrak{I}_{n}\,d\sigma}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}D^{2}_{Ru}\Phi({\mathfrak{R}}_{n},0)[\mathfrak{I}_{n}]\cdot\mathfrak{I}_{n}\,d\sigma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{7n^{2}+4n-12}{12(n+2)(n-1)}a_{n},

where an easy computation show that

is equivalent to the system

\begin{cases}Q\Psi(R,Pu+(1-P)u)=0\\
(1-Q)\Psi(R,Pu+(1-P)u)=0.\end{cases}

If we write

and

if and only if

reduces to

Q\Psi(R,v+\xi(R,v))=0,

(2.7)

where
(R,v)\in B_{\delta}(\bar{R},0)\subset(0,\infty)\times\langle z_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
.

Let us identify
\mathbb{R}
with
\langle z_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
via

.
We now define

It is now easy to see that
\bar{\xi}(R,\tau)\in(\langle z_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}})^{\perp}

and thus integrating against

gives the orthogonality.

\tau

By uniqueness

and then, taking into account (

) all the solutions close to
(\bar{R},0)
are given by
(R,\tau z_{1}+\psi(R,\tau))
. However, these correspond to balls, and are therefore trivial for the problem.

A similar argument shows that there are no nontrivial solutions in

\{u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}):\,u(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=u(-z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=u(z_{1},z_{2},-z_{3})\},

and in

Finally, if we restrict ourselves to the orthogonal complement of the kernel, then by the Implicit Function Theorem the only solutions are given by
(R,0)
.
∎

A.1.
Proof of (
3.6
)

Let
B_{1}
be the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Here we show the claim (
3.6
), that is

\int_{B_{1}}\log{1\over|x-z|}dx=0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\log{1\over|z-z^{\prime}|}d\sigma(z^{\prime})=0.

Proof of (
3.6
).

Since
\int_{B_{1}}\log{1\over|x-z|}dx
is constant on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as a function of
z
, we can fix
z=(1,0)
to get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\log{1\over|x-(1,0)|}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\log\!\big(r^{2}-2r\cos\theta+1\big)\,r\,dr\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}2\log\!\big|1-re^{i\theta}\big|\,r\,dr\,d\theta

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\!\big|1-re^{i\theta}\big|\,d\theta\right)r\,dr.

Let

u(z)=\log|1-z|\qquad(z\in\{w:|w|<1\}).

Since
z\mapsto 1-z
is holomorphic and nonvanishing on the unit disk,
u
is harmonic there (it is the real part of the holomorphic function
\log(1-z)
). Hence by the mean value property for harmonic functions, for every fixed
0\leq r<1

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u\big(re^{i\theta}\big)\,d\theta=u(0)=\log|1-0|=0.

Therefore
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\!\big|1-re^{i\theta}\big|\,d\theta=0
for all
r\in[0,1)
, and consequently by the dominate convergence theorem

\int_{B_{1}}\log{1\over|x-(1,0)|}dx=2\int_{0}^{1}0\cdot r\,dr=0.

Now we treat the integral on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
Also, the function
z\to\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\log{1\over|z-z^{\prime}|}d\sigma(z^{\prime})
is constant on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, thus we fix
z=(1,0)
. So

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\log{1\over|(1,0)-z^{\prime}|}d\sigma(z^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\bigl(2(1-\cos\theta)\bigr)\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\left(\log 4+2\log\left|\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right|\right)\,d\theta.

The first term gives

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log 4\,d\theta=2\pi\log 4.

For the second term, substitute
\phi=\tfrac{\theta}{2}
, so that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}2\log\left|\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right|\,d\theta=4\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(\sin\phi)\,d\phi.

It is known that, see for instance
[
18
, Formula 4.484.3 page 582]

\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(\sin\phi)\,d\phi=-\pi\log 2.

Hence,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\bigl(2(1-\cos\theta)\bigr)\,d\theta=2\pi\log 4-4\pi\log 2=0.\qed

n\geq 2
, remember that
\Omega_{\varphi}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,:\,|x|<\varphi\left({x\over|x|}\right)\right\}
, for some
\varphi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, with
\varphi\geq 0
. Then we have the following lemma.
A proof can be found, for example, in
[
4
, Proposition 4.1]
.

Lemma A.1
.

With the parametrization
x=\varphi(z)z
,
z\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, the unit outer normal to
\partial\Omega_{\varphi}
at
x
is given by

\nu(x)=\frac{\varphi(z)z-\nabla\varphi(z)}{\sqrt{\varphi^{2}(z)+|\nabla\varphi(z)|^{2}}},\quad z\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}.

Moreover, the surface measure
d\sigma(x)
on
\partial\Omega_{\varphi}
is given by

d\sigma(x)=\varphi^{N-2}(z)\sqrt{\varphi^{2}(z)+|\nabla\varphi(z)|^{2}}\,d\sigma(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}.

In particular, the perimeter of the domain
\Omega_{\varphi}
is given by

{\mathrm{Per}}(\Omega_{\varphi})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\varphi^{N-2}\sqrt{\varphi^{2}+|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}\,d\sigma.

A.3.
The constants
C_{1,\lambda},C_{2,\lambda}
and
C(\lambda,N)

In this section, we compute the constants
C_{1,\lambda},C_{2,\lambda}
and
C(\lambda,N)
. We recall they are defined as

\displaystyle C_{1,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{|z-y|^{\lambda}}\,dy,

\displaystyle C_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|^{\lambda}}\,d\sigma(z^{\prime}),

\displaystyle C(\lambda,N)

\displaystyle=(N-\lambda)\,C_{1,\lambda}-C_{2,\lambda}.

Lemma A.2
.

For all
N\geq 2
and
\lambda\in(0,N-1)
, one has

C_{1,\lambda}=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,\Gamma\left({1\over 2}\right)\,{\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over 2\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})}.

Proof.

We observe that
C_{1,\lambda}
does not depends on the choice of
z\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, so that we can take
z
as the north pole
e_{N}=(0,\dots,0,1)
in the definition
C_{1,\lambda}
. For
y\in B_{1}
, we perform the following change of variables
y=r\omega
,
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
,
r\in(0,1)
. Then

C_{1,\lambda}=\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{|z-y|^{\lambda}}\,dy=\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{|e_{N}-y|^{\lambda}}\,dy=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}{r^{N-1}\over|r\omega-e_{N}|^{\lambda}}drd\omega.

Let
\theta\in[0,\pi]
be such that
\omega\cdot e_{N}=\cos\theta
. Then
|r\omega-e_{N}|^{2}=r^{2}-2r\cos\theta+1
. Besides

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}f(\omega\cdot e_{N})d\omega=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,\int_{0}^{\pi}f(\cos\theta)\sin^{N-2}\theta\,d\theta,

for any
f
.
Hence

C_{1,\lambda}=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,\int_{0}^{1}r^{N-1}dr\int_{0}^{\pi}{\sin^{N-2}\theta\over(r^{2}-2r\cos\theta+1)^{\lambda\over 2}}\,d\theta.

By
[
18
, formula 3.665.2, page 409]
one has

\int_{0}^{\pi}{\sin^{2\mu-1}\theta\over(a^{2}+2a\cos\theta+1)^{\nu}}\,d\theta=B\left(\mu,{1\over 2}\right)\,F\left(\nu,\nu-\mu+{1\over 2};\mu+{1\over 2};a^{2}\right),

for
Re(\mu)>0
and
|a|<1
.
We take

a=-r,\quad\mu={N-1\over 2},\quad\nu={\lambda\over 2},\quad\nu-\mu+{1\over 2}={\lambda\over 2}-{N\over 2}+1,\quad\mu+{1\over 2}={N\over 2},

and get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}{\sin^{N-2}\theta\over(r^{2}-2r\cos\theta+1)^{\lambda\over 2}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=B\left({N-1\over 2},{1\over 2}\right)\,F\left({\lambda\over 2},{\lambda\over 2}-{N\over 2}+1;{N\over 2};r^{2}\right).

Now,
[
18
, formula 7.512.4, page 821]
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{\gamma-1}(1-x)^{\rho-1}F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;x)dx={\Gamma(\gamma)\Gamma(\rho)\Gamma(\gamma+\rho-\alpha-\beta)\over\Gamma(\gamma+\rho-\alpha)\Gamma(\gamma+\rho-\beta)},

provided
Re(\rho)>0
,
Re(\gamma)>0
,
Re(\gamma+\rho-\alpha-\beta)>0.

We choose

\gamma={N\over 2},\quad\rho=1,\quad\alpha={\lambda\over 2},\quad\beta={\lambda\over 2}-{N-2\over 2},

and check that

\gamma+\rho-\alpha-\beta=N-\lambda>0,

Thus, writing
r^{2}=s
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}r^{N-1}\,F\left({\lambda\over 2},{\lambda\over 2}-{N\over 2}+1;{N\over 2};r^{2}\right)\,dr

\displaystyle={1\over 2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{N-2\over 2}\,F\left({\lambda\over 2},{\lambda\over 2}-{N\over 2}+1;{N\over 2};s\right)\,ds

\displaystyle={\Gamma({N\over 2})\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over 2\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})},

as
\Gamma(1)=1
.
Since
B(a,b)={\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\over\Gamma(a+b)}
, we conclude

\displaystyle C_{1,\lambda}

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,B\left({N-1\over 2},{1\over 2}\right)\,{\Gamma({N\over 2})\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over 2\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})}

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,\Gamma\left({1\over 2}\right)\,{\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over 2\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})}.\qed

Lemma A.3
.

For all
N\geq 2
and
\lambda\in(0,N-1)
, one has

C_{2,\lambda}=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,2^{N-2-\lambda}{\Gamma({N-1-\lambda\over 2})\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\over\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}.

Proof.

We observe that
C_{2,\lambda}
does not depends on the choice of
z\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, so that we can take
z
as the north pole
e_{N}=(0,\dots,0,1)
in the definition
C_{2,\lambda}
. Thus

\displaystyle C_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\frac{1}{|z-y|^{\lambda}}\,d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\frac{1}{|e_{N}-y|^{\lambda}}\,d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\frac{1}{(2(1-y_{N}))^{\lambda\over 2}}\,d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\sqrt{1-y_{n}^{2}}\,\mathbb{S}^{N-2}}\frac{1}{(2(1-y_{N}))^{\lambda\over 2}}\,d\sigma(y)dy_{N}

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\int_{-1}^{1}{(1-y_{N}^{2})^{N-3\over 2}\over(2(1-y_{N}))^{\lambda\over 2}}\,dy_{N}

\displaystyle={|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\over 2^{\lambda\over 2}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-y_{N})^{N-3-\lambda\over 2}\,(1+y_{N})^{N-3\over 2}\,dy_{N},

Changing variables
2t=1-y_{N}
, we get

\displaystyle C_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=2{|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\over 2^{\lambda\over 2}}\int_{0}^{1}(2t)^{N-3-\lambda\over 2}\,(2(1-t))^{N-3\over 2}\,dt

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,2^{N-2-\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}t^{N-3-\lambda\over 2}\,(1-t)^{N-3\over 2}\,dt

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,2^{N-2-\lambda}B\left({N-3-\lambda\over 2}+1,{N-3\over 2}+1\right),

where
B(a,b)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt
is the Beta function. Since
B(a,b)={\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\over\Gamma(a+b)}
, we conclude

C_{2,\lambda}=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\,2^{N-2-\lambda}{\Gamma({N-1-\lambda\over 2})\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\over\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}.\qed

Lemma A.4
.

For all
N\geq 2
and
\lambda\in(0,N-1)
, one has

C(\lambda,N)=-\lambda\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(\frac{N-\lambda}{2})}.

Proof.

Taking into account Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
and the Legendre multiplication formula (
4.5
), one has

\displaystyle C(\lambda,N)=(N-\lambda)\,C_{1,\lambda}-C_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\Gamma\left({N-1\over 2}\right)\left((N-\lambda){\Gamma({1\over 2})\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over 2\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})}-2^{N-2-\lambda}{\Gamma({N-1-\lambda\over 2})\over\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|{\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\over\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}\left(\frac{N-\lambda}{2(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}{\Gamma({1\over 2})\,\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})}-2^{N-2-\lambda}\Gamma\left({N-1-\lambda\over 2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\sqrt{\pi}{\,\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\over\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}\,\left(\frac{N-\lambda}{2(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})}{\Gamma(N-\lambda)\over\Gamma({N\over 2}+1-{\lambda\over 2})}-\frac{\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(\frac{N-\lambda}{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{N-2}|\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma({N-1\over 2})\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(\frac{N-\lambda}{2})}\,\left(\frac{N-\lambda-1}{N-1-{\lambda\over 2}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(\frac{N-\lambda}{2})}\,\left(\frac{N-\lambda-1}{N-1-{\lambda\over 2}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}}{N-1-\lambda/2}\frac{\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(N-1-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma(\frac{N-\lambda}{2})}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(N-1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(N-{\lambda\over 2})\Gamma\left(\frac{N-\lambda}{2}\right)}.\qed
