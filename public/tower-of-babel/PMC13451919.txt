In vitro evaluation of sacituzumab govitecan in non-small cell lung cancer with actionable genomic alterations
Authors: Yerim Park, Soyeon Kim, Tae Min Kim, Miso Kim, Jeonghwan Youk, Dong-Wan Kim, Bhumsuk Keam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•SG showed cytotoxicity in NSCLC cell lines harboring actionable genomic alterations.•SG efficacy was independent of TROP2 surface expression levels.•SG effectiveness correlated with payload sensitivity and higher SLFN11 expression.•SG induced stronger apoptosis and DNA damage responses than SN-38 alone.•SG was rapidly internalized in NSCLC cell lines with differential drug sensitivity.Keywords: Antibody–drug conjugate, TROP2, Sacituzumab govitecan, Non-small cell lung cancer, Actionable genomic alterations

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for over 85% of all lung cancer cases and is the leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. The advent of sequencing and integrative multi-omics analyses has enabled the discovery of diverse actionable genomic alterations (AGAs), thereby expanding therapeutic opportunities [2,3]. Despite this progress, clinical responses are heterogeneous even among patients with identical AGAs, and the emergence of acquired resistance remains an inevitable challenge [[4], [5], [6]].

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), constituting a monoclonal antibody, a cytotoxic payload, and a chemical linker, have developed as an advanced class of targeted therapeutics [7]. The antibody specifically binds to tumor-associated surface antigens, inducing internalization of the antigen-antibody complex and subsequent intracellular release of the cytotoxic payload, ultimately leading to tumor cell death. TROP2-targeted ADCs, such as sacituzumab govitecan (SG) and datopotamab deruxtecan, have been thoroughly explored across numerous tumor types [8,9]. Clinical studies in NSCLC have shown that TROP2-targeted ADCs have promising antitumor activity, particularly in patients with AGAs or prior immunotherapy resistance, although consistent statistical significance in the overall population has not been shown [[10], [11], [12]]. Mechanistic insights into TROP2-directed ADCs have been largely derived from preclinical investigations in other cancers [[13], [14], [15]], whereas comparable analyses in NSCLC are rare.

The relationship between ADC efficacy and target antigen expression remains complex. For example, SG was approved for triple-negative breast cancer regardless of TROP2 status, with clinical benefit reported across a wide range of expression levels, although patients with intermediate-to-high TROP2 expression tended to exhibit more favorable responses [16]. To clarify this issue and elucidate the activity and mechanistic basis of SG in NSCLC harboring AGAs, we characterized TROP2 expression at multiple levels in 16 NSCLC cell lines and assessed its association with drug sensitivity. We also investigated the role of Schlafen 11 (SLFN11) —a predictive marker of susceptibility to DNA-damaging agents such as SN-38 [17] —to determine whether SG efficacy could extend beyond oncogenic driver dependencies. The purpose of this study is to investigate the in vitro cytotoxicity of SG in NSCLC cell lines with AGAs.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and cell viability assay

The culture media and source information for the 16 NSCLC cell lines used in this study are provided in Table S1. The NSCLC cell lines were seeded in 96-well plates at appropriate densities and stabilized for 24 h. Cells were then exposed to serial dilutions of SG (cat. No. D4002, Selleckchem) or SN-38 (cat. No. S490801, Selleckchem) for 72 h. Cell viability was assessed by incubation with Chromo-CK reagent (cat. No. CH-10,000, Chromogen) at 37°C for 30 min to 4 h, followed by measurement of absorbance at 450 nm. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated using SigmaPlot 15.0 (RRID: SCR_003210), and drug sensitivity was visualized with GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (RRID: SCR_002798).

siRNA transfection

TROP2 knockdown using siRNA was performed as described in the Supplementary Methods.

RT-qPCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed as described in the Supplementary Methods. Relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method and log10-transformed for graphical presentation.

Western blot

Cell pellets were lysed using cell lysis buffer (cat. No. 9803, Cell Signaling Technology). Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and protein-containing supernatants were collected. Protein extracts were mixed with LDS sample buffer (cat. No. NP0007, Invitrogen) and reducing agent (cat. No. NP0009, Invitrogen) and separated on 4–12% bis-Tris acrylamide gels (cat. No. NP0335, Invitrogen). Antibodies used for western blot are described in the Supplementary Methods.

FACS analysis of TROP2 expression

Cell-surface TROP2 expression was evaluated by flow cytometry. Cells were incubated with PE-conjugated TROP2 antibody (RRID: AB_2738975). After fixation with 2% paraformaldehyde, cells were analyzed on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). For intracellular TROP2 detection, cells were permeabilized with Fixation/Permeabilization Solution (cat. No. 51–2090KZ, BD Biosciences) and stained with the same antibody. After fixation, intracellular fluorescence was measured.

Apoptosis assay

CUTO17 and SNU-3173 cells were exposed to SG or SN-38 for 48 h. Following incubation, cells were collected and resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer (cat. No. 556454, BD Biosciences). Cell suspensions were transferred to FACS tubes and stained with Annexin V-FITC (cat. No. 556419, BD Biosciences) and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; cat. No. 00–6993–50, Invitrogen). Apoptotic cells were quantified by flow cytometry within 30 min of staining and data were processed using FlowJo 10.10.0 (RRID: SCR_008520).

Internalization assay

SG was labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 using an Alexa Fluor 488 Conjugation Kit (cat. No. ab236553, Abcam) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were preincubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated SG (SG-Alexa488) for 1 h at 4°C. The cells were then incubated at 37°C for 15 min to 3 h to allow SG internalization. Surface-bound fluorescence was quenched by resuspending the cells with Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (RRID: AB_2532697). After the cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde and analyzed by flow cytometry, the internalization rate was calculated as described in the Supplementary Methods.

Confocal microscopy for lysosomal localization of SG

Cells were stained with Hoechst33342 (cat. No. H3570, Invitrogen), followed by incubation with Lysotracker Deep Red (cat. No. L12492, Invitrogen). The cells were then treated with SG-Alexa488 and preincubated for 1 h at 4°C in the dark. After washing with cold DPBS, the cells were resuspended in fresh medium and incubated at 37°C for 15 min to 3 h. Subsequently, the cells were seeded into coverglass chambers (cat. No. 155411PK, Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by a brief centrifugation. Internalization and lysosomal colocalization were then immediately examined without fixation using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (RRID: SCR_002798). The details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

TROP2 expression was systematically assessed in all NSCLC cell lines at both the RNA and protein levels, including cell-surface and intracellular localization (Fig. 1, Fig. S1 and Table 2). TROP2 transcripts were detected in all cell lines, with particularly low levels observed in NCI-H23 cells (Fig. 1A). TROP2 protein expression was examined together with SLFN11 protein. These analyses revealed heterogeneous expression of TROP2 and SLFN11 across the cell lines (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1). Cell-surface TROP2 was quantified using the normalized membrane ratio (NMR), defined as the proportion of membrane-localized TROP2 relative to cytoplasmic TROP2. Cell-surface expression of TROP2 was further classified into four categories—Negative, Low, Moderate, or High—based on criteria established in this study (Table S2). Four cell lines (HCC827, EBC-1, NCI-H596, and NCI-H3122) exhibited high cell-surface TROP2 level, whereas NCI-H23 had no detectable cell-surface TROP2 expression (Table 2). TROP2 transcript and protein levels were significantly associated with TROP2 cell-surface expression (data not shown).

Comparative analysis of cytotoxic responses to SG and SN-38

We evaluated the in vitro cytotoxicity of SG and its unconjugated payload SN-38 following 3 days of drug exposure (Fig. 2, Table S3, and Fig. S2). A comparative analysis of SG and SN-38 sensitivities is presented in Fig. 2A, with TROP2 cell-surface expression and relative SLFN11 protein levels indicated at the bottom of the panel. The cell lines are arranged in order of increasing drug responsiveness. To assess the efficacy of SG, we compared its cytotoxic activity to that of SN-38 by normalizing the SN-38 IC50 values (Table S3). SG inhibited cell proliferation more potently than SN-38, indicating that ADC-mediated delivery enhanced antitumor activity in the NSCLC cell lines (Fig. 2A–C and Fig. S2). Among these, NCI-H596 cells, which harbor a MET exon 14-skipping mutation, displayed resistance to SN-38, so the IC50 value could not be determined (Table S3). Cell lines that were more responsive to SN-38 also showed more pronounced susceptibility to SG, regardless of oncogenic driver alterations. Histologic subtype did not exhibit a consistent impact on SG activity (Table S3).

To elucidate potential determinants of drug response, we examined correlations between SG cytotoxicity and several factors associated with drug sensitivity (Fig. 2D and 2E; Fig. S3). SG activity did not differ significantly among subgroups stratified by TROP2 cell-surface expression (High vs. Moderate or Low; Fig. 2D). Neither TROP2 mRNA nor TROP2 protein expression showed any notable association with SG activity (P = 0.99 and P = 0.41; Fig. S3A and S3B). Analysis by NMR levels showed a non-significant trend toward greater SG cytotoxic effects in cell lines with higher NMR (P = 0.17; Fig. S3C). Consistent with these findings, siRNA-mediated TROP2 knockdown resulted in comparable SG-induced cytotoxicity to that observed in control cells, suggesting that reduced TROP2 expression alone may not be sufficient to affect SG sensitivity (Fig. S4). By contrast, SG cytotoxicity strongly correlated with SN-38 responsiveness (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2E) and was significantly associated with SLFN11 expression (P = 0.02; Fig. S3D).

Activation of DNA damage response and apoptosis by SG

To delineate the mechanisms underlying differential SG sensitivity, we analyzed apoptosis using Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD staining in the CUTO17 and SNU-3173 adenocarcinoma cell lines, which had EGFR exon 20 insertions and exhibited a large disparity in SG IC50 values between the two cell lines (Fig. 2B and 2C). Following 48 h of treatment with SG or SN-38, the early and late apoptotic fractions were added (Q2 + Q3) to quantify apoptotic cells (Fig. 3A). Compared with SN-38 or the untreated control, SG induced markedly greater apoptotic cell death in both cell lines, with SNU-3173 cells showing particularly pronounced apoptosis induction (P < 0.0001; Fig. 3B). These findings were consistent with the cell viability results (Fig. 2B and 2C).

We also examined markers of DNA damage response (DDR) and apoptosis signaling in both cell lines (Fig. 3C and 3D; Fig. S5). Phosphorylation of ATR, CHK1, and H2A.X was assessed as evidence of DDR, and cleavage of caspase-3, caspase-9, and PARP was evaluated to confirm activation of apoptosis signaling. Cells were exposed to SG or SN-38 for 6–48 h prior to analysis. Both drugs triggered phosphorylation of DDR proteins and cleavage of apoptotic markers, indicating induction of DNA damage-mediated cell death. Notably, SG treatment resulted in stronger activation of DDR and apoptosis pathways than SN-38, and the magnitude of response was consistently higher in SNU-3173 cells than in CUTO17 cells (Fig. 3C and 3D).

Cellular internalization and lysosomal trafficking of SG

To investigate cellular internalization of SG and its influence on drug responsiveness, we incubated CUTO17 and SNU-3173 cells for 15 to 180 min with SG-Alexa488. After incubation, surface-bound fluorescence was selectively quenched using anti-Alexa Fluor 488 antibody, thereby allowing measurement of internalized SG. The resulting fluorescence intensity increased progressively with the incubation time in both cell lines, with more than 50% of the SG taken up within 1 h, indicating rapid uptake of the conjugate. Although the overall internalization kinetics were comparable between the two cell lines, CUTO17 cells, characterized by lower SG sensitivity, showed marginally higher internalization (∼80%) at 180 min compared with SNU-3173 cells (Fig. 4A). When SG-Alexa488 was analyzed without surface quenching, CUTO17 cells showed about two fold higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) compared with SNU-3173 cells (data not shown), reflecting the elevated TROP2 expression in the former (Table 2).

(A) The CUTO17 and SNU-3173 cells were incubated with SG-Alexa488 for 15 to 180 min. The internalization rate (%) was quantified as described in the Materials and methods. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Statistical significance was determined by two-tailed t-test (ns, not significant). Intracellular trafficking of SG was visualized by confocal microscopy after staining with Hoechst33342 (nuclei, blue), LysoTracker (lysosomes, red), and SG-Alexa488 (green) in CUTO17 (Fig. 4B) and SNU-3173 (Fig. 4C) cells. Colocalization of lysosomes and SG was observed in both cell lines, evidenced by yellow fluorescence from the overlap of green and red signals. Internalized SG was trafficked to the lysosomes within 3 h, with no apparent difference in localization pattern between the two cell lines (Fig. 4).

Discussion

SG demonstrated potent in vitro efficacy compared with its unconjugated payload, SN-38, across all 16 NSCLC cell lines, regardless of genomic subtype or TROP2 expression level. Consistent with clinical observations of TROP2-targeted ADCs [11,12], these findings indicate that SG may have potential activity in NSCLC with AGAs.

Previous studies have reported that the relationship between ADC therapeutic efficacy and target antigen expression remains controversial [16]. We characterized TROP2 expression and explored its association with the cytotoxic activity of SG across NSCLC cell lines with diverse genetic and histologic backgrounds. TROP2 was widely expressed, and its membrane abundance generally reflected its transcript and total protein levels. Although antigen presence is required for ADC binding [18], the effectiveness of SG did not appear to be strictly dependent on the TROP2 expression level. Cell lines categorized as High, Moderate, or Low for TROP2 cell-surface expression displayed comparable drug responses. In clinical trials, patients with QCS-NMR-positive tumors (NMR ≤ 0.56) treated with datopotamab deruxtecan demonstrated a more favorable response than those treated with docetaxel, with objective response rates of 32.7% and 10.3%, respectively [19]. By contrast, our experimental results did not show a significant correlation between NMR and SG IC50 values, although cell lines with higher NMR values tended to be more susceptible to SG.

The antitumor activity of SG correlated with the intrinsic responsiveness to SN-38 and the expression level of SLFN11, suggesting that sensitivity to the payload is a major factor of SG efficacy. SLFN11 has been reported to enhance cellular vulnerability to DNA-damaging agents, including SN-38 [17], so we explored its involvement in modulating SG activity. Higher SLFN11 expression has been linked to increased susceptibility to SG in preclinical models [13]; however, its impact on clinical outcomes in NSCLC patients remains uncertain. Our cytotoxicity analyses revealed that SG induced more pronounced inhibition of tumor growth than SN-38, suggesting that ADC-mediated delivery may enhance the antitumor activity of SN-38.

SN-38, the cytotoxic payload of SG, exerts its activity by inhibiting topoisomerase I, an enzyme that relieves DNA supercoiling during transcription and replication. Inhibition of topoisomerase I results in irreversible double-strand DNA breaks, ultimately triggering apoptotic cell death [20]. In line with this mechanism, SG induced more distinct cleavage of caspases and PARP, as well as increased phosphorylation of H2A.X and CHK1, compared with SN-38. We further found that SG was swiftly internalized and trafficked to lysosomes; however, these processes alone could not fully account for the observed differences in SG responses. This implies that additional cellular mechanisms, beyond payload delivery, underlie the therapeutic efficacy of SG.

Several limitations in our study should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were performed under in vitro conditions, without accounting for the influence of tumor microenvironment, pharmacokinetics, or immune-mediated interaction. In vivo validation was not conducted using xenograft or patient-derived models, which restricts the translational relevance of our findings. Second, the biomarker evaluation was exploratory and centered on predefined candidates, rather than employing biomarker discovery platforms such as RNA-sequencing or proteomics. Further studies integrating multi-omics approaches are warranted to identify predictive biomarkers and confirm their associations in clinically relevant settings. Third, the diversity and representativeness of our cell lines were limited. Although the 16 NSCLC cell lines encompass diverse genomic backgrounds, they do not fully reflect the molecular or histologic complexity of patient tumors, and rare mutations or resistant subclones were not included. Fourth, SG was assessed without comparison to an isotype control ADC, and immune-related mechanisms were not investigated. Fifth, the drug concentrations and exposure durations used in vitro may not precisely mirror in vivo pharmacodynamics, which limits direct extrapolation of our IC50 data to clinical outcomes. Finally, the potential contribution of extracellular payload release due to linker instability was not directly assessed in this study.

Despite these limitations, our study comprehensively assessed TROP2 expression, including normalized expression levels, in relation to SG responsiveness, revealing that the antitumor effect of SG is closely associated with the activity of its cytotoxic payload. In addition to cytotoxic profiling, we explored the contribution of internalization dynamics, encompassing cellular uptake and lysosomal trafficking, to drug sensitivity. These data provide preclinical support for the use of SLFN11 expression and intrinsic payload susceptibility as candidate biomarkers for SG response.

In conclusion, SG exhibited marked and consistent antitumor effects in diverse NSCLC cell lines. Its efficacy appeared to be primarily influenced by intrinsic cellular sensitivity to SN-38, rather than by genomic alterations or TROP2 abundance. These results reinforce the therapeutic potential of TROP2-directed ADCs and emphasize the need for integrative multi-omics biomarker strategies and in vivo validation to advance the translational development of ADCs for NSCLC.
