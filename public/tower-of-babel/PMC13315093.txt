Complication assessment in full-endoscopic uniportal lumbar spine surgery: A prospective analysis from 304 patients
Authors: Christoph J Siepe, Christoph Mehren, Franziska Heider, Dominik Mell, Wolgang Hitzl, Uwe Platz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFull-endoscopic uniportal spine surgery is increasingly used as a minimally invasive alternative to conventional open or microsurgical techniques. To date, prospective data on complication profiles and procedure-related risk factors remain limited, at times with inconsistent results across the literature and potential underreporting of complications.Research questionTo prospectively assess complication and reoperation rates following full-endoscopic uniportal spine surgery in a consecutive patient cohort and to identify relevant risk factors that may be associated with adverse events.Materials & methodsA prospective cohort of 304 consecutive patients undergoing full-endoscopic uniportal procedures was analyzed. Introduction

Endoscopic spine surgery performed through a uniportal approach has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to traditional open spine surgery, offering well-known benefits of MIS surgery such as reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery times and others (Jiang et al., 2022; Perez-Roman et al., 2022; Goh et al., 2021; Pairuchvej et al., 2020; Kotheeranurak et al., 2023a, 2023b; Chiang et al., 2022; Komp et al., 2015; Cong et al., 2016; Muthu et al., 2021).

The surgical technique utilizes small incisions and specialized instruments to access and treat spinal pathologies, thereby minimizing access related tissue disruption.

Advancements of the surgical technique and surgeon training have expanded the indications for full-endoscopic uniportal spine procedures, leading to an increasingly widespread use and application on a global scale (Hsu et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2024; Cong et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2022; Ruetten et al., 2008a; Dolas et al., 2025).

Despite its advantages, the adoption of endoscopic spine surgery has been met with concerns regarding its safety and efficacy, particularly in relation to reported complication and reoperation rates. Whilst numerous studies have highlighted the efficacy of endoscopic spine surgery for a range of degenerative spinal conditions, there remains a paucity of high-quality and prospectively gathered data specifically addressing the nature, frequency and management of peri- and postoperative complications. Published evidence remains heterogeneous, with divergent and at times conflicting results, likely reflecting differences in study design, case mix and outcome definitions, furthermore underscoring the need for additional prospectively gathered data (Liu et al., 2023; Ju et al., 2022; R et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022a; Chen et al., 2020).

The primary objective of this study was therefore to analyze the complication rates associated with full-endoscopic spine surgery performed through a single uniportal approach, to compare these findings with existing literature and to discuss the implications for clinical practice. Findings from such complication analyses may guide future technical and procedural developments, thereby further optimizing the technique and enhancing patient safety.

Preoperative diagnosis was established in all patients based on standard X-Rays (ap-, lateral views as well as functional flexion–extension radiographs). Similarly, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed in all cases.

Clinically, patients described corresponding intolerable unilateral or bilateral leg pain with radiculopathy, gait disturbances and/or symptoms of spinal claudication. All patients were non-responders to prior conservative treatment or were included because of intolerable pain or progressive neurological deficit.

All cases were considered technically amenable to a full-endoscopic interlaminar approach. Thus, mediolateral-bordering foraminal pathologies were included, whilst strict extraforaminal disc herniations that are not accessible through the interlaminar corridor were excluded from the present analysis, which was intended to homogenize the present study cohort.

No revision procedures were included in this study. Accordingly, patients with any history of prior lumbar spine surgery at the index level were excluded from participation. Contraindications furthermore included tumor and trauma pathologies as well as previous fusion surgeries.

Surgical technique

Previous studies have often reported pooled outcomes from heterogeneous patient cohorts, combining surgeries performed for different pathologies and using both transforaminal and interlaminar approaches alike.

However, the transforaminal approach differs substantially from the interlaminar technique. It has a unique safety profile with a distinct spectrum of complications which is not comparable to that of the interlaminar approach, and it is also used for a different spectrum of indications (Chang et al., 2025; Jitpakdee et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2020; Hofstetter et al., 2020; Kotheeranurak et al., 2023c). Thus, in order to ensure a methodologically homogenous study cohort, only endoscopic spine surgeries performed via the interlaminar approach were included in this analysis, mainly for the reasons given the procedural and indication-related differences.

Likewise, the biportal technique constitutes a fundamentally distinct surgical approach and it is important to emphasize that the present series exclusively comprises uniportal procedures.

The technique and surgical steps of the interlaminar approach have been thoroughly outlined previously (Komp et al., 2011; Ruetten et al., 2008b, 2009; Siepe et al., 2018a, 2018b, 2023). The surgery is performed under general anesthesia without administration of muscle relaxants. All patients received single-shot antibiotic prophylaxis. The patient is positioned in a prone position. The midline is marked at the targeted level on the patient's skin surface under ap-fluoroscopy. A small, approx. 8 mm skin incision is placed in the midline or slightly paramedian on the side of the approach. The fascia is incised just medial of the spinous process. The dilator, working sleeve and finally the endoscope are placed at the transitional junction between the facet joint and the interlaminar window. From here on the surgery is performed under full-endoscopic visualization with high resolution images being displayed on large HD/4K-screens through 25° angled optics. The surgery is performed under continuous irrigation with sterile saline solution through an endoscopic system which is designed as an ´open system', in which the saline solution is automatically drained outwards, hereby avoiding the risk of creating excessive intraspinal pressure. This outward drainage is a design feature of the endoscope that was used in the present setting which cannot be stopped by the surgeon.

With a wide variety of surgical instruments available such as scissors, Kerrison rongeurs, bone burrs and others, the surgical access to the spinal canal is created by resection of bone and yellow ligaments. Depending on the underlying pathology, a decompression of the neural structures is achieved under full-endoscopic visualization by performing an ipsilateral recess decompression, removal of an accompanying disc herniation with a discectomy or sequestrectomy, by performing on over the top decompression or a combination of the aforementioned as outlined previously.

In cases of lumbar disc herniation, the surgical strategy was to perform a sequestrectomy alone whenever feasible, which also reflects the author's minimally invasive treatment philosophy. Although a more aggressive discectomy may theoretically reduce the risk of recurrent disc herniation by decreasing the amount of residual disc material, extensive disc removal may also contribute to subsequent accelerated disc degeneration (Adams et al., 2006; de Schepper, 2010; Masuda et al., 2005). Therefore, the author's approach is to limit any kind of defect to the annulus as well as intradiscal manipulation whenever possible, and to reserve additional discectomy for selected cases with an increased risk of recurrence, such as in the presence of large annular defects (Miller et al., 2018).

Lastly, a drain was not routinely placed at the end of the surgery. Patients were mobilized immediately after surgery.

Data acquisition

All data were recorded within the framework of an ongoing prospective clinical trial. All cases including postoperative readmissions were entered into the clinics administration system prospectively as part of the institution's quality assurance assessment. The data acquisition was performed by members of the clinic's spine unit. Data on intraoperative complications and reoperations were reported by the surgeons. The peri- and postoperative course of all surgeries was furthermore controlled independently by members of the spine unit who followed all surgeries and surgical protocols and who specifically searched the procedures for any kind of critical event that had occurred. Postoperatively, any patient who re-attended our institution and in whom additional diagnostics confirmed a procedure-related adverse event was recorded in this analysis.

Given that the primary aim of this study was perioperative risk and complication assessment, a minimum observation period of 6 months was defined for inclusion in this study (range: 6-30 months).

According to the institutional quality-assessment criteria, a recurrent disc herniation occurring within the first 3 months after the index procedure was defined as a postoperative complication. Thus, the present analysis focuses on an assessment of early recurrent cases which may be more likely be related to the index procedure, whereas a recurrent disc herniation occurring beyond this interval is more likely to reflect the natural course of degeneration of the affected disc and which was therefore not evaluated in this present investigation.

Statistical analysis

Data were checked for consistency. Univariable logistic regression analysis with odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were used to analyze various risk factors. Odds ratio for age was computed for an increase of 10 years and for time of surgery for an increase of 60 min. Barnard's test for superiority was used to analyze the relation between number of segments >1 and the risk of hematoma. All reported tests were two-sided, and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses in this report were performed by use of STATISTICA 13 (Hill, T. & Lewicki, P. Statistics: Methods and Applications. StatSoft, Tulsa, OK) and PASW 29 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0., Armonk, NY). Data analysis was performed as per protocol, and all statistical analyses were performed by an independent biostatistician.

ASA classification

Based on the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification, ASA I was recorded in 75/304 patients (24.7%), ASA II in 161/304 (53.0%), ASA III in 61/304 (20.1%), and ASA IV in 1/304 (0.3%), respectively (Table 1).

Surgical spectrum

As outlined in Table 2, a bilateral over-the-top decompression was performed for the treatment of clinically symptomatic lumbar spinal canal stenosis with corresponding symptoms of spinal claudication in n = 111/304 operated cases (36.5%).

A clinically relevant compression resulting from a lumbar disc herniation was treated in n = 196 cases. The vast majority of these procedures were performed as a sole sequestrectomy (n = 170/196; 87%), whereas an additional discectomy with further debridement of intradiscal tissue was required in n = 26/196 cases (13%).

A unilateral recess decompression was performed in n = 30/304 cases (9.9%).

The majority of all interventions were performed as combined procedures, consisting of over-the-top decompression, lateral recess decompression, with adjunct discectomy or sequestrectomy where required, depending on the underlying pathology.

A detailed overview of all complications that were encountered in this present investigation is provided below.

Dura lesions

An incidental durotomy that was caused by an intraoperative laceration of the dural sack was noted in n = 2 patients (0.6%). Both lesions were considered relevant lesions and therefore prompted an intraoperative conversion to an open microscopic approach for primary suture repair.

In one additional case (0.3%), a patient became clinically symptomatic with signs of CSF leakage following an initially uneventful surgery, which subsequently required a surgical revision. A remnant prominent bony spike which was in contact with the dural sac appeared to be the most likely explanation for the dura lesion which was removed accordingly.

All three cases healed completely without the need for any further reoperations.

Recurrent disc herniation

A recurrent disc herniation was noted in n = 6 patients during the postoperative course following a previous endoscopic discectomy (n = 196 cases), resembling on overall rate of recurrent disc herniation of 3.1%. All cases were revised surgically.

Epidural hematoma

An epidural hematoma was recorded in n = 7 patients (2.3%), all of which occurred in patients aged 71–88 years at the time of surgery (mean 78.4 ± 5.9 years). Thus, a subsequent analysis revealed that patient age emerged as a significant risk factor for the development of postoperative bleeding with compressive epidural hematoma formation (p = 0.03).

In 3 out 7 postoperative bleeding events the initial surgeries were performed bi-segmentally (n = 3), one case was observed following a 3-segmental intervention, indicating that 4/7 cases occurred after multisegmental surgeries.

Among these 7 patients, n = 5 were classified as ASA II, indicating mild systemic disease, whereas n = 2 patients were categorized as ASA III, consistent with severe systemic comorbidity. Thus, the severity of pre-existing comorbidities as assessed by the ASA classification did not appear to be a relevant risk factor for the occurrence of epidural hematoma in the present cohort (p = 0.48).

Similarly, neither body mass index (BMI, p = 0.95) nor the patient's absolute body weight (p = 0.58) had a statistically significant impact on the occurrence of postoperative epidural hematoma.

Univariable logistic regression analysis of risk factors for postoperative epidural hematoma

In patients with single-level surgeries, a postoperative epidural hematoma occurred in 1.3% (3/232). This rate increased to 5.56% (4/72) when surgeries were performed bi-segmentally (Fig. 1). The corresponding odds ratio was 4.85 (95% CI: 0.98-20.6). Barnard's test for superiority revealed that this increased risk was statistically significant (p = 0.03).

Risk of hematoma versus number of operated segments.

Likewise, a significant correlation was detected between the patient's age and risk of hematoma. Whilst the risk for postoperative epidural hematoma is estimated to be 1.7% for patients with 70 years of age, this risk increased to 8% for patients with 80 years of age, which corresponds to an odds ratio/10 years of 4.85 (95% CI: 1.54-15.29, p = 0.007; Fig. 2). We also found a significant odds ratio/1 h for the duration of surgery, which was 2.34 (95% CI: 1.1-4.96, p = 0.03, Fig. 3).

Increasing operating time was significantly correlated with the occurrence of postoperative hematoma, with an odds ratio/1 h for the time of surgery of 2.34 (95% CI: 1.1-4.96, p = 0.03).

The concomitant presence of multiple risk factors combined was associated with a further substantial increase in the risk of bleeding. A further sub-analysis revealed that the combined effect of both age >70 and the number of segments exceeding >1 on the risk of hematoma was 10.5% (4/38), as compared to only 1.1% (3/266) in patients with age ≤ 70 and only single-level procedures (p < 0.006, Fig. 4). The corresponding odds ratio is 10.3 (95% CI: 2.2-48.1, p = 0.005) suggesting a significant impact that the combined presence of these risk factors may have on the risk for development a postoperative epidural hematoma.

The concomitant presence of multiple risk factors was associated with a substantial increase in the risk of bleeding. The combined effect of both age >70 and the number of segments exceeding >1 on the risk of hematoma was 10.5% (4/38), versus 1.1% (3/266) in patients with age ≤ 70 and only single-level procedures (OR 10.3, 95% CI 2.2-48.1; p < 0.006).

Infection/wound healing

No deep surgical site infections were observed in this present series of 304 patients.

One patient (n = 1/304; female athlete, ultra-distance runner) developed a local reaction to a subcutaneous suture, accompanied by a mild and transient secretion (n = 1/304; 0.3%). Following conservative wound management, this local reaction resolved completely.

General complications/others

One young patient, a high performance athlete, developed a transient 3′rd degree atrioventricular block with complete asystole intraoperatively. Following immediate and appropriate anesthesiologic pharmacological management, the patient stabilized and the procedure was resumed and completed. The patient subsequently underwent intensive care monitoring and was referred for further cardiologic evaluation.

Another patient with lumbar spinal canal stenosis developed recurrent symptoms of radiculopathy several months after a prior over-the-top decompression. The underlying cause was a progressive bulging of the disc which had occurred since the time of surgery. The patient therefore underwent a revision decompression, this time including an additional discectomy.

Discussion

Microsurgical techniques have been established as a ´gold standard' for the surgical treatment of lumbar compression syndromes after failed conservative management. Over the past years, full-endoscopic spine surgeries have been introduced as the next step towards further minimizing the approach-related morbidity and soft-tissue trauma. However, with the implementation of novel surgical techniques, a robust assessment of patient safety, particularly in terms of complication patterns and reoperation rates remains a central prerequisite for their clinical evaluation and broader adoption. To date, the available evidence is still limited. Many published series are based on heterogeneous patient cohorts, and the reported complication and reoperation rates remain, at times, divergent across studies (Liu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022a).

The present study provides a comprehensive analysis of complications associated with full-endoscopic spine surgery, reflecting a real-world clinical scenario and delineating the spectrum and incidence of adverse events that may be expected when implementing this technique into routine practice.

Key strengths of the present study include the clear definition of the patient cohort, resulting in a homogeneous series with well-specified treated pathologies. The cohort was furthermore strictly confined to uniportal full-endoscopic procedures performed via the interlaminar approach, an important aspect given that many previous reports have pooled data from interlaminar and transforaminal techniques within the same cohort, thereby reducing interpretability and limiting the validity of comparative complication analyses (Chang et al., 2025; Jitpakdee et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2020; Hofstetter et al., 2020; Kotheeranurak et al., 2023c).

Given that the primary aim of this study was perioperative risk and complication assessment, a minimum observation period of 6 months, ranging from 6 to 30 months, was considered sufficient for these purposes. It is important to note that the approach used in this present investigation may potentially underestimate complication rates, as events that did not re-present themselves at our institution and that were treated elsewhere may have gone unnoticed. However, as major complications requiring revision surgery are typically referred back to our center, this may mitigate this limitation.

Overall complication assessment

One of the goals of this present study was to provide valuable insights that can inform surgeons, healthcare providers, and patients about the risks and benefits of endoscopic spine surgery. Overall, the present data demonstrate an acceptable rate of complications and reoperations following uniportal full-endoscopic spine surgery with an overall complication rate of 6.6% and a reoperation rate of 4.9%. These results are in line and comparably to complication and reoperation rates which have previously been reported for conventional microsurgical “gold standard” techniques (Chen et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022b).

Importantly, no severe cerebrovascular complications (e.g., stroke) were observed in the present investigation, although such events have previously been reported in association with endoscopic procedures. In this context, a key technical aspect is that all surgeries in this series were performed using spine-specific instruments which were designed as an “open system” in which irrigation fluid continuously drains outwards, a mechanism which cannot be stopped by the surgeon, thereby minimizing or eliminating the risk of excessive intraspinal pressure. Conversely, mostly for reasons of availability or cost, surgeons across the globe may have utilized arthroscopic- or ´non–spine-approved, closed systems' with less controlled outflow characteristics. Thus, one of the recommendations derived from this study is the consistent use of approved, spine-dedicated endoscopic systems in order to mitigate or eliminate the risk of such potentially detrimental neurological complications.

Dural tears

Reported durotomy rates in endoscopic series are largely driven and influenced by indication mix and learning curve. Systematic reviews of endoscopic spine surgery typically report dura lesion rates of 2–3%, with ranges extending to approximately 8–9% in selected series (Muller et al., 2018). In a transforaminal approach, dural tears may potentially remain unrecognized. In contrast, during the interlaminar approach, which was used in the present study, the close-range visualization of neural structures makes unnoticed ´occult' dural injury unlikely. Nevertheless, a small residual risk of unrecognized dural lesions persists, as minor defects may remain clinically silent postoperatively due to the low access-related dead-space volume.

The incidence of dural tears resulting from an intraoperative laceration of the dural sheath was 0.6% (2/304) in this present series. The treatment of such dura lesions may vary depending on the surgeon's judgment and preference. Whilst open revision was the preferred treatment choice for the few cases encountered in this cohort, other treatment options are available for larger or clinically relevant defects, including endoscopic suturing or coverage using patches such as TachoSil® or Lyoplant®. Very small lesions of ≤1 mm may, in selected cases, be left untreated, comparable to an incidental puncture following epidural infiltration, since no relevant dead-space cavity that would sustain continued CSF leakage is created.

Several factors may have contributed to this low incidence of dural tears that was encountered in this present investigation. First, revision procedures were not included, reducing the risk to encounter adhesions or altered intraoperative anatomy. The operations and the surgical steps are also highly standardized, thus furthermore reducing technical variability and contributing to a clear visualization of the neural structures in the hands of an experienced surgeon. However, the cohort also comprised a relevant number of advanced-to-severe stenosis cases as well as a substantial proportion of multilevel interventions (n = 72; 24%) and elderly patients (32% of patients >70 years), a subgroup in whom dural fragility has similarly been linked with a higher susceptibility to incidental dural tears.

Taken all these factors into account, these findings demonstrate that full-endoscopic surgeries performed through a uniportal approach can be performed safely in appropriately selected patients, provided that surgeon experience is sufficient and that the critical procedural steps of endoscopic decompression are strictly adhered to.

Recurrent disc herniation

Muthu et al. in a large meta analysis reported a 4.8% rate of recurrent disc herniation following endoscopic discectomy in 54 out of 1119 patients (Muthu et al., 2021). In a review on recurrent lumbar disc herniation, Shepard and Cho summarized recurrence rates after lumbar discectomy in the range of approximately 5–15% (Shepard et al., 2019). Miller et al. reported a recurrence rate of 7-18%, with an increased risk for patients with large annular defects (OR 2.5) (Miller et al., 2018). Likewise, Abdu et al. reported an 8-year reoperation rate of 9.1% for recurrent intervertebral disc herniation in the SPORT cohort following lumbar discectomy (Abdu et al., 2017).

The data from this present investigation revealed an overall rate of recurrent disc herniation of 3.1% (n = 6/196). In this context, it is important to note that, according to our institution's quality-assessment program, the present data reflect early recurrences occurring within the first 3 months after the index procedure, which may therefore be considered potentially surgery-related. Later recurrences beyond this interval were not captured as complications in the present analysis and may more likely reflect the natural degenerative course of the affected disc, rather than a surgery related adverse event.

When the early recurrences reported in the present study are considered together with later recurrent disc herniations that may be expected during longer-term follow-up, the resulting overall recurrence rate would likely fall within the range of data that have previously been reported in the literature as outlined above.

Postoperative epidural hematoma

The endoscopic approach results in a smaller access-related dead-space compared with open, microscopic, or biportal techniques. While this may be beneficial in the context of limiting CSF leakage, it may, however, allow even small postoperative hematomas to cause a clinically relevant compression earlier when compared to open or microsurgical techniques.

The present data demonstrate that, despite the further reduction in surgical invasiveness that can be achieved with full-endoscopic techniques, postoperative bleeding with formation of a compressive epidural hematoma still remains a relevant adverse event. Previously published data may have underreported the risk of hematoma. Furthermore, controlling intraoperative bleeding has been reported as one of the potential challenges in endoscopic spine surgery (Dolas et al., 2025).

In this series, the observed overall incidence of epidural bleeding was 2.3% (n = 7 out of 304 cases).

These postoperative bleeding events occurred despite of the fact that the majority of these patients (n = 5/7) were being classified as ASA II, indicating only mild systemic disease and who were therefore considered to be otherwise healthy subjects.

Thus, epidural hematoma remains to be one adverse event which is largely influenced by a combination of higher procedural complexity and patient-related vulnerability which needs to be taken into consideration, even in a seemingly healthy but still elderly patient population.

Risk factors for epidural hematoma

The present analysis revealed that patient age emerged as a significant risk factor for the development of postoperative bleeding (Fig. 1, p = 0.03). Among the seven postoperative bleeding events, all patients were aged 71–88 years at the time of surgery (mean 78.4 ± 5.9 years), with no bleeding event noted in patients <70 years of age. Whilst the risk for postoperative epidural hematoma is estimated to be 1.7% for patients with 70 years of age, this risk increased to 8% for patients with 80 years of age, which corresponds to an increase by 4.85 (Fig. 2, p = 0.007).

OR time as well as the number of treated levels similarly emerged as significant risk factors for the development of epidural hematoma (Table 5). In patients with single-level surgeries, a postoperative epidural hematoma occurred in 1.3% (3/232) versus 5.56% (4/72) when surgeries were performed bi-segmentally, which was statistically significant (Fig. 1; p = 0.03).

In this context, however, as multilevel procedures inherently entail longer operating times, their independent effects cannot be reliably separated in the present analysis. Unlike open multilevel decompressions with a continuous surgical corridor that may enable bleeding to reach neighboring segments (potentially additive, cumulative ´pooling'-effect), full-endoscopic interventions require a separate access for each level, making clinically relevant bleeding between segments less likely. Thus, this observation suggests that prolonged operative time (as opposed to number of segments operated) may be the more dominant risk factor, particularly in an elderly patient population.

In summary, our findings suggest that the combination of higher procedural complexity and patient-related vulnerability may be central to this observation.

Technical and procedural considerations

A range of other factors may account for the heterogeneous epidural hematoma rates that have been reported in the literature. For example, the development of a clinically relevant postoperative hematoma is less likely when the decompression is pursued via a transforaminal approach. However, whilst a mere lateral recess may perhaps be addressed via the transforaminal approach, the authors from this study consider the transforaminal approach less suited in this context and would rather recommend the interlaminar approach as the technique of choice for an adequate bilateral over-the-top decompression of spinal canal stenosis.

Finally, previous studies have shown that lumbar decompression surgery may result in an incidence of asymptomatic compressive postoperative epidural hematoma reaching up to 58% of operated cases (Sokolowski et al., 2008a). Conversely, Kao et al. in a study of 15.562 patients who underwent lumbar decompression procedure with/without instrumentation reported that the rate of symptomatic epidural hematoma may be as low as only 0.16% (Kao et al., 2015). These widely diverging data show that the clinical decision making on when to revise an epidural hematoma may vary considerably between studies, depending on radiographic findings, the patient's clinical symptomatology as well as individual, surgeon-specific decision-making (Sokolowski et al., 2008b).

The use of postoperative drains after lumbar decompression remains controversial (Davidoff et al., 2018; Muthu et al., 2020; Kanayama et al., 2010). In our practice, drains are not placed routinely after full-endoscopic lumbar procedures, as their use has not been shown to reliably prevent postoperative epidural hematoma. In addition, drains may be associated with disadvantages such as impaired early mobilization, patient discomfort, even sustained postoperative bleeding as well as the creation of an additional potential portal for infection. Moreover, in healthcare systems where endoscopic spine surgery is performed on an outpatient basis, routine drain placement may be impractical. Therefore, our institutional policy is to avoid drain placement, although the authors acknowledge that surgeons may consider placing a drainage as an appropriate option in selected cases where increased intraoperative bleeding is encountered.

In the present series, revision surgery was undertaken mostly in patients with significant postoperative discomfort. Although some of these cases might have resolved under conservative management, an earlier revision with evacuation of the hematoma was considered to facilitate faster symptom relief and reduce postoperative morbidity.

In summary, there appears to be meaningful potential to further reduce postoperative bleeding rates through upcoming technical advancements. As such, future developments and innovations may contribute to further optimize hemostasis and especially bleedings from cancellous bone. Evaluation of fluid irrigation temperature, a factor that has been shown to influence hemostasis in cerebral/brain surgery, anesthesiologic adjuncts such as PEEP-ventilation or administering medications such as tranexamic acid may furthermore improve peri- and postoperative hemostasis. Collectively, these measures may contribute to further lowering the incidence of postoperative hematoma and, consequently, enable further improvements in the safety profile of full-endoscopic surgeries.

Surgical site infection (SSI)/wound healing complications

The globally increasing burden of antimicrobial resistance (i.e. the “rise of superbugs”) represents a major challenge for modern health care systems with respect to the associated morbidity and mortality, especially in light of increasingly constrained therapeutic options and significantly increasing costs. This development is paralleled by the steadily rising prevalence of overweight, a well-established risk factor that furthermore predisposes to SSI (Agha et al., 2017; James, , 2008).

In this context, the experiences of the last years and decades have revealed that the extremely low rate of surgical site infection (SSI), which was 0% in this present cohort, is one of the main advantages of full-endoscopic, uniportal spine surgeries. These numbers suggest that the risk of SSI can be reduced to a minimum, even in patients with high risk profile such as diabetes, chronic corticosteroid medication, obesity and others.

Several technical factors may explain this observation. The skin incision and wound surface are markedly reduced, and the working cannula provides a tension-free sealing of the access corridor, thereby limiting exposure to environmental contamination compared with an open wound cavity (Kang et al., 2024). The absence of retractor systems similarly avoids tensioning of the surrounding soft tissue. Finally, the procedure is performed under continuous irrigation with sterile saline solution, which may further decrease local bacterial load.

Thus, full-endoscopic uniportal surgery may be particularly advantageous in patients with a relevant risk profile for the development of SSI and wound-healing disturbances.

Conclusion

The current analysis provides relevant additional information about the safety and spectrum of complications of a new surgical technique of full endoscopic spine surgery besides the existing data in this field.

Full endoscopic surgeries performed through a uniportal approach can be considered safe with an acceptable complication and reoperation rate.

In this series, endoscopic surgeries stand out with a 0% complication rate for infection or deep wound healing problems.

Other complications, however, such as the occurrence of epidural hematomas may be underestimated and underreported in the current literature, which may most likely be attributed to different surgical techniques, different inclusion criteria and varying patient comorbidities across cohorts, making it difficult to compare studies.

The data from this present investigation demonstrate that the occurrence of complications is largely influenced by a combination of higher procedural complexity and patient-related vulnerability, even in a seemingly healthy but still elderly patient population.

Future technological developments such as hemostasis control may help to further reduce perioperative risks.
