Introduction

Livestock and poultry manure management in the U.S. is a greenhouse gas (GHG) intensive
process, emitting 81.7 MMT CO2e in 2022 (1.5% of net U.S. GHG emissions).1 The primary GHG
is methane (CH4), with 2,312 kt released in 2022 (9% of U.S. CH4 emissions).1 Manure
management methods are commonly categorized by whether they are anaerobic (“wet”) or
aerobic (“dry”) techniques. Although dry methods manage the largest share of manure, the
majority of GHG emissions are generated during storage of manure in anaerobic conditions –
typically in water-filled tanks, pits, or lagoons.1 There has been a 65% increase in emissions from
1990, primarily due to an increasing cattle population.1 Also, this rise in population has been
coupled with a rise in animal confinement and density, which typically adopt wet manure
management methods.1,2 Within wet methods, anaerobic bacteria proliferate and decompose
volatile solids (VS) within the manure in a process called anaerobic digestion to produce roughly
equal mixtures of CH4 and carbon dioxide (CO2).1,3 These GHGs are fugitive, in that they are
assumed to be released to the atmosphere and contribute to GHGs within U.S. GHG inventories.1
If the methane is captured and purified (i.e., “upgraded”) this simultaneously mitigates GHGs that
would have otherwise been released and produces a valuable energy product known colloquially
as Renewable Natural Gas (RNG). Such processes are acknowledged by U.S. policy through
programs such as the U.S. Renewable Fuels Standard (RFS), the federal Clean Fuel Production
Credit (45Z), and state clean fuel standards (CFS).4–9 In the 45Z and CFS schemes, the GHG
emissions of the business-as-usual (BAU) manure management system is taken as a baseline,
and credits are received based on GHG reductions relative to this baseline. Thus, estimating the
GHG emissions of the BAU scenario (also known as the “counterfactual”) is necessary.

The following outlines key updates to the manure management pathways in the research and
development version of the Greenhouse gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in
Technologies model (R&D GREET):
 • Updates of BAU manure management modeling, which include:
 o Animal populations and national shares of manure management practices for the
 beef, dairy, swine, and poultry (broilers, layers, and turkeys) sectors.
 o Updating methane conversion factors (MCFs) for anaerobic lagoons, liquid/slurry
 tanks, and deep pits of BAU manure management practices in the contiguous
 United States.
 • Adding an estimation methodology for methane emissions from digestate storage after
 anaerobic digestion.

This report illustrates the impacts of these updates on a well-to-wheel basis using the example of
a heavy-duty compressed natural gas (CNG) transit bus. Based on the work in this report, we
highlight opportunities for future model development.

Updates of business-as-usual (BAU) manure management modeling

Background: Historical treatment within R&D GREET 2024 Rev.1
By default, R&D GREET 2024 Rev.1 modeled the BAU GHG emissions (AR6/GWP-100yr) for
animal-manure management (functional unit: 1 lb total solid manure) as a national average of
manure management practices for each sector (i.e. dairy cow, beef, swine, etc).3 The model
accounted for emissions of CH4, CO2, and N2O – as well as accounting for partial carbon
sequestration when any residual manure was land applied.3,10 A depiction of the system boundary
can be found in Figure 1 below. Biogenic CO2 was assumed to be carbon neutral and does not
contribute to GHG emissions.

Background: MCF calculations for wet manure management methods in the EPA GHGI
As mentioned, the EPA GHGI reports methane conversion factors for anaerobic lagoons,
liquid/slurry tanks, and deep pits by U.S. state and sector (𝑀𝐶𝐹𝑖𝑠,𝑚 ).1 The methodology
underpinning the reported MCFs follows Mangino et al. (2001).13 The steps to compute the MCFs
for the EPA GHGI are reproduced below as background:
 1. The monthly weighted-average ambient temperature for a state is calculated using the
 county population estimates and average monthly temperature in each county. Monthly

Updates in R&D GREET 2025
Recently, the IPCC updated its recommendations for estimating region-specific MCFs from liquid
manure storage systems.12 In particular, a MCF calculator was provided for detailed, regionally
specific MCF calculations with the following recommended parameters:
 𝑐𝑎𝑙 𝑐𝑎𝑙
 • The activation energy is changed from 15,175 𝑚𝑜𝑙 to 19,347 𝑚𝑜𝑙 with a reference
 temperature (T1) of 308.15 K based on more recent experiments. This change generally
 results in a smaller 𝑓-factor at all relevant temperatures (Figure 3).
 • Instead of using ambient air temperature, the manure temperature is estimated from the
 air temperature and is used for the 𝑓 factor. Specifically, the manure temperature is
 assumed to lag air temperature by one month, and if there is only one manure cleanout
 per year the ambient temperature is dampened by 3°C, otherwise the ambient
 temperature is used. Thus, manure temperatures are slightly higher than ambient during
 winter months, and slightly lower during summer months. This tends to result in fewer
 methane emissions because the 𝑓 factor is exponentially dependent on temperature.
 Furthermore, a temperature floor of 1°C is assumed.14
 • The cleanout schedule and quantity should be specified, if known. Additionally, a cleanout
 efficiency factor of 95% was proposed (5% of the manure being removed remains in
 storage). This factor was based on the practical observation that manure storages were
 never 100% clean even after a cleanout.12
 • The MDP used in the Mangino et al. (2001) model is no longer used.
 • Emissions should be modeled for a total of 36 months to achieve stabilized methane
 emissions predictions (i.e., steady state) and the last 12 months should be used to
 compute the annual average MCF.
The combination of the above methodological changes and updated parameters (e.g. Ea) will be
denoted as the “IPCC (2019) method”. For R&D GREET 2025, we adopt the IPCC (2019) method
to compute MCFs for anaerobic lagoons, liquid/slurry, and deep pits.

Updated cleanout schedules

Cleanout schedules describe the residence time of manure in anaerobic conditions. The less
frequent manure storage is cleaned out for land application, the longer the manure remains in
anaerobic conditions and generates methane. Manure land application timing is highly regional
and depends on the nutrient needs of local crops, the weather, and on the needs of the farmer.
In North Carolina for instance, certain regulation requires farmers to develop manure nutrient
management plans which list what crops are grown, how much plant available nitrogen farmers
are allowed to apply, and the application window in which they are allowed to apply it.16,17
Application windows can be broad (for example, wheat grain shows a window of September -
March) and application can occur at any time during that window based on the farmer’s needs
(e.g., emptying lagoons before the rainy season to have enough storage).17 At the federal level,
facilities designated as Concentrated Animal Feeding Operations (CAFOs) by the EPA must
submit nutrient management plans (NMPs) as part of water permit approval.18 These plans are
not readily available. Record-keeping rules require farms to maintain land application records,
including manure application timing and quantities, but they are not required to submit them.

 • In the absence of state- or region-specific data, we follow U.S. EPA GHGI assumptions:
 anaerobic lagoons are cleaned annually in October, and liquid/slurry and deep pit systems

Digestate Storage Methane Emissions

Solids exiting an anaerobic digester (the digestate) retain residual methane potential, and measurable
emissions occur when this material is stored under anaerobic conditions.23–28 In recognition of these
emissions, United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) guidance states: “If the
undegraded volatile solid in the effluent from an anaerobic digester is discharged outside the project
boundary without further treatment, these emissions should be treated as leakage and appropriately
reported and accounted.”29 This framework was included in the Climate Action Reserve (CAR) U.S.
Livestock Protocol and incorporated directly in the California Air Resources Board (CARB) Compliance
Offset Protocol and incorporated by reference in the Low Carbon Fuel Standard Program through the CA-
GREET4.0 Tier 1 Calculator for Biomethane from Anaerobic Digestion of Dairy and Swine Manure
Pathways (Tier 1 calculator).30–32 The Tier 1 calculator assumes that 30% of the methane potential in fresh-
manure volatile solids remains in the digestate and is not captured by the digester. This residual methane
potential is converted to fugitive methane emissions during storage by applying the IPCC (2006) methane
conversion factor (MCF) for liquid/slurry tanks, which varies with annual average temperature. 33 Nearly all
certified dairy and swine manure digesters in the LCFS program use anaerobic storage (e.g. effluent ponds)
for digestate storage.34

Internationally, the IPCC (2019) Refinement provides default anaerobic digester emission factors that
depend on digestate storage type (open storage or gas-tight covers).12 In the European Union (EU), the
Renewable Energy Directive (RED II Annex VI) specifies default emissions factors that distinguish between
open and closed tank digestate storage, further highlighting the importance of this unit operation.35

Despite confirmed experimental measurements and widespread adoption in domestic and international
energy programs, and despite current inclusion of these emissions in LCA of wastewater treatment sludge
and municipal solid waste RNG within R&D GREET, previous editions of R&D GREET assumed no
digestate storage emissions for animal-manure based RNG production.10 This update accounts for methane
emissions associated with open digestate storage, with covered storage modeling planned for a future
update. Figure 6 illustrates where the digestate storage unit operation will sit within the manure RNG system
boundary in R&D GREET. We note that this is not an expansion of the system boundary, but rather an
inclusion of an omitted operation within the same boundary.

Recommendations for future work

In this report, we described new features available in R&D GREET 2025 for animal manure-derived
anaerobic digestion pathways, but we underscore the need for further research in this area.

Several important data and methodological gaps remain that we hope can be addressed in future updates:

 1. Cleanout schedules for manure storage during BAU manure management: Accurate
 predictions of methane emissions at the national-level require cleanout schedules for every sector
 in every state because cleanout frequency determines manure residence time in anaerobic
 conditions—a critical driver of predicted methane emissions. This study used the most granular data
 available, but significant gaps remain for swine (all states except Iowa and Minnesota where we
 relied on personal communication), for poultry (all states), and for dairy (only multi-state regional
 schedules are available). According to the EPA CAFO permit requirements, farms are required to
 keep detailed records of manure land application timing on-site.18 We recommend interacting with
 state agencies, USDA, and the EPA to access these records and develop a bottom-up (facility-level)
 anonymized database to hold manure land application timing data to develop county or state-level
 average cleanout schedules. In future versions of R&D GREET, users will also have the flexibility to
 input their own farm-specific cleanout schedules, rather than relying on national or state-level
 averages.

 2. Further empirical validation of MCF modeling approach: While the IPCC (2019) method for
 estimating methane emissions from anaerobic lagoons was partially validated on an annual basis
 (Appendix C), its accuracy diminishes when applied to monthly timesteps (Appendix F). Enhancing
 precision at this temporal resolution is critical, particularly given the relevance of monthly cleanout
 schedules. A recent study has demonstrated promising results using daily timestep first-order
 models to simulate greenhouse gas emissions from wastewater sludge digestate storage.41 To
 advance model development and facilitate robust comparisons, future research should prioritize the
 creation of a comprehensive database of high-quality experimental data that can serve as a
 standardized benchmark.

 3. Digester default performance and digestate storage emissions:
 • In this update, we adopt the CAR and LCFS CA-GREET4.0 approach of using IPCC (2006)
 liquid/slurry MCFs to quantify methane emissions from digestate storage. In future versions, the
 IPCC (2019) approach which will allow users to account for cleanout schedule and monthly
 temperatures will be implemented as an option. Moreover, future versions may include
 capabilities for modeling covered digestate storage tanks, especially when these are more
 commonly adopted in the U.S. as these have been outside of the U.S.35 Additionally, for storage
 that are specifically liquid/slurry tanks, a direct N2O emission factor should also be applied, in
 accordance with IPCC guidelines.33 Currently, we assume the storage has zero direct N2O
 emissions, in-line with an anaerobic lagoon digestate storage system, common for California-
 based digester projects (i.e., an “effluent pond”).23 In the future, we will provide the user the
 option to model either a liquid/slurry tank or lagoon storage and apply the appropriate N2O
 emission factor accordingly.

 • The approach to estimate the residual ultimate methane potential of the digestate in this work
 relies on the default digester technology methane conversion factor, but these factors were
 developed over a decade ago.3 We recommend interacting with CARB to analyze detailed
 operational data submitted by in-the-field anaerobic digesters as part of the CA LCFS. The data
 23

 contained in that program represents the most exhaustive, up-to-date, and verified data on U.S.-
 based anaerobic digester performance. This would serve dual purposes of updating R&D
 GREET modeling capabilities for digesters writ-large and further validating our current estimates
 for emissions from BAU anaerobic lagoons by comparison with covered lagoon digester data
 (point #2 in recommendations). This interaction could also lead to data on commonly employed
 digestate storage types (i.e., effluent ponds or lagoons, tanks, etc.).
 • Additionally, we plan to implement an option for modeling the “U.S. mix” of digesters based on
 distributions available in public databases, such as AgSTAR.42

 4. Adjusting animal populations based on currently deployed digesters: Animal population data
 was not historically adjusted to exclude populations feeding digesters in previous versions of R&D
 GREET, nor for this version. This would have small, but quantifiable impact on all population
 weighted-average parameters in this work. A future update will perform this exclusion at the county-
 level by cross-referencing public databases, such as AgSTAR.42

 5. Solid-liquid separation of raw manure or digestate: Many livestock and anaerobic digestion
 facilities already employ solid–liquid separation units either upstream of the digester, treating raw
 manure, or downstream, treating digestate before liquid storage.34 This separation step reduces the
 VS content in subsequent unit operations, which can decrease biogas production potential in the
 digester when separation occurs upstream and reduce fugitive methane emissions during digestate
 storage when it occurs downstream. The separated solid fraction is typically stockpiled in a manner
 similar to solid manure storage, and the associated greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions should be
 explicitly accounted for. In the next version of R&D GREET, we plan to incorporate functionality that
 allows users to model a range of solid–liquid separation technologies and configurations, including
 their effects on VS distribution, biogas yields, and GHG emissions.
Counterfactual analysis for waste-derived RNG is advancing rapidly and will undergo regular updates and
refinements as more research is conducted.

Appendix C: Justification for employing the IPCC (2019) method for modeling methane emissions from
uncovered anaerobic lagoons

In the original Mangino et al. (2001) study, the authors compared methane production emissions predictions
with two empirical measurements: #1) year-long measurements from a covered anaerobic lagoon at a swine
farm in North Carolina (NC) and #2) 3-month-long winter measurements from a covered anaerobic lagoon
at a dairy farm in North Carolina.13,40 In both cases, their model could be scaled by 0.79 to match
experimental measurements, leading to a recommended MDP factor of 0.8 for the model in general.
However, the authors underscored that this parameter was uncertain and its transferability beyond the
studied systems was unknown.13

For the swine farm measurements in Mangino et al. (#1), only the total biogas production was measured -
a 70% average methane content by volume was assumed to estimate methane production from those
measurements.13 EPA AgSTAR suggests that the methane content of biogas from digesters can vary from
50% to 75%, while other sources indicate contents as high as 80%.40,43,44 Had the authors assumed a range
of average methane contents from 50% to 80%, all of which could have been possible, their predictions
would have still overestimated measurements by 78% and 11%, respectively (Table 5). As such, the
required MDP factor would have been 0.56 had they assumed a 50% methane content, or 0.9 had they
assumed 80%. In contrast, when the same system is modeled using the IPCC (2019) method, the annual
methane production is within the possible range of experimental measurements (Table 5). For this
comparison case, we have only been focused on annual methane production – in Appendix E we show that
all models have reduced accuracy when compared on a monthly-basis to experimental measurements,
highlighting the need for further improvements.

For the dairy farm comparison (#2), Mangino et al. (2001) predicted an average methane production rate
of 86 m3 per day for December through March, while Safley and Westerman (1991) measured a value of
68 m3 per day for the same months (we note that methane content was quantified). 40 To enable their
prediction, Mangino et al. (2001) estimated the monthly volatile solids input flow to the lagoon by considering
the number of dairy cows (150 head), the average weight of the cows (604 kg), and a constant VS excretion
rate (8.45 kg VS per 1,000-kg cow).13 This led the authors to estimate a VS input flowrate of 766 kg VS per
day for this lagoon.13 However, a VS input flowrate of 263.9 kg VS per day was measured and reported by
Safley and Westerman (1991) for this lagoon.40 Thus, Mangino et al. (2001) simulated methane emissions
from a system with nearly three times the VS than was measured. Had Mangino et al. (2001) used the
reported VS input flowrate (as was done in the swine farm comparison (#1)), they would have predicted an
average methane production rate of around 30 m3 per day, which is an underestimate compared with Safley
and Westerman (1991) and would have necessitated an MDP of 2.25.40 Thus, had Mangino et al. (2001)
considered the possible range of methane contents for biogas in experimental comparison #1 and applied
their methodology consistently to #2 by utilizing the measured VS input flowrates, an MDP of 0.8 would
 30

have likely not been recommended. Nevertheless, using the IPCC (2019) method, we estimate an average
methane production rate of 8 m3 per day (using the measured VS inputs), suggesting that both methods
are not capable of reproducing this specific experimental benchmark.

For R&D GREET 2025, we adopt the IPCC (2019) method for estimating emissions from anaerobic lagoons,
as it may offer improved accuracy for some cases without requiring ad hoc adjustments (Table 5). It also
utilizes updated parameters for activation barriers and estimates manure temperature (rather than using
ambient air temperature), which should be more physically relevant (i.e., methane production occurs within
the manure, not within the surrounding air).12 Nevertheless, given the discussion in the preceding
paragraphs, we underscore the need for further empirical validation across more diverse experimental
datasets to rigorously evaluate the reliability of the modeling approaches employed herein.
