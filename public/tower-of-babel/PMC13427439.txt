Protocol for purifying iPSC-derived retinal pigment epithelial cells by sequential digestion and intrinsic autofluorescence sorting
Authors: Fengming Yang, Rui Li, Xinrun Lv, Boling Chu, Jiuhua Luo, Rongjing Ge, Yongjun Luo, Laibin Ren, Bei Liu, Jing Hu, Haijiang Zhang, Hao Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInduced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC)-derived retinal pigment epithelium cells serve as a promising cell source for transplantation therapy in age-related macular degeneration. Here, we present a protocol for purifying the mature retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells post-differentiation. We describe steps for sequentially digesting cells with different enzymes and sorting them based on the autofluorescence of mature RPE cells, avoiding fixation and immunostaining. By maintaining high cell viability and purity, this cost-efficient protocol has broad implications for both in vitro research and transplantation applications.Subject areas: Cell separation/fractionation, Flow Cytometry, Stem Cells, Cell Differentiation

Before you begin

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of vision impairment among the elderly. At present, transplantation therapy shows great promise as a treatment for AMD.1 Several clinical trials have shown that subretinal transplantation of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or embryonic stem cells (ESCs) can help maintain or restore vision in patients.2,3,4 These findings establish a foundation for advancing cell-replacement therapy for AMD. While existing protocols for the differentiation, assessment, and purification of RPE cells are established,5,6,7,8 further optimization is necessary to enhance safety and efficiency while reducing costs. A cost-effective, high-efficiency protocol will benefit both therapeutic applications and related research endeavors.

In this protocol, we describe an efficient and cost-effective approach for the differentiation and purification of RPE cells. We begin with the culture of iPSCs, detailing procedures for thawing, passaging, and freezing. Next, we describe the differentiation of iPSCs into RPE cells using small molecule reagents and recombinant growth factors. We then outline the assessment of RPE cells maturity by immunofluorescence. Following this, we detail a sequential digestion process using Papain and Accutase to digest intercellular connections. Finally, we describe the flow cytometry-based sorting of cells according to the spontaneous fluorescence of mature RPE cells, eliminating the need for antibody staining and thereby reducing processing time and costs. Adhering to this protocol yields purified mature RPE cells with high viability and purity for further research.

Innovation

After differentiation from iPSCs, mature RPE cells typically establish a basement membrane layer between the cells and the culture plate, as well as tight junctions between adjacent cells. These connections complicate enzymatic digestion when adhering to traditional protocols. To address this challenge, we have developed a sequential digestion protocol utilizing two enzymes: Papain and Accutase. Papain targets the basement membrane, while Accutase specifically addresses cell-cell junctions. This innovative approach significantly enhances both the efficiency of digestion and the viability of cells post-digestion.

Previous protocols for achieving the purity of mature RPE cells generally involve fixation and antibody staining prior to FACS, which are always time-consuming and incur additional costs for antibodies. Moreover, fixed cells are unsuitable for subsequent in vivo applications. Consequently, many earlier methods rely on manual selection of target cells using a needle under microscopic observation when preparing cells for transplantation, raising concerns about the reliability of purity. In contrast, our protocol employs flow cytometry to sort cells based on the autofluorescence of mature RPE cells. This method allows for immediate sorting after digestion without staining, thereby minimizing processing time and eliminating antibody costs. Additionally, this approach enhances cell purity while maintaining high viability.

Expected outcomes

We have described a detailed method for differentiating and purifying RPE cells from iPSCs. The differentiated cells were validated by immunofluorescence and subsequently enriched by FACS. When differentiation is initiated from a single well of a 12-well plate, the expected yield at day 60 (before FACS) is approximately 11.2 ± 1.6 million cells (n = 3). During FACS, the proportion of positive cells is typically 51.9 ± 4.5% (n = 3). The positive population exhibits pronounced pigmentation, whereas the negative population appears largely non-pigmented. Post-sorting, the cells maintain high viability and can be readily reseeded. 61.0 ± 3.5% (n = 3) of cells adhere within 24 hours and continue to proliferate over subsequent days (Figure 8).

Reseeding and proliferation of mature RPE cells following FACS

Sorted mature RPE cells retain the ability to attach and proliferate after reseeding, demonstrating their viability and functional capacity post-sorting. Scale bar: 300 μm.

RNA-seq analysis was performed to compare the positive and negative populations after sorting. The positive cells displayed higher expression levels of multiple RPE cell markers. The GO terms analysis of significantly upregulated genes revealed strong enrichment for biological processes and cellular components associated with RPE identity, supporting the effectiveness of our sorting strategy (Figure 9).

RNA-seq analysis of FACS-sorted RPE cell subpopulation

(A) Cluster heatmap of two cell subpopulations, each represented with two biological replicates.

(B) R780-high cells expressed higher levels of RPE cell marker genes, including TYR, RGR, RLBP1, BEST1, and RPE65, which are critical for the functional properties of mature RPE cells.

(C) GO term enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes indicates that R780-high cells are strongly associated with RPE-related functions, whereas R780-low cells display gene expression patterns more closely related to muscle cells.

The RNA of cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the RNA-seq Library Prep Kit from Vazyme. The FASTQ-format reads were trimmed by Trim Galore, and the expression levels of transcripts were quantified using Salmon. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using the DESeq2 package in R, and GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R.

We further compared our approach with a previously published sorting method based on the surface marker CD140b.17 Cells sorted by our approach exhibited greater pigmentation. Correlation and PCA analysis demonstrated clear separation between the two sorting strategies. Differentially expressed gene analysis showed that cells enriched using our strategy expressed higher levels of mature RPE markers (RGR and BEST1) and lower levels of progenitor or early RPE markers (PAX6 and TMEFF2). These results indicate that our approach obtains more mature RPE cells (Figure 10).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Calculation of cell death rate and viable cells per well

The cell death rate and the number of viable cells per well are determined by quantifying the concentrations of dead and live cells using a Neubauer counting chamber with Trypan blue staining (Figure 6D and 6E).

Limitations

The RPE differentiation and purification protocol described here may not be universally applicable to iPSCs from other species (such as murine) or ESC lines. This protocol has only been validated with human iPSCs. We have not attempted to use human ESC lines or iPSCs from other species, and these cell lines may require optimization. Certain lines may not respond effectively to this approach, necessitating the use of an alternative protocol or selecting a different cell line.

The FACS strategy employed in this protocol relies on the intrinsic autofluorescence of mature RPE cells, which is likely attributable to the pigment production. Therefore, non-RPE cells capable of synthesizing pigment, such as melanocytes, may also be captured in the positive subpopulation.

Due to resource limitations, the characterization of FACS-purified cells in this study was primarily based on RNA-seq analysis. Functional assessments, such as transepithelial electrical resistance (TER) measurements and phagocytosis assays, were not performed. Further functional validation would be beneficial to more thoroughly validate and refine this purification protocol.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Some iPSCs may fail to detach after EDTA digestion. Meanwhile, the detached cell aggregates are too small, which will lead to a significant amount of cell loss. Section “iPSC culture and passage”, step 3g.

Potential solution

This issue may arise from passaging iPSCs too late. To avoid this, do not culture cells for more than one week without passaging. If this problem occurs, refrain from prolonging EDTA treatment and discard all undetached cells.

Problem 2

Significant cell death occurs between days 6–8. Section “differentiation of RPE cells from iPSCs”, step 10.

Massive cell death starts at day 6, leading to low cell density by day 8. With continuous cell loss, only a few isolated viable cell spots remain by day 16.

Potential solution

The initial cell density is crucial in addressing this issue. There are some reasons that may contribute to a low starting cell density.

•Centrifuge: On day 0 (step 6), we use D0-2 RDM medium to directly resuspend the iPSCs, thus avoiding centrifugation for cell collection. Approaches that collect cells by centrifugation or by simple sedimentation result in significant loss of small aggregates, which negatively reduces the starting cell density.

•Over-digestion: Over-digestion of iPSCs with EDTA at the onset of differentiation may result in overly small aggregates or a high percentage of single cells. These small aggregates and single cells have reduced survival rates between day 0 to 2, leading to a low starting cell density.

Problem 3

Significant cell death occurs between days 8–16. Section “differentiation of RPE cells from iPSCs”, step 11.

During days 8–16, some cells undergo an increase in volume followed by death. In severe cases, almost all cells exhibit this phenomenon, leading to differentiation failure.

Potential solution

•Effectiveness of recombinant proteins: The reduced effectiveness of recombinant proteins, particularly Activin-A, caused by improper storage may contribute to this issue. To mitigate this, ensure all recombinant proteins are aliquoted and avoid repeated freezing and thawing.

•Cell state: Different iPSC lines may exhibit variable responses to differentiation conditions. Consider switching to an alternative cell line if differentiation repeatedly fails. Additionally, the passage number of iPSCs may impact the differentiation outcomes. In our experience, iPSCs with a passage number greater than 30 tend to fail in differentiation.

Problem 4

Failure of BEST1 Immunofluorescence. Section “identification of mature RPE cells by immunofluorescence”, step 17.

Potential solution

The expression of the RPE marker BEST1 depends on the maturity of the cells. Verify that the RPE Maturation Medium is properly prepared. Additionally, consider extending the maturation period at step 14 if further maturation is required.

Problem 5

Failure to separate cells during digestion. Section “FACS-based purification of mature RPE cells by autofluorescence”, step 18g.

Some cells fail to detach from the plate after Accutase digestion, while some cells remain aggregated together even after prolonged digestion.

Potential solution

Extend the Papain digestion process. Adequate Papain digestion is critical. Incomplete Papain digestion may lead to this issue. After sufficient Papain digestion, cells should be completely detached. At this stage, gently pipetting up and down can help dissociate the cell sheets into smaller aggregates. It is important to note that the digestive actions of Papain and Accutase are distinct, and extending Accutase treatment will not compensate for incomplete Papain digestion.

Problem 6

Cells are contaminated by bacteria after FACS. Section “FACS-based purification of mature RPE cells by autofluorescence”, step 20f.

Potential solution

•Perform FACS in a biosafety cabinet to maintain a sterile environment. Ensure the flow cytometer is thoroughly cleaned before sorting.

Perform FACS in a biosafety cabinet to maintain a sterile environment. Ensure the flow cytometer is thoroughly cleaned before sorting.

•Following flow sorting, supplement the RPE Maturation Medium with twice the standard concentration of penicillin-streptomycin to prevent contamination.
