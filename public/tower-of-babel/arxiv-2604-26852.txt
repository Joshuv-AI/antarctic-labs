Largest eigenvalue and top eigenvector statistics of large Euclidean random matrices Preprint: APS/123-QED
Pasquale Casaburi, Pierpaolo Vivo

Abstract
Euclidean random matrices arise in a wide range of physical systems where interactions are determined by spatial configurations, including disordered media and cooperative phenomena in atomic ensembles. Unlike classical random matrix ensembles, their entries are strongly correlated through the geometry of the underlying random points, making their analytical treatment challenging. While global spectral properties such as the spectral density are relatively well understood, much less is known about extremal eigenvalues and the associated eigenvectors, despite their central role in applications. Here we address the problem of characterising the largest eigenvalue and the corresponding top eigenvector of large Euclidean random matrices with a quadratic kernel. For vectors in any dimension d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 drawn independently from a common distribution, we show that both quantities can be computed within a unified replica-based framework, leading to a set of self-consistent equations involving a small number of parameters. This approach yields an explicit expression for the average largest eigenvalue, fully determined by low-order moments of the underlying distribution, and an analytical characterisation of the distribution of top eigenvector’s components in the large- N N limit. We find that the top eigenvector exhibits a non-trivial geometric structure, with components concentrating on a hypersurface determined by the same parameters controlling the largest eigenvalue. We further perform extensive numerical simulations that confirm these predictions. More broadly, our work provides a general framework to access extremal spectral properties of Euclidean random matrices.

Introduction

Random matrix theory (RMT) is a central tool in modern theoretical physics, providing a powerful framework to describe the statistical properties of large complex systems
[
20
]
. Originally introduced in statistics in the 20s
[
34
,
15
]
and later employed in nuclear physics to model the statistics of energy levels of heavy nuclei, it has since found applications across a wide range of fields, including condensed matter physics, quantum chaos, statistical inference, and wireless communications
[

,
33
,
23
]
.
Ensembles of complex hermitian or real symmetric random matrices for which spectral observables can be characterised analytically usually include either (i) rotationally invariant ensembles, whose joint probability density of the real eigenvalues is explicitly known
[
33
]
, or (ii) matrices with independent entries, which can be often analysed using the replica or cavity methods from the physics of disordered systems
[
24
,
29
,
2
]
. A very important class of models that lie outside this perimeter is provided by
Euclidean random matrices
(ERMs), whose entries are defined through a deterministic function of the positions of randomly distributed points in an Euclidean space
[
21
]
. More precisely, given a set of
N
random points
\{\underline{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an ERM is a matrix whose elements take the form

X_{ij}=f(\underline{x}_{i},\underline{x}_{j})\ .

(1)

This class of matrices naturally arises in a variety of physical problems where disorder is encoded in the spatial configuration of interacting elements, rather than in independent random entries
[
22
,
21
]
. Notable examples include vibrational spectra of glasses and amorphous solids
[
22
,
21
]
, wave propagation and Anderson localisation in random media
[
25
,
10
]
, as well as collective phenomena such as cooperative spontaneous emission in large atomic clouds
[
25
,
26
]
. A closely related class of models are distance matrices, where entries depend on pairwise distances between random points and exhibit non-trivial spectral and localisation properties
[
3
]
.

Despite their broad applicability, the theoretical analysis of ERMs is significantly more challenging than that of “classical” random matrix ensembles. The main difficulty stems from the strong correlations between matrix elements, which are induced by the underlying geometry of the random points and are generally difficult to handle analytically
[
26
]
. As a consequence, many of the standard techniques of RMT cannot be directly applied. While some analytical progress has been achieved in specific limits or using approximate or indirect methods such as high-density expansions, mean-field approximations, or mappings to simpler ensembles, most available results are still limited to global spectral properties such as the spectral density
[
26
,
22
,
5
,
16

.

The study of
largest eigenvalue
and
top eigenvector components
for Euclidean matrices has received comparatively much less attention, in spite of being an area of strong relevance for applications, as discussed below. On the one hand, this is somewhat surprising: since the pioneering work by Tracy and Widom
[
30
]
, extreme eigenvalues of random matrices have been extensively studied for both rotationally invariant ensembles (also in the large deviations regime)
[
19
]
and sparse matrices
[
27
,
17
]
. On the other hand, the
\mathcal{O}(N)
(as opposed to
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
) degrees of freedom that define Euclidean matrices and the strong geometric constraints on the entry-to-entry correlations that ensue make this task in principle way more onerous.

A particularly relevant application, where some analytical control over extreme eigenvalues would be of central importance, is cooperative light-matter interactions in disordered atomic systems. In cold atomic clouds, photon-mediated interactions between randomly distributed atoms lead to collective emission phenomena that cannot be described in terms of independent emitters
[
25
,
32
,
31
]
. In this setting, the system is described by a matrix whose entries depend on interatomic distances, thus naturally defining a Euclidean random matrix. Its eigenvalues correspond to the decay rates of collective excitation modes, distinguishing between enhanced (superradiant) and suppressed (subradiant) emission processes
[
32
,
31
]
. The extreme eigenvalues are therefore of central importance: the largest correspond to strongly superradiant modes, while the smallest are associated with long-lived subradiant states, both relevant for quantum technologies
[
32
,
31
,
12
]
. Understanding their statistics is thus essential to characterise the cooperative behaviour of the system.

Despite this direct and important connection to physical problems, a systematic analytical description of extremal eigenpairs in Euclidean random matrices remains largely underdeveloped. Some progress in this direction has been made in
[
4
]
, where the authors characterise the spectral density of Euclidean random matrices and relate the spectral radius to the Fourier transform of the underlying kernel, although the analysis is restricted to uniformly distributed points in space. Similarly, in
[
9
]
the spectral density of random kernel matrices is characterised and an upper bound for the spectral norm is derived. However, this bound is not expected to be sharp, and a more precise analysis of the largest eigenvalue statistics remains largely open, with only partial results available under strong regularity assumptions on the kernel function
[
13
]
. The analysis is also restricted to specific ensembles, such as Gaussian vectors or points uniformly distributed on the sphere. A more direct approach to the statistics of extreme eigenvalues is pursued in
[
8
]
, where explicit distributional limits for the largest eigenvalue of kernel matrices are derived. However, this analysis is limited to one-dimensional kernels with uniformly distributed inputs. In physically motivated models, such as those describing cooperative emission in atomic clouds, recent works have addressed the behaviour of the smallest eigenvalues for specific kernels and spatial distributions
[
31
]
, revealing non-trivial collective phenomena, but again in restricted settings (e.g., fixed dimension and Gaussian spatial distributions). Overall, while important progress has been made in specific cases, a general analytical framework to characterise extremal eigenvalues of Euclidean random matrices for arbitrary underlying distributions and in any dimension
d\geq 1
is still missing. This gap is even more pronounced for the statistics of the top eigenvector’s components, for which analytical results are virtually absent.

In this work, we develop a direct analytical approach to characterise the statistics of the top eigenpair of a broad class of Euclidean random matrices. Focusing on matrices defined through pairwise squared distances between random vectors in
d
dimensions, drawn independently from a common distribution, we employ the
replica method
from the physics of disordered systems to derive closed equations governing the typical value of the largest eigenvalue and the density of top eigenvector’s components in the large-
N
limit. Replica-based methods provide a powerful framework to compute spectral observables in random matrix problems by mapping them to effective statistical mechanics problems
[
21
,
22
]
. In particular, the replica method can be employed to access not only global spectral properties, such as densities and resolvents
[
29
]
, but also local quantities, including extreme eigenvalues and the associated eigenvectors
[
6
,
27
,
1
,
14
,
18
]
.
Our approach leads to an explicit expression for the average largest eigenvalue in terms of a small set of parameters, determined self-consistently through a nonlinear system of equations that depend only on low-order moments of the underlying distribution of points. This provides a transparent and general framework that applies to a wide range of distributions, including isotropic, independent, and component-wise correlated cases. In addition, we extend the analysis to the structure of the associated top eigenvector. By introducing an auxiliary partition function, we derive an analytical expression for the distribution of its components, revealing a non-trivial geometric structure that reflects the underlying spatial disorder. In particular, we show that the components concentrate on a hypersurface determined by the same parameters controlling the largest eigenvalue, providing a unified description of both eigenvalue and eigenvector statistics. All analytical results have been checked against numerical simulations on randomly generated instances with excellent agreement.

The average largest eigenvalue thus coincides with the Lagrange multiplier

at the saddle point (as usually happens in this type of problems

we indeed perform extensive numerical simulations, solving the system numerically and finding solutions that satisfy the above conditions in all cases considered.

As noted above, the system may admit multiple solutions. In particular, it is apparent from the structure of Eq. (

) that if
(A,\underline{B},C)
is a real solution, then
(-A,-\underline{B},-C)

and

and

. As we will see in Section

Density of the top eigenvector’s components

Let

(44)

(45)

Since the Gibbs measure will localise around

(47)

Averaging over all possible realisations of the matrix
X

\beta\to\infty

T(u)=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\langle\tilde{T}(u)\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{\beta N}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\langle\left[Z_{\varepsilon,\beta}(t,X;u)\right]^{n}\right\rangle\Big|_{t=0}.

(48)

This method has been employed to compute the density of the top eigenvector’s components in several ensembles of sparse matrices
[

27
]

II

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[Z_{\varepsilon,\beta}(t,X;u)\right]^{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\propto\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\lambda}\{\mathcal{D}\rho_{c}\}\{\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}_{c}\}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{2}N\sum_{c=1}^{n}\lambda_{c}+\frac{\beta}{2}N\sum_{c=1}^{n}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\underline{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\underline{y}~\rho_{c}(\underline{x})\rho_{c}(\underline{y})||\underline{x}-\underline{y}||^{2}+\mathrm{i}N\sum_{c=1}^{n}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\underline{x}~\rho_{c}(\underline{x})\hat{\rho}_{c}(\underline{x})\right\}\times

(49)

If we assume that the distribution of each vector is invariant under rotations, i.e.

1.

\langle x_{\ell}\rangle=0\quad\forall\ell=1,\dots,d

2.

\langle x_{\ell}x_{q}\rangle=\sigma^{2}\delta_{\ell q}\quad\forall\ell=1,\dots,d

3.

\langle x_{\ell}x_{q}x_{m}x_{n}\rangle=M(\delta_{\ell q}\delta_{mn}+\delta_{\ell m}\delta_{qn}+\delta_{\ell n}\delta_{qm})

4.

If
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\alpha_{\ell}
is odd then
\langle x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\rangle=0

where
M
and
\sigma

•

with
\ell\neq q

•

\langle||\underline{x}||^{4}\rangle=\sum_{\ell,m=1}^{d}\langle x_{\ell}^{2}x_{m}^{2}\rangle=d(d+2)M\ .

IV.2
Symmetric i.i.d. distribution

If we assume that the components of each vector are i.i.d, i.e.
P(\underline{x})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}P(x_{\ell})
, in the system in Eq. (
39
) all expectation values factorise, and it reduces to

\begin{cases}\displaystyle A=\frac{\sum_{\ell}B_{\ell}\mu_{1}+C}{\sqrt{s}-d\mu_{2}}\ ,\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle B_{q}=-2\,\frac{A[\mu_{3}+(d-1)\mu_{1}]+\sum_{\ell\neq q}B_{\ell}\mu_{1}^{2}+C\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{s}+2\mu_{2}}\ ,\quad\forall q=1,\dots,d\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle C=\frac{A[d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}]+\sum_{q}B_{q}[(d-1)\mu_{2}\mu_{1}+\mu_{3}]}{\sqrt{s}-d\mu_{2}}\ ,\end{cases}

(67)

where
\mu_{n}=\langle x^{n}\rangle
is the
n
-th moment of the distribution. If we now additionally assume that the distribution is even,
P(x_{\ell})=P(-x_{\ell})
, all odd moments are zero and
\mu_{2}=\sigma^{2}
. Therefore the equations for the coefficients further simplify to

\begin{cases}\displaystyle A=\frac{C}{\sqrt{s}-d\mu_{2}}\ ,\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle B_{q}=0\ ,\quad\forall q=1,\dots,d\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle C=\frac{A[d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}]}{\sqrt{s}-d\mu_{2}}\ ,\end{cases}

(68)

which directly implies that

\displaystyle(\sqrt{s}-d\mu_{2})^{2}=d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}\iff\sqrt{s}=d\mu_{2}+\sqrt{d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}}\ ,

(69)

where again we have selected only the positive root to ensure that
\sqrt{s}>0
. Selecting the solution with
A,C>0
we get

\begin{cases}\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{d\mu_{2}}{\sqrt{d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}}}}\ ,\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle C=A\sqrt{d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}}=\sqrt{\frac{d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}}{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{d\mu_{2}}{\sqrt{d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}}}}\ ,\end{cases}

(70)

The average largest eigenvalue can then be computed from Eq. (
42
), yielding

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{max}\rangle=\sqrt{s}=d\mu_{2}+\sqrt{d(d-1)\mu_{2}^{2}+d\mu_{4}}=d\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{d(d-1)\sigma^{4}+d\mu_{4}}\ .

(71)

In general, Eqs. (
68
)-(
71
) do not match the results obtained in Section
IV.1
for isotropic distributions, namely Eqs. (
62
)–(
65
), except in the trivial case of dimension
d=1
, where the two descriptions coincide. More generally, isotropy does not imply independence of the variables, and the two sets of results agree only under the additional condition
\mu_{4}=3\mu_{2}^{2}=3\sigma^{4}
, which, in the isotropic case, corresponds to
M=\sigma^{4}
. This is precisely the multivariate symmetric Gaussian case, known to be the only isotropic distribution with independent components.
For the density of the top eigenvector’s components, the angular integral in Eq. (
60
) is not trivial. Even though Eq. (
68
) gives
\underline{B}=0
, as in the isotropic case,
P(\underline{x})
now is not constant over the sphere and does not factorise in spherical coordinates. Nonetheless, a more convenient approach is possible. Starting from Eq. (
55
) with
\underline{B}=0
and using Eq. (
59
), we obtain

\displaystyle T(u)

\displaystyle=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\underline{x}~P(\underline{x})\delta\left(u-\frac{A||\underline{x}||^{2}+C}{\sqrt{s}}\right)

(72)

\displaystyle=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\underline{x}~\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}P(x_{\ell})\delta\left(\frac{A}{\sqrt{s}}(R^{2}(u)-||\underline{x}||^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{A}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\underline{x}~\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}P(x_{\ell})\delta(R^{2}(u)-||\underline{x}||^{2})=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{A}f_{||\underline{x}||^{2}}(R^{2}(u))\ .

This shows that
T(u)
is proportional to the probability density
f_{||\underline{x}||^{2}}
of the random variable
||\underline{x}||^{2}
, evaluated at
R^{2}(u)
. Since the components
x_{\ell}
are i.i.d., the random variable
||\underline{x}||^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}^{2}
is a sum of i.i.d. random variables, and its distribution can be obtained by convolution. Denoting by
q
the distribution of the squared components
x^{2}_{\ell}
, a change of variables in the probability distribution yields

q(z)=\frac{P(\sqrt{z})+P(-\sqrt{z})}{2\sqrt{z}}\Theta(z)=\frac{P(\sqrt{z})}{\sqrt{z}}\Theta(z)\ ,

(73)

where in the last equality we used that
P
is even. It then follows that

f_{||\underline{x}||^{2}}(R^{2}(u))=q^{*d}(R^{2}(u))=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{1}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{d}~q(z_{1})\dots q(z_{d})\delta\left(R^{2}(u)-\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}z_{\ell}\right)\ ,

(74)

where
q^{*d}
denotes the
d
-fold convolution of
q
with itself. Recalling Eq. (
72
), we can write

\displaystyle T(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{A}f_{||\underline{x}||^{2}}(R^{2}(u))\Theta(R^{2}(u))=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{A}q^{*d}(R^{2}(u))\,\Theta(2Au-1)\ .

(75)

To test the analytical predictions in the i.i.d. symmetric case, we consider a distribution for the components
x_{\ell}
of the form

P(x)=\frac{|x|^{2\gamma-1}}{\Gamma(\gamma)\theta^{\gamma}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{\theta}\right)\ ,

(76)

with
\gamma>0
and
\theta>0
, and first two non-zero moments

\mu_{2}=\sigma^{2}=\gamma\theta\ ,\qquad\mu_{4}=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}\sigma^{4}\ .

(77)

Using Eq. (
73
), it follows that the distribution of the squared components
x_{\ell}^{2}
is a Gamma distribution with shape parameter
\gamma
and scale parameter
\theta

q(z)=\frac{z^{\gamma-1}}{\Gamma(\gamma)\theta^{\gamma}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{z}{\theta}\right)\Theta(z)\ .

(78)

As a consequence, the random variable
||\underline{x}||^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}^{2}
is Gamma distributed with shape parameter
d\gamma
and scale parameter
\theta
, and its distribution evaluated at
R^{2}(u)
is therefore

f_{||\underline{x}||^{2}}(R^{2}(u))=q^{*d}(R^{2}(u))=\frac{R(u)^{2d\gamma-2}}{\Gamma(d\gamma)\theta^{d\gamma}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{R^{2}(u)}{\theta}\right)\Theta(R^{2}(u))\ .

(79)

we compare the theoretical prediction for
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle

\sigma^{2}

IV.3
Multivariate Gaussian distribution

We now consider a different setting that does not fall within the two special classes discussed above. In particular, we assume that each vector
\{\underline{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is drawn independently from a
d
-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution with non-zero mean
\underline{m}
and non-diagonal covariance matrix
\Sigma
. The moments up to fourth order entering the system in Eq. (
39
) are known explicitly and are given by the following set of equations

\begin{cases}\langle x_{\ell}\rangle=m_{\ell}\ ,\\[4.0pt]
\langle x_{\ell}x_{q}\rangle=m_{\ell}m_{q}+\Sigma_{\ell q}\ ,\\[4.0pt]
\langle x_{\ell}^{2}x_{q}\rangle=m_{\ell}^{2}m_{q}+2m_{\ell}\Sigma_{\ell q}+m_{q}\Sigma_{\ell\ell}\ ,\\[4.0pt]
\langle x_{\ell}^{2}x_{q}^{2}\rangle=m_{\ell}^{2}m_{q}^{2}+m_{\ell}^{2}\Sigma_{qq}+m_{q}^{2}\Sigma_{\ell\ell}+4m_{\ell}m_{q}\Sigma_{\ell q}+\Sigma_{\ell\ell}\Sigma_{qq}+2\Sigma_{\ell q}^{2}\ .\end{cases}\qquad\forall\,\ell,q\in\{1,\dots,d\}

(80)

(A,\underline{B},C)

denotes the

-dimensional vector with all components equal to one. In Fig.

IV.4
Distributions with matching moments

An important consequence of our results is that the coefficients
(A,\underline{B},C)
obtained by solving the system in Eq. (
39
) depend only on the moments of the distribution
P(\underline{x})
up to fourth order. Since the average largest eigenvalue
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
is entirely specified by these coefficients through Eq. (
38
), it follows that distributions sharing the same first four moments yield identical theoretical predictions for
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
, even if their higher-order statistics differ. To verify this prediction, we consider the one-dimensional case
d=1
and compare two different even distributions sharing the same first four moments. In particular, we take a triangular distribution supported on
[-L,L]
with mode at zero, and a mixture of two uniform distributions

P_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{L-|x|}{L^{2}}&|x|\leq L\\[6.0pt]
0&|x|>L\end{cases}\qquad P_{2}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{4a}+\dfrac{1}{4b}&|x|\leq b\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{4a}&b<|x|\leq a\\[6.0pt]
0&|x|>a\ ,\end{cases}

(81)

with

a^{2}=\frac{L^{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\ ,\qquad b^{2}=\frac{L^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\ .

(82)

It is straightforward to verify that these two distributions share the same moments up to fourth order,

\mu_{1}=0\ ,\quad\mu_{2}=\sigma^{2}=\frac{L^{2}}{6}\ ,\quad\mu_{3}=0\ ,\quad\mu_{4}=\frac{L^{4}}{15}=\frac{36}{15}\sigma^{4}\ ,

(83)

while higher-order moments differ. In this setting, since
d=1
and the distributions are even, the results of Sections
IV.1
and
IV.2
coincide. From Eqs. (
65
) and (
71
), we therefore obtain the same prediction for both distributions

\lambda_{\mathrm{\max}}=\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{\mu_{4}}=\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{15}}\right)\sigma^{2}\ .

(84)

we compare the theoretical prediction in Eq. (
84
) (black dashed line) with the values of
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
obtained via direct numerical diagonalisation, as a function of
\sigma^{2}
, for the two distributions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
in Eq. (
81
). The triangular distribution is represented by blue crosses, while the uniform mixture is shown with orange circles. In both cases, we observe very good agreement with the theoretical prediction. Moreover, the numerical results for the two distributions coincide within statistical fluctuations, confirming that
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
depends only on the first four moments of the distribution, as expected.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the spectral properties of Euclidean random matrices constructed from a set of
N
random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, drawn independently from a common distribution. In particular, we focused on the average largest eigenvalue
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
and on the density
T(u)
of the components of the associated top eigenvector. Starting from a statistical mechanics formulation of the problem, we expressed
\lambda_{\max}
in terms of an auxiliary replicated partition function, whose average can be computed in the large
N
limit, and derived the distribution
T(u)
by differentiating with respect to an auxiliary source term, as shown in Eqs. (
7
) and (
48
). This approach led to a general set of self-consistent equations for the coefficients
(A,\underline{B},C)
, which fully determine both
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
and the distribution
T(u)
of the top eigenvector’s components. For specific classes of underlying distributions, namely isotropic distributions and distributions with independent and symmetric components, these equations can be solved analytically, yielding explicit closed-form expressions. These results highlight how the spectral properties depend on the low-order moments of the underlying distribution, and clarify the role played by isotropy and independence.
For more general distributions, where analytical solutions are not accessible, we solved the system numerically and used the resulting coefficients to obtain theoretical predictions. In all cases considered, we performed extensive numerical experiments based on direct diagonalisation of large random matrices. The comparison between theory and numerical results shows a very good level of agreement across all regimes explored, both for the scaling of
\langle\lambda_{\max}\rangle
and for the full distribution
T(u)
. Overall, our results provide a general framework to characterise the leading spectral properties of Euclidean random matrices of the form defined in Eq. (
2
), for arbitrary underlying distributions of the vectors. Although closed-form solutions are available only in special cases, the method itself remains fully general, as the relevant quantities can always be obtained by solving the associated self-consistent equations numerically. Several natural extensions of this work can be considered. First, while we have focused on the average largest eigenvalue, the same replica-based framework could in principle be extended to characterise its full distribution in a large deviation sense
[
11
,
14
,
19
]
. Second, the same methodology could be extended to more general matrix constructions with non-quadratic kernels, such as those defined in Eq. (
1
). In this case, one would obtain coupled integral saddle-point equations for the densities
\rho
and
\hat{\rho}
, which are less analytically tractable but can still be solved numerically; this is a direction that we leave for future work. Finally, a similar methodology could be employed to investigate other extremal spectral observables, in particular the smallest eigenvalue or the second largest, which are also expected to encode relevant structural information
[
28
]
.
