On the 3-adic Valuation of a Cubic Binomial Sum
Valentio Iverson

Abstract
In this short note, we prove a conjecture recently posed by Alekseyev, Amdeberhan, Shallit, and Vukusic on the 3 3 -adic valuation of a cubic binomial sum.



1
Introduction

The study of
p
-adic valuations of combinatorial sequences and binomial sums is a classical and active area of research in number theory, often revealing that these arithmetic structures possess well-behaved
p
-adic valuations (see, for example,
[
1
,
3
]
).

Recently, Alekseyev, Amdeberhan, Shallit, and Vukusic
[
1
]
investigated the
p
-adic valuations of Legendre polynomials and related integer sequences. In their paper, they posed a specific conjecture regarding the
3
-adic valuation of the cubic binomial sum
\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}^{3}2^{r}
. Specifically, they proposed an explicit formula for the
3
-adic valuation of this sum that depends strictly on the parity of
n
and the base-
3
sum of digits function,
s_{3}(\cdot)
.

In this short note, we resolve this conjecture in the affirmative. Our proof is elementary and direct. We proceed by applying MacMahon’s identity to transform the cubic binomial sum into a form more amenable to
p
-adic analysis. Subsequently applying Legendre’s formula to the transformed terms, we establish lower bounds on the
3
-adic valuations of the individual summands, employing techniques similar to those used in
[
1
]
. This approach allows us to efficiently isolate the single dominating term that uniquely dictates the valuation of the entire sum.

Our main theorem is as follows:

Theorem 1
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
, we have

\nu_{3}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}^{3}2^{r}\right)=\begin{cases}s_{3}\left(\dfrac{n-1}{2}\right)+1&\text{if }n\ \text{is odd},\\[4.30554pt]
s_{3}\left(\dfrac{n}{2}\right)&\text{if }n\ \text{is even}.\end{cases}

2
Proof of Theorem 1

Let

S_{n}:=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}^{3}2^{r}.

We first note that for
n=1
, we have
S_{1}=3
from which it is easily verified that
\nu_{3}(S_{1})=1=s_{3}(0)+1
, as the theorem has suggested. Therefore, from here onwards, we shall prove the theorem for
n\geq 2
. Our starting point is the following classical identity:

Theorem 2

(MacMahon’s Identity
[
2
]
)
.

For every positive integer
n
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{3}x^{k}y^{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2k}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}x^{k}y^{k}(x+y)^{n-2k}.

Substituting
x=2
and
y=1
into MacMahon’s identity, we can rewrite
S_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}^{3}2^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{n+r}{r}2^{r}3^{n-2r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n+r}{3r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}2^{r}3^{n-2r},

where the last equality can be checked by a straightforward computation.

Let us denote the summands by

A_{r}=\binom{n+r}{3r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}2^{r}3^{n-2r},\quad 0\leq r\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor.

Recall that by Legendre’s formula, for every positive integer
k
, we have

\begin{split}\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2k}{k}\binom{3k}{k}\right)&=\nu_{3}\left(\frac{(3k)!}{(k!)^{3}}\right)\\
&=\nu_{3}((3k)!)-3\nu_{3}(k!)\\
&=\frac{3k-s_{3}(3k)}{2}-3\cdot\frac{k-s_{3}(k)}{2}\\
&=s_{3}(k).\end{split}

(1)

Using this fact, we evaluate
S_{n}
by considering the parity of
n
:

1.

If
n
is even, we can write
n=2m
for some positive integer
m
. We aim to show that
\nu_{3}(S_{2m})=s_{3}(m)
. Notice that

\displaystyle S_{2m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{2m+r}{3r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}2^{r}3^{2m-2r}.

The term for
r=m
is given by
A_{m}=\binom{3m}{3m}\binom{2m}{m}\binom{3m}{m}2^{m}
. Thus, by Legendre’s formula and Equation
1
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{3}(A_{m})

\displaystyle=\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2m}{m}\binom{3m}{m}2^{m}\right)=s_{3}(m).

For every
0\leq r\leq m-1
, we observe the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\nu_{3}(A_{r})

\displaystyle=\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2m+r}{3r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}2^{r}3^{2m-2r}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}\right)+2(m-r)

\displaystyle=s_{3}(r)+2(m-r)

\displaystyle\geq 1+(m-r)+s_{3}(r)

\displaystyle\geq 1+s_{3}(m-r)+s_{3}(r)\geq 1+s_{3}(m),

where the last inequality holds by the subadditivity of the sum of digits function.
Because the valuation of
A_{m}
is strictly less than the valuation of all other terms
A_{r}
, it dictates the valuation of the sum. Therefore,

\nu_{3}(S_{2m})=\nu_{3}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{m}A_{r}\right)=s_{3}(m).

2.

If
n
is odd, we can write
n=2m+1
for some positive integer
m
. We have

S_{2m+1}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{2m+1+r}{3r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}2^{r}3^{2m+1-2r}.

The term for
r=m
is
A_{m}=\binom{3m+1}{3m}\binom{2m}{m}\binom{3m}{m}2^{m}3
, yielding

\displaystyle\nu_{3}(A_{m})

\displaystyle=\nu_{3}\left(2^{m}\cdot 3\cdot(3m+1)\binom{2m}{m}\binom{3m}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2m}{m}\binom{3m}{m}\right)=1+s_{3}(m).

Furthermore, for every
0\leq r\leq m-1
, we see that

\displaystyle\nu_{3}(A_{r})

\displaystyle=\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2m+1+r}{3r}\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}2^{r}3^{2m+1-2r}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\nu_{3}\left(\binom{2r}{r}\binom{3r}{r}\right)+(2m+1-2r)

\displaystyle=s_{3}(r)+2(m-r)+1

\displaystyle\geq 2+(m-r)+s_{3}(r)

\displaystyle\geq 2+s_{3}(m-r)+s_{3}(r)\geq 2+s_{3}(m),

similarly as before. Once again, the valuation is dictated by
A_{m}
, showing that

\nu_{3}(S_{2m+1})=\nu_{3}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{m}A_{r}\right)=s_{3}(m)+1.

This concludes the proof, showing that for every integer
n\geq 1
,

\nu_{3}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}^{3}2^{r}\right)=\begin{cases}s_{3}\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)+1&\text{if }n\ \text{is odd},\\
s_{3}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)&\text{if }n\ \text{is even}.\end{cases}
