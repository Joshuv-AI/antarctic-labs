Abstract
. Smart-home sensor data holds significant potential for several applications, including healthcare monitoring and assistive technologies. Existing approaches, however, face critical limitations. Supervised models require impractical amounts of labeled data. Foundation models for activity recognition focus only on inertial sensors, failing to address the unique characteristics of smart-home binary sensor events: their sparse, discrete nature combined with rich semantic associations. LLM-based approaches, while tested in this domain, still raise several issues regarding the need for natural language descriptions or prompting, and reliance on either external services or expensive hardware, making them infeasible in real-life scenarios due to privacy and cost concerns. We introduce DomusFM, the first foundation model specifically designed and pretrained for smart-home sensor data. DomusFM employs a self-supervised dual contrastive learning paradigm to capture both token-level semantic attributes and sequence-level temporal dependencies. By integrating semantic embeddings from a lightweight language model and specialized encoders for temporal patterns and binary states, DomusFM learns generalizable representations that transfer across environments and tasks related to activity and event analysis. Through leave-one-dataset-out evaluation across seven public smart-home datasets, we demonstrate that DomusFM outperforms state-of-the-art baselines on different downstream tasks, achieving superior performance even with only 5% of labeled training data available for fine-tuning. Our approach addresses data scarcity while maintaining practical deployability for real-world smart-home systems.

1.
Introduction

Over the past decades, technological advancements have driven the widespread adoption of Internet of Things (IoT) devices that integrate a wide range of sensors capable of monitoring diverse phenomena, such as temperature, light intensity, pressure, energy consumption, and human movement
(
9
)
. The availability of such rich sensor data has enabled the development of numerous context-aware smart-home applications, including healthcare monitoring and assistance
(
10
)
.

The growing demand for effective in-house monitoring and digital healthcare accelerated the diffusion of smart-home environments, driving the development of different analytical tasks
(
4
)
. Among these, the most extensively studied is the recognition of Activities of Daily Living (ADL), with important applications in safety assurance, energy management, and healthcare monitoring. Other relevant tasks include next-events prediction for proactive healthcare assistance and domotics, anomaly detection, etc. To date, most approaches rely on supervised deep learning techniques, which require large amounts of labeled data for effective training. However, in the smart-home domain, data collection and annotation are particularly costly, time-consuming, and often impractical due to privacy concerns.

More recently, transfer learning approaches have been proposed to leverage training from related domains or tasks
(
29
)
. Foundation models represent the most recent paradigm of transfer learning, demonstrating the potential of models pretrained on broad data to adapt effectively to diverse downstream tasks with minimal supervision. In the context of behavioral modeling, two main paradigms have emerged: specialized foundation models trained on large-scale inertial sensor data from wearables
(
86
;
44
;
82
;
78
)
, and approaches that leverage existing large language models (LLMs) by encoding sensor events as text
(
21
;
26
;
38
;
28
;
32
;
31
;
40
;
71
)
. While the former have shown impressive results for continuous, high-frequency motion data, they are not well-suited to the sparse, discrete, and semantically-rich nature of smart-home binary sensor events. The latter face practical challenges, including the need for careful prompt engineering and reliance on either proprietary external APIs or costly hardware for local deployment. Importantly, these LLM-based approaches repurpose foundation models trained on text corpora rather than developing foundation models specifically for smart-home sensor data. As a result, this domain still lacks a true foundation model pretrained at scale on diverse smart-home datasets.

In this paper, we introduce DomusFM, the first foundation model pretrained directly on multiple diverse, publicly available smart-home datasets, enabling it to learn generalizable representations natively grounded in the structure and dynamics of home environments. DomusFM employs a two-stage self-supervised contrastive learning framework: the first stage learns robust token-level representations of individual sensor events by modeling their semantic attributes, while the second stage captures long-term temporal and contextual dependencies at the sequence level. These learned representations can be effectively transferred across downstream tasks and environments. We evaluate DomusFM extensively using a leave-one-dataset-out methodology across seven public datasets, demonstrating its effectiveness and superior generalization capability compared to state-of-the-art task-specific baselines.
To summarize, our contributions are the following:

•

We introduce DomusFM, the first foundation model specifically designed and pretrained for Smart-home sensor data. The novel approach of DomusFM consists of first decomposing sensor events into semantic attributes, status, and temporal information, each embedded through specialized encoders, and then contextualizing the resulting event representation considering previous and following events.

•

We adapt a dual contrastive pretraining strategy that allows DomusFM to learn robust representations at multiple levels of granularity. Our attribute-level contrastive loss captures the semantic relationships among event attributes, while the event-level contrastive loss captures temporal dependencies across sequences.

•

Through extensive experiments across seven diverse state-of-the-art datasets and two downstream tasks, we show that DomusFM substantially outperforms existing approaches, particularly under realistic data scarcity conditions. Our leave-one-dataset-out evaluation rigorously validates the model’s ability to generalize to completely unseen smart-home environments.

As an additional contribution, upon acceptance, we will publish DomusFM as open-source software.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Smart-home Scenario

A smart home is a home environment enriched with sensing, actuation, communication, and computation capabilities that permit it to adapt to users’ behaviors
(
25
)
.
We consider smart-home scenarios involving one or more sensorized environments where each sensor activation can be accurately attributed to a specific individual. This attribution may be achieved through either single-occupancy environments or advanced sensing systems capable of identifying the person responsible for each sensor interaction
(
8
)
.

In this scenario, sensors are categorized into two kinds, based on the type of data they produce.
Binary sensors
, such as contact switches or motion detectors, directly output discrete states, like OPEN/CLOSE or ON/OFF, that have an immediate and explicit semantic interpretation related to human actions.
Continuous sensors
, such as temperature sensors or power meters, on the other hand, produce a stream of real values.

Contrary to HAR systems based on wearable devices, the smart-home literature for behavioral modeling rarely relies on continuous sensor data in its raw form. Instead, various strategies have been proposed, such as summarizing them using statistical features
(
46
)
, or, most commonly, converting continuous values into discrete semantic states
(
56
;
35
;
6
)
. Despite methodological differences, these approaches share the principle of abstracting continuous data into discrete representations. This abstraction is motivated by the fact that discrete semantic states capture the behaviorally relevant information needed for analysis while also enhancing interpretability, as opposed to raw numerical values, which often lack clear behavioral significance, and which fine-grained temporal granularity may not carry meaningful information for behavior analysis.

2.2.
Data Scarcity in Smart-home scenarios

Most existing work in smart-home behavior analysis relies on data-driven models, which require large amounts of labeled data for training. However, acquiring labeled data in smart-home environments is particularly challenging, as it is costly both in terms of time and resources, prone to annotation errors, and raises significant privacy concerns due to the prolonged monitoring of users
(
13
)
. Data scarcity introduces additional challenges, including class imbalance between frequent and rare activities, and difficulties in generalizing across the highly personalized nature of smart-home environments with varying layouts, sensor configurations, and user habits.
Nevertheless, while collecting large-scale labeled datasets is often infeasible, obtaining a small annotated dataset may be more realistic; for example, during system installation, users could be asked (assisted by technicians) to perform a predefined set of activities over a limited number of days
(
69
)
. This motivates the need for approaches that can effectively leverage such limited supervision.

2.3.
Foundation Models

To date, no universally accepted formal or mathematical definition of a
foundation model
exists in the literature. The term was first introduced in
(
12
)
, where the authors describe a foundation model as
any model that is trained on broad data (generally using self-supervision) that can be adapted (e.g., fine-tuned) to a wide range of downstream tasks
. Subsequent works have largely adopted this conceptual definition (
(
64

15

For the sake of this work, we build on this widely accepted conceptual definition and, in particular, emphasize two key properties: broad pretraining and adaptability to downstream tasks. We formalize this as follows:
A
foundation model
is a function
g_{\theta}
with trainable parameters
\theta
that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Broad pretraining:

g_{\theta}
is trained on a large and diverse dataset
D
using a general-purpose objective (e.g., self-supervised learning), capturing patterns applicable in a given domain.

(2)

Adaptability:

g_{\theta}
can be adapted to a set of downstream tasks
\mathcal{T}_{d-stream}
, without re-training from scratch.

The same definition has been adopted in various subsequent works spanning a huge set of domains like, language
(
36
;

;

)
, vision-language
(

)
, medical-images
(

, acoustic respiratory model
(

(

)
, or protein analysis
(

.

3.
Related Work

While smart-home environments enable numerous tasks, the literature has primarily focused on a limited subset: Activities of Daily Living (ADL) recognition, which remains the most extensively studied problem, and next events prediction, for which only a few works exist that have framed the problem in different ways. For this reason, in our work, we focus on these two tasks. In the following subsections, we summarize the related work for each of these tasks.

3.1.
ADL recognition in Smart Homes

Early attempts to address the ADL recognition problem primarily relied on knowledge-driven approaches based on ontologies
(
20
;
27
)
. While interpretable, these methods suffer from limited flexibility, high design and maintenance costs, require specialized expertise, and struggle to generalize to unseen activities. With the advent of deep learning, data-driven approaches using CNNs and LSTMs have been widely adopted
(
56
;
76
)
, demonstrating impressive performance but requiring large amounts of labeled data that are difficult to obtain in smart-home scenarios.
To mitigate data scarcity, several approaches have been proposed, including semi-supervised
(
2
)
, transfer
(
62
;
66
)
, and self-supervised learning
(
43
)
, though these methods have shown limited success in smart-home settings despite proving effective for wearable-based activity recognition. More recently, Neuro-Symbolic (NeSy) AI methods have emerged as a promising direction by combining data-driven learning with symbolic reasoning
(
7
;
79
)
, with recent LLM-based approaches for smart-home data exemplifying this paradigm through their implicit common knowledge and reasoning capabilities
(
37
)
.

3.2.
Foundation Models for Human Activity Recognition

Foundation models have recently been extended to time series, learning general-purpose representations transferable across tasks
(
48
;
61
;
41
;
16
;
19
;
1
)
.
Building on this line of research, several recent works have proposed specialized foundation models specifically tailored to HAR, demonstrating strong performance on tasks involving wearable sensing
(
86
;
44
;
82
;
78
)
.
More recently, some approaches have sought to merge time series processing with LLMs in order to exploit the rich semantic knowledge encoded in LLMs. These models typically transform time series into tokenized or textual representations, or couple dedicated temporal encoders with LLM backbones, enabling the incorporation of semantic priors and contextual reasoning into time series analysis
(
54
;
81
;
84
)
.

While these models are effective for inertial data from wearable sensors, their applicability is limited when dealing with binary sensors in smart-home environments and ADLs. Binary sensors generate sparse and discrete event data with long, irregular time gaps and encode only two states (e.g., ON/OFF). In contrast, time series foundation models operate on continuous, high-frequency inertial data with fixed sampling-rates. Resampling smart-home data to feed it to such models would increase computational cost, affect the possibility of capturing rich temporal patterns, and remove their implicit semantics. Unlike wearable sensor foundation models, DomusFM is purpose-built to handle the sparse, discrete, and semantically-rich nature of smart-home binary sensor events.

3.3.
LLMs for ADL recognition

In parallel to specialized foundation models for wearable-based HAR, a distinct line of research has emerged that uses LLMs for smart-home ADL recognition.
Prompting-based approaches leverage LLMs’ zero-shot capabilities through careful prompt engineering, transforming raw sensor data into textual representations that can be processed by pre-trained models to recognize activities without labeled training data
(
21
;
26
;
38
)
.
Fine-tuning strategies have been explored to investigate model adaptation and knowledge distillation techniques to optimize LLM performance for activity recognition tasks
(
28
;
32
;
31
;
40
)
.
However, LLM-based approaches still face practical limitations for real-world use. Prompting methods require careful prompt design and nontrivial engineering to encode sensor data as natural language, and their performance is highly sensitive to these choices. Fine-tuning strategies, while offering better performances, continue to rely on labeled training data that are costly and time-consuming to collect in smart-home environments.

Finally, embedding-based methods represent a distinct paradigm, such as the system proposed in
(
71
)
, which uses either rule-derived or LLM-generated textual descriptions of sensor triggers and extracts embeddings from these sentences, aiming at creating representations transferable across diverse home environments without retraining. While effective at cross-environment generalization, this approach still requires prompting or manual engineering to produce textual descriptions, assumes pre-segmented activities (i.e., the activity duration is known in advance, which is often unrealistic), and depends on mapping all activities to a fixed, predefined label set used during pretraining.

3.4.
Next Events Prediction

Next events prediction has received considerably less attention than ADL recognition. Early probabilistic models
(
18
)
were extended with recurrent neural networks
(
17
)
, but both approaches share critical limitations: reliance on fully supervised learning (requiring large labeled datasets) and focus on single next-event prediction, which offers limited practical utility as isolated events are often ambiguous or uninformative.

More recently, a GPT-2-inspired generative transformer
(
70
)
enabled forecasting sequences of future events rather than single predictions. However, this approach remains fully supervised with the same labeled-data dependency, leaving next events prediction in smart homes largely open, particularly for methods that can operate effectively under limited supervision while producing meaningful multi-event forecasts.

4.
Formalization

4.1.
Continuous and Binary Sensors

Formally, we can model sensors as time-dependent functions following the standard signal-processing convention of treating measurements as signals
x(t)

(
74
)
. Real sensors operate at a finite detection granularity (e.g., 32 measurements per second). Therefore, we model time as the set of positive integers
T\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{+}
, where each element corresponds to a discrete sampling instant. A
sensor
is then a partial function

x:T\rightharpoonup\mathcal{V},

where
\mathcal{V}
is the sensor’s value space. The function is partial to account for missing values: it is not guaranteed that a reading is available for every
t\in T
, due to possible unavailability caused by energy constraints, network issues, or temporary malfunctions. When defined, each reading at time
t
is denoted
x(t)\in\mathcal{V}
. Sensors can be categorized into:

•

Continuous sensors
: A sensor is called
continuous sensor
if
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, i.e., the sensor produces real-valued measurements (e.g., temperature in Celsius).

•

Binary sensors
: A sensor is called
binary sensor
if
\mathcal{V}=\{1,0\}
, i.e., it admits two mutually exclusive states. The used symbols are conventional. In practice, binary sensor states may be encoded as
\{1,0\}
,
\{\top,\bot\}
, or application-specific labels such as {
OPEN
,
CLOSE
}, {
PRESENT
,
ABSENT
}, {
ON
,
OFF
} etc. All such encodings are equivalent under a bijection with
\{1,0\}
.

4.1.1.
Binarization of continuous sensors

As discussed in Section
2.1
, continuous sensor readings are commonly converted into discrete semantic states to capture behaviorally meaningful information.
Formally, let
x:T\to\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a continuous sensor and
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}\}
a set of
m
semantic states (e.g., high temperature, dynamic gestures,…).
The associated binary states are then given by the indicator function

\sigma_{j}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{\{x(t)\in I_{j}\}},\quad j=1,\dots,m,

so that each
\sigma_{j}:T\to\{0,1\}
defines a binary state stream corresponding to the semantic state
I_{j}

(
87
;
67
;
45
;
42
)
.

In the following, the term "sensor" will be used in an extended sense to denote both inherently binary devices and the high-level binary states inferred from continuous sensors’ measurements, while excluding the raw continuous signals themselves.

4.2.
Sensors

Let
S=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{M}\}
be the set of
M
sensors deployed in the smart-home environment. Each sensor
s_{i}
is characterized by the following attributes:

•

s_{i}^{ID}\in\texttt{String}
: the unique identifier of the sensor (e.g., M1, T3, …)

•

s_{i}^{HouseItem}\in\texttt{String}\cup\{\text{NULL}\}
: the appliance or object monitored by the sensor (e.g., stove, bed, shower…)

•

s_{i}^{Room}\in\texttt{String}\cup\{\text{NULL}\}
: the room in which the sensor is installed (e.g., kitchen, bathroom, bedroom…)

•

s_{i}^{Type}
: the type of the sensor (e.g., contact, temperature, motion…)

It is assumed that the association between each sensor and its attributes (
HouseItem
,
Room
, and
Type
) is either implicit in the sensor or established at the time of installation in the home by the responsible technician. For improved interoperability and consistency, it would be advantageous to draw attribute names from existing smart-home ontologies (
(
49
;
34
;
47
;
27
)
). Nevertheless, DomusFM is designed in an open-world fashion: there are no strict constraints on the attribute names, and their definition does not require a prior knowledge engineering effort. This ensures flexibility and applicability across diverse smart-home settings, even when no standard ontology is available.
Note that the
HouseItem
and
Room
can have a NULL value if the sensor is not associated specifically with a certain object or a certain room (e.g., a wearable device).

4.3.
Sensor event stream

Each sensor
s\in S
generates a stream of events, where each event is represented as a tuple:

e=\langle t,s,\sigma\rangle

where:

•

t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
: the timestamp at which the event occurred

•

s\in S
: the sensor responsible for the event

•

\sigma\in\{\text{ON},\text{OFF}\}
: the event status indicating the activation (ON) or deactivation (OFF) of the sensor or inferred high-level feature.

For example, the event:
e=\langle\text{2025-10-15 06:30:00},\text{M1},\text{ON}\rangle

indicates that the motion sensor M1 changed its status to ON at time 2025-10-15 06:30:00.

4.3.1.
Alternation property

For an ideal sensor, the event sequence must alternate. Formally, for any two consecutive events
e=\langle t,s,\sigma\rangle
and
e^{\prime}=\langle t^{\prime},s^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle
with
t<t^{\prime}
and no event from the sensor
s
between them, it holds that

\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}.

In other words, two consecutive
ON
events or two consecutive
OFF
events cannot occur. Any violation of this condition is interpreted as an anomalous or redundant reading. Such anomalies can arise due to sensor malfunctions, temporary communication or network interruptions, or other infrastructure-related issues that cause the sensor to emit repeated values erroneously. These anomalous events can be safely removed during the data cleaning process.

4.4.
Global event stream

Finally, we can define
E=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{T}\}
as the global event stream from all sensors, sorted by timestamp
t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

We assume that the same timestamp will never be assigned to two distinct sensor events. This allows us to treat the sequence of sensor events as totally ordered. The assumption is realistic, since events are typically managed by a single process on the gateway, which assigns unique timestamps even to events occurring simultaneously, without affecting the subsequent processing.

4.5.
Segmentation

Given the global event stream
E=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{T}\}
, a segmentation
\mathcal{W}
divides
E
into a set of (possibly overlapping) windows that cover the entire stream
E
, as:

\mathcal{W}=\{W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{J}\}

Where
W
is a window of
N
consecutive events,

W=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N}\}

The windows in a segmentation can be overlapping, meaning that consecutive windows
W_{j}
and
W_{j+1}
may share common events. The degree of overlap is determined by an overlap factor
\in[0,1)
, which controls the fraction of overlap between consecutive windows.
The segmentation can be performed according to different strategies. In an
event-based
segmentation, the number of events in each window is fixed, i.e.,
N_{j}=N
for all windows
W_{j}\in\mathcal{W}
, where
N
is a predefined constant. Alternatively, in a
time-based
segmentation, windows are defined by a fixed temporal duration
\Delta t
(e.g., 60 seconds), and each window
W_{j}
includes all events whose timestamps fall within the corresponding time interval
[t_{j},t_{j}+\Delta t]
. In this case, the number of events per window
N_{j}
varies across windows.

4.6.
A Conceptual Framework of a Foundation Model for Smart-Home Sensor Data

We now move to formalizing the notion of a
Foundation Model for Smart-home Sensor Data
, starting from its inner components.

We start from the widely accepted definition of a
Feature Extractor
as a function that transforms an input
X
into a latent representation
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
in a
d
-dimensional vector space
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
68
;
75
;
3
)
.
The dimension
d
of the latent space can be chosen arbitrarily for each specific feature extractor, where higher dimensions yield richer representations at the cost of increased computational complexity and greater risk of overfitting if the data is not adequately diverse.

4.6.1.
Event-level Feature Extractor

We first specialize the generic notion of feature extractor to the level of individual events. Let
E
be the global event stream from all sensors as defined above. An
Event-level Feature Extractor
is defined as a mapping:

h_{e}:E\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{e},\quad Z_{e}=h_{e}(e).

where
\mathcal{Z}_{e}
is the event-level latent space. The dimension
d_{e}
can be chosen to balance representational capacity with computational efficiency, where larger values capture more semantic nuances of individual events at the cost of increased complexity. The Event-level Feature Extractor is intended to capture the semantic meaning of an event independently of its surrounding temporal context, and to serve as a building block for higher-level representations.

4.6.2.
Contextualized Event-level Feature Extractor

The Event-level Feature Extractor
h_{e}
captures the semantic meaning of an event independently of its surrounding temporal context. In many applications, however, the interpretation of an event depends critically on the events that precede or follow it in time. We therefore introduce the notion of a
Contextualized Event-level Feature Extractor
, which encodes an event together with information from its window. In particular, given a window
W=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N}\}\in\mathcal{W}
, we say that
W
is the
context
of
e
for each event
e\in W
.

Let
h_{e}
be the Event-Level Feature Extractor. We define the Contextualized Event-level Feature Extractor as a partial function:

h_{cxt}:E\times\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{cxt},\quad Z_{cxt}=h_{cxt}(e,W)=\Phi(h_{e}(e),\{h_{e}(e_{i}):e_{i}\in W,e_{i}\neq e\})

where
\Phi
is a parametrized attention-based function operating on the embedded event space
\mathcal{Z}_{e}
, and
\mathcal{Z}_{cxt}
is the contextualized event-level latent space. The dimension
d_{cxt}
is subject to the same trade-offs between expressiveness and computational complexity as the dimension
d_{e}
.

This mapping produces a representation that encodes both the intrinsic properties of
e
(via
h_{e}(e)
) and the information from its surrounding window (via the embedded events
\{h_{e}(e_{i}):e_{i}\in W,e_{i}\neq e\}
).
The Contextualized Event-level Feature Extractor
h_{cxt}
maintains the granularity of individual event representations: for a window
W=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N}\}
, we can obtain a set of
N
distinct contextualized representations
\{h_{cxt}(e_{1},W),h_{cxt}(e_{2},W),\ldots,h_{cxt}(e_{N},W)\}
, one per event. Since each event
e
contains a timestamp (as defined in Section
4.3
),
h_{e}
and
h_{cxt}
naturally encode both the sequential ordering and relative temporal distances between events in the window.

Note that, for two different events in the same window, the contextualized event-level feature Extractor produces a different representation,

h_{cxt}(e_{i},W)\neq h_{cxt}(e_{j},W),\quad e_{i},e_{j}\in W,i\neq j

4.6.3.
Window Representation

Given a window
W=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N}\}\in\mathcal{W}
, we define a
Window Representation

g_{\theta}
as a parametrized function that extracts contextualized event-level representations for all the events in the window, in particular:

g_{\theta}:\mathcal{W}\to(\mathcal{Z}_{cxt})^{N},\quad g_{\theta}(W)=\{h_{cxt}(e_{1},W),h_{cxt}(e_{2},W),\ldots,h_{cxt}(e_{N},W)\},

where
\theta
denotes the complete set of learnable parameters in both
h_{e}
and
h_{cxt}
, and
(\mathcal{Z}_{cxt})^{N}
represents sequences of
N
contextualized embeddings.
Training the Window Representation
g_{\theta}
consists of adjusting its parameters
\theta
so that the resulting representations
Z=g_{\theta}(W)
capture the relevant information from the input windows
W\in\mathcal{W}
. This is achieved by optimizing an objective function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
, which may combine multiple learning tasks from a training set
\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. Depending on the chosen setup,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}
may include supervised tasks (e.g., activity recognition, sensor forecasting), unsupervised tasks (e.g., masked event modeling, contrastive learning), or both.

4.6.4.
Transferability to downstream tasks

Once trained, the Window Representation
g_{\theta}
can be applied to a set of downstream tasks
\mathcal{T}_{\text{d-stream}}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, feeding the latent representation
Z=g_{\theta}(W)
to a task-specific head
f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}
defined as:

Y_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}=f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}(Z)=f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}(g_{\theta}(W)),\quad Y_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}\text{ output of }\tau_{\text{d-stream}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{d-stream}}

Depending on the downstream task, different strategies can be employed to define the head
f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}
:

(1)

Identity (frozen features):
The Window Representation
g_{\theta}
remains frozen, and the head
f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}
is an identity function, using the window representation directly without any additional trainable parameters.

(2)

Fine-tuning only the head:
A new trainable head
f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}
is adapted to the downstream task while keeping the Window Representation
g_{\theta}
frozen.

(3)

Fine-tuning both the Window Representation and the head:
The entire network is adapted to the downstream task, training both
f_{\tau_{\text{d-stream}}}
and
g_{\theta}
.

4.6.5.
Window Representation as a Foundation Model for Smart-home Sensor Data

The Window Representation
g_{\theta}
can be regarded as a
Foundation Model for Smart-home Sensor Data
, if it satisfies the two core properties outlined in Section
2.3
. First, it must be pretrained on a large and diverse collection of windows sampled from various sensors, environments, users, and activities, enabling it to learn generalizable representations. Second, it must be adaptable, meaning that the learned representations can be effectively applied to different downstream tasks
\mathcal{T}_{\text{d-stream}}
in the smart-home domain without requiring the feature extractors to be re-trained from scratch.
In the remainder of this paper, we show how our proposed model DomusFM matches these two properties.

5.
Architecture and Training

Having defined the concept of a Foundation Model for Sensor-based Activity and Event Analysis in Smart-home, in this section, we now describe how we implement DomusFM. A high-level representation of the architecture is illustrated in Figure
2
. The model processes the global data stream of events collected from the smart-home environment. This data stream feeds into two sequential processing stages: Event-level Feature Extraction and Contextualized Event-level Feature Extraction. These stages sequentially transform the raw sensor events into two types of learned representations: Event-level Embeddings and Contextualized Event-level Embeddings. The Event-level Feature Extraction module processes each sensor event individually to capture its intrinsic characteristics, while the Contextualized Event-level Feature Extraction module takes windows of event-level representations as input and incorporates temporal context from surrounding events (i.e., preceding and subsequent events) to produce enriched contextualized representations.

Figure 2
.
High level architecture

5.1.
Event-level Feature Extraction

The Event-level Feature Extraction module processes individual sensor events by encoding their various attributes into a unified representation, as shown in Figure
3
. Each sensor event is characterized by multiple attributes, including the house item, sensor type, room, status, and timestamp. These attributes are processed through three specialized encoders: a Pretrained LLM for semantic features, a Status Encoder for binary states, and a Temporal Encoder for time-related information. The resulting attribute-specific embeddings are then combined through an Attribute Self-Attention mechanism to produce the final event-level representation.

(a)

Event-level Feature Extraction

(b)

Contextualized Event-level Feature Extraction

Figure 3
.
Detailed architecture of DomusFM’s two main components. The Event-level Feature Extraction encodes sensor event attributes through specialized encoders and integrates them via Attribute Self-Attention. The Contextualized Event-level Feature Extraction uses transformer layers to capture temporal dependencies and produce context-aware event representations.

5.1.1.
Sensor encoding

As described earlier, each sensor is defined by three semantic attributes: the associated house item, the room in which it is located, and the sensor type. These attributes convey the core semantic meaning of a sensor event and are therefore critical for accurate modeling. To effectively capture this semantic information, we leverage a pretrained LLM, which provides rich representations grounded in prior knowledge learned from large-scale textual data. Specifically, each attribute is represented by its textual name, processed independently, and mapped to a dense vector in the pretrained LLM’s embedding space. This approach allows the sensor attributes to be embedded in a semantically meaningful space that enhances generalization. For example, semantically related appliances such as a stove and an oven are naturally embedded closer together than unrelated ones like a stove and a bed, capturing domain-relevant relationships that traditional encoding schemes, such as one-hot encoding, would not represent.

5.1.2.
Status encoding

The status attribute in our model is inherently simple, as it can only assume two primary values: ON or OFF (with an additional MASK state considered during pretraining). Unlike other attributes that may require richer representations, the status information does not necessitate complex encoding. Therefore, we represent the status using a standard embedding approach that maps each possible state to a learned dense vector in the model’s hidden space.
The semantic interpretation of these states is context-dependent: for instance, ON for a motion sensor indicates presence, while ON for a magnetic switch indicates that an object has been opened.

5.1.3.
Temporal encoding

To capture temporal patterns in the data, we extract three time-related attributes from the timestamp: day of the week, hour of the day, and seconds within the hour. Coarser temporal attributes, such as the month, are deliberately excluded, as most datasets span only one or a limited number of months, which would be insufficient for learning meaningful periodic behaviors or habits. The selected attributes reflect recurring daily and weekly cycles that are more consistently observable across datasets.

Different encoding strategies are employed depending on the nature of each temporal attribute. For the day of the week and the hour of the day, we adopt a cyclical encoding that explicitly models their periodic structure
(
65
)
. Each value is transformed using multiple harmonic frequencies of the corresponding base period and represented as a sequence of sine–cosine pairs. This formulation allows the model to preserve temporal continuity and cyclic proximity (e.g., between consecutive hours or days). In line with prior work
(
52
)
, a subsequent projection is applied to both representations, enabling the model to learn which frequency components are most informative, thereby improving robustness to sparse or irregular temporal sampling. In contrast, the seconds-within-the-hour attribute is treated as a discrete variable and encoded using a learned embedding representation, as it does not exhibit meaningful cyclical continuity. Unlike hours or days, where boundary values are semantically close (e.g., 23:59 and 00:01 both correspond to midnight), the wrap-around of seconds coincides with an hour transition, representing a discontinuity rather than a natural cycle.

5.1.4.
Attribute Self-Attention

The encoded attributes are aggregated into a unified event-level representation through a self-attention mechanism applied across all previously described attribute dimensions. This choice is motivated by the observation that individual attributes do not carry meaning in isolation, but rather in relation to each other: for instance, a kitchen sensor activation at 7:00 AM suggests a different activity than the same activation at 11:00 PM. The self-attention mechanism explicitly models these inter-attribute dependencies, producing a unified representation that captures their contextual interactions. As a result, each event is represented by a single vector that serves as the foundational unit for subsequent modeling.

5.2.
Contextualized Event-level Feature Extraction

The Contextualized Event-level Feature Extraction module enriches the individual event representations by incorporating information from surrounding events in the window, as illustrated in Figure
3
.
This module takes as input the event embeddings of all the events in a window and processes them sequentially through a transformer-based architecture. The core of this module consists of stacked transformer encoder layers. The self-attention mechanism enables each event to attend to all other events in the window, allowing the model to capture sequential dependencies, temporal patterns, and contextual relationships between events. This design allows the model to learn complex interactions between events and produce Contextualized Event Embeddings that encode not only the intrinsic characteristics of each individual event but also its role and significance within the broader window.

5.3.
Pretraining the Foundation Model

Figure 4
.
Dual contrastive learning framework for pretraining DomusFM. Attribute-level contrastive loss learns robust event representations by masking individual attributes, while event-level contrastive loss captures temporal context by masking entire events. Both stages use augmented data streams to learn representations through contrastive objectives.

To pretrain our model, we adopted a self-supervised dual pretraining paradigm inspired by
(
53
)
and depicted in Figure
4
. The first phase of this dual contrastive learning framework employs an
attribute-level
contrastive loss. In this phase, we augment events by masking one attribute at a time (i.e, substituting the value of that attribute with a "MASK" token). Events are randomly selected for augmentation according to a predefined probability. For each selected event, we randomly choose which specific attribute to mask. Both the original and the augmented sequences are then independently processed by the model to obtain their corresponding sequence-level embeddings. The original sequence and its masked augmentation form a positive pair, while embeddings from other sequences within the same batch are treated as negative samples. The model is then trained using an InfoNCE loss
(
57
)
to learn robust attribute-level representations.

The second phase introduces an
event-level
contrastive loss, where we augment entire sequences by masking complete events with a certain probability, applying the masking operation to all attributes of the selected events simultaneously (in this case, we substitute the value of all the attributes of that event with a "MASK" token). Similarly, the original sequence and its masked augmentation form a positive pair, while embeddings from other sequences within the same batch are treated as negative samples. This phase also employs an InfoNCE loss for optimization. Following the dual contrastive learning paradigm, the event-level feature extractor is frozen after the first phase, ensuring that the model builds upon the learned attribute-level representations while developing higher-level contextual understanding during the second phase.

5.4.
Task-specific Heads

The components described so far constitute the general-purpose feature extractor that produces rich, contextualized representations of sensor event sequences. To adapt these representations to a specific downstream task, we append a task-specific head on top of the shared encoder during fine-tuning. The task-specific head is designed according to the task of interest and operates on the contextualized event-level embeddings produced by the encoder. This modular design enables the reuse of the same feature extraction backbone across multiple tasks while allowing flexible and targeted optimization for each application. The specific heads we employed in this paper are explained, for each downstream task, in section
7
. It is important to note that the primary contribution of this work lies in the feature extraction framework itself; the task-specific heads employ standard architectures and are included to demonstrate the applicability and effectiveness of the learned representations.

Considered Smart-home Datasets

In this section, we provide an overview of the datasets considered in this work for both pretraining and downstream tasks. We selected publicly available datasets that match the characteristics described in Section

, ensuring diversity in sensors, environments, users, and activities. It is important to note that, as described in Section
7.1

6.1.
Datasets Descriptions

6.1.1.
CASAS Datasets

The CASAS smart-home project provides a comprehensive repository of annotated sensor data for activity recognition research
(
30
)
. The datasets are collected from real smart homes instrumented with PIR motion sensors, magnetic sensors, and temperature sensors. While the CASAS repository include a high number of different datasets, we focused on the two widely adopted Aruba and Milan. That’s because they both capture single-resident scenarios, and are commonly employed as standard benchmarks. Moreover, including additional CASAS datasets would create a severely unbalanced evaluation that could bias our models toward CASAS-specific characteristics.

6.1.2.
Van Kasteren Datasets

The van Kasteren datasets represent one of the earliest and most influential benchmark collections for ADLs in smart homes
(
72
)
. The datasets were collected from real-world residences equipped with wireless sensor networks using simple, non-intrusive binary sensors, including reed switches, pressure mats, PIR motion detectors, mercury contacts, and float sensors. We utilized two houses from this collection: House A and House C. We excluded House B from our experiments due to the lack of explicit semantic information about the sensors, which is crucial for our approach.

6.1.3.
UCI Datasets

The UCI ADL Binary datasets, introduced by Ordóñez et al.
(
58
)
, comprise data collected from two real smart homes (A and B) instrumented with binary sensors, including PIR motion sensors, pressure sensors, magnetic sensors (reed switches), float sensors, and plug sensors for electrical appliances. We excluded Home A from our experiments as previous works have demonstrated that this dataset is too simple and does not provide additional informative value for evaluation
(
26
)
.

6.1.4.
Orange Datasets

The Orange Labs datasets comprise two collections for ambient sensor-based activity recognition. Orange4Home
(
33
)
includes 236 heterogeneous sensors, including binary sensors, water consumption meters, power monitors, and environmental sensors (luminosity, CO2, noise) from a single occupant. MuRAL (Multi-Resident Ambient sensor dataset with natural Language)
(
22
)
extends to multi-resident scenarios with 23 sensors (PIR motion sensors, magnetic contacts, and smart plugs), capturing sessions with 2 to 4 participants. Although from the same provider, these datasets were collected with a significant temporal gap using completely different sensor configurations and experimental scenarios, making them complementary rather than redundant.

7.
Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we show how DomusFM matches the core properties of a foundation model by evaluating its adaptability and generalization capabilities across multiple datasets and tasks, showing consistent improvements over state-of-the-art baselines even under severe data scarcity. As discussed in Section
3
, we consider ADL recognition and next events prediction as downstream tasks, as these are the focus of existing literature, enabling meaningful baseline comparisons.

7.1.
Experimental Setup

To comprehensively evaluate DomusFM and demonstrate its ability to generalize to previously unseen environments, we employed a rigorous leave-one-dataset-out evaluation
(
59
)
across all seven datasets described in Section
6
. In this evaluation strategy, six datasets were used exclusively for pretraining, while the seventh dataset was held out entirely and used only for fine-tuning and testing on downstream tasks. This means that no data from the held-out dataset was exposed to the model during pretraining. The fine-tuning and test data represent completely unseen smart-home environments and users. This procedure was repeated seven times, with each dataset serving as the held-out evaluation dataset exactly once, ensuring a thorough assessment of the model’s cross-dataset generalization capabilities.

7.1.1.
Pretraining

For the pretraining phase, we utilized all available data from each of the six training datasets in their entirety, following the dual contrastive learning framework described in Section
5.3
. To address the substantial size imbalances across datasets (where some datasets contain significantly more samples than others), we applied random oversampling at the dataset level. This technique ensures that smaller datasets contribute proportionally during training, preventing the model from being disproportionately influenced by larger datasets and promoting balanced representation learning across diverse smart-home environments.

7.1.2.
Fine-tuning and testing for Downstream Tasks

To simulate realistic data scarcity scenarios commonly encountered in practical smart-home deployments, we introduced a parameter called the Training Data %, representing the percentage of available labeled training data from the held-out dataset. For each Data scarcity scenario, we randomly sampled the corresponding percentage of instances from the unseen downstream dataset and conducted fine-tuning exclusively on this limited subset. We systematically evaluated our model across four Training Data percentages: 5%, 10%, 15%, and 30% of the training data. The robustness and statistical reliability of our results were ensured through a standard 5-fold cross-validation applied during the fine-tuning and evaluation phase, with performance metrics averaged across all folds.
For the fine-tuning phase on downstream tasks, we trained for 10 epochs. Notably, we did not employ early stopping during fine-tuning because the extremely limited amount of labeled training data made it impractical to extract a separate validation set without significantly compromising model performance. Instead, we relied on cross-validation across folds to ensure robust evaluation.

7.3.
Downstream task 1: ADL Recognition

7.3.1.
Problem Formulation

Before presenting our experimental results, we formally define the ADL recognition task addressed in this work.
Let
W\in\mathcal{W}
be a window of
N
consecutive sensor events, and let
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{M}\}
be the set of
M
target activities of daily living of interest (e.g., cooking, sleeping, grooming). The ADL recognition problem consists of determining the activity
a\in\mathcal{A}
that was being performed (or initiated) at the time of the last event
e_{N}
in the window
W
. In other words, given a sequence of sensor events leading up to and including a specific moment in time, the task is to classify which activity the resident was engaged in at that moment.
It is important to note that this is not the only possible formulation used in the human activity recognition domain. The formulation we adopt follows established works in the literature
(
51
;
39
)
and is particularly well-suited for real-time activity monitoring applications, where the system must continuously infer the current activity based on the most recent sensor observations. This formulation also aligns with the sliding window approach commonly employed in smart-home activity recognition systems.

7.3.2.
Baseline

For comparison, we employed DeepCASAS
(
56
)
as our baseline, which represents a widely-adopted deep learning baseline for activity recognition in smart-home environments. DeepCASAS utilizes a bidirectional LSTM network architecture that captures both forward and backward temporal dependencies in sensor event streams. We implemented the model using the identical architecture and hyperparameters as specified in the original paper, ensuring a fair comparison.

7.3.3.
ADL Recognition Head

For the ADL recognition downstream task, we implement the head as a simple linear layer that takes the contextualized event embeddings as input and outputs a probability distribution over the target classes.

7.3.4.
Results

The improvements are particularly notable in highly constrained settings, where DomusFM achieves significant gains over baseline performance, with the largest benefits seen on some of the more challenging datasets. Even when more labeled data is available, DomusFM continues to outperform alternatives, demonstrating that it not only excels in few-shot scenarios but also leverages additional supervision effectively. These results highlight the strength of our foundation model approach, showing that pretraining on diverse smart-home environments enables robust transfer learning and strong performance even in minimally supervised target settings.

7.4.
Downstream task 2: Next-
k
Events Prediction

7.4.1.
Problem Formulation

The next-
k
events prediction task addresses a different aspect of smart-home understanding compared to ADL recognition. Given a window
W\in\mathcal{W}
of
N
consecutive sensor events and the set of sensors
S
deployed in the environment, the task consists of predicting the next-
k
events that will occur in the event stream. Formally, for each sensor
s\in S
and each status
\sigma\in\{\text{ON},\text{OFF}\}
, we predict the count
C_{(s,\sigma)}
representing how many times the event
(s,\sigma)
will occur in the next-
k
events.

Our formulation diverges from traditional sequence prediction tasks in several important ways. We are not interested in predicting the exact temporal order in which events will occur, nor their precise timestamps. Instead, we adopt a bag-of-events approach, focusing on predicting which sensor events will occur, with what status (ON or OFF), and how many times each event type will appear within the next-
k
events. This formulation is justified by the fine-grained temporal granularity at which we operate. Since we are predicting the immediate next-
k
events, which occur within a short time window and correspond to micro-actions within a single higher-level activity, the exact sequential order of these fine-grained sensor events is neither meaningful nor reliably predictable.

For instance, when a user is preparing a drink, whether they first open the refrigerator to retrieve water and then open the glass cabinet to get a glass, or perform these actions in reverse order, represents negligible behavioral variation at this granularity. Both sequences are part of the same atomic activity and are functionally equivalent from a behavioral modeling perspective. This stands in contrast to coarser-grained predictions over longer time horizons, where sequence order becomes critical: predicting whether a user will cook and then eat versus eat and then cook represents fundamentally different behavioral patterns. However, at the fine-grained level of individual sensor activations within the immediate future, such micro-level ordering carries little predictive value for practical applications. What matters for applications such as energy management, proactive home automation, or anomaly detection is understanding which resources will be accessed and with what frequency in the near future, rather than their precise microsecond-level ordering within an ongoing activity.

We evaluated DomusFM on two prediction horizon
k=10
and
k=30
events. The
k=30
aligns with the window’s length, while with
k=10
we test the performance over a shorter, more immediate period. Comparing these horizons reveals how accuracy changes as the forecasting window varies.

7.4.2.
Baseline

To the best of our knowledge, no existing approach in the literature specifically addresses next-
k
events prediction using our bag-of-events formulation. Therefore, we adapted the method proposed by Takeda et al.
(
70
)
, which represents the most recent work on events prediction in smart homes and is the closest to our formulation. The original approach employs a generative transformer architecture inspired by the GPT-2 model for predicting sequences of future sensor events, trained from scratch with smart-home sensor event sequences. We modified the inference procedure to predict a fixed number of
k
events following our bag-of-events approach, where the model outputs which events will occur and their respective counts. Apart from this adaptation to match our problem formulation, we trained the baseline using the same architecture and hyperparameters specified in the original paper to ensure a fair comparison.

7.4.3.
Next-
k
Events Prediction Head

For next-
k
events prediction, we implement the head as a dual-head architecture consisting of two separate linear layers: the first predicts a distribution over possible future event types, while the second predicts the expected count for each event type within the next-
k
events.

7.4.4.

Let
M_{\text{GT}}
be the multiset of events in the ground truth sequence,

\text{Precision}=\frac{|M_{\text{GT}}\cap M_{\text{PRED}}|}{|M_{\text{PRED}}|},\quad\text{Recall}=\frac{|M_{\text{GT}}\cap M_{\text{PRED}}|}{|M_{\text{GT}}|},\quad\text{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

The results are therefore expressed in terms of F1-score. Since we are evaluating multiset predictions rather than individual class assignments, only an overall F1-score is computed, with no distinction between macro or weighted variants. Detailed results are presented in Table

7.5.
Ablation study

In order to further validate our proposed architecture, we performed an ablation study focusing on the contextualized event-level feature extractor. We compared the full DomusFM architecture against a version without the contextualized event-level feature extractor, where the task-specific heads operate on event-level embeddings rather than the attention-based contextual representations.
For this study, we chose two representative datasets that capture different home layouts and sensor densities: CASAS Milan and Kasteren C. We evaluated the performance of both the full and ablated model (w/o Context) across our two primary downstream tasks: ADL recognition and next-
30
events prediction, considering all the Train Data percentages.
The results, summarized in Tables

and

30

However, it can be noted that even without the contextualization layer, the results remain competitive and stay above the established baselines. This suggests that, while the contextualization clearly provides a performance boost, the event-level feature extractor alone can be effective at extracting meaningful semantic information from individual sensor events.

8.
Discussion

8.1.
Real-life deployment

A key design objective of DomusFM is to ensure practicality and feasibility in real-world smart-home deployments. Unlike many recent approaches that rely on large-scale models or external cloud-based services, DomusFM is explicitly engineered to be lightweight and computationally efficient, making it suitable for execution on edge devices commonly available in smart-home infrastructures.

The complete pretrained DomusFM model has a total memory usage of less than 500 MB, and the average inference time required to process a single window of sensor events is
9.9\pm 1
milliseconds for ADL recognition and
9.6\pm 1
milliseconds for next-
k
events prediction. These characteristics allow DomusFM to operate efficiently on standard smart-home gateways, which typically are responsible for aggregating and processing sensor data, but have limited computational resources compared to cloud servers. As a result, no specialized hardware or additional computational infrastructure is required for deployment.

This lightweight design enables DomusFM to be deployed directly at the edge, where sensor data are generated and collected. Edge deployment offers several important advantages over cloud-based solutions. First, it eliminates the need to transmit sensitive in-home activity data to external servers or third-party APIs, thereby significantly reducing privacy risks and aligning with the stringent privacy requirements of domestic environments. Second, it avoids recurring costs and latency associated with commercial API-based services, making the solution economically sustainable for long-term deployment. Finally, local inference improves system robustness and scalability, as performance is not affected by network connectivity issues or external service availability.

Beyond computational efficiency, the experimental results presented in Section
7
demonstrate that DomusFM can be effectively fine-tuned with limited labeled data while maintaining strong performance across previously unseen environments. In particular, the leave-one-dataset-out evaluation shows that the model generalizes well to new houses, different sensor configurations, and unseen occupants, despite substantial variability in layouts, sensing infrastructures, and activity distributions. This ability to adapt rapidly to novel smart-home settings with minimal supervision is a critical requirement for real-world deployment, where each installation is inherently unique and extensive annotation is impractical.

Overall, these properties ensure that DomusFM can be realistically deployed in real-world smart-home scenarios at low cost, with strong privacy guarantees and without reliance on external services.

8.2.
Comparison with Zero-shot LLM Approaches

As discussed in Section
3.2
, several recent works have proposed the use of large LLMs to address the ADL recognition problem in smart-home environments. These approaches typically frame activity recognition as a language understanding task by transforming sensor event streams into textual representations and leveraging the reasoning capabilities of pretrained LLMs. Importantly, these methods are limited to ADL recognition and do not extend to other smart-home tasks such as next-
k
event prediction.

A key advantage of LLM-based approaches is their ability to operate in a fully zero-shot setting, requiring no labeled training data. While this property is attractive, it is worth noting that, as discussed earlier in the paper, acquiring a limited amount of labeled data in smart-home scenarios is often realistic (e.g., during system installation or short calibration phases). As such, evaluating the trade-off between zero-shot performance and lightweight fine-tuning is crucial for assessing real-world applicability.

To this end, we compared DomusFM against a representative zero-shot LLM-based approach proposed in
(
39
)
, which operates in a setting closely aligned with ours. Specifically, it performs ADL recognition using event-based segmentation with a fixed window length of 30 events, matching our experimental configuration. In addition to reproducing the original ADL recognition setup, we extended this approach to evaluate its performance on the next-
30
events prediction task, enabling a broader comparison across tasks.

The quantitative comparison is reported in Tables

(ADL recognition) and

The results show that DomusFM consistently outperforms the zero-shot LLM-based approach on both datasets considered, even when fine-tuned using the smallest percentage of labeled data. This highlights the effectiveness of our foundation model in leveraging minimal supervision to achieve superior performance compared to purely zero-shot solutions.

It is important to emphasize that the LLM-based approach was evaluated strictly in its zero-shot configuration. A more exhaustive comparison could investigate few-shot prompting strategies, where labeled training instances are provided as in-context examples to the LLM. While such an analysis is beyond the scope of this work, it would come at the cost of increased prompt complexity, memory usage, and inference latency.

8.3.
Limitations

While DomusFM demonstrates strong performance across diverse smart-home environments, some limitations warrant discussion.

8.3.1.
Single-occupancy scenarios

Our approach focuses on environments with either single occupancy or perfect data association, where each sensor activation can be attributed to a specific individual. While single-occupant scenarios are realistic (e.g., elderly individuals living alone), perfect data association in multi-occupant settings remains an open challenge
(
8
)
. However, we believe DomusFM’s rich semantic representations could contribute to addressing this problem. Future work will explore leveraging our learned representations for improved data association in multi-occupant environments.

8.3.2.
Binarization requirement

Our framework requires continuous sensor data to be converted into binary semantic states. While effective in capturing semantically meaningful information, this necessitates preprocessing and may result in information loss. Future work will explore architectures that directly integrate continuous sensor streams alongside binary events, reducing preprocessing requirements while preserving temporal dynamics and semantics.

8.3.3.
Zero-shot generalization

DomusFM does not yet operate in a zero-shot fashion. Some cross-dataset approaches achieve zero-shot performance by mapping all activities to a common set, but this reduces activity complexity and weakens evaluation of true generalization. In contrast, DomusFM preserves environment-specific activity definitions and adapts with minimal supervision (5% labeled data) without activity mapping. Future work will investigate natural language supervision techniques to enable fully zero-shot operation while maintaining the original activity sets.

8.3.4.
Task coverage

We evaluated two tasks: ADL recognition and next-
k
events prediction, the most studied tasks in smart-home research with established baselines for fair comparison. Future work will evaluate additional tasks, including, for instance anomaly detection, behavior change detection, occupancy prediction, etc., developing appropriate baselines where needed.

9.
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduces DomusFM, the first foundation model specifically designed and pretrained for smart-home sensor data. We formalized the conceptual framework for event-level and contextualized representations, designed a novel architecture integrating semantic understanding with specialized temporal encoders, and demonstrated through rigorous leave-one-dataset-out evaluation across seven datasets that DomusFM substantially outperforms state-of-the-art baselines with minimal supervision (5% labeled data).
DomusFM represents a paradigm shift from task-specific models to a general-purpose foundation model that rapidly adapts to diverse environments, infrastructures, and users. Its lightweight architecture enables edge deployment, addressing critical privacy and latency concerns for practical smart-home systems. By demonstrating effective transfer learning despite data scarcity, this work opens new possibilities for the widespread adoption of intelligent home systems.
Future work, as outlined in Section
8.3
, will extend the model to multi-occupant environments, integrate continuous sensor streams directly, enable fully zero-shot generalization, and validate adaptability across broader smart-home tasks.
