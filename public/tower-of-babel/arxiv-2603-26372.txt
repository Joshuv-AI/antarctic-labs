Dynamics of focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation with partial harmonic confinement in higher dimensions
Tianhao Liu, Zuyu Ma, Yilin Song, Jiqiang Zheng

Abstract
. We study the following focusing intercritical nonlinear Schrödinger equation with partial harmonic confinement: { i ∂ t u + Δ z u − y 2 u = − | u | α u , t ∈ ℝ , u ( 0 , z ) = u 0 ( z ) , z = ( x , y ) ∈ ℝ d × ℝ , \begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u-y^{2}u=-|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\\ u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\ z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}},\end{cases} where d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 is an integer and the exponent α \alpha satisfies (0.1) 4 / d < α < 4 / ( d − 1 ) , if ​ d ≥ 2 ; 4 / d < α < + ∞ , if ​ d = 1 . 4/d<\alpha<{4}/\left(d-1\right),\,\,\,\text{if}~~d\geq 2;\quad\quad 4/d<\alpha<+\infty,\,\,\,\text{if}~~d=1. For this model, A. Ardia and R. Carles [Comm. Math. Sci. 19 (2021), 993-1032] established a sharp scattering result below the ground state threshold in dimensions d ≤ 4 d\leq 4 via the concentration-compactness and rigidity argument. However, their approach breaks down in higher dimensions due to the lack of smoothness in the nonlinearity. In this paper, we introduce a new strategy that removes this dimensional restriction and extend their results to higher dimensions by circumventing the concentration-compactness principle. The main ingredients of our work are the interaction Morawetz-Dodson-Murphy estimates and an alternative variational characterization of the ground state threshold.



Keywords:
Schrödinger equation; Partial harmonic oscillator; Scattering; Blow-up; Variational methods.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

Consider the Cauchy problem for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS) with a partial harmonic confinement

(PHNLS)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u-y^{2}u=-|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\end{cases}

where
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, the complex-valued function
u=u(t,z)\in{\mathbb{C}}
is the unknown wave function. The exponent
\alpha
is chosen in the intercritical range

(1.1)

4/d<\alpha<{4}/\left(d-1\right),\,\,\,\text{if}~~d\geq 2;\quad\quad 4/d<\alpha<+\infty,\,\,\,\text{if}~~d=1.

Here, the left endpoint of (
1.1
) corresponds to the mass-critical exponent in
d
dimensions (the free
x
-direction), while the right endpoint corresponds to the energy-critical exponent in
d+1
dimensions (the full space).

The main purpose of this paper is to study the long-time behaviors of the solutions to (
PHNLS
).
In particular, we establish a complete dichotomy classification of scattering/blow-up in dimensions
d\geq 3
. The precise statement of our main result is given in Section
1.2
. We now provide the background and motivation for studying the dynamics of (
PHNLS
).

Equation (
PHNLS
) is a special case of the general NLS with harmonic potential

(1.2)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u-(\kappa_{1}|x|^{2}+\kappa_{2}|y|^{2})u=\mu|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\end{cases}

where
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
,
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with
d,N\geq 1
, and we denote the total spatial dimension by
m=d+N
. The parameter
\mu\in\left\{+1,-1\right\}
with
\mu=+1
corresponding to the defocusing case and
\mu=-1
to the focusing case.
Equation (
1.2
) is a fundamental model in quantum mechanics, which serves as a model for Bose–Einstein condensates confined in laboratory traps, as well as for describing the envelope dynamics of a general dispersive wave in a weakly nonlinear medium. The harmonic potential in (
1.2
) corresponds to the standard modelling for magnetic traps in the context of Bose–Einstein condensation; see, e.g.,
[
32
,
37
]
. Further physical motivation and background can be found in
[
6
,
3
,
15
]
.

For the autonomous case
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=0
, equation (
1.2
) reduces to the standard NLS

(1.3)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u=\mu|u|^{\alpha}u,&(t,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z).\end{cases}

For the defocusing case, the global well-posedness and scattering in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
for (
1.3
) are classical results, we refer to the Cazenave’s textbook
[
13
]
. For the focusing case, the global well-posedness was considered by Weinstein
[
41
]
.
The scattering was later proved by Holmer-Roudenko
[
28
]
for radial initial data and Duyckaerts-Holmer-Roudenko
[
20
]
for non-radial data with
(\alpha,m)=(2,3)
, and ultimately extended to any dimension and any inter-critical power by Akahori-Nawa
[
1
]
, Fang-Xie-Cazenave
[
23
]
and Guevara
[
26
]
. Their method is the application of concentration-compactness argument initially developed in Kenig-Merle
[
33
]
which was the first used to treat the energy-critical case. Later, Dodson-Murphy
[
18
,
19
]
developed a new method to study the scattering for this model without relying on the concentration-compactness argument.

For the non-autonomous case where at least one
\kappa_{j}
is nonzero, the presence of harmonic potential influences strongly the dynamics of the solution. When all
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=1
, the harmonic potential reduces to the (isotropic) quadratic form
|z|^{2}
, leading to the following model

(1.4)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u-|z|^{2}u=\mu|u|^{\alpha}u,&(t,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z).\end{cases}

Global well-posedness for (
1.4
) has been established in a series of works: see
[
11
,
10
,
42
]
for the energy-subcritical case, and
[
34
,
29
,
30
]
for the energy-critical case.
It is well-known that the solutions of (
1.4
) do not scatter because the operator
-\Delta_{z}+|z|^{2}
has a purely discrete spectrum. We would also like to mention that the long-time behavior of (
1.4
) can be compared to the case of the pure torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}
. Indeed, for the cubic NLS with
\alpha=2
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, a non-scattering solution was constructed in
[
16
]
. This behavior was later quantified by Guardia and Kaloshin
[
25
]
. In a similar spirit, Chabert
[
14
]
constructed a solution for the cubic NLS with a harmonic oscillator potential whose
H^{1}
norm grows.

Nevertheless, if the confinement is turned off in some but not all directions, the condensate may still evolve asymptotically freely. This motivates the study of NLS (
1.2
) with (anisotropic) partial harmonic potential.
Without loss of generality, we consider the case
\kappa_{1}=0
and
\kappa_{2}=1
, which leads to the following model

(1.5)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u-|y|^{2}u=\mu|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\end{cases}

where
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
Observe that since the potential is added in
y
-direction, a global-in-time dispersive estimate is not expected for this direction. This can be seen from Mehler’s formula

\displaystyle e^{it(\Delta_{y}-|y|^{2})}f(x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi i\sin(2t))^{\frac{N}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{\frac{i}{\sin(2t)}\big(\frac{y^{2}+\zeta^{2}}{2}\cos(2t)-y\cdot\zeta\big)}f(x,\zeta)d\zeta.

Consequently, one obtains following periodic-in-time dispersive estimate

(1.6)

\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{y}-|y|^{2})}f(x,y)\big\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\lesssim|\sin(2t)|^{-\frac{N}{2}}\|f(x,y)\|_{L_{y}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})},\quad\forall t\notin\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{Z}.

However, by the strong dispersion of the
x
-direction, we have the global-in-time dispersive estimate

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y}-|y|^{2})}f(x,y)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\lesssim|t|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|f(x,y)\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N})}.

Therefore, one can expect the scattering for equation (
1.5
) in the intercritical case

(1.7)

\frac{4}{d}<\alpha<\frac{4}{d+N-2},

which holds if and only if
N=1
. It is therefore meaningful to investigate the long-time dynamics of the solutions to equation (
1.5
) with one-dimensional partial harmonic confinement

(1.8)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u-y^{2}u=\mu|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\end{cases}

under the assumption (
1.1
), where
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
.

Over the past decade, the long-time behavior of (
1.8
) has been studied by several authors.
The pioneering work by Antonelli, Carles, and Silva
[
3
]
first addressed this problem in the defocusing case
\mu=1
. By establishing a new interaction Morawetz estimate associated with the partial harmonic oscillator, they proved scattering phenomenon under the assumption (
1.1
) for dimensions
1\leq d\leq 3
within the fully weighted Sobolev space

\left\{u\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}):|z|u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})\right\}.

Further results on the defocusing case can be found in
[
15
,
17
,
12
]
.

In this paper, we are particularly interested in the long-time behavior of solutions to (
1.8
) in the focusing case, that is, for equation (
PHNLS
). Compared to the defocusing case, the focusing case is more delicate and requires a more subtle analysis. In the study of (
PHNLS
), the following mass and energy quantities are conserved

(1.9)

\displaystyle M(u(t))=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}|u(t,z)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle E(u(t))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}|\nabla_{z}u(t,z)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}z+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}y^{2}|u(t,z)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}z-\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}|u(t,z)|^{\alpha+2}~\mathrm{d}z.

It is natural to take the initial data
u_{0}
from the following weighted Sobolev space

(1.10)

\Sigma:=\left\{u\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}):\|yu\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{2}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}y^{2}|u|^{2}~\mathrm{d}z<+\infty\right\},

equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{\Sigma}^{2}=\|\nabla_{z}u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{2}+\|yu\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{2}.
As established in
[
4
]
, the solution to (
PHNLS
) is locally well-posed in the energy space
\Sigma
. To investigate the global dynamics, we first introduce some essential definitions.

\bullet
Semivirial functional.

In the study of long-time dynamics for focusing NLS, Glassey’s virial identity plays a crucial role.
Recall the standard focusing NLS on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(1.11)

i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{x}u=-|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

which coincides with (
PHNLS
) when the confinement in the
y
-direction is removed (so that
\Delta_{z}-y^{2}
reduces to
\Delta_{x}
).
Define the virial action functional
V(t)
by

(1.12)

V(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}|u(t,x)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}x.

Glassey
[
24
]
established the following virial identity:

(1.13)

V^{\prime\prime}(t)=8\widetilde{Q}(u(t)):=8\left(\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}-\frac{\alpha d}{2\left(\alpha+2\right)}\|u(t)\|_{L^{\alpha+2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\alpha+2}\right).

The quantity
\widetilde{Q}(u(t))
is called the virial functional. Consequently, if the virial functional is bounded above by some negative value, then the solution will blow-up in finite time. Conversely, a positive value of the virial functional may indicate global well-posedness or even scattering, see for instance
[
33
,
41
]
.
Inspired by these ideas, when considering the long-time dynamics of (
PHNLS
), it is natural to introduce a similar quantity

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}|z|^{2}|u(t,z)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}z.

However, due to the presence of partial harmonic confinement
y^{2}
, this functional is of limited use for studying the long-time dynamics of (
PHNLS
). Recall that the confinement in the
y
-direction is not expected to provide large time dispersion, while the
x
-direction should yield large time dispersion. It is therefore natural to consider the dispersive
effects that are only provided by the
x
-direction, posed on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. This leads us to consider the
semivirial
action functional
V_{semi}(t)
defined by

(1.14)

V_{semi}(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}|x|^{2}|u(t,z)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}z.

A direct calculation implies that

(1.15)

V_{semi}^{\prime\prime}(t)=8Q(u(t))=8\left(\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{2}-\frac{\alpha d}{2\left(\alpha+2\right)}\|u(t)\|_{L^{\alpha+2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{\alpha+2}\right).

We call
Q(u(t))
the
semivirial
functional. As we will see in Theorem
1.1
, the condition
Q(\phi)\geq 0
is sharp for global existence.

\bullet
Ground state.

To establish the sharp threshold for the long-time dynamics, it is essential to introduce the notion of a ground state. Consider the following elliptic equation

(1.16)

-\Delta_{z}\phi+y^{2}\phi+\omega\phi=|\phi|^{\alpha}\phi,\quad z=\left(x,y\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}},

where
\omega>0
. A nontrivial solution
\phi_{\mathrm{GS}}\in\Sigma
to equation (
1.16
) is called a ground state
if it minimizes the action functional

S_{\omega}(\phi)=E(\phi)+\frac{\omega}{2}M(\phi)

among all nontrivial solutions of the elliptic problem (
1.16
). That is,

(1.17)

S_{\omega}(\phi_{\mathrm{GS}})=m_{GS}:=\inf\left\{S_{\omega}(\phi):\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}~~\text{solves}~~\eqref{equ elliptic}\right\}.

The existence and properties of ground states
\phi_{\mathrm{GS}}
can be established via variational methods, exploiting the compactness induced by the harmonic potential in the
y
-direction, see
[
4
]
.
Ground states play a key role in characterizing the threshold for the long-time dynamics. In this direction, the work of Ardila and Carles
[
4
]
established the global dynamics below the ground state energy
m_{GS}
for dimensions
1\leq d\leq 4
under assumption (
1.1
). However, their framework does not extend to dimensions
d\geq 5
, a regime that remains open and is considerably more delicate. In this paper, we overcome this dimensional limitation by introducing an alternative strategy that is applicable for all dimensions
d\geq 3
.

1.2.
Main results

For any
\omega>0
, we define

(1.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\omega}^{+}:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}:~S_{\omega}(\phi)<m_{GS},\quad Q(\phi)\geq 0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\omega}^{-}:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}:~S_{\omega}(\phi)<m_{GS},\quad Q(\phi)<0\right\}.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
d\geq 3
and that (
1.1
) holds. Let
u(t)\in C(I,\Sigma)
be a solution of (
PHNLS
) with initial data
u_{0}=u(0)
, where
I=(-T_{-},T_{+})
denotes the maximal lifespan with
T_{\pm}>0
.
Then we have the following dichotomy classification of dynamics.

(1)

If
u_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{\omega}^{+}
, then the solution
u(t)
is globally well-posed and scatters in the sense that there exists
\varphi_{\pm}\in\Sigma
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{{t\to\pm\infty}}\big\|u-e^{it(\Delta_{z}-y^{2})}\varphi_{\pm}\big\|_{\Sigma}=0.

(2)

If
u_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{\omega}^{-}
, then either
u(t)
blows-up in finite time
T_{\pm}<+\infty
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{{t\to\pm T_{\pm}}}\|\nabla_{z}u(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}=+\infty;

or
u(t)
blows-up in infinite time, i.e.
T_{\pm}=+\infty
and there exists a sequence
\{t_{k}\}
with
t_{k}\to\pm\infty
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t_{k}\to\pm\infty}\|\nabla_{z}u(t_{k})\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}=+\infty.

Moreover, if
|z|u_{0}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})
, then
u(t)
blows-up in finite time.

We note that Ardila and Carles
[
4
]
proved Theorem
1.1
for dimensions
1\leq d\leq 4
. Their proof of scattering is based on the concentration-compactness and rigidity argument pioneered by Kenig and Merle
[
33
]
.
This framework, however, does not extend to dimensions
d\geq 5
, because
|\phi|^{\alpha}
fails to be Lipschitz continuous in higher dimensions. More precisely, the argument in
[
4
, Proposition 5.6]
requires the condition
\alpha\geq 1
, which only holds in dimensions
1\leq d\leq 4
. A similar obstruction appears in the study of the standard NLS (
1.3
). To overcome this, Tao-Visan
[
38
]
utilized the fractional calculus to prove a weaker stability result, which is sufficient for the purpose. However, the anisotropic nature will bring us new difficulties and we do not know whether such weaker stability result holds since the order of derivatives in
x,y
-directions are different.

In proving the scattering result for
d\geq 3
in Theorem
1.1
, we introduce an alternative approach utilizing interaction Morawetz-Dodson-Murphy (IMDM) estimates
[
19
]
and a coercivity estimate for the semivirial functional
Q
(see Lemma
5.3
). A key observation is that, despite the lack of spatial translation and scaling invariance in the equation, the method is highly compatible with the roadmap developed by Dodson and Murphy
[
19
]
. In their work, a scattering criterion is introduced and subsequently verified via an interaction Morawetz estimate. A crucial ingredient in this argument is a suitable coercivity property, where the variational structure of the ground state plays a central role, such as the sharp Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality and Pohozaev identities. In
[
19
]
, this coercivity estimate follows from a refined Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (see
[
19
, Lemma 2.1]
)

(1.19)

\|f\|_{L^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-1}}\leq C_{GN}\|f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\|\nabla f\|^{\frac{2}{d-2}}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\|\nabla(e^{ix\cdot\xi}f)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}},

which holds for any
f\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

However, this inequality is not applicable in our context. Indeed, due to the presence of the partial harmonic potential
y^{2}
and the inhomogeneous nature of the nonlinearity, our problem lacks both translation and scaling invariance, rendering refined estimates such as (
1.19
) unavailable. This lack of symmetry not only makes the variational problem much more complicated compared to the free case as in
[
19
]
, but also brings difficulty in proving the interaction Morawetz estimate. It is here that the semivirial functional
Q
becomes indispensable, which serves as the key tool that adapts the Dodson-Murphy roadmap to the non-translation-invariant, non-scale-invariant framework of our problem, thereby distinguishing our approach from
[
4
]
.

We now outline the main steps and ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The blow-up result, which is included here to provide a complete characterization of the dynamics of (
PHNLS
), follows from the Glassey’s convex method
[
24
]
and can be obtained by following the arguments in
[
4
]
. The scattering result, which constitutes the main contribution of this work, can be proved in four steps.

Step 1. To implement the strategy proposed in
[
19
]
, we first establish a scattering criterion for (
PHNLS
) (see Lemma
3.1
). In our setting, the presence of potential
y^{2}
breaks the dispersive estimate. To resolve this, we observe that in the anisotropic norm
L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})
, one can capture the dipsersive effect by the
x
-variable. This requires us to establish such criterion with the anisotropic Strichartz norm.

Step 2. To verify this scattering criterion, we employ an interaction Morawetz estimate developed by Dodson and Murphy
[
19
]
, as detailed in Theorem
5.1
. Notably, our choice of multiplier involves only the
x
-direction and therefore differs from that used in
[
19
]
. In the proof of Theorem
5.1
, a coercivity estimate associated with the virial functional
Q
plays a crucial role, see Lemma
5.3
. This estimate hinges on the following minimization problem

(1.20)

m_{\omega}=\inf\left\{S_{\omega}(\phi):~\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\},\quad Q(\phi)=0\right\}.

In Theorem
4.1
, we prove that this infimum
m_{\omega}
is attained by a minimizer
\phi_{\omega}
, which is also a solution to (
1.16
). Solving this variational problem is highly nontrivial, as the relation

Q(\phi)=0
is not a natural constraint and introduces certain analytical difficulties. We discuss these challenges in detail in Section
4
.

Step 3. We introduce a new set

(1.21)

\displaystyle P_{\omega}^{+}:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma:~\phi\neq 0,\quad S_{\omega}(\phi)<m_{\omega},\quad Q(\phi)\geq 0\right\}.

In Theorem
6.1
, by applying the interaction Morawetz estimate, we prove that any solution
u(t)
of (
PHNLS
) is globally well-posed and scatters if the initial data
u_{0}\in P_{\omega}^{+}
.

Step 4. It remains to show that
P_{\omega}^{+}=\mathcal{K}_{\omega}^{+}
, or, equivalently, that
m_{\omega}=m_{\mathrm{GS}}
, see Lemma
6.1
. This equality is not a priori obvious. While Theorem
4.1
provides the inequality
m_{\mathrm{GS}}\leq m_{\omega}
, the reverse inequality
m_{\omega}\leq m_{\mathrm{GS}}
does not hold in general because the ground state
\phi_{\mathrm{GS}}
may fail to satisfy the constraint
Q(\phi_{\mathrm{GS}})=0
due to the presence of potential
y^{2}
. To establish this condition, we introduce a truncation semivirial functional. More precisely, for any
R>0
, we define

V_{R}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\tilde{\varphi}_{R}(x)|e^{i\omega t}\phi_{GS}(z)|^{2}dz,

where
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}=R^{2}\tilde{\varphi}(\frac{x}{R})
. Here,
\tilde{\varphi}
is a positive radial smooth function in
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
satisfying
\tilde{\varphi}(r)=r^{2}
for
r=|x|\leq 1
and
\tilde{\varphi}(r)^{\prime\prime}\leq 2
for
r\geq 0
. Obviously,
V_{R}(t)
is independent of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which implies that
V_{R}^{\prime\prime}(t)\equiv 0
. Moreover, by a direct calculaion, we have

\displaystyle V_{R}^{\prime\prime}(0)=8Q(\phi_{GS})+A_{R},

where the remainder term
A_{R}
involves the derivatives of
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}(r)
.
Using the property of the weighted function
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}(r)
, we can show that
|A_{R}|\to 0
as
R\to\infty
, which provides that

Q(\phi_{GS})=0.

This completes the proof.

Finally, we emphasize that in comparison with recent works addressing combined nonlinearities (cf.
[
7
]
), where symmetry-breaking also poses challenges, the variational problem we encounter is substantially more intricate. This increased complexity stems not only from the loss of spatial translation/Galilean invariance induced by the potential, but also from the anisotropic characterization of the partial harmonic oscillator, which forces us to treat the
x
and
y
directions with different Sobolev exponents. Consequently, both the resolution of the minimization problem
m_{\omega}
and the derivation of the coercivity estimate become comparably more delicate. As a result, our proof is not a straightforward adaptation of
[
19
,
7
]
; rather, it requires overcoming difficulties specific to the partial harmonic confinement and developing new variational tools to bypass these obstacles.

Remark 1.1
.

(The NLS on the waveguide manifolds)
Our results can be compared to those for NLS posed on waveguide manifolds
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}
, which can be written as

(1.22)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{z}u=\mu|u|^{\alpha}u,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\end{cases}

where
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}
and
d\geq 1
. Observe that the compactness of
\mathbb{T}
indicates that any dispersive effects must be purely provided by the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-side. For the defocusing case
\mu=1
, scattering in the range (
1.1
) was proved by Tzvetkov-Visciglia
[
40
]
. In the focusing case

Remark 1.2

2.1.
Fourier transform and functional spaces

In this subsection, we recall the basic definition and properties of Fourier transform and some useful functional spaces.

First, we define the Fourier transform of function
f
as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}f)(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}f(z)e^{-iz\cdot\xi}\,dz,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}

and the inverse Fourier transform of
\mathcal{F}f
as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}f)^{\vee}(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{iz\cdot\xi}d\xi.

Then, for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we can define the fractional order differential operator
|\nabla|^{s}
as

(|\nabla|^{s}f)^{\wedge}(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}\widehat{f}(\xi).

Similarly, the operator
\langle\nabla\rangle^{s}
can be defined as

(\langle\nabla\rangle^{s}f)^{\wedge}(\xi)=\langle\xi\rangle^{s}\widehat{f}(\xi):=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}\widehat{f}(\xi).

In our paper, we also need the Fourier transform and inverse Fourier transform with respect to one variable. For example, we give the definition of Fourier transform on
x
-variable. For
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, we define

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{x}f)(\xi,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x,y)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}dx,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Let
\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{+},{\mathbb{R}})
be a cut-off function such that
\chi(t)=1
if
t\leqslant 1
and
\chi(t)=0
if
t\geqslant 2
. For
N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we let

\displaystyle\psi_{N}(t)=\psi(N^{-1}t),\quad\varphi_{N}(t)=\psi_{N}(t)-\psi_{N/2}(t).

Define the full Littlewood-Paley projector as

\displaystyle P_{\leqslant N}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\psi_{N}(|\xi|)\widehat{f}(\xi)),\quad x,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1},

\displaystyle P_{N}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\varphi_{N}(|\xi|)\widehat{f}(\xi)),\quad x,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1},

and the partial Littlewood-Paley projector by

\displaystyle P_{\leqslant N}^{x}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{x}^{-1}(\chi_{N}(|\xi|)(\mathcal{F}_{x}f)(\xi,y)),\hskip 4.30554ptx,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,y\in{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle P_{N}^{x}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{x}^{-1}(\varphi_{N}(|\xi|)(\mathcal{F}_{x}f)(\xi,y)),\hskip 4.30554ptx,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Next, we denote the standard Lebesgue spaces by
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})
and its norm is given by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}=\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}|f(z)|^{p}\,dz\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Let
I
be the time interval, we denote the mixed space-time Lebesgue space as
L_{t}^{q}L_{z}^{r}(I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})
with the norm

\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}L^{r}_{z}(I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}=\|\|u(t)\|_{L^{r}_{z}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}\|_{L^{q}_{t}(I)}.

We denote
W^{s,p}
by the inhomogeneous Sobolev space,

\displaystyle W^{s,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}):=\left\{f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}):\|f\|_{W^{s,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}:=\|\langle\nabla\rangle^{s}f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}<+\infty\right\}.

2.2.
Harmonic oscillator and associated functional space

The Schrödinger operator with harmonic potential
H=-\partial_{y}^{2}+y^{2}
with
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
is an important model in mathematics and physics. The one-dimensional harmonic oscillator can form a
L^{2}
-basis. We denote
E_{n}
by the
n
-th eigenspace related to
H
and
\lambda_{n}=2n+1
the
n
-th eigenvalues. The eigenspace can be generated by the Hermite function
e_{n}
which is given by

\displaystyle e_{n}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}2^{\frac{n}{2}}\pi^{\frac{1}{4}}}(-1)^{n}e^{\frac{y^{2}}{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}(e^{-y^{2}}),\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}},

and
e_{n}
statisfies the following equation

\displaystyle(-\partial_{y}^{2}+y^{2})e_{n}(y)=(2n+1)e_{n}(y).

We denote
\Pi_{n}
by the spectral projector on the
n
-th eigenspace
E_{n}
. For any
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})
, we can decompose it as

f(z)=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Pi_{n}f(x,y):=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle f,e_{n}\rangle_{L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}e_{n}(y),

where

\langle f,e_{n}\rangle_{L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x,y)\overline{e_{n}(y)}dy.

For
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
p\geqslant 1
, we denote the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
\mathcal{W}_{y}^{s,p}({\mathbb{R}})
by the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{y}^{s,p}({\mathbb{R}})=\big\{u\in L_{y}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}):\|u\|_{\mathcal{W}_{y}^{s,p}({\mathbb{R}})}:=\|\langle\partial_{y}\rangle^{s}u\|_{L_{y}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})}+\|y^{s}u\|_{L_{y}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})}<\infty\big\}.

If
p=2
, the above norm is equivalent to the following, which was proved in
[
21
]

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}_{y}^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}(2n+1)^{s}\|\Pi_{n}f\|_{L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}.

Throughout this article, we denote
\mathcal{W}_{y}^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}})
by
\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}({\mathbb{R}})
. We denote
L_{x}^{p}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})
by

\displaystyle L_{x}^{p}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})=\bigg\{f\in L_{x}^{p}L_{y}^{2}

\displaystyle({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}):\|f\|_{L_{x}^{p}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})}:=\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}({\mathbb{R}})}^{p}dx\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\|\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(2n+1)^{s}|f_{n}(x,y)|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{L_{y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{p}dx\bigg)^{1/p}<\infty\bigg\},

where
f_{n}=\Pi_{n}f
.
We can define the Hermite-Sobolev spaces
H_{x}^{s_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}
and
\dot{H}_{x}^{s_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}
as

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H_{x}^{s_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})}^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\langle\nabla_{x}\rangle^{s_{1}}f(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}dx.

and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{s_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})}^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\||\nabla_{x}|^{s_{1}}f(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}dx,

For arbitrary time interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and
f:I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
, we can define the mixed norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}W_{y}^{s,r}(I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\Bigg(\int_{I}\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big\|\langle\partial_{y}\rangle^{s}f(t,x,\cdot)\big\|_{L_{y}^{r}({\mathbb{R}})}^{q}dx\bigg)^{\frac{p}{q}}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}(I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\Bigg(\int_{I}\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big\|f(t,x,\cdot)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}({\mathbb{R}})}^{q}dx\bigg)^{\frac{p}{q}}dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

with
1\leqslant p,q,r\leqslant\infty
. We also use the following norms for sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\|f_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}L_{y}^{r}\ell_{n}^{2}(I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{N})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\big\|\|f_{n}\|_{\ell_{n}^{2}({\mathbb{N}})}\big\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}L_{y}^{r}(I\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}})}.

We end this section by giving a Moser type estimate, which is crucial in establishing the nonlinear estimate.

Lemma 2.1

(Moser type estimate,
[
29
]
)
.

For any
m\geq 1
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
p_{i},q_{i},r\in(1,\infty)
satisfying
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{q_{i}}
,
i=1,2
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\big\|H^{\gamma}(fg)\big\|_{L^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim\|H^{\gamma}f\|_{L^{p_{1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\|g\|_{L^{q_{1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}+\|H^{\gamma}f\|_{L^{p_{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\|g\|_{L^{q_{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})},

where
H^{\gamma}
is defined by the functional calculus of the harmonic oscillator
H
.

Remark 2.1
.

In the rest of this paper, unless otherwise specified, integrals with respect to
t
,
x
and
y
are over
{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{R}}
, respectively. For brevity, we suppress the domains of integration in the notation.

2.3.
Local theory

In this section, we will prove the local control result. We also collect the related result such as local well-posedness. Compared to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation without the potential, we use the anisotropic norm as the scattering size.

Before presenting the local theory, we first recall the Strichartz estimates, which is very crucial in establishing both local and global theory for dispersive equations. We shall use the following notation.

Definition 2.1

(Schrödinger admissible pairs)
.

We call
(p,q)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
the admissible pairs if
(p,q)
satisfies the following

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p}=d\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\Big),\quad 2\leqslant p,q<\infty.

Now we can state the Strichartz estimates for linear Schrödinger equations with a partially harmonic oscillator. These estimates were proved in Proposition 3.1 in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Strichartz estimates,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
I
be a interval,
(p,q)
and
(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})
be any Schrödinger admissible pairs, we have the following estimate

(2.1)

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}f\big\|_{L_{t}^{p}(I,L_{x}^{q}L_{y}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}F(s,x,y)\Big\|_{L_{t}^{p}(I,L_{x}^{q}L_{y}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\tilde{p}^{\prime}}(I,L_{x}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{2})},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}f\big\|_{L_{t}^{p}(I,L_{x}^{q}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{\sigma})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{\sigma}},\quad\sigma\geq 0.

Lemma 2.2

(Exotic Strichartz estimates,
[
39
,
40
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
. There exists
q,r,q_{1},r_{1}\in(2,\infty)
such that the following estimate holds

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}F(s,x,y)\Big\|_{L_{t}^{q}(I,L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{\gamma})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\tilde{q_{1}}^{\prime}}(I,L_{x}^{\tilde{r_{1}}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{\gamma})},

where

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q_{1}}<1,\,\,\frac{d-2}{d}<\frac{r}{r_{1}}<\frac{d}{d-2},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}+\frac{r}{2}<\frac{d}{2},\,\,\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{d}{r_{1}}<\frac{d}{2},\,\,\frac{2}{q}+\frac{2}{q_{1}}+\frac{d}{r_{1}}+\frac{d}{r}=d.

If
d=2
, the condition (
2.5
) can be removed.

Remark 2.2
.

The Strichartz estimates for Schrödinger equation with partially harmonic oscillator is very closed to that in the waveguide manifold setting. The exotic Strichartz estimate can be proved by using eigenbasic expansion in
y
-direction and following the strategies in
[
39
,
40
]
.

For the pure harmonic oscillator, the effect of the potential implies that the dispersive estimate can only hold locally-in-time instead of globally-in-time. By Mehler’s formula, the associated heat kernel can be written as

\displaystyle e^{t(\Delta-|z|^{2})}(z,z^{\prime})=e^{\alpha(t)(z^{2}+(z^{\prime})^{2})}e^{\frac{sinh(t)\Delta}{2}}(z,z^{\prime}),\quad(z,z^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1},

where
\alpha(t)=\frac{1-cosh(t)}{2sinh(t)}
. Using the analytic continuation, we can write the solution to the linear Schrödinger equation with partial harmonic oscillator
\Delta_{y}-y^{2}
explicitly,

\displaystyle e^{it(\Delta_{y}-y^{2})}f(x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi i\sin(2t))^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\frac{i}{\sin(2t)}\big(\frac{y^{2}+\zeta^{2}}{2}\cos(2t)-y\cdot\zeta\big)}f(x,\zeta)d\zeta.

Then one can verify that

(2.7)

\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{y}-y^{2})}f(x,y)\big\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim|\sin(2t)|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f(x,y)\|_{L_{y}^{1}},\quad\forall t\notin\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{Z}.

However, by adding the strong dispersion of the
x
-direction, we have the global-in-time dispersive estimate:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}f(x,y)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim|t|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|f(x,y)\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}}.

Before stating the local well-posedness result, we give some notations. We denote
X_{1}(t)
and
X_{2}(t)
by the following

\displaystyle A_{1}(t)=y\sin(t)-i\cos(t)\partial_{y},\quad A_{2}(t)=y\cos(t)+i\sin(t)\partial_{y}.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})
, we have the following identity:

(2.8)

\displaystyle|A_{1}(t)f(x,y)|^{2}+|A_{2}(t)f(x,y)|^{2}=|yf(x,y)|^{2}+|\partial_{y}f(x,y)|^{2}.

By calculating the commutator, we have
[i\partial_{t}-H,A_{j}(t)]=0
, where
H=-\partial_{y}^{2}+y^{2}
. This implies that when operator
A_{j}(t)
acts on
F(u)
, it is similar to take the derivatives. More precise, we have the pointwise bound

(2.9)

\displaystyle\big|A_{j}(t)(|u|^{\alpha}u)\big|\lesssim|u|^{\alpha}|A_{j}(t)u|.

As a direct consequence, we have the following identity

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\Sigma}^{2}=\|u\|_{L_{x}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1}}^{2}+\|A_{1}(t)u\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}+\|A_{2}(t)u\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}.

Now we give the local well-posedness for (
PHNLS
), which was proved in
[
4
]

Lemma 2.4

(Local Cauchy theory)
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
u_{0}\in\Sigma
. Assume that (
1.1
) holds, then there exists
T=T(\|u_{0}\|_{\Sigma})>0
such that (
PHNLS
) admits a unique local solution
u\in C([0,T],\Sigma)\cap L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}
with
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
(q,r)
is the
\dot{H}^{\frac{d}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha}}
-admissible pair. Let
T_{max}
be the maximal lifespan of
u
, then either
T_{max}=\infty
(global-in-time) or
T_{max}<\infty
and satisfies

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{z}u(t)\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}\to\infty,\,\,\mbox{as }t\to T_{max}.

Moreover, if there exists sufficiently small
0<\eta\ll 1
such that
\|u_{0}\|_{\Sigma}<\eta
, then
u\in C({\mathbb{R}},\Sigma)
is globally well-posed and scatters in both time directions.

We will then present a local control result, after first establishing a fractional chain rule for the harmonic oscillator.

Lemma 2.5

(Fractional chain rule)
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
s\in[0,1]
, we have the following Moser type estimate

(2.10)

\displaystyle\big\||u|^{\alpha}u\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}\|u\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}^{\alpha}.

Proof.

For
s\in[0,1]
, it suffices to show the two endpoints. When
s=0
, it is a direct consequence of Hölder’s inequality. When
s=1
, one should use the pointwise bound (
2.9
) and Hölder. Then by interpolation, we have proved the desired estimate.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Local control result)
.

Let
u
be a global energy solution to (
PHNLS
) with
\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}\Sigma}<\infty
and
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1-s_{c})
where
s_{c}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha}
. Then for any admissible pairs
(p,q)
with
p<\infty
and compact interval
I
with length
|I|
, it holds

\displaystyle\big\|u\big\|_{L_{t}^{p}(I,W_{x}^{1-s,q}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}\lesssim\langle|I|\rangle^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Proof.

First, we define the norm

\displaystyle X=\|u\|_{L_{t}^{p}W_{x}^{1,q}L_{y}^{2}}+\|u\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1}}+\|u\|_{L_{t}^{p_{1}}W_{x}^{1,q_{1}}L_{y}^{2}}+\|u\|_{L_{t}^{p_{1}}L_{x}^{q_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1}}.

Now, we turn to give the control of the norm
X
. Let
r_{1}>\frac{\alpha}{d}
be such that
H_{x}^{1-s}\hookrightarrow L_{x}^{r_{1}}
, which is implied by
s<1-s_{c}
. Suppose that
b_{1}
satisfies
\frac{2}{b_{1}}+\frac{d}{r_{1}}=\frac{2}{\alpha}.
Then there exists an admissible pair
(p_{1},q_{1})
such that

\displaystyle\frac{d+2}{2d}=\frac{\alpha}{r_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{1}},\,\,\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\alpha}{b_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{1}}.

Then by Strichartz estimate, Hölder’s inequality, Sobolev embedding
H_{y}^{s}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
and the properties of
A_{j}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\big\|A_{j}(t)(|u|^{\alpha}u)\big\|_{L_{t}^{2}(I,L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}L_{y}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\big\|\|u\|_{L_{x}^{r_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}^{\alpha}\|A_{j}(t)u\|_{L_{x}^{q_{1}}L_{y}^{2}}\big\|_{L_{t}^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+|I|^{\frac{\alpha}{b_{1}}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}(I,L_{x}^{r_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}^{\alpha}\|A_{j}(t)u\|_{L_{t}^{p_{1}}(I,L_{x}^{q_{1}}L_{y}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+|I|^{\frac{\alpha}{b_{1}}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}(I,H_{x}^{1-s}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}^{\alpha}\cdot X\lesssim 1+|I|^{\frac{\alpha}{b_{1}}}X.

Notice that we only treat the terms involving
\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1}
norm in
X
, others are more easier to treat by repeating the estimate above. Following the continuity method, Proposition 2.3 in Carles-Gallo
[
12
]
and the property of
A_{j}(t)
,
we have the expected estimate. Denote by
H=-\Delta+|x|^{2}
with
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we then have

[L^{2},D(H^{\frac{1}{2}})]_{\theta}=D(H^{\frac{\theta}{2}})=\big\{u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):|x|^{s}u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\big\}.

Combining with the abstract vector-valued interpolation space theorem (cf. Theorem 3.1 in
[
2
]
) and the equivalence of Sobolev norms (cf.
[
21
]
), we have the following interpolation estimate

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W_{x}^{1-s,r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{x}^{r}\Sigma}^{s}\|f\|_{W_{x}^{1,r}L_{y}^{2}}^{1-s}

for
r\in(1,\infty)
. Therefore, we have the desired estimate.

Hence, we complete the proof of this lemma.
∎

3.
Scattering criterion

In this section, we will establish the scattering criterion for (
PHNLS
). This type of scattering criterion was initially developed by Dodson and Murphy in
[
19
]
. In our setting, the lack of dispersion effect in
y
-direction requires us to establish such criterion with the anisotropic norm.

Lemma 3.1

(Scattering criterion)
.

Let
u
be a global solution to (
PHNLS
) such that

\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}\Sigma}\leq B

with some
B<\infty
. Then for all
\sigma>0
, there exists
\varepsilon(\sigma,B)>0
sufficiently small and
T_{0}(\sigma,\varepsilon,B)
sufficiently large so that if for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
there exists
T\in(a,a+T_{0})
such that
[T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T]\subset(a,a+T_{0})
and

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}([T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T],L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}\lesssim\varepsilon,

where
(q,r)=(\frac{2\alpha(\alpha+2)}{{2\alpha+4-d\alpha}},\alpha+2)
is an admissible pair of
H^{s_{c}}
-level with
s_{c}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha}
and
s=1-s_{c}
,
then
u
scatters in forward time direction.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, it remains to prove that there exists
T>0
sufficiently large such that the following estimate holds for some
\mu>0
:

\displaystyle\big\|e^{i(t-T)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u(T)\big\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}([T,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\mu}.

By the standard Duhamel formula, one has

\displaystyle e^{i(t-T)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u(T)=e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u_{0}-i\int_{0}^{T}e^{i(t-\tau)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\,d\tau.

For the linear part, by Strichartz estimate, we find that

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u_{0}\big\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{\Sigma}.

By the continuity, there exists a
T\gg 1
sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u_{0}\big\|_{L_{t}^{q}([T,\infty),L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}<\varepsilon,

where
\varepsilon>0
small enough. Then splitting the time interval, we rewrite

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt\int_{0}^{T}e^{i(t-\tau)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}e^{i(t-\tau)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\,d\tau+\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}e^{i(t-\tau)(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle=:G_{1}(t)+G_{2}(t).

From the Strichartz estimate and (
2.10
), one has

\displaystyle\big\|G_{2}(t)\big\|_{L_{t}^{q}([T,\infty),L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\||u|^{\alpha}u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{0}^{\prime}}([T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T),L_{x}^{r_{0}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}\lesssim\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}([T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T),L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s})}^{\alpha+1}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\alpha+1},

where
(q_{0},r_{0})
is a
H_{x}^{s_{c}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
-admissible pairs and satisfies
q_{0}^{\prime}(\alpha+1)=q
and
r_{0}^{\prime}(\alpha+1)=r
. Then it suffices to estimate the term
G_{1}(t)
. By interpolation inequality in
x
-direction, one has

\displaystyle\|G_{1}\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}\leq C\|G_{1}\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}^{\frac{r_{1}}{r}}\|G_{1}\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}^{1-\frac{r_{1}}{r}},

where
2\leq r_{1}<\alpha+2
and
r_{1}=\frac{2d\alpha(\alpha+2)}{d\alpha^{2}+4(d-1)\alpha-8}
. Hence,
(q,r_{1})
is a
L^{2}
admissible pair.
By Duhamel’s formula, we can write

\displaystyle iG_{1}(t)=e^{i(t-T+\varepsilon^{-\sigma})(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u(T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma})-e^{it(\Delta_{x,y}-y^{2})}u_{0}.

Using the Strichartz estimate and the uniform bound of
u
in energy space, we have

\displaystyle\|G_{1}(t)\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}\lesssim 1.

Next, we proceed on estimating
L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}
norm of
G_{1}(t)
.
By dispersive estimate (Lemma
2.3
), Strichartz estimate, Moser type estimate, and the Duhamel formula, we have

\displaystyle\hskip 17.22217pt\|G_{1}(t)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|G_{1}\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}}+\big\|H_{y}^{\frac{s}{2}}G_{1}(t)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\|G_{1}(t)\|_{L_{y}^{2}L_{x}^{\infty}}+\big\|H_{y}^{\frac{s}{2}}G_{1}(t)\big\|_{L_{y}^{2}L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big\|\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big\|e^{i(t-\tau)(\Delta_{y}-y^{2})}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\big\|_{L_{x}^{1}}\,d\tau\Big\|_{L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.61108pt+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\Big\|\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big\|e^{i(t-\tau)(\Delta_{y}-y^{2})}H_{y}^{\frac{s}{2}}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\big\|_{L_{x}^{1}}\,d\tau\Big\|_{L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big\||u|^{\alpha}u(\tau)\big\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}}\,d\tau+\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big\|H_{y}^{\frac{s}{2}}(|u|^{\alpha}u)(\tau)\big\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big\|u(\tau)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\alpha+1}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}}^{\alpha+1}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|u(\tau)\|_{L_{y}^{2}L_{x}^{\alpha+1}}^{\alpha+1}\,ds+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|H_{y}^{\frac{s}{2}}(u)\|_{L_{y}^{2}L_{x}^{\alpha+1}}^{\alpha+1},d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|u(\tau)\|_{H_{x}^{s_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s_{2}}}^{\alpha+1}\,d\tau\lesssim\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\big|t-\tau\big|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|u(\tau)\|_{\Sigma}^{\alpha+1}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-T+\varepsilon^{-\sigma})^{-\frac{d}{2}+1}.

In the last step, we take
s_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\eta
and
s_{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\eta
for some
\eta
close to
0
.
Then taking the time integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|G_{1}(t)\big\|_{L_{t}^{q}([T,\infty),L_{x}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1})}\lesssim\Big(\int_{T}^{\infty}(t-T+\varepsilon^{-\sigma})^{(-\frac{d}{2}+1)q}\,dt\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\sigma(\frac{d-2}{2}-\frac{1}{q})}.

It remains to verify the inequality
\frac{d-2}{2}-\frac{1}{q}>0
for
q=\frac{2\alpha(\alpha+2)}{2\alpha-d\alpha+4}
.
By the basic calculation, it is equivalent to

(d-2)\alpha^{2}+[2(d-2)\alpha-2\alpha+d\alpha]-4=(d-2)\alpha^{2}+3(d-2)\alpha-4>0.

Then we define the function

f(\alpha)=(d-2)\alpha^{2}+3(d-2)\alpha-4
. It reduces to proving that when
\alpha\in\left(\frac{4}{d},\frac{4}{d-1}\right)
,
f(\alpha)>0
.
If
d\geq 3
, one can easily show that
f(\alpha)
is monotone increasing. Then we only need to verify
f(\frac{4}{d})>0
, which is direct by simple computation. Therefore, we complete the proof of this lemma.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

It is worth noting that the
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}

-dimension corresponds precisely to the
\dot{H}^{1}

-dimension. Therefore, we will perform the

.

4.
Variational analysis

The goal of this section is to establish some variational results for proving Theorem
1.1
. Throughout this section, we always assume that
d\geq 2
and that condition (
1.1
) holds.
Consider the minimization problem

(4.1)

m_{\omega}=\inf\left\{S_{\omega}(\phi):\phi\in K\right\},

where

(4.2)

K:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}:~Q(\phi)=0\right\}.

We have the following.

Theorem 4.1

(Existence of minimizer)
.

For any
\omega>0
, the minimization problem (
4.1
) admits a positive minimizer
\phi_{\omega}
, which solves equation (
1.16
).

In the proof of Theorem
4.1
, the constraint
Q(\phi)=0
leads to some unexpected new challenges in the analysis. First of all, we point out that a crucial step in proving the existence of minimizer is to show that the weak limit of a minimizing sequence is non-vanishing. Consider the stationary equation of standard NLS (
1.11
)

(4.3)

-\Delta_{x}\phi+\omega\phi=|\phi|^{\alpha}\phi~~\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

and the corresponding minimization problem

(4.4)

\inf\left\{\widetilde{S}_{\omega}(\phi):\phi\in H_{x}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\setminus\left\{0\right\},~~\widetilde{Q}(\phi)=0\right\},

where
\widetilde{Q}(\phi)
is given by (
1.13
), and
\widetilde{S}_{\omega}(\phi)
is the corresponding functional defined by

(4.5)

\widetilde{S}_{\omega}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}+\frac{\omega}{2}\|\phi\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\|\phi\|_{L_{x}^{\alpha+2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\alpha+2}.

For the minimization problem (
4.4
), the non-vanishing property of the weak limit of a minimizing sequence can be proved using the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Indeed, let
\left(\phi_{n}\right)_{n}
be a bounded minimizing sequence for (
4.4
). Then, in view of (
1.13
), it follows that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

(4.6)

\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}=\frac{\alpha d}{2\left(\alpha+2\right)}\|\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{\alpha+2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\alpha+2}\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\frac{2\alpha+4-\alpha d}{2}}\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}.

Since
\alpha d>4
, we obtain that

(4.7)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\sim\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x}^{\alpha+2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\alpha+2}>0,

which ensures the weak limit is non-vanishing. However, this argument fails for the minimization problem (
4.1
) if one uses the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
, because the constraint
Q(\phi)=0
does not involve the term
\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{2}
. To overcome this difficulty, we shall use the anisotropic
(x,y)
-Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality for
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}

(4.8)

\|\phi\|_{L_{x,y}^{\alpha+2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{\alpha+2}\leq C(d,\alpha)\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|\phi\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{\frac{4-\alpha(d-1)}{2}},

see Lemma
4.1
.

Another difficulty is to show that the minimizer
\phi_{\omega}
solves equation (
1.16
) because the condition
Q(\phi)=0
is not a natural constraint. We will overcome this difficulty by showing that the functional
S_{\omega}
admits a mountain pass structure at level
m_{\omega}
, see Lemma
4.6
. Then, adapting a subtle deformation argument from
[
5
]
(which deals with Schrödinger–Poisson equations with
L^{2}
-prescribed norm, so some modifications are necessary here), we prove that
\phi_{\omega}
is indeed a solution of (
1.16
), see Lemma
4.8
.

Throughout this section, we denote the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}_{x,y}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}
simply by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.

4.1.
preliminary results

Lemma 4.1

(Anisotropic
(x,y)
-Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality)
.

For
\alpha\in\big(0,\frac{4}{d-1}\big)
and
\phi\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})
, there exists a constant
C(d,\alpha)>0
such that

(4.9)

\|\phi\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}\leq C(d,\alpha)\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{4-\alpha(d-1)}{2}}.

Proof.

It follows from the anisotropic Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (see, for example,
[
8
,
22
]
) and Young’s inequality that there exists a constant
C_{1}(d,\alpha)>0
such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(d,\alpha)\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{4-\alpha(d-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\|\partial_{x_{i}}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(d,\alpha)\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{4-\alpha(d-1)}{2}},

where
C(d,\alpha)=C_{1}(d,\alpha)d^{-\frac{\alpha d}{4}}
. The proof is complete.
∎

For any function
\phi\in\Sigma
and a constant
\lambda>0
, we define the scaling operator
\phi^{\lambda}
by

(4.11)

\phi^{\lambda}(x,y)=\lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}\phi\left(\lambda x,y\right).

In what follows, we establish several important properties of the mappings
\lambda\mapsto Q(\phi^{\lambda})
, which will play a crucial role in the proof of our main theorems.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
\omega>0
and
\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, the following properties hold:

(i)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}S_{\omega}(\phi^{\lambda})=\lambda^{-1}Q(\phi^{\lambda})
for all
\lambda>0
;

(ii)

There exists a unique
\lambda_{\star}=\lambda_{\star}(\phi)
such that
Q(\phi^{\lambda_{\star}})=0
. Moreover,

(4.12)

Q(\phi^{\lambda})\begin{cases}>0,\quad\text{ if }~\lambda\in\left(0,\lambda_{\star}\right),\\
<0,\quad\text{ if }~\lambda\in\left(\lambda_{\star},\infty\right).\end{cases}

(iii)

We have the following relations

\lambda_{\star}>1\ \Leftrightarrow\ Q(\phi)>0;\quad\lambda_{\star}=1\ \Leftrightarrow\ Q(\phi)=0;\quad\lambda_{\star}<1\ \Leftrightarrow\ Q(\phi)<0.

(iv)

S_{\omega}(\phi^{\lambda})<S_{\omega}(\phi^{\lambda_{\star}})
for all
\lambda>0
and
\lambda\neq\lambda_{\star}
.

Proof.

By a direct calculation, we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle S_{\omega}(\phi^{\lambda})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\lambda^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\phi\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(4.14)

\displaystyle Q(\phi^{\lambda})=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha d}{2\left(\alpha+2\right)}\lambda^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\phi\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}.

Therefore, the conclusion (i) holds. Let
f(\lambda)=Q(\phi^{\lambda})
. Then

f^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda\left(2\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}d^{2}}{4\left(\alpha+2\right)}\lambda^{\frac{\alpha d-4}{2}}\|\phi\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}\right).

Since
\alpha>4/d
, we can find a unique

\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(\phi)>0
such that
f^{\prime}(\lambda)
is positive on
\left(0,\lambda_{0}\right)
and negative on
\left(\lambda_{0},\infty\right)
. Moreover, we deduce from (
4.14
) that there exists a unique
\lambda_{\star}=\lambda_{\star}(\phi)>\lambda_{0}
such that
f(\lambda_{\star})=0
, and
f(\lambda)
is positive on
\left(0,\lambda_{\star}\right)
and negative on
\left(\lambda_{\star},\infty\right)
. Hence, the conclusion (ii) holds. Observe that
f(1)=Q(\phi^{1})=Q(\phi)
, then the conclusion (iii) is a direct consequence of (ii). Finally, to prove (iv), we deduce from (i) that

(4.15)

S_{\omega}(\phi^{\lambda_{\star}})=S_{\omega}(\phi^{\lambda})+\int_{\lambda}^{\lambda_{\star}}s^{-1}f(s){\mathrm{d}s}.

Hence, conclusion (iv) follows directly from the facts that
f(s)
is positive on
\left(0,\lambda_{\star}\right)
and negative on
\left(\lambda_{\star},\infty\right)
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.2.
The properties of
m_{\omega}

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\omega>0
, we have
m_{\omega}\in\left(0,\infty\right)
.

Proof.

First, we know that
m_{\omega}<\infty
since the set
K\neq\emptyset
. In what follows, we show that
m_{\omega}>0
. Indeed, let
\left(\phi_{n}\right)_{n}\subset K
such that
S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=m_{\omega}+o_{n}(1)
. Then

(4.16)

\displaystyle m_{\omega}+o_{n}(1)

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})-\frac{2}{\alpha d}Q(\phi_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|y\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\omega}{2}\|\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, from
\alpha>4/d
, we know that the sequence
\left(\phi_{n}\right)_{n}
is bounded in
\Sigma
. Now by Lemma
4.1
, we deduce from the fact
\alpha<4/(d-1)
that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\alpha d}{2\left(\alpha+2\right)}\|\phi_{n}\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{\frac{4-\alpha(d-1)}{2}}\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}.

Combined with
\alpha>4/d
, this implies the existence of constant
\widetilde{C}=\widetilde{C}(\omega,\alpha,d)>0
such that

(4.18)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\phi_{n}\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}\sim\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\widetilde{C}.

Therefore, we see from (
4.16
) that

(4.19)

m_{\omega}\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\widetilde{C}>0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Define

(4.20)

\displaystyle I_{\omega}(\phi):

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(\phi)-\frac{2}{\alpha d}Q(\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
\omega>0
, we define

(4.21)

\tilde{m}_{\omega}:=\inf\left\{I_{\omega}(\phi):~\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\},~Q(\phi)\leq 0\right\}.

Then
m_{\omega}=\tilde{m}_{\omega}
.

Proof.

Obviously, we have

(4.22)

\tilde{m}_{\omega}\leq\inf\left\{I_{\omega}(\phi):\phi\in K\right\}=\inf\left\{S_{\omega}(\phi):\phi\in K\right\}=m_{\omega}<\infty.

On the other hand, let
\left(\phi_{n}\right)_{n}\subset\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}
be a minimizing sequence for
\tilde{m}_{\omega}
, i.e.,

(4.23)

\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=\tilde{m}_{\omega}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad Q(\phi_{n})\leq 0,\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By Lemma
4.2
, there exists
\lambda_{n}\in(0,1]
such that
Q(\phi_{n}^{\lambda_{n}})=0
. Then

(4.24)

\displaystyle m_{\omega}\leq S_{\omega}(\phi_{n}^{\lambda_{n}})=I_{\omega}(\phi_{n}^{\lambda_{n}})\leq I_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=\tilde{m}_{\omega}+o_{n}(1).

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we obtain
m_{\omega}\leq\tilde{m}_{\omega}
, which together with (
4.22
), gives that
m_{\omega}=\tilde{m}_{\omega}
. The proof is complete.
∎

In what follows, we show that the functional
S_{\omega}
has a mountain pass structure at level
m_{\omega}
.

Definition 4.1
.

The functional
S_{\omega}
is said to have a mountain pass structure at level
\sigma_{\omega}
if there exist some constants
a>0
and
0<\delta<m_{\omega}
such that

(4.25)

\sigma_{\omega}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}\max_{s\in\left[0,1\right]}S_{\omega}(\gamma(s))>\max\left\{\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(\gamma(0)),\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))\right\},

where

(4.26)

\Gamma_{\omega}:=\left\{\gamma\in C([0,1],\Sigma):\gamma(0)\in A_{a,\delta},~S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))<0\right\},

and

(4.27)

A_{a,\delta}:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma:\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}<a,~~\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}<2(m_{\omega}-\delta)\right\}.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\omega>0
, there exists a constant
a_{0}>0
(independent of
\delta
) such that for
a\in\left(0,a_{0}\right)
, the inequality
Q(\phi)>0
holds for all
\phi\in A_{a,\delta}
.

Proof.

Since
\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}<2m_{\omega}
and
\alpha>4/d
, we deduce from Lemma
4.1
that

(4.28)

Q(\phi)=\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha d}{2\left(\alpha+2\right)}\|\phi\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}\geq\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}-C\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{\frac{\alpha d}{2}}>0,

provided

\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}<a
with
a\in\left(0,a_{0}\right)
for sufficiently small
a_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 4.6

(Mountain pass structure)
.

For any
\omega>0
, there exists
0<\delta<m_{\omega}
such that for any
0<a<\min\left\{a_{0},\delta\right\}
, we have

m_{\omega}=\sigma_{\omega},

and the functional
S_{\omega}
has a mountain pass structure at level
m_{\omega}
.

Proof.

First, we prove that
m_{\omega}=\sigma_{\omega}
. Let

\left(\phi_{n}\right)_{n}\subset K
be a minimizing sequence such that
S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=m_{\omega}+o_{n}(1)
. Similar to (
4.16
) and (
4.18
), we deduce that
\left(\phi_{n}\right)_{n}
is bounded in
\Sigma
, and there exists a constant
\zeta=\zeta(c,d,\alpha)
such that

(4.29)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\zeta.

We may further assume that
\zeta
is small such that
\zeta<4m_{\omega}
. For
n
sufficiently large, we have

(4.30)

m_{\omega}+\frac{1}{4}\zeta\geq S_{\omega}(\phi_{n}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\zeta.

Combining this with (
4.16
), we obtain

(4.31)

m_{\omega}+\frac{1}{4}\zeta\geq S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\zeta.

Set

(4.32)

\delta={\zeta}/{4}\in(0,m_{\omega})~\text{ and }~0<a<\min\left\{a_{0},\delta\right\}.

Then for any fixed
n
sufficiently large, we have

\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}<2(m_{\omega}-\delta).

Since

\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi_{n}^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi^{\lambda}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\partial_{y}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}<2(m_{\omega}-\delta),

and
\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}=\lambda^{2}\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\to 0
as
\lambda\to 0
, we may choose
\lambda_{0}>0
small such that
\phi_{n}^{\lambda_{0}}\in A_{a,\delta}
for
a\in\left(0,a_{0}\right)
. Moreover, since
\alpha>4/d
, it follows from (
4.13
) that
S_{\omega}(\phi_{n}^{\lambda})\to-\infty
as
\lambda\to+\infty
. Thus, we may find
\lambda_{1}>0
large enough such that
S_{\omega}(\phi_{n}^{\lambda_{1}})<0
. Define

(4.33)

\gamma_{n}(s):=\phi_{n}^{s\lambda_{1}+(1-s)\lambda_{0}},\quad s\in\left[0,1\right].

Then
\gamma_{n}(s)\in\Gamma_{\omega}
. Therefore, since
Q(\phi_{n})=0
, we deduce from Lemma
4.2
that

(4.34)

\sigma_{\omega}\leq\max_{s\in\left[0,1\right]}S_{\omega}(\gamma_{n}(s))\leq S_{\omega}(\phi_{n}^{1})=S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=m_{\omega}+o_{n}(1).

Letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain that
\sigma_{\omega}\leq m_{\omega}
.

Next, we prove the inverse inequality. For any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}
, we have
\gamma(0)\in A_{a,\delta}
. Hence, by Lemma
4.5
, we know that
Q(\gamma(0))>0
. We now prove that
Q(\gamma(1))<0
. By contradiction, we assume that
Q(\gamma(1))\geq 0
. Then we have

(4.35)

S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}\gamma(1)\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\|\gamma(1)\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\gamma(1)\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0.

However, by the definition of
\Gamma_{\omega}
, we know that
S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))<0
, which is a contradiction. Thus, there holds
Q(\gamma(1))<0
. By the continuity of
\gamma
, there exists
s_{0}\in\left(0,1\right)
such that
Q(\gamma(s_{0}))=0
. Therefore, we have

(4.36)

m_{\omega}\leq S_{\omega}(\gamma(s_{0}))\leq\max_{s\in\left[0,1\right]}S_{\omega}(\gamma(s)),

from which we obtain
m_{\omega}\leq\sigma_{\omega}
. Hence, we finish the proof of
m_{\omega}=\sigma_{\omega}
.

Finally,
by using the definition of
A_{a,\delta}
and the choice of
a
, we know that for any
\phi\in A_{a,\delta}
, there holds

(4.37)

S_{\omega}(\phi)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq m_{\omega}-\delta+\frac{1}{2}a\leq m_{\omega}-\frac{1}{2}\delta.

For any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}
, by using Lemma
4.3
and the facts
\gamma(0)\in A_{a,\delta}
,
S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))<0
, we obtain

(4.38)

\sigma_{\omega}=m_{\omega}>\max\left\{\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(\gamma(0)),\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))\right\}.

Hence, the functional
S_{\omega}
has a mountain pass structure at level
m_{\omega}
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
The proof of Theorem
4.1

This subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem
4.1
. We begin with two lemmas.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
\omega>0
, the minimization problem
m_{\omega}
admits a non-negative minimizer
\phi_{\omega}\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\left(\varphi_{n}\right)_{n}\subset K
be a minimizing sequence for
m_{\omega}
, i.e.,

(4.39)

\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{\omega}(\varphi_{n})=m_{\omega}\quad\text{and}\quad Q(\varphi_{n})=0,\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By diamagnetic inequality we
know that the variational problem
m_{\omega}
is stable under the mapping
\varphi\mapsto|\varphi|
, thus we may assume
that
\varphi_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Following the same arguments in Lemma
4.3
, the sequence
\left(\varphi_{n}\right)_{n}
is bounded in
\Sigma
. Moreover, it satisfies the non-vanishing property:

(4.40)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\alpha+2}^{\alpha+2}>0.

Then by
[
6
, Lemma 3.4]
, there exists a sequence
\left(x_{n}\right)_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\phi_{\omega}\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{0\right\}
such that

(4.41)

\phi_{n}(x,y):=\varphi_{n}(x-x_{n},y)\rightharpoonup\phi_{\omega}\quad\text{ weakly in }~\Sigma.

Obviously, we have

(4.42)

\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=m_{\omega}\quad\text{and}\quad Q(\phi_{n})=0,\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By the weak lower semi-continuity of norms and the definition of
I_{\omega}
in (
4.20
), we deduce from Lemma
4.4
that

(4.43)

I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}I_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=\liminf_{n\to\infty}S_{\omega}(\phi_{n})=m_{\omega}=\tilde{m}_{\omega}.

Now we prove that
Q(\phi_{\omega})=0
. We divide the proof into two cases:

Case 1.
If the case
Q(\phi_{\omega})<0
happens, we deduce from Lemma
4.2
that there exists
\lambda_{\star}\in\left(0,1\right)
such that
Q(\phi_{\omega}^{\lambda_{\star}})=0
. Then by (
4.43
), we have

(4.44)

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{\omega}\leq I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega}^{\lambda_{\star}})<I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})\leq\tilde{m}_{\omega},

which is a contradiction. Therefore, Case 1 is impossible.

Case 2.
If the case
Q(\phi_{\omega})>0
happens, we consider
w_{n}:=\phi_{n}-\phi_{\omega}
. By using the Brézis-Lieb lemma
[
9
]
, a direct calculation shows that

(4.45)

\displaystyle Q(w_{n})=Q(\phi_{n})-Q(\phi_{\omega})+o_{n}(1),

\displaystyle I_{\omega}(w_{n})=I_{\omega}(\phi_{n})-I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})+o_{n}(1),

Therefore, for
n
large enough, we have
Q(w_{n})<0
. By Lemma
4.2
, there exists
s_{n}\in(0,1)
such that
Q(w_{n}^{s_{n}})=0
. Hence, we deduce from (
4.43
), (
4.45
) that

(4.46)

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{\omega}\leq I_{\omega}(w_{n}^{s_{n}})<I_{\omega}(w_{n})=I_{\omega}(\phi_{n})-I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})+o_{n}(1)\leq\tilde{m}_{\omega}-I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})+o_{n}(1).

This implies that
I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})=0
, which is absurd. Therefore, Case 2 is impossible.

Since Cases 1 and 2 are both impossible, we have
Q(\phi_{\omega})=0
. Therefore, we obtain from (
4.43
) that

(4.47)

m_{\omega}\leq S_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})=I_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})\leq m_{\omega}.

Thus
\phi_{\omega}
is a non-negative minimizer of
m_{\omega}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\phi_{\omega}
be the minimizer as in Lemma
4.7
. Then
\phi_{\omega}
is a positive solution to (
1.16
).

Proof.

Indeed, the statement that
\phi_{\omega}
solves (
1.16
) is equivalent to the condition
S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi_{\omega})=0
. We proceed by contradiction. Suppose instead that
\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi_{\omega})\|_{\star}\neq 0
, where

(4.48)

\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi_{\omega})\|_{\star}=\sup_{\psi\in\Sigma,~\|\psi\|_{\Sigma}\leq 1}|\left\langle S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi_{\omega}),\psi\right\rangle|.

Then there exist constants
\rho>0
and
\kappa>0
such that

(4.49)

\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{\star}\geq\kappa,\quad\text{ for any }\phi\in B_{3\rho}(\phi_{\omega}),

where
B_{3\rho}(\phi_{\omega}):=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma:\|\phi-\phi_{\omega}\|_{\Sigma}\leq 3\rho\right\}
. Let
a
and
\delta
be the constants given by Lemma
4.6
and we may further assume that
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{d}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha}
. Then we define

(4.50)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}:=\frac{1}{4}\left(m(c)-\max\left\{\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(\gamma(0)),\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))\right\}\right),

\displaystyle\delta_{2}:=\min\left\{\delta_{1},\frac{\rho\kappa}{4},\frac{m_{\omega}}{4}\right\}.

Consider the function
L:\Sigma\to[0,\rho]
given by

(4.51)

L(\phi)=\frac{\rho~{\rm dist}\left(\phi,\Sigma\setminus S_{1}\right)}{{\rm dist}\left(\phi,\Sigma\setminus S_{1}\right)+{\rm dist}\left(\phi,S_{2}\right)}\geq 0,

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle:=\Sigma\cap S_{\omega}^{-1}\big([m_{\omega}-2\delta_{2},m_{\omega}+2\delta_{2}]\big),

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle:=B_{3\rho}(\phi_{\omega})\cap S_{\omega}^{-1}\big([m_{\omega}-\delta_{2},m_{\omega}+\delta_{2}]\big).

Let

(4.52)

\widetilde{H}_{\omega}:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma:~S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi)\neq 0\right\}.

Then there exists a locally Lipschitz pseudo gradient vector field
X:\widetilde{H}_{\omega}\to\Sigma
such that for any
\phi\in\widetilde{H}_{\omega}
, we have
X(\phi)\in\Sigma
and

(4.53)

\|X(\phi)\|_{\Sigma}\leq 2\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{\star}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\langle S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi),X(\phi)\right\rangle\geq\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{\star}^{2}.

We now define the vector field
Y:\Sigma\to\Sigma
by

(4.54)

Y(\phi)=\begin{cases}-L(\phi)\|X(\phi)\|_{\Sigma}^{-1}X(\phi),\quad&\text{ if }\phi\in\widetilde{H}_{\omega},\\
0,\quad&\text{ if }\phi\in\Sigma\setminus\widetilde{H}_{\omega},\end{cases}

which is locally Lipschitz continuous. Then the problem

(4.55)

\frac{d}{d\tau}\eta(\tau,\phi)=Y(\eta(\tau,\phi)),\quad\eta(0,\phi)=\phi,\quad\forall\phi\in\Sigma

admits a unique solution
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}\times\Sigma\to\Sigma
. We now verify the following properties of
\eta
:

(i)

\eta(\tau,\phi)=\phi
for all
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
if
\phi\in S_{3}:=\Sigma\setminus S_{\omega}^{-1}\left(\left[m_{\omega}-2\delta_{2},m_{\omega}+2\delta_{2}\right]\right)
;

(ii)

S_{\omega}(\eta(\tau,\phi))\leq S_{\omega}(\phi)
for all
\phi\in\Sigma
and
\tau\in\left[0,1\right]
;

(iii)

\eta(1,S_{\omega}^{m_{\omega}+\delta_{2}}\cap B_{\rho}(\phi_{\omega}))\subset S_{\omega}^{m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}}
.

Here,
S_{\omega}^{M}
denotes
the set
S_{\omega}^{M}:=\left\{\phi\in\Sigma:S_{\omega}(\phi)\leq M\right\}
.

Proof of (i)
.
For any
\phi\in S_{3}
, we have
L(\phi)=0
, and thus
Y(\phi)=0
. By (
4.55
), it follows that

\frac{d}{d\tau}\eta(\tau,\phi)|_{\tau=0}=Y(\eta(0,\phi))=Y(\phi)=0,

which implies
\eta(\tau,\phi)=\eta(0,\phi)=\phi
.

Proof of (ii)
. For any
\phi\in\Sigma
, from (
4.51
) and (
4.53
), a direct calculation shows that

(4.56)

\displaystyle S_{\omega}(\eta(\tau,\phi))

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(\eta(0,\phi))+\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{d}{ds}S_{\omega}\left(\eta(s,\phi)\right)~\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(\phi)+\int_{0}^{\tau}\left\langle S_{\omega}^{\prime}\left(\eta(s,\phi)\right),Y(\eta(s,\phi))\right\rangle~\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(\phi)-\int_{s\in\left[0,\tau\right],\eta(s,\phi)\in\widetilde{H}_{\omega}}\left\langle S_{\omega}^{\prime}\left(\eta(s,\phi)\right),L(\eta(s,\phi))\|X(\eta(s,\phi))\|_{\Sigma}^{-1}X(\eta(s,\phi))\right\rangle~\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq S_{\omega}(\phi)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s\in\left[0,\tau\right],\eta(s,\phi)\in\widetilde{H}_{\omega}}L(\eta(s,\phi))\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\eta(s,\phi))\|_{\star}~\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq S_{\omega}(\phi).

Proof of (iii)
. Let
\phi\in S_{\omega}^{m_{\omega}+\delta_{2}}\cap B_{\rho}(\phi_{\omega})
. Then

(4.57)

S_{\omega}(\phi)\leq m_{\omega}+\delta_{2},\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\phi-\phi_{\omega}\|_{\Sigma}\leq\rho.

If
S_{\omega}(\phi)<m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}
, then by (ii), we have

(4.58)

S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\phi))\leq S_{\omega}(\phi)<m_{\omega}-\delta_{2},

so
S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\phi))\in S_{\omega}^{m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}}
. Now we assume that
S_{\omega}(\phi)\in[m_{\omega}-\delta_{2},m_{\omega}+\delta_{2}]
. Then we deduce from (ii) that

(4.59)

S_{\omega}(\eta(\tau,\phi))\leq S_{\omega}(\phi)<m_{\omega}+\delta_{2},\quad\forall\tau\in\left[0,1\right].

Moreover, for all
\tau\in[0,1]
, we have the estimate:

(4.60)

\displaystyle\|\eta(\tau,\phi)-\phi\|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\|\int_{0}^{\tau}Y(\eta(s,\phi))~\mathrm{d}s\|_{\Sigma}\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}L(\eta(s,\phi))~\mathrm{d}s\leq\rho,\quad\forall\tau\in\left[0,1\right].

where we used the fact
0\leq L(\cdot)\leq\rho
. Combining this with (
4.57
), we obtain
\eta(\tau,\phi)\in B_{2\rho}(\phi_{\omega})
for all
\tau\in[0,1]
. It follows from (
4.49
) that

(4.61)

\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\eta(\tau,\phi))\|_{\star}\geq\kappa,\quad\forall\tau\in\left[0,1\right].

Define

\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\tau\in[0,1]:m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}\leq S_{\omega}(\eta(\tau,\phi))\leq m_{\omega}+\delta_{2}\right\}\neq\emptyset.

For any
\tau\in\mathcal{E}
, we know that
\eta(\tau,\phi)\in S_{2}
, and consequently
L(\eta(\tau,\phi))=\rho
. Combining this with (
4.50
), (
4.56
) and (
4.61
), we conclude that

(4.62)

\displaystyle S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\phi))

\displaystyle\leq S_{\omega}(\phi)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s\in\mathcal{E},~\eta(s,\phi)\in\widetilde{H}_{\omega}}L(\eta(s,\phi))\|S_{\omega}^{\prime}(\eta(s,\phi))\|_{\star}~\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq m_{\omega}+\delta_{2}-\frac{\rho\kappa}{2}\leq m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}.

Hence,
S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\phi))\in S_{\omega}^{m(c)-\delta_{2}}
. The proof of (iii) is complete.

Let

(4.63)

\gamma(s):=\phi_{\omega}^{s\lambda_{1}+(1-s)\lambda_{0}},\quad s\in\left[0,1\right].

We aim to find there exist
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}\geq 0
such that
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\omega}
. Similar to the proof of Lemma
4.6
, we only need to show that

(4.64)

\|\partial_{y}\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}<2(m_{\omega}-\delta).

Indeed, recall that
S_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})=m_{\omega}
and
Q(\phi_{\omega})=0
, we have

(4.65)

\displaystyle m_{\omega}

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})=S_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})-\frac{2}{\alpha d}Q(\phi_{\omega})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}.

Similar to (
4.29
), we deduce from (
4.32
) that
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d}\right)\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\zeta>\delta
, which directly implies (
4.64
).
Since
Q(\phi_{\omega})=0
, it follows from Lemma
4.2
that
S_{\omega}(\gamma(s))\leq S_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})=m_{\omega}
for all
s\in\left[0,1\right]
, from which we get
\gamma(s)\in S_{\omega}^{m_{\omega}}
. We now consider three cases:

Case 1:

If
\gamma(s)\in\Sigma\setminus B_{\rho}(\phi_{\omega})
, then by (ii), we have

(4.66)

S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\gamma(s)))\leq S_{\omega}(\gamma(s))<S_{\omega}(\phi_{\omega})=m_{\omega}.

Case 2:

If
\gamma(s)\in S_{\omega}^{m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}}
, then by (ii), we have

(4.67)

S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\gamma(s)))\leq S_{\omega}(\gamma(s))\leq m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}<m_{\omega}.

Case 3:

If
\gamma(s)\in B_{\rho}(\phi_{\omega})\cap S_{\omega}^{-1}\left([m_{\omega}-\delta_{2},m_{\omega}]\right)
, then by (iii), we have

(4.68)

S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\gamma(s)))\leq m_{\omega}-\delta_{2}<m_{\omega}.

In summary, we know that

(4.69)

\max_{s\in\left[0,1\right]}S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\gamma(s)))<m_{\omega}.

Finally, we claim that

(4.70)

\eta(1,\gamma(s))\in\Gamma_{\omega}.

Once this claim is proved, we are done. Indeed, combining (
4.69
) and (
4.70
) with Lemma
4.6
, we arrive at

(4.71)

\gamma_{\omega}\leq\max_{s\in\left[0,1\right]}S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\gamma(s)))<m_{\omega}=\gamma_{\omega},

which is a contradiction. To prove (
4.70
), we first
observe that the choice of
\delta_{2}
ensures that
m_{\omega}-2\delta_{2}\geq{m_{\omega}}/{2}>0
. Then
S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))<0<m_{\omega}-2\delta_{2}
, it follows that
\gamma(1)\in S_{3}
. Using conclusion (i), we deduce that
S_{\omega}(\eta(1,\gamma(1)))=S_{\omega}(\gamma(1))<0
. On the other hand, the selection of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
yields

(4.72)

S_{\omega}(\gamma(0))\leq m_{\omega}-4\delta_{1}\leq m_{\omega}-4\delta_{2}<m(c)-2\delta_{2},

which implies that
\gamma(0)\in S_{3}
. Using (i) again, we obtain that
\eta(1,\gamma(0))=\gamma(0)\in A_{a,\delta}
. This establishes (
4.70
). We have thus shown that
\phi_{\omega}
is a solution to (
1.16
). The positivity of
\phi_{\omega}
follows directly from the maximum principle. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is just a combinition of Lemmas
4.7
and
4.8
.

5.
Interaction Morawetz estimate

In this section, we devote to prove the following estimate, which plays key role in the proof of Theorem
6.1
. Here we choose the multiplier that is only related to
x
-direction, which differs from that in
[
19
]
. Additionally, as mentioned in the introduction, the coercivity estimates required for the proof (Lemma
5.3
) rely heavily on the variational results we established earlier, rather than being a simple consequence of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality. Let
\eta>0
be a small constant and let
\chi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\left\{0\right\}
be a radial decreasing function
defined by

(5.1)

\chi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&|x|\leq 1-\eta,\\
0,&|x|>1,\end{array}\right.

and
\chi_{R}(x)=\chi(x/R)
for any
R>0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u
be a solution to (
PHNLS
) with initial data
u_{0}\in P_{\omega}^{+}
.
Then for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exist
T_{0}=T_{0}(\varepsilon,u),J_{0}=J_{0}(\varepsilon,u),R_{0}=R_{0}\left(\varepsilon,u\right)
sufficiently large, and
\eta=\eta(\varepsilon)>0
sufficiently small such that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{J_{0}}T_{0}}\int_{a}^{a+T_{0}}\int_{R_{0}}^{R_{0}e^{J_{0}}}\frac{1}{R^{d}}\iiint\big|\chi_{R}(x_{2}-s)u(t,z_{2})\big|^{2}\times

\displaystyle\times\big|\nabla_{x}(\chi_{R}(x_{1}-s)u^{\xi}(t,z_{1}))\big|^{2}\,dz_{1}\,dz_{2}\,ds\frac{dR}{R}\,dt\lesssim\varepsilon,

where
u^{\xi}(t,z)=e^{ix\cdot\xi}u(t,z)
with
\xi=\xi(t,s,R)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\xi=\xi(t,s,R)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rr}-\frac{\mathrm{Im}\int\chi_{R}^{2}(x-s)\bar{u}(t,z)\nabla_{x}u(t,z)\,dz}{\int\chi_{R}^{2}(x-s)|u(t,z)|^{2}\,dz},&\text{if }\int\chi_{R}^{2}(x-s)|u(t,z)|^{2}\,dz\neq 0,\\
0,&\text{if }\int\chi_{R}^{2}(x-s)|u(t,z)|^{2}\,dz=0.\end{array}\right.

Henceforth we abbreviate
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}f\,dz
by
\int f\,dz
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\,ds
by
\int g\,ds
.

Before proving the theorem, we begin by discussing some basic properties of solutions in
P_{\omega}^{+}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
PHNLS
) and assume that there exists some
t
in the lifespan of
u
such that
u(t)\in P_{\omega}^{+}
. Then
u(t)\in P_{\omega}^{+}
for all
t
in the maximal lifespan of
u
.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose that there exists some time
s
in the lifespan of
u
such that
Q(u(s))<0
. Then by continuity of
u
and conservation of energy, we know that there exists some
t^{\prime}
lying between
t
and
s
such that
Q(u(t^{\prime}))=0
and
S_{\omega}(u(t^{\prime}))<m_{\omega}
, which obviously contradicts the definition of
m_{\omega}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
PHNLS
) with initial data
u_{0}\in P_{\omega}^{+}
. Then
u
is global and satisfies

(5.3)

\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\|u(t)\|_{\Sigma}\lesssim m_{\omega}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
,
u(t)\in P_{\omega}^{+}
for all
t
in the maximal lifespan of
u
. Therefore, by the definition of
P_{\omega}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{y}u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|yu(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\omega}{2}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha d})\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=S_{\omega}(u(t))-\frac{2}{\alpha d}Q(u(t))<m_{\omega},

which together with Lemma
2.4
yields global well-posedness and (
5.3
).
∎

We now prove the following coercivity result, which is crucial for the proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Lemma 5.3

(Coercivity estimate)
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
PHNLS
) with initial data
u_{0}\in P_{\omega}^{+}
. Then there exist two time-independent constants

\theta_{0}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to[0,1]^{d}
, it follows from the basic mean value theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{0}}\int_{a}^{a+T_{0}}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\Big\|\nabla_{x}(\chi_{R_{1}}\big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\big)u^{\xi}(t))\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\,dt\lesssim\varepsilon.

Spliting the time interval
\big[a+\frac{T_{0}}{2},a+\frac{3T_{0}}{4}\big]
into
T_{0}\varepsilon^{\sigma}
pieces of sub-intervals with length
\varepsilon^{-\sigma}
. We claim that there exists at least one
T\in[a+\frac{T_{0}}{2},a+\frac{3T_{0}}{4}]
such that
[T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T]\subset[a,a+T_{0}]
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\Big\|\nabla_{x}(\chi_{R_{1}}\big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\big)u^{\xi}(t))\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\,dt\lesssim\varepsilon^{1-\sigma}.

Combining with the refined Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality,

\displaystyle\big\|u\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}}^{4}\lesssim\|u\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\big\|\nabla_{x}u^{\xi}\big\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2},

one has

(6.2)

\displaystyle\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{4}\,dt\lesssim\varepsilon^{1-\sigma}.

From the Sobolev embedding, Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u(t)\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By the Sobolev inequality and the basic property
|\nabla\chi|\lesssim\eta^{-1}
, for
R_{1}>\eta^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)\nabla_{x}u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{R_{1}^{2}}\Big\|(\nabla\chi)_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}

(6.3)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u(t)\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+O(\eta^{-2}R_{1}^{-2})\|u(t)\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|u(t)\|_{\Sigma}^{2}.

Putting these estimates together, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim 1.

Taking the time integral, we have the following estimate directly,

(6.4)

\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\sigma}.

Interpolating between (
6.2
) and (
6.4
) implies

\displaystyle\hskip 25.83325pt\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}([T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Big\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}L_{y}^{2}}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}\Big(\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0}))u(t)\Big\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.61108pt\times\Big(\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{4}\,dt\Big)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.61108pt\times\Big(\int_{T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma}}^{T}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|\chi_{R_{1}}\Big(\cdot-\frac{R_{1}}{4}(w+\theta_{0})\Big)u(t)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{2}\,dt\Big)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d-1}-\sigma}.

In conclusion, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{d-1}{2d}\big(\frac{1}{d-1}-\sigma\big)}.

To finish the proof, we need to choose the parameters carefully. Let
\theta\in(0,1)
to be determined later. Suppose that
(q_{2},r_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}=\frac{(d-1)\theta}{2d}+\frac{1-\theta}{q_{2}},\,\,\frac{1}{r}=\frac{\theta(d-1)}{2d}+\frac{1-\theta}{r_{2}}.

Taking
r^{*}\geq 2,s>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{2}}=\frac{1}{r^{*}}-\frac{s}{d},\,\,\frac{2}{q_{2}}+\frac{d}{r^{*}}=\frac{2}{\alpha}.

Since
(q,r)
is
H^{s_{c}}
-admissible, then we have the following relationship

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha}=\theta(d-1)\big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d}\big)+(1-\theta)\big(\frac{d}{2}-s\big).

Equivalently, we can write

(6.5)

\displaystyle s=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{1-\theta}\Big(\frac{2}{\alpha}-\frac{(d-1)(d+2)\theta}{2d}\Big).

Moreover, we need that
s\in(s_{c},\frac{1}{2})
. By simple calculation,
s>s_{c}
unconditionally.
s<\frac{1}{2}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{d-1}{2}-\frac{2}{\alpha(1-\theta)}+\frac{(d-1)(d+2)\theta}{2d(1-\theta)}<0.

Moreover, since
\alpha<\frac{4}{d-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\theta<\frac{d}{d-1}\big(\frac{2}{\alpha}-\frac{2}{d-1}\big).

By Hölder inequality and Sobolev embedding, one has

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{s}([T-\varepsilon^{-\sigma},T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{\theta}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{2}}L_{x}^{r_{2}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1-s}}^{1-\theta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2d}{d-1}}L_{y}^{2}}^{\theta}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{2}}W_{x}^{s,r^{*}}\mathcal{H}_{y}^{1-s}}^{1-\theta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{(d-1)\theta}{2d}\big(\frac{1}{d-1}-\sigma\big)}\varepsilon^{-\frac{\sigma}{q_{2}}(1-\theta)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{2d}-\sigma(\frac{d-1}{2d}\theta+\frac{1-\theta}{q_{2}})}.

Choosing
\sigma\ll 1
small enough, we can complete the proof of Theorem
6.1
.
∎

Lemma 6.1
.

We have
m_{\omega}=m_{GS}
. Hence,
P_{\omega}^{+}
=
\mathcal{K}_{\omega}^{+}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
and the definition of
m_{\omega}
and
m_{GS}
, we know that

m_{GS}\leq m_{\omega}.

To prove the reserve inequality, we first recall that Ardila and Carles
[
4
]
proved that
m_{GS}
can be attained by a non-trivial ground state
\phi_{GS}\in\Sigma
, i.e.,
S_{\omega}(\phi_{GS})=m_{GS}
. Therefore, it suffices to show that

Q(\phi_{GS})=0.

Recalling the definition of
\phi_{GS}
, we know that
e^{i\omega t}\phi_{GS}
is a solution of (
PHNLS
). Let
\tilde{\varphi}
be a radial smooth solution in
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

\tilde{\varphi}(r)=r^{2},\quad r=|x|\leq 1,\quad\tilde{\varphi}(r)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\varphi}(r)^{\prime\prime}\leq 2,\quad r\geq 0.

and let
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}=R^{2}\tilde{\varphi}(\frac{x}{R})
. Then for any
R>0
, we know that the following truncation semi-virial functional

V_{R}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\tilde{\varphi}_{R}(x)|e^{i\omega t}\phi_{GS}(z)|^{2}dz

is well-defined and is equal to a constant independent of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore,
V_{R}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(0)\equiv 0
for all
R>0
. On the other hand, by a direct computation, we see that

\displaystyle V_{R}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(0)

\displaystyle=8Q(\phi_{GS})+4\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\Big(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}}{r}-2\Big)|\nabla_{x}\phi_{GS}|^{2}\,dz+4\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\Big(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}}{r^{2}}-\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime}}{r^{3}}\Big)|x\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi_{GS}|^{2}\,dz

\displaystyle-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\big(\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}+(d-1)\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime}}{r}-2d\big)|\phi_{GS}|^{\alpha+2}\,dz-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\Delta_{x}^{2}\tilde{\varphi}_{R}|\phi_{GS}|^{2}\,dz

(6.6)

\displaystyle=8Q(\phi_{GS})+A_{R}.

It is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{R}(r)\leq r^{2},\,\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}(r)\leq 2,\,|\Delta_{x}^{2}\tilde{\varphi}_{R}(r)|\leq CR^{-2}.

Using the support condition of
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}
, we see that

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\varphi}_{R})=\operatorname{supp}(|\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}+(d-1)\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime}/r-2d|)\subset\{R\leq|x|<\infty\},

then we can prove that

\displaystyle-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}(\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}+(d-1)\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime}/r-2d)|\phi_{GS}|^{\alpha+2}\,dz\lesssim\|\phi_{GS}\|_{L^{\alpha+2}(|x|\geq R)}^{\alpha+2}.

Also, we can prove that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\Delta_{x}^{2}\tilde{\varphi}_{R}|\phi_{GS}|^{2}\,dz\lesssim R^{-2}\|\phi_{GS}\|_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)},

\displaystyle 4\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\Big(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime\prime}}{r}-2\Big)|\nabla_{x}\phi_{GS}|^{2}\,dz\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{GS}\|_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)}^{2},

\displaystyle 4\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}}\Big(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime\prime}}{r^{2}}-\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{R}^{\prime}}{r^{3}}\Big)|x\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi_{GS}|^{2}\,dz\lesssim\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{GS}\|_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)}^{2}.

Since
\phi_{GS}\in\Sigma
, the right-hand side of the above estimates are well-defined. Then by monotone convergence theorem, we have

|A_{R}|\to 0
as
R\to\infty
, which, in view of (
6.6
), implies
Q(\phi_{GS})=0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The proof of the scattering result is a combination of Theorem
6.1
and Lemma
6.1
. The proof of the blow-up result is proved via Glassey’s convex method
[
24
]
and can be obtained by following the arguments in
[
4
, Section 4]
.
