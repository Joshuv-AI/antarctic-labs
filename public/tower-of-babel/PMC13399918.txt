How to Set Anterior and Posterior Tibial Translation for Combined Anterior Cruciate Ligament and Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Griffin R Rechter, Abid M Khan, Adam J Tagliero, Abhishek S Kannan, Brooke R Levy, Aaron J Krych, Bruce A Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior and posterior cruciate ligament (ACL and PCL) reconstruction poses the risk of residual anterior or posterior tibial translation, respectively. As a result, patients may have residual knee instability and laxity, which can contribute to poor outcomes and graft failure. Ultimately, minimizing anterior and posterior tibial translation through proper ACL and PCL graft tensioning has the potential to improve clinical and functional outcomes in the setting of ACL and PCL reconstruction surgery. This technique aims to show a reproducible method for “setting” anterior and posterior tibial translation in the setting of ACL and PCL reconstructions.

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) are essential for knee stability, especially during dynamic movements. 1 These two ligaments show opposing effects on tibiofemoral biomechanics; thus, each ligament must exert its translational force in a delicate balance with the other to ensure knee stability. 1 Concomitant injuries to the ACL and PCL requiring surgical treatment 2 are common in athletes; 3 therefore, there is a growing need to understand the optimal graft tensioning to recreate the normal anatomic tibiofemoral relationship that could improve clinical and functional outcomes (Video 1). 4 , 5 , 6

This presentation shows a reproducible and step‐by‐step illustration of how to “set” anterior and posterior tibial translation for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) reconstructions: (1) examine both knees and use the normal knee as a reference for what the injured knee should feel like postoperatively, (2) use fluoroscopic posterior drawer stress testing to assess tibial positioning in both knee, once again using the normal knee as a reference, (3) after ACL and PCL grafts have been inserted, begin “cycling” the knee to remove any excess laxity on both grafts, (4) then “set” the knee in full extension while applying an axial load, (5) with the knee flexed to 90° put tension on both the ACL and PCL grafts, and at the same time check to see if normal tibial step‐offs have been recreated, (6) secure the PCL graft at 90°, (7) then secure the ACL graft in full extension, (8) take fluoroscopic posterior drawer stress image of newly reconstructed knee and compare it to the previously assessed normal knee, (9) if there's residual anterior or posterior tibial translation, retension either the ACL or PCL graft respectively, (10) if there is normalization of tibial positioning after initial graft tensioning, then final retensioning of both grafts should be done to maintain normalized tibial positioning. An integral step during ACL and PCL reconstruction is to ensure appropriate graft tensioning to minimize anterior and posterior tibial translation. 1 , 7 Failure to appropriately tension the grafts can impact graft forces, orientation, and laxity, thereby increasing the risk of graft failure. 8 Minimizing extraordinary translation through systematic balancing of the grafts can have a direct impact on knee stability and functional outcomes. 8 This technique note aims to describe a reproducible method to set anterior and posterior tibial translation in the setting of concomitant ACL and PCL reconstructions.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Prior to preparing both the ACL and PCL grafts, both knees undergo preoperative physical examination:

Examination of the uninjured knee (Figure 1B), with attention to the medial and lateral tibial step‐offs as a reference during anterior and posterior drawer testing, enables the surgeon to determine the patient's normal laxity and position of the tibia, relative to the femur. 9 The same examination is then completed on the injured knee (Figure 1A), noting any difference in laxity and tibiofemoral step‐offs. 9

Intraoperative view showing anterior and posterior drawer stress testing of (A) the injured right and (B) normal contralateral left knee at 90° of flexion with the patient in a supine position. The medial and lateral tibial step offs (red circles) are compared during the examination. 9

At this point, the surgeon has manually assessed the variation between the injured and uninjured knees. Intraoperative bilateral fluoroscopic stress examination of the injured knee (Figure 2A) and the contralateral uninjured knee (Figure 2B) is then performed to assess and compare posterior tibial translation, which can be seen in Figure 3. 10 , 11 It's imperative to place the fluoroscope at the same distance from each knee to avoid magnification error.

Intraoperative view showing the position of the knee and fluoroscopy used to perform posterior drawer stress testing of the (A) injured right knee and (B) normal left knee at 90° of flexion with the patient in a supine position, enabling the surgeon to assess and compare posterior tibial translation in each knee.

Preoperative side‐to‐side fluoroscopic posterior drawer stress views of the (A) injured right knee and (B) uninjured left knee at 90° of flexion showing a difference of 10.5 mm in posterior tibial translation. 11

In the example provided, the side‐to‐side comparison between the injured knee (Figure 3A) and uninjured knee (Figure 3B) shows a 10.5 mm difference in posterior drawer stress testing. The fluoroscopic image of the uninjured knee (Figure 3B) serves as a reference and is compared to the fluoroscopic stress view of the injured knee after reconstruction is completed, and sequential graft tensioning is undertaken. 9 , 10 , 11

The authors preferred technique has been previously described and consists of “all‐inside” ACL and PCL reconstruction with allograft, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 creating independent femoral and tibial sockets with adjustable loop cortical fixation. 16 The knee is then cycled (Figure 4) to remove excess laxity on both the ACL and PCL grafts. 17 After cycling to remove creep from ACL and PCL grafts, the knee is then placed in full extension and an axial load is applied to the knee while maintaining tension on both grafts (Figure 5). 17 , 18

Intraoperative view showing how the injured right knee is cycled to remove excess laxity on both the ACL and PCL grafts. The knee is taken from (A) full extension, (B) flexed beyond 90°, and then back to (C) full extension, with tension maintained on both the ACL and PCL grafts throughout each cycle 17 , 18 (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; PCL, posterior cruciate ligament.)

Intraoperative view showing “setting” of the right knee in full extension while applying an axial load to the knee with the patient in a supine position. 17 , 18

After “setting” the knee in full extension while applying an axial load, the knee is flexed to 90°. Next, tension is placed on both the ACL and PCL grafts, 19 and at the same time, the proximal tibial step‐offs are examined (Figure 6) with the goal of re‐establishing the “normal” tibiofemoral step‐off of the contralateral, uninjured knee. 9 , 18 An integral aspect to this step is to maintain tension on both grafts while assessing the proximal tibial step‐offs to accurately determine if the normal tibial step‐off has actually been recreated. 9 , 18 Once the normal relationship of the tibia to the femur is re‐established in the reconstructed knee, the surgeon should then secure the PCL graft first, followed by the ACL graft first (Figure 7), followed by the ACL graft (Figure 8). 8 , 18

Intraoperative view showing (A) the assistant maintaining tension on both ACL and PCL grafts with the right knee flexed to 90° and (B) the surgeon assessing the proximal tibial step‐offs to ensure appropriate tibial‐femoral reduction 9 , 18 (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; PCL, posterior cruciate ligament.)

Intraoperative view showing the PCL graft being tensioned with the right knee flexed to 90° and the patient in a supine position. 8 , 18 (PCL, posterior cruciate ligament.)

Intraoperative view showing the ACL graft being tensioned with the right knee in full extension and the patient in a supine position. 8 , 18 (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Once both grafts have been secured, the knee is flexed to 90°. Again, the fluoroscope must be situated at the same distance from each knee to avoid magnification error. A posterior drawer force is then applied to the knee (Figure 9), and the resulting fluoroscopic image is then compared to the previously assessed fluoroscopic stress view of the normal knee. 9 , 10

Intraoperative view showing posterior drawer stress testing of the injured right knee at 90° of flexion after both the ACL and PCL grafts have been secured, with the patient in a supine position. 9 , 10 (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; PCL, posterior cruciate ligament.)

In this case, the side‐to‐side comparison between the reconstructed knee (Figure 10A) and the normal knee (Figure 10B) shows a 0.3 mm difference in posterior drawer stress testing, which falls within measurement error. 10 , 20 The reduction in the step‐off indicates improvement in posterior tibial translation and now closely resembles the contralateral, uninjured knee. 9 , 10 , 11 , 20

Post‐operative side‐to‐side fluoroscopic posterior drawer stress views of the (A) reconstructed right knee and (B) uninjured left knee at 90° of flexion showing a difference of 0.3 mm in posterior tibial translation. 11

If there remains significant residual posterior translation after final tensioning, then only the PCL graft should be retensioned. 21 Likewise, if there was overreduction of the tibia, evidenced by excessive anterior translation, then only the ACL graft would need to be retensioned. 21 If, as in this case, translation is felt to be normalized compared to the contralateral knee, then final tensioning must be performed equally on both the ACL and PCL grafts to maintain the normalized tibial position (Figure 11). 21

Intraoperative view of the right knee showing final retensioning of (A) the PCL graft at 90° of knee flexion, followed by (B) the ACL graft in full extension, with the patient in a supine position. 21 (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; PCL, posterior cruciate ligament.)

DISCUSSION

This technique shows a reproducible method for “setting” anterior and posterior tibial translation in the setting of ACL and PCL reconstruction. Ultimately, the goal of ACL and PCL graft tensioning during reconstruction is to recreate the normal tibial step‐offs that are found in the normal knee. 9 A thorough preoperative examination enables one to use the proximal tibial step‐offs in the uninjured knee as a reference point. 9

Similarly, fluoroscopic posterior drawer stress testing of both knees is essential to this technique. 22 Side‐to‐side comparisons of these stress views allow the surgeon to use the normal knee as a reference for standardization of tibial positioning in the reconstructed knee after both grafts have been tensioned. 10 This step is advantageous because it acts as a “checkpoint” after both grafts have been initially tensioned, and the surgeon can now determine if there is any residual anterior or posterior tibial translation prior to final retensioning. 9 , 10 , 20 Using adjustable loop cortical fixation devices (Table 1) not only allows the surgeon to “dial in” tibial translation but also allows for “tensioning and retensioning” of the grafts thereby increasing graft stability. One disadvantage of this technique is the reliance on technology and quality of fluoroscopic stress views and obtaining posterior tibial translation measurements by hand (Table 2). However, placing the fluoroscope at equal distances from each knee can allow consistent fluoroscopic stress views and will reduce the risk of inaccurate measurements (Table 3).

The PCL graft is secured before the ACL graft to avoid posterior tibial subluxation, which is the most established graft tensioning sequence, as showed in this technique. 7 , 8 , 23 Markolf et al. reported inferior mean graft forces, as well as consistent posterior displacement of the tibia, when the ACL graft was secured first. 7 Recently, this technique has become controversial due to the emergence of new studies suggesting performing ACL fixation first may lead to improved functional outcomes and restoration of tibiofemoral orientation. 24 , 25 , 26 In spite of these recent studies, fixation of the ACL first still requires exploration, and PCL fixation first remains the prevailing strategy in sequential cruciate graft tensioning.

In conclusion, this technique provides a simple, reproducible method of ensuring restoration of the anatomic ACL and PCL graft tensioning.
