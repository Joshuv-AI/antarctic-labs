A systematic review and meta-analysis on palliative care service utilization and its associated factors among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia
Authors: Abel Desalegn Demeke, Alemayehu Yeshiwas, Habtamu Gebrie, Mequanint Ayehu Akele, Anteneh Gashaw, Abraham Dessie Gessesse, Takla Tamir, Netsanet Bogale, Wondimagegn Wolde Eba, Biazin Yenealem Mekuriaw, Ababo Demeke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPalliative care (PC) is essential for helping cancer patients manage pain, exhaustion, despair, and other crippling symptoms. It also facilitates end-of-life planning and collaborative decision-making. However, studies in Ethiopia are scattered, and there is no thorough synthesis of the literature regarding utilization of PC service and its associated determinants among adult cancer patients.ObjectiveThis study seeks to close this important gap by assessing the pooled size of PC service utilization among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia and determining its associated characteristics.MethodsThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Guideline was followed in conducting the investigation. A detailed search until 31 February 2025 was conducted on Pub Med, HINARI, Goggle Scholar, EMBASE, CINAHL, Science Direct, and Scopus. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) rating method was used to evaluate the quality of the extracted studies. The retrieved data was analyzed using the statistical program STATA v.17. The pooled analysis was estimated using weighted inverse variance random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics. Subgroup analysis was carried out to look in to the reason for heterogeneity.ResultThe combined percentage of Ethiopian adult cancer patients who used PC services was 45.11% (95% CI: 35.06, 55.16). The Sidama region had the greatest utilization rate at 51.51% (95% CI: 29.66, 73.36). Higher use of palliative care was substantially correlated with college or university education (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.31, 5.02).ConclusionPC service utilization in Ethiopia was found to be below 50%, highlighting a critical need for intervention. Higher educational attainment increases the likelihood of using these services, underscoring differences impacted by sociodemographic factors. Therefore, healthcare professionals and policymakers should focus on several methods to enhance the utilization of PC services in Ethiopia.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024554748.

Background

Cancer is the world’s biggest cause of mortality, accounting for 9.7 million deaths in 2022, making it a serious public health concern.1,2 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Ethiopia, the burden of cancer is rising rapidly due to aging populations, lifestyle changes, and limited access to early detection and treatment services.3,4 Palliative care (PC), also referred to as supportive care, comfort care, or symptom management, is a comprehensive strategy designed to enhance the quality of life for individuals with life-threatening illnesses and their families.5,6 Through early detection, accurate evaluation, and treatment of pain and other distressing symptoms, it addresses physical, psychological, and spiritual suffering.5–8

The benefits of PC are well-documented. It reduces symptom burden, enhances patient and caregiver satisfaction, improves quality of life, and in certain situation, even prolongs survival.5,9,10 For cancer patients, especially those with advanced disease, PC plays a critical role in managing pain, fatigue, depression, and other debilitating symptoms, while also facilitating shared decision-making and end-of-life planning.8,10,11 Despite these benefits, global utilization of PC services remains low, particularly in LMICs.12,13 The WHO estimates that just 14% of those in need of PC receive it globally. 5 In sub-Saharan Africa, access is hindered by limited infrastructure, lack of trained personnel, and cultural misconceptions about PC. 14 Several predictors of PC service utilization have been identified in the literature. These include socio-demographic factors (age, sex, education), clinical characteristics (cancer stage, type, comorbidities), health system factors (availability of services, referral pathways), and patient-level factors (knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about palliative care).12,15,16

PC is a critical, yet often overlooked, component of the Ethiopian health system. 17 Studies conducted in Ethiopia reveal that adult cancer patients’ use of PC services is not at its best, with usage rates varying from 26% to 42.8% in different contexts.18,19 Factors contributing to low uptake include inadequate awareness among patients and providers, limited service availability, and poor integration of PC into routine oncology services.17,20,21 However, existing studies in Ethiopia are fragmented, vary in methodology, and often focus on single institutions or regions. Policymakers and healthcare professionals are unable to create evidence-based initiatives since there is presently no thorough synthesis of the extent and correlates of PC service consumption among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia.

This systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) aims to bridge this important gap by evaluating the pooled size of PC service use among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia and determining its associated factors. The findings will provide a robust evidence base to inform national cancer control strategies, improve service delivery, and promote equitable access to PC across the country.

Methods

We reported the result of this study in accordance with the guideline of PRISMA 2020, and the checklist was located in the supplemental file of S 1 Table. A PRISMA flow diagram was used to show how the studies were chosen. This review’s protocol was published online in a peer-reviewed publication 22 and prospectively registered in PROSPERO.

Data source and search strategy

Seven electronic databases (PubMed, Hinari, EMBASE, CINAHL, Science Direct, Scopus, and Google Scholar) was thoroughly searched for articles published up until February 31, 2025. The following search terms, along with a mixture of Boolean operators, were used to find relevant studies: ‘Palliative Care’ or ‘Rehabilitation’ or ‘Supportive Care’ or ‘Oncology Care’ AND ‘Utilisation’ or ‘Usage’ or ‘Uptake’ and ‘Cancer’ or ‘Malignancy’ and ‘Ethiopia.’ A search was conducted from 10 January 2026 to 17 January 2026, and a sample of the search strategy was found in the supplemental file of S 2 Table.

Eligibility criteria

From the inception until February 31, 2025, all published and unpublished research (grey literature or preprint) that reported the extent or determinant of PC service utilization among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia using observational study designs (cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort) was included. Language restrictions were not applied.

Studies without full texts and abstracts, reports, editorials, qualitative research, media stories, or data on the degree of PC service consumption or its predictor were not included.

Study selection

Two review authors (ADD and ADG) separately reviewed each unique study’s title or abstract after duplication was eliminated. After then, entire texts were examined for any articles that might still be relevant. After comparing the final lists of included articles, conflicts were settled by consensus and approved with the involvement of a third author.

Outcomes measurement

Adult cancer patients’ use of PC services is the study’s primary outcome. Adequate PC service utilization is defined as respondents who score above the mean or median level for outcome measuring variables (level of PC service utilization); inadequate utilization of PC service is defined as those who score equal to or below the mean or median level of PC service utilization. 23 Each study provided the number of patients who had sufficiently used PC service using these criteria, allowing for the computation of a pooled magnitude estimate across studies. Our secondary outcome is the variables related to PC service utilization that were assessed in the primary studies using logistic regression analyses and presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) in at least two studies. We calculated the pooled AOR to determine these factors.

Data extraction

Using a standard Excel spreadsheet that was taken from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), ADD and TT separately extracted the necessary data. The author, year of publication, study design, study environment, sample size, response rate, method or instrument used to measure PC service consumption, and prevalence of PC service utilization were among the characteristics that were extracted. During the data extraction process, disagreements were settled by discussion.

Quality assessment

Each included primary study’s methodological quality (risk of bias) was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) critical assessment criteria for reporting prevalence studies. 24 Nine questions make up the instrument, and each criterion has a score of 0 for “no,” “not clear,” or “not applicable,” and 1 for “yes.” Three reviewers (ADD, AY, and AG) separately completed the assessment, and a final agreement was obtained through discussion. A total quality score ranging from 0 to 9 was obtained by summarizing the average scores provided by each author across all items. Based on average scores of 0–4, 5-7, and 7-9, they were classified as low (high risk of bias), moderate (moderate risk of bias), and high quality (low risk of bias), respectively.

Statistical analysis

The statistical program Stata v.17 was used to analyze the data. A weighted inverse variance random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled analysis. The I-squared (I2) statistic was used to measure heterogeneity; values <25, 50–75%, and 75% were categorized as low, moderate, and high, respectively. 25 Subgroup analysis by geography was also performed to look at potential causes of heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess how a single study affected the overall pooled result. However, because this methodology requires at least ten papers, publication bias was not evaluated. 26

Result

Search and selection result

An initial comprehensive search of seven electronic databases (Pub Med, HINARI, Goggle Scholar, EMBASE, CINAHL, Science Direct, and Scopus) yielded a total of 258 articles. Of these, 245 articles remained after 13 duplicate articles were removed. 234 articles were eliminated after the abstract and title were reviewed. After that, the full texts of eleven articles were retrieved, and their eligibility was evaluated. Seven (7) articles were disqualified for different reasons, including the fact that they did not report PC service utilization with a time bound (within the last one year), did not report the outcome of interest, or did not use the mean or median as a cutoff criterion. In the end, this review had four articles (Figure 1).Figure 1.A PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

Of these, two studies were conducted in the Sidama region.27,28 and two of them in Addis Ababa.15,19 Systematic random sampling was employed in two studies,15,19 simple random sampling in one study, 28 and consecutive sampling in one study. 27 The stated response rates are between 92.4% and 100% (Table 1). Palliative care service utilization

The random effects model analysis revealed that the pooled estimated magnitude of PC service utilization among Ethiopian adult cancer patients was 45.11% (95% CI: 35.06, 55.16) and the observed heterogeneity was significant (I2 = 92.93%, P < 0.001), the magnitude ranged from 35.4% to 62.8% (Figure 2).Figure 2.Forest plot using the random effects model illustrates the combined utilization of PC service among cancer patients.

Forest plot using the random effects model illustrates the combined utilization of PC service among cancer patients.

Sub group analysis

To determine the possible cause of the observed heterogeneity, a sub group analysis by region was conducted. Magnitude of PC service utilization by region was highest in Sidama region 51.51% (95% CI: 29.66, 73.36) as compared to Addis Abeba 38.97% (95% CI: 31.72, 46.22) (Figure 3).Figure 3.Regional subgroup meta-analysis of PC service use among adult cancer patients.

Regional subgroup meta-analysis of PC service use among adult cancer patients.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis revealed that the pooled utilization of PC service of 45.11% stayed constant, indicating that there was no single-study effect, confirming the stability and robustness of the meta-analytic estimate. As a result, no single study has a disproportionate impact on the overall PC service utilization, enhancing the validity of the synthesized result (Figure 4).Figure 4.Sensitivity analyses on utilization of PC service among adult cancer patients.

Sensitivity analyses on utilization of PC service among adult cancer patients.

Factors associated with palliative service utilization

In the current SRMA, levels of education were found to be the only factors associated with PC service utilization.

Association between educational level and palliative care service utilization

Two studies assess the association between level of education and PC service utilization. Both studies had found a significant association. Accordingly, the odds of PC service utilization were approximately three times higher among patients with college or university education as compared to those who cannot read and write (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.31, 5.02) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0 %) (Figure 5).Figure 5.A forest plot illustrating the association between adult cancer patients’ use of PC services and their level of education.

Discussion

Palliative care is underutilized among cancer patients, and there is scarce data on the factors associated with service utilization, particularly in Africa. We conducted a thorough search and analysis of research evaluating the use of palliative care services and the factors that influence their use among Ethiopian adult cancer patients. In our study, PC service utilization among Ethiopian adult cancer patients was 45.11%. This level of utilization is higher than rates reported in several earlier multi-country studies from other regions, including Asia (35%). 29 This might be due to differences in the study area and population, in which Asia evidence base was not confined to a single country; instead, it pooled data across heterogeneous settings (e.g., Bangladesh, Philippines, Sri Lanka, and Vietnam) and often reported PC use among those with documented PC awareness (with a relatively small subgroup, n = 234). In contrast, the primary studies included in our review focused specifically on adult cancer patients in Ethiopia and measured PC utilization in a more directly comparable local context.

Additionally, the comparatively higher utilization was observed in developed settings such as the United States (78.1%), 30 Norway (63%) 31 and Sweden (57%). 32 The main reason for this might be due to the differences in the socio-demographics, health policies, health care delivery systems, infrastructure, and accessibility of PC services. These contextual factors likely influence both patients’ opportunities to receive PC and clinicians’ referral practices. Our findings highlight the need for Ethiopia-specific evidence on determinants of PC utilization and for interventions that address identified barriers. By focusing on Ethiopian adult cancer patients, this study can inform future research aimed at identifying modifiable factors that explain utilization gaps and support the development of targeted strategies to improve PC access and referral across the cancer care continuum.

We found evidence indicating that the use of PC services was relatively high among those who had college or university educational status. The odds of PC service utilization were approximately three times higher among patients who had a college or university educational level than those who were unable to read and write. This conclusion is corroborated by a prior study that found individuals with higher levels of education were able to follow prescription or appointment schedules, comprehend written and verbal instructions from medical professionals, and have sufficient knowledge of the healthcare system to receive necessary services. 33 Public education and the need to dispel cultural attitudes and misconceptions about PC services were also emphasized in a Korean study. 34 Similarly, study conducted in Uganda, 35 Kenya, 16 Nigeria, 36 and Norway 37 revealed that individuals with higher educational attainment were more likely to utilize palliative care services compared to their less educated counterparts. As a result, cancer patients with college or university degrees may attend appointments on a regular basis and adhere to recommended treatment regimens, which may have affected the use of PC services.

Strengths and limitations of studies

This review strictly adhered to the preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis guidelines. However, this systematic review and meta-analysis included only few number of studies. In addition, due to the nature of cross-sectional studies, we are not able to make assumptions about causality between variables. Furthermore, due to the methodology requiring inclusion of at least 10 papers, we were unable to evaluate publication bias.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated that PC service use among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia is moderate, with a pooled magnitude of 45.11%. This indicates that nearly half of patients are not utilizing PC services, highlighting the necessity of focused actions to improve PC service accessibility and awareness. Higher educational attainment increases the likelihood of using these services, underscoring differences impacted by sociodemographic factors.

Therefore, healthcare professionals and policymakers should target several methods to enhance the utilization of PC services in Ethiopia. First, focused educational initiatives should be created to increase patient and healthcare provider knowledge of PC, with a focus on highlighting its advantages and dispelling misconceptions. In order to guarantee that all cancer patients have fair access to PC, particular focus should be placed on meeting the requirements of patients with lower educational levels through community outreach and support initiatives. Ethiopian healthcare outcomes can be improved and patient satisfaction raised by putting these techniques into practice.
