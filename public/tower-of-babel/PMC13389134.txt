Foot and Ankle Contributions to Noncontact ACL Injury Risk: A Systematic Review
Authors: Lexy Farrington, Clare Hotze, Roxy Markowsky, Reed Coda, Sterre van den Anker, Bryant Vopat, Lisa Vopat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Noncontact anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are common and carry long-term consequences for the athlete. Altered foot and ankle biomechanics, landing patterns, and prior ankle injury have been implicated in ACL injury risk and may inform prevention strategies.Purpose:To identify key biomechanical risk factors associated with the foot and ankle that may contribute to ACL injury risk.Study Design:Systematic review; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:A systematic review was conducted following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Cochrane, and SPORTDiscus databases were searched for English-language studies (2010-2025) evaluating ACL injuries and reporting on the foot or ankle. Screening was performed using Rayyan, followed by full-text review. Data were extracted, and then risk of bias was assessed using the methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS).Results:Out of 1772 studies evaluated, a total of 48 (2.7%) studies met inclusion criteria. Overall, 33 studies (68.8%) identified variables associated with increased ACL injury risk, while 11 (22.9%) reported decreased risk and 9 (18.8%) found no association. Foot and ankle biomechanics were examined in 32 studies (66.7%), foot strike pattern in 8 (16.7%), toe direction in 7 (14.6%), and prior ankle injury history in 5 (10.4%). Statistically supported findings were present in 81% of studies. Greater dynamic rearfoot motion (eversion/inversion), increased dorsiflexion, and medial arch collapse (eg, navicular drop) were commonly associated with increased risk. Forefoot landings were protective, while rearfoot and flatfoot strikes, as well as toe-in or toe-out positions, were consistently linked to higher injury risk. Sex-specific analysis showed that female athletes demonstrated more high-risk biomechanical patterns than their male counterparts. Sport-specific analysis revealed basketball (n = 14), soccer (n = 8), and handball (n = 6) as the most studied sports, with >75% of these studies identifying ≥1 foot- or ankle-related ACL injury risk factor. Study quality was generally low, with predominantly noncomparative designs and modest MINORS.Conclusion:Foot and ankle factors, including prior injury, alignment, foot strike patterns, and toe progression, contribute meaningfully to ACL injury risk, yet their precise influence on knee stabilization remains unclear. The foot and ankle are understudied, with few investigations addressing their role in dynamic tasks that challenge knee stability. High-quality comparative research that accounts for sex and sport differences is needed to inform targeted prevention and improve risk stratification.Keywords: ACL, knee, ligaments, ankle, foot, biomechanics, general, research (in vivo or in vitro)

Noncontact anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are a significant concern in sports medicine, particularly in athletes competing in soccer, basketball, and volleyball.6,16,62 Unlike contact injuries, which result from direct external force, noncontact ACL injuries occur due to intrinsic biomechanical, neuromuscular, and environmental factors. These injuries often happen during sudden deceleration, cutting, pivoting, or improper landing maneuvers, leading to substantial time loss in competition and a return-to-sport journey that demands resilience.39,62 Annual reported incidence of ACL injuries in the United States alone approximate to 1 in 3500, with around 400,000 ACL reconstructions performed each year. 16 Given the high prevalence and severity of these injuries, identifying key risk factors is essential to developing targeted prevention strategies.

Various risk factors have already been identified for ACL injuries, with a strong emphasis on sex disparities. Female athletes sustain ACL injuries at a higher rate than their male counterparts, largely due to differences in biomechanics, neuromuscular control, and hormonal influences. 47 In addition, decreased knee flexion angle, increased hip internal rotation angle, contralateral pelvic hike, a small femoral notch, and concomitant lower extremity injuries are reported to be associated with increased ACL risk.7,60 However, while these factors have been well-documented, the role of foot and ankle biomechanics in noncontact ACL injuries remains an area of emerging interest. Understanding how foot and ankle positioning contributes to injury mechanisms could provide valuable insights for refining prevention programs.

Dysfunction or maladaptation at the foot and ankle could impose unique, compounding stresses on the knee, potentially elevating ligament injury risk. 8 A previous systematic review identified specific foot and ankle characteristics such as toe-in landing position (defined as internal rotation of the foot relative to the direction of progression during dynamic landing), reduced ankle dorsiflexion range of motion, and decreased plantarflexion angle at initial contact (defined as a relatively more dorsiflexed foot posture) as potential contributors to ACL injury risk. 7 Another study reinforces that toe-in landing position exacerbates biomechanical risk factors associated with ACL injury, while toe-out landing position decreases these factors. 53 To better understand and mitigate ACL injuries, future research should focus on detailed biomechanical analysis of foot and ankle function across varied movements and integrate these findings into prevention and rehabilitation strategies.

While existing literature has explored overall biomechanical risk factors for ACL injuries, there is a gap in research comprehensively evaluating the interplay between the foot, ankle, and ACL injury risk. Addressing this gap presents an opportunity to provide targeted insights that may shape future clinical interventions and reduce the prevalence of noncontact ACL injuries. This systematic review aims to deepen the understanding of how foot and ankle biomechanics influence noncontact ACL injury risk, with a focus on dynamic foot/ankle orientation at initial contact and during loading, sex differences, and the history of prior foot/ankle injuries.

Methods

Literature Search

A systematic review was conducted following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines. A study search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane, and SPORTDiscus databases for English-language studies that evaluated ACL injuries that report on the foot and ankle and were published between January 2010 and January 2025. The search terms to be used are as follows: (("anterior cruciate ligament" OR "ACL") AND ("foot" OR "ankle" OR "toe" OR "achilles" OR "plantar" OR "deltoid ligament" OR "talofibular" OR "spring ligament" OR "bifurcate ligament" OR "talocrural" OR "subtalar" OR "tibialis posterior" OR "peroneal")). Each article was screened by 3 team members (L.F., C.H., R.M.), and discrepancies were discussed and resolved by the first author (L.F.). The studies were screened by title and abstract using the systematic review software Rayyan and were exported to an Excel document. A total of 2386 studies were identified in the initial search, and 614 duplicate studies were removed (Figure 1).

PRISMA flowchart for inclusion of articles. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Study Selection and Eligibility

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported on noncontact ACL injuries, included data on foot or ankle biomechanics, focused on human populations, and were published in the English language between January 2010 and January 2025. Studies were excluded if they did not report on ACL injuries or foot and ankle biomechanics and were published prior to 2010. Animal or cadaveric models, review articles, editorials, case reports, and conference abstracts were excluded.

Once all the studies from the databases listed above were identified, 3 research team members (L.F., C.H., and R.M.) independently screened each article's title using previously determined exclusion criteria in Rayyan. The remaining articles were then screened by their abstracts. The full text of each qualifying article was then reviewed and either included or excluded from the final list. After each step, the respective lists of included and excluded articles were compared. If there were disagreements on inclusions, the majority decision (2/3) made the final determination on the article's inclusion. The previously mentioned team members then divided the final list of included articles and extracted the data from each full-text publication.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by the 3 team members. Relevant information from each included study was systematically collected and recorded in a protected Excel spreadsheet. Extracted data included study design, sample size, participant demographics, mechanisms of injury, sport played, injury history, and reported foot and ankle biomechanical factors. Foot and ankle biomechanical factors were defined as dynamic and static measures of foot and ankle function that may influence knee loading and ACL injury risk, including medial longitudinal arch height, navicular drop (change in navicular height from seated to weightbearing), rearfoot motion (eversion/inversion), ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion range of motion, and other dynamic movement patterns. These variables were primarily assessed via motion capture, force plate analyses, weightbearing imaging when available, and video analyses.

Studies were categorized as direct or indirect based on their outcome measures. For indirect studies, relevant surrogate or pseudo-outcomes were evaluated to infer their potential association with ACL injury risk. Both direct and indirect studies were combined for the overall review, with some analyses conducted separately by category. Outcomes were divided into 4 categories: previous ankle injury history, foot and ankle biomechanics, foot strike patterns, and toe direction or foot progression angle. Foot progression angle, interchangeably referred to as “toe direction,” was defined as the orientation of the foot relative to the direction of travel during dynamic tasks, with toe-in (internal rotation) and toe-out (external rotation) positions evaluated for their association with ACL injury risk. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study characteristics, and findings were analyzed to identify common biomechanical patterns and associations related to noncontact ACL injury risk.

Risk-of-Bias Assessment

Two independent authors (L.F. and S.V.) evaluated the quality of each included study using the methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS) tool (AppendixTable A1). The scoring for each question included a 0 if not reported, 1 if reported but insufficient, and 2 if reported and sufficient. The maximal score for a comparative study was 24 points, and 16 points for a noncomparative study.

Results

A total of 1772 studies were identified and screened during the initial search (Figure 1). Of these, 48 studies (2.7%) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (AppendixTable A1). The included studies consisted of 6 level 2, 16 level 3, and 26 level 4 studies; 31 studies were noncomparative, with a mean MINORS score of 8.33 (range, 6-14), while 17 studies were comparative, with a mean MINORS score of 18.12 (range, 13-24).

A total of 48 studies met inclusion criteria, comprising 20 (41.7%) that directly evaluated foot and ankle risk factors for ACL injury and 28 (58.3%) that reported indirect associations through related biomechanical outcomes. The 20 direct studies included 4272 patients (1393 male, 2879 female) with a mean age of approximately 21.3 years (range, 14.6-28.9). These studies reported 1445 noncontact ACL injuries and consisted of 6 cross-sectional designs, with follow-up durations ranging from 21 days to 20 years. The 28 indirect studies included 794 patients (424 male, 370 female) with a mean age of 21.95 years (range, 16.8-27.7), reported 29 noncontact ACL injuries, and were all cross-sectional without follow-up.

Of the 48 included studies, 32 (66.7%) examined foot and ankle biomechanics, 8 (16.7%) assessed foot strike patterns, 7 (14.6%) investigated toe direction at landing or foot progression angle, and 5 (10.4%) reported previous ankle injury history. Four studies evaluated foot strike and biomechanical variables concurrently. Variables associated with increased ACL risk were identified in 33 studies (68.8%), whereas 11 (22.9%) reported variables associated with decreased risk, and 9 (18.8%) found no association. Four studies evaluated ≥2 biomechanical categories. Statistically supported findings were reported in 39 of the 48 studies (81.3%), including 15 direct and 24 indirect studies.

Previous Ankle Injury History

Five of the 48 studies (10.4%) examined previous ankle injury history. Two of these studies (40.0%) found an association between prior ankle injury and increased ACL risk, while 3 studies (60.0%) found no associations. Ankle sprains were associated with increased ACL risk in 1 study but not in another. Chronic ankle instability was reported as a risk factor in 1 study.

Foot and Ankle Biomechanics

Thirty-two (66.7%) studies assessed foot and ankle biomechanics (Table 1). Of these, 19 (59.4%) reported variables associated with increased ACL risk, 9 (28.1%) identified potentially protective factors, and 6 (18.8%) reported no significant associations. A total of 26 studies (81.3%) were statistically supported.

Navicular drop was examined in 5 of the 32 biomechanics studies (15.6%), with mixed results: 2 studies reported that increased navicular drop was associated with greater ACL risk, while 3 studies found no association. One study reported that increased navicular drop was associated with higher odds of ACL injury (OR = 1.09 males 1.10 females). All 5 were statistically supported. Ankle inversion and eversion were reported inconsistently across studies and, based on the original study definitions, generally reflected hindfoot (subtalar joint) motion during dynamic tasks. Ankle eversion was assessed in 4 of the biomechanics studies (12.5%), with 3 of the studies identifying increased eversion as a risk factor for ACL injury and 1 reporting no association. All 3 risk-related studies were statistically supported. Ankle inversion was evaluated in 2 biomechanics studies (6.3%), both of which found increased inversion associated with elevated ACL risk and were statistically supported.

Foot abduction and external rotation were each reported in 3 and 2 studies, respectively, all of which associated these motions with increased ACL injury risk, though none of these findings were statistically supported. Foot internal rotation was identified as a risk factor in 1 statistically supported study.

Among the foot and ankle biomechanics studies, ankle plantarflexion positioning at landing was evaluated in 10 studies (31.3%). Two of these studies found that decreased plantarflexion during landing was associated with increased ACL injury risk, while 7 studies reported increased plantarflexion as protective. Two studies reported no association, including one with sex-specific differences (decreased ACL injury risk for male patients with increased plantarflexion, but not in female patients). Nine of the 10 studies were statistically supported. Ankle dorsiflexion dynamic positioning at landing was assessed in 12 biomechanics studies (37.5%), all of which found associations with ACL injury risk. Six reported increased dorsiflexion as a risk factor, 5 identified decreased dorsiflexion as a risk factor, and 1 reported increased risk with both excessive and reduced range of motion. In 1 study, greater ankle dorsiflexion was associated with reduced odds of ACL injury (OR = 0.62). Ten of the 12 studies were statistically supported.

Three additional biomechanics studies (9.4%) examined isolated variables: ankle flexion at initial contact, foot rotation at initial contact, and ankle energy absorption during landing. The first 2 found no association with ACL injury risk, while the energy absorption study found that reduced ankle energy absorption at initial contact with increased absorption during the terminal landing phase was protective. All 3 studies were statistically supported.

Foot Strike Pattern

Out of the 48 studies, 8 (16.7%) evaluated foot strike landing patterns in relation to ACL risk. Of these, 7 reported foot strike patterns associated with increased ACL risk with statistical significance. Two studies identified protective effects linked to forefoot strike landing, both statistically supported. One study found no significant association. Specific landing positions were also examined: heel strike landing (2 studies) and rearfoot strike (2 studies) were both consistently associated with increased ACL injury risk, all statistically significant. Anterior and lateral foot landing relative to the center of mass were each reported in 2 studies, with all indicating increased risk, although only half were statistically significant. Flatfoot strike (1 study) was also associated with increased risk, with statistical support.

Toe Direction

Seven of 48 studies (14.6%) reported on toe direction or foot progression angle and ACL injury risk. All 7 identified toe positioning associated with increased ACL injury risk, with 4 studies reaching statistical significance. Toe-out landing was examined in 4 studies; all reported an association with increased ACL injury risk. One study also noted that excessive toe-in or toe-out landings were linked to higher risk. Two studies showed statistically significant results, while 2 did not. Toe-in landing was assessed in 3 studies, all finding an association with increased ACL injury risk. One study additionally reported that excessive toe-in or toe-out landings increased risk. Two studies demonstrated statistical significance, and 1 did not.

Sex-Specific Outcomes

Sex-specific cohorts included 21 male-only studies, 13 female-only, and 14 with both sexes without direct comparison. In male-only cohorts, foot and ankle biomechanics were examined in 13 studies (61.9%), of which 9 identified features associated with increased ACL injury risk, 3 reported decreased risk, and 1 found no association. Foot strike patterns were analyzed in 7 male-only studies (33.3%), with 6 indicating increased risk, 2 reporting decreased risk, and 1 showing no relationship. Toe direction was evaluated in 3 male-only studies (14.3%), and previous ankle injury history was reported in 1 study. Overall, 17 studies (81%) identified foot- or ankle-related factors linked to increased ACL injury risk in male patients, 5 identified decreased risk, and 2 found no association.

In female-only cohorts, foot and ankle biomechanics including but not limited to navicular drop, dorsiflexion, and plantarflexion at landing were evaluated in 8 studies, with 7 (87.5%) reporting features associated with increased ACL injury risk. Prior ankle injury history was assessed in 3 of the 8 studies (37.5%) with one study (33%) reporting increased injury risk, toe direction in 2 studies (25%) with both reporting increased injury risk, and foot strike pattern reporting increased injury risk in 1 study (12.5%). One study identified a protective association, and 5 studies found no relationship between foot/ankle characteristics and ACL injury risk.

Nine of the total 48 studies (18.8%) explicitly compared outcomes by sex (Table 2). Comparative studies revealed that female patients consistently exhibited biomechanical patterns linked to higher ACL injury risk, including differences in ankle dorsiflexion, inversion, and eversion, reduced plantarflexion, and stiffer rearfoot landings. Seven studies comparing sexes reported that female patients had increased risk of ACL injury because of different variables, while 2 studies reported that male patients demonstrated more protective biomechanical characteristics.

Sport-Specific Outcomes

Sport-specific data were provided in 28 studies (58.3%), most frequently basketball (n = 14), soccer (n = 8), and handball (n = 6). Other sports represented included American football (n = 4), badminton (n = 4), rugby (n = 3), volleyball (n = 3), ice hockey (n = 2), judo (n = 2), floorball (n = 2), and dance (n = 1) (Figure 2). In addition, eight studies analyzed more than one sport. Across sport-specific studies, prior ankle injury history was assessed in 3 studies, foot strike pattern in 5 (4 indicating increased ACL injury risk), toe direction in 3, and foot/ankle biomechanics in 21. Among the biomechanical studies assessing foot and ankle movements during landing, 16 reported increased risk, most often linked to increased ankle dorsiflexion, navicular drop, ankle eversion, foot abduction, external rotation, and reduced dorsiflexion range of motion. One study reported decreased risk, and 4 found no association. In total, 21 studies (75%) identified ≥1 foot- or ankle-related factor associated with increased ACL injury risk, 1 reported decreased risk, and 6 found no significant association.

In basketball-specific studies (n = 14), prior ankle injury history was assessed in 2 studies, foot strike pattern in 3, toe direction in 2, and foot/ankle biomechanics in 9. Of the biomechanics studies, 6 reported increased ACL injury risk, most often linked to foot abduction, dorsiflexion, ankle eversion, navicular drop, and external rotation at landing, while 3 found no association between biomechanical landing pattern and ACL injury risk. Across all basketball-specific studies, 10 studies (71.4%) reported ≥1 foot- or ankle-related factor associated with increased ACL injury risk, while 4 reported no significant associations.

In handball-specific studies (n = 6), foot strike pattern was assessed in 1 study, toe direction in 2, and biomechanics in 4. Among biomechanics studies, 2 reported increased ACL injury risk associated with increased dorsiflexion and navicular drop, 1 reported a decreased risk with increased plantarflexion, and 1 found no association between biomechanical landing pattern and ACL injury risk. In total among handball-specific studies, 4 studies (66.7%) reported ≥1 risk factor, 1 reported a protective factor, and 1 reported no association.

In soccer-specific studies (n = 8), prior ankle injury history and foot strike pattern were each assessed in 1 study, toe direction in 2, and biomechanics in 5. Of the biomechanics studies, 4 reported increased ACL injury risk associated with decreased ankle dorsiflexion, foot external rotation at landing, increased ankle eversion, and navicular drop, while 1 study found no association between biomechanical landing pattern and ACL injury risk. Overall in soccer-specific studies, 6 studies (75%) reported ≥1 factor associated with increased risk, and 2 reported no association.

Discussion

This systematic review examined the association between foot and ankle biomechanics and ACL injury risk, identifying several prevalent risk factors such as restricted and excessive dorsiflexion, reduced plantarflexion, excessive eversion, atypical toe-in or toe-out landing patterns, and heel or rearfoot strike. Sex-specific analyses consistently revealed that female athletes demonstrate high-risk landing mechanics, including variations in dorsiflexion angles, inversion, and stiffer rearfoot strikes. Sport-specific trends were particularly evident in basketball, soccer, and handball, with 21 of 28 studies focusing on these sports reporting ≥1 foot- or ankle-related factor associated with elevated ACL injury risk.

Previous Ankle Injury History

The relationship between prior ankle injury and ACL injury risk remains unclear due to inconsistent findings and methodological limitations. Without objective biomechanical data, it is unclear whether ankle injury contributes directly to ACL vulnerability or reflects a broader predisposition to lower extremity injury. Some studies suggest that chronic ankle instability may increase ACL injury risk, while others report no association, pointing to the need for clearer definitions of ankle pathology. 29 Zhang et al 63 found that individuals with chronic ankle instability who experienced >4 ankle sprains showed significantly higher peak ACL loading forces during single-leg landing compared to those with fewer sprains. Although ankle sprains are the most common musculoskeletal injury in sports, few studies distinguish between acute and chronic injuries or evaluate lingering neuromuscular deficits.18,58 Changes in proprioception, range of motion, muscular control, or movement confidence following ankle injury may lead to compensatory knee mechanics and increase ACL loading. Retrospective designs and reliance on self-reporting further limit causal interpretation.

Foot and Ankle Biomechanics

Among 32 studies assessing foot and ankle biomechanics, 6 studies reported increased dorsiflexion as a risk factor, 5 implicated restricted dorsiflexion, and 1 found risk elevated at both extremes. This pattern suggests a nonlinear relationship, emphasizing the importance of an optimal dorsiflexion range for maintaining kinetic chain stability and neuromuscular control. Supporting evidence indicates that ankle dorsiflexion facilitates sensory integration, motor planning, and the activation of key musculature such as the quadriceps and deep core. Limited dorsiflexion has been linked to compensatory movement patterns that elevate knee loading and injury risk. 3 Plantarflexion at landing was evaluated in 10 studies, with increased plantarflexion angles at landing demonstrating decreased ACL risk effects at injury mechanism in 7 studies. Increased plantarflexion was generally protective in male patients, and decreased plantarflexion correlated with elevated ACL injury risk, highlighting the importance of considering sex-based biomechanical differences. Larger ankle plantarflexion angles during landing may reduce knee impact loads and lower the risk of rupture at the lateral femoral attachment of the ACL, potentially by improving shock absorption and force distribution during landing. 59

Navicular drop, ankle eversion, and ankle inversion were frequently associated with increased ACL injury risk across studies. However, findings related to navicular drop were inconsistent, likely due to methodological heterogeneity and variations in participant demographics. In contrast, eversion and inversion consistently correlated with increased risk, reflecting their influence on frontal plane knee loading and dynamic stability. These distal biomechanical factors directly affect proximal joint mechanics, particularly knee valgus and rotational moments, which are well-established contributors to ACL injury.19,49 Effective prevention should focus on restoring ankle dorsiflexion and enhancing plantarflexion during landing through targeted mobility and neuromuscular training to optimize load distribution and motor control. Complementary strengthening and proprioceptive exercises aimed at controlling ankle eversion and inversion can further improve knee stability and reduce injury risk.

Foot Strike

Foot strike patterns significantly influence lower extremity biomechanics by altering load distribution and joint kinematics, with rearfoot strikes (RFS) generally linked to higher ACL injury risk and forefoot strikes (FFS) showing protective effects. Teng et al 51 demonstrated that flatfoot and RFS landings significantly increase vertical ground reaction forces and reduce knee flexion angles at initial contact, both key contributors to heightened ACL loading. RFS also produces anterior ground reaction forces and early stance knee extension moments, whereas FFS generates posterior forces and redistributes joint moments toward the ankle, reducing knee and hip stress.12,13,55 Yi et al 61 shows that FFS cutting movements result in lower vertical and lateral ground reaction forces and reduced loading rates compared with RFS, highlighting a protective effect of forefoot striking. Taken together, this body of evidence underscores foot strike pattern as an indispensable variable in ACL injury risk models. Its modulation not only alters lower extremity kinematics and kinetics but also represents a promising, yet underutilized, target for injury prevention interventions. Prevention strategies may include gait retraining, neuromuscular training programs focused on optimizing foot strike mechanics, and sport-specific movement coaching to promote safer landing patterns. A comprehensive approach to ACL risk assessment and mitigation must therefore integrate foot strike mechanics to fully address the multifactorial nature of ligament injury. 12

Toe Direction

Deviation from a neutral toe position, whether excessive toe-in or toe-out, creates distinct biomechanical conditions that increase ACL loading. Excessive toe-in increases knee abduction moment, tibial internal rotation, peak hip adduction, and knee internal rotation angles, aligning with the high-risk valgus internal rotation mechanism.25,53 Excessive toe-out increases knee valgus angles, varus moments, and tibial internal rotation velocity, generating coupled frontal and transverse plane stresses.7,50,53 Small deviations in foot progression angle propagate through the kinetic chain, altering hip and knee alignment as well as ground reaction force vectors. The consistent association across all 7 of the identified studies on foot progression angle, regardless of statistical significance, supports a genuine biomechanical link rather than random variation. From a prevention perspective, maintaining a neutral toe orientation during landing should be emphasized to reduce abnormal coupled hip motion, with neuromuscular training implemented to correct habitual toe-in or toe-out patterns, particularly in athletes with preexisting valgus tendencies. Toe progression angle remains underrepresented in ACL research, underscoring the need for standardized definitions of excessive positioning, mechanistic studies to clarify load thresholds, and investigation of its interaction with sex-specific and sport-specific risk factors.

Sex-Specific Outcomes

Sex-specific differences in foot and ankle biomechanics play a pivotal role in modulating ACL injury risk, with a notable divergence in risk profiles between male and female patients. In our studies’ male cohorts, foot and ankle characteristics such as specific biomechanical patterns, foot strike, and toe direction were frequently linked to increased ACL risk, though some studies reported protective effects or no association, indicating heterogeneity within male populations. In contrast, female-only studies more consistently demonstrated foot and ankle biomechanical features associated with elevated ACL injury risk, including greater ankle dorsiflexion, inversion, eversion, and stiffer rearfoot landings. Comparative analyses reinforce this sex disparity, with female patients exhibiting biomechanical patterns known to increase ACL strain, such as greatly increased or decreased ankle dorsiflexion angles, increased inversion, reduced plantarflexion, and more rigid rearfoot landing.

Nakatsugawa et al 42 found that the midfoot in female participants contributed substantially to both the absorption of impact forces and the transmission of propulsive forces, highlighting a sex-specific load distribution mechanism that may interact with proximal joint loading. Hansberger et al 22 identified excessive ankle eversion as a female-specific risk factor, while Lee et al 35 demonstrated that increased ankle plantarflexion offered protective effects against knee valgus moments in male but not female patients. 38 Toe-in landing patterns produced significantly greater hip adduction and knee internal rotation in female than male participants, amplifying ACL loading risk. 53 These findings reinforce the necessity of incorporating sex-specific biomechanical data into ACL injury risk models and tailoring prevention programs accordingly. Interventions targeting modifiable factors such as foot strike patterns, ankle range of motion, and landing mechanics, while accounting for midfoot function in female athletes, may yield greater efficacy when customized to the unique biomechanical and neuromuscular profiles of each sex. Addressing these differences is essential not only to improve prevention outcomes but also to advance our understanding of ACL injury mechanisms in male and female athletes alike.

Sport-Specific Outcomes

Sport-specific patterns in foot and ankle contributions to ACL injury risk demonstrate that the unique demands and movement profiles of each sport influence both the prevalence and the nature of biomechanical risk factors. Basketball, soccer, and handball were the most frequently studied sports, each showing evidence of increased ACL injury risk linked to foot and ankle mechanics, although the underlying mechanisms differed. In basketball, high-intensity cutting, explosive jumps, and dynamic landings are commonly accompanied by subtalar pronation, increased calcaneal eversion, reduced plantarflexion control, altered inversion mechanics, and stiff landing patterns. § Together, these factors contribute to increased dynamic valgus and elevated knee loading, thereby heightening ACL injury risk. 10

Soccer presented a similar proportion of studies associating distal segment mechanics with ACL injury risk, yet the primary concerns often involved toe direction and rotational alignment during rapid changes of direction. These findings likely reflect the combined influence of cleated footwear, variable turf conditions, and frequent acceleration and deceleration cycles inherent to the sport. Several studies specifically link decreased ankle dorsiflexion flexibility in female soccer players to greater peak external knee abduction moments, increased knee abduction angles, and reduced knee flexion during landing, all of which elevate ACL risk.14,21,30,38,43,48,56,57 Additionally, toe-in landing positions have been associated with significantly higher knee abduction, tibial internal rotation, hip internal rotation, and hip adduction angles compared with toe-out positions, indicating increased ligament strain during cutting manuevers.14,21,30,38,43,48,56,57

Handball revealed a more heterogeneous pattern, with some findings indicating risk factors while others suggested protective biomechanical adaptations, potentially due to sport-specific training that emphasizes unilateral landing control and repeated jump-throw actions.4,30,31,41,43,56 In less represented sports such as rugby, volleyball, and ice hockey, available studies continue to suggest that foot and ankle factors contribute to ACL risk, though small sample sizes limit definitive conclusions. Furthermore, many studies failed to specify the sport or athletic background of participants, restricting the ability to fully differentiate general from sport-specific mechanisms.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. The included studies showed significant heterogeneity in design, methodology, and outcome reporting, which limited the ability to compare results or conduct pooled analyses. Inconsistent definitions and measurements of foot and ankle biomechanics, foot strike patterns, and toe positioning contributed to variability across findings. The inclusion of both direct and indirect studies provided a broad overview, but indirect studies may not accurately represent actual ACL injury risk, reducing the precision of certain associations. Most studies were noncomparative and lower level in quality, with modest MINORS scores, limiting the strength of the overall evidence. There was also limited representation of sex- and sport-specific analyses, with few studies directly comparing subgroups. This lack of representation restricts the generalizability of findings across diverse populations. Overall, while this review highlights important trends, the current literature remains underdeveloped. Future high-quality, comparative studies with standardized methods are needed to clarify the role of foot and ankle factors in ACL injury risk.

Conclusion

Foot and ankle factors, including prior injury, alignment, foot strike patterns, and toe progression, contribute meaningfully to ACL injury risk, yet their precise influence on knee stabilization remains unclear. The foot and ankle are understudied, with few investigations addressing their role in dynamic tasks that challenge knee stability. High-quality comparative research that accounts for sex and sport differences is needed to inform targeted prevention and improve risk stratification.
