Dennis Gross

Abstract
. Aging bridge networks require proactive, verifiable, and interpretable maintenance strategies, yet reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained solely on reward signals provide no formal safety guarantees and remain opaque to infrastructure managers. We demonstrate COOL-MC as a tool for verifying and explaining RL policies for multi-bridge network maintenance, building on a single-bridge Markov decision process (MDP) from the literature and extending it to a parallel network of three heterogeneous bridges with a shared periodic budget constraint, encoded in the PRISM modeling language. We train an RL agent on this MDP and apply probabilistic model checking and explainability methods to the induced discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC) , which arises from the interaction of the learned policy with the underlying MDP. Probabilistic model checking reveals that the trained policy has a safety-violation probability of 3.5% over the planning horizon, being slightly above the theoretical minimum of 0% and indicating the suboptimality of the learned policy, noting that these results are based on artificially constructed transition probabilities and deterioration rates rather than real-world data, so absolute performance figures should be interpreted with caution. The explainability analysis further reveals, for instance, a systematic bias in the trained policy toward the state of bridge 1 over the remaining bridges in the network. These results demonstrate COOL-MC ’s ability to provide formal, interpretable, and practical analysis of RL maintenance policies.



1.
Introduction

Transportation infrastructure forms the backbone of modern society, enabling the movement
of people, goods, and services that sustain economic growth and quality of life
[
13
]
.
Among the most critical yet vulnerable components of road and rail networks, bridges are subject to accelerating deterioration, deferred maintenance, and insufficient inspection regimes
[
42
,
133
,
123
]
.
The consequences are severe and well-documented: collapses such as the I-35W bridge in Minneapolis (2007) and the Morandi Bridge in Genoa (2018) have claimed dozens of lives, while incidents such as the Carola Bridge in Dresden (2024) serve as stark reminders that failure can strike without warning
[
17
,
25
,
24
]
.
All of these cases are symptoms of a global problem: across the world, a significant share of bridges are approaching or exceeding their design life, with repair backlogs running into the hundreds of billions of dollars
[
42
,
37
]
.
Addressing this challenge requires systematic, proactive strategies for managing bridge networks throughout their lifecycles
[
127
]
.

In a world where resources are limited, it is necessary to adopt rational maintenance management approaches to support decision-makers in prioritising interventions across bridge portfolios
[
42
]
.
One principled framework for this are
Markov decision process models (MDPs)
, which are frequently employed in bridge maintenance
[
40
,
102
,
113
,
106
,
124
,
58
,
92
,
115
,
96
]
: at each time step,
a decision-maker observes the current condition of the bridge network, selects maintenance actions, and receives a reward reflecting the trade-off between intervention costs and structural survival, while each bridge transitions to a new condition state according to probabilistic deterioration dynamics
[
12
]
.
Unfortunately, solving MDPs optimally via
probabilistic model checking
, which verifies policy guarantees by exhaustively analyzing the full MDP
[
12
]
, becomes computationally intractable as the number of structures grows, since the joint state and action space expands exponentially, a challenge known as the
curse of dimensionality

[
84
]
.

Reinforcement learning (RL)
addresses this challenge by training an agent to learn a maintenance policy through repeated interactions with a simulated deterioration MDP
[
108
,
93
]
: the agent observes the bridge network state, decides which bridges to repair, or replace within the available budget, and receives a reward signal that incentivizes long-term structural performance.
Modern RL algorithms represent this policy using a
neural network (NN)
, enabling the agent to generalize across large MDPs
[
93
]
.

However, RL policies can exhibit
unsafe behavior

[
43
]

such as replacing fully functional bridges, as reward signals are often insufficient to capture complex structural
safety requirements
[
124
]
.
NN-based policies are additionally
hard to interpret
, as the internal complexity of NNs hides the reasoning behind each maintenance decision
[
15
]
.
In the bridge maintenance setting, this opacity is particularly problematic: infrastructure managers need to understand
why
a policy recommends a given action for a specific bridge and whether it appropriately accounts for the broader network’s condition.
Without such understanding, adoption of RL-based maintenance policies in practice remains challenging
[
121
,
15
]
.

The tool
COOL-MC
addresses both of these challenges by combining RL with probabilistic model checking and explainability
[
43
,
49
,
47
,
46
]
: After learning a policy via RL, it constructs only the reachable state space induced by that policy, resolving all nondeterminism and yielding a
discrete-time Markov
chain (DTMC)
amenable to probabilistic model checking and explainable RL methods
[
43
]
.
By constructing only the reachable states under the trained policy rather than enumerating the full state space, this approach mitigates the curse of dimensionality
that renders classical methods such as full-MDP model checking intractable
[
43
]
.

To date, however,
COOL-MC
’s capabilities
[
43
,
52
,
51
]
have not been applied to the verification and explanation of RL policies for multi-bridge network maintenance.

In this paper, we encode a network of three heterogeneous bridges as an MDP, extending the single-bridge formulation of Wei et al.
[
125
]
to a parallel network with a shared periodic budget constraint and the full ten-point NBI condition scale.
We train an RL agent on this MDP and apply
COOL-MC
to the induced DTMC
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
to carry out a policy analysis spanning probabilistic model checking
[
43
]
, feature-level explainability
[
49
]
, action-level behavioral profiling
[
49
]
, and counterfactual what-if analysis
[
46
]
.
Probabilistic model checking reveals, for instance, that the trained policy has a safety-violation probability of 3.5% over the 20-year planning horizon, being slightly above the theoretical minimum of 0%, with the caveat that these results are based on artificially constructed transition probabilities and deterioration rates rather than real-world data.
The explainability analysis further uncovers, for instance, that the trained policy is biased toward the state of bridge 1, attributing it significantly more importance than the other bridges in the network.

Our main contribution
is the demonstration of
COOL-MC
as a tool for multi-bridge network maintenance, combining RL with probabilistic model checking and explainability to provide a formal analysis of a bridge network maintenance RL policy, including verified safety and performance properties, feature-level explanations, action-level behavioral profiling, and counterfactual what-if analysis of the trained RL maintenance policy, thereby showcasing
COOL-MC
’s capabilities for maintenance tasks.

Related Work

Maintaining a bridge network in a safe condition requires, as a first step, reliable knowledge of the condition of each structure.
Bridge condition assessment in the USA is governed by the National Bridge Inventory, which requires inspectors to rate each bridge on a 0-9 integer scale, where a rating of 7 or above classifies the structure as being in good condition, ratings of 5 or 6 as fair, and a rating of 4 or below on any primary component as poor
[

[

Bridge condition assessment relies on a range of inspection methods, from routine visual inspections and hands-on tactile examination to advanced non-destructive evaluation techniques such as ground-penetrating radar, acoustic emission monitoring, and unmanned aerial vehicle surveys
[

Empirical studies have nonetheless demonstrated that even trained inspectors exhibit considerable inter-inspector variability, with condition ratings for the same component differing by multiple scale points across inspectors

3.
Background

First, we introduce
Markov decision processes (MDPs)
as the formal framework for sequential decision-making. We then describe probabilistic model checking for verifying policy properties, RL, and different explainability methods for explaining their RL policy decisions.

3.1.
Probabilistic Systems

A
probability distribution
over a set
\displaystyle X
is a function
\displaystyle\mu\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
with
\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)=1
. The set of all distributions on
\displaystyle X
is
\displaystyle Distr(X)
.

Definition 1

(MDP)
.

A
Markov decision process (MDP)
is a tuple
\displaystyle M=(S,s_{0},Act,Tr,rew,\\
AP,L)

where
\displaystyle S
is a finite, nonempty set of states;
\displaystyle s_{0}\in S
is an initial state;
\displaystyle Act
is a finite set of actions;
\displaystyle Tr\colon S\times Act\rightarrow Distr(S)
is a partial probability transition function and
\displaystyle Tr(s,a,s^{\prime})
denotes the probability of transitioning from state
\displaystyle s
to state
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
when action
\displaystyle a
is taken;

\displaystyle rew\colon S\times Act\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a reward function;

\displaystyle AP
is a set of atomic propositions;

\displaystyle L\colon S\rightarrow 2^{AP}
is a labeling function.

An
agent
interacts with the MDP by observing the current state and selecting actions to maximize cumulative reward. The agent’s behavior is governed by a
policy
.

We represent each state
\displaystyle s\in S
as a vector of
\displaystyle d
features
\displaystyle(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
, where
\displaystyle f_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The available actions in
\displaystyle s\in S
are
\displaystyle Act(s)=\{a\in Act\mid Tr(s,a)\neq\bot\}
where
\displaystyle Tr(s,a)=\bot
means that action
\displaystyle a
is not available in state
\displaystyle s
.
In our setting, we assume that all actions are available at all states.

Definition 2

(DTMC)
.

A
discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC)
is a tuple
\displaystyle D=(S,s_{0},Tr,AP,L)

where
\displaystyle S
,
\displaystyle s_{0}
,
\displaystyle AP
, and
\displaystyle L
are as in Definition
1
,
and
\displaystyle Tr\colon S\rightarrow Distr(S)
is a probability transition function.

We write
\displaystyle Tr(s,s^{\prime})
to denote the probability of transitioning from state
\displaystyle s
to state
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
under
\displaystyle Tr
.

In many practical settings, the agent does not have direct access to the underlying state
\displaystyle s\in S
of the MDP. Instead, the agent receives an observation that may represent a transformed view of the true state. We formalize this through an observation function.

Definition 3

(Observation)
.

We define the observation function
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\colon S\rightarrow O
as a function that maps a state
\displaystyle s\in S
to an observation
\displaystyle o\in O
.
An observation
\displaystyle o\in O
is a vector of features
\displaystyle(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
where
\displaystyle f_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The observed features may differ from the exact state features.

A policy operates on observations rather than on the underlying states directly.
In our setting, the environment is fully observable, so the observation function
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
maps each state feature directly to its corresponding observation feature, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
is the identity on the state features.

Definition 4
.

A
memoryless deterministic policy
\displaystyle\pi
for an MDP
\displaystyle M
is a function
\displaystyle\pi\colon O\rightarrow Act
that maps an observation
\displaystyle o\in O
to action
\displaystyle a\in Act
.

Applying a policy
\displaystyle\pi
to an MDP
\displaystyle M
with observation function
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
yields an
induced DTMC

\displaystyle D^{\pi}
where all non-determinism is resolved: for each state
\displaystyle s
, the transition function becomes
\displaystyle Tr(s,s^{\prime})=Tr(s,\pi(\mathbb{O}(s)),s^{\prime})
.
The induced DTMC fully characterizes the observable behavior of the policy: the states visited, the transitions taken, and the probabilities of all outcomes.
The interaction between the policy and environment is depicted in Figure
2
.

Figure 2
.
Sequential decision-making loop. The agent receives an observation and reward from the environment, selects an action according to its policy, and the environment transitions to a new state.

3.2.
Probabilistic Model Checking

Probabilistic model checking
enables the verification of quantitative properties of stochastic systems.

COOL-MC
uses
Storm

[
59
]
internally as its model checker, which can verify properties of both MDPs and DTMCs.
Among the most fundamental properties are
reachability
queries, which assess the probability of a system reaching a particular state.
For example, one might say: “The reachability probability of reaching an unsafe state is 0.1.”

The general workflow for model checking is as follows (see also Figure
3
).
First, the system is formally modeled using a language such as
PRISM

[
99
]
.
Next, the property of interest is formalized in a temporal logic.
Using these inputs, the model checker verifies whether the property holds or computes the relevant probability.

Figure 3
.
General model checking workflow
[
59
]
. The system is formally modeled, the requirements are formalized, and both are input to a model checker such as
Storm
, which verifies the property.

The
PRISM
language
[
99
]
describes probabilistic systems as a collection of
modules
, each containing typed
variables
and guarded
commands
.
A state of the system is a valuation of all variables, and the state space
\displaystyle S
is the set of all possible valuations.
Each command takes the form

\texttt{[action]}\ g\rightarrow\lambda_{1}:u_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}:u_{n}

where
\displaystyle g
is a Boolean guard over the variables, each
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}
is a probability, and each
\displaystyle u_{j}
is an update that assigns new values to the variables.
When the system is in a state satisfying
\displaystyle g
, the command can be executed: update
\displaystyle u_{j}
is applied with probability
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}
, transitioning the system to a new state.
For an MDP, each enabled command in a state corresponds to a distinct probabilistic choice, and the nondeterministic selection among enabled commands is resolved by a policy.
Listing
1
shows a simplified MDP in
PRISM
.
The model declares a single module with a state variable
s
ranging over integer identifiers.
Each command specifies a transition: a guard on the current state, an action label, and a probabilistic update.
For instance, from state
\displaystyle s=0
, taking action
a0
leads to state
\displaystyle s=1
with probability
\displaystyle 0.7
and to state
\displaystyle s=2
with probability
\displaystyle 0.3
.
States
\displaystyle s=2
and
\displaystyle s=3
are absorbing terminal states corresponding to different labels.
A reward structure assigns a reward of
\displaystyle+1
to the
\displaystyle s=2
state.
Additionally,
PRISM
supports
atomic labels
that annotate states with propositions.
These labels are defined using
label
declarations, which assign a name to a Boolean condition over the state variables.
For example,
label "goal" = s=2;
marks all states where
\displaystyle s=2
with the proposition
goal
.
Such labels serve as the atomic propositions referenced in PCTL properties.

COOL-MC
allows automated user-specified state labeling of
PRISM
models.
For a complete formal treatment of the
PRISM
semantics, we refer to
[
99
]
.

Properties are specified using PCTL
[
56
]
, a branching-time temporal logic for reasoning about probabilities over paths.
In this paper, we use two path operators.
The
eventually
operator
\displaystyle\lozenge\,\varphi
(also written
\displaystyle F\,\varphi
) states that
\displaystyle\varphi
holds at some future state along a path.
The
until
operator
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\;\mathcal{U}\;\varphi_{2}
states that
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}
holds at every state along a path until a state is reached where
\displaystyle\varphi_{2}
holds.
A bounded variant
\displaystyle\lozenge^{\leq B}\,\varphi
restricts the eventually operator to paths of at most
\displaystyle B
steps.
A PCTL property has the form

P_{\sim p}(\psi)

where
\displaystyle\psi
is a path formula (such as
\displaystyle\lozenge\,\varphi
or
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\;\mathcal{U}\;\varphi_{2}
),
\displaystyle\sim
is a comparison operator (
\displaystyle<
,
\displaystyle\leq
,
\displaystyle\geq
,
\displaystyle>
), and
\displaystyle p\in[0,1]
is a probability threshold.
Beyond checking whether a property is satisfied,
Storm
can compute the exact probability, denoted
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\psi)
.
For example,
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 200}\;\textit{empty})
computes the exact probability of reaching a stockout within 200 steps.

3.3.
Reinforcement Learning

The standard goal of RL is to learn a policy
\displaystyle\pi
for an MDP that maximizes the expected accumulated discounted reward
[
15
]

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{N}\gamma^{t}\cdot rew(s_{t},a_{t})\right],

where
\displaystyle\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor,
\displaystyle rew(s_{t},a_{t})
is the reward at time step
\displaystyle t
, and
\displaystyle N
is the episode length.

3.4.
Explainability Methods

Explainability methods aim to make trained RL policies understandable
[
91
]
.
Global methods interpret the overall policy’s behavior
[
110
]
, while local methods explain a policy’s decision-making in individual states.
In this work, we use four complementary methods:
feature lumping
,
gradient-based saliency ranking
,
action labeling
, and
counterfactual action replacement
, each combined with PCTL model checking on the induced DTMC.

Feature lumping
[
43
]
provides a global sensitivity analysis by coarsening the observation space.
For a selected feature
\displaystyle f_{i}
, exact values are replaced by binned representatives so that the memoryless deterministic policy
\displaystyle\pi
operates on a reduced observation
\displaystyle o\in O
.
The induced DTMC is reconstructed under the lumped policy on the original MDP
\displaystyle M
, and the PCTL query of interest is re-computed.
A negligible change in the verified probability indicates that
\displaystyle\pi
is robust to coarsened information about
\displaystyle f_{i}
; a large change reveals that precise resolution of that feature is safety-critical.

Gradient-based saliency
[
105
]
complements lumping with a local, per-state measure of feature relevance.
For each state
\displaystyle s
with observation
\displaystyle o=\mathbb{O}(s)
, the saliency of feature
\displaystyle f_{i}
is
\displaystyle\lvert\partial\pi(o)/\partial f_{i}\rvert
, the absolute gradient of the policy output with respect to the
\displaystyle i
-th input.
Aggregating these scores over all visited states yields a global feature-importance ranking, and conditional rankings can be computed over restricted subsets of
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
.

Action labeling annotates each state in the induced DTMC with the joint maintenance action selected by the trained policy
[
52
]
.
These labels serve as atomic propositions that are referenceable in PCTL, enabling formal queries directly over the policy’s maintenance behavior.

Counterfactual action replacement modifies the policy by substituting one action with another across all states where it is selected, then reconstructs and re-verifies the induced DTMC
[
46
]
.
This enables targeted what-if analysis that isolates the effect of specific maintenance choices on safety outcomes without retraining the policy.

4.
Methodology

Our methodology proceeds in four stages.
First, we encode the multi-bridge network maintenance problem as an MDP in the

PRISM
modeling language, defining states, actions, transition dynamics,
and reward structure that capture the joint maintenance planning problem
(§
4.1
).
Second, we train a deep RL policy using PPO to learn a maintenance strategy that
maximizes structural survival while minimizing intervention costs
(§
4.2
).
Third, we verify the trained policy on its induced DTMC by applying PCTL queries
to assess safety properties such as bridge failure probability and budget utilization
(§
4.3
).
Fourth, we explain the policy’s decision-making through feature lumping,
gradient-based saliency ranking, action labeling, and counterfactual action
replacement, combined with PCTL queries on the induced DTMC
(§
4.4
).

4.1.
PRISM MDP Encoding

We encode the multi-bridge network maintenance problem as an MDP

\displaystyle M
in the PRISM language.
The formulation is inspired by the single-component bridge MDP
of Wei et al.
[
125
]
with minor differences
(§
4.1.5
), adapting their deterioration model to
a parallel network of three bridges with a shared periodic budget
constraint and extending the condition representation to the full
ten-point NBI scale.

4.1.1.
State space

Each state
\displaystyle s\in S
is a feature vector
\displaystyle(f_{1},\dots,f_{7})

4.1.2.
Actions

The action set
\displaystyle Act
consists of all joint maintenance decisions
over the three bridges.
Per bridge, four actions are available:
Do Nothing (
dn

Minor Maintenance (
mn
, cost 1),
Major Maintenance (
mj
, cost 2), and
Replacement (
rp
, cost 4).
The joint action space is the Cartesian product, giving

\displaystyle 4^{3}=64
candidate tuples.
Throughout this paper, we index the four per-bridge actions as
\displaystyle 0,1,2,3
corresponding to
dn
,
mn
,
mj
, and

A joint action over the three bridges is written
a
\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle k_{3}

\displaystyle k_{i}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
denotes the action applied to bridge
\displaystyle i
. For example,
a1_1_2
denotes Minor Maintenance on bridges 1 and 2 and Major Maintenance on bridge 3.
The available actions in state
\displaystyle s
are

\displaystyle Act(s)=\{a\in Act\mid Tr(s,a)\neq\bot\}
; here
\displaystyle Tr(s,a)=\bot
whenever the total joint cost exceeds the remaining budget, enforcing the resource constraint.
With
\displaystyle B_{\max}=10
, this yields
63 feasible joint actions
per non-terminal state.
If the trained RL policy selects an unavailable action, the simulator falls back to the first available action in lexicographic order.

The budget feature evolves atomically with each transition.
At the end of every four-year cycle (
\displaystyle\mathit{cycle\_year}=3
),
the budget reloads to
\displaystyle B_{\max}
before the current cost is
deducted (unspent budget does not carry over).

4.1.3.
Transition function

Each bridge transitions independently under

\displaystyle Tr\colon S\times Act\rightarrow Distr(S)
.
Under
dn
, condition deteriorates by at most one level
per year, following a stochastic matrix.
The stay probabilities decrease with condition severity,
reflecting accelerating deterioration.

4.1.4.
Reward function

The reward function
\displaystyle rew\colon S\times Act\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

encodes a survival-with-efficiency objective.
At each non-terminal transition the agent receives a reward
penalised by the normalised joint maintenance cost:

rew(s,a)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\frac{c(a)}{C_{\max}}&\text{if }s^{\prime}\text{ is not terminal,}\\[6.0pt]
0&\text{if }s^{\prime}\text{ is terminal,}\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle c(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{cost}(a_{i})
is the total
joint action cost and
\displaystyle C_{\max}=3\times 4=12
is the
maximum possible cost.
The reward lies in
\displaystyle[0.5,\,1.0]
for all feasible actions
(since
\displaystyle c\leq B_{\max}=10<C_{\max}
), ensuring the agent
is always incentivised to keep bridges alive while preferring
cheaper actions.
The absorbing self-loop at terminal states carries zero reward,
so the RL objective

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{N}\gamma^{t}\cdot rew(s_{t},a_{t})]

is dominated by episode length
\displaystyle N
.

Atomic Propositions and Labeling Function

The labeling function
\displaystyle L\colon S\rightarrow 2^{AP}
annotates states with atomic propositions from
\displaystyle AP
, referenced in PCTL properties.
Key propositions include
"failed"
(any bridge at NBI 0),

"any_serious"
(any bridge at NBI
\displaystyle\leq 3
),

"any_critical"
(any bridge at NBI
\displaystyle\leq 2
),

"all_good"
(all bridges at NBI
\displaystyle\geq 7
),

"budget_empty"
(remaining budget is zero), and

"cycle_end"
(last year of the four-year budget cycle).

4.1.5.
Differences from Wei et al. (2019)

Our MDP draws on the bridge component formulation of
Wei et al.
[
125
]
but departs from it in
several respects.
The transition probabilities for
conditions 4–7 are anchored to the stay-cable values of Wei
et al. and are the only numerical values carried over unchanged.
The extension to the full NBI scale adds states at both ends:
conditions 8–9 (Very Good, Excellent) with slow deterioration
reflecting newly built or recently rehabilitated structures, and
conditions 1–3 (Imminent Failure, Critical, Serious) with
rapidly accelerating deterioration approaching the absorbing
Failed state 0.
The most consequential departure is
\displaystyle rew(s,a)
: where Wei et al. minimise a composite cost model, we use a normalised survival
reward aligned with COOL-MC’s RL interface, making episode length

\displaystyle N
the dominant signal in the RL objective.
The budget constraint makes
\displaystyle Act(s)\subsetneq Act
for
budget-constrained states, a source of nondeterminism absent in
Wei et al. and essential for modeling realistic network-level
resource allocation.
Finally, Wei et al. model the internal component structure of a
single bridge (deck panels, stay cables, etc.), whereas we model
a
network
of three structurally independent bridges, each
abstracted to a single NBI condition state.

Summary

The encoded

\displaystyle\displaystyle S=\{0,\ldots,9\}^{3}\times\{0,\ldots,B_{\max}\}\times\{0,1,2,3\}\times\{0,\ldots,T_{\max}\}\times\{\mathit{true},\mathit{false}\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathit{Act}=\{\textsc{dn},\textsc{mn},\textsc{mj},\textsc{rp}\}^{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathit{Act}(s)=\bigl\{a\in\mathit{Act}\mid c(a)\leq\mathit{budget}\bigr\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle rew(s,a)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{c(a)}{C_{\max}}&\text{if }s^{\prime}\text{ is not terminal,}\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{if }s^{\prime}\text{ is terminal,}\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle c(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{cost}(a_{i})
is the total joint action
cost,
\displaystyle C_{\max}=3\times 4=12
, and a state
\displaystyle s
is terminal if

\displaystyle\mathit{cond\_bi}=0
for some
\displaystyle i
or
\displaystyle\mathit{year}=T_{\max}

4.2.
RL Policy Training

We train a deep RL policy
\displaystyle\pi
on the multi-bridge network MDP using
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
108
]
.
The policy network is a feedforward neural network with four fully connected
hidden layers of 512 neurons each.
The agent is trained for 10,000 episodes with a learning rate of

\displaystyle\alpha=3\times 10^{-4}
, discount factor
\displaystyle\gamma=0.99
, batch size 64,
and a planning horizon of
\displaystyle T_{\max}=20
years.
The budget cap is
\displaystyle B_{\max}=10
with a reload period of four years.

4.3.
Policy Verification

For the trained policy
\displaystyle\pi
,
COOL-MC
constructs the induced DTMC
\displaystyle D^{\pi}

by exploring only the reachable state space under
\displaystyle\pi

(see Algorithm
1
).
Starting from the initial state
\displaystyle s_{0}
, the construction proceeds as a
depth-first traversal.
At each visited state
\displaystyle s

\displaystyle a=\pi(s)\in Act(s)
, and all successor states
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
with

\displaystyle Tr(s,a,s^{\prime})>0
are added to the induced DTMC along with their
transition probabilities.
The procedure recurses into each unvisited successor that is relevant for
the property under verification.
This incremental approach offers two key advantages.
First, it constructs only the fragment of the full MDP that is actually
reachable under
\displaystyle\pi

joint state space, directly mitigating the curse of dimensionality.
Second, it yields an induced DTMC rather than an MDP, since all
nondeterminism is resolved by
\displaystyle\pi

the verification complexity.

4.4.
Policy Explanation

Verification alone establishes
what
the policy does but not
why
it makes particular maintenance decisions.
To address this, we apply the four explainability methods introduced in Section
3.4
: feature lumping, gradient-based saliency ranking, action labeling, and counterfactual action replacement.
For feature lumping, we remap
\displaystyle\mathit{cond\_b1}
to three coarsened bins (conditions 0–3 to 2, conditions 4–6 to 5, and conditions 7–9 to 7) and re-verify the failure probability on the resulting induced DTMC, revealing whether precise NBI ratings for bridge 1 are safety-critical or whether a coarse categorical assessment suffices.
For gradient-based saliency, we compute a global feature importance ranking by aggregating per-state gradients across all reachable states of
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
, and additionally compute conditional rankings restricted to subsets of
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
where each bridge in turn is in poor condition (
\displaystyle\mathit{cond\_bi}\in[0,3]
) while the others are in good condition, revealing whether
\displaystyle\pi
correctly shifts attention to the most deteriorated structure.
For action labeling, we annotate each state of
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
with the joint maintenance action selected by
\displaystyle\pi
and verify PCTL properties directly over the policy’s maintenance behavior, characterizing which actions dominate and whether specific actions are ever chosen.
For counterfactual action replacement, we globally replace actions with other actions across
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
and re-verify the policy behaviour.

5.2.6.
Horizon-gaming detection

To detect horizon-gaming behaviour (where the policy exploits knowledge of the finite time horizon by adopting a different strategy near the end of the budget cycle, years 16–19) in
\displaystyle\pi
, we remap the

\displaystyle\pi
always believes it is in the final budget cycle (years 16–19).
Concretely, years
\displaystyle 0,4,8,12,16
are mapped to 16; years
\displaystyle 1,5,9,13,17
to 17; and so forth. Under this perturbation,
\displaystyle\pi
always operates as if the episode is ending imminently. If
\displaystyle\pi
reduces maintenance effort near the planning horizon, since any bridge failure occurring at
\displaystyle\mathit{year}\geq T_{\max}
is never observed within the episode, this perturbation will reveal such behaviour through an elevated failure probability on the induced DTMC. Under the horizon remap,
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge\;\texttt{"failed"})=0.07535
, compared to the baseline of
\displaystyle 0.0355
(other budget cycles are the same as baseline).
This provides formal evidence that
\displaystyle\pi
has learned horizon-dependent behaviour: when
\displaystyle\pi
believes the episode is always ending soon, it adopts a policy that allows bridges to deteriorate more than under correct temporal awareness, resulting in a substantially elevated failure probability as verified on
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
. This constitutes a form of reward hacking
[
60
]
:
\displaystyle\pi
learns to reduce maintenance costs near the end of the planning horizon because the episode-length incentive in
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{N}\gamma^{t}\cdot rew(s_{t},a_{t})]

6.
Discussion

COOL-MC
provides three capabilities that are inaccessible from
training curves or episode returns alone: formal verification, explainability, and a mix of both.

For instance, formal verification confirmed that the trained PPO policy maintains a

\displaystyle 3.55\%
bridge failure probability.
These are exact, model-checked guarantees, not empirical estimates from
rollouts.

Explainability analysis revealed structural properties of
\displaystyle\pi
that training metrics hide entirely.
For instance, the policy exhibits a structural attention bias toward bridge 1, attending to its condition regardless of which bridge has actually deteriorated most, a coverage gap that motivates retraining with a permutation-invariant architecture.

Counterfactual analysis established that escalating all minor maintenance to major maintenance raises budget exhaustion, quantifying how much the policy’s conservative spending depends on cheap interventions, and that replacing
rp
with
mj
globally leaves the failure probability unchanged at
\displaystyle 3.55\%
, confirming that full replacement is entirely dispensable at no safety cost.

The induced DTMC construction is itself a practical advantage:
\displaystyle|D^{\pi}|
is smaller than the full MDP, making formal verification more
tractable and increasingly valuable as the network scales.

The analyses above do more than characterize the trained policy: they directly inform a principled refinement loop in which verified behavioral anomalies motivate targeted changes to the MDP.
For instance, the feature-lumping result suggests that the full ten-point NBI scale can be safely coarsened to three or five levels, substantially reducing
\displaystyle|S|
and accelerating both training and verification without measurable safety loss.
The conditional saliency bias toward bridge 1 motivates either a worst-bridge penalty reward or a permutation-invariant policy architecture, with the desired outcome that
\displaystyle\mathit{cond\_bi}
ranks first in saliency whenever bridge
\displaystyle i
is worst.
After each such modification,
COOL-MC
provides the formal apparatus to re-verify the refined policy and confirm that the anomaly has been resolved, turning the analysis cycle into an iterative design methodology: train
\displaystyle\to
verify
\displaystyle\to
explain
\displaystyle\to
refine.

A broader implication of this work concerns the relationship between the training environment and the verification model.
Even when an RL policy is trained in a model-free setting on a large or continuous environment, constructing a smaller discrete MDP that captures the essential structure of that environment is sufficient for policy debugging via
COOL-MC
. The formal guarantees and explainability findings apply to the modeled MDP, but the behavioral anomalies they reveal, such as attention bias or horizon gaming, are properties of the policy itself and potentially carry over to similar environments.
A small, interpretable PRISM model therefore serves as a lightweight debugging harness for policies trained at any scale.

7.
Conclusion

We demonstrated
COOL-MC
as a tool for verifying and explaining
RL policies for multi-bridge network maintenance.
Formal verification confirmed that the policy maintains a conservative
budget strategy, and explainability analysis revealed cycle-aware
spending behavior and a dependence on low-cost minor maintenance.
Several directions merit further investigation.
The transition probabilities and reward structure used here are synthetic; co-designing the MDP directly with bridge engineers would ground the model in inspection data and domain knowledge, strengthening the practical relevance of the verified guarantees.
