1.0 Executive Summary
The project is located on Tribal trust land along Reese Rd in Colusa, CA and expands existing medium-
voltage distribution to seven new households with the addition of medium-voltage cabling, step down
transformers, smart meters, and street lighting for the new development. The Tribe utilized their existing
Co-Generation power plant and micro grid to supply the new homes with highly reliable power with
fewer interruptions and at a reduced rate compared with the local utility. The project gives the Tribe
greater independence from traditional power sources and provides jobs for the community. The project
reduces electric energy costs, increases Tribe self-reliance, and provides highly reliable electric power
to seven tribal members’ homes. Construction of the project also provided jobs for the community.

2.0 Background
The Colusa Indian Community Council (CICC) is the governing body of the Cachil Dehe Band of Wintun
Indians. CICC as a Tribal Nation Government determines the tribe’s individual, economic, judicial, social,
political, cultural and community structures. The CICC determines the roles and responsibilities of the
CICC Departments and structure. The CICC Executive Committee is elected by Tribal Council and is the
Governing Board of the Colusa Indian Community Council.
On November 23, 1941, the Constitution and By-Laws for the Cachil Dehe Band of Wintun Indians of
the Colusa Indian Community were ratified and adopted by the tribe’s original 45 members. At the time
of ratification, the Colusa Indian Community resided in the heart of Northern California’s agricultural
land on an 80-acre reservation. This original site stretched along the bountiful Sacramento River, about
four miles north of the Colusa city limits on Highway 45. Two years later, the tribe’s land increased an
additional 210 acres just one mile south of the original reservation. It is at this location where the
current Colusa Casino Resort, Cachil Dehe Village complex, and Cogeneration Plant stand today. It is
from these beginnings that the Colusa Indian Community has grown and prospered to its current 150+
members.

The Tribe continues to work towards economic self-sufficiency through development of vital
infrastructure projects like this one, to ensure future growth and financial prosperity. The Tribe’s long
term energy goal is to continue to strengthen its energy infrastructure and thus increase self-reliance,
save energy costs, and enhance overall tribal sovereignty.

3.0 Project Objectives
The overall project goal was to provide less expensive and more reliable electric service to the tribe for
the seven new households and street lighting.

The project included the following specific goals:
 1. Provide power to seven new homes.
 2. Generate one new, permanent, full-time job for the community.

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
The overall project scope included the design, procurement, and installation of the following major
components:
 • Expansion of 12.47kV underground distribution system
 • Low-voltage service to seven households and street lighting, including meters
 • Advanced meter reading / advanced metering infrastructure

The project was coordinated and phased to minimize disruption of existing electrical service. Various
components were completed, commissioned and put in service before the entire project was complete.
This ensured that any issues with various components were resolved without jeopardizing the overall
success of the project. For example, medium-voltage distribution lines were installed, tested and
energized before service to individual households was established. Individual loads or households
were added to the tribe-owned distribution one at a time initially to resolve any issues without affecting
the entire community.

Finally, automatic meter reading using advanced metering infrastructure was implemented. Before this
automation was fully implemented, manual meter reading was used to track energy usage.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
This project was a great success for the Tribe. This project has allowed more of the Tribe’s members to
enjoy electricity supplied by their own cogeneration plant, via their own micro-grid at a less expensive
rate than the local utility. This power is also ultra-reliable and backed up by a 4 megavolt amperes
(MVA) uninterruptible power supply (UPS) system, unlike the local utility’s power, which is delivered to
the reservation at the end of a very weak circuit with historically very high outage rates.

Tribal members now pay their own Tribal Utility Authority $0.22/kWh vs. the average PG&E rate of
$0.44/kW. The additional load to the microgrid from these new homes was nominal and so no
additional generating capacity was required.

6.0 Lessons Learned
The primary challenge during project implementation arose from a late, post-award change in strategy
to serve the new homes across two separate sites rather than a single location. The Tribe’s housing
authority decided to relocate a portion of the new homes from the existing large housing development
to a newer, more remote area of the reservation. Despite the unexpected nature of this shift, the Office
of Indian Energy was highly accommodating and worked closely with us to secure the necessary
approvals in time to modify the project scope to meet the Tribe’s needs.

Because this change occurred late in the project timeline, it required specifying different equipment,
identifying new conduit trenching routes, and substantially adjusting material orders. Ultimately, our
team successfully completed the project across both sites while keeping total costs below the original
budget. As a result, Tribal members in seven new homes now benefit from reliable, uninterrupted
power at approximately half the cost they would have incurred without this project.
