Convergence of Langevin AIS for multimodal distributions Thanks: This work has been partially supported by the National Science Foundation under grants DMS-2406853, DMS-2342349 and the Center for Nonlinear Analysis.
Akshat Agarwal, Gautam Iyer, Aidan Jameson, Seungjae Son, Wyatt Wimmer

Abstract
. We study convergence rates of the annealed importance sampling algorithm (Neal ’01) combined with Langevin Monte Carlo when the target is a multimodal Gibbs measure. The main result shows that for a fixed error threshold, the time complexity is quadratic in the inverse temperature . We identify a simple and useful quantity that controls the sampling error for AIS in a general setting, and then bound this quantity in our setting using spectral estimates. We also study an autonormalized version and obtain bounds for the time complexity in terms of the inverse temperature.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Results

We begin by stating our main results, following which we survey the literature and place our results in the context of the current literature.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a configuration space and
U\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be an energy function.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, the Gibbs measure with temperature
\varepsilon
is defined by

(1.1)

\pi_{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{\varepsilon}}{Z_{\varepsilon}}\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{\pi}_{\varepsilon}=e^{-U/\varepsilon}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\pi}_{\varepsilon}\,dx\,,

where the last integral is carried out with respect to some reference measure on
\mathcal{X}
.
We typically only consider the case where
\mathcal{X}
is the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
In these cases the reference measure in (
1.1
) is the Lebesgue measure.

Consider the overdamped Langevin equation

(1.2)

dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=-\nabla U(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,dB_{t}\,,

where
B
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
It is well known (see for instance
[
38
]
) that the stationary distribution of the Langevin equation is
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, and the
Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC)
algorithm obtains samples from
\pi_{\varepsilon}
by simulating (
1.2
) for long time.

The disadvantage of LMC is that when
U
is not convex, the rate at which solutions to (
1.2
) converge to the stationary distribution is exponentially small in
1/\varepsilon
.
This is the Arrhenius law
[
2
]
, and is described in more detail below.
We show that this slow rate of convergence can be overcome if one uses
annealed importance sampling (AIS)
[
34
]
, or an autonormalized version of AIS.

1.2.
Literature review

Sampling from distributions is a longstanding problem that arises in many applications such as Bayesian inference, statistical Physics and machine learning.
In many situations of practical interest, the state space
\mathcal{X}
is huge (either finite, but with a computationally intractable size, or a high dimensional manifold).
For any given state
x\in\mathcal{X}
, one can typically compute an energy
U(x)
measuring how favorable the state is.
Standard models (e.g. the canonical ensemble in statistical physics) dictate that the probability of finding the system in state
x
is proportional to
\tilde{\pi}_{\varepsilon}(x)=e^{-U(x)/\varepsilon}
(as in (
1.1
)), where
\varepsilon>0
is a parameter (often the absolute temperature) controlling how fast the system transitions between states.

Practically, the normalization constant
Z^{\varepsilon}
(often called the partition function) is a high dimensional integral (or a sum over huge number of states) and is computationally intractable.
Moreover, even if the normalization constant
Z^{\varepsilon}
is known, the enormous state space requires the use of algorithms that can deliver samples even though they can only inspect a miniscule fraction of all possible states.
Such algorithms aren’t easy to design, or rigorously analyze, and this makes such sampling problems extremely challenging.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a family of algorithms that is often used to address such problems.
These work by simulating a Markov process whose stationary distribution is
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, and date back to the celebrated Metropolis–Hastings algorithm
[
33
,
17
]
.
In Euclidean space the Langevin dynamics (
1.2
) provides a particularly convenient Markov process with stationary distribution
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, and this is the basis of
Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC)
,
Metropolis Adjusted Langevin Monte Carlo (MALA)
and various other sampling algorithms (see for instance
[
26
]
).

One issue that requires attention when using MCMC based algorithms is the rate at which the Markov chain converges to equilibrium.
If this rate is too slow, it may require simulating the Markov chain for impractical amounts of time before obtaining good samples.
In some cases, one has quantitative estimates on the rate of convergence.
For (
1.2
), it is known that if
U
is uniformly convex, then

(1.22)

W_{2}(\dist(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}),\pi_{\varepsilon})\leqslant e^{-C(U)t/\varepsilon}W_{2}(\dist(X^{\varepsilon}_{0}),\pi_{\varepsilon})\,,

which means that simulating (
1.2
) will yield good samples of the Gibbs measure in short time, even when the temperature
\varepsilon
is small.
Vempala and Wibisono
[
47
]
(see also
[
5
]
) showed that this is also true for the Euler–Maruyama discretization of (
1.2
), making LMC a practically viable sampling algorithm for log concave distributions even in high dimensions.

When
U
is not convex, however, the situation is different.
If
U
is multi-modal (for instance, if
\pi_{1}
is a Gaussian mixture), then the convergence rate of (
1.2
) is exponentially small in
1/\varepsilon
.
That is, it takes time
e^{O(1/\varepsilon)}
for the distribution of
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
to become close to
\pi_{\varepsilon}
.
This phenomenon is known as the Arrhenius law
[
2
]
, and occurs for the following reason.
Since the drift in (
1.2
) pulls trajectories towards local minima of
U
, the noise term in (
1.2
) has to go against the drift for an
O(1)
amount of time in order for trajectories to transition from the basin of attraction of one local minimum to another.
This happens with exponentially small probability, leading to the Arrhenius law.

Several methods have been designed to improve the rate of convergence.
Tempering methods introduced in
[
45
,
36
,
29
,
35
]

run a Markov chain on a product of the state space at various temperature levels.
Other methods include ideas based on
birth-death
[
27
]
,
optimization
[
39
]
,
diffusion models
[
4
]
,
warm starts
[
20
,
24
]
.
Authors have also modified (
1.2
) by adding a drift
[
40
,
9
,
7
]
,
or modifying the diffusion
[
11
]
.
In some situations
[
48
,
13
,
44
]

polynomial convergence bounds have been rigorously proved.

The methods most closely related to this paper are known as
Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC)
algorithms.
The first such instance was developed to study of the average extension of molecular chains
[
14
,
41
]
.
SMC methods use a sequence of auxiliary distributions
\nu_{1}
, …,
\nu_{K}
so that
\nu_{1}
is easy to sample from,
\nu_{K}
is the target distribution, and then move samples between distributions using a reweighting / resampling mechanism.
These methods are hugely popular, and we refer the reader to
[
10
,
26
,
6
,
46
]
for a broad overview.
In the context of multi-modal distributions, rigorous convergence bounds were proved in
[
42
,
37
,
31
,
23
,
16
,
15
]
.

The algorithms we use in this paper (Algorithms
1
and
2
) are obtained using Neal’s
Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS)
algorithm
[
34
]
, combined with Langevin Monte Carlo.
While AIS and Langevin AIS are immensely popular, there are very few rigorous convergence bounds that apply in the setting of Theorem
1.1
and
1.7
.
Specifically, in our setting we make no apriori assumption about the mixture decomposition, symmetry, shape of the wells, or apriori assume knowledge of how the mass distribution in wells changes as the temperature varies.
We only assume non-degeneracy of critical points, and regularity of
U
.
(The assumption that
U
is a double well potential with wells of equal depth can be relaxed as mentioned in Remark
1.4
.)
To the best of our knowledge, the only results that apply in this setting are
[
15
,
16
,
44
]
, and we now comment on the relationship between these results and the present paper.

In
[
15
]
the authors used an SMC algorithm which resampled points at every level, instead of reweighting them as we do in Algorithms
1
and
2
.
The advantage of this method is that it is keeps the effective sample size constant, and is extremely popular in practice
[
6
]
.
The disadvantage is that particles are now only exchangeable, and not independent, and so theoretical bounds harder to obtain.
In
[
15
]
the authors show the complexity is exactly the same as that of Algorithm
2
(given by (
1.21
)).

In
[
16
]
the author shows the same SMC algorithm can be used in the non-compact setting when the state space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The author uses a coupling argument in
[
30
]
and obtains error estimates in probability.
For a fixed error, the time complexity of this algorithm is
O(\lvert\ln\varepsilon\rvert^{10/3}/\varepsilon^{7})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Finally in
[
44
]
revisits this sampling problem using Metropolis ball walks and parallel tempering.
The author uses a soft domain decomposition
[
28
,
48
]
to show that for a fixed error, the time complexity behaves like
O(1/\varepsilon^{11})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

As mentioned earlier (Remark
1.3
and (
1.12
)), for a fixed error the time complexity of Langevin AIS (Algorithm
1
) scales like
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The main new contribution of this paper of this paper over
[
15
,
16
,
44
]
is twofold.
First, the algorithm has smaller complexity (
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
, vs
O(1/\varepsilon^{4})
or higher).
Second, the proof identifies a simple and useful quantity,

, that controls the sample error of AIS (equation (

), see also Theorem

and Section

, below) in a general setting.
We presently prove Theorem

3

32
,
15
]
.
There may be room to bound

using different techniques, bypassing the limitation of spectral methods, but this goes beyond the scope of the present paper and is left for future study.

2.
Convergence results for AIS

As mentioned earlier, we prove Theorem
1.1
by showing that the quantity
C_{P}(T)
in (
1.13
) can be used to control the sampling error of AIS in a general setting.
We begin by stating this precisely.

2.1.
Bias and Variance estimates for AIS

Suppose
\pi=\tilde{\pi}/Z
is a target distribution from which samples are desired.
Here
\tilde{\pi}
is an unnormalized probability distribution which is easy to compute, and
Z=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\pi}
is the normalization constant (which is hard to compute).
The AIS algorithm
[
34
]
uses an auxiliary family of distributions with densities proportional to
\tilde{\pi}_{1}
, …,
\tilde{\pi}_{K+1}=\tilde{\pi}
(called a
tempering sequence
), along with reversible Markov transition kernels
P_{1}
, …,
P_{K}
.
The algorithm now successively simulates a Markov chain with kernel
P_{k}
, and then reweights the points using the ration
\tilde{\pi}_{k+1}/\tilde{\pi}_{k}
and is described precisely as Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS)

1:

2:

(1)

Tempering sequence of unnormalized densities
\tilde{\pi}_{1}
, …,
\tilde{\pi}_{K}
.

(2)

Corresponding reversible Markov transition kernels
P_{1}
, …,
P_{K}
.

(3)

Running time
T
.

3:

Start with
w_{0}=1
,
X_{0}
arbitrary.

4:

for

k=1,\dots,K

do

5:

Sample
X_{k}
from
P_{k}^{T}(X_{k-1},\cdot)

6:

\tilde{w}_{k}\leftarrow\tilde{w}_{k-1}\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{k+1}(X_{k})}{\tilde{\pi}_{k}(X_{k})}
.

7:

end

for

8:

return

X_{K}
and the unnormalized weight
\tilde{w}_{K}
.

The goal of this section is to obtain a quantitative error bound on AIS, provided the tempering sequence satisfies the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

For each
k\in\{1,\dots,K+1\}
we have a reversible Markov transition kernel
P_{k}
whose stationary distribution is
\pi_{k}=\tilde{\pi}_{k}/Z_{k}
, where
Z_{k}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\pi}_{k}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists constants
T_{0}
,
C_{w}
such that for every
T\geqslant T_{0}
we have

(2.1)

\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lVert P_{k}^{T}r_{k}^{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant C_{w}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad r_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\pi_{k+1}}{\pi_{k}}\,.

Assumption 2.3
.

The uniform mixing time of
P_{1}
is finite.
Explicitly,

(2.2)

t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\min\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}\nonscript\>\bigg|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\bigg\lVert\frac{P_{1}^{n}(x,\cdot)}{\pi_{1}(\cdot)}-1\bigg\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{1}{2}\bigg\}<\infty

We will now show that if we use AIS with a tempering sequence satisfying Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
, then the bias decreases exponentially with
T/t^{\infty}_{\mathrm{mix},1}
, and variance is bounded by
4C_{w}
.

Proposition 2.4

(Bias and variance bounds)
.

Suppose
\{P_{k}\}_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}
are Markov transition kernels satisfying Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
.
Let
X_{K}
,
\tilde{w}_{K}
be obtained from Algorithm
3
with
K+1
levels, running time
T
, and kernels
\{P_{k}\}
.
For all bounded measurable functions
f
, we have the bias and variance estimates

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert\frac{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}}-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\bigg\rvert\leqslant 2^{2-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\,,\quad\text{provided}\quad T\geqslant t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\var\bigg(\frac{\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}}\bigg)\leqslant 4C_{w}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\,,\quad\text{provided}\quad T\geqslant T_{0}\,.

In practice, one would take
N
independent samples from Algorithm
3
, and estimate
\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}
using the empirical mean.
An immediate consequence of Proposition
2.4
is a quantitative bound on the convergence of the empirical measure.

Theorem 2.5

(Empirical measure convergence)
.

Suppose
\{P_{k}\}_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}
are Markov transition kernels satisfying Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
.
Given
\delta>0
, choose

(2.5)

N=\frac{64C_{w}}{\delta^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad T=\max\left\{T_{0},(4+\lvert\log_{2}\delta\rvert)t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}\right\}\,.

Let
\{X^{i}_{K},\tilde{w}^{i}_{K}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}
be
N
independent realizations of the points and weights returned by Algorithm
3
, with
K+1
levels, running time
T
, and kernels
\{P_{k}\}
.
For every bounded measurable
f
, we have

(2.6)

\Big\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}_{K}f(X^{i}_{K})-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\Big\rVert_{L^{2}(\bm{P})}\leqslant\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\delta\,,\quad\text{where}\quad w^{i}_{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\tilde{w}^{i}_{K}}{\sum_{j=1}\tilde{w}^{j}_{K}}\,.

Moreover, if
\mu_{K+1,N}
is the empirical measure defined by

(2.7)

\mu_{K+1,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}_{K}\delta_{X^{i}_{K}}\,,

then for every
s>d/2

(2.8)

\bm{E}\big\lVert\mu_{K+1,N}-\pi_{K+1}\big\rVert_{H^{-s}}^{2}\leqslant C_{s}\delta^{2}\,.

Remark 2.6

(Time complexity)
.

In order to obtain samples that satisfy (
2.6
), one has to simulate
N
realizations of
T
iterations of each of the chains
P_{1}
, …,
P_{K}
, and so the time complexity is

(2.9)

O(NTK)=\frac{64KC_{w}t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}{\delta^{2}}\big(4+\lvert\log_{2}\delta\rvert\big)

As mentioned earlier, when working with weighted samples it is important to estimate the
effective sample size
(see (
1.10
)) and ensure that the weights don’t concentrate on a few points.
This can be done quickly from the variance bound (
2.4
).

Proposition 2.7

(Effective sample size)
.

Let
w^{1}_{K}
, …,
w^{N}_{K}
be the normalized weights defined in (
2.6
).
Then the sum of the squared weights satisfies

(2.10)

\bm{E}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w^{i}_{K})^{2}\leqslant\frac{8\left(4C_{w}+1\right)}{N}\,.

Consequently, the expected effective sample size is bounded by

(2.11)

\bm{E}\ESS(w^{1}_{K},\dots,w^{N}_{K})\geqslant\frac{N}{8\left(4C_{w}+1\right)}\,.

Before delving into the proofs, we now briefly discuss the assumptions and implications of Proposition
2.5
.

2.2.
Relation to
\chi^{2}
-divergence

To understand the assumptions better, suppose momentarily we were able to choose
T=\infty
in (
2.1
).
In this case, the left hand side of (
2.1
) better can be bounded in terms of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between each of the intermediate distributions.

Proposition 2.8
.

If
T=\infty
then the left hand side of (
2.1
) satisfies

(2.12)

\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lVert P_{k}^{\infty}r_{k}^{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\exp\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\chi^{2}(\pi_{k+1};\pi_{k})\bigg)

Recall the
\chi^{2}
-divergence appearing above is a commonly used measure of the difference between two distributions.
Explicitly, for two distributions with densities
p,q
the
\chi^{2}
-divergence is defined by

(2.13)

\chi^{2}(p;q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\Big\langle\frac{p^{2}}{q^{2}}-1,q\Big\rangle\,.

Let us now momentarily assume that
\pi_{k}
’s are all Gaussian, with
\pi_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
\pi_{K+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon I_{d})
.
If we choose
K=1
then AIS reduces to a vanilla importance sampling, and one can explicitly compute (see for instance Proposition 17.1 in
[
6
]
) that the right hand side of (
2.12
) is
O(1/\varepsilon^{d/2})
.
This is comparable with a standard rejection sampling cost, and is too large to be practical.

However, if we instead choose
K=d/\varepsilon
, then the right hand side of (
2.12
) can be bounded
independent
of both
\varepsilon
and
d
.
Thus, using AIS here with
O(d/\varepsilon)
intermediate levels gives samples with variance
O(1)
, which is a huge improvement.

Proposition 2.9
.

If the target distribution
\pi
is the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon I_{d})
, then choosing

(2.14)

K=\bigg\lceil\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\bigg\rceil\,,

choosing the temperatures
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}
so that
\varepsilon_{K+1}=\varepsilon
and
\{1/\varepsilon_{k}\}_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}
are linearly spaced, and choosing
\pi_{k}
to be the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon_{k}I_{d})
will ensure

(2.15)

\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lVert P_{k}^{\infty}r_{k}^{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant C\,.

In this case, Proposition
2.4
with
T=\infty
will yield a variance bound that is
independent
of both
\varepsilon
and
d
.

The proof of Proposition
2.9
is elementary, and presented in Section
4.4
, below.

3.
Convergence results for Langevin AIS

3.1.
Assumptions

In order to state the assumptions in Theorem
1.1
precisely, we first describe the assumptions that are required on the potential
U
.
In short, we need the potential to be a regular, double-well function with wells of
nearly equal
depth.
The criterion that the wells have nearly equal depth is required for the multimodal sampling problem to be non-degenerate in the sense that the mass in each well remains bounded away from
0
.
These assumptions are the same as the assumptions in
[
15
]
, which are explained in detail in
[
15
, Section 4.1]
.
We quote them here for easy reference, and refer the reader to
[
15
]
for a detailed explanation and motivation.

Assumption 3.1
.

The function
U\in C^{6\vee(1+\lfloor d/2\rfloor)}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{R})
, has a nondegenerate Hessian at all critical points, and has exactly two local minima located at
x_{\min,1}
and
x_{\min,2}
.
We normalize
U
so that

(3.1)

0=U(x_{\min,1})\leqslant U(x_{\min,2}).

Define the saddle height between
x_{\min,1}
and
x_{\min,2}
to be the minimum amount of energy needed to go from the global minimum
x_{\min,1}
to
x_{\min,2}
.
Explicitly, the saddle height is

(3.2)

\hat{U}=\hat{U}(x_{\min,1},x_{\min,2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\omega}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,1]}U(\omega(t)).

Here the infimum above is taken over all continuous paths
\omega\in C([0,1];\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that
\omega(0)=x_{\min,1}
,
\omega(1)=x_{\min,2}
.

Assumption 3.2
.

The saddle height between
x_{\min,1}
and
x_{\min,2}
is attained at a unique critical point
s_{1,2}
of index one.
That is,
the first eigenvalue of
\Hess U(s_{1,2})
is negative and the others are positive.

The
energy barrier
, denoted by
\hat{\gamma}
, is defined to be the minimum amount of energy needed to go from the (possibly local) minimum
x_{\min,2}
to the global minimum
x_{\min,1}
.
In terms of
s_{1,2}
, the energy barrier
\hat{\gamma}
and the saddle height are given by

(3.3)

\hat{\gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}U(s_{1,2})-U(x_{\min,2}),\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{U}=U(s_{1,2})\,.

The ratio
\hat{\gamma}_{r}
is the ratio of the saddle height
\hat{U}
to the energy barrier
\hat{\gamma}
, given by

(3.4)

\hat{\gamma}_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\hat{U}}{\hat{\gamma}}\,.

We recall the basin of attraction around
x_{\min,i}
, denoted by
\Omega_{i}
, is defined by

(3.5)

\Omega_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\Big\{y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\nonscript\>\Big|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak\lim_{t\to\infty}y_{t}=x_{\min,i},\text{ where }\dot{y}_{t}=-\nabla U(y_{t})\text{ with }y_{0}=y\Big\}\,.

Assumption 3.3
.

There exists
0\leqslant\varepsilon_{\min}<\varepsilon_{\max}\leqslant\infty
, a constant
C_{m}
such that

(3.6)

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon\in[\varepsilon_{\min},\varepsilon_{\max}]\\
0<\varepsilon<\infty\end{subarray}}\pi_{\varepsilon}(\Omega_{i})\geqslant\frac{1}{C_{m}^{2}}.

Remark 3.4
.

For simplicity, we have assumed that
U
has only two wells.
If
U
has more than two wells, the techniques we use will still apply provided we impose a non-degeneracy condition on
U
.
The precise assumption is stated in Section 10 in
[
15
]
, and the required modifications to the proof are straightforward.

4.

4.1.
Bias and Variance estimates (Proposition
2.4
)

To prove the bias estimate (
2.3
) we first need an estimate on the unnormalized bias.

Lemma 4.1
.

If Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
hold, then for any bounded measurable function
f
, we have

(4.1)

\left\lvert\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\right]-\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\right\rvert\leqslant 2^{-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle\lvert f\rvert,\pi_{K+1}\rangle.

Proof.

For notational convenience, define

(4.2)

\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{i}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\left\lvert\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{i}f(X_{i})\right]-\frac{Z_{i+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle f,\pi_{i+1}\rangle\right\rvert\,.

Notice

\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{K}(f)\leqslant\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{K}(f^{+})+\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{K}(f^{-}),

and hence it suffices to establish (
4.1
) for non-negative functions
f
.
Thus, without loss of generality, we subsequently assume that
f\geqslant 0
.

Observe that the mean of the estimator can be rewritten as

(4.3)

\displaystyle\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K-1}(\tilde{r}_{K}f)(X_{K})\right]

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K-1}P_{K}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{K}f)(X_{K-1})\right]\,.

On the other hand, using reversibility, the corresponding true mean satisfies

(4.5)

\frac{Z_{K}}{Z_{1}}\langle P_{K}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{K}f),\pi_{K}\rangle=\frac{Z_{K}}{Z_{1}}\langle\tilde{r}_{K}f,\pi_{K}\rangle=\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\,.

Combining (
4.3
), (
4.5
), and the definition of the bias in (
4.2
), we obtain

\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{K}(f)=\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{K-1}\!\left(P_{K}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{K}f)\right).

Iterating this identity down to level
1
yields

(4.6)

\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{K}(f)=\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{1}\!\left(\tilde{f}_{2}\right),

where

\tilde{f}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}S_{i}S_{i+1}\cdots S_{K}f,\qquad S_{k}h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}P_{k}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{k}h).

Next, observe that

\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}(X_{1})\right]=P_{1}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{1}\tilde{f}_{2})(X_{0}),\qquad\langle\tilde{r}_{1}\tilde{f}_{2},\pi_{1}\rangle=\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\langle\tilde{f}_{2},\pi_{2}\rangle.

Since
f\geqslant 0
, positivity of
P_{k}
implies
\tilde{f}_{2}\geqslant 0
.
Now Assumption
2.3
implies

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\tilde{B}ias}_{1}(\tilde{f}_{2})

\displaystyle=\lvert P_{1}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{1}\tilde{f}_{2})(X_{0})-\langle\tilde{r}_{1}\tilde{f}_{2},\pi_{1}\rangle\rvert

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\langle\tilde{r}_{1}\tilde{f}_{2},\pi_{1}\rangle=2^{-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\langle\tilde{f}_{2},\pi_{2}\rangle.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\langle\tilde{f}_{2},\pi_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\langle P_{2}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{2}\tilde{f}_{3}),\pi_{2}\rangle=\frac{Z_{3}}{Z_{1}}\langle\tilde{f}_{3},\pi_{3}\rangle

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\cdots=\frac{Z_{K}}{Z_{1}}\langle\tilde{f}_{K},\pi_{K}\rangle=\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle.

Combining (
4.6
), (
4.7
), and (
4.1
) yields (
4.1
), which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
4.1
and Assumption
2.2
quickly yield Proposition
2.4
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Applying (
4.1
) with the test function
f\equiv 1
, we obtain

(4.10)

\left\lvert\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}-\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\right\rvert\leqslant 2^{-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}.

Consequently, if
T\geqslant t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}
, then

(4.11)

\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}.

Next, we estimate

(4.12)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\right]-\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\right\rvert

(4.13)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\left\lvert\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\right]-\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\right\rvert

(4.14)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lvert\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle-\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\right\rvert

(4.15)

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\eqref{e:bias-first-level}}{\leqslant}2^{-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle\lvert f\rvert,\pi_{K+1}\rangle+\left\lvert\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}-\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\right\rvert\left\lvert\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\right\rvert

(4.16)

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\eqref{e:wktilde-bias}}{\leqslant}2^{\,1-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\langle\lvert f\rvert,\pi_{K+1}\rangle.

Combining (
4.11
) and (
4.16
) yields the desired normalized bias estimate (
2.3
).

We now turn to the variance estimate (
2.4
).
It suffices to prove the bound

(4.17)

\var\!\bigl(\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\bigr)\leqslant\left(\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\right)^{2}C_{w}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.

Indeed, combining this with (
4.11
) immediately implies (
2.4
).

To prove (
4.17
), observe that

\displaystyle\var\!\bigl(\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K}^{2}f(X_{K})^{2}\right]\leqslant\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{2}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K-1}^{2}\tilde{r}_{K}(X_{K})^{2}\right]\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{K-1}^{2}P_{K}^{T}(\tilde{r}_{K}^{2})(X_{K-1})\right]\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K-1}^{2}\,\lVert P_{K}^{T}\tilde{r}_{K}^{2}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.

Iterating this argument over
k=1,\dots,K
yields

(4.19)

\var\!\bigl(\tilde{w}_{K}f(X_{K})\bigr)\leqslant\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lVert P_{k}^{T}\tilde{r}_{k}^{2}\rVert_{\infty}\right)\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.

Finally, we note that

(4.20)

\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lVert P_{k}^{T}\tilde{r}_{k}^{2}\rVert_{\infty}=\left(\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\right)^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lVert P_{k}^{T}r_{k}^{2}\rVert_{\infty}\overset{\eqref{e:Ptrk2-ub}}{\leqslant}C_{w}\left(\frac{Z_{K+1}}{Z_{1}}\right)^{2}.

Combining this with (
4.19
) proves (
4.17
), and hence (
2.4
). This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Convergence of the empirical measure (Theorem
2.5
)

We now use Proposition
2.4
to prove Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

We first prove (
2.6
).
Define

(4.21)

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}.

From the definition of
w_{K}^{i}
in (
2.6
), we have

\frac{1}{N}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}=R\,w_{K}^{i}.

Adding and subtracting the same quantity and applying the triangle inequality yields, for any

,

(4.22)

\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{K}^{i}f(x_{i})-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\Bigr|\leqslant I_{1}+I_{2},

where

(4.23)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{K}^{i}f(x_{i})-\frac{1}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Rw_{K}^{i}f(x_{i})\Bigr|,

(4.24)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\Bigl|\frac{1}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}f(x_{i})-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\Bigr|.

We observe that

(4.25)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|1-\frac{R}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{1}}\Bigr|\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{K}^{i}f(x_{i})\Bigr|

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigl|1-\frac{R}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{1}}\Bigr|\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}=\Bigl|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\frac{\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}-1\Bigr)\Bigr|\lVert f\rVert_{\infty},

(4.27)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\frac{\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}f(x_{i})}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\Bigr)\Bigr|.

We recall that for any i.i.d. random variables
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
in
L^{2}(\bm{P})
,

(4.28)

\bm{E}\left[\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}\Bigr)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\var(Y_{1})+\bigl(\bm{E}Y_{1}\bigr)^{2}.

Applying (
4.22
) with
x_{i}=X_{K}^{i}
and combining (
4.25
), (
4.27
), and (
4.28
), we obtain

(4.29)

\Biggl\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{K}^{i}f(X_{K}^{i})-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\Biggr\|_{L^{2}(\bm{P})}\leqslant I_{3}+I_{4},

where

(4.30)

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{N}}\var\!\Bigl(\frac{\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}\Bigr)^{1/2}\overset{\eqref{e:variance-estimate}}{\leqslant}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{4C_{w}}{N}}\,,

(4.31)

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\var\!\Bigl(\frac{\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}f(X_{K}^{i})}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}\Bigr)^{1/2}+\Bigl|\bm{E}\frac{\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}f(X_{K}^{i})}{\bm{E}\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}}-\langle f,\pi_{K+1}\rangle\Bigr|

(4.32)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{e:variance-estimate},\eqref{e:normalized-bias}}{\leqslant}\sqrt{\frac{4C_{w}}{N}}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}+2^{\,2-T/t_{\mathrm{mix},1}^{\infty}}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}.

Finally, choosing
N
and
T
as in (
2.5
) and combining
(
4.29
), (
4.30
), and (
4.31
)
proves (
2.6
), as desired.

We now prove (
2.8
).
The bound (
2.8
) follows quickly from (
2.6
), and the Fourier representation of the
\dot{H}^{-s}
norm.
Recall, if
\varphi
is a distribution in the homogeneous Sobolev space
H^{-s}=H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then the norm is equivalently defined by

(4.33)

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-s}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{0\}}\frac{\lvert\langle\varphi,e_{n}\rangle\rvert^{2}}{\lvert n\rvert^{2s}}\quad\text{where}\quad e_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}e^{2\pi in\cdot x}\,.

Now,
we note that for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{0\}
, the bound (
2.6
) implies

(4.34)

\bm{E}\langle\mu_{K+1,N}-\pi_{K+1},e_{n}\rangle^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}\,.

Thus

(4.35)

\displaystyle\bm{E}\lVert\mu_{K+1,N}-\pi_{K+1}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{0\}}\bm{E}\frac{\langle\mu_{K+1,N}-\pi_{K+1},e_{n}\rangle^{2}}{\lvert n\rvert^{2s}}\leqslant\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{0\}}\frac{\delta^{2}}{\lvert n\rvert^{2s}}

(4.36)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{s}\delta^{2}\,,

where

(4.37)

C_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{0\}}\frac{1}{\lvert n\rvert^{2s}}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

4.3.
The effective sample size (Proposition
2.7
)

We now prove Proposition
2.7
bounding the effective sample size.
The bound follows from a more general fact about normalized sums of nonnegative i.i.d. random variables.

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

Let
\left\{\tilde{\zeta}_{i}\right\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}
be non-negative i.i.d.
L^{2}
random variables with
\tilde{\mu}=\bm{E}\left[\tilde{\zeta}_{1}\right]
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\var\left(\tilde{\zeta}_{1}\right)
and define

(4.38)

\zeta_{i}=\frac{\tilde{\zeta}_{i}}{S_{N}}\quad\text{where}\quad S_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\zeta}_{i}\,.

We claim that we must have

(4.39)

\bm{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}^{2}\right]\leqslant\frac{8\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\tilde{\mu}^{2}\right)}{N\tilde{\mu}^{2}}\,.

Momentarily postponing the proof of (
4.39
), we now prove (
2.10
) and (
2.11
).
Setting
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{K}^{i}
and taking
f\equiv 1
in (
2.4
) gives

(4.40)

\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\leqslant 4C_{w}\tilde{\mu}^{2}\,.

Combining this with (
4.39
) yields (
2.10
) as desired.
Using this and Jensen’s inequality, implies the lower bound on the effective sample size in (
2.11
).

It remains to prove the claim (
4.39
).
Let
\bar{S}_{N}=S_{N}/N
. Then

(4.41)

\displaystyle\bm{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=N\bm{E}\left[\frac{\tilde{\zeta}_{1}^{2}}{S_{N}^{2}}\bm{1}_{\{\bar{S}_{N}\geqslant\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{2}\}}\right]+\bm{E}\left[\frac{1}{S_{N}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\zeta}_{i}^{2}\bm{1}_{\{\bar{S}_{N}<\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{2}\}}\right]

(4.42)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\tilde{\mu}^{2}\right)}{N\tilde{\mu}^{2}}+\bm{P}\left[\left\lvert\bar{S}_{N}-\tilde{\mu}\right\rvert>\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{8\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\tilde{\mu}^{2}\right)}{N\tilde{\mu}^{2}}\,.

Here we used
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}^{2}\leqslant 1
to obtain the first inequality, and then Chebyshev’s inequality to obtain the second inequality.
This concludes the proof.
∎

.

5.2.
Spectral decomposition

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
, defined by

(5.2)

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}f=-\nabla U\cdot\nabla f+\varepsilon\Delta f\,,

be the generator of (
1.2
).
We know that for any test function
f
, the action of
P_{\varepsilon,t}
on
f
satisfies the
Kolmogorov backward equation
(see for instance
[
38
, Chapter 2]
).
That is, if

(5.3)

u_{t}(x)=P_{\varepsilon,t}f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}P_{\varepsilon,t}(x,dy)f(y)\,,

then

(5.4)

\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u\quad\text{and}\quad u_{0}=f\,.

We now state certain spectral properties of (
1.2
) which will be used in the proofs of Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
.
Let
L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})
denote the weighted
L^{2}
space with inner-product and norm defined by

(5.5)

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}fg\,\pi_{\varepsilon}\,dx\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\lvert f\rvert^{2}\pi_{\varepsilon}\,dx\,,

respectively.
It is well known (see for instance
[
21
, Chapter 8]
) that the operator
-\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is a self-adjoint operator the weighted space
L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})
, and has a discrete spectrum with positive eigenvalues.
We denote the eigenvalues by

(5.6)

0=\lambda_{1,\varepsilon}<\lambda_{2,\varepsilon}\leqslant\lambda_{3,\varepsilon}\cdots\,,

and the corresponding
L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})
normalized eigenfunctions by
\psi_{1,\varepsilon}
,
\psi_{2,\varepsilon}
, etc.
We note that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1,\varepsilon}
is
0
, and the second smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2,\varepsilon}
is strictly positive (i.e.
P_{\varepsilon}
has a spectral gap).
In fact, for the proof of Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
the properties we need were collected in a convenient form in
[
15
]
as Properties 4.6–4.8.
We quote the portions we need here for easy reference.

Property 5.3

(Eigenvalue bounds)
.

For every
H>\hat{U}
, there exists a constant
C_{H}
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
we have

(5.7)

\lambda_{2,\varepsilon}\geqslant C_{H}\exp\Big(-\frac{H}{\varepsilon}\Big).

Also, there exists
\Lambda
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
such that

(5.8)

\lambda_{i,\varepsilon}\geqslant\Lambda,\quad\text{for all }i\geqslant 3.

Property 5.4

(Eigenfunction variation)
.

The function
\varepsilon\mapsto\pi_{\varepsilon}(\Omega_{1})
is of bounded variation on the interval
(0,1]
.
Moreover, for every
\gamma<\hat{\gamma}
, there exists a constant
C_{\gamma}
such that for every
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
we have

(5.9)

\displaystyle\big\lvert\langle\psi_{2,\varepsilon},\pi_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\rangle\big\rvert\leqslant C_{\gamma}\Big(\exp\Big(-\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\Big)+\big\lvert\pi_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\Omega_{1})-\pi_{\varepsilon}(\Omega_{1})\big\rvert\Big)\,.

Property 5.5

(Eigenfunction bounds)
.

There exists constant
C_{\psi_{2}}
, independent of
\varepsilon
such that

(5.10)

\sup\limits_{0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1}\|\psi_{2,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leqslant C_{\psi_{2}}.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.1
, Section 9 in
[
15
]
shows that Properties
5.3
–
5.5
hold.
To briefly discuss the significance of Properties
5.3
–
5.5
, we note that convergence of (reversible) Markov processes can effectively be studied using the spectral decomposition (see for instance Chapter 12 in
[
25
]
).
In particular, for reversible Markov processes the rate of convergence is controlled both above and below by the
spectral gap
, which in our case is simply
\lambda_{2,\varepsilon}
.

For the Langevin system with a multimodal potential, the lower bound (
5.7
) is sharp (see
[
21
, Chapter 8, Proposition 2.2]
, or
[
32
]
), and so spectral gap is exponentially small.
Thus sampling by simulating (
1.2
) directly, will necessarily cost
O(e^{\hat{U}/\varepsilon})
, which is not desirable.

However, in our situation the third eigenvalue is large (i.e. bounded independent of
\varepsilon

This will give fast convergence

Variants of this idea have been used by several authors in many contexts to accelerate convergence

A warm start to Langevin dynamics will also control the projection on the second eigenspace, and this was recently used by Koehler, Lee, and Vuong

In

This is a standard technique used in sequential Monte Carlo algorithms (see for instance

The disadvantage of this approach, however, is that one loses independence of realizations, and as a result the estimates are harder to obtain and weaker than in the i.i.d. case.

In our situation, we reweight points instead of resampling, and the variance is controlled by the product (

.
That is, the reweighting controls the error in terms of how fast the kernel

This in turn is controlled by the projection onto the second eigenspace, which we will bound using Properties

\lVert\psi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in l_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lVert D^{\alpha+e_{i}}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}\,.

We now fix
\alpha\in l_{k}
and compute
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lVert D^{\alpha+e_{i}}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}
.
Integrating by parts gives

(5.64)

\lVert D^{\alpha+e_{i}}\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}D^{\alpha}\psi\partial_{i}^{2}D^{\alpha}\psi\pi_{\varepsilon}dx-I_{1}\,,

where

(5.65)

I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}D^{\alpha}\psi\partial_{i}D^{\alpha}\psi\partial_{i}\pi_{\varepsilon}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{i}(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}\partial_{i}\pi_{\varepsilon}dx=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}\partial_{i}^{2}\pi_{\varepsilon}dx\,.

Combining this with (
5.64
) and summing over
i
in (
5.63
) produces

(5.66)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lVert D^{\alpha+e_{i}}\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}=I_{2}+I_{3}\,,

where

(5.67)

I_{2}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}D^{\alpha}\psi D^{\alpha}(\Delta\psi)\pi_{\varepsilon}dx\quad\text{and}\quad I_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}\Delta\pi_{\varepsilon}dx\,.

We first compute
I_{2}
.
Since
\psi
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
we note

(5.68)

I_{2}=\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}\pi_{\varepsilon}dx-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}I_{4}\,,

where

(5.69)

I_{4}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}D^{\alpha}(\nabla U\cdot\nabla\psi)D^{\alpha}\psi\pi_{\varepsilon}dx\,.

By considering the case when the differential operator
D^{\alpha}
is completely exhausted by hitting
\nabla f
or not, we can split the integral into two parts. Namely,

(5.70)

\displaystyle I_{4}=I_{5}+I_{6}

where

(5.71)

\displaystyle I_{5}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla U\cdot\nabla(D^{\alpha}\psi)D^{\alpha}\psi\pi_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{6}=I_{4}-I_{5}\,.

Integrating
I_{5}
by parts gives

(5.72)

\displaystyle I_{5}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta U(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}\pi_{\varepsilon}dx-I_{5}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla U\cdot\nabla\pi_{\varepsilon}(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}dx\,,

and consequently,

(5.73)

I_{5}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\Delta U\pi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla U\cdot\nabla\pi_{\varepsilon})(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}dx\,.

Combining (
5.66
), (
5.67
), (
5.68
), (
5.70
), and (
5.73
), we get

(5.74)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lVert D^{\alpha+e_{i}}\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}\lVert D^{\alpha}\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}I_{6}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}I_{7}\,,

where

(5.75)

I_{7}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\Delta U\pi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla U\cdot\nabla\pi_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\Delta\pi_{\varepsilon})(D^{\alpha}\psi)^{2}dx\,.

We know
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is a stationary solution to the Kolmogorov forward equation

(5.76)

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{*}\pi_{\varepsilon}=\Delta U\pi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla U\cdot\nabla\pi_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\Delta\pi_{\varepsilon}=0\,.

Hence
I_{7}=0
.

Moreover,

(5.77)

I_{6}=\sum_{\beta<\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(D^{\alpha-\beta}\nabla U)\cdot(D^{\beta}\nabla\psi)D^{\alpha}\psi\pi_{\varepsilon}dx\,,

and by using Cauchy–Schwartz, induction hypothesis, and the fact that
\lvert\beta\rvert\leqslant k-1
,

(5.78)

\displaystyle\lvert I_{6}\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\beta<\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}\lVert U\rVert_{C^{1+\lvert\alpha-\beta\rvert}}\lVert\psi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{\lvert\beta\rvert+1}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}\lVert\psi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}

(5.79)

\displaystyle\leqslant C(k,\lVert U\rVert_{C^{k+1}},d,\Lambda_{1})\lVert\psi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}\,.

Combining (
5.63
), (
5.74
), and (
5.79
) yields

(5.80)

\displaystyle\lVert\psi\rVert_{\dot{H}_{k+1}^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(k,\lVert U\rVert_{C^{k+1}},d,\Lambda_{1})\Big(\Big(\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{k+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{k}\Big)

(5.81)

\displaystyle\leqslant C(k,\lVert U\rVert_{C^{k+1}},d,\Lambda_{1})\Big(\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{k+1}\,,

where we used the lower bound

and increased
C(k,\lVert U\rVert_{C^{k+1}},d,\Lambda_{1})
appropriately to obtain the last inequality.
This concludes the proof.
