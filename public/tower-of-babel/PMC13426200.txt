Spinal epidural angiolipoma of the vascular-predominant type (type 2): A case report
Authors: Yuko Makihara, Takashi Katsube, Aiko Gobara, Satoru Nagano, Takuya Manako, Yasushi Kaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal epidural angiolipoma is a rare benign tumor composed of mature adipose tissue and vascular elements. Here, we report the case of an 80-year-old man with progressive gait instability secondary to this tumor. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a thoracic epidural mass with peripheral fatty components and centrally located vascular tissue showing strong contrast enhancement, extending into the right intervertebral foramen. Surgical excision was performed, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of angiolipoma. This case underscores that recognizing the coexistence of adipose and vascular components on Magnetic resonance imaging is critical for preoperative diagnosis of spinal epidural angiolipoma and is essential for appropriate surgical planning.

Introduction

Angiolipoma is a benign tumor composed of mature adipose tissue and capillary-sized vascular elements. It most commonly arises from the subcutaneous tissues of the forearm, trunk, or upper arm [1]. Spinal epidural angiolipoma is rare. Although previously estimated to represent 0.14%-1.2% of all spinal tumors, a recent database analysis indicates a much lower frequency of 0.05% among primary spinal tumors [2]. It typically occurs in the posterior epidural space of the mid-thoracic spine (T6-T9) [3]. Similar to other benign spinal tumors, epidural angiolipomas typically cause gradually progressive back pain, leg numbness, paraparesis, and late-stage sphincter dysfunction, though intratumoral hemorrhage or thrombosis can trigger sudden neurological deterioration [2,4,5].

On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), spinal epidural angiolipomas characteristically show a mixture of fatty and vascular components: the fatty portion appears hyperintense on both T1- and T2-weighted images and is suppressed on fat-saturated sequences, whereas the vascular portion shows hypointensity on T1-weighted images, hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, and avid contrast enhancement [4,5].

Here, we report the case of type 2 (vascular-predominant) thoracic spinal epidural angiolipoma detected during the evaluation of progressive gait instability.

Case report

An 80-year-old man with a history of Alzheimer’s disease presented with progressive gait instability requiring the constant use of a cane. Six months earlier, he had sustained an ankle fracture after a fall and received treatment at another hospital. Although the fracture healed, his gait instability had progressively worsened. On neurological examination, vibration sense was diminished at the patellae and absent at the ankles bilaterally, the Romberg sign was positive, and the Mann test was markedly impaired, indicating posterior column dysfunction.

These findings raised suspicion of a spinal cord lesion as a causative factor, and MRI of the spine was performed, which revealed a thoracic epidural mass.

Follow-up MRI at our hospital revealed a spindle-shaped epidural lesion extending from T5 to T7. Compared with the initial study, the lesion showed no interval growth. The peripheral portion exhibited high signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted images, which was suppressed on fat-saturated sequences, indicating the presence of fatty components. The central portion showed high signals on T2-weighted images, low signals on T1-weighted images, and robust contrast enhancement (Fig. 1A-D). Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated mild hyperintensity; however, the apparent diffusion coefficient map showed no marked reduction, indicating the absence of diffusion restriction. Computed tomography revealed heterogeneous attenuation with intermixed fat and soft-tissue densities (Fig. 1E). Discussion

In this report, we describe a case of vascular-predominant (type 2) thoracic spinal epidural angiolipoma in an 80-year-old man who presented with progressive gait instability. It most commonly arises in the posterior epidural space of the thoracic spine, particularly at the T6-T9 levels [3]. This predilection may be associated with the relatively poor blood supply in this region, where ischemic events may promote angiogenesis [5,6]. Factors reported to contribute to tumor growth include hormonal changes during pregnancy, increased circulating blood volume, weight gain, and intratumoral hemorrhage [2,4,5].

Histologically, spinal epidural angiolipomas consist of two components—vascular tissue and adipose tissue—and their imaging features reflect the relative proportions of these components. Vascular elements typically appear hypointense on T1-weighted images and variably hyperintense on T2-weighted images, demonstrating marked enhancement without flow voids, and show soft-tissue attenuation on CT. Conversely, fatty components appear hyperintense on T1- and T2-weighted images, are suppressed on fat-saturated sequences, and exhibit fat attenuation on CT [1,5]. Although reports on diffusion-weighted imaging findings are limited, a case series by Wang et al. [7] demonstrated no obvious diffusion restriction in these tumors, which is consistent with the findings in our case [7].

Hu et al. [5] classified spinal epidural angiolipomas into type 1 (fat-predominant, with slit-like or patchy vascular areas) and type 2 (vascular-predominant, with central vascular tissue surrounded by fat). The vascular component is often located adjacent to the spinal cord. In type 2 tumors, as in the present case, the identification of peripheral fat is particularly crucial for establishing the diagnosis.

These tumors frequently extend across multiple vertebral levels (mean span of 2.5 levels) and may protrude through the intervertebral foramen (10%-20%), occasionally resulting in foraminal enlargement [8]. Rare infiltrative forms may invade adjacent structures, such as bone or muscle, and are more commonly located ventral to the dural sac. One proposed mechanism for this infiltrative behavior is spread through the vertebral venous plexus [8].

The differential diagnoses include cavernous hemangioma, schwannoma, meningioma, lymphoma, and lipomatous tumors. The coexistence of fat and strongly enhancing soft-tissue components is a key feature that helps distinguish angiolipoma from these entities. Regarding the differentiation from cavernous hemangiomas, Hu et al. noted that cavernous hemangiomas typically compress surrounding fat, resulting in a clear and smooth border, whereas angiolipomas exhibit a vague and irregular boundary between the vascular and fatty components [5]. However, hemangiomas may occasionally incorporate varying amounts of fatty tissue or exhibit prominent reactive fat overgrowth, making differentiation from angiolipomas challenging on imaging alone [9,10]. Therefore, careful recognition of the coexistence and spatial distribution of fatty and vascular components on MRI is essential for suggesting the correct preoperative diagnosis and for planning appropriate surgical management.

Conclusion

Spinal epidural angiolipoma is a rare tumor; however, careful recognition of the coexistence of fatty and vascular components on MRI is critical for suggesting the diagnosis. Accurate preoperative identification of these characteristic imaging features can facilitate appropriate surgical planning and management.
