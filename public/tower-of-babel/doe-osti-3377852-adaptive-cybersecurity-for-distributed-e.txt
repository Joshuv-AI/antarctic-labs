This report documents the design and evaluation of an integrated online-learning pipeline developed
within the AdCyDER project for Distributed Energy Resource (DER) cybersecurity. The pipeline
couples a Reinforcement Learning (RL) attack classifier — which produces an attack-type probability
distribution — with a Stackelberg game-theoretic (GT) defense selector that consumes those
distributions alongside SME-encoded priors over (defense, attack) effectiveness pairings and per-
defense costs to choose grid-health-preserving defenses. The objective is not attack classification per
se but production of distributions that drive effective defense selection through the Stackelberg layer,
learned from delayed grid-health feedback rather than labeled attack data. AdCyDER as a whole
is broader than the work presented here; this report covers the specific RL/GT loop integration
and its evaluation. We present the integrated pipeline (SCADA telemetry with Fronius inverter
physics, Suricata IDS, time-windowed aggregation, per-facility LSTM classifier, Stackelberg optimizer,
OpenC2 actuators), an experimental campaign of 28 eight-hour iterations across three baseline
modes, and a pipeline-ordered diagnostic protocol. The protocol identifies two distinct failure modes
within the loop: paired supervised ceilings on the same features establish that the deployed online RL
classifier (macro F1 ≈ 0.07) sits at least 4.7× below a same-architecture supervised LSTM (≈ 0.34)
and 10–11× below a linear feature-signal ceiling (≈ 0.70–0.79 depending on per-facility isolation),
localizing the dominant failure to the training procedure; and the reward signal driving online
updates carries weak directional coupling with classifier correctness in the methodology-expected
direction (multi-lens convergent: top-decile P(true) records produce more frequent state changes and
slightly larger improvements, top-vs-bot Cohen’s 𝑑 ≈ −0.19), but at effect magnitudes too small
to drive gradient-based learning at the campaign sample size. The original learning hypothesis is
not supported by the data. The primary contributions are the diagnostic methodology — proposed
as a transferable falsification protocol for online RL/GT defense pipelines learning from delayed
environmental reward — and the open, reproducible experimental infrastructure. We outline reward

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Scope of this report. The AdCyDER project encompasses a broad research effort in
DER cybersecurity. This report covers one strand of that effort: the integrated online-
learning pipeline that couples a Reinforcement Learning (RL) attack classifier with a
Stackelberg game-theoretic (GT) defense selector in a closed feedback loop, the end-to-
end pipeline assembled around it from components developed across the project, and the
experimental evaluation conducted against that integrated system. It does not aim to
summarize AdCyDER as a whole.
 Project objective tested here. The RL/GT loop is not an attack-classification system
in the conventional sense. The RL classifier produces a per-window probability distribution
over attack types rather than a hard label, reflecting the operational reality that attacks
are diverse and may not cleanly fit into a single predefined category; a defender selecting
a mitigation action gains more from a distribution that hedges across plausible attack
types than from an overconfident single classification. The Stackelberg layer consumes that
distribution alongside subject-matter-expert (SME) priors over (defense, attack) effectiveness
pairings and per-defense cost, and selects the defense action that minimizes expected grid
impact. The loop’s objective is therefore to learn to produce probability distributions that,
fed through the SME-priored Stackelberg layer, drive selection of grid-health-preserving
defenses — not to maximize per-window classification accuracy in isolation. The hypothesis
tested was that an online RL classifier trained from delayed grid-health-delta feedback
derived from physically-grounded inverter telemetry could learn to produce such defense-
driving distributions per facility without requiring labeled attack data in production.
The scientific bet was that SME-encoded defense structure plus operationally-grounded
reward could substitute for the labeled training data that is infeasible to collect at scale in
operational DER environments.
 What was built. A complete containerized end-to-end pipeline. The system was originally
architected for AWS deployment with LocalStack-based local replication, but was pivoted
early in the project to run entirely on LocalStack when AWS access proved infeasible
due to organizational approval and cost constraints; the AWS-style service architecture
was preserved. Components: synthetic SCADA telemetry with embedded Fronius inverter
physics and trained anomaly-detection autoencoder; coordinated network attack scenarios
with Suricata-format alerting; Kafka and OpenSearch ingest; MongoDB time-windowed
aggregation; a per-facility LSTM classifier trained online from grid-health-delta reward; a
Stackelberg defense optimizer issuing OpenC2 commands over MQTT; and the actuator
framework that closes the loop back to the simulated environment. The integrated system
passes 335+ automated tests across the main repository and two service submodules.
 What was tested. A three-baseline-mode experimental campaign was conducted in
May 2026, totaling approximately 224 hours of evaluation runtime across 28 eight-hour
iterations: 16 iterations of the online RL/GT loop (full configuration), 8 iterations of a
frozen-classifier baseline (RL classifier trained for three hours, then weights frozen while the
GT defense selector continued operating), and 4 iterations of a Stackelberg-only baseline
(uniform attack-probability prior, no RL classifier). All twelve attack classes were exercised

across five simulated facilities in every iteration. Aggregated, the campaign produced
6,031 classification records suitable for analysis (3,429 online, 1,700 frozen, 902 gt only).
Diagnostic figures were ordered along the data pipeline (physical-layer signal → network-
layer signal → classifier output structure → classifier learning trajectory → reward linkage
→ grid outcome) so that each figure tested a distinct stage of the RL/GT loop.
 What was found. Both upstream signal sources — the autoencoder-based anomaly
detector and the Suricata IDS — were verified functional; the autoencoder produces
partial attack-vs-normal separation at the per-classification-window granularity (Cohen’s
𝑑 ≈ +0.46), and the IDS produces strong separation (𝑑 ≈ +1.38). The online RL/GT loop
did not produce defense-driving distributions distinguishable from chance. The classifier’s
mean P(true class) in the online mode was 0.087 vs the random baseline 0.083 (𝑝 = 0.30, not
significant); argmax accuracy 0.089 (1.06× random); and grid-health over training iterations
was flat across all modes. The frozen baseline performed indistinguishably from random
and slightly below the continuously-trained online mode (argmax 0.077, 0.92× random,
𝑝 = 0.40), so continued online training neither rescues nor materially degrades the early-
bootstrap classifier — both regimes sit at chance. The reward signal driving online updates
was evaluated with a multi-lens robustness test (threshold split, hurdle decomposition,
trimmed-Pearson sensitivity) chosen to avoid the over-interpretation hazard that single-
statistic correlation summaries pose on heavy-tailed zero-inflated reward distributions of
this kind. The convergent reading across all three lenses is that the reward signal carries
weak directional coupling with classifier correctness in the methodology-expected direction:
top-decile P(true) records produce observable grid-state changes more often than bottom-
decile records (composite 69% → 89%, Fisher exact 𝑝 < 10−10 ) and carry slightly larger
negative deltas (composite mean diff −0.046, Mann-Whitney 𝑝 = 0.029, top-vs-bot Cohen’s
𝑑 ≈ −0.19). Effect magnitudes are small (Pearson 𝑟2 < 0.5% on all three reward channels)
and per-class breakdowns do not survive multi-lens convergence at the per-class sample
sizes available; the coupling reading is a pooled-record property, not a per-class one. The
reward channel is informative but underpowered: the gradient information available to
the classifier is in the right direction but at magnitudes that the optimization procedure
cannot extract over 28 iterations. The decisive diagnostic was a pair of supervised ceilings
on the same per-window feature vectors. A same-architecture supervised LSTM trained
offline with cross-entropy on the same per-facility structure reached macro F1 ≈ 0.34 versus
0.07 for the actual online RL classifier (a 4.7× gap with the only difference being the
training procedure). A multinomial logistic-regression classifier under matched per-EID
+ chronological handicaps reached macro F1 ≈ 0.70 (10× deployed) and ≈ 0.79 pooled
across facilities (11× deployed), showing the features themselves support substantially more
signal than even the same-architecture offline-trained variant extracts at this data scale.
The training-procedure deficit is bracketed between 4.7× (lower bound, architecture held
constant) and 10–11× (upper bound, linear classifier on the same features). The features
carry signal that could yield more informative distributions for the GT engine; the online
training procedure does not extract it, and the reward signal that drives that training is
misaligned with the metric the loop is meant to improve.

1 INTRODUCTION
1.1 Research Context
Distributed energy resources — grid-connected inverters serving rooftop solar, behind-
the-meter storage, electric-vehicle chargers, and small-scale wind — are now a material
fraction of bulk-power generation in many regions, and their cybersecurity properties are
correspondingly material to grid reliability. Unlike traditional bulk-generation assets, DER
inverters are deployed at scale across customer premises, are reachable through utility-,
aggregator-, and vendor-managed communication paths, and expose telemetry and control
interfaces over industrial protocols (Modbus, DNP3, IEEE 2030.5) that were not designed
with adversarial environments in mind. The resulting attack surface is well-documented
in the DER cybersecurity literature (Section 2.1), with FDI, MITM, DoS, replay, and
firmware exploitation all reported as feasible against grid-connected inverters and their
control channels.
 The defensive picture is shaped by an operational constraint that does not affect conven-
tional enterprise security ML: it is infeasible to collect labeled attack data at the scale and
on the diversity that supervised classifiers typically need, in production DER environments.
Real attacks are rare, ground-truth labels are expensive to obtain, and attack types observed
in one facility may never recur elsewhere. The training-data scarcity problem motivates
approaches that learn from operationally available signals — post-decision grid-health ob-
servations, anomaly scores, network-side alert telemetry — rather than from human-labeled
attack examples.

1.2 Hypothesis Tested
This report presents the design and evaluation of one such approach: an integrated online
RL / Stackelberg game-theoretic defense pipeline for DER cybersecurity, developed within
the AdCyDER project (DOE CESER funded). The pipeline couples a per-facility online
RL classifier — which produces an attack-type probability distribution from per-window
features — with a Stackelberg defense selector that consumes those distributions alongside
subject-matter-expert priors over (defense, attack) effectiveness pairings and per-defense
costs, and selects the OpenC2 defense action that minimizes expected grid impact. The
classifier is trained online from delayed grid-health-delta reward derived from the post-
defense composite of an autoencoder ADS and a rolling-baseline IDS density, rather than
from labeled attack data.
  The hypothesis tested is that, in this configuration, the loop will learn to produce per-
facility attack-type distributions that drive effective Stackelberg defense selection from
operational outcomes alone — without requiring the labeled training data the operational
environment cannot supply. The scientific bet is that SME-encoded defense structure plus
operationally-grounded reward can substitute for the labels that conventional supervised
approaches require.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
This section reviews the prior work that the AdCyDER design draws on. The discussion
is organized along the data path of the integrated loop — the DER attack surface and
the industrial protocols that expose it; the machine-learning detection techniques used
on operational-technology (OT) traffic; the testbed methodology used to evaluate cyber-
physical defenses under realistic conditions; the Stackelberg game-theoretic framework for
defender decision-making; the reinforcement-learning challenges introduced by sparse and
delayed reward signals; the calibration properties of modern neural-network classifiers; and
the production-ML engineering literature that motivates the diagnostic protocol used in
Section 4.

2.1 DER Attack Surface and Industrial Control Protocol Exposure
Cybersecurity research on distributed energy resources consistently identifies the same set
of attack surfaces: inverter telemetry interfaces, control channels (Modbus, DNP3, IEEE
2030.5), and remote operational endpoints used by aggregators, vendors, and utilities to
monitor and command grid-connected inverters [1–4]. The threat catalogue documented
across these surveys includes false-data injection (FDI), man-in-the-middle (MITM), denial-
of-service (DoS), replay, and firmware exploitation, with smart-inverter communication
and remote-access interfaces highlighted as a primary entry point [1, 5]. Work focused on
grid-connected PV inverters specifically demonstrates FDI, DoS, and replay attacks against
sampled signals and issued commands at the converter interface [6], and Chen et al. study
cyber-attack detection and countermeasures for distributed power-electronic converters in
microgrid contexts [7].
  The protocols that carry these control channels were not designed for adversarial en-
vironments. Modbus was specified without security measures and uses register addresses

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 13

for control; DNP3 was not originally designed with built-in authentication, integrity, or
encryption [3, 8]. The IEEE 1815 (DNP3) standard subsequently added a Secure Authenti-
cation mechanism as an extension and explicitly leaves transport-layer security (IPSec/TLS)
out of scope, implicitly confirming that the base specification lacked these protections [9].
Zografopoulos et al. note the same property and document specific message interception,
modification, and fabrication attacks that the lack of base-protocol security enables [2].
AdCyDER’s choice to instrument Modbus and DNP3 traffic at facilities (Section 3.2) is
motivated by this exposure pattern.

Standardized cyber-defense action and transport. Translating a defender’s chosen response
into a machine-actionable command on a distributed fleet of DER endpoints requires both a
standardized action vocabulary (so that the same instruction can be issued by any analytic
engine and consumed by any responder agent without bespoke integration per pairing)
and a standardized message-transport substrate (so that command delivery scales across
many facilities without per-facility connection management). For the action vocabulary,
AdCyDER adopts Open Command and Control (OpenC2), the OASIS standard for machine-
readable cyber-defense action commands. OpenC2 specifies an action-target-args grammar
(e.g., {action: deny, target: {ipv4 net: 10.0.0.0/24}}) intended to be producer-
and consumer-agnostic; defense logic that emits OpenC2 can be paired with any actuator
framework that consumes OpenC2, decoupling decision-making from enforcement. For the
transport substrate, AdCyDER uses MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport), an
OASIS-standard publish-subscribe protocol designed for many-to-many messaging over high-
latency, intermittently-connected networks — characteristics that match the operational
reality of geographically distributed DER endpoints behind aggregator gateways. MQTT’s
broker-mediated topic structure also lets each facility subscribe only to commands targeted
at it, providing a natural per-facility addressing scheme without a custom routing layer.
AdCyDER’s specific use of OpenC2-over-MQTT is described in Section 3.6.

2.2 Machine-Learning Detection in OT Environments
Two ML detection paradigms are relevant to AdCyDER’s anomaly-detection subsystem
(ADS) and supplementary IDS layer: reconstruction-based unsupervised anomaly detection
and signature- or rule-based intrusion detection.
  Autoencoder-based anomaly detection trains a model on normal-operation data and flags
test inputs whose reconstruction error departs from the learned distribution. Oyedotun
et al. apply this directly in SCADA and DCS environments, training autoencoders on
normal multimodal telemetry and flagging anomalous inputs by reconstruction error [10].
Alharbi reports an analogous setup using a variational autoencoder trained on normal
renewable-energy-grid behavior, with significant reconstruction error used as the anomaly
signal [11]; broader smart-grid surveys document the same pattern of reconstruction-
error thresholding for abnormal behavior [12]. AdCyDER’s embedded autoencoder ADS
(Section 3.2), developed by Plathottam et al. [13], follows this established design.
  Signature-based network intrusion detection uses predefined rule sets and pattern matches
to emit categorized alerts. Mogilicharla et al. describe such a rule-based NIDS in a DER

microgrid intrusion-detection deployment [5]; this is the most direct prior reference in our
corpus to the design pattern that Suricata occupies in our pipeline. We are not aware of a
published evaluation of Suricata specifically on DER traffic, and we treat the rule-based
NIDS layer as an established design pattern rather than a contribution.
 The choice of LSTM lookback window for sequential security-event classification involves a
tradeoff between temporal context and per-entity data volume. Luay et al. report empirical
evaluation across LSTM sequence lengths from 10 to 100 in network intrusion detection,
finding that shorter windows can outperform longer ones because longer sequences dilute
localized temporal patterns [14]. We do not treat this as decisive guidance for our 3-window
(15-minute) lookback, but it indicates that the choice is a real design parameter rather than
a free one.

2.3 Cyber-Physical Testbeds for DER Security Research
Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testbeds combining real protective relays, inverters, smart
meters, RTUs, or PMUs with simulated power-system dynamics are used to evaluate cyber-
physical defense mechanisms under operationally realistic conditions. Nguyen et al. describe
a real-time cyber-physical testbed integrating protective relays, PMUs, RTUs, and an
RTAC with simulated NYS power-grid dynamics for cybersecurity assessment of large-scale
power systems [15]; Mogilicharla et al. report a laboratory-scale testbed integrating a real
plant-level inverter, PV emulator, battery, smart meter, and Modbus communications to
validate a hybrid intrusion-detection method [5]; and Liu et al. note the use of HIL testbeds
in DER cybersecurity research more generally [4].
 OPAL-RT real-time simulators are a recurring component of these testbeds. Sangoleye et
al. describe an SNL microgrid cyber-physical testbed using an OPAL-RT OP5600 simulator
to run real-time simulated generators and networking devices alongside live cyber-attacks
(brute-force, DoS, port-scan) [16], and Nguyen et al. similarly use OPAL-RT (alongside
RTDS and EXata) as a real-time simulator hosting the control center, PDC, and detailed
EMT models including offshore wind farms within a cyber-physical testbed [15]. AdCyDER’s
HIL trajectory described in Section 3 fits within this established methodology.
 A complementary stream of work uses purely synthetic data generators in place of HIL
hardware. Farsi et al. report a simulation-based smart-grid dataset providing reproducibility
and labeled ground truth for cyber-attack scenarios [17]. The general tradeoff — repro-
ducibility and ground truth at the cost of fidelity to operational telemetry — is accepted in
this literature, and AdCyDER’s containerized synthetic generator (Section 3.2) sits at this
end of the spectrum during the campaign reported here.

2.4 Stackelberg Security Games for Defender Decision-Making
The Stackelberg framework for security games models defender–attacker interactions as
sequential leader–follower games in which the defender commits first to a strategy and
the attacker best-responds, with Bayesian extensions accommodating uncertainty over
attacker types. The framework is broadly used across the cyber-physical security and IIoT
literatures: Zhang et al. review attack–defense game models for IIoT and explicitly charac-
terize Stackelberg games as sequential leader-commits/followers-respond interactions [18];

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 15

Clempner formulates Bayesian–Markov Stackelberg security games for deceptive defense
and attack tactics, with defender-commits-first / attacker-best-responds structure and
Bayesian handling of attacker-type uncertainty [19]; Ait Temghart et al. apply Stackelberg
security games to allocate limited defensive resources in cloud-computing security [20];
Sengupta and Kambhampati formulate Bayesian Stackelberg Markov games for adaptive
moving-target defense, with explicit defender-leader / attacker-follower commitment under
attacker-type uncertainty [21]; Zhang and Malacaria formulate cyber-security defense as
a Stackelberg game with a defender committing to a security portfolio and use Bayesian
Stackelberg games for uncertainty over attack types [22]; Li and Zheng describe Stack-
elberg security games as defender-leader / attacker-follower commitments and reference
Stackelberg/Bayesian-Stackelberg models addressing attack-type uncertainty [23]; and Deng
et al. apply Stackelberg Markov games to sequential deception defense against advanced
persistent threats [24]. Cox provides early framing of attacker–defender interactions as
leader–follower (Stackelberg) games with the defender committing to a defensive resource
allocation observed by the attacker [25], and Lian et al. note in passing that Stackelberg
games leverage the defender’s informational advantage [26].
  The defender’s commitment is to a strategy, and AdCyDER’s GT defense selector
(Section 3.5) instantiates exactly this structure: the defender minimizes an expected-loss
functional over a discrete action set, with the loss parameterized by per-action effectiveness
against each attack type, per-attack baseline impact, and per-action cost. The classifier’s
posterior over attack types plays the role of the defender’s belief over attacker types in the
Bayesian extension.

 On the source of game parameters: SME priors versus learned parameters. A live debate
in the Stackelberg-security-games literature concerns where the game’s payoff parame-
ters — per-action effectiveness, per-attack impact, per-action cost — should come from.
Sengupta and Kambhampati argue against relying on security-expert-supplied priors for
these parameters, on the grounds that experts may not provide accurate values for impact,
defense performance, or switching cost, and propose learning them from interaction in-
stead [21]. AdCyDER takes the opposite design choice: the GT layer’s effectiveness matrix,
baseline-impact vector, and cost vector are encoded by SME judgement and held fixed
during operation, while the RL classifier learns only the attack-type posterior that is fed
into the otherwise-static Stackelberg loss. This is a deliberate trade-off given the project’s
operating constraints — ground-truth attack labels are infeasible to collect at the scale
that learning the full game parameter set would require, and SME calibration of the ef-
fectiveness/impact/cost vectors is the available alternative within scope. The trade-off is
itself a candidate failure mode for the loop, and we return to it in the discussion of next
steps (Section 7.2): if the hypothesis tested in this report is to be re-tested under different
parameterizations, jointly learning the GT parameters alongside the classifier posterior — in
the spirit of Sengupta and Kambhampati — is a natural alternative to the SME-prior-only
configuration evaluated here.

2.5 Reinforcement Learning with Delayed Reward
Reinforcement learning faces a credit-assignment problem because the consequences of
an action can manifest many time steps after it is taken, leaving the agent to determine
retrospectively which of its earlier decisions a given reward should be attributed to [27].
AdCyDER’s online RL classifier inherits this difficulty in a particularly demanding form:
the reward signal is the change in grid health following a defense action, which is itself the
consequence of (i) the classifier’s posterior over attack types, (ii) the GT selector’s chosen
defense, and (iii) the actuator’s physical effect on the simulated power system. The chain
from classification decision to grid-health observation is several pipeline stages long, and
the agent receives no per-stage feedback — only the final, lagged grid-health delta.
  Reinforcement-learning approaches to cybersecurity decision-making have been proposed
where labeled attack data is infeasible to collect at scale: Babar et al., for example, motivate
hierarchical RL for autonomous cyber-incident detection in industrial cyber-physical systems
by the impracticality of manual or rule-based maintenance of detection systems [28]. The
broader RL-for-IDS literature is large but, in our reading, focuses primarily on stationary
supervised or batch settings rather than the closed-loop online setting AdCyDER targets.

2.6 Reward Misspecification and Proxy-Objective Divergence
A failure mode well-documented in the AI-safety and RL literatures is reward hacking or
specification gaming: an RL agent achieves high proxy reward under a misspecified reward
function while degrading the true objective [29, 30]. Pan et al. demonstrate empirically
that more capable agents exploit misspecified proxy rewards more aggressively, exhibiting
phase transitions in which the true reward sharply drops as proxy reward rises [31]. Skalse
et al. provide a formal definition of reward hacking as optimization of a proxy reward
leading to decreased true reward, and explicitly reference the Pan et al. phase-transition
observations [32]. Amodei et al. position reward hacking among the foundational accident-
risk problems for RL deployed in real-world systems [30].
 This literature is directly relevant to the findings reported in Section 5: the absent-
or-anti-correlated relationship we observe between AdCyDER’s classifier correctness and
the grid-health-delta reward (Q6) is the diagnostic signature these works describe of
a misspecified or hacked proxy reward. In the framework of Amodei et al., a related
methodological recommendation we adopt is the use of a baseline trained only on labeled
episodes to isolate the effect of label scarcity in semi-supervised RL — the same role our
supervised-LSTM ceiling test plays for online versus offline training [30].

2.7 Neural-Network Calibration
Modern deep neural-network classifiers are commonly miscalibrated: they produce over-
confident softmax probabilities, and calibration tends to deteriorate during training even
as classification accuracy improves [33]. Guo et al. quantify this with expected calibration
error (ECE) and reliability diagrams, showing that confidence and accuracy are conceptu-
ally distinct quantities — a model can be accurate while remaining poorly calibrated, or

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 17

confident while remaining inaccurate [33]. Their proposed remedy — temperature scaling,
a single-parameter variant of Platt scaling — is shown to be effective on most datasets.
  This distinction is central to the diagnostic methodology used in Section 4. Earlier
project status reports for AdCyDER tracked classifier confidence trajectory as the headline
learning-progress metric; the distributional accuracy and per-class P(correct) diagnostics
that supersede that framing in this report are motivated directly by the Guo et al. finding
that confidence and correctness are independent quantities.

2.8 Production ML Systems: Technical Debt and Continual Learning
Two strands of production-ML literature inform the diagnostic protocol applied in Section 4.
 The first is the body of work on hidden technical debt and engineering practice for
ML systems. Sculley et al. introduce the concept of hidden technical debt in ML systems,
documenting that data dependencies, glue code, and configuration debt frequently dominate
the model code itself, and arguing for treating these as first-class engineering concerns [34].
Breck et al. formalize this into the “ML test score,” a rubric for production ML readiness
that requires dedicated tests and monitoring of data, features, infrastructure, and model
behavior beyond conventional unit tests of the model code, and explicitly notes that many
production failures originate in upstream data rather than the model itself [35]. Amershi et
al. document analogous engineering challenges in software engineering for ML, including data
pipelines, feedback loops, and component entanglement [36]. The pipeline-stage diagnostic
protocol used in Section 4 (Q1–Q7 along the data path) is a direct application of this
principle: the protocol forces each stage’s input data, intermediate output, and downstream
behavior to be inspected independently before model-level conclusions are drawn.
 The second is the continual-learning literature on training neural networks on non-
stationary data streams. Parisi et al. and Delange et al. both establish that neural networks
trained continually on non-stationary streams suffer from catastrophic forgetting, and that
standard evaluation contrasts continual-learning methods against joint (offline) training as
an upper reference and naive fine-tuning as a lower-bound baseline [37, 38]. Kirkpatrick et
al. analyze catastrophic forgetting in the sequential-learning setting and contrast against
joint multitask training [39]. AdCyDER’s three baseline modes (online, frozen, gt only) and
the supervised-LSTM ceiling test (Section 4) instantiate the same family of comparisons —
online versus frozen approximates the continually-trained-versus-static-snapshot contrast,
and the supervised offline LSTM provides the joint-training upper-reference that the
continual-learning literature uses to bound achievable performance.

3.2 Synthetic SCADA Generator
Origin and motivation. The SCADA generator was developed as a controlled, fully-
instrumented evaluation environment in response to testbed-integration constraints
encountered during the project. The original program plan called for the integrated
ADS/IDS and mitigator components developed at ANL and UND to run against a
hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testbed maintained by a second university partner, with the
partner team responsible for deploying the attack suite on that testbed and coupling it

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 19

to the upstream detection and downstream actuator components. The framework was
in fact deployed to the partner testbed, but the planned attack-suite deployment and
ADS/IDS/mitigator coupling on the partner side did not materialize by the time of this
report. The testbed’s actuator configuration also supported only firewall-level traffic
blocking — a single action within the broader defense action space (rate limit, quarantine,
redirect, and others) that the GT engine and actuator framework were designed around —
and that action was not implemented during the project. The other mitigations developed
by UND and ANL were not feasible to instantiate on the testbed as configured.
  To enable end-to-end evaluation of the integrated RL/GT loop, the team built the
synthetic SCADA generator described below, in which all twelve attack types and the
full defense action space can be exercised together against physically-grounded inverter
telemetry. This substitution is a meaningful limitation of the work reported here: the
synthetic generator captures attack-type variety, time-alignment across data streams, and
an autoencoder-based physics anomaly signal, but it does not substitute for the operational
realism that a HIL testbed would have provided. We return to this point in Section 7 as
the highest-priority next-step environment for re-testing the underlying hypothesis.

Components. The SCADA generator produces time-aligned synthetic data representing
five DER facilities, each running the DNP3 protocol typical of inverter-tied distributed
generation; its source resides under src/stack/dockerfiles/adcyder-scada-generator/.
The five simulated facilities use a single Fronius Primo inverter profile (same nameplate
capacity, same calibration parameters, same anomaly-detector model and thresholds) and
differ from one another only by their IP subnet (172.21.0.0/24 through 172.25.0.0/24)
and EID label. Each facility draws independently from a shared campaign-selection dis-
tribution (CAMPAIGN MIX in settings.py), so the realized attack streams across facilities
are independent random samples from the same expected distribution; a single shared
CampaignSequencer additionally enforces history-aware constraints (no immediate repeti-
tion, reconnaissance-to-exploitation chaining) across all five facilities globally. The result
is that cross-facility variation in observed data comes from independent attack scheduling
rather than from facility-intrinsic differences. Operational facility diversity that would mat-
ter in production — vendor, firmware, nameplate capacity, baseline traffic load, aggregator
interfaces, distribution-feeder topology — is intentionally unmodeled in this campaign.
The per-facility classifier architecture is designed for the operational case where facilities
additionally differ in physical and network characteristics; our simulation therefore tests
only the data-volume aspect of per-facility specialization (each per-facility classifier sees
one-fifth the data the pooled cross-facility variant sees) rather than the physical-diversity
aspect. We discuss the implication for the supervised LSTM ceiling result in Section 5.9 (the
≈ 1.2× pooled-vs-per-EID gap is a data-volume effect alone, not a physical-diversity effect;
in production with genuine facility diversity the gap could be larger or smaller depending
on whether per-facility specialization gains outweigh the data-volume penalty), and as a
limitation in Section 6.5. The generator orchestrates the twelve attack classes enumerated

autoencoder is loaded from a pre-trained model artifact at container startup and is not
retrained during the closed-loop campaign — this isolates ADS performance from any drift
in the upstream generator.

3.2.2 Mitigation State Propagation. The closing arrow in Figure 1 carries the actuator’s
deployed-mitigation state back into the generator’s mitigator state module. This is the
mechanism by which the simulated environment reflects actions taken by the defense pipeline.
For example, once the GT engine has issued a DENY command against a source IP, the
generator’s subsequent flow synthesis observes the suppression, the inverter physics observes
the (now reduced) attack intensity, and the ADS aggregates over the window therefore
reflect a partially-defended environment. Without this propagation the loop would be open
— the generator would produce attack traffic identical regardless of any defense action,
defeating the purpose of measuring grid-health-delta as a learning signal.

3.3 Ingestion and Feature Aggregation
The ingestion path moves raw streams from the SCADA generator into queryable storage
suitable for the RL/GT loop’s read patterns. Three infrastructure choices determine the
path’s behavior, and the rationale for each is worth recording so that future deployments
evaluating substitutions can reason about the trade-offs.
 Kafka as broker. The four data streams (inverter telemetry, netflow, IDS alerts, ground
truth) are produced at independent cadences by the generator and consumed at different
cadences by downstream services (Fluent Bit at ingest cadence, the aggregator on a 5-minute
window cadence, the RL classifier on training cadence). A pub-sub broker decouples the
producer from the consumers and lets each consumer apply its own backpressure and
retry policy without the producer needing to know about them. Kafka was chosen over
alternatives (Redis Streams, RabbitMQ, NATS) for its at-least-once delivery semantics,
durable replay (so a re-instantiated consumer can re-process from a chosen offset rather
than losing messages), and operational familiarity within the project team. RabbitMQ is
also present in the deployment but serves a different role (RPC-style request/reply for
actuator command acknowledgements, not bulk telemetry transport).
 OpenSearch as searchable telemetry store. The downstream feature aggregator does not
consume the raw event stream linearly; it queries time-windowed slices of telemetry per
facility and per source IP. OpenSearch (an Elastic-compatible distributed search index)
provides the time-range and field-level filtering this query pattern needs at the data scale
we generate (104 –106 events per hour across the five-facility deployment), without per-
aggregator-call full table scans of the underlying telemetry. A Fluent Bit indexing pipeline
(replacing an earlier Logstash configuration retired for resource-overhead reasons) writes
Kafka-resident events to OpenSearch indices on a per-stream basis.
 MongoDB as feature store and operational state. The aggregator’s per-window feature
vectors, the RL classifier’s per-window output (predicted class, confidence, attack-probability
distribution, feature vector), the GT engine’s per-decision logs, the reward records with

grid-health decomposition, and the ground-truth attack records are all persisted to Mon-
goDB. A document store rather than a relational database fits the schema variety here
(each collection has a different field set, several fields are nested objects), and the op-
erational queries are point lookups by composite key ((eid, src ip, window id)) and
time-range scans rather than complex joins. MongoDB’s secondary-index support on (eid,
timestamp) and patterned id provides the per-facility and per-window scoping the analy-
sis pipeline needs without a query-planner-driven schema. The aggregator’s source resides
under src/stack/dockerfiles/opensearch aggregator/.

(RL WINDOW DURATION MS = 300000) and produces one feature record per (Equipment ID,

netflow, IDS alert, and ADS samples within the window and joins them into a single record
stored in the MongoDB features collection. The RL classifier subsequently aggregates each
per-window record into a 22-dimensional numeric feature vector with the following layout.
All values are scalar counts, fractions, or scalar statistics — the LSTM input contains no
categorical or list-valued fields:
 ∙ Netflow (5): total packets, total bytes, flow count, mean packet size, and
 unique dst port count (a scalar count of distinct ports, not the port values them-
 selves).
 ∙ Temporal (3): hour sin, hour cos (a circular encoding of window-start hour-of-

 ∙ Destination IP hash (4): a 4-bin hashing-trick encoding of the most-frequent
 destination IP within the window. Captures which host(s) within the facility were
 targeted, without exposing raw IP values to the classifier.
 ∙ IDS (2): per-alert-category sums over the window, sorted by category key for
 deterministic ordering. The number of IDS feature dimensions is determined at
 startup from the IDS schema observed in the data; for the configuration evaluated

 ∙ ADS (3): mean anomaly (window mean reconstruction error, including zero-scored

 score near zero with occasional large spikes, so a single mean masks attack signal.
 ∙ SCADA (5): two protocol-fraction features (protocol modbus frac and
 protocol dnp3 frac) and three operation-rate features (write rate, control rate,
 and error rate).

3.4 Reinforcement Learning Attack Classifier
The RL classifier (src/stack/dockerfiles/attack classifier/src/rl engine.py) is a
per-facility LSTM that consumes per-window feature vectors and produces per-attack-type
probability distributions rather than hard-label predictions. The probability-distribution
output (rather than argmax classification) is a deliberate design choice: real-world attacks
are diverse and frequently combine elements of more than one of the twelve catalog classes
(e.g., a reconnaissance scan that blends PROBE SCAN and BRUTE FORCE traffic patterns, or a
malware-driven control deviation that overlaps MALWARE C2 and CONTROL OVERRIDE), and
a defender selecting a mitigation gains more from a hedged distribution over plausible
attack types than from an overconfident single-label commitment that may mis-target the
response. Each facility is identified by its aggregator-device Equipment ID (EID), and a
separate model instance is instantiated and trained per EID; per-facility traffic patterns
drive per-facility classification weights without inter-facility cross-contamination.

3.4.1 Architecture. Each per-facility classifier is a Keras Sequential model. We use Keras
for the classifier because the ANL CEEESA autoencoder ADS (Section 3.2) was published
as a Keras artifact, and adopting the same framework throughout the pipeline avoids
cross-framework serialization, version-pinning, and runtime-image-size friction. The layer
stack, identical across all facilities and reproduced in the supervised LSTM ceiling test
described in Section 4:
 (1) Input of shape (RL SEQUENCE LENGTH=3, feature count=22): a sequence of three

3.4.2 Grid-Health Composite Score. The grid-health score that drives the reward signal is a
per-facility composite of the autoencoder-based ADS anomaly score and a rolling-baseline
IDS alert density, computed in the GT engine (gt engine.py:: get current grid health)
as

 grid health(𝑒𝑖𝑑) = 𝑤ADS · 𝑠ADS (𝑒𝑖𝑑) + 𝑤IDS · 𝑠IDS (𝑒𝑖𝑑), 𝑤ADS = 𝑤IDS = 0.5

where both sub-scores are in [0, 1] with 0 indicating normal operation and 1 indicating
maximum anomaly. The composite is computed at decision time from MongoDB state,
not pre-aggregated. The 50/50 weighting reflects the modeling decision that physical-
layer attacks (visible to the autoencoder) and network-layer attacks (visible to Suricata)
are a priori equally important to the loop’s notion of facility health; both weights are
configurable via the GRID HEALTH ADS WEIGHT and GRID HEALTH IDS WEIGHT environment
variables. The two sub-scores are not symmetric in expected firing rate: of the twelve attack
classes (Table 1), only three (CONTROL OVERRIDE, UNAUTHORIZED CONTROL, severe DOS DDOS)
have mechanisms that perturb inverter physics in a way the autoencoder is positioned to
detect, while all twelve generate network-observable traffic and are therefore reachable by
Suricata. The composite reward is consequently expected to be IDS-dominated in aggregate
even when ADS calibration is correct; this is a property of the attack-catalog composition,

3.4.3 Online Training from Grid-Health Feedback. The classifier is trained online from delayed
grid-health reward rather than from labeled attack data. The training cycle is:
 (1) For each completed classification window, the classifier records the (EID, src ip,
 window start) identifier in a pending grid outcomes dict (also persisted to the
 MongoDB pending outcomes collection for crash recovery), along with the pre-
 defense grid-health composite (Section 3.4.2) sampled at the time of the GT decision.
 (2) After the configured grid-health observation delay (defense-dependent, in the 5–
 60 minute range), the GT engine recomputes the grid-health composite for the
 same EID and computes grid delta = grid health after − grid health before using
 the composite definition of Section 3.4.2. A negative delta indicates the composite
 anomaly score decreased (defense worked); positive delta indicates degradation.
 (3) The shaped reward is computed as 𝑟shaped = 𝑐 · tanh(𝑟raw /𝑐) where the clamp
 scale 𝑐 is set by the constant REWARD SOFT CLAMP SCALE = 50 and 𝑟raw is the
 unclamped raw reward, composed as (base reward + ids bonus) · severity multiplier ·
 magnitude multiplier. The base reward depends on the defense scenario — a
 correctly-defended attack scores +20, a missed attack −15, a correct ALLOW on
 benign traffic a small positive value, a wrong defense on benign traffic between −2
 and +2, and so on. The tanh soft-clamp serves two purposes. First, it bounds reward
 magnitude to ±50, preventing any single feedback event with a pathological raw
 reward (e.g., a transient grid-health-delta spike unrelated to the deployed defense, or
 compounding multiplier interactions) from dominating an online weight update and
 destabilizing training. Second, tanh saturates smoothly rather than hard-clipping:
 rewards in the small-to-moderate range (where the signal is most informative) pass
 through approximately linearly and preserve their relative ordering, while extreme
 rewards saturate gracefully toward the clamp scale instead of being truncated. The
 smooth saturation keeps the gradient well-behaved across the full input range, in
 contrast to a hard clip that would zero the gradient outside the clip region.
 (4) Reward records are persisted to rl rewards with a baseline mode tag for down-
 stream comparative analysis.
 (5) The accumulated experience buffer is used to retrain the per-facility classifier in
 batches. The main loop wakes every RL SLEEP INTERVAL seconds (default 300, i.e.
 matching the window cadence) and triggers a training pass whenever at least 10 new
 reward records are available, using minibatches of 10–100 records per pass. Under
 this configuration training is essentially continuous over the lifetime of the deployed
 classifier — not gated to any external cadence.

3.5.3 Boltzmann Exploration. To prevent the GT engine from locking onto an early-learned
defense for any given attack-probability shape, the engine samples defenses from a Boltzmann
distribution over the loss landscape with a decaying temperature schedule (initial 𝑇0 = 50,
decay 0.9999 per decision, minimum 𝑇min = 1.0). Early in training the engine selects defenses
approximately stochastically; at long-run convergence it approaches the deterministic argmin.
When the configured temperature is set to zero or below, the engine selects the deterministic
argmin directly — this mode is used by the unit test suite.

  Trial-duration interaction. The decay schedule is wall-clock-time based, not decision-count
based: 𝑇 (𝑡) = 𝑇0 · exp(−𝜆𝑡) where 𝑡 is wall-clock seconds since the GT engine’s exploration
start. The decay rate 𝜆 is sized via the GT EXPLORATION DECAY TARGET HOURS configuration
parameter (default 8 hours) so that the temperature decays from 𝑇0 = 50 to 𝑇min = 1
over exactly one trial duration. By design, then, each 8-hour evaluation iteration begins in
the exploration regime (𝑇 = 50) and arrives at the near-deterministic regime (𝑇 ≈ 1) by
trial end. Container restart at the start of each iteration resets exploration start time
so each iteration gets a full exploration arc rather than persisting a decayed temperature
across iterations. In persistent production deployment (no per-iteration restart) the temper-
ature would continue decaying past 𝑇min at which point the engine would operate purely
deterministically; the same configuration parameter governs the decay rate in both regimes.

3.6 Actuator Framework and Closed-Loop Coupling
The actuator framework (adcyder-actuator/ submodule) translates the GT engine’s
chosen defense into an OpenC2 action command (Section 2.1) and delivers it over MQTT
to the responder enclave for the targeted facility. Each command carries the action verb
(DENY, ALLOW, RATE LIMIT, etc.), the target specifier (source IP, port, protocol), and
metadata including the originating attack-type prediction and the GT engine’s confidence.
  For the synthetic-data evaluation reported here, the actuator’s role is twofold: (i) record
receipt of the command (and acknowledge to the GT engine, which then schedules the post-
defense grid-health observation), and (ii) update the SCADA generator’s mitigator state
so that subsequent traffic and physics reflect the deployed defense. The responder framework

3.7 Cross-Cutting Design Choices
3.7.1 Window Cadence and Per-EID Isolation. The 5-minute aggregation window is a de-
liberate compromise between feedback latency (shorter is better for online learning) and
statistical power per window (longer is better for distinguishing attack from normal). At 5
minutes a single window sees enough netflow and ADS samples to produce stable aggregates,
while still being short enough that the training loop — which wakes on the same cadence
and trains whenever at least 10 new reward records are available — can update weights
frequently. Per-facility isolation prevents one facility’s classifier weights from influencing
another’s; this is essential to the original learning hypothesis (per-facility adaptation) but
also creates a per-facility data-volume constraint that the supervised LSTM ceiling test
(Section 5) shows is consequential. The 8-hour duration of each evaluation iteration in the
May 2026 campaign was an arbitrary wall-clock budget chosen to keep individual trial runs
tractable, not a system parameter — the deployed classifier would continue training and
inferring indefinitely.

3.7.2 Fail-Loud Error Handling. The pipeline policy is to raise on missing or sentinel data
rather than substitute defaults. When the OpenSearch aggregator cannot produce an
ADS aggregate for a window (e.g., zero samples in the window), it writes a sentinel record
(anomaly score = -1.0, anomaly score missing = True) rather than imputing zero. The
GT engine raises a custom GridHealthUnavailableError on encountering a sentinel rather
than treating it as “healthy.” The RL engine’s grid-outcome registration explicitly skips
sentinel records so they cannot contaminate the reward distribution. Diagnostic logging fires
a loud ERROR after three consecutive all-sentinel windows so that pipeline degradation is
visible in the operations log rather than silent.

3.7.3 AI-Auditor Correctness Remediation. Prior to the May 2026 evaluation campaign, the
integrated pipeline was subjected to three independent AI-driven correctness audits using
Claude Opus, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT 5.2 as adversarial reviewers. The audits identified
six independent classes of pipeline bugs spanning data integrity (reward sign inversions,
JSON double-encoding severing the GT→RL feedback link), feedback-loop construction
(cross-facility model contamination via patterned id format mismatch), command delivery
(lost OpenC2 acknowledgements), and resilience (in-memory pending-outcome state lost
on container restart). All findings were resolved across four structured remediation phases
with regression tests added for each fix; the integrated system now passes 335+ automated
tests across the main repository and two service submodules. The remediation history is the
audit trail demonstrating that the experimental campaign reflects the corrected pipeline
rather than a configuration with known correctness bugs.

3.8 Deployment and Infrastructure
The integrated pipeline runs as a set of Docker-containerized services orchestrated by an
Infrastructure-as-Code (IaC) configuration that targets either AWS (the original deployment
target) or LocalStack (the local emulator that became the primary deployment target after
the AWS-access pivot described in Section 3.2). The same source tree produces both
deployments; the choice is parameterized by a single configuration value.

3.8.1 Containerization. Every service in the pipeline is packaged as a Docker container
with a dedicated Dockerfile located under src/stack/dockerfiles/<service>/ in the

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 33

source tree. Pipeline services include the SCADA generator, Kafka, OpenSearch, Fluent
Bit consumer, OpenSearch aggregator, MongoDB, RL classifier, GT mitigator, MQTT
broker, the actuator framework, and a RabbitMQ broker for the actuator-acknowledgement
RPC channel. We follow the one-process-per-container convention, with each service ex-
posing only the ports it needs for declared inter-service interfaces. Container images use
officially-tagged base images and run as non-root users where possible. Service environ-
ment variables are injected at container startup from the IaC configuration; service code
reads its own configuration through a pair of files — settings.py (committed defaults)
and settingslocal.py (gitignored local overrides) — which the container loads from its
working directory. Container behavior is therefore fully reproducible from the committed
stack definition plus the recorded environment-variable values.

3.8.2 Infrastructure as Code (CDKTF). The deployment topology — service definitions,
networking, storage volumes, and inter-service connections (e.g., RL classifier → MongoDB,
mitigator → MQTT) — is declared in CDKTF (Cloud Development Kit for Terraform), a
Python-based IaC framework that compiles to standard Terraform JSON manifests. Each
service’s resources are declared once in CDKTF and rendered consistently for whichever
target backend the stack is being synthesized against. We chose CDKTF over hand-written
Terraform for two reasons: (i) Python lets the stack definition share validation logic and
constants with the service code (e.g., the same MongoDB collection names are referenced
from both the application and the IaC layer, eliminating a class of name-mismatch bugs),
and (ii) the project team is more fluent in Python than in HCL.

3.8.3 AWS / LocalStack Switching. The same CDKTF stack synthesizes against either
AWS or LocalStack with a single configuration value: an AWS ACCOUNT ID parameter that,
when set to the LocalStack convention value 000000000000, routes every Terraform-defined
service endpoint to the local emulator instead of the public AWS API. No service code
changes are required to switch between the two; the application layer sees the same boto3
/ AWS-SDK interfaces regardless of which backend is responding to the API calls. This
property enabled the pragmatic deployment pivot described in Section 3.2: when AWS
access proved infeasible, no rewrite or restructuring of the stack was required — only a
re-synthesis of the same CDKTF configuration against the alternate endpoint set. The
same property also allowed local development of the integrated pipeline without consuming
AWS resources or budget, and supports the reproducibility commitments documented in
Section 4.6: a future investigator with the source-tree commit and the recorded environment-
variable values can re-instantiate the entire pipeline in either environment without further
infrastructure work.

(uniform priors, no training), so a smaller number of trials is sufficient to characterize its
grid-outcome distribution.

  Trial duration. Each iteration ran for 8 hours of wall-clock time. This duration was an
arbitrary trial budget chosen to (i) be long enough to accumulate ∼96 reward records
per (EID, source-IP) tuple at the 5-minute window cadence, providing multiple per-EID
training passes within the trial; (ii) be short enough to fit several iterations into a continuous
evaluation window without exhausting compute; and (iii) start each iteration with a freshly-
initialized classifier container, so that any cross-iteration state leakage in per-facility LSTM
weights, sequence buffers, or pending-outcomes state was prevented by construction. The
8-hour duration is not a system parameter — the deployed classifier is designed to run
indefinitely — it is solely an experimental-design choice for sampling.

 Attack scenarios. Every iteration exercised all twelve attack classes enumerated in Sec-
tion 3.2 across all five simulated facilities. Attack campaigns were generated by the SCADA
generator’s orchestrator with persistent attacker IPs per (campaign, attack type, subnet) so
that windowed aggregation could collapse multiple flows from the same attacker into well-
populated per-(EID, src ip) windows. Coordinated multi-facility campaigns ran concurrently
across facilities to maximize attack diversity per training hour.

  Data products per iteration. Each iteration wrote classification, reward, GT-decision,
ground-truth, and per-window feature records into the MongoDB collections described in
Table 2. Aggregated across the 28 iterations, the campaign produced 6,031 classification
records suitable for analysis: 3,429 from online, 1,700 from frozen, and 902 from gt only.
The disparity in record counts versus iteration counts reflects per-iteration variation in
the volume of (EID, src ip) windows that produced both a classification and a paired
ground-truth record (some windows produced one without the other and were excluded
from the analyzable record count).

4.3.2 Pipeline-ordered diagnostic figures (Q1–Q7).

  Pipeline ordering. Diagnostic figures are produced from the per-iteration JSON files by

to follow the data path through the loop, so that each figure tests a distinct stage and
a failure at any one stage explains failures downstream of it without contaminating the
interpretation of upstream stages:

 Q2 (IDS upstream signal): does the network IDS produce attack-vs-normal sepa-

 formative about the true class, or is it concentrated on a small set of overconfident
 wrong classes?
 Q5 (classifier learning trajectory): does P(correct) improve over training-elapsed

 Q6 (reward–correctness causal link): is there a meaningful correlation between

  The pipeline ordering matters operationally as well as expositionally. If Q1 or Q2 reveals

4.4 Same-Architecture Supervised LSTM Ceiling Test
The Q1–Q7 figures characterize what each pipeline stage produces but do not, on their own,
distinguish between two distinct failure modes for the loop:
 ∙ Data-quality failure: the per-window features simply do not carry enough attack-
 discriminative signal for any per-EID LSTM to extract meaningful distinctions.
 Online learning would then be expected to fail regardless of the training procedure
 or the reward formulation.
 ∙ Training-procedure failure: the features carry signal that the LSTM architecture
 could in principle extract, but the online RL training procedure — with its delayed,
 sparse, soft-clamped grid-health-delta reward — fails to extract it.
  The same-architecture supervised LSTM ceiling test discriminates between these. It is im-
plemented in scripts/supervised baseline.py and run via make supervised-baseline.
We train an offline supervised LSTM whose per-facility architecture matches the production
classifier exactly — all layers, optimizer, loss, sequence length, and feature width as defined
in Section 3.4 — on the same per-window feature vectors that the production classifier
saw, using gt attack type as the target label. The only variable that differs between the
production classifier and the supervised ceiling is the training procedure itself.

 Choice of comparator. Same-architecture supervised offline training is the conservative
comparator for this question. A different architecture would conflate “training procedure”
with “architecture choice”; by matching the architecture exactly and changing only the train-
ing paradigm, the gap between supervised and online macro F1 is attributable to the training
procedure with as few confounders as possible. The same-architecture ceiling is therefore a
lower bound on the training-procedure deficit. To bound the deficit from above we addition-
ally report a feature-signal ceiling: a multinomial logistic-regression classifier trained on the
same per-window features with no temporal context (scripts/supervised baseline.py).
The linear ceiling answers a different question — how much signal do the features them-
selves carry, independent of any specific architecture — and bounds the training-procedure
deficit from above by the deficit attributable to all procedure choices including architecture.
The two ceilings together bracket the deficit; the same-architecture ceiling identifies the
conservative training-procedure-only contribution, the linear ceiling identifies the maximum
contribution if architecture choice at the available data scale is itself part of the deficit.
We report both under matched per-EID + chronological handicaps so the architecture
comparison is fair, and additionally report the pooled-cross-facility variant of each to isolate
the per-facility-data-isolation contribution.

  Train/test split. We use a chronological 70/30 split per facility: for each (mode, EID)
pair, sequences are time-ordered by trial index and elapsed minutes, and the last 30% are
held out as test. Within the training portion, the last 20% is used as a validation set for
early stopping. Chronological split is essential for time-series data: a stratified random split
would risk train/test pairs being adjacent in time and sharing confounding context (e.g.
the same attack campaign window straddling the split boundary), inflating the apparent

38 Blakely et al.

ceiling. We additionally report a stratified-random-split version of the same protocol for
legacy comparison only — the chronological-split numbers are the publishable result.

  Training schedule. For each (mode, EID) pair with at least 50 sequences and at least 2
represented classes, we train for up to 200 epochs with batch size 16 and Adam at lr = 10−3 ,
with early stopping on val loss (patience 15, restore best weights). The early-stopping
protocol allows each per-EID model to train to its own convergence point regardless of
dataset size — a single fixed epoch budget would either undertrain the data-rich EIDs or
overtrain the data-poor ones. Per-EID stopping epochs in practice ranged from 16 to 115,
confirming that early stopping engaged correctly rather than simply running to the cap.

  Pooling and metric. Per-EID test predictions are pooled across EIDs within each mode,
and per-class F1 is computed once on the pooled set — the same statistic shape produced
by the production classifier’s argmax F1 in compute per attack f1, so the supervised
ceiling and the actual classifier are directly comparable. The headline metric is macro F1
(unweighted mean per-class F1) rather than accuracy or weighted F1, because the loop’s
objective is to produce attack-discriminative distributions across all classes — a weighted
metric would underweight rare attack classes (e.g. MALWARE C2) that are nonetheless
important for defense selection.

  Hard labels rather than soft targets. The supervised LSTM is trained against hard one-hot
labels with sparse categorical cross-entropy. The ceiling question is “can the architecture
extract attack-discriminative signal from these features at all?” — which is most cleanly
answered with the standard hard-label setup. A soft-target loss (e.g. matching a target
probability distribution that reflects which defenses each attack admits) would test a specific
loss formulation rather than the underlying architectural ceiling, and is left as future work.

 Per-EID and pooled variants. The per-EID supervised LSTM described above matches
the production classifier’s per-facility isolation: one model per (mode, EID) trained only on
that EID’s data. We additionally report a pooled cross-facility variant of the same supervised
LSTM, in which a single model per mode is trained on the (sequence, label) pairs pooled
across all EIDs (sequences are still constructed within each EID — no cross-facility sliding
windows — but model weights are shared across facilities). The pooled variant breaks the
per-facility isolation constraint to isolate a separate question: is per-facility data isolation
itself a contributor to the supervised ceiling, distinct from the training-procedure bottleneck
the per-EID ceiling identifies? The chronological-split protocol is applied per-EID before
pooling so the no-leakage property is preserved. Both variants are reported in Section 5.9;
the per-EID variant remains the apples-to-apples comparator for the production loop, and
the pooled variant is interpreted as a higher upper bound that quantifies what cross-facility
weight sharing would add.

4.5 Statistical Methods
P(correct) per record was tested against the random baseline of 1/12 ≈ 0.0833 using a
two-sided Mann-Whitney U test. We chose Mann-Whitney rather than a parametric 𝑡-test
because per-record P(correct) values are bounded in [0, 1] and heavily right-skewed — when

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 39

the classifier is uncertain, the softmax output spreads probability across many classes and
the true class receives only a small slice — so the Gaussian assumption a 𝑡-test requires
does not hold. Per-class F1 was computed without averaging across modes; macro F1 is the
unweighted mean of per-class F1 across the 11 attack classes (NORMAL excluded from the
mean to avoid the dominant-class case skewing the average). Effect sizes for upstream signal
comparisons (Section 5) are reported as Cohen’s 𝑑 on the per-record signal distributions.
All statistical tests use a 0.05 significance threshold; significance is annotated explicitly in
the relevant figures and tables of Section 5.

5.6 Q6 — Reward–Correctness Causal Link
Q6 tests whether the reward signal driving online training is causally coupled to the
correctness metric the loop is trying to improve. The Q6 distribution is heavy-tailed
and zero-inflated (71% of composite deltas are exactly zero, with the remainder spread

across [−1, +1]), and single-statistic correlation summaries on this shape are fragile: at

6 DISCUSSION
This section interprets the per-stage findings of Section 5 jointly: what the evidence
supports and what it does not, where the loop’s failure to learn is localized, what the
reward-correctness diagnostic implies for the proxy reward, what generalizable lessons the
diagnostic methodology carries beyond AdCyDER, and the principal limitations that bound
the conclusions.

6.1 What the Evidence Supports
Three claims are well-supported by the per-stage data presented in Section 5.

  The upstream detection layers carry attack signal. IDS produces a large-effect attack-
versus-normal separation (𝑑 ≈ +1.38, Q2), and ADS produces a partial, attack-class-selective
separation (𝑑 ≈ +0.46, Q1). Neither signal is null, and the IDS signal in particular is large
enough that downstream ML stages have an information-theoretic basis on which to
discriminate. The supervised ceilings (Section 5.9) confirm this from the model side: under
matched per-EID + chronological handicaps a linear classifier on the same features reaches
macro F1 ≈ 0.70, and the same-architecture LSTM reaches ≈ 0.34. Whatever the failure of
the production loop is, it is not that the features are signal-free.

 The dominant bottleneck is the training procedure. The paired supervised ceilings dis-
criminate between two hypotheses: that the per-window features lack the signal to support
attack-type discrimination (data-quality failure) versus that the signal is present but the
online RL training procedure fails to extract it (training-procedure failure). The same-
architecture LSTM ceiling, which matches the production classifier’s architecture and
per-facility isolation exactly and changes only the training paradigm, reaches macro F1
≈ 0.34 on online-mode features versus 0.07 for the actual online RL classifier — a 4.7×
gap attributable to the training procedure alone, with the production architecture held
fixed. The feature-signal (linear) ceiling under the same handicaps reaches ≈ 0.70 (10×
deployed), indicating the per-window features support substantially more discriminative

52 Blakely et al.

signal than even the same-architecture offline-trained variant extracts at this data scale. The
training-procedure deficit is bracketed between 4.7× (same architecture) and 10× (matched
handicaps, simpler classifier), with the gap between the two attributable to the produc-
tion LSTM architecture being over-capacity for the data scale rather than to additional
procedure shortcomings. The pooled cross-facility variant of the same-architecture LSTM
lifts per-facility isolation as a separate constraint and contributes a further ≈ 1.2×. Both
gaps point the same direction: the dominant bottleneck is the training procedure, not the
features.

  Continued online training does not improve over a static snapshot. The online and frozen
baseline modes produce statistically indistinguishable aggregate accuracy (Section 5.8), and
the per-iteration trajectory of P(correct) is flat-to-slightly-degrading over training-elapsed
time (Q5). In the continual-learning vocabulary of Parisi et al. and Delange et al. [37, 38], the
online configuration shows no measurable improvement over the lower-bound baseline that
holds parameters near their initial values — a standard control these surveys recommend
for assessing whether ongoing parameter updates contribute beyond a static snapshot.
AdCyDER’s online-versus-frozen comparison answers this question in the negative for the
configuration tested.

6.2 What the Evidence Does Not Support
Several claims are explicitly not supported by the data, and we want to be precise about
which.
 The data does not support the original research hypothesis as it was stated — that
an online RL classifier coupled to a Stackelberg game-theoretic defense selector, trained
on delayed grid-health-delta reward, will learn per-facility attack discrimination from
operational outcomes alone. In the configuration evaluated, the classifier’s argmax accuracy
is statistically indistinguishable from random in the online mode (Section 5.8), per-class
P(correct) does not improve over training time, and grid outcomes are flat across iterations.
 The data does not support the alternative interpretation that the pipeline is fundamentally
broken at the data layer. Both upstream signals are present, the supervised ceiling reaches
non-trivial F1 on the same features, and the GT defense selector produces diverse defense
allocations rather than collapsing onto a single dominant action. The pipeline’s individual
stages function; the closed-loop training procedure is the failing component.
 The data also does not support a strong claim that confidence trajectory is a misleading
metric in general. What it does support is the specific finding that, on this pipeline, classifier
confidence converged from low to high while classification correctness did not improve —
meaning that confidence convergence on this pipeline did not correspond to discrimination
learning. This is consistent with the more general result of Guo et al. [33] that confidence
and accuracy are conceptually distinct quantities and that modern neural networks can be
confidently wrong, but it does not by itself demonstrate that confidence is uninformative in
all RL classifier settings. It does, however, motivate the use of correctness-based diagnostics
rather than confidence-based ones for any future evaluation of this pipeline.

6.3 The Reward Signal: Weakly Aligned, Underpowered
The Q6 reading reported in Section 5.6 is that the reward signal is weakly aligned with
classifier correctness in the methodology-expected direction across all three robustness lenses
(threshold split, hurdle decomposition, trimmed-Pearson sensitivity), with effect magnitudes
too small to drive gradient-based learning at the campaign sample size. The loop-level
consequence is that the reward channel is informative but underpowered: it carries usable
gradient information about whether the classifier is producing more-correct posteriors, but
at a magnitude (top-vs-bottom decile Cohen’s 𝑑 ≈ −0.19, Pearson 𝑟2 < 0.5%) that is small
relative to the per-step variance the optimization procedure must contend with over 28
iterations.
 In the framework of Skalse et al. [32], this is not a misspecified-reward configuration
but an underpowered one: the proxy (composite grid-health-delta) and the true objective
(correct attack-type classification) exhibit positive directional coupling, just at a magnitude
small enough that optimizing the proxy in expectation produces little improvement on
the true objective at this sample size. The hurdle finding (more-correct predictions are
associated with much more frequent observable grid-state changes, Fisher exact 𝑝 < 10−10
on the composite) is the strongest signal in the Q6 picture and indicates that the loop
is producing differential operational effects driven by classifier output — the magnitude
direction of those effects, however, is small enough that the gradient pulled out of any
individual reward record is comparable to the noise floor of nearby records.
 The mechanism by which a grid-health-delta reward could be underpowered relative
to the optimizer’s noise floor is plausible from the architecture documented in Section 3.
The reward observed in window 𝑡 + 1 reflects the combined effect of (i) the classifier’s
posterior at window 𝑡, (ii) the GT defense selected from that posterior, (iii) the actuator’s
physical effect, and (iv) the synthetic generator’s mitigation propagation. Each link in this
chain attenuates the signal-to-noise of the eventual reward. The credit-assignment problem
documented in the RL literature [27] additionally means the classifier has no per-stage
feedback to disentangle “my classification was wrong” from “my classification was right but
the defense’s physical effect was small.”
 The composite construction of grid-health (Section 3.4.2) is a second candidate location
for additional signal-to-noise improvement. The composite is a 50/50 blend of an ADS sub-
score (autoencoder reconstruction error) and an IDS sub-score (rolling-baseline-normalized
alert density), and gt engine writes the pre- and post-defense values of both sub-scores
into every rl rewards document so that per-channel coupling can be examined. Under the
same multi-lens robustness check as the composite, the ADS sub-channel shows a small
right-signed coupling that is robust under trimming (Spearman 𝜌 ≈ −0.10 on nonzero
records, 𝑝 ≈ 0.04) while the IDS sub-channel and composite show right-signed Pearson
signals that collapse under modest trimming and hurdle effects with the same direction and
significance as the composite. Reward-reformulation candidates worth exploring in follow-on
work include weighting the composite to favor whichever sub-channel proves to carry more
trim-robust signal in a given deployment, lengthening the IDS lookback window from 5
minutes to match the longer end of the defense observation delay (5–60 minutes), and

54 Blakely et al.

replacing the asymmetric clip-at-zero IDS normalization with a symmetric normalization
that lets defenses driving IDS density below baseline register as positive contributions. None
of these reformulations are predicted by the present data to flip the sign of the coupling
— the coupling is already in the right direction — but each is a candidate for raising the
magnitude of the gradient signal the optimizer sees per record.

6.4 Methodology Generalization: Confidence Convergence Is Not Learning
A practical lesson from the project’s own internal trajectory is worth surfacing. Earlier
project status reporting tracked classifier confidence convergence as the headline learning-
progress metric and reported that confidence reached 0.95 ± 0.03 across independent trials
— a reading consistent with what an RL classifier “looks like when it works.” The May
2026 diagnostic round showed that confidence convergence on this pipeline corresponded
to overconfident wrong predictions rather than to discrimination learning, and that the
classifier’s argmax accuracy and per-class P(correct) had not actually improved over time.
  This is a domain-specific instance of the general result of Guo et al. [33] that confidence
and accuracy are conceptually distinct and can decouple in modern neural-network classifiers,
particularly under training procedures that do not directly optimize for calibration. The
methodological recommendation we draw from our own experience is operational rather than
theoretical: any evaluation of an RL or weakly-supervised classifier in this domain should
report correctness-based metrics (per-class P(correct), distributional accuracy, calibration
error, comparison against a same-architecture supervised ceiling) before reporting confidence
trajectories, and confidence trajectories should be interpreted as evidence of training-process
activity rather than of discrimination learning.
  The pipeline-stage diagnostic protocol used in Section 5 (the Q1–Q7 sequence) is the
second methodological contribution we believe generalizes beyond AdCyDER. The protocol
forces the analyst to verify upstream signal presence, classifier output behavior, training-
time learning trajectory, reward–correctness coupling, and end-to-end operational outcome
independently before drawing model-level conclusions. This is a direct application of the
production-ML engineering principle that data-quality and pipeline-stage failures are com-
monly misattributed to model-level causes when stage-wise diagnostics are absent [34, 35].
For online RL/GT loops trained on delayed environmental reward, where the chain from
classification decision to feedback observation passes through several stages each of which
can introduce its own failure mode, this kind of stage-wise inspection is, in our experience,
the difference between catching the failure at the right layer and chasing model-architecture
changes that cannot fix a reward-misspecification problem.
  The supervised same-architecture ceiling test (Section 5.9) is the third generalizable
component. The construction — match the architecture exactly, change only the training
paradigm, and read the F1 gap as an upper bound on what the training procedure can
extract from the available features — isolates the training-procedure-versus-feature-signal
question with as few confounders as possible. This is consistent with the methodological
recommendation in Amodei et al. [30] to compare semi-supervised RL against a baseline
trained only on labeled episodes to isolate the effect of label scarcity, applied here to isolate
the effect of the online delayed-reward training paradigm rather than label scarcity per se.

6.5 Limitations
Several limitations bound the conclusions.

 Synthetic environment. The campaign was conducted entirely against the containerized
synthetic SCADA generator described in Section 3.2, not against an HIL testbed integrating
real inverters or RTUs with simulated grid dynamics. The generator captures attack-type
variety, time-alignment across data streams, and an autoencoder-based physics anomaly
signal, but it does not substitute for the operational realism that an HIL deployment
of the type used by Nguyen et al. [15], Sangoleye et al. [16], or Mogilicharla et al. [5]
would provide. We do not claim that the negative result generalizes to all possible feature
signals: the conclusion that the training procedure rather than the data is the bottleneck is
conditional on the per-window feature distribution this generator produces. A higher-fidelity
environment could change the supervised ceiling and would change the absolute scale of
grid-health-delta variability that the reward sees.

 Single experimental campaign. The May 2026 evaluation campaign comprised one set of
trial runs per baseline mode, with random seeds pinned for reproducibility but no replication
across independent random seeds at the campaign level. The per-mode aggregate statistics
in Section 5.8 are stable in the sense that they pool many windows across many facilities,
but the conclusion that the training procedure is the bottleneck rests on the observed gap
between the supervised ceiling and the production classifier in this campaign. We do not
present multi-seed or multi-campaign variability for this gap.

  Single architecture and reward formulation. The conclusions apply to the per-facility
LSTM(128)→LSTM(64)→Dense(32) architecture, the 5-minute aggregation cadence, and
the soft-clipped grid-health-delta reward documented in Section 3.4. Within this combination
the training procedure does not learn discrimination, but the report does not establish
that no online RL classifier with delayed environmental reward can learn this task. The
pooled supervised LSTM ceiling (Section 5.9) shows that cross-facility weight sharing would
raise the achievable upper bound by ≈ 1.2× on these features — meaningful but not
dominant. A different architecture (longer sequence length, attention), a different reward
formulation (reward shaping with intermediate signals from the IDS or ADS layers, longer
reward windows, dense per-decision reward), or a different exploration schedule could
produce different results. The Q6 reading that the reward signal is directionally aligned
but underpowered is the strongest reason to investigate reward-magnitude reformulation
specifically.

 Calibration of the GT model parameters. The GT defense selector’s effectiveness, baseline
impact, and cost parameters are SME-encoded priors (Section 3.5), not empirically calibrated.
The Stackelberg loss minimization is mathematically well-defined, but its outputs are only
as defensible as those priors. We did not evaluate the sensitivity of the GT layer’s defense
allocations to perturbations in those priors, and we cannot rule out that part of the reward-
misspecification picture in Q6 originates in the GT layer rather than in the reward signal
itself.

56 Blakely et al.

 Generator fidelity to operational telemetry. The synthetic generator’s autoencoder ADS,
IDS, and netflow streams are designed to be physically plausible and time-aligned but
were not validated against operational telemetry from a real DER deployment. The IDS
effect size we observe (𝑑 ≈ +1.38) is sufficiently large that it would be surprising for a
real-world signal to be smaller, but the ADS effect (𝑑 ≈ +0.46, attack-class-selective) is in a
regime where generator characteristics matter more, and its per-feature reconstruction-error
structure (Section 5.1) was instrumented but not analyzed.

6.6 Implications for the Original Research Hypothesis
The original hypothesis tested in this work was that an online RL/GT loop trained on delayed
grid-health-delta reward would learn per-facility attack discrimination from operational
outcomes alone. The evidence in Section 5 is inconsistent with this hypothesis as stated, in
the configuration evaluated. We do not believe the evidence is consistent with the negation
of the hypothesis at the same level of generality; the supervised ceiling test in particular
shows that the features carry signal that the architecture can extract under a different
training paradigm, and the Q6 multi-lens reading identifies an underpowered-but-aligned
reward channel as a specific, plausibly fixable contributor.
  We therefore read the result as a falsified configuration of the hypothesis rather than a
refuted class of approaches. The configuration includes: per-window features as currently
aggregated, a per-facility LSTM as currently sized, a delayed grid-health-delta as the
sole reward, a soft-clipped reward magnitude as currently scaled, and an SME-prior-
parameterized Stackelberg defense selector as currently calibrated. Section 7 consolidates
the recommended next steps in priority order, with reward reformulation at the top given
the Q6 finding.

7 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
7.1 Conclusions
This report has documented the design, implementation, and evaluation of an integrated
online RL / Stackelberg game-theoretic defense pipeline for distributed energy resources, and
has presented a falsified configuration of the underlying research hypothesis. We summarize
the conclusions in three parts.

  The hypothesis was tested cleanly and is not supported by the data. The integrated loop
was deployed end-to-end across three baseline configurations (online, frozen, gt only),
evaluated through a pipeline-ordered diagnostic protocol (Q1–Q7) and a same-architecture
supervised LSTM ceiling test, and analyzed against a chronological hold-out partition
with random seeds pinned. The hypothesis tested — that an online RL classifier coupled
to a Stackelberg defense selector, trained on delayed grid-health-delta reward derived
from physically-grounded inverter telemetry, would learn per-facility attack discrimination
from operational outcomes alone — is not supported in the configuration evaluated. The
classifier’s argmax accuracy in the online mode is statistically indistinguishable from
random (𝑝 = 0.30), per-class P(correct) does not improve over training-elapsed time, and
grid outcomes are flat across iterations.

AdCyDER: Online RL with Stackelberg-Optimized Defenses 57

  The failure is localized to two specific mechanisms. The supervised LSTM ceiling test
(Section 6.1) localizes the failure to the training procedure rather than to the feature signal:
a same-architecture supervised LSTM extracts substantially more F1 from identical features
than the production online classifier, with the only variable being the training paradigm. The
Q6 reward–correctness diagnostic (Section 6.3) identifies a second mechanism: the reward
signal driving online updates is anti-correlated with classifier correctness on a majority of
attack classes — the diagnostic signature, in the framework of Skalse et al. [32] and Pan et
al. [31], of a misspecified or hacked proxy reward.

 The contributions worth carrying forward sit in three areas. First, a transferable diag-
nostic methodology. The pipeline-ordered Q1–Q7 protocol paired with a same-architecture
supervised ceiling test (Section 6.4) is proposed as a falsification protocol for online RL/GT
defense pipelines that learn from delayed environmental reward. We believe it generalizes
to other online-learning security ML pipelines where the chain from classification decision
to evaluable outcome passes through several stages, each of which can introduce its own
failure mode.
 Second, an open and reproducible experimental infrastructure. The integrated pipeline
(synthetic SCADA generator with embedded Fronius inverter physics and autoencoder ADS,
Suricata IDS, time-windowed feature aggregation, per-facility LSTM with three baseline
modes, Stackelberg defense selector with SME-encoded priors, OpenC2 actuator framework)
passes 335+ automated tests across the main repository and two service submodules. The
diagnostic scripts that produced every figure in this report regenerate from the per-iteration
JSON exports under version control, with random seeds pinned and library versions pinned.
The synthetic SCADA generator and the attack simulator (Section A) are released publicly.
Independent groups can re-run the diagnostic protocol against their own configurations of
the loop without rebuilding the infrastructure from scratch.
 Third, a characterization of the loop’s signal ceiling. The per-EID and pooled supervised-
ceiling readings together with the per-class P(true) data establish what the loop can and
cannot do on the synthetic-environment features as currently aggregated, and decompose
the gap from the actual classifier into a 4.7× training-procedure contribution and a 1.2×
per-facility-data-isolation contribution. This characterization is itself a contribution: it
removes “the data is fine, the model just needs to train longer” as a viable interpretation,
names per-facility isolation as a real but secondary constraint, and scopes the search space
for productive next steps.

7.2 Aspirational Future Work
The directions below are written as aspirational rather than planned: at the time of writing,
the project does not have funding for follow-on work, and these directions are recorded
here for the record so that future investigators (within the project team or elsewhere) can
pick up the thread without rediscovering the same priorities. We list them in priority order
based on the bottleneck localization of Section 6.

  Reward reformulation (highest priority). The Q6 multi-lens reading (Section 6.3) is that
the reward signal is directionally aligned but small in magnitude relative to the optimizer’s

noise floor at the campaign sample size. Reward-reformulation work should therefore focus

DCA) on every ADS event, and these were captured at the generator and persist in the
OpenSearch raw-event index but were not propagated through the aggregator into the
MongoDB feature store and were not analyzed in this report (Section 5.1). Re-extracting and
analyzing this data would refine the per-attack-class detection-rate readings of Q1, identify
which channel is responsible for the anomaly score on a given window, and characterize
how channel attribution varies by attack class. This would also provide a finer-grained
diagnostic surface for any reward reformulation work that incorporates ADS sub-signals
into the credit-assignment pathway.

  External validation of the diagnostic methodology. The Q1–Q7 + supervised-ceiling proto-
col was developed inside this project and applied to one pipeline configuration. Its claimed
generality (Section 6.4) would be substantively tested by applying it to other online RL/GT

defense pipelines that learn from delayed environmental reward — whether other DER
cybersecurity pipelines or analogous systems in adjacent domains. Such an external applica-
tion would either confirm that the protocol localizes failures to the same stages it did here,
or surface failure modes it does not currently catch and motivate protocol extensions.

  Workflow-orchestrated simulator extension. The Prefect-based simulator rewrite (Appen-
dix A.3) is functional at unit-test scope but was not deployed in any operational use within
the project. As a follow-on direction, the prototype could be developed into the operational
generation infrastructure for any successor evaluation campaign — enabling per-task error
handling, structured logging, multi-protocol coverage extensions, and Grid-STIX-format
threat-intelligence interchange that the current generator does not support.

  Architecture and exploration alternatives (lower priority). The same-architecture su-
pervised LSTM ceiling already places an upper bound on what the current per-facility
per-window data can support, so architectural changes are unlikely to resolve the headline
negative result without an accompanying improvement in either the feature signal or the
training procedure. That said, longer LSTM sequence lengths, attention mechanisms, cross-
facility weight sharing (which would relax the per-facility data-volume constraint), and
alternative exploration schedules are all natural perturbations to the current configuration
that would inform a more complete picture of the configuration space if and when reward
reformulation work establishes a coherent training signal first.

7.3 Closing
The strongest single result reported here is a falsified configuration of an online RL/GT
defense pipeline that, on its face, looked like it was working — and a diagnostic methodology
that revealed the failure cleanly enough to localize the bottleneck and identify the next-step
priorities. Reporting null and negative results in security ML is, in our view, a contribution
in itself: the configurations that do not work are part of the search space that follow-on
work needs to know about, and the absence of such reports causes resources to be spent
rediscovering the same dead ends. The diagnostic methodology and the open infrastructure
released alongside this report are, we hope, the more durable contributions of this work.

A ADJACENT PROJECT CONTRIBUTIONS
Beyond the integrated RL/GT loop pipeline that is the subject of this report, the project
produced two related software contributions and started a third. They are not part of the
data path evaluated in Sections 4–5, but they are substantive artifacts of the project effort,
partly open-sourced, and relevant to the future-work directions in Section 7.

A.1 adcyder-attack-simulator (Public Release)
The adcyder-attack-simulator is a Python framework for executing cyber attacks against
industrial-control-system targets running the Modbus TCP and DNP3 protocols. Its purpose,
distinct from the synthetic SCADA generator described in Section 3.2, is to drive real
protocol-level attack traffic against operational ICS endpoints — an emulated outstation in
development, or an OPAL-RT-hosted simulated facility in the planned hardware-in-the-loop
environment. Where the synthetic generator produces simulated netflow records, IDS alerts,
and ADS samples consistent with an attack pattern, the attack simulator constructs and
transmits live protocol frames to a target on the network and observes the target’s actual
response. The two are complements: the synthetic generator made evaluation tractable in
the absence of HIL access (Section 3.2, Origin and motivation), while the attack simulator
was the intended attack source for the HIL-deployed framework.
  The simulator implements TCP-based persistent-connection clients for both protocols
with proper transaction-ID handling, MBAP (Modbus Application Protocol) header con-
struction for the Modbus client, and connection-lifecycle management. The DNP3 client
combines the FreyrSCADA dnp3protocol library for standard function-code operations
with a custom raw-protocol module (dnp3 raw.py) for attack-specific frame crafting that
requires fields the library does not expose, including manual CRC computation and
Wireshark-compatible frame generation for protocol analysis. Device discovery uses UDP
broadcast to 255.255.255.255: Modbus discovery via Read Device Identification (func-
tion code 0x2B/0x0E) extracts vendor and model information from responsive devices;
DNP3 discovery uses basic read requests to identify outstations and probe protocol char-
acteristics. Standalone command-line entry points (src/standalone modbus attack.py,
src/standalone dnp3 attack.py) execute attacks directly without infrastructure depen-
dencies, with configurable point/register address ranges to match the OPAL-RT target’s
addressing scheme (the address-range configurability and OPAL-RT-specific notes are
documented in the simulator’s docs/OPAL-RT-COMPATIBILITY.md).
  The simulator implements four attack-vector families: (i) a Watering Hole Attack chaining
initial system compromise via SSH and Metasploit-driven payload execution; (ii) Modbus
attacks including command injection, denial-of-service via resource exhaustion, false data
injection to control registers, and information exfiltration from device memory; (iii) DNP3
attacks including binary and analog point manipulation, control-sequence injection, and
exfiltration across multiple object groups; and (iv) data-streaming attacks targeting the
historian and gRPC data-injector components included in the same codebase. The framework
was open-sourced as ANL-CEEESA/adcyder-attack-simulator on GitHub. It was handed
off to the second university partner’s testbed team for integration with their OPAL-RT-based

infrastructure, but, as noted in Section 3.2, that integration was not completed within the
project timeline.

A.2 test-outstation (Internal)
The test-outstation is an internal-only DNP3 protocol test environment built as a
debugging aide for the attack simulator (Section A.1) during its preparation for the partner-
testbed OPAL-RT integration. It allowed the team to iterate on the simulator’s DNP3 client
behavior against a known-good local outstation without requiring concurrent access to the
OPAL-RT testbed. The environment is complete and functional — it is not under active
development — and remains available as an internal tool for any future DNP3 client work.
  The environment bundles three outstation implementations at varying levels of fidelity (a
full pydnp3-based outstation with pre-populated binary and analog input values, a light-
weight raw-socket DNP3 server with no external library dependency for basic READ-request
handling, and a minimal echo server for link-layer inspection); two client implementations
matching those (a pydnp3 master, and a raw-socket client that constructs DNP3 frames
by hand with manual CRC computation, useful for validating the attack simulator’s own
raw-socket logic against the same wire format); and a packet-capture-and-analyze toolkit
(capture pydnp3.py for parsing live traffic into DNP3 link / transport / application layers,
and a tcpdump-driven shell wrapper for hex-dumping captures alongside the test runs).
The environment is not part of any public release; it is documented here as a substantive
contribution to the project’s debugging tooling.

A.3 Workflow-Orchestrated Simulator Rewrite (Prototype)
A second-generation rewrite of the attack simulator was prototyped using the Prefect 3.5
workflow-orchestration framework, with Pydantic 2.10 type-safe configuration, structured
logging, and integration with Grid-STIX1 for threat-intelligence interchange. The prototype
reorganizes the simulator’s attack execution as composable DAG workflows with automatic
retry, contextual logging, and per-task error handling; protocol coverage is extended to
Modbus TCP, DNP3, SSH, Metasploit, and generic TCP/IP, and outputs can be streamed
to file, Kafka, Kinesis, or gRPC sinks. The prototype is functional at unit-test scope but was
not deployed in any operational use within the project. We document it here for traceability
and as a candidate starting point for future workflow-orchestrated extensions of the attack
simulator (Section 7).
