Loss of CsrA in Acinetobacter baumannii leads to lethal dysregulation of alanine metabolism

Rosa L. Sava, Michael J. Gebhardt

Abstract

In the pathogen Acinetobacter baumannii, the posttranscriptional regulator CsrA is required for growth under certain environmental conditions, including rich growth media and infection-relevant conditions like human serum. However, CsrA is dispensable for A. baumannii grown under nutrient limited conditions, such as a minimal medium containing a single carbon source. Using classical genetic approaches to explore this conditional essentiality, we found that CsrA and Lrp, a global transcription regulator, coordinate expression of several genes involved with D-alanine metabolism. Consistent with D-alanine’s importance to peptidoglycan biosynthesis, csrA mutants exhibit impaired cell envelope integrity. Our findings provide a potential mechanism for the essentiality of CsrA in A. baumannii and further highlight how global regulatory networks intersect to promote fitness under changing environmental conditions.
Posttranscriptional regulation is a key mechanism by which bacteria coordinate gene expression in response to changing environments. In the human pathogen Acinetobacter baumannii, the importance of posttranscriptional gene expression control is exemplified by CsrA, a highly conserved posttranscriptional regulator that is essential for viability in multiple settings, ranging from growth in rich laboratory media to infection-relevant conditions like human serum. However, csrA is dispensable for growth in chemically defined media with a single carbon source. We leveraged this condition-specific essentiality to identify suppressor mutations that regained the ability to grow in rich media. These analyses identified Lrp, a transcription factor that regulates hundreds of genes in A. baumannii. Among the Lrp regulon, we identified a four gene locus involved with uptake and utilization of D-alanine. We determined that two transcripts encoded by this locus, dadA and cycA2, are repressed by CsrA. The absence of csrA leads to overexpression of both dadA and cycA2, which is toxic, even in wild type A. baumannii. In the csrA mutant, this dysregulation contributes to impaired cell envelope integrity and increased sensitivity to several clinically relevant cell-wall targeting antibiotics, including carbapenems. Collectively, our data indicate that Lrp and CsrA coordinately balance expression of D-alanine utilization genes in A. baumannii, at the transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels, respectively. Moreover, it appears that a key aspect of CsrA’s essentiality in A. baumannii is to counter the activities of Lrp, a second global regulator. These findings further highlight the complex interplay between transcriptional and posttranscriptional regulators in bacteria.

Acinetobacter baumannii is a Gram-negative bacterium that is evolving into a significant human pathogen. A. baumannii infections, which can occur in a variety of tissues including the lung, blood stream, and urinary tract, are opportunistic in nature, most frequently occurring in hospitalized or immunocompromised individuals (, ). Infections caused by the organism are especially concerning due to high rates of antibiotic resistance among contemporary clinical isolates. Indeed, antibiotic resistant A. baumannii has been identified as an organism of highest concern by the CDC and WHO (–). In addition, A. baumannii clinical isolates frequently exhibit desiccation resistance and can survive both routine decontamination practices and periods of nutrient limitation in the clinical setting (, ). These emerging antibiotic resistance and stress-resistance phenotypes; referred to as a “persist-and-resist” strategy, when coupled with a trend toward increased virulence potential among currently circulating isolates, has undoubtedly contributed to the rise of A. baumannii as a problematic pathogen (–).

Bacterial survival hinges on the ability to sense and respond properly to fluctuations in the environment. These responses are largely mediated through alterations in gene expression () and occur at both the transcriptional and posttranscriptional level. The bacterial posttranscriptional response is frequently mediated by regulatory RNA binding proteins (RBPs) whose function drives requisite changes in gene expression (, ). In A. baumannii, previous works demonstrate that CsrA, a highly conserved bacterial RBP, plays an important role in the response to many of the persist-and-resist phenotypes mentioned above, including desiccation resistance, biofilm formation, and metabolic versatility (–). Proteins in the CsrA family–first named for its role in carbon storage regulation in Escherichia coli–are found across the bacterial kingdom and exert posttranscriptional control over gene expression by directly associating with target mRNA transcripts, often influencing the translation and/or stability of the target RNA (–). In A. baumannii, the importance of CsrA is highlighted by the observation that CsrA is essential for growth in rich media (–). Furthermore, A. baumannii mutants lacking csrA are impaired for growth in the presence of certain amino acids in the growth medium, including alanine and arginine (, ). The viability defects associated with csrA also extend into infection relevant conditions, where A. baumannii csrA mutants exhibit impaired growth in the Galleria mellonella larval infection model, human urine, and human serum (–). In line with a role in virulence, a recent study by Singh and colleagues demonstrates that CsrA also impacts a phenotypic switch between virulent and avirulent growth states ().

Given the important role of CsrA in regulating A. baumannii physiology and survival in diverse environmental conditions, we sought to identify the mechanisms underlying the conditional essentiality of csrA. Using genetic analyses, we determined that the transcription factor Lrp, which we show is a global regulator in A. baumannii, contributes to suppression of csrA phenotypes. Lrp is a member of the “feast/famine regulatory proteins” family, which are found across the bacterial kingdom and primarily act as transcription regulators (). We further demonstrate that, in conjunction with CsrA, Lrp coordinates expression of a suite of genes involved with D-alanine metabolism, including a D-amino acid dehydrogenase, an alanine racemase, and a D-amino acid permease. Lrp positively regulates the transcription of these genes, whereas CsrA represses their translation. As D-alanine is a key building block required for the synthesis of peptidoglycan (PG) and the bacterial cell wall, we also examined what role CsrA may play on cell envelope homeostasis and determined that cells lacking csrA have altered sensitivities to several antimicrobial compounds targeting cell wall biosynthesis. Moreover, ectopic expression of the CsrA-regulated D-alanine genes causes growth arrest in wild-type A. baumannii cells and impart an antibiotic resistance profile similar to the ∆csrA strain. These findings suggest that at least one aspect of the csrA phenotype results from dysregulation of the D-amino acid pool in the cell. We propose a model whereby two global regulators—Lrp and CsrA—coordinately regulate D-alanine availability in A. baumannii, and so doing, contribute to cell wall homeostasis in this important human pathogen.

Results

Isolation of ∆csrA Suppressors.

Consistent with previous reports, an isogenic strain carrying a deletion of csrA in the A. baumannii AB5075 strain failed to grow on rich media (i.e., LB) but grew nearly indistinguishably from wild-type cells in M9 minimal media with succinate as the sole carbon source (). To extend this observation, we screened a panel of A. baumannii isolates using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi; , ). Knock-down of csrA had little effect on M9-succinate but abrogated growth on LB in each strain tested (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To gain insight into the genetic elements that underlie this phenotype, we isolated 12 independent and spontaneously occurring suppressor clones that exhibited restored growth in LB (hereafter referred to as CS for csrA Suppressor). The suppressors displayed varying growth patterns on LB with most of the mutants producing visibly smaller colonies than wild-type AB5075 (). Interestingly, some of the suppressors (i.e., CS03, CS05, and CS07) produced smaller colonies when grown on M9-succinate, while others resembled WT (i.e., CS02 and CS08). When cells from overnight cultures were serially diluted and plated on LB, most grew to a similar dilution as wild type AB5075 (WT), although CS07, CS10, and CS11 grew poorly on solid LB. Consistent with the results observed on solid media, growth assays conducted in liquid LB showed that the ∆csrA suppressors displayed altered growth kinetics and reached a lower terminal density than WT (). As expected, the suppressors exhibited WT growth kinetics when cultured in liquid M9-succinate, although some variations were observed ().

The observed differences among the ∆csrA suppressors with respect to restoring growth in LB suggests that multiple mutational pathways may suppress the ∆csrA phenotype. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) of the 12 suppressor strains supports this, as we detected large deletions (greater than 15 kb) in five suppressors and distinct single nucleotide polymorphisms in eight suppressors ( and Dataset S1). We did not detect mutations in three suppressors (CS06, CS09, and CS10); these strains may have rearrangements our analysis pipeline failed to detect.

Lrp Is a csrA Suppressor.

The deletions in CS01, CS02, CS04, and CS12 map to the same chromosomal region and share a total of eight genes (), including ABUW_RS18455, encoding an Lrp family transcription regulator, ABUW_RS18465, encoding a predicted TonB-dependent siderophore receptor, ABUW_RS18480, encoding a predicted short-chain dehydrogenase, a noncoding RNA (sRNA106), and several hypothetical proteins (ABUW_RS18470, ABUW_RS18475, ABUW_ RS18485). To determine which of these genes is responsible for suppressing the ∆csrA growth phenotype, we introduced plasmid-borne copies of each gene into suppressor strain CS04. Intriguingly, strain CS04 harboring the lrp plasmid (pLrp) failed to grow on LB (), while none of the other genes affected growth on LB (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). This finding implicates lrp as being responsible for the suppression phenotype in CS04. Interestingly, however, when we introduced pLrp into suppressors CS01, CS02, or CS12, the strains retained the ability to grow on LB (), which suggests that the growth phenotype reversion observed for CS04 may be due to the regulatory effects of Lrp on a gene or genes that were lost in the larger deletions that gave rise to CS01, CS02, CS12.

To further examine Lrp as a possible suppressor of csrA, we deleted lrp from the AB5075 background (∆lrp), which grew similarly to WT in LB and M9-succinate (). When we deleted csrA from the ∆lrp background, the resulting strain (i.e., ∆lrp ∆csrA) grew on both solid and liquid LB (). As with the isolated suppressors, the ∆lrp ∆csrA strain displayed reduced growth in liquid LB compared to WT (, Top panel) and introduction of pLrp into the ∆lrp ∆csrA strain background abrogated growth on LB agar (). Together, the results obtained with the ∆lrp strain largely mirrored the ∆csrA suppressors and reinforce that Lrp, or its regulatory effects, are linked with the ∆csrA growth phenotype.

Lrp Is a Global Regulator in AB5075.

Lrp family proteins can function as global regulators of hundreds of genes, or as local regulators that control only a few genes (, ). Considering this, and because ectopic expression of Lrp had varying effects in the different ∆csrA suppressor clones (), we wished to determine the regulatory targets of Lrp in A. baumannii AB5075. We performed transcriptome analysis on exponentially growing cells of WT AB5075 and the ∆lrp strain using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) which revealed that the transcript abundance for 748 genes was significantly altered in cells of the lrp mutant compared to that of WT cells (Dataset S2). Of the differentially regulated genes, defined as a log2 fold-change greater than or less than 1 with a q-value under 0.05, 328 genes had decreased expression in the absence of lrp, while 420 exhibited increased expression (). That nearly 20% of the ≈4,000 genes in the AB5075 genome are differentially expressed in the ∆lrp strain is consistent with Lrp functioning as a global regulator in A. baumannii.

An Lrp-Controlled Operon Is Responsible for Suppression of ∆csrA.

In the ∆lrp transcriptome, we noticed strong downregulation of an apparent four gene operon adjacent to the lrp locus (). The predicted functions of these four genes relate to D-amino acids, including a D-amino acid dehydrogenase (ABUW_RS18450), an alanine racemase (ABUW_RS18445), a RidA-family enamine/imine deaminase (ABUW_RS18440), and a D-amino acid transporter (ABUW_RS18435). Based on homologs in other organisms, we refer here to the dehydrogenase as dadA, the racemase as dadX, and the transporter as cycA2. Interestingly, in three of the four ∆csrA suppressors harboring deletions in the lrp region, the open reading frames for dadA, dadX, ABUW_RS18440 (the ridA homolog), and cycA2–hereafter called the dad locus–was also lost ().

The significant decrease in expression of the dad locus in ∆lrp cells, along with the observation that ectopic expression of lrp failed to restore the ∆csrA phenotype in CS01, CS02, and CS12–the three suppressor strains that lost the dad locus ()–led us to hypothesize that dysregulation of the dad operon in the ∆lrp background may contribute to suppressing the csrA growth phenotype. To test this, we introduced plasmids containing the dad locus genes under the control of an IPTG-inducible promoter to WT AB5075 and the ∆lrp ∆csrA mutant. In the ∆lrp ∆csrA mutant, ectopic expression of dadA, dadX, or cycA2 impaired growth on LB as the concentration of inducer increased, suggesting that these genes may contribute to the ∆csrA phenotype (). A plasmid-borne copy of the ridA homolog (ABUW_RS18440) did not impact growth of ∆lrp ∆csrA. Intriguingly, at the highest level of induction (2 mM IPTG), ∆lrp ∆csrA cells carrying plasmid-borne copies of dadA, dadX, or cycA2 also displayed reduced growth on M9-succinate. Similarly, in WT cells, ectopic expression of dadA, dadX, or cycA2 led to reduced growth on both M9-succinate and LB as the IPTG concentration increased (). These analyses indicate that increasing dadA, dadX, or cycA2 expression impairs growth of A. baumannii, independently of the growth medium and we infer that dysregulation of the dad genes interferes with critical aspects of A. baumannii physiology and hypothesize that CsrA may negatively regulate one or more genes encoded by the dad locus.

We next asked whether the loss of genes in the dad operon was sufficient to suppress the ∆csrA mutation. In wild-type cells, deletion of individual dad genes did not affect growth in LB (). Likewise, ∆csrA cells lacking ABUW_RS18440 failed to grow on LB, suggesting again that the ridA homolog does not contribute to the ∆csrA phenotype. While strains harboring deletion of either dadA or dadX in combination with the ∆csrA mutation failed to grow in LB, deletion of both dadA and dadX in the ∆csrA strain (i.e., ∆dadA ∆dadX ∆csrA) did grow in LB, albeit poorly (). Despite multiple attempts, we were unable to recover a ∆cycA2 ∆csrA double mutant. Immediately downstream of cycA2 is cycA1, another putative D-amino acid transporter that shares high similarity with cycA2 (82% nucleotide identity; 88% amino acid identity). We reasoned that cycA2 and cycA1 may be functionally redundant and thus attempted to delete csrA from a strain lacking both cycA2 and cycA1. We successfully isolated a ∆cycA1 ∆cycA2 ∆csrA triple mutant, and like the ∆dadA ∆dadX ∆csrA strain, the ∆cycA1 ∆cycA2 ∆csrA mutant exhibited scant growth on LB (). Together, these results indicate that several dad locus genes contribute to the ∆csrA growth phenotype and further suggest that D-amino acid homeostasis–possibly alanine specifically–may be altered in cells lacking CsrA. In line with this, the ∆csrA mutant exhibited a growth defect when cultured in M9-succinate medium supplemented with L-alanine or D-alanine; in both cases, the loss of lrp in the ∆csrA background improved growth ().

CsrA Regulates dadA and cycA2 Translation.

The data presented above suggest that transcripts derived from the dad locus may be regulatory targets of CsrA and may be dysregulated when csrA is deleted. To explore this possibility, we created transcriptional and translational lacZ fusions. The reporters included approximately 500 nucleotides upstream of the dadA start codon to capture the dadA promoter; the transcriptional reporter (PdadA::lacZ) ends at the predicted dadA transcription start and the translational reporter (dadA::lacZ) includes the first several codons of the DadA protein. The two reporters were introduced into WT AB5075 and the ∆csrA strain and assayed for β-galactosidase activity following growth in M9-succinate containing 20 mM L-alanine, which induces dadA promoter activity in an lrp-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Consistent with dadA being a regulatory target of CsrA, the activity of the dadA::lacZ translational reporter was significantly higher in the ∆csrA strain compared to wild-type and this increase could be complemented when a csrA plasmid (pCsrA) was introduced (). On the contrary, complementation with an RNA-binding deficient allele of csrA (Arginine 44 changed to Alanine, pR44A; ) did not alter reporter activity, indicating that CsrA requires the ability to bind RNA to regulate dadA expression (). Importantly, the PdadA::lacZ transcriptional reporter did not exhibit increased activity in the ∆csrA background (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), indicating that the increased signal observed with the dadA::lacZ translational reporter is not due to increased dadA promoter activity in the cells lacking csrA. Analogous experiments conducted with cycA2::lacZ translational () and PcycA2::lacZ transcriptional (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) reporters produced similar results, indicating that both dadA and cycA2 are translationally repressed by CsrA. To extend these analyses, we introduced the cycA2::lacZ translational reporter plasmid into additional A. baumannii isolates, including strains AB0057, AYE, ACICU, and ATCC 17978, and measured reporter activity for cells with an empty vector or pCsrA. In each strain background, the reporter activity decreased in the presence of the CsrA plasmid, indicating that CsrA’s control over cycA2 is most likely conserved across the A. baumannii species (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

CsrA family proteins recognize specific RNA motifs in their RNA targets, including RUACARGGAUGU in E. coli () and CANGGAYG in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (), with the GGA nucleotides comprising a core recognition element for CsrA binding. Near the translation initiation region for both dadA and cycA2, there are two potential CsrA-binding motifs (referred to hereafter as GGA motifs) positioned upstream of the dadA and cycA2 start codons (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). If CsrA exerts its regulatory effects by associating with mRNAs encoding dadA and cycA2, we reasoned that mutation of the GGA motifs would abolish CsrA mediated repression of the translational reporters. Indeed, the activity of the mutant reporters where both GGA motifs were changed to AAG (dadAAAG::lacZ and cycA2AAG::lacZ) no longer responded to CsrA (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Collectively, the results of the lacZ reporter assays are consistent with CsrA repressing the translation of mRNAs encoding dadA and cycA2 by associating with GGA motifs located near the start codons of each open reading frame.

We next asked if CsrA interacts with mRNAs encoding dadA and cycA2, using RNA-immunoprecipitation and reverse-transcriptase qPCR (RIP-qPCR) to do so. We first introduced a Vesicular Stomatitis Virus-G protein (VSV-G) epitope to the C-terminus of the native csrA (CsrA-V). A similar strategy was previously used to study RsmA, a CsrA homolog in P. aeruginosa (), and, importantly, we did not observe any growth differences between WT AB5075 and the isogenic CsrA-V strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). RIP-qPCR with the CsrA-V strain allowed us to detect robust enrichment of CsrB, a regulatory RNA known to interact with CsrA (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) (). We also detected enrichment of dadA and cycA2, indicating that CsrA can directly interact with transcripts encoded by the dad locus (). When we examined the lrp transcript by RIP-qPCR, we failed to detect enrichment, suggesting that lrp is not a direct CsrA target in A. baumannii (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Consistent with this observation, the activity of an lrp::lacZ translational fusion reporter strain was unchanged in ∆csrA cells compared to wild type AB5075 although there was increased activity for a transcriptional lrp::lacZ reporter in the ∆csrA background (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Collectively, the RIP-qPCR analyses and the lacZ reporter data presented above support a model where CsrA posttranscriptionally regulates the translation of the transcript(s) encoding dadA and cycA2.

DadA Activity Is Increased in the ∆csrA Background.

Annotated as a D-amino acid dehydrogenase, DadA is predicted to convert D-alanine to pyruvate. Because DadA expression is repressed by CsrA (), and the ectopic expression of dadA is toxic (), we wondered whether cells lacking csrA exhibit increased DadA enzymatic activity. To test this, we conducted a D-amino acid dehydrogenase assay (hereafter referred to as the Dad assay) that measures the conversion of D-alanine to pyruvate in cell lysates (). Consistent with our prior observations, cell lysates from the ∆csrA strain exhibit increased DadA activity compared to WT (). Importantly, the increased Dad activity observed in the ∆csrA mutant is largely attributable to DadA, as the activity measured in the ∆dadA ∆csrA strain is significantly reduced relative to the ∆csrA strain. Complementation of the ∆csrA strain with pCsrA reduced DadA activity, whereas introducing pCsrA to the ∆lrp ∆csrA mutant did not alter DadA activity compared to the empty vector (). In this same background, however, expression of lrp from a plasmid causes a significant increase in DadA activity, to a level on par with the ∆csrA strain (). Collectively, these findings are consistent with our previous data and support a model where Lrp activates the transcription of dadA and CsrA represses dadA translation.

CsrA Influences the Transcript Abundance of dadA and cycA2.

One limitation of the experiments detailed above is that they were largely performed in conditions where CsrA is not essential (i.e., in M9 minimal media) and thus may not fully recapitulate what happens to cells growing in conditions where CsrA is essential for growth (i.e., rich media). To more directly determine the role of CsrA under CsrA-required conditions, we utilized CRISPRi to deplete CsrA during growth in LB. Importantly, when we targeted csrA with CRISPRi, growth in rich media was impaired at exponential phase and CsrA-V protein abundance was decreased (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We subsequently collected RNA from exponentially growing cultures (OD600 = 0.3 to 0.5) of the CsrA-V-dCas9 strain harboring either a control sgRNA plasmid or the csrA sgRNA plasmid. As shown in , cells expressing the csrA sgRNA had increased transcript abundance of both dadA and cycA2, by 2.4- and 5.3-fold, respectively. Thus, in rich media, CsrA negatively impacts the transcript abundance for both dadA and cycA2. These data also suggest that loss of CsrA results in increased dad gene expression, which likely contributes to the growth defects associated with loss of csrA.

Cells Lacking csrA Exhibit Envelope Integrity Defects.

Alanine is a critical component of the bacterial cell wall, as the pentapeptide side chains that comprise PG monomers begin with an L-alanine residue and end with two sequential D-alanine residues (reviewed in ref. ). We thus wondered whether the observed dysregulation of the dad locus in the ∆csrA background might impact cell envelope integrity in A. baumannii. Indeed, previous reports observed that ∆csrA mutants display morphological defects including elongated cells, which allude to possible cell wall or cell division defects (). To investigate whether the ∆csrA mutant has an impaired envelope, we grew cells on M9-succinate plates containing chlorophenyl red-β-D-galactopyranoside (CPRG), a β-galactosidase substrate that is unable to cross the envelope of gram-negative bacteria (). Therefore, lacZ-positive bacterial cells will only cleave CRPG if the strain exhibits envelope defects (i.e., an impaired cell wall or increased cellular lysis); CPRG cleavage is detected visibly as the cleaved product, chlorophenyl red (CPR), is red. To assess the integrity of the ∆csrA envelope, we used a derivative of strain AB5075 harboring the E. coli
lacZ gene at the Tn7 attachment site (WT Ptac::lacZ) () and deleted csrA from that strain (∆csrA Ptac::lacZ). Importantly, lacZ reporter activity was equivalent in both of these strains (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). When plated on CPRG-containing M9-succinate plates, the ∆csrA Ptac::lacZ strain generated robust CPRG cleavage, consistent with the ∆csrA strain having impaired envelope integrity, while the wild-type strain did not generate any red pigment (). In fact, we only detected CPR production surrounding wild-type cells in the presence of the cell wall targeting antimicrobial meropenem, indicating that CPRG is normally unable to cross the A. baumannii membrane, as anticipated (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Complementation of the ∆csrA strain with wild-type pCsrA, but not the RNA-binding deficient R44A allele, decreased CPR production, indicating that the increased CPR production in the ∆csrA strain results from the loss of CsrA’s role in posttranscriptional regulation (). We thus conclude that the ∆csrA mutant has cell envelope defects that result in increased cellular permeability and/or an increased frequency of cell lysis.

We next investigated if the ∆csrA mutant also exhibits altered sensitivity to cell wall targeting antibiotics and determined the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for four different cell wall-active antibiotics: D-cycloserine, fosfomycin, meropenem, and vancomycin. These antibiotics target discrete steps within the PG biosynthetic pathway: D-cycloserine is a structural analog of D-alanine that inhibits alanine racemase and D-alanine ligase (); fosfomycin irreversibly inhibits MurA which is involved in generating the lipid II PG carrier (reviewed in ref. ); meropenem inhibits penicillin-binding proteins; and vancomycin binds to the terminal D-ala – D-ala dipeptide on PG monomers and prevents PG cross-linking. Compared to wild-type, the ∆csrA mutant was more sensitive to meropenem, vancomycin, and D-cycloserine (). MIC values for the ∆lrp mutant were not significantly different than wild-type for any of the four antimicrobials tested. Interestingly, the ∆lrp ∆csrA double mutant had an intermediate MIC value for D-cycloserine, being more sensitive than wild-type and ∆lrp, but more resistant than the ∆csrA strain. Both ∆csrA and ∆lrp ∆csrA displayed increased resistance to fosfomycin compared to wild-type cells ().

To ascertain whether the altered sensitivity to cell-wall targeting antibiotics in the ∆csrA background results from the dysregulation of the dad locus, we performed two follow-up experiments. We first determined MICs in strains lacking individual dad genes in both wild-type and ∆csrA backgrounds. For vancomycin and fosfomycin, no significant differences were observed in dad locus mutants with respect to the csrA deletion, indicating that the csrA-mediated changes in drug resistance for these antimicrobials are independent of the dad locus (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Meropenem resistance in the ∆dadA ∆csrA strain and the ∆cycA2 ∆cycA1 ∆csrA triple mutant strains increased relative to the ∆csrA strain, but the observed differences were less than twofold, making it difficult to ascribe a definitive role for DadA or CycA2 in the meropenem resistance phenotype associated with the ∆csrA mutation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The increased sensitivity of the ∆csrA strain for D-cycloserine, however, does appear to result from dysregulation of dadA and/or cycA2, as both the ∆dadA ∆csrA and ∆cycA1 ∆cycA2 ∆csrA strains exhibit an MIC closer to wild type cells than that of the ∆csrA mutant (). The loss of csrA in the ∆dadX background mirrored ∆csrA, indicating that dadX does not contribute to D-cycloserine resistance in the ∆csrA background (). We next examined D-cycloserine resistance during ectopic expression of the dad genes in wild-type AB5075. This scenario should mimic the csrA deletion background, where the dad genes are aberrantly over expressed ( and ). In these analyses, ectopic expression with 20 μM IPTG (which does not cause toxicity, see ) of either dadA or cycA2 significantly reduced D-cycloserine resistance, whereas ectopic expression of dadX led to a slight increase in D-cycloserine resistance in wild-type AB5075 (). Because D-cycloserine inhibits alanine racemase enzymes like DadX, we speculate that the moderate increase in DadX abundance produced under the low-induction conditions used here may provide some protection. Consistent with other experimental data, ectopic expression of the ridA homolog encoded by ABUW_RS18440 did not impact D-cycloserine resistance (). Collectively, these analyses indicate that the increased expression of dadA and cycA2 are important drivers of the increased D-cycloserine sensitivity exhibited by cells lacking csrA.

Discussion

Posttranscriptional regulation plays key roles in governing gene expression across the bacterial domain. This is especially true for A. baumannii, where the highly conserved posttranscriptional regulators, Hfq (essential) and CsrA (conditionally essential), are critical for maintaining cellular physiology (–, , , ). We leveraged the conditional essentiality of CsrA through a suppressor search to identify mutations that allow a ∆csrA strain to grow in rich media. These efforts led to the isolation of 12 csrA suppressor clones, several of which contained large deletions in the dad locus. This finding suggests that the dysregulation of one or more genes in this region may drive the ∆csrA growth phenotype. Our follow-up analyses revealed that CsrA represses the translation of multiple transcripts encoded by the dad locus, and the resulting overexpression of these genes contributes to the growth defects associated with loss of csrA (see model in ). We propose that the resulting dysregulation of D-alanine metabolic processes in the absence of csrA significantly impacts A. baumannii cell envelope integrity, as the ∆csrA strain exhibits increased cellular permeability and altered sensitivity to several cell-wall targeting antibiotics.

Central to the Dad/Lrp region is the Lrp transcription factor. In other bacteria, Lrp family regulators sense the nutritional environment through small molecule ligands and regulate expression of diverse genes involved in pathways ranging from metabolism and virulence to motility and antimicrobial resistance (). The ligands recognized by Lrp family proteins thus far are largely amino acids, with direct evidence for several amino acid-Lrp pairs. E. coli Lrp binds leucine and alanine, with either amino acid driving similar regulatory outcomes (, ). The Mycobacterium tuberculosis regulator LrpA recognizes aromatic amino acids with an apparent preference for phenylalanine and histidine (). Crystallographic studies in Neisseria meningitidis indicated that its Lrp homolog can bind leucine and methionine (). In the case of A. baumannii, our experiments indicate that alanine may serve as a ligand for Lrp, as the activity of a PdadA::lacZ transcriptional reporter is activated by alanine in an lrp-dependent fashion (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). It will be important to establish whether additional amino acids influence the activity of the A. baumannii Lrp in future studies.

Lrp family proteins act either as global regulators, controlling expression of hundreds of genes or as local regulators that control only a few (). For A. baumannii, our transcriptomic analyses indicate that Lrp functions as a global regulator, with a regulon approaching 750 genes ( and Dataset S2). Not surprisingly, the predicted functions of the Lrp regulon span many categories, including metabolism and motility (i.e., components of the type IV pilus). Most pertinent to CsrA, however, is our finding that Lrp activates the expression of the dad locus, including dadA, dadX, ABUW_RS18440 (a predicted RidA family protein), and cycA2. This locus initially caught our interest because these genes are missing in three of the four DadA/Lrp region suppressors, and Lrp is positioned immediately upstream and transcribed divergently from dadA in A. baumannii (). In E. coli, dadA and dadX comprise an operon, while cycA is transcribed from a different genomic location, but despite the different genetic context, the E. coli Lrp directly regulates transcription of the dadAX operon and cycA (–). Recent work from the Freddolino group indicates that Lrp influences the expression of nearly one third of the E. coli genome through direct and indirect mechanisms (). We speculate that the A. baumannii Lrp protein functions similarly, as among the Lrp-regulated genes, 36 encode predicted DNA-binding transcription factors. It will thus be of interest to determine the direct regulatory targets for Lrp in A. baumannii.

Our data indicate that CsrA negatively regulates expression of the dad locus, likely through direct interactions. We also considered the possibility that the lrp transcript may be a regulatory target of CsrA in A. baumannii, which was previously observed in E. coli (). Similarly, in Vibrio cholerae, transcriptomic data indicate that cells expressing an RNA-binding deficient CsrA allele exhibit increased expression of lrp transcripts at multiple growth stages, although whether this reflects CsrA directly targeting the lrp mRNA transcript was not determined (). We did not detect enrichment of lrp transcripts RIP-qPCR with CsrA-V (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), nor did we detect differences in activity of an lrp::lacZ translational fusion reporter between wild-type and ∆csrA (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We do note, however, that the loss of csrA may indirectly impact lrp expression, as a Plrp::lacZ transcriptional showed increased activity in the ∆csrA background, although this did not complement when we introduced a plasmid-borne copy of csrA (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Thus, while CsrA may indirectly regulate lrp, the translational reporter data and the RIP-qPCR data argue against CsrA directly associating with the lrp mRNA.

One important question that remains unanswered is how CsrA and Lrp, both of which are global regulators in A. baumannii, coordinate the expression of not only the dad locus but also their other regulatory targets. For the dad locus, our evidence indicates that Lrp activates transcription in response to alanine (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) and that the dad transcript(s) are repressed by CsrA (). What mechanisms might be in place to counteract CsrA-mediated repression of the dad transcript when alanine is present? One possibility is that CsrA levels are stable across different growth conditions and CsrA essentially buffers against overexpression of the dad transcripts. Alternatively, Lrp may indirectly control CsrA activity by modulating the expression of one or more regulatory RNAs that sequester CsrA, such as CsrB (). Intriguingly, CsrB transcript abundance increased approximately twofold in the ∆lrp strain background (Dataset S2), suggesting that a feedback mechanism to coordinate the activities of Lrp and CsrA may exist. Given the importance of finely tuning expression of the dad locus, it is imperative to determine how these two global regulators coordinate their respective activities.

Here we sought to uncover the genetic basis for the conditional essentiality of csrA in A. baumannii. Through a suppressor search, we determined that loss of csrA results in dysregulation of a suite of genes involved with D-alanine metabolism. These genes, whose transcripts are translationally repressed by CsrA, are toxic when ectopically expressed, even in wild type A. baumannii. We suspect that this dysregulation manifests as impaired cellular envelope integrity in the ∆csrA mutant and further alters the antibiotic resistance profile, particularly for cell-wall targeting antimicrobials. Taken together, our findings reveal an intricate balancing act between CsrA and Lrp, two global regulators that coordinate the expression of several genes involved with D-amino acid metabolism. CsrA dampens the expression of potentially toxic proteins, implying that CsrA is essential because it counters the activity of Lrp, which drives the expression of the dad locus. Our data thus provide additional complexity regarding how bacteria utilize posttranscriptional regulation to protect against potentially toxic effects of transcription.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains and Plasmids.

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotides are listed in SI Appendix, Tables S1–S3. A. baumannii AB5075 and mutant strains thereof were cultured in M9 media supplemented with 20 mM of carbon source(s), such as succinate and alanine or in LB. A. baumannii strain AB5075 is known to switch between opaque and translucent subpopulations and thus experiments conducted herein potentially contained a mixture of cells in these two phases (, ). All plasmids and mutant strains were confirmed by sequencing and/or PCR prior to use. Specific details for plasmid and strain construction can be found in SI Appendix.

Isolation of ∆csrA Suppressors.

Individual colonies of ∆csrA were inoculated into 5 mL of LB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm). Every 24 h, cultures were diluted 1:100 into a fresh portion of LB media until cultures were turbid, at which point 100 μL was removed and plated onto solid LB. Suppressors typically arose in the second LB culture. Isolated colonies of the candidate suppressors were purified to M9-succinate plates and growth on LB was confirmed the following day. Colonies that retained growth on LB following passage through M9-succinate were stored at −80 °C in a 20% glycerol solution for further analysis. A total of 12 independent suppressors were isolated from two independently derived ∆csrA strains (six clones from each ∆csrA parent).

Whole Genome Sequencing of Suppressors.

Bacterial genomic DNA was isolated using the MasterPure DNA Purification Kit (Biosearch Technologies) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed by SeqCenter (Pittsburg, PA). WGS analysis was performed with Geneious Prime (v 11.0.20.1+1) and mutations identified are listed in and Dataset S1. Raw WGS sequencing reads have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject ID: PRJNA1391534 ().

β-Galactosidase Assays.

β-galactosidase assays were conducted essentially as described previously (). Briefly, triplicate overnight cultures of the indicated reporter strains were diluted 1:50 in fresh media and cultured until early exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 0.1 to 0.2). Bacterial cells were permeabilized with chloroform and SDS and β-galactosidase activity was determined using 2-nitro-phenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG). Assays were independently repeated at least three times; average values from one representative run are reported. Statistical analyses were conducted by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2.

D-Amino Acid Dehydrogenase Assay.

DadA activity was assayed using a modified D-amino acid dehydrogenase protocol (). AB5075 strains and its derivatives were grown in M9-succinate supplemented with 20 mM L-alanine overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The next day, 1 OD600 unit of cells were washed in phosphate buffer, permeabilized with toluene and diluted 1:10 in phosphate buffer containing 20 mM D-alanine and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min after which 250 μL of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (0.17 mg/mL in 1.2 N HCl) was added to the reaction tubes. The reactions were mixed, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and neutralized with 1 mL of 2.5 N NaOH for 10 min at room temperature. The OD520 of each reaction was recorded and normalized to total protein content as described in SI Appendix. Results are reported as DadA units/mg of protein; assays were conducted with biological triplicate cultures and technical duplicates; at least three replicate runs were completed.

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR.

RNA was extracted from two mL of culture using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research, cat. no. R2052) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. After extraction, one μg of RNA was converted to cDNA using LunaScript RT SuperMix (New England Biolabs, cat. no. E3010) as recommended by the manufacturer. Prior to use in qPCR reactions, the cDNA was diluted 1:10. qPCR reactions were performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad, cat. no. 1725122), and samples were run with technical triplicate reactions. The relative abundances of target genes were normalized to a control transcript (recA) using the 2−∆∆CT method as described previously (). RT-qPCR experiments were independently repeated twice with biological triplicate cultures; results are shown as the mean change in relative transcript abundance for each sgRNA. Error bars represent one SD of the mean. Statistical analysis was conducted with a two-tailed t test. Prior to use, all primers used in RT-qPCR were validated for efficiency using a standard curve and melt-temp analysis.

RNA Immunoprecipitation.

Samples for RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) were collected as previously described (, ). Briefly, 40 OD600 units of exponentially growing AB5075 CsrA-V were processed for RIP as described in SI Appendix; parallel samples were processed without adding antibodies as a mock IP control. Following IP, RNA was extracted, converted to cDNA, and subjected to RT-qPCR as described above with the exception that GlycoBlue (Ambion, cat. no. AM9515) was added to aid RNA extraction. The relative abundance of target transcripts was normalized to that of recA as a control. Data are reported as the enrichment detected in the IP samples compared to the no antibody control. RIP-qPCR experiments were repeated twice with independent biological triplicates. The mean relative enrichment from all six RIP replicates with error bars representing one SD of the mean are shown.

RNA-Sequencing.

RNA was extracted from four mL of triplicate cultures of exponentially growing (OD600 ≈ 0.5) wild-type AB5075 and the ∆lrp strain in LB. DNAse treatment, library preparation, and Illumina sequencing were performed by SeqCenter (Pittsburgh, PA) to produce 150 bp paired end reads. Reads were aligned to the AB5075 genome using Bowtie2 () and differential gene expression was determined using HTSeq () and DESeq2 (). A log2 fold change value greater than/less than 1 and an adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 were used to determine significance. Integrated Genomics Viewer (IGV) software was used for visualization of RNA-seq data (). For the RNA-seq data, the raw sequence read files and processed files have been deposited with the Gene Expression Omnibus repository under accession number GSE313900 ().

CPRG Assay.

Cell wall integrity was assessed using a chlorophenyl red-β-D-galactopyranoside (CPRG) assay (). Strains containing Ptac::lacZ at the Tn7 attachment site () were serially diluted and spotted onto M9-succinate plates containing apramycin (25 μg/mL) and CPRG (20 μg/mL). Plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight and photographed. CPRG assay was repeated independently on different days with freshly transformed strains for a total three total replicate runs.

MIC Testing.

MIC testing was performed in a 96-well plate in biological triplicate for each strain. Overnight cultures were refreshed into M9-succinate (1:50) and allowed to grow for approximately 6 h; when necessary, apramycin was added for plasmid maintenance. Strains were then diluted to OD600 = 0.002 and added to the wells at a 1:2 dilution (final bacterial concentration OD600 = 0.001). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. MIC values were determined using the OD600 values of the wells compared to control wells containing no antibiotic and no bacteria. MIC testing was independently repeated at least three times. The results from three representative experiments are shown.
