1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Overview: This project, led by researchers at UCLA in partnership with Southwest
Solar Technology (SST) and SolGrapH, successfully demonstrated a new method for
producing clean hydrogen fuel and high-quality graphite using concentrated solar
energy. The primary goal was to develop a technology that converts natural gas
(primarily methane) into two valuable products—hydrogen gas and solid
carbon—without releasing carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere. This process,
known as solar thermal pyrolysis, uses heat from the sun to split the methane molecules
directly.

Major Achievements: The project team designed, built, and tested a field-scale reactor
capable of operating in a real-world solar environment. Key accomplishments in
various technological, technoeconomic, and educational impact sub-areas follow.

Solar-Driven Hydrogen Production: The team successfully demonstrated that
concentrated sunlight can be used to decompose natural gas into hydrogen and carbon
at a pilot scale. This process effectively locks away the carbon that would otherwise
become a greenhouse gas, creating a valuable solid product (graphite) instead.

 Page 1

Roll-to-Roll Carbon Capture: A major technical breakthrough was the development
and successful field testing of a ‘roll-to-roll’ manufacturing system. This mechanism
continuously moves a specialized fabric through the reactor to catch the solid carbon
(graphite) as it forms, preventing the system from clogging and allowing for continuous
operation. This is the first known demonstration of such a system in a direct-solar field
experiment to make graphite.

Economic Viability: The project utilized advanced economic modeling to show that
this technology can produce clean hydrogen at a cost of approximately $1.20 per
kilogram. This cost is very close to the U.S. Department of Energy’s “Hydrogen Shot”
target of $1/kg. The low cost is made possible because the solid carbon byproduct is
high-quality graphite, a valuable material used in batteries and other industries. Selling
the graphite offsets the cost of producing the hydrogen.

Impact and Future Potential: This research proves that it is possible to produce
affordable, clean hydrogen from natural gas while simultaneously creating a valuable
domestic supply of graphite. By using solar energy to drive the reaction, the process
eliminates the carbon footprint typically associated with natural gas processing. The
success of the field tests in Casa Grande, Arizona, paves the way for commercializing
this technology, which could play a critical role in decarbonizing the energy sector and
providing low-cost hydrogen for transportation, power generation, and industrial use.

Educational and Community Impact: The project also provided significant training
opportunities for undergraduate and graduate students, including many first-generation
college students, equipping a new generation of engineers with skills in renewable
energy technology. Results were disseminated through peer-reviewed journal
publications and international symposiums and presentations, fostering global
collaboration on low-carbon solutions.

2. ACCOMPLISHMENTS:

a. What are the major goals of the project?
This project aims to advance and commercially translate the technology that uses
concentrated solar radiation to convert natural gas (NG) into hydrogen, and into
high-quality graphite as a value-added byproduct. This solar-driven pyrolysis process
involves no catalyst and releases zero CO2 emissions by capturing the carbon into a
high-value commodity in a readily scalable, roll-to-roll (R2R) process while producing
a high-yield stream of hydrogen gas.

Our specific research aims include:
1. Produce high-yield hydrogen from NG, sufficient for multiple end uses

 Page 2

 2. Demonstrate hydrogen and high-value graphitic carbon production from natural
 gas (NG) using a novel solar-driven thermal process
 3. Advance the technology readiness level (TRL) status of the technology from TRL
 4 (validated in laboratory environment) to TRL 6 (pilot scale in relevant environment)
 for hydrogen production
 4. Demonstrate potential for the system to produce cost-competitive hydrogen
 consistent with the Hydrogen Energy Earthshot target of $1/kgH2 by 2030.

 b. What was accomplished under these goals?

 The majority of effort was directed toward field experiments that would demonstrate
 the process in a relevant real solar environment while introducing mechanisms for
 scale-up such as roll-to-roll (R2R) carbon capture. To this end, the team worked
 extensively at the Casa Grande site to integrate the R2R mechanism and several other
 process and on-line diagnostics improvements. We completed the integration and
 successfully implemented the full system on-sun over several days of testing at the end
 of the project, acquiring the data contained in the remainder of the report. To our
 knowledge, this work represents the first ever demonstration of R2R carbon capture in
 a direct-solar field pyrolysis experiment, and the experiment produced significant yields
 of hydrogen gas, as well as high-quality and -purity graphitic carbon byproduct.
 We achieved the primary project objectives of the program by demonstrating a
 first-of-kind clean, continuous pyrolysis process for production of hydrogen at TRL 6.
 We estimated the graphite co-product to be at TRL 5 because additional advanced
 processing steps must be developed for commercial readiness. Moreover, our results
 enabled a refined model of technoeconomics of the process in which the higher-value
 co-product (graphite) enables substantial reductions in projected hydrogen production
 costs. Our end result in a hydrogen cost of $1.20/kg, which slightly exceeds the
 ‘Earthshot’ target of $1/kg but also represents a much lower cost of hydrogen than any
 other clean hydrogen process known to us.

methane, acetylene, and ethylene – three of the most abundant hydrocarbons during the pyrolysis

of natural gas. Measurements of these three hydrocarbons in the exhaust stream facilitated indirect

3.1 Create Thermochemical System Model 10/1/23-10/1/24

To support the design, optimization, and field demonstration of the direct solar-driven methane
pyrolysis reactor, we developed a thermochemical system model to interpret experimental
behavior, guide engineering decisions, and reduce technical risk during on-sun testing. The
modeling effort progressed from an initial first-order thermochemical description to a
multi-physics framework incorporating optical concentration, heat transfer, gas flow, and reaction
thermodynamics. These models were used throughout the project to inform reactor geometry,
thermal management strategies, and successful operation at TRL 6 for hydrogen production.

The solar-driven pyrolysis process is characterized by strong coupling between non-uniform
radiative heating, heterogeneous reactions within a porous carbon substrate, gas-phase transport,
and heat loss to the surrounding reactor structure. To address this complexity, we employed a
tiered modeling approach that combines multiple complementary tools. A one-dimensional
thermochemical reactor model (Fig. 27) was used to capture reaction trends and the evolution of
gas-phase composition under varying operating conditions. Optical ray tracing simulations were
performed to quantify the spatial distribution of solar heat flux delivered to the substrate. In
addition, a control-volume–based thermal network model was developed to represent heat transfer
between the substrate, reactor walls, window, nipple, coolant interfaces, and the ambient
environment through effective thermal resistances. Together, these models formed a system-level
toolset that directly supported engineering design decisions and field operation planning.

 Fig. 27.Control-volume–based thermal network model schematic

 Page 24

A one-dimensional plug-flow reactor (PFR) model was developed early in the project to describe
methane pyrolysis within the porous carbon substrate. In this formulation, the reacting gas stream
is treated as plug flow through a thin, irradiated porous medium, with species evolution governed
primarily by residence time, temperature, and pressure. This approach is consistent with prior
studies of direct solar irradiation of porous absorbers, which demonstrate that short residence times
lead to kinetically controlled, non-equilibrium product distributions rather than equilibrium
chemistry outcomes.

Independent temperature-estimation studies were used to characterize the thermal environment
within the porous carbon substrate under direct solar irradiation. These analyses showed that
methane decomposition occurs within a thin, highly heated region exhibiting extreme temperatures
and strong spatial gradients, while remaining quasi-steady over experimental timescales.
Consistent with the plug-flow formulation described above, these results indicate that reaction
behavior is governed by short residence times and kinetically limited processes within the
substrate.

Because the porous carbon substrate is the primary locus of both solar energy absorption and
chemical reaction, the modeling effort was intentionally focused on this region. Accordingly, the
one-dimensional thermochemical model treats gas-phase species evolution as a function of
residence time through a representative substrate temperature, providing a computationally
efficient and physically consistent framework for capturing dominant reaction trends without
resolving detailed spatial temperature distributions (Fig. 28).

3.3 Integrate Field Concentrator and Receiver / Reactor and Establish Baseline Yield and
Production Rates UCLA and SST 1/2/25-4/1/25

We successfully tested the reactor integrated on the sun with the field concentrator to establish
baseline yields and production rates. Through these tests, we proved that this process is successful

 Page 30

not only in a laboratory setting but also in the field with real sunlight. These tests began with a
single panel outputting around 4 kW up to a maximum of four panels outputting 16 kW in the ideal
case where there is no cloud cover and the dishes are perfectly aligned and cleaned. Starting with
flow rates similar to lab conditions we walked our way up from 100 SCCM of methane to 3000
SCCM of methane. In future test campaigns, we continued to increase the flow rate. During this
initial test campaign, we troubleshooted multiple issues on the fly including, predominantly, an
issue of substrate size. Due to the larger spot size, we initially encountered problems with melting
the retaining ring that held the substrate. In an initial test, this melted retaining ring fell onto the
main quartz viewport causing it to crack but not break. However, by increasing our nipple and

The major improvement to system performance that we developed was a R2R system for use in the
field. This system consistently rolls new carbon fiber cloth through the reaction zone to allow for
consistent deposition at a higher rate (Fig. 47). In order for this system to operate properly, we
developed a python script run through a RaspberryPi that connects to a stepper driver and then a
stepper motor. The script takes in the initial diameter of the spools of cloth substrate as well as the
roll speed and total time of rolling. From these values an array of frequencies for rolling and thus
delay times for the stepper motors are produced. A high-low square wave is then pulsed to the
stepper motor with these varying delay times in order to change the rotational speed of each spool
as a function of time and their diameter to ensure that there is a consistent linear roll speed across
the reaction zone. In addition a Zircar shield was added in front of the R2R hardware to protect its
components when moving on-sun (Figs. 48 and 49).

Throughout the last quarter of the project, lessons from the past test campaigns of the project were
leveraged to modify the current system for our final test campaign. To start, after observing reactor
temperatures during tests, we realized we could significantly reduce the nacelle covered area of the
reactor (Fig. 50). It was clear the original nacelle design was oversized and as a result, a portion of
the incident concentrated light was being clipped. To improve the transfer of concentrated light
into the reactor and to the porous substrate, a section of the radiation shield was removed.
Additionally, as mentioned earlier, we noticed our butterfly valve that is used to maintain system
pressure during tests, was incapable of deployment across all pressure setpoints of interest. Thus,
we significantly overhauled the system’s PID control by performing a parametric sweep of flow
rate and system pressure across our entire operational space. The Ziegler-Nichols method was
leveraged to obtain the best PID gain values for reactor operation. In addition to these
modifications, a robust methodology for controlling fluids delivery into the reactor was established
to protect against LabVIEW system failure during testing in the field.

4.1 Model Technoeconomics SolGrapH 10/1/23-10/1/24

Preliminary analysis of process technoeconomics focused on comparing the energy cost per
kilogram of hydrogen produced for various hydrogen production technologies. These technologies
include a) steam-methane reforming (SMR), b) water electrolysis, c) direct-solar thermal methane
pyrolysis (this work) and d) methane pyrolysis via resistive heating. From basic thermochemistry,
electrolysis (a) requires 39.7 kWh per kg H2, whereas SMR and the pyrolysis processes (a, c, d)
require similar energy input for chemical conversion: 5.7 kWh per kg of H2 produced for SMR (a)
and 5.2 kWh per kg of H2 for pyrolysis (c, d). A survey of typical SMR efficiencies and first-order
analysis of the pyrolysis process supported an assumption of an 80% thermal-to-chemical
efficiency for SMR and methane pyrolysis. This implies a thermal energy requirement of 7.1
kWh/kg H2 for SMR and 6.6 kWh/kg H2 for pyrolysis. Typical water electrolysis efficiencies are
up to 80%, implying an electricity requirement of at least 49.6 kWh/kg H2 for electrolysis.

All processes were assumed to ultimately derive their energy from solar irradiation to enable
quantitative comparison between the technologies as emissions-free hydrogen production
technologies. SMR (b) and the direct solar-thermal pyrolysis process (c) were both assumed to use
direct solar-heating as the heat source, with a 50% optical-to-thermal efficiency. The
resistive-heating pyrolysis process (d) and the electrolysis process (a) were assumed to derive
energy from photovoltaic (PV) conversion of solar energy to electricity. This process is assumed to
be 20% efficient, based on the current state-of-the-art. The resistive heating process was assumed
to have an additional 75% electrical-to-thermal energy conversion efficiency.

 Page 44

The total solar energy required per kg of H2 produced can be calculated by accounting for all of the
energy conversion efficiencies of the various processes. These energy requirements are
summarized in Table 2, along with overall process efficiencies and co-product production rates.
The proposed solar-thermal pyrolysis technology in this work requires approximately 13 kWh of
solar energy per kg H2, which is 19 times lower than the solar energy required to produce green
hydrogen via water electrolysis. The pyrolysis process is 3 times more efficient when using direct
solar-thermal heating versus resistive heating via renewable solar-derived electricity, highlighting
the benefit of using direct solar heating. The process efficiency / energy requirement for both direct
solar-thermal methane pyrolysis and SMR is very similar, but methane pyrolysis has the advantage
of producing a valuable 3 kg of graphitic carbon co-product per kg H2 as opposed to the production
of 5.5 kg of waste CO2 per kg H2 for SMR.

This initial study served as the basis for the development of a techno-economic modeling tool,
which compared the financial viability of producing clean hydrogen using direct solar-thermal
methane pyrolysis. The model unit economic application uses MATLAB’s application editor
toolbox (Fig. 55). This application is able to be run on any PC using MATLAB’s free runtime
engine. A screenshot of the application is shown in the figure below with arbitrary but
representative input values. The application takes various technoeconomic inputs and outputs
financial metrics which indicate the viability of the solar-thermal hydrogen production process
based on a discounted cash flow analysis. The program dynamically updates the output
parameters/graphs as users change inputs, either through text entry or by manipulating sliders.

Under the “Unit Information” heading, key parameters about the solar-thermal hydrogen
production unit are specified. These parameters include the unit power, which reflects the peak
concentrated solar power for the unit’s solar concentrator. This power is used to inform the
maximum instantaneous rate of methane conversion using the “Solar-to-Chemical Efficiency”
parameter, which characterizes how much of the incident solar energy is available to chemically
convert methane into hydrogen and carbon. This conversion amount is scaled by the insolation
fraction, which represents the effective fraction of the day/year for which peak solar radiation is

 Page 45

incident upon the solar concentrator used for the process. This number is informed by geographical
information from the National Solar Resource Database (NSRDB). For the Casa Grande location
of this project’s demonstration, the insolation fraction is approximately 31%. The total productivity
level is further degraded by the “Operational Downtime” parameter, which captures the amount of
time annually that the system would need to be non-operational for maintenance.

The annual methane conversion is then converted into maximum annual hydrogen and carbon
production amounts via stoichiometry. These numbers are reduced from the ideal maximum
amounts by assumed process inefficiencies/losses. These losses include carbon/hydrogen yields
(completeness of the chemical reaction), graphitic carbon fraction (percentage of deposited carbon
which is graphic in nature), and graphite/hydrogen recovery fractions (fraction of products which
remain after purification or post-processing). The annual production amounts are then multiplied
by the assumed commodity sale prices to estimate the total annual revenue.

  Fig. 55. Image of unit economic model application generated with MATLAB. Input values are
 representative and not finalized

Both capital expenditures (CapEx) and operating expenditures are then calculated. Capital
expenditures are input via a table, and are split into general CapEx required for both graphite and
hydrogen solar-synthesis, and hydrogen-specific CapEx required to purify the output gas stream
(to separate hydrogen from the remaining methane and trace hydrocarbons in the exhaust stream)
and compress the hydrogen to a sufficiently dense form for storage and/or transportation.

Operating expenditures include natural gas cost, solid substrate costs, rent, operations/management
(O&M), graphite finishing costs, miscellaneous expenditures, and electricity costs for the pumps
needed to maintain system vacuum, for hydrogen pressure-swing adsorption, and high-pressure

 Page 46

compression. These costs are summed to find the annual cost of goods sold (COGS). The annual
gross profit is found by taking the difference between the annual revenue and annual COGS.

On the right side of the user interface, outputs are shown. Under “Production/Consumption”, the
daily and annual amounts of methane/electricity consumed and hydrogen/graphite produced are
shown. Below this, a bar chart of the revenue and COGS is shown, with each bar being broken up
into smaller sections indicating the various contributors to revenue and COGS. Below this, the
cumulative cashflow is shown versus time for the entire unit lifetime (another user input). This plot
is generated by cumulatively adding the annual gross profit to the negative of the initial CapEx.
This plot indicates the breakeven year, beyond which the cumulative profits exceed the initial
capital investment.

Financial outputs include the aforementioned annual revenue/COGS, the annual profit, and
breakeven year. Additionally, the “annualized CapEx” is displayed, which represents the amount of
money that needs to be recuperated yearly to recoup the initial capital expenditures, adjusted by an
assumed discount rate (another user input). Additionally, the gross profit margin and internal rate
of return are included to indicate the financial viability of the unit. Financial parameters are also
indicated in the case of a hydrogen tax credit, which are used in this model to reduce the annual
COGS.

The tool also calculates the levelized costs of graphite and hydrogen production based on the
inputs. The contributors to the levelized costs are displayed in pie charts on the upper right corner
of the graphical user interface (GUI). The levelized costs are calculated by dividing the total
discounted costs of production over the unit lifetime (including both CapEx and OpEx) by the
discounted total production (in kg). The costs of production are allocated to the two co-products in
the following way: general capital expenditures (not associated with hydrogen
purification/compression), general process operating expenditures (natural gas cost, substrate
costs), and graphite post-processing costs are allocated to graphite as the more valuable
commodity. Hydrogen-specific CapEx (for hydrogen purification/compression) and electricity
costs for pressure-swing adsorption and compression pumps are allocated to hydrogen. The costs
associated with rent, O&M, and miscellaneous costs are allocated between graphite and hydrogen
on the basis of relative CapEx or revenue.

4.2 Assess Life-Cycle Techno-economics and Emissions SolGrapH 10/2/24-9/30/25

SolGrapH enhanced the techno-economic model with revised inputs relating to system efficiencies,
capital costs, and ongoing costs. This economic modeling was used to predict the levelized cost of
hydrogen using the solar methane pyrolysis process: $1.20/kg. The low cost of hydrogen produced
with this process comes from the co-production of graphite, which is produced in a higher mass
quantity (3:1 carbon to hydrogen) and can be sold for a higher per mass price. As such, a majority
of the capital expenses and operating costs for the process can be recuperated with graphite sales
alone, such that the costs associated with hydrogen production are only those incremental costs
required to capture, purify, and compress the hydrogen exhaust. These costs represent only ~5% of

 Page 47

the capital expenses and ~10% of operating expenses, driving the levelized cost of hydrogen
production down significantly compared to other hydrogen production technologies. Regarding
emissions, we note that the process creates no CO2 emissions, as no oxygen is included in the
reaction.

One important element of the techno-economic analysis relates to the quality of graphite produced.
To this end, SolGrapH extracted samples from the laboratory process (the field graphite became
available too late to test) and performed rough ball milling to produce graphite particles of various
sizes that were not coated with a pitch process (as universally added for battery anode graphite to
enhance lithiation capacity and cyclic stability). It then purchased commercially available
comparable uncoated, battery-grade graphite powder (MTI Corp, synthetic graphite, Lib-CMSG)
and conducted half-coin-cell lithium cycling tests of both samples, as shown in Fig. 56.

 Fig. 56. Lithium half-cell cycling of uncoated solar-derived graphite powder and uncoated

5. IMPACT:

What is the impact of the project? How has it contributed?

a. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?

Graphite has been deemed a critical material by the US government. The reported
technology represents a breakthrough in which catalyst-free solar-thermal hydrogen
production by methane pyrolysis uniquely and rapidly produces high-purity,
high-value graphite. The direct solar-thermal reactor mitigates prior practical
limitations of carbon deposition on the reactor walls and windows. The localized,
concentrated radiation avoids high overall reactor operating temperatures that
increase heat losses and ramp-up times, and allows photo-thermal autocatalytic
effects that are not possible in indirect solar reactors. The solar-driven co-production
of graphite with hydrogen provides a means of providing electrode materials for the
rapidly growing Li-ion battery market, as well as demand for clean hydrogen.

b. What is the impact on other disciplines?

Currently, cost-effective production of clean hydrogen has proved elusive, and there
is no meaningful domestic supply of zero-emission synthetic graphite. Our initial
financial projections indicate basic viability of the co-product business model by
generating multiple product revenue streams over an integrated production cost
structure. However, the projections require more detailed and comprehensive
techno-economic analysis as they contain numerous coarse estimates ranging from
technical assumptions (e.g., process throughput and efficiency), to market factors
(e.g., raw material and capital equipment costs), to post-production finishing
processes (e.g., graphite purification, spheroidization and coating for battery use).

c. What is the impact on the development of human resources?

The project has been predominantly staffed by student researchers. These students
gained invaluable exposure to advanced fundamental scientific principles and methods
as well as systems engineering concepts for integrating functions into an overall
working system, including large-scale solar systems in the field. The topics include:
optics, heat transfer, fluid mechanics, thermochemistry, vacuum science, spectroscopy,

 Page 53

experimental design, and data analysis, among others. The breadth of these topics is
much more extensive than a typical university research project, and we have also
explicitly involved undergraduate students from diverse backgrounds through UCLA’s
CEED program (Center for Excellence in Engineering and Diversity), including cohorts
of students that began in the Fall 2024 and Fall 2025 quarters. At the same time, the
students are taking a variety of formal coursework to build additional foundational
knowledge in many engineering subjects related to project tasks. We have also brought
in a group of 3 undergraduate summer researchers through the Amazon SURE
(Summer Undergraduate Research Experience) program.

d. What was the impact on teaching and educational experiences?

The PI has incorporated the topic of methane decomposition analysis with exergy
principles into his undergraduate Engineering Thermodynamics course as the basis of
its final design project. The class typically consists of approx. 90 students, and the
projects involve teams of 3 students each.

e. What is the impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form
infrastructure?

This project was the first at UCLA to establish a sizeable (>1kW) solar dish
concentrator in the field. As such, the project introduced new infrastructure to the
university and its researchers.

g. What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?

The domestic energy industry has long been the source of economic strength for the
US. The opportunity exists here to create an entirely new domestic source of clean
and cost-effective synthetic graphite to support the clean energy transition. Hydrogen
will be co-produced with the solid graphite at approximately ⅓ of the mass output.
The clean hydrogen produced (no CO2 production involved) will help meet growing
demand for alternatives to steam-methane reforming (SMR), and in doing so, H2
sales into commodity green-H2 markets are expected to replace SMR-produced
hydrogen such that the economics of the overall solar-thermal process will improve

9. PROJECT OUTCOMES:

What were the outcomes of the award?

Major Achievements: The project team designed, built, and tested a field-scale reactor
capable of operating in a real-world solar environment. Key accomplishments in
various technological, technoeconomic, and educational impact sub-areas follow.

Solar-Driven Hydrogen Production: The team successfully demonstrated that
concentrated sunlight can be used to decompose natural gas into hydrogen and carbon
at a pilot scale. This process effectively locks away the carbon that would otherwise
become a greenhouse gas, creating a valuable solid product (graphite) instead.

Roll-to-Roll Carbon Capture: A major technical breakthrough was the development
and successful field testing of a ‘roll-to-roll’ manufacturing system. This mechanism
continuously moves a specialized fabric through the reactor to catch the solid carbon
(graphite) as it forms, preventing the system from clogging and allowing for continuous
operation. This is the first known demonstration of such a system in a direct-solar field
experiment to make graphite.

Economic Viability: The project utilized advanced economic modeling to show that
this technology can produce clean hydrogen at a cost of approximately $1.20 per
kilogram. This cost is very close to the U.S. Department of Energy’s “Hydrogen Shot”
target of $1/kg. The low cost is made possible because the solid carbon byproduct is
high-quality graphite, a valuable material used in batteries and other industries. Selling
the graphite offsets the cost of producing the hydrogen.

Impact and Future Potential: This research proves that it is possible to produce
affordable, clean hydrogen from natural gas while simultaneously creating a valuable
domestic supply of graphite. By using solar energy to drive the reaction, the process
eliminates the carbon footprint typically associated with natural gas processing. The
success of the field tests in Casa Grande, Arizona, paves the way for commercializing
this technology, which could play a critical role in decarbonizing the energy sector and
providing low-cost hydrogen for transportation, power generation, and industrial use.

Educational and Community Impact: The project also provided significant training
opportunities for undergraduate and graduate students, including many first-generation
college students, equipping a new generation of engineers with skills in renewable
energy technology. Results were disseminated through peer-reviewed journal
publications and international symposiums and presentations, fostering global
collaboration on low-carbon solutions.
