Diagnostic accuracy of fluorescence microscopy of hematoxylin–eosin-stained nail sections for onychomycosis
Authors: Erickah Mary Therese R. Dy-Calayag, Claudine Yap Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOnychomycosis requires laboratory confirmation before prolonged oral antifungal therapy is initiated. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining is highly sensitive for the histopathologic diagnosis of onychomycosis but increases cost and turnaround time. This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of fluorescence microscopy of routine hematoxylin–eosin (H&E)-stained nail sections compared with PAS-stained nail sections.MethodsA diagnostic accuracy study was performed using archived nail clipping slides from patients evaluated between 2019 and 2023. Fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail clippings served as the index test, while PAS-stained nail clippings served as the reference standard. Slides were randomly selected from de-identified records and independently assessed. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated.ResultsA total of 84 slides were included, comprising 42 PAS-positive onychomycosis slides and 42 PAS-negative non-onychomycosis slides. Fluorescence microscopy showed a sensitivity of 80.95% (95% confidence interval [CI], 65.88–91.40%) and a specificity of 97.62% (95% CI, 87.43–99.94%). The PPV and NPV were 97.14% (95% CI, 85.08–99.93%) and 83.67% (95% CI, 70.34–92.68%), respectively. False-negative results were primarily associated with sparse fungal elements and yeast forms lacking pseudohyphae, while the single false-positive result was associated with fluorescent plasma globules and neutrophils.ConclusionFluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail sections exhibits high specificity and may serve as a rapid, lower-cost adjunctive diagnostic method for confirming onychomycosis, particularly in resource-limited settings where fluorescence microscopy is available.

Introduction

Onychomycosis, a fungal infection of the nail unit, is a common nail disorder affecting 5.5–10% of the general global population (1, 2). It accounts for at least 50% of all nail diseases, which can impair the quality of life and predispose affected patients to secondary bacterial infection (1, 2). In tropical and low- to middle-income countries, fungal infections are a common reason for dermatology consultation (3). Laboratory confirmation is recommended before prolonged oral antifungal therapy is started because treatment is lengthy, costly, and associated with potential adverse effects (1, 2).

Common diagnostic methods include potassium hydroxide (KOH) examination, fungal culture, and the histopathologic evaluation of nail clippings (4, 5). KOH microscopy is rapid and inexpensive, but its reported sensitivity is variable, with pooled estimates of approximately 61% sensitivity and 95% specificity. Fungal culture can identify the causative organism, but its pooled sensitivity is approximately 56%, and the results may require several weeks to up to 1 month (6, 7). Histopathologic evaluation of nail clippings may be particularly useful when KOH examination and culture are negative or inconclusive despite persistent clinical suspicion, when nail dystrophy is atypical and alternative diagnoses are considered, or when treatment failure, recurrent disease, or immunocompromised status warrants further evaluation (8). Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining is among the most sensitive histopathologic methods (4, 5), and meta-analysis data demonstrate that PAS staining outperforms KOH examination and fungal culture in diagnostic accuracy (4). In addition to demonstrating fungal elements within the nail plate, nail clipping histopathology allows simultaneous evaluation of alternative nail pathologies. However, special stains add cost and processing time, which may be a barrier in resource-constrained settings (9).

Fluorescence microscopy of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections has been proposed as a rapid method for detecting fungal elements without additional special staining. The observed fluorescence has been variably attributed to fungal autofluorescence and fluorescence enhanced by routine H&E staining; therefore, the mechanism may reflect both stain-related and intrinsic fungal structural properties (10–13). Previous studies have suggested that this approach could shorten turnaround time, although its value appeared limited in many superficial and deep mycoses (11–13). Nail specimens may be an exception. Idriss et al. have reported 96% sensitivity and 90% specificity for fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail sections compared with PAS staining in clinically suspected onychomycosis (14). Validation in other settings remains limited.

This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail sections compared with PAS staining for the diagnosis of onychomycosis and described fluorescence findings associated with true-positive, false-negative, and false-positive interpretations.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This diagnostic accuracy study was conducted at the Department of Dermatology, University of the Philippines–Philippine General Hospital (UP-PGH), Manila, from April to June 2024 using archived nail clipping slides from specimens collected between January 2019 and December 2023. The index test was fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail sections. The reference standard was the histopathologic PAS result recorded in the dermatopathology archive.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Nail clipping cases were obtained from the dermatology outpatient service, as well as from charity inpatient, intensive care unit, and emergency room referrals. Only patients clinically diagnosed with onychomycosis, with a final histopathologic diagnosis of onychomycosis or a diagnosis negative for fungal elements based on PAS staining, were included. Cases were excluded if a final histopathologic diagnosis had not been established, if both the slide and paraffin block were irretrievable, or if the archived slide was unfit for review and the block could not be retrieved for replacement sections.

Sample size calculation

A minimum sample size of 70 slides was estimated using the Buderer formula for studies of diagnostic accuracy (16). Assuming 96% sensitivity, 90% specificity, 50% prevalence, and 10% precision, at least 35 PAS-positive onychomycosis cases and 35 PAS-negative non-onychomycosis cases were required based on the prior report by Idriss et al. (14, 16).

Slide selection, masking, and fluorescence microscopy assessment

Eligible cases were identified through a database search using the terms “onychomycosis” and “tinea unguium.” Accession number, age, sex, nail clipping site, final histopathologic diagnosis, and histopathologic characteristics were extracted into a master spreadsheet. A research assistant with access only to de-identified data randomly selected PAS-positive and PAS-negative cases using computer-generated random numbers and assigned each selected slide a unique study code.

The H&E slides were reviewed using fluorescence microscopy by two dermatopathologists (EDC and CYS), with final consensus verification. Both readers were blind to the PAS results during H&E evaluation. Following the criteria described by Idriss et al., fluorescent findings were considered positive when tubular or tubulo-annular structures with a fluorescent rim compatible with fungal elements were identified (11). The observed findings were recorded as positive or negative, and the morphologic features of positive or potentially misleading structures were documented.

Statistical analysis

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were calculated using PAS as the reference standard. Exact 95% CIs were calculated for the main diagnostic accuracy measures. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are reported as number (%).

Ethics approval

The study protocol was approved by the University of the Philippines Manila Research Ethics Board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because archived, de-identified specimens and records were used.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 84 slides, comprising 42 PAS-positive onychomycosis and 42 PAS-negative non-onychomycosis slides, were included in the study. Baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. Patients in the PAS-positive onychomycosis group had a mean age of 59.48 ± 15.28 years, and those in the PAS-negative non-onychomycosis group had a mean age of 54.64 ± 15.45 years. The majority of patients in the PAS-positive onychomycosis group were men (28 of 42, 66.67%), while the majority of patients in the PAS-negative non-onychomycosis group were women (27 of 42, 64.29%). The most common specified nail clipping site among onychomycosis cases was the toenail of the first digit of the right foot.

Diagnostic performance of fluorescence microscopy

Fluorescence microscopy correctly classified 34 PAS-positive slides and 41 PAS-negative slides. There were 8 false-negative results and 1 false-positive result (Table 2). Sensitivity was 80.95% (34/42; 95% CI, 65.88–91.40%), specificity was 97.62% (41/42; 95% CI, 87.43–99.94%), PPV was 97.14% (34/35; 95% CI, 85.08–99.93%), and NPV was 83.67% (41/49; 95% CI, 70.34–92.68%).

False-negative and false-positive findings

Of the 8 false-negative cases, 2 exhibited yeast onychomycosis and 6 exhibited dermatophyte onychomycosis with a paucity of fungal elements on PAS staining. Conspicuous hyphae and pseudohyphae were easiest to identify by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 1). The single false-positive case contained numerous plasma globules and neutrophils that mimicked fluorescent fungal structures. Other potential mimics included parakeratosis, clefts between corneocytes, and ill-defined background fluorescence (Figure 2).

Discussion

In this study, fluorescence microscopy of routine H&E-stained nail clipping sections demonstrated high specificity (97.62%) and moderate sensitivity (80.95%) for the diagnosis of onychomycosis compared with PAS staining. These findings support its potential role as a rapid adjunctive diagnostic method in settings where PAS staining may not be immediately available.

In the 1980s, Graham, Mann, and Margo and Bombardier described “fungal autofluorescence” in H&E-stained tissue sections examined using fluorescence microscopy. Subsequent studies have suggested that this may be due to routine H&E staining or components of the fungal cell wall, although the precise mechanism remains incompletely understood. These studies have suggested that the approach could shorten turnaround time by avoiding special stains (11–13).

The findings should be interpreted within the current diagnostic approach for onychomycosis. Fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail clipping sections is not intended to replace KOH examination, fungal culture, or PAS staining as first-line diagnostic tests. Rather, it is applicable when nail clipping histopathology has already been performed or is clinically indicated. In this setting, fluorescence microscopy may provide rapid preliminary confirmation of fungal elements before PAS staining is completed or when access to additional special stains is limited. Due to its moderate sensitivity, however, negative fluorescence findings do not exclude onychomycosis, and PAS staining remains appropriate when clinical suspicion remains high.

In non-nail specimens, fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained sections appears to have limited sensitivity for many superficial and deep mycoses. In a series of superficial and deep fungal infections, Elston found that strong fluorescence was most consistently observed in superficial dermatophyte infections and that fluorescence intensity was not clearly related to specimen age (15). This observation supports the biological plausibility of applying the technique to nail clippings.

In nail specimens, fluorescence microscopy has been proposed as a rapid screening tool for the diagnosis of onychomycosis (14). In a study involving 48 PAS-positive and 23 PAS-negative H&E-stained nail sections from clinically diagnosed onychomycosis cases, fluorescence microscopy showed 96% sensitivity and 90% specificity for identifying fungi (14). Fungi were identified by the presence of tubular or tubulo-annular structures with a rim of fluorescence (14). A larger sample size for future studies was recommended.

Compared with the study by Idriss et al., this study has shown lower sensitivity and higher specificity (14). Based on the criteria used by Idriss et al., a positive finding was recorded if tubular structures with a rim of fluorescence, with shapes reminiscent of fungal elements, were identified on fluorescence microscopy (14). Of the 42 cases of onychomycosis in this study, 34 were correctly identified. Representative positive findings are shown in Figure 1. Cases with conspicuous hyphal elements were easiest to identify. In contrast, 2 cases of yeast onychomycosis with no pseudohyphae and 6 cases of dermatophyte onychomycosis with a paucity of fungal elements, such as cases with only 1 to 2 identifiable fungal hyphae on PAS staining, could not be diagnosed with onychomycosis using fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail clippings. A paucity of fungal elements was also identified by Idriss et al. as a factor associated with difficulty identifying fungi on fluorescence microscopy (14).

Mimics of fungi on fluorescence microscopy, defined as non-fungal structures that may show a possible rim of fluorescence, were similar to those noted by Idriss et al. (14). These included parakeratosis, clefts between corneocytes, prominent background staining, neutrophils, and plasma globules (Figure 2). These findings were distinguished from fungi in all cases except one because of their differing pattern and shape compared with fungal elements. The nuclei of parakeratotic corneocytes appeared as round structures with a rim of fluorescence in a regular distribution within corneocytes. Clefts between corneocytes showed a more organized pattern that was parallel to one another. Ill-defined background fluorescence of corneocytes was also observed. Neutrophils and small plasma globules appeared as more irregularly shaped elongated structures with less-defined rims of fluorescence.

Unlike fungal culture, which may recover colonizing organisms or contaminants, nail clipping histopathology shows fungal elements within the nail plate, supporting true tissue involvement. However, neither PAS staining nor fluorescence microscopy determines fungal viability or identifies the causative species. Culture or molecular testing remains necessary when species identification may influence clinical management.

This study has limitations. It was retrospective, performed at a single center, and based on archived specimens, with a modest sample size. The study also used PAS staining rather than culture or molecular testing as the reference standard; however, PAS staining is widely regarded as a sensitive histopathologic method for onychomycosis diagnosis (4, 5). Furthermore, because this study used archived histopathologic sections, direct calcofluor white microscopy could not be performed. Future prospective studies should compare H&E fluorescence microscopy with direct KOH microscopy, calcofluor white fluorescence microscopy, PAS staining, fungal culture, and molecular assays using paired specimens while also assessing cost, turnaround time, and interobserver agreement. Despite these limitations, the findings support fluorescence microscopy of routine H&E-stained nail sections as a rapid, practical, lower-cost adjunct in tertiary centers where a fluorescence microscope is already available. A positive fluorescence result may help confirm the diagnosis of onychomycosis quickly, but a negative result does not exclude disease, and PAS staining remains warranted when clinical suspicion is high.

Conclusion

Fluorescence microscopy of H&E-stained nail sections showed high specificity for the diagnosis of onychomycosis. Although it should not replace PAS staining, KOH examination, or fungal culture, it may provide a rapid and cost-effective adjunct for confirming fungal infection in nail clipping specimens when histopathologic evaluation has already been performed, particularly in resource-limited settings where fluorescence microscopy is available.
