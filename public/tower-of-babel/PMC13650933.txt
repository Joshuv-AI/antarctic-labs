Efficient and stable carbon capture from industrial flue gas under humid conditions using amine-functionalized Zr-MOF-808 through statistical process design

Neha Mohiuddin, Akshit Kumar, Rajesh Yadav, Satyapaul A Singh, I Sreedhar

Abstract

Amine functionalization is a well-known approach to enhance CO2 adsorption capacity in metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), especially in humid environments. In this study, triethylenetetramine (TETA) was used to functionalize Zr-MOF-808 via wet impregnation, and its CO2 adsorption studies were performed using wet flue gas. To conduct the adsorption tests, a simulated industrial flue gas mixture was used, containing 12 vol% CO2, 10 vol% H2O, 8 vol% O2, with the remainder being N2. To systematically investigate the effects of various parameters on CO2 adsorption and identify optimal operating conditions, response surface methodology (RSM) combined with a central composite design (CCD) was employed. Under optimal conditions 55 °C temperature, 20 wt% TETA loading, 3 g of adsorbent, and 180 minutes of carbonation time, the model estimated an adsorption capacity of 3.6 mmol g−1, which is very close to the maximum experimental adsorption capacity of 3.4 mmol g−1. The RSM-CCD analysis indicated that both carbonation time and temperature significantly influence the results, while amine loading has a notable quadratic effect. The Avrami model provided the best fit for the kinetic behaviour, whereas the Sips isotherm most accurately described the isotherm behaviour. Moreover, Zr-MOF-808-TETA (20%) retained 88% of its original capacity after 10 consecutive cycles. Therefore, the results indicate that TETA-functionalized Zr-MOF-808 is a potential candidate for CO2 capture under humid simulated flue gas conditions.

Introduction

Escalating levels of atmospheric CO2, driven primarily by industrial activities and fossil fuel combustion, have heightened global concerns about climate change and environmental damage. In response to these challenges, carbon capture and utilization (CCUS) has surfaced as an essential mitigation technology. As global CO2 emissions continue to rise, interest in carbon capture technologies has grown.

The primary aim of Carbon Capture (CC) is to remove CO2 from emissions using methods such as pre-combustion, post-combustion, oxy-fuel combustion, and direct air capture (DAC). Post-combustion has emerged as an effective technology for CO2 capture, employing methods such as membrane separation, absorption, chemical looping combustion, cryogenic distillation and adsorption. Adsorption is employed in this study for its effectiveness, durability, and environmental friendliness.

Various adsorbents employed in carbon capture include zeolites, carbon-based materials (activated carbon, biochar, graphene) zeolitic imidazole framework, covalent organic framework, metal–organic frameworks (MOF) and organic polymers. MOFs are frequently utilised in CC because of their customizable features, large surface area, exposed metal sites and pore volume. Recent studies continue to showcase the versatility of MOFs, which are widely used in adsorption, gas separation, water remediation and various environmental applications owing to their structural stability and tunability. In particular, recent developments in water-stable and hydrophobically modified MOFs have highlighted their potential for CO2 capture under humid and wet flue-gas conditions. MOF-based materials have also been extensively investigated through different modification strategies to improve their adsorption performance and stability. There are different synthesis methods employed in MOFs preparation, which include hydro/solvothermal, microwave-assisted, ultrasonication, and electrochemical. Past studies have found a broad spectrum of MOFs and their modifications. Libo Li and group have conducted comparative studies between M/DOBDC (M = Mg, Ni, Zn, Co, Mn) and other adsorbents, among which Mg/DOBDC has shown a good capture capacity of 248 cm3 g−1. The Mg/DOBDC MOF@GO that Wang and group synthesized exhibited an 8.30 mmol g−1 CO2 uptake, nearly twice that of its parent MOF.

The key challenges in low-concentration CO2 capture in the presence of humidity are the structural decomposition of the MOF, reduced cyclic stability, lower affinity towards CO2, and MOF regeneration after multiple cycles. To overcome these difficulties, the emphasis has shifted to amine-modified MOFs, which exhibit high adsorption capacity under moisture-laden conditions. Amines, being basic, participate in acid–base interactions with CO2, thereby enhancing their affinity towards CO2. Amine modification involves using various amine precursors such as TEPA, PEI, and TETA. M. Anbia has modified MIL-101 with pentaethylenehexamine (PEHA), which showed 50% more CO2 uptake than the parent MOF. D. Zhang and group modified ZIF-8 using deep eutectic solvents, resulting in 13.71 times greater CO2 capture than parent ZIF-8. Liu and group synthesized 5A@MSA-30, a mesoporous silica-supported amine, which showed a high CO2 uptake of 5.05 mmol g−1 at 25 °C from humid flue gas. Z. Yonjia and his team performed a comparative analysis of bimetallic UiO-66 and UiO-66-NH2, using (Zr and Fe) as the metal centres, with adsorption capacities of 2.2 mmol g−1 and 3.5 mmol g−1, respectively.

The novelty of this work lies in the deployment of the TETA-modified Zr-MOF to achieve successful carbon capture from humid flue gas through hierarchical and statistical process design. It examines the simultaneous effects of TETA loading and key parameters on CO2 adsorption in a mixture of 12 vol% CO2, 10 vol% H2O, 8 vol% O2, and N2, which reflects real flue gas conditions. RSM-CCD evaluates the linear, interaction, and quadratic effects of the parameters, as well as their overall contribution to adsorption. The study also investigates adsorption kinetics, equilibrium isotherms, and cyclic stability to characterize adsorption behaviour and regeneration efficiency of TETA-modified Zr-MOF-808 under humid conditions.

Experimental section

Materials and software employed

Zirconium oxychloride octahydrate (ZrOCl2·8H2O), trimesic acid (H3BTC), dimethyl formamide (DMF), formic acid, methanol, and triethylenetetramine (TETA) were all obtained from SRL chemicals at 99% purity and were used without further purification. Origin 2026 student version is used for graphical data visualization, while Design-Expert 11 is employed for process optimization.

Synthesis procedure

Initially, 0.3 g of trimesic acid and 0.7 g of ZrOCl2·8H2O were dissolved in 33.7 ml each of DMF and formic acid, then sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 5 minutes. The solution was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 130 °C for 48 hours. The resulting product was filtered, washed, and dried at 110 °C for 12 hours. The synthesis process is shown in . To prepare amine-modified Zr-MOF-808, 4 g of pristine Zr-MOF-808 was dispersed in 25 ml of methanol, and for a 10 wt% TETA loading, 0.44 g of TETA was dissolved in 25 ml of methanol. Both solutions were stirred at room temperature for 12 hours. The resulting solid was washed once with methanol and twice with DI water, filtered, and dried at 80 °C in a vacuum oven for 6–8 hours. The same method was used for 20 wt% TETA loading, in which 1 g of TETA was dissolved in 50 ml of methanol and 4 g of Zr-MOF-808 in 25 ml of methanol. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 hours, then washed with methanol once and DI water twice, filtered, and dried at 80 °C in a vacuum oven for 6–8 hours. The TETA loading was calculated by using . TETA loadings of 10 and 20 wt% refer to the nominal mass fraction of TETA in the total TETA-Zr-MOF-808 mixture. The 10 and 20 wt% values represent the nominal TETA loadings, which may differ from the actual amount of TETA retained after impregnation and washing due to potential TETA loss during washing and drying.

Physicochemical characterization studies

Structural and morphology studies

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was conducted using the Rigaku ULTIMA-IV model with a Cu X-ray source (λ = 0.154 nm) to analyze phase formation and crystallinity over a 2θ range of 5–40°. The various functional groups were analyzed using FTIR with a Jasco P-200 spectrometer, which operated between 400–4000 cm−1 wavenumbers, providing high-resolution and accurate wavenumber measurements.

Experimental setup

A lab-scale fixed-bed reactor configuration was used to perform CO2 adsorption studies under dry and humid environments. The experimental apparatus has a reactor bed with a length of 0.52–0.58 m and an inner diameter of 0.02–0.025 m. The reactor, packed with the adsorbent, was housed in an electric furnace with temperature monitored using a thermocouple. Inlet gas stream velocities were regulated via mass flow controllers (MFC). The gas flows through the reactor bed, then through the condenser and moisture trap, before reaching the gas detector, which monitors the CO2 levels in the effluent. Gas mixture consists of 12 vol% CO2, 10 vol% H2O, 8 vol% O2 and balance N2. The RH% at optimum conditions, i.e., 55 °C, is 64.3%, and the water partial pressure is 10.13 kPa. The flow rate of the gas mixture is 100 ml min−1. The reactor configuration is shown in .

FTIR analysis was performed to identify the functional groups present in the adsorbent, with the spectra shown in . The spectra show characteristic vibrational bands confirming the framework structure and successful incorporation of TETA into amine-modified Zr-MOF-808. The pristine Zr-MOF-808 displays notable peaks at 500 cm−1 and 650 cm−1, corresponding to Zr–O–Zr and Zr–OH vibrations. The intensity of this broad band increased with TETA loading, particularly for the 20% TETA sample, suggesting greater incorporation of amine species.

Morphology analysis

FE-SEM analysis examined the surface morphology of pristine Zr-MOF-808 and TETA-functionalized Zr-MOF-808, shown in . All images have a scale bar of 500 nm. The pristine Zr-MOF-808 features densely packed, nearly spherical particles with a rough surface texture and evident particle agglomeration. After TETA functionalization, the overall particle shape remained similar, but particles showed increased agglomeration and smoother, more compact surfaces, suggesting TETA deposition on the external surface of the MOF. At 10 wt% TETA loading, the sample resembled pristine Zr-MOF-808, indicating that moderate amine loading had little impact on particle shape. In contrast, at 20 wt% TETA loading, particles exhibited dense packing, increased surface coverage, and agglomeration, indicating greater TETA deposition while maintaining the general particle morphology. The Zr-MOF-808-TETA (10%) and (20%) samples had mean sizes of 498.15 ± 74.58 nm and 516.62 ± 79.53 nm, respectively. The increase in the observed mean particle size after TETA modification suggests that TETA may influence particle agglomeration and the apparent particle size. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) were conducted on both pristine and TETA-functionalized Zr-MOF-808 to assess their thermal stability and decomposition patterns. The samples were heated from 30 °C to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 in presence of N2 gas, and the resulting thermograms are shown in . Pristine Zr-MOF-808 showed an initial weight loss up to approximately 100 °C, retaining 83.8% of its original mass, due to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and residual solvent molecules from the porous structure. A gradual mass decrease between 100 and 350 °C indicates partial decomposition of the coordinated organic linkers, yielding a residual mass of 67.15%. The most significant weight loss occurs above 350 °C, when the MOF structure degrades to form ZrO2, leaving a residual mass of 43.8% at 620 °C. The thermal decomposition behaviour is in good agreement with previously reported studies on Zr-MOF-808, while the relatively high residual mass reflects the excellent thermal stability of this zirconium-containing MOF structure.

In the amine-modified samples, additional mass loss was noted because of the decomposition of impregnated TETA (triethylenetetramine) molecules. Zr-MOF-808-TETA (10%) maintained 88.6% of its original weight up to 110 °C, which indicates the evaporation of physically adsorbed moisture and volatile substances. As the temperature increased further, the remaining mass decreased to 61.5% at 436 °C and to 57.2% at 630 °C. Zr-MOF-808-TETA (20%) retained 84.1% of its original weight up to 100 °C, followed by a gradual decrease to 68.86% at 355 °C, and 50.85% at 630 °C. The greater weight loss in amine-functionalized samples confirms TETA's incorporation into the porous framework, and the decreasing residual mass with higher amine loading suggests more thermally decomposable organic amine groups are present within the adsorbent.

The DTA data also supported the TGA results by showing endothermic and decomposition attributed to solvent evaporation, amine degradation, and framework degradation. Despite these changes, both modified samples remained thermally stable up to about 300–350 °C, highlighting their potential for post-combustion CO2 capture at moderate temperatures. TGA determined TETA incorporation in Zr-MOF-808 by comparing modified and unmodified MOF mass loss. TETA loadings were 0.1986 mmol g−1 at 10 wt% and 0.4506 mmol g−1 at 20 wt%. Based on BET surface areas, surface nitrogen densities were 0.00171 and 0.01544 mmol of N m−2, respectively.

Process optimization using RSM

RSM is employed to develop a statistically sound experimental design to investigate how key operating parameters influence CO2 adsorption. A central composite design (CCD), one of the most commonly applied RSM approaches, was adopted to examine the single, combined and quadratic impacts of the selected variables on the response. The CCD contains central, factorial and axial points. Four key process parameters, namely temperature, carbonation time, % amine loading, and adsorbent mass, were chosen as independent variables and examined at three levels (−1, 0, +1). The ranges and levels for these variables are detailed in . The coded (−1, 0, +1) levels represent the factorial and centre levels, respectively, while additional axial/extended experimental points were included beyond the factorial region for selected factors. The overall ranges were 3–10 g adsorbent loading, 60–240 minutes carbonation time, 30–85 °C temperature, and 0–30 wt% TETA loading. This statistical method aids in understanding the experiment and identifies optimal conditions to improve CO2 adsorption. A total of 30 experimental runs were conducted to assess the impact of each parameter on CO2 uptake. The outcomes of these runs are included in the SI data. Table S2. All experiments were conducted under humid conditions.

Influence of operating parameters on CO2 adsorption

The study analysed parameters such as temperature, amine loading, carbonation time, and adsorbent amount. Notably, higher temperatures reduced CO2 adsorption capacity, indicating an inverse relationship. This trend is consistent with the exothermic nature of the adsorption process, in which higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of CO2 molecules and diminish their interactions with the adsorbent surface. Previous studies on MOF-based adsorbents showed a similar trend: higher temperatures led to reduced CO2 uptake due to reduced physisorption and the destabilisation of the adsorbent structure. In contrast, increased amine loading significantly improved adsorption performance by increasing adsorption sites, thereby enhancing adsorbent efficiency towards CO2. This improvement aligns with the known mechanism of chemisorption involving the amine groups of CO2 molecules. However, excessive amine loading may eventually lead to pore blockage and reduced accessibility to active sites, as reported in several earlier investigations on amine-functionalized porous adsorbents. The carbonation time positively influenced CO2 adsorption capacity, though the increase was modest. Longer durations increase contact between gas molecules and the adsorbent surface, thereby enhancing diffusion and adsorption within the porous structure. In this study, the amount of adsorbent loaded also influenced the CO2 adsorption capacity. While a small decrease in CO2 adsorption was observed as the adsorbent mass increased, the overall effect was statistically significant. This could be due to competing phenomena, such as increased availability of adsorption sites on the one hand and reduced gas–solid interaction efficiency due to particle stacking and diffusion limitations on the other. Overall, ANOVA results indicates that the carbonation time and the amount of adsorbent are the primary factors influencing CO2 adsorption performance, whereas the linear effects of temperature and amine loading were not statistically significant (p > 0.05); however, the significant quadratic effect of amine loading (D2) indicates a nonlinear response and suggests the presence of an optimum amine loading within the investigated range under the conditions studied according to the F-value obtained from ANOVA results presented in Table S3. The AC plot depicts that CO2 uptake decreases with increasing temperature but increases with longer carbonation time. Adsorption kinetic studies

Kinetic modelling describes the adsorption behaviour and provides information on the adsorption rate and possible mass-transfer characteristics of the process. In this study, kinetic experiments were performed to evaluate the suitability of different kinetic models for describing CO2 uptake by Zr-MOF-808-TETA under optimal conditions identified by the RSM-CCD model. Generally, the pseudo-first-order (PFO) model describes adsorption based on the availability of adsorption sites, whereas the pseudo-second-order (PSO) model assumes that the adsorption rate depends on the second-order rate expression. The Avrami model provides an empirical description of uptake profiles that may involve different kinetic regimes, whereas the Elovich model describes various adsorption types on heterogeneous sites. The Weber–Morris model is designed to identify the specific stages of mass-transfer resistance during the adsorption process. The Bangham model evaluates whether adsorption is controlled by internal pore volume and structural features, assuming a uniform pore system. Boyd's diffusion model assesses the contributions of external-film and intraparticle diffusion to the overall adsorption rate between film and particle diffusion, with the latter as the rate-limiting step, relying on assumptions of spherical geometry and a homogeneous solid state. The respective equations are given in . Kinetic fits alone cannot establish adsorption mechanisms: PFO and PSO do not prove physisorption or chemisorption. Mechanistic interpretation also requires isotherms, physicochemical characterization, and thermodynamic evidence, such as isosteric adsorption heat. Kinetic models mainly describe adsorption behaviour and rates.

where Qt and Qe denote the CO2 uptake (mmol g−1) at time t and at equilibrium, respectively. The constants k1, k2, and n represent PFO, PSO, and the Avrami kinetic exponent. In the Elovich model, α denotes the initial adsorption rate, while β is an empirical parameter related to the change in adsorption rate with surface coverage. For Weber–Morris, k represents the intraparticle diffusion rate constant, while C represents the intercept associated with boundary-layer resistance. In the Bangham model, kB is the adsorption rate constant, and Z is the Bangham exponent, while Bt represents the Boyd time parameter. The qe values reported in represent the equilibrium adsorption capacities obtained as fitted parameters from nonlinear regression of the experimental kinetic data, rather than the experimentally measured CO2 uptake under the optimized operating conditions. displays the nonlinear-fitting profiles alongside residual distribution plots, with the related kinetic parameters summarized in .

The adsorption profile showed an initial uptake phase, followed by a slow approach to equilibrium. Such behaviour is commonly observed for microporous MOF-based adsorbents, where abundant vacant active sites are initially available for adsorption, resulting in fast adsorption kinetics during the early stage of adsorption. As adsorption progresses, the remaining active sites become progressively occupied, leading to a slower adsorption rate at longer contact times.

Among the models evaluated, the Avrami model provided the best overall statistical description of the experimental uptake. It exhibited a high R2 of 0.994 and the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), indicating the best balance between goodness of fit and model complexity. The Avrami model statistically outperformed other kinetic models. This observation shows that considering only the R2 value is not sufficient when selecting a non-linear kinetic model. It also shows how important it is to use residual analysis and statistical values such as AIC together for a thorough evaluation of models.

While the PFO, PSO, and Avrami models achieved high R2 values of 0.988, 0.986, and 0.994, respectively, their predicted qe values differed notably. The measured CO2 uptake was 3.4 mmol g−1, whereas the PSO model estimated higher qe values. This discrepancy arises because qe from nonlinear regression is a model-dependent parameter indicating the asymptotic capacity of the fitted kinetic function, whereas the experimental value represents uptake within the observed timeframe. When the profile does not fully reach the asymptotic region, different kinetic models can fit the data well but yield significantly different qe predictions. Similar inconsistencies between experimental and model-predicted CO2 uptake have been reported in previous CO2 adsorption studies. Consequently, the experimental uptake of 3.4 mmol g−1 was retained as the experimental adsorption capacity, while the kinetic models were used primarily to compare the temporal uptake behaviour and goodness of fit. The superior statistical fit of the Avrami model indicates that its empirical functional form describes the observed uptake profile more adequately than the other kinetic expressions considered. The Avrami exponent (n = 1.314) is an empirical fitting parameter describing the shape of the uptake profile. In humid conditions, water molecules may adsorb via bicarbonate pathways, which could contribute to the observed adsorption behaviour. Comparing the Avrami model with the PFO, PSO, Elovich, Weber–Morris, Bangham and Boyds diffusion models, we find that the residuals of the Avrami model are smaller and more uniformly distributed around zero, supporting its superior statistical fit. These findings align well with earlier research on amine-functionalized porous adsorbents based on MOF materials, in which the Avrami model provided the best fit. Additionally, 60P3Al, a PEI used to modify the adsorbent, also fits best with the Avrami model. Similarly, KA-30, a material derived from coal fly ash, demonstrated that the Avrami model best suited the kinetic analysis.

Mechanism of adsorption

The adsorption of CO2 onto TETA-functionalized Zr-MOF-808 was proposed to occur via a synergistic interaction between the porous zirconium-based framework and the nitrogen-rich amine functionalities introduced by TETA impregnation. Understanding adsorption mechanisms links an adsorbent's structure to its separation performance, which depends on both material properties and adsorbent–adsorbate interactions. XRD analysis showed that the Zr-MOF-808 framework retained its crystallinity after TETA impregnation, indicating that the addition of amine molecules does not compromise its structural integrity. Correspondingly, SEM analysis showed that the overall morphology of Zr-MOF-808 remained relatively unchanged after TETA functionalization; however, some agglomeration and increased roughness were observed as amine loading increased, suggesting deposition of TETA on the external surface, while BET analysis suggests partial occupation of the pore structure. The adsorption behaviour of a porous material is closely associated with its pore structure, surface characteristics, accessible functional groups, and molecular transport pathways. Modification of material surfaces can alter the accessibility of active sites and the interaction environment of adsorbed molecules.

FTIR analysis identified distinct functional groups corresponding to TETA, indicating its successful incorporation. TETA reveals N–H stretching vibrations and enhances the lower-region bands associated with N–H and C–N bending vibrations. Incorporating appropriate functional groups into a porous substrate is an effective strategy for modifying chemical environments and introducing additional interaction sites. Such functionalization can alter the transport pathways and the material's affinity for the target species.

End-of-life repurposing

Over multiple cycles, CO2 uptake by the spent adsorbent diminishes due to pore blockage and structural breakdown. Instead of disposing of the MOF through conventional land-filling, it can be reused for other applications, as landfilling may lead to secondary pollution and ecotoxicological effects if not properly managed. One way to reuse spent sorbent is to create fired clay bricks; adding spent sorbent to clay increases compressive strength from approximately 10.8 MPa to 15.8 MPa when it accounts for about 25% of the total mass. Moreover, even though the spent Zr-MOF adsorbents are no longer capable of capturing CO2, they can still be used as catalysts, since the properties of both the porous structure and the metal nodes of the framework can be leveraged for further chemical conversion of captured CO2 into other final materials, such as methanol. Finally, the spent MOFs can also be converted into carbon-based materials through thermal treatment, thereby enabling entirely different uses of the same raw material. A Zn-based MOF has been used to produce a hierarchically porous, nitrogen-doped carbon nanofiber capable of degrading organic pollutants in water treatment applications, while separately, MOF-derived carbon composites have been reported as effective electrode materials for supercapacitors, leveraging the high porosity and tunable pore architecture retained from the parent MOF structure. All these routes suggest that used amine-modified Zr-MOF adsorbents can be converted from waste into construction-quality materials, catalysts, water-cleaning absorbents, or electrodes in energy storage systems, thereby immobilising or repurposing residual amine and metal species while supporting circular-economy handling of end-of-life CO2 capture materials.

Conclusion

This study shows that modifying materials with amines successfully enhances CO2 adsorption under humid environments. Here, 20 wt% functionalized Zr-MOF-808 exhibited good capture capacity of 3.4 mmol g−1 under optimal conditions, viz., 55 °C, 20 wt% TETA, 3 g, and 180 minutes, as determined using the RSM-CCD method. The Avrami model best described the kinetic data, while the Sips model accurately described the equilibrium data. The adsorbent retained 88% of its original uptake capacity over 10 consecutive runs, demonstrating stable performance over the tested cycling period. These findings suggest that TETA-functionalized Zr-MOF-808 holds potential for humid carbon capture. Future work should include developing composites, applying hydrophobic surface modifications, and performing extended cyclic tests to assess performance in humid flue gas conditions.
