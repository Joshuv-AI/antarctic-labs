Clinical outcomes of arthroscopic suture-bridge repair for intratendinous rotator cuff tears converted to full-thickness tears
Authors: Yukihiro Kajita, Yohei Harada, Ryosuke Takahashi, Ryosuke Sagami, Yusuke Iwahori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) in which intratendinous tears (ITTs) were converted to full-thickness tears and repaired via the suture-bridge technique.MethodsPartial-thickness ITTs that were refractory for more than 6 months to conservative treatment were surgically treated using a radiofrequency device to convert the ITTs into full-thickness tears, which were subsequently repaired via the suture-bridge technique. The evaluation parameters included shoulder range of motion (forward elevation and external rotation), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores, Constant-Murley scores, University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) scores, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores, and retear rates (Sugaya classifications IV and V).ResultsTwenty-one patients (8 males, 13 females; mean age: 55.3 ± 14.7 years) out of 25 diagnosed with isolated ITT completed at least 12 months of follow-up after surgery. Significant improvements were observed in the shoulder range of motion (139.5 ± 31.4° preoperatively to 164.5 ± 12.8° postoperatively), external rotation (45.8 ± 15.1° to 54.1 ± 14.7°), and clinical scores including the JOA score (64.4 ± 15.0 to 91.9 ± 7.2), Constant-Murley score (49.1 ± 18.7 to 81.9 ± 16.6), UCLA score (18.1 ± 5.2 to 30.4 ± 6.1), and ASES score (46.9 ± 28.8 to 91.3 ± 9.8). No re-tears were observed (0%).ConclusionsThere is no consensus on the optimal surgical approach for ITT. This study demonstrated the favorable clinical outcomes of ARCR via the suture-bridge technique after conversion of ITT to full-thickness tears.Level of evidenceLevel Ⅳ, retrospective cohort studyKeywords: Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, Intratendinous tears, Partial-thickness rotator cuff tears, Suture-bridge technique

Introduction

Rotator cuff tears are among the most common disorders of the shoulder, often resulting in pain, functional disability, and reduced quality of life. Surgical repair of the rotator cuff has shown excellent results in terms of pain relief and functional improvement in many patients.1,2 Rotator cuff tears are generally classified into full-thickness and partial-thickness tears. Partial-thickness rotator cuff tears (PTRCTs) are further subdivided into bursal-side tears (BSTs), articular-side tears (ASTs), and intratendinous tears (ITTs).3 Recent advances in imaging technology, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have improved the accuracy of diagnosing PTRCTs.4 The optimal management strategy for these lesions is still unclear.5,6 (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4)

Although PTRCTs are commonly encountered in clinical practice, their management remains controversial. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) for PTRCTs has shown favorable results, as it relieves pain and improves shoulder function in many cases.7,8 While conservative management may benefit some patients, surgical intervention is often required if symptoms persist.9 Among PTRCTs, ITTs present unique challenges. Unlike BSTs or ASTs, ITTs do not penetrate the tendon surface, making them more difficult to identify on MRI.10 In our practice, ITTs were suspected preoperatively based on MRI findings showing intrasubstance signal changes without surface disruption, and the diagnosis was confirmed arthroscopically by identifying tendon delamination or intrasubstance degeneration upon probing. In addition, the natural history of these tears is not well understood. As a result, there is no consensus on the optimal management of ITTs, leading to the proposal of a variety of surgical techniques.11,12

ARCR for ITTs generally involves one of three strategies: (1) converting the ITT to a full-thickness tear prior to conventional rotator cuff repair,13 (2) repairing the tear in situ without converting it to a full-thickness defect,14 and (3) converting the ITT to a BST prior to repair.15 The first approach aims to improve healing by providing better access to the tear; however, this approach involves more tendon resection and could compromise the native tendon structure. The second strategy focuses on preserving intact tendon fibers and maintaining the physiological structure of the rotator cuff while providing adequate mechanical stability.14 The third approach aims to improve tendon healing by preserving the integrity of some native tendons while providing an alternative pathway for repair.15

Despite the theoretical advantages and disadvantages associated with the proposed surgical techniques for ITTs, it remains unclear which strategy yields superior clinical and structural outcomes.12 Several studies have evaluated the outcomes of different ARCR techniques; however, data specific to ITTs remain limited.7 Biomechanical studies suggest that in situ repair may preserve the natural tendon structure while providing sufficient mechanical strength, whereas converting the ITT to a full-thickness tear may improve healing at the tendon-bone interface.9 Similarly, the conversion of ITTs to bursal defects may offer a middle ground, facilitating healing while preserving some native tendon integrity.11 Clinical trials comparing these strategies have produced mixed results, and there is no conclusive evidence to support the superiority of any one approach.5

The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes of patients who underwent ARCR after the conversion of ITTs to full-thickness tears. By assessing clinical, MRI, and functional outcomes, we aimed to gain further insight into the effectiveness of this approach and contribute to the development of evidence-based management strategies for this complex condition.

Methods

Patient selection

Patients diagnosed with ITT at our clinic first received conservative treatment, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), steroid injections, and physiotherapy, for at least 6 months. Patients who did not respond to conservative treatment were considered for ARCR.

From January 2016 to December 2022, a total of 25 patients were diagnosed with isolated ITT and followed for at least 12 months postoperatively. After excluding 4 patients who did not attend follow-up visits, a total of 21 patients (follow-up rate: 84%) were included in this study. The cohort included 8 male and 13 female patients. The mean age of the patients was 55.3 ± 14.7 years (Table 1).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) more than half-thickness (>50%) ITT of the entire cuff, as confirmed arthroscopically; (2) failure of conservative treatment; and (3) arthroscopically confirmed ITT. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) PTRCTs other than isolated ITT; (2) associated lesions such as subscapularis tears, acromioclavicular arthritis, Bankart lesions, SLAP lesions, and glenohumeral joint arthritis; (3) patients with less than half-thickness (<50%) ITT of the entire cuff, as confirmed arthroscopically, since ARCR was not performed in such cases; (4) revision surgery; and (5) a history of fractures involving the scapula, humerus, or clavicle. Patients with less than half-thickness (<50%) ITTs were managed conservatively based on our institutional protocol, as these tears typically respond well to non-operative treatment and carry a lower risk of progression.

This study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all patients for participation in the study.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by the senior author with the patient placed in the beach chair position under general anesthesia. The rotator cuff and associated pathology were carefully examined from both the articular and bursal sides. Subacromial decompression was performed in patients with arthroscopic evidence of subacromial impingement. The inflamed bursal tissue was debrided, with minimal decortication of the undersurface and minimal release of the coracoacromial ligament.

ITT was confirmed when tendon delamination or intrasubstance degeneration was evident upon probing and debridement, despite an intact bursal and articular surface. The exact location of the ITT was identified by systematic arthroscopic probing from both the articular and bursal sides. The ITT site was confirmed when tendon delamination or abnormal tendon consistency was detected upon probing, despite intact articular and bursal surfaces. A radiofrequency device was used to create a complete tear, and the greater tuberosity footprint was debrided to expose the bone surface. For patients with preoperative shoulder joint contracture, an arthroscopic pan-capsular release was performed to restore the range of motion. This procedure involved the release of the anterior, posterior, and inferior portions of the glenohumeral joint capsule to alleviate contracture and improve joint mobility.

In cases where more than 25% of the biceps tendon was damaged as confirmed by arthroscopy, biceps tenotomy was performed in patients aged 65 years or older, and biceps tenodesis was performed in patients younger than 65 years.

The rotator cuff was repaired via the suture-bridge technique as the primary approach for all patients. One medial anchor (Healix; DePuy-Mitek, Raynham, MA) was placed near the cartilage edge of the humeral head, and the sutures from this anchor were passed through 4 points of the tendon. Lateral anchors (Quatro Link knotless; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) were placed approximately 5 mm below the outer edge of the greater tuberosity, and 1 to 2 anchors were used to complete the repair.

Postoperative protocol

All patients underwent a standardized rehabilitation program. A sling with an abduction pillow was used to immobilize the shoulder for 4 weeks. To prevent stiffness, range of motion exercises for the elbow, wrist, and fingers were started immediately after surgery. Passive forward elevation exercises were initiated on the first postoperative day. Active-assisted range of motion exercises were introduced at 4 weeks, and active range of motion exercises were introduced at 6 weeks. Muscle strengthening exercises were introduced at eight weeks postoperatively. Light activities were allowed after 3 to 4 months, while return to sports and heavy work was allowed after 6 months.

Evaluation criteria

In this study, patient demographics (age, sex, history of trauma, duration of prior conservative treatment, and laterality of the operated shoulder relative to hand dominance), preoperative and postoperative Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores, Constant-Murley scores, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores, University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) scores, active range of motion (ROM), and re-tear rates were assessed. Traumatic tears were defined as cases where patients had no prior shoulder pain before experiencing acute onset of pain due to trauma.

MRI scans were conducted using a 3-T closed-type scanner (MRT-2000/V2, Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan). The structural integrity of the repairs was assessed using oblique coronal, oblique sagittal, and axial T2-weighted images. MRI evaluations were performed preoperatively and at 6 and 12 months postoperatively. Repair integrity was classified according to the 5-category Sugaya classification system.16 Postoperative re-tears were identified as those falling under Sugaya Type IV or Type V.

All MRI and clinical assessments were performed by a single orthopaedic surgeon specializing in shoulder surgery to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (ver. 18.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) was used for all statistical analyses. The t-test was used for statistical analysis, with the level of statistical significance set to P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 21 patients were included in this study. The cohort consisted of 8 male and 13 female patients, with a mean age of 55.3 ± 14.7 years. Eleven patients (52.4%) had a history of trauma. The mean duration of prior conservative treatment before surgery was 14.9 ± 17.2 months. Surgery was performed on the dominant side in 12 patients (57.1%) and the non-dominant side in 9 patients (42.9%). Arthroscopic pan-capsular release was performed in 6 patients to restore shoulder range of motion. In addition, biceps tenotomy was performed in 6 patients, whereas biceps tenodesis was performed in 1 patient.

The active ROM improved significantly, with forward elevation increasing from 139.5 ± 31.4° preoperatively to 164.5 ± 12.8° postoperatively (P < 0.05), and external rotation improving from 45.8 ± 15.1° to 54.1 ± 14.7° (P < 0.05). The clinical scores showed significant postoperative improvement, with the JOA score increasing from 64.4 ± 15.0 to 91.9 ± 7.2, the Constant-Murley score increasing from 49.1 ± 18.7 to 81.9 ± 16.6, the UCLA score increasing from 18.1 ± 5.2 to 30.4 ± 6.1, and the ASES score increasing from 46.9 ± 28.8 to 91.3 ± 9.8 (P < 0.05 for all comparisons) (Table 2).

MRI evaluations at 12 months postoperatively revealed no re-tears (0%) classified as Sugaya Type IV or V. Fifteen shoulders were classified as Sugaya Type I, five as Type II, and one as Type III. No major postoperative complications, such as infection, stiffness requiring intervention, or hardware-related issues, were reported.

Discussion

In this study, conversion of the ITT to a full-thickness tear was chosen. Favorable results were achieved, with significant improvements in clinical outcomes and no re-tear observed at 12 months postoperatively. The advantage of converting the ITT to a full-thickness tear is that it promotes tendon-bone healing, which may improve the chances of successful repair. However, a potential disadvantage is that this approach may result in the risk of over-tension, which could lead to complications. The choice of approach should remain individualized based on the characteristics of the tear and the experience of the surgeon, and further research is needed to determine the most effective method for ITTs.

ITTs are rare, and many aspects of their pathophysiology remain unclear.17 Unlike BSTs or ASTs, ITTs occur within the tendon substance, making diagnosis challenging. Their natural history is poorly understood, and it is uncertain whether they are inevitably progressive or can remain asymptomatic.18,19

Reports of treatment outcomes for ITT are limited and there is no consensus on the optimal surgical approach.20 While conservative management may be effective in some patients, surgical intervention becomes necessary when symptoms persist despite non-operative management.21 Various repair strategies have been proposed, but there is insufficient evidence to compare their effectiveness. Further research is needed to clarify the best management strategies for ITTs.13,14,22,23

The exact cause of ITTs remains unclear, but several factors have been suggested. Intrinsic tendon degeneration, including collagen disorganization and mucoid changes, may contribute to ITT formation.6 Age-related degeneration and changes in tendon composition have also been associated with these tears.24,25 Biomechanical loading is another potential factor. High tensile and shear forces in the supraspinatus tendon, combined with rotator cuff dysfunction, can lead to localized fiber failure.25,26 In addition, reduced vascularity in the critical zone of the tendon has been associated with ischemic changes that may promote degeneration.27,28 Repetitive microtrauma from overhead activities may further weaken the tendon, with studies reporting a higher prevalence of ITTs in athletes and workers performing repetitive shoulder movements.24,29 Despite these theories, the mechanism of ITTs remains uncertain, highlighting the need for further research.

Several studies have reported favorable clinical outcomes following ARCR for both BST and AST. Previous literature has demonstrated significant improvements in pain relief, functional recovery, and tendon healing rates following ARCR for these lesions, with many patients achieving satisfactory postoperative outcomes.7,8 These studies have shown that surgery can effectively restore shoulder function, particularly in cases where conservative treatment has failed.7,8 Similarly, ARCR for ITTs has shown positive results, with significant improvements in clinical scores and shoulder range of motion.19 In this study, ARCR for ITTs demonstrated significant postoperative improvements in clinical scores and shoulder range of motion. MRI evaluations at 12 months revealed no re-tears (0%) classified as Sugaya Type IV or V, and no major postoperative complications were observed. These results are consistent with those of previous studies on ARCR for ITTs, which reported favorable clinical outcomes. Park et al.26 reported a re-tear rate of 0% in patients with ITTs after ARCR. Other studies, such as those by Burkhart et al.12 and Kim et al.,30 have also demonstrated favorable results in terms of pain relief, functional recovery, and low re-tear rates in ITT repairs. These findings further support the efficacy of ARCR as a treatment option for ITT. The low re-tear rate observed in our study (0%) and the absence of major postoperative complications are consistent with these reports and indicate that ARCR remains a reliable and effective approach for symptomatic ITTs.

Three strategies have been proposed for the ARCR of ITTs: (1) converting the ITT to a full-thickness tear prior to conventional rotator cuff repair, (2) repairing the tear in situ without converting it to a full-thickness defect, and (3) converting the ITT to a BST prior to repair. Previous studies have reported low re-tear rates for all these strategies. For example, a study by Rossi et al.14 found low re-tear rates with both in situ repair and conversion to full-thickness tears. Similarly, the approach of converting to the BST prior to repair has also shown favorable results with a low incidence of re-tears.13,31

This study presents several significant limitations, including a relatively small sample size, lack of control group, single-center design, and limited follow-up period. These factors may limit the generalizability of the results to broader populations and surgical contexts. Although the 12-month follow-up period offers important insights into short-term outcomes and initial healing integrity, extended observation (≥2 years) is required to more thoroughly assess repair longevity and identify potential late complications or re-tears. Another limitation is the lack of direct comparisons between the different surgical approaches for ITTs, as only one strategy (conversion of ITTs to full-thickness tears) was evaluated in this study. Furthermore, the inclusion of concurrent procedures in 13 of 21 patients (62%) is a noteworthy limitation of this study. Six patients underwent pan-capsular release and seven underwent biceps procedures. While clinically necessary, these represent potential confounding factors that may have contributed to clinical improvement, making it difficult to isolate the specific effect of ITT repair alone. Although preoperative subacromial diagnostic injections provided temporary relief in all patients, suggesting rotator cuff pathology as the primary pain source, the contribution of concurrent procedures to outcomes cannot be entirely excluded.

Future studies should consider a larger cohort and longer follow-up, as well as a comparative analysis of various surgical strategies to determine the most effective approach for ITTs.

Conclusions

ARCR after converting ITTs into full-thickness tears proved effective for patients with symptoms refractory to conservative treatment. Further studies are needed to establish optimal treatment strategies for ITTs.
