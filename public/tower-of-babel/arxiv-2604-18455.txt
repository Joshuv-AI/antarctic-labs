Topological rigidity of complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds
Ioannis Gkeneralis

Abstract
We investigate the equivariant topological rigidity of complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds. By reducing the classification to the equivariant rigidity of their quasitoric (or quoric) quotients and the classification of the associated principal bundles, we establish new rigidity results within the category of locally linear actions. We prove that complex moment-angle manifolds are equivariantly rigid: any locally linear manifold equivariantly homotopy equivalent to a complex moment–angle manifold is equivariantly homeomorphic to it. In the quaternionic setting, we establish full equivariant rigidity for manifolds with four-dimensional quoric quotients and provide a primary rigidity statement for higher dimensions based on degree-4 characteristic classes. These results characterize moment–angle manifolds as equivariant strong Borel manifolds, demonstrating that their equivariant homotopy type completely determines their equivariant homeomorphism type.



Keywords:
moment–angle manifold, quasitoric manifold, quoric manifold, equivariant rigidity, Borel manifold, principal torus bundle

Introduction

Rigidity phenomena play a central role in geometric topology. The general question is to what extent the homotopy type of a manifold determines its homeomorphism or diffeomorphism type. Classical examples include the Borel Conjecture for aspherical manifolds and rigidity results related to the Farrell–Jones Conjecture
[
22
]
. When manifolds carry additional geometric or combinatorial structures, it is natural to ask analogous rigidity questions in equivariant or stratified settings.

Toric topology provides a particularly rich framework for studying such questions. In their foundational work, Davis and Januszkiewicz
[
11
]
introduced quasitoric manifolds as topological counterparts of toric varieties. A quasitoric manifold is a smooth
2n
–manifold equipped with a locally standard action (coordinatewise multiplication) of the torus
T^{n}
whose orbit space is a simple polytope. The topology of these manifolds is closely tied to the combinatorics of the polytope together with an associated characteristic function. Rigidity questions for quasitoric manifolds have been studied extensively (see
[
10
]
or
[
30
]
and references therin); in particular, Metaftsis and Prassidis
[
24
]
proved that quasitoric manifolds satisfy equivariant topological rigidity within the class of locally linear torus manifolds.

Closely related to quasitoric manifolds are the moment–angle manifolds introduced by Davis and Januszkiewicz
[
11
]
and further developed by Buchstaber and Panov
[
6
]
. Given a simple polytope
P
with
m
facets, the associated moment–angle manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
carries a natural action of the torus
T^{m}
. These manifolds admit several equivalent descriptions: they can be realized as intersections of real quadrics, and more conceptually as polyhedral products
(D^{2},S^{1})^{K}
determined by the combinatorics of the nerve complex
K
of
P
(see
[
2
,
6
]
). A fundamental feature of moment–angle manifolds is the existence of a free action of a subtorus

K\cong(S^{1})^{m-n},

whose quotient is a quasitoric manifold over
P
. Thus moment–angle manifolds arise as total spaces of principal torus bundles over quasitoric manifolds and play a central role in the interaction between torus actions
[
6
]
, polyhedral geometry
[
2
]
, and homotopy theory
[
15
]
.

More recently, quaternionic analogues of these constructions have been studied. Replacing the complex torus
(S^{1})^{n}
with the quaternionic torus
(S^{3})^{n}
, Hopkinson
[
19
]
introduced quaternionic moment–angle manifolds together with their quotients, called
quoric manifolds
, which serve as quaternionic analogues of quasitoric manifolds. These manifolds admit locally regular actions (conjugation) of
(S^{3})^{n}
whose orbit spaces are simple polytopes. Their geometry reflects the representation theory of
S^{3}
and parallels many features of the classical complex toric setting. Rigidity questions in this quaternionic context have also been investigated: Prassidis and the author
[
16
]
proved that quoric manifolds satisfy equivariant topological rigidity.

The purpose of this paper is to study rigidity properties of moment–angle manifolds from this perspective. More precisely, we ask whether a manifold that is equivariantly homotopy equivalent to a moment–angle manifold must be equivariantly homeomorphic to it. We have two situations in mind; the classical complex moment–angle manifolds and their quaternionic analogues.

Our main results establish equivariant topological rigidity for moment–angle manifolds in both the complex and quaternionic settings. In particular, for the complex case we have the following result.

Theorem

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
be a complex moment–angle manifold and let
Z
be a locally linear
T^{m}
–manifold equipped with a
T^{m}
–equivariant homotopy equivalence

f:Z\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}.

Then
f
is
T^{m}
–homotopic to a
T^{m}
–equivariant homeomorphism.

Thus complex moment–angle manifolds are equivariantly rigid within the class of locally linear
T^{m}
–manifolds.

In the quaternionic setting the situation is subtler. Let
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathbb{H}}_{P}
denote the quaternionic moment–angle manifold associated to a simple polytope
P
. In dimension 4 we have:

Theorem

(Theorem
5.3
)
.

If the quoric quotient has dimension
4
, then quaternionic moment–angle manifolds satisfy equivariant topological rigidity.

In higher dimensions we obtain a weaker rigidity statement based on primary characteristic classes.

Theorem

(Theorem
5.5
)
.

Equivariant homotopy equivalences between quaternionic moment–angle manifolds preserve the degree–
4
characteristic classes of the kernel bundle. Consequently, the corresponding principal
(S^{3})^{r}
–bundles agree at the level of their primary invariants.

The difference between the complex and quaternionic cases reflects a fundamental distinction in the topology of their classifying spaces. Principal
(S^{1})^{r}
–bundles are classified by degree–
2
cohomology classes, since
BS^{1}\simeq K(\mathbb{Z},2)
. Consequently the kernel bundles appearing in the complex case are completely determined by their first Chern classes.

By contrast,
BS^{3}
is not an Eilenberg–MacLane space. While principal
S^{3}
–bundles over
4
–dimensional bases are classified by their degree–
4
characteristic classes, in higher dimensions additional homotopy–theoretic invariants may appear. This phenomenon is closely related to the classical study of quaternionic line bundles and self–maps of quaternionic projective spaces, where higher obstructions arise beyond the primary characteristic class. For this reason the rigidity results in the quaternionic setting are complete only in low dimensions, while in higher dimensions we obtain rigidity at the level of primary invariants.

Our approach separates the rigidity problem into three conceptual layers. First, the quotients of moment–angle manifolds by the free torus or quaternionic torus actions are quasitoric or quoric manifolds, respectively. An equivariant homotopy equivalence between moment–angle manifolds therefore descends to an equivariant homotopy equivalence between these quotient manifolds. The rigidity theorems for quasitoric
[
24
]
and quoric manifolds
[
16
]
then imply that the quotient manifolds are equivariantly homeomorphic.

The second layer concerns the bundle structures of moment–angle manifolds. Both complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds arise as total spaces of principal bundles over their quasitoric or quoric quotients. The classification of these bundles is governed by characteristic classes in cohomology. In the complex case the relevant invariants are the first Chern classes of the circle factors of the kernel torus, which can be described geometrically as the Poincaré duals of the characteristic submanifolds associated to the facets of the polytope. In the quaternionic case the relevant invariants arise from quaternionic line bundles and are detected by degree–four characteristic classes corresponding to codimension–four isotropy strata.

Finally, combining the rigidity of the base spaces with the invariance of these characteristic classes allows the rigidity to be lifted from the quotient manifolds to the total spaces.

As a consequence we obtain equivariant topological rigidity for both complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds.

These results show that moment–angle manifolds exhibit strong rigidity properties analogous to those known for quasitoric and quoric manifolds, and they highlight the close relationship between the topology of these manifolds and the combinatorics of the underlying polytope together with the associated characteristic data.

Organization of the paper.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we recall basic notions concerning the construction of complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds.
Section
3
explains how rigidity questions for these manifolds reduce to known rigidity results for quasitoric and quoric manifolds.
In Section
4
we study the characteristic classes of the principal torus and quaternionic torus bundles appearing in this setting. Finally, Section
5

Moment–angle manifolds

Moment–angle manifolds arise naturally in toric topology as spaces associated with simple polytopes or simplicial complexes. They were introduced by Davis and Januszkiewicz
[
11
]
and later studied extensively in
[
6
]
as a disc-circle decomposition of the Davis-Januszkiewicz universal space. In this section we briefly recall the construction of complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds and fix the notation used throughout the paper.

Recall that moment–angle manifolds admit a natural description in terms of polyhedral products (cf.
[
2
]
). Let
K
be a simplicial complex on the vertex set
[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}
and let
(X,A)
be a pair of topological spaces. The
polyhedral product
associated to
(X,A)
and
K
is the subspace

(X,A)^{K}\subset X^{m}

defined by

(X,A)^{K}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in K}(X,A)^{\sigma},\qquad(X,A)^{\sigma}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in X^{m}\mid x_{i}\in A\text{ for }i\notin\sigma\}.

When
K
is the nerve complex of a simple polytope
P
, this construction yields moment–angle manifolds.

•

The
complex moment–angle manifold
(Section
2.2.2
) is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{P}=(D^{2},S^{1})^{K}.

•

The
quaternionic moment–angle manifold
(Section
2.3.2
) is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}=(D^{4},S^{3})^{K}.

2.1
Manifolds with corners and simple polytopes

We begin by recalling the notion of manifolds with corners (see
[
9
]
), which play an important role in toric topology as orbit spaces of tori actions. We briefly recall the basic definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

A space
X^{n}
is called an
n
–manifold with corners
if it is a Hausdorff, second countable space equipped with an atlas of open sets homeomorphic to open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
such that the transition maps preserve the natural stratification of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.

Let
X^{n}
be an
n
–manifold with corners. For each point
x\in X^{n}
, define
c(x)
as the number of zero coordinates in any chart. The number
c(x)
is independent of the chart.

For
0\leq k\leq n
, a connected component of
c^{-1}(k)
is called a
preface
of codimension
k
. The closure of a preface is called a
face
. The codimension–1 faces are called
facets
, the codimension–
n
faces are
vertices
, and the codimension–
(n-1)
faces are
edges
.

Definition 2.2
.

A manifold with corners
X^{n}
is called
nice
if

1.

for every
0\leq k\leq n
there exists a codimension–
k
face;

2.

for each codimension–
k
face
F
there are exactly
k
facets
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
such that
F=F_{1}\cap\cdots\cap F_{k}
and
F
does not intersect any other facet.

A fundamental example of a manifold with corners that will play a central role in our discussion is a simple polytope.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
P^{n}
be a polytope with facets

\mathcal{F}(P)=\{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}\}.

The polytope
P^{n}
is called
simple
if exactly
n
facets meet at each vertex.

Equivalently, a simple polytope admits the structure of a compact
n
–dimensional manifold with corners.

Typical examples include the
n
–simplex
\Delta^{n}
and the
n
–cube
I^{n}
. In contrast, the cone over the square
I^{2}
(a
3
–dimensional polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) is not simple.

Example 2.4

(Simple polytopes as manifolds with corners)
.

Let
P^{n}
be a simple
n
–dimensional polytope. For each vertex
v\in P^{n}
we denote by
U_{v}
the open subset of
P^{n}
obtained by removing all faces of
P^{n}
that do not contain
v
. The set
U_{v}
is affinely isomorphic to a neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
.

Therefore
P^{n}
is naturally a compact
n
–dimensional manifold with corners, and the collection
\{U_{v}\}
forms an atlas of charts for this manifold with corners structure.

Simple polytopes admit an additional combinatorial description that is useful in studying moment–angle manifolds. Specifically,
P^{n}
admits a natural structure of a cubical complex embedded in the boundary of the
m
–cube
I^{m}
.

Theorem 2.5

(cf
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
P^{n}
be a simple polytope with facets
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
. There exists a canonical embedding

i_{P}:P^{n}\hookrightarrow I^{m}

determined by the facet inequalities of
P^{n}
.

2.2
The complex case

2.2.1
Quasitoric manifolds

Let
S^{1}
denote the standard subgroup of
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
, the multiplicative group of non–zero complex numbers. We consider the
n
-torus
T^{n}=(S^{1})^{n}
as a subgroup of
(\mathbb{C}^{\ast})^{n}
. In the consequent definitions we follow
[
11
]
, taking into account some adjustments from
[
5
]
.

The standard representation of
T^{n}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is given by diagonal matrices in
U(n)
, which defines the
standard action
(coordinatewise multiplication)

(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\cdot(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=(t_{1}z_{1},\dots,t_{n}z_{n}).

The orbit space of this action is naturally identified with the positive cone

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):x_{i}\geq 0\}.

We call a
2n
-dimensional manifold
M^{2n}
with an action of
T^{n}
a
T^{n}
-manifold
. In this paper we consider only closed manifolds of this type.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
M^{2n}
be a
T^{n}
-manifold. A
standard chart
on
M^{2n}
is a triple
(U,f,\psi)
where

(1)

U\subset M^{2n}
is a
T^{n}
–invariant open subset,

(2)

\psi:T^{n}\to T^{n}
is an automorphism of the torus,

(3)

f:U\to W
is a
\psi
–equivariant homeomorphism onto a
T^{n}
–invariant open subset
W\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, such that
f(t\cdot y)=\psi(t)\cdot f(y)
for all
t\in T^{n},\;y\in U
, where
T^{n}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by the standard coordinatewise action.

Definition 2.7
.

An action of
T^{n}
on a
2n
–dimensional manifold
M^{2n}
is called
locally standard
if every point of
M^{2n}
belongs to a standard chart. Equivalently,
M^{2n}
admits an atlas consisting of standard charts.

The orbit space of a locally standard
T^{n}
–action is locally modelled on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. Consequently, the orbit space
M/T^{n}
naturally inherits the structure of a manifold with corners (see Section
2.1
). Manifolds with corners therefore arise naturally in toric topology as orbit spaces of locally standard torus actions. In many important cases, and in particular for quasitoric manifolds, this orbit space is a simple polytope. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 2.8
.

A
quasitoric manifold
is a
2n
–dimensional manifold
M
equipped with a locally standard
T^{n}
–action whose orbit space is a simple polytope
P
.

Here, the orbit map

\pi:M\longrightarrow P

maps each
k
–dimensional orbit to a point in the interior of a codimension–
k
face of
P
, for
k=0,\dots,n
.

Example 2.9
.

All complex projective spaces
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
and their equivariant connected sums and products are quasitoric manifolds.

Following
[
5
]
, we now define the characteristic function. A codimension–
1
connected component of the fixed point set of a circle subgroup of
T^{n}
is called a
characteristic submanifold
. The images of the characteristic submanifolds under the orbit map correspond to the facets of the quotient manifold with corners.

Let
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
be the facets of
P^{n}
and

M_{i}^{2(n-1)}=\pi^{-1}(F_{i})

the corresponding characteristic submanifolds. Each
M_{i}
is fixed by a circle subgroup
T_{F_{i}}\subset T^{n}
. This defines a map

\lambda:\{F_{1},\dots,F_{k}\}\longrightarrow\{T^{\prime}\subset T^{n}:T^{\prime}\cong S^{1}\},\qquad\lambda(F_{i})=T_{F_{i}},

called the
characteristic function
. Each subgroup
T_{F_{i}}
has the form

T_{F_{i}}=\{(e^{2\pi i\lambda_{1i}\phi},\dots,e^{2\pi i\lambda_{ni}\phi})\in T^{n}:\phi\in\mathbb{R}\}

for some primitive vector
(\lambda_{1i},\dots,\lambda_{ni})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

These data satisfy the following property.

Property (*).

If

F_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap F_{i_{m}}\neq\varnothing,

then the induced map

T_{F_{i_{1}}}\times\cdots\times T_{F_{i_{m}}}\longrightarrow T^{n}

is injective.

The canonical model.

Let
P^{n}
be a simple polytope and suppose we are given a map

\lambda:\{\text{faces of }P^{n}\}\longrightarrow\{\text{isotropy subgroups of }T^{n}\}

assigning to each face the isotropy subgroup of the corresponding orbit type, that satisfies Property (*). The pair
(P^{n},\lambda)
is called a
characteristic pair
.

For a point
x\in P^{n}
, let
F(x)
be the smallest face whose relative interior contains
x
. Define an equivalence relation on
T^{n}\times P^{n}
by

(t,x)\sim(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=x^{\prime}\text{ and }t^{-1}t^{\prime}\in T_{F(x)}.

Definition 2.10
.

The quotient

\mathcal{M}(\lambda)=T^{n}\times P^{n}/\sim

is called the
canonical model
associated to the characteristic pair
(P^{n},\lambda)
.

The torus
T^{n}
acts on
\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
by left multiplication on the first coordinate, and the orbit space is
P^{n}
. Hence the space
\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
is a quasitoric manifold.

Definition 2.11
.

For an automorphism
\psi
of
T^{n}
, two quasitoric manifolds
M^{2n}_{1}
and
M^{2n}_{2}
are
\psi
–equivariantly homeomorphic
if there exists a homeomorphism

h\colon M^{2n}_{1}\to M^{2n}_{2}

such that

h(tx)=\psi(t)h(x)

for all
t\in T^{n}
and all
x\in M^{2n}_{1}
.

This leads to the following important result that characterizes when a quasitoric manifold
M^{2n}
is
T^{n}
–homeomorphic to its canonical model
\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
.

Proposition 2.12

(cf.
[
11
, Proposition 1.8]
)
.

Any quasitoric manifold
M^{2n}
over
P^{n}
is
\psi
–equivariantly homeomorphic to its associated canonical model.

2.2.2
Complex moment–angle manifolds

Let
P^{n}
be a simple
n
–dimensional polytope with facets

\mathcal{F}(P)=\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m}\}.

Recall that a convex polytope is called
simple
if exactly
n
facets meet at each vertex. Let
T^{m}=(S^{1})^{m}
denote the
m
–dimensional torus acting on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
by coordinatewise multiplication. Consider the map

\mu_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathbb{C}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m},\qquad(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})\longmapsto(|z_{1}|^{2},\dots,|z_{m}|^{2}).

This map is invariant under the
T^{m}
–action.

Let
i_{P}:P^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be the canonical embedding determined by the facet inequalities of the polytope as in Theorem
2.5
.

We give three equivalent constructions/descriptions of moment–angle manifolds associated to a simple polytope, following
[
5
,
6
]
and references therein.

Pull back model.

The
moment–angle manifold
associated to
P^{n}
is defined as the pullback

\mathcal{Z}_{P}=\{(z,x)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\times P^{n}\mid\mu_{\mathbb{C}}(z)=i_{P}(x)\}.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
fits into the pullback diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{Z}_{P}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{\mathbb{C}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{P}}

Notice that the space
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
is a smooth closed manifold of dimension
m+n
and carries a natural
T^{m}
–action induced from the action on the first factor.

Quotient construction.

An equivalent description of
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
can be given using isotropy subgroups. For each point
x\in P^{n}
let
F_{x}
denote the unique face whose relative interior contains
x
. If

F_{x}=F_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap F_{i_{k}},

we define the coordinate subtorus

T_{F_{x}}=\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in T^{m}\mid t_{j}=1\text{ for }j\notin\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\}.

The moment–angle manifold can then be described as the quotient

\mathcal{Z}_{P}=(T^{m}\times P^{n})/\sim

where

(t,x)\sim(t^{\prime},x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t^{-1}t^{\prime}\in T_{F_{x}}.

Disk–sphere model.

Let
K
be the simplicial complex dual to
P
. The moment–angle manifold admits the decomposition

\mathcal{Z}_{P}=\bigcup_{I\in K}\left(\prod_{i\in I}D^{2}\times\prod_{j\notin I}S^{1}\right).

Here
D^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}
is the unit disc and
S^{1}=\partial D^{2}
its boundary. The pieces are glued according to inclusions of simplices of
K
.

This description makes the isotropy subgroups of the
T^{m}
–action explicit and plays an important role in the study of the topology of moment–angle manifolds.

Relation with quasitoric manifolds.

Moment–angle manifolds are closely related to quasitoric manifolds. If
M
is a quasitoric manifold over
P
, then there exists a subtorus

K\cong(S^{1})^{m-n}\subset T^{m}

acting freely on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
such that

\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K\cong M.

Thus
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
can be viewed as the total space of a principal
(S^{1})^{m-n}
–bundle over a quasitoric manifold (see
[
7
, Proposition 3.1.5]
). This bundle structure will play a central role in the rigidity results proved later in the paper.

2.3
The quaternionic case

2.3.1
Quoric manifolds

Let
\mathbb{H}
denote the algebra of quaternions, and let
Q:=S^{3}\subset\mathbb{H}
be the group of unit quaternions. Thus
Q\cong\mathrm{SU}(2)\cong\mathrm{Sp}(1)
. For
n\geq 1
, we write
Q^{n}=(S^{3})^{n}
and refer to it as the
quaternionic torus
. This subsection is a brief summary of
[
19
]
.

There is a natural action of
Q^{n}
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
given by coordinatewise left multiplication,

((s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}),(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}))\longmapsto(s_{1}h_{1},\ldots,s_{n}h_{n}).

We refer to this as the
standard
Q^{n}
-action
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

This action has the following basic properties:

(1)

The orbits are products of
3
-spheres, and the origin is the unique fixed point.

(2)

The orbit space is naturally identified with the positive cone

\mathbb{H}^{n}/Q^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}

via the quotient map

\pi_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})=(|h_{1}|^{2},\ldots,|h_{n}|^{2}).

(3)

Conjugacy classes are naturally associated with the faces of the orbits. We refer to these classes as
isotropy classes
.

In
[
19
]
, Hopkinson considers actions given by coordinatewise conjugation, which generalize the standard coordinatewise multiplication action. Let
Q^{n}
act on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
by
Q^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

((s_{1},...,s_{n}),(h_{1},...,h_{n}))\mapsto(s_{l_{1}}h_{1}s_{r_{1}}^{-1},...,s_{l_{n}}h_{n}s_{r_{n}}^{-1})

(2.1)

where each subscript is the index of some coordinate subgroup
Q_{k}
(i.e.
Q_{k}=1\times\cdots\times 1\times Q\times 1\times\cdots 1
, with
Q
in the k-th position) of
Q^{n}
or is the empty set, with
s_{\emptyset}:=1
.

In the general setting of equation (
2.1
), conditions
(1)-(3)
may be violated, unless certain criteria are satisfied
[
19
, Chapter 3]
. We now describe the criteria.

Recall that a
face
of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
is determined by setting a subset of the coordinates equal to zero. More precisely, for
\sigma\subset[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
, define

F_{\sigma}=\{(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:r_{i}=0\text{ for }i\in\sigma\}.

Then
F_{\sigma}
is a face of codimension
|\sigma|
, and every face arises in this way. If a point of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
projects to
\operatorname{relint}(F_{\sigma})
, then its isotropy subgroup is conjugate to the coordinate subgroup
Q^{|\sigma|}\subset Q^{n}
.

Thus the general
Q^{n}
–action determines an assignment

\ell:\{\text{faces of }\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\}\longrightarrow\{\text{isotropy classes of subgroups of }Q^{n}\},

called the
isotropy functor
.

Definition 2.13
.

A functor
\ell
is an
acceptable isotropy functor
if
\ell
is injective.

This injectivity condition is the quaternionic analogue of the nonsingularity condition, i.e. Property (*), for characteristic maps in toric topology, ensuring that the local representation at each vertex is isomorphic to the regular action of
Q^{n}
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Definition 2.14
.

Actions that determine an acceptable isotropy functor are called
regular actions
. A space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which is equipped with a regular
Q^{n}
-action is a
regular
Q^{n}
-corner
.

Remark 2.15
.

Any regular corner satisfies the general properties that we have identified in the standard action, namely (1) the orbits are products of
3
-spheres, (2) the orbit space is the product space

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq}
and (3) the isotropy class of an orbit in a face
F_{\sigma}
is a rank
|\sigma|
class of subgroups in
Q^{n}
.

Remark 2.16
.

In
[
19
]
there are different characterizations of acceptability using either matrices or graphs. In
[
19
, p.60]
it is shown that all the characterizations are equivalent.

Let
M^{4n}
be a topological manifold equipped with a continuous
Q^{n}
-action.

Definition 2.17
.

A
regular chart
for the
Q^{n}
-action on
M^{4n}
is a pair
(U,\phi)
, where
U\subset M^{4n}
is a
Q^{n}
-invariant open subset and

\phi:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}

is an equivariant homeomorphism onto
\mathbb{H}^{n}
equipped with the standard
Q^{n}
-action, that is,

\phi(t\cdot y)=t\cdot\phi(y)\qquad\text{for all }t\in Q^{n},\ y\in U.

Definition 2.18
.

A
Q^{n}
-action on
M^{4n}
is called

if it is effective and every point of
M^{4n}
admits a regular chart.

Thus a locally regular action is one which is locally modelled on the regular action of
Q^{n}
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. In particular, all isotropy subgroups are conjugate to coordinate subgroups
Q^{k}\subset Q^{n}
, and only finitely many orbit types occur.

Since the orbit space of the regular action on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, the orbit space of a locally regular action is locally modelled on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with its natural face stratification. In other words, if a
Q^{n}
-action on
M^{4n}
is locally regular, then the orbit space

X:=M^{4n}/Q^{n}

inherits the structure of an
n
-manifold with corners, as in the complex case. Let
P^{n}
be a simple polytope. The face poset of
P^{n}
determines the orbit types of a locally regular
Q^{n}
-action through the isotropy assignment.

Definition 2.19
.

A
characteristic function
on
P^{n}
is an injective map

\lambda:\{\text{faces of }P^{n}\}\longrightarrow\{\text{isotropy classes of subgroups of }Q^{n}\}

assigning to each face the isotropy class of the corresponding orbit type.

A pair
(P^{n},\lambda)
is called a
characteristic pair
.

We now define quoric manifolds.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
P^{n}
be a simple polytope. A
quoric manifold
over
P^{n}
is a
4n
-dimensional manifold
M^{4n}
equipped with a
Q^{n}
-action such that:

(1)

the action of
Q^{n}
on
M^{4n}
is locally regular;

(2)

there is a continuous projection

\pi:M^{4n}\to P^{n}

whose fibres are precisely the
Q^{n}
-orbits.

Thus the orbit space of a quoric manifold is identified with a simple polytope.

The canonical model.

Given a characteristic pair
(P^{n},\lambda)
, one constructs a quoric manifold by a quotient construction analogous to the canonical model in toric topology.

For each point
x\in P^{n}
, let
F_{x}
denote the unique face whose relative interior contains
x
. Choose a representative subgroup

\widehat{\lambda}(x)\subset Q^{n}

in the conjugacy class
\lambda(F_{x})
.

Define an equivalence relation
\sim_{\lambda}
on
Q^{n}\times P^{n}
by

(q,x)\sim_{\lambda}(q^{\prime},x^{\prime})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=x^{\prime}\text{ and }q^{-1}q^{\prime}\in\widehat{\lambda}(x).

Definition 2.21
.

The quotient space

\mathcal{M}(\lambda):=(Q^{n}\times P^{n})/\!\sim_{\lambda}

is called the
canonical model
(or
derived quoric manifold
) associated to the characteristic pair
(P^{n},\lambda)
.

The group
Q^{n}
acts on
\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
by left multiplication on the first factor, and the orbit space is naturally identified with
P^{n}
.

The canonical model construction exhausts all quoric manifolds (see
[
19
, Proposition 4.2.10]
).

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
M^{4n}
be a quoric manifold over a simple polytope
P^{n}
, and let
\lambda
be its associated characteristic function. Then
M^{4n}
is
Q^{n}
-equivariantly homeomorphic to the canonical model
\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
.

In particular, the equivariant homeomorphism type of a quoric manifold is completely determined by its characteristic pair
(P^{n},\lambda)
.

Example 2.23
.

The quaternionic projective space
\mathbb{H}P^{n}
, equipped with its natural
Q^{n}
-action, is a quoric manifold whose orbit space is the simplex
\Delta^{n}
. For detailed calculations see
[
19
, pp.82]
.

Remark 2.25

We continue our presentation by recalling several fundamental properties of quaternionic moment–angle manifolds.

Consider the quaternionic vector space

with the standard

\pi_{\mathbb{H}}:\mathbb{H}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m},\qquad(h_{1},\dots,h_{m})\longmapsto(|h_{1}|^{2},\dots,|h_{m}|^{2}).

Define the

(i.e. the unit polydisk in

(D^{4})^{m}=\{(h_{1},\dots,h_{m})\in\mathbb{H}^{m}\mid|h_{i}|\leq 1\text{ for }i=1,\dots,m\}.

Its boundary is

The quotient map

\pi_{\mathbb{H}}:(D^{4})^{m}\longrightarrow I^{m}=[0,1]^{m},\qquad(h_{1},\dots,h_{m})\mapsto(|h_{1}|^{2},\dots,|h_{m}|^{2}),

The following is an easy observation and details can be found in

Remark 2.26
.

There is a quaternionic analogue of the classical identification

I^{m}\times T^{m}/\!\sim\;\cong\;(D^{2})^{m}.

The standard coordinatewise action of

on the quaternionic polydisc

has orbit space

Equivalently,

In particular,

.

The next result is very important because it establishes that the quaternionic moment-angle space

Theorem 2.27
.

For any simple polytope

with m facets the space

has the canonical structure of a smooth

–dimensional manifold for which the

–action is smooth. Furthermore, there exists a

–equivariant embedding
i_{e}:\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\hookrightarrow(D^{4})^{m}\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho:\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\to P^{n}
denote the orbit projection.

Since

is simple, exactly
n

admits a canonical cubical decomposition consisting of
n

corresponding to vertices

be a vertex of

Indeed, the coordinates corresponding to the

vary over quaternionic discs

, while the remaining coordinates remain on

and contribute a free

is the union

\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}=\bigcup_{v}B_{v},\qquad B_{v}\cong(D^{4})^{n}\times Q^{m-n}.

The gluings between the pieces

Therefore

\dim\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}=4n+3(m-n)=m+n.

The

For the equivariant embedding, consider the canonical embedding

given by the facet inequalities of

The embedding of each

This completes the proof.

3
Reduction to rigidity of quasitoric and quoric manifolds

The topological classification results proved in this paper rely on the equivariant rigidity theorems for quasitoric and quoric manifolds established in
[
24
]
and
[
16
]
, respectively. In this section we recall the relevant constructions and explain how rigidity of the quotient spaces reduces the classification of moment–angle manifolds to the comparison of the associated principal bundles.

We now recall the notion of topological rigidity, which provides the conceptual framework for our approach (see
[
23
, Def. 7.1]
).

Definition 3.1

(Topological Rigidity)
.

Let
N
be a closed topological manifold. We say that
N
is
topologically rigid
if every homotopy equivalence

f:M\longrightarrow N

with
M
a closed topological manifold as source is homotopic to a homeomorphism.

Example 3.2
.

The sphere
S^{n}
is the prototypical example of a topologically rigid manifold.
The classical Poincaré Conjecture asserts that the
n
–sphere
S^{n}
is topologically rigid.

Remark 3.3
.

Topological rigidity provides a broad generalization of the Poincaré Conjecture to manifolds with nontrivial fundamental groups. Classical rigidity results include:

(1)

The Borel Conjecture for aspherical manifolds, asserting that manifolds with contractible universal cover are topologically rigid.

(2)

The Farrell–Jones Rigidity Theorem for negatively curved manifolds.

Our goal is to establish a similar rigidity property for moment–angle manifolds, which in general are not aspherical.

As a preliminary step, we recall that the quasitoric and quoric manifolds are rigid. Since the arguments in the two cases are closely related, we discuss them in parallel. The main difference arises from the noncommutativity of the quaternionic torus
(S^{3})^{n}
, which makes the representation theory more involved than in the classical toric case.

Throughout this section
G
will denote either the torus
T^{n}=(S^{1})^{n}
or the quaternionic torus
Q^{n}=(S^{3})^{n}
. The following Definition as well as details on locally linear actions can be found in
[
4
, p.171]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
N
be a manifold with an action of a compact Lie group
G
. The action is called
locally linear
if for every point
y\in N
with isotropy subgroup
G_{y}
there exists a
G_{y}
–invariant neighbourhood of
y
, a linear representation
V
of
G_{y}
, and an open
G
–equivariant embedding

G\times_{G_{y}}V\longrightarrow N.

The image of such an embedding is called a
linear tube
.

Locally standard torus actions and locally regular quaternionic actions are locally linear (see
[
24
,
16
]
). This condition allows one to analyze the orbit structure of the action using linear representations of the isotropy subgroups.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
be a moment–angle manifold equipped with its natural
G
–action, and let
N
be a locally linear
G
–manifold. Suppose that there exists a
G
–equivariant homotopy equivalence

f:N\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}.

Now we show that the orbit–type structure of
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
transfers to
N
. In particular, the isotropy subgroups and the local linear models of the action on
N
coincide with those of the model space.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
be a moment–angle manifold with the canonical action of
G=T^{m}
(respectively
G=Q^{m}
), and let
N
be a locally linear
G
–manifold. Suppose that

f:N\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}

is a
G
–equivariant homotopy equivalence. Then the following hold:

(1)

the
G
–action on
N
is effective;

(2)

every nontrivial isotropy subgroup of the
G
–action on
N
is a coordinate subgroup of
G
;

(3)

the isotropy subgroups of the actions on
N
and
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
coincide.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that some nontrivial element
g\in G
fixes
N
pointwise. Let
H=\langle g\rangle
. Then
N^{H}=N
. Since
f
is an equivariant homotopy equivalence, it induces a homotopy equivalence (cf.
[
4
, Cor. II.5.5]
)

f^{H}:N^{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{H}.

However, the action of
G
on the moment–angle manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
is effective, so
\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{H}
is a proper submanifold of
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
. This contradicts the fact that
N^{H}=N
. Hence the action on
N
must be effective.

(2) Let
x\in N
and let
G_{x}
denote its isotropy subgroup. Since
f
is equivariant, the fixed point sets satisfy

N^{G_{x}}\simeq\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{G_{x}}.

The isotropy subgroups of the
G
–action on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
are precisely the coordinate subtori corresponding to subsets of the set of facets of
P
. Thus if
\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{G_{x}}\neq\varnothing
, the subgroup
G_{x}
must be a coordinate subgroup of
G
. Therefore every isotropy subgroup of
N
is also a coordinate subgroup.

(3) Since the isotropy groups of
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
are coordinate subtori and the fixed point sets are preserved up to homotopy under the equivariant homotopy equivalence
f
, the set of isotropy subgroups occurring in the action on
N
coincides with the set of isotropy subgroups of
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
.
∎

An important consequence is that the orbit structure of the action on
N
is identical to that of the model space.

Corollary 3.6
.

The isotropy subgroups of the
G
–actions on
N
and
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
are the same.

In both cases there exists a subgroup
K\subset G
acting freely on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
such that the quotient

\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K

is a quasitoric (respectively quoric) manifold over
P
.

Let
N
be a locally linear
G
–manifold equipped with a
G
–equivariant homotopy equivalence

f:N\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}.

The basic next step is to show that the subgroup
K
also acts freely on
N
.

Lemma 3.7
.

The subgroup
K
acts freely on
N
.

Proof.

Suppose that some nontrivial element
k\in K
fixes a point
x\in N
. Let
H=\langle k\rangle
. Then
x\in N^{H}
, so the fixed point set
N^{H}
is nonempty.

Since
f
is a
G
–equivariant homotopy equivalence, it induces a homotopy equivalence

f^{H}:N^{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{H}.

However, by construction the subgroup
K
acts freely on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
, so

\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{H}=\varnothing

whenever
H
is a nontrivial subgroup of
K
. This contradicts the fact that
N^{H}
is nonempty. Therefore no nontrivial element of
K
can fix a point of
N
, and the
K
–action on
N
is free.
∎

Since the action of
K
on both
N
and
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
is free, the map
f
descends to a map between the quotient spaces.

Proposition 3.8
.

The equivariant homotopy equivalence

f:N\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}

induces a homotopy equivalence

\bar{f}:N/K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K.

Proof.

Since
f
is
G
–equivariant, it is in particular
K
–equivariant. Therefore it descends to a continuous map between the quotient spaces. Because
f
is a homotopy equivalence and
K
acts freely on both spaces, the induced map
\bar{f}
is also a homotopy equivalence.
∎

The quotient
\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K
is a quasitoric manifold in the complex case and a quoric manifold in the quaternionic case. The induced map
\bar{f}
is equivariant with respect to the residual action of
G/K
.

We can therefore apply the equivariant rigidity results for quasitoric and quoric manifolds. In the complex case and quasitoric manifolds we have:

Theorem 3.9

(Topological rigidity of quasitoric manifolds
[
24
]
)
.

Let
M^{2n}
be a quasitoric manifold and let
N
be a locally linear
T^{n}
–manifold. If

f:N\longrightarrow M^{2n}

is a
T^{n}
–equivariant homotopy equivalence, then
f
is equivariantly homotopic to a
T^{n}
–homeomorphism.

Corollary 3.10
.

The assumptions of Theorem
3.9
imply that the manifold
N
is a quasitoric manifold.

In the quaternionic case and quoric manifolds we have the following results:

Theorem 3.11

(Topological rigidity of quoric manifolds
[
16
]
)
.

Let
M^{4n}
be a quoric manifold and let
N
be a locally linear
Q^{n}
–manifold. If

f:N\longrightarrow M^{4n}

is a
Q^{n}
–equivariant homotopy equivalence, then
f
is equivariantly homotopic to a
Q^{n}
–homeomorphism.

Corollary 3.12
.

The assumptions of Theorem
3.11
imply that the manifold
N
is a quoric manifold.

Consequently, we obtain the following immediate result.

Corollary 3.13
.

The quotient manifolds
N/K
and
\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K
are equivariantly homeomorphic.

The classification problem for
N
therefore reduces to understanding the possible principal
K
–bundles over the quasitoric or quoric manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K
.

This reduction will be used in the following section to complete the classification of moment–angle manifolds.

4
Characteristic classes of kernel bundles

The principal bundles arising in the moment–angle construction are determined by the kernel of the characteristic homomorphism.

More precisely, let

\Lambda=(\lambda_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}

be the characteristic matrix of the quasitoric manifold. It defines a homomorphism of tori

\Lambda:T^{m}\longrightarrow T^{n}

by

\Lambda(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{\lambda_{1j}},\dots,\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{\lambda_{nj}}\right),

where
t_{j}\in S^{1}
. This gives rise to a short exact sequence of tori

1\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow T^{m}\xrightarrow{\;\Lambda\;}T^{n}\longrightarrow 1,

where
K=\ker(\Lambda)\cong(S^{1})^{m-n}
(see
[
5
, §4.8]
, also compare
[
21
]
).

In the quaternionic case, an analogous construction yields a homomorphism

Q^{m}\longrightarrow Q^{n},

whose kernel is a subgroup
K\cong(S^{3})^{m-n}
(see
[
19
, pp.100]
).

In both cases, the free action of
K
on the moment–angle manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
defines a principal
K
–bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K,

which we refer to as the
kernel bundle
.

The bundle structures appearing in both complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds are governed by characteristic classes that encode the topological twisting of the corresponding principal bundles. In the complex case, moment–angle manifolds arise as principal
(S^{1})^{r}
–bundles over quasitoric manifolds, and their classification is controlled by cohomology classes in
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})
. In the quaternionic case, the analogous construction yields principal
(S^{3})^{r}
–bundles over quoric manifolds, whose topology is determined by characteristic classes in
H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})
. Despite the difference in degree, the two situations admit a strikingly parallel geometric interpretation: the relevant cohomology classes can be described as the Poincaré duals of the characteristic submanifolds arising from the isotropy strata of the torus or quaternionic torus actions.

In this section we recall the classification of these principal bundles (following the classical results of
[
12
,
28
]
) and describe the geometric realization of their characteristic classes as in
[
25
]
, which will play a key role in lifting rigidity results from the base manifolds to the moment–angle manifolds themselves.

4.1
Classification of principal
S^{1}
-bundles

We recall the classification of principal
S^{1}
–bundles (see eg
[
26
,
25
]
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a CW complex. Isomorphism classes of principal
S^{1}
–bundles over
X
are in bijection with homotopy classes of maps

[X,BS^{1}].

Since
BS^{1}\simeq\mathbb{CP}^{\infty}\simeq K(\mathbb{Z},2)
, this set is naturally identified with

H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

Proof.

The classification theorem for principal bundles gives

\mathrm{Prin}_{S^{1}}(X)\cong[X,BS^{1}].

Since
S^{1}
is the unitary group
U(1)
, its classifying space satisfies

BS^{1}\simeq BU(1)\simeq\mathbb{CP}^{\infty}.

The latter is an Eilenberg–MacLane space
K(\mathbb{Z},2)
, and therefore

[X,BS^{1}]\cong[X,K(\mathbb{Z},2)]\cong H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Principal
(S^{1})^{r}
–bundles over
X
are classified by

H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})^{r}.

Proof.

Since
(S^{1})^{r}\cong U(1)^{r}
, we have

B((S^{1})^{r})\cong(BS^{1})^{r}.

Thus

\mathrm{Prin}_{(S^{1})^{r}}(X)\cong[X,(BS^{1})^{r}]\cong[X,BS^{1}]^{r}\cong H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})^{r}

by Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.3

(First Chern class)
.

The cohomology class associated to a principal
S^{1}
–bundle is its first Chern class

c_{1}\in H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

Under the bijection in Theorem
4.1
, this class completely determines the bundle up to isomorphism (see
[
25
]
).

4.2
Characteristic classes of kernel bundles: the complex case

In this subsection we compute the characteristic classes of the kernel bundle arising from the moment–angle construction in the complex case. These classes will later be used to complete the classification of moment–angle manifolds.

Let
P^{n}
be a simple polytope with facets

F_{1},\dots,F_{m},

and let
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
be the associated moment–angle manifold.
The canonical torus action of
T^{m}
on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
contains a subtorus

K\subset T^{m}

acting freely such that the quotient

\pi:\mathcal{Z}_{P}\longrightarrow M:=\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K

is a quasitoric manifold over
P^{n}
.
The subgroup
K
is isomorphic to
(S^{1})^{m-n}
and therefore the orbit map
defines a principal
K
–bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}\xrightarrow{\pi}M.

Facet classes.

Let

M_{i}=\pi^{-1}(F_{i})

be the characteristic submanifold corresponding to the facet
F_{i}
.
Each
M_{i}
has codimension
2
in
M
.

Definition 4.4
.

The cohomology class

x_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[M_{i}]\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})

is called the
facet class
corresponding to the facet
F_{i}
.

Each facet
F_{i}
determines (cf.
[
21
, Section §3.3]
) a
T^{n}
–equivariant complex line bundle

\rho_{i}:\mathcal{Z}_{P}\times_{K}\mathbb{C}_{i}\longrightarrow M.

The first Chern class of
\rho_{i}
coincides with the facet class

c_{1}(\rho_{i})=x_{i}.

These bundles play a fundamental role in the geometry of quasitoric manifolds. In particular, Buchstaber and Panov (see
[
6
, Theorems 7.3.14 and 7.3.28]
) show that the stable tangent bundle admits the decomposition

TM\oplus\mathbb{R}^{2(m-n)}\cong\rho_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\rho_{m}

and consequently the total Chern class of
M
is given by

c(M)=(1+x_{1})\cdots(1+x_{m}).

Local structure near characteristic submanifolds.

The following lemma describes the local geometry of the bundle near a characteristic submanifold.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
M_{i}
be a characteristic submanifold of
M
.
Then a tubular neighbourhood of
M_{i}
is equivariantly diffeomorphic to a neighbourhood of the origin in

\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{\,n-1},

where the first
S^{1}
factor of the torus acts on the first coordinate by complex multiplication.

Proof.

Since the torus action on
M
is locally standard (Definition
2.7
), every point of
M
admits a neighbourhood equivariantly homeomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the standard coordinatewise torus action. Near
M_{i}
the isotropy group is the circle corresponding to the facet
F_{i}
, which acts by standard complex multiplication on the normal coordinate, while acting trivially on the tangent space of
M_{i}
. The remaining coordinates are fixed by this circle, giving the desired local model.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
D^{2}\subset M
be a small disc transverse to
M_{i}
intersecting it once. Then the restriction of the principal circle bundle to the boundary
\partial D^{2}\cong S^{1}
is the Hopf bundle

S^{1}\longrightarrow S^{3}\longrightarrow S^{2}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.5
, the bundle near
M_{i}
is modelled on the standard
S^{1}
–action on
\mathbb{C}
.
Intersecting a small neighbourhood of the origin with the unit sphere produces the Hopf fibration

S^{1}\to S^{3}\to S^{2},

which is therefore the local model of the bundle transverse to
M_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 4.7

(First Chern class)
.

Let

\rho_{i}:\mathcal{Z}_{P}\times_{K}\mathbb{C}_{i}\longrightarrow M

be the complex line bundle associated with the facet
F_{i}
. Then the first Chern class of
\rho_{i}
is the Poincaré dual of the characteristic submanifold
M_{i}=\pi^{-1}(F_{i})
:

c_{1}(\rho_{i})=\mathrm{PD}[M_{i}]=x_{i}.

Proof.

Recall that for a complex line bundle the first Chern class is the Poincaré dual of the zero set of a generic section. The bundle
\rho_{i}
admits a natural section that vanishes transversely and with multiplicity one exactly along the characteristic submanifold
M_{i}
.

By Lemma
4.6
, the restriction of the bundle to a small sphere transverse to
M_{i}
is the Hopf bundle

S^{1}\longrightarrow S^{3}\longrightarrow S^{2}.

Since the Hopf bundle has first Chern class equal to the generator of
H^{2}(S^{2};\mathbb{Z})
, the resulting cohomology class on
M
evaluates to
1
on the transverse
2
–sphere. Therefore the first Chern class of
\rho_{i}
coincides with the Poincaré dual of the homology class
[M_{i}]
, that is,

c_{1}(\rho_{i})=\mathrm{PD}[M_{i}].

∎

The appearance of characteristic classes determined by the Stanley–Reisner algebra is a general phenomenon in toric topology. In particular, Notbohm
[
27
]
constructed vector bundles over the Davis–Januszkiewicz space
DJ(K)
(for the latter see
[
6
, Section 4.3]
) whose characteristic classes are prescribed by the generators of the Stanley–Reisner ring and showed that these bundles are determined by their characteristic classes. These bundles are closely related to those arising from the tangent bundles of quasitoric manifolds and moment–angle complexes.

In more detail, let
P
be a simple polytope and let
K=K_{P}
be the simplicial complex dual to
P
. Let
M
be the quasitoric manifold determined by the characteristic matrix
\Lambda
, and let

f_{\Lambda}:M\longrightarrow DJ(K)

denote the canonical map to the Davis–Januszkiewicz space.

Following
[
27
]
, let
\lambda_{i}\to DJ(K)
be the canonical complex line bundles over
DJ(K)
corresponding to the vertices of
K
.

The relevance of the Davis–Januszkiewicz space to the present discussion is that the facet bundles on
M
are pullbacks of universal line bundles over
DJ(K)
, so the kernel bundle classes can be expressed in terms of universal Stanley–Reisner generators.

Proposition 4.8

(Facet bundles as pullbacks from
DJ(K)
)
.

The facet bundles

\rho_{i}:\mathcal{Z}_{P}\times_{K}\mathbb{C}_{i}\longrightarrow M

are obtained as pullbacks

\rho_{i}\cong f_{\Lambda}^{*}(\lambda_{i}).

Consequently, the facet classes satisfy

x_{i}=c_{1}(\rho_{i})=f_{\Lambda}^{*}(v_{i}),

where
v_{i}=c_{1}(\lambda_{i})\in H^{2}(DJ(K);\mathbb{Z})
are the canonical
generators of the Stanley–Reisner ring

H^{*}(DJ(K);\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[K].

Proof.

Recall that the Davis–Januszkiewicz space
DJ(K)
is defined so that its cohomology ring is the Stanley–Reisner ring

H^{*}(DJ(K);\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[K],

whose degree–two generators correspond to the vertices of
K

[
6
, Section 4.3]
.

By
[
27
]
, over
DJ(K)
there exist canonical complex line bundles

\lambda_{i}\longrightarrow DJ(K),

whose first Chern classes are precisely these generators:

c_{1}(\lambda_{i})=v_{i}.

These bundles arise from the Borel construction applied to the coordinate representations of the torus
T^{m}
.

The quasitoric manifold
M
is obtained as the quotient

M=\mathcal{Z}_{P}/K,

and the characteristic matrix
\Lambda
determines a canonical map

f_{\Lambda}:M\longrightarrow DJ(K)

which induces the standard presentation of the cohomology ring of
M

[
6
, Section 7.3]
.

By construction, the bundles
\rho_{i}
are obtained by quotienting the trivial
T^{m}
–equivariant line bundles
\mathcal{Z}_{P}\times\mathbb{C}_{i}
by the free action of
K(\Lambda)
. On the other hand, the bundles
\lambda_{i}
over
DJ(K)
arise from the universal
T^{m}
–equivariant line bundles via the Borel construction.

The map

f_{\Lambda}:M\longrightarrow DJ(K)

is induced by the classifying map of the principal
T^{m}
–bundle together with the homomorphism
\Lambda
, and the above constructions are natural with respect to this map. In particular, the formation of the associated line bundles commutes with pullback along
f_{\Lambda}
, and hence

\rho_{i}\cong f_{\Lambda}^{*}(\lambda_{i}).

Consequently, their first Chern classes satisfy

c_{1}(\rho_{i})=f_{\Lambda}^{*}(c_{1}(\lambda_{i}))=f_{\Lambda}^{*}(v_{i}).

Since
c_{1}(\rho_{i})=x_{i}
by Corollary
4.7
, the result follows.
∎

The kernel bundle.

Let
\Lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
be the characteristic matrix of the quasitoric manifold
M
, defining a homomorphism of tori

\Lambda:T^{m}\longrightarrow T^{n}.

Recall that its kernel
K=\ker(\Lambda)\cong(S^{1})^{m-n}
acts freely on the moment–angle manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
, yielding a principal
K
–bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}\xrightarrow{\;\pi\;}M,

called the
kernel bundle
.

Choose a matrix

A\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-n)\times m}

whose rows form a basis of
K
. Each row determines a circle subgroup of
K
.

Lemma 4.9
.

The first Chern class of the principal
S^{1}_{k}
–bundle over
M
is given by

c_{1}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ki}\,x_{i},

where

x_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[M_{i}]\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})

are the facet classes dual to the characteristic submanifolds

M_{i}=\pi^{-1}(F_{i}).

Proof.

The proof of this Lemma is based on Yoshida’s description of Euler classes of local torus actions as obstructions to extending local equivariant sections across the facets of the orbit space
[
31
]
.

Let
\{U_{v}\}
be the open cover of the polytope
P
consisting of the vertex neighborhoods described in Section
2.1
. Their preimages

\widetilde{U}_{v}=\pi^{-1}(U_{v})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{P}

give a trivializing cover of the principal
K
–bundle.

Over each
U_{v}
, the bundle admits a local section

s_{v}:U_{v}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}

obtained by choosing the canonical coordinate slice corresponding to the vertex
v
. Hence the kernel bundle is locally trivial over each
U_{v}
.

On an overlap
U_{v}\cap U_{w}
, the two sections
s_{v}
and
s_{w}
differ by a transition function

g_{vw}:U_{v}\cap U_{w}\longrightarrow K.

The geometry of the polytope implies that if the vertices
v
and
w
are separated by a facet
F_{i}
, then the change of coordinates between the two charts corresponds to multiplication by the
i
–th coordinate circle of
T^{m}
.

Projecting this coordinate change to the
k
–th circle factor
S^{1}_{k}\subset K
yields

g_{vw}^{(k)}(b)=\exp(2\pi i\,a_{ki})

for
b\in U_{v}\cap U_{w}
. Thus the transition functions for the
S^{1}_{k}
–bundle form a Čech
1
–cocycle

\{g_{vw}^{(k)}\}\in\check{C}^{1}(\{U_{v}\},S^{1}).

The first Chern class is obtained from this cocycle via the connecting homomorphism

\delta:\check{H}^{1}(\{U_{v}\},S^{1})\longrightarrow\check{H}^{2}(\{U_{v}\},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z}),

which is induced by the exponential sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow S^{1}\longrightarrow 0.

Each crossing of a facet
F_{i}
contributes exactly
a_{ki}
times the generator dual to the characteristic submanifold
M_{i}
. By Corollary
4.7
, this generator is the facet class

x_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[M_{i}].

Summing the contributions over all facets yields

c_{1}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ki}x_{i}.

∎

Proposition 4.10
.

Let

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}\longrightarrow M

be the kernel bundle of the moment–angle manifold. If
A
is a matrix whose rows generate
K
, then the Chern classes of the circle factors of
K
are given by

c_{1}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ki}x_{i},\qquad k=1,\dots,m-n.

Equivalently,

\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
c_{1}^{(m-n)}\end{pmatrix}=A\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{m}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

The result follows directly from Lemma
4.9
, since each
row of the kernel matrix describes the exponent with which the facet
circle acts on the corresponding circle factor of the kernel torus.
∎

The following result is an immediate consequence of Corollary
4.2
and Propositions
4.8
,
4.10
.

Theorem 4.11
.

The principal
(S^{1})^{m-n}
–bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}\longrightarrow M

is completely determined by the characteristic matrix
\Lambda
.

More precisely, if
A=(a_{ki})
is a matrix whose rows generate
\ker(\Lambda)
, then the first Chern classes of the circle factors
are

c_{1}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ki}x_{i},\qquad k=1,\dots,m-n.

Equivalently,

c_{1}^{(k)}=f_{\Lambda}^{*}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ki}v_{i}\right),

where
v_{i}\in H^{2}(DJ(K);\mathbb{Z})
are the canonical generators of the Stanley–Reisner ring.

Remark 4.12
.

The formula in Proposition
4.10
gives an explicit realization of the homotopy-theoretic classification of principal
S^{1}
–bundles by

[X,BS^{1}]\cong H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

Indeed, the first Chern class of each circle factor of the kernel bundle is represented geometrically as a linear combination of the facet classes of the quasitoric base, with coefficients determined by the kernel matrix of the characteristic map.

Remark 4.13

(Relation with Yoshida’s Euler class)
.

The description
c_{1}(\rho_{i})=x_{i}
admits a natural interpretation in the framework of local torus actions developed by Yoshida
[
31
]
. In that setting, a manifold with a torus action modeled on the standard representation is classified by a characteristic pair
(B,\lambda)
together with an Euler class

e\in H^{2}(B;t_{\mathbb{Z}}),

where
B
is the orbit space (manifold with corners) and
t_{\mathbb{Z}}
is the integral lattice of the torus.

In the quasitoric case the orbit space
B
is the simple polytope
P
. Since
H^{2}(P;\mathbb{Z})=0
, the global Euler class vanishes. However, the local Euler classes associated with the facets remain and determine the twisting of the torus action near the characteristic submanifolds. The Hopf bundle model described in Lemma
4.6
shows that the local contribution of the facet
F_{i}
is precisely the generator dual to the characteristic submanifold
M_{i}
. Consequently the Euler class in Yoshida’s classification corresponds to the collection of facet classes

x_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[M_{i}]=c_{1}(\rho_{i}).

4.3
Classification of principal
S^{3}
-bundles

We begin by recalling the classification of principal
S^{3}
–bundles (see eg
[
26
, Theorem 7.4]
). We give a proof for completeness.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
X
be a CW complex. Isomorphism classes of principal
SU(2)
–bundles over
X
are in bijection with homotopy classes of maps

[X,BSU(2)].

If
\dim X\leq 4
, this set is naturally isomorphic to

H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

Proof.

The general classification theorem for principal bundles states that isomorphism classes of principal
G
–bundles over a CW complex
X
are in bijection with homotopy classes of maps
X\to BG
, where
BG
denotes the classifying space of
G
(see
[
25
]
also details can be found in
[
1
]
). Thus

\mathrm{Prin}_{SU(2)}(X)\cong[X,BSU(2)].

Since
SU(2)\cong S^{3}
, the long exact sequence of the universal bundle

SU(2)\to ESU(2)\to BSU(2)

implies

\pi_{k}(BSU(2))\cong\pi_{k-1}(SU(2)).

In particular,

\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}=\pi_{3}=0,\qquad\pi_{4}(BSU(2))=\pi_{3}(SU(2))\cong\mathbb{Z}.

Thus
BSU(2)
is
3
–connected and its
4
–type is
K(\mathbb{Z},4)
. If
\dim X\leq 4
, every map
X\to BSU(2)
factors uniquely through this
4
–type, giving

[X,BSU(2)]\cong[X,K(\mathbb{Z},4)]\cong H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

∎

Remark 4.15

(Dold–Whitney theorem
[
13
]
)
.

Theorem
4.14
can also be viewed as a special case of the Dold–Whitney classification of oriented rank–
4
vector bundles. In that setting, an
SU(2)
–bundle corresponds to a quaternionic line bundle, whose isomorphism class is determined by its second Chern class

c_{2}\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

The next remark is very important and explains the difference between the complex case and the quaternionic one.

Remark 4.16
.

Over a
4
–dimensional base, principal
S^{3}\cong SU(2)
–bundles are determined by their primary degree–
4
characteristic classes. In higher dimensions, however, quaternionic line bundles are not classified in general by
c_{2}
alone, since
BS^{3}
is not an Eilenberg–MacLane space. This is reflected in Granja’s work
[
17
]
on quaternionic line bundles and in the secondary invariants introduced by Crowley and Goette
[
8
]
. Thus, for higher-dimensional quoric bases, the classes computed in this paper should be viewed as primary invariants of the kernel bundle rather than a complete classification.

4.4
Characteristic classes of kernel bundles: the quaternionic case

We now consider the quaternionic analogue of the previous construction. Let
P
be a simple polytope, let
Z_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}
be the associated quaternionic moment–angle manifold, and let
K\subset Q^{m}
be a subgroup acting freely on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}
such that the quotient

q:\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\longrightarrow X:=\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}/K

is a quoric manifold over the polytope
P
. By
[
19
, pp.100]
we know that an identification

K\cong(S^{3})^{m-n}

has been fixed.

The map
q
is therefore a principal
K
–bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\xrightarrow{\;q\;}X.

Our aim is to describe the characteristic classes of this bundle.

Characteristic submanifolds and facet classes.

Let

X_{i}=q^{-1}(F_{i})

be the characteristic submanifold corresponding to the facet
F_{i}
of
P
. Each
X_{i}
has codimension
4
in
X
.

Definition 4.17
.

The cohomology class

y_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[X_{i}]\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})

is called the
quaternionic facet class
corresponding to the facet
F_{i}
.

Remark 4.18
.

The classes of the above Defintion
4.17
play the same role in the quaternionic setting as the facet classes
x_{i}\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
in the complex case (compare with Definition
4.4
).

Remark 4.19
.

Notice that the group of quaternions of unit length is canonically isomorphic to
SU(2)
so there is a 1–1 correspondence between quaternionic line bundles and principal
SU(2)
–bundles.

Associated quaternionic line bundles.

Let
\mathbb{H}_{i}
denote the one–dimensional quaternionic representation of
Q^{m}
induced by projection onto the
i
-th factor of
Q^{m}=(S^{3})^{m}
. Restricting this representation to the subgroup
K\subset Q^{m}
and quotienting by the free
K
-action on
\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}
, we obtain a quaternionic line bundle

\eta_{i}:\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\times_{K}\mathbb{H}_{i}\longrightarrow X.

Remark 4.20
.

Since
S^{3}\cong SU(2)
, a quaternionic line bundle may also be viewed as a rank–
2
complex vector bundle with structure group
SU(2)
. For the purposes of bundle classification, the natural characteristic class is
therefore the second Chern class

c_{2}(\eta_{i})\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

If
\eta_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the underlying oriented real
4
-plane bundle, then

p_{1}(\eta_{i}^{\mathbb{R}})=-2\,c_{2}(\eta_{i}),

so statements formulated in terms of
c_{2}
may equivalently be rewritten in terms of the first Pontryagin class (for more details see
[
17
]
).

Local structure near characteristic submanifolds.

The next lemma is the quaternionic analogue of the local complex model (Lemma
4.5
) used in the previous subsection.

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
X_{i}\subset X
be a characteristic submanifold. Then a tubular neighborhood of
X_{i}
is equivariantly homeomorphic to a neighborhood of the origin in

\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}^{\,n-1},

where the corresponding
S^{3}
-factor acts on the first coordinate by left quaternionic multiplication.

Proof.

By the local regularity of the
Q^{n}
-action on the quoric manifold
X
(Definition
2.18
), every point of
X
admits a neighborhood equivariantly homeomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with the regular coordinatewise action of
Q^{n}
. Near
X_{i}
, the isotropy group is the
S^{3}
-subgroup corresponding to the facet
F_{i}
, and this subgroup acts by quaternionic multiplication on exactly one coordinate. The remaining coordinates are fixed by this subgroup, which gives the desired local model.
∎

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
D^{4}\subset X
be a small
4
-disc transverse to
X_{i}
and meeting it once. Then the restriction of the associated principal
S^{3}
-bundle to the boundary
\partial D^{4}\cong S^{3}
is the quaternionic Hopf bundle

S^{3}\longrightarrow S^{7}\longrightarrow S^{4}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.21
, the bundle near
X_{i}
is modelled on the regular
S^{3}
-action on
\mathbb{H}
. Intersecting a sufficiently small neighbourhood of the origin with the unit sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
produces the quaternionic Hopf fibration

S^{3}\to S^{7}\to S^{4},

which is therefore the local model of the bundle in directions transverse to
X_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 4.23

(Second Chern class)
.

The second Chern class of the quaternionic line bundle
\eta_{i}
is the quaternionic facet class:

c_{2}(\eta_{i})=y_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[X_{i}].

Proof.

The quaternionic Hopf bundle has second Chern class equal to the generator of
H^{4}(S^{4};\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
. By Lemma
4.22
, the restriction of
\eta_{i}
to a small sphere transverse to
X_{i}
is precisely this bundle. Since the transverse
4
-sphere meets
X_{i}
once positively, the resulting cohomology class on
X
is the Poincaré dual of

[X_{i}]
.
∎

The kernel bundle.

Fix the chosen identification

K\cong S^{3}_{1}\times\cdots\times S^{3}_{r}.

For each factor
S^{3}_{k}\subset K
, let

P_{k}\longrightarrow X

denote the corresponding principal
S^{3}
-bundle and let

\xi_{k}\longrightarrow X

be the associated quaternionic line bundle.

Lemma 4.24
.

For each
k=1,\dots,r
, the second Chern class

c_{2}(\xi_{k})\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})

is determined by the restriction of
\xi_{k}
to small
4
-spheres transverse to the characteristic submanifolds
X_{i}
. In particular, these local restrictions are detected by the quaternionic facet classes

y_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[X_{i}]
.

Proof.

Let
\{U_{v}\}
be the open cover of
X
given by the vertex neighborhoods of the polytope. Their preimages

\widetilde{U}_{v}=q^{-1}(U_{v})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{P}

form a trivializing cover of the principal
(S^{3})^{r}
–bundle.

Over each
U_{v}
, the bundle admits a local section

s_{v}:U_{v}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P},

obtained by choosing the canonical coordinate slice corresponding to the vertex
v
. Hence the bundle is locally trivial over each
U_{v}
.

On overlaps
U_{v}\cap U_{w}
, the two sections
s_{v}
and
s_{w}
differ by a transition function

g_{vw}:U_{v}\cap U_{w}\longrightarrow K\cong(S^{3})^{r}.

Projecting to the
k
–th factor gives

g_{vw}^{(k)}:U_{v}\cap U_{w}\longrightarrow S^{3},

so that the principal
S^{3}
–bundle
P_{k}
is described by a Čech
1
–cocycle
\{g_{vw}^{(k)}\}
.

Passing to the classifying space, this cocycle determines a homotopy class of maps

X\longrightarrow BS^{3}.

Since
BS^{3}
is
3
–connected with
\pi_{4}(BS^{3})\cong\mathbb{Z}
, obstruction theory shows that the first nontrivial invariant of such a map lies in

H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}),

and is given by the second Chern class
c_{2}(\xi_{k})
.

To identify this class, consider small
4
–spheres transverse to the characteristic submanifolds

X_{i}=\pi^{-1}(F_{i}).

If two vertices
v
and
w
are separated by a facet
F_{i}
, then the corresponding transition function
g_{vw}^{(k)}
changes by a generator of
\pi_{3}(S^{3})\cong\mathbb{Z}
. By Lemma
4.22
, this local change is modeled on the quaternionic Hopf fibration over a transverse
S^{4}
, and therefore represents the generator of
H^{4}(S^{4};\mathbb{Z})
.

Each such crossing contributes a multiple of the cohomology class

y_{i}=\mathrm{PD}[X_{i}]\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}),

and these contributions are determined by the combinatorial data of the characteristic map.

Summing over all facets, it follows that the class
c_{2}(\xi_{k})
is determined by its local restrictions to transverse
4
–spheres around the
X_{i}
, and hence by the quaternionic facet classes.
∎

The following example gives the general structure of quaternionic moment–angle manifolds discussed above:

Example 4.25

(The simplex case and the quaternionic Hopf fibration)
.

Let
P=\Delta^{1}
be the
1
–simplex. As in Example
2.23
, the associated quoric manifold is

\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}/K\cong\mathbb{H}P^{1}\cong S^{4}.

The corresponding quaternionic moment–angle manifold is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}=(D^{4}\times S^{3})\ \cup\ (S^{3}\times D^{4}),

where the union is taken along the common boundary
S^{3}\times S^{3}
. This space can be identified with the boundary of
D^{4}\times D^{4}
, and hence

\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\cong\partial(D^{4}\times D^{4})\cong S^{7}

(see Example
2.30
).
The projection

S^{3}\longrightarrow S^{7}\longrightarrow S^{4}

is the quaternionic Hopf fibration, which is a principal
S^{3}
–bundle over the quoric manifold
\mathbb{H}P^{1}\cong S^{4}
.

This example illustrates the general structure of quaternionic moment–angle manifolds: the total space
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
is obtained as a principal
(S^{3})^{r}
–bundle over a quoric manifold, and locally the bundle is modeled on the quaternionic Hopf fibration. In particular, the second Chern class
c_{2}
records the twisting of these local Hopf-type contributions, providing the primary characteristic invariant of the bundle.

Proposition 4.26
.

Let

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\xrightarrow{\;q\;}X

be the kernel bundle of the quaternionic moment–angle manifold. Then, after fixing an identification
K\cong(S^{3})^{r}
, the bundle determines a tuple of primary characteristic classes

\bigl(c_{2}(\xi_{1}),\dots,c_{2}(\xi_{r})\bigr)\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})^{r}.

Proof.

By the classification theorem for principal
S^{3}
-bundles, recalled in Section
4.3
, isomorphism classes of principal
S^{3}
-bundles over
X
are classified by
H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})
. Therefore principal

(S^{3})^{r}
-bundles are classified by

H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})^{r}.

The tuple

\bigl(c_{2}(\xi_{1}),\dots,c_{2}(\xi_{r})\bigr)

is precisely the corresponding classifying invariant.
∎

Theorem 4.27
.

Let

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\xrightarrow{\;q\;}X

be the kernel bundle of the quaternionic moment–angle manifold.

(1)

If
\dim X=4
, then the principal
K
-bundle is determined by the tuple

\bigl(c_{2}(\xi_{1}),\dots,c_{2}(\xi_{r})\bigr)\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})^{r}.

(2)

In general, the classes
c_{2}(\xi_{k})\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})
are primary characteristic classes of the kernel bundle, but they do not classify it completely in higher dimensions.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition
4.26
.
∎

We close this section with some remarks and questions on the quaternionic case.

Remark 4.28

(Relation with Euler classes)
.

The degree–
4
classes considered here should not be confused with the Euler class of the orbit map in the sense of
[
3
]
. The latter measures the obstruction to the existence of a global section for the orbit projection and is used to compare a quoric manifold with its canonical model. By contrast, the classes
c_{2}(\xi_{k})
arise only after passing to the free kernel bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}^{\mathbb{H}}\longrightarrow X

and classify this principal bundle. Thus the Euler class of the orbit map and the degree–
4
characteristic classes of the kernel bundle play different roles in the rigidity argument.

Remark 4.29
.

In contrast with the complex case, we do not claim here a matrix formula for the classes
c_{2}(\xi_{k})
in terms of a quaternionic analogue of the characteristic matrix. For the purposes of the present paper this is not needed: the classification Theorem
4.27
for principal
(S^{3})^{m-n}
-bundles shows that the tuple of degree–
4
characteristic classes already determines the kernel bundle, but such a formula is an interesting open combinatorial problem.

Remark 4.30
.

In the quaternionic case the reduction to the quoric quotient applies in all dimensions. When the quoric base has dimension
4
, principal
(S^{3})^{r}
–bundles are determined by their primary degree–
4
characteristic classes, so the reduction yields a full topological rigidity theorem. In higher dimensions, however, quaternionic line bundles are not classified in general by
c_{2}
alone, since
BS^{3}
is not an Eilenberg–MacLane space. Thus the classes computed here should be viewed as primary invariants of the kernel bundle, and a complete lifting theorem requires additional homotopy-theoretic or secondary invariants.

Remark 4.31
.

The case in which the quoric base is the quaternionic projective space
X=\mathbb{HP}^{n}
is closely related to the classical theory of self-maps of
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
. In this situation the kernel bundle

(S^{3})^{r}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta^{n}}^{\mathbb{H}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{HP}^{n}

is determined by maps
\mathbb{HP}^{n}\to BS^{3}
, which classify quaternionic line bundles. Since
\mathbb{HP}^{\infty}
is a model for
BS^{3}
, self-maps of
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
induce operations on these bundles via pullback. The possible degrees of such self-maps were studied by Feder and Gitler, who showed that not every integer can occur as the degree of a map
\mathbb{HP}^{n}\to\mathbb{HP}^{n}
. These restrictions reflect higher homotopy obstructions in the classification of quaternionic line bundles. Thus the special case
X=\mathbb{HP}^{n}
provides a natural connection between the kernel bundle construction considered in this paper and the Feder–Gitler problem (for details see
[
17
,
8
,
14
]
).

Remark 4.32
.

It would be very interesting to determine whether principal
(S^{3})^{r}
-bundles over quoric quotients of dimension
>4
admit a classification in terms of additional explicit invariants beyond the primary classes
c_{2}(\xi_{k})
. Such a classification would likely be the missing ingredient for extending the topological rigidity theorem for quaternionic moment–angle manifolds to higher-dimensional quoric bases.

5
Topological classification of moment–angle manifolds

In this section we combine the results of the previous sections to obtain topological rigidity statements for complex and quaternionic moment–angle manifolds. The general strategy is the same in both cases. A moment–angle manifold admits a free kernel action whose quotient is a quasitoric or quoric manifold. An equivariant homotopy equivalence between moment–angle manifolds therefore descends to an equivariant homotopy equivalence between the corresponding quotient manifolds. The rigidity theorems for quasitoric and quoric manifolds then identify the quotient spaces up to equivariant homeomorphism. The remaining step is to compare the associated principal kernel bundles over the common base.

In the complex case, the kernel bundle is a principal
(S^{1})^{m-n}
–bundle, and Section 4 shows that it is classified by its first Chern classes in degree
2
. In the quaternionic case, the kernel bundle is a principal
(S^{3})^{m-n}
–bundle. Its primary characteristic classes lie in degree
4
, but in contrast with the complex case these classes do not classify the bundle completely in arbitrary dimensions. This forces a distinction between the low-dimensional quaternionic case and the general one.

5.1
Rigidity in the complex case

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
denote the complex moment–angle manifold associated to a simple polytope
P
with
m
facets. The canonical action of
T^{m}
on

\mathcal{Z}_{P}
contains a subtorus

K\cong(S^{1})^{m-n},

and the quotient is the quasitoric manifold
M=M^{2n}
. Thus there is a principal bundle

K\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}\longrightarrow M.

Theorem 5.1

(Topological rigidity in the complex case)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
be a complex moment–angle manifold and let
Z
be a locally linear
T^{m}
–manifold equipped with a
T^{m}
–equivariant homotopy equivalence

f:Z\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{P}.

Then
f
is
T^{m}
–homotopic to a
T^{m}
–homeomorphism. In particular,
Z
is equivariantly homeomorphic to
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
, the kernel subgroup

K\cong(S^{1})^{m-n}

acts freely on
Z
, and the induced map

\bar{f}:Z/K\longrightarrow M

is a
T^{n}
–equivariant homotopy equivalence. By the rigidity theorem for quasitoric manifolds
3.9
, the map
\bar{f}
is
T^{n}
–homotopic to a
T^{n}
–equivariant homeomorphism. Hence we may identify the two quotient spaces.

Now both
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
and
Z
are principal
K
-bundles over the same quasitoric manifold
M
. By Corollary
4.2
, such bundles are classified by

H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})^{m-n}.

By Theorem
4.11
, the kernel bundle of
\mathcal{Z}_{P}
is completely determined by the characteristic matrix
\Lambda
, equivalently by the first Chern classes of its circle factors. The same argument applies to
Z
, and the classes agree because the induced homeomorphism of the base preserves the characteristic submanifolds and hence the facet classes. Therefore the two principal
K
-bundles are isomorphic. It follows that their total spaces are
T^{m}
–equivariantly homeomorphic. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Any locally linear
T^{m}
–manifold that is
T^{m}
–equivariantly homotopy equivalent to a complex moment–angle manifold is itself a complex moment–angle manifold.

Let
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathbb{H}}_{P}

. The quaternionic torus

Lemma

–equivariant homotopy equivalence with

The remaining problem is to compare the principal

Any locally linear

Further discussion.

The equivariant topological rigidity results established in Theorems
5.1
and
5.3
provide a significant extension of the rigidity paradigm to the class of moment–angle manifolds. By reducing the classification to a combination of base-space rigidity and bundle-theoretic invariants, we have shown that the homotopy type of these spaces is remarkably sensitive to their underlying combinatorial and equivariant data. While moment–angle manifolds are generally not aspherical, and thus fall outside the classical scope of the Borel Conjecture, our results demonstrate that they satisfy an analogous rigidity property within the category of locally linear actions. This highlights a fundamental symmetry: just as the Farrell–Jones Conjecture predicts rigidity for manifolds with negative curvature through their fundamental groups, the topology of moment–angle manifolds is constrained by the ”curvatures” of their torus actions, encoded in the characteristic classes of their kernel bundles. This suggests that the interplay between polyhedral products and bundle theory may offer a viable path toward a broader classification of non-aspherical manifolds with rich symmetry groups.

The topological rigidity results established in Theorems
5.1
and
5.3
allow us to classify complex moment–angle manifolds (and quaternionic ones with 4-dimensional quotients) as equivariant
strong Borel manifolds
in the sense of Kreck and Lück (see
[
20
, Problem 0.5]
). Recall that a manifold M is called
strong Borel manifold
if a homotopy equivalence
f\colon N\to M
is homotopic to a homeomorphism.

In
[
20
]
, a manifold is defined to be
Borel
if every homotopy equivalence from a closed manifold is homotopic to a homeomorphism. While the classical Borel Conjecture focuses on aspherical manifolds, Kreck and Lück identified specific classes of non-aspherical manifolds that satisfy this property. Our results demonstrate that, within the category of locally linear equivariant actions, complex moment–angle manifolds are Borel. Specifically, the equivariant homotopy type of these spaces completely determines its equivariant homeomorphism type. In the quaternionic case, this Borel property holds fully when the quoric quotient has dimension 4 (Theorem
5.3
). In higher dimensions, the ”primary” rigidity of Theorem
5.5
suggests that moment–angle manifolds remain ”close” to being Borel, with the potential for full rigidity depending on the vanishing of higher homotopy obstructions in the classification of the associated
S^{3}
-bundles.
