CONSTANT MEAN CURVATURE SURFACES IN THE SUB-LORENTZIAN HEISENBERG GROUP
Samuël Borza, Andrea Pinamonti, Omar Zoghlami

Abstract
We study constant horizontal mean curvature surfaces in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group. We derive the first-variation formula for horizontal area under volume-preserving radial variations and show that smooth isoperimetric candidates have constant horizontal mean curvature away from the characteristic set. We then give a complete classification of smooth boost-symmetric constant mean curvature surfaces: their characteristic sets, causal behaviour, and ambient sub-Lorentzian isometry classes. From this classification, we single out a family of smooth, acausal, boost-symmetric surfaces with nonzero constant mean curvature. Written as a two-sheeted graph over the exterior of a future hyperbola, this family is a natural sub-Lorentzian analogue of the Pansu bubbles and leads us to conjecture that it gives the isoperimetric maximisers in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group.

Introduction

The sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group is the Lorentzian counterpart to the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group. Motion is constrained to the usual horizontal directions, but the horizontal distribution is equipped with a Lorentzian metric instead of a positive definite one. Horizontal vectors may be timelike, spacelike, or null, and admissible curves are horizontal curves with causal velocity.
Compared with sub-Riemannian geometry, sub-Lorentzian geometry is still less developed. It nevertheless fits naturally within the growing framework of synthetic Lorentzian geometry

Constant mean curvature equations are fundamental in geometry and analysis. They arise in isoperimetric problems, where a natural strategy is to search for optimizer candidates among constant mean curvature surfaces, since optimizers have this property. In the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group, the longstanding Pansu conjecture predicts that isoperimetric regions are bounded by special rotationally symmetric surfaces of constant horizontal mean curvature

Constant mean curvature hypersurfaces play an important role in general relativity. Spacelike Cauchy hypersurfaces provide initial data for Einstein’s equations, but this initial data must satisfy the nonlinear Einstein constraint equations. The presence of a Cauchy hypersurface with constant mean curvature, or even better, a spacetime region foliated by constant mean curvature hypersurfaces, greatly simplifies the analysis. 2.1
The sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group

The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}
has been studied extensively when equipped with its natural sub-Riemannian structure; see, for instance,
[
7
,
1
]
. For the purposes of this work, we review the sub-Lorentzian structure of the Heisenberg group, in line with works such as
[
46
,
28
,
26
,
25
,
5
,
6
]
.

The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}
is the Lie group that can be identified with the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the non-Abelian group law

(x,y,z)\ast(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(xy^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y)}}\right),

for all
(x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The identity element is the origin
e=(0,0,0)
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, while
(x,y,z)^{-1}=(-x,-y,-z)
. The left translation by
p\in\mathbb{H}
is the automorphism
L_{p}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
given by
L_{p}(q)=p\ast q
, and a vector field
V
on
\mathbb{H}
is said to be
left-invariant
if
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{q}L_{p}[V(q)]=V(p\ast q)
for all
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
. A left-invariant vector field is uniquely determined by its value at the group identity element, and those that coincide with
\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\partial_{z}
at
e
, respectively, are

X=\partial_{x}-\frac{y}{2}\partial_{z},\quad Y=\partial_{y}+\frac{x}{2}\partial_{z},\quad\text{and}\quad Z=\partial_{z}.

The sub-bundle
\Delta
of the tangent bundle
\T(\mathbb{H})
of
\mathbb{H}
, defined by

\Delta\coloneqq\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X,Y}}\right\}\subseteq\T(\mathbb{H}),

is referred to as the
horizontal distribution
of
\mathbb{H}
and, at any point
p\in\mathbb{H}
, vectors
v\in\Delta_{p}
are called
horizontal vectors
.
Importantly, the distribution
\Delta
is
not
involutive; indeed, we have
[X,Y]=\partial_{z}=Z\notin\Delta
. A vector field
V
on
\mathbb{H}
is called
horizontal
if it is a smooth section of
\Delta
. A curve
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{H}
is said to be
horizontal
if
\gamma
is absolutely continuous and there exist
u,v\in L^{\infty}(I,\mathbb{R})
such that

\dot{\gamma}(t)=u(t)X(\gamma(t))+v(t)Y(\gamma(t)),\qquad\text{for almost every }t\in I.

(1)

For any
p\in\mathbb{H}
and any horizontal curve
\gamma
, the translated curve
p\ast\gamma\coloneqq L_{p}\circ\gamma
is also horizontal. Note that a curve
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))
is horizontal if and only if

\dot{z}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(x(t)\dot{y}(t)-y(t)\dot{x}(t)\bigr),\qquad\text{for almost every }t\in I.

(2)

The sub-Lorentzian structure on
\mathbb{H}
is induced by considering the Lorentzian metric
g
on
\Delta
uniquely determined by the conditions

g(X,X)=-1,\quad g(X,Y)=0,\quad g(Y,Y)=1.

The structure
(\mathbb{H},\Delta,g)
, which is preserved under left translations, is what we call the
sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group
. For any
p\in\mathbb{H}
, we say that a horizontal vector
v\in\Delta_{p}
is

\begin{cases}\text{causal}\\
\text{timelike}\\
\text{null}\\
\text{spacelike}\end{cases}\quad\text{if}\quad g_{p}(v,v)\;\begin{cases}\leq 0\text{ and }v\neq 0\\
<0\\
=0\text{ and }v\neq 0\\
>0\text{ or }v=0\end{cases}.

A causal vector is said to be
future-directed
if
g_{p}(v,X_{p})<0
. Accordingly, we say that a horizontal curve
\gamma
is
causal
(resp.
timelike
,
null
,
future-directed
) if the horizontal vector
\dot{\gamma}(t)
is
causal
(resp.
timelike
,
null
,
future-directed
) for almost every
t
. The
horizontal gradient
of
f
is defined by the condition

g\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}f,V\bigr)=V(f)\qquad\text{for every }V\in\Gamma(\Delta).

For any two points
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
, we say that
p

causally precedes

q
, denoted by
p\leq q
, if
p=q
or there exists a future-directed causal curve
\gamma
joining
p
to
q
. Analogously, we say that
p

chronologically precedes

q
, denoted by
p\ll q
, if there exists a future-directed timelike curve joining
p
to
q
. The
causal
and
chronological futures
of
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}
are defined, respectively, by

J^{+}(A)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{H}\mid\exists x\in A,\ x\leq y\},\quad\text{and}\quad I^{+}(A)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{H}\mid\exists x\in A,\ x\ll y\}.

(3)

Similarly, one can define the causal and chronological pasts
J^{-}(A)
and
I^{-}(A)
. The
causal
and
chronological diamonds
of two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{H}
are given by

J(A,B)\coloneqq J^{+}(A)\cap J^{-}(B),\quad\text{and}\quad I(A,B)\coloneqq I^{+}(A)\cap I^{-}(B).

(4)

By left-invariance, we have
J^{+}(p)=p\ast J^{+}(e)
and
I^{+}(p)=p\ast I^{+}(e)
, and similarly for the pasts. Moreover, it is known that

J^{+}(e)=\{x\geq 0\}\cap\{-x^{2}+y^{2}+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\leq 0\},\quad I^{+}(e)=\{x>0\}\cap\{-x^{2}+y^{2}+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert<0\}.

(5)

The
sub-Lorentzian length
of a future-directed causal curve
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{H}
is given by

L(\gamma)\coloneqq\int_{I}\sqrt{-g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The
time-separation function

\tau
between two points
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
is then defined by

\tau(p,q)\coloneqq\sup\{L(\gamma)\mid\gamma\ \text{is future-directed causal and joins $p$ to $q$}\}\quad\text{if }p\leq q,

and
\tau(p,q)\coloneqq 0
if
p
does not causally precede
q
. It is easy to show that the time-separation function
\tau
satisfies the
reverse triangle inequality
, that is,

\tau(q_{1},q_{3})\geq\tau(q_{1},q_{2})+\tau(q_{2},q_{3})\qquad\text{if }q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq q_{3}\text{ in }\mathbb{H}.

A future-directed causal curve
\gamma
from
p
to
q
is a
maximizing geodesic
if
L(\gamma)=\tau(p,q)
. The
exponential map
from

of the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group is given by

\mathrm{exp}_{e}(u,v,w)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{v(\cosh(w)-1)+u\sinh(w)}{w}\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{v\sinh(w)+u(\cosh(w)-1)}{w}\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sinh(w)-w}{w^{2}}\end{pmatrix}

(6)

for
u>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v}}\right\rvert
and
w\neq 0
. It is extended smoothly to
\mathrm{exp}_{e}(u,v,w)=(u,v,0)
when

. The exponential map describes all constant speed timelike geodesics
\gamma(t)
from the origin, i.e.
\gamma(t)=\mathrm{exp}_{e}(tu,tv,tw)

, see

. By left translation, all other timelike geodesics can be recovered and the exponential map

from any

The map
\tau(p,\cdot)\coloneq q\mapsto\tau(p,q)
is analytic on
\{p\ll q\}
, see

, and satisfies the eikonal equation

g\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot)\bigr)=-1,

(7)

and we also have

\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda)=-\tau\bigl(p,\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda)\bigr)\,\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau\bigl(p,\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda)\bigr).

The following theorem describes the structure of smooth isometries of
\mathbb{H}
, that is, diffeomorphisms
F
for which, for every
p\in\mathbb{H}
, it holds
d_{p}F(\Delta_{p})=\Delta_{F(p)}
and
F^{*}g=g
.

Theorem 2.1
.

A map
F\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is a smooth isometry if and only if

F(x,y,z)\coloneqq q\ast\begin{pNiceArray}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}

for some
q\in\mathbb{H}
and some
\Lambda\in O(1,1)
.

Proof.

The “if” part of the statement is a straightforward computation. To prove the converse implication, by replacing
F
with
F(0)^{-1}\ast F
it is enough to assume that
F(0)=0
and prove that, in this case,
F
is of the above form with
q=0
.
We consider the
1
-forms
X^{\flat},Y^{\flat}
on
\Delta
, dual to
X
and
Y
via the metric
g
, that is, for any
V\in\Delta_{p}
,

X^{\flat}(V)\coloneqq g(X,V),\quad Y^{\flat}(V)\coloneqq g(Y,V).

One immediately sees that
X^{\flat}=-dx_{|\Delta}
and
Y^{\flat}=dy_{|\Delta}
. In particular, it holds that

X^{\flat}\wedge Y^{\flat}=(-dx\wedge dy)_{|\Delta}.

Since
F
is an isometry, the map
d_{p}F:\Delta_{p}\to\Delta_{F(p)}
is a linear isometry between two-dimensional Lorentzian vector spaces. With respect to the bases
\{X_{p},Y_{p}\}
and
\{X_{F(p)},Y_{F(p)}\}
, we denote its matrix by
\Lambda_{p}
so that

F^{*}(X_{F(p)}^{\flat}\wedge Y^{\flat}_{F(p)})=\det(\Lambda_{p})X^{\flat}_{p}\wedge Y^{\flat}_{p}=\det(\Lambda_{p})(-dx\wedge dy)_{|\Delta}.

(9)

Since
X,Y
are pseudo-orthonormal and
F
is an isometry, we have
\Lambda_{p}\in O(1,1)
. In particular,
\varepsilon:=\det(\Lambda_{p})\in\{-1,1\}
, and, by the continuity of
p\mapsto\det(\Lambda_{p})
and the connectedness of
\mathbb{H}
, this sign is constant.

We now consider the contact form
\alpha=dz-\frac{1}{2}(xdy-ydx)
on
\mathbb{H}
. Since
\Delta=\ker(\alpha)
and
F
preserves the horizontal distribution, we must have

F^{*}\alpha=\lambda\alpha

for some
\lambda\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})
. Taking the exterior derivative of both sides, we obtain

F^{*}d\alpha=d\lambda\wedge\alpha+\lambda d\alpha,\quad\text{ and thus }\quad F^{*}(d\alpha_{|\Delta})=\lambda d\alpha_{|\Delta}.

As
d\alpha=-dx\wedge dy
, from (
9
) we obtain

\varepsilon(X^{\flat}\wedge Y^{\flat})=\lambda d\alpha_{|\Delta}=\lambda(-dx\wedge dy)_{|\Delta}=\lambda(X^{\flat}\wedge Y^{\flat}),

which yields
\lambda\equiv\varepsilon
. In particular,
d\lambda=0
, and therefore the identity
F^{*}d\alpha=\varepsilon d\alpha
holds everywhere. If we decompose
dF[Z]=aX+bY+cZ
for some functions
a,b,c\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})
, then, for any horizontal vector field
U
, we have

\begin{split}0&=-\varepsilon(dx\wedge dy)(Z,U)=\varepsilon d\alpha(Z,U)=F^{*}(d\alpha)(Z,U)=d\alpha(aX+bY+cZ,dF[U])\\
&=d\alpha(aX+bY,dF[U])=(X^{\flat}\wedge Y^{\flat})(aX+bY,dF[U]).\end{split}

Since
dF[U]
ranges over the horizontal distribution and
X^{\flat}\wedge Y^{\flat}
is non-degenerate on
\Delta
, this forces
aX+bY=0
. The identites
\alpha(Z)=1
and
F^{*}\alpha=\varepsilon\alpha
imply

c=\alpha(cZ)=(F^{*}\alpha)(Z)=\varepsilon\alpha(Z)=\varepsilon,

and therefore
dF[Z]=\varepsilon Z
. Writing
F=(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})
, this means that
\partial_{z}F_{1}=\partial_{z}F_{2}=0
.

Define a map on the
(x,y)
-plane by
f(x,y)\coloneqq\pi(F(x,y,0))
, where
\pi(x,y,z)=(x,y)
. Thus,
f
is the map given by the first two components of
F
, restricted to the plane
z=0
. By what we have just proved,
\pi(F(x,y,z))=\pi(F(x,y,0))
for all
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{H}
. For any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, setting
c\coloneqq\frac{ay-bx}{2}
we have, at the point
(x,y,0)
,

\displaystyle d\pi\circ dF(a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y})

\displaystyle=d\pi\circ dF(aX+bY+cZ)=d\pi\bigl(\Lambda_{(x,y,0)}(aX+bY)+\varepsilon cZ\bigr)

\displaystyle=d\pi\bigl(\Lambda_{(x,y,0)}(aX+bY)\bigr)=\Lambda_{(x,y,0)}(a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y}),

where we used
dF[Z]=\varepsilon Z
and the fact that
dF
acts as an isometry on the horizontal distribution. Hence
df_{(x,y)}=\Lambda_{(x,y,0)}\in O(1,1)
. At this stage,
\Lambda_{(x,y,0)}
could, in principle, depend on
(x,y)
. However, this cannot actually happen, because a smooth map of the Minkowski plane whose differential lies everywhere in
O(1,1)
must be affine. Therefore
\Lambda\coloneqq\Lambda_{(x,y,0)}
is constant. Since
F(0)=0
, we have
f(0,0)=\pi(F(0,0,0))=(0,0)
, and
f(x,y)=\pi(F(x,y,z))=\Lambda(x,y)
.

From the previous steps, the first two components of
F
are given by
F_{1}=\Lambda_{11}x+\Lambda_{12}y
and
F_{2}=\Lambda_{21}x+\Lambda_{22}y
, while
\partial_{z}F_{3}=\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon=\det(\Lambda)
. Writing explicitly
F^{*}\alpha=\varepsilon\alpha
and rearranging we get

\displaystyle dF_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(F_{1}\,dF_{2}-F_{2}\,dF_{1})+\varepsilon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dz-\frac{1}{2}(x\,dy-y\,dx)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{2}(x\,dy-y\,dx)+\varepsilon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dz-\frac{1}{2}(x\,dy-y\,dx)}}\right)=\varepsilon dz.

Hence
\partial_{x}F_{3}=\partial_{y}F_{3}=0
and
\partial_{z}F_{3}=\varepsilon
. Since
F(0)=0
, we conclude that
F_{3}(x,y,z)=\varepsilon z=\det(\Lambda)z
, which completes the proof.
∎

In view of the previous proposition, an isometry is completely characterised by a point
p\in\mathbb{H}
, which we call the
translation part
of the isometry, and an element
\Lambda\in O(1,1)
, which we call the
Lorentz part
of the isometry.

Definition 2.2
.

An isometry of the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group is called a

(i)

time-preserving boost
if
\Lambda\in O^{+}(1,1)
, that is,
\det(\Lambda)=1
and
\Lambda_{11}>0
;

(ii)

time-inverting boost
if
\det(\Lambda)=1
and
\Lambda_{11}<0
;

(iii)

time-preserving rotation
if
\det(\Lambda)=-1
and
\Lambda_{11}>0
;

(iv)

time-inverting rotation
if
\det(\Lambda)=-1
and
\Lambda_{11}<0
.

In the case of a time-preserving boost with
p=0
, we simply call it a
boost
. In the case of a time-preserving rotation with
p=0
, we call it a
timed rotation
.

We use the following general component decomposition of
O(1,1)
repeatedly. Let
A(x,y,z)=(-x,y,-z)
and
T(x,y,z)=(x,-y,-z)
, and note that their Lorentz parts on the
(x,y)
-plane are

A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad T_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

In general, a time-preserving boost has Lorentz part

B_{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh u&\sinh u\\
\sinh u&\cosh u\end{pmatrix},

for some
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, the four components of
O(1,1)
are parametrized by

B_{u},\qquad A_{0}B_{u},\qquad T_{0}B_{u},\qquad A_{0}T_{0}B_{u},

as
u\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
B_{u}
is a time-preserving boost,
T_{0}B_{u}
a time-preserving rotation,
A_{0}B_{u}
a time-inverting rotation, and
A_{0}T_{0}B_{u}
a time-inverting boost.

2.2
Horizontal geometry of hypersurfaces

We review the geometry of hypersurfaces in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group, including the notion of sub-Lorentzian mean curvature.

Definition 2.3

(Horizontal tangent bundle)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{1}
-surface. At each point
p\in\Sigma
, we define
\mathrm{H}_{p}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\Delta_{p}\cap\T_{p}(\Sigma)
. The
characteristic set
of
\Sigma
is defined as

\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\{p\in\Sigma\ \colon\ \mathrm{H}_{p}(\Sigma)=\T_{p}(\Sigma)\}.

This set is closed in
\Sigma
and, if
\Sigma
is
C^{1,1}
, is negligible by
[
43
, Proposition 13]
. On the manifold
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, we define the vector bundle

\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{p\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\mathrm{H}_{p}(\Sigma),

called the
horizontal tangent bundle
of
\Sigma
. It naturally carries the structure of a rank-one vector bundle over
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, since, for every
p\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, we have

\dim(\mathrm{H}_{p}(\Sigma))=\dim(\Delta_{p})+\dim(\T_{p}(\Sigma))-\dim(\Delta_{p}\oplus\T_{p}(\Sigma))=2+2-3=1.

The sub-Lorentzian metric
g
restricts naturally to a quadratic form on the horizontal tangent bundle
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
over
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that an embedded
C^{1}
-surface
\Sigma
is

(i)

spacelike
if the restriction of
g
to
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
is positive definite;

(ii)

timelike
if the restriction of
g
to
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
is non-degenerate with a negative eigenvalue;

(iii)

null
or
lightlike
if the restriction of
g
to
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
is degenerate.

Notice that
\mathrm{H}_{p}(\Sigma)
is a hyperplane in
\Delta_{p}
for every

p\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. In the first two cases, taking its
g
-orthogonal complement
inside
\Delta_{p}
gives a non-degenerate line. If
\Sigma
is spacelike (resp. timelike), this orthogonal line is timelike (resp. spacelike), and the fixed time orientation (resp. a choice of orientation) selects its future-directed unit vector (resp. its unit vector).

Definition 2.5

(Horizontal unit normal)
.

The
horizontal unit normal
of
\Sigma
is the vector field
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}\in C^{0}\bigl(\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma),\Delta|_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\bigr)
uniquely determined by

g_{p}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p),v)=0\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathrm{H}_{p}(\Sigma),\qquad g_{p}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p),\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p))=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if }\Sigma\text{ is spacelike},\\
1,&\text{if }\Sigma\text{ is timelike},\end{cases}

together with the sign convention that
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
is future-directed when
\Sigma
is spacelike and agrees with a chosen orientation of
\Sigma
when it is timelike. If one wants to regard
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
as defined on all of
\Sigma
, we set
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p)=0
for
p\in\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.

A canonical horizontal connection determined by the left-invariant horizontal frame
X,Y
can then be introduced.

Definition 2.6

(Horizontal connection)
.

Let
\Delta=\operatorname{span}\{X,Y\}
. For a horizontal vector field

V=aX+bY
and any smooth vector field
U
, define

\nabla_{U}V:=U(a)X+U(b)Y.

In other words,
\nabla
is the unique connection on
\Delta
for which the left-invariant horizontal frame
X,Y
is parallel. In particular,
\nabla
is metric compatible:

U(g(V,W))=g(\nabla_{U}V,W)+g(V,\nabla_{U}W)

for all horizontal vector fields
V,W
.

A horizontal vector field along
\Sigma
is a smooth section of
\Delta|_{\Sigma}
. On
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, since
\Delta|_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}=\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)\oplus\operatorname{span}\{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}\}
, every horizontal vector field
V
along
\Sigma
decomposes uniquely into its horizontal tangential and horizontal normal components, which we denote by
V^{\parallel}
and
V^{\perp}
, respectively.

Definition 2.7

(Horizontal tangent connection and second fundamental form)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{1}
spacelike or timelike surface, and
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
denote its horizontal unit normal. We define the
horizontal tangent connection
by

\nabla^{\Sigma}_{U}V\coloneqq\bigl(\nabla_{U}V\bigr)^{\parallel}

(10)

for all horizontal vector field
U
and
V
along
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. The
scalar horizontal second fundamental form
is the bilinear form

h_{\Sigma}(U,V)\coloneqq g(\nabla_{U}V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}}).

Remark 2.8
.

If
V\in\Gamma(\mathrm{H}(\Sigma))
is regarded as a vector field only along
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, then
\nabla_{U}V
in
Equation

10
is computed by choosing any local horizontal extension of
V
. The value along
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
is independent of the chosen extension, because
U\in\Gamma(\mathrm{H}(\Sigma))
is tangent to
\Sigma
.

Since
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
has rank one, we may locally choose a generator
T\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathrm{H}(\Sigma))
normalized with respect to
g
, so that
g(T,T)=1
in the spacelike case and
g(T,T)=-1
in the timelike case. If
T
be a local non-vanishing generator of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
, then any
U,V\in\Gamma(\mathrm{H}(\Sigma))
can be written as
U=uT
and
V=vT
. First notice that
h_{\Sigma}
is
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
-bilinear. For instance,

h_{\Sigma}(U,fV)=g(\nabla_{U}(fV),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=U(f)g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})+fh_{\Sigma}(U,V)=fh_{\Sigma}(U,V),

since
V\in\Gamma(\mathrm{H}(\Sigma))
and
g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=0
. Linearity in the first slot follows from
\nabla_{fU}V=f\nabla_{U}V
. Hence

h_{\Sigma}(U,V)=h_{\Sigma}(uT,vT)=uv\,h_{\Sigma}(T,T)=h_{\Sigma}(vT,uT)=h_{\Sigma}(V,U).

Thus the horizontal second fundamental form
h_{\Sigma}
is symmetric.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{1}
spacelike or timelike surface, and
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
denote its horizontal unit normal. The trace of scalar horizontal second fundamental form with respect to the restriction of
g
to
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
is the
horizontal mean curvature

H\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}_{g|_{\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)}}h_{\Sigma}.

Equivalently, if
\Sigma
is
C^{2}
and
T
is a local
g
-unit generator of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
, then

H=g(T,T)\,g(\nabla_{T}T,\nu_{\mathbb{H}}).

The following proposition computes
H
in terms of the integral curves of
T
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{2}
spacelike or timelike surface, and work on
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. Choose a local generator
T
of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
normalized with respect to
g
. For
p\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, denote by
\gamma
the integral curve of
T
with
\gamma(0)=p
, and set
\sigma\coloneqq\pi\circ\gamma
, where
\pi(x,y,z)=(x,y)
. Then
H(p)
is the signed curvature of
\sigma
in the Minkowski plane
(\mathbb{R}^{2},-dx^{2}+dy^{2})
, namely

H(p)=g(T,T)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\ddot{\sigma}(0),n(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{M}},

where
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}=n_{1}X+n_{2}Y
and
n=(n_{1},n_{2})
is its projection to the Minkowski plane.

Proof.

Write
T=fX+hY
. Since
X
and
Y
project respectively to
\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{y}
, and since
\dot{\gamma}=T_{\gamma}
, we have

\dot{\sigma}=(f\circ\gamma,h\circ\gamma),\qquad\ddot{\sigma}(0)=(T(f)_{p},T(h)_{p}).

The definition of the horizontal connection gives

\nabla_{T}T=T(f)X+T(h)Y.

For some
\eta\in\{-1,1\}
fixed by the chosen convention, the horizontal unit normal is

\nu_{\mathbb{H}}=\eta(hX+fY),

and its projection to the Minkowski plane is
n=\eta(h,f)
, which coincides with the normal to
\sigma
in Minkowski plane. Therefore

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\ddot{\sigma}(0),n(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{M}}=\eta\bigl(-T(f)_{p}h(p)+T(h)_{p}f(p)\bigr)=g(\nabla_{T}T,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})_{p}.

Since
H=g(T,T)\,g(\nabla_{T}T,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})
, the claim follows.
∎

2.3
The area functional and first variation formula

The next step is to introduce a surface measure. To do so, a reference volume measure on the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group must first be fixed. There are several natural choices: the three-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{3}
, which is a Haar measure; the Hausdorff measure induced by the sub-Riemannian distance; and the Lorentzian Hausdorff measure introduced for Lorentzian length spaces in
[
35
]
. In a previous work, it was shown that these measures coincide in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group; see
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
. We therefore take as reference volume form the standard Haar volume
\Omega\coloneqq dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
.

Definition 2.11

(Induced area)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{1}
spacelike or timelike surface, equipped with a choice of horizontal unit normal
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
on
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. We define the induced horizontal area form on
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
by

d\sigma\coloneqq\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}(\Omega)_{|\Sigma}.

If
\Sigma
is such that
\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
is negligible, for instance if
\Sigma
is
C^{1,1}
, we define its horizontal area by

A(\Sigma)\coloneqq\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}d\sigma.

The
divergence
associated with the volume form
\Omega
is the function
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)
defined by

\mathcal{L}_{W}\Omega=\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)\,\Omega

for every smooth vector field
W
. If
W=aX+bY+cZ
, then, since
X,Y,Z
are divergence-free with respect to
\Omega
, we have
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)=X(a)+Y(b)+Z(c)
. In particular, for a horizontal vector field
W=aX+bY
, we have
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)=X(a)+Y(b)
. Equivalently, if
\{E_{0},E_{1}\}
is a local horizontal pseudo-orthonormal frame with
g(E_{0},E_{0})=-1
and
g(E_{1},E_{1})=1
, then for every horizontal vector field
W
,

\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)=-g(\nabla_{E_{0}}W,E_{0})+g(\nabla_{E_{1}}W,E_{1}).

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{2}
spacelike or timelike surface, and let
p\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. If
\widetilde{\nu}
is a local horizontal extension of
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
near
p
such that
g(\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{\nu})
is constant and equal to
g(\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p),\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p))
, then
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\widetilde{\nu})(p)
is independent of the choice of such an extension.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\nu}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{2}
be two such extensions and set
W\coloneqq\widetilde{\nu}_{1}-\widetilde{\nu}_{2}
, so that
W=0
along
\Sigma
. Write
W=aX+bY
and let
T
be a local
g
-unit generator of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
. Since
a
and
b
vanish on
\Sigma
, their derivatives in the tangent direction
T
and
\nabla_{T}W=T(a)X+T(b)Y=0
at
p
.

The extensions also have the same constant
g
-norm, equal to
g(\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p),\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p))
. Thus differentiating the constant function
g(\widetilde{\nu}_{i},\widetilde{\nu}_{i})
in the direction
\widetilde{\nu}_{i}
gives
g(\nabla_{\widetilde{\nu}_{i}}\widetilde{\nu}_{i},\widetilde{\nu}_{i})=0
. Evaluating at
p
and using
\widetilde{\nu}_{i}(p)=\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p)
, we get
g(\nabla_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\widetilde{\nu}_{i},\nu_{\mathbb{H}})(p)=0
for
i=1,2
, and therefore
g(\nabla_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}W,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})(p)=0
.

The horizontal frame
\{\nu_{\mathbb{H}},T\}
is pseudo-orthonormal up to order. The two trace terms of
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)(p)
in this frame therefore vanish by the previous two observations, so
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(W)(p)=0
, and thus
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\widetilde{\nu}_{1})(p)=\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\widetilde{\nu}_{2})(p)
.
∎

Thus the restriction
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\widetilde{\nu})|_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}
is intrinsically determined by
\Sigma
and its chosen horizontal unit normal; we denote it simply by
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})
.

Proposition 2.13

(Divergence formula for the horizontal mean curvature)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{2}
spacelike or timelike surface, and work on
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. Let
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
be its chosen horizontal unit normal and let
T
be a local
g
-unit generator of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
. Then

H(p)=-\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})(p),\qquad p\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma).

Proof.

Choose a local horizontal extension
\widetilde{\nu}
of
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
with constant
g
-norm equal to
g(\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p),\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(p))
. By
Section

2.3
,
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})
is the restriction of
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\widetilde{\nu})
to
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. Extend
T
locally to a horizontal vector field
\widetilde{T}
such that
g(\widetilde{T},\widetilde{T})=g(T,T)
,
g(\widetilde{T},\widetilde{\nu})=0
, and
\widetilde{T}=T
along
\Sigma
. Using the frame
\{\widetilde{T},\widetilde{\nu}\}
in the divergence formula and then restricting to
\Sigma
gives

\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=g(T,T)g(\nabla_{T}\widetilde{\nu},T)+g(\nu_{\mathbb{H}},\nu_{\mathbb{H}})g(\nabla_{\widetilde{\nu}}\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{\nu})\qquad\text{on }\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma).

The second term is zero because
\widetilde{\nu}
has constant
g
-norm. Moreover, differentiating the identity
g(\widetilde{T},\widetilde{\nu})=0
in the direction
\widetilde{T}
and then restricting to
\Sigma
gives
g(\nabla_{T}T,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})+g(T,\nabla_{T}\widetilde{\nu})=0
. Hence

\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=-g(T,T)g(\nabla_{T}T,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=-H,

because
H=g(T,T)g(\nabla_{T}T,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})
.
∎

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{2}
spacelike surface. A variation of
\Sigma
is a smooth map

\Gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{H}

such that
\Gamma_{0}=\operatorname{id}_{\Sigma}
and, for each
s
sufficiently small,
\Gamma_{s}\coloneqq\Gamma(s,\cdot)
is an embedding. We write
\Sigma_{s}\coloneqq\Gamma_{s}(\Sigma)
, denote by
d\sigma_{s}
the horizontal area form on
\Sigma_{s}
, and denote by
\nu_{s}
the horizontal unit normal along
\Sigma_{s}
. The initial velocity of the variation is the vector field along
\Sigma
defined by

V(x)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}\Gamma(s,x)}}\right|_{s=0}.

We say that the variation has
horizontal initial velocity
if its initial velocity satisfies
V\in\Gamma(\Delta|_{\Sigma})
. The variation is
compactly supported away from the characteristic set
if the support of
V
is compact and contained in
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.

Proposition 2.14

(First variation of horizontal area)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an embedded
C^{2}
spacelike surface, and let
\Gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{H}
be a variation of
\Sigma
with horizontal initial velocity
V
. Assume that
V
is compactly supported in
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. Then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}A(\Sigma_{s})=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}H\,g(\nu_{\mathbb{H}},V)\,d\sigma.

Proof.

Whenever necessary, we choose local extensions of the objects defined along
\Sigma
: the initial velocity
V
of the variation is extended to a vector field near
\Sigma
,
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
is extended to a horizontal vector field with constant
g
-norm, and the local generator
T
of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
is extended to a horizontal vector field. For simplicity, we keep the same symbols
V
,
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
, and
T
for these extensions.

By
Section

2.3
,
A(\Sigma_{s})=\int_{\Sigma_{s}}d\sigma_{s}
, where the area form on
\Sigma_{s}
is
\sigma_{s}=\iota_{\nu_{s}}\Omega|_{\Sigma_{s}}
. Therefore,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}A(\Sigma_{s})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}\int_{\Sigma_{s}}\iota_{\nu_{s}}\Omega.

By the Reynolds Transport Theorem, we have that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}\int_{\Sigma_{s}}\iota_{\nu_{s}}\Omega=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}}}\right|_{s=0}\iota_{\nu_{s}}\Omega+\mathcal{L}_{V}(\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega)}}\right).

Along the variation, we set

\nu_{s}(\Gamma_{s}(x))=\alpha_{s}(x)X_{\Gamma_{s}(x)}+\beta_{s}(x)Y_{\Gamma_{s}(x)},\qquad\dot{\nu}(x)\coloneq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}\alpha_{s}(x)}}\right|_{s=0}X_{x}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}\beta_{s}(x)}}\right|_{s=0}Y_{x}

Then, using that
X
,
Y
, and
\Omega
are fixed,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}}}\right|_{s=0}\iota_{\nu_{s}}\Omega

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}}}\right|_{s=0}\bigl(\alpha_{s}\,\iota_{X}\Omega+\beta_{s}\,\iota_{Y}\Omega\bigr)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}\alpha_{s}}}\right|_{s=0}\iota_{X}\Omega+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}\beta_{s}}}\right|_{s=0}\iota_{Y}\Omega=\iota_{\dot{\nu}}\Omega.

Differentiating the identity
g(\nu_{s},\nu_{s})=-1
at
s=0
gives
g(\dot{\nu},\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=0
. Since
\dot{\nu}
is horizontal and
T\in\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
, we have
\Omega(\dot{\nu},T,W)=0
for every vector field
W
tangent to
\Sigma
, and thus
\iota_{\dot{\nu}}\Omega|_{\Sigma}=0
.

We now simplify the Lie derivative term using the following two identities. First, for every smooth function
f
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}(\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega)

\displaystyle=\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\mathcal{L}_{f\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega+\iota_{[f\nu_{\mathbb{H}},\nu_{\mathbb{H}}]}\Omega=f\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}(\mathcal{L}_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega)+\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}(df\wedge\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega)+f\iota_{[\nu_{\mathbb{H}},\nu_{\mathbb{H}}]}\Omega-\nu_{\mathbb{H}}[f]\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega

\displaystyle=f\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega+\nu_{\mathbb{H}}[f]\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega-\nu_{\mathbb{H}}[f]\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega=f\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega.

Because
fT
is tangent to
\Sigma
, we must have that
(\mathcal{L}_{fT}(d\sigma))|_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{L}_{fT}^{\Sigma}(d\sigma)
, where
\mathcal{L}^{\Sigma}
the intrinsic Lie derivative of
\Sigma
. Then, Cartan’s formula (on
\Sigma
), gives

\mathcal{L}_{fT}^{\Sigma}d\sigma=d_{\Sigma}\bigl(\iota_{fT}d\sigma\bigr)+\iota_{fT}d_{\Sigma}(d\sigma)=d_{\Sigma}\bigl(\iota_{fT}d\sigma\bigr),

where
d_{\Sigma}
denotes the intrinsic exterior derivative on
\Sigma
, and where we used that on the surface
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
,
d\sigma
is a top-degree
2
-form and therefore its exterior derivative is zero, i.e.
d_{\Sigma}(d\sigma)=0
.

Since
\Sigma
is spacelike, we can write
V=g(V,T)T-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{V}(\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega)\big|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{g(V,T)T-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}d\sigma=\mathcal{L}_{g(V,T)T}d\sigma-\mathcal{L}_{g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}d\sigma

\displaystyle=d\bigl(\iota_{g(V,T)T}d\sigma\bigr)-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=d\bigl(\iota_{g(V,T)T}d\sigma\bigr)+H\,g(\nu_{\mathbb{H}},V)\,d\sigma.

Since
\iota_{g(V,T)T}d\sigma
is compactly supported in
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
, Stokes’ theorem gives

\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}d\bigl(\iota_{g(V,T)T}d\sigma\bigr)=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

2.4
Lorentzian isoperimetric problem and volume-preserving variations

In a metric measure space
(X,\mathsf{d},\mathfrak{m})
, the
isoperimetric problem

consists in determining, for each prescribed volume
v>0
, the sets with minimal
surface area among all sets of measure
v
. More precisely, one studies minimisers of

\inf\{A(E)\mid E\subseteq X\text{ measurable, }\mathfrak{m}(E)=v\},

(11)

where
A(E)
denotes an appropriate notion of surface area, such as the
De Giorgi perimeter in the abstract sense of
[
2
]
. In the Lorentzian setting, the isoperimetric problem is reformulated by replacing the infimum in
Equation

11
with a supremum and the perimeter with a suitable timelike Lorentzian perimeter, and by restricting the set of competitors to achronal hypersurfaces whose cone from a fixed point, the
pole
, has prescribed volume. Here, a subset
A
of a Lorentzian space
is called
achronal
if no two points of
A
are chronologically related;
that is, for every
p,q\in A
, one has
p\not\ll q
.

In the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group, the formulation is as follows.
For
p\in\mathbb{H}
and
q\in I^{+}(p)
, let
\gamma_{p,q}
denote the
future-directed maximizing geodesic from
p
to
q
, parametrized on

[0,1]
with constant speed. Given a set
\Sigma\subseteq I^{+}(p)
, we define the

cone over
\Sigma
with vertex
p
by

C(\Sigma,p)\coloneqq\bigcup_{q\in\Sigma}\gamma_{p,q}([0,1]).

When
p=e
, we simply write
C(\Sigma)
instead of
C(\Sigma,e)
.
By left-invariance, the pole can always be fixed at the identity
e
, and one seeks
to find the maximisers of

\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{A(\Sigma)\mid\text{$C^{2}$ spacelike compact hypersurface }\Sigma\subseteq I^{+}(e)\subseteq\mathbb{H},\mathcal{L}^{3}(C(\Sigma))=v}}\right\}.

(12)

A fully general formulation would not require the surface
\Sigma
to be regular, and would instead use a non-smooth notion of surface area, such as the
timelike Minkowski content
studied in
[
8
]
. Furthermore, it is not difficult to see that
Equation

12
is infinite unless one imposes an additional constraint on the class of competitors. At present, two such constraints are studied in the literature. In
[
3
]
, the competitor is required to have the “solid angle”, or aperture, of its cone bounded by a fixed constant. In other words, the cone may have prescribed volume, but it cannot spread over arbitrarily many future directions. In
[
8
]
, the authors instead imposes a lower bound
\inf_{q\in\Sigma}\tau(p,q)\geq\tau_{0}>0
on the time separation from the pole
p
. Thus, the cone may open in many directions, but the surface cannot dip arbitrarily close to the pole.

The main point is that, once the additional constraint has been fixed, smooth maximisers can be studied through admissible volume-preserving variations, which are a focus of this work. We start by defining variations that are adapted to the conical nature of the problem.

Definition 2.15

(Radial variation)
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{H}
and let
\Sigma\subseteq I^{+}(p)
be a
C^{1}
hypersurface, possibly with boundary. For every
x\in\Sigma
and given
\lambda_{x}:=(\mathrm{exp}_{p})^{-1}(x)
, the curve
\gamma_{x}(t):=\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x})
is the unique maximising geodesic parametrised by constant speed on
[0,1]
that satisfies

\gamma_{x}(0)=p
and
\gamma_{x}(1)=x
. A
radial variation
of
\Sigma
is a family of the form

\Gamma_{s}(x):=\mathrm{exp}_{p}(r_{s}(x)\lambda_{x}),

where
r:\mathopen{(}-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\mathclose{)}\times\Sigma\to(0,\infty)
is smooth in
(s,x)
and satisfies

r_{0}\equiv 1
. We write
\Sigma_{s}\coloneqq\Gamma_{s}(\Sigma)
and
\dot{r}(x)\coloneqq\partial_{s}r_{s}(x)|_{s=0}
.
The
cone
over
\Sigma_{s}
is

C_{s}\coloneqq\{\mathrm{exp}_{p}(\rho\lambda_{x})\mid x\in\Sigma,\ 0\leq\rho\leq r_{s}(x)\}=F_{s}(\Sigma\times[0,1]),

where we set
F_{s}:\Sigma\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{H}:(x,t)\mapsto\mathrm{exp}_{p}(tr_{s}(x)\lambda_{x})
. The
cone velocity
is the vector field defined by
X_{0}(\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x})):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}}}\right|_{s=0}\mathrm{exp}_{p}(tr_{s}(x)\lambda_{x})
for every
\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x})\in C_{0}
.

We say the radial variation is
compactly supported
if
\operatorname{supp}\dot{r}
is compactly contained in the interior of
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.

The next lemma computes the infinitesimal velocity produced by a radial variation.

Lemma 2.16

(Velocity of a radial cone variation)
.

Let
\Gamma_{s}
be a radial variation. At every point
\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x})\in C_{0}
of the initial cone, the
cone velocity
is given by

X_{0}(\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x}))=-t\,\dot{r}(x)\,\tau(p,x)\,\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot)\bigr)_{\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x})}

(13)

In particular, the initial velocity of the variation
\Sigma_{s}
is

V(x)=X_{0}(x)=-\dot{r}(x)\,\tau(p,x)\,\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot)\bigr)_{x}.

(14)

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{x}
is fixed when differentiating with respect to
s
, the
chain rule gives

X_{0}(\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x}))=t\,\dot{r}(x)\,\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{exp}_{p}(t\lambda_{x}).

Thus
Equation

13
follows from
Equation

8
. Finally, since

F_{0}(x,1)=x
, the cone velocity restricts on the top surface to the initial
velocity
V
of the variation
\Gamma_{s}
. Hence
X_{0}(x)=V(x)
for every

x\in\Sigma
, and
Equation

14
follows by setting
t=1
in
Equation

13
.
∎

We next study how the volume of the cone changes under a compactly supported radial variation. The formula expresses the first variation entirely as a boundary integral over the moving top surface.

Proposition 2.17

(First variation of the cone volume)
.

Let
\Gamma_{s}
be a compactly supported radial variation of a
C^{2}
spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
. Then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s})=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\dot{r}(x)\tau(p,x)\,g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x).

Proof.

By definition,
\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s})=\int_{C_{s}}\Omega
. Applying the
Reynolds Transport Theorem gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s})=\int_{C_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{X_{0}}\Omega=\int_{\partial C_{0}}\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega,

where we use Cartan’s formula on the last equality

\mathcal{L}_{X_{0}}\Omega=d(\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega)+\iota_{X_{0}}(d\Omega)=d(\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega),

together with Stokes theorem.
Indeed,
\Omega
is the ambient volume form on
\mathbb{H}
, hence a top-degree
form on a
3
-dimensional manifold. Therefore
d\Omega
is a
4
-form, so

d\Omega=0
.

The boundary of the initial cone decomposes as
\partial C_{0}=\Sigma\cup C(\partial\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma=F_{0}(\Sigma\times\{1\})
,
C(\partial\Sigma)=F_{0}(\partial\Sigma\times[0,1])
, and
\{p\}=F_{0}(\Sigma\times\{0\})
. Therefore

\int_{\partial C_{0}}\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega=\int_{\Sigma}\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega+\int_{C(\partial\Sigma)}\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega.

The compact support assumption gives
\dot{r}=0
near
\partial\Sigma
and thus, by
Equation

13
, we have
X_{0}=0
on
C(\partial\Sigma)
and
\int_{C(\partial\Sigma)}\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega=0
.
On
\Sigma=F_{0}(\Sigma\times\{1\})
, we have
X_{0}|_{\Sigma}=V
, where
V
is the
initial velocity of the variation
\Gamma_{s}
. Therefore

\int_{\partial C_{0}}\iota_{X_{0}}\Omega=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\iota_{V}\Omega.

Using
d\sigma=\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega|_{\Sigma}
and writing
V=V^{\parallel}-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
with
V^{\parallel}\in\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
, we obtain

\iota_{V}\Omega|_{\Sigma}=\iota_{-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega|_{\Sigma}=-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,\iota_{\nu_{\mathbb{H}}}\Omega|_{\Sigma}=-g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})d\sigma.

We conclude by
Section

2.4
and
Equation

14
, noting that

\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\iota_{V}\Omega=-\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}g(V,\nu_{\mathbb{H}})d\sigma=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\dot{r}(x)\,\tau(p,x)\,g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x),

and the proof is complete.
∎

Combining the first variation of horizontal area with the first variation of cone volume gives the expected Euler–Lagrange condition for radial volume-constrained critical points. Namely, such surfaces have constant horizontal mean curvature away from the characteristic set.

Proposition 2.18

(Radial volume-preserving stationarity implies CMC)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq I^{+}(p)
be a connected embedded
C^{2}
spacelike surface.
Assume that, for every compactly supported radial variation
\Gamma_{s}
of

\Sigma
whose associated cones satisfy

\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s})=\operatorname{Vol}(C_{0})\qquad\text{for all sufficiently small }s,

one has

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}A(\Sigma_{s})=0.

Then the horizontal mean curvature
H
is constant on

\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is spacelike,
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
is timelike. Moreover,

\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot)
is timelike on
I^{+}(p)
, and therefore

g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})
does not vanish on

\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
. Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma))
and choose the radial variation defined by
r_{s}=1+s\varphi
, so that
\dot{r}=\varphi
.
Section

2.4

and
Section

2.3
give

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}A(\Sigma_{s})=-\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}H(x)\varphi(x)\,\tau(p,x)\,g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x).

(15)

On the other hand,
Section

2.4
gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s})=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\varphi(x)\,\tau(p,x)\,g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x).

(16)

We wish to argue that, if
Equation

16
is zero, then
Equation

15
vanishes as well. This would imply, by the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations, that
H
must be constant on
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.

Choose

\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma))
such that

\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\eta(x)\tau(p,x)g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x)\neq 0.

(17)

This is always possible because
\tau(p,x)g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})

is continuous and nowhere vanishing on
\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)
.
For small
(s,a)
, consider the radial variations with
r_{s,a}=1+s\varphi+a\eta
, and note that
\partial_{a}\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s,a})|_{(0,0)}\neq 0
because of
Equation

17
.
By the implicit function theorem, there is a smooth function

a=a(s)
, with
a(0)=0
, such that
\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s,a(s)})=\operatorname{Vol}(C_{0})
for all small
s
. If
Equation

16
is zero, then
a^{\prime}(0)=0
because, by the chain rule
0=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{s}}}\right|_{(0,0)}\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s,a})+a^{\prime}(0)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\partial_{a}}}\right|_{(0,0)}\operatorname{Vol}(C_{s,a})
and therefore

a^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\varphi(x)\tau(p,x)g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x)}{\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{S}(\Sigma)}\eta(x)\tau(p,x)g(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(p,\cdot),\nu_{\mathbb{H}})\,d\sigma(x)}.

The denominator is not zero because of
Equation

17
while the numerator is zero because we assume that
Equation

16
is zero. This implies that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}r_{s,a(s)}(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}\bigl(1+s\varphi(x)+a(s)\eta(x)\bigr)=\varphi(x)+a^{\prime}(0)\eta(x)=\varphi(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}r_{s}(x)

and therefore, by
Section

2.4

and
Section

2.3
again,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}A(\Sigma_{s})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{ds}}}\right|_{s=0}A(\Sigma_{s,a(s)})=0,

concluding the proof.
∎

Following the terminology in the literature, see
[
45
]
, we say that a surface
\Sigma
satisfying the hypotheses of
Section

2.4
is
area-stationary under volume-preserving radial variations
. In particular, any smooth isoperimetric maximiser satisfying the aperture constraint of
[
3
]
or the time-separation constraint of
[
8
]
must satisfy this stationarity condition for all admissible compactly supported radial variations and have constant mean curvature.

A striking result in the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group is that metric balls are not isoperimetric minimisers
[
38
]
, see also
[
7
, Example 4.13]
. We prove the analogous statement in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group by showing that sub-Lorentzian pseudo-spheres do not have constant horizontal mean curvature.

Theorem 2.19
.

Let

\Sigma\coloneqq\{q\in I^{+}(0):\tau(0,q)=1\}

be the unit sub-Lorentzian pseudo-sphere. Then no nonempty relatively open
smooth patch of
\Sigma
is area-stationary under volume-preserving radial
variations. In particular, such a patch cannot be a smooth isoperimetric
maximiser for either the aperture-constrained problem of
[
3
]

or the lower-time-separation constrained problem of
[
8
]
.

Proof.

By the description of the exponential map in
Equation

6
, the
unit pseudo-sphere satisfies

\Sigma=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w)\;\middle|\;u^{2}-v^{2}=1,\ u>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v}}\right\rvert,\ w\in\mathbb{R}}}\right\}.

Since
\Sigma
is the level set
\tau(0,\cdot)=1
, every horizontal tangent vector
V\in\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
satisfies

g\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(0,\cdot),V\bigr)=V\bigl(\tau(0,\cdot)\bigr)=0.

Thus
\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(0,\cdot)
is horizontally normal to
\Sigma
, and since it is unit, see
Equation

7
, we get
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}=\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}\tau(0,\cdot)
, with the chosen orientation.

Differentiating the geodesic

\gamma(t)=\mathrm{exp}_{0}(tu,tv,tw)
given by
Equation

6
, we get

\dot{\gamma}(1)=\bigl(u\cosh w+v\sinh w\bigr)X+\bigl(v\cosh w+u\sinh w\bigr)Y.

Since
u^{2}-v^{2}=1
on
\Sigma
, this curve has unit speed. Therefore,
by the relation between unit speed maximising geodesics and the time
separation gradient,

\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w))=-\bigl(u\cosh w+v\sinh w\bigr)X-\bigl(v\cosh w+u\sinh w\bigr)Y.

The divergence formula gives
\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=X(a)+Y(b)
, where the functions
a,b
are defined by

a\circ\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w)=-u\cosh w-v\sinh w,

and

b\circ\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w)=-v\cosh w-u\sinh w.

On
\Sigma
, we have
u=\sqrt{1+v^{2}}
. The derivatives
X(a)
and
Y(b)
are computed by pulling
X
and
Y
back through the differential of
\mathrm{exp}_{0}
:

(X(a))(\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w))=d(a\circ\mathrm{exp}_{0})_{(u,v,w)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(d\mathrm{exp}_{0})_{(u,v,w)}^{-1}\bigl(X_{\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w)}\bigr)}}\right],

and

(Y(b))(\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w))=d(b\circ\mathrm{exp}_{0})_{(u,v,w)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(d\mathrm{exp}_{0})_{(u,v,w)}^{-1}\bigl(Y_{\mathrm{exp}_{0}(u,v,w)}\bigr)}}\right].

Using the explicit formula for
\mathrm{exp}_{0}
from
Equation

6
and
then restricting to
u^{2}-v^{2}=1
, this gives

H(v,w)=-\operatorname{div}_{\Omega}(\nu_{\mathbb{H}})=\frac{w^{2}\cosh w-w\sinh w}{w\sinh w-2\cosh w+2}.

Here we have used
Section

2.3
for the first equality. This function is clearly not constant on any neighbourhood.
∎

3
Characterisation of constant mean curvature symmetric surfaces

3.1
Boost-symmetric constant mean curvature surfaces

The previous section showed that smooth isoperimetric candidates must have
constant horizontal mean curvature. Since such candidates are also expected to
possess some symmetry, we restrict our attention to
boost-symmetric
surfaces
, namely surfaces invariant under the action of
O^{+}(1,1)
on the
sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group, as described in

Theorem

2.1
. More concretely, for each
u\in\mathbb{R}
, let

B_{u}(x,y,z)=(x\cosh u+y\sinh u,\;x\sinh u+y\cosh u,\;z).

Then
\Sigma
is boost-symmetric if
B_{u}(\Sigma)=\Sigma
for every
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

Locally around each non-characteristic point
p
, choose a unit generator

T
of
\mathrm{H}(\Sigma)
. The corresponding (horizontal) integral curve

\gamma:\mathopen{(}-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\mathclose{)}\to\mathbb{H}
projects to the

(x,y)
-plane as a curve
\sigma
whose signed curvature at
t=0
equals

H(p)
, by
Section

2.2
. When
\Sigma
is boost-symmetric, applying the boosts to this profile gives a
local parametrisation of
\Sigma
away from the
z
-axis, namely

S(t,u)=B_{u}(\gamma(t))
. In particular,
H
is constant whenever the
projected curve
\sigma
has constant signed curvature.

Conversely, if
\sigma
is a curve in the Minkowski plane with constant
signed curvature, then any horizontal lift
\gamma
of
\sigma
to
\mathbb{H}

can be boosted to produce a surface
S(t,u)=B_{u}(\gamma(t))
. Wherever this
map is an immersed spacelike surface, its image is boost-symmetric and has
constant horizontal mean curvature, by

Section

2.2
and the fact that boosts are
isometries. Since we are interested in achronal spacelike surfaces, the
projected horizontal profiles relevant to the construction must be spacelike.

The classification of constant-curvature curves in the Minkowski plane is
classical. We recall the relevant statement next, and refer the reader to the
discussion preceding
[
33
, Theorem 2.10]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Up to the action of the full Lorentz isometry group

O(1,1)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}
and reparametrisation, the spacelike curves in the
Minkowski plane with constant curvature
k\geq 0
are precisely the straight
lines when
k=0
, and, when
k>0
, the hyperbolas

\gamma(t)=\frac{1}{k}(\cosh(kt),\sinh(kt)).

We now carry out the construction explicitly. Starting from the spacelike
constant-curvature curves in the Minkowski plane classified in

Section

3.1
, we horizontally lift them to

\mathbb{H}
and then apply the boosts to the lifted curves to obtain surfaces.

We start with the nonzero curvature case
k>0
. Choosing the arclength
parametrisation and allowing translations in the Minkowski plane, as well as
the two choices of branch, a representative can be written as

(x(t),y(t))=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\cosh(kt)+c,\;\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\sinh(kt)+d}}\right),\qquad\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\},\ c,d\in\mathbb{R}.

(18)

Using
Equation

2
, the horizontal lift of

Equation

18
with
z(t_{0})=0
is given by

z(t)=\frac{1}{2k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-t_{0}+c\varepsilon\sinh(kt)-c\varepsilon\sinh(kt_{0})-d\varepsilon\cosh(kt)+d\varepsilon\cosh(kt_{0})}}\right).

We can then boost the lifted curve
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))
according to
S(t,u)=B_{u}(\gamma(t))
. We now remove the inessential constants, namely those which can be absorbed by
ambient isometries, dilations, or reparametrisation.

The choice of
t_{0}
only fixes the additive constant in the horizontal lift.
Since vertical translations are sub-Lorentzian isometries and commute with
boosts, we may absorb this constant by a vertical translation. Thus, up to
isometry, the profile curve may be written as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\cosh(kt)+c,\;\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\sinh(kt)+d,\;\frac{1}{2k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+c\varepsilon\sinh(kt)-d\varepsilon\cosh(kt)+d\varepsilon}}\right)}}\right).

(19)

The sign
\varepsilon
can also be removed: the isometry

(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,-y,z)
is a time-inverting boost, which commutes with boosts, changes

(\varepsilon,k,c,d)
into
(-\varepsilon,k,-c,-d)
. Since
c,d
range
over all real values, the same family is obtained with
\varepsilon=1
.

Finally, the parameter
k>0
can be absorbed by Heisenberg dilations
\delta_{k}(x,y,z)=(kx,ky,k^{2}z)
. Indeed, applying
\delta_{k}
to
Equation

19
with
\varepsilon=1
gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh(kt)+kc,\;\sinh(kt)+kd,\;\frac{1}{2}\bigl(kt+kc\sinh(kt)-kd\cosh(kt)+kd\bigr)}}\right).

Since
c,d
range over all real values, we may rename
kc
and
kd
as

c
and
d
, and then replace
kt
by
t
. Thus, up to Heisenberg
dilation and reparametrisation, it is enough to consider the case
k=1
.
The dilation
\delta_{\lambda}
preserves boost symmetry and do not alter the causal character of
(c,d)
in the Minkowski plane. Dilations do not however preserve the numerical value of the
mean curvature. It sends a surface with constant mean curvature
H

to a surface with constant mean curvature
\lambda^{-1}H
.

Thus, up to vertical translations, the isometry

(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,-y,z)
, Heisenberg dilations, and reparametrisation, the
nonzero-curvature case is represented by

\begin{split}S_{(c,d)}(t,u)&=B_{u}\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh(t)+c,\;\sinh(t)+d,\;\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+c\sinh(t)-d\cosh(t)+d}}\right)}}\right)}_{=:\gamma_{(c,d)}(t)}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(t+u)+c\cosh(u)+d\sinh(u)\\
\sinh(t+u)+c\sinh(u)+d\cosh(u)\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+c\sinh(t)-d\cosh(t)+d}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(20)

We say that such a surface has
timelike
,
spacelike
, or

null
parameter according to the causal character of the vector

(c,d)
in the Minkowski plane. We shall classify these surfaces according
to this vector below. We often refer to the surface associated with the lift of
the
(c,d)
-translated hyperbola as the
(c,d)
-surface
.

We now turn to the case
k=0
. Choosing an arclength parametrisation and
allowing translations in the Minkowski plane, a spacelike line can be written as

(x(t),y(t))=\bigl(\sinh(\beta)t+\alpha,\;\varepsilon\cosh(\beta)t+\alpha^{\prime}\bigr),\qquad\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta\in\mathbb{R},\quad\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}.

Translating the parameter
t
only changes the point of the line chosen as
origin. Hence, up to reparametrisation, we may assume
\alpha^{\prime}=0
. As above,
the additive constant in the horizontal lift only produces a vertical
translation of the boosted surface, so we choose the lift with
z(0)=0
.

Using
Equation

2
, the horizontal lift is given by
z(t)=\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{2}\cosh(\beta)t
.
Boosting this horizontal lift, we obtain

\begin{split}S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}(t,u)&=B_{u}\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sinh(\beta)t+\alpha,\;\varepsilon\cosh(\beta)t,\;\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{2}\cosh(\beta)t}}\right)}_{=:\gamma_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}(t)}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon t\sinh(u+\varepsilon\beta)+\alpha\cosh(u)\\
\varepsilon t\cosh(u+\varepsilon\beta)+\alpha\sinh(u)\\
\dfrac{\alpha\varepsilon}{2}\cosh(\beta)t\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(21)

These are the boost-symmetric surfaces with zero horizontal mean curvature,
which we shall refer to as the
maximal surfaces
.

Although the rest of this work is devoted to a case-by-case classification of
these surfaces up to the sub-Lorentzian isometries described in

Theorems

2.1
and
2.1
, the following
proposition records a basic symmetry of the parameter space that will be used
repeatedly later.

Proposition 3.2
.

S_{(c,d)}
is isometric to
S_{(c,-d)}
by any timed rotation.

Proof.

Any timed rotation is of the form
A\circ B_{v}
, where
B_{v}
is a boost and

A(x,y,z)=(x,-y,-z)
. Since
S_{(c,d)}
is
boost-symmetric, it is enough to apply
A
. Moreover

A\circ B_{u}=B_{-u}\circ A
, and a direct computation gives

A\gamma_{(c,d)}(t)=\gamma_{(c,-d)}(-t)
. Hence

A(S_{(c,d)}(t,u))=B_{-u}\gamma_{(c,-d)}(-t)
, which parametrises

S_{(c,-d)}
after the change of variables
(t,u)\mapsto(-t,-u)
.
∎

We conclude this section by determining when the parametrisations introduced in
Equations

20
and
21
are
regular. In
Section

3.3
, we shall see that in the non-regular nonzero-curvature case the corresponding
image is genuinely singular, and it will be studied separately.

Proposition 3.3
.

The regularity of the parametrisations
Equations

20
and
21
is as follows:

(i)

S_{(c,d)}(t,u)=B_{u}\gamma_{(c,d)}(t)
is regular if and only if

c^{2}-d^{2}\neq 1
or
c\geq 0
;

(ii)

S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}(t,u)=B_{u}\gamma_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}(t)

is regular if and only if

\alpha\neq 0
. Moreover, when
\alpha=0
, the the image of the parametrisation is the set
\{(x,y,0):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}\cup\{(0,0,0)\}
.

Proof.

Since boosts are diffeomorphisms, it is enough to check regularity at

u=0
. For the first family,
\partial_{t}S_{(c,d)}(t,0)=\gamma^{\prime}_{(c,d)}(t)

is horizontal and nonzero. Also,

\partial_{u}S_{(c,d)}(t,0)=(\sinh t+d,\cosh t+c,0)
. With respect to the
basis
\{X,Y,Z\}
, using
Z^{*}=dz+\frac{y}{2}dx-\frac{x}{2}dy
, these two
vectors have coordinate matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\sinh t&\sinh t+d\\
\cosh t&\cosh t+c\\
0&\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-1+d^{2}-c^{2}+2(d\sinh t-c\cosh t)}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Hence the parametrisation is singular at
(t,0)
if and only if

c\sinh t-d\cosh t=0,\qquad-1+d^{2}-c^{2}+2(d\sinh t-c\cosh t)=0.

(22)

If
c^{2}-d^{2}\neq 0
, this system is
equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}\cosh t\\
\sinh t\end{pmatrix}=\frac{-1-c^{2}+d^{2}}{2(c^{2}-d^{2})}\begin{pmatrix}c\\
d\end{pmatrix}.

The identity
\cosh^{2}t-\sinh^{2}t=1
implies
c^{2}-d^{2}=1
, and then the condition

\cosh t>0
gives
c<0
. Conversely, if
c^{2}-d^{2}=1
and
c<0
, the
displayed formula gives
\cosh t=-c>0
and
\sinh t=-d
, so the rank
drops. Thus
S_{(c,d)}
is regular exactly when
c^{2}-d^{2}\neq 1
or

c\geq 0
.

For the maximal family, again at
u=0
,

\partial_{t}S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}=(\sinh\beta,\varepsilon\cosh\beta,\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{2}\cosh\beta)

and

\partial_{u}S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}=(\varepsilon t\cosh\beta,t\sinh\beta+\alpha,0)
. If
\alpha\neq 0
,
these vectors are clearly linearly independent, while if
\alpha=0
the vector

\partial_{u}S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}
vanishes at
t=0
. Therefore
the parametrisation is regular if and only if
\alpha\neq 0
. When
\alpha=0
, the parametrisation reduces to

S(t,u)=(\varepsilon t\sinh(u+\varepsilon\beta),\varepsilon t\cosh(u+\varepsilon\beta),0)
. Thus
x^{2}-y^{2}=-t^{2}
, so for

t\neq 0
the image is the region
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert
in the plane
z=0
,
while
t=0
gives only the origin.
∎

3.2
Surfaces of spacelike parameters

In this section, we study the surfaces
S_{(c,d)}
associated with spacelike vectors
(c,d)
in the Minkowski plane, that is, vectors satisfying
c^{2}-d^{2}<0
. By
Section

3.1
, these surfaces are already known to be regular. We illustrate such a surface in
Figure

2
. Although no regularity issue arises in the classical sense, these surfaces still exhibit a form of “non-regularity” from the sub-Lorentzian point of view: their set of characteristic points is non-empty. This is described in the following proposition.

Figure 2:
A surface
S_{(c,d)}
with spacelike parameter.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
S_{(c,d)}
be a
(c,d)
-surface with
c^{2}-d^{2}<0
, and set

t^{*}=-\sign(d)\log(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert-c),\qquad\omega=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{*}+c\sinh(t^{*})-d\cosh(t^{*})+d}}\right).

Then the characteristic set of
S_{(c,d)}
is the open half-line

\{(s,\sign(d)s,\omega):s>0\}.

In particular, it is a horizontal null curve, and therefore the surface is
not acausal.

Proof.

By
Section

3.1
, the vectors

\partial_{t}S_{(c,d)}
and
\partial_{u}S_{(c,d)}
span the tangent plane
everywhere. Since
\partial_{t}S_{(c,d)}
is horizontal by construction, a
point is characteristic precisely when
\partial_{u}S_{(c,d)}
is also
horizontal. Boosts preserve the horizontal distribution, so it is enough to
work at
u=0
.

At
u=0
, the boost direction is

\partial_{u}S_{(c,d)}(t,0)=(\sinh t+d,\cosh t+c,0)
. Therefore

Z^{*}(\partial_{u}S_{(c,d)}(t,0))=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\sinh t+d)^{2}-(\cosh t+c)^{2}}}\right),

where, as before,
Z^{*}
is the dual one-form to
Z
with respect to the frame
\{X,Y,Z\}
.
Thus the characteristic condition is

\bigl(\sinh t+d\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(\cosh t+c\bigr)^{2}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 1+c^{2}-d^{2}+2(c\cosh t-d\sinh t)=0.

(23)

Since
c^{2}-d^{2}<0
, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert c}}\right\rvert
, and hence
d\neq 0
, so that the only possible sign in
Equation

23
is

\sinh t+d=\sign(d)(\cosh t+c).

(24)

If
d>0
this is the equation

e^{-t}=d-c
, while if
d<0
it is the equation

e^{t}=-d-c
. In both cases the unique solution is

t^{*}=-\sign(d)\log(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert-c).

At this value of
t
, set
\eta=\cosh(t^{*})+c
. Equation
Equation

24

gives
\sinh(t^{*})+d=\sign(d)\eta
. Moreover
\eta>0
: if
d>0
, then

\eta=(1+d^{2}-c^{2})/(2(d-c))
, while if
d<0
, then

\eta=(1+d^{2}-c^{2})/(2(-d-c))
. Therefore, denoting
the third component of
\gamma_{(c,d)}(t^{*})
by
\omega
, the
characteristic points are

B_{u}(\eta,\sign(d)\eta,\omega)=\bigl(\eta e^{\sign(d)u},\sign(d)\eta e^{\sign(d)u},\omega\bigr).

Since
s=\eta e^{\sign(d)u}
ranges over
(0,+\infty)
, the
characteristic set is exactly
\{(s,\sign(d)s,\omega):s>0\}
.

Finally, the curve
s\mapsto(s,\sign(d)s,\omega)
is horizontal and its horizontal velocity

(1,\sign(d))
is null in the Minkowski plane. Hence the characteristic set
is a horizontal null curve contained in the surface, so the surface is not
acausal.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

No two surfaces of spacelike parameters are related by a time-inverting
isometry.

Proof.

Suppose that a time-inverting isometry sends one such surface to another.
Since isometries preserve the horizontal distribution, they send
characteristic points to characteristic points. By

Section

3.2
, the characteristic set of the
source is
\{(s,\sign(d)s,\omega):s>0\}
, and the characteristic set of the
target is contained in
\{x>0\}
.

Let
\Lambda
be the Lorentz part of the isometry. Since
\Lambda
is time-inverting, any future-directed causal vector gets sent to a past-directed causal vector and in particular to a vector of negative
x
-coordinate.

Applying the isometry to a point
(s,\sign(d)s,\omega)
of the source
characteristic set, its
x
-coordinate is therefore

a+s(\Lambda_{11}+\sign(d)\Lambda_{12})
, where
a\in\mathbb{R}
comes
from the translation part. This is negative for large
s
, contradicting
the fact that the target characteristic set lies in
\{x>0\}
.
∎

We next classify these surfaces up to isometries. We first give a sufficient
condition for two spacelike-parameter surfaces to be isometric by a
time-preserving boost.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
S_{(c,d)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
be two surfaces of spacelike
parameters. If
c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
dd^{\prime}>0
, then they are isometric
by a time-preserving boost.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
S_{(c,d)}
is isometric to
S_{(0,D)}
, by a
time-preserving boost, where

D=\sign(d)\sqrt{d^{2}-c^{2}}
. Indeed, the hypotheses give

d^{2}-c^{2}=(d^{\prime})^{2}-(c^{\prime})^{2}
and
\sign(d)=\sign(d^{\prime})
, so the same value of
D

is obtained from both
(c,d)
and
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
. The desired isometry is then
obtained by composing one reduction with the inverse of the other.

Choose
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sinh\alpha=c/D
. Since
D
and

d
have the same sign and
D^{2}=d^{2}-c^{2}
, we also have

\cosh\alpha=d/D
. Starting from
S_{(0,D)}(t,u)=B_{u}\gamma_{(0,D)}(t)
,
make the change of parameters
s=t+\alpha
,
v=u-\alpha
. Then

t=s-\alpha
,
u=v+\alpha
, and the addition formulae give

S_{(0,D)}(s-\alpha,v+\alpha)=B_{v+\alpha}\gamma_{(0,D)}(s-\alpha)=B_{v}\bigl(B_{\alpha}\gamma_{(0,D)}(s-\alpha)\bigr)

The first two components agree with those of
S_{(c,d)}(s,v)
, while the
third component differs from that of
S_{(c,d)}(s,v)
by the constant

\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+d-D)
, that is to say

S_{(c,d)}(s,v)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,0,\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+d-D)}}\right)\ast S_{(0,D)}(s-\alpha,v+\alpha),

proving the claim.
∎

We now show that the conditions of the previous proposition are also necessary
for two such surfaces to be isometric by a time-preserving boost.

Proposition 3.7
.

If two surfaces of spacelike parameters
S_{(c,d)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}

are isometric by a time-preserving boost, then

c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
dd^{\prime}>0
.

Proof.

By the proof of
Section

3.2
, after composing
with time-preserving boosts, any
spacelike-parameter surface can be put in the form
S_{(0,d)}
. Since

S_{(0,d)}
is boost-invariant, it is enough to prove that two surfaces
S_{(0,d)}
and
S_{(0,d^{\prime})}
cannot be related by a vertical
translation unless
d=d^{\prime}
. Because
(0,d)
and
(0,d^{\prime})
are
spacelike,
d
and
d^{\prime}
must be nonzero.

A vertical translation must send the characteristic set of
S_{(0,d)}
to
that of
S_{(0,d^{\prime})}
since it preserves the horizontal distribution. By
Section

3.2
, these
characteristic sets are
\{(s,\sign(d)s,\omega):s>0\}
and

\{(s,\sign(d^{\prime})s,\omega^{\prime}):s>0\}
. A vertical translation does not
change
x
or
y
, which already forces
\sign(d)=\sign(d^{\prime})
.

Write
S_{(0,d)}(t,u)=(x(t,u),y(t,u),z(t))
, where

z(t)=\frac{1}{2}(t-d\cosh t+d)
. A critical point of
z
satisfies

1-d\sinh t=0,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\sinh t=\frac{1}{d}.

Since
x^{2}-y^{2}
is invariant under boosts, we may set
u=0
. Thus

x(t,u)^{2}-y(t,u)^{2}=x(t,0)^{2}-y(t,0)^{2}=\cosh^{2}t-(\sinh t+d)^{2}.

Similarly, if
S_{(0,d^{\prime})}(t,u)=(x^{\prime}(t,u),y^{\prime}(t,u),z^{\prime}(t))
, then at the
critical points of
z^{\prime}
one has

x^{\prime}(t,u)^{2}-y^{\prime}(t,u)^{2}=-d^{\prime 2}-1
. Since a vertical translation preserves the

x,y
-coordinates and sends critical points of
z
to critical points of

z^{\prime}
, we must have

-d^{2}-1=-d^{\prime 2}-1.

Hence
d^{2}=d^{\prime 2}
, and together with
\sign(d)=\sign(d^{\prime})
, this gives

d=d^{\prime}
. Returning to the original parameters, this is exactly

c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
dd^{\prime}>0
.
∎

We now investigate when two surfaces with spacelike parameters are isometric via a time-preserving
rotation.

Proposition 3.8
.

Two surfaces of spacelike parameters
S_{(c,d)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
are
isometric by a time-preserving rotation if and only if

c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
dd^{\prime}<0
.

Proof.

Assume first that
c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
dd^{\prime}<0
. Then
d
and

-d^{\prime}
have the same sign, and

c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-(-d^{\prime})^{2}
. By

Section

3.2
,
S_{(c,d)}
is isometric to

S_{(c^{\prime},-d^{\prime})}
by a time-preserving boost. By

Section

3.1
,
S_{(c^{\prime},-d^{\prime})}
is isometric to

S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
by a timed rotation. The composition has time-preserving
rotation Lorentz part, proving one implication.

Conversely, suppose that
S_{(c,d)}
is isometric to
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
by a
time-preserving rotation. Composing with the timed rotation

(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,-z)
, which sends
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
to

S_{(c^{\prime},-d^{\prime})}
, gives an isometry from
S_{(c,d)}
to
S_{(c^{\prime},-d^{\prime})}

whose Lorentz part is a time-preserving boost. Hence

Section

3.2
gives

c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-(-d^{\prime})^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d(-d^{\prime})>0,

which is equivalent to
c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
dd^{\prime}<0
.
∎

Having completed the isometry classification for spacelike parameters, we turn our attention to their causal behaviour.

Proposition 3.9
.

Every surface of spacelike parameter is not achronal.

Proof.

By the classification results above, it suffices to prove non-achronality for
S_{(0,d)}
with
d\neq 0
.
We construct two points on the
surface which are chronologically related. Setting
u=-t
in

S_{(0,d)}(t,u)
gives the curve

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-d\sinh(t),\,d\cosh(t),\,\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-d\cosh(t)+d}}\right)}}\right).

Evaluating this curve at
t=-\sign(d)s
, we define

\gamma(s)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert\sinh(s),\,d\cosh(s),\,\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\sign(d)s-d\cosh(s)+d}}\right)}}\right).

By left-invariance,
\gamma(-s)\ll\gamma(s)
is equivalent to

0\ll\gamma(-s)^{-1}\ast\gamma(s)
. A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\gamma(-s)^{-1}\ast\gamma(s)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert\sinh(s),\,0,\,\frac{1}{2}(-2\sign(d)s+2d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert\sinh(s)\cosh(s))}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert\sinh(s),\,0,\,-\frac{\sign(d)}{2}(2s-d^{2}\sinh(2s))}}\right).

For
s>0
, the
x
-component is positive. The condition

0\ll\gamma(-s)^{-1}\ast\gamma(s)
is therefore

-4d^{2}\sinh^{2}(s)+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert 2s-d^{2}\sinh(2s)}}\right\rvert<0.

For
s
sufficiently large,
2s-d^{2}\sinh(2s)<0
, and the left hand side
becomes

-4d^{2}\sinh^{2}(s)+2d^{2}\sinh(2s)-4s=-2d^{2}\bigl(2\sinh^{2}(s)-\sinh(2s)\bigr)-4s.

(25)

The term in parentheses is bounded as
s\to+\infty
, whereas

-4s\to-\infty
. Hence
Equation

25
is negative for all
sufficiently large
s
. Thus
\gamma(-s)\ll\gamma(s)
for large
s
,
so the surface contains chronologically related points and thus is not achronal.
∎

3.3
Surfaces of timelike parameters

In this section, we consider the family of surfaces
S_{(c,d)}
with timelike parameters, that is, the surfaces defined in
Equation

20
for which
c^{2}-d^{2}>0
. An illustration of these surfaces can be found in
Figure

1
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
S_{(c,d)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
be two surfaces of timelike
parameters. If
c^{2}-d^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-d^{\prime 2}
and
cc^{\prime}>0
, then they are isometric
by a time-preserving boost.

Proof.

This follows closely the proof given in
Section

3.2
.
It is enough to show that
S_{(c,d)}
is isometric to
S_{(C,0)}
, by a
time-preserving boost, where

C=\sign(c)\sqrt{c^{2}-d^{2}}
. Indeed, the hypotheses give

c^{2}-d^{2}=(c^{\prime})^{2}-(d^{\prime})^{2}
and
\sign(c)=\sign(c^{\prime})
, so the same value of

C
is obtained from both
(c,d)
and
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
. The desired isometry is
then obtained by composing one reduction with the inverse of the other.

Choose
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sinh\alpha=d/C
. Since
C
and

c
have the same sign and
C^{2}=c^{2}-d^{2}
, we also have

\cosh\alpha=c/C
. Starting from
S_{(C,0)}(t,u)=B_{u}\gamma_{(C,0)}(t)
,
make the change of parameters
s=t+\alpha
,
v=u-\alpha
. Then

t=s-\alpha
,
u=v+\alpha
, and the addition formulae give

S_{(C,0)}(s-\alpha,v+\alpha)=B_{v+\alpha}\gamma_{(C,0)}(s-\alpha)=B_{v}\bigl(B_{\alpha}\gamma_{(C,0)}(s-\alpha)\bigr).

The first two components agree with those of
S_{(c,d)}(s,v)
, while the
third component of
S_{(c,d)}(s,v)
is obtained by adding the constant

\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+d)
. Thus

S_{(c,d)}(s,v)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,0,\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+d)}}\right)\ast S_{(C,0)}(s-\alpha,v+\alpha),

proving the claim.
∎

The next result is parallel to
Section

3.2
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Two surfaces of timelike parameters are isometric via a time-preserving rotation if and only if they are isometric via a time-preserving boost.

Proof.

By
Section

3.1
, recall that the timed rotation

A(x,y,z)=(x,-y,-z)
sends a
(c,d)
-surface to the

(c,-d)
-surface.

Suppose first that a
(c,d)
-surface
is isometric to a
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})

We next describe the characteristic points of these surfaces; this will allow us
to prove that the sufficient condition in the previous proposition is in fact
necessary.

Proposition 3.12
.

The
characteristic set of a
(c,d)
-surface with timelike parameters is non-empty if and only if
c<0

c^{2}-d^{2}\neq 1

i=0,1

r=\sqrt{c^{2}-d^{2}},\qquad\alpha=\arcsinh(d/r),\qquad t_{i}=-\alpha+(-1)^{i}\log r,\qquad\omega_{i}=\sinh(t_{i})+d.

Then the characteristic set is the disconnected union of the two half-lines

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})s,\,\sign(\omega_{i})s,\,\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{i}+c\sinh(t_{i})-d\cosh(t_{i})+d}}\right)}}\right):s>0}}\right\},\quad i=0,1.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Section

3.2
, it is enough to
work at
u=0
. At points where the tangent plane is defined,

\partial_{t}S_{(c,d)}
is horizontal by construction. Hence the
characteristic points are exactly those for which
\partial_{u}S_{(c,d)}
is
horizontal, namely those satisfying
Equation

23
.

Put
r=\sqrt{c^{2}-d^{2}}
. If
c>0
, choose
\alpha
such that

\sinh\alpha=d/r
and
\cosh\alpha=c/r
. The previous equation becomes

1+r^{2}+2r\cosh(t-\alpha)=0
, which has no solution. Hence no
characteristic points occur when

.

Suppose now that
c<0
. Choose
\alpha
such that

\sinh\alpha=d/r
and
\cosh\alpha=-c/r

then

\cosh(t+\alpha)=\frac{1+r^{2}}{2r}=\cosh(\log r).

The equation has two distinct solutions if and only if

. If
r=1

3.1
. Therefore it does not contribute to the

\cosh(t_{i})+c=\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sinh(t_{i})+d}}\right\rvert.

Denote
\omega_{i}=\sinh(t_{i})+d

\omega_{i}\neq 0

in

gives

\begin{pmatrix}\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\omega_{i}}}\right\rvert\cosh u+\omega_{i}\sinh u\\
\omega_{i}\cosh u+\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\omega_{i}}}\right\rvert\sinh u\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{i}+c\sinh(t_{i})-d\cosh(t_{i})+d}}\right)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\omega_{i}}}\right\rvert e^{\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\sign(\omega_{i})u}\\
\sign(\omega_{i})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\omega_{i}}}\right\rvert e^{\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\sign(\omega_{i})u}\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{i}+c\sinh(t_{i})-d\cosh(t_{i})+d}}\right)\end{pmatrix}

Setting
s=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\omega_{i}}}\right\rvert e^{\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})\sign(\omega_{i})u}
, the previous
expression becomes

\begin{pmatrix}\sign(1-c^{2}+d^{2})s\\
\sign(\omega_{i})s\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{i}+c\sinh(t_{i})-d\cosh(t_{i})+d}}\right)\end{pmatrix}

with

. Hence the characteristic set consists of two null

-axis.
To see that
they are disjoint, let

z_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{i}+c\sinh(t_{i})-d\cosh(t_{i})+d}}\right),\quad\text{ so that }\quad z_{0}-z_{1}=\log r-\frac{r^{2}-1}{2}.

The function

indeed its derivative is

. Therefore the two

∎

By
Section

3.1
, the nonzero-curvature
parametrisations fail to be regular precisely when

c^{2}-d^{2}=1,\qquad c<0.

These parameters are timelike, since
c^{2}-d^{2}>0
. By

3.1
, the regular timelike cases are precisely
the complementary cases. Here we study the exceptional case

,

, where the parametrisation loses rank. By

3.3
, all such singular

S_{(-1,0)}(t,u)=B_{u}\gamma_{(-1,0)}(t)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh(t+u)-\cosh u,\;\sinh(t+u)-\sinh u,\;\frac{1}{2}(t-\sinh t)}}\right).

(26)

This expression has a useful interpretation: it is the expression of the
Heisenberg group’s sub-Lorentzian exponential map at the origin, as written in

Equation

6
, evaluated at

(t\sinh u,t\cosh u,t)
. Equivalently, it is the curve

t\mapsto\mathrm{exp}_{e}(t\sinh u,t\cosh u,t)
. This initial datum does not lie in the

(\sinh u,\cosh u)
is spacelike rather than causal.

To locate the singular set, we return to the proof of

Section

3.1
. For

, the parametrisation is
singular at parameter values
(t,u)
where, after reducing to

,

Equation

22

and

, this
becomes

\sinh t=0,\qquad\cosh t=1,

which is equivalent to

.
All these parameter values have the same image

At this stage,
however, this only shows that the chosen parametrisation is regular everywhere except at the
origin. It does not yet rule out the possibility that the image admits another
smooth parametrisation there. We next show that the origin is a genuine
singular point of the image. We first record the projection of the surface and
a useful symmetry.

Lemma 3.13
.

The projection of
S_{(-1,0)}
to the
(x,y)
-plane is

\{y^{2}-x^{2}>0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}.

Moreover, the projection to the
(x,y)
-plane is
injective on

.

Proof.

Set

. Then

S_{(-1,0)}(t,v)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\sinh v\sinh(t/2),\;2\cosh v\sinh(t/2),\;\frac{1}{2}(t-\sinh t)}}\right).

(27)

Hence the projected coordinates satisfy

y^{2}-x^{2}=4\sinh^{2}(t/2)\geq 0.

Equality holds if and only if

. Thus the only projected point with

is
(0,0)
.

Conversely, let
(x,y)
satisfy

and

(t,v)
is uniquely determined by

2\sinh(t/2)=\sign(y)\sqrt{y^{2}-x^{2}},\qquad\tanh v=\frac{x}{y},

∎

Proposition 3.14
.

The singular surface
S_{(-1,0)}
is invariant under the reflection

(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,z)
, but it is not invariant under the map

(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,-z)
.

Proof.

Replacing

(t,u)

shows that

\displaystyle S_{(-1,0)}(t,-t-u)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh u-\cosh(t+u),\;\sinh(t+u)-\sinh u,\;\frac{1}{2}(t-\sinh t)}}\right)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(S_{(-1,0)}(t,u)\bigr),

where

Choose a point
(x,y,z)
on the surface with

. By the first
part,
(-x,y,z)
is also on the surface. If the surface were invariant
under
(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,-z)
, then

∎

We show that the rank
loss at the origin reflects a genuine singularity of the image, rather than
being caused only by the choice of parametrisation.

Proposition 3.15
.

The only point of the image
S_{(-1,0)}
projected to
(0,0)
is the
origin. In particular, the image of
S_{(-1,0)}
is singular at the
origin.

Proof.

Consider the surface
S_{(-1,0)}\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}
. By
Section

3.3
we know that its projection coincides with the set

. In particular, this set is disconnected; as the projection is a continuous map we get that the surface

is disconnected as well. Since

is connected we can conclude that it cannot be locally Euclidean with dimension 2 at the origin, as a two dimensional locally Euclidean surface cannot become disconnected after removing one point.
∎

We start by showing that there are no time-inverting isometries between singular timelike-parameters surfaces.

Corollary 3.16
.

No two singular timelike-parameter surfaces are related by a

Proof.

By
Section

3.3
, all singular
timelike-parameter surfaces are isometric to

by
time-preserving boosts. It is therefore enough to rule out time-inverting

S_{(-1,0)}
.

By
Section

3.3
, the
origin is the unique singular point of
S_{(-1,0)}
, so any time-inverting self-isometry fixes the

time-preserving symmetries of
S_{(-1,0)}
, namely boosts and the timed

Section

3.1
, the Lorentz part of this isometry can be reduced to the map
A(x,y,z)=(-x,y,-z)
. This would
force

, contradicting

Section

3.3
.
∎

We also note that this singular surface has no characteristic points.

Proposition 3.17
.

The singular surface

has no characteristic points.

Proof.

As in the proofs of

Sections

3.2
and
3.3
,
it is enough to work at
u=0
. At regular points,

\partial_{t}S_{(-1,0)}
is horizontal by construction, so a point is
characteristic precisely when
\partial_{u}S_{(-1,0)}
is horizontal.
Setting
c=-1
and
d=0
in

Equation

23
, this condition becomes

2-2\cosh t=0,

∎

As a consequence, the sign of
c
is an isometry invariant for timelike-parameter surfaces.

Corollary 3.18
.

Two surfaces with timelike parameters
(c,d)
and
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
are not
isometric if
c
and
c^{\prime}
have different signs.

Proof.

Suppose that
c<0
and
c^{\prime}>0
. If
c^{2}-d^{2}=1
, then by

Section

3.1
the parametrisation has a singularity.
In
Section

3.3
we have shown that this surface is genuinely
singular, as it is not locally Euclidean at that point; on the other hand,
the
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
-surface is smooth by
Section

3.1
. Thus
the two surfaces are not even homeomorphic. We may therefore assume

c^{2}-d^{2}\neq 1

Any isometry between the two surfaces must map the characteristic set of one

3.3
, the
(c,d)

∎

We extend
Section

3.3
to any surfaces of timelike parameters.

Corollary 3.19
.

There is no time-inverting isometry between any pair of surfaces with timelike parameters.

Proof.

We prove the statement for non-singular surfaces, as the singular case is
treated in
Section

3.3
. By

Section

3.3
and the previous corollary, it is
enough to consider the
(c,0)
- and
(c^{\prime},0)
-surfaces with
cc^{\prime}>0
.

Assume first that
c,c^{\prime}>0
. Let

P=\{x^{2}-y^{2}\geq(c+1)^{2}\}\cap\{x\geq c+1\},\qquad P^{\prime}=\{x^{2}-y^{2}\geq(c^{\prime}+1)^{2}\}\cap\{x\geq c^{\prime}+1\}

be the projections of
S_{(c,0)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
onto the Minkowski
plane, respectively.
The sets
P
and
P^{\prime}
are future-closed but nowhere past-closed: if
p

belongs to
P
(resp.
P^{\prime}
), then
J^{+}(p)
is contained in

P
(res.
P^{\prime}
), while
J^{-}(p)
is not.
Since a time-inverting Lorentz map reverses the causal order, it sends
future-closed sets to past-closed sets. Therefore
P
and
P^{\prime}
cannot be
mapped onto one another.

It remains to consider
c,c^{\prime}<0
. It remains to consider
c,c^{\prime}<0
. Up to composing with time-preserving isometries of
S_{(c,0)}
and

S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
, a time-inverting isometry has Lorentz part
A(x,y,z)=(-x,y,-z)
,
followed by a left translation. Indeed, any time-inverting boost is the
composition of a time-preserving boost with

, and the surfaces

S_{(c,0)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
are invariant under time-preserving boosts.
Since
A
fixes the
z
-axis and the
characteristic half-lines of both surfaces start on this axis, the
translation must be vertical. The midpoint of the two starting points is the
origin for both surfaces, so this vertical translation is trivial.

Thus only
A
remains. The function
f:S_{(c,0)}\to\mathbb{R}:(x,y,z)\mapsto x
has a unique critical point on
the
(c,0)
-surface, namely
(c+1,0,0)
. Since

, its
image must be the unique critical point of

on the
(c^{\prime},0)
-surface,
namely
(c^{\prime}+1,0,0)
. Therefore
-c-1=c^{\prime}+1
, hence
c+c^{\prime}=-2
.
Applying the same argument to
g(x,y,z)=z
, and using

, shows that
c\geq-1
and
c^{\prime}\geq-1
,
since
z

has critical points on
S_{(c,0)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
only in these cases. Together with

c+c^{\prime}=-2
, this forces
c=c^{\prime}=-1
. This corresponds exactly to the singular case, but we had assumed that the surfaces were not singular.
∎

The next lemma shows that a surface with timelike parameters and
c>0
eventually escapes the causal future of the origin.

Lemma 3.20
.

Consider a surface with timelike parameters and
c>0
. Then, for
z
large
enough, no point
(x,y,z)
on the surface belongs to
J^{+}(0)
. In
particular, none of these surfaces is entirely contained in the future of the

Proof.

With respect to the standard parametrisation

20
, the
z
-component depends only
on the variable
t
. Denote

\alpha=\arcsinh(d/\sqrt{c^{2}-d^{2}})
. Since

, we can write it as

z(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+\sqrt{c^{2}-d^{2}}\sinh(t-\alpha)+d}}\right).

The function

is strictly increasing, hence the parameter

is
determined by the
z
-coordinate. For a point on the surface we compute the quantity

. Hence there exists

The following proposition is the missing necessity statement for the timelike classification in

3.3
.

Proposition 3.21
.

If two surfaces with timelike parameters
(c,d)
and
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
are such
that
c
and
c^{\prime}
have the same sign but

Proof.

By
Section

3.3
, it is enough to prove the
statement for
S_{(c,0)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
, with
cc^{\prime}>0
and

c\neq c^{\prime}
. By

Section

3.3
, any isometry between
them is time-preserving. Since
S_{(c,0)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
are
invariant under boosts and, by
Section

3.1
, under
timed rotations, we may assume that this isometry is a left translation.

Assume first that
c,c^{\prime}<0
. If exactly one of the two surfaces is singular,
then no isometry exists by
Section

3.3
. If both are

c=c^{\prime}=-1
, contrary to
c^{2}\neq(c^{\prime})^{2}

By
Section

3.3

z

z

-coordinates of the characteristic half-lines have sign

\sign(1-c^{2})
for
S_{(c,0)}

\sign(1-(c^{\prime})^{2})
for

\sign(1-c^{2})=\sign(1-(c^{\prime})^{2})

\sign(1+c)=\sign(1+c^{\prime})
as
c,c^{\prime}<0

Writing
S_{(c,0)}(t,u)=(x(t,u),y(t,u),z(t))

x(t,u)^{2}-y(t,u)^{2}=1+c^{2}+2c\cosh t.

Since
c<0
, the maximum value of

is
(c+1)^{2}
, attained at

. Similarly, on

S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
the maximum is
(c^{\prime}+1)^{2}

(c+1)^{2}=(c^{\prime}+1)^{2}

Together with
\sign(1+c)=\sign(1+c^{\prime})

.

It remains to consider
c,c^{\prime}>0
. Up to exchanging the two surfaces, assume

. The

(c+1,0,0)
on

, and at
(c^{\prime}+1,0,0)

. Hence the left translation must be by
(c^{\prime}-c,0,0)
. The

B_{u}\gamma_{(c,0)}(0)=\bigl((c+1)\cosh u,(c+1)\sinh u,0\bigr).

Its image under the left translation by

is

\biggl((c+1)\cosh u+c^{\prime}-c,\,(c+1)\sinh u,\,\frac{1}{2}(c^{\prime}-c)(c+1)\sinh u\biggr),

and this curve lies on

. Along it,

\begin{split}-x^{2}+y^{2}+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert&=-(c+1)^{2}-(c^{\prime}-c)^{2}-2(c^{\prime}-c)(c+1)\cosh u+2(c^{\prime}-c)(c+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sinh u}}\right\rvert\\

Since also
x>0
,
Equation

5
implies that the
translated curve is contained in
J^{+}(0)
. If
c^{\prime}>c
, its

-coordinate tends to

as

, contradicting

Section

3.3
applied to

S_{(c^{\prime},0)}
.

In both cases, an isometry forces

, contradicting

. Therefore the two surfaces are not isometric.
∎

The next lemma identifies two full null lines in the

In contrast with the case

and the

to

, hence

. The points

and

have

same sign, because

∎

Lemma 3.29

meet the surface more than once.

The presence of vertical lines gives an isometry invariant that distinguishes
the degenerate null surface from all other null-parameter surfaces.

Corollary 3.30
.

The surface
S_{(0,0)}
is not isometric to any surface

S_{(c,\omega c)}
with
c\neq 0
.

Proof.

When

, the parametrisation reduces to

S_{(0,0)}(t,u)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh(t+u),\;\sinh(t+u),\;\frac{t}{2}}}\right).

Hence
S_{(0,0)}
is the vertical cylinder over the hyperbola

x^{2}-y^{2}=1
,
x>0
. In particular, it contains, for every

, the vertical line

\{(\cosh a,\sinh a,z):z\in\mathbb{R}\}.

By
Theorem

, the first two components of every
isometry of
\mathbb{H}
are independent of the

vertically aligned points are sent to vertically aligned points. If

S_{(0,0)}
were isometric to a surface

with

contradicts

3.4
.
∎

We now describe the characteristic set of the null-parameter surfaces, in
parallel with

Sections

3.2
and
3.3
.

Proposition 3.31
.

The characteristic set of a surface
S_{(c,\omega c)}
with null
parameters is non-empty if and only if
c<0
. In this case, set

t^{*}=\omega\log(-2c),\qquad z^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{*}-\omega ce^{-\omega t^{*}}+\omega c}}\right).

Then the characteristic set is the open half-line

\{(s,-\omega s,z^{*}):s>0\}.

In particular, it is a horizontal null curve.

Proof.

As in the proofs of

Sections

3.2
and
3.3
,
it is enough to work at
u=0
. Since
\partial_{t}S_{(c,\omega c)}
is
horizontal by construction, a point is characteristic precisely when

\partial_{u}S_{(c,\omega c)}
is also horizontal. Setting
d=\omega c

in
Equation

23
, this

1+2c\bigl(\cosh t-\omega\sinh t\bigr)=0,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad 1+2ce^{-\omega t}=0.

This equation has a solution if and only if

, and in that case the

. Set
\eta=\cosh(t^{*})+c

\eta=\frac{1}{-4c}>0,\qquad\sinh(t^{*})+\omega c=-\omega\eta.

Substituting

∎

Once again, the characteristic set provides a useful isometry invariant.

Corollary 3.32
.

Let
S_{(c,\omega c)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
be two surfaces with
null parameters. If
c<0<c^{\prime}
, then the two surfaces are not
isometric.

Proof.

By

3.4
, the surface

S_{(c,\omega c)}
has non-empty characteristic set, while

S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
has empty characteristic set because

Since isometries preserve the horizontal distribution, they preserve

∎

We now rule out time-inverting isometries between null-parameter surfaces.

Proposition 3.33
.

There is no time-inverting isometry between any two surfaces

S_{(c,\omega c)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
with null parameters.

Proof.

By

Sections

3.4
and
3.4
,
it is enough to consider the cases where either
c,c^{\prime}<0
, or
c,c^{\prime}>0
,
or
c=c^{\prime}=0
.

When
c,c^{\prime}<0
, the argument is the same as in

Section

3.2
: the characteristic
set is, by

Section

3.4
, a null half-line contained in
\{x>0\}
, while a time-inverting
isometry sends such a half-line eventually into
\{x<0\}
.

Suppose now that
c,c^{\prime}>0
. By
Section

3.4
, up to
composing with time-preserving boosts, we may reduce to the case

S_{(1,\omega)}
and
S_{(1,\omega^{\prime})}
. Both surfaces project to the same region of the Minkowski plane,

P=\{x^{2}-y^{2}>1\}\cap\{x>0\}.

The region

is unbounded only in future timelike directions: along every

escaping to infinity, the

-coordinate tends to

A time-inverting Lorentz map sends future timelike directions to past timelike

, even after a translation, is unbounded in the

\{x>0\}
.

Finally, when

, the surface

is the vertical cylinder
over the future branch

∎

We are able to complete the boost-isometry classification for surfaces with
null parameters.

Proposition 3.34
.

Two surfaces
S_{(c,\omega c)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
with null
parameters are isometric by a time-preserving boost if and only if either

cc^{\prime}>0
and
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
, or
c=c^{\prime}=0
.

Proof.

The implication from right to left follows from

Section

3.4
when
cc^{\prime}>0
, and is trivial when

c=c^{\prime}=0
, since then both parametrisations give the same surface

S_{(0,0)}
.

Conversely, suppose that the two surfaces are isometric by a time-preserving
boost. By

Sections

3.4
and
3.4
,
and after possibly exchanging the two surfaces, either
c=c^{\prime}=0
, or

cc^{\prime}>0
. It remains to prove, in the second case, that

\omega=\omega^{\prime}
.

Assume first that
c,c^{\prime}<0
. By

Section

3.4
, the characteristic set of

S_{(c,\omega c)}
is a null half-line with direction
(1,-\omega)
in
the Minkowski plane, and similarly for
S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
. A
time-preserving boost preserves each future null direction. Since isometries
preserve characteristic points, the two directions must agree, and therefore

\omega=\omega^{\prime}
.

Assume now that
c,c^{\prime}>0
. By
Section

3.4
, after
composing with time-preserving boosts we may reduce to the case of

S_{(1,\omega)}
and
S_{(1,\omega^{\prime})}
. Since the surfaces are
boost-invariant, any time-preserving boost between them can then be reduced
to a left translation. Both surfaces project to

P=\{x^{2}-y^{2}>1\}\cap\{x>0\}.

The only translation of the Minkowski plane preserving
P
is the trivial
one and therefore the left translation must be
vertical.

On
S_{(1,\omega)}
, the quantity
r=x^{2}-y^{2}
satisfies

r=1+2e^{-\omega t}
. The
z
-coordinate, as a function of

z_{\omega}(r)=\frac{\omega}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r-1}{2}}}\right)-\frac{r-1}{2}+1}}\right).

In other words,
S_{(1,\omega)}
is the graph over

given by

S_{(1,\omega)}=\{(x,y,z_{\omega}(x,y)):(x,y)\in P\}
, and similarly for

S_{(1,\omega^{\prime})}
. If a vertical translation

to

, then

z_{\omega}(x,y)+\lambda=z_{\omega^{\prime}}(x,y)
for every

would be constant on

. This is impossible when
\omega^{\prime}=-\omega
, since then

z_{\omega^{\prime}}=-z_{\omega}
and

is not constant. Thus

\omega=\omega^{\prime}
.
∎

We also obtain the corresponding classification under time-preserving rotations.

Proposition 3.35
.

Two surfaces
S_{(c,\omega c)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
with null

and

, or

.

Assume first that
cc^{\prime}>0
and
\omega=-\omega^{\prime}
. By

Section

3.1
, a timed rotation sends

S_{(c,\omega c)}
to
S_{(c,-\omega c)}
. Since

-\omega=\omega^{\prime}
,

3.4
gives a
time-preserving boost from

to

S_{(c^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
. The composition is a time-preserving rotation. If

c=c^{\prime}=0
, then both parametrisations give the same surface

,
and this surface is invariant under the timed rotation.

Conversely, suppose that
S_{(c,\omega c)}
and

are isometric by a time-preserving rotation.
Composing this isometry with the timed rotation

to
S_{(c^{\prime},-\omega^{\prime}c^{\prime})}
, gives a

to

, either

, or

and

.
∎

We now discuss the causal behaviour of null-parameter surfaces, starting with the case
c\geq 0
.

Proposition 3.36
.

Every surface
S_{(c,\omega c)}
with
c\geq 0
is acausal. In
particular, it is achronal.

Proof.

We first consider
c=0
. In this case

S_{(0,0)}(t,u)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh(t+u),\;\sinh(t+u),\;\frac{t}{2}}}\right),

so the surface is the vertical cylinder over the future branch

\{x^{2}-y^{2}=1,\ x>0\}
. If two points of this surface were joined by a
causal curve, then their projections would be joined by a causal curve in
the Minkowski plane with endpoints on this hyperbola. Since the future branch is acausal in the Minkowski plane, the projected curve
must be constant. But a future-directed causal curve with constant

x
-coordinate is constant, so the two points coincide.

Now let
c>0
. By
Section

3.4
, it is enough to
prove the claim for
S_{(1,\omega)}
. Put
r=x^{2}-y^{2}
and define, on

P=\{r>1,\ x>0\}
,

F_{\omega}(x,y,z)=z-\frac{\omega}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r-1}{2}}}\right)-\frac{r-1}{2}+1}}\right).

The surface
S_{(1,\omega)}
is contained in the zero level set of

F_{\omega}
. A direct computation gives

X(F_{\omega})=-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y-\omega x\frac{r+1}{r-1}}}\right),\qquad Y(F_{\omega})=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-\omega y\frac{r+1}{r-1}}}\right),

and therefore

g\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}F_{\omega},\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}F_{\omega}\bigr)=-\frac{r^{2}}{(r-1)^{2}}<0.

Thus
\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}F_{\omega}
is timelike and has fixed time
orientation on
P
.

Suppose, for contradiction, that two points of
S_{(1,\omega)}
are
joined by a non-constant future-directed causal curve
\gamma
. Its
projection to the Minkowski plane is future-directed causal. Since

P=\{r>1,\ x>0\}
is future-closed in the Minkowski plane, the projected
curve stays in
P
, so
F_{\omega}\circ\gamma
is defined. Moreover

\frac{d}{dt}(F_{\omega}\circ\gamma)(t)=g\bigl(\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}F_{\omega},\dot{\gamma}(t)\bigr)

has a fixed non-zero sign wherever
\dot{\gamma}\neq 0
, because both

\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}F_{\omega}
and
\dot{\gamma}
are causal and

\operatorname{grad}_{\mathbb{H}}F_{\omega}
is timelike. Hence

F_{\omega}\circ\gamma
is strictly monotone. This contradicts the fact that
it vanishes at both endpoints. Therefore no two distinct points of

S_{(1,\omega)}
are causally related.
∎

In contrast, the null-parameter surfaces with
c<0
contain
chronologically related points.

Proposition 3.37
.

Every surface
S_{(c,\omega c)}
with
c<0
is not achronal.

Proof.

Since achronality is invariant under isometries,

Section

3.4
allows us to prove the claim for

S_{(-1,-1)}
. By
Equation

35
, its
parametrisation is

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cosh(t+u)-e^{u},\;\sinh(t+u)-e^{u},\;\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+e^{-t}-1}}\right)}}\right).

The curve obtained by taking
t=0
is

\gamma(a)=(-\sinh a,-\cosh a,0).

We also consider the curve on the surface with
y=0
. Writing

s=e^{u}
, it is parametrised by

\sigma(s)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s},\,0,\,\frac{1}{2}B_{s}}}\right),\qquad s>0,

where

A_{s}=\sqrt{1+s^{2}}-s,\qquad B_{s}=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sqrt{1+s^{-2}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+s^{-2}}}-1.

A direct computation gives

\gamma(a)^{-1}\ast\sigma(s)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sinh a+A_{s},\,\cosh a,\,\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{s}-A_{s}\cosh a}}\right)}}\right).

For fixed

and

sufficiently large, the

-coordinate is
positive and
B_{s}-A_{s}\cosh a<0
. Therefore, using

Equation

,

-x^{2}+y^{2}+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert=1-A_{s}^{2}+2A_{s}e^{-a}-2B_{s}\longrightarrow 1-A_{s}^{2}-2B_{s}\qquad\text{as }a\to+\infty

As
s\to 0^{+}
, we have
A_{s}\to 1
and
B_{s}\to+\infty
, so the last
limit tends to
-\infty
. Hence we may choose

sufficiently small,
and then

sufficiently large, so that

\gamma(a)^{-1}\ast\sigma(s)\in I^{+}(0).

By left-invariance, this means
\gamma(a)\ll\sigma(s)
. Thus the surface
contains chronologically related points, and is not achronal.
∎

We conclude the classification of the surfaces with non-zero horizontal mean
curvature by showing that surfaces whose parameter vectors have different causal
characters are not isometric.

Proposition 3.38
.

Let
S_{(c,d)}
and
S_{(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})}
be two surfaces with non-zero
horizontal mean curvature. If the parameter vectors
(c,d)
and

(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
have different causal characters, then the two surfaces are not
isometric.

Proof.

Isometries preserve regularity and characteristic sets. In particular, a
singular surface of timelike parameters cannot be isometric to a regular surface of spacelike or null
parameters, by
Section

3.3
. We may therefore restrict to
regular surfaces.

First suppose that one surface has spacelike parameters. By

Section

3.2
, its characteristic set is one
horizontal null half-line. A regular surface of timelike parameters has characteristic set
either empty or equal to two disjoint half-lines, by

Section

3.3
. Hence a
surface of spacelike parameters cannot be isometric to a surface of timelike ones.

It remains, in the spacelike case, to compare with null parameters. If the
surface of null parameters has empty characteristic set, then the characteristic set
already distinguishes the two surfaces. Otherwise both characteristic sets
are single half-lines starting from the
z
-axis. Then any isometry
between them must have vertical translation part, and the Lorentz part
preserves the function

f(x,y,z)=x^{2}-y^{2}.

Thus the image of each surface under
f
would have to be the same. This
is impossible: after reducing the surface of spacelike parameters to
S_{(0,d)}
, we
have

f(S_{(0,d)}(t,u))=1-d^{2}-2d\sinh t,

which ranges over all of
\mathbb{R}
, while for a nonzero surface of null parameters

S_{(c,\omega c)}
,

f(S_{(c,\omega c)}(t,u))=1+2ce^{-\omega t},

whose image is bounded on one side by
1
.

Finally consider a regular surface of timelike parameters and a surface of null ones. If their
characteristic sets are not both empty, they are distinguished by

Sections

3.3
and
3.4
.
Thus we only have to consider the case in which both are empty. By

Sections

3.3
and
3.4
, we may
reduce to a surface of timelike parameters
S_{(c,0)}
with
c>0
and to

S_{(1,\omega)}
. Their projections to the
(x,y)
-plane are,
respectively,

\{x^{2}-y^{2}\geq(c+1)^{2}\}\cap\{x>0\},\qquad\{x^{2}-y^{2}>1\}\cap\{x>0\}.

A sub-Lorentzian isometry acts on the
(x,y)
-plane by a Lorentz map
followed by a translation. The Lorentz part preserves
x^{2}-y^{2}
, and a
translation is a homeomorphism. It cannot send a projected region with
boundary to one without boundary. Hence the surface of timelike and null parameters are
not isometric.
∎

\alpha\alpha^{\prime}<0
.

The closures of the two characteristic half-lines of each surface start on
the
z
-axis. Therefore the translation part of the isometry must be vertical.
Since Lorentz maps and vertical translations preserve
x^{2}-y^{2}
, the
maximum value of
x^{2}-y^{2}
on the two surfaces must agree. On

S_{(1,\alpha,\beta)}
, this quantity can be computed at
u=0
:

x^{2}-y^{2}=\bigl(\alpha+t\sinh\beta\bigr)^{2}-t^{2}\cosh^{2}\beta=-\bigl(t-\alpha\sinh\beta\bigr)^{2}+\alpha^{2}\cosh^{2}\beta.

Its maximum is
\alpha^{2}\cosh^{2}\beta
. The same argument for

S_{(1,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
gives the maximum

(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\cosh^{2}\beta^{\prime}
. Hence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert\cosh\beta=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha^{\prime}}}\right\rvert\cosh\beta^{\prime}.

Conversely, assume

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha}}\right\rvert\cosh\beta=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\alpha^{\prime}}}\right\rvert\cosh\beta^{\prime}
. Since
(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,-y,z)
commutes with boosts and sends

\gamma_{(1,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}(t)
to

\gamma_{(1,-\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}(-t)
, it maps

S_{(1,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
onto
S_{(1,-\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
. Therefore, after possibly applying this
isometry, it is enough to prove the claim when

\alpha\cosh\beta=\alpha^{\prime}\cosh\beta^{\prime}.

A vertical translation by
(0,0,-(\alpha^{2}\cosh\beta\sinh\beta)/2)

followed by the change of variables

s=t-\alpha\sinh\beta,\qquad v=u+\beta,

sends
S_{(1,\alpha,\beta)}
to

S_{(1,\alpha\cosh\beta,0)}
:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,0,-\frac{\alpha^{2}\cosh\beta\sinh\beta}{2}}}\right)\ast S_{(1,\alpha,\beta)}(t,u)=S_{(1,\alpha\cosh\beta,0)}(s,v),

where the first two components are simplified using the addition formulae
for
\cosh(v-\beta)
and
\sinh(v-\beta)
.
Applying the same reduction to
S_{(1,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
gives the same
surface, because
\alpha^{\prime}\cosh\beta^{\prime}=\alpha\cosh\beta
.
∎

For maximal surfaces, fixing the time orientation also determines when boost
and rotation isometries can occur.

Corollary 3.42
.

Let
S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}
and

S_{(\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
be two maximal surfaces. They are
isometric by a time-preserving (respectively, time-inverting) boost if and
only if they are isometric by a time-preserving (respectively,
time-inverting) rotation.

Proof.

By
Section

3.5
, and possibly taking
inverse isometries,

S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}
can be mapped onto

S_{(-\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}
both by a time-preserving rotation and
by a vertical translation, the latter being a time-preserving boost.

Suppose, for instance, that

S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}
is isometric to

S_{(\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
by a time-preserving boost
F
. Let

R
be a time-preserving rotation sending

S_{(\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
to

S_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
, and let
V
be a vertical
translation sending
S_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
back to

S_{(\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
. Then
V\circ R\circ F
is a
time-preserving rotation from
S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}
to

S_{(\varepsilon^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}
.

The converse is identical, with boost and rotation interchanged. The same
argument applies to time-inverting isometries, since the auxiliary maps

R
and
V
are time-preserving and therefore do not change whether the
resulting isometry preserves or reverses time orientation.
∎

In the following proposition, we show that all regular maximal surfaces are not achronal.

Proposition 3.43
.

For every
\alpha\neq 0
, the maximal surface
S_{(\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta)}

is not achronal.

Proof.

By

Sections

3.5
and
3.5
,
and since achronality is invariant under isometries, it is enough to prove
the claim for
S_{(1,\alpha,0)}
with
\alpha>0
, using

Equation

21
.
Setting
p=S_{(1,\alpha,0)}(0,0)=(\alpha,0,0)
, it is enough to show that the surface intersects
I^{-}(p)
, since then it
contains a point chronologically preceding
p
. Fix
c<0
and, for
u\neq 0
, choose

t(u)=\frac{c-\alpha\cosh u}{\sinh u},

so that the
x
-coordinate of
S_{(1,\alpha,0)}(t(u),u)
is
c
. Then

S_{(1,\alpha,0)}(t(u),u)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,\,\frac{c\cosh u-\alpha}{\sinh u},\,\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{c-\alpha\cosh u}{\sinh u}}}\right).

Letting
u\to-\infty
, these points converge to

q(c)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,-c,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}}\right),

so
q(c)
belongs to the closure of the surface.

We now check that
q(c)\in I^{-}(p)
for
|c|>0
sufficiently large. By left-invariance, this is equivalent to

q(c)^{-1}\ast p\in I^{+}(0)
. A direct computation gives

q(c)^{-1}\ast p=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha-c,\,c,\,-\frac{\alpha^{2}+\alpha c}{2}}}\right).

Its
x
-coordinate is positive because
c<0
. Moreover, for

c<-\alpha
, we have
\alpha^{2}+\alpha c<0
, and

-x^{2}+y^{2}+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert=-(\alpha-c)^{2}+c^{2}-2(\alpha^{2}+\alpha c)=-3\alpha^{2}<0.

By
Equation

5
,
q(c)^{-1}\ast p\in I^{+}(0)
, and
therefore
q(c)\in I^{-}(p)
.

Since
I^{-}(p)
is open and
q(c)
lies in the closure of the surface,
the surface itself intersects
I^{-}(p)
. Thus it contains a point

∎

Having studied the singular surfaces with positive mean curvature, we now return to the singular maximal surface. By

Section

3.1
, all maximal surfaces with parameter

\alpha=0
have the same image,

\Sigma_{0}=\{(x,y,0):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}\cup\{(0,0,0)\}.

This image is singular at the origin, for the same topological reason as in

Section

3.3
: removing the origin separates the two components

\{y>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}
and
\{y<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}
. One could ask whether the singularity can be removed by extending the image near
the origin. However, any smooth extension connecting the two components

\{y>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}
and
\{y<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}
would have to contain points in the
missing boundary directions
\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}
. These directions are null in
the Minkowski
(x,y)
-plane, since
x^{2}-y^{2}=0
. Thus such an extension would
have null tangent directions and would no longer be spacelike.

Regular maximal surfaces are never achronal by

Section

3.5
. In contrast, the singular maximal
surface is acausal.

Proposition 3.44
.

The singular maximal surface
\Sigma_{0}
is acausal.

Proof.

Let
p=(a,b,0)
and
q=(c,d,0)
be two points of
\Sigma_{0}
, and
suppose that
p\leq q
, i.e.
p^{-1}\ast q\in J^{+}(e)
. Since

p^{-1}\ast q=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c-a,\ d-b,\ \frac{1}{2}(bc-ad)}}\right),

Equation

5
gives

-(c-a)^{2}+(d-b)^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert ad-bc}}\right\rvert\leq 0.

(39)

We prove that
p=q
. If
p=e
, then

Equation

5
applied to
q
forces

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert c}}\right\rvert\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d}}\right\rvert
, so
q=e
. The same argument holds if
q=e
, so
we may assume that both points lie in

\{(x,y,0):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\}
.

The two points cannot lie in different components of this set. Indeed,
suppose without loss of generality that
b>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a}}\right\rvert
and
d<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert c}}\right\rvert
. Then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert d-b}}\right\rvert=b-d>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a}}\right\rvert+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert c}}\right\rvert\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert c-a}}\right\rvert,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad-(c-a)^{2}+(d-b)^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert ad-bc}}\right\rvert>0,

contradicting
Equation

39
.

We therefore assume that
p
and
q
lie in the same connected
component.
Applying the timed rotation
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,-z)
if
necessary, we may assume
b>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a}}\right\rvert
and

R=b+a,\qquad S=b-a,\qquad R^{\prime}=d+c,\qquad S^{\prime}=d-c.

Then

, and a computation gives

\displaystyle-(c-a)^{2}+(d-b)^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert ad-bc}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=(R^{\prime}-R)(S^{\prime}-S)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert RS^{\prime}-SR^{\prime}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=RS+R^{\prime}S^{\prime}-2\min\{RS^{\prime},SR^{\prime}\}.

the quantity in

non-negative, and equality holds only when

and
S=S^{\prime}
. Together with

39
, this forces equality in

Equation

R=R^{\prime}

so
a=c
and
b=d
.
∎
