Intelligent Automation Improved Efficiency in Pharmacovigilance Safety Signal Assessment
Authors: Jeffrey Warner, Luis Henrique De Souza Teodoro, Anaclara Prada Jardim, Claudia Albera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pharmacovigilance is the science involving the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse events associated with drugs, biologics, or medical devices. In pharmacovigilance, information that suggests a new potential causal relationship between an intervention and an adverse event is called a safety signal. Following their detection, safety signals are assessed via a comprehensive, structured analysis to more fully elucidate whether a correlation exists. This key process often requires the manual assessment of many individual case safety report (ICSR) narratives to extract meaningful information in a labor‐intensive, time‐consuming, and variability‐prone manner. In this retrospective feasibility study, we describe the potential utility of an intelligent automation system leveraging the GPT‐4o large language model to automate the extraction of case elements of interest from a series of ICSR narratives while maintaining human expert oversight. Our proprietary platform allowed users to extract the presence or absence of risk factors and responses to dechallenge and rechallenge via instructional prompts built on a common template structure. Case elements for five historical signal assessments were selected based on need for and feasibility of artificial intelligence extraction. Performance ranged from F1 = 0.444 to 1.000 for risk factors and from F1 = 0.429 to 0.909 for responses to dechallenge and rechallenge. Even when considering the need to verify GPT‐4o outputs for accuracy, potential time savings were identified. To the best of our knowledge, our results are the first to demonstrate an intelligent automation platform that may streamline signal management workflows using a machine‐first, human‐verified operational workflow while maintaining regulatory compliance.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing (NLP) have been applied in pharmacovigilance to automate tasks such as MedDRA coding, duplicate detection, and case triage. These approaches improve efficiency but generally focus on structured outputs or prioritization rather than extracting clinical elements from unstructured narratives.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

Can an intelligent automation platform leveraging a large language model (LLM) accurately and efficiently extract key causality‐related elements (e.g., dechallenge, rechallenge, and risk factors) from individual case safety report (ICSR) narratives to support signal assessment while maintaining expert oversight?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Our retrospective feasibility study suggested that an LLM‐powered system (leveraging GPT‐4o) can perform multiplexed extraction of clinically relevant case elements from unstructured ICSR narratives within a human‐in‐the‐loop framework, achieving high accuracy and potentially reducing review time expenditure compared with manual assessment.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

By enabling rapid, structured extraction of critical causality information from ICSRs, this approach may help accelerate signal assessment and improve the timeliness of safety decisions, representing a potential step toward AI‐augmented signal management.

Pharmacovigilance is the regulated science involving the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse events to support a favorable benefit–risk balance. 1 , 2 One goal of pharmacovigilance is the detection and management of safety signals (information that suggests a new, potentially causal association between an intervention and an adverse event or a new aspect of a known adverse drug reaction). 3 , 4 Signal assessment is a critical step in signal management involving a comprehensive evaluation to confirm the presence of a new risk or new aspect of a known risk of a medicinal product. 3 , 4 This process leverages multiple evidence sources including individual case safety reports (ICSRs), clinical trial data, pharmacoepidemiologic studies, nonclinical findings, and scientific literature. 3 , 4 , 5 At the ICSR level, reviewers examine factors such as temporality, dose responsiveness, and outcomes of dechallenge or rechallenge, while accounting for alternative explanations and confounders. 3 , 4 Often, manual ICSR narrative review is required to extract case elements which are not hard‐coded in the sponsor's safety database and therefore cannot be queried programmatically. This review is labor‐intensive, time‐consuming, and variability‐prone. 6 , 7 These limitations have sparked growing interest in innovative approaches including artificial intelligence (AI) and intelligent automation, which aim to reliably extract and structure key information to improve efficiency and consistency without compromising scientific rigor. 8 , 9

These technologies are revolutionizing many industries including pharmacovigilance, where AI solutions are employed for data generation, data structuring for ICSR intake, deduplication, causality assessment, and automated reporting, among others. 6 , 7 , 8 Intelligent automation represents a progression beyond simple rule‐based automations, robotic process automation, and natural language processing (NLP) by incorporating AI components such as large language models (LLMs) to enable greater flexibility in processing unstructured data. However, the application of intelligent automation to support signal assessment remains underexplored, particularly for narrative‐based case element extraction. 7 , 8 , 10 Herein, we conducted a retrospective feasibility study to determine whether a proprietary intelligent automation system leveraging an LLM could accurately and efficiently extract key causality‐related elements from ICSR narratives to support signal assessment while maintaining expert oversight.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data acquisition

Given data limitations from publicly available ICSR data, current de‐identified ICSR data from five representative historical signal assessments was retrieved from Lilly's safety database for three blinded products using MedDRA v28.0 (Table 1 ). Risk factors to extract were identified in a topic‐by‐topic manner and were assessed in a binary fashion (present or absent). This study focused on risk factors which would traditionally require manual extraction. Therefore, concomitant medications were excluded, as they are typically hard‐coded to a searchable standard dictionary. Where applicable, dechallenge and rechallenge were assessed in a tertiary fashion (positive de‐/rechallenge, negative de‐/rechallenge, or not applicable/unknown) then coded to binary outcomes for metrics calculation. Dechallenge and rechallenge assessment did not consider pharmacokinetics nor the use of treatment medications.

Intelligent automation platform

A proprietary platform with multiplex AI prompting capability was developed to extract case elements from ICSR narratives by integrating a configurable prompting interface with the GPT‐4o LLM. Briefly, users input free text prompts to instruct the LLM to extract the desired case element. Prompts used in this study generally followed a templated framework consisting of a role and task assignment, case element details, and standard instructions (Figure S1 ). Prompts were concatenated with ICSR narratives to form the complete prompts provided to GPT‐4o. LLM outputs were forced to a binary response via system prompts (“Yes” for presence and “No” for absence of a case element) and provided in a tabular, case‐by‐case format.

Test case analysis

ICSR narratives from the five test cases described above were assessed in two steps: first, a manual human review and case element extraction was performed to determine true‐positives and true negatives. Subsequently, LLM prompts (per case element) were input alongside ICSR narratives to the platform. Human values were neither provided nor considered by the platform. LLM case element extraction results were coded to true‐positive, true‐negative, false‐positive, and false‐negative categories by comparing against human values.

LLM performance assessment

For risk factors, true‐positives signified instances where both the human and LLM assessed the risk factor as present; false‐positives signified instances where the LLM assessed the risk factor as present, but the human as absent, and so on. Dechallenge and rechallenge were split into four distinct elements to enable calculation of F1 score: positive dechallenge, negative dechallenge, positive rechallenge, and negative rechallenge. Accuracy and F1 score were calculated (F1 score does not incorporate true negatives). Where lack of true‐positives made F1 score calculation not possible, only accuracy was presented.

Prompt iteration

Where initial performance was deemed insufficient, prompts were iteratively improved by studying the false results to determine whether discrepancies were attributable to human or LLM misclassification. For human errors, the correct classification was recorded, and metrics were recalculated. For LLM errors, the corresponding narrative was manually reassessed to identify contributing factors (e.g., ambiguous phrasing, negation, or temporal qualifiers). Then, modifications were made to the Case Element section of the prompt (Figure S1 ), the case element was reassessed in the platform, and metrics were recalculated.

Time savings measurement

To roughly estimate time savings afforded by the platform, the reviewer of the Product C dysphonia topic performed the case review twice while recording time expenditure. To reduce bias and “reset” the reviewer's memory of the ICSR narratives, the reviews were conducted 3 weeks apart—the unassisted review first with ICSRs sorted alphabetically followed by the platform‐assisted review with ICSRs sorted randomly.

RESULTS

We identified five historical safety topics across blinded Lilly products totaling 1,115 ICSRs ranging from 69 to 697 cases per topic (Table 1 ). Two topics (interstitial lung disease and dysphonia) were deemed eligible for dechallenge and rechallenge assessment based on the topics' medical natures. Where assessable, F1 scores ranged from 0.444 to 1.000; accuracy values ranged from 94.32% to 100.00% (Table 2 ). For some case elements, sufficient performance was achieved on the first attempt; however, others required refinement. For example, in the Product A interstitial lung disease topic, the first prompt for the smoking risk factor performed at F1 = 1.00. By contrast, the inhaled irritant exposure prompt required three iterations, with the first prompt yielding an F1 score of 0.58 with 19 false‐positives, the second improving to F1 = 0.96 with 1 false‐positive, and the third producing the same F1 score, suggesting a performance plateau (Table S1 ).

Dechallenge and rechallenge extraction performance was evaluated for products A–C (Table 2 ). For the reviews of interstitial lung disease, for Product A, positive dechallenge extraction achieved F1 = 0.788 with an accuracy of 86.21%, while negative dechallenge yielded an F1 = 0.746 and 95.20% accuracy. Positive rechallenge demonstrated an F1 = 0.667 (accuracy 98.81%), whereas negative rechallenge showed lower performance with F1 = 0.429 and 90.51% accuracy. The interstitial lung disease review for Product B included a smaller ICSR count with fewer true‐positives, preventing our ability to compute F1 scores for all but positive dechallenge, which demonstrated a similar performance (F1 = 0.643 vs. 0.667 for Product B and A, respectively). Regarding negative dechallenge, accuracy was within 5%, and for both positive and negative rechallenge, accuracy was within 1% between products B and A. Lastly, for the Product C dysphonia review, positive dechallenge extraction achieved an F1 score of 0.909 with 98.55% accuracy. Negative dechallenge and positive and negative rechallenge demonstrated 89.86%, 97.10%, and 100% accuracy, respectively.

A rough time savings estimation exercise was performed for the Product C dysphonia review (n = 69 ICSRs). Manual ICSR review required 395 minutes (~6.6 hours) at an average of ~5.56 minutes per ICSR. The platform‐assisted review spanned 145 minutes (~2.4 hours) at an average of ~2.05 minutes per ICSR: a ~63.1% time savings.

DISCUSSION

We developed an intelligent automation platform leveraging GPT‐4o, a foundational LLM with general domain knowledge, to automate the extraction of relevant case elements from ICSR narratives with a human‐in‐the‐loop. Our retrospective feasibility experiments demonstrated high (albeit variable) extraction performance and suggested decreased time expenditure while maintaining expert oversight in a range of representative topics, aligning with benefits demonstrated by others in pharmacovigilance workflow optimizations, 11 electronic health record data extraction, 12 and ICSR prioritization. 13 Others also explored deep learning frameworks for duplicate detection and predictive signal analysis 14 and Bayesian networks for causality assessment. 7 These approaches advanced automation in pharmacovigilance but focused on case coding, prioritization, or causality scoring. To our knowledge, our platform is the first to leverage an LLM‐powered system for signal assessment.

The human‐first, machine‐second review paradigm leveraged here was useful experimentally, but operationally, we propose the opposite: a first‐pass LLM case element extraction followed by a case‐by‐case human verification to correct misclassifications in an additional column to maintain audit history. As prompts would be untested upon first use, users must consider previously used prompts (perhaps in a prompt library) for similar case elements, or more likely, leverage generalizable prompt templates, avoiding iterated prompts that may be overfitted to a specific use case thereby decreasing their cross‐topic utility. This workflow maintains expert control of risk factor definition, prompt design, verification, and final safety decisions, consistent with established signal management principles 3 , 4 and AI guidance. 6 A human‐in‐the‐loop approach is key because LLMs including GPT‐4o are prone to occasional classification errors. 15 Indeed, AI performance varied across case elements; the lowest F1 score (0.444, accuracy 94.32%) was observed for head, neck, or total body radiotherapy in hypothyroidism cases, where incomplete documentation and ambiguous narrative language were common. For all other risk factors, performance exceeded 97.73%, with 10 achieving 100% accuracy. Performance was influenced by clinical complexity; for example, identifying tobacco use was simpler than interpreting dechallenge or rechallenge responses. These latter elements required more descriptive prompts and more iterations achieving F1 scores of only ~0.43–0.8 (accuracy > 84%). Notably, prompts targeted potential rather than definitive dechallenge/rechallenge, excluding pharmacokinetic considerations and use of treatment medications, 16 sidestepping the integration of multiple clinical variables (timing, dose adjustments, and corrective therapies) which may prove more difficult yet for an LLM. This complexity is well recognized in causality frameworks like the WHO‐UMC system. 17 While the LLM may not perform perfectly, neither do expert clinicians. A retrospective examination of the hypothyroidism review discovered nine human misclassifications (roughly 2%) which were “caught” by GPT‐4o. Indeed, in summary, LLM–human collaboration is poised to improve performance compared with either party alone. 18 , 19

Performance aside, one potential benefit of our platform may be time savings. Other studies suggest that LLM support improves clinician task performance in complex, time‐bounded scenarios, 20 including ICSR processing. 21 , 22 Despite the need for human verification, our platform allowed users to focus on narrative content rather than notation. Importantly, our time savings data must be considered preliminary and explorative only due to methodological weaknesses coupled with the fact that there may be considerable differences in time savings topic‐by‐topic based on inter‐reviewer variability, database differences, and other factors. Our study had other limitations: among our selected safety topics, some case elements had few or no true‐positives, limiting our ability to examine the platform's ability to identify “hits.” That said, real‐world signal assessments may indeed include very few meaningful cases, and the ability of the platform to faithfully return “no” is important. Additionally, LLM understanding of case narratives may vary between enterprises due to differences in internal narrative writing practices, format, and quality. Also important is the development of generalizable prompt templates rather than validated prompts tied to a specific LLM, ensuring continuity as LLM technology rapidly evolves. A recent review identified 43 unique LLMs released between October 2019 and May 2023. 23 Prompts used in this study may not suit later LLMs such as the newer GPT5, for example. Despite these limitations, our data support the potential application of AI in ICSR evaluation while maintaining a human‐in‐control approach aligned with regulatory guidance with expert oversight throughout. Future research should assess comparative performance of new LLMs and whether this technology can automate the assessment of other case elements or data reviews outside signal assessment. With proper human oversight, signal management workflows may be well‐positioned to incorporate AI, affording safety teams more agility in the AI age.
