Water, Hygiene and Inequality: Keys to Understanding and Combating the Current Cholera Epidemic in the Democratic Republic of Congo—A Narrative Review
Authors: Hermann Yokolo, Ahmed Tayebelasma, Gaaitri Lohano, Basma Khawar Khan, Ayesha Junaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimThe Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) is among the countries most affected by cholera in the world. Since the 1970s, endemic foci have persisted in the eastern lake provinces, where chronic armed conflict, massive population displacement, and extreme socioeconomic inequalities create particularly favorable conditions for transmission. Despite a national elimination plan involving more than 22 ministries, the epidemic continues: 46,800 cases and 1362 deaths were recorded as of August 2025. This review synthesizes recent data on the structural determinants, public health responses, and persistent barriers that characterize cholera in the DRC.MethodsThe PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases were searched for publications from 2015 to 2025. Grey literature from WHO, UNICEF, MSF, Africa CDC, and the DRC Ministry of Health was also considered. Main themes were defined a priori.ResultsCholera transmission is facilitated by insufficient access to safe drinking water, poor sanitation, and socioeconomic fragility exacerbated by conflict and population displacement. Case fatality rates vary considerably from one province to another, reaching 11.1% in some areas. The collapse of the health system, community mistrust, and vaccine hesitancy further compromise the effectiveness of interventions. Vaccination campaigns and water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions have demonstrated a measurable impact but remain insufficient when implemented in isolation.ConclusionThe sustainable fight against cholera in the DRC requires an integrated approach combining long‐term investments in WASH, health system strengthening, community engagement, and equity‐oriented policies in order to break the cycle of transmission in this particularly complex context.Keywords: cholera, Democratic Republic of the Congo, epidemic, inequalities, WASH services

Introduction

Cholera remains a worrying global health emergency in 2025, with more than a billion people exposed, while approximately 305,903 cases and 3522 deaths have already been reported in 28 countries between January 1 and June 29, 2025 [1]. These figures are part of a broader upward trend; in 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) recorded more than 751,400 cases and more than 5200 deaths worldwide [2]. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), cholera is a recurring challenge. Since the 1970s, endemic foci have persisted, particularly in the eastern lakeside areas, which are widely considered epicenters for the spread of epidemics to other regions [3]. Over the period 2000–2021, multisectoral cholera elimination plans (MCEPs) documented more than 520,000 suspected cases and 12,561 deaths, highlighting the need for sustainable strategies in high‐risk provinces such as North Kivu, South Kivu, Tanganyika, Haut‐Lomami, and Haut‐Katanga [4].

What makes the cholera crisis in the DRC particularly unique is the convergence of several aggravating factors rarely encountered simultaneously in other endemic contexts. First, the country hosts one of the world's largest populations of internally displaced persons, with 2.4 million in North Kivu alone as of September 2024, creating densely overcrowded settlements where sanitation is virtually absent [5]. Second, the chronic armed conflict in the eastern provinces has systematically destroyed health infrastructure, disrupted water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) systems, and hampered humanitarian access, rendering conventional intervention models largely ineffective [6]. Third, the extreme provincial disparities in case fatality rates reflect deep‐seated structural inequalities in access to healthcare that persist despite successive national elimination plans [7]. Finally, the DRC's unique geographical position, at the heart of the African Great Lakes region, makes its endemic foci a persistent regional reservoir, fueling cross‐border transmission to neighboring countries [8]. Together, these factors explain why the DRC consistently accounts for a disproportionate share of cholera cases worldwide, despite decades of international commitment.

The objective of this narrative review is to analyze the structural determinants, primarily related to access to drinking water, hygiene, and socioeconomic inequalities, that perpetuate community vulnerability to cholera in the DRC. By combining recent epidemiological data, endemic histories, and infrastructure issues, this synthesis aims to shed light on levers for sustainable prevention. A better understanding of these dynamics is essential for guiding public policies, strengthening local capacities, and fueling collective mobilization around an effective response to this persistent threat.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize recent evidence on the ongoing cholera outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), focusing on contextual determinants, public health responses, and long‐term control prospects. The key themes guiding this review—namely, the epidemiology of cholera in the DRC, socioeconomic and environmental factors of transmission, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) factors, and prevention and control strategies—were defined prior to the literature search to ensure a structured and focused approach to synthesizing the evidence.

A systematic literature search was conducted in three electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The following keywords were used independently: “cholera,” “Democratic Republic of the Congo,” “outbreak,” “outbreak,” “public health response,” and “WASH interventions.” The search was limited to publications appearing between 2015 and 2025 to capture the most recent epidemiological trends and intervention data. Peer‐reviewed articles were included if they addressed the epidemiology of cholera in the DRC, the socioeconomic and environmental factors contributing to its spread, or prevention and control strategies. Publications focusing exclusively on other countries or on microbiological aspects unrelated to the DRC context were excluded. In addition to peer‐reviewed literature, relevant grey literature was included, notably reports and official documents from the World Health Organization (WHO), the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), Doctors Without Borders (MSF), the DRC Ministry of Health, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC), the Global Cholera Alliance, and the International Rescue Committee (IRC). These institutional sources were selected for their recognized authority on cholera surveillance, humanitarian response, and public health policies in the DRC.

Given the narrative nature of this review, no formal quality assessment tools were applied. Source selection was based on their relevance to the predefined themes and the scientific or institutional credibility of the publications. All relevant sources identified during the search were included. No additional selection or screening procedures were applied beyond the criteria described above.

Current State of the Epidemic in the DRC

Recent data indicate a significant increase in the prevalence of cholera in the DRC. Between January 1 and July 31, 2025, 40,487 cholera cases were reported, of whom 44% were under 15 years of age, and 41% were women, along with more than 1100 deaths in 18 of the 26 provinces, according to the Africa CDC [9]. In several coastal localities of the Great Lakes region, cases are reported almost continuously, with particularly affected provinces such as Tanganyika, Haut‐Katanga, South Kivu, Maniema, North Kivu, and Tshopo [10]. As of August 10, 2025, the DRC had recorded 46,800 cases and 1362 deaths, representing a case fatality rate of 2.9%. Cases have been reported in 16 provinces across the country, with Kwango being the most affected since Week 31 after the start of the epidemic. During Week 32, 1887 new cases and 84 deaths were reported, representing a decrease of 4.8% and 1.2%, respectively, compared to the previous week [9, 10].

The recent geographic distribution shows a high concentration of cases in the west and east of the country: over the past 4 weeks, 57.4% of all cases were recorded in the provinces of Kinshasa, North Kivu, South Kivu, and Tshopo. The capital, Kinshasa, has been particularly affected, with 1781 cases and 136 deaths (case fatality rate of 8%). However, a downward trend was observed during Week 31: Kinshasa reported 126 cases and 5 deaths, with a weekly case fatality rate of 4%, lower than in previous weeks [10, 11] (Figure 1).

Taken together, these data paint a worrying picture: despite a slight downward trend observed during epidemiological Weeks 31 and 32, the cholera burden in the DRC remains structurally and persistently high, with marked geographical disparities that reflect deep‐rooted vulnerabilities rather than temporary fluctuations. The concentration of cases in the eastern provinces affected by the conflict and in Kinshasa underscores the persistent role of structural risk factors that go well beyond seasonal or environmental variations.

Structural Determinants of the Epidemic

Limited Access to Drinking Water

In cholera‐endemic areas of the DRC, unreliable water supply remains a major structural driver. Intermittent piped water supply forces households to rely on unsafe alternatives, adopt risky storage practices, and reduce hygiene behaviors. Supply interruptions also create low‐pressure conditions in piped networks, facilitating the entry of pathogens into the water supply and further degrading water quality. Evidence shows that inconsistent access to improved water sources is directly linked to microbial contamination at the point of use [12]. In Goma, Maisha et al. reported that limited infrastructure compels residents to rely on unsafe sources such as Lake Kivu, Lake Vert, and the Mubambiro River [13]. Similarly, a time‐series study in Uvira (2009–2014) showed that a single day without tap supply increased cholera incidence by 155% over 12 days, with nearly one‐quarter of cases attributable to interruptions [14]. An intervention trial in Uvira further demonstrated that higher water service quality, quantity, and continuity reduced cholera risk: each additional 5 L per user per day or 1.2 h of daily supply lowered confirmed cases by 24%–25%. These findings highlight that continuous, sufficient piped water supply is one of the most effective strategies to reduce endemic cholera [14].

Hygiene and Sanitation Deficiencies

Hygiene and sanitation gaps drive cholera transmission in the DRC. Household members of cholera patients face nearly a 100‐fold higher infection risk during the first week after a case is admitted, highlighting how poor hygiene accelerates spread [15]. The Preventative‐Intervention‐for‐Cholera‐for‐7‐days (PICHA7) program aimed to reduce this risk through health education, simple WASH kits (handwashing station, safe water container, chlorine tablets), and weekly mobile reminders. Barriers like low awareness, soap cost, and irregular water access were addressed by promoting alternatives such as soapy water or ash and improving water storage. Pilot results showed high acceptability, suggesting targeted WASH interventions can curb household transmission during high‐risk periods [15]. At a broader level, innovative hygiene strategies effectively contain outbreaks. During the 2017–2018 Kinshasa epidemic, a cluster grid response targeted case households and surrounding communities with emergency water supply, household disinfection, and hygiene promotion. Within 4 weeks, weekly cases dropped 71% across Kinshasa Province, demonstrating the impact of targeted WASH interventions in dense urban areas [16].

Socio‐Economic Inequalities and Vulnerabilities

Cholera disproportionately affects low‐ and middle‐income countries, where fragile social structures, poverty, and weak healthcare systems facilitate rapid spread and high mortality. Conflict is a major determinant, disrupting services, reducing household income, and forcing displacement [17]. In the DRC, one of the world's poorest nations, poverty, inadequate sanitation, and unsafe water contribute to recurring outbreaks of malaria, typhoid, cholera, and shigellosis. In 2022, 21 million malaria cases with 13,000 deaths occurred, while 2023 saw over 31,000 cholera cases, largely affecting children. These epidemics reflect deep inequalities in healthcare, water, and sanitation access, highlighting the need for sustainable water safety and sanitation systems [17]. Conflict has been shown to increase cholera risk 2.6‐fold, with 12.3% of outbreaks directly attributable to conflict settings, emphasizing the need for rapid humanitarian response alongside long‐term mitigation of vulnerabilities [18]. Geospatial analyses show highest incidence in conflict‐affected provinces, with spillover to surrounding areas due to population displacement, as displaced individuals may asymptomatically carry and spread the pathogen through reliance on contaminated water sources [17]. Socioeconomic fragility, displacement, and inequitable resource access thus amplify cholera vulnerability in conflict‐prone regions (Table 1).

Health and Socioeconomic Burden of Cholera in the DRC

Morbidity and Mortality

Cholera imposes a devastating burden on the DRC, contributing markedly to the estimated 3.0 million global cholera deaths from 1990 to 2019 [19, 20]. In the past, the country reported 500,670 cases and 11,560 deaths from 2000 to 2013, with a national case fatality rate (CFR) of 2.3% and striking provincial differences, such as a CFR of 11.1% in Lubumbashi in 2002 [7]. The crisis remains serious, with over 185,000 cases mentioned in 2023 and a severe outbreak in North Kivu in early 2025, which saw nearly 600 possible cases and 14 fatalities in just 17 days [13, 21]. Multi‐country monitoring further confirms CFRs in the region varying from 0% to 10% [22], underscoring the persistent lethality of cholera across the region.

Health System Collapse and Disruption of Care

The healthcare system is severely strained by the dual burden of cholera and conflict. The collapse of over 70 healthcare facilities has destroyed infrastructure, leaving the remaining Cholera Treatment Centers (CTCs) dangerously overwhelmed and under‐resourced [7]. These CTCs often lack essential medical supplies, electricity, and even clean water, threatening patient care and turning treatment sites into possible sites for transmission [13]. This systemic breakdown is further exacerbated by violence against health workers, creating a critical vacuum in qualified staff and leaving the population with critically limited access to lifesaving care.

Socioeconomic Disruption and Community‐Level Consequences

Cholera in the DRC is both a consequence and a driver of socioeconomic collapse. In Goma, approximately 70% of the population lacks access to safe drinking water, forcing reliance on the contaminated Lake Kivu as a primary water source [13]. Armed conflict has displaced nearly 1 million people into overcrowded urban settlements, where inadequate sanitation and extreme population density substantially accelerate transmission. From an economic standpoint, recurrent outbreaks disproportionately affect livelihood‐dependent groups such as fishermen and market vendors, whose occupational activities simultaneously increase their exposure to contaminated water and render them particularly vulnerable to income disruption during epidemic periods. At the community level, the recurrence of outbreaks has eroded public trust in formal health interventions, fostering reliance on ineffective home remedies and reinforcing a vicious cycle in which cholera perpetuates poverty, weakens social cohesion, and further undermines the community resilience needed to break the chain of transmission [7, 13].

Collectively, the health and socio‐economic consequences of cholera in the DRC go far beyond immediate morbidity and mortality. The cumulative effects of the collapse of the health system, population displacements, and economic disruptions create a vicious cycle where poverty fuels transmission and repeated epidemics exacerbate poverty, a dynamic that fundamentally challenges short‐term, disease‐focused response strategies.

Public Health Response: Governance, Vaccination, and WASH Interventions

National Governance and International Partnership Frameworks

The Government of the DRC has operationalized a comprehensive, multisectoral strategy to mitigate cholera epidemics across its provinces. This approach integrates disease surveillance, WASH interventions, social mobilization, clinical treatment, and the deployment of oral cholera vaccines [23]. To enhance these efforts, the government collaborates with multiple international partners, including the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), the International Organization for Migration (IOM), Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF), United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), the World Food Programme (WFP), and Save the Children [23]. The overarching objective of these partnerships is to strengthen the response to cholera outbreaks in the context of ongoing conflicts. These collaborative efforts have facilitated the successful mass administration of oral cholera vaccines and have contributed to a more comprehensive understanding of outbreak dynamics and response coordination [24, 25].

Oral Cholera Vaccine Deployment and Campaign Strategies

The DRC has conducted numerous vaccination campaigns, dating as far back as September 2016. These campaigns have been consistently implemented in response to recurrent cholera outbreaks, with the most recent occurring as recently as April 2025 [26]. In January 2023, vaccination efforts targeted three health zones (HZs), achieving partial coverage of 96.4% and immunizing 351,207 individuals aged 1 year and older. That same year marked a significant transition from the traditional two‐dose vaccine regimen to a single‐dose schedule, thereby contributing substantially to alleviating the global vaccine shortage for the DRC and other affected countries [27].

WASH Infrastructure and Community‐Based Hygiene Interventions

An Infection Prevention and Control (IPC)/WASH commission has been established by the North Kivu Provincial Health Division (PHD) and is supported by partners, including the WHO and UNICEF. Trained teams systematically carry out decontamination of households and public spaces, while specialized training for safe and dignified burials is also conducted. In internally displaced persons (IDP) sites, a total of 1706 latrines, 293 showers, 91 handwashing stations, and 62 well sites have been established, with a regular water supply ensured through cisterns. Healthcare and hygiene workers receive ongoing training in IPC practices [23]. Additionally, in response to a severe outbreak in South Kivu, a water treatment facility, 100 latrines, and 25 supervised handwashing points were recently established [27]. The implementation of these programs was prompted by a dramatic population influx from 1500 to 12,000 individuals in the affected site, which resulted in severe overcrowding and a complete lack of waste management [9].

Limitations of Interventions and Persistent Challenges

Deficiencies in Health Infrastructure and Service Delivery

Many health facilities in cholera hotspots such as North Kivu and Tanganyika provinces are ill‐equipped to carry out surveillance, diagnosis, and case management. A recent mixed‐methods study conducted by Schedwin et al. in these provinces found that only about half of the facilities with access to a supposedly “safe” water source had water actually available when needed, undermining both patient care and infection control. Provider knowledge was variable and often low, especially among drug‐shop vendors and traditional practitioners [28]. Such weaknesses delay recognition and response to outbreaks, reduce the effectiveness of oral rehydration therapy and case management, and contribute to higher case‐fatality rates.

Armed Conflict, Displacement, and Disruption of Health Systems

In eastern DRC, persistent armed conflicts have led to large‐scale displacement, repeated assaults on health infrastructure, and restricted access to vital health services. By the end of 2022, North Kivu alone hosted nearly 1.9 million internally displaced persons (IDPs), with an additional 450,857 newly displaced due to conflict, most of whom were living in overcrowded camps with poor sanitation and limited safe water [29]. Such conditions sustain a fragile context that heightens cholera transmission risks. For instance, in North Kivu, many health centers have been destroyed or rendered non‐functional, and WHO has documented recurrent attacks on hospital infrastructure, further eroding access to care in areas experiencing rising cholera incidence. Conflict also means that preventive WASH infrastructure, already fragile, faces severe resource limitations and cannot be adequately maintained or expanded, further compounding cholera risks [24, 29, 30].

Community Distrust, Vaccine Hesitancy, and Misinformation

Despite the availability of interventions, participation may be constrained by suspicion of health authorities and Non‐Governmental Organizations (NGOs), vaccine‐related myths or concerns, and cultural interpretations of disease causation that conflict with biomedical perspectives. A recent community‐based survey in Bukavu highlighted widespread hesitancy toward the oral cholera vaccine: over 80% of respondents indicated reluctance to accept it, noting apprehensions regarding vaccine safety, religious considerations, and limited familiarity with the vaccine [31]. In Goma, a recent qualitative study by Maisha et al. highlighted local perceptions that natural water bodies, such as Lake Kivu, were often viewed as safe or even “immune‐building.” The study also noted that while chlorine use was promoted, barriers to effective and sustained disinfection were primarily financial and logistical, rather than stemming from mistrust of chlorination itself. Furthermore, communities expressed skepticism that NGO‐led interventions were short‐term “emergency” measures rather than long‐term solutions [13].

Toward Sustainable Cholera Control: Integrated Strategies and Policy Recommendations

To effectively combat the cholera epidemic in the DRC, it is imperative to adopt a comprehensive and sustainable approach focused on infrastructure strengthening, community mobilization, multisectoral coordination, and enhanced epidemiological surveillance.

Long‐Term Investment in Safe Water Access and Sanitation Infrastructure

Access to safe drinking water and adequate sanitation facilities remains insufficient, particularly in conflict zones such as North Kivu. Targeted interventions, such as the PICHA7 program, have shown a significant reduction in cholera transmission through mobile WASH kits and community training [15]. It is essential to invest in sustainable infrastructure, such as drinking water networks and community sanitation systems, while ensuring their maintenance in high‐risk areas.

Community Engagement and the Role of Local Leadership

Community participation is essential for cholera prevention and control in the DRC, where mistrust of institutions and cultural interpretations of the disease's causes are significant barriers to the adoption of biomedical interventions. Initiatives led by community health workers have proven effective in raising awareness about hygiene and sanitation practices in the DRC [32, 33]. However, sustained community engagement requires moving beyond awareness campaigns and toward a model of genuine co‐ownership of the response. In practical terms, this means systematically integrating traditional leaders, religious authorities, and women's associations into the design and implementation of cholera prevention programs, as these actors possess a social legitimacy that official health authorities often lack in isolated and conflict‐affected communities. In areas where vaccine hesitancy exceeds 80%, as documented in Bukavu [28], community‐led dialog sessions have proven more effective in addressing vaccine safety concerns and religious considerations. Furthermore, given that financial and logistical barriers, rather than ideological resistance, are the primary obstacles to implementing sustainable hygiene practices such as chlorination [9], community microfinance mechanisms and subsidized distribution of WASH supplies should be explored as complementary strategies to hygiene promotion in high‐risk areas.

Multisectoral and Integrated Approaches

The DRC's National Cholera Elimination Plan adopts a multisectoral approach involving more than 22 ministries, recognizing that the determinants of cholera extend far beyond the health sector alone [34]. This strategy should be expanded nationwide, integrating the agriculture, education, and environment sectors to ensure a more coherent and sustainable response. This perspective is supported by recent data highlighting that vaccination‐based strategies, while useful, are insufficient when implemented in isolation. Khan and Kamil demonstrated that, despite a significant reduction in cholera incidence following single‐dose oral cholera vaccination in several endemic countries, including the DRC, the impact on mortality remained limited, emphasizing that health system failures, and not just gaps in prevention, are a major cause of cholera deaths [35]. The authors advocate a comprehensive and equitable approach in which investments in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure are systematically integrated with vaccination efforts, with a recommended allocation of at least 30% of cholera response budgets to WASH by 2026 [35]. This is fully consistent with the multisectoral framework advocated in the DRC's national plan and underscores the urgent need to move from short‐term, disease‐focused responses to structurally sustainable cholera control strategies.

Strengthening Epidemiological Surveillance and Local Research Capacity

Strengthening epidemiological surveillance is essential for the rapid detection of outbreaks and the timely adaptation of interventions in the DRC. However, current surveillance capacities are severely limited by the same structural vulnerabilities that facilitate cholera transmission: health facilities in conflict‐affected provinces often lack the staff, communication resources, and supplies necessary for systematic case reporting, resulting in significant underreporting and distorting the true epidemiological picture [25]. Addressing this gap requires targeted investments in three interdependent areas. First, decentralized sentinel surveillance networks should be established in high‐risk health zones, enabling real‐time data collection even in areas with limited infrastructure. Second, the integration of genomic surveillance into routine epidemiological surveys, as demonstrated by studies in Bukavu that clarified transmission dynamics and guided vaccine deployment strategies [36], should be extended to other endemic provinces, particularly those along the Great Lakes corridor, where cross‐border transmission is documented. Third, and crucially, it is imperative to strengthen local research capacity by training Congolese epidemiologists and public health specialists, funding DRC‐led research initiatives, and systematically integrating local researchers into study design and publication. A sustainable fight against cholera cannot rely solely on externally conducted research; it requires the establishment of a robust national scientific infrastructure capable of producing, interpreting, and utilizing locally relevant evidence.

Conclusion

The cholera epidemic in the DRC remains a persistent public health challenge driven by inadequate water access, deficient sanitation, socioeconomic inequalities, and chronic armed conflict. Sustainable control requires an integrated approach combining long‐term WASH investment, health system strengthening, and community engagement. As an immediate actionable strategy, we recommend the establishment of a risk‐stratified WASH resource allocation framework prioritizing conflict‐affected and high‐incidence provinces, guided by real‐time surveillance data and implemented through systematic involvement of community leaders and local authorities within the DRC's existing national cholera elimination framework.
