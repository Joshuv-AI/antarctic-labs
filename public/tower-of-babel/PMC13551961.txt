Tumor markers in rheumatoid arthritis -associated interstitial lung disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zeng Qiaoqian, Wu Qing, Li Bowen, Wei Qijiao, Wang Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is a serious extra-articular complication of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and a leading cause of mortality. This meta-analysis aims to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic utility and clinical relevance of tumor-associated biomarkers in patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis-Associated Interstitial Lung Disease (RA-ILD).MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, CBM, Wan-Fang, and CNKI databases from inception to April 1, 2025. We included case-control and cohort studies that compared tumor marker levels between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD patients, following the PICOS framework. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Pooled effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using random-effects models. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using summary receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsThirty-six studies comprising 7300 RA patients were included. Compared to RA-non-ILD patients, those with RA-ILD showed significantly elevated levels of CA125 (SMD = 1.10, 95%CI 0.47–1.72), CA199 (SMD = 0.90, 95%CI 0.56–1.23), CA153 (SMD = 1.70, 95%CI 1.16–2.23), CEA (SMD = 0.58, 95%CI 0.24–0.92), KL-6 (SMD = 1.68, 95%CI 1.22–2.14), NSE (SMD = 0.32, 95%CI 0.16–0.48), and HE4 (SMD = 1.87, 95%CI 1.60–2.14). After adjustment for publication bias using trim-and-fill analysis, CYFRA21-1 was also significantly elevated (SMD = 0.48, 95%CI 0.06–0.89). For distinguishing RA-ILD, KL-6 demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.964), followed by CYFRA21-1 (AUC = 0.906).ConclusionOur findings, which are primarily based on evidence from Chinese cohorts, support the utility of CA125, CA199, CA153, CEA, KL-6, NSE, HE4, and CYFRA21-1 as biomarkers for RA-ILD. The superior diagnostic performance of KL-6 and CYFRA21-1 suggests their potential clinical utility as part of a multi-marker panel discriminating RA-ILD from RA-non-ILD and risk stratification, offering a complementary approach to imaging for identifying this high-risk RA complication.Registration numberPROSPERO CRD42022362487.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41927-026-00664-6.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease, Tumor markers, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by chronic joint inflammation, with interstitial lung disease (ILD) representing one of the most severe extra-articular manifestations and a leading cause of mortality [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that ILD affects RA patients, and its presence significantly reduces 5-year survival rates [2]. While high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) remains the gold standard for diagnosing RA-ILD [3], its sensitivity for detecting early-stage pulmonary interstitial lesions is limited, and it fails to provide molecular insights into underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Furthermore, subclinical pulmonary damage may precede detectable imaging abnormalities, underscoring the urgent need for early biomarkers.

Recent studies have highlighted the aberrant expression of tumor markers in RA-ILD [4]. Despite promising associations between tumor markers and RA-ILD, the diagnostic utility of individual markers remains suboptimal, and a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence is lacking. This meta-analysis aims to synthesize existing clinical evidence to assess the diagnostic accuracy of tumor markers in differentiating RA-ILD from RA without ILD. Our findings may inform the development of multidimensional early-detection strategies to optimize clinical management of RA-ILD.

Materials and methods

Literature-search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in several databases, including MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), the Wan-Fang Database, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). The search was performed from database inception until April 1, 2025, with no language restrictions. The search strategy combined terms for rheumatoid arthritis, interstitial lung disease, and specific tumor-associated biomarkers of interest (including but not limited to KL-6, surfactant protein D, CEA, CA125, CA19-9, CA15-3, CYFRA21-1, NSE, HE4, SCC, AFP, and CA242). Both controlled vocabulary (MeSH in PubMed, Emtree in EMBASE) and free-text terms were used to ensure comprehensive coverage. The full search strategy for PubMed is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study selection followed the PICOS framework: Population (P): Patients diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) based on the 1987 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria or the 2010 ACR/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria. Index test (I): Serum levels of tumor-associated biomarkers (including but not limited to CA125, CA19-9, CA15-3, CEA, KL-6, CYFRA21-1, NSE, HE4) measured using any standard immunoassay method (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA], chemiluminescence immunoassay [CLIA]). Comparator (C): RA patients without ILD (RA-non-ILD). ILD diagnosis in the RA-ILD group was required to be confirmed by high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest, interpreted by a radiologist or pulmonologist according to established guidelines. Outcome (O): Primary outcomes were (1) pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) of biomarker concentrations between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD groups, and (2) summary diagnostic accuracy metrics (area under the curve [AUC], sensitivity, specificity) for differentiating RA-ILD from RA-non-ILD. Study design (S): Observational studies, including case-control and cohort studies, were eligible. Exclusion criteria consisted of review articles, letters, case reports, commentaries, editorials, animal studies, and studies lacking sufficient quantitative data for meta-analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was independently performed by two investigators (Zeng Qiaoqian and Wu Qing) using a standardized, pre-piloted data extraction form. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (Wei Qijiao). Extracted data included: (1) study characteristics (first author, year, country, study design); (2) participant demographics (sample size, age, sex distribution, RA disease duration); (3) diagnostic criteria for RA and ILD; (4) tumor marker details (name, assay method, units); (5) quantitative data for the RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD groups (means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, or exact p-values for continuous outcomes); and (6) diagnostic accuracy data (sensitivity, specificity, cutoff values, AUC) where available.

Methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies. The NOS evaluates three domains: selection of cohorts/cases, comparability, and ascertainment of outcome/exposure. Studies were categorized as high quality (NOS ≥ 7) or moderate quality (NOS = 5–6). Analysis methods

Data synthesis was performed using Stata 11.0. As the primary outcome was continuous (biomarker concentrations), the pooled effect measure was the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For studies reporting data as medians with interquartile ranges, means and standard deviations were estimated using validated methods. A random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird) was applied a priori for all meta-analyses, as it accounts for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies (e.g., differences in populations, assay methods, and study designs).

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.10 indicating significant heterogeneity) and the I² statistic. Subgroup analyses were pre-specified to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on study design (cohort vs. non-cohort), geographic region (China vs. non-China), sample size (≥ 100 vs. <100), and mean age (≥ 60 vs. <60 years). These subgroups are presented in Table 4.

Summary of the subgroup analysis results

Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method, in which the meta-analysis was iteratively repeated after excluding one study at a time, to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates.

Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and by Egger’s linear regression test. This analysis was performed only for outcomes where a sufficient number of studies (≥ 10) were available. When significant publication bias was detected (p < 0.05), the trim-and-fill method was applied to adjust for the potential bias and provide corrected effect estimates.

Synthesis of reported diagnostic accuracy data

We extracted diagnostic accuracy metrics (AUC, optimal cutoff, sensitivity, specificity) from studies that reported these data for distinguishing RA-ILD from RA-non-ILD. These data were tabulated and summarized descriptively. This analysis does not constitute a formal diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis; no pooled sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, or hierarchical models (e.g., bivariate or HSROC) were applied. The reported AUC values and related metrics are presented as originally reported by each study, serving as an exploratory summary rather than a pooled estimate of diagnostic performance. Results are presented in the results section and Fig. 5.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 2,696 potentially relevant articles were initially identified through systematic literature searches employing predefined search strategies and eligibility criteria. After removing 79 duplicate records, 2,617 publications underwent title/abstract screening. This preliminary screening excluded 279 review articles, 190 case reports, 131 book chapters/commentaries, 55 conference abstracts, 27 animal studies, and 1,082 articles focused on diagnostic/therapeutic approaches. Subsequently, 783 articles were excluded due to irrelevance to the study’s scope: 379 unrelated to RA, 203 unrelated to ILD, and 201 unrelated to tumor biomarkers. Full-text evaluation was conducted for 70 potentially eligible articles. After detailed assessment, we excluded 5 publications with overlapping datasets, 2 studies lacking tumor marker classification, 26 articles without ILD/non-ILD group comparisons, and 1 study failing to report quantitative tumor marker data. Ultimately, 36 studies [5–39] met all inclusion criteria and were selected for final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the literature search process

Study characteristics

All included studies underwent rigorous full-text evaluation, with key study characteristics systematically documented (Tables 1 and 2). Data extraction was performed across 32 retrospective studies, 2 case-control studies, 2 cross-sectional studies. The final analytical cohort comprised 4,958 RA patients without ILD and 2,342 RA patients with ILD, aggregating data from 7,300 individuals. Demographic parameters including mean age, gender distribution, and study period were comprehensively recorded in Table 1, stratified by ILD status.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Of the 36 included studies, 28 studies (77.8%) were rated as high quality (NOS score ≥ 7), and 8 studies (22.2%) were rated as moderate quality (NOS score 5–6). No studies were considered low quality (NOS < 5) and were excluded. The detailed NOS scores for each study are presented in Table 3.

Analysis of methodological weaknesses revealed several common issues. First, regarding selection, several studies did not explicitly state that the outcome of interest (ILD) was not present at the start of the study, which could introduce selection bias. Second, regarding comparability, only a subset of studies controlled for key confounders such as age, sex, smoking status, or RA disease duration, potentially affecting the validity of the observed associations. Third, regarding outcome, while all studies used HRCT to diagnose ILD, the assessment was not always blinded to the patient’s RA and biomarker status, and the adequacy of follow-up for the cohort studies was often not reported. These methodological considerations highlight the need for cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates.

Data synthesis

The distribution of biomarkers across the 36 eligible studies is summarized in Table 2. The analysis revealed distinct patterns of biomarker elevation between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD groups. Significantly elevated levels were observed for CA125 (SMD = 1.097, 95%CI [0.474–1.720], P = 0.001), CA199 (SMD = 0.899, 95%CI [0.564–1.234], P < 0.001), CA153 (SMD = 1.698, 95%CI [1.162–2.234], P < 0.001), CEA (SMD = 0.576, 95%CI [0.235–0.917], P = 0.001), KL-6 (SMD = 1.678, 95%CI [1.217–2.139], P < 0.001), NSE (SMD = 0.320, 95%CI [0.162–0.479], P < 0.001), and HE4 (SMD = 1.869, 95%CI [1.603–2.135], P < 0.001) in the RA-ILD cohort. Notably, KL-6 demonstrated the strongest association among all biomarkers. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were detected for AFP, CA242, or SCC between the groups. After adjusting for publication bias using the trim-and-fill method, CYFRA21-1 was also significantly elevated in RA-ILD patients (SMD = 0.475, 95%CI [0.058–0.892], p = 0.026), consistent with the findings reported in the abstract.

To aid clinical interpretation, the magnitude of the effect sizes was assessed using standard thresholds. HE4 and CA153 showed large effect sizes (SMD > 0.8), with HE4 demonstrating a massive effect (SMD = 1.87), indicating a very strong separation between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD groups. KL-6 and CA125 also demonstrated large effect sizes (SMD = 1.68 and 1.10, respectively). CA199 and CEA showed moderate-to-large effects (SMD = 0.90 and 0.58, respectively). In contrast, NSE showed a small but statistically significant effect (SMD = 0.32).

Both studies investigating YKL-40 reported significant elevations in RA-ILD patients, while the two studies examining Pro GRP and CA724 found no significant associations. Limited evidence from single studies suggested non-significant differences for CA211 but significant elevation of CA50 in RA-ILD. Note that several biomarkers (YKL-40, Pro GRP, CA724, CA211, and CA50) were investigated in fewer than three studies, indicating the need for further validation. Heterogeneity analysis

Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies for CA125, CA199, CEA, KL-6, AFP, and CA153. To explore its sources, we conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses based on study design, geographic region, sample size, and mean age (Table 4).

The subgroup analyses revealed several patterns. For CA125, heterogeneity remained high across all subgroups, though the effect size appeared larger in studies with sample size < 100 (SMD = 2.56) and in Chinese cohorts (SMD = 1.19). For CA199, heterogeneity was markedly reduced in studies with sample size ≥ 100 (I²=94.0%) compared to those with sample size < 100 (I²=97.6%), suggesting that smaller studies contributed substantially to the overall heterogeneity. Similarly, for CEA and KL-6, studies conducted in non-Chinese populations showed lower heterogeneity but with wider confidence intervals, likely due to the limited number of studies (n = 1 for CEA, n = 5 for KL-6). For AFP, no significant difference was observed between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD groups in any subgroup, consistent with the main analysis. For CA153, heterogeneity was notably lower in studies with sample size ≥ 100 (I²=77.1%) compared to those with sample size < 100 (I²=98.2%), and in non-Chinese populations (I²=0.0%), though the latter was based on only two studies.

Overall, while the subgroup analyses did not identify a single predominant source of heterogeneity, they suggest that sample size and geographic region may contribute to the observed variability, particularly for CA199 and CA153. The persistent heterogeneity across subgroups may also reflect unmeasured factors, including differences in assay platforms(e.g., ELISA vs. chemiluminescence), variations in RA disease activity levels across cohorts, patient selection criteria, or unadjusted confounders.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates for key biomarkers. For CA125, CA153, and CA199, the pooled estimate fell outside the 95% confidence interval after excluding the study by Wu Qi et al., indicating that this study had a notable influence on the overall effect for these markers. However, the direction and significance of the effect remained unchanged for all three biomarkers after removal of any single study. For KL-6, the pooled SMD remained statistically significant (range: 1.48 to 1.79) after the sequential removal of each study, with no single study substantially altering the overall estimate or its significance, indicating that the strong association between KL-6 and RA-ILD was highly robust. For HE4, the pooled SMD remained statistically significant and stable (range: 1.81 to 1.93) after the sequential removal of each of the three included studies, confirming the robustness of this finding. Diagnostic performance of tumor markers in distinguishing RA-ILD from RA-non-ILD

Based on the diagnostic accuracy metrics reported in the original studies, tumor markers demonstrated significant discriminatory ability for distinguishing RA-ILD from non-ILD RA patients (P < 0.001). Among the reported findings, KL-6 showed the highest AUC value (AUC = 0.964, 95% CI = 0.908–1.000) with an optimal cutoff of 376.8 U/mL (sensitivity = 93.3%), followed by CYFRA21-1 (AUC = 0.906, cutoff = 3.40 ng/mL, sensitivity = 75.0%). These values represent study-level findings as originally reported and are presented descriptively without pooling. Other markers showed moderate performance: CA199 (AUC = 0.796, cutoff = 13.42 U/mL, sensitivity = 87.0%), CA153 (AUC = 0.799, cutoff = 23.43 U/mL, sensitivity = 66.7%), CEA (AUC = 0.758, cutoff = 2.89 ng/mL, sensitivity = 63.6%), and CA125 (AUC = 0.617, cutoff = 19.93 U/mL, sensitivity = 88.9%) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, encompassing 36 studies and 7,300 RA patients, provides compelling evidence for the diagnostic and pathogenic relevance of tumor markers in RA-ILD. Our findings establish a hierarchy of effect sizes: HE4 and CA153 demonstrated large effects (SMD > 0.8), KL-6 and CA125 showed moderate-to-large effects, while NSE exhibited a small but significant elevation. In terms of diagnostic performance, KL-6 (AUC = 0.964) and CYFRA21-1 (AUC = 0.906) emerged as the most robust discriminators of RA-ILD. These results carry significant implications for discriminating RA-ILD from RA-non-ILD strategies, pathophysiological insights, and clinical management of this life-threatening complication.

KL-6, a glycoprotein expressed on alveolar type II pneumocytes and bronchiolar epithelial cells, has been extensively studied in ILD due to its role in epithelial repair and fibrosis. Its exceptional sensitivity, large effect size (SMD = 1.68), and highest reported AUC (0.964) in this analysis align with its established function as a marker of alveolar epithelial injury. However, it should be noted that this AUC reflects study-level findings and has not been pooled through a formal diagnostic meta-analysis. Elevated KL-6 levels in RA-ILD likely reflect subclinical pulmonary damage preceding radiographic changes, making it a critical tool for early intervention. Mechanistically, KL-6 may directly contribute to fibrogenesis by promoting fibroblast proliferation via the PI3K/Akt pathway, as demonstrated in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) models. This dual role as both a biomarker and a potential mediator of fibrosis underscores its clinical relevance [40].

CYFRA21-1, a soluble fragment of cytokeratin 19, similarly showed favorable discriminatory ability in the reported studies, with a modest effect size (SMD = 0.48) but a high reported AUC (0.906) [41]. Its elevation in RA-ILD patients, even after adjusting for publication bias, suggests its utility in detecting epithelial apoptosis—a hallmark of progressive lung injury. Unlike KL-6, which is specific to alveolar epithelium, CYFRA21-1’s broader expression in bronchial epithelium may explain its higher specificity in distinguishing RA-ILD from non-ILD RA patients. The complementary roles of KL-6 (sensitivity-driven) and CYFRA21-1 (specificity-driven) advocate for their combined use in multi-marker panels to optimize diagnostic accuracy.

The moderate performance of CA199 (SMD = 0.90) and CA153 (SMD = 1.70) highlights their association with systemic inflammation and mucin hypersecretion, processes implicated in both RA and ILD. CA199, a sialylated Lewis antigen, is overexpressed in inflamed tissues and may reflect endothelial activation in pulmonary vasculature [42].

CA125, despite a large effect size (SMD = 1.10), showed a lower AUC (0.617) but demonstrated high sensitivity, suggesting utility as a “rule-out” test. Its elevation may reflect mesothelial injury or pleural involvement, common in advanced RA-ILD. However, the variability in CA125’s diagnostic performance across studies underscores the influence of confounding factors such as concurrent ovarian pathology or cardiovascular comorbidities, necessitating cautious interpretation in clinical practice [43].

Significant heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analyses of CA125, CA199, CEA, KL-6, AFP, and CA153. Our pre-specified subgroup analyses (Table 4) suggested that sample size and geographic region may contribute to this variability. For CA199 and CA153, heterogeneity was notably lower in studies with larger sample sizes (≥ 100), indicating that smaller studies may have inflated effect estimates. For KL-6, studies conducted in non-Chinese populations showed larger effect sizes but also higher heterogeneity, possibly reflecting differences in disease severity, assay methods, or ethnic variations in baseline KL-6 levels [44].

The persistent heterogeneity across subgroups suggests that other unmeasured factors may also play a role. For markers such as CA125 and CA199, where heterogeneity remained extremely high (I² > 90%), the choice of assay platform (e.g., ELISA vs. chemiluminescence) is a potential contributor, as different platforms have distinct analytical sensitivities, reference ranges, and antibody specificities. Additionally, variations in RA disease activity across cohorts may significantly influence serum concentrations of these biomarkers, given that systemic inflammation can upregulate tumor‑associated antigen expression. Unfortunately, the included studies did not uniformly report disease activity scores or assay platform details, precluding formal meta‑regression. Other potential sources include differences in patient selection criteria and unadjusted confounders. Standardizing assays, adjusting for disease activity in future primary studies, and establishing population‑adjusted reference ranges are imperative to enhance reproducibility and clinical applicability.

The Trim and Fill adjustment validated CYFRA21-1’s association with RA-ILD, reinforcing its reliability despite initial publication bias. Conversely, KL-6’s results remained stable post-adjustment, attesting to the robustness of its association. However, the persistent bias in KL-6 studies may reflect selective reporting of positive findings in smaller cohorts, emphasizing the need for prospective validation in larger, diverse populations.

While this analysis clarifies the diagnostic utility of major biomarkers, several limitations warrant attention. First, the predominance of retrospective studies introduces risks of selection and recall bias. Second, the lack of longitudinal data limits insights into biomarker dynamics during disease progression or treatment response. Third, several biomarkers such as YKL-40 and Pro GRP were reported in fewer than three studies, precluding definitive conclusions—a notable gap especially given YKL-40’s established role in lung inflammation.

Fourth, the summary of diagnostic accuracy metrics (AUC, sensitivity, specificity) was based on data reported in the original studies rather than a formal diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis, which limits our ability to derive pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, the geographic distribution of included studies showed a strong predominance of cohorts from China (32 out of 36), which may affect the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic and regional populations. Fifth, despite subgroup analyses, substantial heterogeneity remained for several biomarkers, indicating that unmeasured clinical or methodological factors continue to influence the pooled estimates. Future prospective, multicentric studies across diverse settings are needed to validate these biomarkers and establish standardized assay protocols. Finally, the diagnostic accuracy data were summarized descriptively without formal hierarchical meta-analysis; thus, the reported AUC values should be interpreted as exploratory study-level findings rather than pooled estimates.

Prospective multicenter studies should prioritize three objectives. First, develop consensus guidelines for biomarker cutoff standardization, accounting for ethnic, age-related, and comorbidity-driven variability. Second, integrate biomarkers with multi-omics data to elucidate their roles in RA-ILD pathogenesis. For instance, KL-6’s interaction with fibroblast growth factor receptors merits investigation as a therapeutic target. Third, validate multi-marker panels against HRCT and pulmonary function tests in pre-symptomatic RA patients, enabling risk stratification and personalized monitoring.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 36 studies comprising 7,300 RA patients demonstrates that several tumor-associated biomarkers are significantly elevated in RA-ILD. CA125, CA199, CA153, CEA, KL-6, NSE, HE4, and CYFRA21-1 were identified as significantly associated biomarkers between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD. Among these, KL-6 and CYFRA21-1 exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy, with large effect sizes and favorable AUC values, supporting their potential utility in discriminating RA-ILD from RA-non-ILD and risk stratification. These findings provide a serological basis for incorporating selected tumor markers into multidimensional screening strategies for RA-ILD. Importantly, while KL-6 and CYFRA21-1 show high diagnostic potential, they should currently be viewed as screening “red flags” to prompt HRCT imaging rather than replacements for radiological diagnosis. Prospective validation and assay standardization are warranted before routine clinical implementation.
