Laparoscopic versus open liver resection following transarterial radioembolization for hepatocellular carcinoma: a retrospective propensity score-matched study
Authors: Yi Ping Sng, YoungRok Choi, Su young Hong, Hyo-Cheol Kim, Jin Woo Choi, Gayoung Kim, Min Kyoung Kim, Sang Hyuk Park, Jiyoung Kim, Jae-Yoon Kim, Jeong-Moo Lee, Suk Kyun Hong, Kwang-Woong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study was performed to compare perioperative and oncologic outcomes between laparoscopic and open liver resection following transarterial radioembolization (TARE) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). TARE is increasingly used as a bridge therapy for HCC when immediate resection is not feasible. However, the safety and efficacy of subsequent laparoscopic liver resection compared to open surgery remain unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 65 patients who underwent liver resection after TARE for HCC between 2012 and 2023 at a single high-volume center. Propensity score matching based on age, sex, tumor size, and tumor markers (AFP, PIVKA-II) yielded 18 matched pairs for laparoscopic and open surgery.ResultsThe laparoscopic group demonstrated shorter time from TARE to operation (99.7 days vs. 166.9 days, P = 0.04) and reduced hospital stay (11.2 days vs. 15.6 days, P = 0.04). Operative outcomes were comparable between groups, including operative time (243.9 minutes vs. 210.0 minutes, P = 0.13) and blood loss (438.9 mL vs. 775.0 mL, P = 0.12). Major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥III) occurred in 11.1% of laparoscopic vs. 27.8% of open cases (P = 0.40). Recurrence rates (55.6% vs. 44.4%, P = 0.51), overall survival (56.5 months vs. 44.7 months, P = 0.10), and progression-free survival (28.1 months vs. 32.4 months, P = 0.815) were similar between groups.ConclusionLaparoscopic liver resection following TARE appears to be a safe and effective approach for selected HCC patients, offering comparable oncologic outcomes to open surgery while achieving shorter hospitalization. These findings support the consideration of minimally invasive approaches in appropriate candidates after TARE at experienced centers.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Laparoscopy, Minimally invasive surgical procedures, Transarterial radioembolization, Yttrium radioisotopes

INTRODUCTION

Transarterial radioembolization (TARE) has emerged as an effective locoregional therapy that delivers microspheres loaded with radioactive isotope Yttrium-90 (Y90) selectively to tumor-bearing liver segments for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Compared to transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), TARE has demonstrated greater efficiency in downstaging potential transplant candidates with comparable overall survival (OS) [1,2,3,4]. Recent studies have shown meaningful response rates for TARE in solitary unresectable HCC less than 8 cm [5], with the TRACE Phase II randomized controlled trial suggesting a longer time to tumor progression and improved OS compared to conventional therapies [6,7].

TARE offers several advantages over TACE, including better tolerability, less posttreatment pain, and fewer adverse effects [8]. Unlike TACE, TARE primarily targets tumor-feeding arteries, resulting in enhanced localized tumor response while preserving the non-tumorous liver. This selective approach often leads to compensatory hypertrophy of the unaffected liver segments [9,10], potentially creating an opportunity for curative resection in previously unresectable patients.

Following TARE, treatment response is typically assessed through imaging studies interpreted using the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria. While TARE can achieve significant tumor response, complete pathological necrosis is not consistently observed, and responses vary between patients. Therefore, surgical resection remains the most definitive approach to ensure complete tumor eradication when feasible. However, post-TARE changes in the hepatic parenchyma, including peritumoral fibrosis, vascular alterations, and variable patterns of tumor necrosis, increase the complexity of resection and potentially elevate the risk of biliary complications. Consequently, open surgical approaches have traditionally been favored for post-TARE cases.

Recent advances in laparoscopic liver surgery, including improved instrumentation, high-definition 3-dimensional (3D) visualization systems, and intraoperative fluorescence imaging with indocyanine green (ICG), have expanded the application of minimally invasive approaches to increasingly complex hepatic resections. Despite these advances, the role of laparoscopy in post-TARE HCC resection remains poorly defined, with limited comparative data available to guide surgical decision-making.

This study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of laparoscopic liver resection in patients with HCC following TARE treatment, comparing perioperative outcomes and long-term oncologic results with the traditional open surgical approach.

METHODS

Patients

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients with HCC who underwent TARE followed by surgical resection at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) between January 2012 and December 2023. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of SNUH (No. 2504-072-1629), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Exclusion criteria included mixed tumors with cholangiocarcinoma features, extrahepatic metastasis, and poor performance status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status >2). Treatment decisions were made through multidisciplinary tumor board discussions with patient consent.

All patients underwent baseline assessment with triphasic dynamic CT and MRI, with volumetric liver assessment using 3D virtual liver surgery planning software (Dr Liver, POSTECH). Laboratory evaluation included liver function tests (AST, ALT, platelets, INR, bilirubin, albumin), tumor markers (AFP and protein induced by vitamin K antagonist-II [PIVKA-II]), and hepatitis serology.

Transarterial radioembolization procedure

Prior to TARE, diagnostic angiography and 99mTc-MAA (technetium-99m-labeled macroaggregated albumin) scans were performed to evaluate hepatic vasculature and assess potential hepatopulmonary shunting. Details of the TARE technique have been previously described [11]. Following TARE, patients were monitored with imaging studies (CT or MRI) and tumor marker assessment at 1-, 3-, and 6-month intervals. Tumor response was evaluated using the mRECIST.

Surgical resection was considered when tumors demonstrated a complete or partial response to TARE and the future liver remnant volume was sufficient (>30% of functional liver volume). For patients with suboptimal response to initial TARE, additional locoregional therapy with TACE or external beam radiation was considered before surgical evaluation.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by hepatobiliary surgeons with at least 10 years of experience in complex liver resections. The selection between open and laparoscopic approaches was based on tumor characteristics (size, location), extent of planned resection, underlying liver function, vascular involvement, and patient preference after informed discussion.

For open procedures, an inverted L incision was used. For laparoscopic procedures, a standardized 5-port technique was employed with the patient in the reverse Trendelenburg position. In select laparoscopic cases, intraoperative ICG fluorescence imaging was utilized to identify tumor margins and biliary structures.

In both open and laparoscopic approaches, careful adhesiolysis was performed to address perihepatic inflammatory adhesions commonly encountered after TARE. Inflow control with the Pringle maneuver was routinely applied. Parenchymal transection was performed using a cavitron ultrasonic surgical aspirator (CUSA, Integra LifeSciences Corp.) in both approaches, while laparoscopic cases additionally utilized 3D visualization and ultrasonic devices. For large tumors, an anterior approach was preferred to minimize manipulation and reduce rupture risk. A 15-French Jackson-Pratt drain was routinely placed to monitor for bile leakage and ascites production.

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

The primary endpoints were OS and progression-free survival (PFS). OS was calculated from the date of TARE to death from any cause or last follow-up. PFS was measured from TARE to the first documented recurrence or last follow-up. Recurrence was assessed with dynamic liver CT or MRI and serum tumor markers every 3 months. When tumor markers increased without clear radiological evidence of recurrence, PET scans were performed to evaluate for extrahepatic metastasis.

Secondary endpoints included perioperative outcomes such as operative time, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, and 90-day morbidity and mortality. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo grading system, with major complications defined as grade III or higher.

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, while continuous variables were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U-test. Due to highly skewed distributions of baseline AFP and PIVKA-II values (ranging from 10 to 30,000), natural logarithm transformation was applied to normalize their distribution before propensity score matching.

Propensity score matching was performed using R software ver. 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with one-to-one nearest neighbor matching and a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Matching variables included age, sex, tumor size, log-transformed AFP, and log-transformed PIVKA-II. Balance between groups was assessed by calculating weighted standardized mean differences, with values <0.2 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank tests for group comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 29.0 (IBM Corp.), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 65 patients with HCC underwent surgical resection following TARE treatment between January 2012 and December 2023 (Supplementary Fig. 1). After propensity score matching, 36 patients were included in the final analysis, with 18 patients each in the open and laparoscopic groups.

Both groups were well-matched for age, sex distribution, and etiology of liver disease, with HBV infection being the predominant cause (55.6% in the open group vs. 61.1% in the laparoscopic group, P = 0.735). Median AFP levels were 74.40 ng/mL in the open group and 18.10 ng/mL in the laparoscopic group (P = 0.635), while median tumor size was comparable (74.61 mm vs. 74.33 mm, P = 0.924). Portal vein invasion was observed in 38.9% of open cases compared to 11.1% of laparoscopic cases, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.136) (Table 1).

Transarterial radioembolization response

Mean target tissue dose was similar between groups (289.39 Gy in open vs. 203.11 Gy in laparoscopic, P = 0.079). Both groups demonstrated good radiological response to TARE before operation, with complete response achieved in 44.4% of open cases and 33.3% of laparoscopic cases. At 1 month after TARE, median AFP decreased by 39.96 ng/mL in the open group and 13.8 ng/mL in the laparoscopic group (P = 0.961). Median PIVKA-II decreased by 704.00 mAU/mL in the open group and 1,233.00 mAU/mL in the laparoscopic group (P = 0.636) (Table 2). The laparoscopic group demonstrated a significantly shorter time from TARE to operation (99.7 days vs. 166.9 days, P = 0.04) compared to the open group (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Perioperative outcomes

The types of surgical procedures performed were comparable between groups, with right hepatectomy being the most common procedure in both groups (38.9% in open vs. 61.1% in laparoscopic, P = 0.445). Operative outcomes were comparable, with a mean operative time of 243.9 minutes in the laparoscopic group vs. 210.0 minutes in the open group (P = 0.13) and a mean estimated blood loss of 438.9 mL vs. 775.0 mL, respectively (P = 0.12). The laparoscopic group had significantly shorter postoperative hospital stays (11.2 days vs. 15.6 days, P = 0.04). While the laparoscopic group experienced fewer major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥III) compared to the open group (11.1% vs. 27.8%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.40). There was no 90-day mortality in either group (Table 3).

Oncologic outcomes

With a median follow-up of 36.2 months, oncologic outcomes were comparable between the laparoscopic and open groups. Recurrence occurred in 55.6% of laparoscopic cases vs. 44.4% of open cases (P = 0.51). Mean OS was 56.5 months in the laparoscopic group compared to 44.7 months in the open group (P = 0.10). Mean PFS was 28.1 months in the laparoscopic group vs. 32.4 months in the open group (P = 0.815) (Fig. 1). Patterns of recurrence were similar between groups, with intrahepatic recurrence being most common in both the laparoscopic group (60.0%) and open group (62.5%). Three patients (8.3%) from the whole cohort underwent liver transplantation due to recurrence after liver resection (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

TARE with Yttrium-90 has been an integral component of the treatment strategy for advanced HCC, including those with portal vein thrombosis [12], given its superior local tumor control compared to TACE or tyrosine kinase inhibitor [7,13,14,15]. It is also particularly well suited for tumor control in large HCC, where immediate surgical resection may not be feasible [16]. In addition to achieving effective local tumor control, TARE can induce compensatory hypertrophy of the contralateral liver lobe, enabling some patients initially deemed unresectable to become candidates for safe major liver resection [17,18]. The benefit of downstaging to resection or transplantation has been demonstrated in the LEGACY study, which reported a 3-year OS rate of up to 92.8% [5]. However, postradiation changes such as hepatic fibrosis and intra-abdominal adhesions can render subsequent liver resection technically challenging and high risk. This propensity score-matched study represents one of the first comparative analyses of laparoscopic vs. open liver resection following TARE for HCC. Our findings demonstrate that the laparoscopic approach is not only feasible but may offer certain advantages over traditional open surgery in appropriately selected patients.

What stands out, however, is the shorter time interval from TARE to operation in the laparoscopic group (99.7 days vs. 166.9 days, P = 0.04). The shorter interval between TARE and surgery in the laparoscopic group requires careful interpretation. The difference likely reflects earlier clinical and radiological readiness for surgery rather than the oncologic advantage of the laparoscopic approach. Patients with longer intervals between TARE and surgery were predominantly those who demonstrated stable disease or partial response at the 1-month post-TARE assessment and therefore required additional treatment to achieve an improved tumor response before surgery (Table 2). This finding suggests that patients who may be suitable for laparoscopic resection are also presented with earlier operations, hence potentially decreasing the waiting time during which disease progression could take place. As the surgical approach was selected only after resection eligibility had been established, the observed timing difference should not be interpreted as a causal factor influencing long-term outcomes.

Furthermore, OS and PFS were calculated from the time of TARE, which served as a uniform baseline for all patients. As surgical resection occurred after TARE in both groups, this analytical approach minimizes the potential impact of lead-time bias related to differences in surgical timing. The optimal timing for resection after TARE remains open for discussion. Nevertheless, most experts have recommended a 3- to 6-month interval to allow for maximal tumor response and future remnant hypertrophy while avoiding excessive radiation-induced fibrosis that might complicate surgery [19,20]. Our data align with this timeframe, as both groups underwent surgery within this window, though patients in the laparoscopic group tended to receive earlier intervention. Nonetheless, selection bias remains a limitation of this retrospective analysis.

Liver resection after TARE presents unique technical challenges due to radiation-induced changes, such as peritumoral fibrosis, vascular alterations, and variable tumor necrosis patterns [21,22]. These changes have historically raised concerns about the feasibility of using a laparoscopic approach, which is often considered technically more demanding than open surgery. However, in our study, perioperative outcomes, including operative time (243.9 minutes vs. 210.0 minutes, P = 0.13) and estimated blood loss (438.9 mL vs. 775.0 mL, P = 0.12), were comparable between both groups. A case-matched series in 2022 comparing laparoscopic major liver resection in patients with or without TARE showed results of similar blood loss and operative time [23]. This suggests that, with an experienced team in a high-volume center, laparoscopic liver resection after TARE is doable and the anticipated technical difficulties can be overcome. Interestingly, although not statistically significant, the laparoscopic group showed a trend of reduced blood loss. This might reflect the benefit of enhanced magnified visualization using a 3D laparoscope and precise dissection in a laparoscopic setting.

Furthermore, the major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥III) occurred in only 11.1% of laparoscopic cases compared to 27.8% of open cases. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.40), likely due to our sample size, it suggests a trend toward reduced major morbidity with the laparoscopic approach. Previous studies have reported complications such as increased biliary leakage following TARE [24], making our low complication rate in the laparoscopic group particularly remarkable. The significantly shorter hospital stays in the laparoscopic group (11.2 days vs. 15.6 days, P = 0.04) is another encouraging outcome for both the patient and the hospital. This finding echoes the established advantages of minimally invasive liver surgery in other settings, as smaller incisions and less postoperative pain translate into better patient experience and quality of life [25,26,27].

From an oncologic perspective, our data demonstrate that laparoscopic resection does not compromise cancer-related outcomes. Recurrence rates were similar between the 2 groups (55.6% vs. 44.4%, P = 0.51), as well as OS (56.5 months vs. 44.7 months, P = 0.10), and PFS (28.1 months vs. 32.4 months, P = 0.815). These results showed that a minimally invasive approach does not affect long-term tumor control. Our findings are in line with previous literature [28,29], which has established that laparoscopic liver resection for HCC can achieve equivalent oncologic outcomes to open surgery when performed by experienced surgeons. 3-year OS of the laparoscopic group in our study is up to 100%, which is better than the previous studies [21,30].

We also observed that radiologic response assessed by preoperative imaging did not consistently correlate with the degree of tumor necrosis on final pathology (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, 2 patients were classified as having achieved a complete radiologic response, yet their pathological results revealed less than 90% necrosis. Conversely, 1 patient was suspected to have disease progression based on imaging, but the final histology demonstrated 100% tumor necrosis. These discrepancies highlight the limitations of current imaging-based response criteria, which rely primarily on tumor size change and enhancement patterns. Our findings underscore that only complete surgical resection allows for a definitive assessment of tumor viability and remains essential for a potential cure.

Technical advances have facilitated the laparoscopic approach in this challenging setting. The use of 3D laparoscopy, energy devices, and intraoperative ICG fluorescence imaging has enhanced visualization and precision during laparoscopic resection of post-TARE HCC. Additionally, experience with laparoscopic major hepatectomy has grown substantially over the past decade [31], lowering the technical barrier to performing these complex procedures minimally invasively.

The main limitations of our study include its retrospective nature, single-center design, and relatively small sample size despite being one of the largest comparative analyses on this topic. Selection bias may exist despite our propensity score matching, particularly regarding surgeon preference for open vs. laparoscopic approaches based on unmeasured factors. Additionally, our findings from a high-volume center with experienced surgeons may not be generalizable to all practice settings.

In conclusion, laparoscopic liver resection following TARE for HCC appears to be a safe and effective approach in selected patients, offering comparable oncologic outcomes to open surgery while potentially reducing hospital stay and postoperative morbidity. These findings support the consideration of laparoscopic approaches in appropriate candidates after TARE, particularly in high-volume centers with expertise in both advanced laparoscopic liver surgery and post-TARE hepatectomy. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts would be valuable to further validate these findings and better define patient selection criteria for this approach.
