An analytical approach for predicting vibrations in new tunnels undercrossing existing double-track railways
Authors: Yunsi Liu, Yueyuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As urban rail transit networks become increasingly dense, new tunnels frequently undercross existing operational lines. The cyclic loads from existing double-track trains pose a threat to the structural safety of the underlying sections. This paper proposes a computationally efficient analytical model for the ‘double-track train–soil layer–underpass tunnel section’ system. In this model, the double-track train is represented as a moving two-degree-of-freedom (2-DOF) sprung mass system, the soil is modeled as a spring-damper system, and the underpass tunnel roof structure is treated as an Euler-Bernoulli simply supported beam. The system response is solved using the modal superposition method. Key parameters are calibrated via displacement back analysis, and the model’s predictive capability is validated against FLAC3D simulations and independent field measurement data. A systematic parametric study investigates the influence of train speed, tunnel beam length, overburden thickness, equivalent stiffness, and soil mechanical parameters. The analysis reveals the underlying mechanisms of system resonance and critical design parameters. For the specific case study, resonance peaks were observed at speeds around 48 km/h and 72 km/h, a dual-peak resonance phenomenon emerged at beam lengths of approximately 25 m and 45 m, and a vibration amplification effect was identified at a soil cover thickness near 5 m. These findings highlight the resonance mechanisms that should be avoided in design, while the specific critical parameter values are case-dependent and should be re-calibrated for other projects. Increasing the equivalent beam stiffness can effectively suppress the vibration response, while grouting reinforcement modulated the system’s dynamic response. This study provides a theoretical framework and an efficient preliminary assessment tool for understanding vibration mechanisms and aiding early-stage design in similar projects.

Introduction

In recent years, with the deepening urbanization process and continuous development of rail transit networks in China, newly constructed subway tunnels inevitably require passing beneath existing double-track lines. The long-term cyclic loads generated by the operation of existing double-track trains are transmitted to the tunnel sections excavated beneath them1–4,which can degrade the strength of the.

initial support and threaten structural safety. Table 1 summarizes well-documented hazards associated with train-induced vibrations. Research on vibration response under such complex conditions is still under development, with primary challenges lying in accurately modeling the dynamic coupling effects of double-track train loads and the interaction mechanisms between the underpass section and surrounding soil. Therefore, conducting in-depth studies on the vibration response of newly constructed underpass sections beneath existing double-track lines not only contributes to refining the design theoretical framework for rail transit underpass projects but also provides critical technical support for ensuring operational safety.

Regarding vibration response measurements in underpass tunnels under train operation conditions, Guo et al.25,26 employed accelerometers to measure the dynamic response of tunnel linings and surrounding soil at various locations. Analysis in both time and frequency domains revealed that internal tunnel structures can significantly reduce the dynamic response of linings and surrounding soil. Yang et al.27,28 established zoning for graded tunnels under train vibration loads, indicating that under train vibration loading29,30, the zoning of graded tunnels is determined by multiple factors including train speed, operating mode, rock mass type, tunnel height difference, and intersection angle. However, field measurements cannot comprehensively capture vibration responses under all conditions. Li et al.31,32 developed finite element models to investigate the dynamic response at different locations within existing multi-track tunnels. Their research indicates that the influence zone of train operational loads within existing tunnels forms an elliptical region centered on the existing track plane. Zhou et al.33 investigated the dynamic characteristics of double-track tunnels using a centrifuge test numerical model. Experimental results indicate that the interaction between twin tunnels significantly amplifies the dynamic response of the loaded tunnel.

Trains and the ground essentially form a mutually coupled system34,35. Some researchers have employed ABAQUS, FLAC3D, and other software to establish three-dimensional numerical models36, simulating the interaction between train loads, soil, and structures. Although highly accurate, these models are complex and computationally intensive, making them impractical for rapid preliminary engineering assessments and multi-scenario calculations. Therefore, investigating train-induced vibration requires comprehensive analysis from a coupled-system perspective. Tang et al.37,38 developed an analytical model for the train-track-shield tunnel-subgrade soil coupled system. They randomly assigned physical and mechanical parameters to the subgrade soil based on random field theory and performed dynamic response analysis for trains operating on the surface during shield tunnel construction deformation. He et al.39 developed a computationally efficient method to simulate ground vibrations caused by simultaneous passage of double-track trains in underground tunnels, calculating the transfer function of the track-tunnel-ground system in a multi-layer half-space. Lyratzakis et al.40,41 employed an improved analytical approach. By introducing reasonable assumptions, they derived closed-form solutions or reduced-dimension models for the system’s dynamic response, predicting train-induced ground vibrations and single-tunnel responses. This approach significantly enhances computational efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Building upon this foundation, Yarmohammadi et al.42,43 established a coupled train-track-soil model, successfully predicting ground vibrations. However, most current studies focus on single-track train loading44, with insufficient research on complex scenarios involving simultaneous or asynchronous operation of common double-track trains, leading to incomplete elucidation of their influence mechanisms. Furthermore, existing studies on underpass structures often neglect the bidirectional coupling between these structures and the overlying train-soil system45, frequently employing simplified static methods that struggle to accurately predict the system’s resonance behavior.

This paper establishes a coupled dynamic model of “double-track train–soil mass–underground tunnel excavation,” combining modal superposition analysis with displacement back analysis. Despite the inherent simplifications (representing the train as a simplified 2‑DOF system and the soil as a massless spring-damper network), the model’s primary value lies in its analytical tractability, enabling rapid, low-cost parametric analyses and physical mechanism discovery that would be computationally prohibitive with full 3D numerical models. The validity of the analytical model is verified by comparing it with FLAC3D numerical simulations and field measurement results. The study systematically reveals the coupled interaction mechanisms among train operating speed, tunnel span length, soil thickness, equivalent stiffness, and soil parameters, providing a theoretical framework and an efficient preliminary assessment tool for construction safety and vibration control in related engineering projects﻿.

Coupled system dynamics model

Basic assumptions

To establish the dynamic model of this paper, the following fundamental assumptions are proposed:

Train model

Each train is simplified as a two‑degree‑of‑freedom (2‑DOF) system. The car body (mc) has an independent vertical degree of freedom and an independent horizontal degree of freedom. The vertical dynamics further incorporate an unsprung wheelset mass, mw, which is assumed to remain in permanent contact with the underlying structure; its vertical motion is kinematically constrained to equal the beam deflection at the contact point, thereby contributing inertia and gravity forces without introducing an additional independent coordinate. The primary suspension connects the car body and the wheelset in the vertical direction, capturing the essential bounce dynamics. In the horizontal direction, only the car body mass is considered, supported by equivalent horizontal soil stiffness and damping.

Soil model

The soil beneath the train is modeled as a massless spring-damper system. Vertically, one spring-damper is placed directly beneath each train’s mass, representing the soil’s support function. Horizontally, one spring-damper is placed between the two train masses, simulating the coupled interaction transmitted by the soil.

Tunnel model

Considering that support is installed every 150 m during excavation, all excavation within the horizontal range of the two existing lines has been completed46. confirmed that the vibration response transmitted to the underpass section, after attenuation, is primarily manifested in its roof structure47. demonstrated that simplifying soil into equivalent springs is effective for such analyses. Hence, the roof is modeled as a simply supported Euler-Bernoulli beam. Given the depth of the tunnel and the resulting wavelength of low-frequency vibrations being much larger than the roof thickness, the Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, which neglects shear deformation, is deemed adequate for this study, as its length-to-thickness ratio (L/h) far exceeds the applicability limit.

Coupled model

The entire system is modeled as a unified mass-spring-damper coupled system, where the train is connected to a simply supported Euler-Bernoulli beam (simply supported beam) via spring and damper elements.

Excitation model

The excitation source is the moving weight of the train, which generates a harmonic-like forcing function as the wheels pass over the rail at regular intervals defined by the bogie wheelbase, d. The excitation frequency fe is therefore kinematically linked to the train speed v by fe = v/d. This deterministic representation is justified by the fact that for a train moving on a well-maintained track, the dominant periodic component of the wheel-rail force arises from the regular passage of wheel axles over the rail. The fundamental excitation frequency is governed by the train’s speed and the bogie axle spacing. Higher-frequency components and random irregularities are of smaller magnitude and their effects can be approximately accounted for through equivalent viscous damping in the coupled system. The harmonic representation captures the primary resonance mechanism while maintaining analytical tractability. The present model deliberately omits random wheel-rail irregularities, which would primarily increase the overall vibration level without significantly altering the location of resonance peaks. As the main objective is to identify resonance conditions, this simplified representation is sufficient. It should be noted that this treatment is based on a dominant speed-dependent harmonic component; a full moving-load formulation with detailed wheel-rail contact mechanics is not developed here. Although the vehicle model does not explicitly include bogie rotational degrees of freedom, the external harmonic excitation frequency fe = v/d is derived directly from the fixed bogie axle spacing d. This preserves the dominant periodic wheel-rail force component caused by the regular passage of axle groups, while ignoring higher-order bogie pitch and bounce modes, thus retaining the essential resonance excitation source within a simplified framework.

Displacement back-analysis method

To achieve precise identification of key physical parameters in the coupled system dynamic model and strengthen the connection between theoretical models and engineering practice, this section introduces a displacement back analysis method to establish an integrated “monitoring-inversion-verification” parameter identification workflow. By integrating field monitoring data with numerical forward modeling, this method systematically identifies key parameters such as soil equivalent stiffness, damping coefficient, and tunnel beam equivalent bending stiffness, laying the physical foundation for subsequent parameter influence mechanism analysis.

Construction of back-analysis models and definition of objective functions

The objective of displacement back analysis59 is to identify the physical and mechanical parameters of the system model. Where m is the total number of parameters to be solved for. Mainly includes coupling system parameters Kv1、Kv2、Kv12、cv1、cv2、cv12、EI、ρ

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{1}^{0} ,U_{2}^{0} ,...,U_{n}^{0}$$\end{document} are the measured displacement value at the measurement points;n are the number of measurement points.

Displacement U is a function of the unknown parameter ξ, expressed as U = f(ξ₁, ξ₂, …, ξ_n). When the values of the unknown parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi = [\xi _{1} ,\xi _{2} ,...,\xi _{m} ]^{T}$$\end{document} are determined, the calculated displacement values at the measurement points are obtained through numerical forward calculation as follows:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U = [U_{1} ,U_{2} ,...,U_{n} ]^{T}$$\end{document}

Where U1, U2, …, Un is the calculated displacement value at each measurement point.

Each parameter has a corresponding permissible range, whose upper and lower limits can be determined through laboratory testing or engineering experience. The optimal parameter values are found by minimizing the error function, typically defined as the sum of squared differences between the calculated and measured displacements:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi (\xi _{1} ,\xi _{2} ,...,\xi _{n} ) = \sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{n} {(u_{i}^{0} - u_{i} )^{2} }$$\end{document}

The purpose of displacement back analysis is to obtain calculated displacement values with minimal error relative to measured displacements. The parameters corresponding to these calculated displacement values then represent equivalent parameters that align with actual conditions.

When the two trains operate at different speeds (0/80 and 80/0km/h), the total excitation energy input to the system is reduced compared with the same‑speed case (40/40 km/h). Consequently, the vibration amplitude decreases. This indicates that speed combination itself, without introducing phase lag, significantly influences the coupled response. Future studies could further investigate other different-speed combinations such as 48/60, 60/48, 72/60, and 60/72 km/h to explore the resonance behavior more comprehensively.

As shown in Fig. 11, as the soil layer thickness increases from H = 1 m to H = 20 m, the displacement response sharply decreases from 5.8 mm to 1.5 mm, representing a reduction of nearly 75%. This coupling results in a localized amplification of the dynamic response, with the peak displacement reaching 6.0 mm. This confirms that soil layer thickness primarily influences the amplitude transfer and energy attenuation of vibrations, rather than the system’s natural frequency.

Discussion and conclusions

Discussion

The analytical model proposed in this paper is based on several key simplifications, the implications of which further discussion.

The train is modeled as a two-degree-of-freedom system consisting of an independent vertical car body degree of freedom, an independent horizontal car body degree of freedom. This simplification captures the dominant vertical-track interaction but neglects vehicle pitch and roll motions. During asynchronous or staggered operation of double-track trains, these rotational degrees of freedom could induce differential settlements or asymmetric loading on the tunnel roof. Therefore, the current model is best suited for scenarios where both trains operate synchronously or where the excitation is dominated by vertical heave. For cases with significant phase differences or long car bodies, an extended model incorporating rotational inertia may be required.

The tunnel roof is modeled as a simply supported Euler-Bernoulli beam. This classical beam theory neglects shear deformation and moment of inertia. The Euler-Bernoulli assumption remains valid for slender beams where the length-to-thickness ratio (L/h) is large, typically > 10. In our case, with L = 80 m and h = 0.7 m (combined thickness of primary and secondary lining), L/h = 114, well within the slender beam regime. For future applications involving significantly thicker roof sections (L/h < 10), adopting a Timoshenko beam model would be more appropriate.

The soil is represented as a massless spring-damper system. Although this model is highly efficient, it neglects the inertial effects and wave propagation characteristics in the soil. It implicitly assumes that the soil’s reaction is local and instantaneous. This simplification is reasonable for shallow overburden depths (H < 20 m in this study) and for low-frequency excitations (typically < 20–25 Hz), where the wavelength of the dominant vibration is much larger than the soil layer thickness. For deeper soil layers or higher-frequency vibrations, a more sophisticated continuum or layered half-space model would be necessary to capture phenomena like wave reflection and amplification.

The horizontal train response was found to be negligible for this case study due to relatively high horizontal soil stiffness and small lateral excitation. However, under different geological conditions or with significant train lateral dynamics, the horizontal motion could become non-negligible and would require coupled lateral-torsional beam modeling.

Conclusions

This study systematically analyzed the effects of dual-train operating speeds, tunnel span lengths, and soil layer thickness on structural vibration responses by establishing a dynamic analytical model of the coupled system comprising “dual-train-soil layer-underpass tunnel section.” The findings were validated through comparisons with FLAC3D numerical models and field measurement results, yielding the following key conclusions:

When double-track trains operate at identical speeds for the calibrated case, resonance occurs at v = 48 km/h and v = 72 km/h due to matching excitation frequencies with the natural frequencies of the coupled system. When the two trains operate at different speeds (0/80 and 80/0 km/h), the total excitation energy input to the system is reduced compared with the same‑speed case (40/40 km/h), leading to a decrease in vibration amplitude. This indicates that speed combination itself, without introducing any phase lag, significantly influences the coupled response.
