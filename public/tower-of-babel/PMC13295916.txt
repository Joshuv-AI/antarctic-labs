Building evidence on digital psychotherapies for adults living with depression: a systematic scoping review of populations, interventions and design features of ongoing and planned randomised controlled trials
Authors: Etienne Karl Duranté, Christopher James Veal, Blandine Thibout, Victoire Maçon-Dauxerre, Philippe Ravaud, Astrid Chevance
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital psychotherapies may offer scalable options for depression, but most randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are at moderate or high risk of bias. Mapping the methodologies, populations, interventions and outcomes of completed, ongoing and terminated/withdrawn RCTs of digital psychotherapies for depression could help anticipate future evidence gaps and redundancies.Study selection and analysisWe conducted a systematic scoping review of RCTs registered in the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, ClinicalTrials.gov, European Union Clinical Trials Register and European Union Clinical Trials Information System between 2018 and 2025. Eligible RCTs evaluated digital psychotherapies for adults with depressive disorders, bipolar depression or depressive symptoms. Extracted data encompass methodological features, population characteristics, settings, description of intervention, control, funders, registration timing and reporting practices.FindingsAmong the 681 RCTs, 51.8% were completed, 34.1% published their results, 78.1% were prospectively registered and 8.5% were industry funded. Multicentre RCTs accounted for 23.1%, with 42.9% being planned in the USA, Germany or Canada and 25.1% recruiting in low- or middle-income countries. Inclusion relied on self-reported symptoms only for 61.5% and suicidal ideation was an exclusion criterion in 61.8%. The most frequent type of digital psychotherapy was cognitive-behavioural therapy for 64.6%. Human support was a component of the intervention in 44.2%. Chatbots were assessed by 4.6%. Waitlist control was used in 21%, with 49.3% of RCTs being open-label. The most frequent primary outcome was the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 for 35.7%.ConclusionStrengthening future evidence on digital psychotherapies requires timely dissemination of results, use of active controls, diversifying assessed psychotherapies and studying efficacy in populations with limited access to psychotherapy.Keywords: Mood Disorders, Psychotherapy, Depressive Disorder, Mental Health Services, Bipolar and Related Disorders

Introduction

Depression is a global public health concern, affecting approximately 330 million adults, with depressive disorders ranking as the second leading cause of years lived with disability.1 2 Psychotherapies are effective interventions for depression, with many national guidelines recommending them as first-line treatment for mild to moderate major depressive disorder (MDD) and as an adjunct to pharmacological treatment for severe depressive disorders.3 4 However, access to psychotherapy can be limited by economic, human and time constraints.5 In this context, digital psychotherapies have emerged as a potentially cost-effective and scalable way to treat depression.6,9

Definitions of digital psychotherapy vary across the literature, with study-specific operationalisations reflecting differing emphases on delivery mode (eg, web-based, mobile), technological features (eg, chatbots, interactive modules) or therapeutic content (eg, cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), mindfulness, psychoeducation).7 10 In this work, we adopt an inclusive definition: digital psychotherapy refers to any intervention that includes at least one psychotherapeutic component and is delivered in an automated manner via a digital device. This distinguishes it from telepsychotherapy (synchronous human delivery) and digital wellness applications without a psychotherapeutic rationale.710,12 Such interventions can be offered either as an augmentation to human-delivered psychotherapy, that is, ‘blended’ interventions or as a substitute.7 13

For digital psychotherapies to be considered as credible alternatives or supplements to standard care, they must be supported by evidence to prevent ineffective or harmful interventions.7 14 However, the evidence base remains limited. In 2022, only 15% of available mental health apps had supporting evidence—substantially less than 66% reported for digital therapeutics overall (ie, health software delivering a therapeutic intervention).14 15 Moreover, when available, evidence generated by randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of digital psychotherapies appears to be of low quality, with 71% of 176 RCTs of mobile apps for anxiety and depression judged at moderate to high risk of bias, due to allocation concealment, inadequate sample size and high attrition rates.16 17

To inform the design of more robust and relevant trials, it is necessary to better understand how trials are currently designed and conducted across the research landscape. However, syntheses based on published results capture only a subset of completed studies and overlook ongoing, discontinued or unpublished trials.18 19 Examining the broader ecosystem of registered research is therefore essential to identify methodological limitations and inform future trial design. Mapping trial registries provides a comprehensive view of the research landscape by capturing ongoing, planned and discontinued (eg, terminated or withdrawn) RCTs, which are typically not represented in evidence syntheses based on published results.19,21 As a result, relying solely on published trials may yield a partial and potentially biased picture of research activity.

Building on this broader coverage, mapping approaches enable: (i) tracking interventions under development, (ii) identifying gaps, redundancies and imbalances in the interventions or studied populations and (iii) examining research practices, including publication bias and compliance with reporting requirements.19,22 Importantly, including discontinued trials provides insights into feasibility challenges, implementation barriers and mismatches between interventions and target populations, thereby informing the design of future studies. These insights are complementary to traditional meta-analyses, which focus on estimating treatment effects from published trials without capturing the broader research pipeline.

This study aims to systematically map registered RCTs evaluating digital psychotherapies for depression. We sought to describe the landscape of interventions under development, the populations investigated and key methodological features of these trials, with the goal of identifying gaps, redundancies and opportunities to improve future research.

Methods

We conducted a systematic scoping review of registered records of RCTs of digital psychotherapies for depression. The protocol was preregistered on the Open Science Framework platform (https://osf.io/u2pfe/) with documented deviations (online supplemental material 1). The reporting of this study follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).23 24

Eligibility criteria

We included records of RCTs evaluating the efficacy or effectiveness of digital psychotherapies for depression on one or more symptoms of depression (eg, low mood, repetitive negative thinking), as stated in the outcomes section of the RCT record. We defined digital psychotherapies as digitally delivered and automated psychotherapeutic interventions. Eligible digital psychotherapies could be delivered as a stand-alone intervention, with or without human guidance, or as adjunct to other therapeutic interventions with human involvement (eg, pharmacological intervention, neurostimulation, face-to-face psychotherapy), with or without human guidance.

We considered RCTs of adults, that is, people ≥18 years of age, with depressive disorders, bipolar depression or depressive symptoms, as identified by a diagnosis by a healthcare professional or a scoring above any threshold on a clinician-reported outcome measure (eg, the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) or a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM, eg, the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)) or a self-reported diagnosis of depression.

We included both unipolar and bipolar depression, as the symptomatic presentation of a major depressive episode is largely similar across these conditions, and the psychological processes targeted by psychotherapies are therefore expected to overlap. From a pragmatic perspective, the distinction between unipolar and bipolar depression is often not established at the time of intervention—both in clinical practice and in digital self-guided settings—making this broader inclusion more reflective of real-world use.

We excluded RCTs exclusively focusing on primary prevention, as our objective was to evaluate digital psychotherapies for people actively experiencing depression. We excluded RCTs that exclusively evaluated symptom monitoring, treatment adherence, art-based interventions or lifestyle interventions (eg, physical activity) as they are not strictly considered psychotherapies. The detailed eligibility criteria are reported in online supplemental material 2A.

Information sources, search strategy and management of exported databases

We systematically searched one international clinical trial meta-registry, the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, and three clinical trial registries: ClinicalTrials.gov, European Union Clinical Trials Register and European Union Clinical Trials Information System from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2025. RCT record updates were tracked until 31 December 2025 (eg, result posting, planned outcomes). The detailed search strategy is provided in online supplemental material 2B. Retrieved records from the four databases were standardised by one researcher (EKD) before being merged into a single database. Duplicate records were removed under human supervision. Two researchers (EKD and CJV) independently screened each RCT record for eligibility. Discrepancies were solved during a meeting with a third researcher (AC). For all included RCTs, EKD systematically searched for any available protocol in trial registries and, using the trial identification number, in Medline and Google Scholar, and reached out to principal investigators by email when no protocol could be identified.

Data extraction

For each RCT, we extracted trial design characteristics (treatment allocation, masking, estimated sample size, number of arms, number of recruiting centres, maximal follow-up length of eligible participants), name and country of funding sources, name and country of the primary sponsor, trial status (eg, completed, ongoing, terminated), date of registration, actual date of enrolment start (or estimated if actual was not reported), actual completion date (or estimated if actual was not reported), whether results were posted on the trial record, eligibility criteria, maximal and minimal age of eligible participants, gender of eligible participants, intervention description and name, intervention total duration in weeks, intervention duration per week, control description and name, number of primary outcomes and the name(s) or acronym(s) of primary outcomes.

To extract data, we first exported trial data from online trial registry or the meta-register, for example, RCT identification number, date of registration (online supplemental material 3A). EKD then assessed 100% of all exported data for quality control, then manually extracted the remaining data, following a standardised extraction procedure developed during a pilot extraction of 100 RCT records conducted with two other researchers (VM-D and AC) (online supplemental material 3A). When records contained unclear or missing data, EKD screened all entries of the trial in the registries and the full text of the protocol, if any. Overall, 76.4% of manually extracted data were checked by either BT, VMD or CJV (online supplemental material 3C).

Data analysis and synthesis

The mapping of RCTs includes proportions and frequencies of RCTs’ characteristics (eg, design, masking). Using public domain maps and available data from the Global Burden of Disease 2023 and WHO, we compared the prevalence of depressive disorders with the settings of recruitment of mapped RCTs and the availability of mental healthcare workforce (psychiatrists, psychologists, mental healthcare nurses and social workers).25,27

Free-text entries such as eligibility criteria, description of intervention and control and domains of primary outcomes were analysed using abductive qualitative analysis based on pre-existing typologies.428,30 Regarding the intervention, we followed the typology developed by the metapsy research group, while inductively coding for types that corresponded to ‘Other’ type in metapsy’s typology.4 31 We selected this pre-existing framework because of its wide recognition as a standard, allowing our mapping to be compared with the broader psychotherapy literature. Regarding human support within the digital psychotherapies, we distinguished between human guidance (eg, human feedback on digitally delivered ingredients), whether at the beginning or throughout the intervention, and human encouragement (eg, motivational human feedback to boost intervention adherence without feedback or support on ingredients). For outcomes, we coded the domains covered by the primary outcome measurement instruments using the reference typology developed by Dodd et al.28 All qualitative analyses were performed by EKD, after a pilot on 100 RCTs conducted with AC.

International recommendations and statements on clinical trial registration, for example, International Committee of Medical Journal Editors and the Declaration of Helsinki, require that RCTs be registered prospectively—that is, before the enrolment of the first participant, with some authors considering 30 days as acceptable.32 33 We calculated the proportion and the delay of retrospective registration (>30 days after starting the enrolment of participants). For RCTs reported as completed or terminated on or before 31 December 2025, we assessed the cumulative probability of result posting and/or publication using time-to-event analyses. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to estimate the cumulative probability of publication or posting of results following RCT completion, overall and by registration timing.

Statistical analyses and data visualisations were performed by EKD with Python V.3.14.4 and R V.4.5.1. The data analysis is described in detail in online supplemental material 3B.

From 2018 to 2025, there was an average of 85.1 RCTs (SD=21.2) that were registered each year, with a tendency to increase after the COVID-19 pandemic (online supplemental material 5B).

A total of 51.8% (353/681) of RCTs were completed while 232 (34.1%) were published (table 1, online supplemental material 5A). Of the 58 trial records that reported funding by commercial bodies, 40 (69%) were completed. Among the 234 RCTs reported as completed or terminated at least 2 years before the last update of our review, 26.5% (62/234) had results available at 1-year postcompletion and 54.7% (128/234) at 2-year postcompletion (online supplemental material 6). Among the 106 RCTs that were reported as completed and did not have available results at 2-year postcompletion, 34% (36/106) were retrospectively registered, 52.8% (56/106) did not report any information regarding the funder and 75.5% (80/106) did not have a protocol.

Overall, among the 624 parallel-group RCTs, 82.1% had two arms (512/624), while 3.5% (24/681) of all RCTs used a factorial treatment allocation. Overall, the mean target sample size was 352 (SD=1740) participants per RCT, with 33.5% (228/681) of RCTs including fewer than 100 participants, 39.6% (270/681) including between 100 and 249 participants and 26.9% (183/681) including 250 or more participants. For all RCTs, the median maximal duration of follow-up was 112 days (Q1–Q3=70–215 days).

Regarding the settings, participants were recruited from primary care populations in 45.2% of RCTs (308/681) (table 2).

Participants of at least one low- or middle-income country were eligible to participate in 25.1% (171/681) of RCTs (figure 1, table 2 and online supplemental material 7). Of the 681 RCTs, 157 (23.1%) were multicentre, with 1.9% of RCTs being international (13/681), for example, at least two different countries where participants could be recruited (table 1, figure 1 and online supplemental material 7).

Regarding the eligible population, 62.7% of RCTs (427/681) aimed to include solely participants with ‘depressive symptoms’, for example, symptoms of depression without a psychiatric diagnosis of depressive disorder or bipolar depression. In 20.7% (141/681) of RCTs, solely participants with a diagnosis of MDD were eligible to participate. RCTs aiming to recruit participants with bipolar depression accounted for 9% of all RCTs (61/681) (table 2). PROMs were used to assess depression at eligibility, with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8 or PHQ-9) being the most used (42.9%, 292/681) (online supplemental material 8). Regarding exclusion criteria, 70.8% (482/681) of RCTs cited at least one comorbidity, with a disorder from the schizophrenia spectrum being the most reported (64.8%; 441/681), followed by suicidal ideation (61.8%, 421/681), and by bipolar disorder (59.2%; 403/681) (online supplemental material 8). Overall, 12.2% (83/681) of RCTs aimed to recruit only women, of which 72.3% (60/83) were focused on depression during the perinatal period (table 2 and online supplemental material 8). A total of 95 RCTs (14%) targeted young adults aged 18–30 years, with 4.0% (27/681) dedicated to older adults, that is, participants aged ≥65 years, while such older adults were excluded in 23.5% of RCTs (160/681) (table 2).

Regarding interventions, digital psychotherapies were evaluated either as a stand-alone intervention (434/681, 63.7%) or as adjunct to another intervention in 36.3% (247/681) of RCTs, such as treatment as usual (14.2%; 97/681), face-to-face CBT (8.2%; 56/681) or pharmacological treatment (7.2%; 49/681) (online supplemental material 8).

Human support was considered part of the digital psychotherapies for 44.2% (301/681) of RCTs, with 37.3% using human guidance to provide feedback on digitally delivered psychotherapy (254/681) (table 3).

Stand-alone digital psychotherapies were more evaluated in RCTs that recruited participants with depressive symptoms (75.6%, 332/439) than those with a diagnosis of MDD (43.2%, 108/250) (online supplemental material 9). Most RCTs (64.6%; 440/681) investigated first-wave or second-wave CBT, followed by third-wave CBT (3WCBT) (45.7%; 311/681) (table 3). Digital psychotherapies were delivered via the internet, for example, web-apps or websites, in 56.7% of RCTs (386/681), or via mobile apps—including text messaging apps—for 39.5% (269/681) of RCTs (table 3). Delivery features were most frequently written or graphical content in 88.1% (600/681) of all evaluated digital psychotherapies, followed by video recordings for 26% (177/681) and audio recordings in 19.5% (133/681). Conversational agents, for example, chatbots, were investigated in 4.6% of RCTs (31/681) (table 3).

Overall, the most frequent digital psychotherapy was a combination of CBT or 3WCBT, delivered in a fully self-guided mode, via the web or a mobile app (38.8%; 264/681) (figure 2). When reported (37%; 252/681), the mean amount of time that should be dedicated by the participant to use the digital psychotherapy was 80 min per week (SD=99). The mean duration of the intervention was 9 weeks (SD=7).

Regarding the control arm, treatment as usual (TAU) was used by 29.7% of RCTs (202/681), with 21% (143/681) using a waitlist control (online supplemental material 10). Open-label RCTs represented 49.3% of RCTs (336/681), with 23.6% of RCTs (161/681) reporting masked participants (table 1). Participants were masked in 31% (18/58) of industry-funded RCTs and 20.9% (53/253) of academic-funded RCTs (online supplemental material 11). In RCTs where participants were masked, the most frequent attention control was a digital sham (19.9%; 32/161) (online supplemental material 10). Outcome assessors were masked in 40.8% (102/250) of MDD RCTs and in 26.4% (116/439) of RCTs including participants with depressive symptoms (online supplemental material 12). Overall, digital shams were used in 7% (48/681) of RCTs, most commonly sham mobile apps or computer software (27 RCTs, 4%) and sham online content (17 RCTs) (online supplemental material 10).

Regarding outcomes, 45.5% (310/681) of RCTs reported a unique primary outcome measurement instrument. Most RCTs (83.8%; 571/681) assessed primary outcome domains related to psychiatric outcomes, primarily with the PHQ-9 or PHQ-8 (38.6%; 263/681), the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (16.9%; 115/681) or the BDI (either I or II) (9.5%; 65/681) (online supplemental material 9).

No information regarding the funding source was reported for 35.5% of RCTs (242/681), and 8.5% were industry-funded (58/681) (table 1). Overall, 21.9% (149/681) of RCTs were retrospectively registered, with a median registration delay of 224 days (Q1–Q3=99–554 days), and 11.5% (78/681) were registered 200 days or more after the start of enrolment (online supplemental material 13). There were 5.9% (40/681) of RCTs which were registered after their completion. Among the industry-funded RCTs, 77.6% (45/58) of RCTs were prospectively registered (online supplemental material 14). Overall, 52.6% (358/681) of RCTs were prospectively registered and reported information on their funder, with 25.1% of RCTs (171/681) being prospectively registered, reporting information on their funder and having an available protocol.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first mapping of RCTs of digital psychotherapies for depression. Among the 681 registered RCTs, most digitally delivered psychotherapies were stand-alone CBT or 3WCBT and delivered via web or mobile app for participants with depressive symptoms. Only 36.7% of RCTs included participants with a diagnosis of MDD, with 72% of these RCTs assessing CBT. Completed RCTs accounted for 51.8% of the total sample, while 34.1% were published. No funding information was reported for 35.5% of RCTs.

Despite international agreement that timely reporting is an ethical and scientific duty, only 54.7% of completed trials had disseminated results within 2 years.33 34 Likewise, retrospective registration, correlated with increased risk of biases in subsequently published trials, was frequent among RCTs.33 35

Most included RCTs were planned for populations in high-income countries. While depression is a health burden across all regions, a concentration of RCTs in high-income countries creates an asymmetry in the evidence base.1 4 19 In line with previous research on psychotherapy for depression, we show that most RCTs excluded participants with suicidal ideation.36 37 This exclusion might reflect safety concerns but may limit the generalisability of findings for a population commonly affected by depression.38 In our sample, several RCTs incorporated dedicated components addressing suicidality, suggesting that inclusion with appropriate safeguarding procedures is feasible and may provide an ethically balanced approach.39

The number of RCTs planned to assess digital CBT and 3WCBT for depressive symptoms echoes concerns in the literature on research waste due to redundancy and a misalignment between care and research.4 40 In line with previous concerns, we wonder whether this proliferation of similar trials may contribute to research waste, as the benefit of another trial of digital CBT versus wait-list controls in a high-income country is likely small.4 40 41 Resources might be diverted from evaluating other psychotherapy types, delivery modes or underserved clinical populations, while flooding the landscape with redundant digital CBTs.40

Overall, caveats identified by this review echo previous findings by evidence syntheses for ‘traditional’ psychotherapeutic interventions.4 42 43 While absence of masking and use of waitlist control are known to inflate the effect size of the intervention arm, these methodological choices were frequent in our sample of RCTs.44 Even the use of TAU or attention controls, also both frequent in our sample, is considered decreasingly useful as the number of effective interventions that could be used as control increases.45

Digital psychotherapies might be an opportunity to dismantle the efficacy of individual psychotherapeutic ingredients while considering their delivery features. However, only 3.5% of trials had a factorial design, a design deemed valuable to determine which assessed psychotherapeutic ingredients are beneficial.46 47

Our study had limitations. First, our choice to study ongoing and planned RCTs led us to work on trial registry data, which may constrain the quality of extracted information, as some records offer inconsistent and unstandardised details. We attempted to retrieve protocols to enrich these data, but only 34.8% of RCTs had a protocol.

Second, discrepancies may exist between what was specified in registries and what was ultimately implemented during a trial. Such deviations could not be assessed within our review, as the design relied exclusively on registry and protocol information. We did not perform RoB assessment for each individual RCT, as validated RoB tools necessitate data generated during the conduct of the trial. Evaluating inconsistencies between planned and actual trial conduct was beyond the scope of this review, as it was designed to complement meta-analyses of completed RCTs, which already incorporate RoB assessments.16 17

Third, variables such as eligibility criteria or intervention’s description were extracted from brief free-text fields in registries. Although we performed qualitative data analyses with quality checks, some degree of interpretive variability is unavoidable.

Despite these limitations, our findings have clinical and research implications. Clinically, the under-representation of people with MDD, suicidal ideation and living in low-resource settings may limit the translation of evaluated interventions into routine care (online supplemental material 15). From a research perspective, we highlight the need to move from redundant trials towards transparently reported and strategically prioritised RCTs comparing novel interventions against active comparators, using designs capable of informing mechanisms and implementation (online supplemental material 15).
