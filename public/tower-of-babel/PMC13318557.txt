Comparative proteomics of human naso-oropharyngeal swabs from COVID19 patients for identification of potential biomarker candidates
Authors: Sabyasachi Bandyopadhyay, Megha Brijwal, Arhaan Haridas, Aashish Choudhary, Punit Kaur, Lalit Dar, Gururao Hariprasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCOVID19 continues to be a public health issue with subvariants of the main strains causing infections globally. This study looks to identify potential biomarker candidates that can help to understand molecular etio-pathogenesis. Proteins from naso-oropharyngeal samples of healthy Control and COVID19 subjects were trypsin digested and subjected to liquid chromatography assisted mass spectrometry analysis on Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (ThermoFisher). Proteomic analysis was carried out using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (ThermoFisher) using human and SARS-CoV-2 FASTA databases from UniProtKB. Proteins with fold Change ≤0.5, or ≥2 were designated as differentially expressed and taken up for interactomic analysis using STRING and Cytoscape, and pathway analysis using KEGG, Reactome, and Wiki pathway. Area under curve for receiver operating characteristic was done in MetaboAnalyst (v6.0) to estimate diagnostic parameters of sensitivity and specificity. Proteomic analysis of naso-oropharyngeal swabs from COVID19 patients identified 26 differentially expressed human proteins of which 11 are potential biomarker candidate proteins. In addition, 29 SARS-CoV-2 specific peptides from spike protein, nucleocapsid, membrane, ORF1ab, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF8 and ORF9b proteins have been identified of which 6 were mutated. These SARS-CoV-2 proteins make an array of interactions with the identified human proteins establishing their roles in pathogenesis of viral infection. Human biomarker candidate proteins are associated with alterations in glycolytic pathways and immune dysregulation, which are known to facilitate viral invasion, replication, and contribute to disease severity. Identified biomarker candidates have a minimum of 70% and a maximum of 100%, sensitivity and specificity thereby having translational value in developing protein-based tests that can complement currently available diagnostics for COVID19.Keywords: LC-MS/MS proteomics, COVID19, Biomarkers, Glycolysis and immunity dysregulation, SARS-CoV-2 peptides

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID19) is a pandemic caused by the novel Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) that has affected millions of people worldwide since December, 2019 [1]. While the majority of patients experienced mild to moderate symptoms such as fever, body aches, and sore throat, a significant number developed severe complications including pneumonia, sepsis, respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multiorgan dysfunction [2,3]. High morbidity and mortality, particularly among the elderly and individuals with comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and chronic organ diseases, posed major challenges to patient management and treatment [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

COVID19 can mimic a variety of viral and bacterial infections, including influenza, dengue, other viral haemorrhagic fevers, bacterial pneumonia, and tuberculosis as they share common symptoms [5]. Owing to the varied clinical presentation, it also resembles certain autoimmune and inflammatory disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus, Kawasaki disease, vasculitis, and multisystem inflammatory syndrome [6]. In addition, COVID19 affects different organ systems thereby presenting with organ-specific disorders such as myocardial infarction, myocarditis, acute coronary syndromes, pulmonary embolism, gastrointestinal, and neurological disorders making differential diagnosis challenging [3,4]. Burden of the disease therefore makes it essential to map the molecular events at the site of infection to have a better understanding of virus-host interaction in causing infection.

The gold standard for detecting active infection is the reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test [7]. However, its sensitivity varies between 60% and 90%, depending on the timing of the test, sample type, and quality of sample collection [8,9]. This variability can result in false-negative outcomes, especially in cases with low viral loads or improper sample handling. In contrast, serology or antibody tests are used to detect past infection or immune response to SRAS-CoV-2. These tests typically measure, IgM (appears within 5 to 10 days of infection) and IgG (appears after 10 days of infection) or combined IgM/IgG antibodies that suggests late stage or recovery from infection [8,9]. Optimal sensitivity and specificity of these serology-based assays generally range from 70% to 90% after 14 days of symptom onset, however, results are susceptible to false positives due to cross-reactivity with antibodies from other respiratory or influenza viruses [8,9].

Molecular pathogenesis involves SARS-CoV-2 invading the host through interaction of viral Spike (S) protein with Angiotensin Converting Enzyme-2 (ACE-2) receptor on host cells present in naso-oropharyngeal cells and lungs [10]. Proteases, particularly TMPRSS2 and cathepsins, cleave and activate the S protein, facilitating viral and cellular membrane fusion releasing its positive-sense RNA genome into the cytoplasm [11]. While in some cases, SARS-CoV-2 can delay or suppress type I interferon responses, allowing the virus to replicate efficiently in the early stages of infection [12]. In a few severe cases, uncontrolled cytokine release, known as a cytokine storm leads to systemic inflammation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multi-organ failure [6,13].

Proteomics has emerged as a powerful tool in understanding the molecular and pathophysiological mechanisms to unravel host–virus interactions, immune responses, and disease progression of COVID19 [3,14]. Liquid Chromatography assisted tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) based proteomics has been extensively used for biomarker identification and validation, drug repurposing efforts, vaccine development, identification of therapeutic targets by uncovering viral and host protein interactions [[15], [16], [17]]. Targeted assays LC-MS/MS have been employed to quantify viral and host peptides with high sensitivity [18]. Mass spectrometry has also been extensively used to characterize post-translational modifications (PTMs) of SARS-CoV-2 proteins, in particular, site-specific glycan profiling of the spike glycoprotein [19]. Integrated analyses of the phosphoproteome, glycoproteome, and ubiquitinome has provided critical insights into complex virus and host interactions and mechanisms of pathogenesis [20]. Furthermore, automated sample preparation combined with high-speed liquid chromatography enables high-throughput MS has enabled analysis of hundreds of clinical samples per day [21].

In this study, comparative proteomics of naso-oropharyngeal swabs has been used as a platform to identify differentially expressed proteins in COVID19. This paves the way to understand molecular pathways implicated in the pathogenesis at the site of infection.

Materials and methods

Ethics, patient screening, and sample collection

The study received ethical approval from the Institute Ethics Committee, All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, India (IEC-558/19.06.2020). Methodology followed in this study is provided as an illustration (Fig. 1). Detailed clinical history was taken, examination was conducted, and pharmacological interventions were noted. For screening COVID19 patients Naso-oropharyngeal samples were collected in viral transport medium (VTM, HiMedia, Mumbai, India) and Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) tests were conducted at the virology laboratory. RT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2 targeting ORF1ab and Envelope genes (COVIDsure Multiplex Real Time RT-PCR kit, Trivitron Healthcare Pvt. Ltd., Chennai, India) was performed following RNA extraction and cycle threshold (CT) values were noted. Human RNAse P gene served as an internal control. Subjects who tested negative for RT-PCR were designated as Healthy controls while subjects with CT value of ≤22 and with either severe pneumonia or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) were designated as COVID19 patients. For this study, 2 ml of VTM was collected from each 10 Healthy Control (HC) subjects and 10 COVID19 patients. All samples collected from 20 subjects were partially inactivated by sterilizing at 56°C for 30 min in the biosafety cabinet in virology laboratory of Department of Microbiology, AIIMS, New Delhi and aliquoted equally in two microcentrifuge tubes (MCTs) and stored at −80°C until further use [22].

Overview of the methodology followed in this study.

Details of reagents and solvents used

Ammonium bicarbonate (solid, purity ≥99%), Urea (solid, purity >99%), Dithiothreitol (solid, purity ≥99%), Iodoacetamide (crystalline, purity ≥99%) and Benzonase Nuclease (Purity >99%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich – Merck, India and used without any further purification. Mass spectrometry grade Trypsin (lyophilized powder) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. HPLC and LCMS grade solvents were either purchased from Sigma Aldrich – Merck, India or Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. Unless otherwise mentioned all reactions were performed at room temperature.

Protein extraction, estimation and trypsin digestion

For protein extraction experiment one MCT (containing 1 ml of VTM) was taken out from −80°C freezer and the viruses were inactivated completely by heating at 90°C for 15 min [22]. Samples were then treated with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (pH7.8) containing 0.5% ProteaseMax (Promega, USA) and 8 M urea. Furthermore, 250 U of Nucleases were added to solubilize the proteins trapped in the mucous membrane. Proteins were estimated using nano-drop technique in BioTek Cytation 1 cell imaging multimode reader (Agilent, USA) at a wavelength of 280 nm. 100 μg of protein was incubated with 10 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT) at 60°C for 30 min, followed by treatment with 50 mM Iodoacetamide (IAA) at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The solution was diluted with 50 mM Ammonium Bicarbonate (pH ∼8.0) to reduce the urea concentration below 1 M and digested using Trypsin proteolytic enzyme (1:25 Trypsin to protein ratio) by incubating at 370C for overnight. The digestion was quenched by 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) treatment and the resultant peptide mixture was desalted using C18 Reverse Phase (RP) column (Pierce, Thermo Scientific, USA). The purified peptide mixture was concentrated in Labconco CentriVap Benchtop Vacuum Concentrators (Labconco, USA) and reconstituted in 0.1% FA containing LC-MS grade water (loading buffer).

Liquid chromatography assisted tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

LC-MS/MS acquisition was carried out on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer coupled with an Easy-nLC 1200 nano-flow LC system (both Thermo Scientific). 1 μg of peptides were loaded onto a C18 trap column (length, diameter, particle size, pore size; 2 cm × 75 μm × 3 μm, 100 Å, respectively; Thermo Scientific) and then separated in a C18 analytical column (length, diameter, particle size, pore size; 15 cm × 75 μm × 3 μm × 100 Å, respectively; Thermo Scientific, USA) and injected through a glass spray tip in Nano-Flex ion source (Thermo Scientific, USA). Mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid, loading buffer) and mobile phase B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid, elution buffer) were used to establish a 130 min separation gradient (3 min - 5% B; 120 min - 45% B; 123 min - 90% B; 125 min - 90% B; 130 min - 5% B) with 300 nL/min flow rate. For the analysis in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, each scan cycle consisted of one full-scan mass spectrum in Orbitrap analyzer, MS1 (R = 120 K, AGC = 3e6, max IT = 50 ms, scan range of 350 – 1800 m/z) followed by 20 MS/MS events in Linear Ion Trap (Normalized AGC target = 200%, max IT = 50 ms and data type = centroid). High energy collision induced dissociation (HCD) energy was set to 30 to generate fragment ions. Isolation window for precursor selection was set to 1.2 Da and dynamic exclusion was employed for 60 s excluding all charge states for a given precursor. During the LC-MS/MS experiment loading buffer and 500 ng of HeLa digest were included in between the experimental runs to wash the column and to check the QC/QA over the run, respectively. Raw files generated from LC-MS/MS experiment were analyzed in Proteome Discoverer (V2.4, Thermo Scientific, USA) software. Proteins were searched in Sequest algorithm against human proteome database contained 20,430 reviewed proteins and SARS-CoV-2 database contained 17 reviewed and 7024 unreviewed proteins. The databases were downloaded from UniprotKB (https://www.uniprot.org/) [23]. Peptide Spectrum Matches (PSMs) were searched in ‘Sequest’ algorithm with full tryptic specificity, allowing maximum of 2 missed cleavages. Precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set at 10 ppm and 0.6 Da, respectively. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine, and N-terminal acetylation were considered as variable modifications during database search. For human proteins the searched peptides were validated in ‘Percolator’ validation node with false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01 for peptide spectrum match (PSM) and protein identification. Identified peptides were quantified by Label Free Quantification (LFQ) approach where peptide intensity was measured in “Precursor Ions Quantifier” node which was followed by protein quantification considering both unique and razor peptides. Filter criteria of ‘two unique peptides’ and ‘found in all samples’ were applied to identify the proteins. For SARS-CoV-2 proteins the searched peptides were validated in ‘Fixed value PSM validator’ node keeping other parameters same.

Bioinformatics analysis

Human proteins having at least two unique peptides and present in all samples were considered and protein abundance data was exported as CSV to conduct statistical analysis in MetaboAnalyst (V6.0; https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) [24]. The samples were median normalized, Log (base 10) transformed and outliers (if any) were identified by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with 95% confidence limit. Up- and down-regulated proteins were mapped on a volcano plot by applying p-value threshold of ≤0.05 using Student's t-test approach and Fold Change (FC) threshold of 0.5 ≥ FC ≥ 2. Fold Change (FC) was calculated dividing normalized protein abundance of ten COVID19 patients with normalized protein abundance of ten HC. Differential protein expression was visualized in heatmap with enrichment score ranging from +2 to −2. Variable in Importance Projection (VIP) was obtained for individual proteins that had VIP scores of ≥1. Area Under the Curve of classical univariate Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUC-ROC) analysis was generated for individual proteins by plotting True Positive Rate against False Positive Rate with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with threshold criteria of 0.7.

Identification of SARS-CoV-2 specific peptides

SARS-CoV-2 peptides found with abundance value more than 15,000 in at least 5 COVID19 subjects, and either completely absent or present with abundance value ≤ 3000 in HC were manually curated and exported in Excel format. In addition to the endogenous SARS-CoV-2 peptides, mutated peptides were also identified using unreviewed UniprotKB database. The mutation sites were confirmed by aligning the mutated sequences with the wild type sequences (NCBI sequence id – NC_045512.2) in Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo) [25]. All the SARS-CoV-2 peptides were verified by performing BLAST in UniprotKB (https://www.uniprot.org/blast) to confirm the sequence identity [23].

Protein-protein interactome (PPI) and functional enrichment analyses

Differentially expressed proteins were studied in Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING, version 11.5, https://string-db.org) [26] database for protein-protein interactomes (PPIs) using homo sapiens as background organism. This was followed by functional enrichment analyses, such as, Gene Ontology for Biological Processes (GO_BP, https://www.geneontology.org/), Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html), Reactome pathways (https://reactome.org/) and Wiki pathway analysis (https://www.wikipathways.org/) considering FDR threshold of ≤0.05 [26]. The PPI of host protein with SARS-CoV-2 viral protein was also studied in Cytoscape (v 3.4, https://cytoscape.org/) [27] using Biogrid plug-in (https://thebiogrid.org/) [28]. All SARS-CoV-2 proteins found in this study were considered to build the interactome with differentially expressed human proteins. Non-interacting SARS-CoV-2 and human proteins were removed from the PPI analysis. Final set of potential biomarker candidates were identified using a Venn diagram in InteractiVenn (https://www.interactivenn.net/) [29] that incorporated differentially expressed proteins, VIP score, AUC_ROC, represented in pathways, and interacting with SARS-CoV-2 proteins.

Results

Clinical phenotyping

Twenty naso-oropharyngeal samples were collected that included ten from healthy controls and ten from COVID19 patients. Healthy controls had a mix of 4 males and 6 females with median age of 32.5 years, and COVID19 patients had a mix of 6 males and 4 females with a median age of 51.5 years (Table 1). Amongst COVID19 patients, six were diagnosed to be having pneumonia and the rest were diagnosed to be having acute respiratory distress syndrome. Six of the ten patients had CT values for ORF1ab and Envelop gene below 20.

Proteomic analysis

Proteomic analysis of twenty naso-oropharyngeal samples identified 298 unique human protein groups of which 135 proteins were shortlisted and taken up for further analysis. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) confirms that all the samples are confined in 95% confidence region of two distinguishable clusters of controls and diseased without any outliers (Fig. 2A). Volcano plot for the 135 proteins showed 26 differentially expressed proteins of which 12 were down-regulated and 14 were up-regulated in COVID19 patients compared to the controls (Fig. 2B). Of the differentially expressed proteins 12 proteins, Alpha-enolase (ENO1), Pyruvate Kinase PKM (PKM), Actin, cytoplasmic 1 (ACTB), Fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A (ALDOA), Cofilin-1 (CFL1), Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), H4 histone 16 (H4-16), Peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase FKBP1A (FKBP1A), Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2A (EIF2A), Histone H2B type 2-E (H2BC21), Prolactin-inducible protein (PIP) and neuroblast differentiation-associated protein AHNAK (AHNAK) were down-regulated while 14 proteins, Heat shock cognate 71 kDa (HSPA8), l-lactate dehydrogenase B chain (LDHB), Myosin-9 (MYH9), Haptoglobin (HP), Hemoglobin subunit beta (HBB), Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein 1 (ORM1), Myristoylated alanine-rich C-kinase substrate (MARCKS), Non-histone chromosomal protein HMG-17 (HMGN2), Haemoglobin subunit alpha 1 (HBA1), Non-histone chromosomal protein HMG-14 (HMGN1), CD166 antigen (ALCAM), Statherin (STATH), Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit 2 (EIF2S2), and Hsp90 co-chaperone Cdc37 (CDC37) were up-regulated in COVID19 patients as compared to healthy controls. While the down-regulated proteins had a fold change difference between 2 and 100, up-regulated proteins had a fold change between 2 and 130. Heatmap analysis helped to map concentrations for the 26 differentially expressed proteins across twenty individuals recruited in the study (Fig. 2C). Variable Importance in Projection identified 22 proteins with threshold score of >1 (Fig. 2D). Along with human proteins a total of 23 endogenous and 6 mutated SARS-CoV-2 peptides were also identified, originating from three structural proteins, spike (S), nucleoprotein (N), and membrane (M) and five open reading frames (ORFs): ORF1ab, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF8, and ORF9b (Table 2). Of these, 11 peptides were exclusively detected in SARS-CoV-2-infected samples. Among these 11 peptides, one peptide each was derived from S, M, ORF7a, and ORF8, while ORF6 and ORF1ab contributed 2 and 5 peptides, respectively. In COV1, COV2, COV3, COV6, and COV8 that had CT values ≤ 18, at least 15 viral peptides were identified that have mass spectrometric intensities of 60,000 or higher. Notably, COV1, COV3, and COV8, that had CT values of ≤15, at least 20 viral peptides were identified that have maximum mass spectrometric intensities. In contrast, three control samples HC6, HC7, and HC10 showed no detectable SARS-CoV-2 peptides, while three others, HC2, HC3, and HC 5 each contained only a single peptide. In addition, 6 mutated peptides were also identified (Table 2). Mass shifts (Δm) of parent peptide ion along with their fragmented ions y and b were picked up on MS1 and MS2, respectively (Fig. 3). Each of these mutated peptides were detected in at least five COVID19 samples. Two mutations, T345S and R346K, were found on a single peptide of spike protein (Receptor Binding Domain); two mutations, S197L and P207L, were detected on nucleoprotein (linker chain); two mutations, P2110S and L2609F, were identified on two separate peptides of ORF1ab (Non-structural protein 3) and one mutation, V6624L was identified on ORF1ab (Non-structural protein 15).

Interactomics, functional enrichment and pathway analysis

Interactomics of 12 down-regulated and 14 up-regulated proteins contains 70 edges with the average clustering ecoefficiency of 0.57 and (Protein-Protein Interaction) PPI enrichment with p-value of 1.96−10 (Fig. 4A). It is represented by one central core network consisting of 13 strongly interacting proteins, while 13 other proteins on the outside have a moderate or weak interactions with the core or amongst themselves. Fig. 4B shows the functional enrichment processes of the dysregulated proteins in the interactome. Glycolytic and immune mediated dysregulations are the main pathways featuring in the analysis. Apart from these two, SARS-CoV-2 signalling too has an implication in the pathogenesis. SARS-CoV-2 protein interaction with human proteins shows seven SARS-CoV-2 structural and ORF proteins interacting with 20 differentially expressed human proteins. HSPA8, MARCKS, and ALDOA make 6 interactions, while ENO1, LDHB, CFL1, EIF2S2 and MYH9 make 5 interactions with SARS-CoV-2 proteins. Amongst the SARS-CoV-2 proteins, the nucleocapsid protein (N) makes the maximum of 17 interactions, followed by spike protein (S) makes 15 interactions and membrane (M) makes 14 interactions with the identified human proteins (Fig. 5).

Identification of potential biomarker candidate proteins

Based on certain essential parameters such as VIP score, AUC-ROC, Pathway representation, SARS-CoV-2 interaction and differential expression 11 proteins were identified as potential biomarker candidates (Fig. 6). Functions and relevance of these proteins is discussed in Table 3. While translational utility of these proteins was obtained by plotting AUC_ROC curves, diagnostic accuracy was obtained by Box-Whisker plots (Fig. 6B–L). They show a minimum of 87% of sensitivity and 70% of specificity. ENO1 is the only protein that has 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity.

Discussion

All the COVID19 patients included in this study either presented with pneumonia or acute respiratory distress syndrome. Representation of higher age group individuals in the patient category reflects the predilection of old people to COVID19 infection. All the patients had a CT value of <22 for ORF1ab and Envelop genes indicating a high viral load [41].

PCA analysis showed intra-group homogeneity and inter-group variability among the clusters of two different phenotypes without the trace of any outlier. Differentially expressed proteins are a cumulative effect of body's systematic response to SARS-CoV-2 virus. While identification of DE immune regulatory proteins (PKM, ACTB, ALDOA, CFL1, HSPA8, MYH9, HP and HBB) is indicative of systemic inflammatory response, identification of DE glycolytic and metabolic proteins (GAPDH, LDHB, ENO1, and HSPA8) is a clear indicative of the glycolytic alteration triggered by the viral invasion. All these differentially expressed proteins which showed consistent regulation pattern across the HC and COVID19 phenotypes is a clear indicative of causal association between protein expression profiling and clinical outcomes in COVID19 patients. Moreover, the VIP analysis highlights the relative importance of the human proteins to be predicting biomarker candidates.

Protein analysis revealed the presence of SARS-CoV-2 peptides in both patient and healthy control samples. Interestingly, multiple SARS-CoV-2 peptides were detected in HC1, HC4, HC8, and HC9, suggesting that these individuals may have been infected with the virus but had viral loads below the RT-PCR detection threshold, resulting in undetermined outcomes. High intensity of viral peptides in certain patient samples with low CT values of RT-PCR highlights the counter validation between the proteomic and genomic data.

Emergence of various mutations in SARS-CoV-2 proteins is one of the key factors contributing to COVID19 severity. Mutations enable the virus to evade neutralizing antibodies, resulted enhanced transmissibility, and reduced vaccine efficacy [11,42]. T345S and R346K mutations on spike protein was identified in 70% of COVID patients. These two mutations reside in the Receptor Binding Domain which is responsible for engaging the primary ACE2 receptor to enable viral entry into host cells [2,43]. Mutations S197L and P207L were identified on nucleocapsid protein in at least 50% of the patient samples. These mutations are located in the linker region of nucleocapsid protein, that regulates its function through interactions with NSP3 protein and plays a crucial role in regulating the virus interaction with the host cell [44,45]. Mutations P2110S, L2609F, V6624L on ORF1ab can affect both the structure and function of the encoded NSPs, potentially influencing the virus ability to replicate and interact with the host immune system [46,47].

Human protein-protein interactomics was done to reflect the cumulative physiological responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection. The salient features of the analysis are: (1) LDHB that interacts strongly with GAPDH, ALDOA, ENO1 and PKM catalyzes the conversion of lactate to pyruvate, favouring oxidative metabolism over anaerobic glycolysis suggesting a metabolic shift toward oxidative phosphorylation [14]; (2) GAPDH and ALDOA are two interacting proteins that are central enzymes in glycolytic pathway that influence oxidative phosphorylation which affects downstream ATP production and energy generation necessary for cellular processes in host [12,48]; (3) ENO1-PKM converts 2-phosphoglycerate to pyruvate thereby influencing ATP production [30,31]; (4) PKM and ALDOA are key regulators of immune cell metabolism, where PKM regulates macrophages and activate T cells, ALDOA through its interaction with ACTB influences cell migration essential for immune surveillance [49]; (5) ACTB and CFL1 are involved in actin depolymerization and cytoskeletal reconstitution which is crucial for many immune cell functions [35,50] (6) ACTB interacts with MYH9 which is a motor protein and essential for actin cytoskeleton formation found in immune cells [51]; (7) ORM1, HP, HBB and HBA1 are four acute phase proteins strongly interacting with each other and implicated dampening of immune responses mostly through haemoglobin mediated functions [[52], [53], [54]]; (8) HSPA8 interacts with key glycolytic enzymes, GAPDH and PKM2 to ensure proper folding and prevents aggregation during cellular stress or high metabolic demand. The protein acts as a binding and internalizing receptor for viruses, facilitating their entry into host cells through clathrin-dependent endocytosis [55].

Interactomics data further helped to understand the functional roles of the proteins and their associated pathways. Firstly, LDHB, GAPDH, ALDOA, PKM, HSPA8 and ENO1 are majorly involved in glycolytic functions and pathways that includes ATP and hexose metabolism, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis and HIF-1 signaling pathway. Secondly, CFL1, ALDOA, MYH9, HBB, ORM1, PKM, HSPA8, ACTB, HP and FKBP1A in the interactome that are majorly contributing to immune regulatory functions and immune related pathways. Human protein and SARS-CoV-2 protein interaction was done to decipher the possible mechanisms of virus infection in host. Notable among the interactions are: (1) HSPA8, a molecular chaperone, forms a stable complex with viral spike protein and assists in protein folding, thereby stabilizing viral proteins and facilitating viral replication [55]; (2) ALDOA binds to ORF proteins of the virus facilitating in viral invasion [36]; (3) MYH9 acts as an ACE2 coreceptor and colocalizes with the viral spike protein through S2 subunit and supports viral entry through endocytic pathway [39]; (4) GAPDH binds to spike protein at its receptor-binding domain and interferes with viral docking to ACE2 receptors [13,56,57]. It is therefore clear that the identified differentially expressed proteins, through interactions amongst themselves compromises the glycolytic metabolism and immune response, while interactions with viral proteins facilitates viral invasion and replication. Seven mutations were identified from the mass spectrometric analysis. These mutations were seen to be on Receptor Binding Domain of spike protein, linker chain of nucleoprotein, and NSP3 and NSP15 of ORF1ab. These proteins have been known to influence strength of viral interaction, modulate host immunity, and play crucial role in viral replication, respectively [2,44,45]. This therefore highlights the role of clinical proteomics and mass spectrometry in identification of viral mutations and mapping of dys-regulated host proteins that dictate virus-human response in COVID19 infection

Implication and limitations

This proposed study will help to develop a proteomic platform that will subsequently help in designing diagnostics for COVID19 from naso-oropharyngeal samples. This will help clinicians in early detection of COVID19 pathogenicity in patients and to streamline patient management. However, there a few limitations such as: (1) limited number of samples have been assayed; (2) not supported by the validation experiments; and (3) some endogenous peptides and two SARS-CoV-2 mutated peptides were identified in the MS analysis of control subjects, suggesting a potential false-positive identification of the viral peptide.

Conclusions

Proteomic analysis of naso-oropharyngeal swabs from COVID19 patients helped to identify 26 differentially expressed human proteins of which 11 are potential biomarker candidate proteins. In addition, 29 SARS-CoV-2 specific peptides from spike protein, nucleocapsid, membrane, ORF1ab, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF8 and ORF9b proteins have been identified of which 6 were mutated. These SARS-CoV-2 proteins make an array of interactions with the identified human proteins establishing their roles in pathogenesis of viral infection. Human biomarker candidate proteins are primarily associated with alterations in glycolytic pathways and immune dysregulation, which are known to facilitate viral invasion, replication, and contribute to disease severity. This study lays a platform to develop protein-based tests that can supplement RT-PCR; detect host protein changes at primary site of infection; understand host-virus interaction; identify pathways involved in viral replication and immune evasion; and lastly identify mutational sites on Spike protein as therapeutic targets.
