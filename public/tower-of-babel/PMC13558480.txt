Sustained improvement in survival for primary ocular adnexal lymphoma from 2005 to 2024 by model-based period analysis
Authors: Jing Zeng, Xianfen Cao, Xi Yin, Qing Zhou, Jun Lyu, Xiangyin Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePrimary ocular adnexal lymphoma (POAL) is the most common malignancy affecting para-ocular tissues, making prognosis and survival critical concerns. This study analyzed trends in five-year relative survival rates (RSR) to evaluate survival outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients diagnosed with POAL from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database. Five-year RSR was calculated for the periods 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. Data were stratified by demographic and clinical characteristics. Model-based period analysis was conducted to assess RSR trends and project RSR for 2020–2024.ResultsThe five-year RSR for POAL patients increased from 96.1% to 99.1% between 2005 and 2019, with projections suggesting continued favorable trends. RSR decreased with advancing age but improved across all age groups; for 2020–2024, RSR was projected to approach 100% for patients aged < 50 and 50–69 years, and 98.3% for those ≥ 70 years. Females consistently showed slightly higher RSR than males. White patients demonstrated steady improvement, while Black patients exhibited a fluctuating trend and other racial groups showed modest increases. Higher RSR was observed in patients with conjunctival involvement, unilateral disease, and mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) or follicular lymphoma subtypes.ConclusionSurvival for POAL patients improved between 2005 and 2019. Projected estimates for 2020–2024, while subject to inherent uncertainty, suggest continued favorable outcomes, providing useful prognostic information for clinicians and patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05457-4.

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is the most common hematological malignancy globally and the eighth leading cause of cancer death in the United States. Primary ocular adnexal lymphoma (POAL), although relatively rare, constitutes approximately 8% of extranodal NHL cases [1]. POAL can occur in various parts of the eye, including the eyelid, conjunctiva, lacrimal gland, and orbit. The most common histologic subtype of POAL is marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), followed by follicular lymphoma (FL), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), and mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) [2].

The primary treatment methods for POAL include surgery, external beam radiation therapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, systemic antibiotics, and combination therapy. With continuous advancements in understanding the pathogenesis of POAL, immunotherapy has become a focal point of research. The approval of rituximab by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) in 1997 represented a landmark event in the therapeutic landscape of lymphomas. Following this regulatory milestone, U.S. population-based data have documented a progressive and sustained decline in NHL mortality rates beginning in the late 1990s [3]. After 2000, the success of rituximab in treating systemic NHL also led to its widespread use in treating POAL [4]. As physicians continue to explore treatment strategies, the prognosis for POAL has gradually improved [5, 6]. The five-year and 10-year overall survival rates for patients with POAL who received radiotherapy were reported to be 93.8% and 84.9% [7], respectively. However, for certain subtypes such as DLBCL and MCL, the five-year survival rates were reported to be 36% [8] and 43–51% [9], respectively. Understanding recent survival trends in POAL and its subtypes is crucial for evaluating the effectiveness of major medical policies, diagnostic measures, and treatment strategies, as well as for estimating the prognosis of patients with POAL.

The five-year relative survival rate (RSR) is a key epidemiological measure that isolates the excess mortality attributable to a cancer diagnosis. It is calculated as the ratio of the observed survival of cancer patients to the expected survival of a comparable group from the general population, matched by age, sex, and calendar year [10]. By accounting for competing mortality, RSR provides a more accurate assessment of disease-specific prognosis than overall survival, enabling meaningful comparisons across populations and time periods.

However, population-based cancer registries such as the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program typically report survival data with a four-year lag. Consequently, recently diagnosed patients are not yet represented in the latest estimates. When survival is improving over time, reliance on outdated RSR estimates may yield overly pessimistic prognoses. To address this limitation, we evaluated temporal trends in POAL survival using SEER data from 2005 to 2019. We then applied a model-based period analysis to project five-year RSR for 2020–2024 and explored factors that may explain observed disparities.

Methods

Data source

The SEER Program of the United States National Cancer Institute is widely recognized as a high-quality open-access cancer database. Its broad coverage, long time span, comprehensive information, and credibility in data collection make it an excellent platform for cancer research. All data presented in this paper were accessed from the SEER database, specifically the 2000–2020 dataset submitted in November 2022, which includes 17 population-based cancer registries.

Patients diagnosed with NHL, with the primary site restricted to the ocular adnexa (C44.1 eyelid, C69.0 conjunctiva, C69.5 lacrimal gland, and C69.6 orbit, NOS), were included for the period from 2005 to 2019. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cases with a death certificate or autopsy only; (2) patients alive with no survival time recorded. The follow-up time was calculated until December 2020.

Variables

The general indicators included in this study were age (< 50, 50–69, and > 70), sex (male and female), race (White, Black, and Other [comprising American Indian/Alaska Native and Asian/Pacific Islander]), and residence (metropolitan area and non-metropolitan area). Clinically relevant indicators included primary sites (eyelid, conjunctiva, lacrimal gland, and orbit), laterality (unilateral and bilateral), histologic subtypes (MALT, FL, DLBCL, and others) and treatment modalities. Treatment information was extracted as binary variables indicating whether patients received surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy. Based on these variables, patients were further classified into the following treatment groups: surgery alone, radiotherapy alone, chemotherapy alone, surgery plus radiotherapy, surgery plus chemotherapy, radiotherapy plus chemotherapy, all three treatments, or none of the three treatments.

Results

The study included 2,555 patients diagnosed with POAL during 2005–2019, comprising 1,170 males and 1,385 females. Table 1 provides a detailed summary of the distribution of the eligible population according to demographic and clinical characteristics in each observation period. The majority of patients were female, White, aged 50–69 years old, residing in metropolitan areas, with orbital involvement, unilateral presentation, and a histologic subtype of MALT. In terms of treatment, radiotherapy alone was the most frequently employed modality. From 2005 to 2019, the number of cases and the proportion of groups categorized by age, sex, race, residence, and laterality remained generally stable. The orbit was the most common site for POAL, accounting for half (50.4%) of all cases. The most prevalent histologic subtype was MALT, with its proportion increasing by approximately 12% from 54.1% during 2005–2009 to 66.1% during 2015–2019.

Regarding treatment trends, the proportion of patients receiving surgery alone remained relatively stable throughout the study period. Radiotherapy alone showed an increasing trend, rising from 25.4% in 2005–2009 to 36.2% in 2015–2019, while chemotherapy alone gradually declined from 12.4% to 8.0%. Among combination therapies, surgery plus radiotherapy remained relatively stable, whereas regimens involving chemotherapy all demonstrated decreasing trends. The proportion of patients receiving none of the three treatments remained generally stable, ranging from 15.0% to 16.6%.

Five-year RSR for patients with POAL was calculated for the entire population and analyzed by demographic aspects. Overall, five-year RSR showed a steady increase throughout the study period, with model-based projections suggesting continued favorable outcomes through 2020–2024 (Fig. 1). Standard errors for these projected estimates are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Both male and female patients exhibited significant increases in their five-year RSR from 2005 to 2019. Females consistently showed higher survival rates than males across all study periods, and this pattern was projected to persist into 2020–2024 (Fig. 1a). Among age groups, patients < 50 years old consistently presented with higher five-year RSR, while those aged ≥ 70 years had relatively lower rates. Compared to 2005–2009, all age groups experienced significant improvements in survival by 2015–2019 (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, these trends of improvement are projected to continue through 2020–2024, according to the generalized linear models.

Divergent survival patterns were observed among racial groups. During the 2005–2019 observation period, both White and Other racial groups demonstrated steady increases in five-year RSR, with model projections suggesting these favorable trends will continue. Based on the generalized linear model, five-year RSR of White patients is projected to approach 100% in 2020–2024. In contrast, Black patients exhibited a fluctuating trend over the observed period, with model-projected RSR reaching 94.7% by 2020–2024, representing a decline from the initial level while still maintaining a relatively high survival rate (Fig. 1c). Regarding geographic residence, in 2005–2009, metropolitan residents had a five-year RSR of 96.6%, which was superior to that of non-metropolitan residents (92.3%). However, the survival rate of non-metropolitan residents improved rapidly in recent years, and the gap between the two groups was model-projected to narrow considerably by 2020–2024 (Fig. 1d).

Figure 2 provides a detailed overview of the five-year RSR trends in clinical feature groups. Throughout 2005–2019, patients with conjunctival lymphoma exhibited the highest five-year RSR, approaching 100.0% in the observed periods. Survival rates for lacrimal gland and orbital lymphoma demonstrated gradual improvement over time, a trend model-projected to continue through 2024. In contrast, patients with eyelid lymphoma exhibited a variable pattern across the observation periods (Fig. 2a). Patients with unilateral lymphoma showed a higher five-year RSR compared to those with bilateral lymphoma. However, the survival rate for bilateral lymphoma improved more markedly over the observation period, and the gap between the two groups was projected to narrow substantially, such that both were projected to approach 100% during 2020–2024 based on the model (Fig. 2b).

Among the four histologic subgroups, the trend for DLBCL appeared markedly different from the other three subgroups (Fig. 2c). The generalized linear model projected that MALT and FL subtypes would maintain the highest five-year RSR, nearly 100%, while the DLBCL subtype was projected to have the lowest RSR at 62.2% in 2020–2024. For treatment subgroups with relatively larger sample sizes, model fitting and survival projections were performed. The results showed that patients receiving surgery plus radiotherapy maintained five-year RSR approaching 100% throughout the entire study period (2005–2024). Patients treated with surgery alone or radiotherapy alone showed slightly lower but still high RSR, ranging from 97% to 99% across the observation and projection periods. In contrast, the chemotherapy alone group exhibited considerable fluctuations in RSR with no clear temporal trend. Patients receiving none of the three treatments demonstrated a modest upward trend in RSR over time, despite some fluctuations (Fig. 2d).

Discussion

This study employed model-based period analysis to assess temporal trends in five-year RSR for POAL from 2005 to 2019 and to project estimates through 2024. Our population-based analysis revealed sustained improvements in POAL survival over the 15-year observation period, with projections suggesting continued favorable trends through 2024. However, these improvements were not uniform across subgroups, with notable variations observed by sex, age, race, residence, primary site, histologic subtype and treatment modalities.

Sex differences

Consistent with previous studies [11–14], females in our cohort exhibited higher RSR than males across all periods, with this pattern projected to persist through 2024. Several mechanisms may contribute to this disparity. Hormonal factors have been implicated, with research suggesting that the female survival advantage diminishes after menopause in DLBCL and FL [15, 16]. Reproductive factors such as full-term pregnancies and breastfeeding have been inversely associated with NHL risk, potentially mediated by progesterone expression and pregnancy-related immune tolerance [12]. Sex-differentiated genetic effects on drug metabolism enzymes and transporter genes may also influence disease risk and treatment responses [17]. Additionally, lifestyle factors may contribute to survival differences—males generally have higher proportions of smoking, alcohol consumption, overweight, and occupational toxic exposures, factors closely associated with reduced overall survival [18, 19]. It is noteworthy that despite males exhibiting lower RSR, their five-year RSR remained consistently > 95% throughout the study period.

Impact of older age

POAL commonly affects older adults, particularly those in their sixth decade of life [3, 7, 20]. Our findings indicate that five-year RSR was highest among patients < 50 years and progressively decreased with advancing age, although all age groups experienced significant improvements over time. This age-related gradient aligns with previous studies that identified older age as a negative prognostic factor in POAL [21] and being < 60 years as a protective factor in orbital lymphoma [22]. However, some smaller studies have not reached consensus on the prognostic role of age [23, 24]. In systemic NHL, age exerts a more pronounced impact on relative survival, with one study reporting a precipitous decline among patients > 75 years (58%) compared to other age groups (75.8% to 86.0%) [25]. In contrast, our projections suggest that five-year RSR may approach 100% for patients < 70 years and reach 98.3% for those ≥ 70 years by 2020–2024, indicating that POAL poses a relatively lower threat to patient survival compared to systemic NHL.

Racial disparities

Reports indicate that POAL incidence is highest among Asia-Pacific Islanders, followed by White and Black individuals [2]. However, within the SEER database, White patients constituted the majority (76.8%). Due to the small absolute numbers of Asia-Pacific Islanders and American Indians, these groups were combined into an “Other” category. Our analysis revealed divergent trends across racial groups: White patients showed steady improvement, with projected RSR approaching 100% by 2020–2024; the Other group demonstrated modest increases from 89.6% to 92.2%; while Black patients exhibited a fluctuating downward trend, from 98.0% to a model-projected 94.7%, despite maintaining relatively high survival.

Of note, Alfaar et al. [2] using the NAACCR database (covering nearly 100% of the US population), reported an increasing trend in relative survival among Black patients, contrasting with our findings. This discrepancy may be attributed to two factors. First, differences in study populations may introduce selection bias: the SEER database covers approximately 28–30% of the US population, whereas the NAACCR database used by Alfar et al. provides near-complete national coverage, potentially leading to differences in population composition. Second, the study periods differ substantially: Alfar et al. reported trends from 1995 to 2012, while the present study provides observed trends from 2005 to 2019 and forward projections to 2024, offering more contemporary estimates. Additionally, multiple factors may contribute to racial disparities in survival, including genetic susceptibility, socioeconomic status, educational attainment, and access to specialized care. However, given the small number of POAL cases in Black and other racial groups, survival estimates for these subgroups may be statistically unstable, and the observed fluctuating trend among Black patients should be interpreted cautiously, as it may be influenced by limited sample size. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in larger, more diverse cohorts.

Urban-rural disparities

Disparities in healthcare access between metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas are well documented [26–29]. Our study found that metropolitan residents had higher RSR in 2005–2009 (96.6% vs. 92.3%). However, non-metropolitan residents showed more rapid improvement, and the gap was projected to narrow considerably by 2020–2024. This convergence may reflect improved dissemination of diagnostic and therapeutic resources to non-metropolitan areas, increased awareness among rural healthcare providers, and better access to specialized lymphoma care through telemedicine and referral networks.

Anatomical site, histologic subtype, and laterality

Our observations indicate that the orbit was the most common site (approximately 50%) but exhibited the lowest RSR, despite gradual improvement over time. This may be related to diagnostic challenges associated with deeper anatomical location, potentially leading to treatment delay [30, 31]. Concurrently, different lymphoma subtypes show predilections for specific ocular adnexal tissues [32]: aggressive DLBCL accounts for a relatively higher proportion of orbital lymphomas, which may partially explain the poorer survival at this site. In contrast, superficial sites (eyelid, conjunctiva, lacrimal gland) demonstrated higher RSR, possibly due to earlier detection, with indolent lymphomas such as MALT and FL more frequently involving the conjunctiva. Although T-cell lymphomas have been reported to occur more commonly in the eyelid [32], their incidence is substantially lower than that of B-cell lymphomas; this may contribute to the fluctuating survival pattern observed for eyelid lesions.

Regarding histologic subtypes, our projections suggest that MALT and FL will maintain RSR approaching 100% through 2024, consistent with their indolent nature. In contrast, DLBCL was projected to have the lowest RSR (62.2%), reflecting its more aggressive clinical behavior. However, given the relatively small sample size for DLBCL in later periods, this estimate should be interpreted with caution.

Regarding laterality, this study is the first to report sustained improvement in relative survival for bilateral lymphoma, increasing from 91.8% in 2005–2009 to 97.5% in 2015–2019, with the gap from unilateral disease narrowing to less than 2%. This improvement may be associated with the widespread use of rituximab, which has enhanced response rates for aggressive and bilateral diseases, potentially reducing the need for radiotherapy and chemotherapy and minimizing recurrence [5, 6, 33]. MCL subtypes involving the conjunctiva and eyelid [34, 35], as well as chronic lymphocytic leukemia/lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma involving the orbit [32], are more likely to present with bilateral involvement; treatment responses in these subtypes warrant further investigation.

Treatment-specific survival differences

This study provides the first exploration of relative survival trends across different treatment groups for POAL. For subgroups with relatively larger sample sizes, model fitting and projections revealed that patients receiving surgery plus radiotherapy maintained RSR approaching 100% throughout the study period. Patients treated with surgery alone or radiotherapy alone showed slightly lower but still high RSR (97–99%). The chemotherapy-alone group exhibited considerable fluctuations with no clear temporal trend, while patients receiving none of the three treatments showed a modest upward trend over time.

These observations require cautious interpretation for several reasons. First, treatment allocation in observational studies is subject to indication bias—patients with more aggressive disease or advanced stage are more likely to receive chemotherapy, which may explain the seemingly poorer outcomes rather than reflecting treatment effectiveness. Alfar et al. [2] reported that beam radiation was the most commonly used treatment modality overall, and the use of biological response modifier therapy increased significantly after 2011. However, that study did not perform subgroup analyses by treatment modality or further explore associations with survival outcomes, reflecting the inherent limitations of registry-based data regarding treatment details. Second, the SEER database does not capture immunotherapy information. Whether patients received immunotherapies (e.g., rituximab) in addition to surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy is unknown, which may affect assessment of treatment outcomes, particularly for B-cell lymphomas. Third, “receiving none of the three treatments” does not preclude other therapeutic interventions; survival estimates for this group should be interpreted with particular caution. The favorable outcomes observed in the surgery plus radiotherapy group may reflect effective local disease control. Due to the lack of detailed treatment information in SEER (e.g., radiation dosage, chemotherapy regimens, specific immunotherapy protocols), causal inferences regarding treatment effects cannot be drawn.

It should also be noted that RSR values approaching or reaching 100% in certain subgroups may reflect methodological artifacts—including life table matching limitations, small sample sizes, or random variation—rather than literal absence of excess mortality. These estimates should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, POAL is relatively rare, resulting in limited sample sizes for certain demographic (e.g., Black patients), histologic (DLBCL), and treatment subgroups. This may affect the stability of trend estimates and precludes causal inference; associations observed may reflect underlying disease biology, treatment selection bias, or unmeasured confounders. Second, SEER lacks detailed information on treatment specifics (e.g., radiation dosage, chemotherapy regimens) and does not capture recurrence data, which may introduce confounding. Third, lymphoma staging information was not incorporated: the traditional Ann Arbor system has limited discriminatory value for POAL, as most patients present with localized disease (stage IE); while the AJCC 7th edition and above may be more suitable, its adoption in SEER is recent and data were insufficient for stage-specific analysis. Fourth, as SEER reflects US cancer epidemiology, generalizability to other populations requires further validation.

Conclusion

Between 2005 and 2019, five-year RSR for POAL improved steadily, with projections suggesting continued favorable outcomes through 2024. Higher RSR was associated with younger age, female sex, White race, metropolitan residence, conjunctival involvement, unilateral disease, indolent histologic subtypes (MALT, FL), and surgery plus radiotherapy. The observed racial disparities and urban-rural gaps highlight the need for targeted interventions and equitable healthcare access. While causal inferences cannot be drawn from this observational study, the findings provide up-to-date prognostic information for clinicians and patients, and generate hypotheses for future research. Given the inherent lag in cancer registry data, model-based projections offer a valuable tool for timely assessment of current survival trends.
