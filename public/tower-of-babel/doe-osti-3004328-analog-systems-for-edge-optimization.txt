ABSTRACT
Over the past decade, analog computing has the subject of substantial research interest providing
a path toward improved computational efficiency in the post-Dennard era. Analog matrix vector
multiplication (MVM) accelerators provide a popular approach given the ubiquity of MVM opera-
tions in numerous applications. However, historically analog computing systems can struggle with
applications requiring high precision due to the inherent susceptibility of these systems to analog
non-idealities. Therefore, prior work on analog systems has focused either on applications known
to be tolerant of limited precision (e.g., neural network inference), or using expensive techniques
to emulate high-precision using many analog MVM operations.
In this work, we propose an alternative approach. Motivated by recent advances in inexact nonlinear
solvers and optimizers, we explore the potential of co-designing optimization algorithms which
can take full advantage of the fundamentally inexact analog MVM operations. To enable these
co-designed algorithms we also develop a general mathematical theory of the precision and energy
efficiency of analog operations, and a new system architecture for tightly-coupled analog and
digital computation. Finally, we examine the applicability of analog computing to a wider class
of symmetric positive definite systems and find potential in using analog operations as a sparse
approximate inverse preconditioner. With these core innovations, this project provides a path
toward effectively implementing optimization algorithms on power-constrained autonomous and
semi-autonomous systems.

1. INTRODUCTION

Power-efficient computing plays a crucial role in the control, guidance and navigation of various
edge devices, including autonomous ground vehicles, industrial robots, robotic rovers, unmanned
aerial vehicles, precision-guided munitions, and nano-satellites. As the demands for autonomy
and precision grow, the onboard control systems of these devices can benefit greatly from modern
numerical algorithms for predictive physics-based simulation and optimization of the laws of
dynamics, which govern guidance and navigation. However, a significant hurdle in integrating
modern dynamics simulations into edge devices is the energy demand associated with the algebraic
operations required for their implementation. In contrast, edge devices are typically constrained
by a very limited supply of onboard power, little of which can be dedicated to computational
simulations. By focusing on the algebraic kernel of matrix-vector multiplication (MVM), which
dominates the computational costs of a wide range of modern nonlinear solver and optimization
algorithms, this paper combines recent advances in power-efficient analog MVM with developments
in inexact nonlinear solvers and optimizers, with the future goal of co-designing algorithms and
computational hardware for dynamical system simulation and optimization on edge devices.

Analog computing systems use the physical properties of an analog system—in the case of analog
MVMs a resistive mesh—to perform computation. These systems have been widely the subject of
significant research attention over the past decade due to the slowdown in digital computer scaling
and recent developments in dense, reprogrammable, CMOS compatible memories. Simulation
studies show the potential for multiple orders-of-magnitude greater energy efficiency than digital
systems in a single analog array [35], at least an order of magnitude greater efficient at the
system level [52], and a recent array prototype was measured at nearly an order of magnitude
more efficient than existing digital systems [3]. However, this efficiency comes at a cost. Unlike
digital systems, analog systems must content with numerous circuit level non-idealities including
imprecise resistance values encoding the weights, quantization due to finite circuit analog range,
non-linearities in the circuits handling data conversion to and from analog, and parasitic wire
resistances. Taken together these non-idealities can create challenges even on problems known
to relatively tolerant of imprecise computation such as neural networks [53]. Several papers have
proposed systems to achieve higher precision for iterative linear solvers [21, 19, 47]; however, these
approaches require numerous analog operations and/or extensive digital operations to achieve high
precision.

A more efficient approach to high precision computations using analog MVMs relies on algorithmic
co-design. This project focuses on four contributions to enable this approach. First, this project
analyzes hybrid analog-digital systems which provide significantly greater flexibility to implement
algorithms combining both analog and digital hardware. By treating analog processing elements as
tightly-integrated coprocessors within conventional CPU cores, hybrid systems allow an algorithm

 15

designer to use a full range of functionality while constructing their algorithms. However, develop-
ers must still be careful to avoid too-much digital computation which could become a performance
or efficiency bottleneck.
Second, this project proposes a new approach for solving nonlinear equations using analog MVMs.
By carefully partitioning the algorithm between analog and digital operations we can control the
convergence of solver while only using a small number of digital operations. We also demonstrate
how to adapt existing algorithms for optimization to the unique requirements of analog MVMs;
specifically, the long programming time benefits from the identification of a constant matrix across
all analog MVMs. Finally, we demonstrate how the problem can be modified at low digital cost to
substantially reduce the errors caused by the analog MVMs.
Third, this project develops a model to quantify the amount of precision available in an analog
MVM operation. Analog MVMs have numerous potential sources of error which makes seemingly
simple questions like, "how many bits of precision does this operation provide?" difficult to answer.
We develop a model of how analog output precision depends on a variety of analog properties using
Signal to Quantization Noise Ratio (SQNR) as a primary figure of merit. We further use surveys of
analog to digital converters (ADCs) to determine the optimal ADC behavior for a given problem.
Fourth, this project performs an initial analysis of the direct application of analog computing to
Krylov subspace solvers. This analysis focuses on symmetric positive definite systems and explain
some of the challenges of the general application of analog MVMs to solving these systems rather
than the algorithmic co-design noted above. We then present numerical results (using a simulator)
that shows a flexible conjugate gradient method with a sparse approximate inverse preconditioner
can solve the majority of relevant SPD test problems in the SuiteSparse matrix collection. Together
these four contributions enable greater levels of autonomy on power-constrained edge systems
and lay the groundwork for future of analog MVMs potentially including for traditional HPC
applications.

2. ANALOG SYSTEMS BACKGROUND

Let 𝑀 ∈ R𝑚×𝑛 be a given matrix, and let 𝑣 ∈ R𝑛 be a given vector. Let 𝑤 ∈ R𝑚 . We consider the
matrix-vector multiplication
                                          𝑤 = 𝑀𝑣.                                        (2.1)

The conceptual analog MVM circuit is shown on the left-hand side of Figure 2-1. At the start of the
problem the resistances of the array are programmed with conductances (the inverse of resistance)
proportional to the values in 𝑀. To perform a matrix vector multiplication a digital vector 𝑣 is
converted in analog voltage vector proportional to 𝑣 using a digital to analog converter (DAC)1 By
Ohm’s law the current flowing through each resistive element is the product of the conductance
and row input voltage, and the currents along the shared column will sum by Kirchoff’s current
law. Therefore, the current at the bottom of the column will be a current proportional to 𝑤 and is
subsequently converted back into digital through an analog to digital converter (ADC).
However, as noted previously, each of the analog quantities used in this example are subject to
multiple sources of error. These are annotated on the right-hand side of Figure 2-1. The DACs and
ADCs which convert signals to and from the analog domain have a finite fixed-point bit precision—
typically 4–8 bits for most proposed designs and fabricated prototypes. Increasing the precision
beyond this point is possible; however, it comes at a substantial cost to energy, area, or conversion
time. Beyond the errors introduced by quantization, these circuits can introduce other errors due
to a non-linear input-output response, static offsets in the quantization levels, and other problems
related to fabrication variation between the circuits. For a deeper discussion of the effects of the
circuit properties on MVM precision see [48].

The other major source of error is from imprecision in the programmed conductances representing
each matrix element. These errors are heavily dependent on the specific technology used to
implement a reprogrammable resistor, in this work we focus on two major categories:
 • Conductance variability or programming error describes the imprecision with which a pro-
 grammed device conductance matches the target conductance. These devices are typically
 programmed using an iterative write-verify scheme where individual device resistances are
 iteratively updated and compared to a target resistance. The comparison is often performed
 using a similar ADC, or the same ADC, to the one described above. Therefore, the individual
 conductances are themselves holding only a quantized fixed-point representation. However,
 since the conductances are themselves continuous analog values the exact conductance of a
 device is represented as a random variable.
 • Conductance noise or read noise describes the cycle-to-cycle variations seen in a device.
 Even if the conductance could be exactly programmed to a target, each time the conductance
 was read, or an MVM was performed, there would be small differences in the MVM results
 caused by a variety of physical phenomena including thermal noise, shot noise, and random
 telegraph noise.
One important implication of the iterative write-verify for programming the cell conductances is
writing arrays takes significantly longer than performing MVMs. Additionally, due to the high
voltages required for changing cell conductance—a large gap in the write voltage as compared to
the MVM voltage helps ensure that individual MVMs do not change the precisely programmed ana-
log states—reprogramming typically consumes substantially more energy than performing MVM
operations. This means for many technologies target applications should have a matrix which is
substantially, or ideally completely, fixed across numerous MVM operations. For this reason, many
analog accelerators target neural network inference [51], digital signal processing [54], or iterative
linear solvers [21, 19, 47].
In this paper we focus on silicon–oxide–nitride–oxide–silicon (SONOS) memory [2]. SONOS is a
mature memory technology capable of being fabricated at large scale with demonstrated arrays of
up to 1024 × 1024. Additionally, SONOS is one of the most accurate multi-level analog memories
available today with less than half of the error of similarly mature memory technologies across the
conductance range [52]. In addition to this generally low error, SONOS has two key properties which
can be exploited for further error reduction. First, SONOS device error, both programming error
and read noise, scales heavily with the target conductance. At low conductance values SONOS has
significantly lower error, falling to effectively zero error in the lowest conductance state. Second,
SONOS devices has a minimum programmable conductance several orders of magnitude lower
than other device technologies. This enables SONOS devices to accurately represent zero values
within a matrix. Prior work has shown that these two properties make SONOS an ideal memory
technology for neural network inference applications because neural network weights tend to be
strongly peaked around zero [52].
Analog MVM alone however is insufficient to implement a wide range of applications. Even for
applications like neural network inference which the vast majority of computation is in the MVM
operations, digital logic is still required for auxiliary calculations. For instance in a neural network

3. SIMULATING HYBRID ANALOG-DIGITAL SYSTEMS

ith the end of Dennard Scaling, the current technology trajectory for computer systems is toward
higher power consumption for higher performance. This trend, which applies from small embedded
processors up to the largest high-performance computing and data centers, means that increasing
problem scale or complexity requires an increasing system power budget. To meet this concern, new
technology paradigms, which do not rely on conventional digital technology, have become a major
research interest with several showing potential for orders-of-magnitude improvements in energy
efficiency at the operation level. Analog linear algebra acceleration is one such paradigm, taking
advantage of the ability of analog circuits to encode various computations. Although not a new idea,
developments over the past decade in resistive memory technologies provide dense, programmable,
and CMOS-compatible memories, enabling high programmability and integration scale for these
analog accelerators. Using these technologies, recent analog matrix-vector multiplication (MVM)
prototypes have demonstrated greater-than-digital energy efficiency in systems with millions of
individual resistive memories [3].

A key challenge for the use of analog accelerators is how to effectively integrate them with conven-
tional digital processing. Even if analog components are responsible for the vast majority of the
operations, digital systems are still needed for supporting operations such as system configuration,
memory and data movement, and other operations which are ill-suited to computation in digital
systems. To date, most work on analog MVM accelerators that has considered the role of digital in
system design has focused on fixed-function digital pipelines or systems specialized for a narrow
range of applications with specialized programming models. Neither of these approaches are suited
to more complex algorithms and workflows such as those found in high-performance computing
(HPC) applications.

The RISC-V ISA offers an alternative path. By taking advantage of the open ISA, analog accelerators
can be directly integrated with digital logic as tightly-coupled accelerators while also having access
to a robust set of digital operations and common software ecosystem.

This paper presents the first analysis of a tightly-coupled analog accelerator with a general purpose
digital processor. To analyze these systems we implement the RISC-V ISA extensions in the cycle-
level Vanadis RISC-V CPU model of Structural Simulation Toolkit (SST) [43] and developed a new
SST element for simulating analog accelerators. Using this simulation infrastructure we implement
a pair of iterative linear solvers, conjugate gradient (CG) and stabilized biconjugate gradient (BiCG-
Stab), and evaluate the scalability of these solvers on analog/digital hybrid systems. As part of this
analysis we examine how different architectural choices—different numbers of analog arrays per
core—affects the runtime of the iterative solvers and how those architectural choices impact the
balance of analog and digital work in the full system. This analysis shows the potential of using
RISC-V processors and analog computing hardware for HPC workloads.

3.1. Analog MVM Accelerators

Analog MVM accelerators have been widely explored over the past decade as a potential solution
digital scaling slowdowns. The concept of performing MVM operations in the analog domain
predates this recent interest [22]; however, the end of Dennard Scaling and improvements in
resistive memory technologies have attracted renewed interest in these accelerators. At their core,
analog MVM accelerators take advantage of Ohm’s Law and Kirchoff’s Current Law to perform
a matrix vector multiplication. As described in Chapter 2To perform a computation 𝑀𝑣 = 𝑤, the
resistive memory elements in a memory array are programmed inversely proportional to the values
of 𝑀 and a voltage input proportional to 𝑣 is applied to all of the wordlines in a memory array. By
Ohm’s Law each resistive element performs a multiplication, and the resulting currents are summed
down the bitlines where the dot product can be read out using an analog to digital converter.
There are two important points about this core analog MVM concept which are important for this
paper. First, for most resistive memories take substantial time and energy to program necessitating
applications where the matrix is substantially fixed for the duration of the application. Second,
the analog values used in the computation may only have 4–8 bits of precision and require various
techniques to improve the overall precision of the computation. A full discussion of techniques
for increasing precision and otherwise optimizing analog MVM accelerators is beyond the scope
of this paper, but an interested reader can find a longer discussion in a previous review by Xiao et
al. [51].

3.1.1. Analog MVM Accelerators for Linear Solvers

The requirements for applications which are tolerant of mixed precision and have substantially fixed
matrices has led directly to research on analog accelerators focusing heavily on neural network
inference in particular. However, several prior works have looked at applications requiring higher
precision including iterative linear solvers. Importantly, although iterative linear solvers require
high precision, the iterative nature of the algorithm provides a similar fixed matrix to amortize
costly matrix setup time. Feinberg et al., proposed a technique using multiple arrays to emulate
high-precision floating point using low-precision fixed point analog arrays [19]. Subsequent work
by Song et al., improved the convergence and representation efficiency through a modified floating
point representation [47]. Another approach by Le Gallo et al., proposed using analog MVMs
within an iterative refinement scheme to provide high precision results even with low precision
MVMs [21].

3.1.2. Programming Applications for Analog MVM Accelerators

Programmability has not been a major focus for prior work on analog MVM-based accelerators.
Part of this is downstream of the emphasis on neural network inference where a relatively small
number of fixed function units can provide the bulk of the functionality [52]. PUMA provides an
intermediate approach with a specialized ISA for neural network inference allowing flexibility in
layer functionality; however still limited to inference-specific operations [4]. However, PUMA still

 22

requires a specialized software toolchain to support the custom programming model and ISA. This
creates a major gap when attempting to apply analog accelerators to complex HPC applications.
Although the iterative linear solvers evaluated in this paper could be effectively implemented using
the PUMA ISA, these applications are merely a starting point for HPC workloads. Moreover, the
use of standard RISC-V toolchains simplifies porting existing applications to the new hardware
accelerators.

3.2. System Architecture

This work focuses on an HPC accelerator architecture that combines general-purpose RISC-V cores
and an analog coprocessor consisting of one or more analog MVM arrays in a single tile. This
architecture takes full advantage of the open RISC-V ISA to integrate the analog coprocessor as a
functional unit for each core invoked through ISA extensions. Treating the analog arrays as discrete
functional units allows the system to effectively use a specialized computational kernel accelerator
due to the low overhead of data movement into and out of the analog arrays.
Each tile also contains local SRAM, in the evaluated implementation using hardware-managed
caches for programming simplicity; however, using a mix of caches and software-managed scratch-
pad memories is an important potential architectural optimization. Tiles are connected through a
high-bandwidth mesh router, and are for programmer simplicity fully cache coherent.
Notably, the proposed tile architecture looks similar to digital RISC-V accelerators such as the
Tensix cores in the Tenstorrent Grayskull. [9] In both instances, specialized functional units—
matrix and vector FPU for Tensix, analog arrays for the proposed tile—are tightly integrated with
scaler compute cores to enable fine-grained offload of more complex operations. The proposed
architecture makes several simplifications to reduce design complexity, specifically the use a cache
for local storage rather than the specialized circular buffer SRAM, using a single general RISC-V
core rather than specialized baby cores, and as discussed in the next section, the communication
between the analog arrays and general-purpose core using local scratchpad rather than a specialized
packer and unpacker cores. It is likely that many of the Tensix core optimizations would benefit the
proposed analog-enabled core; however the increase in design and programming model complexity
makes these optimizations beyond the scope of this initial study.

3.2.1. Analog Coprocessor

The analog coprocessor is organized into one or more compute arrays, each serving as an inde-
pendent processing unit for analog operations. Every compute array contains three ISA-visible
components:

 • Analog array—contains the programmed analog conductance values. Importantly, in this
 work we do not optimize the implementation for programming the analog arrays as such work
 requires substantial device-specific details and for program-once applications is not a major
 factor in overall system performance.

 • Input buffer—holds the input operand vector for the MVM operation. These values are
 converted into analog voltages by the array’s digital-to-analog converters (DACs).

 • Output buffer—holds the result of the analog computation as digital values after conversion
 by analog-to-digital converters (ADCs).

For the CPU to coprocessor interface we adopt the conventions of the Rocket Custom Coprocessor
(RoCC) interface. [6] The RoCC interface is an extension point that lets custom coprocessors
integrate directly with the CPU pipeline. Five core instructions form the basis of accelerator
and are represented in Figure 3-1. As noted above, rather than passing individual values in the
instructions, we opt to use the local SRAM for passing data between the core and coprocessor.
This is a significant advantage when using multiple arrays per coprocessor. By allowing each
coprocessor to individually perform memory accesses through a dedicated memory port—shared
among all arrays within the coprocessor—the RISC-V CPU can perform other operations rather
than individually writing operands through the RoCC interface.

This design choice also leads to a uniform structure for the ISA extensions. Each instruction uses
rs1 to specify the array within the coprocessor, and rs2 to specify the starting address of the memory
access for the given operation. Finally, since we are focusing on an HPC accelerator, we use a
floating point interface for the coprocessors under the assumption that each ISA-visible array is
actually multiple arrays using a scheme for combining outputs as in prior work [19, 47]. In systems
tailored for neural network inference operations, these operations would likely need to be performed
on the general purpose CPU rather than in dedicated hardware within the coprocessor.

A typical analog computation begins with the processor configuring a compute array using mvm.set
(Symbol A in Figure 3-1), followed by mvm.l (Symbol B) to load the operand vector. Once both
the matrix and vector are staged locally, the mvm instruction (Symbol C) initiates computation
within the analog array, producing results in the output buffer. These results can either be stored
back to memory with mvm.s (Symbol D) or forwarded directly to another array using mvm.mv
(Symbol E) to enable multiple-stage processing without additional memory traffic. Cascading
MVM operations across multiple arrays within the same tile allows for complex transformations to
be executed entirely without off-chip memory accesses, keeping all intermediate data at the source
of computation.

3.2.2. System Simulation Model

The architecture in Figure 3-2, is realized by three specialized SST components: Vanadis, MemHier-
archy, and Golem . Vanadis models an out-of-order RISC-V core with configurable reorder buffers,
pipeline widths, functional units, and load/store queues. MemHierarchy implements the private L1
and shared L2 caches, a directory-based MESI protocol, and DRAM controllers linked through a
high-radix mesh router. The mesh provides dedicated ports for every core, accelerator, and mem-
ory controller enabling multi-core computation. Golem models the analog accelerator, including
crossbars and buffers. Together, these components enable full-system execution of RISC-V binaries
while tracking pipeline behavior, performance and utilization in a HPC-class models.

3.3. Software Stack

For software development on the proposed system we have developed an initial software infrastruc-
ture to support the development of complex applications. This software stack builds on the existing
RISC-V software infrastructure with modifications to support the proposed accelerator. Specifi-
cally, we modify LLVM 15.0 to emit the RoCC instructions mentioned in the previous section and
develop a low-level user library to invoke those functions. On top of this low-level library we build
custom BLAS-like kernels for matrix-vector and matrix-matrix operations, and use OpenMP to
distribute tasks across the tiles of the proposed system.

3.3.1. Analog BLAS

Given the ubiquity of BLAS in HPC applications, we select it as the primary user-facing interface to
the analog arrays. Importantly, we only implement BLAS kernels that can be readily mapped to the
proposed analog coprocessor, specifically gemm and gemv in both real and complex variations. In

addition to providing a well-known interface, these functions also perform two essential operations
for the analog coprocessor, scaling, and size-agnostic mapping.

Analog arrays typically require some form of scaling for optimal performance. When using a
fixed-point interface, values must be scaled into the range of [-1,1] or [0,1] to ensure that the
underlying analog ranges are fully used. With a floating-point interface as evaluated here, scaling
is less essential; however, it can take load off of the hardware floating-pointing emulation within
the coprocessor.

Unlike digital systems where BLAS functions can take arbitrarily sized matrices, each tile’s analog
coprocessor has a maximum supported matrix size. Moreover, matrices and vectors which are not
integer multiples of the underlying array size require partitioning and/or zero-padding for mapping
to a given array.

To handle both of these operations, the developed AnalogBLAS functions used a specialized
AnalogMatrix object to provide an interface for programmed arrays. Internally AnalogMatrices
allocate arrays from a tile coprocessor’s pool, track which array identifiers are associated with each
matrix, and track scale factors which are used to undo scaling operations providing the programmer
with a transparent interface to the BLAS functions. Importantly, in our current implementation a
given tile can only allocate arrays within the tile, support for problems spanning multiple tiles is
handled through OpenMP.

3.3.2. Multi-tile Operations using OpenMP

To scale problems beyond a single tile, we use an unmodified RISC-V OpenMP implementation.
Importantly, in the current implementation matrices and inputs are manually partitioned such that
each tile’s portion of the matrix is at most the maximum matrix capacity of each tile based on
the number of analog arrays. This leads to an interesting insight for analog acceleration for HPC
workloads, the use of analog arrays implies that the system must use weak rather than strong
scaling. Although it is possible to reduce the overall portion of the problem allocated to each tile,
under-utilizing arrays creates significant inefficiencies which should be avoided.

3.3.3. Implementing Iterative Linear Solvers

Using our Analog BLAS implementation and OpenMP the iterative solvers we implement a pair
of iterative linear solvers, Conjugate Gradient CG and BiCG-Stab. The matrix is partitioned with
a two level recursive partitioning, first allocating contiguous blocks to the tiles, and subsequently
partitioning those blocks across multiple arrays. CG and BiCG are the same solvers implemented
by Feinberg et al., in prior work [19]; however, by building on the RISC-V software stack we are
able to build a more robust and extensible implementation using standard libraries.

3.4. Evaluation

To evaluate our system architecture, we selected two widely-used iterative linear solvers CG and
BiCG-Stab. Importantly, although iterative linear solvers are typically used for sparse matrices, to
focus the analysis in this work we use a pair of synthetic dense matrices. The convergence of the
solvers was verified on smaller test cases such that only 10 iterations were simulated for this larger
benchmarking effort. Additionally, we assume that the floating-point emulation from prior work
avoids additional iterations caused be analog imprecision. Co-simulation of both analog precision
and performance is an important topic for future work.

3.4.1. Experimental Setup

Our first experiment measures how the runtime of CG and BiCG-Stab scales with increasing
problem size 𝑁, comparing single core executions with and without a single analog accelerator.
In this analysis the tile has as co-processor array size of 64 × 64 such that only a single array is
needed. This comparison allows us to quantify the accelerator’s contribution to performance and
identify the scaling behavior of each solver. We vary 𝑁 over a range in multiples of two. The
results provide insight into how hardware acceleration affects runtime efficiency.

Our second experiment uses a fixed-size 1024 × 1024 dense matrix with 128 × 128 analog MMV

3.4.2. Discussion

In our first experiment, shown in Figure 3-3 , runtimes without the accelerator increase sharply as
𝑁 approaches 64, whereas accelerator-enabled trials scale far more gracefully. This single-core,
single-accelerator comparison provides evidence that the accelerator delivers performance gains,
underscoring its potential for efficient deployment in HPC workloads.

Across the architectural and CPU configurations, BiCGStab consistently required longer runtimes
than CG. The marginal performance gap between the small and large pipeline designs informs
that I/O operations—not core or pipeline width—are the dominant bottleneck, compounded by
accelerator communication overhead. In both solvers, runtime decreased as core count increased,
demonstrating that performance scales with parallelism. More investigation must be looked into in
this problem space.

3.5. Conclusion

We have presented the first full-system simulation study of a hybrid analog–digital architecture
that tightly couples analog matrix–vector multiplication accelerators with general-purpose RISC-V
cores. Using our SST-based simulator, we have demonstrated the potential benefits of analog
hardware for HPC applications, and shown how the openness of the RISC-V ISA speeds the
development of new hardware by taking advantage of a mature software stack.

Importantly, this work is only the first step in both the development of hybrid analog-digital
systems using general purpose RISC-V processors. There are potential improvements across the
entire hardware stack which researchers can explore using the simulation infrastructure presented in
this work. On the hardware side, there are significant potential improvements to both the memory
system, replacing our caches with scratchpads, and optimizing these scratchpads for the memory
access patterns common for analog accelerators. Furthermore, since our general-purpose CPU is
specifically supporting the analog hardware, vector support using the RISC-V Vector extension
would likely improve system throughput. Additionally, we plan to explore the costs of moving
the emulation of floating point operations from a hardened—and by extension inflexible—block
within the accelerator to a software implementation running on our general-purpose tile processor.
On the software side, we are exploring further extensions to our analog BLAS library and working
on an MLIR-based compiler infrastructure to further simplify the deployment of applications for
analog accelerators. On the algorithm side, we are exploring methods to improve the realism of
our algorithms, incorporating preconditioners and extending our work to GMRES and other widely
used iterative linear solvers. Finally, on the simulation side, by coupling these SST simulations

with CrossSim, an accuracy simulator for analog arrays, we plan to explore the potential for mixed-
precision analog solvers. Potentially increasing the total number of iterations to convergence while
improving overall system performance through more efficient analog mappings. By building on
our extensible simulation framework for hybrid architectures, researchers from numerous domains
can make explore important aspects of these analog + RISC-V architectures for HPC and other
applications.

4. NONLINEAR SOLVERS AND OPTIMIZERS FOR EDGE
 COMPUTING WITH MIXED DIGITAL AND ANALOG
 MATRIX-VECTOR MULTIPLICATION

Motivated by the computational precision challenges of modern analog devices, we propose to
enable dynamical system simulation and optimization through nonlinear solvers and optimizers
that support inexact linear system solves. Our goal is to blend high-accuracy digital computations,
including a small number of digital MVMs, with low-accuracy analog MVMs. The latter will
be exclusively used to solve linear systems, which dominate computational cost and energy use.
Due to the extent of accuracy challenges with analog MVM and the additional need for high-
precision numerical solutions, we emphasize algorithmic robustness. Therefore, we are interested
in methods with global convergence guarantees. Global convergence of inexact Newton methods
for nonlinear equations is studied in [10, 16, 17, 18]. For the handling of inexactness in nonlinear
optimization, trust-region methods have become indispensable. Inexact gradient computations in
trust-region methods were first discussed in [36] and [50] for unconstrained and convex-constrained
problems respectively. Modern trust-region methods leverage the inexact gradient conditions treated
in [12, 13]. These inexactness conditions motivate extensions to trust-region sequential quadratic
programming (SQP) methods for equality-constrained optimization, in form of optimal control [27]
and in its general form [26], where inexactness conditions for linear system solves are developed.
Inequality constraints can be additionally handled without changes to these conditions, through
augmented Lagrangian methods, see [5].

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we propose a nonlinear equation solver, based on Powell’s
dogleg trust-region method [40, 41] and motivated by the requirements of [26], that handles
low-precision iterative linear system solves with analog MVMs using relatively inexpensive high-
precision digital safeguards. Second, based on similar ideas, we extend the matrix-free trust-region
SQP method [26] for nonlinear optimization to handle inaccuracy in linear system solves with
analog MVMs. In both contributions, we ensure global convergence of the methods through the
proposed digital safeguards. Third, for the simulation and optimization of dynamical systems,
we address the lengthy programming times of analog devices through a collocation discretization,
where the quantities that change at every nonlinear iteration can be computed efficiently in digital
and where the collocation matrix, which dominates the cost of linear systems solves, is constant
and handled by the analog device. For applications with similar discretization needs, this choice
enables portability of the analog device without any tailoring of the matrix to the physics of the
application. Fourth, we demonstrate that small tradeoffs between analog and digital operations
in the application of the collocation matrix can lead to significant improvements in the accuracy
of analog MVMs and vastly improved performance of nonlinear solvers and optimizers. These
demonstrations underscore the importance of algorithm-hardware co-design.

4.3. Application to dynamical systems

In this section, we discuss spectral collocation discretizations of dynamical systems. These dis-
cretizations are well-suited to the analog MVM algorithms introduced in the previous sections for
two reasons.
 1. They can be highly accurate. Using an ℎ-method to approximate a sufficiently regular
 function over an interval (like the midpoint rule) is algebraically accurate—the decay of
 approximation error is proportional to the size of the time grid, 𝑑 + 1, raised to some fixed
 power. In contrast, the decay of approximation error for spectral collocation discretizations
 can be faster than (𝑑 + 1) 𝛼 for all exponents 𝛼. Such discretizations may require a smaller

 dense linear systems. But as we will explain...

 dominates the cost of linear solves but whose entries are constant across nonlinear iterations
 and even different problems.

The safeguarded SQP method uses Algorithm 2 to compute quasi-normal steps for the nonlinear

Table 4-5 demonstrates the robustness of our approach as the analog error models become more

energy savings. We note that the number of violations of condition (4.21), necessitating digital
constraint null-space projections, i.e., digital linear system solves, is quite modest, even for the
most complex error model.

4.5. Conclusions

This paper presents a novel approach to enhance energy-efficient computing for edge devices through
the integration of mixed digital and analog matrix-vector multiplication in nonlinear solvers and
optimizers. By leveraging the strengths of analog computing, particularly in performing energy-
efficient low-precision matrix-vector multiplication, alongside high-accuracy digital computations,
we have developed efficient algorithms that maintain global convergence in the presence of analog
imperfections. Our proposed methods, including modifications to Powell’s dogleg method and the
matrix-free trust-region sequential quadratic programming method [26], demonstrate significant

5.4. Analog MVM Power Efficiency

An accurately measured signal-to-quantization-noise ratio (SQNR) provides a direct bridge between
the precision requirements of an AIMC neural network and the energy–efficiency trends cataloged
in Murmann’s long-running ADC survey[37]. By mapping the network’s target SQNR (or the
SQNR needed to preserve latent-representation geometry and accuracy) to the surveys empirical
relationship between effective number of bits (ENOB), sampling rate, and energy per conversion
step, one can locate the operating point that minimizes power for the required precision. In practice,
the designer first determines the SQNR threshold at which accuracy degrades negligibly, converts
this to an equivalent ENOB, and then uses the Murmann data to identify ADC architectures and
bias conditions that achieve that ENOB at the lowest reported energy per conversion. This cross-
referencing reveals a “sweet spot” where the AIMC system meets its accuracy target while operating
on the lower envelope of the ADC energy–efficiency curve, guiding both architectural choices and

5.5. Future Work & Conclusion

This report highlights how accurately characterizing the signal-to-quantization-noise ratio (SQNR)
of an AIMC pipeline makes it possible to pinpoint a ’sweet spot’ precision that minimizes power
while maintaining target operational quality. By converting the required SQNR to an equivalent
effective number of bits (ENOB) and mapping that to Murmann’s comprehensive ADC survey [37]
of energy-per-conversion versus ENOB, we identify operating points that sit on the lowest-energy
envelope for the needed precision. This approach shows that power efficiency is governed not only
by device technology but also by careful co-optimization of ADC resolution, sampling rate, and
network precision requirements.
Future work will focus on (1) runtime adaptive precision, where real-time SQNR monitoring enables
dynamic adjustment of biasing to stay on the minimum-energy trajectory; (2) layer-aware precision
budgeting, using per-layer SQNR targets to allocate higher ENOB only where necessary, further
reducing total system power; and (3) technology scaling, validating the method across different
analog memory devices, and high-throughput workloads to ensure the identified SQNR–power
trade-off holds at larger scales. These steps aim to translate the SQNR-guided “sweet spot” into
robust, deployable power-efficient AIMC systems.

6. HYBRID ANALOG-DIGITAL SOLVERS

We are interested in solving large, sparse, linear systems on hybrid analog-digital systems, where
the analog part has in-memory analog crossbars that allow fast (but inexact) matrix-vector mul-
tiplication (MVM). The key issue is that most iterative methods, in particular, Krylov methods
[45] like CG or GMRES, assume the matrix-vector product is calculated in high precision. Some
recent work has been done on analyzing iterative methods in mixed precision [1], but a common
assumption is that the lower precision is reproducable. Analog hardware is typically noisy and
usually does not give the exact same result every time.

6.1. Krylov methods

For SPD systems, the most efficient Krylov method is conjugate gradients (CG), while for symmetric
but indefinite systems, MINRES is the method of choice. For unsymmetric systems, there are many
options but GMRES is the most popular method.
CG and MINRES use a 3-term recurrence to avoid orthogonalization but this assumes exact matvecs.
With analog matvecs, this relationship may break down, forcing us to use a flexible method that
allows variable preconditioning. Several flexible CG methods have been developed [24, 39]. They
typically involve reorthogonalization, so are more expensive than the standard CG method.
Another concern is that the noise is not symmetric, so even if the exact matrix is symmetric, the
MVM using analog hardware may not be. This may cause CG to break down. One option might
be to use an inexact Krylov method [46]. However, these methods are difficult to use in practice
and require a nonsymmetric method like GMRES.
In this report, we focus on SPD problems and variations of CG as the solver.

6.2. Preconditioners

Preconditioning is essential to accelerate convergence of Krylov methods. For CG, the precondi-
tioner itself must be symmetric. On a hybrid analog-digital system, one question is whether the
preconditioning step should be done in analog or digital. Simple and inexpensive preconditioners
such as Jacobi (diagonal) can be computed quickly in digital, but provide limited speedup for
ill-conditioned problems. Therefore, we focus on preconditioners that can exploit the fast MVM
provided by analog crossbars. Sparse approximate inverses use an explicit approximation of the
matrix, and thus relies on SpMV (sparse MVM). Unfortunately, most variations of approximate
inverses do not guarantee symmetry nor positive definiteness. One exception is the Factored Sparse
Approximate Inverses (FSAI) [33, 28] which are SPD by design.

 69

6.3. Results

We used CrossSim [20] to simulate computations on an analog in-memory crossbar. We set the
parameters to emulate the current SONOS design.
We initially did some experiments with analog MVM in the Krylov method, but this would often
fail to converge. Therefore, we abandoned this option. We believe high accuracy (digital hardware)
is essential in the Krylov method but wish to explore low accuracy (analog) preconditioning. When
the preconditioner is relative expensive, this can potentially both give speedup and reduce energy.
We implemented a factored sparse approximate inverse where the setup is done in digital to get an
accurate preconditioner, but the apply operation is done in analog. This appears to be a promising
approach. Note that another option is to compute the setup in analog but allow more fill. A denser
preconditioner will still be fast to apply in analog. We leave this as future work.
We wish to investigate the tradeoff between accuracy in the solution (as measured by the residual)
and the number of iterations. We could not use standard CG as it failed to converge in many cases.
Instead, we used flexible CG with partial reorthogonalization.
We compare the effectiveness of the solver with analog preconditioning (low accuracy) as compared
to the digital solver (high accuracy). We compare the number of iterations to reach a desired toler-
ance. If the analog preconditioner takes the same number of iterations as the digital preconditioner,
we say it is 100% effective.
We ran our experiments on all SPD matrices in the SuiteSparse collection with dimension between
100 and 2000 where CG converged in < 1000 iterations (which was 27). The results are shown

6.4. Conclusions

We have shown that noisy analog hardware can be used to solve SPD matrices using CG-type
solvers. We found that factored sparse approximates is a good preconditioner for analog in-
memory accelerators. Results from a simulator show that for low accuracy (high tolerances),
analog preconditioning is almost as good as digital, while for high accuracy (low tolerances),
analog preconditioning requires more iterations.

7. CONCLUSION

Analog MVM accelerators hold immense promise to provide substantial energy-efficiency improve-
ments in the post-Dennard era. By broadening the scope of applications which can be accelerated
by analog MVMs from neural networks to a wide range of MVM-dominated applications we can
unlock these benefits more broadly. This project demonstrates that applications which intuitively
seem dependent on high-precision operations can effectively take advantage of analog MVMs with
careful algorithm co-design. These innovations can enable greater levels of autonomy on power
constrained edge systems and lay the groundwork for future innovations on the use of analog MVMs
for traditional HPC applications.
