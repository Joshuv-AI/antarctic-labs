LARK: Learnability-Grounded Trajectory Selection for Efficient Reasoning Distillation
Tianrun Yu, Kaixiang Zhao, Chih-Chun Chen, Amanda Hughes, Taylor W. Killian, Fenglong Ma, Weitong Zhang, Porter Jenkins, [0.5em] Brigham Young University The Pennsylvania State University University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill [0.4em] *Corresponding author: pjenkins@cs.byu.edu

Abstract
We study trajectory selection for reasoning distillation, where teacher-generated reasoning trajectories are selectively used as supervision for a student model. Existing methods rely on heuristics such as trajectory quality or model confidence, but they often overlook whether a trajectory is learnable by the student. In this paper, we present LARK 1 1 1 Our code is available at https://github.com/Tianrun-Yu/LARK . , a learnability-grounded method for reasoning trajectory selection. LARK selects trajectories that the student can learn efficiently while preserving the generalization of the full training distribution. At the core of LARK is a learnability factor ρ \rho , which characterizes the rate at which the student’s training loss decreases. To estimate this rate efficiently and maintain generalization, we introduce a learnability proxy and a χ 2 \chi^{2} -regularized selection policy that balances learnability and distributional coverage, both with strong theoretical guarantees on their estimation error. Empirically, LARK consistently outperforms data selection baselines across multiple base models and reasoning tasks. Diagnostic analyses show that the LARK score predicts downstream training utility and that LARK-selected trajectories induce faster supervised fine-tuning loss reduction.

Introduction

Reasoning distillation has emerged as an effective paradigm for transferring chain-of-thought reasoning abilities from larger teacher Large Language Models (LLMs) to smaller student models
(
9
;
33
)
. In this setting, a teacher model generates reasoning trajectories, and a student model is fine-tuned to imitate them. Recent studies have shown that
a small set of carefully selected reasoning examples
can yield substantial performance gains
(
31
;
22
)
, often rivaling the use of much larger training sets. These findings suggest that reasoning distillation requires both
generating
sufficient reasoning data and
identifying
the supervision that is most useful for the student.

Many existing data selection methods for reasoning distillation still inherit the classical formulation of data selection in LLM fine-tuning
(
25
;
26
)
, where selection is performed at the question or sample level
(
32
;
36
;
18
)
. However, reasoning distillation presents a finer-grained selection problem. For a single question, we often have multiple candidate reasoning trajectories, generated by different teacher models, sampling runs, or reasoning styles. Even when several trajectories lead to the same correct final answer, they may provide very different training signals to the current student model.
A trajectory that appears natural, high-quality, or well aligned with the student model is not necessarily the one that the student can learn from most efficiently.

Existing trajectory selection methods largely rely on heuristic criteria. Some methods use external verifiers or LLM-as-a-judge scores to assess reasoning quality
(
37
;
28
)
, while GRAPE, Local Naturalness, and RSR score candidates using the student model itself
(
35
;
12
;
30
)
.
Although these methods are useful, they do not explicitly measure whether a trajectory is
learnable
by the student. This gap motivates the central question:

Can we design a principled trajectory selection criterion that ensures

distilled reasoning trajectories are learnable by the student model?

We answer this question by proposing
LARK
—
L
earnability-grounded
A
nchor-time
R
an
k
ing—a learnability-grounded method for reasoning trajectory selection, as illustrated in Figure
1
. LARK identifies the subset of trajectories from which the student model can learn most efficiently. At its core, LARK introduces a principled selection criterion that characterizes trajectory learnability from an optimization perspective, while preserving generalization through
\chi^{2}
-regularization. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We formulate reasoning trajectory selection as a learnability-grounded policy optimization problem. We introduce the anchor-time learnability rate
\rho
, which characterizes the decay rate of the post-training loss and thereby turns learnability into a principled optimization objective.

•

To estimate the learnability rate
\rho
efficiently while preventing the selector from hacking the learnability criterion, we use a first-order Taylor expansion around the unselected data distribution and derive a
\chi^{2}
-regularized policy optimization problem. Theoretically, we show that this policy optimization implicitly increases data learnability and can be solved in closed form under fixed-budget trajectory selection.

•

Empirically, we show that LARK outperforms existing baselines across multiple base models and tasks. Diagnostic analyses further support our theoretical claims and validate the learnability-grounded perspective for trajectory selection.

Related Work

Reasoning distillation and data selection.

Reasoning distillation transfers chain-of-thought traces from
stronger teacher models to smaller student models
(
9
;
33
)
. Recent work has shown that a small but carefully
chosen set of reasoning examples can rival much larger training
sets
(
32
;
31
;
22
)
. This has
motivated a growing body of work on data-selection methods that filter
examples by diversity, difficulty, uncertainty, or learning
impact
(
36
;
18
;
16
)
.

Most of these methods operate at the question or example level. In contrast, we study a finer-grained problem that arises in reasoning distillation. For a given question, multiple candidate reasoning trajectories may be available. These trajectories can all lead to correct answers while providing substantially different training signals to the student. LARK therefore asks which trajectory, among candidates for the same question, is most learnable for the current student model.

Trajectory selection and optimization-grounded weighting.

Existing trajectory-selection methods typically score candidates by either
external quality signals or student-side fit. External methods rely on correctness checks, verifiers, or LLM-as-a-judge evaluation to estimate the quality of a reasoning trajectory
(
5
)
. Student-side methods instead use the student model’s own behavior, such as likelihood (GRAPE
(
35
)
),
local-context likelihood
(Local Naturalness
(
12
)
), or rank-surprisal
alignment (RSR
(
30
)
), to identify trajectories that appear better aligned with the student.

LARK differs from both families by deriving its selection criterion from
a local learnability objective. Specifically, it asks whether a
trajectory yields a favorable
optimization signal
for the
current student. This perspective connects our work to gradient-based
data selection rooted in the Polyak–Łojasiewicz
framework
(
19
;
13
;
25
)
,
though unlike those methods we avoid per-trajectory backward passes
by using a forward-pass proxy. For multi-trajectory selection, we
adopt a
\chi^{2}
-regularized weighting that is related in spirit to
regularized alignment and offline
optimization
(
23
;
10
;
27
)
,
yielding a closed-form, budgeted rule.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Supervised Fine-Tuning for Reasoning Distillation

We consider the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of the student model. Let
\mathbf{x}
be the question and
\mathbf{y}_{k}{=}(y_{1}^{k},\cdots,y_{|a_{k}|}^{k})^{\top}
denote the
k
-th trajectory from the teacher with length
|a_{k}|
. The SFT loss is defined by

\displaystyle\textstyle{\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y}_{k})=\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|a_{k}|}-\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y_{t}\mid\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{y}_{<t})},

where
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the student language model with parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Over a set of response candidates
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we consider the finite-sum loss over a weight
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{K})^{\top}
written by

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{q})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\cdot\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y}_{k}),\quad\text{where }\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}=1,q_{k}\geq 0,\forall k\in[K]},

(1)

where
\mathbf{q}
encodes which trajectories should be emphasized during the SFT. For instance, when
q_{k}=\tfrac{1}{K}
for all
k\in[K]
, Eq. (
1
) reduces to the standard SFT loss over
K
trajectories. When
B
trajectories are selected for training, it is equivalent to finding a weight vector
\mathbf{q}
on the simplex
\Delta^{K}
with the number of non-zero elements exactly equal to
B
.

3.2
Gradient Flow Loss Decay Identity

We characterize the learnability from the lens of optimization theory, in which Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition of over-parameterized
optimization
(
2
;
17
;
3
)
centers on the ratio of the squared
gradient norm to the loss. We write this ratio as

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\triangleq{\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}}/{\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathbf{q})},

(2)

which we refer to as the
loss decay rate function
.
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{q})
characterizes the landscape of the optimization process. For instance, when
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{q})\geq\mu
, Eq. (
2
) implies
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\|^{2}\geq\mu\mathcal{L}
, which is commonly used in the analysis of over-parameterized optimization
(
17
)
(where optimal loss
\mathcal{L}^{*}{=}0
).

Decay rate of gradient flow.

Let
T>0
denote the total fine-tuning time. For a fixed weight vector
\mathbf{q}
, the
gradient flow

\phi_{\mathbf{q}}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{|\boldsymbol{\theta}|}
describes the evolution of the parameters when the student is fine-tuned on the weighted objective
\mathcal{L}(\cdot;\,\mathbf{q})
, defined as the solution to the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{\mathbf{q}}(s)=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q}),\qquad\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(0)={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{ref}},\qquad s\in[0,T].

Here
\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(0)=\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the starting point of fine-tuning,
\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(T)
is the parameter at time
T
, and
s
is the continuous gradient-flow time variable. By the chain rule and Eq.(
2
), we have

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})=-\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\|^{2}=-\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{q})\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{q}),\quad\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(0)=\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}.

Solving this ordinary differential equation yields

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(T);\mathbf{q})=\mathcal{L}(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\cdot\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\mathrm{d}s\right),}

(3)

which implies the integral
\int_{0}^{T}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\mathrm{d}s
plays an important role in controlling the loss decay.

4
Proposed Method

In this section, we present the methodology of LARK. Specifically,
§
4.1
establishes that the time-integrated learnability
objective in Eq. (
3
) can be controlled by
the anchor-time learnability rate
\rho(\cdot)
evaluated at the
initial student model.
§
4.2
then approximately optimizes

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
through local
linearization around the uniform prior, develops a forward-pass
proxy
\hat{g}_{k}
that estimates the local gradient of
\rho

without backward propagation, and bounds the residual errors by a

\chi^{2}
-regularization term to preserve generalization and prevent
reward hacking of the learnability criterion.
§
4.3
combines these pieces into a closed-form,
budget-parameterized selection rule with no hyperparameter to tune.

4.1
Estimating the Learnability Objective with Anchor-Relative Condition

As shown in Eq. (
3
), selecting trajectories that are
learnable
by the student model can be formulated as optimizing the time-integrated learnability objective
\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\int_{0}^{T}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\mathrm{d}s
, which favors trajectory distributions under which the post-training loss decays rapidly during SFT. However, directly optimizing this objective is infeasible, since it requires tracking the factor
\rho
along the entire SFT optimization trajectory. To address this challenge, we introduce the following structural condition, which allows the learnability objective to be approximated from a fixed anchor model.

Condition 1
.

We say the model satisfies the anchor-relative condition if there exists an absolute constant
\kappa>0
such that, for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
and every
s\in[0,T]
,
\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\geq\kappa\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
.

Under Condition
1
, the learnability rate evaluated at the initial student parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
provides a tractable proxy for the full time-integrated objective. This anchor-relative condition is consistent with the lazy fine-tuning behavior often observed in over-parameterized LLMs during SFT. In this regime, the model parameters remain close to the initialization
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
, and consequently the learnability rate
\rho
remains close to its anchor-time value
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
. We provide a theoretical justification of this condition under the neural tangent kernel regime
(
11
, NTK;)
in Appendix
A.2
, together with empirical validation in Appendix
A.3
.

The following proposition then follows naturally; we defer its proof to Appendix
A.1
.

Proposition 1
.

Under Condition
1
, for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
, the post-training loss of the student model satisfies
\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(T);\mathbf{q})\leq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\cdot\exp\bigl(-\kappa T\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\bigr).

Proposition
1
shows that, under the anchor-relative condition, the decay of the SFT loss can be controlled by the learnability rate evaluated at the anchor model
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Therefore, instead of directly optimizing the intractable trajectory-wide objective
\int_{0}^{T}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\mathrm{d}s
, we can optimize the anchor-time surrogate
\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
to encourage faster loss decay for the student model.

4.2
Approximately Optimizing Learnability
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
through Local Linearization

In practice, directly optimizing
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
is problematic. As shown in Appendix
B.1
, this objective induces a quasi-convex maximization problem over the simplex
\Delta^{K}
, whose optimum is attained at a vertex of the simplex. Consequently, exact maximization would lead to a degenerate solution that places all probability mass on a single trajectory, thereby “hacking” the learnability criterion instead of producing a useful training distribution.

This observation motivates treating the learnability criterion
\rho
as a
local
indicator around the uniform distribution
\mathbf{p}=(\tfrac{1}{K},\cdots,\tfrac{1}{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
which corresponds to standard SFT without trajectory selection. Rather than globally maximizing
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
, we restrict attention to distributions
\mathbf{q}
in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{p}
and locally linearize the objective around
\mathbf{p}
. The first-order Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})-\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q}),\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\rangle+o(\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\|_{2})}

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\sum_{k=1}^{K}}\underbrace{\tfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{k}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\mid_{\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}}}_{g_{k}^{*}}\cdot(q_{k}-p_{k})+\underbrace{o(\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\|_{2})}_{R_{2}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})}.

(4)

Two steps remain for optimizing Eq. (
4
). First, we need to estimate the first-order derivative
g_{k}^{*}
in a computationally efficient manner. Second, we need to bound the residual
R_{2}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
carefully.

4.2.1
Estimating gradient
g_{k}^{*}

According to the definition of
\rho
,
g_{k}^{*}=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{k}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{k}}\tfrac{\|\sum_{i}q_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i}q_{i}\ell_{i}}
, where
\mathbf{g}_{i}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell_{i}
and
\ell_{i}=\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y}_{i})
. With detailed derivations deferred to Appendix
B.3
, we can write

\displaystyle g_{k}^{*}=\frac{2\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\sum_{i}q_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}}{\sum_{i}q_{i}\ell_{i}}-\frac{\|\sum_{i}q_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{j}q_{j}\ell_{k}}\cdot\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}q_{i}\ell_{i}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}}=\frac{2\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\sum_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}-\frac{\|\sum_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\cdot\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}},

(5)

where the second equality follows from
q_{k}=p_{k}=\tfrac{1}{K}
. Based on Eq. (
5
), we make two observations. First, in many practical overparameterized networks, the per-trajectory gradients
\mathbf{g}_{k}
are nearly orthogonal in high-dimensional parameter space, i.e.,
\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{j}\approx\delta_{jk}\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}
. Second, for the single-trajectory
\rho
-factor defined in Eq. (
2
), denoted by
\rho_{k}^{*}=\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}/\ell_{k}
, the gradient norm can be written as
\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}=\rho_{k}^{*}\ell_{k}
. Plugging these observations into Eq. (
5
),
g_{k}^{*}
can be approximated by

\displaystyle g_{k}^{*}\approx\frac{2\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{j}\|\mathbf{g}_{j}\|^{2}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\cdot\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}=\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\left(2\rho_{k}^{*}-\frac{\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{*}\ell_{i}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\right).

(6)

In practice, calculating
\rho_{k}^{*}
requires backward propagation to compute
\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}
, which is computationally expensive at selection time. Fortunately, we can avoid this gradient computation by leveraging the softmax structure of the last layer in next-token prediction, as stated in the following lemma:

Lemma 1

(Forward-pass
\rho_{k}^{*}
estimation, informal)
.

For any
k\in[K]
, let
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|}
be the probability vector produced by the student model for the
t
-th token, conditioned on the question
\mathbf{x}
and the previous response tokens
\mathbf{y}_{<t}^{k}
, and let
\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|}
be the one-hot vector corresponding to the ground-truth token from the teacher model.
Let

\hat{\rho}_{k}\triangleq\frac{\sum_{t}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\|^{2}}{\ell_{k}}
,
then under standard structural conditions, there exist absolute positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
where
\hat{\rho}_{k}\in[C_{1}\rho_{k}^{*},C_{2}\rho_{k}^{*}]
.

Lemma
1
suggests that
\rho_{k}^{*}
can be estimated in a well-controlled manner by the forward-pass quantity
\hat{\rho}_{k}
, without explicitly computing gradients. We defer the detailed proof to Appendix
B.4
. Substituting
\hat{\rho}_{k}
into Eq. (
5
) yields our practical estimator of the local gradient:

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{k}=\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\left(2\hat{\rho}_{k}-\frac{\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\right),\text{ where }\hat{\rho}_{k}\triangleq\frac{\sum_{t}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\|^{2}}{\ell_{k}}.

(7)

4.2.2
Bounding residuals
R(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})

We next control the residual errors introduced by the local linearization and the gradient estimation. More precisely, substituting
g_{k}^{*}
with
\hat{g}_{k}
in Eq. (
4
) yields

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})-\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{p})=\textstyle{\sum_{k=1}^{K}}\hat{g}_{k}\cdot(q_{k}-p_{k})+\underbrace{\textstyle{\sum_{k=1}^{K}}(g_{k}^{*}-\hat{g}_{k})\cdot(q_{k}-p_{k})}_{R_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})}+\underbrace{o(\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\|_{2})}_{R_{2}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})},

where
R_{1}
captures the error from estimating the true local gradient
g_{k}^{*}
by
\hat{g}_{k}
, and
R_{2}
captures the higher-order error from the Taylor expansion. The following lemma shows that both terms can be controlled by the
\chi^{2}
distance from the uniform distribution.

Lemma 2

(
\chi^{2}
controls both error terms, informal)
.

Under standard structural conditions, there exist constants
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
such that for all
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
,

\displaystyle|R_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})|\leq\alpha_{1}\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{p})+1,\qquad|R_{2}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})|\leq\alpha_{2}\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{p}).

(8)

Lemma
2
shows that the residual errors are controlled by the
\chi^{2}
distance between the selected distribution
\mathbf{q}
and the uniform distribution
\mathbf{p}
, with explicit forward-computable constants
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
given in Appendix
B.5
. This further supports our intuition that optimization should be performed in a local neighborhood around the original, non-selected data distribution
\mathbf{p}
, to preserve generalization and prevent the selector from drifting toward degenerate solutions. Combining the linearized objective with the residual bounds gives the following lower bound on the original learnability improvement; we defer its proof to Appendix
B.6
.

Theorem 1

(LARK objective, informal)
.

There exist absolute constants
\alpha>0
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})-\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{p})\geq\textstyle{\sum_{k=1}^{K}}\hat{g}_{k}\cdot(q_{k}-p_{k})-\alpha\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\parallel\mathbf{p})+C.

(9)

Theorem
1
reduces the intractable learnability improvement to a tractable surrogate that depends on
\mathbf{q}
only through (i) the inner product
\langle\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p},\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}\rangle
between the trajectory deviation and the forward-pass score vector, and (ii) the
\chi^{2}
distance to the uniform prior. The constant
C
is independent of
\mathbf{q}
and therefore does not affect the maximizer over
\Delta^{K}
. This reduction sets the stage for the closed-form selection rule developed in §
4.3
.

4.3
LARK: Closed-form Solution for Fixed-Budget Trajectory Selection

Theorem
1
reduces the intractable learnability
improvement to a tractable
\chi^{2}
-regularized optimization based on

\{\hat{g}_{k}\}
. In practice, many data selection algorithms operate in
the fixed-budget setting, where the agent needs to select
B\leq K

trajectories per question, meaning that the support size of
\mathbf{q}

is
B
. This fixed-budget optimization admits a closed-form
solution, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(
B
-parameterized closed form)
.

Sort
\hat{g}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\hat{g}_{K}
and assume
1\leq B<K
with
\hat{g}_{B}>\hat{g}_{B+1}
.
Under the uniform prior
p_{k}=1/K
, the maximizer of optimization objective

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{q}}=\arg\textstyle{\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K},\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{q})=B}}\left\{\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}_{k}(q_{k}-p_{k})-\tfrac{\tau(B)}{2}\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{p})\right\},

(10)

with regularizer
\tau(B)=\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{B}(\hat{g}_{j}-\hat{g}_{B+1})
takes the closed form
\hat{q}_{i}=(\hat{g}_{i}-\hat{g}_{B+1})/\sum_{j=1}^{B}(\hat{g}_{j}-\hat{g}_{B+1})
for
i\leq B
, and
\hat{q}_{i}=0
otherwise.

Input:

Candidates
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
; reference
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}

with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
; budget
B
.

Output:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\hat{\mathbf{q}})=\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\hat{q}_{k}\,\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{y}_{k})
.

1

for

k=1,\ldots,K

do

2

Forward pass of
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
on
\mathbf{y}_{k}
; record

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k},p_{t}
for
t\in\{1,\ldots,|a_{k}|\}

3

\ell_{k}\leftarrow\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{t}-\log p_{t}

4

\hat{\rho}_{k}\leftarrow\dfrac{\sum_{t}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\|^{2}}{\sum_{t}-\log p_{t}}

5

6

for

k=1,\ldots,K

Remark 2

5.3
RQ2: How Much Does Each Component Contribute?

We ablate the two design choices in LARK: the trajectory score and
the weighting rule.

Trajectory score.

Fixing the weighting to
\chi^{2}
-
B
, we compare four scores
(Figure
2(a)
): the inverse loss

1/\ell_{k}
, the Brier residual
\sum_{t}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\|^{2}
, the proxy

\hat{\rho}_{k}
of Lemma
1
, and the induced score

\hat{g}_{k}
. The inverse loss is the weakest, confirming that
likelihood alone does not characterize learnability;
\hat{\rho}_{k}

already improves substantially by combining residual error with
loss; and
\hat{g}_{k}
is the best across all four benchmarks, since
it captures the marginal contribution of each trajectory around

\mathbf{p}
rather than scoring trajectories in isolation.

Weighting rule.

Fixing the score to
\hat{g}_{k}
, we vary the weighting at
B{=}3

(Figure
2(b)
) over uniform,
score-proportional, softmax, and
\chi^{2}
-
B
weights. The

\chi^{2}
-
B
rule consistently outperforms the alternatives, with
the largest gains on AIME (where score noise matters most) and on
MATH-500 (where the benefit accumulates over many problems). LARK’s
improvement therefore comes not only from selecting high-
\hat{g}_{k}

trajectories but also from assigning robust weights within the
selected support.

Efficiency.

LARK is forward-pass only and therefore does not require the
per-trajectory backward passes needed to compute the exact directional
derivative
g_{k}^{*}
. Its scoring cost matches the lowest-cost
student-side baselines, with full complexity analysis and wall-clock
measurements reported in Appendix
D.1
.

6
Conclusion

We presented
LARK
, a learnability-grounded method for reasoning trajectory selection in distillation. LARK estimates trajectory learnability with a forward-pass surrogate
\hat{g}_{k}
and uses a closed-form
\chi^{2}
-regularized soft top-
B
rule to construct a weighted SFT objective, avoiding expensive per-trajectory backward passes while preserving distributional coverage. Across three student models and four reasoning benchmarks, LARK consistently outperforms strong trajectory selection baselines under both single-trajectory and multi-trajectory budgets. Ablation and diagnostic analyses show that both the learnability score and the weighting rule are important, and that LARK-selected trajectories accelerate SFT loss decay while providing structured local correction signals. Overall, our results support learnability as an effective principle for efficient reasoning distillation, with future extensions to noisier candidate pools and broader reasoning domains.

Appendix A
Anchor-Time Analysis of the Learnability Objective

This appendix supports the anchor-time reduction in Section
4.1
. We first prove the post-training loss bound (Appendix
A.1
). We then provide a theoretical justification of the anchor-relative trajectory bound through the NTK lazy-training framework (Appendix
A.2
), and verify it empirically on real SFT trajectories (Appendix
A.3
).

A.1
Proof of the Anchor-Time Loss Decay Bound

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Fix any
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
. By the gradient-flow decay identity (
3
) and Condition
1
, which gives
\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\geq\kappa\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
for every
s\in[0,T]
, we have

\int_{0}^{T}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\,ds\;\geq\;\int_{0}^{T}\kappa\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\,ds\;=\;\kappa T\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q}).

Substituting into (
3
) and using the non-negativity of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
together with the monotonicity of
x\mapsto\exp(-x)
on
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(T);\mathbf{q})\;=\;\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\cdot\exp\!\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\,ds\right)\;\leq\;\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\cdot\exp\!\bigl(-\kappa T\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\bigr).

(11)

Since
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
was arbitrary, the bound holds for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
.
∎

A.2
NTK-Based Justification of the Trajectory Bound

We provide a theoretical justification of Condition
1
through the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework. Under standard NTK lazy-training conditions for pretrained language model fine-tuning, combined with a local non-degeneracy condition on the ground-truth probability, we derive a strictly positive constant
\kappa
for which Condition
1
provably holds.

Note on terminology.

Throughout this subsection, we distinguish between two kinds of structural premises.
Condition
1
is the anchor-relative condition stated in the main text, which we aim to
justify
.
Assumptions
A.1
–
A.4
below are standard NTK lazy-training premises, which we use to
derive
Condition
1
as a theorem (Proposition
2
).

NTK setup.

We adopt the token-level objects from Section
4.2
. For each candidate trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
, recall the per-token residual
\delta_{t}=\pi_{t}-\mathbf{e}_{y_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
and the stacked residual matrix
\Delta_{k}=[\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{|a_{k}|}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|a_{k}|\times V}
, with
p_{t}=\pi_{t}(y_{t})
the probability assigned to the ground-truth token. We additionally introduce the following appendix-only objects.

Logits Jacobian.
For trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
, define

J_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\triangleq\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{|a_{k}|})\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{|a_{k}|V\times|\boldsymbol{\theta}|}.

(12)

Vectorized residual.
Let
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\triangleq\mathrm{vec}(\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\in\mathbb{R}^{|a_{k}|V}
.

Weighted NTK matrix and weighted residual.
Given
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
, let

\tilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{q_{1}/|a_{1}|}\,J_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{q_{K}/|a_{K}|}\,J_{K}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{pmatrix},\qquad\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{q_{1}/|a_{1}|}\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{q_{K}/|a_{K}|}\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{pmatrix},

(13)

and define the weighted empirical NTK matrix

\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\triangleq\tilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\,\tilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})^{\top}.

(14)

NTK identity.
By the standard chain rule for softmax cross-entropy (
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{t}}\ell_{t}=\delta_{t}
, see Section
4.2
), the weighted gradient admits the factorization

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})=\tilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})^{\top}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q}),

which yields the NTK identity

\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}\;=\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})^{\top}\,\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q}).

(15)

When some
q_{k}=0
, the corresponding trajectory block contributes neither to the weighted loss nor to its gradient. The zero-weight blocks are therefore harmless in the identity above, but they should not be used to impose full-matrix positive definiteness. This is why Assumption
A.3
below conditions the NTK only along the residual directions that enter the weighted objective.

Assumptions.

We adopt four conditions for the NTK analysis of language model fine-tuning.

Assumption A.1

(Lazy training)
.

There exists a constant
R>0
such that, for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
,

\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\|\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s)-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|\;\leq\;R.

(16)

Assumption A.2

(NTK stability)
.

There exists
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that, for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
and every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in B(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},R)
,

(1-\epsilon)\,\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\;\preceq\;\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\;\preceq\;(1+\epsilon)\,\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q}).

(17)

Assumption A.3

(Residual-direction NTK conditioning at anchor)
.

There exist constants
0<\lambda_{-}\leq\lambda_{+}<\infty
such that, for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
and every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in B(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},R)
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})^{\top}\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\;\geq\;\lambda_{-}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2},

(18)

and

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})^{\top}\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\;\leq\;\lambda_{+}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}.

(19)

Assumption A.4

(Bounded ground-truth probability)
.

There exist constants
0<p_{-}\leq p_{+}<1
such that, for every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in B(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},R)
, every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
, every
k\in[K]
, and every token position
t
,

p_{-}\;\leq\;p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;\leq\;p_{+}.

(20)

Assumption
A.1
encodes lazy fine-tuning behavior, classical in NTK theory
[
11
,
4
]
and supported empirically for pre-trained transformer fine-tuning by
20
. Assumption
A.2
requires the empirical NTK matrix to remain near-constant within the lazy ball, with rate
O(1/\sqrt{m})
at width
m

[
14
,
11
]
, and is supported empirically by
20
for fine-tuning. Assumption
A.3
is a residual-direction conditioning condition: it does not require the full weighted NTK matrix to be positive definite in directions corresponding to zero-weight trajectories, but only requires non-degenerate curvature along the residual directions that determine the weighted loss and its gradient. Assumption
A.4
is a local non-degeneracy condition on teacher-forced token probabilities:
p_{t}>0
holds strictly under finite logits, while
p_{t}<1
excludes already-converged tokens for which the residual signal vanishes.

Loss-residual relation.

Before establishing the main result, we relate the loss
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})
to the squared residual norm
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}
. The relation depends only on Assumption
A.4
, and the constants
\nu_{-},\nu_{+}
are derived explicitly from
p_{-},p_{+}
.

Lemma 4

(Loss–residual two-sided bound)
.

Under Assumption
A.4
, for every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in B(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},R)
and every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
,

\nu_{-}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\;\leq\;\nu_{+}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2},

(21)

where

\nu_{-}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad\nu_{+}\;=\;\frac{\log(1/p_{-})}{(1-p_{+})^{2}}.

(22)

Proof.

We first establish corresponding pointwise bounds at the token level: for every token position
t
, with
p\triangleq p_{t}\in[p_{-},p_{+}]
,

\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\delta_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\ell_{t}\;\leq\;\frac{\log(1/p_{-})}{(1-p_{+})^{2}}\,\|\delta_{t}\|^{2}.

(23)

Lower bound.

\displaystyle\|\delta_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}(1-p)^{2}+\sum_{v\neq y_{t}}\pi_{t}(v)^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}(1-p)^{2}+\Bigl(\max_{v\neq y_{t}}\pi_{t}(v)\Bigr)\sum_{v\neq y_{t}}\pi_{t}(v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}(1-p)^{2}+(1-p)\cdot(1-p)\;=\;2(1-p)^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leq}}2(1-p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leq}}2\ell_{t},

(24)

where (a) is the definition
\delta_{t}=\pi_{t}-\mathbf{e}_{y_{t}}
together with
\pi_{t}(y_{t})=p
; (b) uses
\sum_{v}a_{v}^{2}\leq\max_{v}a_{v}\cdot\sum_{v}a_{v}
for non-negative
\{a_{v}\}
; (c) uses both
\max_{v\neq y_{t}}\pi_{t}(v)\leq\sum_{v\neq y_{t}}\pi_{t}(v)=1-p
and
\sum_{v\neq y_{t}}\pi_{t}(v)=1-p
; (d) uses
1-p\leq 1
; and (e) uses the elementary inequality
-\log p\geq 1-p
for
p\in(0,1]
.

Upper bound.

\displaystyle\ell_{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{=}}-\log p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(g)}}{{\leq}}\log(1/p_{-})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(h)}}{{=}}\frac{\log(1/p_{-})}{(1-p_{+})^{2}}\,(1-p_{+})^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\log(1/p_{-})}{(1-p_{+})^{2}}\,(1-p)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(j)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\log(1/p_{-})}{(1-p_{+})^{2}}\,\|\delta_{t}\|^{2},

(25)

where (f) is the definition of
\ell_{t}
; (g) uses
p\geq p_{-}
and the monotonicity of
-\log
; (h) is multiplication and division by
(1-p_{+})^{2}>0
; (i) uses
(1-p_{+})^{2}\leq(1-p)^{2}
from
p\leq p_{+}<1
; and (j) uses
\|\delta_{t}\|^{2}\geq(1-p)^{2}
from the
y_{t}
-th coordinate.

Aggregation.

Summing (
23
) over
t=1,\ldots,|a_{k}|
and dividing by
|a_{k}|
yields

\nu_{-}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}\|^{2}/|a_{k}|\leq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{y}_{k})\leq\nu_{+}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}\|^{2}/|a_{k}|,

where
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{t}\|\delta_{t}\|^{2}
. Multiplying by
q_{k}
and summing over
k
gives

\nu_{-}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{q_{k}}{|a_{k}|}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\;\leq\;\nu_{+}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{q_{k}}{|a_{k}|}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}\|^{2},

(26)

and the result follows from

\sum_{k}(q_{k}/|a_{k}|)\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}\|^{2}=\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}.

∎

Main result.

We now establish the main result.

Proposition 2

(NTK-based justification of Condition
1
)
.

Under Assumptions
A.1
–
A.4
, Condition
1
holds with

\kappa\;=\;\frac{(1-\epsilon)\,\lambda_{-}\,\nu_{-}}{\lambda_{+}\,\nu_{+}}\;=\;\frac{(1-\epsilon)\,\lambda_{-}\,(1-p_{+})^{2}}{2\,\lambda_{+}\,\log(1/p_{-})}\;>\;0.

(27)

Proof.

Fix any
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
and
s\in[0,T]
, and set
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s)
. Assumption
A.1
gives
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in B(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},R)
.

Writing
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\triangleq\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\top}\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}(1-\epsilon)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\top}\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geq}}(1-\epsilon)\,\lambda_{-}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|^{2},

(28)

where (a) is the NTK identity (
15
); (b) applies the lower-bound part of Assumption
A.2
; and (c) applies the residual-direction lower bound (

) from Assumption

. Combined with the upper bound
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{q})\leq\nu_{+}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|^{2}

(29)

Writing
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\triangleq\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\Theta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leq}}\lambda_{+}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2},

(30)

where (d) is (
15
) at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
and (e) applies the residual-direction upper bound (
19
) from Assumption
A.3
. Combined with the lower bound
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\geq\nu_{-}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2}

,

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\;=\;\frac{\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})\|^{2}}{\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})}\;\leq\;\frac{\lambda_{+}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2}}{\nu_{-}\,\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2}}\;=\;\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\nu_{-}}.

(31)

Combining (
29
) and (
31
), both being positive,

\frac{\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})}{\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{\geq}}\;\frac{(1-\epsilon)\,\lambda_{-}/\nu_{+}}{\lambda_{+}/\nu_{-}}\;=\;\frac{(1-\epsilon)\,\lambda_{-}\,\nu_{-}}{\lambda_{+}\,\nu_{+}},

(32)

where (f) uses the lower bound on the numerator from (

) and the upper bound on the denominator from (
31
). Since
\mathbf{q}
and

were arbitrary,

\inf_{s\in[0,T]}\rho(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}(s);\mathbf{q})\;\geq\;\frac{(1-\epsilon)\,\lambda_{-}\,\nu_{-}}{\lambda_{+}\,\nu_{+}}\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q}),

which is Condition

with

\kappa=\frac{(1-\epsilon)\lambda_{-}\nu_{-}}{\lambda_{+}\nu_{+}}.

Substituting
\nu_{-}=1/2
and
\nu_{+}=\log(1/p_{-})/(1-p_{+})^{2}
from Lemma

∎

Remark on
\kappa
.

The expression

\kappa=\frac{(1-\epsilon)\lambda_{-}(1-p_{+})^{2}}{2\lambda_{+}\log(1/p_{-})}

admits a clean interpretation. The ratio
\lambda_{-}/\lambda_{+}
is the effective condition number of the empirical NTK along the residual directions that determine the weighted loss;
1-\epsilon
captures NTK stability along the trajectory; and
(1-p_{+})^{2}/\log(1/p_{-})
is a geometric correction accounting for the cross-entropy loss, arising from the unbounded growth of
-\log p
near
p=0
. Under squared loss, this last factor reduces to a constant and
\kappa
collapses to
(1-\epsilon)\lambda_{-}/\lambda_{+}
, recovering the classical NTK convergence-rate dependence on the kernel condition number
[
11
,
6
]
. The post-training loss bound in Proposition
1
thus holds with this
\kappa
, providing theoretical justification for using
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})
as the anchor-time selection objective in Section
4.1
.

and that its (constrained or unconstrained) maximum collapses to a one-hot solution; this is the formal justification for the local-linearization strategy adopted in Section
4.2
. Appendix
B.2
fixes the additional notation and structural assumptions used by the rest of the appendix. Appendix
B.3
collects auxiliary lemmas (gradient identities and bounds at the LM-head and backbone, together with the Taylor decomposition of
\rho
around
\mathbf{p}
) that are reused by the subsequent proofs. Appendices
B.4
,
B.5
,
B.6
, and
B.7
respectively give the formal versions and full proofs of Lemma
1
(forward-pass
\rho_{k}^{*}
estimation), Lemma
2
(
\chi^{2}
controls both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
), Theorem
1
(the LARK objective lower bound), and Lemma
3
(the
B
-parameterized closed form). Finally, Appendix
B.8
provides a quantitative error bound on the surrogate
\hat{g}_{k}
relative to the exact directional derivative
g_{k}^{*}
, together with empirical evidence that the two are tightly correlated on real student models.

B.1
Quasiconvexity of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})
and Collapse to One-Hot

Throughout this subsection, fix
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
and recall from Section
4.2
that
\mathbf{g}_{k}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell_{k}
with
\ell_{k}=\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf{y}_{k})>0
. Define

N(\mathbf{q})\;\triangleq\;\Bigl\|\textstyle\sum_{k}q_{k}\mathbf{g}_{k}\Bigr\|^{2}\;=\;\mathbf{q}^{\top}G\,\mathbf{q},\qquad D(\mathbf{q})\;\triangleq\;\textstyle\sum_{k}q_{k}\ell_{k},

(37)

where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
with
G_{ij}=\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\,\mathbf{g}_{j}\rangle
is the gradient Gram matrix. Since
G\succeq 0
,
N(\mathbf{q})
is a convex quadratic in
\mathbf{q}
, and
D(\mathbf{q})>0
is affine, so
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})=N(\mathbf{q})/D(\mathbf{q})
.

Proposition 3

(Quasiconvexity of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})
)
.

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})
is quasiconvex in
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for every
c\geq 0
, the sublevel set
\mathcal{S}_{c}\triangleq\{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}:\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})\leq c\}
is convex. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{S}_{c}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\iff}}\;\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})\leq c

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\iff}}\;\frac{N(\mathbf{q})}{D(\mathbf{q})}\leq c

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\iff}}\;N(\mathbf{q})\leq c\cdot D(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\iff}}\;\mathbf{q}^{\top}G\,\mathbf{q}-c\textstyle\sum_{k}q_{k}\ell_{k}\leq 0,

(38)

where (a) is the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{c}
; (b) substitutes
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})=N(\mathbf{q})/D(\mathbf{q})
; (c) multiplies both sides by
D(\mathbf{q})>0
; and (d) substitutes the definitions of
N
and
D
from (
37
). The left-hand side of the final inequality in (
38
) is the sum of the convex quadratic
\mathbf{q}^{\top}G\,\mathbf{q}
(since
G\succeq 0
) and the affine term
-c\sum_{k}q_{k}\ell_{k}
, hence convex in
\mathbf{q}
. Therefore
\mathcal{S}_{c}
is the intersection of a convex sublevel set with the convex set
\Delta^{K}
, which is convex.
∎

Corollary 1

(Maximum at a vertex)
.

\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})
is attained at an extreme point of
\Delta^{K}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\;\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})\big|_{\text{extreme point}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\;\max_{k\in[K]}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{e}_{k}),

(39)

where (a) follows because every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
can be written as a convex combination of the extreme points
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{K}\}
, namely
\mathbf{q}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}
, and quasiconvexity implies

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})\leq\max_{k\in[K]:\,q_{k}>0}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{e}_{k})\leq\max_{k\in[K]}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{e}_{k}).

The reverse inequality is immediate since each
\mathbf{e}_{k}\in\Delta^{K}
. Hence the maximum over
\Delta^{K}
is attained at an extreme point. Step (b) identifies the extreme points of
\Delta^{K}
as the standard basis vectors
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{K}\}
.
∎

Corollary 2

(One-hot optimum)
.

\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})\;=\;\max_{k\in[K]}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{e}_{k})\;=\;\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{\ell_{k}}\;=\;\max_{k\in[K]}\rho_{k}^{*}.

(40)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\;\max_{k\in[K]}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{e}_{k})

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\;\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{N(\mathbf{e}_{k})}{D(\mathbf{e}_{k})}\;=\;\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{\ell_{k}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\;\max_{k\in[K]}\rho_{k}^{*},

(41)

where (a) applies Corollary
1
; (b) substitutes
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{e}_{k}
into the definitions in (
37
), giving
N(\mathbf{e}_{k})=\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}
and
D(\mathbf{e}_{k})=\ell_{k}
; and (c) uses the definition
\rho_{k}^{*}=\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}/\ell_{k}
from Section
4.2
.
∎

B.2
Notation and Structural Assumptions

This subsection fixes the additional notation and structural assumptions used throughout Appendices
B.3
–
B.8
.

B.2.1
Notation

Symbols inherited from the main text.

We use the same symbols as Section
4.2
:
\mathbf{x}
is the question;
\mathbf{y}_{k}=(y_{1}^{k},\ldots,y_{|a_{k}|}^{k})
is the
k
-th candidate trajectory, of length
|a_{k}|
;
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|}
is the predictive distribution of the reference student
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
at position
t
under teacher forcing;
\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|}
is the one-hot vector for the ground-truth token;
\ell_{k}=\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf{y}_{k})
is the length-normalized cross-entropy of trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
;
\mathbf{g}_{k}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell_{k}
is its gradient;
\rho_{k}^{*}=\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}/\ell_{k}
is the per-trajectory rate;
\hat{\rho}_{k}
is its forward-pass proxy (see Lemma
1
);
\hat{g}_{k}
and
g_{k}^{*}
are the surrogate and exact directional derivatives at
\mathbf{p}
;
R_{1},R_{2}
are the residual terms in (
4
);
\mathbf{p}=(1/K,\ldots,1/K)\in\Delta^{K}
is the uniform prior.

Parameter decomposition.

We split the student parameters as
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}})
, where
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
is the LM head matrix and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}}
collects all backbone parameters. For each trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
and position
t
, the backbone produces a hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the LM head produces logits
\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
, and
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{t})
. The token-level loss is
\ell_{t}=-\log\pi_{t}^{k}(y_{t}^{k})
, so that
\ell_{k}=\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\sum_{t}\ell_{t}
.

Token-level residual.

We write the per-token prediction residual as
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\triangleq\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
, which coincides with
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{t}}\ell_{t}
by the standard softmax–cross-entropy identity (see Lemma
5
below).

Stacked matrices.

For each trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
we collect the per-token residuals and hidden states into matrices

\boldsymbol{\Delta}\;\triangleq\;[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{|a_{k}|}]^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{|a_{k}|\times|\mathcal{V}|},\qquad\mathbf{H}\;\triangleq\;[\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{|a_{k}|}]^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{|a_{k}|\times d}.

(42)

We also write
\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\triangleq[\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}}]_{t=1}^{|a_{k}|}\in\mathbb{R}^{|a_{k}|\,d\times|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}}|}
for the sequence-level Jacobian of the hidden states with respect to the backbone parameters. The dependence on
\mathbf{y}_{k}
is suppressed when the trajectory is clear from context.

Aggregated quantities.

Two aggregations of the per-token residual norms appear repeatedly. Define the (length-normalized) Brier score

\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\;\triangleq\;\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|a_{k}|}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\|^{2}\;=\;\tfrac{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}}{|a_{k}|}.

(43)

With this notation, the forward-pass proxy of Lemma
1
can be written as

\hat{\rho}_{k}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{t}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\|^{2}}{\sum_{t}\ell_{t}}\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})}{\ell_{k}},

(44)

so that the
1/|a_{k}|
factors cancel exactly in numerator and denominator.

Multi-trajectory quantities.

Across the candidate pool, write
G\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
for the gradient Gram matrix with
G_{ij}=\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{g}_{j}\rangle
, and let
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{K})^{\top}
. We use the shorthands

\bar{\mathbf{g}}\;\triangleq\;\tfrac{1}{K}\textstyle\sum_{k}\mathbf{g}_{k},\qquad\bar{\ell}\;\triangleq\;\tfrac{1}{K}\textstyle\sum_{k}\ell_{k},

(45)

together with
\hat{\rho}_{\max}\triangleq\max_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}
,
\ell_{\min}\triangleq\min_{i}\ell_{i}
, and
\ell_{\max}\triangleq\max_{i}\ell_{i}
. We also write
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\triangleq\sum_{k}p_{k}v_{k}^{2}
for the
\mathbf{p}
-weighted Euclidean norm; under uniform
\mathbf{p}
,
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k}v_{k}^{2}
.

B.2.2
Structural assumptions

We make four structural assumptions on the reference student
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and its candidate-pool statistics. All four are mild conditions that are either standard in the convergence analysis of deep networks or directly verifiable from a single forward pass.

Assumption B.1

(Hidden-state norm stability)
.

There exist constants
0<C_{-}\leq C_{+}
such that for every candidate trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
and every token position
t
,

C_{-}^{2}\;\leq\;\|\mathbf{h}_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;C_{+}^{2}.

(46)

Remark 3
.

This holds naturally in modern LLMs employing RMSNorm
[
34
]
, which constrains hidden-state norms to a tight interval; we have
C_{+}/C_{-}\approx 1
in practice.

Assumption B.2

(Local Jacobian conditioning)
.

There exists
\Lambda>0
such that for every candidate trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
,

\lambda_{\max}\bigl(\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\,\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})^{\top}\bigr)\;\leq\;\Lambda.

(47)

Remark 4
.

This is a standard local Lipschitz continuity assumption on the backbone, widely adopted in convergence analyses of SFT
[
17
]
.

Assumption B.3

(Matrix cosine alignment)
.

There exists
\zeta_{-}>0
such that for every candidate trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
,

\zeta(\mathbf{y}_{k})\;\triangleq\;\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}}\;\geq\;\zeta_{-}\;>\;0.

(48)

Remark 5
.

\zeta(\mathbf{y}_{k})
is the squared Frobenius cosine similarity between the residual matrix
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and the hidden-state matrix
\mathbf{H}
. Assumption
B.3
requires that the joint signal between prediction residuals and hidden states does not degenerate, and is verifiable from a single forward pass.

Assumption B.4

(Strictly positive trajectory loss)
.

\ell_{\min}=\min_{i\in[K]}\ell_{i}>0
.

Remark 6
.

Under finite logits,
\ell_{t}>0
at every token, so
\ell_{k}>0
for every trajectory and Assumption
B.4
holds automatically. We state it explicitly because it is needed in Appendix
B.5
to ensure the Hessian of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})
is uniformly bounded over
\Delta^{K}
via
D(\mathbf{q})=\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{q}\geq\ell_{\min}
.

B.3.1
LM head and backbone gradient identities and bounds

Lemma 5

(LM head gradient)
.

Let
\ell_{t}=-\log\pi_{t}^{k}(y_{t}^{k})
,
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{t})
, and
\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t}
. Then

\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\,\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d},\qquad\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}\;=\;\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}.

(49)

Proof.

We prove the first identity; the second is immediate from the chain rule applied to
\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t}
. For any direction
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}},\,\mathbf{U}\right\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\;\left\langle\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}},\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}[\mathbf{U}]\right\rangle

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\;\left\langle\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}},\,\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\;\sum_{v=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial z_{t,v}}\,[\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t}]_{v}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\;\sum_{v=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\bigl(\pi_{t}^{k}(v)-\mathbf{1}[v=y_{t}^{k}]\bigr)\,[\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t}]_{v}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{=}}\;\langle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k}),\,\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{=}}\;\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\,\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\top},\,\mathbf{U}\rangle_{F},

(50)

where (a) is the chain rule for the Fréchet derivative; (b) follows from the linearity of
\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t}
in
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}
, giving
\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}[\mathbf{U}]=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t}
; (c) expands the inner product componentwise; (d) is the standard softmax–cross-entropy gradient identity,
\partial\ell_{t}/\partial z_{t,v}=\pi_{t}^{k}(v)-\mathbf{1}[v=y_{t}^{k}]
, obtained by applying the quotient rule to
\pi_{t}^{k}(y_{t}^{k})=e^{z_{t,y_{t}^{k}}}/\sum_{v^{\prime}}e^{z_{t,v^{\prime}}}
; (e) recognizes the sum as
\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t},\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t}\rangle
with
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}(y_{t}^{k})
; and (f) uses the trace identity
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{c}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a}\mathbf{c}^{\top},\mathbf{B}\rangle_{F}
. Since
\mathbf{U}
was arbitrary,
\partial\ell_{t}/\partial\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\top}
.
∎

Lemma 6

(LM head gradient bounds)
.

Under Assumptions
B.1
and
B.3
, for every candidate trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
,

\zeta_{-}\,C_{-}^{2}\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\;\leq\;\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}\ell_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\;\leq\;C_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k}).

(51)

Proof.

By Lemma
5
and the definition
\ell_{k}=\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\sum_{t}\ell_{t}
,

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}\ell_{k}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\textstyle\sum_{t}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\top}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H},

(52)

where the second equality follows from the definitions of
\boldsymbol{\Delta},\mathbf{H}
in (
42
). Hence
\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}\ell_{k}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}/|a_{k}|^{2}
.

Lower bound.

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}\ell_{k}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\;\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}\;\frac{\zeta_{-}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geq}}\;\frac{\zeta_{-}\,|a_{k}|\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\cdot|a_{k}|\,C_{-}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\zeta_{-}\,C_{-}^{2}\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k}),

(53)

where (a) is (
52
); (b) applies Assumption
B.3
, which gives
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}\geq\zeta_{-}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}
; and (c) substitutes
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}=|a_{k}|\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})
from (
43
) together with
\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{t}\|\mathbf{h}_{t}\|^{2}\geq|a_{k}|\,C_{-}^{2}
from Assumption
B.1
.

Upper bound.

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}}\ell_{k}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\;\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leq}}\;\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{\leq}}\;\frac{|a_{k}|\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\cdot|a_{k}|\,C_{+}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\;=\;C_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k}),

(54)

where (d) is (
52
); (e) is the matrix Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}
; and (f) substitutes
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}=|a_{k}|\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})
from (
43
) together with
\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{t}\|\mathbf{h}_{t}\|^{2}\leq|a_{k}|\,C_{+}^{2}
from Assumption
B.1
.
∎

Lemma 7

(Backbone gradient bound)
.

Under Assumptions
B.1
and
B.2
, for every candidate trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{k}
,

\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}}}\ell_{k}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\Lambda\,\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{|a_{k}|}\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k}).

(55)

Proof.

By the chain rule together with the second identity of Lemma
5
,
\partial\ell_{t}/\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}
, hence

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}}}\ell_{k}\;=\;\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})^{\top}\,\mathrm{vec}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\right).

(56)

Then

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{rest}}}\ell_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\;\lambda_{\max}\bigl(\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\,\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})^{\top}\bigr)\left\|\mathrm{vec}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{|a_{k}|}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\;\frac{\Lambda}{|a_{k}|^{2}}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\;\frac{\Lambda\,\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{|a_{k}|^{2}}\,\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\;\frac{\Lambda\,\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{|a_{k}|}\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k}),

(57)

where (a) uses
\|\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}
with
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{J}_{H}(\mathbf{y}_{k})
; (b) applies Assumption
B.2
together with the identity
\|\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{M})\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}
; (c) uses the submultiplicativity
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{F}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}
; and (d) substitutes
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}=|a_{k}|\,\mathrm{Brier}(\mathbf{y}_{k})
from (
43
).
∎

B.3.2
Taylor decomposition of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})

The next lemma packages the algebraic preliminaries for the linearization argument: the closed form of the exact directional derivative
g_{k}^{*}
, the definition of the forward-pass surrogate
\hat{g}_{k}
, and the resulting decomposition of the change in
\rho
into a linear term plus two residuals.

Lemma 8

(Taylor decomposition)
.

Let
N(\mathbf{q})\triangleq\|\sum_{k}q_{k}\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}=\mathbf{q}^{\top}G\mathbf{q}
and
D(\mathbf{q})\triangleq\sum_{k}q_{k}\ell_{k}=\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{q}
, so that
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})=N(\mathbf{q})/D(\mathbf{q})
. Then:

(i)

(
Closed form of
g_{k}^{*}
.
) The exact directional derivative at
\mathbf{p}
satisfies

g_{k}^{*}\;=\;\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{k}}\frac{N(\mathbf{q})}{D(\mathbf{q})}\right|_{\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}}\;=\;\frac{2\,\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}}{\bar{\ell}}\;-\;\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{p})\,\ell_{k}}{\bar{\ell}}.

(58)

(ii)

(
Forward-pass surrogate.
) Applying the diagonal approximations

B.3.3
Forward-computable bound on the gradient Gram matrix

The next lemma upgrades the per-trajectory bound of Lemma
1
to a uniform, forward-computable bound on every entry of the gradient Gram matrix
G
. It is the main tool that lets us replace gradient-dependent quantities (such as
\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}
,
\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|^{2}
, and
\|G\|_{\mathrm{op}}
) by forward-only quantities throughout the proofs of Lemma
2
and Theorem

.

Lemma 9

(Gram-matrix entry bound)
.

Assume Assumptions
B.1
–
B.3
, and let

E_{+}\;\triangleq\;C_{+}^{2}\max_{i\in[K]}\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{head}}(|a_{i}|)\bigr),\qquad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{head}}(|a_{i}|)\;=\;\frac{\Lambda\,\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{C_{+}^{2}\,|a_{i}|},

(63)

so that Lemma
1
yields the uniform inequality
\rho_{i}^{*}\leq E_{+}\,\hat{\rho}_{i}
for every
i\in[K]
. Then for every
i,j\in[K]
,

|G_{ij}|\;\leq\;\sqrt{G_{ii}\,G_{jj}}\;=\;\sqrt{\rho_{i}^{*}\,\rho_{j}^{*}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}}\;\leq\;E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{i}\,\hat{\rho}_{j}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}}.

(64)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle|G_{ij}|

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\;\sqrt{G_{ii}\,G_{jj}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\;\sqrt{\rho_{i}^{*}\,\rho_{j}^{*}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}}

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\;E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{i}\,\hat{\rho}_{j}\,\ell_{i}\,\ell_{j}},

(65)

where (a) is the entry-wise Cauchy–Schwarz bound for inner products,
|\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{g}_{j}\rangle|\leq\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|\|\mathbf{g}_{j}\|
; (b) uses
G_{ii}=\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}=\rho_{i}^{*}\,\ell_{i}
from the definition
\rho_{i}^{*}=\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}/\ell_{i}
; and (c) applies the upper part of Lemma
1
entry-wise to each of
\rho_{i}^{*}
and
\rho_{j}^{*}

B.3.4
Hessian of
\rho
on the simplex

Lemma 10

(Hessian formula)
.

On the relative interior of
\Delta^{K}
, the Hessian of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})=N(\mathbf{q})/D(\mathbf{q})
in
\mathbf{q}
is

\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{q}}\,\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\,\mathbf{q})\;=\;\frac{1}{D(\mathbf{q})}\Bigl[\,2\,G\;-\;\boldsymbol{\ell}\,(\nabla\rho)^{\top}\;-\;(\nabla\rho)\,\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\top}\,\Bigr],

(66)

where
\nabla\rho=(2\,G\mathbf{q}-\rho\,\boldsymbol{\ell})/D(\mathbf{q})
.

Proof.

The quotient rule applied to
\rho=N/D
gives
\nabla\rho=(\nabla N-\rho\,\nabla D)/D=(2\,G\mathbf{q}-\rho\,\boldsymbol{\ell})/D
, or equivalently
D\,\nabla\rho=2\,G\mathbf{q}-\rho\,\boldsymbol{\ell}
. Differentiating both sides with respect to
\mathbf{q}

\nabla D=\boldsymbol{\ell}
and the product rule
\nabla(D\nabla\rho)=\boldsymbol{\ell}\,(\nabla\rho)^{\top}+D\,\nabla^{2}\rho

\boldsymbol{\ell}\,(\nabla\rho)^{\top}+D\,\nabla^{2}\rho\;=\;2\,G\;-\;(\nabla\rho)\,\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\top}.

(67)

Solving for

and noting that the left-hand side is symmetric (which forces the symmetrized form of the rank-2 correction) yields (
66
).

B.5
Proof of Lemma
2
(
\chi^{2}

This subsection states and proves the formal version of Lemma
2
. We use the Taylor decomposition in Lemma
8
. Let

\boldsymbol{\eta}\triangleq\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*}
. The two residual terms in (
60

R_{1}\;=\;-\langle\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p},\,\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle,\qquad R_{2}\;=\;\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})-\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{p})-\langle\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{g}^{*}\rangle.

(76)

Since
p_{k}=1/K
, we also have

\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\,\|\,\mathbf{p})=\sum_{k}\frac{(q_{k}-p_{k})^{2}}{p_{k}}=K\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\|^{2}.

(77)

Let

\displaystyle E_{+}

\displaystyle\triangleq C_{+}^{2}\max_{i\in[K]}\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{head}}(|a_{i}|)\bigr),

(78)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rho}

\displaystyle\triangleq\max\!\Bigl(\bigl|1-\zeta_{-}C_{-}^{2}\bigr|,\;\max_{i\in[K]}\bigl|C_{+}^{2}(1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{head}}(|a_{i}|))-1\bigr|\Bigr).

(79)

By Lemma
11
, for all
i\in[K]
,

\rho_{i}^{*}\leq E_{+}\hat{\rho}_{i}
and

|\hat{\rho}_{i}-\rho_{i}^{*}|\leq\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{i}
.
We also define

\hat{\rho}_{\max}\triangleq\max_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}
,

\ell_{\min}\triangleq\min_{i}\ell_{i}
,

\ell_{\max}\triangleq\max_{i}\ell_{i}
, and

\bar{\ell}\triangleq K^{-1}\sum_{i}\ell_{i}
.

Lemma 12

(Formal version of Lemma
2
)
.

Under Assumptions
B.1
–
B.4
, for every
\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{K}
,

|R_{1}|\;\leq\;\alpha_{1}\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\,\|\,\mathbf{p})+1,\qquad|R_{2}|\;\leq\;\alpha_{2}\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\,\|\,\mathbf{p}),

(80)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{3\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max}}{\sqrt{K}}+\frac{2E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{\max}\ell_{\max}\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}}{\sqrt{K}\,\bar{\ell}}+\frac{E_{+}\ell_{\max}\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}{K\bar{\ell}^{2}}\right)^{2},

(81)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{+}\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}{K\ell_{\min}}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{K}\,\ell_{\max}}{\ell_{\min}}\right)^{2}.

(82)

Proof.

We first control
R_{1}
. By Cauchy–Schwarz and (
77
),

\displaystyle|R_{1}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k}(q_{k}-p_{k})\eta_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{K\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\eta_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\,\|\,\mathbf{p})}\,\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{p}},

(83)

where
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\triangleq K^{-1}\sum_{k}v_{k}^{2}
under the uniform prior. It remains to bound
\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{p}}
.

Introduce the intermediate vector

\tilde{g}_{k}\triangleq\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\left(2\rho_{k}^{*}-\frac{\sum_{i}\rho_{i}^{*}\ell_{i}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\right).

(84)

This vector keeps the exact per-trajectory rates
\rho_{i}^{*}
but uses the same diagonal approximation that leads to
\hat{g}_{k}
. Therefore,

\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\tilde{\mathbf{g}})+(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*})\triangleq\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{prox}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{diag}}.

(85)

For the proxy-substitution term, subtracting (
84
) from (
59
) gives

\eta_{\mathrm{prox},k}=\frac{\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\left(2(\hat{\rho}_{k}-\rho_{k}^{*})-\frac{\sum_{i}(\hat{\rho}_{i}-\rho_{i}^{*})\ell_{i}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}\right).

(86)

Using
|\hat{\rho}_{i}-\rho_{i}^{*}|\leq\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max}
for all
i
, we obtain

|\eta_{\mathrm{prox},k}|\leq\frac{3\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max}\ell_{k}}{\sum_{i}\ell_{i}}.

(87)

Hence

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{prox}}\|_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}|\eta_{\mathrm{prox},k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(3\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max})^{2}}{K}\frac{\sum_{k}\ell_{k}^{2}}{(\sum_{i}\ell_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(3\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max})^{2}}{K},

(88)

where the last inequality uses
\sum_{k}\ell_{k}^{2}\leq(\sum_{i}\ell_{i})^{2}
. Thus

\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{prox}}\|_{\mathbf{p}}\leq\frac{3\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max}}{\sqrt{K}}.

(89)

We next bound the diagonal-approximation error. Define

o_{k}\triangleq\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}-\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}}{K}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\neq k}G_{ki},\qquad O\triangleq\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{i}\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}G_{ij}.

(90)

Combining the closed form of
g_{k}^{*}
in Lemma
8
(i) with (
84

(91)

By Lemma
9

|G_{ij}|\leq E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{j}\ell_{i}\ell_{j}}

\displaystyle K|o_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\neq k}|G_{ki}|

\displaystyle\leq E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{k}\ell_{k}}\sum_{i\neq k}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{k}\ell_{k}}\sqrt{K\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}.

(92)

Taking the maximum over
k
yields

\max_{k}|o_{k}|\leq\frac{E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{\max}\ell_{\max}\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}}{\sqrt{K}}.

(93)

Similarly,

\displaystyle K^{2}|O|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\neq j}|G_{ij}|

\displaystyle\leq E_{+}\left(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq E_{+}K\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i},

and therefore

95

Remark 8

(Operational meaning of
\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}
)
.

The quantity
\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}
defined in (
98
) is a
\mathbf{p}
-weighted RMS bound on the surrogate error:

\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}|^{2}}=\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{p}}\leq\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}.

(108)

The constant

) pairs
\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\,\|\,\mathbf{p})}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{p}}
.

(116)

Substituting (

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{(i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}+\frac{\hat{g}_{(i)}-\hat{g}_{(B+1)}-\tau}{\tau K}

B.8
Theoretical and Empirical Validation of the Surrogate
\hat{\mathbf{g}}

The forward-pass surrogate
\hat{\mathbf{g}}
is the central computational shortcut underlying the LARK algorithm: it replaces the exact directional derivative

\mathbf{g}^{*}=\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}};\mathbf{q})|_{\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}}
,
whose evaluation requires one backward pass per candidate trajectory, with a quantity computable from forward passes alone. This subsection studies the relationship between
\hat{\mathbf{g}}
and
\mathbf{g}^{*}
from two complementary perspectives: a theoretical RMS error bound and an empirical comparison on real student models.

B.8.1
Theoretical Error Bound

The bounds derived in Appendix
B.5
immediately yield a quantitative bound on
\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*}\|
in the
\mathbf{p}
-weighted norm

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\triangleq K^{-1}\sum_{k}v_{k}^{2}
.

Corollary 3

(
\mathbf{p}
-weighted RMS error of
\hat{\mathbf{g}}
)
.

Under Assumptions
B.1
–
B.4
,

\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{p}}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl|\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}\bigr|^{2}}\;\leq\;\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}},

(124)

where
\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}
is the RMS upper bound from (
98
), given by

\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{3\,\Delta_{\rho}\,\hat{\rho}_{\max}}{\sqrt{K}}}_{\text{proxy term}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{2\,E_{+}\,\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{\max}\,\ell_{\max}\,\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\,\ell_{i}}}{\sqrt{K}\,\bar{\ell}}}_{\text{off-diagonal Gram term}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{E_{+}\,\ell_{\max}\,\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\,\ell_{i}}{K\,\bar{\ell}^{\,2}}}_{\text{aggregate off-diagonal term}}.

(125)

The constant
\alpha_{1}
in Lemma
12
is recovered as

\alpha_{1}=(\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}})^{2}/4
.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{p}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{prox}}\|_{\mathbf{p}}+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{diag}}\|_{\mathbf{p}}
,
where
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{prox}}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{diag}}
are defined in the proof of Lemma
12
. Combining the bounds in (
89
) and (
96
) gives (
124
), with
\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}
given by (
125
).
∎

The three terms in (
125
) correspond to the three sources of surrogate error. The first term,

3\Delta_{\rho}\hat{\rho}_{\max}/\sqrt{K}
, is the proxy-substitution error: it comes from replacing the exact per-trajectory rates
\rho_{i}^{*}
in the closed form of
g_{k}^{*}
by the forward-pass proxies
\hat{\rho}_{i}
. Its size is controlled by
\Delta_{\rho}
, which measures the multiplicative gap between
\rho_{i}^{*}
and
\hat{\rho}_{i}
in Lemma
11
. The second term,

2E_{+}\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_{\max}\ell_{\max}\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}}/(\sqrt{K}\,\bar{\ell})
, controls the per-coordinate off-diagonal Gram residual

o_{k}=\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}-\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}/K
, which is ignored by the diagonal approximation

\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}\approx\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|^{2}/K
. The third term,

E_{+}\ell_{\max}\sum_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\ell_{i}/(K\bar{\ell}^{2})
, controls the aggregate off-diagonal mass

O=\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|^{2}-K^{-2}\sum_{i}\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}
left over by the approximation

\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|^{2}\approx K^{-2}\sum_{i}\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|^{2}
. The latter two terms are small when the per-trajectory gradients are close to orthogonal in parameter space.

The bound in (
124
) is an aggregate RMS bound across the
K
candidates, not a per-coordinate guarantee. By the equivalence of finite-dimensional norms,

\max_{k\in[K]}|\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}|^{2}}\;=\;\sqrt{K}\,\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{p}}\;\leq\;\sqrt{K}\,\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{rms}}.

(126)

Thus a single coordinate can have a larger error in the worst case, while the RMS error remains controlled. This RMS norm is the relevant quantity for the residual analysis because the Cauchy–Schwarz step in (
83
) naturally pairs
\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{p})}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{p}}
.

with context

\mathbf{c}_{t}^{k}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{<t}^{k})
, define the
surprisal and rank as

s_{t}^{k}\;\triangleq\;-\log\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}(y_{t}^{k}),\qquad r_{t}^{k}\;\triangleq\;1+\!\!\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\!\!\mathbb{I}\!\left[\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(v\mid\mathbf{c}_{t}^{k})>\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}(y_{t}^{k})\right].

Ranks are clipped at
r_{\max}
(we use
r_{\max}=100
):

\bar{r}_{t}^{k}\triangleq\min(r_{t}^{k},r_{\max})
. The trajectory-level
score is

\mathrm{score}_{\text{RSR}}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\;\triangleq\;\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{|a_{k}|}\bar{r}_{t}^{k}}{\sum_{t=1}^{|a_{k}|}s_{t}^{k}},\qquad\mathbf{y}^{\star}=\arg\min_{k\in[K]}\mathrm{score}_{\text{RSR}}(\mathbf{y}_{k}).

Surprisal and clipped rank are computed from a single forward pass;
clipping at
r_{\max}
avoids the
O(|\mathcal{V}|)
full sort while
remaining exact (Appendix
C.3
).

[

(

The Avg column in Table

Decoding configuration.

For every benchmark and every fine-tuned student, we generate
5

independent samples per problem with the following decoding
parameters:

•

temperature
=0.6

•

top-
p=0.95

•

top-

(no top-
k

•

maximum generation length
=

32{,}768
tokens (matching the
SFT context length)

The same decoding configuration is used across all baselines and
LARK to ensure that any difference in downstream accuracy is
attributable to the selection rule rather than to inference-time

Metric (ACC@5).

We report
ACC@5
as our primary evaluation metric. For each
test problem, we sample
5
independent generations under the
decoding configuration above; the problem is marked
correct

if any of the
5
generations contains the correct final answer
under the extraction rule described below. ACC@5 captures whether
the student model is capable of producing a correct reasoning
trajectory for the problem within a small sampling budget, and is
less sensitive to single-sample variance than greedy ACC@1. Each
experiment is repeated under three independent decoding seeds, with
the selected training data and the SFT seed held fixed; we report
the mean and standard deviation of ACC@5 across these three
decoding seeds, so the reported variability captures inference-time
sampling noise rather than training-seed noise.

System prompts.

We use a fixed system prompt per benchmark family, distinguishing
numeric-answer tasks (AIME, AMC), multiple-choice tasks (GPQA), and
free-form-answer tasks (MATH-500). All three prompts share the same
overall structure — enforcing English output, separating the
reasoning chain from the final answer, and requiring the answer in a

\boxed{...}
environment for unambiguous extraction — and
differ only in the answer-format specification.

System Prompt: AIME / AMC (Numeric Answer)

You are an AI mathematician. All content you output MUST be in English. Use the question to deduce the correct numeric answer.

Finish all your reasoning, then on a NEW line output only
boxed{<number>}
.

The content inside
boxed{<number>}
must be the final numeric answer only, with no expressions, variables, or additional text.

System Prompt: GPQA (Multiple Choice)

You are an AI mathematician. All content you output MUST be in English. Use the question to deduce the correct choice.

Finish all your reasoning, then on a NEW line output only
boxed{<letter>}
.

The content inside
boxed{<letter>}
must be exactly one of A, B, C, D.

System Prompt: MATH (Free-form Answer)

You are an AI mathematician. All content you output MUST be in English. Use the question to deduce the correct answer.

Finish all your reasoning, then on a NEW line output only
boxed{<answer>}

Answer extraction rule.

We extract the predicted final answer from each generation using the
following deterministic rule:

•

If the generation contains at least one
\boxed{...}

token, we take the content inside the
last

\boxed{...}
as the predicted answer. The last (rather
than first) occurrence is used because models occasionally
produce intermediate boxed expressions during reasoning;
only the final boxed value is treated as the answer.

•

If the generation contains no
\boxed{...}
token, the
generation is treated as
incorrect
, regardless of
whether the surrounding text contains a correct value.

For numeric-answer tasks (AIME, AMC), the extracted string is
canonicalized by stripping surrounding whitespace and converting
fractions
a/b
to their decimal form before comparison with the
ground truth. For multiple-choice tasks (GPQA), the extracted string
is uppercased and compared against the gold letter in

\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B},\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}\}
. For free-form
answers (MATH-500), we follow the canonicalization rules of

8
, which normalize fraction, square-root,
and exponent notation prior to string matching.

Efficient scoring for rank-based metrics.

The RSR baseline of Appendix
C.2
requires the
clipped rank
\bar{r}_{t}^{k}=\min(r_{t}^{k},r_{\max})
at every token
position. A naive implementation would sort the full

|\mathcal{V}|
-dimensional logit vector at each token, costing

O(|\mathcal{V}|\log|\mathcal{V}|)
per token. We avoid this by
observing that under rank clipping at
r_{\max}
, only the relative
order of the top-
r_{\max}
logits matters: any token whose true
rank exceeds
r_{\max}
is mapped to
r_{\max}
regardless of its
exact rank. We therefore extract the top-
r_{\max}
logits via

torch.topk
(cost
O(|\mathcal{V}|\log r_{\max})
per token),
check whether the gold token
y_{t}^{k}
is among them, and assign

\bar{r}_{t}^{k}
accordingly. This procedure is exact under rank clipping
and reduces the per-token cost by a factor of

\log|\mathcal{V}|/\log r_{\max}
, which is roughly

5\times
–
6\times
for
|\mathcal{V}|\approx 1.5\times 10^{5}
and

r_{\max}=100
. The same forward pass that produces the surprisal

s_{t}^{k}=-\log\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}(y_{t}^{k})
is reused to produce the
top-
r_{\max}
logits, so RSR scoring requires only one forward pass
per candidate trajectory.

Prompt consistency.

We use a consistent chat template across (i) selection-time scoring
(when the student model evaluates each candidate trajectory under
teacher forcing), (ii) SFT loss formatting (when the model is
fine-tuned on the selected trajectories), and (iii) evaluation-time
prompting (when the fine-tuned model is queried on benchmark
problems). Using the same chat template at all three stages reduces
train–test mismatch and ensures that selection scores measured on

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}
remain meaningful for the
fine-tuned model. All inputs are truncated to the same maximum
sequence length of
32{,}768
tokens used for SFT.

D.1
Computational Cost

We separate every per-trajectory score into two parts: a single
forward pass over the trajectory through the student model, with cost

C_{\mathrm{fwd}}(|a_{k}|)
, and a per-token post-processing step on
the resulting logits. Decomposing the total cost in this way exposes
the differences across methods, which would otherwise be hidden by
the dominant forward-pass term.

Per-trajectory cost decomposition.

A single forward pass through a transformer with
L
layers, hidden
dimension
d
, and vocabulary size
|\mathcal{V}|
on a trajectory of
length
|a_{k}|
has cost

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{fwd}}(|a_{k}|)=\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(L|a_{k}|d^{2})}_{\text{MLP}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(L|a_{k}|^{2}d)}_{\text{attention}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{O}(|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|\cdot d)}_{\text{LM head}}.

After this forward pass, every per-trajectory selection method applies
a post-processing operation to the logits at each token. GRAPE computes
the cross-entropy of the gold token from a
|\mathcal{V}|
-dimensional
log-sum-exp, with per-token cost
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)
. LARK
computes both the cross-entropy and the Brier component

\sum_{v}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{k}(v)^{2}
, again with per-token cost

\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)
. RSR additionally selects the
top-
r_{\max}
entries of each token distribution to form a partial
rank statistic, with per-token cost

\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|\log r_{\max})
. Local Naturalness instead
runs
m+1
independent forward passes per trajectory (one for each
masked rollout), each followed by an
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)

per-token operation.

Total complexity.

Let
N
denote the number of training problems and
K
the number of
candidate trajectories per problem. Aggregating the forward and the
post-processing terms over all
NK
candidates yields the total cost
of each method:

\displaystyle\text{GRAPE}\!:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\big(NK\cdot[C_{\mathrm{fwd}}(|a_{k}|)+|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|]\big),

\displaystyle\text{LARK}\!:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\big(NK\cdot[C_{\mathrm{fwd}}(|a_{k}|)+|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|]\big),

\displaystyle\text{RSR}\!:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\big(NK\cdot[C_{\mathrm{fwd}}(|a_{k}|)+|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|\log r_{\max}]\big),

\displaystyle\text{Local Naturalness}\!:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\!\big(NK\cdot[(m+1)\,C_{\mathrm{fwd}}(|a_{k}|)+|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|]\big).

LARK matches the lowest cost achieved by GRAPE. RSR adds a

\log r_{\max}
factor on the per-token term because of its rank
selection step, and Local Naturalness multiplies the dominant forward
pass by
m+1
because of its repeated rollouts. The strict ordering

\text{GRAPE}=\text{LARK}<\text{RSR}
and

\text{GRAPE}=\text{LARK}<\text{Local Naturalness}
follows from

|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|<|a_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}|\log r_{\max}

and from
C_{\mathrm{fwd}}<(m+1)\,C_{\mathrm{fwd}}
for
m\geq 1
.
We do not assign a model-independent asymptotic formula to
LLM-judged Quality because its cost depends on the judge model and
serving setup; we therefore report it in the wall-clock comparison.

Empirical wall-clock cost.

We benchmark each scoring method by measuring its per-sample wall-clock
cost on
8
A100 GPUs.

s/sample) matches GRAPE
(

s/sample) to within

The walkthrough illustrates three points anchored at a concrete problem: trajectory-level (rather than teacher-level) variation in

-

truncation when the top-ranked trajectory is sharply separated from the threshold.

Appendix E
Limitations

LARK provides a practical and theoretically motivated approach for
trajectory-level selection in reasoning distillation. Its forward-pass
score makes the method efficient to apply to large candidate pools, and
the closed-form weighting rule avoids additional tuning while preserving
the benefits of multi-trajectory supervision. The empirical results
across multiple student models and benchmarks suggest that learnability
is a useful principle for selecting reasoning trajectories.

There are two natural limitations of the present study. First, our
experiments use a correctness-verified candidate pool, where all retained
trajectories reach the correct final answer. This setting isolates the
trajectory-selection problem from the separate problem of correctness
verification, but future work could study LARK in noisier pools that
include incorrect or partially correct teacher trajectories. Second, due
to the computational cost of full-parameter SFT, our reported standard
deviations are computed over independent decoding seeds while keeping
the training configuration fixed. Running multiple independent SFT
seeds would provide an even more complete estimate of training-time
variance.
