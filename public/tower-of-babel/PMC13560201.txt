Molecular mechanisms of FSP1-regulated ferroptosis and therapeutic implications in various cancers
Authors: Congyue Zhang, Chao Sun, Yuemin Nan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractFerroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation. In addition to the canonical glutathione peroxidase 4 pathway, ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1), also known as apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondria-associated 2, functions as a major glutathione-independent suppressor of ferroptosis. Current evidence indicates that FSP1 restrains lipid peroxidation mainly through the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (phosphate) [NAD(P)H]-dependent coenzyme Q10 axis. FSP1 has also been implicated in the noncanonical vitamin K cycle, which further expands its redox role. Emerging studies suggest that liquid–liquid phase separation and membrane repair may broaden the functional landscape of FSP1, although these mechanisms remain less fully defined. We then summarize the multilayer regulation of FSP1 and discuss how it contributes to ferroptosis resistance, tumor adaptation, and therapeutic responses in cancer. Finally, we review current preclinical efforts to target FSP1, with emphasis on small-molecule inhibitors, mechanism-based combinations, candidate biomarkers, and major translational challenges. Together, these observations position FSP1 as an important determinant of ferroptosis resistance in cancer and a promising focus for further mechanistic and translational study.Graphical Abstract Keywords: FSP1, Ferroptosis, Cancer therapy, Precision oncology, Tumor microenvironment, Biomarkers

Introduction

Ferroptosis is a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by the iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides [1, 2]. Unlike apoptosis or necrosis, ferroptosis is executed through oxidative damage to membrane phospholipids and failure of the systems that limit lipid peroxidation [3]. As therapy-resistant tumor cells often depend on antioxidant defenses for survival, ferroptosis has attracted considerable attention in cancer biology and drug development [4, 5]. Induction of ferroptosis is therefore being explored as a strategy to eliminate cancers that are poorly controlled by conventional treatment [6, 7].

Among the known ferroptosis defense systems, the glutathione (GSH)-dependent glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway has long been regarded as central [8, 9]. However, variable responses to GPX4 inhibition suggested that additional protective mechanisms may exist. A major advance came with the identification of ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) as a GSH-independent ferroptosis suppressor that acts in parallel with GPX4 [10]. FSP1 is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (phosphate) [NAD(P)H]-dependent ubiquinone oxidoreductase that reduces ubiquinone to ubiquinol and thereby limits lipid bilayer peroxidation [11, 12]. This discovery expanded the current understanding of ferroptosis defense beyond the GSH system.

Subsequent studies have broadened the biological scope of FSP1. In addition to the coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) axis, FSP1 has been linked to the noncanonical vitamin K cycle and to multiple regulatory layers, including transcriptional control, RNA-level regulation, and protein turnover [13, 14]. FSP1 expression correlates positively with ferroptosis resistance in several cancer cell lines and xenograft models [15]. In parallel, recent studies suggest that some Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS)-mutant tumors require FSP1 upregulation to bypass a ferroptotic barrier during tumor initiation [16]. Together, these findings have increased interest in therapeutic strategies that target FSP1 alone or in combination with other ferroptosis-related pathways.

Several recent reviews have summarized the role of FSP1 in ferroptosis regulation [17, 18]. In the present review, our intent is not to provide another descriptive overview of FSP1. Instead, we recapitulate current evidence through a membrane-centered framework and distinguish more firmly supported mechanisms from emerging concepts. Within this framework, the CoQ10 axis is presented as the central mechanism of FSP1-mediated ferroptosis suppression and the one supported by the strongest experimental evidence, whereas the vitamin K cycle, liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), and endosomal sorting complex required for transport III (ESCRT-III)-associated membrane repair are discussed as linked but less fully defined extensions of the membrane defense model [19]. We further discuss how these mechanisms may be connected within the membrane environment. One possibility is that LLPS locally concentrates FSP1 together with its cofactors, thereby increasing reducing capacity. We also examine multilayer regulation of FSP1 in tumor adaptation and therapy resistance, followed by elaboration on the main opportunities and limitations that shape its translational relevance.

Biochemistry and mechanisms of FSP1

Membrane localization and the nonmitochondrial coenzyme Q system

FSP1 is localized mainly to cellular membranes, and this localization is essential for its anti-ferroptotic activity [10]. Membrane targeting is mediated by N-terminal myristoylation, which facilitates recruitment of FSP1 to the plasma membrane as well as to other membrane compartments, including the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus [20]. Mutation of the myristoylation site or inhibition of N-myristoyltransferase (NMT) activity disrupts membrane localization and markedly weakens the ability of FSP1 to suppress ferroptosis [21, 22]. As a component of the nonmitochondrial CoQ defense system, FSP1 acts directly at the lipid bilayer, limiting the spread of lipid peroxidation [23]. The requirement of N-terminal myristoylation for membrane localization and ferroptosis suppression is firmly established by mutagenesis and chemical inhibition studies. However, the relative contribution of FSP1 at different membrane compartments—plasma membrane, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi—to ferroptosis resistance remains to be explicitly defined (Fig. 1).

The CoQ10–NAD(P)H axis in parallel with GPX4

FSP1 suppresses ferroptosis mainly through the CoQ10–NAD(P)H antioxidant axis. In this pathway, FSP1 catalyzes the reduction of ubiquinone to ubiquinol, and the reduced form of CoQ10 acts as a lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidant within the lipid bilayer [10]. Through this reaction, FSP1 limits the propagation of lipid peroxidation and protects cells from ferroptotic death. This pathway operates independently of the GPX4 system and therefore provides a parallel route of ferroptosis defense [10]. Loss of FSP1 function sensitizes cells to GPX4 inhibition and other ferroptosis inducers, whereas increased FSP1 expression is associated with ferroptosis resistance in multiple cancer models [24, 25]. Taken together, these findings define the CoQ10–FSP1 axis as the principal biochemical basis of FSP1-mediated ferroptosis suppression. This pathway is the most extensively validated mechanism of FSP1, supported by biochemical reconstitution, genetic deletion, pharmacological inhibition, and lipidomic analyses across multiple cell lines and in vivo models (Fig. 1).

Participation of FSP1 in the noncanonical vitamin K cycle

FSP1 has also been implicated in the noncanonical vitamin K cycle. In this pathway, FSP1 reduces vitamin K to vitamin K hydroquinone (VKH2), which can function as a radical-trapping antioxidant and thereby contributes to the suppression of lipid peroxidation [13, 26]. This finding expands the biochemical scope of FSP1 beyond coenzyme Q metabolism. Studies identifying FSP1 as a warfarin-resistant vitamin K reductase further support this notion in vitamin K redox biology [14]. By contrast, this activity should be distinguished from the membrane-centered CoQ10 pathway, which remains more extensively characterized in ferroptosis research. The observation that warfarin resistance does not require myristoylation also suggests that different biochemical outputs of FSP1 may rely on distinct molecular settings. It should be noted, however, that the vitamin K cycle has been characterized primarily in the context of warfarin resistance and noncancer models; its contribution to ferroptosis suppression in tumors remains less extensively studied than the CoQ10 axis (Fig. 1).

Spatial organization and liquid–liquid phase separation

Recent studies suggest that FSP1 may also be modulated by spatial organization, including liquid–liquid phase separation [22, 27]. In this model, FSP1 forms biomolecular condensates that enrich the enzyme together with reducing cofactors and lipid-soluble substrates, thereby increasing local reducing flux at membrane regions vulnerable to oxidative damage [20, 27]. This concept adds a spatial dimension to FSP1 biology and may help explain how its membrane-proximal antioxidant activity is locally organized. However, it remains unclear whether condensate formation is a general feature of FSP1 biology or a phenomenon observed under selected experimental conditions. For this reason, LLPS should be viewed as an emerging regulatory layer rather than as a core biochemical pathway of FSP1 [7, 17]. If validated, LLPS could serve as a spatial organizer that coordinates FSP1 with NAD(P)H and CoQ10 to enhance local antioxidant flux (Fig. 1).

ESCRT-III-mediated membrane repair pathway

In addition to its antioxidant role, FSP1 has been linked to membrane repair processes involving ESCRT-III [28]. Ferroptotic stress is accompanied by membrane injury, and recruitment of charged multivesicular body protein subunits to the plasma membrane has been proposed as part of a repair response that limits membrane rupture [27]. Studies showing that FSP1 loss impairs this process, whereas restoration of membrane repair components can partially rescue ferroptotic sensitivity, suggest that FSP1 may contribute to membrane protection in more than one way. Nevertheless, the relationship between FSP1 and ESCRT-III remains less clearly defined than the CoQ10 pathway. It is unclear whether this connection reflects a direct role in repair signaling or an indirect consequence of reduced membrane oxidation [29]. One interpretation is that ESCRT-III acts as a damage-response module downstream of the CoQ10 and vitamin K axes when antioxidant capacity is overwhelmed (Fig. 1).

Toward an integrated framework

Collectively, the CoQ10 axis and vitamin K cycle constitute the core antioxidant machinery; LLPS may enhance their efficiency through spatial concentration, and ESCRT-III repair may serve as a fail-safe when damage occurs. This integrated action—though partially hypothetical—provides a testable model for how FSP1 coordinates multiple outputs at the membrane.

Regulation of FSP1 across molecular layers

The regulation of FSP1 can be classified into three interconnected layers: (1) transcriptional control by stress-responsive transcription factors; (2) posttranscriptional regulation by noncoding RNAs; and (3) RNA modifications and protein-level mechanisms that determine mRNA stability and protein turnover. These layers collectively fine-tune FSP1 expression in response to oncogenic and therapeutic stress [10, 22].

Transcriptional control of FSP1

FSP1 expression is regulated by several transcriptional programs that are closely linked to oxidative stress adaptation and tumor progression [5, 30, 31]. Among these, the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)–nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) axis is one of the most consistently supported [32, 33]. NRF2 harnesses antioxidant gene expression, and studies in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and non-small-cell lung cancer indicate that FSP1 can be regulated, at least in part, through this pathway [34, 35]. This relationship is particularly relevant in tumors with altered KEAP1 signaling, where ferroptosis defense may be strengthened by a broader redox program [32, 35].

Other transcription-associated mechanisms have also been proposed. Oncogenic KRAS signaling has been linked to increased FSP1 expression during tumor initiation, suggesting that FSP1 may facilitate survival under oncogene-driven oxidative stress [16, 32]. The tumor suppressor p53 has also been reported to bind the FSP1 promoter, although the direction and significance of this regulation remain to be clarified [36]. Additional regulators, including specificity protein 1 (SP1), hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α)-related programs, and lineage-associated transcriptional networks, have also been discussed, but the evidence is less compelling [37]. Among these, KEAP1–NRF2 and KRAS–mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) are supported by genetic and pharmacological evidence across multiple models, whereas the roles of p53, SP1, and HIF-1α rest on fewer studies and require further validation.

RNA-level regulation of FSP1

Noncoding RNAs are also involved in the regulation of FSP1, although the robustness of mechanistic support varies across studies. Several reports suggest that FSP1 expression can be influenced through N6-methyladenosine (m6A) associated mechanisms involving methyltransferase 3 (METTL3) and related RNA-processing pathways [38, 39]. Other studies have linked long noncoding RNAs, including FEZ family zinc finger 1-antisense RNA 1 (FEZF1-AS1) and long intergenic noncoding RNA for kinase activation (LINK-A), to regulatory networks that may affect FSP1 expression indirectly through tumor-associated signaling programs [40]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that FSP1 is embedded in a broader posttranscriptional regulatory network, but direct noncoding RNA control remains less clearly defined than transcriptional regulation.

Many noncoding RNA-related observations remain associative or are embedded within larger signaling circuits. Future studies should therefore identify direct regulators of FSP1 from pathways that influence its expression indirectly through upstream transcriptional or epigenetic programs.

Posttranslational regulation and protein homeostasis

FSP1 abundance and activity are further shaped by RNA modification and protein-level control. At the transcript level, N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) stabilizes FSP1 messenger RNA through N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification [41]. m6A related regulation has also been reported, although its effects appear to vary across biological settings [42]. In parallel, alternative polyadenylation and RNA-binding protein-associated mechanisms may influence FSP1 transcript stability and translation efficiency.

At the protein level, tripartite motif-containing 21 (TRIM21)-mediated ubiquitination has been reported to regulate FSP1 stability and subcellular localization [43, 44]. Additional mechanisms, including promoter methylation and inflammatory signaling, have been described in selected noncancer disease models and may provide useful clues for understanding how FSP1 is controlled under stress conditions [45, 46]. Collectively, these findings suggest that FSP1 is regulated across multiple layers, from transcript processing to protein turnover. This multilayer regulation is likely to contribute to the marked heterogeneity of FSP1 expression and activity observed in different biological settings. For example, METTL3 promotes FSP1 mRNA degradation in vascular smooth muscle cells but stabilizes FSP1 mRNA in glioma, highlighting context-dependent effects that remain mechanistically unexplained [43].

Roles of FSP1 in cancer biology and the tumor microenvironment

FSP1 as a determinant of ferroptosis resistance and therapy adaptation

FSP1 influences cancer cell metabolism and therapeutic responses by increasing the threshold for ferroptotic death. By sustaining membrane redox buffering, FSP1 allows tumor cells to tolerate oxidative stress that would otherwise drive phospholipid peroxidation and lipid bilayer injury [4, 30, 47]. This role is especially relevant under persistent metabolic stress, therapeutic pressure, or partial inhibition of GPX4 activity. In such settings, FSP1 functions as an alternative ferroptosis defense system and supports tumor cell survival.

Elevated FSP1 expression correlates with ferroptosis resistance in multiple cancer cell lines, and studies in xenograft models further support its contribution to tumor persistence under oxidative challenge. KRAS-mutant cancers provide a particularly informative example, as these tumors appear to require FSP1 upregulation to bypass a ferroptotic barrier during tumor initiation [16, 32]. This observation may help explain why FSP1 is enriched in refractory tumors, including those with mesenchymal features and enhanced oxidative stress adaptation [5, 31]. FSP1 is also relevant to cancer cell plasticity, as therapy-resistant or dedifferentiated states often exhibit altered lipid metabolism and increased reliance on antioxidant defense [48].

Overall, these findings indicate that FSP1 contributes to cancer biology by enhancing tolerance to oxidative stress rather than by acting in a tumor-type-restricted manner. This perspective also helps explain why FSP1 has attracted sustained therapeutic interest: tumors that operate close to the ferroptotic threshold may become disproportionately dependent on FSP1-mediated protection, making this pathway especially relevant in refractory or stress-adapted diseased situations. Still, the magnitude of FSP1 dependence is unlikely to be uniform across tumor types. It probably reflects the combined influence of oncogenic background, membrane lipid composition, antioxidant redundancy, and the nature of the stress imposed by therapy [17, 49].

FSP1 and the tumor immune microenvironment

FSP1 as a potential modulator of immune signaling

Interest in the relationship between ferroptosis and antitumor immunity has expanded considerably, and FSP1 is increasingly being addressed within this context [50, 51]. By suppressing lipid peroxidation in tumor cells, FSP1 may alter the release of oxidized lipids, damage-associated molecular signals, and other mediators that influence the surrounding immune milieu [4, 52]. This possibility is biologically plausible because ferroptotic stress is not purely cell autonomous; it can also foster communication between malignant cells and stromal or immune populations within the tumor microenvironment (TME). The relevance of FSP1 to tumor immunity may therefore depend on its ability to shape the redox conditions under which tumor–immune interactions occur, rather than in a single defined immune pathway.

Several observations corroborate a potential connection between FSP1 and immune regulation. FSP1 is expressed in immune-related settings, including macrophage-associated contexts, and ferroptosis itself has been linked to the function of TAMs and cytotoxic T cells [50, 53]. In tumors, maintenance of the CoQ10 antioxidant system by FSP1 may reduce the production of lipid peroxidation products that would otherwise modify immune signaling. Correlations between FSP1 expression and immune infiltration have also been reported in specific cancer datasets, including ovarian and gastric cancer, suggesting that FSP1 may be associated with remodeling of the tumor immune environment [54, 55].

These correlative observations have prompted further investigation delving into whether FSP1 directly influences immunogenic cell death (ICD) and responses to immunotherapy (Fig. 2).

FSP1 and ICD

FSP1 has also been discussed in the context of ICD and combination immunotherapy. In principle, inhibition of FSP1 could promote ferroptotic injury, increase oxidative membrane damage, and alter the release of tumor-associated antigens or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [56]. Such changes may instigate and enhance innate/adaptive immune responses under several conditions. Preclinical studies have therefore proposed that FSP1 inhibition may enhance the response to immune checkpoint-based therapy, either by increasing tumor cell susceptibility to ferroptosis or by altering the TME to favor antitumor immunity [51, 52].

Despite these observations, the evidence across this area remains uneven. In many cases, immune effects are inferred from broader ferroptosis-related findings rather than from direct and exclusive analysis of FSP1’s functional role. For these reasons, FSP1 should presently be regarded as a plausible regulator of tumor–immune interactions rather than as a well-built determinant of ICD or checkpoint response [7, 17]. Further work is needed to determine when immune effects arise directly from FSP1 inhibition and the circumstance where they reflect more general consequences of ferroptotic stress. Direct comparisons between studies are complicated by differences in experimental systems and ferroptosis inducers applied (Fig. 2).

FSP1 in noncancer diseases

In addition to its role in cancer, FSP1 has been implicated in several nonmalignant pathological conditions. In neurological settings, FSP1 upregulation has been verified to suppress ferroptosis in models of Parkinson’s disease [57] and to support recovery after intracerebral hemorrhage [58]. Protective effects of FSP1 have also been described in acetaminophen-induced liver injury [59], osteoarthritis [60], and sepsis-associated acute kidney injury [61]. These findings reinforce the notion that FSP1 serves more broadly as a suppressor of lipid peroxidation-associated injury [62–64].

Although these findings lie outside the main scope of the present review, they remain relevant for two reasons. First, they indicate that the biology of FSP1 cannot be understood solely within a tumor framework. Second, they position cancer-focused observations within a broader physiological setting. Noncancer studies therefore provide useful context for interpreting the biological range of FSP1 and for understanding how its functions extend beyond tumor biology.

Therapeutic strategies targeting the FSP1 axis

Current status of FSP1-targeting compounds

Interest in pharmacological targeting of FSP1 has increased substantially, but the field remains in its infancy [65]. Early compounds such as inhibitors of FSP1 provided proof of concept that the pharmacological inhibition of FSP1 can sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis [21, 22]. Subsequent work identified additional classes of FSP1-directed compounds, including inducers of condensation of FSP1 and structurally diverse ferroptosis sensitizers such as ferroptosis sensitizer 1 (FSEN1) [66]. These agents have been useful for clarifying that FSP1 is druggable in principle and that distinct modes of inhibition may produce different biological outcomes [67].

Current compounds should still be interpreted mainly as experimental tools rather than clinically mature agents. Their selectivity, pharmacokinetic properties, and in vivo performance remain to be defined more comprehensively [18, 21, 22]. This distinction is important because several compounds appear to inhibit enzymatic activity directly, whereas others alter the localization or higher-order organization of FSP1 [27, 65]. Such mechanistic differences are likely to influence how medicinal effects are elucidated. The current pharmacological literature encourages further investigation into the druggability of FSP1, but a comprehensive inhibitor framework has yet to been established. A key limitation is that inhibitor of FSP1 of FSP1 (iFSP1) and FSEN1 fail to inhibit mouse FSP1, which restricts their application in homozygous mouse models; furthermore, the condensation-induced mechanism of inducer of condensation of FSP1 (icFSP1) raises additional concerns regarding selectivity as well as off-target effects.

Mechanism-based combination strategies

A major rationale for combination therapy lies in the organization of ferroptosis defense across distinct antioxidant systems. As FSP1 and GPX4 suppress lipid peroxidation via distinct biochemical routes, simultaneous disruption of these pathways can generate stronger ferroptotic stress than inhibition of either pathway alone [49, 68]. Preclinical studies in colorectal cancer, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and other model systems support this principle [69]. These observations suggest that synergistic targeting of GPX4 and FSP1 may be especially relevant in tumors embracing dual antioxidant buffering.

Combination strategies have also been proposed beyond direct GPX4 coinhibition. FSP1-directed approaches may enhance the activity of selected chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, or other ferroptosis-inducing treatments [70]. The link between FSP1 and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH)-directed strategies is also of interest because these pathways operate in different subcellular settings and may impose oxidative stress across a broader membrane network [49]. Adaptive upregulation of FSP1 in drug-tolerant states, including triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) models, further supports the rationale for mechanism-based therapeutic regimens [71, 72].

Even so, the translational implications of these combinations should be interpreted with caution. Most available evidence remains preclinical, and the effectiveness of combined approaches is likely to depend on tumor state, metabolic background, and treatment sequence [7, 18]. Accordingly, combination therapy involving FSP1 is a mechanistically attractive modality, but it has not yet advanced to a level where general clinical conclusions can be drawn. Clinical feasibility is further constrained by the poor pharmacokinetics of GPX4 inhibitors and the unknown tolerability of incorporating them with FSP1-directed agents (Fig. 3).

Potential resistance mechanisms to FSP1-targeted therapy

Potential resistance to FSP1-targeted therapy is unlikely to be explained by a single mechanism. As ferroptosis defense is inherently redundant, tumor cells may attenuate ferroptotic stress through several compensatory routes when FSP1 activity is inhibited. These include activation of antioxidant transcriptional programs such as NRF2 [32], reinforcement of mitochondrial antioxidant defenses [73], lipid metabolic reprogramming [31], and increased reliance on parallel systems that preserve membrane integrity. Together, these adaptive responses partially explain why FSP1 inhibition alone may be insufficient contextually.

At present, however, resistance circuits to FSP1 inhibition have not been illustrated comprehensively. Many proposed mechanisms are derived from selected experimental models, and their relative significance and involvement are likely to differ across tumor types, metabolic states, and treatment conditions [17, 44]. Future studies should therefore distinguish recurrent resistance routes from model-specific observations and clarify which compensatory pathways arise constitutively rather than post-therapy pressure.

An additional translational constraint is the physiological role of FSP1 outside tumors. As FSP1 contributes to protection from oxidative membrane damage in nonmalignant tissues, systemic inhibition may narrow the therapeutic window if sustained suppression is required for anti-tumor efficacy [74]. Accordingly, drug development in this area will require not only potent inhibitors but also careful attention to selectivity, exposure, dosing strategy, and tissue vulnerability.

Collectively, these considerations suggest that the challenge is not simply to identify FSP1 inhibitors but to define the tumor settings in which FSP1 dependence is biologically meaningful, therapeutically exploitable, and pharmacologically separable from normal tissue protection (Fig. 3).

Clinical translation and biomarkers

Candidate contexts for patient stratification

FSP1 is overexpressed in several malignancies and has been associated with poor prognosis in various clinical cohorts [75]. Studies in HCC indicate that FSP1 contributes to ferroptosis resistance and correlates with adverse clinical outcomes [55], while investigation in ovarian cancer suggests that high FSP1 expression may be linked to platinum resistance and disease recurrence [76]. In breast cancer, increased FSP1 expression has likewise been associated with reduced survival in specific subtypes. Pan-cancer analyses further implicate that FSP1 is heterogeneous at the levels of RNA expression, DNA methylation, and protein regulation, supporting the statement that its biological and clinical relevance varies across tumor types [43] (Table 1).

Current data indicate that FSP1-related features may be beneficial to identify tumors with greater reliance on ferroptosis defense. However, prognostic association should not be equated with clinical stratification. The value of FSP1 as a biomarker will likely depend on how it is immersed with other variables, including GPX4 status, redox pathway activation, lipid metabolic state, and TME features [17, 18]. Multiomics and single-cell approaches may further help define FSP1-related tumor states, particularly where ferroptosis regulation intersects with immune composition and therapy adaptation [77].

At present, however, no validated patient-selection framework based on FSP1 has been proposed. Accordingly, FSP1 should presently be regarded as a candidate biomarker with potential stratification value rather than as a clinically deployable stratification tool [17, 18]. Further work is warranted to determine which combinations of molecular, metabolic, and microenvironmental features best capture FSP1 dependence in tumors. From a practical standpoint, FSP1 immunohistochemistry (IHC) lacks standardized scoring systems and prospective validation, precluding current clinical use.

Candidate biomarkers and assay strategies

Several categories of readout may be informative in future studies of FSP1-targeted therapy. These include tissue-based assessment of FSP1 expression, functional indicators of lipid peroxidation such as malondialdehyde (MDA) or 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) [3], and metabolite-level changes involving CoQ10 or vitamin K-related redox pools [13, 14]. Integrated biomarker models that combine these measurements with genomic or transcriptomic features may ultimately provide more information than any single marker alone [17, 77].

Most of these approaches, however, are still in their exploratory phase. Their analytical validity, reproducibility, and clinical effectiveness have not been established in prospective settings. The central challenge is to determine which readouts truly reflect FSP1 dependence and therapeutic susceptibility in the context of tumors. Among candidate readouts, FSP1 IHC has the strongest correlative evidence, but prospective validation is indispensable [43, 55] (Fig. 4).

Pharmacodynamic monitoring and early translational studies

Pharmacodynamic evaluation of FSP1 inhibition will likely require more than one type of readout. Serial tissue sampling, lipid peroxidation markers, metabolomic profiling, and imaging-based approaches are all conceivable in principle [78]. In preclinical settings, these strategies may help determine whether an intervention engages the FSP1 axis and increases ferroptotic stress within tumors.

However, there is currently no established pharmacodynamic standard for FSP1-targeted therapy. The central challenge is not only how to detect anti-tumor activity but also how to verify that a given effect is on-target, ferroptosis-related, and sustained in vivo. Accordingly, practical monitoring approaches should be developed alongside therapeutic agents from the outset, rather than being introduced retrospectively.

At present, the main translational task is not immediate clinical deployment, but the establishment of monitoring frameworks that can verify target engagement and pathway dependence in preclinical and early translational studies (Fig. 4).

Models and methodological considerations

Investigation of FSP1-regulated ferroptosis has relied on complementary in vitro and in vivo model systems. Multiple cancer cell lines have been used to examine the relationship between FSP1 expression and ferroptosis resistance [10, 30], while isogenic KRAS-mutant models assist in defining the role of FSP1 to overcome a ferroptotic barrier during tumor initiation [16]. Lung cancer cell culture systems and mouse xenograft models have further supported the contribution of FSP1 to ferroptosis resistance in malignant settings [35]. Outside cancer, additional models, including oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) systems, have been used to explore the broader role of FSP1 in oxidative injury [58, 79].

The methodological toolkit for elaborating on FSP1 is correspondingly broad. Standard approaches such as western blotting and immunofluorescence remain important for assessing expression and subcellular localization. Ubiquitination assays, transcript-level analyses, and synthetic lethality screens have been useful for defining mechanisms pertinent to regulation and pathway interaction [43, 44]. In parallel, pharmacological tools including inhibitors of FSP1, inducers of condensation of FSP1, and FSEN1 have enabled functional interrogation of FSP1 in combination with ferroptosis inducers [21, 22, 65]. Ferroptosis sensitivity assays typically integrate measurements of lipid peroxide accumulation, such as C11-BODIPY-based readouts, with viability endpoints [2, 3, 73].

As ferroptosis phenotypes can be shaped by cell state, lipid composition, and compound-specific effects, mechanistic claims regarding FSP1 require careful validation. The use of multiple perturbation strategies, rescue experiments, and specificity controls is therefore particularly important in this field. Robust chemical–genetic integration will remain essential for distinguishing direct FSP1-dependent effects from broader oxidative stress responses.

Outstanding questions and future directions

Several mechanistic and translational questions remain open in this field. First, although the CoQ10–NAD(P)H pathway is now well established, the broader functional range of FSP1 is still under intensive investigation. The proposed roles of LLPS, membrane repair, and tumor–immune regulation are conceptually important, but their mechanistic depth and generality across models remain uneven [7, 17, 18]. Clarifying which of these observations represent central features of FSP1 biology and which are more restricted phenomena will be the next critical step.

Second, the development of therapeutically useful FSP1 inhibitors remains challenging. As FSP1 is a reductase with physiologically relevant functions, inhibitor selectivity, normal-tissue safety, and off-target liability are likely to be major issues [21, 22, 67]. This challenge is compounded by pathway heterogeneity across tumor types and by the possibility that different modes of FSP1 inhibition may not produce equivalent biological outcomes. Improved medicinal chemistry, explicit pharmacokinetic profiling, and more precise delivery strategies will therefore be needed before the field can move beyond proof-of-concept studies [18, 65]. The key unresolved issue is not simply whether FSP1 can be inhibited, but under which biological conditions selective and tolerable inhibition is mechanistically realistic.

Third, translational progress is limited by the lack of robust biomarkers and pharmacodynamic tools. Although FSP1 expression and related molecular features correlate with outcome in several cancers, standardized assays and validated patient-selection frameworks are still lacking [76, 77]. Real-time monitoring of FSP1 pathway activity, lipid peroxidation, and tumor–immune consequences also bring difficulties [78]. Thus, future work should prioritize the development of reliable pathway readouts, including imaging-based, metabolomic, and single-cell approaches that can capture ferroptosis-relevant biology more directly. A major unresolved challenge is to determine which readouts genuinely capture FSP1 pathway dependence rather than broader oxidative injury or downstream tissue damage.

Viewed together, these unresolved issues define a practical agenda for the next phase of the field. Rather than inquiring whether FSP1 is druggable, future studies are supposed to determine when FSP1 is dominant, how it is regulated in different tumor states, and how its inhibition can be measured and exploited with sufficient precision.

Conclusions

FSP1 is now recognized as a major GSH-independent suppressor of ferroptosis [10]. Current evidence places the CoQ10–NAD(P)H axis at the center of its biochemical function, while the noncanonical vitamin K cycle adds further depth to its redox biology [13]. In parallel, growing work on transcriptional control, RNA-level regulation, and protein homeostasis has shown that FSP1 expression and activity are shaped across multiple biological layers [17, 18]. These findings help explain why FSP1 is repeatedly linked to ferroptosis resistance, tumor adaptation, and oxidative stress tolerance [16, 30, 32].

Several parts of the field nevertheless remain in development. Proposed aspects involving liquid–liquid phase separation, membrane repair, and tumor–immune interactions are conceptually important, but the depth of mechanistic support is uneven [7, 15]. Similar caution applies to translational interpretation. Although FSP1 is a compelling target in preclinical models, clinically validated inhibitors, robust biomarkers, and standardized pharmacodynamic tools are still lacking [18, 21, 22].

In this context, current evidence supports FSP1 as an important determinant of ferroptosis resistance in cancer and as a useful framework for understanding how tumor cells survive under stressful phospholipid peroxidation. The integrated, membrane-centered framework proposed here—emphasizing cooperation among the CoQ10 axis, vitamin K cycle, and LLPS—represents a conceptual advance over descriptive portraits [17]. A clearer definition of when FSP1 is dominant, how it is regulated in different tumor states, and how it interacts with parallel ferroptosis defense would help translate this biology into a truly therapeutic benefit.
