Flexibility of Codimension One C 1 , θ C^{1,\theta} Isometric Immersions
Dominik Inauen

Abstract
. We study the problem of constructing C 1 , θ C^{1,\theta} isometric immersions of Riemannian metrics on n n -dimensional domains into ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} . While the classical Nash–Kuiper theorem established the flexibility of C 1 C^{1} isometries, subsequent work has extended this to C 1 , θ C^{1,\theta} isometries for certain θ \theta , though the optimal exponent remains unknown. In this work we show that any short immersion can be uniformly approximated by C 1 , θ C^{1,\theta} isometric immersions for θ < 1 / ( 1 + 2 ​ ( n − 1 ) ) \theta<1/(1+2(n-1)) , improving upon the previously known exponent for n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . The improvement is obtained via a convex integration scheme incorporating a refined iterative integration by parts procedure resting on a detailed structural analysis of error terms and the interaction of multiple frequency scales.

1.
Introduction

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. An isometric immersion
u:M\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a continuously differentiable map
u
such that the induced metric
u^{\sharp}e
equals
g
, where
e
denotes the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In local coordinates on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, this condition reads

(1.1)

Du^{T}Du=g\,,

where
Du
is the Jacobian of the map
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
g
is identified with the positive definite symmetric matrix field of coefficients representing the metric in these local coordinates.

Equation (
1.1
) is a system of
n_{*}:=n(n+1)/2
non-linear partial differential equations in
m
unknowns. For low codimension
m=n+1
and
n\geq 3
it is overdetermined, having
n_{*}>m
equations. Nevertheless, the groundbreaking Nash-Kuiper theorem
[
37
,
31
]
established a surprising abundance of solutions to (
1.1
): in any
C^{0}
-neighborhood of any
short
immersion
\underline{u}
(meaning
\underline{u}^{\sharp}e\leq g
in the sense of quadratic forms), there exists an isometric immersion
u
of regularity
C^{1}
.

This so-called flexibility contrasts classical rigidity results for smooth isometric immersions. A prominent example
is the rigidity theorem of the Weyl problem: if
g
is any metric of positive Gaussian curvature on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then any two isometric immersions
u,v:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
of class
C^{2}
differ only by a rigid motion, a result due to Cohn-Vossen
[
14
]
and Herglotz
[
25
]
(see also
[
40
]
for a more general result, implying the above).

This dichotomy of flexibility at low regularity and rigidity at high regularity motivates the following question: is there a critical Hölder threshold
\theta_{0}\in]0,1[
separating the two regimes? More precisely, does there exist
\theta_{0}
such that

(i)

isometric immersions
u\in C^{1,\theta}(M,\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
exhibit (some sort of) rigidity if
\theta>\theta_{0}
,

(ii)

the Nash–Kuiper theorem extends to
C^{1,\theta}
for all
\theta<\theta_{0}
?

Analogous phenomena occur in other geometric PDE, such as the Monge–Ampère equation, where sufficiently regular solutions with positive right hand side are rigid (convex), whereas very weak
C^{1,\theta}
solutions exhibit flexibility for low
\theta
, see
[
32
,
34
,
35
,
33
,
26
,
27
]
. A particularly well-studied parallel arises in fluid dynamics: Onsager’s conjecture for the incompressible Euler equations asserts that
C^{0,\theta}
weak solutions conserve energy (rigidity) for
\theta>1/3
, while many non-conservative solutions exist for
\theta<1/3
(flexibility). The rigidity was established in
[
15
]
and flexibility in
[
29
]
(see also
[
6
]
) following
[
19
,
20
,
5
,
7
]
.

Both the existence and value of such a threshold
\theta_{0}
for isometric immersions remain unknown. Parallels with Onsager’s conjecture, in particular the shared convex integration methodology, suggest
\theta_{0}=1/3
as a potential threshold.
On the other hand,
[
18
,
10
]
demonstrate that
C^{1,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
is a critical space
in a suitable sense
, suggesting
\theta_{0}=1/2
(see also
[
23
, Question 36]
,
[
21
, Section 10]
,
[
28
]
,
[
9
]
).

C^{1,\theta}
isometries: known results.
The first results on isometric immersions of intermediate regularity
C^{1,\theta}
were established by Yu. F. Borisov in the mid-20th century. He proved in
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
that the rigidity theorem of Cohn-Vossen and Herglotz remains true for isometries of class
C^{1,\theta}
for
\theta>2/3
(see also
[
16
]
for a short proof, and
[
39
]
).

On the other hand, he announced in
[
4
]
that the Nash-Kuiper theorem remains true for isometric immersions of open, bounded
n
-dimensional sets of regularity
C^{1,\theta}
for

\theta<1/(1+n(n+1))
,
the Borisov-exponent, with a potential improvement to
\theta<1/5
for
n=2
. These claims were confirmed in
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
and extended to compact manifolds in
[
12
]
.

Recently,
[
8
]
improved the exponent to
\theta<1/(1+n(n-1))
,
achieving the Onsager threshold
\theta<1/3
when
n=2
.

Higher codimension.

For
m>n+1
, the flexibility regime extends considerably. Existence results for smooth isometries are due to Nash
[
38
]
, with codimension bounds improved by Gromov and Günther
[
22
,
24
]
. Results for
C^{1,\theta}
isometries in higher codimension can be found in
[
30
,
18
,
10
,
13
,
43
,
36
]
.

Main result.

In this paper, we focus on the most constrained case of codimension one, and improve the exponent of
[
8
]
when
n\geq 3
. More precisely, we have

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
g\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega},\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+})
be a Riemannian metric on a smooth, bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any short immersion
\underline{u}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, any
\varepsilon>0
and any Hölder exponent

(1.2)

\theta<\frac{1}{1+2(n-1)}\,,

there exists an isometric immersion
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
such that

(1.3)

\|u-\underline{u}\|_{0}<\varepsilon.

Remark 1.1
.

As in
[
8
]
, Theorem
1.1
extends to the case of embeddings by standard arguments.
Moreover, combining the present construction with the global framework developed in
[
12
]
, the result carries over to compact manifolds.
Finally, the same mechanism applies in the setting of the very weak Monge–Ampère equation, yielding an analogous improvement of the Hölder exponent.

The improvement relies on a refined integration-by-parts mechanism as compared to
[
8
]
, which is based on a more precise structural analysis of the error terms arising in the convex integration scheme.
In what follows, we provide a heuristic sketch of the proof: first recalling the classical Nash iteration (Section
1.1
), then explaining
C^{1,\theta}
convergence (Section
1.2
), reviewing the iterative integration by parts of
[
8
]
, and finally presenting the refinement leading to Theorem
1.1
. Since the details of the construction are very technical, the discussion is heuristic.

1.1.
The Nash iteration

Following Nash
[
37
]
, we construct the isometric immersion
u
as the limit of an iterative scheme, the Nash iteration.
Starting from the initial short map
\underline{u}
, we build a sequence of smooth short immersions
\{u_{q}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
converging in
C^{1,\theta}
and such that the
metric deficit

g-Du_{q}^{T}Du_{q}

decays exponentially:

(1.4)

\|g-Du_{q}^{T}Du_{q}\|_{0}\leq K^{-1}\|g-Du_{q-1}^{T}Du_{q-1}\|_{0}

for some large constant
K\geq 1
.
In fact, for technical reasons, we enforce a double-exponential decay of the defect.

Given
u_{q-1}
, the construction of
u_{q}
, called a
stage
, begins by decomposing the positive definite defect into a finite sum of so-called primitive metrics:

(1.5)

g-Du_{q-1}^{T}Du_{q-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\,\eta_{i}\otimes\eta_{i},

where the coefficients
a_{i}
are smooth and
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. An elementary linear algebra argument (see Lemma
2.1
) shows that if the defect is sufficiently close to a constant positive definite matrix (something that can be arranged along the sequence by translation and rescaling), then the decomposition holds with
N=n_{*}=n(n+1)/2
.

We then add
N
corrugations
w_{q,1},\dots,w_{q,N}
sequentially:

u_{q,0}=u_{q-1},\qquad u_{q,i}=u_{q,i-1}+w_{q,i},\quad i=1,\dots,N,

and set
u_{q}=u_{q,N}
.

Each perturbation
w_{q,i}
is designed to increase the induced metric by the corresponding primitive metric:

(1.6)

Du_{q,i}^{T}Du_{q,i}=Du_{q,i-1}^{T}Du_{q,i-1}+a_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}\otimes\eta_{i}+E_{i},

up to an error term
E_{i}
satisfying

(1.7)

\|E_{i}\|_{0}\leq(NK)^{-1}\|g-Du_{q-1}^{T}Du_{q-1}\|_{0},

which yields (
1.5
) by a summation over
i
.

The basic ansatz for a perturbation achieving (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) is

(1.8)

w_{q,i}=\frac{a_{i}}{\nu_{i}}\left(\gamma_{1}(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})\tau_{i}+\gamma_{2}(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})\zeta_{i}\right),

where
\nu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\nu_{N}
are large frequency parameters,

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
are oscillatory functions,
and
\tau_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
are tangential and normal vector fields to
u_{q,i-1}
, respectively.
From (
1.8
) and (
1.5
) one sees that

\|Du_{q}-Du_{q-1}\|_{0}\leq C\|g-Du_{q-1}^{T}Du_{q-1}\|_{0}^{1/2}+\frac{C}{\nu_{1}},

so the sequence is Cauchy in
C^{1}
provided the frequencies are chosen large enough.

1.2.
C^{1,\theta}
convergence

We now explain how to obtain a sequence converging in
C^{1,\theta}
. A systematic choice of frequency parameters
\nu_{i}
is crucial. To see why, set

\delta_{q}=K^{-q}\|g-Du_{0}^{T}Du_{0}\|_{0}

so that the sequence
\{u_{q}\}_{q}
of the previous subsection satisfies

\|g-Du_{q}^{T}Du_{q}\|_{0}\leq\delta_{q}\,.

Now observe that if
\nu_{i}
are large, then
\|D^{2}u_{q}\|_{0}\leq C\delta_{q-1}^{1/2}\nu_{N}
, since the leading contribution to
D^{2}u_{q}
arises when both derivatives fall on the rapidly oscillating perturbation. Thus, controlling the growth of frequencies controls the blow-up of the second derivative.

This requires an analysis of the error terms
E_{i}
. An estimate disregarding the specific structure of
E_{i}
yields

\|E_{i}\|_{0}\leq C\delta_{q-1}\frac{\nu_{i-1}}{\nu_{i}}\,,

with
\nu_{0}=\|D^{2}u_{q-1}\|_{0}/\delta_{q-1}^{1/2}
.
In order to satisfy (
1.7
), one is therefore forced to choose an increasing sequence
\nu_{i}=K\nu_{i-1}
, resulting in the exponential blowup

\|D^{2}u_{q}\|_{0}\leq C\|D^{2}u_{q-1}\|_{0}K^{N}\leq C(K^{N})^{q}\,.

Since however
\|Du_{q}-Du_{q-1}\|_{0}\leq C\delta_{q-1}^{1/2}=CK^{-(q-1)/2}
, an interpolation between the
C^{1}
and
C^{2}
norms shows that this sequence is Cauchy in
C^{1,\theta}
for all
\theta<1/(1+2N)
. Taking
N=n_{*}
yields the Borisov exponent. In
[
17
]
, a diagonalization of the defect (available in two dimensions via isothermal coordinates) reduces
N
from
3
to
2
, thereby explaining the exponent
1/5.

1.3.
Iterative integration by parts

In
[
8
]
, a better regularity is achieved by an iterative integration by parts process.
The key observation is that all (except one) leading-order error terms in
E_{i}
are of the form

\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})\,M,

where
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
has mean zero, and
M
is a symmetric matrix oscillating at a lower frequency
\mu\leq\nu_{i-1}
.

Any symmetric matrix
M
can be decomposed as

(1.9)

M=\mathrm{sym}(\alpha(M)\otimes\eta_{i})+\pi(M),

for some vector
\alpha(M)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a remainder
\pi(M)
.
Writing
\gamma^{1}
for the mean-zero primitive of
\gamma
, one obtains

\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})M=\mathrm{sym}\left(D\left(\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})}{\nu_{i}}\alpha(M)\right)\right)-\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})}{\nu_{i}}\mathrm{sym}(D\alpha(M))+\gamma\,\pi(M).

This is referred to in
[
8
]
as an integration by parts.
Since
M
oscillates at frequency
\mu
, the second term on the right-hand side is now of smaller size
\|M\|_{0}\,\mu/\nu_{i}
. Iterating this procedure
J
times yields

\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})M=\mathrm{sym}(Dw_{c})+\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{F},

where
w_{c}
is a corrector vector field,

\|\mathcal{E}\|_{0}\leq C\Big(\frac{\nu_{i-1}}{\nu_{i}}\Big)^{J}\|M\|_{0},

and
\mathcal{F}
is a remainder of size comparable to
\|M\|_{0}
.

In
[
8
]
, a modified perturbation (different from (
1.8
)) is chosen so that an additional symmetric gradient appears in the induced metric:

Du_{q,i}^{T}Du_{q,i}=Du_{q,i-1}^{T}Du_{q,i-1}+a_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}\otimes\eta_{i}+\mathrm{sym}(Dw)+E_{i}.

Choosing
w=-w_{c}
cancels the leading order error term
\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})M
up to the much smaller error
\mathcal{E}
and the remainder
\mathcal{F}
.
Applying this cancellation to all leading error terms in
E_{i}
shows that, disregarding
\mathcal{F}
, condition (
1.7
) can be achieved by choosing

\nu_{i}=K^{1/J}\nu_{i-1}.

Since
J
can be taken arbitrarily large, this allows the frequencies to grow arbitrarily slowly, that is,
\nu_{i}\approx\nu_{i-1}
.

The remaining difficulty lies in the presence of the large remainder
\mathcal{F}
. However, if
\eta_{i}\cdot e_{1}\neq 0
, one checks that
\pi(M)
, and hence
\mathcal{F}
, is a symmetric matrix supported in the bottom-right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block.
In
[
8
]
, the directions
\eta_{i}
are therefore ordered so that
\eta_{i}\cdot e_{1}\neq 0
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and such that

\{\eta_{j}\otimes\eta_{j}:j=n+1,\dots,n_{*}\}

spans the space of symmetric matrices supported in the bottom-right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block.

With this ordering, the first
n
perturbations can be corrected using suitable
w_{c}
, producing an error of size
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nu_{i-1}/\nu_{i})^{J}
and a remainder of size
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i-1}/\nu_{i}
lying in the span of
\{\eta_{j}\otimes\eta_{j}:j=n+1,\dots,n_{*}\}
.
This remaining term can then be canceled exactly by adjusting the coefficients of the perturbations
j=n+1,\dots,n_{*}
.

Consequently, the first
n
frequencies can be chosen essentially constant,

\nu_{n}\approx\dots\approx\nu_{1}\approx\nu_{0}

(for large
J
), whereas for
i=n+1,\dots,n_{*}
one must use the standard choice
\nu_{i}=K\nu_{i-1}
.
This leads to

\nu_{n}=K^{\,n_{*}-n+n/J}\nu_{0},

and therefore convergence in
C^{1,\theta}
for all

\theta<\frac{1}{1+2(n_{*}-n+n/J)}.

Letting
J\to\infty
yields the exponent in
[
8
]
.

1.4.
Novelties

In
[
8
]
, we chose
w=0
in the perturbations
j=n+1,\ldots,n_{*}
for the following reason.
When trying to apply the integration by parts process to a term
\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})M
with
\eta_{i}\cdot e_{1}=0
, the decomposition (
1.9
) still holds, but the large remainder
\pi(M)
— which cannot be handled by integration by parts — no longer lies in the lower-dimensional span of primitive metrics yet to be added.
Consequently, the full remainder cannot be canceled by adjusting the coefficients
a_{j}
for
j\geq i+1
, and one must choose
\nu_{i}=K\nu_{i-1}
so that the uncancelable part of the remainder is sufficiently small.

The crucial insight of this paper is that, in fact, three frequencies are present in the definition of
u_{q,i}
: the frequency
\nu_{i}
of the perturbation added to
u_{q,i-1}
, the frequency
\nu_{i-1}
at which
u_{q,i-1}
(and hence its tangential and normal vector fields) oscillate, and the frequency
\nu_{0}
at which the coefficient
a_{i}
oscillates.
To illustrate heuristically how this matters, consider the scalar setting of the very weak Monge-Ampère equation and assume

u_{q,i}=u_{q,i-1}+\frac{a_{i}}{\nu_{i}}\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i}).

A closer analysis of the error terms in
E_{i}
reveals two types of leading-order contributions (see (
4.40
), (
4.41
)):

\frac{a_{i}}{\nu_{i}}\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})D^{2}u_{q,i-1},\qquad\frac{\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})}{\nu_{i}^{2}}\nabla a_{i}\otimes\nabla a_{i}.

The second term is of order
\nu_{0}^{2}/\nu_{i}^{2}
and is negligible as soon as
\nu_{i}\geq K^{1/2}\nu_{0}
. The first term is of order
\nu_{i-1}/\nu_{i}
, seemingly requiring
\nu_{i}=K\nu_{i-1}
. However, expanding
D^{2}u_{q,i-1}
gives

D^{2}u_{q,i-1}=D^{2}u_{q,i-2}+\nu_{i-1}a_{i-1}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1})\eta_{i-1}\otimes\eta_{i-1}+\gamma^{\prime}\nabla a_{i-1}\otimes\eta_{i-1}+\frac{\gamma}{\nu_{i-1}}D^{2}a_{i-1}.

Disregarding the last two terms (which are of size
\leq C\nu_{0}
) and, for the moment,
D^{2}u_{q,i-2}
, the leading-order error is (see (
4.33
))

\frac{\nu_{i-1}}{\nu_{i}}\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})a_{i}a_{i-1}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1})\eta_{i-1}\otimes\eta_{i-1}=\frac{\nu_{i-1}}{\nu_{i}}\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})A(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1},x)\otimes\eta_{i-1},

where we defined
A(t,x)=\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)a_{i}(x)a_{i-1}(x)\eta_{i-1}
.
Ideally, we would like a corrector
w_{c}
such that

\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})A(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1},x)\otimes\eta_{i-1}=\mathrm{sym}(Dw_{c})+O((\nu_{i-1}/\nu_{i})^{J})+\mathcal{F},

with
\mathcal{F}
in the span of
\eta_{j}\otimes\eta_{j}
,
j>i
. As explained above, this is not always possible. However, if
\eta_{i}
and
\eta_{i-1}
belong to the
same subfamily
, the decomposition works up to an additional error of size
\nu_{0}/\nu_{i}
.

To explain the notion of a subfamily, let
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the standard basis vector, and define, for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, the unit vectors

\eta_{ij}=\frac{e_{i}+e_{j}}{|e_{i}+e_{j}|}.

For
i=1,\dots,n
, consider moreover the subspace

\mathcal{V}_{i}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\eta_{kl}\otimes\eta_{kl}:i\leq k\leq l\leq n\},

of symmetric matrices supported in the bottom-right
(n-i+1)\times(n-i+1)
block. In particular,
\mathcal{V}_{1}=\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, so that the decomposition (
1.5
) holds with
\{\eta_{ij}\}
replacing
\{\eta_{i}\}
(see Lemma
2.1
).
If both
\eta_{i}
and
\eta_{i-1}
belong to the same subfamily
\{\eta_{kl}\}_{l\geq k}
for some fixed
k
, then in fact

A(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1},x)\otimes\eta_{i-1}=\mathrm{sym}(\alpha(A\otimes\eta_{i-1})\otimes\eta_{i})+\pi,

for a remainder
\pi\in\mathcal{V}_{k+1}
belonging to the span of primitive metrics yet to be added. Moreover, the derivative splits as

D(A(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1},x))=\nu_{i-1}\partial_{t}A\otimes\eta_{i-1}+D_{x}A.

Crucially, the leading term again has a decomposition whose remainder
\pi
lies in the lower-dimensional space
\mathcal{V}_{k+1}
(canceled later), while
D_{x}A
is of size
\nu_{0}
and hence small. Thus, iterating the integration by parts procedure produces a corrector
w_{c}
such that

\gamma(\nu_{i}x\cdot\eta_{i})A(\nu_{i-1}x\cdot\eta_{i-1},x)\otimes\eta_{i-1}=\mathrm{sym}(Dw_{c})+O((\nu_{i-1}/\nu_{i})^{J})+\mathcal{F}_{l}+\mathcal{F}_{m},

with
\mathcal{F}_{l}\in\mathcal{V}_{k+1}
and
\|\mathcal{F}_{m}\|_{0}\leq C\nu_{0}/\nu_{i}
, which is small enough for
\nu_{i}\geq K\nu_{0}
.

When crossing from one subfamily to the next, i.e.,
\nu_{i-1}
and
\nu_{i}
do not belong to the same subfamily,
D^{2}u_{i-1}
contains terms that cannot be handled by integration by parts in direction
\eta_{i}
. Thus, the frequency must be increased by a factor
K
at each family transition.
Denoting by
\nu_{ij}
the frequency of the perturbation corresponding to the primitive metric
\eta_{ij}\otimes\eta_{ij}
, for large
J
, the resulting sequence of frequencies is

\displaystyle\nu_{1n}\approx\dots\approx\nu_{11}\approx\nu_{0},

\displaystyle\nu_{2n}\approx\dots\approx\nu_{22}=K\nu_{1n},

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle\nu_{n-1,n}\approx\nu_{n-1,n-1}=K\nu_{n-2,n},

\displaystyle\nu_{nn}=K\nu_{n-1,n}.

This yields a final frequency
\nu_{n}\approx K^{n-1}\nu_{0}
, explaining the exponent (
1.2
) by the argument in Section
1.2
.

1.5.
Structure of the paper

We conclude this introduction by fixing notation. In Section

we collect the necessary preliminaries: a lemma for the decomposition of the defect into primitive metrics, and the iterative integration by parts result, Proposition

, which relies on the algebraic matrix decomposition given in Lemma

.In Section

.

1.6.
Notation

For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we denote by
\mathrm{sym}(M)=\frac{1}{2}(M+M^{T})

its symmetric part, and we write
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
for the space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices. For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we set
a\odot b=a\otimes b+b\otimes a=2\,\mathrm{sym}(a\otimes b)
.
The symbol
C
denotes a positive constant whose value may change from line to line. If
C
depends on a parameter
p
, we write
C=C(p)
or indicate the dependence explicitly.

For
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, we define

(1.10)

\eta_{ij}=\frac{e_{i}+e_{j}}{|e_{i}+e_{j}|},

where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the
i
-th standard basis vector.

For
i=1,\dots,n
, we introduce the subspace

(1.11)

\mathcal{V}_{i}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\eta_{kl}\otimes\eta_{kl}:i\leq k\leq l\leq n\},

which consists of all symmetric matrices supported in the bottom-right
(n-i+1)\times(n-i+1)
block. We also set

(1.12)

\mathcal{V}_{n+1}=\{0\},

and define the positive definite matrix

(1.13)

H_{0}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\eta_{ij}\otimes\eta_{ij}\,.

2.
Preliminaries: Decomposition and iterative integration by parts

In this section we collect auxiliary results needed for the iteration scheme. We begin with a basic algebraic decomposition of symmetric matrices in terms of the primitive metrics
\eta_{ij}\otimes\eta_{ij}
. We then recall a refined decomposition result (Lemma
2.2
), which allows one to absorb certain error terms into the primitive decomposition. For completeness, we include the elementary proof of the basic algebraic lemma, while referring to
[
8
]
for the refined argument.

Finally, in Subsection
2.2
, we establish the iterative integration by parts proposition, which will serve as the analytic core of the construction.

2.1.
Decomposition

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{\eta_{ij}\}
be defined as in (
1.10
). There exists
r_{D}=r_{D}(n)>0
and linear maps
L_{ij}:\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any symmetric matrix
M
it holds

(2.1)

M=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}L_{ij}(M)\eta_{ij}\otimes\eta_{ij}\,,

and
L_{ij}(M)\geq r_{D}
for all
M
such that
|M-H_{0}|<r_{D}
.

Proof.

The existence of
L_{ij}
follows from the fact that
\{\eta_{ij}\otimes\eta_{ij}\}
is a basis for
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, the existence of
r_{D}
from the linearity of the maps
L_{ij}
and
L_{ij}(H_{0})=1
.
∎

As mentioned in Subsection
1.3
, one of the error terms arising in the iteration is not directly amenable to integration by parts, as it consists of a symmetric matrix multiplied by a highly oscillatory periodic function with non-zero mean. Following
[
8
]
, we overcome this difficulty by subtracting the average of the oscillatory factor. This produces an additional term of the form

2\pi/\nu^{2}\,\nabla a\otimes\nabla a,

which no longer carries rapid oscillations.

The resulting term can be treated by reabsorbing it into the primitive decomposition. Since gradients of the coefficients appear, this absorption is not purely algebraic. Nevertheless, because no highly oscillatory factor is present, a Picard-type iteration allows one to incorporate the term into the decomposition up to an arbitrarily small error. We refer to
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
for the detailed argument.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
N_{K}\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists
r_{K}=r_{K}(n,N_{K})>0
such that the following holds for any
1\leq\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
and
H\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathrm{Sym}_{n})
satisfying

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|H-H_{0}\|_{0}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{1}}<r_{K}\,,

(2.3)

\displaystyle[H]_{k}\leq\lambda_{0}^{k}\text{ for }k=1,\ldots,N_{K}+1.

For any
J=0,\ldots,N_{K}
, there exists a vector
a^{J}=(a^{J}_{11},\ldots,a^{J}_{nn})\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n_{*}})
and an error term
E^{J}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathrm{Sym})
such that

(2.4)

H=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}^{n_{*}}(a^{J}_{ij})^{2}\eta_{ij}\otimes\eta_{ij}+\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{l}^{2}}\nabla a^{J}_{1l}\otimes\nabla a^{J}_{1l}+E^{J}\,,

and the estimates

(2.5)

\displaystyle a^{J}_{ij}\geq r_{K},\text{ for }1\leq i\leq j\leq n\,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|a^{J}\|_{k}\leq C\lambda_{0}^{k}\text{ for }k=0,\ldots,N_{K}-J+1\,,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|E^{J}\|_{k}\leq C\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{2(J+1)}\lambda_{0}^{k}\text{ for }k=0,\ldots,N_{K}-J\,,

hold. The constant
C
only depends on
n,N_{K}
.

2.2.
Integration by parts

In the integration by parts argument, the following algebraic decomposition of a matrix is crucial.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and let
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
satisfy

\xi_{k}=0\quad\text{for all }k<i,\qquad\xi_{i}\neq 0,

(where the first condition is void if
i=1
).
Then there exist linear maps

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle:\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\alpha(M)_{k}=0\text{ for }k<i,

\displaystyle\pi_{m}

\displaystyle:\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\to\{M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}:M_{kl}=0\text{ for all }k,l\geq i\},

\displaystyle\pi_{l}

\displaystyle:\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\to\mathcal{V}_{i+1},

such that for every
M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
one has the unique decomposition

(2.8)

M=\alpha(M)\odot\xi+\pi_{m}(M)+\pi_{l}(M).

Proof.

Observe that the sets
\{M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}:M_{kl}=0\text{ for all }k,l\geq i\}
and
\mathcal{V}_{i+1}
are vector subspaces of
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
of dimensions
n_{1}=n_{*}-(n-i+1)_{*}
and
n_{2}=(n-i)_{*}
respectively. Consider then the linear map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n-i+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\to\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
defined as

\Phi(\tilde{\alpha},\beta,\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\tilde{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\odot\xi+\sum_{k<i,l\geq k}\beta_{kl}e_{k}\odot e_{l}+\sum_{i+1\leq k\leq l\leq n}\gamma_{kl}\eta_{kl}\otimes\eta_{kl}.

It holds

n-i+1+n_{1}+n_{2}=n-i+1+n_{*}-(n-i+1)_{*}+(n-i)_{*}=n_{*}\,,

since

(n-i+1)_{*}=n-i+1+(n-i)_{*}.

Thus
\Phi
is a linear map between vector spaces of the same dimension. In order to show the claim, it therefore suffices to show that
\Phi
is injective.
Let therefore
\Phi(\tilde{\alpha},\beta,\gamma)=0
. Observe that the first and the third matrix is supported in indices
i\leq k\leq l\leq n
and
i+1\leq k\leq l\leq n
respectively. Hence, for all indices
k<i,l\geq k
,

0=e_{k}^{T}\Phi(\tilde{\alpha},\beta,\gamma)e_{l}=b_{kl}\,.

On the other hand,

0=e_{i}^{T}\Phi(\tilde{\alpha},\beta,\gamma)e_{i}=2\tilde{\alpha}_{1}\xi_{i}

from which
\tilde{\alpha}_{1}=0
follows in view of
\xi_{i}\neq 0
. Now looking at entry
il
for
l>i
yields
\tilde{\alpha}=0
in view of
\xi_{i}\neq 0
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{1}=0
:

\tilde{\alpha}_{l-i+1}\xi_{i}=\tilde{\alpha}_{l-i+1}\xi_{i}+\tilde{\alpha}_{1}\xi_{l}=e_{i}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\tilde{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\odot\xi e_{l}=0\,.

Thus

0=\Phi(\tilde{\alpha},\beta,\gamma)=\sum_{i+1\leq k\leq l\leq n}\gamma_{kl}\eta_{kl}\otimes\eta_{kl},

which implies
\gamma=0
, since
\{\eta_{kl}\otimes\eta_{kl}\}
is a linearly independent family. ∎

We now formulate the iterative integration by parts proposition. Its assumptions should be compared with the heuristic discussion in Subsection
1.4
. There, the frequencies
\lambda,\mu,\nu
correspond to
\nu_{0},\nu_{i-1},\nu_{i}
, respectively, while the unit vectors
\xi,\eta
correspond to
\eta_{i},\eta_{i-1}
. The vector field
A(t,x)
plays the role of
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)a_{i}(x)a_{i-1}(x)\eta_{i-1}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
satisfy

\xi_{k}=\eta_{k}=0\text{ for all }k<i,\qquad\xi_{i}\neq 0\,,

and consider a vector-field
A\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
with

(2.9)

A_{k}=0\text{ for all }k<i.

Let
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
be periodic with zero mean.

Fix frequencies
\nu\geq\mu\geq\lambda\geq 1
. Then for any natural
J\in\mathbb{N}
there exist vectorfields

w^{J}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad A^{J}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n}),

a periodic
\gamma^{J}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
with mean zero, and remainders

\mathcal{F}^{J}_{m},\mathcal{F}^{J}_{l}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathrm{Sym}_{n})

such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\gamma(\nu x\cdot\xi)A(\mu x\cdot\eta,x)\odot\eta=2\,\mathrm{sym}(Dw^{J})+\Big(\tfrac{\mu}{\nu}\Big)^{J}\gamma^{J}(\nu x\cdot\xi)A^{J}(\mu x\cdot\eta,x)\odot\eta+\mathcal{F}^{J}_{m}+\mathcal{F}^{J}_{l},

and

(i)

A^{J}
satisfies (
2.9
),

(ii)

\mathcal{F}^{J}_{m}
is the mixed-block part of the remainder:

(2.11)

\mathcal{F}^{J}_{m}\in\{M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}:M_{kl}=0\text{ for all }k,l\geq i\},

(iii)

\mathcal{F}^{J}_{l}
is the lower-block part of the remainder:

(2.12)

\mathcal{F}^{J}_{l}\in\mathcal{V}_{i+1}.

Moreover, if
N_{I}\in\mathbb{N}
and
A
satisfies

(2.13)

\|\partial_{t}^{l}D_{x}^{m}A\|_{0}\leq C_{A}\lambda^{m}\text{ for all }l+m\leq N_{I},

for some constant
C_{A}\geq 1
,
then for any
J\leq N_{I}
the following estimates hold

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{l}D_{x}^{m}A^{J}\|_{0}\leq C\lambda^{m},\qquad\text{ for all }0\leq l+m\leq N_{I}-J,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}^{J}_{m}\|_{0}\leq C\frac{\lambda}{\nu}\,,

(2.16)

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{F}^{J}_{l}\right]_{k}\leq C\nu^{k}\,,\qquad\text{ for all }k=0,\ldots,N_{I}-J

(2.17)

\displaystyle\left[w^{J}\right]_{k}\leq C\nu^{k-1}\,,\quad\text{ for all }k=0,\ldots,N_{I}-J+1,

where the constant
C
only depends on
n,N_{I},\gamma,\xi_{i}
and
C_{A}
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition by induction on
J
. For
J=1
we start by decomposing

\eta=\frac{\eta_{i}}{\xi_{i}}\xi+\eta^{\bot}\,,\qquad A=\frac{A_{i}}{\xi_{i}}\xi+A^{\bot},

so that
\eta^{\bot}
and
A^{\bot}
satisfy

(2.18)

\eta^{\bot}_{k}=A^{\bot}_{k}=0\text{ for all }k\leq i.

We can therefore write, omitting the arguments
\nu x\cdot\xi
and
(\mu x\cdot\eta,x)
of
\gamma
and
A
,

\displaystyle\gamma A\odot\eta

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\eta_{i}}{\xi_{i}}A\odot\xi+\gamma A\odot\eta^{\bot}

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\eta_{i}}{\xi_{i}}A\odot\xi+\gamma\frac{A_{i}}{\xi_{i}}\xi\odot\eta^{\bot}+\gamma A^{\bot}\odot\eta^{\bot}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(\frac{\eta_{i}}{\xi_{i}}A+\frac{A_{i}}{\xi_{i}}\eta^{\bot}\right)\odot\xi+\gamma A^{\bot}\odot\eta^{\bot}\,.

Observe that by (
2.18
) it holds
\gamma A^{\bot}\odot\eta^{\bot}\in\mathcal{V}_{i+1}
, so that this term will contribute to
\mathcal{F}_{l}^{1}
. For the first term, we abbreviate

(2.19)

\tilde{A}(t,x)=\frac{\eta_{i}}{\xi_{i}}A(t,x)+\frac{A_{i}(t,x)}{\xi_{i}}\eta^{\bot}.

Note that by assumption (
2.9
), it holds
\tilde{A}_{k}=0
for all
k<i
. We can now write

\displaystyle\gamma(\nu x\cdot\xi)\tilde{A}(\mu x\cdot\eta,x)\odot\xi

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{sym}\left(D\left(\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu x\cdot\xi)}{\nu}\tilde{A}(\mu x\cdot\eta,x)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-2\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu x\cdot\xi)}{\nu}\mathrm{sym}\left(\mu\partial_{t}\tilde{A}\otimes\eta+D_{x}\tilde{A}\right)\,,

where
\gamma^{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is the primitive of
\gamma
with zero mean

\gamma^{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)\,ds-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)\,ds\right)dt\,

and
\partial_{t}\tilde{A}
and
D_{x}\tilde{A}
are evaluated at
(\mu x\cdot\eta,x)
. By Lemma
2.3
we can now write

\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A})=\alpha\left(\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A})\right)\odot\eta+\pi_{m}(\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A}))+\pi_{l}(\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A})),

so that when combining with the above, we find (
2.10
) for
J=1
when defining

\displaystyle w^{1}(x)=\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu x\cdot\xi)}{\nu}\tilde{A}(\mu x\cdot\eta,x),\quad A^{1}(t,x)=-\partial_{t}\tilde{A}(t,x)-\frac{2}{\mu}\alpha\left(\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A}(t,x))\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{1}_{m}=-2\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu x\cdot\xi)}{\nu}\pi_{m}\left(\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A})\right),\quad\mathcal{F}^{1}_{l}=\gamma A^{\bot}\odot\eta^{\bot}-2\frac{\gamma^{1}(\nu x\cdot\xi)}{\nu}\pi_{l}\left(\mathrm{sym}(D_{x}\tilde{A})\right)\,,

where
A^{\bot}
and

are evaluated in
(\mu x\cdot\eta,\eta)
.
By definition of

and the property of the linear map

,

satisfies (
2.9
). Similarly, (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) are satisfied for

.

Now let

satisfy (

)–(

) for some

.
Let
\gamma^{J+1}
be the primitive of

with zero mean and define

by (

respectively (recall the

so that (

Assume now that

satisfies the bounds (

with

, so that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{l}D_{x}^{m}A^{j}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\partial_{t}^{l+1}D_{x}^{m}\tilde{A}^{j-1}\|_{0}+\mu^{-1}\|\partial_{t}^{l}D_{x}^{m+1}\tilde{A}^{j-1}\|_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\partial_{t}^{l+1}D_{x}^{m}A^{j-1}\|_{0}+\mu^{-1}\|\partial_{t}^{l}D_{x}^{m+1}A^{j-1}\|_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{m}

for all

3.
Step perturbation

In this section we define our perturbation (Proposition
3.4
). It is constructed from normal and tangential vector fields together with suitable oscillatory functions, whose definitions and properties we collect in the following preliminary subsection.

3.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\rho\geq 1
. We will say that a smooth map
u\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
(P_{\rho})
if

(3.1)

\frac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{Id}\leq Du^{T}Du\leq\rho\mathrm{Id},

i.e., the matrices
\rho\mathrm{Id}-Du^{T}Du
and
Du^{T}Du-\rho^{-1}\mathrm{Id}
are positive semi-definite.
Note that due to the lower bound, such a map is an immersion. Moreover, by taking the trace we see that (
3.1
) implies

(3.2)

\|Du\|_{0}\leq\sqrt{n\rho}\,.

The quantitative immersion condition
(P_{\rho})
guarantees the existence of a well-behaved normal vector field and allows one to define a tangential projection with controlled derivatives. These objects will be used to construct the perturbation.

Lemma 3.1

(Normal vectorfields)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set and let
u\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
satisfy
(P_{\rho})
for some
\rho\geq 1
. Then there exists a normal vectorfield
\zeta=\zeta_{u}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
such that

(3.3)

|\zeta|=1\,,\quad Du^{T}\zeta=0\,

and such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the estimates

(3.4)

[\zeta]_{k}\leq C_{k}[u]_{k+1}

hold, with constants
C_{k}
depending only on
n,k
and
\rho
. Moreover, if
v\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
satisfies
(P_{\tilde{\rho}})
,
then the normal vector field
\zeta_{v}
satisfies

(3.5)

\|\zeta_{u}-\zeta_{v}\|_{0}\leq C[v-u]_{1}

for a constant only depending on
n
and
\max\{\rho,\tilde{\rho}\}
.

Proof.

We let

\tilde{\zeta}=\star(\partial_{1}u\wedge\partial_{2}u\wedge\ldots\wedge\partial_{n}u)\,,

where
\star
denotes the Hodge-star in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Since
u
satisfies
(P_{\rho})
, it is an immersion and therefore
\tilde{\zeta}\neq 0
. In fact, we have

|\tilde{\zeta}|=\sqrt{\det\left(Du^{T}Du\right)}\geq\rho^{-n/2}\,.

We can therefore set

\zeta=\frac{\tilde{\zeta}}{|\tilde{\zeta}|}\,.

Since by the properties of the Hodge-star

\partial_{i}u\cdot\tilde{\zeta}\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}=\partial_{i}u\wedge\partial_{1}u\wedge\ldots\wedge\partial_{n}u=0

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, (
3.3
) holds.
To show (
3.4
), we recall (
3.2
) and observe that the map

F:\left(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\right)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,,(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\mapsto\frac{\star(v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{n})}{\sqrt{\det\left((\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)_{ij}\right)}}

is analytic in the set
V_{\rho}=\overline{B}_{\sqrt{n\rho}}^{(n+1)n}\cap\{\det\left(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\right)\geq\rho^{-n/2}\}
, with bounded derivatives of all orders (where the bound only depends on
n,\rho
and the order of the derivative). Thus it follows from Lemma
A.1
that

[\zeta]_{k}=\left[F|_{V_{\rho}}\circ(\partial_{1}u,\ldots,\partial_{n}u)\right]_{k}\leq C[u]_{k+1}\left(\left[F|_{V_{\rho}}\right]_{1}+\|Du\|_{0}^{k-1}\left[F|_{V_{\rho}}\right]_{k}\right)\leq C_{k}[u]_{k+1}

for a constant only depending on
n,k
and
\rho
.

Lastly, (
3.5
) follows from the mean-value inequality :

\|\zeta_{u}-\zeta_{v}\|_{0}=\|F(Du)-F(Dv)\|_{0}\leq\|DF\|_{0}\|Du-Dv\|_{0}\leq C[v-u]_{1}\,,

where the supremum in

4.
Iterative Proposition: Stage

In this section we state and prove the main iteration proposition. It allows us to construct, from an immersion

whose metric deficit is decreased by the factor

\displaystyle\|u_{0}-\underline{u}\|_{0}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\mu},\quad\|u_{0}\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\mu^{M},\quad\|\mathcal{E}_{0}\|_{0}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\mu}\,,

for a constant

3.1

Thus we have

(5.4)

We now show that for a suitable choice of
\delta_{0},\lambda_{0}
and
\mu\geq\mu_{0}(\underline{u},g)

satisfies the induction assumptions. For this, take

with some constant
1>\tau>0
and large constant
a
to be fixed below. For
r\leq 1
to be fixed below in Subsection
5.2

(5.5)

\mu:=\max\{C_{1}r^{-1}a^{\tau},2C_{1}\varepsilon^{-1},\mu_{0}(\underline{u},g)\}=C_{1}r^{-1}a^{\tau},

provided that
a
is large. In particular, with this choice we have

(5.6)

\frac{C_{1}}{\mu}\leq\min\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\,r\delta_{0}\}.

By taking
a
larger if necessary (depending on
\underline{u},g,r,\tau,\varepsilon
and

(5.7)

\delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{0}=a^{(M+\frac{1}{2})\tau}\geq C_{1}^{M+1}r^{-M}a^{M\tau}=C_{1}\mu^{M}.

Hence from (

), (
5.6
) and (

), we get the initial short immersion

such that

(5.8)

\begin{split}&\|u_{0}-\underline{u}\|_{0}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\|u_{0}\|_{2}\leq\delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{0},\\
&\|g-Du_{0}^{T}Du_{0}-\delta_{0}h_{0}\|_{0}\leq r\delta_{0}.\end{split}

5.2.
Iteration

We now want to iteratively apply Proposition
4.1
to generate the sequence
\{u_{q}\}

\theta<\frac{1}{1+2(n-1)}.

We now choose

with this choice, our sequence of immersions converges in

To manage the loss of domain due to mollification in each application of the stage, we choose a sequence
\{V_{q}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
of smooth bounded, open domains such that
\Omega\Subset V_{q+1}\Subset V_{q}
for any

\mathrm{dist}(V_{q},\partial V_{q-1})\geq 2^{-q}\mathrm{dist}(\Omega,\partial V_{0})=:d_{q}\text{ for }q\geq 1\,.

Define then

(5.11)

\delta_{q}=\delta_{0}a^{1-b^{q}},\quad\lambda_{q}=\lambda_{0}a^{\frac{1}{2\beta}(b^{q}-1)}

for some

(5.13)

\displaystyle u_{q}\in C^{2}(\bar{V}_{q},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\,,

(5.14)

\displaystyle\|g-Du_{q}^{T}Du_{q}-\delta_{q}h_{0}\|_{0}\leq r\delta_{q}\,,

(5.15)

\displaystyle\|u_{q}\|_{2}\leq\delta_{q}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{q}\,,

(5.16)

\displaystyle\|u_{q+1}-u_{q}\|_{k}\leq C\delta_{q}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{q}^{-k},\text{ for }k=0,1,

where the constant

Indeed, by (

from Subsection
5.1

in the definition of
\mu

is the constant in Proposition
4.1
with

defined as in (

has already been constructed. Taking
a

\delta_{q+1}\leq\frac{r\delta_{q}}{|H_{0}|}\,.

Upon choosing the constant
\Lambda
in (

with

4.1
we can apply Proposition
4.1

with

to get

. Set

Then (

5.11

\displaystyle\|g-Du_{q+1}^{t}Du_{q+1}-\delta_{q+1}h_{0}\|_{0}\leq

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\delta_{q+1}\big(\Lambda^{-J}+a^{b-1-\frac{\tau}{2}}+a^{-2N\tau-\tau-1+b}\big)

5.3.
Convergence

Finally, we show that the sequence constructed in the previous section converges in
C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
to an isometric immersion on
\bar{\Omega}
. By interpolation and
\delta_{q}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{q}\leq\delta_{q+1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{q+1}
, it holds for any
q

\displaystyle\|u_{q+1}-u_{q}\|_{1,\theta}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\theta}\|u_{q+1}-u_{q}\|_{1}^{1-\theta}\|u_{q+1}-u_{q}\|_{2}^{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{q}^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\theta)}(\delta_{q+1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{q+1})^{\theta}

\displaystyle=C\delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{0}^{\theta}a^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\theta}{2\beta}}a^{\frac{1}{2}b^{q}(\frac{\theta}{\beta}{b}-1-(b-1)\theta)}\,.

Since
\theta<\beta
, we can take

1<b<\min\left\{1+\frac{\tau}{2},\,\frac{\beta-\theta\beta}{\theta-\theta\beta}\right\}=1+\min\left\{\frac{\tau}{2},\,\frac{\beta-\theta}{\theta-\theta\beta}\right\}

and also
a
large such that

\|u_{q+1}-u_{q}\|_{1,\theta}\leq\left(\delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{0}^{\theta}a^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\theta}{2\beta}}\right)2^{-q-1},

and then
\{u_{q}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in

. Let

be the limit. From (
5.14
) and
\delta_{q}\to 0
as
q\to\infty,
we have

g-Du^{T}Du=\lim_{q\to\infty}(g-Du_{q}^{T}Du_{q})=0.

Thus

is an isometric immersion for
g
on
\bar{\Omega}.
Furthermore, by (
5.16
) for
k=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|u-u_{0}\|_{0}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\|u_{q+1}-u_{q}\|_{0}\leq C\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\delta_{q}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{q}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{0}^{-1}a^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta}}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}a^{-b^{q}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta})}

by taking

Therefore, the proof is complete.

Appendix A
Hölder spaces: interpolation and regularization

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
we define the (semi-)norms

\|f\|_{0}=[f]_{0}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}|f(x)|\,,\qquad[f]_{k}=\max_{|\sigma|=k}\|D^{\sigma}f\|_{0}\,,\text{ for }k\in\mathbb{N},

where for a multi-index
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
,
D^{\sigma}f=\frac{\partial^{\sigma_{1}}}{\partial x_{1}}\ldots\frac{\partial^{\sigma_{n}}}{\partial x_{n}}f
and
|\sigma|=\sigma_{1}+\ldots+\sigma_{n}
. For
\alpha\in]0,1]
, the Hölder semi-norms are defined as

[f]_{0,\alpha}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Omega\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad[f]_{k,\alpha}=\max_{|\sigma|=k}[D^{\sigma}f]_{0,\alpha}\,.

We sometimes abbreviate
[f]_{k+\alpha}:=[f]_{k,\alpha}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\alpha\in]0,1]
the Hölder norm is given by

\|f\|_{k,\alpha}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}[f]_{j}+[f]_{k,\alpha}\,.

As usual,
C^{k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{m})
denotes the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\|f\|_{k,\alpha}<\infty
.

We recall the classical interpolation inequalities for the Hölder seminorms and
0\leq r\leq s\leq t
:

[f]_{s}\leq C[f]_{r}^{\frac{t-s}{t-r}}[f]_{t}^{\frac{s-r}{t-r}}\,

for a constant
C
only depending on
r,s,t,n,m
and
\Omega
. Combining these inequalities with the product rule yields the following Leibniz-rule (see
[
17
]
for a proof):

If
f_{1},f_{2}\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, then we have

(A.1)

[f_{1}f_{2}]_{k,\alpha}\leq C(\|f_{1}\|_{0}[f_{2}]_{k,\alpha}+[f_{1}]_{k,\alpha}\|f_{2}\|_{0}),

where the constant only depends on
k,\alpha
and
\Omega
.
We also reacll the following lemma for compositions of Hölder maps (see e.g.
[
17
]
).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},V\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be bounded open sets and
f:U\to V
and
F:V\to\mathbb{R}
be two smooth functions. Then, for every natural number
l>0
, there is a constant
C

(depending only on
l,m,n
) such that

\displaystyle[F\circ u]_{l}\leq

\displaystyle C[u]_{l}\left([F]_{1}+\|u\|_{0}^{l-1}[F]_{l}\right);

\displaystyle[F\circ u]_{l}\leq

\displaystyle C([F]_{1}[u]_{l}+\|DF\|_{l-1}[u]_{1}^{l-1}).

Finally, we recall the standard mollification estimates. For
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let
f\ast\varphi_{\ell}
denote the convolution of
f
with a standard, radially symmetric mollifier
\varphi_{\ell}
, i.e.
\varphi_{\ell}(x)=\ell^{-n}\varphi(x/\ell)
for some non-negative, radially symmetric
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
with
\int\varphi\,dx=1
. We then have the following estimates (see
[
16
]
for a proof).

Lemma A.2
.

For any
r,s\geq 0,
and
0<\alpha\leq 1,
we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle[f*\varphi_{l}]_{r+s}\leq Cl^{-s}[f]_{r},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\|f-f*\varphi_{l}\|_{r}\leq Cl^{2-r}[f]_{2},\text{ if }0\leq r\leq 2,

(A.4)

\displaystyle\|(f_{1}f_{2})*\varphi_{l}-(f_{1}*\varphi_{l})(f_{2}*\varphi_{l})\|_{r}\leq Cl^{2\alpha-r}\|f_{1}\|_{\alpha}\|f_{2}\|_{\alpha},

with constant
C
depending only on
n,s,r,\alpha
and
\varphi.
