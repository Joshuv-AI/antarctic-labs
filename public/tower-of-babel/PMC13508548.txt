Prevalence and determinants of low-birth-weight deliveries at four district hospitals in Kigali, Rwanda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gashaija Absolomon, Richard Kalisa, Jeanine Condo, Felix K. Rubuga, Marie Josée Mwiseneza, Candide Mugeni Girimpundu, Japhet Ishimwe, Solange Nikwigize, Patricia Moreland, Etienne Nsereko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow birth weight (LBW) is a significant public health concern, particularly in developing countries, because it is associated with increased neonatal and infant morbidity and mortality. In Rwanda, LBW remains a critical issue affecting the overall health and development of children. Understanding the factors contributing to LBW is essential for designing effective interventions to reduce its prevalence and improve maternal and child health outcomes. We aimed to determine the prevalence and determinants of LBW among neonates delivered at four district hospitals in the City of Kigali Province, Rwanda.MethodsA hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted among neonates delivered at three public and one private district hospital located in the City of Kigali from November 2023 to February 2024. All available neonatal medical records that met the inclusion criteria were reviewed using retrospective chart review approach. Data analysis was performed via STATA version 17 to conduct descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate logistic regression to determine the adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding P-values for the determinants of LBW.ResultsThe prevalence of LBW was 8.2% (95% CI: 7.6–8.8; n = 453/5520). Significant determinants included HIV-positive status (AOR = 2.07, 95% CI [1.01–4.22], p = 0.046), indicating more than twice the odds of delivering an LBW infant compared to HIV-negative mothers; female neonates (AOR = 1.52, 95% CI [1.21–1.89], p < 0.001); preeclampsia/eclampsia (AOR = 4.46, 95% CI [2.32–8.58], p < 0.001); and hospitalization during pregnancy (AOR = 4.34, 95% CI [2.20–8.56], p < 0.001). Preterm birth (< 38 weeks gestation) was the strongest determinant (AOR = 16.85, 95% CI [13.19–21.52], p < 0.001).ConclusionThe prevalence of LBW among neonates in Kigali was 8.2%. Maternal HIV-positive status, female child sex, preeclampsia/eclampsia, and prior admission during current pregnancy were key determinants of LBW. Strengthening antenatal care and targeted maternal health programs, especially for teenage mothers, HIV-positive pregnant women, and women with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, can help address these factors and reduce LBW rates in Rwanda.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09465-8.

Introduction

Low birth weight (LBW), defined as a birth weight below 2,500 g, is a critical global public health issue, affecting approximately 20 million neonates annually and accounting for 15–20% of all births [1]. This burden disproportionately affects low-income countries, which account for 95.6% of LBW cases [2]. In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of LBW remains alarmingly high, with rates as high as 13.1% in South Africa compared to 0.9% in Chad [3]. Despite global efforts, such as the World Health Assembly’s target to reduce LBW by 30% by 2025, the annual decline rate of 1.2% is insufficient to meet this goal [1, 4].

In the East African region, LBW prevalence varies considerably across countries, ranging from approximately 10% in Uganda and Tanzania to as high as 17% in Ethiopia, with a regional pooled estimate of approximately 13–17% [3, 5, 6]. In Rwanda, the 2019–2020 Rwanda Demographic and Health Survey reported an LBW prevalence of 7%, while Rwanda-based studies have linked LBW to maternal, obstetric, neonatal, and antenatal-care-related factors [7–11]. Although Rwanda’s reported prevalence appears lower than the East African regional average, this estimate is derived from national household survey data and may not fully capture the burden at the facility level, particularly in urban settings. Urban areas such as the City of Kigali (CoK) present unique challenges, including disparities in access to prenatal care, socioeconomic inequalities arising from rural-urban migration, and complex maternal health dynamics. These factors emphasize the need for targeted, facility-based research to understand the determinants of LBW and develop effective contextual interventions.

The impact of LBW on infant survival is profound, as neonates weighing less than 2,500 g are at approximately 20 times greater risk of mortality than those with normal birth weights [12]. Globally, an estimated 14.6% of all babies are affected by LBW [13], contributing to numerous challenges in child development, including delayed linear growth, cognitive development issues, and increased risk of obesity and metabolic and cardiovascular diseases later in life [2, 14, 15]. Insufficient prenatal care and low socioeconomic status, including poverty and low education levels, are also associated with higher rates of LBW [16–20]. Pregnant women receiving iron and folic acid supplementation are less likely to give birth to LBW neonates [10].

The Rwandan government has introduced various initiatives to address LBW, including guidelines for treating neonates, the establishment of neonatal special care units in every public hospital, maternal health education programs, the provision of free iron and folic acid supplements, maternal micronutrient supplements, and the implementation of community-based health insurance schemes. Although national and selected facility-based evidence on LBW is available in Rwanda, evidence specific to urban district hospitals in the City of Kigali, where socioeconomic inequalities, rural-to-urban migration, and unique health system dynamics may modify LBW determinants, remains limited. This study therefore contributes by providing hospital-based, urban-specific evidence on the prevalence and determinants of LBW in Kigali, filling a critical gap in the existing literature and informing targeted local interventions. We aimed to determine the prevalence and determinants of LBW among neonates delivered at four district hospitals in Kigali.

Methods

Study design and data source

A hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted among neonates delivered at three public and one private district hospital located in the City of Kigali from November 2023 to February 2024. Data were extracted retrospectively from existing medical records, including the neonatology unit registers and maternity ward delivery registers maintained at each facility.

Study setting and population

This study was conducted in three public hospitals (Muhima, Kibagabaga, and Masaka) and one private hospital called La Croix Du Sud Hospital. These four facilities were purposively selected because they are the principal district-level hospitals serving the City of Kigali, collectively representing the major public delivery points in all three districts of the city (Nyarugenge, Gasabo, and Kicukiro), plus one private facility providing complementary coverage. Together, they serve a predominantly urban population, including a large proportion of migrants from rural areas, and collectively account for a substantial share of all institutional deliveries in Kigali. On a monthly basis, each of these hospitals admits and conducts an average of 200 deliveries. Hospitals outside Kigali were not included, as this study was deliberately focused on the urban context of the City of Kigali, where unique socioeconomic and health system dynamics may shape LBW determinants differently from rural settings. Future studies should extend this work to rural and other provincial settings.

Study participants

The study population consisted of all neonates delivered at the four selected hospitals (Muhima, Kibagabaga, Masaka, and La Croix Du Sud) between November 2023 and February 2024. Eligibility was determined through retrospective chart review. Neonates were eligible for inclusion if they were: (i) delivered during the specified study period; (ii) born alive; (iii) free of congenital anomalies affecting birth weight; and (iv) had a recorded birth weight and sufficient core information to confirm the eligibility criteria. Records were excluded from the primary analytic dataset if birth weight was missing, if the neonate was not born alive, if congenital anomalies affecting birth weight were recorded, or if valid maternal consent for participation in the primary study was absent. Variable-specific missing data in covariates were not used as a reason to exclude a record from the overall prevalence analysis; instead, missing covariate values were handled during the relevant analyses as described in the Data Analysis section. A census approach was used, whereby all eligible records across the study period were reviewed, yielding a final sample of 5,520 mother-neonate pairs. Birth weight was measured using calibrated digital neonatal weighing scales available in the delivery and neonatal care units of each hospital. Measurement was performed within the first hour of life in accordance with standard clinical practice at each facility.

Sampling and sample size

Sampling

A census sampling approach was used; all available neonatal medical records that satisfied the eligibility criteria at the four selected hospitals during the study period were reviewed. No random sampling or probability-based selection was applied. This approach was chosen to maximise sample completeness and ensure that all eligible deliveries during the three-month period were captured. A total of 5,520 eligible mother-neonate records were accessed and included in the prevalence analysis. Because the extracted analytic dataset contained only records that had already satisfied eligibility screening at abstraction, no additional excluded records were available for enumeration after extraction. It is important to note that the sample size of 246 represented the minimum threshold required for statistical adequacy in the primary study. In this sub-study, a census approach was adopted, whereby all eligible neonatal records over the data collection period (November 2023-February 2024) were included. This resulted in a substantially larger final sample of 5,520 mother-neonate pairs, which greatly exceeds the minimum threshold and enhances the precision and reliability of prevalence and determinant estimates. The number of non-missing observations is shown in the relevant tables; where denominators differ from 5,520, this reflects variable-specific missing data. Complete-case analysis was applied only to analyses involving variables with missing entries.

Data collection and materials

Data were collected retrospectively through a structured review of medical records from neonates delivered at Muhima, Kibagabaga, Masaka, and La Croix Du Sud during the study period. The primary data sources included the neonatology unit registers, maternity ward delivery registers, and individual patient medical files maintained at each facility. Using a structured data collection tool in Kobo Toolbox, relevant information was extracted directly from the medical records by trained data collectors. The data collection tool captured variables across three categories: (i) sociodemographic details (including maternal age, education level, place of residence, marital status, occupation, and health insurance type); (ii) obstetric and medical history (including parity, gravidity, gestational age, ANC visits, HIV status, malaria, anaemia, gestational hypertension, and hospitalization during pregnancy); and (iii) neonatal outcomes (including sex of the neonate, birth weight, and gestational age at delivery). No direct interaction with participants occurred, as all data were derived from existing documentation. As this study relied on secondary data extracted from hospital records, potential limitations include inconsistencies in record-keeping practices across facilities, missing entries in registers, and possible inaccuracies in recorded values. These limitations were mitigated by cross-checking multiple record sources where available, and by excluding records with incomplete data on key study variables. Data collection continued until the target three-month period was completed.

Study variables

Dependent variable

In this study, the primary outcome of interest was a neonate's birth weight, which was divided into two categories: LBW (less than 2,500 g) or normal birth weight (birth weight of 2,500 grams or above). To facilitate analysis, the birth weight was coded numerically, with normal birth weight assigned a code of zero (0) and LBW assigned a code of one (1). This binary coding simplified the statistical analysis of factors associated with LBW. By standardising the outcome variable in this manner, the study aimed to provide clear and quantifiable insights into the prevalence and determinants of LBW.

Explanatory variables

Maternal sociodemographic factors

Maternal sociodemographic characteristics included the hospital of delivery, with participants delivering at one of four hospitals: La Croix du Sud, Kibagabaga, Masaka, and Muhima. Maternal age at birth was categorised into five groups: less than 18 years, 18–20 years, 21–30 years, 31–40 years, and 41 years or older. Although the 18–20 year group spans a narrower two-year interval compared to the nine-year intervals of adjacent groups, this categorisation was retained because mothers aged 18–20 years represent a clinically distinct subgroup of older adolescents whose obstetric risk profile differs meaningfully from women in their twenties, as supported by established literature on adolescent pregnancy outcomes [21]. Education level was classified into four levels: no formal education, primary education, secondary education, and higher education. Place of residence was recorded as either rural or urban. Health insurance was divided into four categories: none, CBHI (Community-Based Health Insurance), RSSB (Rwanda Social Security Board health insurance), and other types (referring to private commercial insurance schemes or employer-based insurance plans not classified under CBHI or RSSB). Marital status was categorised as cohabitating, divorced/widowed/never married, or married. The number of living children was grouped into none, fewer than four, or four or more. Maternal occupation was classified as working or not working.

Medical and antenatal factors

Medical factors included maternal anaemia (haemoglobin < 11 g/dL or ≥ 11 g/dL), malaria during pregnancy (yes/no), HIV status (positive/negative), hospitalization during pregnancy (yes/no), gestational hypertension (preeclampsia and eclampsia) (yes/no), and antenatal care (ANC) visits classified as fewer than four or four or more.

Obstetric and neonatal factors

Obstetric factors included maternal parity (primiparous, 2–4 births, or grand multiparous), maternal gravidity (primigravida or multigravida), and gestational age at birth (< 38 weeks, 38–40 weeks, or > 40 weeks).

It is acknowledged that several variables known to influence birth weight were not captured in this dataset, including behavioural factors such as smoking, alcohol use, domestic violence, and psychological distress, as well as nutritional indicators such as maternal body mass index (BMI) and dietary habits. The absence of these variables may represent residual confounding and is discussed further in the limitations section.

Data analysis

Data were collected using Kobo Toolbox, transferred to Microsoft Excel, and subsequently imported into STATA version 17 for cleaning, coding, and analysis. Frequency tables were generated for all categorical variables. Pearson’s chi-square tests were used to compare categorical characteristics by LBW status, and chi-square statistics with corresponding p-values are presented in Table 3. Bivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify variables significantly associated with LBW. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in bivariate analyses were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable model.

Multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of LBW. A backward stepwise regression approach was used, with inclusion/exclusion of variables assessed via the likelihood ratio test and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); no variable exceeded the threshold of VIF > 10, confirming the absence of significant multicollinearity. All assumptions required for logistic regression were verified prior to analysis. The final adjusted model used a significance threshold of p < 0.05 (two-sided). Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Missing data were managed through complete-case analysis for the specific variables or models in which missing values occurred. All 5,520 records included in the prevalence analysis had valid birth-weight information. Variable-specific missingness is reflected in the denominators reported in Tables 1 and 2. Most variables had complete data; missing values were observed for number of living children (1,691 missing; 30.6%), maternal occupation (9 missing; 0.2%), and number of ANC visits (7 missing; 0.1%). These missing values were handled using complete-case analysis for the corresponding analyses.

Results

Maternal sociodemographic characteristics

Out of 5,520 mother-neonate pairs assessed the largest proportion of mothers were aged 21–30 years (49.1%, n = 2,711), while only a small proportion were under 18 years of age (2.2%, n = 121) and 4.9% (n = 268) were aged 41 years or older. Nearly half of the mothers had attained secondary education (49.8%, n = 2,748), the majority resided in urban areas (73.1%, n = 4,034), and were enrolled in at least one form of medical insurance (92.5%, n = 4,695), predominantly the Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI/RSSB) scheme. The majority were married (58.0%, n = 3,203), and over half were employed (66.6%, n = 3,673). Detailed distributions are provided in Table 1.

Maternal medical and obstetric characteristics

Among the 5,520 mother-baby pairs, 50.6% (n = 2,795) of the neonates were female, and 8.2% (n = 453) had LBW. Most mothers attended four or more antenatal care (ANC) visits 76.6% (n = 4,221), had two to four previous births 55.1% (n = 3,042), and experienced multiple pregnancies 68% (n = 3,753). The majority of neonates were born at 38–40 weeks gestation 73.7% (n = 4,069), while 11.3% (n = 623) were preterm. Maternal anaemia 7.6% (n = 422), gestational hypertension 1.2% (n = 66), and hospitalisation during pregnancy 0.9% (n = 52) were uncommon. Deliveries were most frequent at Muhima Hospital 32.4% (n = 1,787). Detailed distributions are provided in Table 2.

Prevalence of LBW

Out of 5,520 mother-baby pair charts assessed from four hospitals, the prevalence of LBW was 8.2% (95% CI: 7.6–8.8; n = 453/5,520) in Kigali.

Determinants of LBW

Bivariate logistic regression analysis

Bivariate analysis using Pearson’s chi-square tests identified significant associations between LBW and several factors, including maternal age (p = 0.047), maternal occupation (p = 0.024), HIV-positive status (p = 0.001), health insurance type (p < 0.001), hospitalisation during pregnancy (p < 0.001), gestational hypertension (p < 0.001), sex of the child (p < 0.001), gestational age (p < 0.001), and hospital name (p < 0.001). These nine variables were therefore eligible for entry into the multivariate logistic regression model. Factors such as malaria during pregnancy, maternal education, place of residence, marital status, number of living children, maternal anaemia, ANC visits, maternal parity, and gravidity were not significantly associated with LBW (p > 0.05). Detailed results, including chi-square statistics and p-values, are presented in Table 3.

Discussion

This study identified several determinants of LBW among neonates delivered in four district hospitals in Kigali, Rwanda. Key findings include the association of LBW with HIV-positive status, female child sex, and preeclampsia/eclampsia and hospitalization during pregnancy. The study underscores the importance of addressing maternal health issues and implementing targeted interventions to reduce LBW prevalence.

The prevalence of LBW in this study was 8.2%, slightly higher than the national average of 7% reported by the Rwanda Demographic and Health Survey (RDHS 2019–2020) [9]. Although slightly below the pooled East African regional estimate of 8.9% [22], The modest excess over the national survey estimate of 7% may partly reflect the improved quality of antenatal and intrapartum care in Kigali’s district hospitals, which facilitates earlier identification and facility-based management of high-risk pregnancies, thereby increasing the recorded proportion of LBW deliveries at these facilities relative to community-based national survey estimates. In comparison, neighbouring East African countries report considerably higher LBW rates: Ethiopia reports a prevalence ranging from 12.3% to 16.2% [23], while Uganda and Tanzania report rates of approximately 10% [22]. Rwanda’s urban LBW rate of 8.2% therefore places it between the East African regional average and its own national survey estimate, reflecting a persistent public health challenge [3, 5, 6]. This finding highlights the need for additional efforts to address maternal and neonatal health in urban settings. The slight increase in Kigali’s prevalence compared to the national average may reflect the concentration of high-risk pregnancies at district hospital level, referral patterns from surrounding areas, and socioeconomic inequalities specific to urban settings.

HIV-positive mothers were over two times more likely to deliver LBW infants (AOR = 2.07). This finding is consistent with studies in Tanzania and Zambia, indicating the adverse impact of maternal HIV on neonatal health [24, 25]. HIV infection contributes to poor maternal nutrition, increased risk of opportunistic infections, and complications during pregnancy. Rwanda’s prevention of Mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) program has made substantial progress since its national rollout in 2004, integrating PMTCT into routine maternal and child health services at facility and community levels. However, our finding that HIV-positive status remains a significant independent determinant of LBW suggests that PMTCT programs while effective in reducing vertical transmission may not fully mitigate the nutritional and obstetric complications driving LBW risk in this population. This implies a need to strengthen the nutritional support and obstetric monitoring components of Rwanda’s PMTCT program, ensuring that HIV-positive pregnant women receive not only antiretroviral therapy (ART) but also targeted dietary supplementation and enhanced antenatal surveillance [26, 27]. Strengthening PMTCT programs and ensuring comprehensive care for HIV-positive pregnant women, including nutritional support and early initiation of antiretroviral therapy, is crucial to addressing this issue. Some studies, however, report that with optimal ART coverage and nutritional support, the gap in birth weight outcomes between HIV-positive and HIV-negative mothers may narrow, suggesting that the observed association may be attenuated as program quality improves.

Female neonates were more likely to have LBW compared to male neonates (AOR = 1.52), with findings aligning with studies from Eritrea, India, and Bangladesh, Studies from Bangladesh also confirm higher odds of LBW among female neonates (AOR = 1.31), and a nationally representative Indian study similarly found female neonates had 1.25 times higher odds of LBW [5, 28–30]. This sex disparity may be partially explained by biological differences, such as slower fetal growth rates in females. Additionally, sociocultural factors that prioritise male children’s well-being could influence maternal nutrition and stress levels during pregnancy. Interventions should consider these biological and cultural dynamics to design effective strategies for reducing LBW. Some studies suggest prenatal androgens and cultural preferences as contributing factors to this disparity [31, 32]. However, some studies from sub-Saharan Africa have not found a significant sex difference in LBW prevalence [25], indicating that this association may vary across settings and populations. Interventions should therefore consider both biological and sociocultural dimensions of this disparity, including gender-sensitive nutrition counselling during pregnancy and community-level awareness campaigns addressing gender equity in health care-seeking behaviour.

Gestational hypertension (preeclampsia/eclampsia) (AOR = 4.46, 95% CI [2.32–8.58], p < 0.001) and prior hospitalisation during the current pregnancy (AOR = 4.37, 95% CI [2.20–8.56], p < 0.001) were significantly and strongly associated with LBW. These conditions reflect severe pregnancy complications that impair placental function and restrict fetal growth. Hospitalisation during pregnancy as a marker for high-risk obstetric conditions may capture a composite of complications including severe anaemia, preeclampsia, infection, or preterm labour that collectively increase LBW risk. Early detection and management of gestational hypertension through regular ANC visits are critical to mitigating these risks. Strengthening healthcare systems to provide timely intervention for high-risk pregnancies can reduce the burden of LBW. These findings align with previous studies conducted in Eritrea, India, and Ethiopia [32–34].

Preterm birth (< 38 weeks gestation) was the strongest determinant of LBW in this study, with preterm neonates having 16.93 times higher odds of being LBW compared to neonates born at 38–40 weeks. This is consistent with the well-established pathophysiological link between preterm birth and restricted intrauterine growth, and aligns with findings from studies across East Africa and globally [3, 8, 35]. The magnitude of this association underscores the critical importance of preventing preterm birth and ensuring skilled management of preterm neonates. Rwanda’s neonatal care protocols, including Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) for LBW infants, represent important existing interventions, though their consistent implementation across district-level facilities remains an area for monitoring.

Rwanda has invested substantially in maternal and child health over the past two decades. The national Community Health Worker program comprising over 45,000 CHWs including dedicated Agents de Santé Maternelle (ASMs) at village level provides home-based antenatal surveillance, referral linkage, and postnatal follow-up, including five postnatal visits specifically for LBW infants. The PMTCT program, integrated into primary health care since 2004, provides HIV testing, ART, and psychosocial support to HIV-positive pregnant women. Rwanda’s Maternal and Neonatal Health Strategic Plan (2018–2024) and neonatal care protocols, including KMC, address LBW management at facility level. The findings of this study broadly align with the priorities of these programs confirming that HIV, adolescent pregnancy, gestational hypertension, and preterm birth remain the critical target conditions. However, the persistence of these risk factors as significant LBW determinants in an urban hospital setting suggests that program reach and quality in urban Kigali may need strengthening, particularly with regard to nutritional support for HIV-positive pregnant women and adolescent-sensitive ANC services.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, the large sample size of 5,520 mother-baby pairs drawn from four hospitals across three administrative districts of Kigali enhances the statistical power and precision of the estimates. Second, the inclusion of both public and private hospitals increases the representativeness of the findings within the urban district-hospital setting. Third, the use of a structured, validated data collection tool administered through Kobo Toolbox facilitated standardised data extraction across all four facilities.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the identified determinants and LBW; the observed associations should be interpreted as statistical relationships rather than causal effects. Second, as a retrospective study relying on secondary medical record data, the findings are susceptible to recall bias, incomplete documentation, and inaccuracies inherent to routine hospital records. Variables such as maternal nutritional status, smoking, alcohol use, domestic violence, psychological distress, and body mass index, all established contributors to LBW, were not captured in the dataset and represent an important source of unmeasured confounding. Third, the study was conducted exclusively in urban district hospitals within the City of Kigali, limiting the generalisability of findings to rural and peri-urban settings in Rwanda. Fourth, the absence of data on the timing of birth weight measurement (immediate versus delayed weighing post-delivery) introduces a potential source of measurement variability. Future research should address these gaps by adopting prospective longitudinal designs, incorporating rural and peri-urban facilities, and collecting data on behavioural and anthropometric variables not captured in routine hospital records.

Conclusion

This study determined that the prevalence of LBW among neonates delivered at four district hospitals in the City of Kigali, Rwanda was 8.2% (95% CI: 7.6–8.8), slightly exceeding the national estimate of 7% reported by the RDHS 2019–2020, and consistent with the elevated burden observed at the facility level in urban settings. The key determinants of LBW identified through multivariate logistic regression analysis were: HIV-positive maternal status, female neonate sex, gestational hypertension (preeclampsia/eclampsia), and hospitalisation during the current pregnancy, each independently increasing the odds of LBW. Conversely, having 2–4 previous births and having no formal education were associated with lower odds of LBW after adjustment, findings that warrant further contextual investigation. These findings underscore the complex, multifaceted nature of LBW as a public health issue in urban Rwanda and highlight the need for targeted, determinant-specific interventions.

To reduce the prevalence of LBW and improve neonatal health outcomes, the following specific interventions are recommended. First, programmes targeting adolescent reproductive health must be strengthened, including expanding access to comprehensive sexual and reproductive health education, scaling up adolescent-friendly ANC services, and reinforcing community-based initiatives that delay early pregnancy. Second, the existing PMTCT programme should be enhanced beyond virological suppression alone to incorporate nutritional screening and supplementation, psychosocial support, and enhanced clinical monitoring of fetal growth for all HIV-positive pregnant women. Third, given that gestational hypertension and hospitalisation during pregnancy were the strongest determinants of LBW (AORs > 4), Community Health Workers (CHWs) should be equipped and trained to screen for early signs of preeclampsia during home visits, with clearly defined referral pathways to district hospitals. Fourth, while 85.1% of mothers were enrolled in CBHI, the persistent burden of LBW suggests that insurance enrolment alone is insufficient to guarantee quality maternal care. Specific interventions should focus on improving the quality, timeliness, and continuity of ANC services delivered under CBHI, including blood pressure measurement, haemoglobin testing, and growth monitoring at every contact.

Future research should adopt prospective cohort study designs to establish the temporality and causality of the identified determinants of LBW. National-level multisite studies incorporating both urban and rural facilities across all provinces of Rwanda are needed to generate more generalisable estimates. Operational research evaluating the effectiveness of existing maternal health programmes, including PMTCT, the CHW programme, and CBHI, specifically on LBW outcomes, would provide critical evidence to guide programme optimisation. Furthermore, future studies should collect data on variables not available in this study, including maternal nutritional status, pre-pregnancy body mass index, psychosocial factors, substance use, and household socioeconomic indicators.
