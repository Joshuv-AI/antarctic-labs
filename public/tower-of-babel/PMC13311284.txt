Reciprocal regulation between JOSD2 and CREB1 drives aerobic glycolysis and progression of renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Qian Zhang, Hao Yuan, Haiyun Xiong, Hong Xiao, Yiyu Sheng, Bin Xu, Yupeng Guan, Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysregulated aerobic glycolysis represents a defining metabolic feature of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), yet the regulatory networks driving this metabolic shift remain incompletely elucidated. In the present study, we found that the deubiquitinase Josephin domain-containing protein 2 (JOSD2) is necessary for both glycolytic and proliferative activities in RCC. Furthermore, JOSD2 is significantly upregulated in RCC and its high expression correlates with poor clinical outcomes. Functional studies demonstrated that JOSD2 enhances RCC cell growth, metastatic capacity, and glycolytic activity in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, JOSD2 directly interacts with the transcription factor CREB1 and prevents its proteasomal degradation by removing K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, thereby stabilizing the CREB1 protein. Meanwhile, CREB1 transcriptionally upregulates JOSD2 by binding to its promoter, forming a reciprocal regulatory circuit between the two molecules. Reciprocal activation between JOSD2 and CREB1 leads to increased expression of key glycolytic regulators, including GLUT1, HK2, and PFKP, thereby driving metabolic reprogramming in RCC cells. Importantly, the tumor-promoting functions of JOSD2 were found to be largely dependent on CREB1 activity. Together, these results support a role for the JOSD2-CREB1 reciprocal axis in glycolysis-associated RCC progression and suggest its potential prognostic and therapeutic relevance.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, JOSD2, CREB1, Glycolysis, Reciprocal regulatory axis

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for approximately 3% of all adult cancers. Both RCC incidence and mortality appear to be increasing, although the factors driving this trend are not understood [1]. Clear cell RCC (ccRCC) is the most frequent RCC histological subtype, representing 70-80% of RCC diagnoses [2]. Early-stage RCC is frequently asymptomatic, which contributes to delayed diagnosis. Consequently, approximately 15% of cases already show distant metastases when diagnosed. This advanced disease stage at diagnosis significantly compromises surgical efficacy and long-term survival [3]. Moreover, RCC exhibits intrinsic resistance to conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy and frequently develops resistance to targeted therapies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as sunitinib [4]. Together, these clinical challenges underscore the need to determine the factors responsible for RCC progression and identify new targets for treating the disease.

A characteristic of tumor cells is metabolic reprogramming, particularly the preferential reliance on aerobic glycolysis, termed the Warburg effect [5,6]. This metabolic shift is characterized by enhanced glucose uptake and increased lactate production even under normoxic conditions. Aerobic glycolysis supports rapid tumor growth by providing metabolic intermediates for nucleotide and biomass synthesis, while lactate accumulation within the tumor microenvironment promotes invasion, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance [7]. In RCC, heightened glycolytic activity has been closely linked to tumor metastasis and poor patient prognosis. Accordingly, inhibition of glycolytic pathways holds promise for suppressing RCC progression and dissemination [8,9].

Protein ubiquitination is a reversible post-translational modification central to regulating protein turnover, subcellular localization, signaling activity, and degradation. This process involves covalent ubiquitin moiety attachment to target proteins through a coordinated enzymatic cascade, thereby controlling a wide range of cellular processes, including signal transduction, proteostasis, and intracellular trafficking [10]. Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) counterbalance ubiquitination by removing ubiquitin chains from substrate proteins, thereby fine-tuning protein stability and function. DUBs are critically involved in various processes, including the cell cycle, stress responses, repair of DNA damage, and oncogenic signaling, highlighting their importance in maintaining cellular homeostasis [11].

Josephin domain-containing protein 2 (JOSD2) belongs to the family of Machado-Joseph domain (MJD) proteases, the smallest subgroup of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) [12]. While the MJD family has been implicated in diverse cellular processes, the specific biological functions of JOSD2 remained largely uncharacterized until recently. The first functional characterization of JOSD2 was published online in October 2020, which identified it as a positive regulator of glucose metabolism through deubiquitinating a multienzyme complex comprising phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase, phosphofructokinase-1, and aldolase A [13]. Subsequent studies further expanded our understanding of JOSD2's roles: in June 2021, it was reported that JOSD2 promotes cholangiocarcinoma progression by stabilizing YAP/TAZ [14]; later work demonstrated its oncogenic function in hepatocellular carcinoma via deubiquitinating β-catenin [15], and its cardioprotective role through targeting SERCA2a [16]. Despite these advances, the function of JOSD2 in controlling glucose metabolism and tumor progression in RCC has not been fully elucidated and warrants systematic investigation.

Here, the goal was to define the function of JOSD2 in RCC and to dissect the molecular factors underlying its oncogenic activity. We focused on JOSD2 interactions with the transcription factor CREB1, demonstrating that JOSD2 stabilizes CREB1 through deubiquitination, while CREB1 reciprocally enhances JOSD2 transcription, forming a reciprocal regulatory relationship. This regulatory circuit promotes RCC cell proliferation, metastatic behavior, and glycolytic reprogramming. The results indicate the importance of JOSD2 as a metabolic regulator in RCC and support its potential in treating the disease.

Materials and methods

Patients and specimens

Paired ccRCC tumor samples and adjacent non-cancerous tissues from 12 patients who underwent surgical resection between January 2023 and December 2024 at the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Shenzhen, China) were used for these analyses. The patients had not received targeted therapy, immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy. In addition, a commercial tissue microarray (TMA) containing 90 matched pairs of ccRCC and normal tissues was obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company; the TMA was accompanied by clinical staging, pathological diagnoses, and survival information. Written informed consent was provided by all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Cells

HK-2 human renal tubular epithelial cells and ACHN, A498, 786-O, Caki-2, and OS-RC-1 RCC cells were acquired from the ATCC. HEK293T cells were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences. HK-2 cells were maintained in Keratinocyte Serum-Free Medium (Gibco), HEK293T and ACHN cells were grown in DMEM (Gibco), and the remaining RCC cells in RPMI 1640 (Gibco), with all media including 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were grown at 37°C with 5% CO2. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was used to confirm all cell lines, and all cultures were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

RNA interference, plasmid, and lentivirus construction

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting JOSD2 and CREB1, as well as a non-targeting scrambled control, were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Target sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Expression plasmids encoding JOSD2-HA, CREB1-Flag, the catalytically inactive mutant JOSD2C24A-HA, JOSD2-GST, Ub-Myc, K6-only Ub-Myc, K11-only Ub-Myc, K27-only Ub-Myc, K29-only Ub-Myc, K33-only Ub-Myc, K48-only Ub-Myc, K63-only Ub-Myc, K-null Ub-Myc, K48R Ub-Myc, and corresponding empty vectors were constructed and verified by sequencing. Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293T cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) as directed. Media were replaced 8-12 h following transfection, with collection of viral supernatants at the 24 and 48 h time points. Following filtration (0.45 μm), viral preparations were utilized to infect target cells in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Santa Cruz). Stable cell lines were established by selection with 2 μg/mL puromycin.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using a kit (Takara, Japan) as directed. qRT-PCR was performed using 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara) on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. Relative expression was determined with the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

CCK-8 assay

Proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 assay (Servicebio, China). Briefly, 1500 cells/well were inoculated in 96-well plates. At appropriate times, CCK-8 solution (10 μL/well) was added for 2 h at 37°C, and absorbances at 450 nm were measured in a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

To assess colony formation capacity, 800 cells were plated into 6-well plates supplemented with 2 mL of complete culture medium, and maintained under standard culture conditions for 2 weeks. After the incubation period, cell colonies were fixed with 0.4% crystal violet solution. The total number of visible colonies was finally quantified via ImageJ analysis software. Three separate experiments were performed.

EdU assay

DNA synthesis was examined by EdU incorporation and the EdU assay kit was obtained from RiboBio Biotech (Guangzhou, China). The order of reagent addition and the working concentrations were strictly implemented in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 1000 cells were seeded into each well of 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. EdU (50 μM) was then introduced for 2 h. Cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100, 20 min), and washed with PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL/well of 1×Apollo reaction solution was added for 30 min protected from light. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342, and images from five randomly selected fields of view per group were captured using a Leica fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). The cell proliferation rate was subsequently calculated in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol.

Wound healing assay

For wound healing assay, cells were grown in 6-well plates until ∼90% confluent. A linear scratch was introduced into the monolayer using a sterile pipette tip, with imaging of the cells at time 0 h and 24 h to evaluate wound closure. Cell migration and motility were assessed by quantifying changes in scratch width.

Cell migration and invasion assays

The migratory and invasive potential of RCC cells was characterized using 24-well Transwell chamber systems (Corning, NY, USA). For invasion detection, the insert polycarbonate membranes were precoated with Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h prior to cell plating. For both migration and invasion tests, 5 × 104 cells were resuspended in 200 μL serum-free RPMI 1640 medium and then added into the upper inserts. The lower chambers were filled with RPMI medium containing 10% FBS, which acted as a chemotactic factor to induce cell movement. After incubation for 12 h (migration) or 24 h (invasion), cells adhering to the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, followed by 30 min staining with 0.4% crystal violet (both reagents from Beyotime, China), and finally quantified via manual counting under an optical microscope.

Western blotting

RIPA buffer (Beyotime) with protease inhibitors and PMSF was used for total protein extraction. Protein concentrations were quantified, and equal protein quantities underwent SDS-PAGE separation before transfer onto PVDF membranes. The blots were blocked (5% non-fat milk, 1 h, ambient temperature) and treated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies before washing with PBST, and probing with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (2 h, ambient temperature). Visualization utilized a chemiluminescent detection system (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies were against JOSD2 (SAB2103354, Sigma-Aldrich), JOSD1 (ab118221, Abcam), ATXN3 (ab259828, Abcam), ATXN3L (MBS766974, MyBioSource), CREB1 (ab32515, Abcam), GLUT1 (ab115730, Abcam), HK2 (ab227198, abcam), PFKP (ab119796, Abcam), β-tubulin (ab6046, Abcam), GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam), IgG (ab109489, Abcam), Flag (F7425, Sigma-Aldrich), HA (ab236632, Abcam), and Myc (ab206486, Abcam). HRP-linked anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies from Abcam.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were inoculated in confocal culture dishes and grown for 24 h. After PBS washes, cells were fixed as above and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Cells were blocked using 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h. After overnight treatment at 4°C with primary antibodies against JOSD2 (orb1819483, Biorbyt) or CREB1 (ab178322, Abcam), cells were probed with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (MCE), and cells were imaged under confocal laser scanning microscopy (Zeiss).

Detection of glucose uptake and key glycolytic metabolites

We evaluated cellular glucose uptake activity using 2-NBDG, a fluorescent glucose analog. In brief, cells were incubated with 10 μM 2-NBDG for 1 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator, rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS to remove excess fluorescent probe, and then analyzed on a BD flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was recorded and normalized to the shCtrl group.

Lactate secretion levels were measured via a colorimetric detection method. Cells were resuspended in pyruvate-free DMEM, plated into 96-well plates with 6 × 104 cells per well, and maintained under standard culture conditions for 6 h. The culture medium was collected, and diluted 1:6 in the dedicated lactate assay buffer. Lactate content in the medium was quantified using a commercial lactate colorimetric assay kit (BioVision, Inc.) as per the manufacturer's protocol, with the final value corrected by deducting the background lactate level from the blank medium without cell incubation.

For acetyl-CoA detection, 1 × 106 cells were lysed in 500 μL assay buffer, and the lysate was centrifuged at 10,000× g for 15 min at 4°C to remove insoluble debris. The supernatant was collected and deproteinized with 1 N perchloric acid (2 μL per mg of total protein), followed by neutralization with 3 M KHCO3.

Assessment of glycolytic phenotype and mitochondrial respiration via extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) profiling

We performed real-time monitoring of cellular metabolic phenotypes via detection of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, a core marker of glycolytic activity) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR, a gold-standard indicator of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation) using the Seahorse XF Extracellular Flux Analyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), with all operations performed as per the manufacturer's standard protocol. Briefly, RCC cells were harvested, counted, and seeded into Agilent Seahorse XF96 microplates at a density of 8000 cells per well. The plates were incubated overnight in a 37°C 5% CO2 incubator to reach 70–80% confluency on the day of the assay.

Prior to detection, the culture medium was aspirated, and cells were rinsed with pre-warmed XF Base Medium, followed by 1 h of equilibration in a 37°C non-CO2 incubator. The glycolytic stress test and mitochondrial stress test were then conducted sequentially. For the glycolytic stress assay, ECAR was dynamically measured after sequential injection of glucose (10 mM final concentration), oligomycin (1 μM final concentration), and 2-DG (50 mM final concentration) to assess glycolytic capacity, glycolytic reserve and non-glycolytic acidification. For the mitochondrial stress assay, OCR was continuously recorded after stepwise addition of the following compounds: oligomycin (1.5 μM final concentration), FCCP (1 μM final concentration), and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 μM final concentration for each reagent), to evaluate mitochondrial respiratory function, including basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity. Upon completion of the assay, cells in each well were lysed, and total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All ECAR and OCR data were normalized to the total protein content of the corresponding well to eliminate the influence of cell number differences.

13C-glucose tracing assays

To profile glucose metabolic flux reprogramming in RCC cells, we performed U–13C6-glucose tracing assays coupled with ultra-performance liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC-TQMS) detection. Briefly, RCC cells were seeded into 10 cm culture dishes and maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, until reaching approximately 50% confluency. The original culture medium was then aspirated, and cells were incubated in glucose-free DMEM containing 10% FBS. U–13C6-labeled d-glucose (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# CLM-1396-1g, isotopic purity 99%) was added to the medium at a final concentration of 4500 mg/L, followed by 24 h of continuous culture under standard conditions. At the end of the labeling period, the medium was discarded, and cells were rinsed twice with pre-chilled normal saline to remove residual tracer. After complete removal of the saline solution, 0.5 mL of 80% methanol pre-cooled to −20°C was added to each dish, and adherent cells were harvested by scraping with a sterile cell scraper. The obtained cell lysates were transferred to 1.5 mL sterile centrifuge tubes and immediately stored at −80°C until detection. UPLC-TQMS-based metabolic flux assays were commissioned and performed by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using Waters MassLynx software (version 4.1, Waters Corporation), including peak extraction, peak area integration, metabolite annotation and identification, as well as relative quantitative analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and mass spectrometry

Transfected or treated cells (24-h treatment) were harvested. Cell pellets were lysed in 1 mL of IP buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 100 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails [MCE]). Lysed cells were sonicated and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4°C). A portion of the supernatant (50 μL) was retained as input, while the remaining lysate was incubated overnight at 4°C with Protein A/G magnetic beads and the appropriate antibody under rotation. After four washes with chilled IP buffer, beads were suspended in 2× loading buffer, and boiled for 15 min. After centrifugation, supernatants were collected and subjected to immunoblotting. All primary antibodies used for IP were as follows: JOSD2 antibody (SAB2103354, Sigma-Aldrich), CREB1 antibody (ab32515, Abcam), Flag antibody (F7425, Sigma-Aldrich), HA antibody (ab236632, Abcam), Myc antibody (ab206486, Abcam), normal rabbit IgG antibody (ab172730, Abcam).

Protein-protein interactions involving JOSD2 were assessed via LC-MS/MS. Cells were lysed with NP-40 buffer to extract total proteins, which were then immunoprecipitated with anti-JOSD2 antibodies or control IgG. The eluted samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE, and the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to display protein bands. The target band corresponding to the enriched JOSD2-interacting proteins was dissected from the gel, and subjected to in-gel digestion and subsequent liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis, which was performed by FitGene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC staining was conducted on paraffin-embedded sections (4 μm) which were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval. After blocking with 20% goat serum for 30 min, sections were treated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against JOSD2 (orb1819483, Biorbyt), CREB1 (ab178322, Abcam), or Ki-67 (ab21700, Abcam). On the following day, sections were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies for 30 min. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB detection kit (Absin), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Sections were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists, with the scoring system of 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong), while the positive cell percentage was 0 (0-5%), 1 (5-25%), 2 (26-50%), 3 (51-75%), or 4 (76-100%). Staining index (SI) values were determined as: SI = intensity score × percentage score. For immunohistochemical scoring of tissue microarray sections, The H-score system was adopted to evaluate the immunoreactivity of JOSD2/CREB1 based on both staining intensity and the proportion of positively stained cells. Staining intensity was scored on a 4-point scale from 0 to 3 (0 = absent staining; 1 = weak staining; 2 = moderate staining; 3 = strong staining). The final H-score (ranging from 0 to 300) was calculated by multiplying the corresponding intensity score by the percentage of positive cells (0% to 100%). Each tissue slide was independently evaluated by two pathologists blinded to patient clinical data, and the average score from the two assessments was used for final analysis.

GST pull-down assays

Full-length JOSD2 and CREB1 coding sequences were amplified by PCR from HA-JOSD2 and Flag-CREB1 plasmids and subcloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector. After transformation of GST or GST-JOSD2 constructs into Escherichia coli BL21 cells, and protein expression was induced with 100 μM IPTG at 16°C for 12 h. Bacterial pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris [pH 7.4], 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP40, 1 mM EDTA, protease inhibitors), followed by sonication and centrifugation. Supernatants were mixed for 1 h with glutathione agarose beads at 4°C. Beads were subsequently incubated with cell lysates containing the interacting protein at 4°C for 4 h while rotating. RIPA buffer was used for elution, with eluted proteins analyzed via western blotting.

Protein stability assessment via cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

To determine the regulatory effect of JOSD2 on CREB1 protein stability, we performed cycloheximide chase assays. Briefly, cells were subjected to transfection with control shRNA or JOSD2-specific shRNA for 48 h. Cycloheximide (CHX), a classical protein synthesis inhibitor, was added to the cell culture medium at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL to block de novo protein synthesis. Cells were collected at 0 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 12 h after CHX administration, followed by total protein extraction. The obtained protein lysates were analyzed by Western blot using primary antibodies targeting CREB1 and β-tubulin (served as the internal loading control). The gray intensity of each protein band was quantified using ImageJ software, and the relative protein level of CREB1 was normalized to the corresponding β-tubulin level.

Ubiquitination

The pRK5-Myc-ubiquitin-WT/K6/K11/K27/K29/K33/K48/K48R/K63/K-null plasmids were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, and ubiquitin coding sequences were further subcloned into the pcDNA3.1-Myc vector. Cells underwent co-transfection with Ub-Myc and CREB1-Flag plasmids, with or without JOSD2-HA or the catalytically inactive mutant JOSD2C24A-HA. Following a 36-h period, cells were exposed to MG132 for 6 h followed by lysis with SDS buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% SDS), followed by heating (99°C, 10 min) and dilution with IP buffer prior to immunoprecipitation, as described for Co-IP. For the in vitro deubiquitination assay, HEK293T cells underwent transfection with Ub-Myc and CREB1-Flag plasmids for 48 h before treatment with 10 μM MG132 for 6 h, and CREB1 protein was purified using anti-Flag magnetic beads. Purified JOSD2-HA or JOSD2C24A-HA was then incubated with ubiquitinated CREB1 in deubiquitination buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, pH 7.5, 10 mM ATP) at 37°C for 2 h.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Putative CREB1 binding motifs in the promoter region of JOSD2 were identified via in silico prediction using the JASPAR CORE database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/). Site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted binding sites was subsequently performed through inverse PCR. For the reporter assay, cells were co-transfected with the corresponding recombinant reporter vectors and the pRL-TK Renilla luciferase internal control plasmid. At 48 h post-transfection, firefly luciferase activity was detected using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme Biotech, China), with Renilla luciferase activity serving as the internal reference for normalization of transfection efficiency differences.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

To validate the direct binding of CREB1 to the endogenous JOSD2 promoter in vivo, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays following standard experimental procedures. Briefly, cells were lysed with ChIP lysis buffer, and formaldehyde-crosslinked chromatin-protein complexes were sheared into appropriate fragments via sonication. For the IP reaction, chromatin samples were incubated with anti-CREB1 specific antibody or normal isotype control IgG overnight at 4°C. The enrichment of the JOSD2 promoter fragment bound by CREB1 was detected via quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). The relative binding enrichment was normalized to the corresponding input DNA sample, and the primer sequences used for qPCR amplification are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Murine xenograft experiments

Four-week-old BALB/c nude mice (obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University) were subcutaneously administered 5 × 106 RCC cells per mouse (n = 6 mice per group). Tumor dimensions were measured weekly using a digital caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × a × b2, where a represents the longest tumor diameter and b represents the shortest perpendicular diameter. Tumor growth curves were plotted based on the mean tumor volume of each group over time. For metastasis assays, mice were intravenously injected with 1 × 106 RCC cells via the tail vein instead, and lung tissues were harvested 4 weeks post-injection. Metastatic burden was quantified as the total number of visible metastatic nodules in both lungs per mouse. All experimental mice were maintained in the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with temperature and humidity strictly regulated. All experimental protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Care Committee of Sun Yat-sen University, and were in compliance with both institutional regulations and the NC3Rs ARRIVE guidelines.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 was utilized for all statistical assessments. Intergroup data comparisons were performed using Student's t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas correlations were evaluated by means of Pearson correlation coefficients. Kaplan-Meier curves were applied to analyze patient survival outcomes, alongside the log-rank test for statistical validation. Quantitative data obtained from no fewer than three independent replicate experiments are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P-value of <0.05 was set as the cutoff for statistical significance (∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01).

Results

JOSD2 is a deubiquitinating enzyme associated with glucose metabolism in RCC

To clarify DUBs with potential oncogenic roles in RCC, we first interrogated transcriptomic data from the TCGA database. Comparison of tumor and matched normal tissues revealed 33 DUBs that were significantly overexpressed in RCC specimens (Fig. 1A, Table S2). To functionally screen these candidates, we generated an siRNA library targeting all 33 DUBs and individually silenced their expression in the 786-O RCC cell line. Given the close association between metabolic reprogramming and tumor progression, we simultaneously assessed cell viability and lactate production following DUB knockdown to identify candidates potentially involved in glucose metabolism. This screening revealed that suppression of 13 DUBs markedly reduced cell viability, whereas silencing of 8 DUBs led to decreased lactate production. Notably, four DUBs were found to influence both cellular viability and glycolytic output (Fig. 1B). Among these, knockdown of JOSD2 resulted in the most pronounced drop in both cell viability and lactate secretion (Fig. 1C), prompting further investigation. To confirm the differential expression of JOSD2 and determine its clinical function in RCC, we examined TCGA datasets comprising 530 RCC tumor samples and 72 adjacent normal tissues. JOSD2 expression was markedly raised in tumor relative to normal tissues (Fig. 1D). This observation was further corroborated by paired analysis of 72 matched tumor-normal samples, which consistently demonstrated higher JOSD2 expression in RCC tissues (Fig. 1E). Moreover, correlation analyses revealed that elevated JOSD2 levels were markedly linked with advanced TNM stage, higher Fuhrman grade, and reduced overall survival following surgery (Fig. 1F–H). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression established JOSD2 levels as independently predictive of RCC outcomes (Table 1). In addition, we analyzed 12 paired RCC specimens collected at our institution and confirmed robust upregulation of JOSD2 protein in tumors relative to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1I–K). Consistent expression patterns were also observed across multiple RCC cell lines (Fig. 1L–M). Collectively, these findings suggest that JOSD2 is aberrantly overexpressed in RCC and may contribute to disease progression, potentially through its involvement in metabolic regulation.

To further define the specificity of JOSD2 in regulating glycolytic reprogramming in RCC, we examined the expression and function of three additional Machado-Joseph domain (MJD) family members: ATXN3, ATXN3L, and JOSD1. Functional screening revealed that knockdown of ATXN3, ATXN3L, or JOSD1 had no effect on lactate production in 786-O cells; only ATXN3 and JOSD1 silencing caused a mild reduction in cell viability, in sharp contrast to the simultaneous and significant inhibition of both cell proliferation and glycolysis observed upon JOSD2 depletion (Fig. S1A). Analysis of 12 paired ccRCC tumor-normal tissues showed no significant differences in ATXN3 and ATXN3L expression at both mRNA and protein levels between tumor and adjacent normal tissues (Fig. S1B–H). JOSD1 was only slightly upregulated in tumors, with a much lower magnitude than JOSD2 (Fig. S1D and H). Consistent with clinical findings, expression analysis in normal HK-2 cells and five RCC cell lines revealed distinct patterns of MJD family members: ATXN3 mRNA was elevated only in 786-O cells and reduced in A498 cells, with ubiquitous but modestly variable protein expression (Fig. S1I and L); ATXN3L showed comparable mRNA levels across all lines with barely detectable protein (Fig. S1J and L); JOSD1 mRNA was upregulated only in A498 and Caki-2 cells without a consistent trend, and its protein expression showed no uniform upregulation in RCC cells relative to HK-2 (Fig. S1K and L).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that JOSD2 showed the most consistent upregulation and the strongest effect on both viability and lactate production among the tested MJD family members in RCC cells. Accordingly, subsequent experiments focused on elucidating the mechanistic role of JOSD2 in RCC glucose metabolism.

JOSD2 promotes aerobic glycolysis in RCC cells

Based on endogenous JOSD2 expression levels in various RCC cell lines (Fig. 1L–M), we selected 786-O and A498 cells for functional studies and established stable JOSD2 knockdown using lentiviral transduction. Efficient suppression of JOSD2 expression was confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 2A). Given the emerging role of JOSD2 in metabolic regulation, we hypothesized that JOSD2 modulates glycolytic activity in RCC cells. To test this hypothesis, we first quantified glucose uptake capacity, the initial rate-limiting step of glycolysis. Knockdown of JOSD2 using either shRNA significantly reduced glucose uptake in both 786-O and A498 cells compared to the non-targeting shCtrl control group (Fig. 2B–C). Consistently, lactate secretion, the primary end product of aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells, was also markedly decreased in JOSD2-depleted RCC cells (Fig. 2D–E). To further characterize the dynamic changes in glycolytic flux, we performed Seahorse extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) analysis, which allows real-time measurement of glycolytic activity. Sequential addition of glucose, the ATP synthase inhibitor oligomycin, and the glycolytic inhibitor 2-DG enabled us to evaluate basal glycolysis, maximal glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve. JOSD2 depletion significantly diminished overall glycolytic flux, with both basal glycolysis (measured after glucose addition) and maximal glycolytic capacity (measured after oligomycin treatment) being markedly reduced in 786-O and A498 cells (Fig. 2F–I). In contrast to the reduced glycolytic phenotype, JOSD2 knockdown resulted in a significant increase in oxygen consumption rate (OCR), a key indicator of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Both basal respiration and maximal respiratory capacity were elevated in JOSD2 knockdown cells compared to shCtrl controls (Fig. 2J–M), suggesting that loss of JOSD2 induces a metabolic shift away from aerobic glycolysis towards mitochondrial OXPHOS.

To directly confirm this metabolic reprogramming and trace the fate of glucose carbon in living cells, we performed stable isotope tracing experiments using uniformly labeled 13C6-glucose. As illustrated in the metabolic pathway schematic (Figs. 2N), 13C6-glucose is metabolized through glycolysis to produce M3-labeled lactate and M3-labeled pyruvate. A portion of pyruvate is then converted to acetyl-CoA via oxidative decarboxylation, which enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to generate M2-labeled TCA intermediates. In both 786-O and A498 cells, JOSD2 knockdown significantly reduced the M3 isotopic enrichment of lactate and pyruvate (Fig. 2O–Q), providing direct biochemical evidence for decreased glycolytic flux. Conversely, the M2 isotopic enrichment of all measured TCA cycle intermediates, including citrate, cis-aconitate, α-ketoglutarate, succinate, and fumarate, was significantly reduced in JOSD2-depleted cells (Fig. 2P–R), demonstrating that glucose carbon flux is redirected from glycolysis into the mitochondrial TCA cycle upon JOSD2 silencing.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that JOSD2 is a critical positive regulator of aerobic glycolysis in RCC cells, and its depletion induces a metabolic switch from glycolysis to mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.

JOSD2 facilitates RCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Based on the relationship between JOSD2 expression, metabolic reprogramming, and poor clinical outcomes, we next investigated whether JOSD2 directly influences malignant phenotypes in RCC cells. CCK-8 assays showed that shRNA-mediated JOSD2 silencing significantly inhibited the time-dependent proliferative activity of 786-O and A498 cells, with the most prominent suppression at 96 h (Fig. 3A–B). Colony formation assays further revealed that JOSD2 knockdown markedly reduced the long-term clonogenic capacity of both RCC cell lines (Fig. 3C). EdU incorporation assays consistently confirmed that JOSD2 silencing significantly decreased the proportion of EdU-positive proliferating cells in 786-O and A498 cells (Fig. 3D–E). Collectively, these three independent assays demonstrated that JOSD2 depletion substantially impairs RCC cell proliferation.

We next assessed the effect of JOSD2 on RCC cell motility. Wound healing assays showed that JOSD2 knockdown significantly delayed wound closure in 786-O and A498 cells, indicating impaired horizontal migratory ability (Fig. 3G–H). Transwell migration assays further confirmed that JOSD2 silencing markedly suppressed the directional chemotactic migration of RCC cells (Fig. 3I and J). Matrigel-coated transwell invasion assays revealed that JOSD2 depletion also significantly reduced the extracellular matrix invasion capacity of both RCC cell lines (Fig. 3I and K).

Collectively, our data indicate that JOSD2 promotes RCC tumorigenesis by enhancing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

JOSD2 interacts with CREB1 and positively regulates CREB1 protein levels

The diverse biological functions of DUB are largely dictated by their substrate specificity. To elucidate the molecular mechanism through which JOSD2 promotes RCC progression and metabolic reprogramming, we sought to identify proteins interacting with JOSD2. Immunoprecipitation was performed in 786-O cells expressing HA-tagged JOSD2, followed by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. A prominent protein band of approximately 40 kDa was detected (Fig. 4A), excised, and subjected to mass spectrometry analysis. A total of 43 potential JOSD2-interacting proteins were identified in this assay. The complete list of these candidates is provided in Table S3. Notably, CREB1 emerged as a key candidate interactor in this analysis (Table S3, Fig. 4B).

To validate this interaction, endogenous co-immunoprecipitation assays were conducted in 786-O and A498 cells. JOSD2 and CREB1 were found to reciprocally co-precipitate, whereas no interaction was observed with control IgG, confirming an endogenous association between the two proteins (Fig. 4C). These interactions were further confirmed by exogenous co-IP in HEK293T cells co-transfected with Flag-JOSD2 and HA-CREB1 constructs, in which robust reciprocal pull-down was observed (Fig. 4D). To determine whether the interaction between JOSD2 and CREB1 is direct, GST pull-down assays were performed. These experiments demonstrated that JOSD2 directly binds to CREB1 in the absence of additional cellular components (Fig. 4E). Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated co-localization of JOSD2 and CREB1 in 786-O and A498 cells, mostly in the nucleus (Fig. 4F). Finally, to map the interaction domains, a series of truncation mutants of JOSD2 and CREB1 were generated and analyzed by co-immunoprecipitation in HEK293T cells. These experiments revealed that the Josephin-associated (JAB) domain of JOSD2 (aa 81-180) mediates direct binding to the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domain of CREB1 (aa 280-341) (Fig. 4G–H). These results establish CREB1 as a direct interacting partner of JOSD2 and provide a molecular basis for subsequent functional regulation.

To clarify whether JOSD2 influences the expression pattern of CREB1, we performed qRT-PCR and Western blotting. These experiments revealed that depletion of JOSD2 markedly reduced CREB1 protein abundance, whereas CREB1 mRNA levels remained largely unchanged (Fig. 4I–J). This discrepancy suggested that JOSD2 may regulate CREB1 predominantly at the post-transcriptional level by modulating protein stability rather than transcription. To investigate this possibility, RCC cells with stable JOSD2 knockdown were exposed to cycloheximide (CHX) to prevent de novo synthesis of proteins, enabling assessment of CREB1 protein turnover over time. As shown in Fig. 4K–L, loss of JOSD2 substantially reduced the CREB1 protein half-life relative to the controls, indicating accelerated degradation in the absence of JOSD2. These findings imply that JOSD2 enhances CREB1 stability by suppressing its degradation. To further delineate the degradation pathway involved, JOSD2-depleted cells were treated with inhibitors, namely, MG132 (proteasomes) or chloroquine (lysosomes). Notably, MG132 treatment fully rescued the reduction in CREB1 protein levels caused by JOSD2 knockdown, whereas chloroquine had no such effect (Fig. 4M). These results support a model in which JOSD2 stabilizes CREB1 by preventing proteasome-dependent degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Given that JOSD2 functions as a DUB, we next examined whether its enzymatic activity is required for CREB1 stabilization. Previous studies have identified cysteine 24 as a critical residue for JOSD2 catalytic function, and mutation of this residue (C24A) partially abolishes its deubiquitinating activity [17]. Consistent with this, ectopic expression of wild-type JOSD2 significantly increased CREB1 protein levels, whereas the catalytically inactive JOSD2C24A mutant failed to do so (Fig. 4N). These data indicate that the ability of JOSD2 to stabilize CREB1 depends on its deubiquitinating activity.

JOSD2 removes K48-linked polyUb from CREB1

To directly test whether JOSD2 stabilizes CREB1 through its deubiquitinase activity, we performed a series of cell-based and in vitro deubiquitination assays. All ubiquitination experiments were conducted in cells treated with 10 μM MG132 for 6 h prior to harvest to inhibit proteasomal degradation and enrich for ubiquitinated CREB1 protein. Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed using anti-Flag antibody for exogenous CREB1-Flag or anti-CREB1 antibody for endogenous CREB1, followed by immunoblotting (IB) with anti-Myc antibody (for tagged ubiquitin) or anti-ubiquitin antibody. Quantitative analysis of band intensities from at least three independent experiments is provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. First, in HEK293T cells co-transfected with CREB1-Flag and Myc-Ub, knockdown of endogenous JOSD2 using two independent shRNAs significantly increased the polyubiquitination level of CREB1 (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Conversely, ectopic expression of wild-type JOSD2-HA markedly reduced CREB1 polyubiquitination, whereas the catalytically inactive JOSD2C24A-HA mutant completely lacked this deubiquitinating activity (Fig. 5B; Fig. S2B). Critically, to confirm the biological relevance of this finding in the disease context of RCC, we performed endogenous ubiquitination assays in 786-O RCC cells. Consistent with the results in HEK293T cells, stable knockdown of JOSD2 in 786-O cells significantly elevated the polyubiquitination level of endogenous CREB1 (Fig. 5C; Fig. S2C). Furthermore, overexpression of wild-type JOSD2-HA, but not the JOSD2C24A-HA mutant, significantly decreased endogenous CREB1 polyubiquitination in 786-O cells (Fig. 5D; Fig. S2D). These results demonstrate that JOSD2 directly deubiquitinates CREB1 in RCC cells in a catalytic activity-dependent manner.

To further confirm that JOSD2 directly mediates CREB1 deubiquitination, we performed cell-free in vitro deubiquitination assays using purified proteins. Briefly, JOSD2-HA and the catalytically inactive JOSD2C24A-HA were individually expressed in HEK293T cells and purified using anti-HA magnetic beads. Ubiquitinated CREB1-Flag was obtained by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with CREB1-Flag and Myc-Ub, followed by purification with anti-Flag magnetic beads. Western blotting analysis revealed that wild-type JOSD2 efficiently removed polyubiquitin chains from CREB1 in vitro, whereas the catalytically inactive JOSD2C24A mutant had no deubiquitinating activity (Fig. 5F; Fig. S2E).

To determine the specific type of ubiquitin chain linkage targeted by JOSD2 on CREB1, we used a panel of ubiquitin mutants that retain only a single lysine residue (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, or K63) or have all lysines mutated to arginine (K-null). As shown in Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. S2F, JOSD2 selectively and efficiently removed K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from CREB1, but had no significant effect on polyubiquitin chains formed through any other lysine residue. This specificity was further confirmed using the K48R ubiquitin mutant, in which only lysine 48 is mutated to arginine, preventing the formation of K48-linked chains. The ability of JOSD2 to deubiquitinate CREB1 was completely abolished when the K48R ubiquitin mutant was used (Fig. 5H; Supplementary Fig. S2G). These results demonstrate that JOSD2 stabilizes CREB1 by specific removal of K48-linked polyUb, thereby blocking its proteasomal degradation.

CREB1 positively regulates JOSD2 transcription

CREB1 is known to modulate the expression of thousands of genes in eukaryotic cells [18]. To explore downstream targets of CREB1 in RCC, we performed bioinformatic predictions to identify genes potentially regulated by CREB1. Unexpectedly, JOSD2 emerged as a candidate CREB1 target (Fig. 6A). This prompted us to examine whether CREB1 directly regulates JOSD2 expression. Strikingly, knockdown of CREB1 in RCC cells led to marked reductions in JOSD2 mRNA and protein (Fig. 6B–C), suggesting transcriptional regulation. Analysis of the JOSD2 promoter using the JASPAR database identified three putative CREB1-binding sites: region 1 (−741 to −752), region 2 (−838 to −849), and region 3 (−1889 to −1990) relative to the transcription start site (Fig. 6D–E). We first performed dual-luciferase reporter assays in HEK293T cells, which are widely used for reporter gene studies due to their high transfection efficiency. Mutation of the R1 binding site almost completely abolished CREB1-induced JOSD2 promoter activity, while mutations in R2 or R3 had only minimal effects (Fig. 6F). Critically, to confirm the physiological relevance of this transcriptional regulation in RCC, we repeated the dual-luciferase reporter assays in both 786-O and A498 RCC cell lines. Consistent with the results in HEK293T cells, mutation of the R1 site completely abrogated CREB1-mediated activation of the JOSD2 promoter in both RCC cell lines, whereas mutations in R2 and R3 did not significantly affect promoter activity (Fig. 6G–H). These results demonstrate that the R1 region is the primary functional CREB1 binding site in the JOSD2 promoter. To further verify that CREB1 directly binds to the JOSD2 promoter in vivo in RCC cells, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays in 786-O and A498 cells. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that CREB1 was significantly enriched at the R1 region of the JOSD2 promoter, but not at the R2 or R3 regions, in both cell lines (Fig. 6I–J). Together, these findings establish JOSD2 as a direct transcriptional target of CREB1.

CREB1 promotes the transcriptional expression of key glycolytic enzymes in RCC

Previous studies in other cancer types, including colon cancer, have shown that CREB1 directly binds to the promoters of glycolytic genes such as GLUT1, HK2, and PFKP, thereby promoting glycolysis and tumor progression [[19], [20], [21]]. We therefore investigated whether CREB1 exerts similar regulatory effects in RCC. Consistent with this hypothesis, silencing CREB1 in RCC cells significantly reduced the mRNA and protein levels of GLUT1, HK2, and PFKP (Fig. S3A–C). These results indicate that CREB1 transcriptionally activates key glycolytic enzymes in RCC, thereby facilitating glycolytic reprogramming.

CREB1 overexpression reverses the metabolic reprogramming induced by JOSD2 knockdown in RCC cells

To confirm that the metabolic regulatory functions of JOSD2 are mediated through CREB1, we performed functional rescue experiments by ectopically expressing CREB1 in JOSD2-depleted 786-O and A498 RCC cells. Western blotting analysis confirmed efficient knockdown of endogenous JOSD2 and successful re-expression of CREB1 in the rescue groups (Fig. 7A). First, we examined whether restoration of CREB1 expression could reverse the glycolytic defects caused by JOSD2 silencing. As shown in Fig. 7B–E, JOSD2 knockdown significantly reduced glucose uptake and lactate secretion in both 786-O and A498 cells, and these effects were partially rescued by CREB1 overexpression. Consistent with these findings, Seahorse ECAR analysis demonstrated that the decreased basal glycolysis and maximal glycolytic capacity in JOSD2-deficient cells were significantly restored upon CREB1 re-expression (Fig. 7F–I). Next, we investigated the effect of CREB1 rescue on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. JOSD2 knockdown resulted in increased oxygen consumption rates (OCR), including both basal and maximal respiration, as previously observed. Importantly, ectopic expression of CREB1 partially reversed this metabolic shift, reducing OCR levels back toward those of control cells (Fig. 7J–M).

CREB1 overexpression reverses the impaired proliferation, migration and invasion induced by JOSD2 knockdown in RCC cells

Having established that JOSD2 regulates metabolic reprogramming in a CREB1-dependent manner, we next investigated whether the oncogenic phenotypes induced by JOSD2 are also mediated through CREB1. First, we evaluated the effect of CREB1 rescue on cell proliferation. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that JOSD2 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferative capacity of both 786-O and A498 cells, and this inhibition was partially reversed by CREB1 overexpression (Fig. 8A–B). Consistent with these findings, colony formation assays showed that the reduced clonogenic potential in JOSD2-deficient cells was significantly restored upon CREB1 re-expression (Fig. 8C–D). Furthermore, EdU incorporation assays revealed that the decreased percentage of EdU-positive cells caused by JOSD2 silencing was effectively rescued by CREB1 overexpression (Fig. 8E–F). Next, we assessed the effect of CREB1 rescue on cell motility. Wound healing assays indicated that JOSD2 knockdown markedly delayed wound closure in both cell lines, and this migratory defect was partially rescued by CREB1 overexpression (Fig. 8G–H). Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays further confirmed that the suppressed migratory and invasive capacities in JOSD2-depleted cells were significantly restored upon CREB1 re-expression (Fig. 8I–K). These results demonstrate that restoration of CREB1 expression is sufficient to reverse the impaired proliferation, migration and invasion induced by JOSD2 knockdown.

JOSD2 promotes RCC progression through CREB1 in vivo

To verify the cellular findings, mouse models were used to examine the function of JOSD2 in RCC progression. Nude mice were given subcutaneous injections of RCC cells harboring stable JOSD2 knockdown. Compared with control tumors, JOSD2-deficient xenografts exhibited substantially reduced tumor volumes and weights (Fig. 9A–C). Immunohistochemical analysis of 786-O xenografts confirmed reduced JOSD2 expression, accompanied by decreased CREB1 protein levels and Ki-67 proliferation index in JOSD2-deficient tumors (Fig. 9D–E), consistent with impaired proliferation. Additionally, measurement of intratumoral lactate levels revealed a significant reduction in lactic acid content following JOSD2 knockdown (Fig. 9F), indicating suppressed glycolytic activity in vivo. To assess metastatic potential, a tail vein injection model was employed. As shown in Fig. 9G, silencing JOSD2 substantially diminished metastatic burden in nude mice. Collectively, these in vivo data support our findings and show that JOSD2 promotes RCC growth and metastasis by stabilizing and activating CREB1.

JOSD2 and CREB1 are concomitantly overexpressed in ccRCC specimens

To evaluate the clinical utility of JOSD2 and CREB1 levels in ccRCC, we performed IHC staining using a previously established ccRCC tissue microarray cohort [22]. This cohort included 90 ccRCC tumor samples paired with adjacent normal renal tissues. JOSD2 levels were substantially raised in tumor tissues relative to their normal counterparts (Fig. S4A). To further assess the relationship between JOSD2 and CREB1 in clinical samples, staining intensities for both proteins were stratified into low- and high-expression cohorts according to the median H-score, following established scoring criteria. Notably, tumors exhibiting low JOSD2 expression were predominantly associated with weak CREB1 staining. In contrast, samples with high JOSD2 expression showed a significantly greater proportion of strong CREB1 positivity (Fig. S4B–C). Together, these findings demonstrate a strong positive correlation between JOSD2 and CREB1 expression in ccRCC, supporting the clinical relevance of the JOSD2-CREB1 axis in this disease.

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming, especially the aberrant activation of glycolysis, is widely recognized as a defining feature of malignant transformation. Many cancer types rely on enhanced glycolytic flux, characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate production, to support anabolic biosynthesis and sustain rapid proliferation. In RCC, elevated glycolysis has been closely linked to aggressive tumor growth, greater metastatic potential, and poor patient prognosis [23,24]. Despite growing interest in cancer metabolism, the regulatory networks governing these metabolic alterations have not been elucidated. Here, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism linking deubiquitination, transcriptional regulation, and glycolytic reprogramming, thereby expanding current understanding of metabolic control in RCC.

JOSD2 is a member of the MJD subfamily of DUBs, which also includes Ataxin-3, Ataxin-3L, and JOSD1 [25]. JOSD2 has been found to be involved in tumorigenic processes. For example, JOSD2 has been shown to promote cholangiocarcinoma progression by deubiquitinating and stabilizing YAP/TAZ, thereby preventing their proteasomal degradation [14]. Additionally, Huang et al. found that JOSD2 interacts with β-catenin and reduces its polyubiquitination, resulting in activation of Wnt signaling [15]. However, the biological role and mechanistic contributions of JOSD2 in RCC had remained largely unexplored. Our findings demonstrate that elevated JOSD2 expression is linked to worse overall survival in RCC patients, further highlighting the oncogenic potential of this MJD family member. Consistent with previous reports identifying cysteine 24 (C24) as a critical catalytic residue [26], the present results confirm that this site is essential for the deubiquitinating activity of JOSD2. Importantly, this catalytic residue may represent a viable target for the development of JOSD2-specific inhibitors.

Mechanistically, JOSD2 was found to exert its tumor-promoting effects by interacting with and stabilizing CREB1, a transcription factor with well-established functions in glucose metabolism. CREB1 has previously been reported to undergo ubiquitin-mediated regulation [[27], [28], [29]], but the identity of the DUB responsible for this process in RCC had not been defined. To our knowledge, these data identify JOSD2 as a CREB1-associated deubiquitinase in RCC and demonstrate that this interaction is dependent on the catalytic activity of JOSD2 at C24. Furthermore, our ubiquitin linkage analyses reveal that JOSD2 preferentially removes K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from CREB1, rather than other types of ubiquitin chains. This specificity is mechanistically important, as K48-linked ubiquitination serves as the canonical signal for proteasomal degradation, whereas other linkage types, such as K63, are more commonly associated with non-proteolytic signaling functions [30,31].

An important finding is that of a reciprocal regulatory relationship between JOSD2 and CREB1. CREB1 directly binds to the JOSD2 promoter and transcriptionally upregulates its expression, while JOSD2, in turn, stabilizes CREB1 by preventing its proteasomal degradation. This bidirectional regulation contributes to enhanced expression of glycolytic genes and promotes a pro-tumorigenic cellular state. Similar bidirectional regulatory mechanisms have been described in other oncogenic pathways, such as hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma, where they contribute to signal robustness and disease progression [32]. The strong positive correlation between JOSD2 and CREB1 protein expression in patient-derived samples underscores the pathological relevance of this regulatory axis in human RCC.

From a therapeutic standpoint, the JOSD2-CREB1 reciprocal regulatory axis represents an attractive and previously unrecognized target in RCC. The intrinsic resistance of RCC to conventional chemotherapy and the frequent emergence of resistance to targeted therapies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors, highlight the need for alternative treatment strategies. Targeting JOSD2, particularly its catalytic activity, could disrupt this bidirectional regulatory circuit, attenuating oncogenic transcriptional programs and partially reversing glycolytic metabolic reprogramming. Our in vivo findings, demonstrating that JOSD2 depletion suppresses tumor growth and metastasis, provide compelling preclinical support for this approach.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while our core findings have been rigorously validated in two independent and representative RCC cell lines, future studies extending these observations to additional RCC cell lines, patient-derived organoids, and genetically engineered mouse models would further strengthen the generalizability of our conclusions across diverse RCC molecular subtypes. Second, the E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for CREB1 ubiquitination in RCC has not yet been identified, and defining this enzyme would complete the regulatory framework governing CREB1 stability.Third, although our data establish CREB1 as a key transcriptional regulator of JOSD2, other transcription factors may also contribute to JOSD2 overexpression in RCC and should be explored in further investigations. Finally, additional work is required to assess the therapeutic efficacy and safety of pharmacological JOSD2 inhibition in advanced and clinically relevant RCC models.

In summary, the results of this study highlight a central role for the JOSD2-CREB1 reciprocal regulatory axis in driving RCC progression by promoting aerobic glycolysis and enhancing malignant phenotypes (Fig. 10). These results indicate that JOSD2 is both a prognostic biomarker and a novel vulnerability for treating RCC, offering new opportunities to improve outcomes for individuals with this disease.

Proposed mechanistic model illustrating how the JOSD2-CREB1 reciprocal regulatory axis promotes RCC progression by enhancing aerobic glycolysis and malignant phenotypes. The deubiquitinase JOSD2 directly interacts with the transcription factor CREB1 and prevents its proteasomal degradation by removing K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, thereby stabilizing the CREB1 protein. Meanwhile, CREB1 transcriptionally upregulates JOSD2 by binding to its promoter, forming a reciprocal regulatory circuit. Reciprocal activation between JOSD2 and CREB1 leads to increased expression of key glycolytic regulators, including GLUT1, HK2, and PFKP, thereby driving metabolic reprogramming and tumor progression in RCC cells.
