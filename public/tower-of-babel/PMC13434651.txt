Intermediate accumulated upon interruption of fatty acid oxidation flux promotes tumoral ferroptosis and improves immunotherapy
Authors: Yuhan Zhou, Jing Li, Fangfang Liu, Yuan Gao, Songlin Yin, Liguo Yang, Xiaoxiao Li, Junhong Lin, Yan Li, Haotian Shang, Xiang Cheng, Tengfei Chao, Qian Chu, Fujia Lu, Weimin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic strategies targeting cancer metabolism are advancing rapidly. However, perturbing distinct nodes within the same metabolic pathway often yields divergent outcomes. Ferroptosis, a metabolic cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has garnered attention for potentiating antitumor immunity. Here, we demonstrate that interruption of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) at hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADHA) node promotes tumoral ferroptosis, whereas targeting upstream enzymes does not. HADHA inhibition causes accumulation of hydroxylated C18 (C18-OH) acylcarnitine to exacerbate mitochondrial lipid peroxidation. In vivo, HADHA ablation or acylcarnitine C18-OH supplementation suppresses tumor growth, enhances antitumor T-cell immunity, and potentiates PD-1 blockade therapy. Clinically, elevated plasma acylcarnitine C18-OH correlates with improved prognosis and immunotherapy response in lung cancer patients. Trimetazidine, an approved anti-ischemic drug and HADHA inhibitor, similarly delays tumor progression and augments immunotherapy. Together, our findings identify HADHA as a ferroptosis regulator and offer a clinically actionable strategy to enhance ferroptosis and immunotherapy through metabolic intervention.Subject terms: Cancer metabolism, Cell death, Tumour immunology

Introduction

Ferroptosis is a form of lytic cell death triggered by the accumulation of lipid peroxidation on cellular membranes1,2. It has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in oncology, with the potential to not only eliminate tumor cells directly, but also augment the therapeutic efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. Initial studies demonstrated that CD8+ T cells promote tumor cells ferroptosis by releasing IFNγ, which suppresses cystine uptake3 or promotes arachidonic acid (AA) incorporation into membrane phospholipids4. Subsequent evidences suggest that ferroptosis possesses features of immunogenic cell death (ICD). Inducing ferroptosis of tumor cells can promote the activation and maturation of dendritic cells, thereby amplifying antitumor T cell response5. Concurrently, strategies that target ferroptosis in preclinical models have been demonstrated to enhance antitumor T-cell immunity and potentiate checkpoint blockade therapy. These strategies include the use of cyst(e)inase to degrade cystine and cysteine3, treatment with imidazole ketone erastin (IKE)6, low doses of RSL37, dietary supplementation of AA4 or intermittent dietary deprivation of methionine6. However, the clinical translation of ferroptosis as a therapeutic target for cancer is still in its infancy, with no approved agents designed to induce tumoral ferroptosis. Furthermore, due to the limited repertoire of druggable targets within the ferroptotic pathway, the development of ferroptosis-targeting therapy has reached a plateau8,9. Therefore, there are unmet needs to identify novel ferroptosis regulators and to screen for new molecules or to repurpose clinically approved drugs, which have the potential to induce tumoral ferroptosis.

Tumor sensitivity to ferroptosis is intricately modulated by oncogenic signaling and metabolic rewiring10,11. Mitochondria serve as hubs for reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, iron metabolism, and energy production via oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and FAO12,13. Recent studies revealed that endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondria contact sites (EMCS) are initial sites of phospholipids peroxide formation to promote ferroptosis14. Moreover, both the TCA cycle and electron transport chain (ETC) have been implicated in cysteine-deprivation-induced ferroptosis15. However, as the primary catabolic pathway for long-chain fatty acids, the specific role of FAO in ferroptosis remains poorly understood. FAO initiates with the mitochondrial import of fatty acids by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1), which are subsequently reconverted to acyl-CoAs by CPT2 and degraded through β-oxidation. This multistep process is catalyzed by enzymes including long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD) and the mitochondrial trifunctional protein (MTP), which is composed of subunit alpha (HADHA) and subunit beta (HADHB)16,17. Previous reports have shown that inhibiting CPT1A18 or DECR119, an auxiliary FAO enzyme that facilitates the reduction of 2, 4-dienoyl-CoA, can promote tumoral ferroptosis. However, the impact of other FAO enzymes on ferroptosis and their underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Theoretically, inhibiting upstream enzymes blocks FAO initiation, whereas targeting downstream enzymes, such as HADHA, may interrupt metabolic flux and trigger the accumulation of toxic intermediates.

Here, we reveal a unique ferroptosis sensitization effect upon the inhibition of HADHA, distinguishing it from other FAO enzymes. We find that the enzymatic activity of HADHA is elevated by intracellular spermidine at the early stage of ferroptosis to serve as a cellular protective checkpoint. HADHA ablation disrupts FAO flux, resulting in the accumulation of the intermediate metabolite acylcarnitine C18-OH, which can sensitize tumor cells to ferroptosis. Furthermore, loss of HADHA, administration of acylcarnitine C18-OH or treatment with HADHA inhibitor trimetazidine (TMZ) can promote tumor cell ferroptosis, thereby enhancing antitumor T-cell immunity and augmenting checkpoint blockade therapy. Our study shed light on the regulatory role of HADHA in the ferroptosis sensitivity, reveals a previous unknown function of acylcarnitine C18-OH, and proposes a viable strategy to target tumoral ferroptosis and potentiate immunotherapy by utilizing TMZ.

Results

Interruption of FAO flux at HADHA node promotes tumoral ferroptosis

To investigate how perturbation of FAO affects ferroptosis, we systematically knocked down genes encoding key FAO enzymes in B16F0 mouse melanoma cells (Fig. 1a), including Cpt1a, Cpt2, Acadl, HADHA and HADHB (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We then evaluated the sensitivity of these cells to RSL3, a classical ferroptosis inducer20. Knockdown of upstream enzymes Cpt1a (Fig. 1b), Cpt2 (Fig. 1c) or Acadl (Fig. 1d) had minimal impact on RSL3-induced cell death. In contrast, knockdown of either HADHA or HADHB significantly increased ferroptosis sensitivity, as evidenced by decreased cell viability (Fig. 1e, f), enhanced cell death (Fig. 1g) and lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1h, i). Similarly, in TDCL and Panc02 mouse tumor cell lines, HADHA or HADHB knockdown could also promote RSL3-induced cell death and lipid peroxidation (Supplementary Fig. 1b- e). To further reinforce our findings, we used CRISPR-Cas9 approach to generate HADHA knockout B16F0 cells (Fig. 1j). Consistently, HADHA knockout markedly enhanced cellular sensitivity to RSL3-induced ferroptosis, revealed by reduced cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 1f), increased cell death (Fig. 1k) and lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1l). Notably, both cell death and lipid peroxidation could be completely reversed by the specific ferroptosis inhibitor deferoxamine (DFO) and ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 1k, l), but not by the pan-caspase inhibitor IDN-6556 and necroptosis inhibitor GSK872 (Fig. 1k). Moreover, HADHA deficiency potentiated ferroptosis induced by other agents such as ML210 (Supplementary Fig. 1g) and Erastin (Supplementary Fig. 1h). In addition, we tested whether HADHA inhibition could sensitize human tumor cells to ferroptosis. We knocked down HADHA in human A549 lung carcinoma cells (Fig. 1m), and observed increased death (Fig. 1n) and lipid peroxidation level (Fig. 1o) of these cells in response to RSL3. Together, these results indicate that disruption of FAO at the HADHA node, but not upstream, selectively sensitizes both murine and human tumor cells to ferroptosis.

Next, we asked whether HADHA confers ferroptosis resistance via its enzymatic activity. The most frequent pathogenic mutation in HADHA is a c.1528 G > C substitution (E510Q) in exon 15, which abolishes its dehydrogenase function21 and causes long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHADD), a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder22,23. We constructed the HADHA E510Q mutant and reintroduced it or the wildtype (WT) HADHA into HADHA-deficient B16F0 cells (Fig. 1p). The enzymatic activity assay confirmed that only HADHA WT, but not the E510Q mutant, restored NADH-generating activity (Fig. 1q and Supplementary Fig. 1i). Correspondingly, re-expression of HADHA WT, but not the mutant rescued HADHA-deficient cells from RSL3-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 1r). These data indicate that the ferroptosis-regulatory role of HADHA depends on its enzymatic activity.

HADHA enzymatic activity is elevated by spermidine during ferroptosis

Considering the unique ferroptosis sensitization observed upon HADHA inhibition, we hypothesized that HADHA may function as a metabolic checkpoint restraining ferroptosis execution. Upon RSL3 treatment, HADHA enzymatic activity rapidly increased in B16F0 cells (Fig. 2a). However, neither HADHA mRNA nor protein levels were altered by RSL3 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), suggesting that the increase of enzymatic activity may be regulated at the post-translational level. Recent studies have shown that intracellular spermidine (SPD) can bind to HADHA and HADHB and enhance their enzymatic activities24. Moreover, intracellular SPD level was found to rise during RSL3-induced ferroptosis25. Based on these findings, we speculated that SPD might be the mediator to enhance the enzymatic activity of HADHA upon ferroptosis induction. By quantifying the intracellular content of SPD in cell lysates using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Supplementary Fig. 2c), we confirmed that RSL3 treatment led to a rapid accumulation of intracellular SPD in B16F0 cells (Fig. 2b). Exogenous SPD supplementation further increased intracellular SPD levels (Fig. 2c), and concomitantly enhanced mitochondrial HADHA enzymatic activity (Fig. 2d). To further explore the functional consequences of SPD in ferroptosis, we pretreated B16F0 cells with exogenous SPD followed by RSL3 treatment. Exogenous SPD significantly suppressed RSL3-induced cell death in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2e), and markedly reduced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 2f). Notably, this protective effect of SPD was abolished in HADHA-deficient and HADHB-knockdown cells (Fig. 2g), indicating that SPD mitigates ferroptosis through a HADHA/HADHB-dependent mechanism. We next sought to decrease intracellular SPD levels by targeting the polyamine biosynthesis pathway. Ornithine decarboxylase 1 (ODC1), the rate-limiting enzyme in this pathway, catalyzes the conversion of ornithine to putrescine26. Treatment with difluoromethylornithine (DFMO), an irreversible ODC1 inhibitor, significantly lowered intracellular SPD content (Fig. 2h) and enhanced RSL3-induced ferroptosis in B16F0 cells (Fig. 2i). In addition, spermidine synthase (SRM) carries out the final step of the pathway by converting putrescine to SPD27. Knockdown of SRM using two independent shRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 2d) effectively reduced the abundance of intracellular SPD (Fig. 2j), and sensitized B16F0 cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 2k). Overall, these results indicate that HADHA enzymatic activity is elevated by intracellular SPD during ferroptotic stress, thereby functioning as an adaptive mechanism to suppress ferroptosis.

Acylcarnitine C18-OH accumulated from HADHA inhibition confers to ferroptosis sensitization

We then tried to elucidate the mechanisms by which HADHA ablation enhances ferroptosis sensitivity. Since HADHA functions downstream in the FAO cascade, its loss is expected to interrupt metabolic flux and potentially lead to the accumulation of toxic intermediates. We therefore tested whether HADHA ablation-mediated effect depends on fatty acid influx into the FAO pathway. We knocked down upstream FAO enzymes Cpt1a, Cpt2 and Acadl in both wildtype and HADHA deficient B16F0 cells. As expected, HADHA deficiency promoted RSL3-induced ferroptosis in cells expressing scramble shRNA (Supplementary Fig. 3a). However, this sensitization effect was largely abolished by co-knockdown of CPT1a, CPT2 or LCAD (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). These results suggest that fatty acid entry into the FAO pathway is necessary for HADHA-mediated ferroptosis sensitization.

To identify the potential intermediate metabolites, we performed LC-MS/MS-based lipidomic profiling of mitochondria isolated from HADHA-deficient B16F0 cells reconstituted with either wild-type HADHA or empty vector. Through categorizing different subclasses of lipids, we found the most significant difference in acylcarnitine, whose accumulation was pronounced in mitochondria from HADHA deficient cells (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Notably, volcano plot analysis revealed that hydroxylated C18 (C18-OH) acylcarnitine was the most significantly enriched metabolite among 1,143 lipid species, except for other two acylcarnitine species C18:1-OH and C16-OH, which were undetectable in HADHA-expressing control cells (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 3f). We next assessed whether acylcarnitine C18-OH functionally regulates ferroptosis. Treatment with lower concentration of exogenous C18-OH (5-20 μM) did not impair the viability of B16F0 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3g), but significantly enhanced RSL3-induced cell death (Fig. 3c). An even stronger sensitizing effect was observed in human A549 cells in response to the combined treatment of IKE and RSL3 (Supplementary Fig. 3h, l) or to another GPX4 inhibitor ML162 (Fig. 3d). The lipidomic data also revealed the accumulation of non-hydroxylated acylcarnitine C18:0, but its supplementation exerted less potence to sensitize B16F0 and A549 cells to ferroptosis inducers compared with the effect of acylcarnitine C18-OH (Supplementary Fig. 3j–m), highlighting the functional importance of the hydroxyl group. These results suggest that HADHA deficiency drives acylcarnitine C18-OH accumulation, which in turn promotes ferroptosis.

Given that FAO occurs in mitochondria, which is the primary site for ROS generation and lipid peroxidation initiation. We first examined whether acylcarnitine C18-OH exacerbates mitochondrial oxidative stress. HADHA knockout (Fig. 3e) or knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b) could both increase the level of mitochondrial ROS in B16F0, Panc02 and A549 cells upon RSL3 treatment. Likewise, exogenous acylcarnitine C18-OH elevated mitochondrial ROS level in RSL3-treated B16F0 cells (Fig. 3f). Next, staining for mitochondrial lipid peroxidation showed significantly higher signals in HADHA-deficient cells than in wild-type controls (Supplementary Fig. 4c- e). And acylcarnitine C18-OH further augmented this mitochondrial lipid peroxidation under ferroptotic stress (Fig. 3g, h). Consequently, the overall lipid peroxidation was also exacerbated by this intermediate (Fig. 3i). Acylcarnitine C18-OH contains a hydroxyl group, which could be more reactive to facilitate phospholipid peroxidation. To test this, we incorporated acylcarnitine C18-OH into egg phosphatidylcholine liposomes loaded with the fluorescent lipid peroxidation probe STY-BODIPY28. Fluorescence-based quantification revealed that acylcarnitine C18-OH incorporation markedly increased the peroxidation of egg phosphatidylcholine, which were completely blocked by ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 3j). These data suggest that acylcarnitine C18-OH can actively promote lipid peroxidation initiated from mitochondria.

To further validate this mechanism, we sought to neutralize mitochondrial oxidative stress in HADHA-deficient cells. Treatment with mitoquinone (MitoQ), a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, completely blocked the induction of lipid peroxidation (Supplementary Fig. 4f) and ferroptotic cell death (Fig. 3k) in both RSL3-treated WT and HADHA deficient cells. Similarly, ectopic expression of mitochondria-localized GPX4 (mito-GPX4) (Fig. 3l) abrogated RSL3-induced lipid peroxidation (Supplementary Fig. 4g) and subsequent cell death (Fig. 3m) that were all promoted by HADHA deficiency. Collectively, these results indicate that HADHA loss leads to mitochondrial accumulation of acylcarnitine C18-OH, which enhances mitochondrial lipid peroxidation and sensitizes tumor cells to ferroptosis.

HADHA loss suppresses tumor growth in vivo

Ferroptosis-targeting strategies have demonstrated antitumor efficacy across various cancer models3,4,6,18. To evaluate the impact of HADHA deficiency on tumor growth in an immunocompetent setting, we subcutaneously inoculated HADHA deficient B16F0 cells into C57BL/6 mice. The growth of HADHA deficient tumors was significantly suppressed compared to wildtype tumors, as evidenced by reductions in both tumor volume and weight (Fig. 4a, b). To further test if HADHA deficiency impact ferroptosis vulnerability of tumor cells in vivo, we assess the level of lipid peroxidation in single cells isolated from subcutaneous tumors. We found that lipid peroxidation in CD45- tumor cell population from HADHA deficient tumors was dramatically increased compared to the one from wildtype tumors (Fig. 4c). Further, we wondered whether increased ferroptosis contributed to HADHA deficiency-mediated tumor suppression. We knocked out long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4 (ACSL4), which mediates the incorporation of PUFA into phospholipids and is required for ferroptosis execution29, in HADHA deficient B16F0 cells (Fig. 4d). ACSL4 deficiency (ACSL4-/-) could restore the resistance of HADHA deficient cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 4e), and fully reversed the tumor-suppressive effect mediated by HADHA loss in vivo, as shown by restored tumor volume (Fig. 4f) and weight (Fig. 4g). In addition, we evaluated ferroptosis markers in tumor tissues. The inductions of PTGS2 mRNA (Fig. 4h), malondialdehyde (MDA) content (Fig. 4i) and 4-HNE content (Fig. 4j) mediated by HADHA loss were all abolished by ACSL4 deficiency. Overall, these results indicate HADHA inhibition suppresses tumor growth by promoting tumoral ferroptosis in vivo.

Tumoral HADHA inhibition potentiates cancer immunotherapy

Given that tumoral ferroptosis has the potential to enhance anti-tumor immune response, we investigated whether tumoral HADHA deficiency reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). We analyzed tumor-infiltrating immune cells isolated from HADHA deficient B16F0 tumors (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The infiltrations of mature DCs (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c) and inflammatory macrophage (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e) were significantly increased in HADHA deficient tumors. Additionally, the effector functions of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, as indicated by their production of IFNγ and TNFα, were enhanced by tumoral HADHA loss (Fig. 5a–f and Supplementary Fig. 5f). These results indicate that ablation of HADHA enhances antitumor immune response. Next, we inoculated wildtype or HADHA deficient B16F0 tumor cells into immunodeficient non-obese diabetic (NOD)-PrkdcscidIL2rgem1/Smoc (M-NSG) mice, and observed that tumor growths were comparable across control and HADHA deficient group (Fig. 5g, h), suggesting that HADHA deficiency inhibits tumor growth in an immune-dependent manner. To further identify the subtype of T cells responsible for this antitumor effect, HADHA-deficient B16F0 cells were inoculated into C57BL/6 mice in which CD4+ or CD8+ T cells were depleted by administration of the anti-CD4 or anti-CD8 antibody (Supplementary Fig. 5g, h). As shown by both tumor volume and tumor weight, anti-CD8, but not anti-CD4 antibody, largely restored the growth of HADHA-deficient B16F0 tumors (Fig. 5i, j). Collectively, these results indicate that CD8+ T cells are the primary effector population mediating the antitumor effect of HADHA ablation.

Since HADHA deficiency enhances CD8+ T cell effector function, which is essential for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, we wondered whether it could improve ICB outcomes. We inoculated wildtype and HADHA deficient B16F0 cells into C57BL/6 mice and treated them with anti-PD-1 antibody. Although both tumors were responsive to the therapy, HADHA deficient tumor were more sensitive, revealed by the decreased tumor volume (Fig. 5k) and improved animal survival (Fig. 5l). These results indicate that ablation of tumoral HADHA potentiates PD-1 blockade therapy.

We then assessed the clinical relevance of HADHA in cancer immunotherapy. From analyzing publicly available transcriptomic datasets, we found that lower level of HADHA mRNA correlated with prolonged overall survival of patients with melanoma (Supplementary Fig. 5i). In melanoma cohort who received ICB therapy, lower HADHA expression was also associated with improved overall survival and progression-free survival (Supplementary Fig. 5j, k). To further evaluate the expression of HADHA at the protein level, we performed the staining using an antibody recognizing HADHA and HADHB complex on a tissue microarray containing 86 biopsy specimens from lung cancer patients received immunotherapy together with or without chemotherapy and anti-angiogenesis therapy. We found that HADHA and HADHB were predominantly localized in cytoplasm within cancer cells (Fig. 5m). Their higher expression was associated with shorter overall survival (OS) (Fig. 5n) and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 5o). Furthermore, in our cohort of lung cancer patients, the lower HADHA and HADHB protein expression predicted better response to immunotherapy (Fig. 5p). Together, these results suggest that tumoral HADHA contributes to immune resistance and tumor progression, while its inhibition enhances the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Acylcarnitine C18-OH supplementation augments PD-1 blockade therapy

We next assessed whether supplementation of acylcarnitine C18-OH could exert similar antitumor effect in vivo. We subcutaneously inoculated B16F0 cells into C57BL/6 mice and treated them with intraperitoneal administration of acylcarnitine C18-OH. Tumor growth was significantly inhibited by acylcarnitine C18-OH, as shown by reduced tumor volume (Fig. 6a) and weight (Fig. 6b). As HADHA inhibition has been shown to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy, we then tested whether acylcarnitine C18-OH supplementation could augment PD-1 blockade therapy. In subcutaneous B16F0 tumors treated by administration of acylcarnitine C18-OH and anti-PD-1 antibody (Supplementary Fig. 6a), we observed that their combinational therapy exerted a superior tumor growth inhibition compared with either single treatment (Fig. 6c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). Importantly, acylcarnitine C18-OH administration did not affect body weight of animals (Supplementary Fig. 6d), suggesting favorable tolerability in vivo. To further investigate whether tumoral ferroptosis contributes to the efficacy of above combinational therapy, we treated wild-type B16F0 tumor-bearing mice with the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 following by combination therapy of acylcarnitine C18-OH and PD-1 blockade (Fig. 6e). Liproxstatin-1 co-treatment largely abolished their therapeutic efficacy as evidenced by rescued tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6f, g). Similarly, ACSL4 deficient B16F0 tumors did not benefit from the combination therapy (Fig. 6h, i). Therefore, these data suggest that acylcarnitine C18-OH supplementation boosts cancer immunotherapy by promoting tumoral ferroptosis.

Clinically, we wondered whether acylcarnitine C18-OH levels were associated with prognosis and immunotherapy responses in cancer patients. We assessed acylcarnitine C18-OH levels in plasma of 68 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients received immunotherapy using LC-MS/MS. Higher plasma levels of acylcarnitine C18-OH were associated with improved PFS (Fig. 6j) and enriched among responders to immunotherapy (Fig. 6k). As a control, body mass index (BMI) was comparable between responder and non-responders (Fig. 6l). These results indicate that circulating acylcarnitine C18-OH levels correlate with favorable immunotherapy outcomes.

Trimetazidine inhibits tumor growth and enhances immunotherapy

Subsequently, we investigated whether pharmacological inhibition of HADHA could recapitulate the pro-ferroptotic effects observed with genetic depletion. Trimetazidine (TMZ) is a selective inhibitor of HADHA and is conventionally used for treatment of angina pectoris30. We confirmed that TMZ treatment could significantly inhibit the enzymatic activity of HADHA in B16F0 cells (Fig. 7a) and resulted in the accumulation of intracellular acylcarnitine C18-OH (Fig. 7b). Correspondingly, TMZ treatment also enhanced the levels of mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 7c), lipid peroxidation (Fig. 7d) and cell death (Fig. 7e) in B16F0 cells in response to RSL3. In murine Panc02, TDCL and human A549 tumor cells, TMZ exerted the similar sensitization effect on RSL3-induced ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). These data indicate that TMZ promote ferroptosis across multiple tumor types in vitro.

We then wondered whether TMZ could exert antitumor effect in vivo. We intragastrical administrated TMZ into C57BL/6 mice bearing TDCL tumors (Supplementary Fig. 7d), and found that TMZ treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth, as demonstrated by reductions in tumor volume (Fig. 7f) and weight (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Similarly, in B16F0 melanoma model, TMZ suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 7g and Supplementary Fig. 7f) and increased MDA content in tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 7g). We further assessed the role of tumoral HADHA in TMZ therapy by comparing the response of WT or HADHA KO tumors to TMZ treatment. TMZ significantly inhibited the growth of WT tumor but failed to further suppress HADHA KO tumors (Fig. 7h and Supplementary Fig. 7h), suggesting that TMZ exerts anti-tumor effect by targeting cancer cell intrinsic HADHA. Moreover, we analyzed tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes by flow cytometry, and observed increased frequencies of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells producing IFNγ or TNFα following TMZ treatment (Fig. 7i, j), suggesting that TMZ also enhances antitumor T cell response. Based on these findings, we assessed whether TMZ could augment PD-1 blockade therapy. We treated B16F0 tumor-bearing mice with TMZ in combination with anti-PD1 antibody (Fig. 7k). Their combination synergistically inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 7l) and reduced tumor weight (Fig. 7m), and resulted in elevated CD8+ T cell infiltration (Fig. 7n) and increased IFNγ and TNFα production (Fig. 7o). Altogether, these results indicate that TMZ treatment improves T cell-based immunotherapy.

Finally, we evaluated the clinical benefits of TMZ use in cancer patients receiving immunotherapy. In our institutional cohort of lung cancer patients who received ICB therapy, we compared the clinical survival outcomes between the subgroup with or without prescription records of TMZ usage before and/or during ICB therapy (Supplementary Fig. 7i). The baseline characteristics including sex, age, therapy lines, or type of immunotherapy (monotherapy or combination therapy) between TMZ use and control groups showed no significant differences (Supplementary Fig. 7j), indicating that the two groups were well-balanced. Interestingly, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that TMZ group exhibited the improved overall survival compared to control group (Fig. 7p). These data support the potential of TMZ as a clinically relevant adjunct to immunotherapy.

Discussion

HADHA dimerizes with HADHB to form MTP that catalyzes the middle steps of FAO, which is the primary catabolic pathway to degrade long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) into acetyl-CoA. Ferroptosis is a type of cell death driven by the peroxidation of membrane phospholipids containing polyunsaturated LCFAs. Therefore, in theory cellular response to ferroptosis is tightly regulated by FAO pathway. Indeed, previous studies showed that CPT1a knockdown increased lipid peroxidation in both human and mouse lung cancer cells in response to erastin, although it minimally affects erastin-mediated tumor inhibition in vivo18. DECR1, a rate-limiting enzyme for the FAO of PUFA but no other FAs, was found to protect prostate tumor cells from phospholipids peroxidation and ferroptosis19. However, the specific contribution of individual FAO enzymes to ferroptosis progression remain systematically unexplored. We individually knocked down four key enzymes within FAO pathway in mouse B16F0 cells and find that only inhibition of HADHA, but not its upstream enzymes sensitized tumor cells to ferroptosis. Knockout of HADHA resulted in more significant sensitization and its effect fully depends on HADHA’s enzymatic activity. HADHA deficiency in B16F0 also inhibited subcutaneous tumor growth through induction of ferroptosis in vivo. Previous studies have suggested that HADHA may serve as either an oncogenic facilitator31 or a tumor suppressor32 in different tumor types with distinct metabolic dependency, and their effects were assessed in immunodeficient models. Our results indicate that genetic ablation of HADHA suppress tumor growth in an immunocompetent setting. Mechanistically, HADHA loss causes a unique metabolic reprograming that facilitate tumoral ferroptosis, leading to enhanced antitumor T-cell response. Therefore, our findings contribute to defining HADHA acts as an immune-related therapeutic vulnerability.

Considering that the FAO is consisted of a series of enzymatic reactions, interruption of different enzymes may cause the accumulation of different intermediates and the shortage of downstream products. The diverse metabolic intermediates may exert previously unrecognized effects on ferroptosis. By means of lipidomic analysis, we identify that HADHA inhibition results in accumulation of acylcarnitine C18-OH in mitochondrial fractions. Consistent with our findings, HADHA deficiency or mutation in cardiomyocytes causes the accumulations of multiple LCFAs including 14:1, 16:1, 18:1, 18:2 and 18:3 and also the abnormal profiles of LCFA-containing cardiolipin at the whole cell level. Notably, the significant increase of hydroxylated acylcarnitine was also identified in HADHA deficiency cardiomyocytes, including acylcarnitine C18-OH33. Inhibition of HADHA by TMZ treatment, which is also used in our cells to enhance ferroptosis, leads to the accumulation of hydroxylated acylcarnitine (C18-OH, C18:1-OH, C18:2-OH, C16-OH, C14-OH) in A549 tumor cells34. In fact, the accumulation of hydroxylated acylcarnitines in the plasma of patients with inborn errors of FAO was also observed and has historically been used as diagnostic markers for this disease35,36. However, whether these hydroxylated acylcarnitines could cause direct cellular or tissue damages are still unclear. The long-chain acylcarnitine palmitoylcarnitine has been reported to decrease OXPHOS-dependent mitochondrial respiration with complex I/II and complex II substrates, and induce mitochondrial membrane hyperpolarization and the subsequent production of ROS37,38. A recent study revealed that RSL3 treatment led to a marked decrease of several FAs that serve as FAO substrates, while an increase of their intermediate products, particularly acylcarnitines39. In our HADHA deficient tumor cells, the accumulated acylcarnitine C18-OH was considered as a pro-ferroptotic metabolite. Exogenous acylcarnitine C18-OH treatment enhanced the mitochondrial ROS and lipid peroxidation, resulting in increased ferroptosis of tumor cells in response to RSL3. Loading acylcarnitine C18-OH into liposome directly accelerated the autoxidation of phospholipid, suggesting that acylcarnitine C18-OH may function as an amplifier of lipid peroxidation on mitochondrial or plasma membrane to promote ferroptosis execution. More interestingly, we found that supplementation of acylcarnitine C18-OH could improve the efficacy of PD-1 blockade therapy in vivo, and the sensitization effect could be largely abolished by liproxstatin-1 or ACSL4 deficiency The levels of acylcarnitine C18-OH in the plasma of cancer patients are positively associated with patient survival and the efficacy of immunotherapy. As such, acylcarnitine C18-OH holds potential as a biomarker for predicting cancer patient survival and assessing immunotherapy efficacy.

Gene mutations in neonatal MTP cause the occurrence of the autosomal genetic disease LCHADD and mitochondrial trifunctional protein deficiency (MTPD), leading to multiple organic dysfunctions and even death. The severe symptoms in these patients include cardiomyopathy, cardiac arrhythmias, hepatic encephalopathy, myopathy and episodic rhabdomyolysis, which are induced by metabolic stress from FAO interruption40–43. Currently, the therapeutic approaches for this genetic disease focus on dietary intervention and management of long-term complications. Reported mutations in HADHA or HADHB often located in the catalytic sites and affect their enzymatic function. The most frequent cause of LCHADD is a homozygous 1528 G > C (E510Q) missense mutation in HADHA21,40. We found that re-expression of E510Q mutant in HADHA deficient tumor cells failed to exert the enzymatic activity and recover ferroptosis resistance. These results suggest that tissues with HADHA mutations may also become sensitive to ferroptosis, which may contribute to the pathologies of LCHADD and MTPD. Future studies could investigate whether tissues or organs of HADHA-mutant mice undergo ferroptosis in vivo. If confirmed, ferroptosis inhibitors may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for this genetic disease.

More interestingly, we found that the enzymatic activity of HADHA was increased upon ferroptosis induction, which is due to the accumulation of intracellular SPD. It has been reported that SPD could directly bind HADHA and HADHB and allosterically activate their enzymatic activities in human Hela cells24. A subsequent study further validated the interaction between SPD or its analogs and HADHA protein in HEK293 cells44. We observed a rapid elevation of SPD content in B16F0 cells within a short time of RSL3 treatment. Supplementation of exogenous SPD causes the increase of intracellular SPD level and enhanced HADHA activity, resulting in the resistance to RSL3-induced ferroptosis. We thus conclude that the elevation of HADHA activity represents an adaptive protective mechanism to inhibit ferroptosis. However, the underlying mechanism how SPD content is increased by RSL3 treatment is still unclear. Moreover, a recent study showed that pre-treatment of SPD could promote ferroptosis of several tumor cell lines by the production of H2O225, suggesting that SPD may have diversified role in the process of ferroptosis. It will be worth studying the effects of enzymes in the SPD metabolic pathway on ferroptosis regulation.

TMZ is a widely employed anti-ischemic medication for the long-term management of coronary artery diseases45–47. It inhibits the activity of mitochondrial trifunctional protein (MTP) and disrupts FAO, thereby preventing the decline of intracellular ATP levels and protecting myocardial cell from hypoxia or ischemia exposure48. Interestingly, TMZ has been shown to exert antitumor activity across several preclinical tumor models, including murine Ehrlich solid-phase carcinoma49, human pancreatic50 and lung carcinomas34. TMZ treatment can also enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy, such as doxorubicin or CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib51. A recent retrospective clinical study identified 6561 IHD patients with lung cancers and compared the clinical outcomes of patients who received TMZ versus non-users. The result indicates that TMZ usage is associated with higher overall survival in IHD patients with lung cancers52. Currently, a phase III clinical trial is currently undergoing to evaluate the efficacy of cardiovascular intervention in patients with lung cancer, and TMZ treatment is included. These results highlight the potential of TMZ as a novel therapeutic agent for cancer therapy. However, its underlying mechanisms in vivo remains poorly characterized. In particular, the potential of TMZ for cancer therapy in immunocompetent models has not been elucidated. Our results showed that TMZ administration could inhibit the growths of murine melanoma and lung carcinoma in an immunocompetent setting. Moreover, TMZ in combination with PD-1 blockade could more efficiently suppress tumor growth and enhance the number and function of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, suggesting that TMZ is a potential combination strategy to overcome the resistance of immunotherapy. By analyzing the cohort of lung cancer patients who received ICB therapy and were diagnosed with IHD, we found that TMZ in combination with ICB therapy correlated with the improved overall survival. Mechanistically, we revealed that TMZ could sensitize both human and murine tumor cells to ferroptosis. In vivo, TMZ treatment caused the increase of MDA content, a marker of ferroptosis. Tumoral ferroptosis is considered as an immunogenic cell death and thus inducing it has the potential to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy53,54. However, no clinically approved agent that targets tumoral ferroptosis is available to date. We therefore propose a feasible, clinically translatable strategy to target tumoral ferroptosis by leveraging the anti-ischemic drug TMZ for boosting cancer immunotherapy efficacy.

In summary, our research indicates that HADHA functions as a ferroptosis suppressor, whose enzymatic activity is elevated upon ferroptosis induction. Ablation of HADHA causes the interruption of FAO flux, promotes tumoral ferroptosis, increases antitumor CD8+ T cell response, and potentiates PD-1 blockade therapy in preclinical tumor models. More importantly, we identify that acylcarnitine C18-OH accumulated from HADHA inhibition is a key intermediate metabolite that confers to ferroptosis sensitization and immunotherapy improvement, and establish TMZ as a combination drug to augment ICB therapy through inhibition of tumoral HADHA activity.

Ethics statement

All studies reported in this manuscript comply with the relevant ethics regulations. Tissue collection was approved by the institutional ethics board of Tongji Hospital of Tongji Medical College (TJ-IRB 202505053). The informed consent was obtained from all human participants for the publication of the study results. The animal studies were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) of Huazhong University of Science and Technology ([2024] IACUC Number: 4292). For mouse tumor experiments, all tumor sizes/burdens were permitted by the IACUC. Maximal tumor burden of 2000 mm3 permitted by the IACUC was not exceeded in this study.

Cell lines and culture conditions

B16F0, A549 cell lines were from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC). 293 T cell line was from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Panc02 cell line was obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College (http://cellresource.cn). Mouse lung carcinoma cell line TDCL that was isolated from lung tumor tissues of the KrasLSL-G12D Trp53flox/flox mice was a gift from Dr. Shuguo Sun (Huazhong University of Science and Technology)55.

B16F0, TDCL and A549 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cegrogen Biotech). 293 T and Panc02 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and routinely tested for mycoplasma.

Plasmid construction and establishment of gene-edited cell lines

Single guided RNA (sgRNA) sequences targeting HADHA, ACSL4 were designed by public online CRISPR design tools Synthego. Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) sequences targeting Cpt1a, Cpt2, Acadl, HADHA and HADHB were designed by public online tool Sigma Predesigned. The cDNA of wild-type or mutant HADHA, mito-GPX4 were amplified by PCR with the corresponding primers using the Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase Kit (P505, Vazyme). The sgRNA, shRNA and amplification primers sequences were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

sgRNAs were cloned into the LentiCRISPR-V2 vector (Addgene, 52961). shRNAs were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector (Addgene, 8453). The PCR amplification products were cloned into the pLenti-CMV-3xFlag vector. The target plasmids and packaging plasmids psPAX2, pMD2.G were transfected into 293 T cells with transfection reagent PEI to produce lentivirus. The culture medium was refreshed after 24 h, and lentivirus was harvested after 48 h. Cancer cells were infected with lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (Sigma, 107689-10 G). The culture medium was refreshed after 12 h, and cells were selected with puromycin after 48 h for 3–4 days. Single cell clones were sorted in 96-well plates for proliferation and the gene deletion in each cell clone was verified by immunoblotting.

HADHA KO tumor cells that re-expressed flag-tagged wild-type or mutant HADHA were firstly constructed by tumor cells that were transiently transfected with HADHA sgRNA plasmids, selected by puromycin, and isolated for individual clones. The gene deletion in each cell clone was verified by immunoblotting to obtain HADHA KO cells without puromycin resistance, which were infected with lentivirus and selected with indicated concentration of puromycin to obtain HADHA KO tumor cells that re-expressed wild-type or mutant HADHA. The overexpression efficiency was verified by immunoblotting. WT and HADHA KO tumor cells that expressed flag-tagged mito-GPX4, shCpt1a, shCpt2 or shAcadl were constructed as above.

Lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial ROS measurement

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with reagents for the indicated concentration and time, then harvested by trypsinization. Cells were stained with 5 µM BODIPYTM 581/591 C11 (D3861, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min (for lipid peroxidation) or 5 µM MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Yeasen, 40778ES50) for 15 min (for mitochondrial ROS) at 37 °C, washed and resuspended in 200 µl PBS. Fluorescence was assessed immediately on a flow cytometer (Invitrogen Attune NxT) with a minimum of 10,000 single cells analyzed per sample.

To quantify the lipid peroxidation in samples from tumors, subcutaneous tumor tissue was ground by grinder and prepared into single cell suspension using 70 μm cell strainers. Tumor cells and immune cells were pre-enriched using density gradient centrifugation. Cell pellet was stained with anti-CD45 antibody (30-F11, Biolegend) and followed by BODIPY 581/591 C11. Cells were strained through a 40 μM cell strainer and assessed immediately with a flow cytometer (BD FACSVerse).

Quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted with Freezol (Vazyme, R711). 1000 ng total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA by primeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, RR036A). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using 2x Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, p222-01) with triplicate samples on a QuantStudio® 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The threshold cycle (Ct) values of target gene were normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences for qPCR were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunoblotting

Cell pellets were collected, washed and lysed by RIPA buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0010, MedChemExpress). The protein concentration was quantified by using BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The absorbance at a wavelength of 562 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader (Biotek, Synergy H1, USA). 4x loading buffer was added to the protein samples and heated at 95 °C for 10 min.

Protein samples were separated in 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinyldifluoride (PVDF) membranes (1620177, Millipore). Membranes were blocked in TBST containing 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, Membranes were washed in TBST and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoblots were imaged by ChemiDoc Imaging System (Tanon-5200Multi). The following antibodies were used: anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP, 1:5000), anti-Flag (Sigma, F1804, 1:2000), anti-HADHA (Abcam, ab203114, 1:2000), anti-ACSL4 (Santa Cruz, sc365230, 1:2000), anti-GPX4 (Proteintech, 67763-1-Ig, 1:1000), anti-αTubulin (Proteintech, 11224-1-AP, 1:5000), anti-4-HNE (JaICA, HNEJ-2, 1:1000). The uncropped and unprocessed scans of blots were supplied in the Source Data file.

Quantification of spermidine by LC-MS/MS

Cultured cells were washed by PBS, and incubated with pre-chilled methanol:water (80:20, v/v) solution on ice for 20 min. Following extraction, cell lysates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Clear supernatants were collected and dried in a vacuum-assisted centrifuge (Eppendorf, Concentrator plus). Dried metabolites were then reconstituted in 200 μl water:acetonitrile (50:50, v/v) solution, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C prior to analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

LC-MS/MS measurements were performed using a SCIEX Triple Quad™ 5500 + LC-MS/MS system-QTRAP™ Ready. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a XBridge BEH Amide XP Column (2.1 ×100 mm, 2.5 μm; Waters) at 40 °C using a gradient of mobile phase A (20 mM ammonium formate in 5% methanol, pH 3.0) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile/methanol/mobile phase A, 90:5:5). 5 μL of samples kept at 4 °C in the autosampler were injected into the LC-MS/MS system at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with 15% mobile phase A. At 0.37 min, mobile phase A were increased linearly to 30% at 4.1 min, then to 50% A at 5.0 min, followed by a rapid increase to 85% at 5.8 and hold until 6.8 min. Mobile phase A were then returned to initial conditions at 7.8 min with 1.7 min column re-equilibration. Detection was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) scan mode using positive electrospray ionization (ESI) with optimized parameters: curtain gas (30.0 psi), ESI voltage (5500 V), source temperature (550 °C), Gas 1 (70 psi) and Gas 2 (60 psi). The ion transitions (precursor–product ion) for spermidine was m/z (mass/charge ratio) 146.2 to 72.1. Mass spectrometry data were processed using the Analyst 1.7.1 software.

Fluorescence microscopy and image analysis

For live-cell microscopy, tumor cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes (NEST, 801001) at 4 × 105 cells/dish and incubated overnight. The next day, cells were treated with RSL3 or Carnitine C18-OH for the indicated time, and washed by PBS. Subsequently, cells were stained with 5 µM BODIPYTM 581/591 C11 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D3861), 100 nM MitoTracker® Deep Red FM (Yeasen, 40743ES50) and 5 µg/mL Hoechst for 30 minutes at 37 °C, washed by PBS and imaged in fresh medium. The fluorescence images were acquired by Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with 100× oil immersion objective with the following lasers: Hoechst (405 nm laser), Oxidized BODIPY C11 (488 nm laser), Reduced BODIPY C11 (561 nm laser), MitoTracker Deep Red (647 nm laser).

To analyze mitochondrial lipid peroxidation, images that stained with oxidized BODIPY C11 and MitoTracker were opened and converted to 8-bit by FIJI software. Oxidized BODIPY C11 and MitoTracker signals in all samples were adjusted the threshold with the same parameters. M1 & M2 coefficients to analyze the fraction of mitochondria overlapping oxidized BODIPY C11 in immunofluorescence images were calculated by Just Another Colocalization Plugin (JACoP). Colocalization analysis between oxidized BODIPY C11 and mitochondrial signals was performed by the line-scan plot profile that listed the signal gray value at different distances.

HADHA enzyme activity assay

HADHA enzyme activity was measured based on previously reported designs24,56. Cells were seeded in 10 cm cell culture dishes at 5 × 106 cells/dish and incubated overnight. The next day, cells were treated with reagents for the indicated concentration and time, then washed and harvested. Mitochondria were isolated using Cell Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime, C3601). In brief, mitochondrial isolation reagent supplemented with PMSF was added to suspend cells on ice for 10 min. Cell suspension was homogenized with a homogenizer, and centrifuged at 1000× g for 10 min at 4 °C for twice. The supernatant was centrifuged again at 3500× g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet, mainly containing mitochondria was resuspended in the assay buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) and 1.2 mM NAD+. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding the substrate β-hydroxybutyryl-CoA (125 μM). The NADH production was monitored by the absorbance at a wavelength of 340 nm every 1 min for 15 min. NADH was calculated by the following formula: b = A/(ε*c), with b as the concentration of NADH (mol/L), A as the absorbance, ε as the extinction coefficient (6.22 mM−1cm−1) and c as the optical path.

Lipidomic profiling and data analysis

Lipids were extracted from cells and mixed with internal standard mixture. Four replicates were analyzed for each cell line. Lipids analyses were conducted using LC-ESI-MS/MS system (SCIEX, UPLC, ExionLC AD; MS, QTRAP® 6500+ System). Chromatographic separation was achieved by a Thermo Accucore™C30 Column (2.6 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm i.d.) using a mobile phase A acetonitrile/water (60/40, V/V, 0.1% formic acid, 10 mmol/L ammonium formate) and B acetonitrile/isopropanol (10/90 V/V, 0.1% formic acid, 10 mmol/L ammonium formate). 2 μL samples were injected into the LC-MS/MS system at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min with 20% mobile phase B. Mobile phase B linearly increased up to 30% at 2.0 min, to 60% at 4.0 min, to 85% at 9.0 min, to 90% at 14.0 min, to 95% at 15.5 min and held until 17.3 min. The gradient returned back to 20% at 20.0 min. MS data were acquired on a QTRAP® 6500 + LC-MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (SCIEX). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows, ion source, turbo spray; source temperature 500 °C, ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (Positive), −4500 V (Negative); ion source gas 1 (GS1), gas 2 (GS2) and curtain gas (CUR) were set at 45, 55, and 35 psi, respectively. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 μmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. QQQ scans were acquired as MRM experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to 5 psi. DP and CE for individual MRM transitions was done with further DP and CE optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.

Lipids contents were detected and integrated based on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform. Mass spectrometry data were processed using the Analyst 1.6.3 software. The qualitative and quantitative analyses of lipids in samples were performed according to the internal standard LC–MS peaks and peak area. The content of lipids containing the same number of double bonds in the lipid acyl chains were summed to represent chain unsaturation. For fold-change analysis, the ratio between two groups was log2 transformed and presented as scatter plots and volcano plots. Differential metabolites were determined by VIP (Variable Importance in Projection) and P-value. VIP values were obtained from OPLS-DA analysis. P-value was performed by Student’s t test and presented as -log10(P.value) in RStudio.

Detection of acylcarnitines by UHPLC-MS/MS

For plasma sample preparation, 20 μL plasma was mixed with 180 μL of methanol solution containing internal standard. For cell samples, cells in dish were scrapped in 500 μL of ice-cold acetonitrile/methanol/water (4:4:2, v/v/v) including internal standards. The sample was vortexed, centrifuged at 4 °C, 16000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was dried under nitrogen gas. The dried residue was derivatized with 100 μL of 3 M acetyl chloride in 1-butanol at 65 °C for 15 min. At the end of derivatization, the solution was dried and redissolved in 50 μL of 80% aqueous acetonitrile for detection.

The UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system coupled to a 6470 A Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometry (Santa Clara, CA, United States). Samples were injected onto a Waters BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) at column temperature of 40 °C and flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. Mobile phase (A) was 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile. The chromatographic separation was conducted by a linear gradient elution program as follows: 20% B at 0 min; 80% B at 9 min; 100% B at 10 min and held to 12 min; 20%B at 12.1 min and held to 14 min. The chromatographically eluted analysts were ionized in positive-ion mode using ESI and subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry. The temperatures of source drying gas and sheath gas were 300 °C and 350 °C, respectively. The flow rate of source drying gas and sheath gas were 5 and 11 L/min, respectively. The pressure of nebulizer was 40 psi, and capillary voltage was 4000 V. The dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (dMRM) was used to acquire data in optimized MRM transition (precursor -> product), fragmentor and collision energy (CE).

MassHunter software (version B.08.00, Agilent) was used to control instruments and acquire data. The raw data were processed by MassHunter Workstation Software (version B.08.00, Agilent) by using the default parameters and assisting manual inspection to ensure the qualitative and quantitative accuracies of each compound. The peak areas of each compound in all samples were integrated. Calibration curves of eleven-point were constructed by plotting the peak area ratio of each compound to internal standard against concentration of each compound. The concentrations of acylcarnitine in prepared sample (for determination) were quantified automatically, and finally output for quantitative calculation of samples.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

Malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured using the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (BL904A, Biosharp) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, tumor tissues of mice were homogenized by an Ultrasonic Homogenizer (Jingxin, JXFSTPRP-24) and centrifugated at 12,000× g for 10 min. Supernatant was mixed with MDA Detection Buffer that was formulated with antioxidants and thiobarbituric acid (TBA), heated at 100 °C for 15 min, and centrifugated at 1000× g for 10 min. The absorbance of supernatant at a wavelength of 532 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader. Standard curves of MDA were generated along with samples. The concentration of MDA was calculated based on the standard curve and normalized to protein concentration detected by BCA Protein Assay Kit.

Egg phosphatidylcholine (EggPC) autoxidation

The experiment was performed as previously described28. In brief, eggPC (Sigma, 840051 P) was resolved in CHCl3 to 20 mM, 2 ml of the solvent was then evaporated to yield a thin lipid film in a 10 ml vial and placed in a vacuum oven to dry overnight. The film was then hydrated with 2 ml of PBS (pH 7.4) to yield a 20 mM lipid suspension. The lipid suspension was processed for ten freeze (dry ice, 5 min)–thaw (room temperature, 5 min)–sonication (room temperature, 5 min) cycles and extruded 20 times with a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane to construct control liposome. Besides, we preloaded acylcarnitine C18-OH into the liposome during the liposome-preparation process, leading to more efficient incorporation into the liposome membrane. In brief, acylcarnitine C18-OH (10 mM) was mixed with eggPC at the ratio of 10% and evaporated to yield a thin lipid film for the subsequent liposome preparation.

1 mM eggPC liposome or 10% acylcarnitine C18-OH liposome was mixed with 1 μM STY-BODIPY (Cayman Chemicals, 27089). 250 µl of the mixture was added to a black 96-well polypropylene plate, and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, and vigorously mixed for 5 min to start autoxidation. Data were acquired by STY-BODIPY excitation at 488 nm and emission was measured at 518 nm on the microplate reader. The data were transformed from the raw RFU values to ox-STY-BODIPY (μM) by a response factor of 1.57 × 104 RFU per μM.

Animals

Female wild-type C57BL/6 mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. M-NSG mice were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. All animals were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities in groups of five mice per cage, maintained in a humidity-controlled environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 22 °C–25 °C, and fed with Lab Rat and Mouse Breeding Irradiated Diet (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd., XT101SL-002).

For subcutaneous tumor models, B16F0 cells (3 × 105 cells/mice) or TDCL cells (3 × 106 cells/mice) were suspended in PBS and subcutaneously injected into the right flank or armpit of wild-type C57BL/6 mice or M-NSG mice. Tumor volume was measured every two days when tumors were palpable using calipers and calculated by length × width2/2. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume reached 2,000 mm3, but neither body weight loss nor inability to reach food/water was observed. At the endpoint, tumors of mice were stripped and weighed. TMZ (Selleck, S4543) was intragastrically injected to mice at the dose of 30 mg/kg. Acylcarnitine C18-OH (TRC, H953630) was intraperitoneally injected at the indicated days as the schematic at the dose of 5 mg/kg. Anti-PD-1 antibody (BE0146, BioXCell) was intraperitoneally injected every 3 days started from Day 6 post tumor inoculation at the dose of 100 μg/mice. Anti-CD8a antibody (clone: 2.43; Selleck, A2102) or anti-CD4 antibody (clone: GK1.5; Selleck, A2101) was intraperitoneally injected every 3 days started from two days prior to tumor inoculation at the dose of 160 μg/mice (anti-CD8a antibody) or 100 μg/mice (anti-CD4 antibody). Liproxstatin-1 (10 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected to mice every day. For survival analysis, tumor volume was measured every day and the end point of death was set at the tumor volume of 2000 mm3, but neither body weight loss nor inability to reach food/water was observed.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes analysis

Tumor tissues of different groups of mice were collected at the end point, ground by grinder and prepared into single cell suspension using 70 μm cell strainers. Cell suspension was centrifuged and resuspended in Lymphocyte Separation Medium (0850494, MP Biomedicals). Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were harvested by density gradient centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 30 min, then suspended in complete culture medium and stimulated with Monensin (1: 1000), Ionomycin (1 μg/ml), Brefeldin A (1: 1000) and PMA (20 ng/ml) at 37 °C for 4 h in an incubator. After 4 h, cells were washed, stained with Zombie NIR Dye (77184, Biolegend), and then stained with anti-CD45 (30-F11, Biolegend), anti-CD3 (562600, BD), anti-CD4 (GK1.5, Biolegend) and anti-CD8 (53-6.7, Biolegend) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were washed, fixed and permeabilized by Fixation Buffer (420801, Biolegend) and Perm/Wash Buffer (421002, Biolegend), respectively. Then, cells were stained with anti-IFNγ (XMG1.2, Biolegend) and anti-TNFα (MP6-XT22, Biolegend) diluted in Perm/Wash Buffer for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, cells were washed and resuspended in 500 μL PBS, and assessed immediately on a flow cytometer (BD FACSVerse). The flow cytometry data was analyzed by FlowJo software.

Human subject

Biopsy specimens of 86 lung cancer patients received immunotherapy contained in tissue microarrays (related to Fig. 5), baseline treatment-naive human plasma collected from 68 subjects with advanced NSCLC receiving first-line immunotherapy (related to Fig. 6), and prescription records of TMZ for 756 lung cancer patients receiving immunotherapy during May 2018 to June 2022 (related to Fig. 7) were collected in Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The overall survival, progression-free survival and response to immunotherapy were recorded, evaluated, and kindly provided by doctors in Tongji Hospital. The studies were approved by the institutional ethics board of Tongji Hospital of Tongji Medical College (TJ-IRB 202505053). All samples and information were collected under the informed consent of participants.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using IHC Prep & Detect Kit for Rabbit/Mouse Primary Antibody (20031553, Proteintech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, for staining of MTP (HADHA and HADHB) in human lung carcinoma in TMA, the slide was heated at 65 °C for 2 h, and then was deparaffinized and hydrated with xylene and ethanol of decreasing concentration. Section slide was heated in Antigen Retrieval Buffer for 15 min by microwave to achieve antigen retrieval and cooled slowly to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase of tissues was inactivated by Quenching Buffer for 10 min and blocked by incubating with Blocking Buffer for 1 h. Then, slides were incubated with Anti-HADHA + HADHB antibody [4A8BG12] (ab110302, Abcam, 1:500) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, slides were washed, stained with polymer HRP-goat anti-Rabbit/Mouse Secondary Antibody for 30 min, and colored with diaminobenzidine (DAB). The nucleus was stained with Couter Staining Reagent. Finally, slides were dehydrated using ethanol of increasing concentration and sealed using Mounting Media and coverslip. Bright field images of section slides were obtained by an automated slide-scanning platform (Pannoramic SCAN, 3DHISTECHS), and analyzed by CaseViewer2.4 software (3DHISTECH). Three fields of each tumor sample were selected to perform H score, which was automatically measured by FIJI IHC Profiler to get the percentage of High Positive, Positive, Low Positive and Negative. H score was calculated by 1 × (% low positive) + 2 × (% positive) + 3 × (% high positive). According to H score ranking, tumor samples were divided into high and low MTP expression group.

Statistics & reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism9 software. All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. The comparisons were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or ANOVA models. Data distribution was assumed to be normal when using t-tests, but this was not formally tested. Individual data points are shown. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated by log-rank tests. Exact P values are indicated in the related figures. The experiments in this study were set up using three to ten samples per independent group, condition or repeat. Biological replications and statistics are indicated in the legends. The fluorescence imaging and immunoblotting experiments were shown as representative results from three independent experiments with similar results. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications3,6,57. No data were excluded from the analyses. For animal experiments, all mice were sex and aged matched randomized into different groups, whereas the other experiments were not randomized. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments, except for the IHC score evaluation.
