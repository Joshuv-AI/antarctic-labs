Abstract
. In this work, we build a bridge between the Pólya–Schur program and Voiculescu’s free probability theory. A cornerstone of the former is the Pólya–Benz Theorem, classifying a central family of real-root preserving operators on the space of polynomials, as those given by f ( ∂ z ) f(\partial_{z}) for a Laguerre–Pólya function f f and the derivative operator ∂ z \partial_{z} . We prove that any free (additive) infinitely divisible distribution can be attained as the weak limit of root distributions of Appell polynomials f n ( ∂ z ) z n f_{n}(\partial_{z})z^{n} as n → ∞ n\to\infty , for a suitably chosen sequence f n f_{n} of Laguerre–Pólya functions. Such questions on the (global) limiting distributions of real rooted polynomials belong to the active research area of finite free probability. In contrast to its standard tools, our approach allows for non-compactly supported limiting distributions, (barely) complex rooted polynomials and even provides the full microscopic description of the roots. Moreover, we extend our results to differential operators generating free multiplicative infinitely divisible distributions, to the rectangular free convolution, and to f n ( ∂ z ) p n f_{n}(\partial_{z})p_{n} for real rooted polynomials p n p_{n} , implying a generalization of the recent connections between the heat flow and free Brownian motion to any free Lévy process. As corollaries, we identify free stable distributions by choosing f n f_{n} to be a fixed rescaled Laguerre–Pólya function, and we prove various convergence results on the zero distributions of Jensen polynomials, e.g. the limiting root distribution of Jensen polynomial of the Riemann Ξ \Xi -function is given by the Cauchy distribution.

Introduction

1.1.
The Laguerre–Pólya class

Let
f
be a real entire function of order less than or equal to
2
, of the form

(1.1)

f(z)=Cz^{m}e^{-cz-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}z^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\alpha_{j}z\right)e^{\alpha_{j}z}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{k!}z^{k},

where
c,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\{\alpha_{j}^{-1}\}_{j\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\ii[-M,M]
for some
M\geq 0
such that
\sum_{j}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}<\infty
, so that the infinite product is absolutely convergent. For any
M\geq 0
denote by
\mathcal{LP}_{M}
the set of all such functions, and let
\mathcal{LP}_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{M\geq 0}\mathcal{LP}_{M}
. The set
\mathcal{LP}:=\mathcal{LP}_{0}
is known as the
Laguerre–Pólya class
and it is the closure of hyperbolic polynomials under the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets. If
\sigma=0
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\in[0,\infty)
,
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}<\infty
, and
c\geq\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}
, then
f
is in the
Laguerre–Pólya class of Type
\mathcal{I}
, or simply
f\in\mathcal{LPI}
. Functions in
\mathcal{LPI}
are those which are the uniform limits of polynomials with only non-negative roots.

For a function
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
, we consider two different infinite order differential operators, which we refer to as
Pólya–Benz type operator

(1.2)

f(\partial_{z})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{k!}\partial_{z}^{k},

and
Pólya–Schur type operator

(1.3)

f(z\partial_{z})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{k!}(z\partial_{z})^{k}.

We will study how the roots of
f(t\partial_{z})p(z)
and
f(tz\partial_{z})p(z)
evolve for a polynomial
p
and
t>0
. These infinite differential operators and their effect on roots of polynomials and entire functions have been considered by various authors
[
36
,
2
,
57
,
35
]
in the context of the
Pólya–Schur program
, which aims to understand linear operators which preserve geometric features of roots, more details in §
3.4
. From this perspective, our work not only asks
where
the zeros are distributed, but also
how
they are distributed in the large degree limit.
The names for the operators (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are motivated by two classical results: The Pólya–Benz Theorem (Proposition
1.1
) and Pólya–Schur Theorem (Proposition
2.8
, see also
[
2
]
).

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
denote be the space of polynomials with complex coefficients. We call
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})

real-rooted
if all its roots are real and say a linear operator
T:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
preserves real-rootedness if
T[p]
is real-rooted for any real-rooted polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proposition 1.1

(Pólya–Benz Theorem)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
be a linear operator which commutes with
\partial_{z}
. Then,
T
preserves real-rootedness if and only if
T=f(\partial_{z})
for some
f\in\mathcal{LP}
.

1.2.
Appell and Jensen polynomials

For any
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{\infty}
, we define the
Appell polynomials
of
f
by

(1.4)

A_{n,f}(z)=f(\partial_{z})z^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{k!}\partial_{z}^{k}z^{n},

where we note the right hand side is a terminating series.

The
Jensen polynomials
,
J_{n,f}
, of
f
are the reciprocal polynomial of
A_{n,f}
given by

(1.5)

J_{n,f}(z)=z^{n}A_{n,f}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right).

Given the power series expansion of
f
in (
1.1
),
A_{n,f}
and
J_{n,f}
have the simple formulas

(1.6)

A_{n,f}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\gamma_{k}\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k},\text{ and }J_{n,f}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\gamma_{k}\binom{n}{k}z^{k}.

A central feature of Appell sequences is the differential identity
A_{n,f}^{\prime}=nA_{n-1,f}
. The exponential generating function of the Appell polynomials is given by

(1.7)

f(z)e^{xz}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{k,f}(x)\frac{z^{k}}{k!}.

We note that another common way to associate an Appell sequence to some analytic function
g
is to through (
1.7
) with
f(z)=\frac{1}{g(z)}
, see
[
27
,
29
,
16
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{\infty}
. Then,
f\in\mathcal{LP}
if and only if
A_{n,f}
has only real roots for every
n\geq 0
. Moreover, uniformly on compact subsets,

(1.8)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}J_{n,f}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)=f(z).

1.3.
Free probability

Voiculescu’s free probability
[
87
]
is a non-commutative probability theory motivated by problems in operator algebras, and has since found great successes in a number of fields including random matrix theory and more recently the study of polynomial roots.

Definition 1.3

(Following
[
71
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
. The
Cauchy transform

G_{\mu}:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
of
\mu
is the analytic function

(1.9)

G_{\mu}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-t}\mathrm{d}\mu(t).

For
\alpha,\beta>0
, set
\Gamma_{\alpha,\beta}=\{z:\alpha\operatorname{Im}(z)>|\operatorname{Re}(z)|,\ \operatorname{Im}(z)>\beta\}
, and
\Delta_{\alpha,\beta}=\{z:z^{-1}\in\Gamma_{\alpha,\beta}\}
.
For every
\alpha>0
there exists
\beta>0
and the so-called
R
-
transform

R_{\mu}\colon\Delta_{\alpha,\beta}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying

(1.10)

G_{\mu}\left(R_{\mu}(z)+\frac{1}{z}\right)=z,\text{ for all }z\in\Delta_{\alpha,\beta},

and

R_{\mu}\left(G_{\mu}(z)\right)+\frac{1}{G_{\mu}(z)}=z,\text{ for all }z\in\Gamma_{\alpha,\beta}.

With these tools at hand, it is possible to analytically introduce the following central concept of free probability.

Definition 1.4
.

For probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
, the
free additive convolution

\mu\boxplus\nu
is the unique probability measure such that
R_{\mu\boxplus\nu}=R_{\mu}+R_{\nu}
.
A probability measure
\mu
is
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible
(or
\boxplus
-ID) if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability measure
\mu_{n}
such that
\mu_{n}^{\boxplus n}=\mu
.

There exists the following classification of
\boxplus
-ID distributions, which serves as a
free Lévy–Khintchine formula
.

Proposition 1.5

(See
[
21
]
, Theorem 5.10 or
[
20
]
, Theorem 3.2)
.

A probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
is
\boxplus
-ID if and only if
R_{\mu}
can be analytically extended to the lower half-plane and there exists a positive finite Borel measure
\Sigma
on
\mathbb{R}
and a real number
c
such that

(1.11)

R_{\mu}(u)=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u+x}{1-xu}\mathrm{d}\Sigma(x).

We will refer to
\Sigma
as the
weighted Lévy measure
of
\mu=\mu_{c,\Sigma}
and
(c,\Sigma)
as the
Lévy pair
of
\mu
.

Remark 1.6
.

The measure
\Sigma
can be written in the form
\mathrm{d}\Sigma(x)=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{d}\delta_{0}(x)+\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}\mathrm{d}\nu(x)
, where
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\nu
is a measure such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\min(x^{2},1)\mathrm{d}\nu(x)<\infty
and
\nu(\{0\})=0
. It is common to refer to
(c,\sigma^{2},\nu)
as the
Lévy triple
of
\mu=\mu_{c,\sigma^{2},\nu}
, and
\nu
as the
Lévy measure
of
\mu
.

An important subclass of
\boxplus
-ID distributions are the
\boxplus
-stable distributions. A non-degenerate probability measure
\mu
is said to be
\boxplus
-stable with stability index
\alpha\in(0,2]
if
\mu\boxplus\mu=\mathcal{D}_{2^{1/\alpha}}\mu\boxplus\delta_{b}
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{2^{1/\alpha}}\mu
is the dilation of
\mu
by
2^{1/\alpha}
. For
\alpha=2
, the stable law is a semicircle law with Lévy pair
(c,\sigma^{2}\delta_{0})
and for
\alpha<2
their weighted Lévy measure has density

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{\alpha,\theta}(x)=\alpha(\theta\mathbf{1}_{{x>0}}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{1}_{{x<0}})\frac{|x|^{1-\alpha}}{1+x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

for some
\theta\in[0,1]
.

The theory of
finite free probability
studies zeros of real-rooted polynomials as the finite dimensional analogue of eigenvalue distributions in free probability theory, see §
3.2
and
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
14
,
52
]
for more details. Using polynomial operations that mimic the free convolution, determining the limiting empirical root measures of
A_{n,f_{n}}
is sufficient in order to determine the limiting empirical root measures of
f_{n}(\partial_{z})p_{n}(z)
for any real–rooted
p_{n}
and any
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}_{0}
.
Recently, the backwards heat flow operator
e^{-\frac{t}{2n}\partial_{z}^{2}}p_{n}
has been linked to the free convolution with the semicircle law and repeated differentiation
\partial_{z}^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}p_{n}
has been linked to the free convolution semigroup, see for instance
[
11
,
81
,
82
,
32
,
44
,
48
,
50
,
49
,
34
,
55
,
45
]
.

In this work, we unite and vastly generalize these previous results.

1.4.
Summary of the results

•

In our main result, we prove that any
\boxplus
-ID distribution
\mu_{c,\Sigma}
can be obtained as the limiting empirical root measures of
f_{n}(\partial_{z})z^{n}
for some
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
, whose reciprocal roots converge to
\Sigma
after suitable rescaling (see Assumption
A
and Theorem
2.1
).

•

These results continue to hold if the roots of
f_{n}
are not too far from
\mathbb{R}
, even though
A_{f,n}
has complex roots. We consider this as a step towards “weakly non-Hermitian” finite free probability.

•

If the sequence functions is given by
f_{n}(z)=e^{-c_{n}{z}}f(b_{n}z)
for a fixed function
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
, then the limiting empirical measure is the spectral measure of a free
stable
Lévy process determined by the growth of the roots of
f
at infinity (Corollary
2.3
).

•

As another corollary, we show that the law
\mu\boxplus{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}^{\boxplus t}
of a
free additive Lévy process
starting from the distribution
\mu
can be realized as the limiting empirical root measure of
f_{tn}(t\partial_{z})p_{n}(z)
for some sequence of polynomials
p_{n}
(Corollary
2.5
).

•

We derive analogues for the
multiplicative free convolution
\boxtimes
and Pólya–Schur type operators
f_{n}(z\partial_{z})
, for more general operators
T_{n}
commuting with
z\partial_{z}
, and for
rectangular differentiation

z\partial_{z}^{2}+(1+\beta)\partial_{z}
. (Theorems
2.9
,
2.10
and
2.12
).

•

Our approach enables us to determine the microscopic structure of the roots of
f(\partial_{z})z^{n}
in the bulk, whose lattice spacing is determined by the density of the limit
\mu_{c,\Sigma}
and which generalizes the Plancherel–Rotach asymptotics for Hermite polynomials (Theorem
2.7
).

•

The proof of Theorem
2.1
relies on a novel combination of the saddle point method and insights from free probability, without tools from finite free probability (apart from some corollaries). We believe that this approach is of independent interest in order to remove typical restrictions of finite free probability, such as compact supports, real-rootedness and the lack of microscopic description.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. After fixing the notation, we rigorously present our main results in Section
2
, first on Pólya–Benz type operators then on Pólya–Schur type operators and its rectangular sibling. We will provide an outline of the proofs in Section
2.4
. Section
3
is devoted to a more detailed discussion of necessary concepts from (finite) free probability, the Pólya–Schur program and related works. In Section
4
, we prove our main result Theorem
2.1
. Section
5
presents proofs of the corollaries and Section
6
deals with the Pólya–Schur case.

For an analytic function

We will use the notation

2.
Main results

We will assume the following condition on a sequence of functions in
\mathcal{LP}_{\infty}
for all our results.

Assumption A
.

Let
0\leq M_{n}=o(n)
, and let
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be such that
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}_{M_{n}}
and of the form

(2.1)

f_{n}(z)=e^{-c_{n}z-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{2}z^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\alpha_{j,n}z\right)e^{\alpha_{j,n}z}.

We say the sequence
\{f_{n}\}
satisfies Assumption
A
if the finite complex valued measures

(2.2)

\Sigma_{n}:=n\sigma_{n}^{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2}\alpha_{j,n}^{2}}{n^{2}\alpha_{j,n}^{2}+1}\delta_{n\alpha_{j,n}}\to\Sigma

converge vaguely to some positive weighted Lévy measure
\Sigma
as
n\to\infty
, and if

(2.3)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}c_{n}-\int t\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{n}(t)=c.

Since the Lévy pair is continuous (as real numbers and vaguely as finite positive measures) with respect to weak convergence of probability measures, Assumption
A
is a continuity statement about a sequence of
\boxplus
-ID measures in the case when
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
and
\Sigma_{n}
is a positive measure. Allowing for complex roots creates some technical obstacles in our proofs, which
\Sigma_{n}
being a complex measure is a relatively minor one. By the Hahn–Jordan decomposition this complex measure can be written as a linear combination of four finite positive measures

\Sigma_{n}=\Sigma_{n,+}-\Sigma_{n,-}+\ii\Sigma_{n,+\ii}-\ii\Sigma_{n,-\ii}.

Implicit in (
2.2
) is that three of these measures converge vaguely to the zero measure.
Assumption
A
can easily be rephrased in terms of the triple
c_{n},\sigma_{n}
and the positive infinite measure
\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}\delta_{n\alpha_{j}}
on
\mathbb{C}
to avoid complex valued measures entirely. However, it turns out that our choice of working with (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) will keep the proofs cleaner and less technical.

Assumption
A
should be viewed as an optimal condition for our results and we will explicitly discuss necessity in Remark
2.11
below.

2.1.
Results for Pólya–Benz type operators

We will begin with
\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket
when
f_{n}(0)\neq 0
.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
\{f_{n}\}
satisfy Assumption
A
, then weakly as probability measures

(2.4)

\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu_{c,\Sigma},

where
\mu_{c,\Sigma}
is the
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible distribution with Lévy pair
(c,\Sigma)
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
relies on a novel combination of the saddle point method and free probability identities, and we will outline it in Section
2.4
.
There, we will explain that
-\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(nu)}{f_{n}(nu)}
is ideally the
R
-transform of an
\boxplus
-ID measure
\mu_{n}
, which is close to
\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket
and converges to
\mu_{c,\Sigma}
. For
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
and compactly supported measures on
\mathbb{R}
, this approximation can be made rigorous using moments, see
[
34
,
10
]
, but we are especially interested in (stable) laws which have very few moments requiring new techniques.

In order to get a better picture of Theorem
2.1
, let us discuss some consequences. We immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of entire functions satisfying Assumption
A
. Then,

(2.5)

\llbracket J_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu_{c,\Sigma}^{-1},

where
\mu_{c,\Sigma}^{-1}
is the push-forward of the measure
\mu_{c,\Sigma}
by the map
x\mapsto x^{-1}
.

One particularly illuminating special case of Theorem
2.1
is the case when
f_{n}
is defined via a fixed function
f
, up to a shift and normalization of the roots of
A_{n,f}
.
Then, Assumption
A
becomes a condition on the behavior of the root density of
f
near
\pm\infty
. For any
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
we define

\displaystyle n_{f,+}(r)

\displaystyle=|\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\alpha_{j}^{-1}\in(0,r)\times\ii[-M,M]\}|

\displaystyle n_{f,-}(r)

\displaystyle=|\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\alpha_{j}^{-1}\in(-r,0)\times\ii[-M,M]\}|

and
n_{f}(r)=n_{f,+}(r)+n_{f,-}(r)
. Let

(2.6)

b_{n}=\inf\{b>0:n_{f}(b)\geq n\},

be the pseudo-inverse and, for any real sequence
E_{n}\to E\in\mathbb{R}
, define

(2.7)

a_{n}=E_{n}-c\frac{b_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{3}}{1+(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}},

where

comes from the definition (
1.1
) of
f
. A function

\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(xr)}{g(r)}=x^{\alpha}.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let

(1)

If

\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mathsf{sc_{\sigma}},

Corollary 2.4
.

Let

(2.12)

\xi(z)=\frac{1}{2}z(1-z)\pi^{-z/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)\zeta(z),

(2.13)

\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{1}{x^{2}+1}\mathrm{d}x.

The Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that

. However, all that can currently be said about

So far, all our results on Appell polynomials can be understood as the operator

applied to the monomial

with initial root distribution being the Dirac delta

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{LP}

where

Corollary

extends Kabluchko’s

For the previous results we assumed that

has a root of multiplicity

at

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\tilde{f}_{n}(z)=z^{m_{n}}f_{n}(z)

We also obtain the following full local description of the roots of

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}
satisfy Assumption

has bounded density at

Informally, Theorem

In the classical example

2.2.
Results for Pólya–Schur type operators

For Pólya–Schur type operators we will focus on the analogues of Corollary
2.5
. We will consider two versions of the result. First, we will consider the special case when
T=f(z\partial_{z})
for some function
f\in\mathcal{LP}
with only negative roots. Second, we will consider general
T
classified by the Pólya–Schur theorem.

Proposition 2.8

(Pólya–Schur Theorem)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
be a linear operator which commutes with the diagonal (in the monomial bases) operator
z\partial_{z}
and let
\tau_{k}
be such that
Tz^{k}=\tau_{k}z^{k}
. Additionally, let
\Phi_{T}
be the formal power series

(2.17)

\Phi_{T}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{k}}{k!}z^{k}=T\left[\exp(z)\right].

Then,
T
preserves real-rootedness if and only if either
\Phi_{T}(z)
or
\Phi_{T}(-z)
is an entire function in
\mathcal{LPI}
. Moreover,
T
maps non-negative roots to real roots if and only if
\Phi_{T}\in\mathcal{LP}
.

To classify the limiting distribution we will need to briefly introduce the free multiplicative distribution
\boxtimes
. To this end define the
\psi
-transform of a probability measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
to be the function
\psi_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z:z^{-1}\in\supp\mu\}
given by

(2.18)

\psi_{\mu}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{uz}{1-uz}\mathrm{d}\mu(u).

If
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
,
\psi_{\mu}
has an inverse defined on a neighborhood of
(-1+\mu(\{0\}),0)
and the
S
-transform of
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
is the function defined by

(2.19)

S_{\mu}(z)=\frac{1+z}{z}\psi_{\mu}^{-1}(z),

for
z\in(-1+\mu(\{0\}),0)
. The free multiplicative convolution of two probability measures,
\mu_{1}\neq\delta_{0}
and
\mu_{2}\neq\delta_{0}
, on
[0,\infty)
is the probability measure
\mu_{1}\boxtimes\mu_{2}
with
S
-transform

(2.20)

S_{\mu_{1}\boxtimes\mu_{2}}(z)=S_{\mu_{1}}(z)S_{\mu_{2}}(z),

for
z
on some common interval
(-\varepsilon,0)
with
S_{\mu_{1}}
and
S_{\mu_{2}}
both defined. A probability measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
is
\boxtimes
-infinitely divisible (or
\boxtimes
-ID) if
\mu^{\boxtimes t}
exists for all
t>0
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{LP}
be a sequence of functions with only negative roots and which satisfies Assumption
A
. If
t\geq 0
and
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of positively-rooted polynomials indexed by their degree such that
\llbracket p_{n}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu
as

, then

(2.21)

\left\llbracket f_{\lfloor tn\rfloor}(tz\partial_{z})p_{n}(z)\right\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu\boxtimes\tilde{\mu}_{c,\Sigma}^{\boxtimes t},

where
\tilde{\mu}_{c,\Sigma}
is the
\boxtimes
-infinitely divisible probability measure on
[0,\infty)
with
S
-transform

(2.22)

S_{\tilde{\mu}_{c,\Sigma}}(z)=\exp\left(-R_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z+1)\right),

for

.

The special cases when
f(z)=e^{-z^{2}+z}
or
f(z)=(z+b)^{c}
were previously studied in
[
53
,
56
]
under the names of multiplicative Hermite and multiplicative Laguerre polynomials, respectively.

A definition for
\mu\boxtimes\nu
where only
\nu
(or
\mu
by symmetry) is assumed to be supported on

has been available using operator algebras since
[
88
]
, however a definition in terms of

-transforms was not available until the recent work of Arizmendi, Hasebe, and Kitagawa

[

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\{T_{n}\}
be a series of operators which commute with

such that
\Phi_{T_{n}}\in\mathcal{LP}
as defined in (
2.17

\left\llbracket T_{n}p_{n}\right\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu\boxtimes\mathcal{D}_{-1}\mu_{c,\Sigma}^{-1},

Remark 2.11

The polynomials considered in Theorem

2.3.
Results for rectangular differentiation

We next consider a class of operators

which do not commute with

such that

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{LPI}
and satisfy Assumption

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathsf{MP}_{1,\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\lambda x}\sqrt{\big((1+\sqrt{\lambda})^{2}-x\big)\big(x-(1-\sqrt{\lambda}\big)^{2}}\big)\mathrm{d}x\text{ on }x\in[(1-\sqrt{\lambda})^{2},(1+\sqrt{\lambda})^{2}].

The case of

with non-negative roots, similar to Corollary

2.4.
Outline of the proofs

The proof of Theorem
2.1
will follow from the representation of any Appell polynomial in terms of
f
, given by

(2.26)

\displaystyle A_{n+d,f}(z)

\displaystyle=f(\partial_{z})z^{n+d}

\displaystyle=f(\partial_{z})\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii}\oint_{\Gamma}e^{xz}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{n+d+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii}\oint_{\Gamma}f(x)e^{xz}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{n+d+1}},

where
\Gamma
is any simple closed contour around the origin. The idea behind the saddle point method is to choose the contour
\Gamma
such that
\left|f(x)e^{xz}/x^{n}\right|
is maximized on
\Gamma
at a single point
x_{n}^{*}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and that the integral is asymptotic to the contribution in a neighborhood of this point. Holomorphic functions do not have local maximums, but the point
x_{n}^{*}
should still be at a critical point of
f(x)e^{zx}/x^{n}
. Solving for this critical point we are looking for solutions to

\frac{f(x)e^{xz}}{x^{n}}n\left(\frac{z}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}-\frac{1}{x}\right)=0.

This equation likely has an infinite number of solutions. If
f\in\mathcal{LP}
, then
\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}
is the
R
-transform of the
\boxplus
-ID measure
\mu_{f,n}=\mu_{f}^{\boxplus\frac{1}{n}}
, see
[
34
, §2.1]
. By (
1.10
), one critical point is given by

x_{n}^{*}(z)=G_{\mu_{f,n}}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right),

which is separated from all the critical points close to
\mathbb{R}
.
To justify that this saddle point is dominating the others on some carefully chosen contour
\Gamma
, and that this fact continues to hold for
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
, is one of the technical steps of the proof. Here, we will make use of Assumption
A
, the unique definition (
1.10
) of the
R
-transform in the relevant domain, and some harmonic analysis involving the limiting saddle point equation as well as topological arguments on its sublevel sets, see Figure
1
below.

If we assume that the saddle point argument holds, then

A_{n+d,f}(z)\approx\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii}\int_{x\approx x_{n}^{*}(z)}f(x)e^{xz}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{n+d+1}}.

Then, applying this with
d=-1,0
we arrive at the following heuristic approximation of the Cauchy transform
G_{n}
of
\llbracket A_{n,f}\rrbracket

G_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\frac{A_{n,f}^{\prime}(z)}{A_{n,f}(z)}=\frac{A_{n-1,f}(z)}{A_{n,f}(z)}\approx\frac{\frac{(n-1)!}{2\pi\ii}\int_{x\approx x_{n}^{*}(z)}f(x)e^{xz}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{n}}}{\frac{n!}{2\pi\ii}\int_{x\approx x_{n}^{*}(z)}f(x)e^{xz}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{n+1}}}\approx nx_{n}^{*}(z)=nG_{\mu_{f,n}}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right).

From here one would want to normalize the polynomials in
n
such that the right-hand side converges to the Cauchy transform of some probability measure. This argument is made rigorous in Section
4
.
Rescaling the full asymptotic of the saddle point method then allows to obtain the local spacing of Theorem
2.7
.

The proofs of our other results follow from our general main Theorem
2.1
, or technical results proven along the way, in combination with identities in free probability. For instance, the proof of Theorem
2.9
does not need another saddle point problem, but instead uses exponential profiles of the respective coefficients obtained from the logarithmic potential of
\Sigma
.

3.
Expanded background and related work

In this section we present more technical background needed in the proofs and how our work relates to the existing literature.

3.1.
Free probability

We now discuss free probability in more detail, however we still encourage the interested reader to see
[
71
,
72
]
and other texts for a more thorough introduction to free probability.

There exists a bijection between
\boxplus
-ID measures and classical infinitely divisible measures, known as the Bercovici–Pata bijection after
[
20
]
. We omit the details, but the Bercovici–Pata bijection is in fact a larger bijection between probability measures which preserves the domains of attraction to infinitely-divisible distributions. So the theory of classical infinite-divisibility can be immediately translated in many cases to free probability. It is worth pointing out that in
[
20
,
21
,
19
,
22
]
and other references we will use, it is more common to work with the
Voiculescu
transform

(3.1)

\phi_{\mu}(z)=R_{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right),

defined on the upper-half plane and the
F
-transform

(3.2)

F_{\mu}(z)=\frac{1}{G_{\mu}(z)}.

For our purposes, it is more convenient to work with the
R
-transform and Cauchy transform, and the results we need to apply from these other papers can be immediately translated to our setting using (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).

Free Lévy processes were introduced by Biane
[
24
]
in the study of processes
(X_{t})_{t>0}
with free increments. Notably these processes do not necessarily have both stationary increments and stationary Markov transition functions, leading to two different potential definitions of what could naturally be called a free Lévy process. When we refer to the distribution of a free Lévy process we always mean stationary increments with distributions of the form
\mu_{t}=\mu^{\boxplus t}
for some
\boxplus
-ID
\mu
.

Free Lévy processes are a helpful way of understanding why the limit in Corollary
2.3
must be free stable. For
f\in\mathcal{LP}
, let
\mu_{f}
denote the
\boxplus
-ID measure with
R
-transform
R_{\mu_{f}}(u)=-\frac{f^{\prime}(u)}{f(u)}
. As discussed in Section
2.4
, the measure
\mu_{n}
with
R
-transform
R_{\mu_{n}}(u)=R_{\mu_{f}}(nu)
is our
\boxplus
-ID approximation of
\llbracket A_{n,f}\rrbracket
. It additionally follows that
\mu_{n}=\mathcal{D}_{n}\mu_{f}^{\boxplus{1}/{n}}
. If
\mu_{n}
is a good approximation of
\llbracket A_{n,f}\rrbracket
, then the limit in Corollary
2.3
must be the small time limit of a shifted and re-scaled free Lévy process. The only possible such limits must be stable for both classical and free Lévy processes
[
13
,
61
]
, and the conditions of Corollary
2.3
are known to be necessary and sufficient for this limit to exist.

3.2.
Finite free probability

Finite free probability emerged out the celebrated work of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava
[
62
,
63
,
64
]
on interlacing polynomials. It has become a successful tool for studying how differential operators affect polynomial roots. We encourage the interested reader to see
[
14
,
64
,
52
]
for more on finite free probability.

The key tools for extending the actions of differential operators from monomials to more general polynomials are the finite free additive
\boxplus_{n}
and multiplicative
\boxtimes_{n}
convolutions on the space of polynomials. These have many equivalent definitions, see
[
64
, §1.1]
, even though the original definition already appeared in
[
91
]
and
[
83
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
be degree
n
monic polynomials with corresponding polynomials
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
of degree
n
such that
p_{n}(z)=P_{n}(\partial_{z})z^{n}
and
q_{n}(z)=Q_{n}(\partial_{z})z^{n}
, respectively. Then, the finite free additive convolution of
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
is the unique degree
n
polynomial
p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n}
such that

(3.3)

p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n}(z)=Q_{n}(\partial_{z})P_{n}(\partial_{z})z^{n}=Q_{n}(\partial_{z})p_{n}(z)=P_{n}(\partial_{z})q_{n}(z).

Equivalently, if
p_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(p_{n})\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k}
and
q_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(q_{n})\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k}
, then

p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}z^{n-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}e_{j}(p_{n})e_{k-j}(q_{n})\Bigg).

For our work, (
3.3
) will turn out to be most convenient. The polynomials
P_{n},Q_{n}
are unique if their degree
n
is fixed, but the same holds for any other formal power series that is equivalent modulo
z^{n}
.
If
p_{n},q_{n}
are characteristic polynomials of Hermitian
n\times n
matrices
A,B
, one can also represent
p_{n}\boxplus q_{n}
as the characteristic polynomial of
A+UBU^{*}
for Haar-unitary
U
. This explains the name of the
finite
analogue of the free convolution.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
be degree
n
monic polynomials with corresponding unique polynomials
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
such that
p_{n}(z)=P_{n}(z\partial_{z})(z-1)^{n}
and
q_{n}(z)=Q_{n}(\partial_{z})(z-1)^{n}
, respectively.
Then, the finite free multiplicative convolution of
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
is the unique degree
n
polynomial
p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n}
such that

p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n}(z)=Q_{n}(z\partial_{z})P_{n}(z\partial_{z})(z-1)^{n}.

Equivalently, if
p_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(p_{n})\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k}
and
q_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(q_{n})\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k}
, then

p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(p_{n})e_{k}(q_{n})\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k}.

These convolutions have many interesting properties, including the fact that
p_{n}\mapsto p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n}
preserves real roots for any real-rooted
q_{n}
, and
p_{n}\mapsto p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n}
preserves real roots for any non-negatively-rooted
q_{n}
, see
[
64
]
. We will use that these induce convolutions on empirical root measures, which “converge” to the free convolutions of §
1.3
in the large degree limit, see
[
65
,
14
,
11
,
9
,
52
,
40
]
for various settings and proofs.

Lemma 3.3

3.3.
Differential operators on polynomials

Finite free probability has lead to significant progress on understanding the effects of differential operators acting on polynomial roots and how they connect to free probability. Two operators which have received substantial attention in free probability are
\partial_{z}^{tn}
and
e^{-\frac{t}{2n}\partial_{z}^{2}}
, where
n
is the degree of the polynomial on which they act. Let us discuss the previous results in more detail.

For
repeated differentiation
, the corresponding Appell polynomial is trivially a rescaled monomial again, whose limiting zero distribution is
\delta_{0}
. Historically, the most fundamental result is the Gauss–Lucas theorem
[
66
, Theorem 6.1]
, locating the roots of the derivative inside the convex hull of the roots of the original polynomial. In particular, a finite number of differentiations
\partial_{z}^{k}p_{n}
does not change the limiting root distribution
\llbracket p_{n}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu
, see
[
30
,
84
,
68
,
3
,
4
,
69
]
. Interestingly, if the order of differentiation
\partial_{z}^{tn}
is the same as the degree of the polynomial, then a new limiting root distribution emerges, which first have been described via non-local PDE’s in

[
81
]
. It was then connected to free projections and free convolution powers in
[
79
,
82
,
67
,
58
]
. Finally, the weak convergence
\llbracket\partial_{z}^{tn}p_{n}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{1-t}\mu^{\frac{1}{1-t}}
was proven in
[
50
]
, see also Corollary
2.6
and
[
11
]
. By now, generalizations include
(z^{a}\partial_{z}^{b})^{tn}(z-1)^{n}
with some
\boxtimes
-ID limiting root distribution, see
[
52
]
, and the polar differentiation with limiting distribution described via the Möbius transform. Moreover, local descriptions of roots under repeated differentiation are available from an approximation
\partial_{z}^{n-d}p_{n}(\tfrac{z}{\sqrt{n}})\approx\mathrm{He}_{d}(z)
for fixed
d
and as
n\to\infty
, see
[
49
,
33
,
8
,
42
]
. Notably, the framework has been generalized to some complex rooted polynomials
\partial_{z}^{tn}p_{n}
, see
[
32
,
31
,
47
,
41
]
, but still the full universality of complex rooted polynomials remains open.

For the (holomorphic, backwards)
heat flow
, we saw in Corollary
2.3
that the corresponding Appell polynomials are rescaled probabilist’s Hermite polynomials
\exp(-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2n}\partial_{z}^{2})z^{n}=\He_{n}(\sqrt{n}z/\sigma)
whose limiting root distribution is the semicircle law
\mathsf{sc_{\sigma}}
of variance
\sigma^{2}
. More generally, it has been shown in
[
55
,
90
,
53
]
that if
p_{n}
is real rooted with
\llbracket p_{n}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu
, then
\llbracket\exp(-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2n}\partial_{z}^{2})p_{n}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu\boxplus\mathsf{sc}_{\sigma}
is the free heat semigroup. Also here, specific settings of complex rooted polynomials have been studied in
[
45
,
44
,
46
,
48
]
. Related exponentiated differential operators of the form
\exp\big(-(\frac{z\partial_{z}}{n})^{k}\partial_{z}\big)z^{n}
have been studied in
[
53
]
whose limiting root distributions is some specific
\boxtimes
-ID distribution, as well as
f\big(\frac{\partial_{z}}{n}\big)^{n}z^{n}=(\mathcal{D}_{1/n}A_{n,f})^{\boxplus_{n}n}(z)
in
[
34
]
whose limiting root distribution is some specific
\boxplus
-ID distribution.

Therefore,
\partial_{z}^{tn}p(z)
and
e^{-\frac{t}{2n}\partial_{z}^{2}}p(z)
provide finite free analogues of
free projection
and
free Brownian motion
, respectively. Since both
z^{\lfloor tn\rfloor}
and
e^{-\frac{t}{2n}{z}^{2}}
are in
\mathcal{LP}
, Corollary
2.6
unites these previous result and generalizes them to the largest possible class of power series in
\partial_{z}
which preserve real roots.
Many of the previous results on roots of real-rooted polynomials under differential flows primarily rely on combinatorial tools from finite free probability involving moments of the roots, hence they are intrinsically restricted to (1) real rooted polynomials, (2) usually with bounded support and (3) global results on the limiting root distribution.

Remark 3.6
.

Let us comment on a PDE-perspective of the differentiation flow
\llbracket f_{\lfloor tn\rfloor}\left(t\partial_{z}\right)p_{n}(z)\rrbracket\Rightarrow\mu\boxplus\mu_{c,\Sigma}^{\boxplus t}
of Corollary
2.5
. For the heat flow of time
t>0
and at any initial distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, it goes back to Voiculescu
[
89
, §4.7]
that the Cauchy transform
G_{t}
of the distribution
\mu\boxplus\mathsf{sc}_{t}
satisfies the Burger’s equation

(3.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G_{t}(z)=-G_{t}(z)\partial_{z}G_{t}(z)\text{ on }z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\text{ with }G_{0}(z)=G_{\mu}(z),

see also
[
23
,
86
]
. More generally, for a free convolution flow
\mu\boxplus\mu_{c,\Sigma}^{\boxplus t}
, its
R
-transform satisfies
R_{t}=R_{\mu}+tR_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}
by Definition
1.4
. Hence, the inverse Cauchy transform is given by
G_{t}^{-1}=G_{\mu}^{-1}+tR_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}
. Differentiating yields
0=\partial_{t}\big(G_{t}(G_{\mu}^{-1}(z)+tR_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z))\big)
, which yields

(3.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G_{t}(z)=-R_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(G_{t}(z))\partial_{z}G_{t}(z)\text{ on }z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\text{ with }G_{0}(z)=G_{\mu}(z),

generalizing the Burger’s equation (
3.8
).

3.4.
The Pólya–Schur program

The Pólya–Schur program
[
25
,
26
,
36
,
18
,
76
,
78
,
59
]
is a series of results aiming to solve the following problem.

Problem 3.7
.

For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C},\Omega)\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
denote all univariate polynomials with all their zeros in
\Omega
. Classify the set of all linear operators
T
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})
such that
T(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C},\Omega))\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C},\Omega)
.

A natural and important version of this problem is the case of operators preserving real roots. Special cases date back to the works of Laguerre, Pólya, Schur, and Benz
[
18
,
76
,
78
,
59
]
among others. The full classification was given by Borcea and Brändén
[
25
]
, nearly a century after these classical results.

One can go beyond asking not only
where
the zeros are distributed, but also
how
they are distributed in the large degree limit.

Question 3.8
.

For a sequence of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C},\Omega)
of growing degree and an operator
T
preserving roots in
\Omega
, how are the roots of
Tp_{n}
distributed as
n\rightarrow\infty
?

Our results answer this question for the operators classified by the classical works of Pólya, Schur, and Benz.
Laguerre provided a earlier family of operators which preserve real roots, which can be seen as a special case of the Pólya–Schur theorem.

Lemma 3.9

(Laguerre’s Theorem)
.

Let
f
be a function in the Laguerre–Pólya such that
f
has only negative roots. If all the roots of
p
are real, then all the roots of
f(z\partial_{z})p(z)
are real.

Problem
3.7
remains open in the case when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and we were unable to find a version of the following lemma in the literature.

Lemma 3.10

(Weak Pólya–Schur Theorem for
\mathscr{L}_{\beta}
)
.

Let
T
be a linear operator that commutes with
\mathscr{L}_{\beta}
.
T[z^{n}]
has only non-negative roots for every positive integer
n
if and only if

(3.10)

T=f(\mathscr{L}_{\beta}),

for some function
f\in\mathcal{LPI}
.

The proof of this lemma follows much in the same way as the Pólya–Schur theorem, and we include it in Appendix
A
. We were also unable to find a proof of either the Pólya–Schur or Pólya–Benz theorems in any open source reference in English. The “only if” direction of our proof of Lemma
3.10
can also serve as a proof of the Pólya–Benz theorem with
\mathscr{L}_{\beta}
replaced by
\partial_{z}
plus some obvious adjustments.

4.
The proof of Theorem
2.1

To prove Theorem
2.1
we proceed by proving asymptotics for the Appell polynomials as
n\rightarrow\infty
in Theorem
4.4
below. Before stating this theorem we establish some notation and set up the main ideas of the proofs.

Applying (
2.26
) to
A_{n+d,f_{n}}
for some fixed
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we set up a saddle point argument, where the exact contour may change from line to line,

(4.1)

\displaystyle A_{n+d,f_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii}\oint_{\Gamma}e^{xz}f_{n}(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{n+d+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii}\oint_{\Gamma}\exp\left(xz+\log f_{n}(x)-n\log x\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii n^{n+d}}\oint_{\Gamma}\exp\left(n\left[uz+\frac{1}{n}\log f_{n}(nu)-n\log u\right]\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}.

We define the function

(4.2)

h_{n}(u):=zu+\frac{1}{n}\log f_{n}(nu)-\log u,

where
\log f_{n}(nu)
is an analytic function on the simply connected domain
\{u:\operatorname{Im}u<-\frac{M_{n}}{n}\}
with real part
\log|f_{n}(nu)|
and such that
e^{\log f_{n}(nu)}=f_{n}(nu)
. This uniquely defines
h_{n}
on this domain up to an imaginary constant where the exact choice has no effect on the value of

(4.3)

\int_{\Gamma^{\prime}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}},

for any curve
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\{u:\operatorname{Im}u<-\frac{M_{n}}{n}\}
.
h_{n}
can also be defined in an analogous way as a holomorphic function on any simply connected domain which does not contain
0
nor a root of
f_{n}(nu)
.

In the saddle point method one chooses the contour
\Gamma
to pass through a critical point
u_{n}^{*}
of
h_{n}
such that
\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u)<\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u_{n}^{*})
for all other
u\in\Gamma
and the integral can be approximated using a Taylor expansion of
h_{n}
about
u_{n}^{*}
, and the contribution from the remainder of the contour is asymptotically negligible. See
[
73
]
for more details on the saddle point method. It is worth noting that
h_{n}
is not holomorphic on a neighborhood of the entire contour
\Gamma
, which is a typical assumption for the most straightforward rigorous statements of the saddle point method, for example in
[
73
, Theorem 1.2]
. However, the function we are truly integrating in (
4.1
) is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and we can define
h_{n}
piece-wise on simply connected neighborhoods of
\Gamma
which cover
\Gamma
minus possibly a finite number of points at zeros of
f_{n}(nu)
, such that
\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u)
is continuous on
\Gamma
, minus possibly a finite number of points where
\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u)
tends to
-\infty
, and

(4.4)

\oint_{\Gamma}\exp\left(nh_{n}(u)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}=\oint_{\Gamma}e^{nuz}f_{n}(nu)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{n+d+1}}.

We will elaborate more in the proof of Theorem
4.4
and for now we proceed to study
h_{n}
on the domain
\{u:\operatorname{Im}u<-\frac{M_{n}}{n}\}
.

We are interested in solutions to the saddle point equation

(4.5)

h_{n}^{\prime}(u)=z+\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(nu)}{f_{n}(nu)}-\frac{1}{u}=0.

This equation may have an infinite number of solutions in
\mathbb{C}
, particularly near the roots of
f_{n}(nu)
for large
n
, and it is not immediately obvious which of these is actually relevant to our analysis. To help identify this distinguished point we define the meromorphic function

(4.6)

R_{n}(u)=-\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(nu)}{f_{n}(nu)}.

If
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
, then
R_{n}
is in fact the
R
-transform of a
\boxplus
-ID probability measure
\mu_{n}
and

(4.7)

G_{\mu_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{u}+R_{n}(u)\right)=u,

for all
u\in\Omega=\{w\in\mathbb{C}_{-}:\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{1}{w}+R_{n}(w)\right)>0\}
, where
G_{\mu_{n}}
is the Cauchy transform of
\mu_{n}
.

It is now reasonable to guess that
R_{n}
is close to an
R
-transform, and that solutions
u_{n}^{*}(z)
to the saddle point equation
R_{n}(u)+1/u=z
are close to a Cauchy transform. Let us record these observations in the following proposition and lemma.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfy Assumption
A
.

(1)

Let
R_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}
be the
R
-transform of
\mu_{c,\Sigma}
, then

(4.8)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(nu)}{f_{n}(nu)}=R_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(u),

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}_{-}
.

(2)

Define
\left[\log(1-xw)\frac{x^{2}+1}{x^{2}}+\frac{w}{x}\right]\Big|_{x=0}:=-\frac{w^{2}}{2}
by continuous extension, then

(4.9)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log f_{n}(nu)-\frac{1}{n}\log f_{n}(-n\ii)=-c(u+\ii)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\frac{1-ux}{1+\ii x}\right)\frac{x^{2}+1}{x^{2}}+\frac{u+\ii}{x}\mathrm{d}\Sigma(x),

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}_{-}
.

(3)

For any
M\in\mathbb{R}
define
\log_{M}|z|:=\max(\log|z|,-M)
, then

(4.10)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log_{M}|f_{n}(nu)|-\frac{1}{n}\log_{M}|f_{n}(-n\ii)|

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left(-c(u+\ii)\right)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\log_{M}\left|{1-ux}\right|-\log_{M}\left|{1+\ii x}\right|\right]\frac{x^{2}+1}{x^{2}}+\operatorname{Re}\frac{u+\ii}{x}\mathrm{d}\Sigma(x),

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}_{-}\cup\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

The choice of
\ii
in
\frac{1}{n}\log f_{n}(-n\ii)
in (
4.9
) was arbitrary. The purpose of this term is to guarantee an antiderivative of
R_{n}
which is not blowing up in
n
.

Remark 4.2
.

As discussed in Remark
2.11
, the asymptotic (
4.9
) is equivalent to convergence of the exponential profiles of the polynomials considered in Theorem
2.9
, as we will see during its proof. Additionally, recalling that
\Delta\log|z|=2\pi\delta_{0}
in the distributional sense, it is not hard to show (
4.9
) is equivalent to Assumption
A
when
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
.

The following lemma captures the properties of solutions to (
4.5
) we will need.

Lemma 4.3
.

(1)

Let
\mu
be any freely infinitely divisible distribution. Then, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{R}:-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G_{\mu}(z+\ii 0)<0\}

(4.11)

z-\frac{1}{u}-R_{\mu}(u)=0

has a unique solution
G_{\mu}(z)
in
\mathbb{C}_{-}
. Hence, if
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{R}:-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z+\ii 0)<0\}
, then (
4.5
) has a unique solution in
\mathbb{C}_{-}
given by
u_{n}^{*}(z)=G_{\mu_{n}}(z)
.

(2)

If
\{f_{n}\}
satisfy Assumption
A
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
(
4.5
) has at most one solution in
\{u\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}u<-\varepsilon\}
for
n
sufficiently large. Additionally, if
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then this unique solution
u_{n}^{*}(z)
exists.

(3)

Let
\{f_{n}\}
satisfy Assumption
A
and let
B\subset\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{R}:-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z+\ii 0)<0\}
be a compact subset. Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough such that (
4.5
) has a unique solution
u=u_{n}^{*}(z)\in\{u\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}u<-\varepsilon\}
for all
z\in B
. Then,
u_{n}^{*}
is analytic on a neighborhood of
B
and

(4.12)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}^{*}(z)=G_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z)=:u^{*}(z)

uniformly on
B
. Moreover,
u^{*}
can be analytically continued from the upper-half plane to a neighborhood of
\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{R}:-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G(z+\ii 0)<0\}
and we will denote this extension also by

.

For fixed

, we may drop its dependence and write
u_{n}^{*}(z)=u_{n}^{*}
and
u^{*}(z)=u^{*}
.
We are now ready to state our main result on the asymptotics of Appell sequences.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions satisfying Assumption
A
and let
d
be a fixed integer.

(1)

If
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, then

(4.13)

\displaystyle A_{n+d,f_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{n^{n+d}}\sqrt{\frac{(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi n}}\frac{f_{n}\left(nu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)\exp\left(nzu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)}{u_{n}^{*}(z)^{n+d+1}}(1+o(1)).

(2)

If
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z+\ii 0)<0
, then

(4.14)

\displaystyle A_{n+d,f_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{(n+d)!}{n^{n+d}}\sqrt{\frac{(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi n}}\frac{f_{n}\left(nu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)\exp\left(nzu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)}{u_{n}^{*}(z)^{n+d+1}}\right](1+o(1)),

where
(u^{*})^{\prime}
is the derivative of the analytic extension of
u^{*}
from
\mathbb{C}_{+}
to
\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{R}:-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G(z+\ii 0)<0\}
as defined in Lemma
4.3
.

Moreover, (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) hold uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\{z\in\mathbb{R}:-\infty<\operatorname{Im}G_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z+\ii 0)<0\}
, respectively.

Note that by Stirling’s formula, the prefactor satisfies
\frac{(n+d)!}{n^{n+d}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi n}}\sim e^{-n}
. However, while we only consider fixed
d
we choose to leave the prefactor in this form as a potential prediction when
d
is growing with
n
.

With Proposition
4.1
, Lemma
4.3
, and Theorem
4.4
in hand we are ready to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
G_{n}
be the Cauchy transform of
\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}\rrbracket
. It is straightforward to check that because
\{A_{j,f_{n}}\}_{j\geq 1}
is an Appell sequence that

(4.15)

G_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\frac{A_{n,f_{n}}^{\prime}(z)}{A_{n,f_{n}}(z)}=\frac{A_{n-1,f_{n}}(z)}{A_{n,f_{n}}(z)}.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
we have by Theorem
4.4
, (
4.13
) and Lemma
4.3
that

(4.16)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}G_{n}(z)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}^{*}(z)(1+o(1))=G_{\mu_{c,\Sigma}}(z).

As
A_{n,f_{n}}
is a real polynomial it is then trivial to prove a similar limit for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{-}
. Let
B\subset\mathbb{C}_{+}
be some set with piece-wise
C^{1}

Let

We then have that

Following
[

one can compute explicitly, either from the Hessian of

Let

. Moreover, since

We can adjust the contour

. In particular, we deform the part of contour

u=u_{n}^{*}(z)+\frac{\phi w}{\sqrt{n}}
for some

for

. Finally, we connect these two adjusted pieces along the circle

. Let
\Gamma_{+,n}
be the reflection of
\Gamma_{-,n}
across the real line, and define

. Observe that for any

(4.35)

H_{n}(\overline{u})\leq H_{n}(u),

for any
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
with equality if and only if
\operatorname{Im}(z)=0
.

Let
\Gamma_{-,n,1}=\{u\in\Gamma_{-,n}:|u-u_{n}^{*}|<\tilde{\varepsilon}/2\}=\Gamma_{-,n}\cap B_{\tilde{\varepsilon}/2}(u_{n}^{*})
and
\Gamma_{-,n,2}=\Gamma_{-,n}\setminus\Gamma_{-,n,1}

to be the contour in (

) and divide the integral into four pieces

\displaystyle\oint_{\Gamma_{n}}\exp\left(nh_{n}(u)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\{+,-\},k=1,2}\int_{\Gamma_{j,n,k}}\exp\left(nh_{n}(u)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}.

Again, we note that while
h_{n}
is only piece-wise holomorphic, it holds that
\operatorname{Re}h_{n}
is upper-semicontinous,
h_{n}
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\Gamma_{-,n,1}

\left|e^{nh_{n}(u)}\right|\leq\left|e^{nh_{n}(u_{n}^{*})}\right|,

for all

, with equality only if

.

We first consider the contribution from
\Gamma_{-,n,1}
. Let
\psi_{n}
be the holomorphic function such that
h_{n}(u)=h_{n}(u_{n}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}h_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u_{n}^{*})(u-u_{n}^{*})^{2}(1-\psi_{n}(u))

. By Proposition
4.1
,
\psi_{n}
converges uniformly to an analogous

with

replaced by

. Hence
\sup_{u\in B_{\varepsilon}(u^{*})}|\psi_{n}(u)|\leq C_{\psi}
for some

. This uniform bound is what allows us to adjust
[

]
to
h_{n}
depending on

. By the integral version of Taylor’s theorem
[

with
w=\sqrt{N}\phi^{-1}|(u_{n}^{*})^{\prime}(z)|^{-1/2}(u-u_{n}^{*})
such that

(4.37)

\displaystyle e^{n(h_{n}(u)-h_{n}(u_{n}^{*}))}\frac{1}{u^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=e^{-w^{2}/2}e^{w^{2}\psi_{n}(u)/2}\frac{1}{u^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=e^{-w^{2}/2}\left(\frac{1}{(u_{n}^{*}(z))^{d+1}}+\frac{(u-u_{n}^{*})}{2\pi\ii}\int_{|v-u_{n}^{*}|=\tilde{\varepsilon}}\frac{e^{w^{2}\psi_{n}(v)/2}}{(v-u_{n}^{*})(v-u)}\frac{1}{v^{d+1}}\mathrm{d}v\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-w^{2}/2}\left(\frac{1}{(u_{n}^{*}(z))^{d+1}}+O\left(\frac{e^{C_{\psi}w^{2}\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}}|u-u_{n}^{*}|\right)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-w^{2}/2}\frac{1}{(u_{n}^{*}(z))^{d+1}}+O(e^{-w^{2}/4}|u-u_{n}^{*}|),

if
\tilde{\varepsilon}
is sufficiently small, but independent of
n
. Making the change of variables

(4.38)

u=u_{n}^{*}(z)+\frac{\phi w}{\sqrt{n}},

it follows from (

) that for some

, which can be chosen uniformly in

by Proposition

(4.39)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=e^{nh_{n}(u_{n}^{*})}\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}^{\prime}}\frac{\exp\left(-\phi^{2}\frac{1}{(u_{n}^{*})^{\prime}\left(z\right)}\frac{w^{2}}{2}\right)+O(e^{-cw^{2}})n^{-1/2}}{\left(u_{n}^{*}(z)\right)^{d+1}}{\mathrm{d}w}

\displaystyle=e^{nh_{n}(u_{n}^{*})}\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}^{\prime}}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2|(u_{n}^{*})^{\prime}\left(z\right)|}\right)+O(e^{-cw^{2}})n^{-1/2}}{\left(u_{n}^{*}(z)\right)^{d+1}}\mathrm{d}w,

where
\Gamma_{-,n,1}^{\prime}
is the image of
\Gamma_{-,n,1}
in the

plane, and recall for the choice of
\Gamma_{-,n}
that
\Gamma_{-,n,1}^{\prime}
is a real interval
[-\sqrt{n}\tilde{\varepsilon}/2,\sqrt{n}\tilde{\varepsilon}/2]
. We note that again by Lemma
4.3
that
(u_{n}^{*})^{\prime}(z)
converges to

which is bounded away from

for both

and

such that

, which follows from (for example)
[

, Lemma 2.6]
. Hence,

\sup_{w\in[-\sqrt{n}\tilde{\varepsilon}/2,\sqrt{n}\tilde{\varepsilon}/2]}\left|\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2|(u_{n}^{*})^{\prime}\left(z\right)|}\right)+O(e^{-cw^{2}})n^{-1/2}}{\left(u_{n}^{*}(z)\right)^{d+1}}\right|\leq C\left|\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2|(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)|}\right)}{(u^{*}(z))^{d+1}}\right|,

for some constant

. We may then use the dominated convergence theorem to conclude that

(4.40)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=e^{nh_{n}(u_{n}^{*})}\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}^{\prime}}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2|(u_{n}^{*})^{\prime}\left(z\right)|}\right)+O(e^{-cw^{2}})n^{-1/2}}{\left(u_{n}^{*}(z)\right)^{d+1}}\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=e^{nh_{n}(u_{n}^{*})}\frac{\phi}{(u_{n}^{*}(z))^{d+1}\sqrt{n}}(1+o(1))\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2|(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)|}\right)\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-\frac{2\pi(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)}{n}}\frac{f_{n}\left(nu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)\exp\left(nzu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)}{u_{n}^{*}(z)^{n+d+1}}(1+o(1)).

We next show the contribution from
\Gamma_{-,n,2}
, and hence also
\Gamma_{+,n,2}
, is negligible. Since
\Gamma_{-,n,2}
is fixed away from the saddle point and
H_{n}
converges uniformly as in (
4.28
), we have
\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u)-\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u_{n}^{*})<-c
for some

and all

by Proposition

. Thus,

(4.41)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,2}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,2}}\exp(n\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{|u|^{d+1}}

\displaystyle<\exp({n\operatorname{Re}(h_{n}(u_{n}^{*}))})\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,2}}\exp(-cn)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{|u|^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=o\left(\left|\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}\right|\right),

where we used that the length of
\Gamma_{-,n}
is bounded in
n
.

For the contribution of
\Gamma_{+,n,1}
we have two cases: First, if
\operatorname{Im}z>0
, then a similar argument shows that the contribution is negligible, as
\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(\overline{u_{n}^{*}})-\operatorname{Re}h_{n}(u_{n}^{*})=\operatorname{Im}z\operatorname{Im}u_{n}^{*}<0
. In conclusion, using the contour integral representation (
4.1
) of Appell polynomials, we obtain the claim

\displaystyle A_{n+d,f_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii n^{n+d}}\oint_{\Gamma_{n}}\exp\left(nh_{n}(u)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+d)!}{n^{n+d}}\sqrt{\frac{(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi n}}\frac{f_{n}\left(nu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)\exp\left(nzu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)}{u_{n}^{*}(z)^{n+d+1}}(1+o(1)).

Second, if
z\in\mathbb{R}
, then
H_{n}(\bar{u})=\overline{H_{n}(u)}
and hence, we have two dominant saddle points
u_{n}^{*}(z)
and
\overline{u_{n}^{*}(z)}
along
\Gamma_{n}
. Additionally, the direction of travel along
\Gamma_{n}
at
\overline{u_{n}^{*}(z)}
is the negative conjugate of the direction of travel at
u_{n}^{*}(z)
. Thus,

(4.42)

\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}=-\overline{\int_{\Gamma_{+,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}},

and

(4.43)

\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii n^{n+d}}\int_{\Gamma_{-,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}=\overline{\frac{(n+d)!}{2\pi\ii n^{n+d}}\int_{\Gamma_{+,n,1}}\exp(nh_{n}(u))\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{d+1}}}.

Hence the contributions of integrals around the two dominating conjugate saddle points to
A_{n,f_{n}}(z)
are conjugates of each other. This completes the proof.
∎

5.1.
Proof of Corollary
2.3

Fix some function
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
for some
M\geq 0
.

If
\sigma^{2}>0
, let
f_{p}(z)=e^{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}z^{2}}f(z)
be the non-Gaussian component of
f
. Then
f_{p}
is either a function of order less than
2
or of order
2
and type
0
. Define the finite measure

(5.1)

\Sigma_{f_{p}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|{\alpha_{j}^{2}}|\delta_{\alpha_{j}},

which has no mass at
\{0\}
. Since
f_{n}(z)=f(z/\sqrt{n})
we have
\alpha_{j,n}=\alpha_{j}/\sqrt{n}
, hence for any bounded

(5.2)

\displaystyle\left|\Sigma_{n}(B)-\sigma^{2}\delta_{0}(B)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\sqrt{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}{1+(\sqrt{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}\delta_{\alpha_{j}}(B/\sqrt{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\Sigma_{f_{p}}(B/\sqrt{n})\to 0,

by continuity from above of the finite measure
\Sigma_{f_{p}}
.
Additionally,
c_{n}=O(1/\sqrt{n})
is trivial. Therefore, Assumption
A
is satisfied with
c=0
and
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}\delta_{0}
, corresponding to the semicircle law.

If
\sigma^{2}=0
, let

(5.3)

f_{n}(z)=e^{-a_{n}z}f\left(\frac{b_{n}}{n}z\right)=Ce^{-c\frac{b_{n}}{n}z-\left(E_{n}-c\frac{b_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{3}}{1+(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}\right)z}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\alpha_{j}\frac{b_{n}z}{n}\right)e^{\alpha_{j}\frac{b_{n}}{n}z},

for
f
,
b_{n}
and
a_{n}
as in (
1.1
), (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), respectively. Then,

(5.4)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}c\frac{b_{n}}{n}+\left(E_{n}-c\frac{b_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{3}}{1+(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{3}}{1+(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}=E,

which gives the limit in (
2.3
) of Assumption
A
.

It follows from standard properties of regularly varying functions
[
77
, Proposition 0.8 (v)]
that
b_{n}=h(n)n^{1/\alpha}
for some slowly varying function
h
. The following computation, which proves convergence of
\Sigma_{n}
to
\Sigma_{\alpha,\theta}
when
f\in\mathcal{LP}
, will be used in the general case when
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
. Let
f\in\mathcal{LP}
,
r_{j}=\alpha_{j}^{-1}
denote the roots of
f
, and
\Lambda_{f}=\sum_{j}\delta_{r_{j}}
. It follows from integration by parts and our assumptions that for any
y>0

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}([0,y])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}{1+(b_{n}\alpha_{j})^{2}}\delta_{b_{n}\alpha_{j}}([0,y])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{n}^{2}}{r_{j}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}}\delta_{r_{j}}([b_{n}/y,\infty))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{b_{n}/y}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(t/b_{n})^{2}+1}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{f}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\frac{n_{f,+}(b_{n}/y)}{(1/y)^{2}+1}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{b_{n}/y}^{\infty}\frac{2t/b_{n}^{2}}{[(t/b_{n})^{2}+1]^{2}}n_{f,+}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\frac{n_{f,+}(b_{n}/y)}{(1/y)^{2}+1}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{1/y}^{\infty}\frac{2s}{[s^{2}+1]^{2}}n_{f,+}(b_{n}s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{\theta}{y^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{(1/y)^{2}+1}+\int_{1/y}^{\infty}\theta\frac{2s^{\alpha+1}}{[s^{2}+1]^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{x^{2}+1}\theta\alpha\mathrm{d}x,

where in the second to last line, we use (
2.10
) and the definition of regularly variation, and the final line is another integration by parts and change of variables. The last line gives (
1.12
) as claimed, and an identical computation gives the limits of
\Sigma_{n}([-y,0])
, completing the proof for
f_{n}\in\mathcal{LP}
.

We now show the general case
f\in\mathcal{LP}_{M}
. For any
B\subseteq\mathbb{C}
we let
\operatorname{Re}(B)
be the projection to
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
x\in\operatorname{Re}(B)
if and only if
\{x\}\times\ii\mathbb{R}\cap B\neq\emptyset
. We decompose
\Sigma_{n}
as two finite signed measures

\Sigma_{n}=\Sigma_{n,\mathbb{R}}+\ii\Sigma_{n,\ii\mathbb{R}}.

We next note for any measurable
B
the total variation of
\Sigma_{n,\ii\mathbb{R}}
for large
n
can be bounded by

(5.6)

\displaystyle|\Sigma_{n,\ii\mathbb{R}}|(B)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{b_{n}^{2}}{r_{j}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}}\right)\right|\delta_{b_{n}\alpha_{j}}(B)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2|\operatorname{Re}r_{j}|M}{b_{n}^{2}\left(1+\left[\frac{\operatorname{Re}r_{j}}{b_{n}}\right]^{2}\right)^{2}}\delta_{b_{n}\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{j}}(\operatorname{Re}B),

where the weight is bounded from above and changing from
\delta_{b_{n}\alpha_{j}}(B)
to
\delta_{b_{n}\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{j}}(\operatorname{Re}B)
over counts the number of
b_{n}\alpha_{j}
in the set. An argument with steps identical to (
5.5
) (using integration by parts and regular variation) shows

(5.7)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2|\operatorname{Re}r_{j}|M}{b_{n}^{2}\left(1+\left[\frac{\operatorname{Re}r_{j}}{b_{n}}\right]^{2}\right)^{2}}\delta_{b_{n}\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{j}}\rightarrow 0,

where we use
0
to denote the zero measure. Thus, the total variation of
\Sigma_{n,\ii\mathbb{R}}
converges vaguely to the zero measure, as does
\Sigma_{n,\ii\mathbb{R}}
.

For
\Sigma_{n,\mathbb{R}}
, an identical argument can be used to upper bound the total variation by

(5.8)

\displaystyle|\Sigma_{n,\mathbb{R}}|(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{b_{n}^{2}}{r_{j}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}}\right)\right|\delta_{b_{n}\alpha_{j}}(B)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{n}^{2}}{(\operatorname{Re}r_{j})^{2}+b_{n}^{2}}\delta_{b_{n}\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{j}}\left(\operatorname{Re}B\right),

for
n
sufficiently large so that

. As shown in (
5.5
) the measure on the right hand side of (

) converges to finite positive measure with no mass at

. Thus,

(5.9)

|\Sigma_{n}|(B_{\varepsilon}(0))=O_{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}\right),

where

is the asymptotic notation as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and the bound can be taken uniformly in
n
.

We next consider
\Sigma_{n}
on sets bounded away from
0
, where we drop the weight function and invert the support to consider the root counting measures

(5.10)

\Lambda_{f,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\delta_{r_{j}/b_{n}},

on bounded sets. Recall

, hence for any

,
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, and

sufficiently large

(5.11)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{f,n}\left((0,x)\times\ii[-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}n_{f,+}\left(x{b_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}n_{f}\left(xb_{n}\right)\frac{n_{f,+}\left(xb_{n}\right)}{n_{f}\left(x{b_{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle\sim\theta x^{\alpha}.

Similarly, for any

(5.12)

\Lambda_{f,n}\left((x,0)\times\ii[-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon]\right)\sim(1-\theta){|x|^{\alpha}}.

Additionally, if
B

, then
\Lambda_{f,n}(B)=0
for

sufficiently large. Thus,
\Lambda_{f,n}
converges vaguely to the measure

on

with density

\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{\alpha,\theta}(x)=\alpha\left(\theta\mathbf{1}_{{x>0}}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{1}_{{x<0}}\right)|x|^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}x.

We now combine these facts to complete the proof. Let

be a continuous function with compact support. Then, from (

\left|\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}f\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{n}\right|=O_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}),

uniformly in

. Additionally,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)^{c}}f(t)\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1/\varepsilon}(0)}f\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\frac{1}{s^{2}+1}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{f,n}(s)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\int_{B_{1/\varepsilon}(0)}f\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\frac{1}{s^{2}+1}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{\alpha,\theta}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)^{c}}f(t)\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{\alpha,\theta}(t)

Combining (
5.13
) and (

) completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem

Theorem

\displaystyle A_{n+d,f_{n}}(z)\sim 2\operatorname{Re}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{(n+d)!}{n^{n+d}}\sqrt{\frac{(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi n}}\frac{f_{n}\left(nu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)\exp\left(nzu_{n}^{*}(z)\right)}{u_{n}^{*}(z)^{n+d+1}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\sim 2\operatorname{Re}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{(n+d)!}{n^{n+d}u^{*}(z)^{d+1}}\sqrt{\frac{(u^{*})^{\prime}(z)}{2\pi n}}

Example 5.1

Recall that for any linear

6.1.
The proof of Theorem
2.9

We will work under the assumption that
t=1
. Extending to general
t>0
is straightforward and similar to what we have seen in the proof of Corollary
2.5
, and the statement is a tautology when
t=0
. Let
A_{n,f_{n}}^{*}(z)=f_{n}(z\partial_{z})(z-1)^{n}
.

It follows from Laguerre’s Theorem (Lemma
3.9
) that

has only real roots. Additionally, it follows from

that

A_{n,f_{n}}^{*}(z)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}f_{n}(k)z^{k}.

Its coefficients alternate, hence all the roots must be non-negative and since
f_{n}(0)\neq 0
all roots of

are positive.
Thus, to prove Theorem
2.9
it is sufficient by (

) and Lemma

to prove that

(6.3)

\llbracket A_{n,f_{n}}^{*}\rrbracket\Rightarrow\tilde{\mu}_{c,\Sigma}.

To this end, we make use of the following.

Lemma 6.1

(Proposition 5.1 in
[
53
]
)
.

Let

be a polynomial with real nonnegative roots of the form

(6.4)

and suppose for every

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}b_{\lfloor un\rfloor,n}=e^{\tilde{g}(u)}

for some function
\tilde{g}:(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then,
\llbracket Q_{n}\rrbracket
converges weakly to the probability measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
with

We aim to apply Lemma

to

Proof of the Pólya–Schur theorem for rectangular differentiation

Proof of Lemma
3.10
.

The “if” direction follows from (
7.2
) and
[
64
, Theorem 1.16]
, as discussed in the proof of Theorem
2.12
.

For the “only if” direction we follow the original approach of
[
78
,
18
]
. Define the polynomials

(A.1)

T[z^{n}]=p_{T,n}(z).

It follows from commutativity and a straightforward computation that
T
is degree non-increasing, i.e. upper-triangular with constant diagonal in the monomial basis
\{1,z,\dots\}

in the form

(A.2)

p_{T,n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}t_{k,n}\frac{\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\beta-k+1)}\binom{n}{k}z^{n-k}

From the commutativity relation

\mathscr{L}_{\beta}p_{T,n}(z)=Tn(n+\beta)z^{n-1}=n(n+\beta)p_{T,n-1}(z).

Comparing coefficients we see that

(A.4)

t_{k,n}\frac{\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\beta-k+1)}\binom{n}{k}(n-k)(n-k+\beta)=t_{k,n-1}\frac{\Gamma(n+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\beta-k)}\binom{n-1}{k}n(n+\beta),

and

(A.5)

t_{k,n}=t_{k,n-1}

and hence we drop the
n
notation and use
t_{k}
. We will assume for simplicity that
t_{0}=1
and we let
J_{T,n}(z)=z^{n}p_{T,n}(1/z)

t_{r}

and make the obvious adjustments below. For any
k
let
x_{1,k},\dots,x_{k,k}
denote the roots of
p_{T,k}
.
Then,

(A.6)

J_{T,k}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-x_{j,k}z\right),

(A.7)

\displaystyle t_{1}(k+\beta)k

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j,k},

\displaystyle t_{k}\frac{\Gamma(k+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}
