Development of a Dedicated Prostate Phantom for Technical Evaluation of Diffusion-weighted MR Imaging Sequences
Authors: Kazunori Ohashi, Shigehiro Soh, Yoshiomi Sato, Ryoma Miura, Ayumu Funaki, Kaoru Watanabe, Toshiyuki Iwahata, Kazutaka Saito, Kazunori Kubota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to demonstrate the technical feasibility of a dedicated prostate phantom for standardized qualitative evaluation of diffusion-weighted MRI sequences, focusing on signal-to-noise characteristics and image artifacts. A custom-designed phantom simulating prostate tissue, tumor lesions, and rectal gas was used to evaluate 6 diffusion-weighted imaging techniques at 1.5 and 3.0 tesla. Quantitative assessments included signal-to-noise measurements and susceptibility artifacts, while spatial distortion was evaluated by comparison with T2-weighted imaging. Qualitative image quality was assessed using corresponding clinical images as references. Clinical images were evaluated by a radiologist and a urologist, and phantom images were evaluated by 2 radiological technologists. The phantom demonstrated high inter-reader agreement for qualitative image assessment. Clinical image evaluation was performed to confirm whether artifact trends observed in the phantom were similarly observed in clinical images and showed comparable tendencies across sequences. These findings indicate that the proposed prostate phantom provides a practical and reproducible platform for qualitative evaluation.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second leading cause of cancer-related death in men, with its incidence expected to double by 2030 due to the global aging population.1 Prostate MRI is recognized for improving PCa detection and characterization. Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI), which includes T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging (DCE-MRI), is now the standard. Experts from the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) and the European Association of Urology (EAU) Section of Urologic Imaging (ESUI) recommend routine image quality assessment for diagnostic reliability. Biparametric MRI (bpMRI), which excludes DCE and uses only T2WI and DWI, has also demonstrated high diagnostic performance. A comparison between bpMRI and mpMRI showed comparable results across multiple subgroups. The ESUR published its first prostate MRI guidelines in 2012, and Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) was revised to version 2.1 in 2019. In PI-RADS, DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps are essential for lesion assessment. However, susceptibility artifacts from rectal gas can degrade image quality and impair interpretation. Single-shot echo-planar imaging DWI (SS-EPI DWI) remains the clinical standard. However, advanced DWI techniques have been developed to reduce artifacts. ZOOMit DWI (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) uses spatially selective RF pulses to narrow the FOV and shorten echo trains.2,3 Readout-segmented echo-planar imaging (RESOLVE) applies segmented EPI and parallel imaging to improve resolution and reduce distortion.4 Turbo spin-echo DWI (TSE DWI) uses 180° refocusing pulses to minimize susceptibility effects and distortion.5–8 However, no study has systematically compared these DWI sequences under identical conditions.

We developed a dedicated prostate phantom for the systematic evaluation of DWI sequences. To investigate different technical aspects of prostate DWI in a structured manner, this study employed a stepwise phantom design. Phantom A enabled quantitative evaluation of SNR and ADC and reproduced representative T2 and ADC values simulating prostate cancer. Phantom B modeled rectal gas and was designed to isolate susceptibility artifacts caused by rectal gas. Phantom C integrated these components, allowing qualitative image quality assessment through comparison with clinical images. The comparison with clinical images was not intended to evaluate diagnostic performance or lesion detectability. Instead, it was primarily performed to assess whether qualitative image characteristics and trends observed in the phantom experiments were consistent with those observed in clinical images. This approach provides supportive validation of the phantom findings.

The purpose of this study was to develop a dedicated prostate phantom that enables systematic technical evaluation of prostate DWI sequences by assessing SNR characteristics, diffusion metrics, and susceptibility artifacts under controlled conditions.

Materials and Methods

Phantom preparation

To simulate prostate cancer lesions in the peripheral zone (PZ), Phantom A was designed to match previously reported quantitative values of prostate cancer tissue. According to Langer et al.,9 T2 values in the PZ of prostate tumors typically range from 71.3 to 136.7 ms, and ADC values range from 0.937 to 1.637 × 10−3 mm2/s. According to PI-RADS, an ADC threshold of 0.75–0.90 × 10−3 mm2/s helps distinguish benign from malignant tissue in the PZ, with lower values indicating clinically significant cancer. To enable a structured technical evaluation of prostate DWI, this study employed a stepwise phantom design: Phantom A was used for quantitative assessment of SNR and ADC, Phantom B for evaluation of susceptibility artifacts, and Phantom C for integrated qualitative image quality assessment with comparison to clinical images.

Phantom A

Phantom A was developed to simulate prostate cancer. It consisted of a plastic bottle measuring 15 cm in diameter and 7 cm in height filled with 1.3 mL of gadobutrol (Gadovist 1.0 mmol/mL; Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany) dissolved in 1300 mL of neutral dishwashing detergent (Kyukyutto; Kao, Tokyo, Japan), containing non-ionic and anionic surfactants alkyl ether sulfate and alkylamine oxide. The T2 and ADC values of Phantom A were measured, and a central marker was added at the center of Phantom A (Fig. 1).

Phantom B

Phantom B was designed to evaluate susceptibility artifacts by simulating rectal gas using a table tennis ball (3-Star Premium 40; Nittaku, Tokyo, Japan) placed at the center of a plastic bottle (15 cm in diameter × 7.5 cm in height) filled with gelatin (Cook Gelatin; Morinaga, Tokyo, Japan). Gelatin was prepared using food-grade powder dissolved in hot water at 10 wt%.10 This concentration was selected based on previous literature reporting that gelatin is inexpensive, readily available, easy to handle, and easily prepared due to its favorable solubility characteristics. A central marker was added at the center of Phantom B.

Phantom C

Phantom C was constructed to replicate prostate tissue and rectal gas using a kiwi fruit and a table tennis ball embedded in layered gelatin (Fig. 2). The simulated lesions included in Phantom C were designed to reproduce realistic imaging conditions and contrast patterns rather than to evaluate lesion detectability or diagnostic performance. Kiwi fruit has previously been used as a surrogate model for the human prostate in MRI phantom studies due to its internal structure.11,12

The simulated prostate cancer solution prepared for Phantom A was used. The solution was loaded into three 1 mL syringes (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). One of the 3 syringes was adjusted to achieve an ADC value of 1.5 × 10−3 mm2/s to simulate a lower-grade prostate cancer lesion. Each syringe was cut to create shorter cylindrical inserts. One insert was placed in the transitional zone, while the other two were inserted into the right and left sides of the kiwi fruit to simulate lesions in the peripheral zone of the prostate. The right-sided lesion represented a lower-grade tumor. Gelatin was prepared by dissolving food-grade gelatin powder at a concentration of 5g per 100 mL of hot water. First, 300 mL of gelatin solution was prepared and poured into a plastic bottle (15 cm in diameter × 7.5 cm in height) to form the base layer. The container was placed in a refrigerator and cooled for approximately 3 hours until fully solidified.

After solidification, a cavity was created in the gelatin, and a table tennis ball was inserted to simulate rectal gas. To stabilize the ball and prevent flotation, an additional 100 mL of freshly prepared gelatin solution (5g per 100 mL) was poured over the ball. A small weight was temporarily placed on the ball to prevent displacement, and the phantom was refrigerated again for approximately 3 hours until solidified.

Next, a cavity was created in the solidified gelatin containing the table tennis ball. The kiwi containing the 3 syringe inserts was positioned within this cavity. Finally, 300 mL of freshly prepared gelatin solution (5g per 100 mL) was poured to completely embed the kiwi fruit and stabilize the internal structures. The phantom was refrigerated until complete solidification.

This multilayer embedding approach ensured stable positioning of both the simulated rectal gas and intraprostatic tumor inserts while minimizing movement or flotation within the gelatin matrix.

Patients

This retrospective study was ethics-approved with informed consent waived. Between April 2022 and March 2024, consecutive patients with elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels (range, 4–10 ng/mL) and Gleason score (range, 6–10) underwent mpMRI for suspected prostate lesions. During this period, 20 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. The median age was 63 years (range, 55–80). Patients with prior prostate treatment or incomplete imaging were excluded. All MRI data were reviewed for image quality, and only cases meeting the quality criteria were included in the analysis.

Image acquisition

A 3.0T MRI system (Ingenia CX with dS Anterior Coil and dS Posterior Coil; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) was used for TSE DWI (TE = 75 ms,) and T2WI. Another 3.0T MRI system (Skyra with Body 18ch and A 3T Tim Coil; Siemens Healthineers) was used for SS-EPI DWI (TE = 82 ms), ZOOMit DWI (TE = 75 ms), and RESOLVE (TE = 86 ms). A 1.5T MRI system (Avant with Body 6ch and A 1.5T Tim Coil; Siemens Healthineers) was used for SS-EPI DWI (TE = 96 ms), RESOLVE (TE = 101 ms), and MAPit (Avant with Body 6ch and A 1.5T Tim Coil, Skyra with Body 18ch and A 3T Tim Coil; Siemens Healthineers). Imaging parameters were based on PI-RADS v2.1: TR 5000–10000 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm2, matrix size 128 × 128, slice thickness 3.0 mm, and b-values of 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 s/mm2. For all DWI sequences, TE was set to the shortest achievable value under the b = 2000 s/mm2 condition for each sequence. To ensure consistency in SNR and ADC comparisons, the same TE was maintained across all other b-values. All DWI sequences were acquired with matched distortion directions to enable fair comparison of susceptibility artifacts. The phase-encoding direction was set to right–left (RL) for TSE DWI and anterior–posterior (AP) for all other DWI sequences. For ZOOMit DWI, the phase FOV was reduced to 120 mm. T2WI used the same FOV, matrix size, and slice thickness. All DWI sequences and MAPit sequences were performed at room temperature (24°C). To minimize variability in ADC values due to temperature, the phantom was kept at room temperature for 24h to reach thermal equilibrium. To ensure fair comparison across sequences, acquisition time for all sequences were unified to approximately 5 minutes. In addition, spatial resolution was prioritized for standardization, as these factors directly influence SNR characteristics and overall image quality. In contrast, parameters such as TR and TE were not strictly standardized because they are inherently dependent on sequence-specific implementations and vendor-specific system constraints. Therefore, TR and TE were adjusted within the allowable ranges for each sequence while maintaining comparable acquisition time and spatial resolution. This approach is intended to reflect practical clinical conditions and allows interpretation of differences in SNR and ADC as a result of sequence characteristics under comparable imaging constraints. Two certified radiological technologists assessed image quality at b = 1500 s/mm2 using Phantom C. A radiologist and a urologist evaluated clinical prostate DWI at the same b-value. Furthermore, to assess the quality control of Phantom C, repeated acquisitions were performed on a 3.0T MRI system (Skyra with Body 18ch and A 3T Tim Coil; Siemens Healthineers) using RESOLVE and MAPit under identical parameters 5 times per day over a 10-day period. Geometric distortion was evaluated using RESOLVE: TR 10000 ms, TE 86 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm2, matrix 128 × 128 and slice thickness 3.0 mm and b-value 1500 s/mm2. T1 and T2 values of the kiwi fruit were obtained using MAPit. For T1 measurements: TR 15 ms, TE 1.76 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm2, matrix 128 × 128, and slice thickness 3.0 mm. For T2 measurements: TR 1000 ms, TE 13.8–69.0 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm2, matrix 128 × 128, and slice thickness 3.0 mm. Temporal variations in these values were then evaluated.

For Phantom A, T2 measurements were performed on a 1.5T MRI (Avant with Body 6ch and A 1.5T Tim Coil; Siemens Healthineers) using MAPit: TR 3000 ms, TE 15–105 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm2, matrix 128 × 128, and slice thickness 3.0 mm. The measured T2 value was 75.5 ms. ADC measurements were performed on the same system using SS-EPI DWI: TR 10000 ms, TE 80 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm2, and b-value 0 and 600 s/mm2. The measured ADC value was 0.9 × 10−3 mm2/s.

All phantom experiments were performed using the coil configurations specified for each MRI system. To ensure consistent positioning, a dome-shaped plastic support platform was placed at the center of the coil, with the coil positioned above the dome and the phantom placed below it. A marker was positioned at the center of each phantom. Subsequently, for all scans, the phantom was aligned so that the central marker corresponded to the magnet isocenter.

Image analysis

Phantom A: SNR and ADC

Objective evaluation of Phantom A images was conducted by measuring SNR and apparent ADC using 10 repeated scans for each DWI sequence. The National Electrical Manufacturers Association (NEMA) difference method was used for SNR calculation, with a ROI of approximately 20 mm2 defined by a qualified radiological technologist. The same ROI size was applied to all DWI sequences, including ZOOMit DWI, despite its smaller FOV (120 mm2). The ROI was set at the center of the image (Fig. 1). Two images obtained under identical conditions were used to generate an average image and a difference image. SNR was then calculated using the following formula, in accordance with the NEMA MS 1-2008 standard: (1)SNR=SIavg2/SDdiff

where SIavg is the average signal intensity within the ROI on the average image, and SDdiff the standard deviation of the signal within the same ROI on difference image. All measurements were performed using ImageJ (version 1.53k; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).13

ADC values were calculated using the 2-point method based on the following equation: (2)ADC=−In(S1S2)b1−b2

where S1 denotes mean signal intensity within the ROI at b1 = 0 s/mm2, and S2 represents mean signal intensity within the ROI at each respective b-value (b2). The 2-point method was used to calculate ADC values to ensure consistency across different MRI systems and vendors, as vendor-provided ADC maps may be generated using different fitting algorithms depending on scanner implementation.

Phantom B: susceptibility artifacts

Phantom B was scanned using 10 repeated scans under identical conditions for each DWI sequence and T2WI sequence, and the average values were used for analysis. T2WI served as a reference for comparison with each DWI sequence, consistent with Dan et al., who also used T2WI as a reference.14 To assess susceptibility artifacts along internal structures of the phantom, signal intensity profiles were generated from marked images. Pixel values were extracted along a defined line (red line in Fig. 1d). To minimize SNR variability, all profiles were normalized using their respective maximum and minimum signal intensities, according to the following equation: (3)Inorm(x)=I(x)−I(min)I(max)−I(min)×100 (%)

where I (x) represents the signal intensity at position x, I (max) is the maximum and I (min) is the minimum signal intensity within each sequence’s profile. This normalization scales the signal intensity to a range of 0% to 100%, enabling direct comparison of susceptibility artifacts between each DWI sequence. A qualified radiological technologist measured the profile curve at the central marker using ImageJ.

Phantom C: qualitative evaluation, clinical validation, and stability assessment

For subjective analysis, 2 qualified radiological technologists visually evaluated images obtained from 5 repeated scans at the central marker in Phantom C (Fig. 3). The evaluation criteria for noise, artifacts, and overall image quality were defined by consensus of the raters before image assessment. The images were then independently evaluated using a 5-point scale. The scores used for statistical analysis were based on these independent evaluations. The same evaluation method was applied to the clinical images, which were visually assessed by a radiologist with 10 years of experience in prostate MRI and a certified urologist with 12 years of clinical experience. The clinical images were reviewed independently and blinded to the phantom evaluation results. Each DWI sequence was evaluated for image noise, artifacts, and overall image quality using the predefined 5-point scale. Detailed scoring criteria are summarized in Table 1.

Distortion in Phantom C over the 10-day period was evaluated using 5 repeated scans per day, with the Day 1 image used as the reference, following the same method as applied to Phantom B. T1 and T2 values were analyzed under identical conditions, with 5 ROIs placed on the marked images.

Statistical analysis

To compare objective image quality, SNR and ADC were calculated as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from Phantom A for each DWI sequence. Comparisons with the reference 3.0T SS-EPI DWI were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test, which appropriately adjusts for multiple comparisons among DWI sequences. Artifact profile curves were generated from Phantom B images and compared with the reference T2WI. Correlations between T2WI and each DWI sequence profile were assessed using Spearman rank correlation, as the data were ordinal and non-normal. This method suited monotonic comparisons without assuming linearity.

Subjective image quality indices were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Friedman test with Bonferroni correction. Nonparametric tests were used due to the ordinal, non-normally distributed nature of the data. To evaluate image quality for both Phantom C and clinical prostate DWI, the Friedman test was applied, following Akai et al., who used it to compare prostate MRI under varying conditions.15 A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s weighted kappa coefficient.16 Interpreted as poor (0.00–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), good (0.61–0.80), and excellent (0.81–1.00). Image quality between Phantom C and clinical images was compared using a nonparametric test selected according to the study design. While Crop et al. used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired comparisons of DWI techniques.17 The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied in the present study because different image sets were evaluated by independent reader groups.

Artifact profile curves from Phantom C acquired over 10 days were compared using Day 1 as the reference, following the same method as for Phantom B. T1 and T2 values were also compared with Day 1. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using EZR version 1.55 (Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan).18

Results

Phantom A: SNR and ADC

Figure 4 shows SNR for each DWI sequence at various b-values. At b = 500 s/mm2, 3.0T SS-EPI DWI (81.4 ± 6.3) had significantly higher SNR than all the other DWI sequences (P < 0.05), including 3.0T ZOOMit DWI (51.2 ± 5.5), 3.0T RESOLVE (54.8 ± 5.8), 1.5T SS-EPI DWI (30.7 ± 4.8), 1.5T RESOLVE (24.1 ± 3.5), and 3.0T TSE DWI (26.0 ± 6.6). This trend persisted across b = 1000, 1500, and 2000 s/mm2, where 3.0T SS-EPI DWI consistently outperformed other DWI sequences (all P < 0.05). Figure 5 shows ADC values 10−3 mm2/s under different b-values. At b = 500 s/mm2, 3.0T SS-EPI DWI (0.89 ± 0.06), 3.0T ZOOMit DWI (0.98 ± 0.06), 3.0T RESOLVE (0.90 ± 0.06), 1.5T SS-EPI DWI (0.98 ± 0.05), 1.5T RESOLVE (0.95 ± 0.11), and 3.0T TSE DWI (1.17 ± 0.12) were observed. Similar trends were noted at b = 1000, 1500, and 2000 s/mm2, with 3.0T TSE DWI consistently showing the highest ADC values. Overall, as the b-value increased, significant differences became more pronounced, especially between 3.0T TSE DWI and 1.5T RESOLVE. (all P < 0.05).

Phantom B: Susceptibility artifacts

Figure 6 shows artifact signal intensity profile curves obtained from Phantom B at b = 1500 s/mm2. Table 2 summarizes the quantitative evaluation results derived from these profiles. Spearman rank correlation analysis demonstrated the highest similarity to T2WI for 3.0T TSE DWI (ρ = 0.92), followed by 1.5T RESOLVE (ρ = 0.87), 1.5T SS-EPI DWI (ρ = 0.85), and both 3.0T ZOOMit DWI and 3.0T RESOLVE (ρ = 0.84). The lowest similarity was observed for 3.0T SS-EPI DWI (ρ = 0.79). All correlations were statistically significant (all P < 0.05). These findings indicate that 3.0T TSE DWI showed the closest agreement with T2WI, whereas 3.0T SS-EPI DWI demonstrated the greatest distortion.

Phantom C: Qualitative evaluation, clinical validation, and stability assessment

Phantom C results are shown in Table 3. Two independent readers evaluated each DWI for noise, artifacts, and overall quality. For image noise, both readers observed no significant difference between 3.0T SS-EPI DWI and either 3.0T RESOLVE or ZOOMit DWI (P = 0.317). However, both noted significant differences between 3.0T SS-EPI DWI and 1.5T SS-EPI DWI, 1.5T RESOLVE, and 3.0T TSE DWI (all P < 0.05). For image artifacts, both readers observed significant differences between 3.0T SS-EPI DWI and the other DWI sequences (all P < 0.05). For overall image quality, significant differences were found between 3.0T SS-EPI DWI and most of the sequences (P < 0.05), except for 1.5T RESOLVE (P = 0.083) and 3.0T TSE DWI (P = 0.157) by Reader 2. Inter-reader agreement was high: κ = 0.96 (noise), 0.93 (artifacts), and 0.86 (overall quality). Clinical image results are shown in Table 4. For noise, Reader 3 reported significant noise differences between 3.0T SS-EPI DWI and the other DWI sequences except 1.5T RESOLVE (P = 0.11). Reader 4 found significant differences with 1.5T RESOLVE and 3.0T TSE DWI (P < 0.05), but not with 3.0T RESOLVE (P = 0.16) or 3.0T ZOOMit DWI (P = 0.56). Inter-reader agreement for noise was moderate (κ = 0.42). For artifacts, both readers reported significant differences between 3.0T SS-EPI DWI and the other DWI sequences (P < 0.05), with moderate agreement (κ = 0.51). For overall quality, both found significant differences vs. all sequences (P < 0.05), except 3.0T TSE DWI (P = 1.0 by Reader 4) and 1.5T RESOLVE (P = 0.08 by Reader 4). Agreement was substantial (κ = 0.65). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test results comparing Phantom C images and clinical images are shown in Table 5. For noise, no significant differences were found between the phantom and clinical images in any sequence (all P > 0.05). For artifacts, no significant differences were found except for 1.5T SS-EPI DWI, where clinical images showed significantly more artifacts (P < 0.05). For overall quality, no significant differences between phantom and clinical images for each DWI sequence (all P > 0.05). Median scores ranged from 1 to 4.

Discussion

This study presents a prostate-dedicated phantom designed for both technical and qualitative evaluation of DWI sequences in prostate MRI, with particular emphasis on susceptibility artifacts. The phantom enables controlled quantitative assessment of SNR, diffusion metrics, and susceptibility artifacts, providing a reproducible experimental framework. Importantly, these quantitative evaluations provide a robust technical basis for interpreting and supporting qualitative image assessment, thereby enabling systematic and objective comparison of DWI sequences. While this framework is not intended to assess diagnostic performance, it establishes a clear link between quantitative physical validation and qualitative image evaluation.

Furthermore, the phantom provides a controlled and reproducible environment for comparing different DWI sequence implementations under clinically relevant conditions, including rectal gas distortion. The relaxation and diffusion properties of the phantom were designed to approximate values reported for prostate tissue, supporting the quantitative validity of the model. 9,11,12

Single-shot EPI remains the most widely used DWI technique in clinical prostate MRI; however, its vulnerability to susceptibility artifacts, particularly at 3.0T, is well recognized. In this context, the present phantom design allows systematic visualization of the trade-off between SNR and artifact behavior across multiple DWI strategies. The observed trends, including higher SNR with SS-EPI and improved distortion suppression with RESOLVE, ZOOMit, and TSE-based approaches, are consistent with known sequence characteristics and prior reports, thereby supporting the technical validity of the phantom-based observations.

A key contribution of this work is the separation of phantom components according to technical objectives. Phantom A enabled quantitative assessment of SNR and ADC behavior. Comparisons of ADC values showed greater differences at higher b-values, particularly for 3.0T TSE DWI and 1.5T RESOLVE. These findings are consistent with those of Kim et al., who reported that increased SNR improves ADC accuracy, whereas higher b-values reduce SNR and compromise measurement reliability.19 Therefore, the relatively low SNR of these sequences may have increased the influence of noise on signal decay, potentially resulting in ADC overestimation.

Phantom B isolated susceptibility effects attributable to rectal gas, and Phantom C integrated these features to approximate realistic imaging conditions. This modular design facilitates targeted evaluation of specific technical factors and may be advantageous for protocol refinement.

Subjective image quality assessment using Phantom C demonstrated consistent qualitative trends across readers, with high agreement for phantom-based evaluations. These findings suggest that the phantom provides a stable visual reference for comparing DWI sequences than serving as a substitute for clinical assessment.

Clinical images were included solely to contextualize phantom-based findings, allowing comparison of qualitative trends in artifact appearance and image quality. The general concordance of trends between the phantom and representative clinical images supports the relevance of the phantom for technical comparison; however, no claims regarding diagnostic accuracy or clinical performance can be drawn from these observations. Differences between the phantom and clinical results were observed, particularly in 1.5T SS-EPI DWI, which showed significantly higher artifact scores in the clinical evaluation. This discrepancy may be primarily related to the relatively low SNR of 1.5T SS-EPI DWI, which may increase susceptibility to image degradation. In addition, physiological motion and patient-related factors not reproduced in the phantom experiments may have further contributed to these artifacts.

From a technical perspective, ZOOMit DWI and RESOLVE demonstrated a favorable balance between artifact suppression and SNR, consistent with their sequence design. ZOOMit benefits from reduced FOV excitation and shortened echo trains, and Thierfelder et al. reported that ZOOMit DWI provides higher image quality with fewer artifacts than SS-EPI DWI.20 RESOLVE, with segmented acquisition and shorter echo trains, improves diffusion measurement stability and reduces artifacts, as supported by Li et al.21 TSE DWI suppresses artifacts using RF refocusing and reduced sensitivity to field inhomogeneity, however its inherently low SNR results in lower image quality scores.22,23 While effective for distortion reduction, this SNR limits clinical utility. Overall, ZOOMit DWI and RESOLVE offer a favorable balance between artifact suppression and SNR, with strong performance in both phantom and clinical evaluations (Fig. 8).

This study has limitations. Although this novel prostate-dedicated phantom simulates key clinical factors such as prostate cancer and rectal gas, it does not fully replicate physiological conditions such as motion or tissue heterogeneity. Subjective evaluations by a small number of readers may also limit generalizability, despite moderate to substantial inter-reader agreement. In Phantom C, artifact profile curves showed significant correlations with those on Day 1 over 10 days, although the correlation coefficients decreased over time. T1 and T2 values of the kiwi fruit showed no significant differences until Day 5 but significant changes from Day 6 onward. These results indicate that Phantom C is stable for short-term evaluation, however temporal changes time should be considered in quality control and longitudinal use.

A further limitation of this study is that MRI systems from 2 different vendors were used. Therefore, the observed differences among DWI techniques may partly reflect vendor-specific implementations or hardware factors in addition to intrinsic sequence characteristics. Furthermore, the present phantom is intended as a platform for controlled technical evaluation of DWI sequences rather than for direct clinical or diagnostic application, and ADC values were used primarily for relative comparison of diffusion characteristics among the evaluated sequences rather than to establish reference diffusion metrics.

Conclusion

This prostate-dedicated phantom provides a practical and technically informative platform for evaluating DWI sequences in prostate MRI. By enabling controlled quantitative assessment of SNR characteristics, diffusion metrics, and susceptibility artifacts, the phantom offers a reproducible experimental framework for evaluating technical differences and facilitating qualitative comparison among DWI sequences.
