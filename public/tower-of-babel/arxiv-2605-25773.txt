Efficient Benchmarking Is Just Feature Selection and Multiple Regression
Sam Bowyer, Acyr Locatelli, Kris Cao

Abstract
Efficient benchmarking techniques aim to lower the computational cost of evaluating LLMs by predicting full benchmark scores using only a subset of a benchmark’s questions. By reframing this problem as an instance of multiple regression with feature selection , we find that existing efficient benchmarking methods can be greatly improved by simply using kernel ridge regression at the prediction stage. Additionally, using an information-theoretic feature-selection algorithm called minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR), we can further improve upon these methods by selecting question subsets that will be maximally useful for prediction. Except in very data-poor settings, these approaches consistently achieve smaller prediction errors (in both MAE and RMSE), and greater ranking correlation between predicted and true scores (in both Spearman ρ \rho and Kendall τ \tau ) across a range of benchmarks using both binary and continuous metrics. Furthermore, mRMR subsampling is much faster than competitor methods (which often involve fitting probabilistic models or running clustering algorithms), and is more likely to select the same questions under different random seeds or training data splits. Tutorial code can be found at https://github.com/sambowyer/mrmr_eval .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) continue to exhibit an incredible range of capabilities across a variety of tasks and domains.
In order to accurately quantify, measure and interpret these capabilities, the need for rigorous evaluation is becoming increasingly important for both LLM developers and end-users.
Moreover, as the size of these models increases, the corresponding inference costs also increase, particularly on complex, long-horizon benchmarks.
Traditional benchmarking involves evaluating an LLM on datasets containing roughly hundreds to thousands of examples (e.g.
Zellers et al. 2019
;
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Liang et al. 2023
;
Fourrier et al. 2024
), but the cost of evaluating larger, more complicated benchmarks, or even many benchmarks at the same time, can quickly become prohibitive.
Therefore, a recent line of work
(
Vivek et al. 2024
;
Maia Polo et al. 2024
;
Kipnis et al. 2025
;
Balkır et al. 2026
)
investigates more efficient ways to measure model performance, primarily by sub-selecting particularly informative evaluation examples using data-driven approaches that make use of existing model evaluations.

As an example use-case, consider model training.
Ideally, a researcher would be able to evaluate every checkpoint of a training run on dozens of benchmarks in order to track the development of a variety of capabilities and skills that they would like a model to learn.
However, this not only takes up a lot of time—delaying any informed changes to the training procedure—but also takes up a considerable amount of compute that could otherwise be used on the training itself.
This problem becomes particularly acute if each benchmark question involves a complicated eval procedure, such as code execution
(
Jimenez et al. 2024
)
, multi-turn conversation
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, chain-of-thought reasoning steps
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, or multiple-answer generation (for example to compute a pass@
k
metric
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
—for which low-variance estimates require many more than
k
generations).
Even once training is complete, a researcher or user may want to run many ablations where they can vary inference-time options, such as sampling hyperparameters
(
Holtzman et al. 2020
;
Renze 2024
;
Minh et al. 2025
)
, prompt formats
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Sclar et al. 2024
;
Romanou et al. 2026
)
or the number of few-shot examples
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
.

Our approach

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a benchmark dataset containing
|\mathcal{D}|=N
questions, and let
s(m,q_{i})
be the score achieved by model
m
on question
q_{i}
,
i\in[N]
.
Given per-question results from
M

source-models
,
\{s(m,q_{i})\}_{m\in[M],i\in[N]}
, we define the task of efficient benchmarking in two stages:

Stage 1:

Select a
coreset
of questions
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}
of size
|\mathcal{C}|=n<|\mathcal{D}|=N
.

Stage 2:

Learn a function
f_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that predicts the full-benchmark score on test model
m^{*}
using only the coreset question scores:
f_{\mathcal{C}}([s(m^{*},q_{i})]_{q_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}))\approx\bar{s}(m^{*},\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s(m^{*},q_{i})
.

The main conceptual step we propose is to reframe the problem of efficient benchmarking as an instance of a classic statistical problem: regression with feature selection.
Table
1
shows the approaches taken to both stages by various methods.
In particular, for stage one, we look to the feature-selection literature
(
Barbieri et al. 2024
;
Theng and Bhoyar 2024
)
and investigate the use of a popular feature selection technique known as
Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance
(mRMR;
Peng et al. 2005
;
Ding and Peng 2005
;
Zhao et al. 2019
).
For stage two, we primarily focus on ridge regression and polynomial kernel ridge regression
(
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
;
Saunders et al. 1998
;
Hastie et al. 2001
)
, the latter of which allows us to effectively predict benchmark scores using scores of both individual questions and combinations of questions.
These also have the benefit of naturally extending to both benchmarks with binary scoring (correct/incorrect) and continuous scoring (such as ROUGE-L
(
Lin and Och 2004
)
or pass@
k
).

Thanks to the simplicity of mRMR and (kernel) ridge regression, our method is fast and easily adaptable to both discrete and continuous metrics.
As can be seen in
fig.

1
(a), using
M=30
source models on binary-valued benchmarks to construct and predict with coresets 5% the size of the original dataset, our method not only predicts with low error (RMSE), but also maintains high correlation between predicted rankings of models and ground truth rankings (Kendall’s
\tau
).
In
fig.

1
(b) we see that our method is still strong for continuous metrics, specifically, predicting pass@
k
with
k\in\{1,2,4,\ldots,64\}
on 5% coresets constructed using pass@1 scores from
M=15
models.
Interestingly, applying polynomial kernel ridge regression to the existing AnchorPoints method
(
Vivek et al. 2024
)
also achieves comparable results, improving upon its original weighted mean predictions.
Furthermore, we find that coreset construction with mRMR is also
stable
in the questions it selects, in the sense that it is more likely than competitor methods to select the same questions upon repeated runs with varying source model data (see
fig.

3
).

2.1
mRMR: Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance

Assume that there are
N
features
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
with which we can predict a target variable
Y
.
For a feature-set (or
coreset
1
1

1

It is common to refer to collections of data points as
coresets
and collections of data features as
feature sets

(
Moser et al. 2026
)
. This distinction blurs in our setting, so we use the terms interchangeably to denote subsampled collections of questions.
)
\mathcal{C}
, containing
|\mathcal{C}|=n\leq N
features, we define its
relevance
to be the average mutual information (MI) between each of its features and the target
Y
, and the
redundancy
to be the average MI between each pair of features:

\text{Relevance}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}I(X_{i},Y),\qquad\text{Redundancy}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{X_{j}\neq X_{i}}I(X_{i},X_{j}).

(1)

Mutual information measures the dependence between two variables, and can be expressed in terms of the entropy of the variables
H(\cdot)
(with joint entropy
H(\cdot,\cdot)
and conditional entropy
H(\cdot|\cdot)
):

I(X,Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y).

(2)

Equivalently, the MI can be expressed in terms of a KL-divergence between the joint distribution of the two variables
P_{X,Y}
, and the outer-product distribution of their marginals
P_{X}\otimes P_{Y}
.
For two continuous random variables
X
and
Y
with sample spaces
\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
, marginal densities
p(x)
and
p(y)
, and joint density
p(x,y)
this can be written as:

I(X,Y)=D_{KL}(P_{X,Y}\;||\;P_{X}\otimes P_{Y})=\int_{\Omega_{X}}\int_{\Omega_{Y}}p(x,y)\log\left(\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\right)dydx\geq 0.

(3)

The goal of mRMR is to construct a coreset
\mathcal{C}
with maximal relevance and minimal redundancy.
Finding the globally optimal subset is NP-complete
(
Davies and Russell 1994
)
, so a greedy approach is usually taken.
Starting with a coreset containing the feature with maximal relevance
\mathcal{C}_{(1)}=\{\argmax_{X_{i}}I(X_{i},Y)\}
, we iteratively add the feature that maximises an importance function
g
:

\mathcal{C}_{(t)}=\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)}\cup\argmax_{X_{i}\notin\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)}}g(X_{i};\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)},Y).

(4)

This continues until we have a coreset
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{(n)}
of desired size
n
.
Many forms of
g
exist (e.g. using F-statistics
(
Ding and Peng 2005
)
or random-forests
(
Zhao et al. 2019
)
; see
section

E.1
for more details), but the two most common are:

\displaystyle\text{(MI Difference)}\quad g^{\text{MID}}(X_{i};\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)},Y)=

\displaystyle I(X_{i},Y)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)}|}\sum_{X_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)}}I(X_{j},X_{i}),

(5)

\displaystyle\text{(MI Quotient)}\quad g^{\text{MIQ}}(X_{i};\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)},Y)=

\displaystyle\left.I(X_{i},Y)\middle/\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)}|}\sum_{X_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{(t-1)}}I(X_{j},X_{i})\right).\right.

(6)

In practice, we find that
MIQ
leads to better downstream performance than MID or a redundancy-ignorant approach (see
section

E.1
) for our use-case, and we therefore adopt it for our experiments.
We discuss the mRMR algorithm more in
appendix

A
and
appendix

F
.

2.2
Coreset construction via mRMR

To apply mRMR feature selection to efficient benchmarking on a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of
|\mathcal{D}|=N
questions, we simply need to decide how to represent the questions as features, and exactly what form the relevance target
Y
should take.
Assuming we have per-question scores for
M

source
LLMs, we represent each question as a vector containing per-model scores
\mathbf{q}_{i}=[s(m_{1},q_{i}),\ldots,s(m_{M},q_{i})]\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
i\in[N]
,where
s(m,q)
denotes the score achieved by model
m
on question
q
.
For the relevance target
Y
, we simply use a vector containing the per-model summary-scores
\bar{s}(m,\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{D}}s(m,q)
:

\bar{\mathbf{s}}=[\bar{s}(m_{1},\mathcal{D}),\ldots,\bar{s}(m_{M},\mathcal{D})]\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

(7)

Now we have
M
datapoints for each question,
q_{i}
, and for the summary-score,
\bar{s}
, with which to estimate MI.
That is, we have
M
samples from the joint space of
N+1
random variables,

s(\cdot\;,q_{1}),\;s(\cdot\;,q_{2}),\;\ldots,\;s(\cdot\;,q_{N}),\;\bar{s}(\cdot\;,\mathcal{D}).

(8)

If the benchmark being considered only admits binary scores,
s(m,q)\in\{0,1\}
, then the redundancy part of our mRMR selection criteria (
eq.

5
and
eq.

6
) involves calculating the empirical MI between two binary random variables,
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
,

\displaystyle I(Q_{i},Q_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{q_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{q_{j}\in\{0,1\}}p(q_{i},q_{j})\log\left(\frac{p(q_{i},q_{j})}{p(q_{i})p(q_{j})}\right).

(9)

The necessary probabilities can be defined using the empirical proportions of zeros and ones across our
M
models.
That is, for
a,b\in\{0,1\}
, and with the indicator function
\mathbb{I}
, we have:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{q}_{i}=a,\mathbf{q}_{j}=b)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}(q_{i}^{(m)}=a)\mathbb{I}(q_{j}^{(m)}=b),

(10)

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{q}_{i}=a)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}(q_{i}^{(m)}=a),\quad\text{ and }\quad p(\mathbf{q}_{j}=b)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}(q_{j}^{(m)}=b).

(11)

To calculate the relevance term involving
I(X_{i},Y)=I(\mathbf{q}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{s}})
, we require a discrete-continuous MI estimator, since the summary scores
\bar{\mathbf{s}}
are not in general binary. We employ the estimator derived in
Ross 2014
, based on the continuous-continuous Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger (KSG) estimator
(
Kraskov et al. 2004
)
.
(We avoid simply discretizing our data and using a discrete-discrete MI estimator as this will give poor estimates unless
M
is very large.)

In the case that a benchmark admits continuous scores,
s(m,q)\in\mathbb{R}
, we use a PCA-corrected KSG estimator
(
Gao et al. 2015
)
.
Note that both this and the estimator above involve computing the
k
-nereast-neighbours for each datapoint.
Therefore we have one hyperparameter
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which we search through
k=3,4,\ldots,9
, finding an optimal
k=5
for binary data (
section

3.1
) and
k=8
for continuous data (
section

3.2
). (Ablations on the value of
k
and the standard non-PCA-corrected KSG estimator can be found in
section

E.1
.)

2.3
Prediction using coreset results

Once we have the coreset
\mathcal{C}=\{q_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},\ldots,q_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\}
, we must learn a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which predicts the full benchmark score
\bar{s}(m,\mathcal{D})
using only the coreset-question scores
\{s(m,q_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}),\ldots,s(m,q_{\mathcal{C}_{n}})\}
.
Any suitable regression technique here should work, however, we proceed with ridge regression
(
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
;
Frank and Friedman 1993
;
Hastie 2020
;
Hastie et al. 2001
)
since it is simple, quick and effective.
(In
section

E.2
we investigate the use of alternative regression methods.)

Ridge Regression

Given
m
datapoints each with
n
features
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{q}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{q}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and a target
\mathbf{y}=\bar{\mathbf{s}}
, ridge regression learns
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to minimise an
L_{2}
-penalised least squares objective:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w},w_{0}}\nolimits\left\{||\mathbf{y}-(w_{0}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\mathbf{w}||_{2}^{2}\right\}=(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},

(12)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularisation coefficient (which we select via cross-validation) and
\mathbf{I}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix.
This gives us the prediction function
f(\mathbf{x})=w_{0}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{w}
for the per-question response vector of a test model
\mathbf{x}=[s(m,q_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}),\ldots,s(m,q_{\mathcal{C}_{n}})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The
L_{2}
penalty shrinks coefficients and renders the matrix
(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I})
invertible, allowing for a closed-form solution.

Kernel Ridge Regression

By allocating only a single weight per coreset question, standard ridge regression assumes that the summary score
\bar{s}
is an additive linear function of individual question scores.
However, the performance gain signalled by correctly answering a pair of coreset questions may be greater (or less) than the sum of each question’s individual contribution. To deal with this, we also investigate the use of
kernel
ridge regression
(
Saunders et al. 1998
;
Hastie et al. 2001
)
with degree-
d
polynomial kernels.
Let
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the
i
th and
j
th rows of
\mathbf{X}
, representing the score vectors for models
i
and
j
on the
n
coreset questions.
We solve for dual weights
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
using the kernel matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
where
[K]_{ij}=\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\rangle+1)^{d}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\alpha}}=\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}}\nolimits\{||\mathbf{y}-K\bm{\alpha}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda\bm{\alpha}^{T}K\bm{\alpha}\}=(K+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m})^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(13)

Thanks to the kernel trick (
Boser et al. 1992
; see
section

B.1
) the degree-
d
polynomial kernel allows us to predict
\mathbf{y}
using the products of all groups of features up to size
d
(e.g.
d=2
lets us consider
pairs
of features; and setting
d=1
differs from regular ridge regression only in a rescaled bias term
w_{0}
).

2.4
Other methods

Random

We sample
n
questions from the benchmark independently at random without replacement, then take the mean score among these
n
coreset questions as an estimate for the full benchmark score.

Lasso

Replacing ridge regression’s
L_{2}
penalty with an
L_{1}
penalty causes many of the coefficients
w_{i}
to shrink to 0
(
Tibshirani 1996
;
Hastie et al. 2001
)
.
This gives an all-in-one coreset prediction strategy: we increase the regularisation strength
\lambda>0
until we only have
n
nonzero coefficients.

Anchor Points

Vivek et al. 2024
perform K-means clustering over
\mathbf{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},i\in[N]
, and select the
K=n
medoid questions to form
\mathcal{C}
.
To predict, coreset scores are combined in a weighted mean using each medoid’s cluster size (normalised to sum to 1).
(For conciseness, we omit the "predictor" variant of this in which every non-coreset question score is predicted via an ensemble of univariate regressions on each coreset-question as it underperforms the weighted-mean approach.)

Item Response Theory (IRT)

Much recent work has focused on using IRT
(
Lord et al. 1968
;
Baker and Kim 2004
;
Van Der Linden 2018
)
to provide informative representations of benchmark questions and model abilities
(
Lalor et al. 2016
;
Rodriguez et al. 2022
;
Jo and Wilson 2025
)
.
The two-parameter IRT model represents the probability of model
m
correctly answering question
i
as:

p_{i,m}=\mathbb{P}(s(m,q_{i})=1\;|\;\theta_{m},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})=1/\left(1+\exp(-\alpha_{i}^{T}\theta_{m}+\beta_{i}\right),

(14)

where
\theta_{m},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are latent variables representing, respectively, model abilities, question difficulty and a bias term, fitted using MCMC or variational inference. TinyBenchmarks
(
Maia Polo et al. 2024
)
defines two IRT-based methods.
In the first, Performance-IRT (
p-IRT
), we estimate
\theta_{m^{*}}
for a new test model
m^{*}
via maximum likelihood on
eq.

14
with fixed
\hat{\alpha}_{i},\hat{\beta}_{i}
, and obtain per-question success probabilities
p_{i,m^{*}}
that we use as stand-ins for the true question scores on non-coreset questions. The second method, Generalised p-IRT (
gp-IRT
), predicts a convex combination of the p-IRT score and an AnchorPoints score obtained via K-Means on the fitted mean latents
(\hat{\alpha}_{i},\hat{\beta}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
to get normalised cluster-size weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i:\;q_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}
:

f_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{gp-IRT}}([s(m^{*},q_{i})]_{q_{i}\in\mathcal{C}})=\lambda\sum_{i:\;q_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}\nolimits w_{i}s(m^{*},q_{i})+(1-\lambda)f_{\mathcal{C}}^{\text{p-IRT}}([s(m^{*},q_{i})]_{q_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}).

(15)

Here
\lambda\in[0,1]
is chosen via a heuristic depending on the sample variance of all question scores across all source models,
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
, and an approximation of the IRT prediction bias.
The gp-IRT method tends to produce smaller errors than both the AnchorPoints method and p-IRT, so we omit p-IRT results.

However, the IRT model only supports binary metrics and running IRT inference is computationally expensive and leads to high variance-predictions (
Madaan et al. 2025
), hurting ranking prediction quality.
To adapt gp-IRT to continuous data, we use the
\beta
-IRT model from

p
and other continuous IRT models.)

Metabench

Kipnis et al. 2025
propose an IRT-based method for constructing a coreset from amongst various different benchmarks.
After fitting an IRT model, as in
eq.

14
, the authors split the range of fitted abilities
\theta_{i}
into
n
quantile bins and for each bin select the most informative question (in terms of Fisher information).
Prediction happens on coreset questions using a linear GAM.
This method requires a large
M
to get a good model fit and so tends to underperform in our setting.

Refitting Ridge

We also experiment with alternative versions of AnchorPoints and gp-IRT methods in which we learn a (kernel) ridge predictor on their pre-constructed coresets.
We denote these methods with an appended ‘+’ for standard ridge regression and a ‘++’ appended when using kernel ridge regression with
d=2
.
(We investigate higher values of
d
in
section

B.3
and find that
d=2
performs the best.)
For example, ‘AnchorPoints+’ and ‘AnchorPoints++’ refer to using ridge and kernel ridge regression respectively to make predictions with the coreset chosen by AnchorPoints.

Exhaustive Random Search

Finally, we look at a hard baseline labelled
Search+
(and
Search++
), where we sample 10,000 coresets at random, use 75% of the evaluation models to fit a ridge regressor, and test the regressor on the remaining 25%.
We then select the coreset with the lowest test MAE and refit the regressor using the full model pool before computing the test error on the test models.

Note that both Search+ and Random+ were proposed as strong baselines in
Zhang et al. 2025b
.
We find that they can be beaten using more principled coreset construction and kernel ridge regression.

Metrics

In each experiment setting we focus on four key metrics to assess the quality of prediction (for full details see
appendix

C
).
Firstly we look to minimise prediction error, specifically root-mean-squared error (RMSE) and mean-absolute error (MAE).
Since we would like to compare these errors over multiple datasets, each of which may have their own level of summary-score variance, we report normalised versions of both RMSE and MAE, in which we weight each dataset’s contribution by
\sigma_{d}^{-1}/(\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{D}\sigma_{d^{\prime}}^{-1})
where
\sigma_{d}
is the standard deviation of summary scores on dataset
d
.

Often, having high quality comparisons between models is more useful than knowing exact benchmark scores.
Therefore we also look at two
rank correlation coefficients
.
These lie in
[-1,1]
and measure the agreement between predicted and true rankings of a set of test models. We look at the Spearman
\rho

(
Spearman 1904
)
(the correlation between the two ranking vectors) and the Kendall
\tau

(
Kendall 1938
)
(
\tau=\tau^{*}
corresponds to having
(1-\tau^{*})/2
pairs of models ranked the wrong way around).

We also want to measure the
stability
of our coresets—the extent to which each method chooses the same questions given different random seeds or training data. We use a stability metric
\hat{\Phi}
from the feature-selection literature
(
Nogueira et al. 2018
)
, where
0
corresponds to every feature having an equal probability of being selected, and
1
corresponds to selecting the exact same coreset every time.

4
Discussion

We’ve shown that ridge regression and kernel ridge regression work well in a range of settings and for a range of coresets.

fig.

5
shows true versus predicted summary scores for the methods Random, AnchorPoints, gp-IRT and kernel ridge (with mRMR coresets) on the ARC-C dataset.
This shows that kernel ridge achieves much lower training RMSE than the other methods (0.006 compared to 0.040 (Random), 0.081 (AnchorPoints), and 0.020 (gp-IRT)).
Interestingly we can also see the negative effect of AnchorPoints’ low-stability and weighted-mean approach: two clear modes of training-point prediction behaviour representing very different coresets chosen in different data splits (see the per-seed version in
fig.

).
Whilst mRMR++ produces lower-variance predictions around the centre of performance compared to other methods, it has a tendency to over-predict at the floor of true model performance and under-predict at the ceiling, leading to a slight ‘S’ shape, a common artifact of mean regression (this is more visible in other datasets, see
fig.

27
).
This suggests that our method’s coresets should not be used to test models that are significantly worse or significantly better than the source models, which may fail or pass all questions in the coreset.
Finally, in

; the number of datapoints per parameter) affects the performance of all discussed strategies.

5
Related work

We’ve shown that in very common and important efficient benchmark setting requiring
static
coresets (for simplicity, interpretability and/or increased throughput via batching) and
in-distribution
predictions (e.g. during model training or running non-frontier ablation experiments), we can leverage relatively simple feature selection and regression techniques to improve existing methods.
However, there is much recent work outside of this setting for which applying these techniques is not so straightforward.

Zhang et al. 2025b
focuses on out-of-distribution efficient benchmarking—using a technique from prediction-powered inference methods
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2023
)
to predict the scores of more powerful models using only data from less powerful source models.
Similarly, there is much work on adaptive methods: dynamically selecting questions from the benchmark to suit each test model
(
Li et al. 2025
;
Hofmann et al. 2025
;
Yuan et al. 2025
;
Balkır et al. 2026
)
).
This has many attractive features, e.g. selecting more informative examples per model, and the potential for better use of compute through early stopping.
Another setting for efficient benchmarking involves utilising a wider range of data than just per-question per-model scores, whether that be model output distributions (e.g. per-class logits)
(
Rubinstein et al. 2025
)
,
hand-crafted features
(
Saranathan et al. 2025
)
, LLM activations
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
or the effect of in-context learning
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
.
Other work has focused on different granularities of prediction, either using only summary scores
(
Papailiopoulos 2026
)
, or learning a model to predict individual question scores
(
Pacchiardi et al. 2024
)
, or accounting for the cost of human evaluation of coreset questions
(
Zouhar et al. 2025
)
.

6
Conclusion

We suggest that a reframing of efficient benchmarking as multiple regression with feature selection can help researchers explore a range of already well-studied techniques, such as mRMR and kernel ridge regression.
These two techniques in particular beat every other method on binary-scored datasets.
Whilst improving mRMR’s coresets on continuous valued datasets remains future work (whether that be with improved MI estimators for our relatively few-data regime, or alternative feature importance functions
g
), we show that simply applying kernel ridge regression to existing clustering based approaches works extremely well and lead to coresets that generalise to multiple related metrics.
This provides significant compute savings, and we hope that this work also opens up fruitful avenues for future efficient benchmarking research.

Appendix B
Ridge regression details

B.1
The kernel trick

We consider polynomial kernels of degree
d
,

\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\rangle+c)^{d},

(16)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
contain coreset question scores for models
i
and
j
and
c=1
is fixed.
Using these in kernel ridge regression allows us to regress on the products of groups of features up to size
d
.
In our case, this means considering the scores on not just each individual questions, but also the products scores on all pairs of questions, all triples of questions, and so on.
This phenomenon is commonly referred to as the
kernel trick
.
To see how it works, it’s helpful to start with the
d=2
and
c=0
case.

Let
x_{i1}\in\mathbb{R}
be the
k
th element of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, i.e. the score of model
i
on the
k
th coreset question.
If we first suppose that there are only two coreset questions,
n=2
, then expanding out
eq.

16
gives us

\displaystyle(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\rangle)^{2}

\displaystyle=(x_{i,1}x_{j,1}+x_{i,2}x_{j,2})^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle=x_{i,1}^{2}x_{j,1}^{2}+x_{i,2}^{2}x_{j,2}^{2}+2x_{i,1}x_{j,1}x_{i,2}x_{j,2}

(18)

\displaystyle=\langle\underbrace{(x_{i,1}^{2},x_{i,2}^{2},\sqrt{2}x_{i,1}x_{i,2})}_{\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})},\underbrace{(x_{j,1}^{2},x_{j,2}^{2},\sqrt{2}x_{j,1}x_{j,2})}_{\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{j})}\rangle,

(19)

where
\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a mapping of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to a higher dimensional feature space (the
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
, or RKHS) which contains not just the squares individual scores of both coreset questions, but also the product of their scores (scaled by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
).
If we used a nonzero constant
c
, e.g.
c=1
, then we’d obtain a mapping to a larger feature space which includes a constant and linear terms as well as the quadratic terms above:

\displaystyle(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\rangle+1)^{2}

\displaystyle=(x_{i,1}x_{j,1}+x_{i,2}x_{j,2}+1)^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle=x_{i,1}^{2}x_{j,1}^{2}+x_{i,2}^{2}x_{j,2}^{2}+2x_{i,1}x_{j,1}x_{i,2}x_{j,2}+2x_{i,1}x_{j,1}+2x_{i,2}x_{j,2}+1

(21)

\displaystyle=\langle(x_{i,1}^{2},x_{i,2}^{2},\sqrt{2}x_{i,1}x_{i,2},\sqrt{2}x_{i,2},1),(x_{j,1}^{2},x_{j,2}^{2},\sqrt{2}x_{j,1}x_{j,2},\sqrt{2}x_{j,2},1)\rangle,

(22)

Note that if we increased the coreset size
n>2
, then our
d=2
quadratic expansion would still lead to a feature space that computes all pairwise interactions between coreset question scores, and as long as
c\neq 0
, we’d also have the linear terms (i.e. individual coreset question scores) and a bias term as well.
Furthermore, setting
d>2
would give us a feature mapping that computes all possible monomials up to degree
d
, e.g. triple-question-products for
d=3
or pairs of squared question scores for
d=4
.
This flexibility leads to greater predictive performance in
section

3.1
, however, it can lead to under- or over-fitting if
d
is set too high.
The effect of this is explored in
section

B.3
.

B.2

Ridge regression was originally developed to deal with underspecified regression problems, where we have fewer datapoints than parameters. causing ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression (with no regularisation penalty) to be unable to identify suitable coefficients
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
, since the matrix
\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
(from
eq.

12
with
\lambda=0
) is not full-rank and therefore non-invertible.

In our setting, underspecification means we have fewer source models than coreset-questions,
M<n
.
Whilst (polynomial) ridge regression
can
deal with relatively small values of the ratio
M/n
, if this ratio grows too small then our signal-to-noise ratio among the coreset question scores can overwhelm and lead to poorer predictive performance.
This is shown in
fig.

where we’ve rerun the experiments in

3.1
but instead target
fixed coreset sizes

n\in\{50,100,250\}
rather than relative sizes of
5\%,10\%
and
15\%
.
Here we see that the relative performance gain from applying polynomial ridge regression (using both random and mRMR-constructed coresets) compared to gp-IRT and AnchorPoints decreases as the ratio
M/n
gets closer to zero.
In

, show the same results but on the
5\%,10\%
and
15\%
relative-size coresets from

Appendix C
Metric descriptions

Here we provide more details on the metrics used to compare different efficient benchmarks in
section

3
.

C.1
Errors

Assume that we have a set of test models
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
of size
M^{\prime}=|\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|
with true benchmark scores
\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{D}}=[\bar{s}(m^{\prime}_{1},\mathcal{D}),\ldots,\bar{s}(m^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}},\mathcal{D})]\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
and predicted scores
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathcal{D},f_{\mathcal{C}}}=[f_{\mathcal{C}}(m^{\prime}_{1}),\ldots,f_{\mathcal{C}}(m^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}})]\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
.
Firstly we consider two types of prediction error: mean absolute error (MAE;
eq.

23
) and root mean squared error (RMSE;
eq.

24
):

\displaystyle\text{MAE}(f_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\left|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathcal{D},f_{\mathcal{C}}}^{(m)}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{D}}^{(m)}\right|,

(23)

\displaystyle\text{RMSE}(f_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|}\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathcal{D},f_{\mathcal{C}}}^{(m)}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{D}}^{(m)}\right)^{2}}.

(24)

Since we want to look at the average error across multiple benchmarks, we aggregate errors over benchmarks
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{D}
with a simple mean weighted by each benchmark’s summary score standard deviation across test models (normalised across benchmarks):

\displaystyle\text{Agg-MAE}(f_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{D}_{1:D},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{\sigma_{d}^{-1}}{\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{D}\sigma_{d^{\prime}}^{-1}}\text{MAE}(f_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{D}_{d},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}),

(25)

\displaystyle\text{Agg-RMSE}(f_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{D}_{1:D},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{\sigma_{d}^{-1}}{\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{D}\sigma_{d^{\prime}}^{-1}}\text{RMSE}(f_{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{D}_{d},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}),

(26)

where

\sigma_{d}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}^{(m)}-\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}\right)^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|}\sum_{m=1}^{M^{\prime}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}^{(m)}.

(27)

C.2
Rank correlation coefficients

Pearson’s
r

[
Pearson 1895
]
For two vectors
\bar{\mathbf{s}},\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
representing, respectively, ground-truth and predicted scored for
M
models, the Pearson correlation is given by

r(X,Y)=\frac{\text{Cov}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}})}{\text{StdDev}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})\text{StdDev}(\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}})}\in[-1,1],

(28)

where
\text{Cov}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}})
is the covariance between the variables
\bar{\mathbf{s}}
and
\hat{\bar{\mathbf{s}}}
.
This tells us whether prediction
values
are correlated with true
values
, however, it will be affected by different models to different extents, depending on the magnitude of their summary scores (both predicted and true).

Spearman’s
\rho

[
Spearman 1904
]
Improves upon the Pearson correlation coefficient calculated between the rank variables
R_{\mathcal{D}},\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}}\in[M^{\prime}]^{M^{\prime}}
, where the
i
-th element of
R_{\mathcal{D}}
(resp.
\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}}
) is the rank of the true (resp. predicted) score of the
i
-th model in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
:

\rho(\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}},R_{\mathcal{D}})=\frac{\text{Cov}(\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}},R_{\mathcal{D}})}{\text{StdDev}(\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}})\text{StdDev}(R_{\mathcal{D}})}\in[-1,1].

(29)

By looking at the ranks of models rather than their true values, we get a better sense of how effective an efficient benchmarking method is when comparing different models on the same benchmark, removing the dependence on the actual raw summary scores.

Kendall’s
\tau

[
Kendall 1938
]
Like Spearman’s
\rho
, this also uses rank variables rather than raw summary scores.
In particular, the Kendall
\tau
of two rankings is computed as the number of pairwise comparisons which are concordant (in the same relative order in both rankings) minus the number of discordant pairs (in the opposite relative order), divided by the total number of such pairs,
\binom{n}{2}
:

\tau(\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}},R_{\mathcal{D}})=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}\leq M^{\prime}}\text{sign}(\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{1}}-\hat{R}_{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{2}})\text{sign}(R_{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{1}}-R_{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{2}})\in[-1,1].

(30)

Note that a value of
\tau
means the proportion of incorrectly ranked pairs is
(1-\tau)/2
.

C.3
Stability metrics

We report two stability metrics designed to measure the extent to which coreset-construction methods select the same questions when presented with different numbers of source models,
M
, and different random seeds (which are also used to sample models for the train-test split).
Both metrics lie in
[0,1]
with larger values indicating a greater tendency to select the same questions.
(In both, a stability of 1 is only possible if a method always selects the exact same coreset, however, a stability of 0 has different interpretations for each metric.)
We report aggregated stability metrics across different benchmarks by simply taking the mean.

Noguiera’s
\hat{\Phi}

[
Nogueira et al. 2018
]

Given a collection of
N
features
\mathcal{D}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
, and
L
feature-sets
\mathfrak{C}=\{\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\}
, we first compute the average size of the sets
\bar{n}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}|\mathcal{C}_{l}|
and for each feature
X_{i}
we also calculate the proportion of the
L
sets in which
X_{i}
is present:

\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{l}),

(31)

as well as the unbiased sample variance of this feature’s coreset-inclusion,
\hat{s}_{i}^{2}=\frac{L}{L-1}\hat{p}_{i}(1-\hat{p}_{1})
.

This frequency based approach aims to construct a metric which has expected value 0 under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that every choice of coreset
\mathcal{C}\in 2^{\mathcal{D}}
is equally likely (which is the authors’ definition of minimum stability).
In this case, it can be shown that the expected sample variance is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}\;\Big|H_{0}\right]=\frac{\bar{n}}{N}\left(1-\frac{\bar{n}}{N}\right).

(32)

Therefore the stability metric is defined as

\hat{\Phi}(\mathfrak{C})=1-\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}\;\Big|H_{0}\right]}=1-\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}}{\frac{\bar{n}}{N}\left(1-\frac{\bar{n}}{N}\right)}.

(33)

This has many desirable properties, not only accounting for random coreset selection, but also being naturally robust to coresets of varying sizes.

Hamming stability

[
Dunne et al. 2002
]
Rather than taking a frequency-based approach, it is also common to define stability using pairwise differences between feature-sets.
One very common such metric is the following, in which we represent a feature-set
\mathcal{C}
as a binary vector
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C})
of length
N
such that the
i
th entry indicates whether
\mathcal{C}
contains feature
X_{i}
:

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C})_{i}=\begin{cases}0,&X_{i}\notin\mathcal{C},\\
1,&X_{i}\in\mathcal{C}.\end{cases}

(34)

For a collection of
L
feature-sets
\mathfrak{C}_{n}=\{\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{(L)}\}
all of size
n\leq N
, we define the
Hamming stability for coresets of size
n
to be the average Hamming distance (equivalent to
L_{1}
distance for binary vectors) between all pairs of feature-sets,

\displaystyle H(\mathfrak{C}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{L}{2}}\sum_{1\leq l_{1}<l_{2}\leq L}||\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}^{(l_{1})})-\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}^{(l_{2})})||_{1}

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{L}{2}}\sum_{1\leq l_{1}<l_{2}\leq L}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{(l_{1})})\otimes\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{(l_{2})}),

(36)

where
\otimes
represents the XOR operator.
In other words, this represents the average number of features which differ between two feature-sets chosen from
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
.
To combine Hamming stability scores from collections of feature-sets of different sizes,
\mathfrak{C}=\{\mathfrak{C}_{n_{1}},\ldots,\mathfrak{C}_{n_{K}}\}
, for integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}
where

|\mathcal{C}|=n_{k}\quad\forall\mathcal{C}\in\mathfrak{C}_{n_{k}},

(37)

we simply take the mean Hamming scores across the coreset sizes, with each score inversely weighted by the size of its coreset collection
|\mathfrak{C}_{n}|
:

H(\mathfrak{C})=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}H(\mathfrak{C}_{n_{k}})/|\mathfrak{C}_{n_{k}}|}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}1/|\mathfrak{C}_{n_{k}}|}.

(38)

This helps maintain the interpretation that Hamming stability measures the average number of features which differ between a collection of feature-sets.
(In our experiments, we actually always have
|\mathfrak{C}_{n_{1}}|=\cdots=|\mathfrak{C}_{n_{K}}|
, so
eq.

Appendix G
IRT details

G.1
gp-IRT heuristic

In
eq.

15
,
\lambda=\hat{b}^{2}/(\hat{b}^{2}+\hat{\sigma}^{2}/4n)
is calculated using the sample variance of all question scores across all source models,
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
, and an approximation of the IRT prediction bias,
\hat{b}
, estimated by the mean absolute difference between true scores in a validation set (containing half the source-model data) and predicted scores from an IRT model trained on the other half of source-model data.

G.2
IRT models specification

The binary IRT model in
eq.

14
is implemented with the following priors and hyperpriors for a given dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
with model
m
obtaining score
Y_{m,j}
on question
j
.
Note that the two continuous models only admit
d=1
.

Hyperpriors:

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta},\mu_{\theta,d},\mu_{\gamma,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10)

\displaystyle u_{\beta},u_{\theta,d},u_{\gamma,d}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)

Priors:

\displaystyle\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\beta},u_{\beta}^{-1})

\displaystyle\theta_{id}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta,d},u_{\theta,d}^{-1})

\displaystyle\gamma_{md}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\gamma,d},u_{\gamma,d}^{-1})

Likelihood:

\displaystyle z_{im}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}(\gamma_{md}\theta_{id})-\beta_{m}

\displaystyle Y_{im}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\text{logit}=z_{im})

In sections
section

3.2
and
section

3.3
we report gp-IRT results using a Beta IRT model
[
Noel and Dauvier 2007
]
in place of the standard binary 2PL model from
eq.

14
.
Here we describe this IRT model, as well as three alternatives that we implemented but found to lead to slightly worse results (although LEGO-IRT
[
Liao et al. 2025
]
is often very close in performance and sometimes even better).
Each of the models aims to construct a different parameterisation that mirrors the 2PL likelihood in
eq.

14
.

Beta IRT

[
Noel and Dauvier 2007
]

Hyperpriors:

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta},\mu_{\theta,d},\mu_{\gamma,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10)

\displaystyle u_{\beta},u_{\theta,d},u_{\gamma,d}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)

Priors:

\displaystyle\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\beta},u_{\beta}^{-1})

\displaystyle\theta_{id}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta,d},u_{\theta,d}^{-1})

\displaystyle\gamma_{md}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\gamma,d},u_{\gamma,d}^{-1})

Linear Predictor:

\displaystyle z_{im}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}(\gamma_{md}\theta_{id})-\beta_{m}

Likelihood:

\displaystyle\mu_{im}=\exp(z_{im}/2)

\displaystyle Y_{im}\sim\text{Beta}(\mu_{im}+10^{-6},\mu_{im}^{-1}+10^{-6})

LEGO-IRT

[
Liao et al. 2025
]

Hyperpriors:

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10),\quad u_{\beta}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10),\quad u_{\theta,d}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)

\displaystyle\mu_{\gamma,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10),\quad u_{\gamma,d}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)

Priors:

\displaystyle\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\beta},u_{\beta}^{-1})

\displaystyle\theta_{id}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta,d},u_{\theta,d}^{-1})

\displaystyle\gamma_{md}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\gamma,d},u_{\gamma,d}^{-1})

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}\sim\text{HalfNormal}(1)

Linear Predictor:

\displaystyle\eta_{im}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}(\gamma_{md}\theta_{id})-\beta_{m}

Likelihood:

\displaystyle\text{logit}(Y_{im})\sim\mathcal{N}(\eta_{im},\sigma_{m})

Heteroskedastic Gaussian IRT

[
Balkır et al. 2026
]

Hyperpriors:

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta},\mu_{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10),\quad u_{\beta},u_{\theta}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)

Priors:

\displaystyle\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\beta},u_{\beta}^{-1})

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta},u_{\theta}^{-1})

\displaystyle k_{m}\sim\text{Gamma}(2,2)

Likelihood:

\displaystyle\mu_{im}=\sigma(\theta_{i}-\beta_{m})

\displaystyle\text{var}_{im}=k_{m}\mu_{im}(1-\mu_{im})+10^{-8}

\displaystyle Y_{im}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{im},\sqrt{\text{var}_{im}})

\beta^{3}
IRT

[
Chen et al. 2019
]

Priors:

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\sim\text{Beta}(1,1)

\displaystyle\delta_{m}\sim\text{Beta}(1,1)

\displaystyle\alpha_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(1,1)

Parameters:

\displaystyle\log A_{im}=\alpha_{m}(\ln\theta_{i}-\ln\delta_{m})

\displaystyle\log B_{im}=\alpha_{m}(\ln(1-\theta_{i})-\ln(1-\delta_{m}))

Likelihood:

\displaystyle A_{im}^{*}=\exp(\text{clamp}(\log A_{im}))+10^{-6}

\displaystyle B_{im}^{*}=\exp(\text{clamp}(\log B_{im}))+10^{-6}

\displaystyle Y_{im}\sim\text{Beta}(A_{im}^{*},B_{im}^{*})
