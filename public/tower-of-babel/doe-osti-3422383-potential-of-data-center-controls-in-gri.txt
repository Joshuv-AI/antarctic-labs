 Abstract
 The rapid proliferation of large data centers brings both challenges and opportunities for grid
 reliability. The data center resources and their potential flexibility have the potential to contribute
 resources to grid operations. Through capabilities like energy shifting and resource
 coordination, data centers can help reduce their net demand on the transmission network, as
 well as provide additional grid services to support reliable operation on the grid. While transient
 and long-term grid planning and operations are the scenarios that draw most attention, the
 quasi-steady state timeseries (QSTS) operation of data centers and grid bring interesting
 scenarios that can help evaluate the data center controls to aid grid services. This work is
 focused on modeling data centers for QSTS applications – incorporating the AI data center load
 profiles and building on the PNNL digital twin model for the thermal management loads to
 enable simulation studies to reveal the impact of data center controls on grid performance. This
 includes the integration of a QSTS battery and natural gas generator model to incorporate local
 resource impacts to the system. The simulation study is performed with a modified IEEE 24-Bus
 transmission system. Scenarios are focused on evaluating the data center load impacts on the
 transmission system and leveraging both data center and local generation controls to mitigate
 those impacts and provide additional grid services.

 The data center controls revealed the ability to contribute to two main kinds of grid services:
 preventing congestion on a weak grid by coordinating the data center resources with the
 collocated BESS and onsite generation; and the ability to help the grid operations during
 stressed times of operation like during a contingency. Leveraging these and other capabilities
 has the potential to help data centers become grid responsive assets, aiding in both their
 integration into the power system and grid reliability.

 1.0 Introduction
 Rapid growth in artificial intelligence (AI) compute is driving a new wave of large,
 energy‑intensive data center development across the United States. Unlike many conventional
 commercial loads, AI data centers—particularly those supporting large‑scale model training and
 inference—can present high load densities, fast ramping behavior at the facility level, and
 concentrated interconnection requests in constrained transmission zones. At the same time,
 these facilities increasingly include on‑site energy assets such as battery energy storage
 systems (BESS), backup generation, sophisticated power electronics (e.g., UPS systems), and
 advanced energy management systems. These characteristics create both operational
 concerns and a potential opportunity: AI data centers may become “grid‑interactive” resources
 capable of supporting quasi‑steady‑state grid operations (seconds‑to‑minutes balancing, load
 following, congestion management, and other operating‑reserve timeframes) if properly
 integrated through market rules, interconnection requirements, and operating practices.

 U.S. reliability organizations and federal agencies have highlighted the operational and planning
 implications of rapidly changing system conditions, including large new loads and an evolving
 resource mix. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) emphasizes that
 accurate demand forecasting, deliverability of reserves, and performance of power‑electronic
 resources are increasingly central to reliable operations [1-3]. In parallel, the Federal Energy
 Regulatory Commission (FERC) is advancing reforms to generator interconnection and
 long‑term regional transmission planning intended to respond to rapid changes in resource
 siting and grid needs while maintaining just and reasonable rates and reliable operations [4,5].
 The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and National Laboratories have documented pathways
 for more flexible demand, enhanced grid services from end‑use assets, and the role of storage
 and advanced inverter controls in maintaining reliability during the energy transition [6-8]. In this
 context, AI data centers represent a fast‑growing class of “large flexible loads” that could either
 stress quasi‑steady‑state operations—or be structured to help stabilize them.

 1.1.1 Quasi‑Steady‑State Operations and Why AI Loads Matter

 Power system operations broadly break down into two timescales: dynamics behaviors that
 occur in 1 µs to 1 second, and quasi-steady state behaviors that are 1 second to longer time
 durations (typically up to the hourly or daily level). Quasi‑steady‑state power system operations
 refer broadly to the operating regime in which the grid is managed around a continuously
 changing but near‑equilibrium balance of generation and demand. This includes regulation via
 automatic generation control (AGC), load following, contingency and operating reserves,
 interchange scheduling, and congestion management under normal conditions and credible
 contingencies. While transient stability and fast frequency response remain critical, day‑to‑day
 reliability is strongly shaped by quasi‑steady‑state challenges such as forecast error, ramping
 needs, transmission constraints, and the availability and performance of reserves. AI data
 centers can affect these operations in several ways.

 1.1.1.1 Scale and geographic concentration

 Many new AI campuses request hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts of service, sometimes
 clustered within the same load pocket or constrained corridor. This can change local power
 flows, import requirements, and planning assumptions. NERC’s long‑term reliability
 assessments underscore that regional adequacy and operating risk can be sensitive to

Introduction 8

 concentrated changes in load growth and to delays in transmission development relative to
 changing system needs [1-3].

 1.1.1.2 Operational variability behind the meter

 Although data centers are often described as steady loads, AI training clusters can exhibit
 sudden changes in energy demand due to job scheduling, redundancy events, cooling
 dynamics, and power capping schemes. At the bulk system level, these step changes can
 manifest as increased intra‑hour variability, which in turn can increase regulation and
 load‑following requirements. DOE’s grid modernization and flexibility work has highlighted that
 end‑use flexibility—enabled by controls, measurement, and communications—can reduce
 operational stress and support reliability when properly integrated [6-8].

 1.1.1.3 Power‑electronics‑rich interfaces and control interactions

 AI data centers rely on rectifiers and inverters in their uninterruptible power supply (UPS) and
 DC power systems, and increasingly on‑site BESS interfaced through inverters. These power
 electronic devices often have controls to quickly respond to changes on the system, like
 performing voltage control or responding to a disturbance. The fast-acting flexibility of these
 devices also means they have the ability to provide (or avoid degrading) essential reliability
 services. NERC reliability guidelines and multiple national laboratory efforts emphasize that
 performance expectations, models, and validation are necessary to ensure
 power‑electronics‑rich assets support—not undermine—bulk power system reliability [3, 9, 10].

 These factors imply that integrating AI data centers is no longer only about supplying energy
 and capacity; it also requires defining a robust operational relationship between data centers
 and the grid—what the grid requires from these large loads to ensure reliability and what
 services these facilities could provide in return.

 1.1.2 AI Data centers as Providers of Grid Services in the Quasi‑Steady‑State
 Regime

 A central opportunity is to treat AI data centers as controllable, grid‑interactive resources
 capable of providing services traditionally supplied only by generators. DOE and National
 Laboratories have long identified demand flexibility as a potential contributor to reliability and
 cost reduction, especially when enabled by advanced controls, telemetry, and verification [6-8].
 From [11], the six grid services are briefly defined as follows:
 • Energy Service – a scheduled production or consumption of energy at an electrical location
 over a committed period.
 • Reserve Service – a specified capacity to produce or consume energy at an electrical
 location when called upon over a committed period.
 • Regulation Service – continuously provides an increase or decrease in active power from
 an electrical location over a specified schedule period against a predefined active power
 basepoint following a service requestor’s signal. The signal interval is typically one to
 several seconds, and the associated performance period is ignorantly shorter in duration
 than the typical energy service period.
 • Frequency Response Service – responds to change in system frequency nearly
 instantaneously by consuming or producing power over a committed period.

Introduction 9

 • Voltage Management Service – provides voltage support (raise or lower) within a specified
 upper and lower voltage range at an electrical location over a committed period.
 • Blackstart Service – energize or remain available without grid electrical supply to energize
 part of the system over a committed period.

 1.1.2.1 Regulation and fast load following (seconds to minutes)

 Data centers can modulate non‑critical compute, adjust power capacities, or shift workloads
 among geographically distributed sites. While the energy impact may be modest, the ramping
 capability can be significant in MW/min terms. Where on‑site BESS exists (either as part of a
 UPS architecture or as dedicated storage), the facility can provide regulation with minimal
 disruption to compute by buffering power at the point of interconnection. National laboratory
 work on advanced inverters and grid‑forming/grid‑supportive control concepts further support
 the technical feasibility of fast, accurate responses from inverter‑interfaced assets when
 appropriately designed and validated [12].

 1.1.2.2 Operating reserves (contingency, supplemental) via load reduction and/or
 behind‑the‑meter assets

 Depending on market rules and interconnection arrangements, data centers may offer
 interruptible load, controllable demand, or injections from BESS to support reserves. NERC’s
 reliability framing for resource adequacy and operating risk emphasizes that reserve products
 must be deliverable, measurable, and dependable under stressed conditions—requirements
 that imply data center participation must include clear performance standards, telemetry,
 testing, and verification [1,2].

Introduction 10

 1.1.2.3 Congestion management and local capacity support

 In constrained areas, flexible consumption (or injection from BESS) can reduce loading on
 critical transmission elements during peak periods, forecast errors, or N‑1 events, thereby
 improving operational margin. FERC’s transmission planning reforms reflect the growing need
 for proactive, long‑horizon solutions to manage congestion and changing power flow patterns,
 and they provide a policy backdrop for considering non‑traditional solutions—provided they are
 transparent and do not substitute for needed transmission expansion where it is the least‑cost
 long‑term solution [5].

 1.1.2.4 Voltage support and power quality services

 Advanced inverter functions in UPS and BESS systems can provide reactive power and voltage
 control at the point of interconnection, subject to interconnection requirements and coordination
 with utility voltage regulation and protection practices. NERC guidance on inverter‑based
 performance, along with national laboratory inverter research, emphasizes that settings,
 controls, and models must be coordinated and validated to ensure predictable system behavior
 [3, 12].

 Implementing the services discussed in 1.2.2.1 - 1.2.2.4 requires more than technical capability.
 There must also be:

 (i) Participation pathways (utility programs and/or wholesale market rules)

 (ii) Operational visibility (telemetry, forecasting, and communications)

 (iii) Interconnection agreements that define response time, dispatchability, performance, and
 cyber/physical security requirements.

 DOE grid modernization work highlights that scalable flexibility depends on verification and
 interoperability; similarly, FERC’s interconnection reforms reinforce the importance of clear
 processes and requirements as new assets connect at scale [4].

 1.1.3 Role of Onsite Generation and BESS for Reliability and Grid Services

 Most large data centers are built around stringent reliability targets, historically achieved through
 redundant utility service, uninterruptible power supplies, and on‑site backup generators
 (commonly diesel; increasingly natural gas or alternative fuels), with growing adoption of BESS.
 These assets can be leveraged to manage both facility resilience and grid interaction, but they
 also introduce operational considerations that must be addressed explicitly.

 Onsite generation and BESS can influence quasi‑steady‑state operations in two ways:

 Potential reliability value: During system stress events, data centers can reduce net load by
 transferring to on‑site generation, providing rapid load relief. Where permitted and technically
 configured, certain facilities may be able to export power or operate in coordinated modes that
 support local reliability (subject to protection, safety, and market participation rules). In
 quasi‑steady‑state terms, this can resemble a dispatchable reduction in demand or a localized
 capacity contribution.

Introduction 11

 Operational and coordination challenges: Backup generators are often designed for
 emergency use and may have constraints on runtime, emissions permitting, black‑start
 dependence, or fuel supply logistics. From a reliability perspective, NERC’s emphasis on
 dependable performance under extreme conditions suggests that any reliance on onsite
 generation for grid services must include clear availability definitions, testing, and realistic
 assumptions about fuel and environmental constraints [1,2]. Poorly coordinated generator
 operation can also create local voltage/power quality issues or complicate restoration priorities.

 Battery energy storage systems (BESS) are particularly well suited to quasi‑steady‑state
 services due to fast response, accuracy, and controllability. However, they may still have
 constraints similar to standard backup generation from a grid interaction and reliability concern,
 with local voltage and power quality issues having equal relevance for BESS devices.
 Furthermore, managing the state of charge of the BESS can look similar to the fuel supply
 challenges for traditional generation, so proper management is needed to maximize their
 benefit. These challenges could be addressed with the right mix (energy source) and sizing of
 onsite generation.

 The DOE’s Energy Storage Grand Challenge roadmap identifies storage as a key flexibility
 resource that can support reliability and resilience across multiple time scales, especially when
 paired with advanced controls and appropriate operational frameworks [8]. For AI data centers,
 BESS can:

 • Smooth ramps and mitigate step changes in facility demand, reducing regulation burden
 on the bulk system.

 • Provide regulation and reserves without curtailing compute, by buffering power at the
 grid interface.

 • Support voltage/reactive needs through inverter controls (subject to interconnection
 requirements and coordination).

 • Improve ride‑through and resilience for short‑duration disturbances, potentially reducing
 the likelihood that a data center disturbance propagates as a noticeable grid event.

 National laboratories’ work on grid‑forming inverters highlights that inverter‑interfaced resources
 can provide “grid supportive” functions traditionally associated with synchronous machines, but
 that performance depends on control design, testing, and coordination with system operators
 [12]. Consequently, a key integration requirement for data center BESS is ensuring that the
 facility’s inverter controls (UPS and storage) are modeled, validated, and operated in a manner
 consistent with reliability expectations and local utility practices [1,12].

 1.1.4 Motivation and Scope

 Given accelerating AI data center deployment, the power sector faces a near‑term need to
 integrate these loads without undermining quasi‑steady‑state operating reliability, while also
 exploring whether they can contribute to flexibility and essential reliability services. This work is
 motivated by the need for a practical framework that:

 1. Characterizes AI data center load behavior and controllability relevant to
 quasi‑steady‑state operations;

Introduction 12

 2. Identifies grid services data centers can provide through flexible load, on‑site BESS, and
 coordinated use of onsite generation;

 3. Identify potential implementation requirements— performance verification, modeling, and
 coordination—to enable data centers to function as dependable participants in
 reliability‑critical functions.

 The broader objective is to bridge data center power engineering (focused on uptime and power
 quality inside the fence) with bulk power system operations (focused on balancing, reserves,
 deliverability, and system security). As DOE, FERC, and NERC publications collectively
 indicate, the grid is evolving toward a system where flexibility, visibility, and controllability across
 both supply and demand are increasingly important for maintaining reliability and managing
 costs [1–12]. AI data centers—given their scale, power‑electronics‑rich infrastructure, and
 emerging on‑site capabilities—are poised to be a pivotal test case for how large loads can be
 integrated as active participants in quasi‑steady‑state grid operations.

 2.0 QSTS Model of Data centers
 Quasi-Static Time Series (QSTS) simulation is widely used in power-system studies to evaluate
 how voltages, power flows, and equipment loading evolve over time in response to time-varying
 loads and generation along with energy storage variations [13], [14]. Unlike a single steady-
 state “snapshot” power flow, QSTS runs many sequential steady-state solutions using time-
 series inputs (e.g., seconds to minutes to hourly), with control actions updated at each step
 (e.g., capacitor switching, inverter volt/VAR control, transformer tap changes) [13-15]. This
 approach is well-suited to data centers because their demand is large and operationally
 dynamic (IT workload changes, cooling response, UPS behavior, generator testing), and
 because modern facilities increasingly include onsite resources (UPS batteries and standby
 generators) that interact with the grid over time [15,16]. QSTS provides a practical middle
 ground between fast electromagnetic transient (EMT) analysis and system-level planning
 studies, enabling assessment of feeder voltage excursions, transformer loading, losses, and
 VAR behavior over realistic operating horizons [13-15].

 A QSTS model of a data center typically represents: (i) the grid interconnection and service
 infrastructure (utility network service transformer, switchgear); (ii) the facility electrical
 distribution and major conversion stages (MV/LV distribution, UPS, PDUs); (iii) end-use load
 decomposition (IT load, cooling, auxiliaries); and (iv) onsite sources and controls (generators,
 UPS battery, and energy management logic) [17]. A key modeling decision is the level of
 aggregation appropriate for the study objective: distribution-utility QSTS often uses an
 equivalent “single large load” at the point of common coupling (PCC) with simplified control
 behavior, while facility-level studies may represent multiple internal buses and redundant paths
 to capture constraints and failover behavior [17,18].

 2.1 Types of Data centers
 Data centers can be categorized by ownership/tenancy, size, and availability requirements.
 Enterprise (on premises) data centers are owned and operated by a single organization, and
 their electrical and cooling systems are often constrained by existing building infrastructure and
 upgrade cycles [16,18]. Colocation facilities host multiple tenants; load growth can be stepwise
 as new halls or customer suites are commissioned, making staged capacity expansion and
 power allocation key operational concerns [18]. Hyperscale data centers are purpose-built at
 very large scale and engineered for high efficiency; they increasingly influence grid planning due
 to their large, relatively steady demand and potential participation in flexibility programs [18, 19]
 Edge data centers are smaller sites distributed close to users for low-latency services; while
 individually modest, their proliferation can create significant aggregate impacts on distribution
      networks [19,20].

 2.2 Data center Operating Timescales
 Data centers exhibit behavior across multiple timescales, and each scale maps to different
 modeling approaches and QSTS step-size choices.

 Seconds to minutes (s–min): Transfer sequences following disturbances—UPS ride-through,
 generator start, and staged motor restarts—occur on this timescale. These behaviors can be
 represented in QSTS using 1–10 s resolution when the objective is to capture step changes and
 control interactions on the distribution feeder or within the facility.

 Minutes to hours (min–h): IT workload variability, cooling control (e.g., economizer
 enable/disable), and utility voltage regulation actions often occur over minutes to hours. QSTS
 with 1–15 min steps are commonly used to evaluate voltage profiles, transformer loading, and
 VAR flow impacts over representative operational days or events such as demand response.

 2.3 Architecture and Components of a Typical Data center
 A typical data center comprises integrated subsystems: electrical supply and distribution, IT
 equipment, cooling/thermal management, and supervisory monitoring/control.

 Electrical supply and distribution: Medium-to-large data centers are commonly served at
 medium voltage through utility service equipment to facility switchgear and step-down
 transformers feeding low-voltage distribution. Redundant architectures (A/B feeds) provide
 alternate power paths to IT loads and reduce the risk of downtime [21-23].

 UPS and short-term power backup: UPS systems provide ride-through during grid events and
 condition power to sensitive IT loads. UPS behavior affects facility efficiency, power factor, and
 the load seen at the PCC. In QSTS, UPS is commonly represented as a controllable power

QSTS Model of Data centers 15

 conversion block with an efficiency curve and battery state-of-charge constraints rather than a
 switching-level inverter model [21-23].

 Onsite generation and long duration BESS: Standby generators and BESS support long-
 duration outages and may be tested periodically. QSTS representations typically include start-
 up delay, ramp limits, real/reactive power limits, and control mode (fixed PF, voltage control, or
 VAR support if allowed) [21-23].

 IT load and PUE context: Facility energy performance is often summarized by Power Usage
 Effectiveness (PUE), defined as total facility power divided by IT equipment power. While PUE
 is useful for benchmarking, QSTS studies often benefit from decomposing total demand into IT,
 cooling, and conversion losses using time-series inputs and simplified performance curves,
 because cooling and conversion losses vary with ambient conditions and loading [16, 21-23].

 Cooling plant: Cooling systems include compute room air handler (CRAH) / compute room air
 conditioning (CRAC) units, chillers (or direct evaporative systems), pumps, towers, and fans.
 Cooling power is strongly dependent on outdoor conditions and control strategies. QSTS can
 model cooling demand as an empirical function of IT load and weather or through a simplified
 physics-based COP approach where appropriate [16].

 2.4 QSTS Modeling of a Data center
 To examine grid impacts of the data center, models of the various components need to be
 selected. This section will outline the different components of the data center QSTS models, as
 well as the assumptions built into their operation.

 2.4.1 Modeling of Electrical Load

 The electrical load in a data center primarily consists of the compute load, thermal management
 load and a small portion of auxiliary loads.

 2.4.1.1 Modeling of the Auxiliary Loads

 Auxiliary loads are often smaller components of the data center load, representing components
 such as lighting, security systems, and general facility control and monitoring systems. The
 auxiliary load is modeled as a part of the static load on the transmission load bus where the
 data center is integrated. There is no specific model added to model the data center auxiliary
 load and it is lumped with the compute load for the simulations in this report.

 2.4.1.2 Modeling of Thermal Management Load

 Modern data centers are mission‑critical infrastructures that support cloud services, artificial
 intelligence, and high‑performance computing, and their rapid load growth has intensified the
 demands placed on power and cooling systems. As IT equipment converts nearly all consumed
 electrical energy into heat, precise thermal modeling becomes essential for ensuring safe
 operation, maximizing efficiency, and preventing hotspots that jeopardize reliability. Traditional
 thermal analysis often relies on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to resolve airflow patterns
 and heat transfer within rooms, racks, and cooling subsystems, but these models can be
 computationally expensive and sensitive to modeling assumptions. As data centers evolve

QSTS Model of Data centers 16

 toward higher rack densities and more dynamic workload patterns, scalable and physically
 grounded thermal modeling approaches are increasingly important for design, monitoring, and
 operational decision‑making.

 The case study considers a chilled-water–cooled AI data center equipped with rear-door heat
 exchangers (RDHx) (shown in Figure 3). The system is representative of heavily coupled
 thermal and electrical dynamics: rack heat loads drive rear-door heat exchange, chilled-water
 return temperature depends on aggregate IT-side heat removal and flow, chiller power depends
 on both chilled- and condenser-water conditions, and cooling-tower performance depends on
 ambient wet-bulb temperature. The IT load for each rack is determined by scaling down a
 typical AI data center (taken from [24]) to emulate IT load at the rack-level. For simplicity, it
 assumed similar IT-load at the individual rack-level for the data center.

 Figure 3. Chilled door cooling configuration

 The thermal load of the AI data center is evaluated using a physics‑based Modelica model that
 represents the coupled behavior of chillers, cooling towers, pumps, rear‑door heat exchangers
 (RDHx), rack loads, and room‑level thermal dynamics. Figure 4 presents the mapping between
 subsystem boundaries and the plant and IT variables.

 The Modelica framework provides a system‑level simulation that enforces energy balances
 across water‑side and air‑side loops, incorporates measured rack‑level IT power as the
 heat‑generation input, and propagates the

 ing thermal loads through plant and room components. The chilled-water pump network
 corresponds to the primary chilled-water pump block. The cooling-tower network corresponds to
 the cooling-tower fan and heat rejection block. The chiller network corresponds to the chiller
 block and includes condenser-water return as an output. The rack networks correspond to rack
 thermal models that map room inlet conditions and rack loads to rack outlet air temperature.
 The RDHx networks correspond to the rear-door heat exchanger coil and fan power behavior.
 The room network corresponds to a reduced-order representation of the data hall volume
 temperature driven by aggregated rear-door outlet conditions and room setpoint.

 This physics-based model captures how changes in IT power, flow rates, setpoints, and ambient
 conditions influence return‑water temperatures, cooling capacity, and room thermal response.
 Through this structured representation of interconnected subsystems, the Modelica model
 enables controlled evaluation of counterfactual scenarios and provides a reliable baseline for
 understanding and predicting the thermal behavior of data centers.

 3.0 Test System and Scenario Definitions
 The test system used for the case studies is the modified IEEE 24-Bus transmission system
 model. This model has 10 generation buses and 17 load buses with two major voltages: 230 kV
 (transmission system) and 138 kV (sub transmission system). Figure 17 shows the one-line
 diagram of the IEEE 24-Bus test system with the data center integrated.

 Figure 17. One line diagram of the modified IEEE 24-bus test system with the data center

 The test system is modified to increase the base case load to reflect a system with limited
 capacity to integrate additional load where a few transmission lines are overloaded to ~135% of
 its rated capacity. The system is in a heavily-stressed condition, where significant changes in
 the load could cause further outages or violate stability operating criteria (e.g., N-1 security
 constraints). The focus of using this model will be on the data center impacts in this heavily
 loaded case – an actual system would include other controls such as additional imports from
 neighboring territories, generator redispatch or load shed/demand response programs.

 On the modified test system, a data center load is added at Bus 4 along with some collocated

 local voltage support (Volt/VAR mode) at the data center bus, helping maintain operational
 voltages within constraints.

 The data center load is modeled according to the developed QSTS model of the data center

 3.1 Scenario Definitions
 The scenarios for evaluating the potential of controlling data center resources under different
 operating conditions of the grid. The various scenarios that are being evaluated are:

 1. Base Case System Performance: This scenario reflects on the grid performance when
 the data center load is integrated.

 2. Grid Constrained Operation: This scenario is aimed to illustrate the utility of the on-site
 generation and BESS at the data center in cases of limited grid capacity to support the
 data center loads. This scenario is closely tied to the “bring your own capacity” approach
 that is being evaluated by the industry and the utilities.

 3. Controlling Onsite Resources: These scenarios illustrate how the grid-constrained
 operation can be supported by specific control of the local resources like the collocated
 BESS. These controls are evaluated with limited grid capacity and unlimited grid
 capacities.

 4. Controlling the Data Center Load: These scenarios illustrate the effects of controlling the
 data center load on grid performance. These scenarios inform the potential of data
 center load control aimed to support constrained grid operations.

 4.0 Simulation Studies
 The simulation studies aim to understand scale of the challenge of data center load integration
 on a transmission system and the ability of the data center resources (loads and onsite
 generation with collocated BESS) to help address some of the challenges faced by the grid
 operators. The test system is modified to be overloaded to test and evaluate how the data
 center controls can help to improve the grid operating conditions. Demonstrating grid flexibility
 during normal operating conditions is helpful to overall grid stability, but demonstrating data
 center contributions on the overloaded system helps quantify a data center role in providing
 critical grid services to aid in stabilizing the stressed power system. All plots and analysis will
 focus on active power and congestion on the system. Reactive power capabilities were utilized
 for local voltage regulation and power factor correction, but the VAR injections had minimal
 impact on the overloaded system; plots of this behavior are omitted for space. Furthermore, all
 simulations and scenarios in this section demonstrate the impact and flexibility of data center
 load and local resources on the grid. The data center resources are expected to be a part of the
 overall grid services that include demand response, generation redispatch and capacity
 reserves. The focus of these simulations is to explore the flexibility of data center controls.

 4.2.2.2 Collocated BESS – Regulation Services on Charge and Discharge

 The prior control scenario demonstrated the onsite NG and BESS can provide beneficial
 services to the grid, but the prioritized SOC recovery for the BESS still resulted in large system
 demand requests and fast ramping adjustments being absorbed by the larger electric grid.
 Such a scenario may be deployed if the BESS is primarily serving as a backup power supply,
 and grid services are a marginal element of the dispatch. In this control variant, the BESS is not
 primarily a backup power source, so charging is not prioritized and the target BESS SOC is
 reduced to provide both charging and discharging headroom (SOC starts at 60%).

 Figure 25(a) shows the data center load, which is identical to the profile shown in Figure 22(a).
 However, Figure 25(b) shows the approach to meeting the demand has changed, with the
 BESS leveraging its charging to regulate toward a zero-grid power condition. When data center
 load drops below the set output of the NG, the BESS switches to charging mode and absorbs
 the excess power, but does not draw additional power from the grid. In this control regime, the
 BESS is providing both energy reserve and regulation service capabilities. The low demand
 time regulation (t = 900 to 1250 s) is more effective due to the BESS not reaching the power
 limit (BESS has charging and discharging headroom), whereas the high load scenarios (t = 360
 to 900 s), some portion of the regulation needs to come from the grid. However, in both cases,
 the large 100 MW swings in data center load are absorbed by the BESS and mitigate their
 impact on potential grid stability during the stressed condition.

 4.3 Onsite Generation and Collocated BESS Size and Control Impact
 In the previous section, the results revealed the benefits of the onsite generation and BESS

 present. For both scenarios, the grid connection is limited to 30 MW of demand. Any load
 beyond 30 MW, the data center must either curtail its load (compute/IT and thermal) or utilize
 the local resources to meet the excess demand. The generation is assumed to be dispatchable

 further constraints. If used as emergency backup generation only, the BESS may have SOC
 limits to ensure critical loads can run for a specific duration, and the NG or other fuel-based
 generation may have runtime limitations from local or federal regulations.

 4.4 Controlling Data center Load
 The flexible load components of the data center are mainly the thermal management load and
 the computational load (referenced as IT load). Both the IT and thermal load are based on the
 model explained in Section 2.4. Changes to either property are reflected in detailed data center
 model, which captures the interaction between IT/compute load and the thermal load of the

Simulation Studies 43

 system to properly update the total data center load presented to the transmission system
 model.

 The simulation duration for these scenarios is modified to four hours from 16:00 hours to 20:00
 hours to consider a peak demand duration on the grid (17:00 to 20:00). Greater simulation
 fidelity is used when examining data center load, which helps capture thermal management load
 and the IT load interactions. For example, sudden IT load changes do not instantaneously
 impact the cooling load; the chilled air and chilled water systems both have thermal mass that
 slowly changes in temperature. The cooling system responds to this mass temperature, so
 thermal control actions and dynamics will often occur a short time period after any IT load
 change and will be more gradual as that thermal mass is heated or chilled.

 4.4.1 Controlling Thermal Management Load

 Cooling loads are adjusted by changing the chiller water target temperature, as was described
 in Section 2.4. Per the overview, a peak demand condition occurs on the grid between 17 h and
 20 h, so demand limitations are imposed during that time period. To get maximum short-term
 demand, the chiller water is set from 18oC to 50oC for 17 h to 17.5 h. However, this high
 temperature setpoint can only be accommodated for a short interval, so temperatures are
 adjusted down back to 38oC for the 17.5 h to 19 h. Another short-term operating limit is reached,
 so at 19 h the temperature has to be adjusted back to 28oC to maintain the hardware
 temperature. Here it would remain until the demand limitation expires, which is beyond the time
 shown in the simulation.

 The demand impact of these changes is shown in Figure 33. Note that the cooling load demand
 does not change instantaneously with the different temperature set point changes above. As the
 set point adjusts, the pumps, air conditioning system, and even the working fluid (air and water)
 have dynamics associated with the system adjusting to meet the new requirement. While the
 response is within two minutes, it limits the cooling load adjustments to only be useful for slower
 grid service applications like reserve or energy services.

 5.0 LDDL Control and Grid Services
 The data center control for grid support and grid services have potential to impact the overall
 grid performance. The exact impact will depend on the size of the available resources at the
 data center (generation resources, IT load, and cooling load). Larger data centers have greater
 resources and more flexibility to provide larger grid services, but are also a larger load and
 impact on the grid. A large power and large energy-capacity BESS provides the greatest
 flexibility and ability to provide grid services, but at a large capital expense. The simulation
 studies discussed in the previous section demonstrated that with sufficient observability and
 controllability, the data center resources can provide varying levels of capability back to the
 electric grid. However, there are several considerations that need to be taken into account
 when planning for grid services from data center resources.

 5.1 Data center Generation and BESS related Controls and Grid
 Services
 1. BESS can prove to be a very useful resource as it has the potential to inject or absorb
 power, but the state-of-charge (SOC) of the BESS needs to be maintained at certain
 levels to derive this flexibility. The BESS may also have underlying SOC limits if it is in
 place to provide backup power, so that limit will need to be respected as well. The
 BESS control will need to be a function of the SOC.

 2. BESS can provide reactive power to help regulate local voltage. While the reactive
 power flexibility does not significantly impact SOC significantly, the overall rating of the
 inverter needs to consider expected active and reactive power (MW and MVAR) to
 ensure proper sizing – active power capabilities are expected to be the primary
 capability of interest from the BESS.

 3. Onsite generation, especially leveraging the BESS, can be crucial in helping with peak
 shifting and peak shaving applications (energy and reserve grid services). This is true
 both in normal operations and during contingencies to help minimize the data center
 impact on the grid.

 5.2 Data center Load Control and Grid Services
 1. Data center compute/IT load control will be heavily dependent on the types of
 applications the data center hosts. Results were shown for LLM training loads, which
 tend to be the “worst case” in terms of magnitude and fluctuations. Data centers that are
 not based on client web services (e.g., social media organizations, crypto currency
 mining) will have different load profiles, and potentially greater compute load flexibility.
 Crypto mining applications have especially shown great value in providing energy and
 reserve services [19].

 2. Data center thermal management load control also provides some capability for grid
 services. Detailed knowledge of the cooling system (e.g., direct HVAC, chilled water
 systems, and rack vs. facility-level controls) is needed to define the capabilities of the
 system to provide grid services. During stressed grid operating conditions, the load
 control of data centers can be important as it provides short-term demand relief.

 6.0 Conclusions
 Data center controls, including controlling the data center onsite generation, collocated BESS
 and the constituent data center IT and thermal load, has potential to contribute to enhance grid
 performance. This report has discussed data center modeling for QSTS timescales and
 integration of the models with transmission models with commercial solver, PSS-E.

 The data center QSTS model includes model development based on the available AI data
 center load profiles from the NERC data center whitepaper. The modeling also included models
 coupling data center compute/IT load and thermal loading to help evaluate potential capabilities
 there, leveraging prior PNNL modeling efforts. Onsite generation and local resource
 contributions were considered though inclusion of a natural gas turbine and BESS. Impacts on
 an overloaded IEEE 24-bus transmission model helped evaluate the impact of different control
 actions, especially on grid congestion/line load in the region.

 With the scenarios examined, the key findings from this analysis are broadly threefold:

 1. The data center onsite resources are capable of directly and indirectly contributing to grid
 operations through grid services like:

 a. Reactive power control to improve the local grid voltage regulation.

 b. Providing energy and/or reserve services by supplying the data center load in
 durations of limited grid capacity.

 c. Regulation services can be provided through fast acting controls of BESS to help
 absorb some of the data center load fluctuations to produce a relatively smooth
 demand on the grid.

 2. The data center load control can potentially help with demand management on the grid
 during normal and stressed operating scenarios:

 a. The thermal load management and adjustment of internal cooling temperatures can
 provide some demand relief. However, thermal load and savings are much smaller
 compared to the larger compute/IT load, so grid service potential is oriented more
 toward smaller requirements or marginal gains during times of extreme grid stress or
 contingencies. Thermal load management coupled with local thermal energy storage
 solutions may provide greater value, but that scenario was not explored in this study.

 b. The IT load control (e.g., data storage management scheduling) can also be useful in
 stressed operating conditions with limited grid capacity. Coordination with the
 compute application requirements is necessary to determine the level of reduction
 feasible. Geographic redispatch of the compute/IT load to other data centers needs
 to ensure the grid impact is not simply moved to an equally-stressed region.

 3. Use of the local resources (onsite generation and BESS) and manipulation of the data
 center load can provide grid services and overall positive grid impacts. However,
 compute application sensitivities and “roles” of the local resources must be considered in
 their potential to provide grid services. If local generation and the BESS are sized
 primarily for backup capabilities, grid services must be balanced against the increased
 risk to data center operations.
