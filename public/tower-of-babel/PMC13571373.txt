Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is associated with substantial morbidity, recurrent heart failure hospitalizations, and impaired functional capacity.1 Because many patients are older and carry multiple comorbidities, surgery is often deferred and transcatheter therapies have emerged as important treatment options. Transcatheter tricuspid valve edge-to-edge repair (TV TEER) has shown favorable safety and efficacy in selected patients, including those with advanced TR and anatomically complex tricuspid valve disease2,3

Successful TV TEER depends not only on leaflet pathology, coaptation gap, and imaging quality, but also on obtaining a favorable access trajectory and device coaxiality relative to the tricuspid valve. Anatomical structures within the right atrium may interfere with this process. We report a case of successful TV TEER performed through a right internal jugular approach after failure of the standard transfemoral approach due to a prominent Eustachian ridge.

Case Presentation

A 79-year-old man was referred for evaluation of symptomatic severe TR. He reported exertional dyspnea and lower-extremity edema consistent with NYHA functional class III symptoms, with several recent hospital admissions for acute decompensated heart failure. His medical history was notable for functional TR, wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis treated with acoramidis for the previous 2 years, hypertension, atrial fibrillation status post left atrial appendage closure, pulmonary hypertension, congestive hepatopathy, and chronic kidney disease.

Transesophageal echocardiography showed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, a severely enlarged right ventricle with moderately reduced systolic function, and torrential TR caused by central malcoaptation of all 3 leaflets. Tricuspid annular dimensions on computed tomography (CT) were 56.5 × 42.5 × 52.3 mm, with a perimeter of 162 mm.

Heart team assessment concluded that because of his multiple comorbidities and high surgical risk, transcatheter treatment was preferable. Transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) was considered but not favored. In this patient, long-term anticoagulation was undesirable because anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation had previously been discontinued due to recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding associated with anemia, which ultimately led to left atrial appendage closure. Therefore, TV TEER was selected as the preferred transcatheter strategy.

Investigations

Preprocedural imaging was notable for a prominent Eustachian ridge at the inferior vena cava–right atrial junction. This structure was visible on both transesophageal echocardiography and CT, including 3-dimensional reconstruction (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2), and measured approximately 27 mm in length. Based on preprocedural imaging, the ridge was recognized as a potentially relevant anatomic feature; however, its procedural impact became fully evident only during attempted transfemoral device delivery.

Management

The patient underwent TV TEER under general anesthesia using the TriClip G4 system from the right femoral approach.

However, despite multiple attempts, clip delivery through the femoral route was not feasible. The prominent Eustachian ridge significantly limited maneuverability and biased the system toward an anterior and septal orientation, preventing adequate coaxiality with the regurgitant jet and target grasping plane.

After intraprocedural discussion, the femoral strategy was abandoned and the procedure was converted to a right internal jugular approach. From this superior trajectory, the delivery system advanced into the right atrium without the limitation imposed by the prominent Eustachian ridge, allowing improved coaxiality and more favorable device trajectory (Figure 2).

Fluoroscopic Image After Conversion to the Right Internal Jugular Approach, Showing Improved Trajectory and More Favorable Alignment of the Delivery System Toward the Anterior-Septal Coaptation Line

Because the delivery sheath orientation from the jugular approach is reversed relative to the transfemoral approach, device manipulation required adaptation. The control knobs generated different directional responses compared with the standard femoral orientation (Figure 3); for example, the “F” knob flexed the device toward the anterior direction rather than posteriorly. With this in mind, 2 XTW clips were implanted in the anterior-septal position, selected on the basis of the coaptation gap and leaflet length. Adequate leaflet grasping was achieved, resulting in a reduction of TR from torrential to mild without an increase in transvalvular gradient (Video 3). The final mean gradient was 1.0 mm Hg.

The right internal jugular access site was closed with 1 figure-of-eight suture with good hemostasis. Total procedural time was approximately 2.5 hours. No intraprocedural hypotension, arrhythmias, or other complications occurred.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged 2 days after the procedure. Transthoracic echocardiography at 24 hours and again at 30 days demonstrated both clips in stable position, no significant tricuspid stenosis, and mild residual TR. At 30-day follow-up, the patient had improved to NYHA functional class II, had no rehospitalizations, and his diuretic dose had been reduced by 50%.

Discussion

This case highlights 3 important procedural concepts in contemporary tricuspid intervention: first, TV TEER is an effective transcatheter option for selected high-risk patients with severe TR; second, procedural success depends not only on leaflet anatomy but also on achieving a favorable access trajectory and device coaxiality; and third, alternative venous access may be decisive when standard transfemoral delivery is limited by right atrial anatomy.

TV TEER has become an established therapy for selected patients with severe symptomatic TR who are poor surgical candidates.2 Clinical studies have shown that edge-to-edge repair can safely reduce TR severity and improve symptoms, including in patients with advanced disease and challenging anatomy.3,4 Our patient was an appropriate transcatheter edge-to-edge repair candidate because he had torrential functional TR, recurrent heart failure admissions, and high surgical risk. Importantly, TTVR was less attractive in this case because postprocedural anticoagulation is generally required after valve replacement, whereas this patient had previously discontinued anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation because of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding and anemia, which had prompted prior left atrial appendage closure. For this reason, leaflet repair represented a more suitable transcatheter strategy than valve replacement.

The present case also underscores that successful TV TEER depends not only on regurgitation severity or coaptation gap, but also on the ability to achieve stable and coaxial alignment of the delivery system relative to the tricuspid valve. In patients with complex tricuspid anatomy, procedural difficulty may arise from large coaptation gaps, severe leaflet tethering, acoustic shadowing, multiple leaflet segments, or multiple coaptation planes.3 In our case, however, the dominant procedural limitation was not leaflet grasping per se, but the inability to orient the delivery system appropriately from the femoral route because a prominent Eustachian ridge displaced the system anteriorly and septally, preventing an adequate trajectory toward the regurgitant jet and target grasping plane.

The Eustachian ridge is a normal right atrial anatomic structure located near the inferior vena cava (IVC) orifice and can be identified with modern 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional imaging techniques.5 Although often clinically silent, when prominent it may have important procedural implications and may be underrecognized if not specifically sought on preprocedural imaging.6 In this patient, the Eustachian ridge was visible on both transesophageal echocardiography and preprocedural CT, but its full significance became clear only during the procedure. In retrospect, the prominent Eustachian ridge was evident on preprocedural CT, although it was not initially thought likely to preclude a transfemoral approach. In our practice, CT is routinely performed as part of the heart team evaluation to determine whether patients are better suited for TV TEER or TTVR. Although CT is not required for all TV TEER procedures, this case highlights that, when available, careful review of right atrial anatomy may help predict unfavorable transfemoral trajectory in selected anatomically complex cases.

Although left femoral venous access can be a useful alternative in complex tricuspid valve interventions because it may provide a more superior and lateral trajectory compared with right femoral access,7 particularly in cases with inadequate right atrial height, we considered that any femoral approach would likely encounter the same limitation in this case because the primary obstacle was the prominent Eustachian ridge at the IVC-right atrial junction. In our TTVR experience,8 switching from right to left femoral access may improve right atrial height in selected patients, but this strategy is not universally effective, particularly when the key limitation is not chamber height but an anatomic barrier along the IVC entry course. Once a superior access strategy was selected, the right internal jugular vein was preferred because it offered a shorter and less tortuous course and could be adopted immediately without major changes in room setup. Although the left internal jugular vein may reduce the likelihood of septal hugging or depth-related issues in some cases, this consideration is more relevant in TTVR, given the complexity of valve delivery and device characteristics.

Although some of the earliest tricuspid edge-to-edge repair procedures were performed through a transjugular approach using the MitraClip system,9,10 contemporary practice with dedicated tricuspid repair platforms has largely evolved toward transfemoral delivery as the standard workflow. In this context, our case highlights that, although not commonly performed with the TriClip system, a transjugular approach remains a valuable alternative in selected anatomically challenging cases when standard transfemoral access does not provide an adequate trajectory for successful repair.

Finally, this case reinforces the importance of multimodality imaging and intraprocedural flexibility. Careful review of preprocedural imaging, recognition of right atrial anatomic constraints, and willingness to convert to an alternative access route allowed successful completion of repair with excellent clinical results.

Conclusions

A prominent right atrial Eustachian ridge can significantly complicate transfemoral TV TEER by limiting device maneuverability and preventing adequate coaxial alignment with the tricuspid valve. In selected cases, a right internal jugular approach may serve as a useful alternative when femoral delivery is anatomically unfavorable, restoring a more favorable trajectory and enabling successful repair. This case emphasizes the importance of careful preprocedural assessment of right atrial anatomy, flexible procedural planning, and familiarity with the technical differences of transjugular device manipulation.
