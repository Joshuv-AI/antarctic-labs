Investigation of the effect of a pressure sore care bundle in reducing the development of operating room pressure injury in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery: a non-randomized controlled study
Authors: Hatice Eğilmez, Ayla Yava, Aynur Koyuncu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrthopedic surgical procedures pose a significant risk for operating room-acquired pressure injuries development in patients due to the long operating times, immobilization, and special positioning requirements. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a pressure injury care bundle in preventing operating room-acquired pressure injuries in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery. The secondary aim was to assess its impact on pain, fear of falling, and patient comfort.MethodsThis non-randomized controlled study was conducted between July 2021 and April 2022 in the orthopedics and traumatology clinic of a public hospital. A total of 190 patients (control group: 122, study group: 68) were included in the study. Data were collected using the Patient Identification Form, the 3 S Intraoperative Risk Assessment Scale of Pressure Sore (3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore), the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP, 2016) Pressure Injury Staging System, the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), the Fear of Falling Scale (FFS), and the Shortened General Comfort Questionnaire (SGCQ). Statistical analyses were performed using the Independent Samples t-test, the Mann-Whitney U test, and Chi-square test. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of the patients included in the study was 72.01 ± 11.58. The majority were female and had at least one chronic disease. At baseline, the control and study groups were similar and homogeneous in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics (p > 0.05). Still, the 3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore risk score was significantly higher in the study group (p < 0.05). The incidence of pressure injuries in the study group (5.7%) was lower than in the control group (18.4%), and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Additionally, on postoperative day 3, the study group had lower pain scores, significantly reduced fear of falling, and higher comfort levels (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study demonstrated that the pressure sore care bundle was effective in reducing operating room-acquired pressure injuries. Additionally, this intervention had positive effects on patients’ pain, fear of falling, and comfort levels.

Background

Each year, millions of patients worldwide undergo surgical procedures, and operating room-acquired pressure injuries (ORPIs) remain a serious clinical problem despite advances in modern medicine, surgical techniques, and care practices. Globally, ORPIs account for approximately 45% of all hospital-acquired pressure injuries, with reported prevalence ranging from 4% to 45% depending on the clinical setting [1, 2]. These injuries typically occur within the first 48–72 h postoperatively and negatively affect patient safety, recovery, and quality of care [3, 4]. In Türkiye, the incidence of ORPIs has been reported to range between 12.9% and 40.4% [5–7]. These figures highlight the importance of the issue within local healthcare services and indicate the need for the development of effective prevention strategies at the national level.

ORPIs are serious complications that arise due to the prolonged immobility inherent to surgical procedures, anesthesia-related physiological changes, and preoperative intrinsic and extrinsic factors, and they negatively affect patient safety and quality of care [3, 4].

Patients undergoing orthopedic surgery constitute a particularly high-risk group because of their restricted mobility and prolonged time in surgery. Indeed, the literature reports that the prevalence of pressure injuries in orthopedics and traumatology clinics can reach as high as 18.5%, while the incidence of ORPIs following orthopedic surgery has been reported to be up to 20.6% [7, 8]. To prevent this problem, “care bundles,” which integrate multiple evidence-based interventions into a structured approach, have recently come to the forefront in nursing practice [1, 9]. Pressure sore care bundles typically include risk assessment, skin and tissue assessment, skin care, adequate nutrition and fluid intake, repositioning, early mobilization, and the use of support surfaces [1, 10]. Studies have demonstrated that such bundles significantly reduce the incidence of pressure injuries; for example, in an intensive care unit study, the incidence decreased from 32.8% to 7.1%, in elderly patients with hip fractures the application of polyurethane foam at the sacrum reduced the rate from 15.4% to 4.5%, and in another perioperative care bundle study, a 50% reduction was observed in the first year with no cases reported in the subsequent two years [10–12]. However, most existing bundles tend to focus on single preventive strategies (e.g., repositioning alone or the use of pressure-distributing materials) and do not adequately incorporate holistic nursing interventions that combine skin care, mobilization, nutrition, and hydration [13, 14]. The limited number of studies evaluating the effectiveness of comprehensive, evidence-based care bundles in preventing ORPIs highlights the necessity for further research in this area.

This study is based on the need to examine the clinical effectiveness of an evidence-based and holistic care approach for the prevention of ORPIs. The current literature provides limited findings regarding the use of such care bundles, and comprehensive data are particularly scarce for patients undergoing orthopedic surgery. In this context, the results obtained are expected not only to contribute to the literature but also to enhance the quality of nursing care in clinical practice, support patient safety, and promote the widespread implementation of evidence-based strategies for the prevention of complications. The primary aim of this study was thus to evaluate the effectiveness of a pressure injury care bundle in preventing ORPIs in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery. The secondary aim was to assess its impact on pain, fear of falling, and patient comfort.

Methods

This non-randomized controlled study with control and study groups of post-orthopedic surgery patients was conducted in the orthopedics and traumatology clinic of a public hospital between July 1, 2021, and April 30, 2022.

The study population comprised 1652 patients who underwent surgery in the clinic on the dates mentioned above, while the sample consisted of voluntary patients who met the inclusion criteria and those considered at “risk” for pressure injury (score of ≥ 18) on the 3 S Intraoperative Risk Assessment Scale of Pressure Sore (3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore), (n = 190) (Fig. 1).

Inclusion Criteria: Patients undergoing general or spinal anesthesia, those aged ≥ 18 years, those without a history of surgery in the last month, those without communication issues, those with ≥ 18 points on the 3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore, those admitted to the emergency clinic, those scheduled for trauma-related surgery within 24 h in the emergency clinic, and those volunteering to participate in the study.

Exclusion Criteria: Patients whose surgery was canceled, those who had pressure injuries, those who were bedridden, those who received local or regional anesthesia, those who were transferred to the ICU postoperatively, and those who wished to opt out of the study after previously volunteering.

3S intraoperative RAS of pressure sore

The 3S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore was originally developed by Gao et al. (2015) [15], and its Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Soyer and Özbayır (2018) [16]. The Turkish version of the scale was used in this study. Permission to use the Turkish adaptation of the scale was obtained via email from the authors prior to the start of the study. This scale consist of nine items, each scale is scored between 1 and 4, and the score obtained ranges from 9 to 36. Studies have identified a cutoff point of 23; however, some studies have also reported that pressure injury develops in patients with a score < 23. The risk of experiencing operating room pressure injuries increases with an increasing score [15, 16]. In this study, patients with scores of ≥ 18 and ≤ 17 were considered to be at high and low risk of pressure ulcer development, respectively.

Randomization was not applied in this study. The main reason for this was that patients in the orthopedic clinic were accommodated in double rooms. Having both control and study group patients in the same room at the same time could have led to information and practice transfer (contamination bias), thereby hindering an accurate assessment of the intervention’s true effect. Therefore, the most appropriate and feasible approach was to adopt a non-randomized design in which the control and intervention groups were formed in two consecutive time periods. The data of the control and study groups were collected between July 1, 2021, and January 15, 2022, and between January 16, 2022 and April 30, 2022, respectively.

The orthopedics and traumatology clinic has a total capacity of 30 beds, with 15 rooms and two patient beds in each room. The routine care provided to the control group consisted of the standard practices of the clinic. This care included regular monitoring of vital signs, administration of analgesia and fluid therapy as prescribed by the physician, postoperative wound dressing, support for mobilization, and general nursing observations. However, the routine care did not include systematic skin care, protocol-based repositioning at specific intervals, standardized early mobilization practices, or structured monitoring of nutrition and hydration, which were part of the pressure injury prevention care bundle. Therefore, compared to routine care, the care bundle was a more comprehensive, protocol-based, and structured approach.

The G*Power version 3.1.9.2 (G*Power, Düsseldorf, Germany) was used to determine the sample size. A previous study conducted using the 3 S Intraoperative Risk Assessment Scale of Pressure Sore (3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore) was taken as a reference [16]. The minimum sample size was calculated as 190 patients (122 and 68 in the control and study groups, respectively) with a power, error, and effect size of 95%, 5%, and d = 0.50 respectively. When the required number was reached, the sample size was calculated with the 3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore data of the study using the G*Power software, as mentioned above. It was determined that at least 95 patients should be included in the control group, and at least 53 in the intervention group with a power, error, and two-tailed effect size of 95%, 5%, and d = 0.62, respectively. Accordingly, the sample size was considered to be sufficient, and data collection and implementation of the research were terminated.

Data collection tools

Patient identification form

This form was prepared by the researchers to collect data for the study. The form included questions about the sociodemographic characteristics and diagnostic features of the patients, such as age, gender, height, weight, educational status, history of chronic diseases, and medical diagnosis.

Visual analog scale (VAS)

The VAS was used to assess pain perception. A vertical line was divided into equal segments between 0, representing “no pain”, and 10, representing “the worst pain.”

Fear of falling scale (FFS)

The FFS was used to determine the patient’s fear of falling, and VAS was used for quantitative evaluation of the data. A vertical line was divided into equal segments between 0, representing “I am not afraid of falling at all”, and 10, representing “I am extremely afraid of falling.”

Shortened general comfort questionnaire (SGCQ)

This was used to determine the patient’s comfort level during their hospitalization. The scale was developed by Kolcaba in 2006 and comprises three subdimensions, namely, relief (nine items), relaxation (nine items), and superiority (10 items), and 28 items in total. All items are scored with a six-point Likert-type scale. The score varies between 28 and 168, and the comfort level increases with an increase in the scale score. In the original validity and reliability study of the scale, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated to be 0.82, 0.83, and 0.86 at different times [17, 18]. However, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was calculated to be 0.82 in the Turkish validity and reliability study, and those of its subdimensions were 0.81, 0.81, and 0.76 for relief, relaxation, and superiority, respectively [18]. In this study, the coefficient of the scale was 0.90, and those of its subdimensions were 0.74, 0.71, and 0.81 for relief, superiority, and relaxation, respectively.

National pressure ulcer advisory panel (NPUAP, 2016) pressure injury staging system

This was used to assess and classify patients’ pressure injuries. It is recommended that a pressure ulcer classification system be used for wound assessment, classification, and documentation of the level of tissue loss [1]. Focusing on pressure sore prevention and management, the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) serves as a unique resource for health professionals, government, the public and health care organizations. The NPIAP addresses pressure sore issues through the use of educational materials, participation in national conferences, and support of public policy, education, and research efforts [19].

Pressure sore sites and stages follow-up record form

The researchers created this form using information from the literature and knowledge about clinical conditions. The evaluation results of patients at risk of experiencing pressure injury were recorded on this form, encompassing details such as date, time, and pressure injury sites and stages.

Pressure sore care bundle clinical data collection form

This form was prepared by the researchers in accordance with the most recently published clinical guideline [1], high-level evidence studies [1, 19–24], and the practices of the orthopedics and traumatology clinic where the study was conducted. The pressure sore care bundle comprises 17 subdimensions, which include the following four parameters: skin care, positioning, early mobilization, and nutrition and fluid intake (Fig. 2). The Lawshe technique was used to calculate the content validity of the bundle [25]. Opinions were obtained from eight experts in the field of surgical diseases and nursing. A scoring form containing all items of the care bundle was distributed to the experts, and their suggestions and ratings were obtained. Corrections and calculations were made in accordance with the suggestions received. The content validity value, which was considered statistically significant at p = 0.05, was calculated to be 0.796 [26].

Fig. 2Pressure sore care bundle parameters

Pressure sore care bundle parameters

Patients in the study group and their relatives were informed about the pressure sore care bundle and its implementation, and their cooperation was ensured. Implementing the care bundle required compliance with all parameters. During the implementation phase, the individual characteristics of the patients (such as age, medical diagnosis, history of chronic disease, and pre-operative activity status) were considered and evaluated to ensure patient compliance. Specifically, the researcher and clinic nurses implemented the care bundle, and the researcher evaluated and reviewed the records.

Content of the care bundle

The care bundle consists of four main components.

Skin care

Assessment of skin integrity before and after surgery, use of barrier products, and moisturizers.

Positioning

Regular repositioning using appropriate support materials for pressure points.

Early mobilization

Mobilization within the first 24 h after surgery, followed by regular ambulation in the subsequent days.

Nutrition and fluid intake

Adequate protein- and calorie-based nutrition, hydration monitoring, and nutritional support when necessary.

The pressure sore care bundle was implemented in collaboration with the physician, nurse, dietitian, and nutrition nurse. A product with a pH of 5.5 was used to clean the skin. While positioning the patient, they were moved with bilateral support from the nurse and the assistant (caregiver and/or companion). The researcher performed the initial mobilization of all patients. Vital signs, saturation value, dizziness, nausea, and vomiting were evaluated before the patient was mobilized. Subsequently, the patients were mobilized according to the results of this evaluation. The gag reflex was assessed with an Abeslang 2 h postoperatively to ensure the early postoperative resumption of oral intake; this was started with fluids in patients with gag reflexes. No complications developed during the early commencement of oral intake. Daily nutritional goals were set upon consultation with the dietitian and nutrition nurse, and the patients were given a protein-supplemented diet. Patients with insufficient oral intake and albumin value of ≤ 3 were administered a protein-supplemented formula under the guidance of the nutrition nurse.

Details of the care bundle

Frequency of interventions

Skin care

Twice daily postoperatively (morning and evening).

Positioning

Every 2 h during the first 24 h, then every 2–4 h from the second day onwards.

Mobilization

At least once within the first 24 h, and at least twice daily from the second day, depending on patient tolerance.

Nutrition and fluid intake

Oral fluids initiated 2–4 h postoperatively, followed by a protein-supplemented diet.

Staff responsibilities

Skin care and positioning: Clinical nurses.

Mobilization: Nurses, with physiotherapist support when required.

Nutrition and fluid monitoring: Nurses, dietitian, and nutrition nurse.

All interventions were supervised by the research nurse.

Compliance monitoring

Each intervention was recorded on a standardized form. Forms were updated every shift, and daily compliance rates were calculated by the research nurse. For missing interventions, root cause analysis was performed and corrective measures were planned.

Data collection

The researcher collected data in accordance with the study design. Each patient was observed separately, and patient files, hospital information management systems, and nursing care practices were analyzed. Since intra-operatively acquired pressure ulcers developed within 72 h of the surgery, the day of the surgery was considered day 0, and the patients were evaluated for 3 days postoperatively. Each application was recorded daily, and data were collected. Notably, no pre-operative and intraoperative intervention was administered to either the group patients within the scope of the study.

The presence and staging of pressure injuries were performed using the internationally recognized NPUAP (2016) Pressure Injury Classification System. Before data collection, the researcher and clinical nurses received training on the study protocol, the NPUAP classification system, and objective evaluation criteria. A standardized form was developed for all assessments and completed systematically for each patient. To minimize bias, staging decisions were made jointly by the research nurse and clinical nurses, and in cases of uncertainty, consensus was reached through discussion.

The control group received routine care, and the patients were informed about the study either the day before or the day of the surgery. Patients who agreed to participate were asked to sign a consent form, after which the Patient Identification Data Form was filled out.

The clinical and scrub nurses who had been briefed on the 3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore, and the clinical nurse responsible for the patient’s pre-operative preparation, completed the items of the form for preoperative evaluation. The items for intra-operative evaluation were completed by the scrub nurse who was present during the intra-operative period. After the patient was transferred from the recovery unit to the clinic, the researcher performed the first general body assessment during the postoperative greeting and dressing of the patient. The second assessment was made that evening at 21:00 h. Subsequent general body assessments were performed twice daily (09:00 and 21:00 h) for 3 days. Pressure sore sites and stages were recorded on the follow-up form during each evaluation. The patients were asked to mark the place on the VAS that corresponded to the perceived pain level before the analgesic was administered according to the physician’s daily order. Fear of falling was evaluated using the VAS immediately before commencing the first postoperative mobilization of the patient (out of bed). Finally, the patient was asked to answer the SGCQ a day before discharge.

In the study group, a pressure sore care bundle was applied with the routine practices. It was initially planned to apply the bundle for 3 days postoperatively. However, the care bundle was ultimately applied until the patient was discharged from the clinic in order to prevent the development of a new pressure injury after the third day and any existing pressure injury from progressing to an advanced stage. In the data analysis, only the 72-hour follow-up period was considered in relation to ORPIs.

The applications and evaluations for determining pain perception, fear of falling, and comfort levels of the patients in the study group were performed as in the control group.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26 ((IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Number, frequency, mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values were used to express the data. The SGCQ, FFS, and VAS scores were normally distributed, whereas the 3 S Intraoperative RAS of Pressure Sore scores were non-normally distributed. The Independent sample t-test was used to compare normally distributed variables between paired groups and the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare non-normally distributed variables. The chi-square test was used to compare pressure injury formation and various categorical data between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 for all analyses. Effect sizes of variables with significant differences were calculated using partial eta squared (η²), with 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively [27].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was non-randomized and controlled and was registered and received approval from the Ethics Committee review board of the Faculty of Health Sciences and Non-Invasive Research Ethics at Hasan Kalyoncu University, Committee (approval number: 2021/072). The researcher works as a nurse in the clinic.

Results

When medical diagnoses were analyzed, the most common diagnosis in the control group was gonarthrosis (41.8% (n = 51)), whereas in the study group, it was hip fracture (44.1% (n = 32)). The mean total comfort score for the control group was 87.12 ± 18.5, and for the study group it was 109.01 ± 14.81. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001, η² = 0.54). The study group’s relief score (31.04 ± 5.86) was significantly higher than that of the control group (24.93 ± 6.20) (p < 0.001, η² = 0.45); the relaxation score was 37.74 ± 4.86 for the study group and 37.74 ± 6.80 for the control group (p < 0.001, η² = 0.64); and the superiority score was 40.24 ± 5.71 in the study group versus 34.42 ± 7.15 in the control group (p < 0.001, η² = 0.41) (Table 5).

Discussion

Pressure injury development in patients undergoing surgical procedures is a significant clinical issue that negatively impacts postoperative recovery. Orthopedic surgery patients, in particular, are at high risk for developing ORPIs [6–8]. The implementation of multi-component care bundles for the prevention of pressure injuries in high-risk patients is effective in both reducing incidence and improving quality of care [9–11, 28–30].

In this study, the similarity of the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the control and study groups allowed for the effects of the pressure sore care bundle to be evaluated independently of these variables. It has been reported that gender, advanced age, body mass index, and chronic diseases increase the risk of pressure injury in surgical patients [10, 31]. Ensuring sociodemographic and clinical homogeneity between groups strengthens the control of potential confounding variables in comparing intervention effects, thereby increasing the validity of the findings.

The 3S Intraoperative RAS of pressure sore scores used in pressure injury risk assessment were found to be significantly higher in the study group compared to the control group. Studies using the 3 S İntraoperatif RAS of pressure sore in the literature have shown that pressure injury risk scores are significantly affected by surgery-related factors such as operating time, intraoperative blood loss, hypothermia, positioning, and physiological stress [30, 31]. In this regard, it can be said that an increase in 3 S Intraoperative RAS of pressure sore scores is expected in high-risk surgery types such as orthopedic surgery due to the concentration of intraoperative risk factors. Within the scope of the study, the higher 3 S Intraoperative RAS of pressure sore scores of the study group compared to the controls can be explained by the intensity of intraoperative processes and the presence of surgical risk factors, independent of sociodemographic and basic clinical characteristics.

In this study, the incidence of ORPIs was lower in the study group than in the control group (5.7% vs. 18.5%), and a significant difference was found. Which could be attributed to the effectiveness of the care bundle in preventing pressure injury development.

A randomized study conducted in an orthopedic clinic revealed that the incidence of pressure injury was 21.7% and 8.7% in the control and intervention groups, respectively [24]. Another study using polyurethane foam multilayer dressing to prevent pressure injury development in the sacral region of patients with hip fractures showed that the incidence was 15.4% and 4.5% in the control and intervention groups [12]. Additionally, a study that developed an evidence-based peri-operative pressure injury prevention bundle showed that a 50% reduction was achieved in the first calendar year after implementing the bundle, and the incidence remained 0 for at least two years [10]. A quasi-experimental study demonstrated that the incidence of pressure injury was 15.5% and 2.1% before and after the intervention, respectively [29]. In another study, a care package designed to prevent pressure ulcers was implemented for six years as part of a nurse-led quality improvement intervention in all treatment clinics of a hospital, the prevalence of such injuries decreased from 17.3% to 2%, and in the following year, only one Stage 3 pressure injury was reported in the hospital [30].

The findings of this study, along with those previously mentioned, show that the incidence of pressure injuries in patients receiving the care bundle was significantly lower compared to the control groups. This consistent outcome among orthopedic surgery patients can be attributed to the systematic implementation of targeted interventions based on risk assessment scores within the care bundle.

In cases where patients have limited mobility during the postoperative period, components of the care bundle—such as regular position changes, the use of appropriate bed and support surfaces, nutritional assessments, and effective risk scoring tools—are particularly effective when utilized together [9, 10, 12, 24].

A positive change was observed in the pain level of the study group on postoperative day 3. The assessment pain levels and the recording of pain, route, dose, and time of administration of analgesic drugs were conducted similarly in the control and study groups. Adjustments made to analgesic protocols over time, increased awareness and attention of nursing staff, or improved clinical experience may also have contributed to the results. However, no planned changes were made to care protocols during the study period, the nursing team remained the same, and standard care practices continued. These factors may have limited the impact of alternative explanations, but these possibilities should still be considered when interpreting the findings.

The results showing that the pressure sore care bundle was effective in reducing pain levels in the study group are consistent with the existing literature demonstrating the effectiveness of structured care approaches in pain management. Numerous previous studies have reported that standardized care bundle ensure regular pain assessment, contribute to the timely implementation of both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, and thereby reduce patients’ perceived pain levels [28, 32, 33].

The mean level of worst pain was calculated to be 5.3 using the VAS in one study evaluating the pain level after total knee arthroplasty [32]. The postoperative pain levels of patients are influenced by the nursing interventions, analgesic treatment and implementation of evidence-based interventions in the form of protocols [20, 28, 33]. The Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) systematic review of rapid recovery protocols post-spine surgery showed a significant reduction in pain levels with the use of ERAS protocols. Following the implementation of these, decreased postoperative pain levels and opioid consumption were observed in patients who were subject to the protocols compared to those who did not [20, 28, 32].

In the present study, fear of falling was significantly reduced in the study group. This may be attributed to the implementation of the pressure sore care bundle, better communication and higher levels of trust between nurses and the patients, the patients feeling more comfortable due to the information they had received, and decreased levels of fear because of the intervention.

Fear of falling increases in postoperative patients, subsequently intensifying the fear of movement and the risk of falling during mobilization [34]. In a Cochrane systematic meta-analysis, practicing various exercises reportedly resulted in a small-to-moderate decrease in the fear of falling in community-dwelling older adults [23]. A systematic review stated that fear of falling was inversely correlated with quality of life. Furthermore, fear of falling can lead to complications, such as restricting one’s activities, decreased physical functionality, muscle atrophy, loss of balance, and emotional problems, including depression [35].

The higher comfort level in the study group than in the control group indicates that evidence-based interventions applied in clinical settings positively affect comfort, in addition to improving the overall health status of the patients.

In a study where the comfort level of patients after surgical intervention was calculated to be 56%, a significant increase was noted in the comfort level when a companion could be present and as the time allocated by the nurse to the patient increased [36]. Reportedly, the application of evidence-based oral care to non-intubated patients in the ICU reduced the risk of nosocomial pneumonia and enhanced patient comfort [21]. Furthermore, a randomized study conducted to determine the effectiveness of a care bundle for patients with pressure injuries revealed that the bundle improved the stage of injuries, the patients’ self-care effectiveness, self-management skills, and quality of life by increasing patient awareness [37].

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted at a single orthopedic clinic using a non-randomized, sequential group design. Data were collected first from the control group and then from the study group. While this approach may raise concerns about the impact of time-related variables, it ensured that the care bundle was applied under routine clinical conditions and prevented any cross-over between the groups. The control group received standard care, while the study group received standard care in addition to the pressure sore care bundle. Assessments were carried out by researchers and experienced clinical nurses. Although this setup carries the risk of observer bias, it allowed for measurements that reflected real-life conditions. Additionally, the care bundle was applied only during the postoperative period and did not include preoperative and intraoperative processes; thus, the findings pertain solely to the postoperative period.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study assessed the effectiveness of a care bundle aimed at preventing pressure injuries in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery. The results showed that implementing the care bundle led to positive outcomes, including a reduction in the incidence of pressure injuries, decreased postoperative pain and fear of falling, and improved overall patient comfort.

Based on these findings, it is recommended that the care bundle be integrated into standard care protocols for orthopedic surgery patients. To enhance the generalizability of these results, it would be beneficial to evaluate the care bundle’s applicability in various surgical clinics. Future research should involve randomized, multicenter studies to examine the effects of the care bundle on preoperative and intraoperative processes.
