Gradient blowup of smooth vacuum solutions to 1D compressible Euler equations
Juhi Jang, Jiaqi Liu, Nader Masmoudi

Abstract
We consider the isentropic compressible Euler equations in the half-line which govern the motion of gaseous fluids in contact with stationary vacuum boundary. We construct a large class of solutions that are initially smooth and square-integrable, and which, in finite time, transition to C 1 − μ C^{1-\mu} regularity for μ ∈ [ 1 / 2 , 1 ) \mu\in[1/2,1) near the boundary, leading to the gradient blowup at the boundary. It is based on stability analysis of self-similar waiting time solutions [ 27 ] recently constructed by the authors.

Introduction

We consider one-dimensional polytropic gases governed by compressible Euler equations:

\begin{split}\rho_{t}+(\rho u)_{x}&=0,\\
(\rho u)_{t}+(\rho u^{2}+p)_{x}&=0.\end{split}

(1.1)

Here
\rho:(t_{0},T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
u:(t_{0},T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
p:(t_{0},T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
represent the density, velocity, and pressure of the gas, and
\Omega
is the spatial domain given by non-vacuum region. The pressure obeys the
\gamma
-law

p=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\rho^{\gamma}

(1.2)

where
\gamma\in(1,3)
.
The speed of the sound
c=\sqrt{\frac{dp}{d\rho}}
is given by

c=\sqrt{(\gamma-1)}\rho^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}

and in non-vacuum region, the Euler system (
1.1
) can be equivalently written as

\begin{split}u_{t}+uu_{x}+\frac{2}{\gamma-1}cc_{x}&=0,\\
c_{t}+uc_{x}+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}cu_{x}&=0.\end{split}

(1.3)

In the case of
\gamma=2
, the system (
1.1
) for
\rho
and
u
is known as the shallow water equations where
\rho
represents the height of water level and
u
the velocity.

In this article, we are interested in classical
solutions to (
1.1
) when they are continuously in contact with vacuum region. Specifically, we consider the Euler system (
1.1
) in the spatial domain

\Omega=\{0<x<\infty\}

representing the support of the fluid. The left boundary
x=0
is the first point of contact to vacuum state satisfying the following non-moving vacuum boundary conditions

c(t,x)>0\ \text{ for }\ 0<x<\infty,\quad c(t,0)=0,\quad u(t,0)=0.

(1.4)

We consider initial data

c(t_{0},x)=c_{0}(x),\ \ u(t_{0},x)=u_{0}(x)\text{ for }x>0

(1.5)

where

c_{0}(x)>0\ \text{ for }x>0,\ c_{0}(0)=0,\ \text{ and }u_{0}(0)=0.

Well-posedness theory for strong solutions to (
1.3
)-(
1.5
) depends on the behavior of the sound speed near vacuum. Among others, if
c_{0}(x)\sim x
near
x=0
, Liu and Yang
[
35
]
showed the problem (
1.3
)-(
1.5
) is well-posed in Sobolev spaces at least for a short time. On the other hand, if
c_{0}(x)\sim x^{\frac{1}{2}}
near
x=0
,
(c_{0}^{2})_{x}\sim 1
near
x=0
. As it can be seen from the first equation of (
1.3
), it may trigger a non-trivial acceleration near the boundary; hence even if
u_{0}(0)=0
, the boundary may move for
t>t_{0}
. In fact, well-posedness problem in such a case is best understood in the vacuum free boundary framework, where the domain is allowed to move:
\Omega=\{b(t)<x<\infty\}
and the left boundary is determined through the kinematic boundary condition
\dot{b}(t)=u(t,b(t))
. The initial condition
(c_{0}^{2})_{x}\sim 1
near
x=0
is then propagated along the vacuum boundary such that

0<\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial x}(t,b(t))<\infty.

(1.6)

This condition (
1.6
) is called the physical vacuum boundary condition and it arises in various contexts ranging from gas dynamics to astrophysics when studying the dynamics of isolated compactly supported bodies
[
7
,
23
,
25
,
34
,
44
]
. There has been a lot of progress over the past decade on the local and global behavior of solutions for the vacuum free boundary problems for compressible fluids featuring (
1.6
), for instance see
[
7
,
13
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
22
,
24
,
26
]
.

In
[
25
]
, the first and third authors studied well and ill-posedness questions of various vacuum states satisfying
c_{0}(x)\sim x^{\alpha}
for a range of
\alpha
.
In particular, it was shown that when
\alpha=1

the boundary behavior
c\sim x
may not persist for long in general, and conjectured that after some time the boundary should start moving and attain the physical vacuum boundary (
1.6
) (so-called waiting time solutions), whereas for other ranges of
\alpha
an instantaneous change of behavior into the physical vacuum might occur; see also
[
37
]
.
We note that waiting time solutions or instantaneous change of the boundary behavior are also of great interest in the context of the shallow water equation and the vacuum dam-break problem
[
4
,
12
]
. Since the Euler equation from gas dynamics covers the shallow water equation (
\gamma=2
), rigorous progress on the Euler system will have direct applications there. We also mention that the waiting
time behavior or the instantaneous change of behavior of certain vacuum states have been thoroughly studied for nonlinear diffusion equations such
as the porous medium equation, for instance see
[
3
,
33
]
.

The first existence of
waiting time solutions to the Euler system (
1.3
) undergoing the transition from
\alpha=1
to
\alpha=1/2
with the physical vacuum boundary was recently given by the authors
[
27
]
inspired by recent progresses on rigorous construction of self-similar solutions in fluid and gas dynamics
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
28
,
29
,
31
,
32
,
38
]
. The construction is based on the self-similar reduction thanks to the scaling symmetry of the Euler system and analysis of the self-similar ODE system. Moreover, an interesting recent work
[
30
]
of Jenssen shows that some self-similar solutions to (
1.1
) exhibit an instantaneous change of the vacuum boundary, namely the vacuum boundary moves immediately even if
u_{0}(0)=0
, while some other self-similar solutions are confined by the stationary interface
x=0
. Of course, self-similar waiting time solutions themselves arise from carefully prepared initial data and they are unbounded in the far field. It is therefore natural and important to ask whether nonself-similar smooth and bounded initial data can also exhibit similar singular phenomena such as gradient blowup or a transient phase in which a static boundary evolves into a physical vacuum moving boundary, and also how generic they are.

The goal of this article is to construct a large class of smooth, square integrable, nonself-similar solutions to the Euler equation (
1.3
) with the stationary vacuum boundary (
1.4
), which exhibit self-similar blowup
[
27
]
near the blowup point.
We are interested in vacuum solutions initially more regular than
C^{2}
.
It is convenient to
introduce

\mu\in[\frac{1}{2},1)\ \text{ and }\ \beta=\frac{1}{1-\mu}\geq 2,

(1.7)

where
\mu
is related to the scaling symmetry of the Euler system, and
\beta

characterizes the regularity of our solutions.
We now state the informal statement of our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\gamma\in(1,3)
and
\mu\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
. Let
t_{0}<0
with
|t_{0}|
sufficiently small. There exists a finite-codimensional set of
C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor,\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}
initial data at

such that they launch strong solutions to the vacuum boundary Euler system (
1.3
)-(
1.5
) on
[t_{0},0)
transitioning to be
C^{1-\mu}
-Hölder continuous near
x=0
at
t=0
.
The vacuum boundary remains at
x=0
for
t\in[t_{0},0)
and the first gradient blow-up occurs at
(t,x)=(0,0)
. The velocity and the speed of the sound are bounded in
L^{2}
for each
t\in[t_{0},0)
and the density remains positive
\rho>0
for all
(t,x)\in[t_{0},0)\times(0,\infty)
.

A precise formulation of Theorem
1.1
including the specification

Remark 1.2

Initial data (

have the following asymptotics near the boundary

\begin{split}u_{0}(x)&=\frac{2}{(\gamma+1)t_{0}}x+\sum_{i=2}^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}a_{i}x^{i}+a_{0}x^{\beta}+o(x^{\beta}),\\

where

b_{i}:=b_{i}(a_{2},\dots,a_{i})
. Moreover, for given

solutions constructed in Theorem

\displaystyle u(t,x)=-\tilde{a}x^{1-\mu}+o(|t|),\ c(t,x)=\tilde{b}x^{1-\mu}+o(|t|)

Notation
.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem

provides the first rigorous result featuring the descriptive gradient blowup of smooth, square integrable vacuum solutions to the compressible Euler equation.
Loss of regularity from (

) to (

In the study of self-similar shocks for the Burgers’ equation,

self-similar solutions are obtained for
\beta=2k+1
,

. Moreover, it is well-known that self-similar solutions with

are the most stable one and they exhibit gradient blowup with

cusp singularity
[

,
11
]
. Burgers self-similar shock solutions have been successfully used as base profiles or guiding principle to study the cusp-type singularity formation of other models such as compressible Euler equations
[
1
,
2
,
42
]
, the Burgers’ equation through transverse viscosity
[
8
]
or fractional KdV and fractional Burgers’ equation
[

]
. We also mention that in
[
41
]
,
stability of self-similar Burgers’ equation with
\beta\in(1,\infty)
were studied,
and in the range of
\beta\in(1,2]
the profiles have been used to demonstrate stable
C^{\frac{1}{\beta}}
cusp singularities for the 1D compressible Euler equations.

In our problem, we also use Burgers self-similar solutions as base profiles and study the perturbed dynamics. The key difference arises from the presence of the stationary vacuum boundary condition (
1.4
). Solutions are confined in the region
\{x>0\}
and we do not insist on smoothness across the boundary. In particular, our Burgers self-similar index satisfies
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}

and the corresponding initial data are smoother than
C^{2}
near the boundary. As a result, Theorem
1.1
allows a broad class of initial data to evolve into Hölder continuous solutions
C^{1-\mu}
for
\mu\in[1/2,1)
, of which first derivatives blow up at
(t,x)=(0,0)
. Moreover, our result deciphers the precise finite co-dimensional set of initial data that lead asymptotically to the self-similar gradient blowup.

In Theorem
1.1
, the first blowup time has been fixed as
t=0
for simplicity and a systematic study of solutions for all
\mu\in[1/2,1)
. Other blowup times are possible thanks to the time translation symmetry of the Euler system. In particular, by modulating the linear coefficients (
1.8
), one can see that initial slopes
u_{x}(t_{0},0)
and
c_{x}(t_{0},0)
determine the final blowup time, analogously to the Burgers equation; see Appendix
D
for discussion on time translation symmetry and modulation. Among all self-similar Burgers profiles indexed by
\mu\in[1/2,1)
, the profile associated with
\mu=1/2
can be viewed as the most stable one in the sense that the three conditions imposed on the initial data (
1.8
) for
\mu=1/2
, which are
a_{1}=b_{1}=0
and
b_{2}=b_{2}(a_{2})
, are all related to the symmetries of the system; see Remark
2.10
.

We discuss the methodology of Theorem
1.1
along with some technical points. The proof is based on
stability analysis of self-similar solutions in line with recent developments on self-similar solutions and stability
[
2
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
17
,
36
,
40
,
39
,
43
]
. In Section
2
, we introduce self-similar reformulation for the system of Riemann invariants
(\hat{z},\hat{w})
in which self-similar solutions constructed in
[
27
]
are realized as steady states
(\bar{z},0)
. To obtain square integrable solutions, we study perturbations around localized self-similar solutions
\mathring{z}=\chi\bar{z}
using the time dependent cutoff function transported by the transport velocity for
\hat{z}-\bar{z}
. Two transport velocities for the perturbed system are both outgoing in the self-similar variables (cf. (
2.34
)): the damping effect is amplified upon taking the derivatives and high frequency part is stabilized. Low frequency part in general faces a norm growth just like for self-similar shocks for the Burgers’ equation, and such a growth can be controlled through modulations.
Motivated by this observation, we decompose the perturbations into a low frequency component serving as an approximate profile for our solutions near the singular point, and a remainder containing the high frequency contribution. We remark that such a decomposition is a standard tool in the analysis of blowup problems; see, for example,
[
2
,
8
,
9
,
40
,
43
]
.

More specifically, we seek our solutions
(\hat{z},\hat{w})
of the following form

\binom{\hat{z}}{\hat{w}}=\binom{\mathring{z}}{0}+\sum_{i=2}^{{\bf{n}_{\mu}}-1}\varphi\binom{z_{i}}{w_{i}}\frac{y^{i}}{i!}+\binom{Z}{W}

where
\varphi
is a compactly supported smooth cutoff function, the second finite sum represents modulations, where
(z_{i},w_{i})
are
i
-th order Taylor coefficients of solutions at the origin, and the last term is a remainder vanishing up to
({\bf{n}_{\mu}}-1)
-th order. Here
{\bf{n}_{\mu}}
is the regularity index (
2.35
) intimately tied to linear stability.
In Section
3
, the modulation ODEs for
(z_{i},w_{i})
are derived by imposing vanishing conditions at the origin of high order equations. In particular, Lemma
3.1
identifies the initial data set for
(z_{i},w_{i})
for
2\leq i\leq\lceil\beta\rceil
leading to the time decay,
namely a stable manifold of the system of modulation ODEs. For the remainder
(Z,W)
, we adopt a robust approach based on energy estimates with an appropriately chosen singular weight and high order energy. Specifically, we analyze the system for
(\frac{Z}{y^{\bf{n}_{\mu}}},\frac{W}{y^{\bf{n}_{\mu}}})
as well as
(\partial_{y}^{\bf{n}_{\mu}}Z,\partial_{y}^{\bf{n}_{\mu}}W)
, of which linear part becomes strictly dissipative with our choice of
{\bf{n}_{\mu}}
. Linear stability analysis is carried out in Section
4
. We remark that singular weights via suitable local vanishing conditions at the origin on perturbations of the interior profile have been successfully used in recent years for other blowup problems
[
5
,
21
,
6
,
36
]
.
For nonlinear analysis in Section
5
, we employ the bootstrap argument by regulating different decay or growth conditions for various norms including
\dot{H}^{1}
and
L^{2}
norms. The bootstrap assumptions are improved upon establishing several energy estimates with the aid of Hardy and interpolation inequalities.

We note that non-integer
\beta

\mathring{z}
contain

need to be chosen carefully. In fact, when

is a half-integer, the regularity requirement to ensure strict coercivity of the linear part induces non-integrable
singular behavior of interaction terms between low modulation variables
(z_{2},w_{2})
and the profile itself. In this case, we impose the additional constraint

as in (
2.40
).

An interesting question is whether Hölder continuous solutions obtained in Theorem

could be developed for

Self-similar reformulation

The system (
1.3

u(t,x)\to\nu^{\delta-1}u(\frac{t}{\nu},\frac{x}{\nu^{\delta}}),\quad c(t,x)\to\nu^{\delta-1}c(\frac{t}{\nu},\frac{x}{\nu^{\delta}})

We will work with Riemann variables. Riemann invariants are

Remark 2.9

For

Remark 2.10
.

By Remark

does not relate to any underlying symmetry of the system in the

Appendix A
Interpolation inequalities

Lemma A.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
and
\mathtt{a}>0
. Consider the function
f\in H^{n}([0,\infty))
satisfies
f(0)=\partial_{y}f(0)=\dots=\partial_{y}^{n-1}f(0)=0
. Then, for any
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, we have

\left\lVert y^{-(n-i)}\partial_{y}^{i}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}([0,\infty))}\leq A\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}([0,\infty))}

(A.1)

where
A
is a positive constant.

Proof.

Direct computation shows that for
f\in H^{n}([0,\infty))
with
f(0)=\partial_{y}f(0)=\dots=\partial_{y}^{n-1}f(0)=0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(\partial_{y}^{i}f)^{2}}{y^{2(n-i)}}dy=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(\partial_{y}^{i}f)^{2}}{y^{2(n-i-1)}}(\frac{1}{y})^{\prime}dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2\partial_{y}^{i}f\partial_{y}^{i+1}f}{y^{2(n-i-1)+1}}dy-2(n-i-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(\partial_{y}^{i}f)^{2}}{y^{2(n-i)}}dy.

Hence,

\displaystyle(n-i-\frac{1}{2})\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(\partial_{y}^{i}f)^{2}}{y^{2(n-i)}}dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial_{y}^{i}f\partial_{y}^{i+1}f}{y^{2(n-i-1)+1}}dy\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \left\lVert\frac{\partial_{y}^{i}f}{y^{n-i}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}([0,\infty))}\leq A\left\lVert\frac{\partial_{y}^{i+1}f}{y^{n-i-1}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}([0,\infty))}.

Iterating this estimate for
i,i+1,\dots,n-1
gives (
A.1
).
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.
Consider the function
f\in H^{n}([0,\infty))
satisfies
f(0)=\partial_{y}f(0)=\dots=\partial_{y}^{n-1}f(0)=0
. Then, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and
y_{*}>0
, we have

\left\lVert y^{i-n}\partial_{y}^{i}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}([y_{*},\infty))}\leq C_{*}\left\lVert y^{-n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-\theta}\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\theta}

(A.2)

where
\theta=\frac{i}{n}
, and
C_{*}:=C_{*}(y_{*})
is a positive constant.

Proof.

We first define
f_{\lambda}(y)=f(\lambda y)
and compute

\int_{a}^{b}(\partial_{y}^{i}f(y))^{2}\ dy=\lambda^{1-2i}\int_{a/\lambda}^{b/\lambda}(\partial_{y}^{i}f_{\lambda}(y))^{2}\ dy,\ \ \int_{a}^{b}y^{-2n}(f(y))^{2}\ dy=\lambda^{1-2n}\int_{a/\lambda}^{b/\lambda}y^{-2n}(f_{\lambda}(y))^{2}\ dy.

We then set
\lambda_{j}=2^{j}y_{*}
, and define
f_{j}:=f_{\lambda_{j}}
for
y\in[2^{j}y_{*},2^{j+1}y_{*}]
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{y_{*}}^{\infty}y^{-2(n-i)}(\partial_{y}^{i}f)^{2}\ dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{1-2n}\int_{1}^{2}(\partial_{y}^{i}f_{j})^{2}\ dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{1-2n}C_{1}\Big(\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}([1,2])}^{\frac{2i}{n}}\left\lVert f_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}([1,2])}^{2-\frac{2i}{n}}+\left\lVert f_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}([1,2])}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}C_{1}\Big(\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}([\lambda_{j},2\lambda_{j}])}^{\frac{2i}{n}}\left\lVert y^{-n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}([\lambda_{j},2\lambda_{j}])}^{2-\frac{2i}{n}}+\left\lVert y^{-n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}([\lambda_{j},2\lambda_{j}])}\Big)

where we have used the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality in the second inequality.
We then use the Hölder inequality on the first summation and together with Lemma
A.1
, we deduce the result.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
f\in\dot{H}^{1}([0,\infty))\cap\dot{H}^{n}([0,\infty))
. Suppose
\partial_{y}^{j}f(0)=0
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Then,

\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{y}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-\frac{2j-1}{2(n-1)}}\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{2j-1}{2(n-1)}}.

(A.3)

Proof.

Direct computation shows

\displaystyle(\partial_{y}^{j}f)^{2}=\int_{0}^{y}2\partial_{y}^{j}f\partial_{y}^{j+1}fd\tilde{y}.

Hence, by using Holder inequality, we obtain

\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{j+1}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By the Gagliardo–Nirenberg interpolation inequality

\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{y}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-\frac{j-1}{n-1}}\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{j-1}{n-1}},\ \ \ \left\lVert\partial_{y}^{j+1}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{y}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-\frac{j}{n-1}}\left\lVert\partial_{y}^{n}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{j}{n-1}},

we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

(B.5)

Appendix C
On the regularity index
{\bf{n}_{\mu}}

We next consider the weighted operator

considered in Lemma

\mathcal{L}^{m}\phi=(\mu-1)\phi+\frac{\gamma+1}{4}\partial_{y}\bar{z}\phi+(y+\frac{\gamma+1}{4}\bar{z})\partial_{y}\phi+m(1+\frac{\gamma+1}{4}\frac{\bar{z}}{y})\phi.

In other words,

for

in the context of Lemma

. It is clear that positive

. In fact, if

,

\displaystyle\frac{d\phi_{a}}{d\bar{z}}=\phi_{a}\Big(\frac{-(a+1)-(\frac{a}{1-\mu}+1)(-\bar{z})^{\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}}}{(\mu-1)\bar{z}+(-\bar{z})^{\frac{1}{1-\mu}}}-m\frac{-1-\frac{1}{1-\mu}(-\bar{z})^{\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}}}{-\bar{z}+(-\bar{z})^{\frac{1}{1-\mu}}}\Big).

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{m}\phi_{a}=a\phi_{a},\quad\text{ with }\quad\phi_{a}=\frac{y^{-m}(-\bar{z})^{\frac{1+a}{1-\mu}}}{1-\mu+(-\bar{z})^{\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}}}.

(C.2)

When

, we have

(C.3)

In order to have

, which is equivalent to
