Greedy bases and relational complexity of diagonal type groups
Hong Yi Huang, Colva M. Roney-Dougal

Abstract
. A base for a subgroup G G of Sym ⁡ ( Ω ) \mathrm{Sym}(\Omega) is a sequence of elements of Ω \Omega with trivial pointwise stabiliser. The size of the smallest base for G G is denoted b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) . There is a natural greedy algorithm to compute a base for G G , and it was conjectured by Cameron in 1999 that there exists an absolute constant c c such that if G G is primitive then any base returned by this algorithm has size at most c ​ b ​ ( G ) cb(G) . In this paper we determine the size of every base returned by the greedy algorithm when G G is a primitive group of diagonal type, and hence prove Cameron’s conjecture for these groups. The relational complexity RC ⁡ ( G ) \mathrm{RC}(G) of G G is a measure of the way in which the orbits of G G on Ω k \Omega^{k} for various k k determine the action of G G on Ω \Omega . Very few precise values of relational complexity are known, and in particular it is not known which primitive groups have relational complexity 3 3 . In this paper we prove that if G G is primitive of diagonal type then RC ⁡ ( G ) ⩾ 4 \mathrm{RC}(G)\geqslant 4 , that this lower bound is attained by infinitely many such G G , and that the relational complexity of the groups of diagonal type is unbounded.

1.
Introduction

A
base
for a subgroup
G
of
\mathrm{Sym}(\Omega)
is a sequence of elements of
\Omega
with trivial pointwise stabiliser in
G
. For finite
G
the
base size

b(G)
is the minimal size of a base for
G
. This classical invariant of permutation groups has been studied since the 19th century, and gained particular importance in computational group theory
[
31
]
, as the running time of many permutation group algorithms is a function of the size of a known base. A base
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
is
irredundant
if the pointwise stabiliser in
G
of
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{i+1}
is a proper subgroup of the pointwise stabiliser of
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{i}
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
. The size of the largest irredundant base is denoted by
I(G)
: it is straightforward to see that
I(G)\leqslant b(G)\log n
(our logarithms are to the base 2 unless otherwise specified).

In general,
b(G)
can be as large as
|\Omega|-1
, but much smaller bounds are known for most types of primitive groups. The problem of determining
b(G)
has been considered for all five O’Nan–Scott classes. For example, the precise base sizes of primitive groups of diagonal type are determined by the first author
[
22
]
(see Theorem
2.1
below), and so far this is the only O’Nan–Scott family for which the base sizes are known in all cases.

There are no efficient algorithms
for computing
b(G)
, or for constructing a base of minimal size. Indeed, Blaha
[
2
]
proves that computing a base of size
b(G)
is NP-hard. To find a base of “relatively small” size,
there is a natural greedy algorithm, first systematically studied by Blaha. Let
\beta_{1}
be any point in any longest orbit of
G
. Now define a sequence
(\beta_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
of points of
\Omega
, terminating as soon as a base is reached, where
\beta_{m}
lies in a longest orbit of the pointwise stabiliser
G_{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m-1}}
.
The sequence
(\beta_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
is called a
greedy base
for
G
, and the
greedy base size

\mathcal{G}(G)
is defined to be the largest size of a greedy base for
G
. Blaha
[
2
]
shows that
\mathcal{G}(G)
is “small” in the sense that there is an absolute constant
d
such that

(1)

\mathcal{G}(G)\leqslant db(G)\log\log|\Omega|

for every permutation group
G\leqslant\mathrm{Sym}(\Omega)
. And in the same paper, he proves that for a fixed integer
k
and a sufficiently large integer
n
, there exists a permutation group
G
of degree
n
such that
b(G)=k
and
\mathcal{G}(G)\geqslant\frac{1}{5}k\log\log n
, so the bound (
1
) is best possible.

For primitive groups, Cameron conjectured in 1999
[
10
]
that a substantially tighter bound than (
1
) holds.

Conjecture

(Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture)
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that
\mathcal{G}(G)\leqslant cb(G)
for every finite primitive group
G
.

Since every greedy base is irredundant,
\mathcal{G}(G)\leqslant I(G)
. Building on
[
19
]
, Kelsey and the second author showed in
[
24
]
that if
G
is primitive and not large base then
I(G)\leqslant 5\log n
, which automatically bounds
\mathcal{G}(G)
for these groups, but does not directly address Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture. The conjecture has been proved for many almost simple primitive groups
[
13
,
14
]
, and been proved (with
c=1
) for groups of odd order
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we focus on primitive groups of diagonal type. In this setting,
G\leqslant\mathrm{Sym}(\Omega)
has socle
T^{k}
, where
T
is a non-abelian simple group and
k\geqslant 2
is an integer. Here
|\Omega|=|T|^{k-1}
and

T^{k}\trianglelefteqslant G\leqslant T^{k}.(\mathrm{Out}(T)\times\mathrm{S}_{k}).

The subgroup
P\leqslant\mathrm{S}_{k}
induced by the action of
G
by conjugation on the set of factors of
T^{k}
is called the
top group
of
G
. We define

Q=\{(1,\dots,1)\sigma:\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{k}\}\cap G=\mathrm{S}_{k}\cap G.

Note that
Q
is naturally isomorphic to a subgroup of the top group
P
.

For groups of diagonal type, Gill, Lodá and Spiga prove in
[
19
]
that
I(G)\leqslant\log|\Omega|+1
, giving the same upper bound on
\mathcal{G}(G)
. Our first main theorem is the following, which gives the first O’Nan-Scott family for which
\mathcal{G}(G)
is known in all cases.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
be a primitive group of diagonal type with socle
T^{k}
and top group
P
, and let
\ell=\lceil\log_{|T|}k\rceil
.

(i)

If
k=2
, then
\mathcal{G}(G)\in\{3,4\}
, with
\mathcal{G}(G)=4
if and only if
T\in\{\mathrm{A}_{5},\mathrm{A}_{6}\}
and
G=T^{2}.(\mathrm{Out}(T)\times\mathrm{S}_{2})
.

(ii)

If
P\notin\{\mathrm{A}_{k},\mathrm{S}_{k}\}
, then
\mathcal{G}(G)=2
.

(iii)

For
k\geqslant 3
, if
P\in\{\mathrm{A}_{k},\mathrm{S}_{k}\}
then
\mathcal{G}(G)\in\{\ell+1,\ell+2\}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{G}(G)=\ell+2
if and only if either
k=|T|^{\ell}
, or
k\in\{|T|^{\ell}-2,|T|^{\ell}-1\}
and
Q=\mathrm{A}_{k}
.

In addition, every greedy base for
G
has size
\mathcal{G}(G)
.

Combining Theorem
1
with the values of
b(G)
found in
[
22
]
(see Theorem
2.1
), we get the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
G
be a primitive group of diagonal type with socle
T^{k}
and top group
P
, and let
\ell=\lceil\log_{|T|}k\rceil
. Then
\mathcal{G}(G)\in\{b(G),b(G)+1\}
, with
\mathcal{G}(G)=b(G)+1
if and only if
P\in\{\mathrm{A}_{k},\mathrm{S}_{k}\}
and one of the following holds:

(a)

k=|T|^{2}-2
,
Q=\mathrm{S}_{k}
, and either
T\notin\{\mathrm{A}_{5},\mathrm{A}_{6}\}
or
G\neq T^{k}.(\mathrm{Out}(T)\times\mathrm{S}_{k})
: here
\mathcal{G}(G)=4
;

(b)

k=|T|^{\ell}-2
with
\ell\geqslant 3
and
Q=\mathrm{S}_{k}
: here
\mathcal{G}(G)=\ell+2
;

(c)

k=|T|^{\ell}
with
\ell\geqslant 2
and
Q=\mathrm{A}_{k}
: here
\mathcal{G}(G)=\ell+2
.

In particular, for all
b\geqslant 3
, there exist infinitely many primitive groups
G
of diagonal type such that
b(G)=b<\mathcal{G}(G)
.

If a transitive group
G
has base size
2
, then in particular
G
has a regular suborbit, and so
\mathcal{G}(G)=2
as well. Combining this observation with Corollary
1
proves
Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture for primitive groups of diagonal type.

Corollary 2
.

Let
G
be a primitive group of diagonal type. Then
\mathcal{G}(G)\leqslant\frac{4}{3}b(G)
, with equality attained infinitely often.

In a different direction, relational complexity is a topic connecting permutation groups with the study of homogeneous structures in model theory, going back to pioneering work of Lachlan and Cherlin
[
28
,
11
]
.
For much more background, including an explanation of the model-theoretic connection, see
[
20
]
. Let
s\leqslant t
be positive integers, and let
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t}),\Sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t})\in\Omega^{t}
. Then
\Lambda
and
\Sigma
are
s
-subtuple complete
with respect to a subgroup
G
of
\mathrm{Sym}(\Omega)
, and we write
\Lambda\sim_{s}\Sigma
, if for every subset of
s
indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{s}
there exists an element
g\in G
such that
\lambda_{i_{j}}^{g}=\sigma_{i_{j}}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
. The
relational complexity
of
G
, denoted
\mathrm{RC}(G)
, is the smallest
s\geqslant 2
such that for all
t\geqslant s
and all
\Lambda,\Sigma\in\Omega^{t}
, if
\Lambda\sim_{s}\Sigma
then
\Lambda\in\Sigma^{G}
.

Cherlin conjectured in
[
11
]
a classification of the finite primitive permutation groups
G
for which
\mathrm{RC}(G)=2
, and in a dramatic breakthrough in
[
20
]
, Gill, Liebeck and Spiga proved this conjecture: the only examples are the symmetric group in its natural action, or of affine type. The alternating group
\mathrm{A}_{n}
in its natural action has relational complexity
n-1
, so almost simple groups can achieve all possible relational complexities. It is well known (see for example
[
19
, (1.1) and (1.2)]
) that
\mathrm{RC}(G)\leqslant I(G)+1
, so the previously-mentioned bounds on
I(G)
immediately yield bounds on
\mathcal{G}(G)
. Recently, Gill asked what lower and upper bounds could be placed on the relational complexity of groups in each of the remaining O’Nan–Scott classes. In this paper we answer this question completely for primitive groups of diagonal type.

Firstly, we show that there are no diagonal type groups with relational complexity
3
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
G
be a primitive group of diagonal type. Then
\mathrm{RC}(G)\geqslant 4
.

This bound is best possible, as there are infinitely many primitive groups
G
of diagonal type with
\mathrm{RC}(G)=4
(see Proposition
5.1
).

Secondly, we show that the relational complexity of the primitive groups
G
of diagonal type is unbounded. Recall that Gill, Lodà and Spiga proved in
[
19
]
that each such
G
of degree
n
satisfies
\mathrm{RC}(G)\leqslant\log n+1
. We prove a lower bound that occurs infinitely often that is asymptotically close to this.

Theorem 3
.

There are infinitely many values of
n
for which there exists at least one primitive group
G
of diagonal type of degree
n
such that
\mathrm{RC}(G)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log n/\log\log n
.

Structure of the paper

In Section
2
, we give basic notation for diagonal type primitive groups. Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
(i). Lemma
3.7
is a key ingredient, and is a new probabilistic method to find a greedy base of size
3
. Theorem
1
(ii) is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.1
(ii), as
b(G)=2
implies
\mathcal{G}(G)=2
. In Section

we prove Theorem

(iii) and complete the proof of Theorem

. We determine the minimum two-point stabiliser by working with

(see Lemmas

and

), and a detailed analysis of partition stabilisers in alternating and symmetric groups is used to determine greedy bases. Finally, in Section
5
, we establish Theorems
2
and
3
.

Lemma 3.5

, so it suffices to show that

By Lemma

.

For Case (b),

(v) (with

. Thus Lemma

(i) shows that

q-1&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

.

Corollary 3.11

\beta=1

(5)

from Lemma

4.1.
Some important partitions

We now introduce several important partitions of
[k]
and analyse their stabilisers in
\mathrm{S}_{k}
and
\mathrm{A}_{k}
. Recall that in Theorem
1
we define
\ell=\lceil\log_{|T|}k\rceil
. We define

then

, noting that
m\in\{|T|^{\ell-2}+1,\ldots,|T|^{\ell-1}\}
.

We say a partition
\Pi
is of
type

[a_{1}^{b_{1}},\dots,a_{s}^{b_{s}}]
, written as

\Pi\sim[a_{1}^{b_{1}},\dots,a_{s}^{b_{s}}],

if

, for each

there are precisely

parts in
\Pi
of size

,
and these are all of the parts of
\Pi
. We also write
\Pi_{1}\sim\Pi_{2}
if
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
are of same type.

Let

\Gamma_{k,|T|}

be a partition of
[k]
into
|T|
parts so that all parts have size
m
or
m-1
,
noting that
\Gamma_{k,|T|}
is well-defined up to
\sim
.
Secondly, for
k\geqslant|T|+1
we let
\Sigma
be a partition of
[k]
such that

(8)

\Sigma\sim\begin{cases}[(m-2)^{1},(m-1)^{|T|-3},m^{2}]&\mbox{if $k=(m-1)|T|+1$}\\
[(m-2)^{1},(m-1)^{|T|-4},m^{3}]&\mbox{if $k=(m-1)|T|+2$}\\
\Gamma_{k,|T|}&\mbox{if $(m-1)|T|+3\leqslant k\leqslant m|T|-3$}\\
[(m-1)^{3},m^{|T|-4},(m+1)^{1}]&\mbox{if $k=m|T|-2$}\\
[(m-1)^{2},m^{|T|-3},(m+1)^{1}]&\mbox{if $k=m|T|-1$}\\
[(m-1)^{2},m^{|T|-4},(m+1)^{2}]&\mbox{if $k=m|T|$.}\\
\end{cases}

For a subgroup
H
of
\mathrm{S}_{k}
and partition
\Pi=\{\pi_{t}:t\in T\}
of
[k]
, where some of the parts
\pi_{t}
are allowed to be empty, we write
H_{\{\pi_{t}\}}
for the setwise stabiliser of the subset
\pi_{t}
of
[k]
, write

H_{(\Pi)}=\bigcap_{t\in T}H_{\{\pi_{t}\}}

for the setwise stabiliser of each part of
\Pi
, and write

for the subgroup of

that permutes the parts of
\Pi
.

We show next that if
k\geqslant|T|+1
then
\Gamma_{k,|T|}
is the unique type of partition with smallest stabiliser in
H\in\{\mathrm{A}_{k},\mathrm{S}_{k}\}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
k\geqslant|T|+1
, and let
\Pi
be a partition of
[k]
into exactly
|T|
parts (some of which may be empty) such that
\Pi\not\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
. Let the smallest and largest parts of
\Pi
have size
d
and
e
, respectively. Let
H
be
\mathrm{A}_{k}
or
\mathrm{S}_{k}
acting naturally on
[k]
. Then

|H_{(\Pi)}|\geqslant\frac{e}{d+1}|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|>|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|,

with equality in the first bound if and only if
\Pi
has either a unique part not of size
m
or
m-1
, and this part has size
m-2
or
m+1
, or exactly two such parts, one of size
m-2
and one of size
m+1
.

Proof.

Suppose for now that
H=\mathrm{S}_{k}
.
Let
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
be parts of
\Pi
of size
d
and
e
, respectively. Consider the partition
\Pi_{1}
which is identical to
\Pi
except one point from
[k]
has been moved from
\pi_{2}
to
\pi_{1}
.
Then

\frac{|H_{(\Pi)}|}{|H_{(\Pi_{1})}|}=\frac{d!e!}{(d+1)!(e-1)!}=\frac{e}{d+1}.

Since not all parts of
\Pi
have size
m
or
m-1
, the difference
e-d\geqslant 2
, so this is strictly greater than
1
.
If
\Pi_{1}\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
then we are done, otherwise we may iterate this process to produce a sequence of partitions with decreasing stabiliser orders until no part has size greater than
m
or less than
m-1
, namely a partition equivalent to
\Gamma_{k,|T|}
.

If
H=\mathrm{A}_{k}
, then from

we see that at least one part of each
|T|
-part partition has size at least two, and so the stabiliser in
\mathrm{S}_{k}
contains a transposition. Hence the stabilisers in
\mathrm{A}_{k}
have order precisely half that of the corresponding stabilisers in
\mathrm{S}_{k}
, and the ratio of stabiliser orders is unchanged.
∎

The next lemma shows that if
k\geqslant|T|+1
then, up to
\sim
, the partition
\Sigma
has at worst the third smallest stabiliser in

amongst all partitions of

, and that its stabiliser is less than twice as big as the smallest.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
k\geqslant|T|+1
and let
\Pi
be a partition of
[k]
into
|T|
parts such that
\Pi\not\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
,
\Pi\not\sim\Sigma
and
\Pi\not\sim[(m-1)^{1},m^{|T|-2},(m+1)^{1}]
. Let

be
\mathrm{A}_{k}
or
\mathrm{S}_{k}
as before. Then
|H_{(\Pi)}|>|H_{(\Sigma)}|
. Furthermore,
|H_{(\Sigma)}|\leqslant 2|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|
.

Proof.

If
k\in\{(m-1)|T|+3,\ldots,m|T|-3\}
then
\Sigma\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
and this is immediate from Lemma
4.1
, so suppose otherwise. Let
d
and
e
be the sizes of the smallest and largest parts of
\Pi
, respectively. Then Lemma
4.1
shows that

|H_{(\Pi)}|\geqslant\frac{e}{d+1}|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|,

with equality only under very restricted conditions. We compare this bound with the value of
|H_{(\Sigma)}|
for the remaining values of

.

Suppose
k=(m-1)|T|+a
with
a=1,2
, where
\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[(m-1)^{|T|-1},m]
or
\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[(m-1)^{|T|-2},m^{2}]
, respectively.
Then Lemma
4.1
shows that

|H_{(\Sigma)}|=\frac{m}{m-1}|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|\leqslant 2|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|.

Now
e\geqslant m
, so if
d<m-2
then the result follows for all
e
. Furthermore,
d\leqslant m-2
(since
k=(m-1)|T|+a
), so if
e\geqslant m+1
then the result follows. The only remaining case is

and
e=m
, but now

shows that
\Pi
has at least

in this case as well.

Our next cases are

with

, where
\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[(m-1)^{2},m^{|T|-2}]
,
\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[(m-1),m^{|T|-1}]
, or
\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[m^{|T|}]
, respectively. This time Lemma
4.1
shows that

|H_{(\Sigma)}|=\frac{m+1}{m}|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|<2|H_{(\Gamma_{k,|T|})}|.

Now
e\geqslant m
, so if
d\leqslant m-2
then the result follows. Hence we may assume that
d=m-1
, and so (since
\Pi\not\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
) we deduce that

. Furthermore, if
e\geqslant m+2
then the result follows, so we may assume that

and

. But
\Pi\not\sim\Sigma
and
\Pi\not\sim[(m-1)^{1},m^{|T|-2},(m+1)^{1}]
, so
\Pi
has at least
2

if

, and at least three if

and
\Pi\sim[(m-1)^{\alpha},m^{|T|-2\alpha},(m+1)^{\alpha}]
for some

\frac{|H_{(\Pi)}|}{|H_{(\Sigma)}|}=\frac{((m-1)!)^{\alpha}(m!)^{|T|-2\alpha}((m+1)!)^{\alpha}}{((m-1)!)^{2}(m!)^{|T|-4}((m+1)!)^{2}}=\frac{((m-1)!)^{\alpha-2}((m+1)!)^{\alpha-2}}{(m!)^{2\alpha-4}}=\frac{(m+1)^{\alpha-2}}{m^{\alpha-2}}

, as required.
∎

\mathbf{s}\in T^{k}
with
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{s}}=\Sigma
and
|G_{D,D\mathbf{s}}|=|Q_{(\Sigma)}|
. Let
\mathbf{t}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{k})\in T^{k}
be such that
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\not\sim\Sigma
. It suffices to show that
|G_{D,D\mathbf{t}}|>|Q_{(\Sigma)}|
.

Corollary
4.4
(i) shows
G_{D,D\mathbf{t}}\geqslant Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}
, and Lemma
4.2
shows that if
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\not\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\not\sim[(m-1)^{1},m^{|T|-2},(m+1)^{1}]
then

|Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}|>|Q_{(\Sigma)}|
, which is equal to
|G_{D,D\mathbf{s}}|
.
Thus, we may assume that either
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}
(with five possibilities for
k
modulo
|T|
), or
k=m|T|
and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\sim[(m-1)^{1},m^{|T|-2},(m+1)^{1}]
.

First suppose
k=m|T|+\epsilon 1
, with
\epsilon=\pm
. Then
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[(m+\epsilon 1)^{1},m^{|T|-1}]
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
|\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m+\epsilon 1
. Then for any
x\in T
,

\{t:|\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m\}=T\setminus\{1\}=(T\setminus\{1\})^{x}=\{t:|\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m\}^{x}.

Since
m\geqslant 2
there exists
\sigma\in\mathrm{A}_{k}
such that

j^{\sigma}=i\mbox{ if and only if }g_{j}^{x}=g_{i}.

Hence (
9
) shows that
(x,\dots,x)\sigma\in T\wr\mathrm{A}_{k}\leqslant G
, and we readily check that

D^{(x,\ldots,x)\sigma}=D\quad\mbox{ and }\quad D\mathbf{t}^{(x,\dots,x)\sigma}=D(g_{1^{\sigma^{-1}}}^{x},\dots,g_{k^{\sigma^{-1}}}^{x})=D(g_{1},\dots,g_{k})=D\mathbf{t}.

Hence
|G_{D,D\mathbf{t}}|\geqslant|T|\cdot|Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}|>2|Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}|\geqslant|Q_{(\Sigma)}|
by Lemma
4.2
, as required.

Next, suppose
k=m|T|+\epsilon 2
, so
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\sim[(m+\epsilon 1)^{2},m^{|T|-2}]
, and assume that
|\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m+\epsilon 1
. Let
z\in T\setminus\{1\}
be the other element with
|\mathcal{P}_{z}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m+\epsilon 1
. Again, for any
x\in C_{T}(z)
,

\{t:|\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m\}=T\setminus\{1,z\}=(T\setminus\{1,z\})^{x}=\{t:|\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\mathbf{t}}|=m\}^{x}.

Hence again there exists
\sigma\in\mathrm{A}_{k}\leqslant Q
such that

j^{\sigma}=i\mbox{ if and only if }g_{j}^{x}=g_{i},

and it is easy to check that
(x,\dots,x)\sigma\in G_{D,D\mathbf{t}}
. Since no element of a non-abelian simple group has a centraliser of order
2
,
it follows that
|G_{D,D\mathbf{t}}|\geqslant|C_{T}(z)|\cdot|Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}|>2|Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}|\geqslant|Q_{(\Sigma)}|
by Lemma
4.2
, as required.

Finally, if
k=m|T|
, then either
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\sim\Gamma_{k,|T|}\sim[m^{|T|}]
or
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}}\sim[(m-1)^{1},m^{|T|-2},(m+1)^{1}]
. We leave it as an exercise in each case to show that
|G_{D,D\mathbf{t}}|>2|Q_{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{t}})}|\geqslant|Q_{(\Sigma)}|
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
m,n,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
. Then
\lceil m/n^{r}\rceil/n=\lceil m/n^{r+1}\rceil
.

Proof.

Write
m=an^{r+1}+bn^{r}+c
, where
b\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
and
c\in\{0,\ldots,n^{r}-1\}
.
If
c=0
then
\lceil m/n^{r}\rceil/n=(an+b)/n=a+\lceil b/n\rceil=\lceil m/n^{r+1}\rceil
. Otherwise
\lceil m/n^{r}\rceil/n=(an+b+1)/n=a+\lceil(b+1)/n\rceil=a+1=\lceil m/n^{r+1}\rceil
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
k\geqslant|T|+1
. Let
\beta_{i}=(D,D\mathbf{t}_{1},\ldots,D\mathbf{t}_{i})
be the first
i+1
points chosen by the greedy algorithm, for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell+1\}
. Let

m^{\prime}=\begin{cases}m&\mbox{if $k\leqslant m|T|-3$};\\
m+1&\mbox{if $k\in\{m|T|-2,m|T|-1,m|T|\}$}.\end{cases}

Then
G_{\beta_{i}}=Q_{(\Pi_{i})}
, where
\Pi_{i}
is a partition with largest part of size

\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{i-1}\rceil
.
Furthermore, if
(k\pmod{|T|^{i}})\not\in\{-1,-2\}
then there are at least two parts of this largest size.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
i
.
For
i=1
, Lemma
4.6
shows that
G_{\beta}=Q_{(\Pi_{1})}

for some partition
\Pi_{1}\sim\Sigma
. We see from (
8
) that the largest part of
\Pi_{1}
has size
m^{\prime}
,
and that if
k\not\in\{m|T|-1,m|T|-2\}
then
\Pi_{1}
has at least two parts of size
m^{\prime}
.

Now suppose
i>1
and
G_{\beta_{i-i}}=Q_{(\Pi_{i-1})}
, where the
largest part of
\Pi_{i-1}
has size
\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{i-2}\rceil
. Then
G_{\beta_{i}}=G_{\beta_{i-1},D,D\mathbf{t}_{i}}=Q_{(\Pi_{i-1})}\cap Q_{(\Pi^{\prime})}
, where
\Pi^{\prime}
is a partition of
[k]
into
|T|
parts such that the common refinement of
\Pi_{i-1}
and
\Pi^{\prime}
has minimal order stabiliser in
Q
. Since
Q_{(\Pi_{i-1})}=(\mathrm{S}_{a_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathrm{S}_{a_{\ell}})\cap Q
is either
\mathrm{S}_{a_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathrm{S}_{a_{\ell}}
or the intersection of this with
\mathrm{A}_{k}
, it follows from Lemma
4.1
that
\Pi_{i}
splits the
j
th part of
\Pi_{i-1}
into
|T|
parts, of sizes
\lfloor a_{j}/|T|\rfloor
and
\lceil a_{j}/|T|\rceil
. In particular the largest part has size

\left\lceil\frac{\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{i-2}\rceil}{|T|}\right\rceil,

which is equal to
\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{i-1}\rceil
by Lemma
4.7
.

For the final claim, if
(k\mod|T|^{i-1})\not\in\{-1,-2\}
then there were at least two parts of the largest size in
\Pi_{i-1}
, and hence there will be at least two parts of the largest size in
\Pi_{i}
. Otherwise, if
\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{i-1}\rceil=\lceil(m+1)/|T|^{i-1}\rceil\neq a|T|+1
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
there will also be at least two parts of the largest size in
\Pi_{i}
. Hence there is a unique part of the largest size if and only if
m/|T|^{i-1}
is divisible by
|T|
, so that
m
is divisible by
|T|^{i}
, and
(k\pmod{|T|^{i}})\in\{-1,-2\}
, as required.
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

Suppose
k\geqslant|T|+1
. Then every greedy base has size
\mathcal{G}(G)\in\{\ell+1,\ell+2\}
, with
\mathcal{G}(G)=\ell+2
if and only if either
k=|T|^{\ell}
, or
Q=\mathrm{S}_{k}
and
k\in\{|T|^{\ell}-1,|T|^{\ell}-2\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.8
, after the greedy algorithm has chosen
\ell
base points,
the remaining stabiliser is
Q_{(\Pi_{\ell-1})}
, for some partition
\Pi_{\ell-1}
of
[k]
into parts of size at most
\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{\ell-2}\rceil\geqslant 2
. This is equal to
2
only when
m\leqslant 2|T|^{\ell-2}<|T|^{\ell}-2
, so there are at least two parts of the largest size. After one more base point is fixed, the largest part of
\Pi_{\ell}
has size at most
\lceil m^{\prime}/|T|^{\ell-1}\rceil
, which is
1
unless
m^{\prime}=m+1=|T|^{\ell-1}+1
. This happens only when
k\geqslant|T|^{\ell}-2
by Lemma
4.8
, so if
k<|T|^{\ell}-2
then every greedy base has size
\ell+1
.

Suppose
k\geqslant|T|^{\ell}-2
, and notice that
Q_{(\Pi_{\ell+1})}=1
by Lemma
4.8
. If
k=|T|^{\ell}
then there are at least two parts of size
2
in
\Pi_{\ell}
, so
Q_{(\Pi_{\ell})}\neq 1
, and hence every greedy base has size
\mathcal{G}(G)=\ell+2
. If
|T|^{\ell-1}-2\leqslant k\leqslant|T|^{\ell-1}-1
then
\Pi_{\ell}
has a unique part of size
2
, and so
Q_{(\Pi_{\ell})}=1
if and only if
Q=\mathrm{A}_{k}
. The result follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We proved Part (i) at the end of Section
3
. As noted in the introduction,
if
b(G)=2
then
\mathcal{G}(G)=2
, so
Part (ii) is immediate from Theorem
2.1
(ii), and it remains to prove Part (iii) for the groups with
b(G)>2
. By Theorem
2.1
(iii), if
b(G)>2
then either
k\in\{|T|-2,|T|-1\}
and
Q=\mathrm{S}_{k}
, or
k\geqslant|T|
. The cases with
k\geqslant|T|+1
are handled in Proposition
4.9
. For all other cases it is shown in
[
17
, Proposition 3.5]
that every pair of distinct points in
\Omega
can be extended to a base of size
3
, which yields
\mathcal{G}(G)=3=b(G)
. Part (iii) follows.

For the final statement, if
\mathcal{G}(G)=b(G)
then every greedy base has size
b(G)
. The only situation where this does not occur is discussed in Proposition
4.9
, which proves that every greedy base has size
\mathcal{G}(G)
.
∎

5.
Relational complexity

In this section we prove Theorems
2
and
3
, and also show that the lower bound in Theorem
2
is attained infinitely often.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By
[
20
, Corollary 1.4]
, there is no primitive group
G
of diagonal type with
\mathrm{RC}(G)=2
, so it suffices to show that
\mathrm{RC}(G)\neq 3
. Let
G
have socle
T^{k}
.

If
T\in\{\mathrm{A}_{5},\mathrm{A}_{6}\}
and
k=2
then we may check the result using
Magma

[
4
]
,
so we may assume that if
k=2
then
T\notin\{\mathrm{A}_{5},\mathrm{A}_{6}\}
from now on. If
k=2
then by Theorem
2.1
(i) the group
G
has a base of size
3
, so let
x,y\in T
be such that

(D,D(x,1),D(y,1))
is a base.
If
k\geqslant 3
then let
(x,y)
be a generating pair of
T
. For all
k
, let

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=D

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=D(x,1,\dots,1)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=D(y,1,\dots,1)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=D(xy^{-1},1,\dots,1)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=D(y^{-1}x,1,\dots,1)

be elements of
\Omega
, and define
I=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta_{1})
,
J=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta_{2})
.

We claim that
xy^{-1}\neq y^{-1}x
: suppose otherwise. If
k=2
then this implies that
(x,x)
fixes
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
, which contradicts our assumption that
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is a base for
G
. If
k\geqslant 3
then
xy^{-1}=y^{-1}x
contradicts
\langle x,y\rangle=G
.
Hence these five points are distinct. We shall show that
I\sim_{3}J
but
I\not\sim_{4}J
, from which the result will follow.

We first calculate

\displaystyle\beta^{(y^{-1}x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})}

\displaystyle=D(xy^{-1}x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})=D(x,1,\dots,1)=\beta

\displaystyle\gamma^{(y^{-1}x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})}

\displaystyle=D(x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})=D(y,1,\dots,1)=\gamma

\displaystyle\delta_{1}^{(y^{-1}x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})}

\displaystyle=D(xy^{-1}y^{-1}x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})=D(y^{-1}x,1,\dots,1)=\delta_{2}.

We therefore deduce that
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)^{1}=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, whilst

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\delta_{1})^{(x,\dots,x)}=(\alpha,\beta,\delta_{2})

\displaystyle(\alpha,\gamma,\delta_{1})^{(y,\dots,y)}=(\alpha,\gamma,\delta_{2})

\displaystyle(\beta,\gamma,\delta_{1})^{(y^{-1}x,xy^{-1},\dots,xy^{-1})}=(\beta,\gamma,\delta_{2})

and so
I\sim_{3}J
.

It remains to show that
I
and
J
are not in the same
G
-orbit. For
k=2
this follows from
our assumption that
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is a base. For
k\geqslant 3
, we may assume that

G=W
. Then by Corollary
4.4
(ii),
G_{\alpha,\beta}=C_{\mathrm{Aut}(T)}(x)\times P_{1}
and
G_{\alpha,\gamma}=C_{\mathrm{Aut}(T)}(y)\times P_{1}
. From
\langle x,y\rangle=T
we deduce that
G_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=P_{1}
. However,
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
lie in distinct
P_{1}
-orbits, whence
I\not\sim_{4}J
.
∎

It is now easy to show that the bound in Theorem
2
is attained infinitely often.

Proposition 5.1
.

There are infinitely many diagonal type groups
G
with
\mathrm{RC}(G)=4
.

Proof.

Let
f\geqslant 2
be an integer and let
G=T^{2}
with
T=\mathrm{L}_{2}(2^{f})
. Then
[
30
, Theorem 1.1]
shows that
I(G)=b(G)
, so
I(G)=3
by Theorem
2.1
. Therefore the bound
\mathrm{RC}(G)\leqslant I(G)+1
yields
\mathrm{RC}(G)\leqslant I(G)+1=4
. Combining with Theorem
2
now shows that
\mathrm{RC}(G)=4
.
∎

We conclude this section by determining a lower bound on the relational complexity of a family of groups of diagonal type.

Proposition 5.2
.

For all
m\geqslant 3
and all
k\geqslant 3
, every primitive group
G=\mathrm{A}_{m+2}^{k}{:}P
of diagonal type satisfies
\mathrm{RC}(G)\geqslant m
.

Proof.

Let
T=\mathrm{A}_{m+2}
. For
2\leqslant i\leqslant m+1
, let
t_{i}=(1,2,i+1)\in T
. Define
\alpha_{1}=D
,
\alpha_{i}=D(t_{i},1,\dots,1)
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
,
\beta=D(t_{m},1,\dots,1)
and
\gamma=D(t_{m+1},1,\dots,1)
. Let
I=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m-1},\beta)\in\Omega^{m}
and
J=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m-1},\gamma)\in\Omega^{m}
. We shall show that
I\sim_{m-1}J
but
J\not\in I^{G}
, from which the result follows.

Since
k\geqslant 3
, we can apply Corollary
4.4
with parts of size
1
and
k-1
to see that
G_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{i}}=C_{T}(t_{i})\times P_{1}
, and
C_{T}(t_{2},\dots,t_{m-1})=C_{T}(\mathrm{A}_{m})=1
. Therefore
I
and
J
are in distinct
G
-orbits. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, let
s_{i}=(i,m+1,m+2)\in T
. Then
s_{1}^{-1}t_{i}s_{2}=t_{i}
for
i\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
, whilst
s_{1}^{-1}t_{m}s_{2}=t_{m+1}
, so

(\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{m-1},\beta)^{(s_{2},s_{1},\dots,s_{1})}=(\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{m-1},\gamma).

Similarly, one can check that for all
2\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
,

(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{m-1},\beta)^{(s_{i+1},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{i+1})}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{m-1},\gamma),

so
I\sim_{m-1}J
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

By Proposition
5.2
, for all
m\geqslant 3
and
k\geqslant 3
there is a diagonal type group
G
with
\mathrm{RC}(G)\geqslant m
and degree
n=|\mathrm{A}_{m+2}|^{k-1}
. Setting
k=3
and using the bounds
d^{d}/e^{d-1}\leqslant d!\leqslant d^{d+1}/e^{d-1}
(see, for example,
[
27
, p. 52]
), we get inequalities

\left(\frac{(m+2)^{m+2}}{2e^{m+1}}\right)^{2}\leqslant n\leqslant\left(\frac{(m+2)^{m+3}}{2e^{m+1}}\right)^{2},

so for sufficiently large
m
we can bound

\displaystyle\log n

\displaystyle\leqslant 2((m+3)\log(m+2)-(m+1)\log e-1)\leqslant 2m\log(m+2)

\displaystyle\log\log n

\displaystyle\geqslant\log\left((2m+4)\log(m+2)-(2m+2)\log e-2)\right)\geqslant\log(2m+4)\geqslant\log(m+2)

and hence

\log n/\log\log n\leqslant 2m,

as required.
∎
