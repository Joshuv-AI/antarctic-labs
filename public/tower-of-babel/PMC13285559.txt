Advances in liver transplantation for hepatic malignancies
Authors: Ingrid Woelfel, Shirley Lowmaster, Mariana Chavez-Villa, Sandra Arias, Channa Jayasekera, Michele Barnhill, Adyr Moss, Amit K Mathur, Nitin N Katariya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransplant Oncology is an evolving field. While liver transplantation was once contraindicated in patients with cancer, the safety and survival benefit of utilizing transplantation as a curative-intent treatment for primary and metastatic hepatic malignancies has been demonstrated. Currently, liver transplantation is offered to selected patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, perihilar and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, colorectal cancer with isolated liver metastases, and neuroendocrine tumors with isolated liver metastases. We will cover the current inclusion and exclusion criteria and clinical and pathologic factors of each oncologic indication, and future directions in liver transplant oncology. Successful oncologic outcomes for liver transplantation hinge on careful selection of patients. The evolving role of immunotherapy and targeted tumor therapies have allowed for previously transplant-ineligible patients to be considered for curative-intent transplantation, rather than palliative-intent care. Increasing our knowledge through coordinated multi-institutional studies of each disease process with liver transplantation will be imperative to continue to safely broaden the indications and offer hope to these complex groups of patients. Reframing recipients with oncologic indications for transplant should occur carefully and with attention to rigorous analysis of clinical outcomes. The objective of this article is to situate the evolving treatments for hepatic malignancies within the expanding scope of transplant oncology.Keywords: Liver transplantation, transplant oncology, hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, colorectal liver metastasis

Introduction

Cancer treatment continues to evolve in complexity as novel therapeutics emerge. Transplant oncology operates at the confluence between the oncologic treatment of a patient with a primary or secondary liver malignancy and the utilization of curative-intent solid organ transplantation to achieve the maximize long-term survival. As a field, it aims to appropriately sequence the currently available oncologic therapies in combination with liver transplantation (LT) to potentially cure patients and improve their quality of life. Increasingly, advances in nonsurgical oncologic therapies have safely expanded the indications for LT with concurrent improvements in patient survival [1]. Attention to continued evolution in treatments for these cancers is essential as liver cancer is the third leading cause of cancer death worldwide [2]. LT for primary hepatic malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) in addition to a select group of patients with secondary hepatic malignancies including colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) and metastatic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), are now either the standard-of-care or available at specialized centers that report superior outcomes than non-transplant management alone [3].

LT was first utilized for HCC in patients to address malignant disease burden in cirrhotic patients who could not undergo safe surgical resection. The initial suggested inclusion criteria were based on tumor size and number based on radiological imaging alone. Over the next three decades, criteria have evolved based on increasing understanding of molecular pathways, pathological assessment of explanted livers and the further expansion of size criteria. Additionally, successful treatments with novel immunotherapies and local regional therapies have allowed for the expansion of LT as a treatment to more advanced liver malignancy, biliary malignancies and even metastatic adenocarcinomas [4]. Care has significantly changed for patients with hepatic malignancies as a result,but must be balanced with the limited availability of liver grafts and the lifetime risk of immunosuppression medications. The objective of this review is to discuss the evolving role, as a therapeutic modality, of LT for patients with hepatic malignancies in order to inform practice.

Methods

The goal of this paper was to discuss the evolving role of transplantation in oncologic indications. The PubMed database was primarily searched with the following terms: transplant oncology, liver transplantation, hepatocellular carcinoma, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, colorectal liver metastasis, hilar cholangiocarcinoma. Articles published from 1990s to 2025 were included if seminal in their findings. Peer reviewed studies in the English language were included. This review is not exhaustive and the references were hand selected by the authors for inclusion due to the contribution of the reference to the oncology field and high impact on clinical practice.

Hepatocellular carcinoma: the first cancer cured with liver transplantation with modern controversies

Patients with HCC can have significant survival benefits from LT [5]. Fair outcomes of LT for HCC was first shown in the 1996 publication of the Milan Criteria, which initiated more formally standardized criteria by the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer group in 1999 [6]. However, the scarcity of organ availability originally constrained the growth of LT for HCC. Additionally, careful selection in these patients is essential to ensuring appropriate long term survival benefit with minimal recurrence [7]. While initial selection criteria primarily focused on the size and the number of tumors, more contemporary work has concentrated on the aggressiveness of the tumor biology, utilizing response to locoregional therapy (LRT) and tumor markers such as alpha-feta protein (AFP) and others. Additionally, novel immunotherapy drugs have demonstrated significantly positive outcomes in advanced HCC and is being used in combination with LRT or by itself to downstage HCC into Milan criteria [8]. Understanding the timing of immunotherapy administration and its impact post-transplant in the setting of immunosuppression regimens will continue to be a key area of investigation. The purpose of this section is to address these two areas of controversy in transplant for HCC.

Transplanting patients beyond Milan—what time is right?

Milan Criteria initially published in 1996 established a pivotal turning point for LT with HCC [6]. The group outlined tumor size and number characteristics which laid the foundation for selection for liver transplant for decades to come. Their criteria was defined as the presence of a single tumor smaller than 5 cm or up to three tumors each smaller than 3 cm with the absence of macrovascular invasion or extrahepatic spread [6]. They categorically excluded lesions < 2 cm as these were thought to best be treated with resection. This criterion was established in a 48-patient study which found significantly improved survival rates within Milan compared to outside of Milan criteria. In 2006, further refinement and the standardization of radiographic reporting of liver lesions on cross-sectional imaging allowed for consistent and clear interpretation of liver lesions. This classification system evolved from breast imaging (BI-RADSs) and was eventually termed LI-RADS (Liver Imaging and Data Reporting System) This system can, in cirrhotic livers, help predict the relative risk of hepatocellular carcinoma and has been significantly improved with increasing resolution of CT and MRI scanners.

Multiple researchers have questioned and attempted to expand these criteria [9]. The goal of this expansion is to increase the pool of LT candidates without a concurrent decrease in survival rates and moderation of recurrence [10]. For patients outside of Milan criteria, an important consideration is tumor biology as assessed by response to LRT prior to LT. University of California San Francisco was one of the first centers to explore transplantation for patients beyond Milan [11]. They found that in selected patients, treatment with LRT followed by a minimum of a 3-month waiting period had equivalent outcomes to patients transplanted within Milan [12]. Tumor downstaging was successful in 70.5% of patients with LRT [12–14]. Response to LRT has also been standardized using a modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors categories (RECIST) [15]. In a multivariate analysis, independent pre-LT predictors of recurrence included: responsiveness to LRT with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and a maximum radiologic size of tumor < 3 cm. HCC recurrence rates for those not responsive to TACE who had tumors greater than 3 cm was 35% compared to 1.9% for those with larger tumors that were not responsive to TACE LRT [15]. The boundaries of downstaging HCC with LRT will continue to be explored utilizing increasingly effective LRT including the increased use of transarterial radioembolization (TARE) and other technologies such as histotripsy [16].

In addition to response to tumor control with LRT, tumor markers and molecular assessments of tumor behavior have emerged as potential strategies to provide enhanced prediction of longitudinal outcomes including local control, metastasis and patient survival. The first biomarker evaluated in HCC was alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) [17]. High AFP levels were associated with poor patient survival according to the AFP Model and the Metroticket 2.0 which were designed to assess HCC reocurrence with treatment [17–19]. Metroticket 2.0 utilizes the size of the largest viable tumor, number of vital tumor nodules and the AFP level to estimate 5-year survival. HALT-HCC is another risk calculator that uses the tumor burden score, AFP and is the only score that incorporates MELD [20]. Multivariate Cox modeling demonstrated that low HALT-HCC scores were associated with overall survival even after adjustment for risk factors not related to HCC [20]. In a national study comparing multiple recurrence scoring methods, UCSF, HALT-HCC and Metroticket 2.0 were found to allow candidate expansion without a clinical outcome detriment [21]. Future directions for tumor behavior prediction incorporating artificial intelligence may allow for more accurate predictions on liver transplant benefit [22,23]. Ideal tumor markers and scoring systems have yet to be developed [24]. Increasing the sensitivity and specificity of these scoring systems that incorporate biomarkers and a relevant disease-specific clinical criteria will allow clinicians to better evaluate each patient for stage-specific treatments including LRT and LT from a nuanced position than before.

Immunotherapy for HCC—a new tool to get more patients to cure

The liver is an immune-privileged organ that has been a focus for clinical tolerance research for many years. Immunotherapy is of particular interest in HCC given the unique immune microenvironment of the liver [25]. Due to its location between the hollow viscous organs via the portal venous blood drainage and the systemic venous drainage, the liver has a microenvironment that is characterized by a balance between efficient pathogen clearance and tolerance toward non-harmful exogenous molecules. An example of this is found in liver dendritic cells (DC). Compared to extrahepatic DC, liver DC show poor expression of costimulatory molecules and are less effective at activating other immune cells [26]. Additionally, T-cells residing in the liver are programmed for apoptosis within three days of activation and are depleted by high antigen concentrations [27]. This mileu, in part, may explain the lower rates of rejection and immunosuppression requirements in transplant livers compared to other organs, with multiple clinical demonstrations of operational tolerance [28]. However, in the setting of chronic, inflammatory insults such as alcohol use, viral hepatitis or metabolic syndrome this tolerogenic environment may facilitate cancer development through inefficient clearance of oncogenic transformations in cells in the liver. Additional examples of this decreased immune effectiveness can be found in other cells lines. Cancer cells specifically exploit this environment. For example, cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells are the primary infiltrating lymphocyte subtype in HCC but display an attenuated activity and defective production of interferon-gamma which limits their effectiveness [29]. Natural killer cells have decreased expression of cytotoxic granules [30]. Tumor associated macrophages (TAM) show an inactive M2 phenotype and are associated with lymphocyte depletion [31]. These cellular changes are also accompanied by a spatial barrier at the interface between the HCC and the patient’s immune system which is characterized by hypoxic conditions. Hypoxia stimulates the expression of SPP1 which orchestrates macrophages and cancer-associated fibroblasts to remodel the extracellular matrix to inhibit lymphocyte infiltration to the tumor core [32]. Deletion of SPP1 in murine models is associated with destruction and this barriers and sensitization of HCC cells to immunotherapy [32]. The rationale for boosting immune responses is based on the knowledge that HCC can exploit immune checkpoints for immune evasion.

In response to these known tactics of immune evasions, immunotherapy provides a potentially promising treatment avenue. It amplifies immune responses to cancer by blocking co-inhibitory pathways. The most common pathways with currently available drug targets include the PD-1/PD-L1 or the CTLA-4 pathways. PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor that is expressed by T-cells in conditions of chronic activation. Blocking this receptor will stop the inhibition of T-cell activity [33]. PD-L1 binds to a different receptor, B7-1(CD80) which also results in inhibition of cytokine production and cellular proliferation [34]. PD-L1 is overexpressed in HCC and is a primary component in how the tumor evades immune activity [35]. PD-L1 expression has been shown to be predictive of response to immunotherapy [36]. CTLA-4 acts in a similar manner and has high avidity binding to CD80, similar to PD-L1, and results in defective T-cell activation in lymph nodes [37]. In addition to treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors, combinations with an anti-angiogenic agent has been shown to produce synergistic effects. HCC is a highly vascularized tumor and VEGF mediates neo-angiogenesis and vascular permeability [38].

Therapeutic agents targeting these effector mechanisms have become the standard of care for advanced HCC. Systemic therapy was limited to sorafenib for patients with advanced HCC since 2007 [39]. In 2020, the combination of atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody) and bevacizumab (Anti-VEGF monoclonal) became the first regimen to show superior survival to sorafenib. The landmark study IMbrave 150 was a phase III trial including 501 treatment naive patients with advanced HCC. The regimen gained FDA approval as a new frontline standard treatment for unresectable or metastatic HCC [40]. The trial demonstrated the superiority of the combination regimen with a 42% decreased mortality risk and median overall survival of 19.2 vs 13.4 months [40]. Updated efficacy and safety data from the trial with an additional 12-month follow-up resulted in an over 2-month additional progression free survival with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab compared to sorafenib [41]. Other combinations have been tested combining immunotherapy drugs with different mechanisms including durvalumab, tremelimumab, nivolumab and ipilimumab [42]. The natural extension of the efficacy observed with these therapies in the advanced setting is their application and inclusion in a multi-modal treatment including LRT. Downstaging of HCC has previously been limited to LRT such as TACE of Y90, but has not demonstrated survival benefit in the advanced HCC setting. Combined LRT and immunotherapy has improved progression-free survival in advanced HCC based on the findings of two trials published in 2025, EMERALD-1 and LEAP-012. Progression free survival was improved in EMERALD-1 with TACE combined with durvalumab/bevacizumab and LEAP-012 with TACE combined with pembrolizumab/Lenvatinib [43,44].

These represent a paradigm shift in the advanced HCC setting, but also suggest there could be benefit in the Barcelona Clinical Liver Cancer guideline early-stage or intermediate-stage HCC settings. Specifically, combined LRT and systemic therapy may allow for stage migration when these treatments are used as downstaging therapies. These results are critical for transplant oncology for two reasons. First, lengthening the duration of progression free survival for patients allows more patients to receive a potentially curative transplant in the age of organ shortages and long waiting times. Second, the opportunity for increasingly efficacious treatments that can downstage a patient’s tumor burden from outside of Milan to within Milan or the center specific criteria for transplantation could expand access to more patients. Other investigators are evaluating the potential for combination LRT and immunotherapy to convert intermediate-stage un-resectable HCC patients to resectable status [45].

In one of the first prospective, randomized, open-label pilot study promising results are being found for downstaging and recurrence-free survival in LT. A total of 22 patients were enrolled in either the control group (treated with LRT) or the PLENTY group which underwent treatment with neoadjuvant therapy with a PD-1 blockade and a tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Both groups subsequently underwent LT with 30-month recurrence free survival of 37.5% in the PLENTY group compared to only 12.5% in the control group [46]. More trials are pending with attempts to utilize ipilimumab and nivolumab for downstaging for transplantation (NCT05199285, NCT03510871).

However, there are important clinical implications of utilizing neoadjuvant immunotherapy prior to solid organ transplantation. Immunotherapeutic agents activates the immune system and are known to mimic autoimmunity syndromes and carry immune-associated side effects such as severe hepatitis [47,48]. This activity against the tumor is favorable for the recipient. However, in the post-transplantation state this activation of the immune system is not favorable and can result in significant rejection with potential graft loss. Fatal hepatic allograft necrosis and death have been reported as well [49]. Timing from the last dose of immunotherapy is critical to assess. A review of limited case series single-center data found that none of the patients who underwent transplantation > 3 months after their most recent immunotherapy had biopsy proven acute cellular rejection at 2–16 month follow-up [50].

Utilization of these therapies is exciting because it provides significant advances in the capability for downstaging HCC and reducing tumor burden. Downstaging of HCC has previously been limited to LRT such as TACE or Y90. There is still much to be learned about immunotherapy use in neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings as well as its role with LRT and the timing of treatments in relation to liver transplant to minimize toxicity and optimize transplant and oncologic benefit.

Battle of the biopsy—role of liquid biopsies in further risk stratification of patients

Researchers are continuing to work on novel and more accurate tumor markers and pathways that could be targets for future therapies. HCC is often challenging to diagnose due to the fact that early disease is often asymptomatic and late disease can present with a wide range of ambiguous symptoms. Prolonged somatic alterations due to hepatic damage, aging or inherited genetic susceptibility result in a microenvironment that result in hepatocarcinogenesis. Due to the varying presentation of HCC, surveillance programs with imaging in patients at high risk are the cornerstone of early detection. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and the European Association for the Study of the Liver currently recommend biannual ultrasound surveillance with or without serum Alpha-Fetoprotein measurement [51,52]. Cross-sectional CT imaging is then obtained if the ultrasound has concerning findings. The Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) is an established scoring system for HCC diagnosis among patients with cirrhosis [53]. Diagnosis had historically been limited to imaging with tissue biopsies reserved for ambiguous cases only. This has limited advancements in personalized, precision medicine based on molecular analysis. Liquid biopsies of blood, ascites or urine are poised to bridge this gap by providing predictive molecular tumor data via less invasive sample collection methods.

There are no currently approved biomarkers to diagnose or predict reoccurrence of HCC. Work on liquid biopsies assessing circulating tumor cells, tumor DNA, serological biomarkers, extracellular vesicles and protein methylation with “omics” approaches are all poised to create the next advance in caring for patients with HCC [54]. PIVKA-II, GPC3, osteopotin have all been implicated in the presence of HCC, demonstrating potential future role in an early diagnostic testing battery [55–57]. Efforts to isolate circulating tumor cells (CTC) have been ongoing. Though there is not yet a standard method for isolating CTCs current efforts are focusing on EpCAM+/CK+/CD45 − cells which are then utilized for downstream analytics purposes [58]. Next-generation sequencing has identified the TERT promoter as the most common mutation found in cell-free HCC DNA and is associated with HCC recurrence and survival [59]. Methylation of circulating tumor DNA is another encouraging avenue for biomarker discovery and may provide insight for future HCC management [60]. These technologies have the opportunity to provide increasingly personalized management by facilitating cancer screening, prognosis assessment, and treatment response prediction [54]. Furthermore, these pathways will be important to understand to continue to advance therapy options for patients with HCC and allow more predictive pathways. For example, detection of mutations in mammalian targets of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway genes or downstream activation of mTOR signaling in subsets of patients can often lead to tumor metabolic dysregulation, suggesting a role for clinically available mTOR inhibitors in select patients [61]. Investigation into other novel treatments include oncolytic viral therapy and irreversible electroporation for locoregional therapy will continue to yield new avenues for treatment [62]. Ultimately, deeper clinical understanding of tumor biology is likely to improve transplant candidate selection in the HCC population by further identifying prognostic tumor profiles.

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma—the intersection between biliary and hepatic malignancy

After HCC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) is the second most frequent liver malignancy and continues to have an increasing incidence worldwide in both those with primary sclerosing cholangitis and de novo cases [63]. The cause of this increase is debated but likely due to a decreasing rate of classification of liver tumors as adenocarcinoma of unknown primary [63]. Furthermore, epidemiological trends include increasing rates of diabetes, obesity, alcohol use and viral hepatitis, all of which are associated risk factors for the development of iCCA [64].

The mainstay of iCCA treatment is surgical resection. However, the 5-year overall survival is still only 33% [65]. iCCA also often presents late with unresectable disease, further challenging management. In the 1980s and 1990s physicians utilized LT for iCCA with curative intent. However, the data revealed unacceptably low survival rates of iCCA following transplantation with 26% 5-year patient survival which labeled iCCA as a contraindication to LT [66–68]. Further studies, including a Spanish analysis of patients with iCCA identified only on explant liver specimens after transplant found that 5-year survival for these patients was 73% [69]. This, as well as data on early-stage mixed HCC-iCCA tumors, suggested early-stage patients may have the opportunity for cure and the iCCA approach should be taken [70]. To address the lack strong clinical evidence, current International Liver Transplant Society Transplant Oncology Consensus Conference guidelines state the potential role for LT in early iCCA in cirrhosis, but transplant should only be offered within strict clinical and research protocols [70].

The role for systemic therapy utilized alongside LT for patients with iCCA has also been explored. Unfortunately, most patients (60–70%) are diagnosed with advanced stage disease necessitating some form of systemic treatment [71]. Since 2010, first line treatment has included gemcitabine and cisplatin for advanced iCCA. This treatment was established by the landmark ABC-02 trial, which demonstrated a median survival of 11.7 months compared to 8.1 months compared to gemcitabine alone [72]. In 2025, the first line treatment of advanced iCCA evolved to include combination chemotherapy with immunotherapy, including gemcitabine/cisplatin with durvalumab (based on TOPAZ-1) or pembrolizumab (based on KEYNOT-966) [73,74]. Transplantation continues to be entertained as potential curative intent treatment in highly-selected patients, particularly in the setting of chronic liver disease. Prior to the TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966, a prospective, single center case-series was published on the outcomes of LT for locally advanced unresectable, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma without extrahepatic disease or vascular involvement. This clinical protocol included neoadjuvant gemcitabine-based systemic therapy, in combination with capecitabine or cisplatin, or other second or third-line therapy per local institutional protocol. Patients with a minimum of 6 months of radiographic response or stability were listed for transplantation. 21 patients were referred for evaluation, 12 patients were accepted and 6 underwent LT. Median duration from diagnosis to transplant was 26 months and median follow-up with 36 months. Overall, survival was 100% at 1 year (95% CI 100–100), 83.3% (27–97.4) at 3 years and 88.3% (27.3–97.5%) at 5 years. Three patients (50%) developed recurrent disease at a median of 7.6 months after transplantation [75]. Subsequent long-term outcomes from the same study were also reported with 18 patients who ultimately underwent LT. Most patients (66.7%) in the cohort that underwent LT had no preexisting liver disease. Across a median follow-up of 26 months overall survival at 1-year, 3-year and 5-year rates were 100%, 71% and 57%. And overall survival was significantly longer among patients who underwent LT. Recurrence free survival was 52% at 36 months post-transplantation [76]. This data suggest neoadjuvant systemic therapy and inclusion of time-based observation of tumor biology allowed for transplant candidate selection with post-LT oncological outcomes similar to other types of hepatic malignancies.

A national study of utilization of LT as a treatment of iCCA between 2010 and 2018 found that 2412 (94.0%) underwent resection and only 153 (5.96%) underwent LT of whom 54.9% underwent neoadjuvant therapy. Unadjusted 5-year overall survival was higher for LT compared to resection (59.8% vs 39.9%, p = 0.0067) but adjusted analysis revealed no significant difference in mortality [77]. In recognition of the increasing role of transplant for this malignancy, the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) recently proposed a MELD exception for patients with biopsy proven, unresectable, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. Inclusion criteria restricts acceptable candidates to those with tumor sizes less than or equal to 3 cm and 6 months of tumor stability after locoregional or systemic therapy. These patients are considered for MELD exception points 3 points less than the median MELD at Transplant of their listing center. Standardized inclusion and exclusion criteria will allow for continued assessment of the outcomes of patients as this indication for transplant continues to evolve.

Future studies exploring the role of LT in patients with iCCA should focus on optimizing pre-operative systemic therapy with immunotherapy regimens in order to maximize those eligible for transplantation, and extrapolating our understanding of LRT from HCC to include multi-modal tumor control in longitudinal tumor biology assessment. It will be imperative to continue to define the limits and bounds on this potentially curative treatment strategy for certain patients with surgically unresectable disease and may even be considered for certain patients with resectable disease [78,79].

Hilar cholangiocarcinoma—evolving protocols for treatment

Hilar cholangiocarcinoma (hCCA) is a common type of cholangiocarcinoma and accounts for 40–60% of cases. Treatment for hCCA depends on the stage of the disease, presence or absence of extrahepatic disease, assessment of regional nodes and feasibility of complete resection. Resection of the disease is first line treatment if technically feasible and in the absence of metastatic disease [80]. Although, surgery with curative intent is an option in only a minority of patients with hCCA and is associated with high rates of post-operative morbidity and mortality and high recurrence rates [81]. Metastatic disease to extrahepatic sites is an absolute contraindication to surgical intervention, including transplantation, as it is a significant risk factor for recurrence and diminished survival [82]. Nodal assessments typically include both the hepatic hilar lymph nodes (91%) and celiac artery lymph nodes (70%) [83]. Most centers (96%) utilized a combination of chemotherapy and radiation therapy as part of their neoadjuvant protocol [83]. While there are many similarities among transplant centers, there is still variation in treatment protocols for patients prior to listing for liver transplant, which complicates the national assessment of outcomes.

Qualifications for LT in hCCA include patients with unresectable hCCA without regional nodal disease by either fine needle aspiration via EUS and confirmed by staging lymphadenectomy, who have undergone neoadjuvant chemoradiation and chemotherapy without progression of disease over 6 months [84]. There is debate over the treatment of borderline resectable de novo hCCA patients. Croome et al. suggests that for patients with Bismuth-Corlette IV lesions LT have an improved overall survival compared to resection [85].

Carefully developed institutional protocols demonstrate that patients with hCCA can be successfully treated. Analysis of a 65 patients who underwent LT from 1993 to 2006 at Mayo Clinic found patient and disease-free survival were 76% and 60% at five years after LT [86]. Predictors of recurrence were older age, pretransplant cancer antigen (CA 19–9 > 100 U/ml), prior cholecystectomy, residual tumor > 2 cm in explant and perineural invasion at explant [86]. A group in Ireland treated unresectable CCA patients with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by LT in their cohort of patients similar to the Mayo protocol. Results showed 9 patients remained disease free at a median follow-up of 37 months further confirming this as a viable treatment option [87]. A more modern, international study examined patients in 17 centers across 2 continents from 2014 to 2018. Patients were treated similarly to the Mayo protocol. 134 consecutive patients underwent LT after completion of neoadjuvant therapy. Five-year disease-free survival was largely superior to a matched group of nodal negative patients who underwent curative liver resection (62% vs 32%, p < 0.001) [88].

The landscape of LT for hCCA is changing due to standard-of-care systemic therapy that is often initiated prior to liver transplant evaluation. Incorporation of systemic therapy into the neoadjuvant treatment bundle should be considered [89]. Access to deceased donor transplant is also improving with the swift adoption of machine perfusion to rehabilitate liver allografts prior to implantation. Machine perfusion has the ability to potentially expand the donor pool, especially with extended criteria donors. These medical and surgical innovations may have an impact on disease-free survival and overall survival, but further data is needed [90].

Colorectal liver metastasis—a new player on the block with increasing rates of disease

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the fourth most diagnosed and the third most deadly cancer worldwide. CRC incidence has been steadily rising with various factors considered to be driving this growth including a sedentary lifestyle, increased red meat consumption, greater obesity rates, and higher rates of alcohol and tobacco use in developing countries [91]. According to the American Cancer Society (ACS), approximately 154,270 Americans are expected to be diagnosed with CRC in 2025, with an estimated 52,900 deaths [92]. Studies have estimated that among those patients diagnosed with CRC, 25–50% develop liver metastasis over the span of their illness, with a higher rate of incidence in males compared to females [93]. The liver is the most common site of metastasis in patients with colorectal cancer due to the venous drainage of the colon and rectum by the portal system [94]. However, liver involvement in metastatic CRC is a significant negative prognostic indicator. Patients with colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) are estimated to have a median survival of 5–20 months with no treatment and a poor 5-year prognosis [94].

Surgical resection for CRLMs with systemic therapy is associated with survival benefit compared with systemic therapy alone [95]. Previous contraindications to resections, such as the presence of extrahepatic disease and inadequate resection margin of less than 1 cm, have been challenged with modern surgical techniques [95]. The timing and resection of liver metastasis along with the status of the primary colorectal tumor are some of the factors that determine systemic and surgical treatment options and their effectiveness. Additional local control strategies have been applied to treating CRLMs as an extension of surgical treatment. Local control and systemic therapy strategies are data-driven but frequently individualized based on location, size of tumor and tumor genetic mutations (e.g., MMR/MSI status, KRAS/NRAS/BRAF, and HER2 mutation) [93]. Treatment options are vast and include systemic chemotherapy, targeted biologic agents, regional chemotherapy, ablation, embolization and radioembolization as well [93]. Even so, the treatment option for most patients with CRLM is palliative chemotherapy which has a 5-year OS of 10% [96]. Given that only 20% of patents with CRLM are candidates for resection, there is a need for alternative strategies to optimize outcomes for unresectable cancer patients. One such option is LT.

Liver transplantation has emerged as a potential treatment strategy for patients with unresectable CRLMs who meet certain criteria [97]. Liver transplants for CRLM were first explored in the early 1990s; however, due to organ shortages and poor outcomes, with 5-year OS less than 20%, the approach was largely abandoned [96]. This field of research was subsequently revitalized by the outcomes reported in the SEcondary CAncer-I (SECA-I) pilot study published by the Oslo group. In this study, 21 eligible patients with unresectable CRLM underwent LT between 2006 and 2011 [98]. Patients were included based on the following inclusion criteria: complete radical excision of the primary tumor, minimum 6 weeks of chemotherapy, and a good performance status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] score of 0 or 1) [98]. The Kaplan–Meier estimate for overall survival for 1, 3, and 5 years was 95%, 68%, and 60%, respectively [98]. In this study, four factors were reported to be associated with inferior survival including a carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) greater than 80 µg/L, largest hepatic tumor greater than 5.5 cm, more than 2 years from primary surgery to LT, and progressive disease on chemotherapy [96]. These factors have since been incorporated into the Oslo score for risk stratification. The SECA-I study established the use of LT in unresectable CRLM as a viable alternative treatment option. Subsequently, the Oslo group performed a prospective study of non-resectable liver-only metastatic patients with at least a 10% response to chemotherpy and a greater than 1 year time from diagnosis to transplant [99]. Four-year overall survival was 73% compared to surgical resection in which the majority of patients would develop recurrent disease at 3 years [98]. A concurrent study with patients outside of the strict criteria for SECA-II were transplanted with extended criteria grafts so as to not impact the donor pool and eight out of ten patients had relapse of metastatic colorectal cancer at a median of 23 months of follow-up [100]. This trio of studies demonstrate the importance of selective and well-thought out criteria in altering the survival of patients after LT.

Current data continues to demonstrate the use of LT as an effective means to increase survival in patients with unresectable CRLM. For example, a nonrandomized controlled cohort study by the Oslo group followed 61 LT patients to determine disease recurrence, survival after relapse, and OS [96]. A 10-year survival of 88.9% and 80.0% was seen with an Oslo score of 0 and a Fong Clinical Risk score of 1, respectively [96]. Additional data from the University of Rochester demonstrated recurrence-free survival of 40% at 3 years for CRLM patients who underwent living donor LT [101].

The TRANSMET trial was the first randomized controlled trial in transplant oncology, and evaluated the efficacy of LT with pre-LT chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in patients with CRLM. The eligibility requirements were restricted to patients who were less than 65 years old, had undergone gold-standard resection of the primary tumor and had surgery-confirmed unresectable liver metastasis. 157 cases were submitted to an independent committee from 20 centers across France, Belgium and Italy. Only 94 patients were included in the study and then randomly assigned to continue chemotherapy during which they were placed on the transplant waiting list and prioritized so transplant could occur within 2 months after the last chemotherapy. Importantly, 5 year survival was the primary endpoint. The transplant arm had a median overall survival of 73% vs 9% with chemotherapy alone. 42% had no evidence of disease with long-term follow-up [102]. Overall, these studies suggests that patients with liver-only metastases and favorable prognostic scoring have comparable long-term survival to patients with conventional indications for liver transplant; thus, potentially providing LT as a curative treatment option for patients traditionally limited to palliative therapy. The OPTN has recently established a MELD-exception policy for CRLM patients demonstrating the evolving understanding of the role of LT in the treatment of secondary liver tumors.

Neuroendocrine tumors—expanding consideration for additional metastatic diseases

Neuroendocrine tumors (NET) are rare tumors with indolent growth patterns which typically originate in the small intestine, pancreas and colon and metastasize via portal drainage. Although there are established OPTN criteria for these patients to be included on the waiting list in the United States, LTs for NET constitute only 0.2–0.3% of the total LTs given their rarity and the stict criteria for qualification for transplantation [103]. Metastatic NET (mNET) to the liver has a variety of clinical presentations, including function tumors that precipitate endocrine syndromes and nonfunctional tumors that may carry an indolent course. Qualifying criteria for OPTN MELD exception points for mNETs include low histologic grade, < 50% of hepatic volume involved by tumor, recipient age, tissue of origin and surgical control of the primary tumor. This is primarily based on the utilization of the MILAN-NET criteria which found that among carefully selected patients the transplant group showed a significant 10 year survival advantage of 88.8% vs 22.4% [104]. Currently, somatostatin analogs are recommended as the first-line systemic therapy for patients with Grade 1 and Grade 2 metastatic and well-differentiated NETs [105]. Additional combinations with peptide receptor radionuclide therapy and multikinase inhibitors has the potential to shape future treatment for these tumors [106,107]. A challenging issue in mNET management is the often uncertain course with systemic therapy alone compared to the post-LT course. Careful patient selection is essential.

Future perspective

The landscape of LT for hepatic, biliary and metastatic malignancies has dramatically evolved over the last twenty years with significant leaps forward, especially in cases that were once thought purely palliative due to the extent of the disease. Criteria revolving around tumor characteristics continue to expand as do the patient demographics including older and increased complexity of medical comorbidities (Table 1). Promising immunotherapies are becoming integrated into the care for downstaging prior to LT as are liver directed therapies such as radioembolization. Together they may even have synergistic effects. A comprehensive and multifaceted evaluation of each candidate should be undertaken with careful attention to their underlaying disease biology, true resectability and the utility of time. Transplant centers should continue to innovate and push to expand criteria for oncology patients. Critical to the success of these programs will be utilizing multidisciplinary committees including medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, interventional radiologists, transplant hepatologists and transplant surgeons. Advancing translational and clinical research will be imperative to building confidence in the success of LT for expanded oncologic indications. Growth in living donor liver volumes and expansion of these programs could ease the distance between the availability of deceased donor liver grafts and the need of transplant candidates though must be considered carefully populations with high risk of reoccurence.

Through all this, we must be careful in aligning the overarching goals of care with patients (Table 2). Of course, patient survival and quality of life metrics are of utmost importance. We must also consider other components such as time off systemic therapy and mitigation of post-transplant complications such as rejection. We must also highlight that reoccurence should not always be considered failure especially, particularly in the setting of increased patient survival or quality of life. Continued vigilance in a multidisciplinary approach for each of these disease processes is vital for progress. As we have seen advances in HCC, we will see the same for CCA and CRM. Finally, progress in organ preservation and perfusion will allow for an expanded donor pool that will give access to organs that were not considered in the past in jurisdictions with active deceased donor organ donation programs. Transplant oncology is evolving in an exciting time with the added treatment of LT for multiple malignancies.
