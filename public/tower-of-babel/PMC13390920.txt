Impact of an integrated electronic Clinical Decision Support System with proactive Antimicrobial Stewardship Feedback on prescribing appropriateness: a 2-year single-centre time-series analysis
Authors: Marianna Meschiari, Lorenzo Onorato, Maddalena Albertini, Beatrice Fontana, Valentina Menozzi, Samuele Cantergiani, Mattia Simion, Antonella Santoro, Erica Franceschini, Gabriella Orlando, Stefania Casolari, Adriana Cervo, Marianna Menozzi, Andrea Bedini, Barbara Beghetto, Giulia Nardini, Enrica Roncaglia, Mario Sarti, Jacopo Baldi, Laura Cancian, Stefania Casini, Cristina Mussini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess whether an electronic Clinical Decision Support System (eCDSS), embedded within a hospital-wide antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programme, can meaningfully reduce antimicrobial use and improve prescribing quality in routine clinical practice.MethodsWe conducted a prospective quasi-experimental interrupted time-series study in a 1200-bed tertiary-care hospital in Italy (April 2022–April 2024). The eCDSS, integrated into computerized order entry and linked to pharmacy data, triggered real-time identification of high-impact antimicrobial prescriptions and enabled infectious diseases (IDs)-led prospective audit and feedback. Primary outcomes were overall antibiotic use and carbapenem consumption; secondary outcomes included guideline adherence, stewardship intensity, antimicrobial duration and costs.ResultsAcross 5890 prescriptions in 2604 patients (9336 ID evaluations), the intervention was associated with a significant and sustained reduction in overall antibiotic consumption (CL: −3.27 DDD/100 patient-days, 95% CI −6.06 to −0.47; CT: −0.25 DDD/100 patient-days/month, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.07). Carbapenem use showed a parallel significant decline across both DDD and DOT metrics. In contrast, no significant reductions were observed for other monitored antibiotics or antifungals. Prescribing quality improved over time, with increasing adherence to local guidelines (CT: +1.59%/month; P = 0.001). Despite high-intensity stewardship activity (mean 1.6 ID interventions per prescription), antimicrobial expenditures remained stable, although a downward trend was observed.ConclusionsAn eCDSS integrated into a human-led AMS programme was associated with a significant reduction in overall antibiotic and carbapenem use and improved prescribing quality. These findings demonstrate that digital stewardship tools do not replace but amplify ID expertise and pharmacy collaboration, enabling sustained optimization of antimicrobial use.

Background

The global rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has led to an increased reliance on broad-spectrum antibiotics, which are often prescribed empirically to cover a wide range of potential pathogens.1,2 While these agents are invaluable in the management of severe infections, their inappropriate and excessive use exacerbates the development of resistance, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that jeopardizes the effectiveness of current treatments.3,4 In response, antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programmes have been established worldwide to promote the appropriate use of antimicrobials, ensuring optimal patient outcomes while mitigating the emergence of resistance.5–7

The World Health Organization's AWaRe classification system offers a framework to guide antibiotic use.8,9 Access antibiotics, considered first-line agents due to their efficacy and lower resistance potential, should represent most of prescriptions. However, recent data from Italy indicate that only 53% of prescriptions fall within the Access category, placing the country among the lowest consumers in Europe.10 This highlights a pressing need to improve prescribing practices and promote the preferential use of Access antibiotics over Watch and Reserve agents, which carry higher risks of fostering resistance and are intended for more restricted clinical scenarios.

AMS strategies typically involve both restrictive and persuasive interventions, such as formulary limitations, pre-authorization requirements and post-prescription reviews.11,12 While effective, these approaches can be resource-intensive and challenging to implement consistently, particularly in high-acuity hospital settings.13–15 To address these barriers, increasing attention has been directed toward leveraging health information technology to support AMS initiatives.16

Electronic Clinical Decision Support Systems (eCDSSs), particularly those integrated within electronic prescribing and medication management (EMM) platforms, offer a promising solution.16,17 These systems utilize patient-specific data and evidence-based guidelines to provide real-time, context-specific recommendations on antimicrobial selection, dosing and duration. Front-end decision support features—such as rule-based alerts, default treatment regimens and condition-specific order sets—can enhance prescribing accuracy, reduce variability and streamline workflows.18 Furthermore, eCDSSs can facilitate the implementation of AMS policies by incorporating antimicrobial restrictions, alerting prescribers to deviations from guidelines and enabling real-time feedback.

While the adoption of eCDSSs has demonstrated potential benefits, their effectiveness is maximized when coupled with expert oversight from infectious disease (ID) physicians and pharmacists.19 Passive decision support tools, such as mobile applications and web-based guidelines, also play a complementary role but may be underutilized by senior clinicians or limited by infrastructure challenges, such as inadequate wireless connectivity.20,21

In this context, we designed a 2-year, single-centre time-series analysis to evaluate whether the implementation of a real-time, integrated eCDSS combined with proactive feedback on prescribing indicators could improve antimicrobial prescribing practices.22 Specifically, our study aimed to assess the impact of this intervention on overall antibiotic consumption and the appropriateness of prescriptions according to local clinical guidelines for adults admitted to a tertiary-care university hospital.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective quasi experimental study conducted at the 1200-bed tertiary care University Hospital of Modena, Northern Italy, over a 2-year period from April 2022 to April 2024. To enhance AMS efforts, an eCDSS was introduced in April 2022, which also defined the start of the intervention period. For analytical purposes, the intervention phase was divided into two comparable periods (April 2022–April 2023 and May 2023–April 2024), selected to ensure similar seasonal coverage, minimize seasonal variability in antimicrobial consumption and allow evaluation of a potential maturation effect over time. The system integrates patient-specific data with pharmacy records and automatically alerts the AMS team to relevant prescriptions. To evaluate its impact, a time-series analysis was adopted comparing antimicrobial prescriptions quality.

The study was approved by the Modena University Hospital institutional review board (reference number 0007073/22 of 09/03/2022), which waived the need for the patients to sign the informed consent since the ecological nature of the project.

Data collection and definitions

The intervention consisted of an eCDSS integrated into the computerized provider order entry platform and linked to pharmacy records. The eCDSS was designed to automatically identify prescriptions of selected broad-spectrum and high-impact antimicrobials and generate a real-time alert within 48 hours of prescription. Alerts included patient-specific information (e.g. prescribed antimicrobial, ward and prescribing details) and were simultaneously transmitted to hospital pharmacists and the antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) team.

The eCDSS did not provide automated therapeutic recommendations or perform autonomous prescription reviews. Instead, it functioned as an automated surveillance and case-finding tool, systematically identifying eligible prescriptions and facilitating timely prospective audit and feedback. Consequently, the intervention represented a combined digital–human stewardship model, in which clinical decision-making and stewardship recommendations were provided by IDs specialists in collaboration with clinical pharmacists.

The newly implemented eCDSS acted as both a restrictive and persuasive AMS strategy, monitoring all WHO ‘Watch’ and ‘Reserve’ antibiotics, as well as selected high-impact antifungal agents (Supplementary Appendix).8,9 Prescriptions required electronic authorization by the prescribing unit and were recorded in a shared database accessible to pharmacists and ID specialists.

Following alert generation, ID specialists performed a structured review of antimicrobial therapy based on clinical, microbiological and pharmacological data. Stewardship interventions included treatment initiation, confirmation, intravenous-to-oral switch, escalation, de-escalation, discontinuation, dose optimization and recommendations for additional diagnostic investigations or follow-up assessments. De-prescribing was defined as either antimicrobial de-escalation or discontinuation.

Based on the ID specialist’s recommendations, pharmacists dispensed only the number of antimicrobial doses required to complete the approved treatment course or until the planned reassessment date, thereby reinforcing stewardship recommendations and limiting unnecessary antimicrobial exposure.

Initial prescription data collected by the pharmacy included patient demographics, antimicrobial agent, clinical unit and available microbiological information. During daily review, ID specialists recorded whether consultation occurred, treatment indication, therapy type (empirical or targeted), adherence to local guidelines, presence of bloodstream infection and compliance with Italian Medicines Agency (AIFA) reimbursement criteria.

AMS interventions were classified as initiation, confirmation, switch, escalation, de-escalation or discontinuation, with deprescribing including de-escalation or discontinuation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize antimicrobial prescriptions, infection characteristics and AMS interventions. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or means with standard deviations (SD), as appropriate. A before–after comparison was performed exclusively for descriptive baseline characteristics of antimicrobial prescriptions to summarize differences in case-mix and prescribing patterns before and after implementation of the eCDSS for AMS support.

Interrupted time-series analysis was used to assess the impact of the intervention on primary and secondary outcomes evaluated. First, we used the pre-intervention data to define the ARIMA model for the series; each variable was evaluated at monthly intervals, and a non-seasonal autoregressive model was chosen because the limited duration of the pre-intervention period did not allow to adjust for seasonality. Thereafter, the model was used to forecast the post-intervention values, which were compared to the observed data to estimate the intervention effect. The effect size was estimated based on two parameters: the change in trend (CT), defined as the difference before the pre- and post-intervention slopes, and the change in level (CL), defined as the difference between the observed outcome at 12 months of the intervention and that predicted by the pre-intervention trend.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with adherence to local empirical therapy guidelines. Since more than one evaluation was conducted for the same targeted treatments in some patients, a mixed-effects binary logistic regression model clustering multiple evaluations of the same prescription was adopted to analyse independent predictors of deprescribing. Variables with a P-value <0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate models using a backward stepwise approach.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

During the study period, a total of 5890 antimicrobial prescriptions were analyzed, corresponding to 2604 individual patients (Table 1). The median age at hospital admission was 70 years (interquartile range [IQR] 58–81), and 61.1% of patients were male (n = 1590), while 38.9% were female (n = 1014). Slightly less than half of the prescriptions (41.3%) were issued in medical wards, followed by intensive and sub-intensive care units (30.3%). Most prescriptions (76.2%) were initiated following ID specialist consultation. Of all prescriptions, 50.5% (n = 2972) were empirical, and 49.5% (n = 2918) were targeted. The most frequently isolated pathogens were Klebsiella pneumoniae (19.1%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (13.6%), and Escherichia coli (10.2%). (Table S2). Among empirical therapies, 66.4% were deemed appropriate according to local hospital guidelines.

Off-label use was documented in 25.3% of all prescriptions. The most common on-label indications were hospital-acquired pneumonia (24.8%) and intra-abdominal infections (17.7%), while the leading off-label indications were complicated urinary tract infections due to ESBL producers (11.2%) and bloodstream infections (10.8%). The most prescribed high-impact antibiotic classes included carbapenems (35.9%), oxazolidinones (22.2%) and lipopeptides (8.0%), while echinocandins (7.2%) were the most used antifungal agents. The median duration of treatment was 7 days (IQR 4–11). The mean time from therapy initiation to first ID evaluation was 2.4 days (standard deviation [SD] ± 1.8). Regarding AMS activities, 94.7% of prescriptions received at least one ID specialist evaluation, 45.6% received two evaluations and 13.0% had three or more evaluations. A total of 9336 evaluations were conducted, with an average of 1.6 evaluations per prescription (SD ±0.2). AS interventions included therapy initiation (23.6%), discontinuation (26.9%), de-escalation (5.3%), escalation (1.9%), switch (2.7%) and confirmation (34.9%).

A significant reduction in overall antibiotic consumption was observed following 1 year of eCDSS implementation, with a decrease in consumption level (CL: −3.27 DDD/100 PDs, 95% CI −6.06 to −0.47, P = 0.024) and a reduction in trend over time (CT: −0.25 DDD/100 PDs/month, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.07, P = 0.01). A decrease, not reaching the statistical significance, was observed for eCDSS-monitored antibiotics both in level (CL: −1.49 DDD/100 PDs, 95% CI −4.78 to 1.79, P = 0.352) and trend (CT: −0.152 DDD/100 PDs/month, 95% CI −0.37 to 0.07, P = 0.165). Similarly, no significant changes were detected in eCDSS-monitored antifungal consumption (CL: 0.64 DDD/100 PDs, 95% CI −1.61 to 2.89, P = 0.557; CT: 0.04 DDD/100 PDs/month, 95% CI −0.10 to 0.19, P = 0.543).

Regarding expenditures, a non-significant reduction in costs was observed following eCDSS implementation for both antibiotics and antifungals. Antibiotic costs decreased by €14 389 at level (CL: −€14 389, 95% CI −€66 004 to €37 225, P = 0.566) and by €2524 per month in trend (CT: −€2524/month, 95% CI −€5989 to €940, P = 0.143). Similarly, antifungal costs showed a non-significant decrease at level (CL: −€13,956, 95% CI −€67,736 to €39,824, P = 0.593), with no meaningful change in trend (CT: €372/month, 95% CI −€3202 to €3947, P = 0.830).

Regarding the consumption of carbapenems, a significant reduction during post-intervention phase was demonstrated both in trend (CT: −0.11, 95% CI −0.19 to −0.02 DDD/100 PDs/month, P = 0.015) and in level (CL: −1.56, 95% CI −2.79 to −0.32 DDD/100 PDs, P = 0.016) (Figure 2).

The impact of the intervention on meropenem use was confirmed when evaluating the consumption as DOTs: CT: −0.15, 95% CI −0.28 to −0.02 DOT/100 PDs/month, P = 0.028; CL: 2.17, 95% CI −4.43 to −0.3 DOT/100 PDs/month, P = 0.024. Impact of eCDSS implementation on adherence to local guidelines

Moving to the secondary outcomes, we evaluated the impact of our intervention on the adherence to local guidelines, reporting a significant increase in trend (CT: 1.59, 95% CI 0.7% to 2.5%/month, P = 0.001) and an increase in level almost reaching the statistical significance (CL: 13.6, 95% CI −0.1% to 27.3%, P = 0.051) (Figure 3, Table S3).

Factors associated with adherence to hospital guidelines for empirical therapies at the univariate analysis were ID prescription (OR 2.08, 95% CI 2.06–2.82, P = <0.001), more than one evaluation (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.60–2.72, P < 0.001), prescription in surgical ward (OR 2.51, 95% CI 1.95–3.23, P < 0.001), second period of observation (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.23–1.67, P < 0.001), prescription of daptomycin (OR 2.95, 95% CI 2.26–3.84, P 0.001) and tigecycline (OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.71–3.33, P 0.001), presence of bacteremic infection (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.16–1.72, P < 0.001) and intra-abdominal (OR 1.41, 95% ci 1.14–1.74, P 0.001) and skin and soft tissue (OR 3.41, 95% CI 2.43–4.79, P 0.001) sites of infections along with prosthetic material infections (OR 2.60, 95% CI 1.40–4,81, P 0.002) (Table 3). Conversely, factors associated with lack of adherence to guidelines were: off-label indication (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.57–0.80 P < 0.001); prescription in medical ward (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.63–0.85, P < 0.001); carbapenems prescriptions (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.50–0.68, P < 0.001); urinary tract infections (or 0.47, 95% CI 0.34–0.66, P 0.001) and community acquired pneumonia (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.17–0.31, P < 0.001). After adjustments for covariates at the multivariate analysis, prescriptions started by an ID consultant (OR 2.38, CI 1.93–2.92, P < 0.001), those receiving more than 1 evaluation (OR 1.85, CI 1.29–2.65, P < 0.001), prescriptions for bloodstream infections (OR 1.80, CI 1.37–2.37, P < 0.001) or intra-abdominal infection (OR 4.23, CI 2.89–6.18, P < 0.001), as well as daptomycin prescriptions (OR 3.37, CI 1.93–5.90, P < 0.001) resulted independently associated to guidelines adherence; conversely, antimicrobials prescribed in medical wards (OR 0.76, CI 0.62–0.92, P = 0.006), as well as carbapenem (OR 0.2, CI 0.23–0.37, P < 0.001) or linezolid prescriptions (OR 0.30, CI 0.17–0.50, P < 0.001) were less likely to be adherent to local recommendations (Table 3).

Impact of eCDSS implementation on the monthly volume of infectious disease consultations, type of AMS interventions and average duration of antimicrobial therapy

Analysis of monthly evaluations for monitored antibiotics revealed a significant decreasing trend in assessments of novel beta-lactams (CT −0.063, CI −0.12 to −0.004 evaluations/100PDs/month, P = 0.037), with a non-significant reduction of the level (CL −0.43, CI −1.29 to 0.43 evaluations/100 PDs, P = 0.31), which might be related to a progressive reduction in the incidence of prescriptions of monitored molecules; similar findings were observed for monitored anti-Gram positive drugs (CT: −0.27, CI −0.49 to −0.05 evaluations/100PDs/month, P = 0.018; CL: −3.2, CI −6.4 to 0.008 evaluations/100 PDs, P = 0.05) and for carbapenems (CT: −0.37, CI −0.49 to −0.25 evaluations/100PDs/month, P < 0.001; CL: −4.92, CI −6.8 to −3.1 evaluations/100 PDs, P < 0.001).

Although the prevalence of confirmed prescriptions and deprescribing (de-escalation or discontinuation) among all AMS interventions significantly decreased from 28.6% to 24.9%, 34.3% and 30.4%, respectively (Table 1), no significant changes were detected in the distribution of AMS intervention types when assessed through time-series analysis, either in terms of trend (P = 0.89) or level (P = 0.79) of confirmation rates (Table 1). No significant changes were observed in the type of AMS interventions in terms of either trend (P = 0.89) or level (P = 0.79) of confirmation rates. A significant decreasing trend in deprescribing interventions was noted during the post-intervention phase (change in trend [CT] −2.3%/month, 95% CI −4.0 to −0.4; P = 0.014; change in level [CL] 30.7%, 95% CI −57.7 to −3.7; P = 0.027), potentially reflecting increased adherence to guideline-concordant prescribing.

At the multivariate analysis, independent factors associated with deprescribing strategies turned out to be the use of carbapenems (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.14–1.98, P = 0.004), oxazolidinones (aOR 1.86, 95% CI 1.37–2.51, P < 0.001) and daptomycin (aOR 2.42, 95% CI 1.67–3.51, P < 0.001), as well as prescription for urinary tract infection (aOR 1.35, 95% CI 1.02–1.79, P = 0.039) or bloodstream infections (aOR 1.55, 95% CI 1.27–1.88, P < 0.001). Conversely, ID prescription (aOR 0.21, 95% CI 0.17–0.25, P < 0.001), second period of observation (aOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.47–0.65, P < 0.001) and prescriptions due to gram positive microorganisms (aOR 0.59, 95% CI 0.45–0.78, P < 0.001) were associated to a lower rate of deprescribing (Table S4).

Finally, despite an observed reduction of approximately 1 day in treatment duration across all infection types (Table 1), no significant variation was observed in both trend (P = 0.79) and level (P = 0.92). Concerning duration for specific indication, a significant reduction in the mean duration level was observed for skin and soft tissue infection (CL: −9.3, CI −17.7 to −0.96, P = 0.03). No other significant differences were found between pre- and post-intervention phases (Table S3).

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental study, we evaluated the impact of the implementation of an eCDSS integrated within a hospital-wide antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programme. Our findings indicate that implementation of the eCDSS was associated with a significant reduction in overall antibiotic consumption, driven in part by decreased use of key high-impact agents, especially carbapenems. These changes were accompanied by improved adherence to local prescribing guidelines and a trend towards reduced antibiotic-related costs, supporting the potential role of eCDSS as an effective stewardship tool in optimizing antimicrobial use.6 This study highlights the real-world synergy between digital and human-driven stewardship strategies, integrating real-time eCDSS alerts with proactive antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) interventions, structured ID feedback and pharmacist–clinician collaboration. Periodic re-evaluations performed by IDs specialists in close collaboration with hospital pharmacists were associated with a reduction in unnecessary broad-spectrum antimicrobial use and facilitated optimization of treatment duration in accordance with site-specific, evidence-based guidelines. Importantly, the intervention maintained a consistently high level of ID specialist engagement, thereby reinforcing stewardship activities and contributing to sustained optimization of antimicrobial prescribing.24

Our findings highlight several key strengths of the intervention in a real-world stewardship setting. A consistent trend toward reduced use of high ecological-impact antimicrobials was observed, alongside a parallel decrease in antimicrobial expenditures. Although these changes did not reach statistical significance, the observed cost reductions may still represent a relevant economic signal, suggesting a potential return on investment through further optimization of digital resources within a cost-effectiveness framework.25 We also acknowledge that antimicrobial expenditures are influenced by multiple contextual and structural factors, including patent expiration, the introduction of high-cost novel agents and evolving prescribing patterns such as carbapenem-sparing strategies. As a result, ecological improvements, including reduced use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, do not necessarily translate into immediate or proportional cost reductions. Furthermore, it is important to contextualize these findings within the framework of a long-standing antimicrobial stewardship programme initiated in 2016. Although substantial cost savings (approximately €300 000/year) were achieved during the early implementation phase (2017–2018), antimicrobial expenditures have subsequently reached a plateau, with only marginal additional reductions observed in recent years (∼€30 000/year), suggesting a stabilization of the economic impact over time. Importantly, we also observed a progressive increase in adherence to local guidelines over time. Finally, improved monitoring and optimization of treatment duration according to infection site further underscore the system’s contribution to standardising and refining prescribing practices.26–28

Our results are in line with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis published in JAMA Network Open (2023),29 which reported that decision support tools were associated with a 16% reduction in antibiotic prescriptions (95% CI, 2%–30%). The authors highlighted that these systems could enhance the efficiency of AMS programmes by facilitating prospective audit and feedback (PAF), thus reducing the human effort and time required for stewardship interventions. Similarly, in our study, the high proportion of prescriptions undergoing ID review and the frequent stewardship interventions confirm that eCDSS can serve as a catalyst for timely and targeted AMS actions.

Notably, prior studies reported heterogeneous results regarding the effectiveness of CDSS in improving antimicrobial outcomes.30–33 For example, the COMPASS trial did not show a significant reduction in overall antibiotic use, which was partly attributed to limited uptake, relatively low baseline antibiotic consumption and delays in system use following patient admission.34 Our intervention differs by being fully embedded into existing AMS processes, with mandatory ID review and close integration with microbiology data, which likely contributed to the observed benefits on guideline adherence and carbapenem sparing. Furthermore, studies adopting a need-based and focused approach—such as prioritizing high-impact antimicrobials—have shown greater improvements in appropriateness, consistent with our finding. Despite the observed benefits, evidence that CDSS alone improves hard clinical outcomes such as mortality, length of stay or infection cure rates remains scarce. Reviews have underscored that the impact of CDSS is influenced by several contextual factors, including implementation processes, type of infections targeted, clinician compliance and system design. Improvements in usability, user interface and real-time integration of clinical and microbiological data are repeatedly identified as key determinants of success.33

Our study adds to the existing evidence by focusing on both antimicrobial consumption and guideline adherence while also analyzing stewardship activities and consultation dynamics. Strengths include the large number of prescriptions analyzed, the use of robust interrupted time-series analysis to evaluate trends over time and the detailed assessment of AMS interventions. The significant reduction in carbapenem use, alongside improved adherence to guidelines, highlights the potential of eCDSS to address high-priority targets for resistance containment. Moreover, the integration of the tool into routine AMS workflows and the mandatory involvement of ID specialists ensured consistent uptake and minimized variability in use, addressing some of the limitations described in previous negative trials.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-centre design within a well-established AMS programme may limit generalizability. A key limitation is thatthe analysis compares the first and second year after eCDSS implementation—potentially reflecting a maturation effect—while pre-intervention consumption data were not included due to the lack of structured data on appropriateness and adherence, as well as the potential confounding impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on antimicrobial use patterns. Although there was an overall reduction in carbapenem and total antibiotic use, this effect was not consistent across all WHO ‘Reserve’ agents, nor did it translate into uniform changes in antimicrobial costs. Notably, the baseline use of ‘Reserve’ antibiotics in our institution was already low (<8% of total prescriptions), which is comparatively limited for a tertiary-care university hospital setting. Patient-level clinical outcomes were not assessed. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—including concurrent infection control measures, local resistance trends, seasonal variations of antimicrobial prescriptions and the COVID-19 pandemic—may have influenced prescribing patterns.35

Conclusions

The implementation of an eCDSS integrated into an established AMS programme was associated with reduced use of ‘Watch’ and ‘Reserve’ antibiotics and improved adherence to guidelines. These findings support the role of decision support tools in optimizing antimicrobial prescribing, particularly when closely linked to IDs specialist input and prospective audit and feedback. Future research should evaluate the long-term effects of eCDSS on AMR and patient outcomes. Further financial investment and technological development are needed to enhance functionality, reduce human resource demands and maximize uptake.
