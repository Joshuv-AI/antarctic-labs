Tumor-derived HMGB2 induces M2-like macrophage polarization via TRIM65-mediated NLRP3 degradation to promote DLBCL progression
Authors: Sanxiu He, Yi Liu, Yifeng Tang, Liuyue Zhai, Xin Luo, Mingyu Zhao, Qing Xiao, Xiaoqin Xie, Huihui Fu, Jun Li, Ya Li, Xuejiao Shu, Zailin Yang, Yao Liu, Xiaomei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma. Although standard immunochemotherapy is available, 30%–40% of patients develop refractory or relapsed disease, underscoring the need to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of therapy resistance. High mobility group box 2 (HMGB2) is overexpressed in DLBCL; however, its role in the pathogenesis of DLBCL and in mediating therapy resistance remains unclear.MethodsHMGB2 expression and prognostic value in DLBCL were analyzed using public databases, validated by quantitative reverse transcription-PCR and western blot analysis. In vivo and in vitro HMGB2 knockdown models were constructed to explore its role in DLBCL. Macrophage-DLBCL co-culture systems were established to investigate HMGB2-mediated macrophage polarization. Transcriptome sequencing was performed to identify downstream targets of HMGB2, with molecular mechanisms confirmed by western blot analysis, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunofluorescence. Exosome isolation and protease protection assays were used to determine the HMGB2 secretion pathway.ResultsHMGB2 is overexpressed in DLBCL and correlates with poor prognosis. While HMGB2 knockdown showed no cell-intrinsic effects in vitro, it significantly suppressed tumor progression in vivo by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. We identified a novel mechanism whereby exosomal HMGB2 derived from DLBCL cells upregulates the E3 ubiquitin ligase tripartite motif containing 65 (TRIM65) in macrophages, promoting ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. This cascade drives M2-like macrophage polarization and impairs phagocytic function. Therapeutic targeting of this axis restored macrophage phagocytosis and synergized with CD20 immunotherapy to enhance its antitumor efficacy.ConclusionOur study defines the HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 axis as a pivotal immunosuppressive pathway in DLBCL. Targeting this axis represents a promising combinatory strategy to reprogram the tumor microenvironment and overcome therapy resistance.Keywords: Macrophage, Phagocytosis

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) represents the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, with approximately 150 000 new cases diagnosed globally each year, accounting for roughly 30% of all cases.1 The introduction of rituximab (RTX) in combination with the cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP) regimen has significantly improved patient outcomes. Nevertheless, 30%–40% of individuals still experience disease relapse or progression, highlighting a substantial clinical challenge.2 Elucidating the mechanisms underlying treatment failure in this patient subgroup is essential for developing more effective therapeutic strategies and improving long-term survival.

The pathogenesis of DLBCL involves both intrinsic genomic alterations in B cells and extrinsic regulation by the tumor immune microenvironment (TME).3 While recurrent mutations in genes such as MYD88, CD79B, and BCL2 are well-established intrinsic drivers,4 5 the extrinsic components of the TME, particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), are increasingly recognized as pivotal mediators of both disease progression and therapeutic response.3 Macrophages exhibit remarkable plasticity, existing on a spectrum from antitumor, pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotypes to pro-tumor, anti-inflammatory M2-like states.6 Furthermore, macrophage polarization status directly impacts treatment efficacy. In DLBCL, a high density of M2-like TAMs is correlated with poor prognosis and resistance to rituximab-CHOP.7 8 Conversely, M1-like macrophages are potent effectors of antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), a key mechanism underlying the efficacy of RTX.9 10 Therefore, understanding the factors that govern macrophage polarization is essential for developing novel immunotherapeutic strategies.

The high mobility group box (HMGB) family, the most prevalent group of non-histone nuclear proteins in eukaryotes, comprises four members HMGB1–4.11 HMGB1 and HMGB2 are the most abundant HMGB proteins. HMGB1, the most extensively studied member, functions as a prototypical damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) when released into the extracellular space. It actively promotes tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance across various cancer types, including lymphomas.12 13 In contrast, HMGB2, which shares 80% amino acid sequence homology with HMGB1, remains far less characterized in oncology. Its expression is predominantly restricted to hematopoietic and lymphoid organs under physiological conditions,14 suggesting potentially specialized roles in the immune system and hematological malignancies. Emerging studies have begun to illuminate HMGB2’s multifaceted functions: it modulates T cell differentiation and contributes to CD8+ T cell exhaustion in chronic infection and cancer contexts.15 In hepatocellular carcinoma, HMGB2 fosters an immunosuppressive milieu by dampening CD8+ T cell activity while simultaneously accelerating tumor progression.16 17 Structurally, HMGB2 exhibits strong DNA-binding affinity and participates in essential nuclear processes such as DNA repair and chromatin remodeling.18 Although HMGB2 has demonstrated oncogenic potential in some solid tumors through mechanisms such as Akt activation and β-catenin signaling,19 20 its expression pattern, functional significance, and mechanistic contributions in DLBCL pathogenesis remain largely unexplored. This represents a significant knowledge gap with potential therapeutic implications for this aggressive lymphoma.

In this study, we identify HMGB2 as a key tumor-promoting factor that is highly expressed in DLBCL and correlates with poor patient outcomes. Our findings reveal a novel molecular axis whereby tumor-derived HMGB2, primarily secreted via exosomes, upregulates the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM65 in macrophages, promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. This signaling cascade drives immunosuppressive M2-like macrophage polarization and impairs phagocytic function, thereby facilitating tumor progression and conferring treatment resistance. Importantly, we provide preclinical evidence that targeting this HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 pathway can restore macrophage-mediated antitumor immunity and synergize with CD20 immunotherapy (eg, RTX). Our work thus defines a previously unrecognized immune evasion mechanism and proposes a promising therapeutic strategy to reduce resistance in DLBCL.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

DLBCL (n=4) and hyperplastic lymph node (HLN, n=4) samples were collected from Chongqing University Cancer Hospital.

Survival analysis and macrophage infiltration analysis

Transcriptomic and clinical data from DLBCL tissues were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets GSE32918 (n=172) and GSE117556 (n=928). Macrophage infiltration was analyzed using EcoTyper, with statistical analysis performed in GraphPad Prism 8.

Animal models

Female BALB/c mice (18–22 g) aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to sterile standard chow and water. The animals were maintained under controlled conditions (temperature: 22±2°C, relative humidity: 50±10%, 12-hour light/dark cycle).

HMGB2 knockdown model

After a 7-day acclimation period, mice were randomly assigned to the A20_HMGB2-knockdown (KD) group or A20_negative control (NC) group (n=5 per group) using the random number generator in GraphPad Prism 8. Allocation concealment and blinding were implemented. Under isoflurane anesthesia, 1×10⁷ A20 cells (100 μL) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor volume was measured weekly using a caliper and calculated as length×width²×0.5. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached 1500 mm³ or distress signs appeared. Tumors were excised, weighed, and subjected to further analysis.

Combination therapy model

To evaluate therapeutic synergy, A20 cells (1×10⁷ cells/mouse) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm³ (day 7 after injection), the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n=6 per group): vehicle control, tannic acid (TA, 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, administered every other day; HY-B2136, MedChemExpress (MCE)), CD20 antibody (10 mg/kg, intravenous injection, administered twice weekly; HY-P990806, MCE), and TA plus CD20 antibody. Tumor volumes were measured every 7 days and calculated as length×width²×0.5. Tumor weights were recorded at the end point (day 21). Body weight, general health, and signs of distress were monitored throughout the experiment. No unexpected deaths or data exclusion occurred.

Cell culture

DLBCL cell lines (OCI-LY1, Toledo, U2932, A20) were maintained in RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Human peripheral blood-derived macrophages were differentiated in RPMI-1640 with 10% human serum for 7 days. For co-culture experiments, 1×10⁶ DLBCL cells and 2×10⁵ macrophages were cultured in Transwell chambers (0.4 μm pore size) for 48 hours.

Short hairpin RNA and small interfering RNA transfection

Lentiviral-mediated HMGB2 KD was performed using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences (human: GAATTCAAGTGCAGCTCAA; mouse: GAACACCCAGGCCTGTCTATT). TRIM65 KD in macrophages was achieved using small interfering RNA (siRNA) (CCAACCGTCACTTCTATCTT). Transfections were performed with Polybrene or Lipofectamine 3000.

Cell Counting Kit-8 assay

Logarithmically growing DLBCL cells were seeded at a density of 4×104 cells/well in 96-well plates. Cell proliferation was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours postseeding. At each time point, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (C0039, Beyotime) was added to each well and incubated for 2 hours. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy).

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay

DLBCL cells from co-culture or logarithmic growth phase were incubated with 20 μM EdU for 2 hours, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (T8200, Solarbio), and subjected to Click reaction in the dark for 30 min. After Hoechst 33342 staining, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Novocyte Advanteon) using the EdU proliferation detection kit (C0075S, Beyotime).

Apoptosis analysis

Collect the DLBCL cells after co-culture for apoptosis assays. Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then resuspended in 100 μL of binding buffer. Next, 5 μL of Annexin V-phycoerythrin and 5 μL of 7-aminoactinomycin D (559763, BD Pharmingen) were added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min in the dark. After adding 400 μL of binding buffer, cell apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry (Novocyte Advanteon).

Cell cycle assay

Cell cycle analysis was performed using a Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1052, Beyotime). Logarithmically growing DLBCL cells were seeded at a density of 5×105 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured for 48 hours. Cells were collected and fixed with ice-cold 70% ethanol for 24 hours. After removing the fixative, cells were incubated with a staining solution containing propidium iodide and RNase A for 20 min, followed by cell cycle analysis using flow cytometry (Novocyte Advanteon).

DLBCL cell migration and invasion assays

For migration and invasion assays, 5×10⁵ DLBCL cells in serum-free medium were seeded in Transwell inserts (3421, Corning), with 20% FBS as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After 24 hours, migrated cells were quantified. For invasion assays, membranes were precoated with Matrigel before cell seeding.

Macrophage migration assay

Supernatants from 48-hour cultures of OCI-LY1_NC and OCI-LY1_KD cells were collected as conditioned medium. Macrophages (1×10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in Transwell inserts (5 μm pore; 3421, Corning) and exposed to the conditioned medium for 24 hours. Migrated cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime), and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71, Japan).

Macrophage phagocytosis assay

For phagocytosis assays, DiI (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate)-labeled DLBCL cells (2×10⁶) were co-cultured with adherent macrophages (1×10⁵) for 1 hour. After washing away non-phagocytosed cells, phagocytosis was quantified by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus IX71) or flow cytometry (Novocyte Advanteon) using DiI dye (C1036, Beyotime).

RNA isolation and RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and complementary DNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara). Messenger RNA (mRNA) expression was quantified by SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) with normalization to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and relative fold changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Flow cytometry

Cells were stained with flow cytometry antibodies for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, washed with PBS, and analyzed on a BD FACS Canto. Antibodies included: F4/80 (123109), CD86 (105011), CD206 (141719/321104), CD8a (105011), CD4 (100548), CD25 (102029), CD49b (108907), human leukocyte antigen-DR (307626), CD3 (100219), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (124333) (all from BioLegend).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA buffer) and quantified with bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (P0012S, Beyotime). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (1620184, Bio-Rad), blocked with 5% non-fat milk, and incubated with primary antibodies: GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), HMGB2 (RP01718, Abclonal), HMGB1 (10829-1-AP, Proteintech), HMGB3 (27465-1-AP, Proteintech), HMGB4 (12787-1-AP, Proteintech), TRIM65 (bs-17120R, Bioss), NLRP3 (27458-1-AP, Proteintech), and ubiquitin (10201-2-AP, Proteintech). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000), protein bands were visualized using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (34095, Thermo Scientific).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% PFA (10 min), permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (T8200, Solarbio; 5 min), and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (1 hour). Primary antibodies against CD206 (ab64693, Abcam), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (ab15323, Abcam), and NLRP3 (27458-1-AP, Proteintech) were applied overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab150153/ab150073, Abcam). Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (C1005, Beyotime), and images were acquired using an Olympus IX71 fluorescence microscope or Zeiss LSM 510 confocal microscope.

Inhibitor treatment to assess HMGB2 secretion pathway

DLBCL cells (OCI-LY1 and Toledo) were seeded at 3×10⁶ cells/well in 24-well plates and treated with the following inhibitors for 6 hours: GW4869 (10 μM, M4974, AbMole), Brefeldin A (5 μg/mL, 420601, BioLegend), chloroquine (25 μM, M9559, AbMole), 3-methyladenine (5 mM, M2296, AbMole), or glyburide (50 μM, M3547, AbMole). Vehicle controls received dimethyl sulfoxide or PBS. Conditioned media were collected and centrifuged at 300× g for 5 min and 2000× g for 10 min to remove cells and debris. HMGB2 levels in cell lysates and supernatants were analyzed by western blot analysis. Cell viability was assessed using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay (C0016, Beyotime).

Exosome isolation, characterization, and protease protection assay

Exosomes were isolated from culture supernatants using the Total Exosome Isolation (from cell culture media) kit (4478359, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The exosomal marker TSG101 (F1543, Selleck) was detected by western blot analysis to confirm successful isolation. For the protease protection assay, exosomes were incubated with Proteinase K (10 μg/mL, EO0491, Thermo Fisher Scientific) alone or in combination with 0.1% Triton X-100 on ice for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding SDS-PAGE loading buffer followed by heating at 95°C for 10 min. HMGB2 and TSG101 protein levels were then analyzed by western blot analysis.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

Macrophages stimulated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (00-4976, eBioscience) were treated with PBS or 100 ng/mL recombinant HMGB2 (rHMGB2) (RP01718, Abclonal) for 24 hours. Cells were lysed and immunoprecipitated using the Pierce Classic Magnetic immunoprecipitation (IP)/co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) Kit (88804, Thermo Scientific) with IgG (AC005, Abclonal) or HMGB2 antibody (A9168, Abclonal). Samples were denatured and analyzed by immunoblotting following SDS-PAGE (PG112, Epizyme) and transfer to PVDF membrane.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Macrophages treated with PBS or 100 ng/mL rHMGB2 for 24 hours were collected for RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Scientific). Transcriptome sequencing was performed by Majorbio (Shanghai), with subsequent data analysis conducted on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Raw sequencing data are available in the GEO database under accession number PRJNA1279412.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using R software 4.3.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. Statistical significance was determined by unpaired or paired Student’s t-test, and one-way and two-way analysis of variance tests. Correlations between variables were analyzed by Pearson’s correlation analysis. All data are expressed as the mean±SD of three biological replicates. Data were considered statistically meaningful at p<0.05.

Results

HMGB2 is highly expressed in DLBCL and correlates with poor prognosis

In our previous study,21 single-cell transcriptome sequencing demonstrated that HMGB2 expression was significantly elevated in malignant B cells compared with normal B cells (figure 1a). Additionally, analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database revealed that HMGB2 expression levels were markedly higher in DLBCL compared with HLN samples (figure 1b). Correspondingly, our western blot analysis results confirmed that HMGB2 expression was significantly increased in DLBCL tissues relative to HLN (figure 1c). We then compared survival outcomes between groups with differing HMGB2 expression levels in the GSE32918 (n=172) and GSE117556 (n=928) cohorts. The results showed that patients with high HMGB2 expression had significantly shorter overall survival compared with those with low HMGB2 expression (figure 1d). These findings suggest that elevated HMGB2 expression in DLBCL may be linked to the malignant progression of the disease. To further clarify the role of HMGB2 in DLBCL progression, and considering the high expression levels of HMGB2 in DLBCL cell lines, we employed shRNA lentivirus to construct HMGB2 KD human (OCI-LY1, Toledo) and murine (A20) DLBCL cells (online supplemental figure 1a). To exclude off-target effects on other HMGB family members, we examined HMGB1, HMGB3, and HMGB4 expression in KD DLBCL cells. Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (online supplemental figure 1b) and western blot analysis (online supplemental figure 1c) confirmed that HMGB2 shRNA did not significantly alter the mRNA or protein levels of HMGB1, HMGB3, or HMGB4, confirming the specificity of HMGB2 KD. Using this validated KD model, we then evaluated its impact on tumor growth in vivo. Subcutaneous injection of A20 NC or KD cells into BALB/c mice (figure 1e) revealed that HMGB2 KD significantly suppressed lymphoma progression, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and weight (figure 1f–h). These findings suggest that HMGB2 may play a pro-tumorigenic role in DLBCL. However, when we evaluated Ki67 protein levels in the KD and NC tumor tissues, no significant difference was observed (figure 1i), indicating that HMGB2 KD does not affect the proliferative capacity of DLBCL cells in vivo. Additionally, no significant differences were detected in proliferation (figure 1j, online supplemental figure 1d, apoptosis (online supplemental figure 1e), cell cycle (online supplemental figure 1f), migration (online supplemental figure 1g), or invasion (online supplemental figure 1h) between NC and KD DLBCL cells in vitro. These results suggest that HMGB2 may promote the progression of DLBCL by modulating the TME, rather than directly affecting the behavior of tumor cells.

HMGB2 knockdown reprograms TME by shifting macrophage polarization

To determine whether HMGB2 KD suppresses DLBCL tumor progression by modulating TME, we profiled immune cell infiltration in the A20 murine lymphoma model with or without HMGB2 KD. HMGB2 KD significantly increased the proportion of M1-like macrophages while decreasing M2-like macrophages, resulting in a reduced M2/M1 ratio, although total macrophage and PD-L1+ macrophage populations remained unaltered (figure 2a,b). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the proportions of CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells, or natural killer cells within the TME between the two groups (figure 2c–f). To further validate the relationship between HMGB2 expression in DLBCL and macrophage phenotypes within the TME, we analyzed DLBCL datasets GSE32918 and GSE117556 using EcoTyper cell annotation.22 The results revealed that DLBCL tissues with high HMGB2 expression exhibit reduced infiltration of M1-like macrophages, a cell population associated with favorable patient prognosis (figure 2g,h), consistent with established literature.23 These results indicate that HMGB2 may primarily promote tumor burden by skewing macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype. TAMs are known to originate from peripheral blood monocytes, and the effects of tumor cells on macrophage chemotaxis and polarization can alter the abundance of TAM.24 Therefore, we tested HMGB2’s effect on macrophage recruitment using a migration assay. No significant differences were observed in macrophage chemotaxis toward the KD and NC tumor cell supernatants (figure 2i,j). Thus, the shift in macrophage polarization following HMGB2 KD in lymphoma cells is likely due to direct modulation of polarization rather than alterations in chemotaxis.

HMGB2 knockdown in DLBCL inhibits M2-like macrophage polarization and enhances phagocytosis

In vivo experiments demonstrated that HMGB2 KD reduced DLBCL tumor burden. However, in vitro, this effect was independent of tumor cell-intrinsic changes. Given our observation of a decreased M2/M1 macrophage ratio in TME, we hypothesized that HMGB2 modulates macrophage function. To test this, we established a co-culture system of macrophages with NC or HMGB2 KD DLBCL cells (figure 3a). Co-culture with KD DLBCL cells significantly promoted M1-like macrophage polarization while suppressing the M2-like phenotype (figure 3b–d). We next investigated the functional consequences of this polarization shift. Macrophages eliminate tumor cells primarily through two distinct mechanisms: (1) non-contact killing via cytotoxic factors (eg, tumor necrosis factor-α, interferon-γ) and (2) direct phagocytosis,25 26 with the efficacy of these mechanisms being polarization-dependent.27 In our co-culture system, we found no significant differences in the proliferation (online supplemental figure 2a) or apoptosis (online supplemental figure 2b) of NC versus KD DLBCL cells, indicating that macrophage-mediated non-contact cytotoxicity was unaffected. We therefore focused on phagocytosis. First, we confirmed that, as previously reported,28,30 M1-like macrophages demonstrate a greater capacity for tumor cell phagocytosis than M2-like macrophages (figure 3e, online supplemental figure 2c,d). Critically, co-culture with KD DLBCL cells significantly enhanced the phagocytic activity of macrophages compared with co-culture with NC cells (figure 3f,g). Furthermore, we observed no significant difference in macrophage phagocytic selectivity between NC and KD DLBCL (online supplemental figure 2e), indicating that HMGB2 KD does not alter the intrinsic susceptibility of DLBCL cells to phagocytosis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HMGB2 KD in DLBCL can remodel macrophage function, promoting an antitumor M1-like phenotype and enhancing their capacity for tumor cell phagocytosis.

Extracellular HMGB2 directs macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype with diminished phagocytic activity

Although HMGB2 is generally considered to be predominantly localized in the nucleus, evidence indicates that HMGB1—which shares 80% sequence homology with HMGB2—can be secreted and modulates TME.31 32 We therefore hypothesized that DLBCL cells might similarly secrete HMGB2 to influence macrophage phenotypes. Western blot analysis confirmed that HMGB2 was readily detectable in the supernatant of DLBCL cells, with levels significantly reduced in the supernatant of HMGB2-KD DLBCL cells (figure 4a, online supplemental figure 3a). This confirms that DLBCL cells secrete HMGB2 and that its extracellular level correlates with its intracellular expression. We first performed bioinformatic analysis using SignalP 6.0, which classified human HMGB2 as ‘other’ (online supplemental figure 3b), confirming the absence of a canonical N-terminal signal peptide. This result suggests that HMGB2 secretion may not rely on the classical endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-Golgi pathway. We then treated DLBCL cells with a panel of pharmacological inhibitors targeting distinct secretion routes. Only GW4869 (an exosome inhibitor) significantly reduced supernatant HMGB2 levels, whereas Brefeldin A (ER-Golgi inhibitor), chloroquine (lysosome inhibitor), 3-methyladenine (autophagy inhibitor), and glyburide (ABC transporter inhibitor) had no effect (figure 4b, online supplemental figure 3c). LDH release assays confirmed that none of the treatments caused significant cytotoxicity (online supplemental figure 3d). Furthermore, exosomes isolated from DLBCL-conditioned medium contained HMGB2 along with the exosomal marker TSG101, and exosome-depleted supernatant showed markedly reduced HMGB2 levels (figure 4c, online supplemental figure 3e). Protease protection assays revealed that HMGB2 was largely susceptible to Proteinase K digestion in intact exosomes and became completely degraded on membrane permeabilization with Triton X-100 (figure 4d, online supplemental figure 3f), indicating that the majority of HMGB2 is exposed on the exosome surface. To isolate the direct effect of HMGB2 on macrophages from the complexity of co-culture, we treated macrophages with 100 ng/mL rHMGB2 and assessed their polarization and function (figure 4e). Both flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that rHMGB2 promoted M2-like polarization while suppressing the M1-like phenotype (figure 4f–h). This shift was functionally consequential, as rHMGB2-treated macrophages exhibited a significant reduction in tumor cell phagocytosis (figure 4i,j, online supplemental figure 3g). To further validate the specific role of HMGB2, we performed rescue experiments. In the NC DLBCL cells co-culture system, the addition of an HMGB2-neutralizing antibody (ANTI) increased M1-like polarization and significantly enhanced phagocytic capacity. Conversely, in the KD DLBCL cells co-culture system, the supplementation of rHMGB2 promoted M2-like polarization (figure 4k–l, online supplemental figure 3h–l) and markedly suppressed phagocytosis (figure 4m,n, online supplemental figure 3m). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HMGB2 secreted by DLBCL cells is a critical mediator in promoting pro-tumorigenic macrophage phenotypes.

HMGB2 drives pro-tumorigenic macrophage phenotypes by suppressing NLRP3 signaling

NLRP3 plays a crucial role in regulating macrophage functional phenotypes.33 Typically, DAMPs from tumor cells activate the Toll-like receptor (TLR)/P2X7 purinoceptor (P2X7)-NLRP3 signaling axis,34 35 which processes pro-interleukin (IL)-1β and pro-IL-18 to drive an M1-like phenotype.36 Given that NLRP3 activation is also linked to enhanced phagocytic capacity,37 38 we investigated whether HMGB2 influences macrophages by modulating this pathway. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed high NLRP3 expression in M1-like macrophages, which was significantly reduced following treatment with rHMGB2 (figure 5a). This suppressive effect was corroborated by western blot analysis, showing that rHMGB2 decreased NLRP3 protein levels in a dose-dependent manner (figure 5b). To establish a direct functional link, we administered the NLRP3 agonist BMS-986299 (BMS) to rHMGB2-treated macrophages. The agonist significantly reversed the effects induced by HMGB2, restoring the M2/M1 ratio (figure 5c–e) and rescuing the phagocytic capacity of the macrophages (figure 5f–h). In summary, these findings demonstrate that HMGB2 secreted by DLBCL cells suppresses the NLRP3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting an M2-like, pro-tumorigenic macrophage phenotype and impairing antitumor phagocytic function.

HMGB2 triggers NLRP3 ubiquitin-mediated degradation via upregulation of TRIM65

To elucidate how HMGB2 regulates the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages, we first assessed its expression. Treatment with rHMGB2 did not alter NLRP3 mRNA levels (figure 6a), but induced a dose-dependent decrease in NLRP3 protein (figure 5a), indicating post-transcriptional regulation. As NLRP3 stability is known to be modulated by ubiquitination,39 we hypothesized that HMGB2 promotes its ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. Transcriptomic analysis of rHMGB2-treated macrophages revealed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (figure 6b and c), which was confirmed by western blot analysis showing elevated total ubiquitinated proteins (figure 6d). Interrogation of the transcriptome data identified several upregulated E3 ubiquitin ligases, including TRIM65 (figure 6e), a known regulator of NLRP3 ubiquitination and degradation.40 41 The upregulation of TRIM65 by HMGB2 was validated at the mRNA and protein levels (figure 6f,g, online supplemental figure 4a,b). Furthermore, co-IP assays confirmed the interaction between TRIM65 and NLRP3 and demonstrated that rHMGB2 treatment increased both TRIM65 expression and the abundance of ubiquitinated NLRP3 (figure 6h). To establish TRIM65’s functional role, we knocked it down in macrophages using siRNA (figure 6i). TRIM65 silencing reversed the HMGB2-induced depletion of NLRP3 protein (figure 6j). Consequently, loss of TRIM65 promoted an M1-like polarization state, suppressed the M2-like phenotype (figure 6k, online supplemental figure 4c,d), and enhanced tumor cell phagocytosis (figure 6l,m, online supplemental figure 4e). In summary, these results demonstrate that HMGB2 upregulates the E3 ligase TRIM65, which in turn targets NLRP3 for ubiquitination and degradation. This pathway suppresses the inflammatory and phagocytic functions of macrophages, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive TME in DLBCL.

Targeting the HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 axis potentiates CD20 immunotherapy by enhancing macrophage phagocytosis

CD20 monoclonal antibody (eg, RTX) is a cornerstone of DLBCL treatment. Its efficacy relies in part on activating macrophages to mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and ADCP.42 Since enhanced phagocytosis is known to improve the therapeutic effect of CD20 antibodies,43 44 we investigated whether the HMGB2 pathway influences this process. Our data establish a model, where DLBCL-secreted HMGB2 upregulates the E3 ligase TRIM65 in macrophages, promoting ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. This cascade impairs phagocytosis by skewing macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype (figure 7a). We therefore hypothesized that inhibiting this axis could potentiate CD20 immunotherapy. Consistent with this, we found that while RTX treatment enhanced macrophage phagocytosis, the addition of rHMGB2 significantly suppressed this RTX-induced effect (figure 7b). Conversely, enhancing NLRP3 signaling with an agonist robustly augmented the pro-phagocytic activity of RTX (figure 7c). Similarly, silencing TRIM65 in macrophages also significantly enhanced RTX-mediated phagocytosis (figure 7d). To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting HMGB2 in combination with CD20 immunotherapy in vivo, we established a subcutaneous A20 murine lymphoma model. Mice were treated with PBS (control), TA (an HMGB2 inhibitor),16 antimouse CD20 antibody (anti-CD20), or TA plus anti-CD20. As shown in figure 7e–g, anti-CD20 monotherapy significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with control, consistent with its established therapeutic efficacy. TA alone also showed modest inhibitory effects. Notably, the combination of TA and anti-CD20 further reduced tumor volume and tumor weight compared with either monotherapy (figure 7e,f). Tumor growth curves confirmed that the combination treatment resulted in the most pronounced inhibition throughout the observation period (figure 7g). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 axis represents a promising therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of CD20 immunotherapy in DLBCL.

Discussion

The TME is a critical determinant of progression and therapeutic response in DLBCL. Here, we identify the chromatin-associated protein HMGB2 as a novel, tumor-derived modulator of the DLBCL immune landscape. By linking elevated tumorous HMGB2 expression to impaired macrophage-mediated antitumor immunity, our findings reveal a novel immune evasion pathway in this aggressive lymphoma.

We first established that HMGB2 is highly expressed in DLBCL and correlates with poor patient survival. Contrary to its reported cell-intrinsic proliferative role in solid tumors,45 46 HMGB2 KD in DLBCL cells did not affect proliferation in vitro. This functional divergence aligns with emerging evidence of HMGB2’s context-dependent roles across malignancies, where it has been shown to regulate CD8+ T cell differentiation and stemness in chronic viral infection and cancer, and drive immunosuppressive microenvironment remodeling in hepatocellular carcinoma through multi-omics defined mechanisms.15 17 The critical requirement for HMGB2 in maintaining tumorigenicity in vivo, despite its dispensability for proliferation in vitro, suggested an extrinsic mechanism of action. We therefore hypothesized that HMGB2 promotes DLBCL progression primarily by remodeling the TME. This premise was substantiated by our finding that HMGB2 KD in tumor cells orchestrated a shift in macrophage polarization from a pro-tumorigenic M2-like toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype in vivo. This phenomenon aligns with HMGB2’s unique localization in the hematopoietic system. Under physiological conditions, HMGB2 localizes predominantly to the nucleus, where it modulates chromatin structure and gene transcription.11 47 In DLBCL, however, tumor cells actively secrete HMGB2 into the extracellular space, where it functions as a key tumor-derived signal shaping the protumoral niche. Collectively, these findings reveal a previously underappreciated role of HMGB2 in DLBCL pathogenesis and highlight its functional diversity across cancer types.

This difference raises a key question: why are lymphoid malignancies particularly sensitive to the extrinsic immunomodulatory role of HMGB2? We propose that this functional divergence may be explained by the intrinsically stronger B cell-macrophage crosstalk inherent in lymphoid tissues. Indeed, a recent study demonstrated that tissue-resident B cells spatially colocalize with macrophages and actively regulate their polarization and function, promoting an anti-inflammatory phenotype via IL-10 secretion, which contributes to bacterial clearance and prevents excessive tissue damage during infection.48 This homeostatic B cell-macrophage communication is likely hijacked by DLBCL cells to promote an immunosuppressive niche. Supporting this notion, multiple studies have shown that DLBCL cells can polarize macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype through various mechanisms, including gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) secretion,49 the CCL2/CCR2 axis,5 and tumor-derived exosomes.50 In contrast, solid tumors, which originate from non-immune cells, lack such evolutionarily programmed crosstalk with macrophages and may therefore rely more heavily on cell-intrinsic HMGB2 signaling for proliferation. Collectively, these observations support the notion that lymphoid malignancies are particularly sensitive to HMGB2-mediated immunomodulation due to the inherent B cell-macrophage communication network established during evolution.

The functional relevance of this macrophage phenotypic shift is substantiated by clinical evidence. Specifically, high HMGB2 expression in DLBCL tissues correlates with reduced infiltration of M1-like macrophages, a population associated with favorable prognosis (figure 2g,h). This observation aligns with the established role of M2-like macrophages in fostering an immunosuppressive TME and poor clinical outcomes.7 8 Notably, HMGB2 KD in DLBCL cells selectively altered macrophage polarization without significantly impacting T or NK cells, underscoring its specific role in modulating the macrophage compartment—a finding that complements recent reports of HMGB2’s immunomodulatory versatility, including its dual role in bolstering CD8+ T cell function while inhibiting tumor growth in hepatocellular carcinoma.16 As M1-like macrophages exhibit superior phagocytic capacity compared with their M2-like counterparts,30 51 we investigated and demonstrated that HMGB2-driven M2-like polarization significantly impairs this function. Given that phagocytosis is a critical effector mechanism of anti-CD20 antibody therapy,52 our findings provide a plausible mechanistic link between HMGB2 expression and resistance to standard care.

A key finding of our study is the elucidation of the mechanistic pathway connecting extracellular HMGB2 to macrophage reprogramming. We discovered that HMGB2 acts by destabilizing the pattern recognition receptor NLRP3 in macrophages. NLRP3 has emerged as a central regulator of macrophage functional polarization, with its expression and activation favoring an M1-like state and enhancing phagocytosis.37 Our data demonstrate that HMGB2 induces a post-translational loss of NLRP3 protein, paralleling its previously reported role in mediating nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) degradation in T cells.16 Transcriptomic analysis implicated ubiquitination pathways, which led us to identify the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM65 as the critical intermediary. TRIM65 mediates K48/K63-linked ubiquitination and degradation of NLRP3.40 Since LPS has been reported to reduce TRIM65 levels, and TRIM65 KD promotes macrophage activation,53 we investigated its role in our pathway. We found that HMGB2 upregulates TRIM65, and that TRIM65 is indispensable for HMGB2-mediated NLRP3 degradation, M2-like polarization, and the consequent impairment of phagocytosis. Collectively, this HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 axis defines a novel signaling cascade through which tumor cells directly incapacitate innate immune effector cells.

The translational implications of our work are significant. Given that macrophages are pivotal for the efficacy of RTX, the HMGB2-induced impairment of phagocytosis provides a direct molecular explanation for a potential mechanism of resistance. Our intervention using an NLRP3 agonist to reverse HMGB2-mediated immunosuppression and restore phagocytic function in vitro provides proof-of-concept that targeting this axis can re-sensitize tumors to antibody-based therapy. The identification of TA as a specific HMGB2 inhibitor16 offers a promising starting point for developing combinatorial strategies to overcome macrophage-mediated resistance in patients with high HMGB2 expression.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. The molecular mechanism governing HMGB2 surface anchoring on DLBCL-derived exosomes remains undefined. Furthermore, how exosomal HMGB2 is recognized by macrophages to upregulate TRIM65 warrants further investigation. Finally, our in vivo findings are limited to a single mouse model (A20) and a single HMGB2 inhibitor (TA), warranting validation in additional models and with other inhibitors.

In conclusion, our study redefines the role of HMGB2 in DLBCL from a nuclear protein to a potent, extracellular immunosuppressive mediator. By delineating the HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 pathway, we bridge a critical gap in understanding how a tumor-intrinsic factor subverts innate immunity. This work provides fundamental insights into DLBCL biology and unveils a therapeutically targetable axis to reprogram the TME and overcome treatment resistance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study establishes HMGB2 as a previously unrecognized, tumor-derived orchestrator of immune suppression in DLBCL. We delineate a complete molecular axis: DLBCL cells secrete HMGB2 via exosomes, which upregulates the E3 ligase TRIM65 in macrophages, leading to ubiquitin-mediated degradation of NLRP3. This cascade functionally reprograms macrophages toward an M2-like, pro-tumorigenic phenotype and critically impairs their phagocytic capacity—a key effector mechanism of anti-CD20 therapy. Consequently, this pathway provides a direct mechanistic link between HMGB2 expression and resistance to standard care. Importantly, our preclinical intervention demonstrates that pharmacological restoration of NLRP3 signaling or direct HMGB2 inhibition can reverse this immunosuppression and synergize with CD20 immunotherapy to suppress tumor growth. These findings expand the functional repertoire of HMGB2 in cancer biology and unveil the HMGB2-TRIM65-NLRP3 axis as a novel and promising therapeutic target to reprogram the TME and overcome treatment resistance in DLBCL.
