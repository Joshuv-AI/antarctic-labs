Purulent Pericarditis and Tamponade From a Migrating Esophageal Foreign Body
Authors: Ziad R. Hindosh, Beda M. Cha, Jonathan Soverow, Kaylin Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPurulent pericarditis is rare, typically affecting immunocompromised individuals from hematogenous spread, contiguous extension, or direct inoculation. Esophageal foreign body migration as an etiology has not been previously described.Case SummaryA 42-year-old immunocompetent male presented with pleuritic chest pain and dysphagia after ingestion of a tripe burrito. He developed hemodynamic instability from cardiac tamponade. Emergent pericardiocentesis was performed, yielding fluid that grew Streptococcus constellatus. Computed tomography and esophagogastroduodenoscopy identified a penetrating foreign body in the distal esophagus. He improved following video-assisted pericardial washout and antibiotic therapy. Follow-up imaging demonstrated migration of the object between the thoracic aorta and azygos vein.DiscussionRare cases of purulent pericarditis in immunocompetent patients have been reported via respiratory translocation; however, this appears to be the first reported case of purulent pericarditis due to esophageal foreign body migration. Multidisciplinary consensus supported conservative management rather than surgical pericardiectomy and led to a full recovery.Take-Home MessagesEarly recognition of purulent cardiac tamponade due to foreign body ingestion, along with prompt pericardiocentesis and antibiotic therapy, was critical for stabilization and individualized management.

History of Presentation

A 42-year-old man presented to the emergency department with 3 days of sharp, pleuritic chest pain that began suddenly after taking a bite of a foil-wrapped tripe burrito. The pain was localized to the lower substernal region, worse with swallowing solids but not liquids, and radiated to the neck, back, and left arm. He denied preceding viral symptoms, fever, night sweats, abdominal pain, weight loss, or other symptoms.

On arrival, he was afebrile with a blood pressure of 118/84 mm Hg, heart rate was 70 beats/min, respiratory rate was 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation was 97% on room air. Physical examination revealed normal cardiac auscultation without murmurs or extra heart sounds. Lungs were clear without wheezes or crackles. Patient had no evidence of hypervolemia such as jugular venous distention or peripheral edema.

Labs were notable for leukocytosis, creatinine of 1.23 mg/dL elevated from baseline of 0.85 mg/dL, normal cardiac troponin, normal lactate, and D-dimer of 0.94 μg/mL. Computed tomography (CT) angiography of the chest showed no pulmonary embolism or acute thoracic pathology. Electrocardiography demonstrated diffuse ST-segment elevation and PR-segment depression concerning for acute pericarditis (Figure 1). Point-of-care ultrasonography showed no pericardial effusion. Patient was discharged with ibuprofen for acute pericarditis.Figure 1Electrocardiograph Upon Arrival With Diffuse ST-Segment Elevation and PR-Segment Depression, ST-Segment Depression and PR-Segment Elevation in aVR

The patient re-presented to the emergency department 3 days later with ongoing chest and epigastric pain despite ibuprofen therapy. He was febrile at 38.4 °C, tachycardic to 103 beats/min with a blood pressure of 100/69 mm Hg. Repeat electrocardiography redemonstrated sinus rhythm with diffuse ST-segment elevation and PR depression (Figure 2). In the emergency department, he became acutely hypotensive with systolic blood pressure of 48 mm Hg, which improved with fluid resuscitation. His pulsus paradoxus was measured to be <10 mm Hg. A CT angiogram of the chest demonstrated a large pericardial effusion with pericardial thickening and enhancement, along with a linear, radiopaque structure embedded in the distal esophagus (Figure 3). Past Medical History

The patient had a history of controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus. No history of cardiac disease, prior pericarditis, esophageal disease, thoracic trauma, or known foreign body ingestion. He denied alcohol or illicit drug use and had no history of immunosuppression or recent instrumentation.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial presentation with pleuritic pain with diffuse ST-segment elevation and leukocytosis made acute pericarditis most likely. However, given his dysphagia and acute symptom onset following consumption of food, gastroesophageal pathology such as esophagitis, foreign body ingestion, or food impaction was considered. Acute coronary syndrome was considered but unlikely given negative cardiac markers and chest pain features. The patient developed a pericardial effusion and tamponade rapidly following his initial presentation, bringing concern for a hyperinflammatory response from a viral or autoimmune pericarditis.

Investigations

He had an elevated rheumatoid factor but normal cyclic citrullinated peptide. Sedimentation rate was 85 mm/h, and C-reactive protein was 141.8 mg/L. Pericardiocentesis yielded 450 mL of cloudy, purulent, serosanguineous fluid with a lactate dehydrogenase of 961 U/L and nucleated cell count of 19,400/μL with 96% segmented neutrophils. Bacterial culture from pericardial fluid ultimately grew Streptococcus constellatus; blood cultures remained negative.

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) demonstrated a thin, linear foreign body that protruded into the distal esophageal wall 1 cm above the gastroesophageal junction, appearing to penetrate through the esophageal wall (Figure 4). Endoscopic removal was deferred and patient was referred for surgical intervention. There was no evidence of esophagitis, perforation, or hiatal hernia. Thoracic surgery was consulted, and the patient was transferred to a center with cardiothoracic surgery capabilities for multidisciplinary management. Given the complexity of extracting a foreign body, the patient was transferred to a location that had cardiac anesthesia and cardiac bypass circuit capabilities accessible.Figure 4Esophagogastroduodenoscopy With Buried Foreign Body in the Distal Esophagus, 1 cm Proximal to the Gastroesophageal Junction (Red Arrow)

At the receiving hospital, a repeat CT thorax showed persistent 3.5-cm radiodensity in the distal esophagus without contrast extravasation, and pericardial drainage was minimal but bilious. Repeat EGD and bronchoscopy did not visualize the foreign body; esophagram demonstrated a thin curvilinear density projecting over the distal esophagus, consistent with the CT findings. Serial transthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs) showed decreasing effusion and thoracic surgery deferred intervention given the absence of pericardial tract. However, repeat imaging demonstrated a small recurrent effusion with new pericardial thickening and gas locules within the pericardial space, bringing concern for constrictive pericarditis.

Management

The patient was initiated on broad-spectrum antibiotics in the emergency department. Given the growth of S. constellatus, infectious disease was consulted, who recommended a 6-week course of antibiotics including intravenous ceftriaxone, followed by transition to amoxicillin-clavulanate. He was also started on ibuprofen 600 mg 3 times daily with colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily given his inflammatory pericarditis.

Hemodynamics improved after the emergent pericardiocentesis, but persistent tachycardia, dyspnea, and radiographic concern for constrictive physiology led to further evaluation. The pericardial thickening, purulence, and loculated fluid with gas seen on TTE led to a video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with pericardial window creation and pleural drainage. He was found to have purulent pericardial fluid, thickened, inflamed pericardium, and green-tinged fluid within the pleural space.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient improved following VATS drainage and completion of antibiotics. He was no longer febrile, denied chest pain or dyspnea, and maintained stable hemodynamics. Pericardial fluid cultures from the operating room resulted sterile. Repeat TTE showed trivial pericardial effusion with improved filling patterns and repeat CT thorax demonstrated stable appearance of the curvilinear density in the distal esophagus. He was deemed stable for discharge without further interventions with a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug taper, 3 months of colchicine, and follow-up with cardiology and thoracic surgery.

At 6-month follow-up, patient remained asymptomatic. CT thorax showed migration of the foreign body external to the esophagus, between the thoracic aorta and azygos vein (Figure 5). Gastroenterology deemed repeat EGD to be of low utility given migration of the object and high procedural risk given proximity to the aorta, and dislodgement could cause vascular injury. Thoracic surgery confirmed stable positioning on repeat CT and elected conservative management with interval imaging in 1 year.Figure 5Computed Tomography Thorax Demonstrating Hyperdense Object Between Thoracic Aorta and Azygos Vein (Red Arrow)

Discussion

Purulent pericarditis is rare, representing <1% of pericardial disease in developed countries, and has high rates of morbidity and mortality when treatment is delayed.1 There are typically 4 primary routes of spread: hematogenous, particularly in immunosuppressed patients; contiguous spread from adjacent organs, such as the pleura; from instrumentation or postoperative contamination; or direct inoculation, as in this case.2 Purulent pericardial tamponade from S. constellatus, part of normal oral and intestinal flora, is exceedingly rare.

The presence of a foreign body embedded into the esophagus raised suspicion of bacterial translocation across a microperforated esophageal wall seeding the pericardial space. Although EGD, bronchoscopy, and esophagram could not find an obvious perforation, endoscopy does not rule out a transmural or sealed foreign body penetration.3 The bilious drainage from the pericardial drain and migration of the object on follow-up imaging further supports transmural passage. Although the pericarditis with pericardial effusion and esophageal body were at one point considered 2 distinct entities, imaging and frequent evaluations ultimately supported a unifying diagnosis of purulent pericarditis from direct inoculation.

Pericardial disease guidelines indicate urgent pericardiocentesis and antibiotic therapy for purulent pericarditis.1 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines recommend surgical pericardiectomy for constrictive physiology refractory to medical treatment.1 However, not all cases of constriction require aggressive surgical intervention. Transient constrictive pericarditis, described in collagen vascular disease, purulent bacterial pericarditis, and chemotherapy,4 is a temporary condition that resolves once the inflammatory process is treated.1

Regarding long-term surveillance of the retained foreign body, which is now between the thoracic aorta and azygous vein, interval CT imaging was recommended given the risk of vascular injury, secondary infection, or further migration. In the absence of symptoms or radiographic progression, conservative management is appropriate, with a low threshold for surgical interventions if complications arise.

In this patient, several features justified undergoing a VATS pericardial window over pericardiectomy. The uncertainty of an esophageal microperforation or contained fistulous tract brought an operative risk, as major thoracic operations in a contaminated mediastinum is associated with higher morbidity.5 Furthermore, the patient's hemodynamics improved after pericardiocentesis, antibiotics, and VATS washout, which reduced the need for more aggressive surgery.

Conclusions

This case highlights an exceptionally rare presentation of purulent pericarditis in an immunocompetent adult caused by bacterial translocation from an embedded esophageal foreign body. Early recognition of cardiac tamponade with pericardiocentesis and antibiotic therapy were important for stabilization. Multidisciplinary coordination among cardiology, thoracic surgery, gastroenterology, and infectious disease was key in managing this complex thoracic infection. Although serial multimodal imaging and diagnostic studies were not consistently revealing, clinical judgment and tailored, guideline-based interventions helped this patient make a full recovery.
