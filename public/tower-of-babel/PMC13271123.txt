Association of Triglyceride-to-HDL Cholesterol Ratio with Osteoarthritis: Multicenter Evidence and Application of an Interactive Risk Visualization Tool
Authors: Junrui Chen, Weishi Hong, Xue Tian, Wei Wang, Yu Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease increasingly recognized to be closely associated with lipid metabolism dysregulation. The triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, a composite marker of metabolic abnormalities, is widely used to predict the risk of metabolic diseases, yet its link with OA risk remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the association between TG/HDL-C and OA risk, explore potential nonlinear dose–response relationships and subgroup heterogeneity, and develop an interactive visualization tool for clinical application.MethodsA total of 6,570 participants were included in this multicenter cross-sectional study. Multivariable logistic regression, random forest modeling, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were conducted to assess independent associations, variable importance, and nonlinear trends. Stratified analyses by sex and diabetes status identified subgroup-specific thresholds. An interactive risk visualization tool was created using the R Shiny platform.ResultsEach unit increase in TG/HDL-C was associated with an 11% higher OA risk (OR = 1.11; 95% CI: 1.077–1.144). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear association, with lower inflection points and steeper risk curves in females and diabetic individuals, indicating greater sensitivity to TG/HDL-C elevation. In the random forest model, TG/HDL-C ranked second only to age in importance; moreover, the random forest model outperformed logistic regression in predictive accuracy. The visualization tool illustrated higher OA risk among older women and diabetic participants with elevated TG/HDL-C ratios.ConclusionTG/HDL-C is an independent metabolic predictor of OA, showing nonlinear and subgroup-specific effects. The proposed model and visualization tool may aid early identification and individualized risk stratification.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common chronic degenerative joint disease that affects approximately 7.6% of the global population and represents a major contributor to disability worldwide.1 With the aging of the population and the rising obesity rate, OA has become the major contributor to the decline in physical function and quality of life among the middle-aged and elderly population.2 OA is mainly manifested as cartilage destruction, synovial inflammation and subchondral bone remodeling. These manifestations are related to multiple factors such as aging, trauma, gender, biomechanical abnormalities and obesity.3

Recently, new studies have shown that dysregulation of lipid metabolism is associated with an increased risk of OA.4 Emerging evidence indicates that cholesterol and its oxidative derivatives can activate the lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 (LOX-1), thereby triggering the NF-κB signaling pathway and promoting the expression of the pro-inflammatory chemokine monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1).5,6 This cascade contributes to chondrocyte inflammation and apoptosis, ultimately leading to the structural degradation of articular cartilage. A recent clinical study published in 2024 demonstrated that elevated total cholesterol levels and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentrations are significantly associated with the exacerbation of knee joint symptoms and pathological changes.7 These findings suggest that dysregulation of lipid metabolism may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of OA and could represent a potential target for therapeutic intervention.

Elevated triglyceride (TG) levels and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol are recognized as key metabolic contributors to the progression of OA.8 Specifically, hypertriglyceridemia has been shown to induce the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α),9,10 and to activate the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, thereby aggravating synovial inflammation and accelerating cartilage extracellular matrix degradation.11 Accumulation of saturated fatty acids and n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) further activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to the upregulation of IL-6 and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which in turn induce chondrocyte apoptosis and matrix breakdown—a mechanism validated in both in vitro and in vivo studies.12 Conversely, decreased HDL-C levels may reflect impaired HDL antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, facilitating lipid accumulation and oxidative stress, damaging the synovial barrier, and disrupting local joint tissue homeostasis.13 Moreover, growing evidence suggests that impaired or reduced HDL-C is frequently associated with diminished antioxidant capacity and decreased activity of paraoxonase 1 (PON1), rendering it ineffective in preventing oxidative injury and inflammation.14,15 Collectively, these alterations contribute to synovial cell dysfunction, increased MMPs/ADAMTS activity, and aberrant bone remodeling processes.16,17

The triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C), a marker of lipid metabolic dysfunction, has been widely used to predict the risk of metabolic syndrome,18 atherosclerosis,19 and type 2 diabetes mellitus.20 Previous studies have shown that individual lipid parameters are also associated with osteoarthritis (OA). Elevated triglyceride (TG) levels have been associated with greater symptom severity and structural damage in knee OA,21 and with OA incidence and progression, particularly in older populations. In contrast, reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels have been associated with increased OA risk,22 and poorer functional outcomes. Population-based studies further support a significant association between HDL-C and OA.23 TG and HDL-C reflect different aspects of lipid metabolic dysfunction—namely increased lipid burden and enhanced pro-inflammatory status, as well as impaired anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacity.24–26 Therefore, compared with individual lipid parameters, the TG/HDL-C ratio may more comprehensively reflect OA-related metabolic abnormalities. This is consistent with previous findings suggesting that osteoarthritis is associated with the combined effects of multiple metabolic disturbances.27

Previous studies have explored the role of TG/HDL-C and related metabolic indicators in arthritis from different perspectives. In osteoarthritis (OA), evidence regarding TG/HDL-C remains limited, and its population-level association with OA risk has not been systematically established.28,29 In addition, population-based studies have investigated composite metabolic indices incorporating TG and HDL-C, such as the visceral adiposity index (VAI) and cardiometabolic index (CMI), both of which have been associated with an increased risk of OA.30,31 However, although these multifactorial indices have predictive value, they rely on multiple parameters and do not allow clear differentiation of the independent effects of lipid components, which limits their clinical applicability. Furthermore, the nonlinear relationships, threshold effects, and population heterogeneity of TG/HDL-C in relation to OA risk have not been systematically evaluated.32

Using multicenter data from 6,570 participants, the present study examined the association between TG/HDL-C, a simple and metabolically representative indicator, and OA risk. Multivariable regression models were used to assess independent effects, while restricted cubic splines explored potential nonlinear dose–response relationships. Stratified analyses identified risk heterogeneity across metabolic backgrounds (eg., sex and diabetes status). Additionally, a random forest model with SHAP interpretation was applied to capture complex nonlinear relationships, and an interactive risk visualization tool was developed to enhance clinical interpretability and applicability.

Methods

Study Population

This cross-sectional study included 6,570 adult participants from three tertiary hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China—Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital (Yangzhou), Jiangsu Province People’s Hospital (Nanjing), and Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing)—where all participants underwent standardized clinical interviews, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and imaging assessments under protocols approved by the institutional ethics committees of all participating centers (Approval No: 2024ky307) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data from all participating centers were extracted from electronic medical record systems using a unified data collection protocol. Because the data were retrospectively retrieved, this multicenter design enhanced the representativeness of the study population and improved the generalizability of the findings across different clinical settings in Jiangsu Province. Osteoarthritis (OA) was diagnosed based on comprehensive assessments of clinical symptoms and imaging findings. Participants were classified as having OA based on the presence of persistent or recurrent joint pain, stiffness, or limited mobility, or radiographic evidence (X-ray or MRI) of degenerative changes, including joint space narrowing, cartilage degradation, or osteophyte formation. Radiographic OA was defined using the Kellgren–Lawrence (K–L) grading system,33 with grades ≥2 considered indicative of OA, while individuals without these features were classified as non-OA. All diagnoses were made by experienced clinicians according to internationally recognized criteria, with other joint disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis and gout excluded.

Study Variables

The selection of covariates was based on prior research evidence and the epidemiological characteristics of known OA risk factors, while also considering the availability and quality of data in this cohort. The included covariates covered demographic characteristics, chronic disease status, metabolic markers, and liver and kidney function indicators. Specifically, the following variables were assessed: age (in years), sex (male or female), body mass index (BMI, in kg/m2; categorized into five groups in the results section according to WHO criteria: <18.5, 18.5–23.9, 24.0–27.9, 28.0–31.9, and ≥32.0), diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, the apolipoprotein A1/B ratio (APOA1/B), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, mmol/L), triglycerides (TG, mmol/L), apolipoprotein A1 (g/L), apolipoprotein B (g/L), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), lipoprotein(a) (mg/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), AST mitochondrial isoenzyme (U/L), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT, U/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, U/L), globulin (g/L), total bilirubin (μmol/L), direct bilirubin (μmol/L), and uric acid (UA, μmol/L). All information was obtained from the electronic medical record systems of the three participating hospitals under a unified data collection protocol.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.4.3) and SPSS (v27.0.1). Categorical variables were summarized as counts (%) and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD. Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, Levene’s test assessed variance homogeneity. Depending on variance equality, independent or Welch’s t-tests were used. For non-normal data, the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied. Significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided). Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding extreme values of TG/HDL-C. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), under the assumption that data were missing at random. Five imputed datasets were generated, and the results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. To minimize the influence of extreme values, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding outliers of the TG/HDL-C ratio. Univariate and Multivariable Regression Analysis

Univariate analysis was first conducted to compare baseline differences between OA and non-OA groups. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed with OA status as the dependent variable and TG/HDL-C as the primary exposure, adjusting for sex, age, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, alkaline phosphatase, γ-glutamyl transferase, and APOA1/B. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values were calculated. This analysis was conducted in the full sample to evaluate the independent association between TG/HDL-C and OA. To further assess the discriminative ability of different lipid indicators, the dataset was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a ratio of 7:3. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed using TG, HDL-C, and TG/HDL-C separately under the same covariate adjustment, and their performance was evaluated in the testing set using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings.

Random Forest Modeling and Feature Importance

A random forest (RF) model was constructed using the same 7:3 training–testing split as described above. Continuous variables (TG/HDL-C, BMI, and APOA1/B) were winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles based on the training set distribution, and the same thresholds were applied to the testing set. Hyperparameter tuning was performed in the training set using grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation. The search space included num.trees (300–1000), mtry (2–6), max.depth (4–12), and min.node.size (5–20). The mean cross-validated AUC was used to select the optimal parameter set. The final RF model was trained on the full training set and evaluated in the testing set using ROC curves and AUC with 95% confidence intervals. Probability calibration was performed using Platt scaling, and model performance was further assessed by the calibrated AUC, Brier score, and calibration curves based on 10 equally spaced probability bins. Feature importance was evaluated using permutation importance, and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was applied to interpret variable contributions and reduce potential bias associated with Gini-based importance.

Quartile and Subgroup Analyses

TG/HDL-C was divided into quartiles (Q1–Q4) according to its distribution in the study population. Using Q1 as the reference, adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for Q4 were calculated within subgroups defined by sex, diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease. A forest plot was generated to visualize and compare effect estimates across subgroups.

Nonlinear Risk Assessment

To examine the nonlinear association between TG/HDL-C and OA risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were constructed with knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. The overall and nonlinear trends were tested using Wald statistics. Inflection points and stratified risk curves were plotted to explore metabolic-specific nonlinear patterns.

Clinical Utility and Visualization Development

To evaluate risk heterogeneity across different metabolic backgrounds, random forest models were additionally constructed within sex- and diabetes-stratified subgroups. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to examine the net clinical benefit across varying threshold probabilities, while net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) metrics were calculated to quantify incremental model gains. Finally, an interactive visualization platform was developed using the R Shiny framework. This platform was based on out-of-fold calibrated probabilities derived from the random forest model and incorporated a three-dimensional scatter plot to depict the joint distribution of TG/HDL-C ratio, age, and predicted risk, along with stratified radar plots to compare risk probabilities across sex- and diabetes-defined subgroups. By integrating TG/HDL-C with multivariable distributions, the tool supports real-time parameter input and dynamic visualization of individualized OA risk, thereby generating personalized risk maps to facilitate clinical application.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 6,570 participants were included in this study, comprising 3,285 patients with osteoarthritis and 3,285 non-OA controls. Compared with the non-OA group, the OA group had a significantly higher proportion of women, as well as higher prevalences of diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease (all P < 0.001). The mean age was also significantly greater in the OA group than in the non-OA group (61.64 ± 16.02 vs. 55.31 ± 13.71 years, P < 0.001). Similarly, body mass index (BMI) was higher among patients with OA (25.88 ± 3.68 vs. 24.54 ± 3.34, P < 0.001). Regarding metabolic parameters, there were no significant differences in serum uric acid (P = 0.304) or total cholesterol (P = 0.326) between the two groups. In contrast, the triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio (2.60 ± 2.94 vs. 1.79 ± 2.01, P < 0.001), along with other metabolic indices, differed significantly between groups. Collinearity Diagnostics

To assess potential multicollinearity among covariates, variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated. The VIF values for AST mitochondrial isoenzyme and AST exceeded 5, indicating potential multicollinearity between these variables, which may compromise the stability of the regression model. In contrast, the VIF values for TG/HDL-C, BMI, and sex were below 5, suggesting that multicollinearity among these covariates is not a major concern. Independent Predictors of Osteoarthritis

An elevated TG/HDL-C ratio was positively associated with an increased risk of OA, with each unit increment corresponding to an approximately 11% higher risk, underscoring its potential as a metabolic marker for OA risk assessment. Age was also an important predictor, with each additional year conferring an estimated 3% increase in risk. In addition, women had a higher risk than men, and individuals with diabetes were at significantly greater risk. Higher BMI, hypertension, and coronary heart disease were likewise associated with increased risk, whereas the APOA1/B ratio demonstrated a protective effect. Other variables, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), also reached statistical significance in the multivariable model. However, their effect sizes (with ORs approximating 1) were minimal, indicating limited clinical relevance; therefore, to maintain model parsimony and reduce the risk of overfitting, these two variables were not included in subsequent analyses. The multivariable logistic regression analysis described above was conducted in the full sample to evaluate the independent association between TG/HDL-C and osteoarthritis. Based on this framework, further analyses were performed to assess the discriminative performance of different lipid indicators. Discriminative Performance of TG/HDL-C Compared with Individual Lipid Indicators

To further evaluate the discriminative performance of the TG/HDL-C ratio compared with individual lipid parameters (TG and HDL-C), multivariable logistic regression models were constructed using TG, HDL-C, and TG/HDL-C under identical covariate adjustments and evaluated in the testing set. In the testing set, the TG/HDL-C model achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.701, 95% CI: 0.678–0.723), exceeding that of the HDL-C model (AUC = 0.692) and the TG model (AUC = 0.678). DeLong test results further confirmed that the TG/HDL-C model demonstrated significantly better discriminative ability than both the TG model (P < 0.001) and the HDL-C model (P = 0.009). Discrimination and Calibration Performance of the Random Forest Model in OA Risk Assessment

Based on the finding that the TG/HDL-C ratio demonstrated superior discriminative ability compared with individual lipid indicators, we further compared the performance of a traditional multivariable logistic regression model and a random forest model for osteoarthritis risk assessment. Both models were constructed using the same covariates and evaluated under an identical training–testing split strategy. Hyperparameter tuning for the random forest model was performed using grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation. The optimal parameter combination was identified as num.trees = 800, mtry = 5, max.depth = 10, and min.node.size = 10. Detailed tuning results are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

In the testing set, the random forest model demonstrated higher discriminative performance (AUC = 0.819, 95% CI: 0.790–0.832) compared with the logistic regression model (AUC = 0.701, as shown in Figure 3A). Regarding model calibration, calibration curves constructed using Platt scaling with 10 equally spaced probability bins indicated good calibration performance. The calibration curve of the random forest model was close to the ideal diagonal line, with a calibrated AUC of 0.81 and a Brier score of 0.20. Building upon this foundation, a feature importance analysis was conducted within the random forest framework, and SHAP was applied to further interpret the results, thereby mitigating the potential bias of Gini-based importance toward continuous variables. The results demonstrated that TG/HDL-C ranked as the second most influential variable, following age, and its importance score was markedly higher than that of other conventional risk factors. Although age emerged as the strongest predictor, it primarily serves as a demographic benchmark—an established yet immutable risk factor with limited interventional relevance. In contrast, TG/HDL-C represents a biologically grounded and modifiable metabolic biomarker that reflects insulin resistance and lipid dysregulation. Its pronounced structural importance not only underscores its value for OA risk prediction and clinical translation but also provides a novel perspective for investigating metabolic mechanisms, thereby conferring innovative significance. Subgroup Analyses Revealing Heterogeneity in the TG/HDL-C–OA Association

Given the uneven distribution of TG/HDL-C across clinical subgroups, as shown in Table 5, we further evaluated whether these disparities translated into heterogeneity in OA risk. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex, diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease were conducted, with all models adjusted for age, BMI, and comorbidities to ensure robustness. Across all subgroups, the odds ratio (OR) for Q4 versus Q1 was significantly greater than 1 (all P < 0.01), indicating a consistent association between elevated TG/HDL-C and increased OA risk. Notably, among patients with diabetes, the risk was higher (OR = 2.496) than in non-diabetic individuals (OR = 1.873); similarly, women had a greater risk (OR = 2.556) compared with men (OR = 2.098). These findings suggest that sex and diabetes are not merely risk factors but may embody more complex effect patterns within the TG/HDL-C–OA relationship, contributing to differential risk profiles across populations. Metabolism-Dependent Nonlinearity of the TG/HDL-C–OA Association

Given that the preceding subgroup analyses indicated higher OA risk among women and individuals with diabetes in the TG/HDL-C–OA association, but the risk thresholds and specific nonlinear patterns remained unclear, we further evaluated the dose–response relationship within key metabolic strata. Specifically, we fitted restricted cubic spline (RCS) models in populations stratified by sex and diabetes status. In women, the risk curve showed an inflection point at approximately 1.46, whereas in men it occurred near 2.73, suggesting a lower threshold and greater susceptibility to TG/HDL-C elevation in females. Among non-diabetic individuals, TG/HDL-C was positively associated with OA risk, with an inflection around 1.56; by contrast, in diabetic individuals, the inflection appeared earlier at approximately 0.95 and then plateaued, indicating heightened metabolic sensitivity in this group. Collectively, these results highlight marked subgroup-specific heterogeneity: both women and individuals with diabetes exhibit lower risk thresholds and greater sensitivity to changes in TG/HDL-C, underscoring the role of metabolic dysfunction in OA pathogenesis and the importance of incorporating individualized metabolic profiles into clinical risk assessment, as presented in Figure 8. The model incorporated TG/HDL-C, age, sex, diabetes, BMI, APOA1/B, hypertension, and coronary heart disease.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the risk of OA, revealing that elevated TG/HDL-C levels are significantly and independently associated with increased risk of OA. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, each unit increase in the TG/HDL-C ratio was associated with an approximately 11% higher risk of OA. In the random forest model, TG/HDL-C ranked as the second most important predictor after age, surpassing traditional risk factors such as BMI, coronary heart disease, and hypertension, all of which had markedly lower importance scores. These findings highlight the TG/HDL-C ratio not only as a statistically significant independent risk factor but also as a key structural contributor in nonlinear modeling frameworks. This underscores its potential clinical utility and broad applicability in OA risk prediction. Moreover, our findings further support the advantage of machine learning approaches in capturing complex, nonlinear relationships within clinical data.34,35

Notably, this study revealed a metabolism-dependent pattern in the association between TG/HDL-C and OA risk. In sex-stratified analyses, the inflection point for TG/HDL-C was approximately 1.46 in females and 2.73 in males. In diabetes-stratified analyses, the inflection point was 1.56 in individuals without diabetes, while it shifted earlier to around 0.95 in those with diabetes, with a subsequent plateau in the risk curve. These differences suggest that the risk effect of TG/HDL-C may be modulated by endocrine factors (eg., sex hormones), insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolic abnormalities, with a more pronounced impact observed in females and individuals with diabetes. From a mechanistic perspective, an elevated TG/HDL-C ratio reflects underlying insulin resistance and dysregulation of lipid metabolism.36 Insulin resistance has been associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, which may be linked to synovial and cartilage damage, while high triglycerides and low HDL-C levels have been implicated in processes that may contribute to joint degeneration via oxidative stress and lipid deposition.37 In postmenopausal women, estrogen deficiency may exacerbate lipid-induced inflammation by activating NF-κB and IL-6 signaling pathways, thereby increasing OA susceptibility.38,39 Among individuals with diabetes, the pervasive presence of insulin resistance may impair intra-articular homeostasis even with modest elevations in TG/HDL-C, triggering an earlier threshold of OA risk. This aligns with the emerging concept of a “diabetic osteoarthritis” phenotype, which emphasizes the synergistic role of dysregulation of lipid metabolism and chronic inflammation in joint degeneration.40 These potential mechanisms may help explain the nonlinear nature of the TG/HDL-C–OA association and the observed heterogeneity across subpopulations.

Systematic investigations of the nonlinear relationship and potential threshold effects between TG/HDL-C and osteoarthritis (OA) remain limited, as previous studies have primarily relied on ROC analysis or logistic regression, focusing mainly on overall associations or discrimination.28 Similarly, the nonlinear relationship and threshold effects of individual lipid markers (eg., triglycerides or HDL-C) in relation to OA have not been well characterized, with existing evidence largely restricted to linear associations or overall risk assessment, without accounting for heterogeneity across metabolic contexts.32 In contrast, recent studies on insulin resistance-related metabolic indicators have expanded this field by demonstrating significant nonlinear associations and threshold effects in OA risk. These markers have been increasingly incorporated into OA risk assessment. For example, analyses based on NHANES data have shown that each unit increase in the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is associated with an approximately sevenfold increase in OA risk.41 In addition, related indicators—including TyG-derived indices, the lipid accumulation product (LAP), and the uric acid-to-HDL-C ratio (UHR)—have also been linked to OA risk. Evidence suggests that insulin resistance–related markers exert stronger effects at moderate to high levels,42 with risk plateauing at higher levels (eg., a threshold of approximately 131.16 for LAP43 and 0.109 for UHR).44 These findings collectively indicate that metabolic abnormalities may be associated with OA risk through nonlinear and threshold-dependent mechanisms. Building on this evidence, our study further demonstrates a similar nonlinear association between TG/HDL-C and OA risk, with thresholds varying across metabolic contexts: lower in females, earlier in individuals with diabetes, and plateauing at higher levels. This pattern differs from the single-threshold model typically reported in general populations, suggesting that the effect of TG/HDL-C may be modulated by endocrine and metabolic status, thereby resulting in stratified risk patterns.

These findings further highlight the critical role of lipid metabolic abnormalities in the development and progression of OA. Previous studies have shown that metabolic syndrome is associated with an increased risk of OA, with elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL-C identified as key contributing factors.45 Moreover, large population-based studies have linked specific lipid metabolic profiles and their remodeling patterns to the onset, progression, and prognosis of OA.8 However, these studies largely rely on linear models or single markers, limiting their ability to capture the complex relationship between lipid metabolism and OA. In contrast, our study integrates random forest and restricted cubic spline approaches to model TG/HDL-C across multiple levels, enabling more refined risk assessment across populations. Through feature importance analysis and interactive visualization, TG/HDL-C is further used as a key variable for individualized risk stratification, thereby enhancing the clinical interpretability and applicability of the model.

As an easily accessible and modifiable biomarker, the TG/HDL-C ratio has important clinical utility. It may help identify high-risk individuals—particularly women and patients with diabetes—within metabolically vulnerable populations, thereby supporting earlier screening and risk-oriented clinical assessment. In addition, TG/HDL-C levels can be improved through lifestyle modification or pharmacological therapies, including lipid-lowering agents and insulin sensitizers,46,47 supporting its role in risk-oriented metabolic management in OA. Previous studies have demonstrated that machine learning–based models are feasible and accurate for OA risk prediction in older populations.48 Building on this, our study incorporated TG/HDL-C into a personalized risk stratification model and developed an interactive tool using the R Shiny platform, which has been widely applied in medical prediction visualization.49

This tool captures nonlinear interactions among TG/HDL-C, age, sex, and diabetes status, revealing clustering patterns in females and diabetic individuals with elevated TG/HDL-C, and addressing the limitations of traditional models in representing multidimensional risk structures.50,51 Similar visualization-based approaches have shown value in chronic disease management, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer.52,53 Extending this paradigm to OA, our study provides a visually driven framework for personalized risk stratification with improved interpretability and applicability.54 The tool is particularly suitable for outpatient and primary care settings, where routine clinical data can be integrated to generate individualized risk estimates. By translating complex nonlinear and subgroup-specific patterns into interpretable outputs, it supports rapid risk stratification and patient-level risk communication. Future integration with electronic health record (EHR) systems may further enable automated data extraction and real-time risk estimation, facilitating the incorporation of individualized OA risk assessment into routine clinical workflows.

This study has several limitations. First, as this was a multicenter retrospective cross-sectional analysis conducted across three tertiary-care hospitals in Jiangsu, the findings still require validation in larger, prospective cohorts and in regions beyond Jiangsu to enhance external generalizability. Second, although a wide range of covariates were adjusted for, the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors—such as dietary habits, physical activity, and socioeconomic status—cannot be excluded. Incorporating these lifestyle-related variables in future research may help refine and extend the current findings. Lastly, the TG/HDL-C ratio was measured at a single time point, which may not fully reflect long-term lipid metabolic status; longitudinal monitoring could enhance the accuracy of risk assessment in future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that elevated TG/HDL-C ratios are independently associated with an increased risk of osteoarthritis. As a simple and modifiable biomarker, TG/HDL-C holds potential clinical value for early identification and risk stratification, particularly among women and individuals with diabetes. By integrating TG/HDL-C with demographic and clinical variables into a machine learning–based model, we developed a practical tool capable of improving comprehensive OA risk prediction, identifying subgroup-specific nonlinear risk patterns, and transforming complex interactions into individualized risk estimates. These findings underscore the critical role of metabolic dysregulation in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis and support the incorporation of lipid-related indicators into risk stratification strategies. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies are warranted to further establish causal relationships, refine risk thresholds, and facilitate clinical translation.
