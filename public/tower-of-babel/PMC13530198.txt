Explainable AI for diabetic retinopathy detection using vision transformers
Authors: Mustafizur Rahaman, Masrufa Akter Muni, Saima Tasnim, Sanjida Shahid Juthi, Rakibul Islam, Khandakar Rabbi Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe heterogeneous acquisition, variability of orientation, and subtle lesions continue to challenge the screening of diabetic retinopathy through color fundus photographs. We formulate DR grading as a binary triage task (No-DR vs DR) and propose the All-ViT Hybrid framework, integrating complementary pretrained transformer backbones within a stability-oriented training schedule (head-only warm-up, partial unfreezing), optimized using AdamW with OneCycle scheduling, class-weighted cross-entropy, and mixed precision. Preprocessing includes luminance-space CLAHE and retinal field-of-view masking, and data splitting is performed using stratified sampling to preserve class balance. We perform post-hoc threshold tuning and test-time augmentation (optional), which is available for operating-point control and robustness. Over the competitive baselines (ConvNeXt, MobileNetV3-Large, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet201), All-ViT Hybrid has an Accuracy of 0.9754, F1 of 0.9761, Precision of 0.9658, Recall of 0.9866, and measures of agreement κ of 0.9509, MCC of 0.9511, and Jaccard of 0.9532. Compared to the best baseline, F1 achieves a gain of +1.11 percentage points, an improvement in accuracy of +1.13, and a recall improvement of +0.81 percentage points without compromising controllable precision and these experiments were conducted on the APTOS 2019 Blindness Detection dataset. These visualizations provide qualitative interpretability and are not quantitatively validated against lesion-level annotations due to dataset limitations. These findings suggest that integrating complementary transformer representations within a unified fusion pipeline provides strong and threshold-adjustable performance for DR triage under real-world variability. The framework remains modular and extensible, with potential applicability to larger input resolutions, multi-class grading, and multi-modal clinical settings. Introduction

The diabetic retinopathy (DR) may be regarded as one of the most frequent micro-vascular cripples of diabetes and a major cause of avoidable blindness globally1,2. The syndrome consists of four stages during which microaneurysms do not become symptomatic3,4; however, eventually turn into hematoma, exude, and form neovascularization resulting in permanent loss of vision, which cannot be reversible, unless they are treated in time5,6. That early detection should therefore be done through regular screening, however, hand screening to examine the retina images of the fundus is not only time consuming but also expensive and it is prone to inter-observer bias7,8. There exists a grave need, based on these challenges, of computer-aided and automated diagnostic systems that could aid with the screening of diabetic retinopathy in masses to rely on the outcomes9.

In recent years, deep learning (DL) has transformed medical image analysis by achieving state-of-the-art results in disease classification. and lesion detection, and segmentation, and many more applications10. The use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in detecting DR as well as models like DenseNet, EfficientNet, and ConvNeXt, has been widely researched and demonstrates high potential in various cases of retinal image detection11–13. These models are outstanding at extracting discriminative local features and therefore, they are able to retrieve the retinal patterns like microaneurysms and hemorrhages.14. Although this is a good sign but CNN-based models mainly depend on their receptive fields along with convolutional kernels and that destroy their ability to observe long-range retina relations15. This has been one of the main issues that can be a problem with the diabetic retinopathy (DR), since minimal pathological indicators are distributed all over the world to different regions of the retina16. And also because of CNNs showing their black box nature which offer limited transparency in decision-making, as a result it creates a major obstacle for clinical implementation where interpretability is as important as accuracy17.

In recent times researchers are moving toward transformer based models to mitigate these challenges, because transformers use self-attention mechanisms to capture global contextual relationships within images18. Data-efficient Transformers (DeiT), Vision Transformers (ViT), and Bidirectional Encoder Image Transformer (BEiT) are well suited in retinal disease analysis due to their better performance on tasks in computer vision19. But for the clinical purpose the accuracy is not a sufficient quality to consider, but interpretability is also necessary. Attention rollout and token-gradient Class Activation Mapping (CAM) explainable AI (XAI) methods have become state-of-the-art methods to be capable of giving visual interpretations to the model output and define the most relevant areas of the retina that influence the results of decisions made by the model20. These approaches close the gap between algorithmic outputs and clinical reasoning, providing a way for clear and reliable AI-assisted diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection.

This paper describes an explainable AI model employed to identify diabetic retinopathy using Vision Transformers. Our approach incorporates hybrid transformer models (ViT, DeiT, BEiT) with convolutional baselines (ConvNeXt, EfficientNet, MobileNetV3, DenseNet201) to systematically analyze classification performance. To make the image quality better in the dataset, we have employed preprocessing techniques such as Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), which improves local contrast and highlights minor impairments. We have also integrated the explainability methods, which are attention rollout and token-gradient Class Activation Mapping (CAM), to obtain a visual representation of the model’s prediction. These methods especially construct heatmaps that display the affected areas of the retina, thereby lessening the gap between the output of algorithms and the interpretation by clinicians.

Literature review

In recent DR literature, hand-crafted features have since been replaced by deep CNNs, large ensembles, and Vision Transformers, which encourage long-range retinal context. Recent achievements in self-supervised/Masked-Image-Modeling pretraining, solutions inspired by class imbalance, and uncertainty/thresholding have made models more robust and clinical. Nevertheless, there are still problems: heterogeneous acquisition, label noise, and operating-point control. In our review, hybrid transformer fusion lies between these strands, underlining the diversity of reporting and deployment-related dependability.

Vijay, Krishnan, and Anjali21 proposed an integrated Swin Transformer-based framework that combines attention based on hierarchies to capture both multi-scale pathological information, classifies diabetic retinopathy severity on retinal fundus images, followed by the help of York Rite dots to aid transparency in decision-making, whilst retaining high levels of predictive accuracy based on the Messidor dataset. It has 88.46% accuracy in severe cases and 95.83% overall accuracy in 4 levels of severe cases. Nevertheless, further validation in different clinical environments and groups of people is required to ensure generalizability and clinical utility.

Alavee et al.22 designed a CNN-based diabetic retinopathy-detection framework that is compared against baselines of transfer-learning (DenseNet121, Xception, Resnet50, VGG16/19, InceptionV3) and classical models (SVM, RNN) on the varied diabetic retinopathy in retinal images on multi-label softmax and categorical cross-entropy loss functions, and combined with Grad-CAM as interpretable alternatives. The approach achieves 95.27% and 100% accuracy on binary tasks, outperforming Xception at 82% on the same dataset. Nevertheless, further assessment on various datasets and clinical circumstances or setups is required to validate the findings with others, as well as prevent overfitting behind nearly flawless scores.

Shahzad et al.23 proposed a DR-diagnosis model based on XAI, in order to categorize retinal fundus pictures and generate intelligible rationale to support the clinician; experimentation is conducted with retinal pictures (no details given regarding the dataset). This method adds greater transparency through explainable outputs and learning-curve validation with a reported 94% diagnostic accuracy and lower loss than conventional techniques. Nonetheless, due to the lack of dataset disclosure and multi-center external validation, which generalizes it, it should be considerably expanded in the scope of clinical testing.

Manoranjitham and Dev24 introduced A DR-assessed structure of Multiple Instance Learning and Vision Transformers that classifies fundus images and adds further former YOLOv8 grouping lesions (microaneurysms, hemorrhages, exudates) and EigenCAM, giving understandable heatmaps; results have not been specified, and the system is executable through an interface on Streamlit to allow real-time applications. It is reported to have 93.09% accuracy and an AUC-ROC of 0.96% (EfficientNetB0), which is better than the traditional CNN baselines. Nevertheless, the lack of information about the data sets and external multi-center validation affects their generalizability and reliability to clinical settings, so wider testing in different settings and populations should be conducted.

Refat et al.25 designed a hybrid deep learning (VR-FuseNet) that comprises VGG19 and ResNet50v2 that predicts diabetic retinopathy using retinal fundus images, which has been trained on a hybrid dataset consolidated across five publicly available DR databases using SMOTE enhanced against class imbalances and structured using CLAHE to rectify image variation issues. This method incorporates a curated multi-source dataset, a complementary features re-sample-based fusion architecture, and XAI interpretations that highlight lesions (microaneurysms, hemorrhages, exudates), providing clinical interpretability with an accuracy of 91.824% compared to the underlying backbones. Nonetheless, there is a need to conduct generalizability and clinical utility validation in a more exhaustive, multi-centre-based study, with more specific per-class measures and assessments in real clinical work environments.

Elsharkawy et al.26 proposed AETNet-fused atlas-guided segmentation of 11 OCT retinal layers using handcrafted biomarkers (reflectivity, Laplacian-based thickness, and tortuosity at a boundary), which improves, compresses them using variational autoencoders, and an auto-policymaker named lightweight transformer attention classifier validated on OCT scans of 481 subjects. It provides a reasonably sized, interpretable pipeline that models cross-layer dependencies, achieving adversarial accuracy of 93.08% ± 4.21, 93.33% ± 4.84 96.00% ± 5.96, 94.48% ± 3.21, precision, recall, F1, and understandability, respectively, surpassing even conventional ML and deep learning baselines. Nevertheless, external validation by multiple centres and the ability to resist segmentation errors and domain drift remain to be proven.

Rezaee27 designed a CNN Transformer model, which identifies DRs and scores the severity of perceptual change by learning local features with high-resolution images, both on and beyond the patch-centric feature, is found when trained on an augmented APTOS 2019 dataset (addressing class imbalance) and evaluated on IDRiD for five image types and four, finely tuned, severity levels. It shows 94.28% APTOS and 95.23% on IDRiD accuracies using standard measures (AUC, specificity, and sensitivity), indicating high generalizability. What is required, however, is a bigger multi-center, prospective appraisal and more transparent provisions of interpretability to establish clinical utility.

Deo et al.28 designed an XAI-based DR diagnosis model that combines Grad-CAM++ to provide an interpretation of visual logic over retinal images, which are considered interpretable. Convergence plots are utilized to substantiate learning; however, no details of the dataset are provided. The method enhances clinician trust by promoting transparency and reducing bias, as expert reporting is interpreted in conjunction with other sources, resulting in a 94.4% diagnostic accuracy rate. Nonetheless, the provenance of data sets and the generalizability and clinical reliability are to be proved due to the lack of external and multi-centric validation.

Saleh et al.29 proposed an OCT-based classifier is a transformer-based classifier, where each scan is subdivided into 11 layers of the retina, using an atlas approach after which per-layer morphological/texture features (curvature, thickness, reflectivity) are extracted, and per-label predictions are made, which are then aggregated by majority vote to identify DR/AMD: it is trained/tested on 500 scans of 150 patients (Heidelberg Spectralis, Zeiss Cirrus; 350/150 split). It provides layer-preserving accuracy, is interpretable, and decision-wise, with 93.68% accuracy, a Cohen’s kappa of 91.07, and an F1 score of 93.12. The natural sample size is moderate; however, it has not been externally validated across multiple centers and may be sensitive to segmentation errors, which restricts its ability for generalization.

RetinoDeep, suggested by Kansal et al.30, is a hybrid DR classifier comprising EfficientNetB0+SPCL transformer, ResNet50+Bi-LSTM, GA/PSO-optimized Bi-LSTM, and a Shap-explained Bi-LSTM. These models are trained on an augmented, curated fundus dataset of 757 images and then evaluated against strong CNN/transformer/RNN baselines. It provides scalable and interpretable screening using optimized hybrids and SHAP views in concordance with retinal biomarkers. The PSO-optimized Bi-LSTM achieves the lowest stability and best scores in terms of accuracy/F1/precision. Nevertheless, the size of the augmented dataset is small, and there is no multi-center validation external to consequences, hence limiting generalizability and clinical readiness.

Ahmed and Uddin31 developed OcuViT, a ViT-based transfer learning (a transfer-learned version of ViT-Base-Patch16-224) with a simplified preprocessing/tensorization pipeline, which transfers ViT-Base-Patch16-224 through binary or five-class transfer of DR and AMD, applied to retinal fundus data at APTOS (binary and five-class transfer) and the IChallenge-AMD dataset. It optimally scales ViT across ophthalmic images, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy and making significant contributions to CNN/ViT baselines on five-class DR and AMD, as well as competitive binary-DR. However, in the absence of granular measures, external multi-center validation, and a measure of computational resource cost and domain conditioning, generalizability and clinical deployability can be established only with limited assertions.

Dong et al.32 designed a multimodal system, A transformer-based multimodal system (Trans-MUF), combining fundus-based images with clinical properties, that classifies diabetic nephropathy in comparison to non-diabetic renal disease, trained on an 87-95 consumer signature dataset based on an internal retrospective cohort and evaluated using prospective and multimodal cross-machine cross-validation. It adds a non-invasive solution that incorporates clinician-assistant visualizations. Conclusion, AUC 0.980 (internal), 0.989 (prospective), and 0.932 (multicenter), with a 21% improvement in accuracy through collaboration with the nephrologist. Nevertheless, there is a requirement for randomized prospective impact studies and more extensive demographic coverage and workflow deployment studies to verify the real-life usefulness.

Amna and Azhar33 proposed a hybrid framework, ResViT FusionNet, that was combined with ResNet50 for feature extraction and Vision Transformers for capturing long-range dependencies, used to classify multiclass diabetic retinopathy on retinal fundus images, with data balancing and augmentation applied alongside explainability through LIME and Grad-CAM. The dataset consisted of preprocessed and augmented multiclass fundus images. The model achieved superior results compared to standard CNNs and ViTs, with 93.01% accuracy, 93.07% precision, 93.00% recall, 92.75% F1-score, 0.8944 MCC, 0.8935 Kappa, and 0.8749 Jaccard Index. However, further external validation on diverse clinical datasets and real-world patient groups is necessary to establish its generalizability and clinical applicability.

Al Shaf et al.34 proposed a transformer-based knowledge distillation (KD) framework for diabetic retinopathy detection, addressing class imbalance in retinal fundus images by combining deep learning models with data balancing strategies such as cost-sensitive learning and augmentation. The APTOS dataset of RGB fundus images was used, with models including ResNet34, DenseNet121, GoogLeNet, MobileViTv2, DeiT3, and KD applied for five-class DR classification. MobileViTv2 with cost-sensitive learning achieved the best performance, with a kappa score of 0.94 and macro F1 of 0.73, while KD using MobileViTv2 as teacher and GoogLeNet as student achieved a WKS of 0.90 with fewer parameters. However, further evaluation on diverse datasets and real-world clinical environments is required to validate the robustness and generalizability of the proposed framework.

Ahnaf Alavee et al.35 proposed a CNN-based framework for diabetic retinopathy detection, evaluated alongside transfer learning models including DenseNet121, Xception, ResNet50, VGG16, VGG19, InceptionV3, machine learning models (SVM), and neural network models (RNN) for both binary and multi-class classification. The dataset consisted of retinal fundus images, with experiments applying multi-label classification using softmax and categorical cross-entropy. Study results showed that Xception achieved 82% accuracy, while the proposed CNN model outperformed all benchmarks with 95.27% accuracy in multi-class tasks and perfect accuracy in binary classification. Nevertheless, despite strong performance, further validation across diverse datasets and clinical environments is needed to establish robustness, generalizability, and practical clinical applicability.

Shahzad et al.36 proposed an explainable AI based diagnosis model for detecting diabetic retinopathy, aiming to overcome the limitations of traditional AI black-box models by enhancing interpretability and providing transparent decision-making support for clinicians. The study utilized retinal fundus images (dataset not explicitly specified) to train and validate the proposed model, with convergence history plots confirming stable learning and reliability. The model achieved 94% diagnostic accuracy, surpassing traditional methods while improving interpretability and applicability in clinical practice. However, despite the promising results, further testing on diverse datasets and real-world clinical environments is needed to confirm its robustness, generalizability, and practical utility.

Lalithadevi and Krishnaveni37 proposed an OptiDex model for diabetic retinopathy detection and severity classification, combining a deep CNN framework with an Explainable AI module for transparent decision-making and an enhanced cat swarm optimization (ECSO) algorithm to optimize network parameters. The model was evaluated on both clinical datasets from SRM Hospital and public datasets including APTOS, IDRiD, and Messidor, with segmentation achieved through morphological operations, hysteresis thresholding, and Nas-Mob feature extraction. Experimental results showed high performance, achieving 97.65% accuracy, 96.46% sensitivity, and 93.45% specificity. Nevertheless, despite the strong outcomes, broader validation across diverse populations and clinical environments is required to establish robustness, generalizability, and scalability of the OptiDex framework.

Yurdakul et al.38 proposed a Global Context Vision Transformer (ROPGCViT) model for the diagnosis of Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP) in fundus images, enhanced with Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks and Residual Multilayer Perceptron (RMLP) structures to capture both local and global contextual information. The model was evaluated on a dataset of 1,099 fundus images and compared against 50 deep learning models (25 CNNs and 25 ViTs). ROPGCViT achieved superior performance with 94.69% accuracy, 94.84% precision, 94.69% recall, 94.60% F1-score, and a Cohen’s kappa of 93.10%, while Grad-CAM visualizations provided interpretability by highlighting clinically relevant regions. However, further validation on larger, multi-center datasets is necessary to confirm the model’s robustness, generalizability, and practical applicability in diverse clinical settings.

Hussain et al.39 proposed a hybrid deep learning model, EFFResNet-ViT, to enhance medical image classification by combining EfficientNet-B0 and ResNet-50 CNN backbones with a Vision Transformer (ViT) module, integrating local feature extraction with global context modeling. The architecture employs feature fusion, a post-transformer CNN, and global average pooling, while Grad-CAM and t-SNE visualizations provide interpretability. The model was evaluated on two benchmark datasets: brain tumor (BT) CE-MRI and a retinal image dataset for ophthalmological diagnosis. EFFResNet-ViT achieved 99.31% accuracy on BT CE-MRI and 92.54% accuracy on the retinal dataset, outperforming existing CNN and ViT-based hybrid models. However, further testing on larger, diverse clinical datasets is necessary to confirm its robustness, generalizability, and practical applicability in real-world medical settings.

Methodology

In this section, we have presented diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening as a binary detection task: No-DR vs. DR, using high-intensity fundus images in subject-paired left/ right fields. We eliminate cross-eye contamination and retain prevalence distributions through carefully managed, meticulous data management with image-level stratified split. The extraction of features is performed through a hybrid transformer that combines complementary representations from ViT, DeiT, and BEiT, which are then fused by a lightweight attention-based head. Training is performed on a stability-centered regimen. Initially, there is a head-only warm-up, followed by the selective unfreezing of a layer of the last transformers. It uses class-weighted cross-entity optimization with AdamW mixed with one cycle LR. The choice of model is based on the performance of the validation, and post-hoc threshold tuning to clinically relevant operating points should be considered. Dataset description

We used the publicly available APTOS 2019 Blindness Detection dataset from Kaggle (https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/aptos2019-blindness-detection/data, accessed March 2025). The dataset consists of 3661 high-resolution retinal fundus images annotated for diabetic retinopathy severity on a five-level scale (0–4).

We use a large set of high-resolution color fundus pictures; also, each subject includes a pair of right and left fields. Each image in the dataset was originally graded according to the following five-class diabetic retinopathy scale: 0 (No DR), 1 (Mild), 2 (Moderate), 3 (Severe), and 4 (Proliferative DR). Clinicians initially rated the images using a five-tier diastolic retinopathy (DR) scale (0: No DR; 1-4: Mild to Proliferative), and for the purposes of this study, the five-class labels were reformulated into a binary screening task: No-DR (grade 0) versus DR (grades 1-4). The binary formulation is aligned with real-world screening settings, where the primary goal is to identify referable DR cases rather than perform fine-grained severity grading

The image collection comprises various camera models and optical settings, resulting in significant heterogeneity in color balance, illumination, field of view, and orientation (anatomical versus inverted), all of which can be determined by the geometries of the macula and optic disc, or the presence of the peripheral notch. In the world, artefacts are not uncommon, and these can be blur, under-exposure, over-exposure, glare, eyelid occlusions, eyelash occlusions, dust, and occasional label noise.

The dataset was divided into 80% training and 20% validation subsets using stratified sampling based on the diagnosis labels to preserve class distribution. This resulted in 2928 training images and 733 validation images. Our partitioning is stratified to avoid information leakage and replicate clinical prevalence; the training data provide metadata and ground truth (Fig. 2).

Image quality control.We performed initial integrity screening to remove unreadable or severely corrupted images prior to downstream processing. Files that failed to decode, exhibited zero or near-zero dimensions, or contained truncated JPEG markers were excluded. We additionally enforced a minimum spatial resolution (shorter side >256 px) to ensure sufficient retinal detail for feature learning and to avoid instability in batching.

The anatomical cropping (circular retinal mask).To reduce peripheral non-retinal background and acquisition-related border artifacts (e.g., eyelids, imaging boundaries), we estimate the retinal field-of-view using grayscale conversion, Gaussian smoothing, thresholding, and morphological closing. The largest connected component is identified to approximate the retinal disk, from which an inscribed circular region is extracted. Pixels beyond a conservative radius threshold (95% of the inscribed circle) are attenuated to suppress peripheral artifacts while preserving retinal content. This step is intended to reduce background variability without altering central retinal structures. We acknowledge that a formal sensitivity analysis of the masking radius would further clarify its quantitative impact and consider this an area for future investigation.

Backbone selection rationale

The selection of ViT-S/16, DeiT-S/16, and BEiT-B/16 was motivated by architectural compatibility and complementary pretraining paradigms. All three backbones employ a 16±16 patch tokenization scheme, ensuring consistent spatial granularity and facilitating feature-level fusion without requiring structural modification. From a representation-learning perspective, the chosen models reflect three distinct training paradigms. ViT-S/16 represents the standard supervised transformer baseline trained on large-scale labeled datasets. DeiT-S/16 incorporates knowledge distillation to improve data efficiency and optimization stability. BEiT-B/16 is pretrained using masked image modeling, enabling self-supervised representation learning that captures contextual semantics.

By integrating these complementary paradigms, the hybrid framework aims to leverage diverse inductive biases and representation characteristics, which may be beneficial under heterogeneous fundus imaging conditions involving variable illumination, acquisition devices, and subtle lesion patterns.

Hardware and software configuration

All experiments in this study were conducted using Google Colab Pro. Nvidia A100 GPU (40 GB VRAM) was provided to the project, which provided the project with the required power to efficiently train the deep learning models on the high-resolution data (fundus). The selection of A100 did provide faster convergence and allowed to use of mixed precision training, to reduce the training time and memory overhead. On the software side, the software has been written in Python 3.10 using PyTorch (torch and torchvision) deep learning software as the main deep learning library. The timm library was very useful for importing state-of-the-art architectures such as ConvNeXt, ViT, DeiT and BEiT. Data augmentation and preprocessing pipelines implemented using Albumentations and OpenCV, using scikit-learn for evaluation metrics and stratified splitting, and analysis of the ROC Curve. For creating interpretive figures for visualization tasks such as attention rollout and token-gradient CAM heatmaps, we used Matplotlib and Seaborn. Additionally, NumPy and Pandas were used for handling the data and logging the experiments. This combination of powerful hardware and optimized software sui generis ensured that the experiments were computationally feasible as well as reproducible, to meet the rigorous standards expected for clinical-grade research in explainable diabetic retinopathy detection experiments.

These heatmaps identify the retinal regions that contribute the most to model predictions and are therefore crucial to understand how the network processes different subtle pathological indications. This implementable explainability not only justifies the decision-making process of the model but also contributes to clinical trust, which is understanding obtained through explainable AI in medical imaging.

The interpretabilities in Fig. 17 show the visualisation of the proposed All-ViT Hybrid model with the help of token-level Grad-CAM. Separate heatmaps were generated for the backbones of the ViT, DeiT, and BEiT architectures, followed by their aggregated mean in a hybrid. Each visualization reveals the regions of the retina that had the highest input in the decisions of the model and thus provides direct insight into the way that transformer-based architectures for diabetic retinopathy detection reason internally.

Token-level Grad-CAM visualizations from ViT, DeiT, BEiT, and the All-ViT Hybrid highlighting lesion-focused regions in diabetic retinopathy detection.

The individual backbone explanation might indicate that ViT, DeiT, and BEiT are targeting clinically relevant areas like the macula, optic disc, and vascular regions where the first pathological signs usually appear. However, each backbone has a slightly different focus - while ViT is focused on gathering global context features, DeiT is focused on fine vascular features, and BEiT is focused on strong attention around the optic disc. This variety of attention patterns shows the complementary virtues of each of the three transformers. Importantly, aggregated attention distributions within a more coherent and stable heatmap can be computed by the aggregated All-ViT Hybrid visualization (bottom right). The hybrid explanation is not only good for reducing noise, but it can also give us sharper localization around lesion-related areas like microaneurysms and hemorrhages. This fused interpretability is a powerful argument for the quantitative superiority of the model because it demonstrates that its high accuracy is not brought about through accidental correlations, but via clinically meaningful reasoning.

In a real-life screening setting, these visualizations provide open knowledge in how the predictions come to be, thereby maintaining the trustworthiness of All-ViT Hybrid. The ability to highlight the relevant retinal regions to the disease is an aligned model with the clinical interpretation of expert doctors, thereby fulfilling the model of explainable AI, ensuring the potential for safe deployments would be used in diabetic retinopathy detection.

Comparative analysis and discussion

Table 5 presents the comparative performances of the currently available methods and our proposed model for diabetic retinopathy detection. Ikram and Imran 33 proposed ResViT FusionNet on fundus images of the retina for multiclass DR with 93.01% accuracy. Their hybrid approach of using convolutional layers alongside transformers highlighted the value of using multi-level feature extraction, but the performance was still lower than models focused purely on transformers. Vijay et al. 21 applied Swin Transformers on the Messidor dataset, giving an accuracy of 95.83%. The hierarchical attention mechanism successfully extracted multiscale features and in turn could detect the presence of lesions more effectively. Balaha et al. 40 used Vision Transformers (ViT) for several fundus datasets, achieving 95.00% accuracy, implying that pure transformer pipelines have the capability for robust retinal image analysis. Shahzad et al. 23 applied CNN on the Kaggle DR dataset, resulting in 94.00% accuracy but demonstrating the well-known limitation of CNNs in modeling global dependencies. Jamshidiha et al. 41, by merging patch encoders and transformer layers, proposed Retformer, showing an accuracy range of 92-94% on OCT and fundus datasets. While this indicates stable performance, it was less powerful in predictive capability compared with other transformer models. Alavee et al. 22 postulated a Custom CNN trained on APTOS 2019, Messidor2, and IDRiD datasets to achieve 95.27% accuracy, which highlights the advantage of dataset diversity and the depth of CNN architectures.

By contrast, our proposed All-ViT Hybrid model, trained on the APTOS 2019 dataset only, was superior to all the baseline studies with an accuracy of 97.54%. The integration of ViT, DeiT, and BEiT as backbones provides hybrid fusion and is supported by the attention rollout and token-level Grad-CAM ensures better predictive capability. Conclusion

We proposed All-ViT Hybrid, a feature-level fusion system that combines complementary inductive biases among ViT, DeiT, and BEiT using a small classification head and a stability-focused first-to-final equivalent training system (head-only warm-up, partially unfreezing, AdamW+OneCycle, class-weighted loss, mixed precision). This pipeline features subject-wise evaluation and leakage-conscious data processing, including threshold-tunable decision-making, allowing for clinically meaningful operating points in DR triage while maintaining computationally tractable operation points. Combining heterogeneous transformer pretraining goals helps improve representational diversity, better differentiate No-DR and DR, and is resilient to device, orientation, and acquisition variance. Optional test-time inversion additionally stabilizes predictions. The study offers a blueprint of transformer fusion that the reader can apply to retina screenings, as well as a solid approach to transformer fusion (a methodological replication, schedule, and threshold choice) that can be easily translated into practice. This study has some limitations. The evaluation was conducted on a single dataset with internal validation only, and external multi-center testing was not performed. The task was restricted to binary DR triage rather than fine-grained multi-class grading. In addition, the potential sensitivity to preprocessing steps (e.g., CLAHE and circular masking) and the absence of formal probability calibration analysis should be considered. While attention-based visualizations provide qualitative interpretability, no quantitative validation against lesion annotations or clinician-confirmed correspondence was performed. Future work will address external validation, calibration assessment, preprocessing robustness, explainability validation, and extension to multi-class and multi-modal settings. Also, future work will include quantitative validation using annotated datasets (e.g., IDRiD) with localization metrics In general, the All-ViT Hybrid provides a solid and implementable basis on which to build a dependable and practical system for screening diabetic retinopathy.
