An association between epicardial adipose tissue and prognosis in acute myocardial infarction: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Wang, Nengzong Deng, Yuan Huang, Ziming Liu, Hong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality worldwide. Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), a metabolically and inflammatorily active visceral fat depot, has been increasingly recognized for its potential role in cardiovascular risk stratification. However, its prognostic significance in patients with AMI remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between EAT and clinical outcomes in AMI.MethodsThis meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were systematically searched up to October 16, 2025. Studies reporting quantitative EAT assessment [e.g., EAT thickness, volume, or density measured by echocardiography, computed tomography (CT), or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)] and outcomes in AMI patients were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool. Hazard ratios (HRs) were pooled for time-to-event outcomes, including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). For continuous outcomes, weighted mean differences (WMDs) were applied when measurements were consistent across studies [e.g., left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)], whereas standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used when measurement scales differed (e.g., infarct size). Random-effects models were applied in the presence of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.ResultsA total of 10 studies involving 2,772 patients were included. Higher EAT was significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE [HR 1.96, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.47–2.63], particularly in studies with follow-up duration ≤12 months. No significant association was observed between EAT and LVEF (WMD −0.04, 95% CI: −2.47 to 2.38). Similarly, EAT was not significantly associated with infarct size (SMD 0.23, 95% CI: −0.38 to 0.84), although substantial heterogeneity was present across studies.ConclusionsElevated EAT is significantly associated with an increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes in patients with AMI, particularly MACE, suggesting its role as a marker of metabolic and inflammatory burden. However, its association with LVEF and infarct size remains inconclusive. Further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to clarify the clinical utility of EAT in AMI risk stratification.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this meta-analysis of 10 observational studies including 2,772 patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), increased epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) was significantly associated with a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (hazard ratio 1.96, 95% confidence interval: 1.47–2.63). No significant association was observed between EAT and left ventricular ejection fraction or infarct size.

What is known and what is new?

• EAT is a metabolically active visceral fat depot linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes in coronary artery disease. However, its prognostic significance specifically in AMI remains uncertain, with inconsistent findings across small observational studies.

• This is the first meta-analysis focusing exclusively on AMI patients, demonstrating that elevated EAT is consistently associated with increased post-AMI MACE risk, particularly in the early post-infarction phase, while showing no clear relationship with acute myocardial injury markers.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• EAT may serve as a non-invasive imaging marker reflecting local metabolic–inflammatory burden rather than acute infarct severity. Incorporating EAT assessment into post-AMI risk stratification models may improve early prognostic evaluation. Future prospective studies should standardize EAT measurement and explore its role as a potentially modifiable therapeutic target.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains one of the leading causes of cardiovascular death globally (1). Patient survival has been greatly improved due to recent optimizations in reperfusion therapy and secondary prevention of coronary artery disease. However, a high proportion of patients still develop post-discharge major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (e.g., heart failure, recurrent infarction, and cardiac death) (2-5). Available assessment tools for AMI risk, such as the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE), Synergy Between Percutaneous Coronary Intervention with Taxus and Cardiac Surgery (SYNTAX), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), primarily rely on biochemical markers, clinical characteristics, and cardiac function parameters. They are valuable for overall risk stratification, but remain less capable of reflecting local inflammatory burden and metabolic status (6). This underscores the need to explore novel imaging parameters or biomarkers that can complement traditional assessment systems, thereby further optimizing risk stratification and long-term management of AMI.

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) has garnered great attention recently as a distinct type of visceral adipose tissue. Located between the myocardium and visceral layer of the pericardium, EAT lacks a fascial barrier but shares the coronary microcirculatory system with the myocardium (7-9). Under physiological conditions, EAT may protect the heart by supplying local energy and secreting anti-inflammatory adipokines (7,8,10). In AMI, characterized by severe ischemia, inflammatory activation, and metabolic stress, however, the biological functions of EAT may undergo significant changes. Specifically, its protective effect may diminish or even be reversed (10-12). Observational studies on AMI patients have suggested that EAT may be associated with multiple clinical outcomes in the case of acute ischemia and metabolic stress. However, both endpoints and emphases vary across studies, which prevents a unified understanding (13-16).

Previous relevant studies were single-center investigations with limited sample sizes and great heterogeneity in study design, EAT assessment methods, and follow-up outcomes (15-18). Therefore, current evidence is insufficient to fully elucidate the clinical significance of EAT in AMI prognosis. Moreover, previous findings were scattered and inconsistent, and systematic quantitative assessments regarding the relationship between EAT and AMI prognosis [particularly key outcomes such as MACE, LVEF, and infarct size (IS)] are still lacking. By integrating the available evidence, therefore, this study intends to quantitatively assess the strength and direction of the association of EAT-related imaging parameters with AMI prognosis and further clarify the potential clinical value of EAT in AMI risk stratification and prognostic evaluation. The findings are expected to present a more robust evidence-based basis for clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1164/rc) (19).

Methods

Study design and registration

The protocol of this study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251173800).

Data sources and search strategy

We systematically searched Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library up to October 16, 2025. A combination of subject terms and free-text terms, including “Myocardial Infarction”, “Epicardial Adipose Tissue”, “Heart Infarction”, and “Epicardial Fat”, was utilized. No restrictions were applied regarding language or publication type (Appendix 1).

Study selection

Study selection was performed independently by two reviewers (J.W. and H.Z.), and the results were reviewed by a third reviewer (Y.H.). Inclusion criteria: (I) Population: adult patients with AMI; (II) Intervention: Quantitative EAT assessment (e.g., thickness, volume, or density); (III) Comparator: Different levels or stratifications of EAT, with definitions of elevated EAT and corresponding cut-off values determined according to individual study-specific methodologies and imaging modalities; (IV) Outcomes: MACE, LVEF, or IS; and (V) Study design: cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional studies. Exclusion criteria: (I) non-original studies (reviews, systematic reviews, case reports, and conference summaries); (II) No reporting of quantitative EAT assessment or primary outcomes; (III) duplicate publications, overlapping data, or unavailable valid data; (IV) non-AMI or mixed cohorts where AMI subgroup data could not be separately extracted; and (V) extremely small sample sizes (e.g., <10 cases).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (J.W. and H.Z.), and a third reviewer (Y.H.) reviewed the results and resolved discrepancies. Using a pre-designed standardized spreadsheet, the following data were extracted: (I) basic information: first author, country/region, and publication year; (II) subject characteristics: sample size, male proportion, mean age, AMI type, and EAT assessment method; (III) outcomes: MACE, LVEF and IS (LVEF and IS were defined as acute-phase parameters, typically measured during index hospitalization or shortly after reperfusion, reflecting early myocardial injury rather than long-term cardiac remodeling. Detailed information regarding imaging modality, measurement construct/unit, and assessment timing for studies reporting LVEF and/or IS is provided in Table S1); (IV) effect sizes and statistics: hazard ratio (HR), odds ratio (OR), or risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for dichotomous data (e.g., MACE), noting whether confounders were adjusted. Where feasible, multivariable-adjusted HRs were prioritized for pooled analyses. Continuous data (e.g., LVEF and IS) were presented by mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR).

Risk of bias assessment

Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (J.W. and H.Z.), and a third reviewer (Y.H.) reviewed the results and resolved discrepancies. Study quality was assessed using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies. This tool assesses key methodological domains, including: (I) clarity of study objectives and population definition; (II) accuracy and consistency of exposure and outcome measurements; (III) control of confounding factors; and (IV) completeness of follow-up and transparency of data reporting. Each item was rated as “Yes”, “No”, or “Unclear” according to NIH guidance. Based on the overall methodological rigor, studies were categorized as good, fair, or poor quality. Assessment results were used to describe the overall methodological quality of included studies and to inform the interpretation of findings.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

When continuous data (e.g., LVEF, IS) were reported by median and IQR, they were converted to mean and standard deviation using a previously validated method (20,21). For continuous outcomes, the weighted mean difference (WMD) was used when measurement units were consistent across studies (e.g., LVEF, reported as percentage), whereas the standardized mean difference (SMD) was applied when outcome measurements were reported using different methods or scales (e.g., IS). HR served as the primary pooled effect size for time-to-event outcomes (e.g., MACE). When HR was not reported, RR or OR served as approximate estimates of time-dependent risk effects for exploratory pooled analyses, provided event rates were low. All effect sizes (HRs/ORs/RRs) underwent log transformation prior to pooling to stabilize variances and approximate normal distributions. Effect estimates were combined using inverse-variance weighting.

Stata 17.0 was utilized. Cochran Q and I2 statistics were adopted for inter-study heterogeneity, and a fixed-effects or random-effects model was applied in the case of low (I2≤50% or P>0.10) or high heterogeneity (I2>50%), respectively. The source of heterogeneity was investigated by subgroup analyses [EAT assessment method, age, body mass index (BMI), and AMI type] and sensitivity analyses. Given that fewer than 10 studies were available per outcome, publication bias was assessed qualitatively by visual inspection of funnel plots and interpreted as exploratory. Formal statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry and trim-and-fill analyses were not performed.

Results

Selection results

Initially, 1,554 relevant studies were retrieved. After 284 duplicates were removed, the remaining 1,270 studies were reviewed for the title and abstract. After 1,171 were excluded due to inappropriate population, study topic, or study design, the remaining 99 studies underwent full-text examination. Then, 89 studies were excluded primarily due to no quantitative EAT data, no outcomes of interest, and non-AMI or mixed populations. Ultimately, 10 studies were included (Figure 1) (13-18,22-25).

Study characteristics and risk of bias

As shown in Table 1, most of the included observational studies were prospective cohort studies. The population primarily consisted of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non-STEMI patients, and EAT was assessed via echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), or computed tomography (CT). The included studies primarily evaluated EAT thickness or volume, while no eligible studies assessing EAT density met the inclusion criteria for quantitative synthesis. The primary outcome was MACE, and LVEF or IS was also reported in some studies. Overall quality was judged as good in 9 studies and fair in one study; no study was rated as poor. Overall, most studies possessed high methodological quality and low risk of bias (Table S2).

Meta-analysis results for primary outcomes (main analysis)

EAT and MACE

Six studies (n=2,162) reported the association of EAT with MACE in AMI. The pooled analysis revealed moderate inter-study heterogeneity (I2=64.9%, P=0.014), thus we employed a random-effects model. Higher EAT corresponded to a significantly elevated risk of MACE (HR 1.96, 95% CI: 1.47–2.63) (Figure 2).

Forest plot for the association of epicardial adipose tissue with major adverse cardiovascular events in acute myocardial infarction. CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio.

EAT and LVEF

Six studies (n=1,517) reported the association of EAT with LVEF in AMI. Due to moderate to high inter-study heterogeneity (I2=73.9%), we employed a random-effects model. The pooled analysis revealed a slightly lower LVEF in the case of higher EAT, without a statistically significant difference (WMD −0.04, 95% CI: −2.47 to 2.38) (Figure 3).

Forest plot for the association of epicardial adipose tissue with left ventricular ejection fraction in acute myocardial infarction. CI, confidence interval; WMD, weighted mean difference.

EAT and IS

Five studies (n=651) assessed the association of EAT with IS in AMI. Studies by Gohbara et al. (16) and Bière et al. (15) showed that higher EAT was associated with smaller IS, whereas the opposite result was obtained in the remaining studies. As a result, the overall heterogeneity markedly increased (I2=91.5%, P<0.001), thus we employed a random-effects model. The pooled analysis revealed a general increasing trend of IS in the case of higher EAT, without a statistically significant difference (SMD 0.23, 95% CI: −0.38 to 0.84) (Figure 4).

Subgroup analysis on MACE

In terms of EAT assessment methods, elevated EAT was associated with a significantly increased risk of MACE in studies using echocardiography. Despite the consistent direction of effect, no statistically significant association was observed between the two in studies using CT, with high inter-study heterogeneity. In terms of age, elevated EAT was associated with an increased risk of MACE in both <60 years and ≥60 years patients, with higher pooled effect sizes in the latter and also high inter-study heterogeneity. Moreover, higher EAT indicated an elevated risk of MACE regardless of BMI (≤28 or >28 kg/m2), with low heterogeneity in studies with BMI >28 kg/m2. Additionally, EAT displayed a stronger association with MACE when the follow-up duration ≤12 months, while the association persisted but weakened when the follow-up duration >12 months. Finally, the direction of the pooled effect was consistent regardless of whether HR or OR was applied (Table 2, Figures S1-S5).

Subgroup analysis on LVEF

Subgroup analyses based on EAT measurement method, age, and BMI showed variable effect directions, but most subgroup estimates did not reach statistical significance (Table 3). For instance, a statistically significant positive association (EAT associated with higher LVEF) was only observed in the single study of patients aged ≥60 years (WMD 5.00; 95% CI: 1.16–8.84). In contrast, the subgroup with BMI ≥28 kg/m2 showed a non-significant negative point estimate (WMD −3.41; 95% CI: −6.63 to 0.18), while the BMI <28 kg/m2 subgroup showed a non-significant positive estimate. Given the limited number of included studies and wide confidence intervals, no definitive conclusions regarding effect modification by these factors could be drawn (Figures S6-S8).

Subgroup analysis on IS

Subgroup analyses revealed inconsistent effect directions. A significant positive association between EAT and IS was observed only in the BMI ≥28 kg/m2 subgroup (SMD 0.85; 95% CI: 0.43–1.27; I2=0%). Estimates for other subgroups were either non-significant or based on a single study (age ≥60 years), with high heterogeneity in some cases. Due to the limited number of studies per subgroup, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn (Table 4, Figure S9).

Sensitivity analysis

We performed leave-one-out sensitivity analyses on MACE, LVEF, and IS to assess the robustness of the pooled results. After sequentially excluding each study, the direction and statistical significance of the pooled effect sizes remained unchanged, suggesting good robustness of the primary results (Figure 5, Figures S10,S11).

Publication bias

Given that fewer than 10 studies were included for each outcome, assessment of publication bias was limited to visual inspection of funnel plots and should be considered exploratory. The funnel plots for MACE, LVEF, and IS showed some degree of asymmetry, which may reflect small-study effects, between-study heterogeneity, or random variation rather than true publication bias. Therefore, no definitive conclusion regarding publication bias can be drawn (Figures S12-S14).

Discussion

Key findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis integrating currently available observational evidence demonstrated that increased EAT was significantly associated with a higher risk of MACE in patients with AMI. In contrast, no significant overall association was observed between EAT and LVEF or IS. Notably, subgroup analyses indicated that the association between EAT and MACE was more pronounced in studies with shorter follow-up durations (≤12 months), suggesting that EAT may be particularly relevant to early post-infarction risk.

Collectively, these findings suggest that EAT may function more as a marker of adverse metabolic-inflammatory milieu and subsequent clinical risk rather than a direct indicator of acute infarct severity.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis specifically focusing on the prognostic significance of EAT in AMI patients, thereby addressing a clinically important and previously underexplored topic. Second, we followed PRISMA 2020 guidance and applied a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases. Third, multiple clinically relevant outcomes were evaluated, including hard events (MACE), cardiac function (LVEF), and tissue injury burden (IS). Fourth, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore heterogeneity and assess robustness.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all included studies were observational in nature, precluding causal inference. Second, heterogeneity existed across studies regarding EAT measurement modality, anatomical site, timing of assessment, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions. Third, the number of eligible studies for some endpoints was limited, reducing statistical power and restricting the reliability of subgroup analyses. In addition, although emerging CT-derived adipose tissue attenuation parameters, such as pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) attenuation, may provide additional information regarding coronary inflammation and plaque activity, eligible studies evaluating these parameters in AMI populations were limited and were therefore not included in the present quantitative synthesis (26). Fourth, most studies relied on single baseline imaging measurements, and longitudinal changes in EAT after AMI were rarely reported. Finally, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite the use of adjusted estimates in several studies.

Comparison with similar research

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous systematic reviews conducted in broader coronary artery disease populations. Prior meta-analyses have shown that increased EAT thickness or volume is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes across diverse ischemic populations (27). However, those studies mainly included heterogeneous cohorts with stable coronary disease, acute coronary syndrome, or mixed cardiovascular conditions.

The present study extends existing evidence by focusing specifically on AMI, a high-risk clinical setting characterized by intense inflammation, neurohormonal activation, and dynamic ventricular remodeling. Within this more homogeneous population, we confirmed that elevated EAT remained significantly associated with adverse prognosis, particularly recurrent cardiovascular events. This supports the concept that EAT retains prognostic relevance even after the index infarction and may be particularly important during post-AMI recovery.

Explanations of findings

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed association between EAT and worse outcomes after AMI. EAT is anatomically contiguous with the myocardium and coronary arteries without a fascial barrier, enabling direct paracrine and vasocrine signaling (8). It secretes multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, and oxidative mediators that may aggravate endothelial dysfunction, plaque instability, and adverse myocardial remodeling (8,28-30). Importantly, ischemic injury during AMI may directly influence EAT biology. Reduced local perfusion, hypoxia, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation after infarction may promote phenotypic alterations within EAT, including increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, adipocyte dysfunction, and fibrotic remodeling (11,12,28,31). These bidirectional interactions between ischemic myocardium and adjacent EAT may further amplify local inflammatory responses and adverse ventricular remodeling, potentially contributing to worse cardiovascular outcomes after AMI. In addition, EAT may promote lipotoxicity and metabolic dysregulation in ischemic myocardium through excess free fatty acid flux, thereby impairing myocardial recovery after infarction (8,30).

The lack of a clear association with LVEF or IS, both of which were assessed during the acute phase of AMI, may indicate that EAT is more strongly related to long-term systemic and local risk processes than to immediate infarct extent. Moreover, IS findings were inconsistent across studies. Some CMR-based studies, particularly those by Bière et al. (15) and Gohbara et al. (16), suggested a possible “EAT paradox”, in which higher EAT was associated with greater myocardial salvage, whereas other studies linked EAT with larger infarcts and microvascular dysfunction (14,17). These discrepancies likely reflect differences in imaging modality, timing of assessment, AMI stage, and study design.

The stronger association observed during follow-up ≤12 months suggests a potential time-dependent effect. This finding was mainly driven by studies focusing on early post-infarction outcomes (13,25). Early after AMI, inflammatory activity, thrombogenicity, and remodeling processes are most active, which may amplify the adverse prognostic signal carried by EAT (11). With longer-term guideline-directed therapy and risk-factor control, the relative impact of baseline EAT may attenuate over time.

Implications and actions needed

These findings have several important clinical implications. First, EAT may serve as a readily obtainable, non-invasive imaging biomarker for risk stratification in AMI patients, particularly in the early post-infarction period. Second, incorporating EAT assessment into existing risk models may improve prognostic accuracy by capturing metabolic and inflammatory dimensions not reflected in traditional markers.

Furthermore, EAT may represent a potential therapeutic target. Emerging evidence suggests that EAT volume and activity can be modified through pharmacological interventions (e.g., statins, SGLT2 inhibitors) and lifestyle modifications (32,33). Future prospective studies are warranted to evaluate whether targeting EAT can improve clinical outcomes in AMI.

Finally, standardized measurement protocols and large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify the role of EAT in clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that an increase in EAT thickness or volume is closely associated with MACE in AMI, but its overall correlation with LVEF and IS remains unclear. EAT may serve as an imaging marker for metabolic and inflammatory burden, which correlates with unfavorable AMI outcomes and possesses potential clinical value in risk stratification. Given the observational nature and high heterogeneity of the included studies, these findings require further validation in multicenter, large-scale prospective studies.
