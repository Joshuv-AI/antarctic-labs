Geometric Aspects of Covariant Phase Space Formalism: Solution Space Slicings and Surface Charge Integrability
M. Golshani, M.M. Sheikh-Jabbari, V. Taghiloo, M.H. Vahidinia

Abstract
The Covariant Phase Space Formalism (CPSF) provides a robust framework for deriving symplectic structures and surface charges in diffeomorphism-invariant theories. By construction, the CPSF operates on two distinct manifolds: the spacetime and the Solution Phase Space (SPS). In this paper, we advance the formalism by establishing a strictly parallel geometric formulation for both manifolds. Within this framework, we systematically analyze diffeomorphisms and frame changes on both spaces. While spacetime diffeomorphisms have been extensively studied in the literature, transformations on the SPS have been largely overlooked; we rigorously define and investigate these as changes of slicing on SPS. We demonstrate that the standard Wald-Zoupas criterion for the integrability of surface charge variations is inherently slicing-dependent. To resolve this issue, we develop the Frobenius theorem on the SPS and use it to extends the Wald-Zoupas condition into an inherently slicing-independent criterion for integrability. The Frobenius theorem on the SPS also yields a rigorous and natural definition of fundamental geometric quantities on the solution space, specifically the SPS connection, torsion, and curvature. Furthermore, this geometric machinery naturally distinguishes between fundamentally different surface fluxes: “fake” fluxes are identified mathematically as pure gauge artifacts of the SPS connection, while “genuine” fluxes manifest as non-vanishing SPS torsion, which directly relates to the physical gravitational News tensor. Finally, we present a geometric formulation of the Liouville theorem on the SPS, offering a unified classification scheme for theories with and without propagating bulk degrees of freedom.

Introduction

Noether’s theorem stands as one of the most beautiful and profound results in theoretical physics, formally establishing that symmetries and conserved quantities are two sides of the same coin
Noether:1918zz
. From this theorem, we understand that the conservation laws of energy, momentum, and electric charge are intrinsically rooted in underlying symmetry principles. More precisely, Noether’s first theorem dictates that
global
continuous symmetries yield conserved currents. However, while this applies elegantly to global symmetries, the story becomes significantly more complex when considering local (gauge) symmetries. Noether’s second theorem states that local symmetries yield Noether currents that identically vanish on-shell–amounting to identities among the equations of motion. Consequently, it was long held that local symmetries are merely redundancies in the physical description of a system.

The crucial insight developed over the last half-century is that this paradigm drastically shifts in the presence of boundaries (e.g. asymptotic boundary of AdS space, spatial infinity, null infinity, black hole horizons, or general boundaries at finite distance). At a boundary, a measure-zero subset of local gauge symmetries yield physical, asymptotic or boundary symmetries, leading to non-vanishing, physically meaningful charges. Covariant phase space formalism: Geometric structure and mathematical tools

In the covariant phase space formalism (CPSF), we deal with two distinct manifolds: spacetime
\mathcal{M}
and field space
\Gamma
. In this section, we introduce the geometric structures associated with the field space, drawing a parallel with those of the spacetime manifold. Throughout, we assume that the spacetime dimension is
d
.

2.1
Geometric tools of CPSF: Variational bi-complex

In this subsection, we introduce the geometric tools of both spacetime and field space in parallel.

Coordinates.

To perform calculus on manifolds, we need to introduce charts or coordinate systems. In the spacetime, we use coordinates denoted by
x^{\mu}
, where
\mu=0,1,\dots,d-1
. In a similar way, in the field space, the coordinates are given by the fundamental or dynamical fields of a given theory. We denote these dynamical fields as
\phi^{I}(x)
, where
I=1,\cdots,D
labels the
D
discrete field components. Note that
\phi^{I}(x)
may be scalars, vectors, forms or a generic tensor on spacetime.
The collection of all dynamical fields will be denoted by
\Phi=\{\phi^{I}\}
. Just as spacetime is described by a finite set of coordinates, field space is a manifold whose coordinates form an infinite-dimensional set.

Tangent space.

At each point on a manifold, there exists a vector space of the same dimension as the manifold, known as the tangent space. In the case of spacetime, the tangent space is spanned by the basis vectors
\partial_{\mu}
, and any vector field is written as a linear combination of these basis vectors
V(x)=V^{\mu}(x)\,\partial_{\mu}
. In parallel with spacetime, we introduce a basis on the tangent space of field space as follows

\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{1.80835pt}{\rule{3.18884pt}{0.48222pt}}\cr}}}}{\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{1.80835pt}{\rule{3.18884pt}{0.48222pt}}\cr}}}\phi^{I}(x)}

\hat{V}[\Phi]:=\hat{V}^{I}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{I}=\sum_{I}\int\operatorname{d}\!{}^{d}x\,\hat{V}^{\phi^{I}(x)}\frac{\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}}{\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}\phi^{I}(x)}\,.

(2.1)

2.5
Definition of covariance

As discussed in the introduction, the notion of covariance over spacetime was the guiding principle in the formulation of general relativity and equivalence principle. Similarly, the same notion over the field space may be attributed to the background independence. Of course, covariance on the field space has not been as extensively studied as the same notion on spacetime. We have established the covariance of the fundamental field
\Phi^{I}
in (
2.20
). In what follows, we generalized it for more general objects. Indeed, if diffeomorphisms are allowed to have field-dependence one should revisit the notion of covariance. Here, we study the covariance properties of a broad class of natural quantities defined on the field space.

The most general object we will be dealing with in our analysis takes the form

X=X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\xi_{2};\Phi)\,,

(2.31)

where
X
is linear in its first four arguments and may depend nonlinearly on the background fields
\Phi
. We say that
X
is a covariant quantity if its transformation under a field space vector
\hat{\zeta}
is given by (see Appendix
C
for a proof)

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69054pt\begin{split}\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\xi_{2};\Phi)&=\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\xi_{2};\Phi)+\mathrm{I}_{\widehat{\delta\zeta}}\,X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\xi_{2};\Phi)\\
&\hskip-28.45274pt+X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\zeta\textrm{]}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi_{1},\xi_{2};\Phi)+X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\textrm{[}\xi_{2},\zeta\textrm{]}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi_{2};\Phi)\,,\end{split}

(2.32)

where
\widehat{\delta\zeta}
is the vector corresponding to
\delta\zeta
on the field space, which is defined as follows

\widehat{\delta\zeta}=\left(\delta\,\delta_{\zeta}-\delta_{\zeta}\,\delta\right)\Phi=\delta\hat{\zeta}-\delta_{\zeta}(\delta\Phi)\,.

(2.33)

As we see
\widehat{\delta\zeta}\neq\delta\hat{\zeta}
and for field independent transformations
\delta\hat{\zeta}=\delta_{\zeta}(\delta\Phi)
.

For field independent diffeomorphisms,
\delta\xi=0=\delta\zeta

2.32

\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}X=\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\xi_{2};\Phi)+X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\zeta\textrm{]},\xi_{2};\Phi)+X(\delta_{1}\Phi,\delta_{2}\Phi,\xi_{1},\textrm{[}\xi_{2},\zeta\textrm{]};\Phi)\,.

(2.34)

See Appendix
C
for more detailed discussions.

Equation (
2.32
) is one of the main equations of this paper and shows that, under a transformation, the quantity
X
defined in (
2.31
) receives contributions from several distinct sources:

1.

X
transforms as a spacetime tensor. This contribution is captured by the spacetime Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}
and reflects the transformation of the spacetime indices carried by
X
.

2.

X
depends explicitly on the diffeomorphism generators
\xi

\zeta
. This induces additional contributions governed by the commutator
[\xi,\zeta]
.

3.

Further contributions arise from the field dependence of the generators
\xi[\Phi]
and
\zeta[\Phi]
, leading to additional variations associated with their implicit dependence on the dynamical fields.

Note also that to calculate the Lie derivative along
\widehat{\delta\zeta}

FrolicherNijenhuis1956
(see
e.g.

KolarMichorSlovak1993

X^{\mu}_{[\nu_{1}\nu_{2}\cdots\nu_{k}]}
as follows

\mathcal{L}_{X}=i_{X}\,\differential+(-1)^{k}\differential\,i_{X}\,,

(2.35)

and in the field space for any vector‑valued forms
\hat{X}^{I}_{[J_{1}J_{2}\cdots J_{k}]}

\mathrm{L}_{\hat{X}}=\mathrm{I}_{\hat{X}}\,\delta+(-1)^{k}\delta\,\mathrm{I}_{\hat{X}}\,.

(2.36)

Some consistency checks.

To understand better (
2.32
), we perform some consistency/sanity checks for some specific

, we have

\mathcal{L}_{{\zeta}}\xi^{\mu}=\textrm{[}\zeta,\xi\textrm{]}^{\mu}\,,\qquad\mathrm{I}_{\widehat{\delta\zeta}}\,\xi^{\mu}=0\,.

(2.37)

Therefore,

\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi^{\mu}=\textrm{[}\zeta,\xi\textrm{]}^{\mu}+\textrm{[}\xi,\zeta\textrm{]}^{\mu}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi^{\mu}=\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi^{\mu}\,.

(2.38)

As another check, consider
X\equiv\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}^{\mu}

\mathcal{L}_{{\zeta}}\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}^{\mu}=\textrm{[}\zeta,\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}\textrm{]}^{\mu}\,,\qquad\mathrm{I}_{\widehat{\delta\zeta}}\,\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}^{\mu}=0\,.

(2.39)

Then,

\begin{split}\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}^{\mu}&=\textrm{[}\zeta,\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}\textrm{]}^{\mu}+\textrm{[}\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\zeta\textrm{]}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\textrm{]}^{\mu}+\textrm{[}\xi_{1},\textrm{[}\xi_{2},\zeta\textrm{]}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\xi_{2}\textrm{]}^{\mu}\\

3
Solution phase space, symplectic form and surface charges

Having established a parallel geometric framework for both the field space and the spacetime manifold, we now introduce three crucial structures defined on the field space: (1) the
solution phase space
, as a subsector in the field space which is a symplectic manifold; (2) the
symplectic form
, an invertible
(0,2)
-form over the solution phase space; and (3) the
surface charge variation
, a
(0,1)
-form over the solution phase space. It is important to note that we will be working with covariant quantities, such as
(p,Q)
-forms, which remain well-defined on the solution phase space. We assume that the solution phase space has a trivial topology. Before proceeding to these concepts, we briefly review the fundamental definitions of the symplectic form and the Poisson bracket.

Symplectic structure and Poisson bracket.

As a warm up, we recall that manifolds may be supplemented with additional structures, e.g. we can have a metric on the manifolds (metric spaces) and/or a symplectic form, symplectic manifolds (phase space). A symplectic manifold or a phase space in physics terminology, is a manifold equipped with a non-degenerate, invertible, and conserved symplectic 2-form

\Omega[\Phi]=\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{IJ}\,\delta\phi^{I}\curlywedge\delta\phi^{J}\,,

(3.1)

where
\operatorname{d}\!{}\Omega[\Phi]=0
and
\delta\Omega[\Phi]=0
, and there exists an antisymmetric bi-vector

\begin{split}&\Omega^{-1}[\Phi]=\Omega^{IJ}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{I}\circledast\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}\,,\end{split}

(3.2)

such that

\Omega_{IK}\Omega^{KJ}=\delta_{I}^{J}\,.

(3.3)

Using the symplectic structure, we can define the Poisson brackets. Let
F[\Phi]
and
G[\Phi]
be two arbitrary functions. Their Poisson bracket is defined as follows

\{F,G\}_{\text{\tiny{P.B.}}}=\Omega^{{{IJ}}}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{I}F\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}G\,.

(3.4)

3.1
Explicit construction of symplectic form over solution phase space

As mentioned, field space is not (necessarily) a symplectic manifold, however, there exists a sector of it, the solution phase space, that is equipped with a symplectic form. In the following, we review the systematic method to construct the solution phase space and compute the symplectic form for a given theory starting from its Lagrangian.

The action for a physical system on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
is given by

S=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,L[\Phi]\,,

(3.5)

where the Lagrangian
L[\Phi]
is a scalar density over
\mathcal{M}
. One may either work directly with
L[\Phi]
or adopt the language of differential forms
Lee:1990nz
;
Iyer:1994ys
by utilizing its Hodge dual,
\textbf{L}[\Phi]
, which is a top-form over
\mathcal{M}
.

Variation of the Lagrangian
\delta{L}
upon a generic variation of fields
\delta\Phi
, is given by

\delta{L}={E}_{\Phi}[\Phi]\,\delta\Phi+\partial_{\mu}\Theta^{\mu}[\delta\Phi;\Phi]\,,

(3.6)

where
{E}_{\Phi}[\Phi]=0
gives equations of motion and
\Theta^{\mu}
is the Hodg-dual of the pre-symplectic potential
\bm{\Theta}
which is a
(d-1,1)
-form.
We can rewrite the variation of the action as

\delta S=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\operatorname{d}\!{}^{d}x\,{E}_{\Phi}\,\delta\Phi+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\operatorname{d}\!{}x_{\mu}\,\Theta^{\mu}\,,

(3.7)

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes the boundary of
\mathcal{M}
. Therefore, if we restrict our field configurations to those satisfying field equations,
\delta S
reduces to a boundary integral,
\delta S=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\operatorname{d}\!{}x_{\mu}\,\Theta^{\mu}
. Next, we need to define the cotangent space for the manifold consisting of set of all solutions to
{E}_{\Phi}[\Phi]=0
. We do so, by taking
\Phi+\delta\Phi
to be also satisfying
{E}_{\Phi}[\Phi+\delta\Phi]=0
. That is,
\delta\Phi
should satisfy linearized field equations around the point
\Phi
with
E(\Phi)=0
in the field space. Explicitly, we define the solution space through,

{E}_{\Phi}=0\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\delta{E}_{\Phi}}{\delta\Phi}\delta\Phi=0\,.

(3.8)

According to the CPSF
Lee:1990nz
, the solution space is equipped with a
(0,2)
-form that is defined as the variation of the integral of the total pre-symplectic potential over a codimension-1 hypersurface

\Omega[\delta\Phi\,,\delta\Phi;\Phi]=\delta\int_{\mathcal{B}}\operatorname{d}\!{}x_{\mu}\,\Theta^{\mu}\,.

(3.9)

\Omega
, while by-definition a closed 2-form over the solution space, is not necessarily invertible on the solution space defined by (
3.8
). As discussed in
Lee:1990nz
;
Wald:1999wa
;
Grumiller:2022qhx
, in systems with gauge/diffeomorphism symmetries the equations defining the solution space (
3.8
) are gauge covariant and hence solution space involves gauge-equivalent configurations over which
\Omega
is not invertible. However, one can make
\Omega
invertible by modding out solution space by the gauge-equivalent classes
Lee:1990nz
. In what follows, we use
solution phase space (SPS)
to denote the subset of solution space over which
\Omega
has been made invertible. That is, the solution phase space is a symplectic manifold.

In standard Hamiltonian mechanics, the symplectic structure–such as the symplectic form and the Poisson bracket–is defined kinematically, conceptually independent of the specific dynamics or Hamiltonian of the system. It is therefore desirable to formulate a definition of the symplectic potential that is similarly independent of the underlying Lagrangian. Equation (
3.7
) implies that
\mathrm{d}\delta\bm{\Theta}=0
on the solution phase space. Consequently, one is led to the following general definition: A symplectic potential
\bm{\Theta}
is a
(d-1,1)
-form satisfying

\displaystyle\delta\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}=\mathrm{d}\delta\bm{\Theta}=0\qquad\text{(on the solution phase space)}\,.

(3.10)

The most general solution to (
3.10
) is

\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Theta}_{0}+\delta\bm{W}+\operatorname{d}\!{}\bm{Y}\,,

(3.11)

where
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
is a specific solution to (
3.10
) and
\bm{W},\bm{Y}
, respectively a
(d-1,0)
and
(d-2,1)
are two integration constants. If we require these freedoms to be covariant, then
\bm{W}
and
\bm{Y}
freedoms will have the following covariance properties (
2.32
),

\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\,W^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\,W^{\mu}\,,\qquad\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\,Y^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\,Y^{\mu\nu}+\mathrm{I}_{\widehat{\delta\xi}}\,Y^{\mu\nu}\,,

(3.12)

where
W^{\mu},Y^{\mu\nu}
are spacetime Hodg duals of the corresponding forms
\bm{W},\bm{Y}
, and
\widehat{\delta\xi}
is a
(1,1)-
form on the field space (
2.33
).
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
may be chosen to be the (canonical) Lee-Wald symplectic potential
\bm{\Theta}_{\text{\tiny{LW}}}

Lee:1990nz
and
\bm{W}
may be viewed as a boundary Lagrangian, while
\bm{Y}
has no appearance in the original Lagrangian of the theory.

The
(0,2)
symplectic form may then be defined as

\Omega[\delta\Phi,\delta\Phi;\Phi]=\delta\int_{\mathcal{B}}\,\bm{\Theta}=\delta\int_{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{\Theta}_{0}+\delta\bm{W}+\operatorname{d}\!{}\bm{Y})\,.

(3.13)

We now reach a subtle point: whether the variation operator
\delta
can be passed through the integral. In
Adami:2024gdx
(see also
Ciambelli:2021nmv
;
Freidel:2021dxw
;
Carrozza:2022xut
;
Liu:2026bao
), we showed that in the presence of boundary fluctuations, one cannot simply pass
\delta
through a boundary integral. In other words,
\mathcal{B}
may be a codimension-1 surface which depends on the field configurations and its
\delta
-variation may not be zero. In this work, we consider
rigid
boundaries and do not take their fluctuations into account. Therefore,

\Omega[\delta\Phi,\delta\Phi;\Phi]=\int_{\mathcal{B}}(\delta\bm{\Theta}_{0}+\operatorname{d}\!{}\delta\bm{Y})\,.

(3.14)

As we see, although the
W
-freedom contributes to the symplectic potential, it drops out in the symplectic form; only the
Y
-freedom gives a nontrivial contribution to the symplectic form.

3.2
Physical interpretation of
W
and
Y
freedoms

In this subsection, we briefly discuss the physical interpretations of the
W
and
Y
freedoms in the CPSF. For extensive discussion refer
Parvizi:2025shq
and references therein.

The
W
-freedom plays a crucial role in the variational principle, as it generates changes in the boundary conditions imposed on the hypersurface
\mathcal{B}
. Consequently, the choice of
W
is partially constrained by the requirement of a well-posed action principle
skenderis2002lecture
;
deHaro:2000vlm
;
Henningson:1998gx
;
Mann:2005yr
;
Papadimitriou:2005ii
;
Emparan:1999pm
. In the context of holography, this freedom acts as a boundary Lagrangian and corresponds to multitrace deformations of the dual boundary theory
Witten:2001ua
;
Parvizi:2025shq
.

By contrast, the
Y
-freedom does not affect the bulk action or its boundary conditions. Instead, it modifies the symplectic current/form itself and can be interpreted as the symplectic structure intrinsic to the boundary theory
Parvizi:2025shq
. Specifically, it governs the canonical pairing of sources and responses at the corner (the codimension-2 entangling surface). As (
3.14
) shows,

\Omega
involves two kind of terms that can be written as codimension-2 integrals, integrals over boundary of
\mathcal{B}
, the “corner terms” and, codimension-1 integrals (integrals over
{\mathcal{B}}
) that can’t be written as corner terms. The
Y
-terms are corner terms, while
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
term may also involve corner terms. In general one can choose the
Y
-term so that all corner terms are in the
Y
-term and
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
term only involves codimension-1 terms. Using this, we can decompose the SPS coordinates (fields)
\phi^{I}
into “boundary modes”
\varphi^{a}
(with
a=1,\cdots,P
) that span the codimension-2 part of
\Omega
, and the rest of them, the “bulk modes”
\varphi^{A}
(with
A=1,\cdots,D-P
) that span the codimension-1 terms. Hence, in general
\Omega
involves three kind of components,
\Omega_{ab},\Omega_{aA},\Omega_{AB}
. The components
\Omega_{ab}
correspond to corner terms, while
\Omega_{aA}
and
\Omega_{AB}
generally arise from codimension‑1 integrals.

We note that there is a redundancy or overlap between the
W
and
Y
freedoms: a
W
-term that is exact in spacetime,
\bm{W}=\mathrm{d}\bm{Z}
, can be equivalently represented as a shift in the
Y
-freedom via
\bm{Y}=\delta\bm{Z}
, where
\bm{Z}
is a
(d-2)
-form. Physically, just as
\bm{W}
can be viewed as a boundary Lagrangian,
\bm{Z}
corresponds to a corner Lagrangian term.

Let us summarize what we discussed in this section. The presymplectic form
\delta\Omega=\delta^{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\,\bm{\Theta}=0
is by definition a closed (0,2)-from in field space. Furthermore,
\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}\approx 0
(modulo boundary terms) on the solution space, guaranteeing the conservation of the symplectic form,
\mathrm{d}\Omega\approx 0
. However, to establish
\Omega
as a proper symplectic form on the physical phase space, it must be non-degenerate (invertible) in addition to being closed. In theories with gauge or diffeomorphism invariance, this may be achieved by modding out the solution space by these gauge directions, yielding
solution phase space
, which is equipped with a well-defined symplectic 2-form
Lee:1990nz
;
Wald:1999wa
;
Grumiller:2022qhx
.

3.3
Surface charges

Given the symplectic 2-form, one can associate a surface charge variation to a symmetry generator
\xi
. The surface charge variation, which is a 1-form on the SPS, is defined as

\not{\delta}Q(\xi):=-\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\,\Omega[\Phi]=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\delta(\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\bm{\Theta})-\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\bm{\Theta}\right]\,,

(3.15)

where in the second equality we have used Cartan’s identity (
2.16
). We emphasize that the above expression must be evaluated the SPS, as defined in the previous subsection.

If
\xi
belongs to the set of local (gauge) symmetries of the theory, it is said to generate a non-trivial (physical) gauge transformation whenever
\not{\delta}Q(\xi)
is finite and non-vanishing. These are (measure zero) subset of gauge transformations/diffeomorphisms which unlike “proper gauge transformations/diffeomorphisms” do not render symplectic form non-invertible; explicitly, they parameterize a part of SPS. We will discuss this point further below. Although
\Omega
is defined as an integral over a codimension-1 boundary
\mathcal{B}
, when
\xi
generates a gauge symmetry the resulting surface charge variation reduces to a local codimension-2 integral, i.e. an integral over a surface
\mathcal{S}
. This feature justifies the terminology
surface charge
, in contrast with generic Noether charges, which are volume integrals over codimension-1 constant-time hypersurfaces. This localization property is a fundamental and distinguishing characteristic of local symmetries.

The charge variation (
3.15
) may be expressed in terms of its components as

\not{\delta}Q(\xi)=-\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\bigl(\Omega_{0}[\Phi]+\delta Y[\delta\Phi]\bigr)\,,\qquad Y[\delta\Phi]=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{d}\!{}x_{\mu\nu}\,Y^{\mu\nu}[\delta\Phi]:=Y_{I}\,\delta\phi^{I}\,.

(3.16)

Equivalently, in component form, one finds

\displaystyle\not{\delta}Q(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\Omega^{0}_{IJ}\,\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\phi^{I}\,\delta\phi^{J}-\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}Y_{I}\,\delta\phi^{I}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\phi^{I}\,\delta Y_{I}

(3.17)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\Omega^{0}_{IJ}\,\hat{\xi}^{I}+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}Y_{I}\bigr)\delta\phi^{I}+\hat{\xi}^{I}\,\delta Y_{I}\,.

The right-hand side of (
3.15

Noether charge.

5
Charge Integrability and Slicings

In this section, we study the integrability of surface charges. As discussed earlier, the infinitesimal charge variation
\not{\delta}Q(\xi)
is not, in general, an exact 1-form on the SPS; that is, it cannot always be written as
\not{\delta}Q(\xi)=\delta Q(\xi)
. Using the Frobenius theorem and the geometry of the covariant phase space, we will derive the necessary and sufficient conditions for the integrability of surface charge variations and establish a rigorous framework to distinguish coordinate artifacts from genuine physical radiation.

5.1
Criteria for Charge Integrability

What is the precise criterion for charge integrability?
This question has a classic answer within the covariant phase space formalism
Wald:1999wa
. From a geometric viewpoint, the surface charge variation
\not{\delta}Q_{\xi}
defines a 1-form on the SPS. Integrability–namely, the existence of a state function
Q(\xi)
such that
\not{\delta}Q_{\xi}=\delta Q(\xi)
–is therefore equivalent to the condition that this 1-form be closed:

\delta\not{\delta}Q_{\xi}=0\,,\qquad\text{(Wald--Zoupas integrability criterion)}\,.

(5.1)

We refer to (
5.1
) as the strict Wald–Zoupas (WZ) integrability criterion. (See footnote
1
for more comments on the naming).

Applying this criterion to our generalized charge expression (
4.1
), one finds the integrability conditions:

\delta\mu^{a}=0\,,\qquad\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\bm{\beta}_{a}=\delta\mathrm{Q}_{a}\,.

(5.2)

The first condition reflects the usual specific choice of field dependence for the symmetry generators (here taken to be strictly field-independent), while the second condition enforces the integrability of the charge aspect 1-forms.

Despite the fact that it is wide used–particularly for exact symmetries and field-independent diffeomorphisms–the strict WZ criterion (
5.1
) is not universally satisfactory and may lead to physically counterintuitive conclusions. Two notable situations illustrate its critical limitations:

•

Theories without bulk flux:

The non-integrability of surface charges is commonly attributed to a flux of propagating bulk degrees of freedom (e.g., gravitational radiation) escaping through the boundary. Nevertheless, in certain two- and three-dimensional gravitational theories that do not have propagating bulk modes (gravitational waves), the surface charges are not integrable by the WZ criterion

;
Adami:2021nnf
;
Adami:2022ktn
;

;
Ruzziconi:2020wrb
;
Geiller:2021vpg
;
Geiller:2024amx
;
Ciambelli:2023ott
;
Ciambelli:2024vhy
. The WZ integrability condition seems to unphysically tight.

•

Dependence on the choice of slicing:

As illustrated by (
5.2
), the WZ criterion is sensitive to the SPS slicing. There are cases in which (
5.1
) indicates non-integrability, yet a change-of-slicing (
4.3
) renders the same surface charge integrable

;
Adami:2021nnf

Adami:2022ktn

. This demonstrates that the standard WZ criterion can be a coordinate/slicing-dependent artifact rather than an invariant physical statement.

In the subsequent subsections, we resolve these issues by reformulating charge integrability geometrically. We first generalize the WZ condition (
5.1
) using the Frobenius theorem over the SPS to obtain a slicing-independent integrability criterion. We then construct a complete Cartan geometric framework on the covariant phase space. This framework allows us to rigorously define the geometric connection, curvature, and torsion of the solution space and definitively separate “fake flux” (a pure slicing artifact) from “genuine flux” (true physical radiation).

5.2
Charge integrability and Frobenius theorems

Let us begin with a slicing (
5.2
) in which the surface charge is integrable in the strict Wald–Zoupas sense, meaning
\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}=0
. We now perform a field-dependent change-of-slicing on the SPS, parametrized by an invertible matrix
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}
(
4.3
), such that the old charge aspects are related to the new ones as
\bm{\beta}_{b}=\Lambda^{c}{}_{b}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{c}
, or equivalently
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{a}=(\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{a}\bm{\beta}_{b}
.

Using the integrability condition of the original slicing (
\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}=0
), we compute the phase-space exterior derivative of the charge aspect in the new slicing:

\delta\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{a}=\delta\!\left((\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{a}\right)\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}=\delta\!\left((\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{a}\right)\curlywedge\left(\Lambda^{c}{}_{b}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{c}\right)\,.

(5.3)

Using the identity
\delta(\Lambda^{-1}\Lambda)=0
, one can define the matrix-valued phase-space connection 1-form
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}
,

\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}:=\delta\!\left((\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{a}\right)\Lambda^{c}{}_{b}=-(\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{a}\,\delta\Lambda^{c}{}_{b}\,.

(5.4)

This result has a profound implication. We conclude that an integrable frame exists
if and only if
there exists a matrix-valued 1-form
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
on the SPS such that the charge aspects satisfy the generalized differential equation:

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{a}=\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}\,.

(5.5)

Equation (
5.5
) provides a fully slicing-independent, covariant criterion for charge integrability. The Wald–Zoupas criterion (
5.1
) is a special case where one starts with the “correct” integrable slicing, corresponding to
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}=\delta^{a}_{b}
and consequently
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}=0
. Whenever a non-zero connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
satisfying (
5.5
) can be found, the apparent non-integrability is an artifact of the slicing. The integrating factor–namely, the change-of-slicing matrix
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}
that returns the system to the strict WZ frame–can be reconstructed by solving the differential equation (
5.4
).

Additionally, the symmetry generator condition
\delta\mu^{a}=0
in the original slicing dictates the required field-dependence in the new slicing. Recalling (
4.3
),
{\tilde{\mu}^{a}=\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}\,\mu^{b}}
, and (
5.4
) we learn that,

\delta\tilde{\mu}^{a}+\mathcal{D}_{b}{}^{a}\,\tilde{\mu}^{b}=0\,.

(5.6)

In an integrable slicing (if it exists),
\mu^{a}
will be called chemical potentials associated with the charges
\mathrm{Q}_{a}
,
\bm{\beta}_{a}=\delta\mathrm{Q}_{a}
.

Although conceptually powerful, condition (
5.5
) is not always convenient in practical calculations, as it requires explicitly guessing or constructing the connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
. This can be bypassed by projecting
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
out of the equation altogether. Taking the wedge product of (
5.5
) with all available
\bm{\beta}
1-forms trivially annihilates the right-hand side, yielding a purely algebraic, connection-free criterion:

\displaystyle\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}\curlywedge\mathscr{B}=0\,,\qquad\mathscr{B}:=\bm{\beta}_{1}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{2}\curlywedge\cdots\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{P}\,.

(5.7)

Relation to the Dual Frobenius Theorem.

The criteria derived above are not accidental algebraic constructions; rather, they are the direct application of the Frobenius theorem formulated in terms of differential forms–often referred to as the
dual Frobenius theorem

Wald:1984rg
;
Lee2013
.

In differential geometry, a set of
P
linearly independent 1-forms
\{\bm{\beta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{P}\}
generates an algebraic ideal
\mathcal{I}
. The Frobenius theorem states that this ideal is a
differential ideal
(i.e.,
\operatorname{d}\!{}\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{I}
) if and only if

\operatorname{d}\!{}\bm{\beta}_{a}\wedge\bm{\beta}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\bm{\beta}_{P}=0\,.

In the covariant phase space formalism, the exterior derivative is the phase-space variation
\delta
. Consequently, condition (
5.7
) is the exact mathematical requirement for the charge aspect 1-forms
\bm{\beta}_{a}
to generate a differential ideal on the SPS.

Physically, this guarantees that the SPS admits a smooth foliation by codimension-
P
submanifolds. On these submanifolds, the appropriately sliced charge aspects become locally exact, rendering the surface charges integrable. The change-of-slicing procedure
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}
is the physical realization of the integrating factors whose existence is guaranteed by the Frobenius theorem.

The essential takeaway is this: when the standard Wald–Zoupas criterion fails (
\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}\neq 0
), physical integrability is not necessarily lost. Instead, one must test the algebraic Frobenius condition (
5.7
) to determine whether an integrable foliation of the SPS exists, hidden behind a non-optimal choice of slicing.

5.3
Change-of-Slicing as SPS Parallel Transport

The appearance of the connection 1-form
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
allows us to recast the integrability conditions into a profoundly geometric language: that of parallel transport over the SPS.

In differential geometry, a tensor field is parallel transported along a manifold if its covariant derivative vanishes. If we define the phase-space covariant exterior derivative
D_{\mathcal{D}}
associated with the connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
, we can rewrite the generalized Frobenius condition (
5.5
) as:

D_{\mathcal{D}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{a}:=\delta\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{a}-\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}=0\,.

(5.8)

This equation implies that for an integrable system, the charge aspect 1-forms
\bm{\beta}_{a}
are
covariantly constant
over the SPS. As we vary the physical fields (moving from one solution to another via the exterior derivative
\delta
), their variation is completely governed by the connection. The charge aspects are effectively parallel transported across the solution space.

This parallel transport mechanism extends to the other fundamental objects defining our slicing. The chemical potentials
\tilde{\mu}^{a}
, which parameterize the asymptotic symmetry generators, also obey a strict transport law (
5.6
). From the integrability requirement, their variation across SPS is dictated by
\delta\tilde{\mu}^{a}=\mathcal{D}_{b}{}^{a}\tilde{\mu}^{b}
, which can be rewritten as:

D_{\mathcal{D}}\tilde{\mu}^{a}:=\delta\tilde{\mu}^{a}+\mathcal{D}_{b}{}^{a}\tilde{\mu}^{b}=0\,.

(5.9)

Equation (
5.9
) states that the symmetry generators are covariantly constant with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
. As the physical configuration evolves along the phase space, the generators are parallel-transported by the connection to preserve their alignment with the underlying integrable foliation.

Similarly, the transformation matrix
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}
that connects different slicings satisfies a transport equation derived directly from the definition of the connection. By rearranging
\mathcal{D}=-\Lambda^{-1}\delta\Lambda
, we find:

\delta\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}+\mathcal{D}_{c}{}^{a}\Lambda^{c}{}_{b}=0\,.

(5.10)

Collectively, (
5.8
), (
5.9
), and (
5.10
) depict a cohesive geometric picture of an integrable system. Integrability implies the existence of a highly structured “charge bundle” over the SPS, where the fundamental objects–the charge aspects, the symmetry generators, and the slicing frames–are all parallel transported along the solution space using the same connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
.

Recognizing this structure immediately invites a fundamental geometric question: Is this parallel transport
path-independent
? If we vary the fields along two different paths in phase space, do the transported slicing frames match? To answer this, we must evaluate the curvature of the connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
.

5.4
The Maurer–Cartan equation and fake flux

To address the question posed above, we investigate the geometric properties of the SPS connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
introduced in (
5.5
). Hereafter, and for the ease of notation, we drop the tilde on integrable slicing quantities and hence the integrability condition reads as
\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}=\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}
. Let us apply the SPS exterior derivative
\delta

\delta

\delta^{2}\equiv 0

\displaystyle 0=\delta^{2}\bm{\beta}_{a}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}-\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\delta\bm{\beta}_{c}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}-\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\left(\mathcal{D}_{c}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\delta\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}-\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\mathcal{D}_{c}{}^{b}\right)\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}\,.

(5.11)

The term inside the parentheses naturally defines the curvature 2-form
\mathcal{R}_{a}{}^{b}
associated with the connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}

\mathcal{R}_{a}{}^{b}:=\delta\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}-\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\mathcal{D}_{c}{}^{b}\,.

(5.12)

Equation (
5.11

\mathcal{R}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}=0
. In other words,
\mathcal{R}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}=0

5.4

\mathcal{D}=-\Lambda^{-1}\delta\Lambda\,.

(5.13)

Substituting this explicit form into the definition of the curvature (

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=\delta(-\Lambda^{-1}\delta\Lambda)-(-\Lambda^{-1}\delta\Lambda)\curlywedge(-\Lambda^{-1}\delta\Lambda)

(5.14)

Using
\delta^{2}\Lambda=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{a}{}^{b}=\delta\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}-\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\mathcal{D}_{c}{}^{b}=0\,.

(5.15)

This is the celebrated

Physical interpretation: Fake flux.

is a
pure-gauge

Consequently, if a physical system satisfies the algebraic Frobenius condition (

3.20

5.7

F

fake flux

3.20

5.7

if the associated boundary SPS curvature vanishes, the parallel transport defined by
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
is path-independent. This guarantees that there is no geometric obstruction preventing us from performing a finite gauge transformation (a change-of-slicing
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}

\delta\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{a}=0

This elegantly resolves the first limitation of the standard Wald–Zoupas criterion: in theories like 3D gravity without bulk propagating modes, the charge aspect 1-forms always satisfy the generalized Frobenius condition (
5.7

However, a critical question remains: how do we mathematically describe a system where genuine physical radiation
is

5.5
Genuine flux and the Cartan geometric framework

We have established that when a system lacks bulk propagating degrees of freedom, the apparent non-integrability of surface charges is merely a slicing artifact (fake flux), characterized by the Frobenius condition
\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}=\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}
and a globally flat pure-gauge connection (
\mathcal{R}_{a}{}^{b}=0

However, in cases where besides the boundary modes, we also have bulk modes–such as those encountered in higher-dimensional Einstein gravity–genuine physical radiation can cross the boundary. In such cases, the algebraic Frobenius condition (
5.7
) is not satisfied. It becomes impossible to find a pure-gauge connection
\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}
that satisfies the integrability equation; i.e., there is no choice of slicing that can globally eliminate the variations in the charge aspects
\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}
. Driven by the dynamics of bulk degrees of freedom, the covariant phase space intrinsically resists foliations by integrable submanifolds.

To mathematically capture this physical obstruction, we must abandon the assumption of a pure-gauge connection and elevate the SPS geometry to a complete Cartan geometric framework, allowing for non-vanishing curvature. This is acheived by considering both boundary and bulk parts of the SPS. We introduce a general

\displaystyle\delta\bm{\beta}_{a}=\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}+\mathscr{F}_{a}\,.

(5.16)

Here, the 2-form
\mathscr{F}_{a}
is the

of the SPS.

This formulation is the rigorous SPS analogue of the standard spacetime Cartan structure equation,
\operatorname{d}\!{}e=\omega\wedge e+T

Physically,

Gauge covariance and slicing independence.

A robust physical observable must transform covariantly under a change-of-slicing. Let us perform a change-of-slicing parametrized by
\Lambda^{a}{}_{b}

\bm{\beta}_{a}=\Lambda^{c}{}_{a}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{c}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{A}}_{a}{}^{b}

\displaystyle=(\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{c}\,\mathscr{A}_{d}{}^{c}\,\Lambda^{d}{}_{a}-(\Lambda^{-1})^{b}{}_{c}\,\delta\Lambda^{c}{}_{a}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{a}

(5.18)

Equation (

This provides a definitive resolution to the second limitation of the Wald–Zoupas criterion: while the strict WZ flux (

The Bianchi identity: Flux as the source of curvature.

We can now finally address the core geometric nature of genuine flux. If pure-gauge (fake) flux corresponds to a flat phase space, how does genuine physical radiation manifest geometrically?

To answer this, we enforce the nilpotency of the SPS exterior derivative (
\delta^{2}\equiv 0

5.16

\displaystyle 0=\delta^{2}\bm{\beta}_{a}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}-\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\delta\bm{\beta}_{c}+\delta\mathscr{F}_{a}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}-\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\left(\mathscr{A}_{c}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}+\mathscr{F}_{c}\right)+\delta\mathscr{F}_{a}

\displaystyle=\left(\delta\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{b}-\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\mathscr{A}_{c}{}^{b}\right)\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}+\left(\delta\mathscr{F}_{a}-\mathscr{A}_{a}{}^{c}\curlywedge\mathscr{F}_{c}\right)\,.

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{a}{}^{b}

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\mathscr{A}}\mathscr{F}_{a}

(5.21)

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{a}{}^{b}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{b}+\text{D}_{\mathscr{A}}\mathscr{F}_{a}=0\,.

(5.22)

This equation is one of the central results of this section and provides a profound physical interpretation. It reveals that the covariant derivative of the genuine flux

When genuine flux is present (

5.6
Boundary Liouville theorem

In this subsection, we establish a Liouville-like theorem governing the evolution of the volume form in the boundary SPS, utilizing the Cartan geometric framework developed in the previous sections.

Let us define the

\bm{\mathscr{A}}_{a}{}^{b}

(5.26)

The term

(5.27)

Substituting this back into the evolution equation gives:

\delta\bm{\mathcal{B}}=-\delta\ln\det(\Lambda)\curlywedge\bm{\mathcal{B}}+\sum_{a=1}^{P}(-1)^{a-1}\bm{\beta}_{1}\curlywedge\cdots\curlywedge\bm{\mathscr{F}}_{a}\curlywedge\cdots\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{P}\,.

(5.28)

Multiplying both sides by
\det(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\delta\!\left[\det(\Lambda)\,\bm{\mathcal{B}}\right]=\det(\Lambda)\sum_{a=1}^{P}(-1)^{a-1}\bm{\beta}_{1}\curlywedge\cdots\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{a-1}\curlywedge\bm{\mathscr{F}}_{a}\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{a+1}\curlywedge\cdots\curlywedge\bm{\beta}_{P}\,.

(5.29)

This fundamental relation captures the complete geometric evolution of the boundary SPS (surface charge phase-space) volume. We can draw two vital physical conclusions from this equation:

1.

Boundary Liouville Theorem (integrable case):
If the system exhibits no physical radiation (
\bm{\mathscr{F}}_{a}=0

2.

SPS Boundary Volume Dissipation (non-integrable case):
When genuine flux is present (

Vector field involution and the standard Frobenius theorem

5.8
A theorem on symplectic orthogonality

In the previous subsections, we established the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of an integrable slicing, culminating in the involution of the bulk vector fields
\hat{\bm{V}}_{A}

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{a}

Let us now assume that there exists a specific slicing of the SPS where the basis vectors

\bm{\Omega}_{ab}:=\bm{\Omega}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{a},\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{b}),\qquad\bm{\Omega}_{AB}:=\bm{\Omega}(\hat{\bm{V}}_{A},\hat{\bm{V}}_{B}),\qquad\bm{\Omega}_{aA}:=-\bm{\Omega}_{Aa}=\bm{\Omega}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{a},\hat{\bm{V}}_{A})=0\,.

\text{L}_{\hat{\bm{\xi}}}\bm{\Omega}_{aA}=0\,.

(5.39)

Since the symplectic form is closed (

7
Discussion

A distinctive feature of the Covariant Phase Space Formalism (CPSF) is that it is a very geometric formalism, involving two distinct manifolds: the physical spacetime and the Solution Phase Space (SPS). In this work, we have reformulated CPSF to treat the two manifolds in parallel, demonstrating that the concept of covariance serves as the fundamental link between them. A crucial ingredient that we identified and explored is the nature of coordinate transformations within the SPS. In particular, we established a rigorous mathematical framework for these transformations, the “change-of-slicing” technique as a local frame transformation on the SPS, and obtained several significant results.

We revisited the Wald-Zoupas criterion for integrability of surface charges
Wald:1999wa
. In our geometric framework, we established that Frobenius theorem (or dual-Frobenius theorem) provides a covariant and SPS slicing-independent integrability condition. This refined condition is robust and physically precise, as it is solely sensitive to the genuine origin of non-integrability: the radiation of bulk propagating degrees of freedom that couple to boundary modes through the flux-balance equations, like Bondi or Damour equations
Bondi:1962px
;
Sachs:1962wk
;
PhD:Damour
. This geometric clarity allowed us to draw an unambiguous line between what we have termed
fake flux
and
genuine flux
. We have shown that fake flux arises as a gauge artifact, mathematically represented by the connection on the SPS, which can be altered or eliminated by a suitable choice of slicing; precisely in the same sense that in a standard differential geometry one can always find a frame in which (Christoefel connections) vanish. In contrast, genuine flux is tied directly to the torsion of the SPS, a geometric invariant representing physical radiation that cannot be removed by a coordinate transformation. Furthermore, this comprehensive geometric framework facilitated a novel formulation of the Boundary Liouville Theorem, enabling us to carefully delineate the different physical regimes that emerge in the presence or absence of bulk propagating modes. The power and utility of this approach were then demonstrated through detailed analysis of various explicit examples, showing how our methods can be used to systematically remove fake flux and isolate the true physical dynamics at the boundary.

There are several promising directions for further consideration that naturally emerge from the geometric framework established in this work:

•

Memory Effects, Holonomies, and Algebras.
One of the main objectives of this study is to develop rigorous definition of geometric quantities–connection, torsion, and curvature–intrinsic to the boundary sector of the SPS. This formalism provides a natural language to revisit memory effects, which are traditionally categorized into soft and hard parts. A crucial open question is to rigorously distinguish slicing-dependent and slicing-independent parts of these memory effects. We propose the following geometric identification: within our framework, the soft memory effect manifests as the holonomy associated with the boundary connection on the SPS which is slicing-dependent, whereas the hard memory corresponds to the SPS torsion which is slicing-independent.
Furthermore, this geometric perspective is deeply tied to the algebraic formulation of memory effects. While the standard Barnich-Troessaert (BT) bracket provides a framework for computing the algebra of the integrable parts of surface charges (boundary modes), a complete description of the radiative phase space requires understanding the cross-commutation relations between the boundary and bulk modes. Recent progress has established brackets for the isolated boundary-boundary and bulk-bulk sectors
Adami:2023wbe
;
Ciambelli:2023mir
, but the boundary-bulk relations remain to be fully explored. Our curvature-based framework cleanly isolates genuine physical flux from the fake flux that is coordinate artifacts, thus, it provides the precise slicing-independent foundation needed to uniquely define these cross-sector brackets. This suggests an algebraic formulation of the memory effect. We postpone the explicit development of formulation of geometric/algebraic memory effect to future work.

It would be highly illuminating to employ our geometric tools–specifically the SPS connection and the slicing-independent integrability criterion–to rigorously demonstrate how a preferred slicing might naturally emerge to yield a supertranslation-invariant and physically robust definition of angular momentum.

•

Boundary Liouville Theorem and SPS Ergodicity.
In classical Hamiltonian mechanics, Liouville’s theorem guarantees the conservation of phase space volume for
closed
systems. However, by explicitly isolating genuine flux (torsion) from gauge artifacts (fake flux), our formalism accommodates
open

When genuine physical radiation crosses a boundary–such as falling into a black hole or escaping to null infinity–the boundary SPS volume form evolves, reflecting the dissipative nature of the spacetime. This geometric non-conservation inherently breaks ergodicity within the boundary SPS, providing a purely phase-space-geometric arrow of time. It is a fascinating open question to connect this generalized boundary Liouville theorem directly to macroscopic entropy production, thereby basing the second law of thermodynamics in gravitational systems (see

•

Revisiting Black Hole Thermodynamics.
The first law of black hole mechanics is fundamentally an integrability condition relating the variations of conserved charges, naturally formulating itself as a 1-form relation on the SPS. This geometric perspective makes it highly compelling to investigate the precise role of slicing changes within the first law. Physically, it is strongly anticipated that a change-of-slicing in the SPS corresponds to a transition between different thermodynamic ensembles. Furthermore, in

, the standard framework of black hole thermodynamics was extended from strictly stationary configurations to dynamical regimes, yielding an interpretation of the gravitational equations as an open local thermodynamic system. Revisiting this extended formalism, equipped with the rigorous geometric tools developed in this paper, promises to clarify the underlying thermodynamic structure and provide a robust, coordinate-independent formulation for dynamical black holes.

•

While the construction presented in this paper is predicated on the assumption of rigid boundaries, the CPSF has been recently extended to accommodate fluctuating or dynamical boundaries

Specifically, understanding how the SPS connection, torsion, and curvature transform in the presence of such dynamical boundaries remains a compelling direction for future research.

•

Geometric Structure of the BT Bracket.
The Barnich-Troessaert (BT) bracket is the standard tool for handling the algebra of non-integrable charges. However, our work demonstrates that the splitting of the charge variation into an integrable part and a flux term is inherently slicing-dependent. It is therefore crucial to investigate how this freedom is reflected on the overall structure of asymptotic and boundary symmetry algebras. Specifically, future studies should analyze how various components of the BT bracket–including central extensions and cocycles–transform under a change-of-slicing. By utilizing the SPS connection to systematically disentangle quantities that are genuinely covariant from those that are mere artifacts of the slicing choice, one could potentially formulate a rigorously “slicing covariant” BT-bracket.

•

While this paper exclusively analyzed pure Einstein gravity across various spacetime dimensions, a natural next step is to test the robustness of our geometric formulation by incorporating matter fields and extending it to alternative gravitational theories. Furthermore, whereas our primary illustrative example focused on finite-distance null boundaries, it is crucial to apply this machinery to the most widely studied asymptotic regions: the asymptotic timelike boundaries of AdS spacetimes and the null infinity of asymptotically flat spacetimes. In this appendix, we provide a detailed proof of the relation (

Consider two field space vector fields
\hat{\eta}
and
\hat{\zeta}

\eta

(B.1)

Here,
\delta_{\eta}
represents the spacetime transformation (e.g., Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
for diffeomorphisms or gauge transformation) associated with the parameter
\eta

The Lie bracket of these two vectors in field space is defined as (

\hat{[}\hat{\eta},\hat{\zeta}\hat{]}^{I}:=\hat{\eta}^{J}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}\hat{\zeta}^{I}-\hat{\zeta}^{J}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}\hat{\eta}^{I}\,.

(B.2)

The operator
\hat{\eta}^{J}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}
acts as the field space Lie derivative
\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\eta}}

\hat{\zeta}^{I}

\hat{\zeta}^{I}

\Phi

\begin{split}\hat{\eta}^{J}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}\hat{\zeta}^{I}&=\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\eta}}\left(\delta_{\zeta[\Phi]}\phi^{I}\right)\\
&=\delta_{\zeta}\left(\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\eta}}\phi^{I}\right)+\delta_{\left(\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\eta}}\zeta\right)}\phi^{I}\,.\end{split}

(B.3)

In the first term on the right-hand side, the variation acts on the argument of the transformation (the field
\phi^{I}

. In the second term, the variation acts on the parameter

\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}\hat{\zeta}^{I}=\delta_{\zeta}\big(\hat{\eta}^{I}\big)+\delta_{\big(\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\eta}}\zeta\big)}\phi^{I}\,.

\hat{\zeta}^{J}\text{
\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\bm{\Game}$\cr\raisebox{2.58334pt}{\rule{3.99994pt}{0.68889pt}}\cr}}}_{J}\hat{\eta}^{I}=\delta_{\eta}\big(\hat{\zeta}^{I}\big)+\delta_{\big(\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\zeta}}\eta\big)}\phi^{I}\,.

This completes the proof for generic tensor fields.

\begin{split}\left\{Q^{\text{\tiny{I}}}(\xi_{1}),Q^{\text{\tiny{I}}}(\xi_{2})\right\}_{\text{\tiny{BT}}}=&\,\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_{2}}\Omega-\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_{2}}F({\xi}_{1})+\mathrm{{I}}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}F(\xi_{2})\\

=&\quantity(\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2}\delta-\mathrm{I}_{\hll\hat{\xi}_1,\hat{\xi}_2\hrr}-\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_2}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1} + \mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2} \mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_1})\Theta\\
=&\quantity(\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2}\delta-\mathrm{I}_{\hll\hat{\xi}_1,\hat{\xi}_2\hrr}-\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2}\delta\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1}-\delta\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1} + \mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2} \mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1}\delta+ \mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_2} \delta\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_1})\Theta\\

=&-\mathrm{I}_{\hat{[}\hat{\xi}_{1},\hat{\xi}_{2}\hat{]}}\Theta=-Q^{\text{\tiny{I}}}\quantity({\llbracket}\xi_1,\xi_2{\rrbracket})\,.\end{split}

(D.2)

where in the above we used the fact that for any scalar
\Phi

is a scalar for any vector on the field space
\hat{\xi}

) central term vanishes.

only gets non-vanishing values through the non-scalar part of the charge
Q_{0}
, see footnote

\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\Theta+A(\xi;\Phi)=\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\tilde{\Theta}:=\tilde{Q}^{\text{\tiny{I}}}(\xi)\,,\qquad-\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\Theta-\delta A(\xi;\Phi)=-\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\tilde{\Theta}\,,

(D.3)

where

\tilde{\Theta}=\Theta+\delta W,\qquad A(\xi;\Phi)=\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}}W[\Phi],

(D.4)

The central term

Appendix E
Surface charge algebra

In this appendix we derive the algebra of integrable surface charges and determine the associated central extension. We start from the Noetherian splitting

Q^{\text{\tiny I}}(\xi)=Q^{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi)\,,\qquad F(\xi;\delta\Phi)=\not{\delta}Q(\xi)-\delta Q^{\text{\tiny I}}(\xi)\,,

(E.1)

The algebra of integrable charges is defined through the Barnich–Troessaert (BT) bracket (
3.22
).
To determine the central term
K_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}
, we compute separately the two contributions entering the BT bracket.

Variation of the integrable charge.

Using the definition of
Q^{\text{\tiny I}}
we obtain

\begin{split}\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_{2}}Q^{\text{\tiny I}}(\xi_{1})&=\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_{2}}Q_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1})\int_{\cal S}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}Q^{\mu\nu}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1})+Q_{\text{\tiny N}}\!\left([\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]+\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_{2}}\xi_{1}\right)\end{split}

(E.2)

In deriving this relation we used

\int_{\cal S}\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}\!\left(\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}Q^{\mu\nu}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1})\right)=\int_{\cal S}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}Q^{\mu\nu}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1}),\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}Q^{\mu\nu}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1}):=\big(\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}Q_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1})\big)^{\mu\nu}.

(E.3)

Contraction with the flux.

Next we evaluate the contraction of the flux with the vector field
\hat{\xi}_{1}
,

\begin{split}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}F(\xi_{2};\delta\Phi)&=-Q_{\text{\tiny N}}(\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}\delta\xi_{2})-2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\Big(\xi_{2}^{\mu}\mathrm{I}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}\Theta^{\nu}\Big)\\
&=-Q_{\text{\tiny N}}(\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_{1}}\xi_{2})-2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\xi_{2}^{\mu}\xi_{1}^{\nu}L-\int_{\cal S}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}Q^{\mu\nu}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1}).\end{split}

(E.4)

In obtaining this expression we used

-\int_{\cal S}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{2}}Q^{\mu\nu}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1})=2\int_{\cal S}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\xi_{2}^{\mu}\partial_{\alpha}Q^{\nu\alpha}_{\text{\tiny N}}(\xi_{1}).

(E.5)

Surface charge algebra.

Combining (
E.2
) and (
E.4
) we obtain

\left\{Q^{\text{\tiny I}}(\xi_{1}),Q^{\text{\tiny I}}(\xi_{2})\right\}_{\text{\tiny BT}}=Q^{\text{\tiny I}}\!\left(\llbracket\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\rrbracket\right)+K_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}.

(E.6)

The central extension is therefore

\displaystyle K_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}=-2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{d}\!{x}_{\mu\nu}\,\xi_{2}^{\mu}\xi_{1}^{\nu}L\;.

(E.7)

A similar result has been also obtained in
Freidel:2021cbc
;
Freidel:2021dxw
.
This term satisfies the cocycle condition

K_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}],\xi_{3}}-\mathrm{L}_{\hat{\xi}_{3}}K_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}+\text{cyclic permutations}=0.

The last contribution can be written in a manifestly covariant form as

\int_{\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{d}\!{}x_{\mu\nu}\,\xi_{2}^{\mu}\xi_{1}^{\nu}L=\int_{\mathcal{S}}i_{\xi_{1}}i_{\xi_{2}}\left(\operatorname{d}\!{}^{d}x\,L\right).

(E.8)
