Asymptotic theory and bias correction for the Wallace–Freeman estimator
Enes Makalic, Daniel F. Schmidt

Abstract
The Wallace–Freeman estimator is a classical invariant point estimator whose large-sample properties have not been fully developed in a modern asymptotic framework. We show that the estimator can be formulated as a penalised M-estimator with a specific penalty weight, yielding a unified route to its asymptotic analysis. This representation allows us to establish existence, consistency, an asymptotic linear expansion, and asymptotic normality under standard regularity conditions. We further derive the first-order difference between the Wallace–Freeman estimator and the maximum likelihood estimator, and show that this induces an explicit O ⁡ ( n − 1 ) O(n^{-1}) bias correction determined by the gradient of the penalty. As a consequence, the Cox–Snell bias formula for the maximum likelihood estimator extends naturally to the Wallace–Freeman estimator by the addition of a penalty-driven correction term. As an illustration, we derive the first-order bias of the Wallace–Freeman estimator for the Weibull model and show how the penalty modifies the corresponding maximum likelihood bias. These results place the Wallace–Freeman estimator within the general theory of penalised likelihood and provide a rigorous asymptotic basis for its use in parametric inference.

Introduction

The Wallace–Freeman minimum message length estimator
WallaceFreeman87
is a classical invariant point estimator that arises from the minimum message length principle
WallaceBoulton68
;
WallaceBoulton75
;
WallaceDowe99a
;
WallaceDowe99c
;
Wallace05
, but its large-sample properties have not been fully integrated into the modern asymptotic theory of statistical estimation. In particular, while the estimator has long been motivated through information-theoretic and coding arguments, its relation to standard likelihood-based and penalised estimation methods remains insufficiently understood. This paper places the Wallace–Freeman estimator within the framework of penalised M-estimation and develops a corresponding asymptotic theory. We show that the estimator admits a penalised likelihood representation with vanishing penalty weight, derive its first-order asymptotic behaviour, and obtain an explicit
O(n^{-1})
bias expansion that extends the classical Cox–Snell formula for maximum likelihood estimators
CoxSnell68
;
CordeiroKlein94
. These results provide a unified theoretical interpretation of the Wallace–Freeman estimator and clarify its role as a bias-modified likelihood procedure in regular parametric models.

Our starting point is that, up to an additive constant, the Wallace–Freeman criterion can be written as a penalised empirical risk in which the penalty combines a prior density term with the Fisher information. This representation places the estimator within the general framework of penalised M-estimation and makes it possible to study its asymptotic behaviour using standard tools from likelihood and estimating-equation theory. In particular, the vanishing order of the penalty implies that the Wallace–Freeman estimator is first-order equivalent to maximum likelihood, while still differing at order
n^{-1}
through an explicit deterministic correction. This structure also makes transparent how the penalty modifies the first-order bias, thereby linking the Wallace–Freeman estimator to classical bias expansions of Cox–Snell type and to related penalised likelihood procedures.

As an illustration of the general theory, we study the Wallace–Freeman estimator for the Weibull model. The Weibull family provides a convenient example because the corresponding maximum likelihood estimator is known to exhibit non-negligible first-order bias, especially for the shape parameter. In this setting, the general bias formula yields an explicit correction term for the Wallace–Freeman estimator, allowing direct comparison with the Cox–Snell expansion for maximum likelihood. The example shows concretely how the Wallace–Freeman penalty alters the leading bias term and serves to illustrate the theoretical results in a familiar parametric model.

2
Minimum message length and the Wallace–Freeman approximation

Minimum message length (MML)
WallaceBoulton68
;
WallaceBoulton75
;
WallaceFreeman87
;
Wallace05
is a two-part coding approach to parametric inference. The key idea is to cast parameter estimation and model selection in the framework of data compression and information theory. Given data
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the total codelength is written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta})+\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta})
is the
assertion
codelength (i.e., encoding of the model parameters and structure) and
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})
is the
detail
codelength (i.e., encoding of the data given the nominated model). The MML estimator is the value of
\bm{\theta}
that minimises the total codelength
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta})
:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta}).

(2)

The exact solution is known as strict minimum message length (SMML)
WallaceBoulton75
;
Wallace05
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\}
be a measurable partition of
\mathcal{X}^{n}
, let
\pi(\bm{\theta})
denote a prior density on
\Theta
, and define the marginal distribution

\displaystyle r(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Theta}p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}.

If
\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}\in\Theta
denotes the representative estimate attached to cell
P_{j}
, with coding probability

\displaystyle q_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x}),

(3)

then the expected SMML codelength is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{P})=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}\log q_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\log p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}),

(4)

where, for fixed
\mathcal{P}
, the representative
\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}
minimises the expected detail codelength within cell
P_{j}

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\log p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}).

(5)

The SMML solution is the partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
minimising (
4
) over all partitions of
\mathcal{X}^{n}
.

In general, computing the optimal SMML partition is NP-hard
FarrWallace02
, although tractable solutions exist in certain low-dimensional settings
FarrWallace02
;
Dowty13
. The most widely used approximation is the Wallace–Freeman codelength
WallaceFreeman87
, which replaces the global partition optimisation by a local approximation based on Fisher-information curvature and high-rate quantisation. This yields a computationally feasible approximation to SMML and leads to the Wallace–Freeman estimator studied in this paper.

3
The Wallace–Freeman estimator

Exact computation of the SMML codelength requires optimisation over all measurable partitions of the sample space. This is intractable in general: even for finite data and parameter spaces, the corresponding optimisation problem is NP-hard
FarrWallace02
. The Wallace–Freeman approximation
WallaceFreeman87
;
Wallace05
replaces this global partition search by a local approximation based on Fisher-information curvature and high-rate parameter-space quantisation. The resulting codelength is the standard computationally feasible approximation to strict minimum message length used in practice.

The next theorem states the corresponding local approximation to the SMML codelength. Its proof is deferred to Appendix
A
.

Theorem 3.1
(Local derivation of the Wallace–Freeman codelength)

Let
P^{*}\subset\mathcal{X}^{n}
denote a data-space cell of an optimal SMML partition, and let
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the corresponding assertion. Assume that:

(A1)

The parameter space
\Theta
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the model is identifiable, meaning that

p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{1})=p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{2})\quad\text{almost everywhere implies}\quad\bm{\theta}_{1}=\bm{\theta}_{2}.

(A2)

The log-likelihood
\ell(\bm{\theta}):=\log p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})
is continuously differentiable with respect to
\bm{\theta}
for almost all
\mathbf{x}
.

(A3)

The expected Fisher information matrix

J_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}\right\}

exists, is finite, and is positive definite for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
.

(A4)

For
{\bf u}
in bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

n\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{\bm{\theta}+J_{n}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}{\bf u}}\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf u}\|^{2}+O(n^{-1/2}),

uniformly for
\bm{\theta}
in compact subsets of
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
.

(A5)

The prior density
\pi(\bm{\theta})
is twice continuously differentiable and strictly positive on a neighbourhood of each
\bm{\theta}
under consideration, with the corresponding derivatives locally bounded. Consequently, the subsequent expansions hold uniformly on compact subsets of
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
.

(A6)

The sequence of discrete SMML data-space partitions
\Pi_{n}
induces a continuous parameter-space quantisation such that, as
n\to\infty
:

(i)

Assertion-mass localisation:
each data-space cell
P^{*}
induces a measurable region
W_{n}(\bm{\theta})\subset\Theta
such that

{\rm Pr}_{\bm{\vartheta}}(\mathbf{x}\in P^{*})=1-O(n^{-1})\quad\text{for }\bm{\vartheta}\in W_{n}(\bm{\theta}),

and
o(1)
uniformly outside
W_{n}(\bm{\theta})
. The induced coding probability satisfies

\displaystyle q_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\int_{W_{n}(\bm{\theta})}\pi(\bm{\vartheta})\,d\bm{\vartheta}\,\{1+o(1)\},

(6)

uniformly for
\bm{\theta}
in compact subsets of
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
.

(ii)

Cell scaling:
writing

\displaystyle W_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}+J_{n}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}C_{n}(\bm{\theta}),

(7)

for each compact
K\subset\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
, the family

\{C_{n}(\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in K,\ n\geq 1\}

is uniformly bounded and centered:

\displaystyle\int_{C_{n}(\bm{\theta})}{\bf u}\,d{\bf u}={\bf 0}.

(8)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}\bigl(W_{n}(\bm{\theta})\bigr)=O(n^{-1/2}).

(9)

(iii)

Lattice quantisation:
the normalized second moment of the local cell satisfies

\displaystyle\kappa(C_{n}(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{1}{d\,\mathrm{Vol}(C_{n}(\bm{\theta}))^{1+2/d}}\int_{C_{n}(\bm{\theta})}\|u\|^{2}\,du=\kappa_{d}+o(1),

(10)

uniformly for
\bm{\theta}
in compact subsets of
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
, where
\kappa_{d}
denotes the minimal normalized second moment of an optimal
d
-dimensional high-rate lattice quantiser.

Under these assumptions, the Wallace–Freeman codelength approximation to strict minimum message length (
4
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{87}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta})=-\log\pi(\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{2}\log|J_{n}(\bm{\theta})|+\frac{d}{2}\log\kappa_{d}+\frac{d}{2}-\log p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(11)

uniformly for
\bm{\theta}
in compact subsets of
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
.

The theorem should be read as a conditional local derivation of the Wallace–Freeman codelength from an asymptotic SMML quantisation scheme.
The parameter value minimising the Wallace–Freeman codelength,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{MML}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{I}_{87}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta}),

(12)

is called the Wallace–Freeman estimator
WallaceFreeman87
;
Wallace05
. For
d=1,2,3
, the optimal quantisation constants
\kappa_{d}
are known
AgrellAllen23
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\frac{1}{12},\qquad\kappa_{2}=\frac{5}{36\sqrt{3}},\qquad\kappa_{3}=\frac{19}{192\times 2^{1/3}}.

For large
d
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}\bigl(\log\kappa_{d}+1\bigr)\approx-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2}\log(d\pi)-\gamma,

where
\gamma
denotes the Euler–Mascheroni constant
Wallace05
.

The Wallace–Freeman approximation has been used in a wide range of statistical models, including factor analysis
WallaceFreeman92
, decision trees
WallacePatrick93
and mixture models
WallaceDowe00
. In the next section, we exploit the representation (
12
) to formulate the Wallace–Freeman estimator as a penalised M-estimator and derive its large-sample properties.

3.1
The Wallace–Freeman estimator as a penalised M-estimator

The Wallace–Freeman estimator (
12
) can be written, up to
\bm{\theta}
-independent constants, as the minimiser of a penalised empirical criterion of the form

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\theta})+\lambda_{n}\operatorname{pen}(\bm{\theta}),

(13)

where

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\theta})=-\log p(\mathbf{x}_{i}|\bm{\theta}),\quad\operatorname{pen}(\bm{\theta})=-\log\pi(\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{2}\log|I(\bm{\theta})|,

(14)

and
\lambda_{n}=1/n
. Thus,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[-\log p(\mathbf{x}_{i}|\bm{\theta})\bigr]+\frac{1}{n}\left(-\log\pi(\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{2}\log|I(\bm{\theta})|\right)\right\}.

(15)

The prior contribution favours regions of larger prior mass, while the Fisher-information term penalises regions of high local curvature. Since the penalty enters at order
n^{-1}
, the Wallace–Freeman estimator is asymptotically first-order equivalent to maximum likelihood while differing at smaller order through an explicit deterministic correction.

Let

\displaystyle\psi(x,\bm{\theta})=\nabla_{\theta}\rho(x,\bm{\theta}),

(16)

and define the stationarity equation

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\theta})+\lambda_{n}\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{pen}(\bm{\theta}).

(17)

The next theorem gives standard large-sample properties of the Wallace–Freeman estimator under regularity conditions. Its proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Theorem 3.2
(Properties of the Wallace–Freeman estimator)

Assume that:

(B1)

The function
Q(\bm{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left\{\rho(X,\bm{\theta})\right\}
has a unique minimiser at
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in{\rm int}(\Theta)
.

(B2)

\rho(x,\bm{\theta})
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and

\displaystyle{\bf A}:=\nabla^{2}_{\theta}Q(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left\{\nabla_{\theta}\psi(X,\bm{\theta}_{0})\right\}

exists and is positive definite, where
\psi({\bf x},\bm{\theta})=\nabla_{\theta}\rho({\bf x},\bm{\theta})
.

(B3)

For all compact sets
K\subset\Theta
.

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in K}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho({\bf x}_{i},\bm{\theta})-Q(\bm{\theta})\right|\xrightarrow{p}{}0.

(18)

(B4)

\lambda_{n}\to 0
and
\sqrt{n}\lambda_{n}\to 0
. In particular,
\lambda_{n}=1/n
satisfies this.

(B5)

{\rm pen}(\bm{\theta})
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\nabla_{\theta}{\rm pen}(\bm{\theta})
is locally bounded near
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

(B6)

Either (i)
\Theta
is compact, or (ii)
\Theta
is closed and
Q_{n}(\bm{\theta})
is coercive with probability approaching 1, ensuring the minimum is attained.

(B7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(X_{i},\bm{\theta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}N({\bf 0},{\bf B}),\qquad{\bf B}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\{\psi(X,\bm{\theta}_{0})\psi(X,\bm{\theta}_{0})^{\prime}\}.

(19)

(B8)

For some neighbourhood
U
of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in U}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}\psi(X_{i},\bm{\theta})-\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\{\nabla_{\theta}\psi(X,\bm{\theta})\}\right\|\xrightarrow{p}0.

(20)

Assume that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. under
p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}_{0})
. The stationarity condition for the Wallace–Freeman estimator is

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi({\bf x}_{i},\bm{\theta})+\lambda_{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\rm pen}(\bm{\theta})=0,

(21)

with
\lambda_{n}=1/n
.

(i)

Existence: A global minimizer
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in\Theta
exists with probability tending to 1.

(ii)

Consistency:
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}({\bf x})\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\theta}_{0}

(iii)

Rate of convergence:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}=-{\bf A}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi({\bf x}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{0})+\lambda_{n}\nabla_{\theta}{\rm pen}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right]+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}+\lambda_{n}),

and hence

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}+\lambda_{n}).

(iv)

Asymptotic distribution:

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}{\rm N}({\bf 0}_{d},{\bf A}^{-1}{\bf B}{\bf A}^{-1}),\quad{\bf B}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left\{\psi(X,\bm{\theta}_{0})\psi(X,\bm{\theta}_{0})^{\prime}\right\}.

(22)

Moreover, if
\rho({\bf x},\bm{\theta})=-\log p({\bf x}|\bm{\theta})
is correctly specified so that
{\bf A}={\bf B}={\bf I}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
, then

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}{\rm N}({\bf 0}_{d},{\bf I}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}).

Proof sketch.

The result follows from standard penalised M-estimation arguments. Under Assumptions (B1)–(B8) the criterion
Q_{n}
admits a global minimizer, converges uniformly on compacts to its population counterpart, and the penalty vanishes asymptotically. A first-order Taylor expansion of the estimating equation (
17
) around
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, together with the central limit theorem and the local law of large numbers for the derivative process, yields the asymptotic linear expansion and limiting distribution. Full details are given in Appendix
B
.

\square

For large sample sizes
n\to\infty
, because the penalty is of order
n^{-1}
, the Wallace–Freeman codelength is asymptotically equivalent to the well-known Bayesian information criterion (BIC)
(
Schwarz78
)

\mathcal{I}_{87}({\bf x},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML})=-\log p({\bf x}|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML})+\frac{d}{2}\log n+O(1),

(23)

where the
O(1)
term depends on the prior density, the Fisher information and the number of parameters
d
. This confirms that the Wallace–Freeman criterion is asymptotically first-order equivalent to standard information criteria, while differing at smaller order through its prior and Fisher-information terms.

3.2
Bias of the Wallace–Freeman estimator

We now quantify the first-order difference between the Wallace–Freeman estimator and the maximum likelihood estimator, and the resulting effect on asymptotic bias. Let
\ell(\bm{\theta})=\log p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})
denote the full-sample log-likelihood, and write

\displaystyle\ell_{i}=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta_{i}},\qquad\ell_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}},\qquad\ell_{ijk}=\frac{\partial^{3}\ell}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}\partial\theta_{k}},

(24)

with corresponding expectations

\displaystyle\kappa_{ij}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\{\ell_{ij}\},\qquad\kappa_{ijk}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\{\ell_{ijk}\},\qquad\kappa_{ij,k}=\left.\frac{\partial\kappa_{ij}}{\partial\theta_{k}}\right|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}.

(25)

Let

K:=K(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\{-\kappa_{ij}\}=nI(\bm{\theta}_{0}),\qquad K^{-1}=\{\kappa^{ij}\}.

The classical first-order bias expansion for the maximum likelihood estimator due to Cox and Snell
CoxSnell68
is

\displaystyle[\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE})]_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\kappa^{si}\kappa^{jl}\left(\kappa_{ij,l}-\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{ijl}\right)+O(n^{-2}),\qquad s=1,\ldots,d,

(26)

or, equivalently
CordeiroKlein94
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE})=K^{-1}A\,\operatorname{vec}(K^{-1})+O(n^{-2}),

(27)

where the
(d\times d^{2})
cumulant matrix
A
is

\displaystyle A=\bigl[A^{(1)}\mid A^{(2)}\mid\cdots\mid A^{(d)}\bigr],\qquad A^{(l)}=\{a_{ij}^{\,l}\},\qquad a_{ij}^{\,l}=\kappa_{ij}^{(l)}-\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{ijl},\qquad\kappa_{ij}^{(l)}=\frac{\partial\kappa_{ij}}{\partial\theta_{l}},

(28)

for
i,j,l=1,\ldots,d
.

The next theorem shows that the Wallace–Freeman estimator differs from the maximum likelihood estimator by an explicit
O(n^{-1})
shift, and that the Cox–Snell expansion is modified by an additional penalty-driven bias term. The proof is deferred to Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3.3
(Bias of the Wallace–Freeman estimator)

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
be generated from the model with true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
. Assume that:

(C1)

\ell(\bm{\theta})
is three times continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, the required third derivatives have finite moments, and differentiation and expectation may be interchanged up to third order;

(C2)

the observed information satisfies

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\ell(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE})=K(\bm{\theta}_{0})+O_{p}(n^{1/2}),

(29)

with
K(\bm{\theta}_{0})
nonsingular;

(C3)

the maximum likelihood estimator is root-
n
consistent:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE}-\bm{\theta}_{0}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2});

(C4)

the vector

\displaystyle{\bf a}(\bm{\theta}):=-\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{pen}(\bm{\theta})=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi(\bm{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}\log|I(\bm{\theta})|

(30)

is continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
;

(C5)

the remainder in the first-order Taylor expansion of
\nabla\ell(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML})
about
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE}
is
o_{p}(n^{-1})
, and the corresponding error term is uniformly integrable after multiplication by
n
.

Then

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE}=K(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}{\bf a}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+o_{p}(n^{-1})=\frac{1}{n}I(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}{\bf a}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+o_{p}(n^{-1}).

(31)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE})+K(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}{\bf a}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+o(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=K(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}A(\bm{\theta}_{0})\operatorname{vec}\!\bigl(K(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}\bigr)+K(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}{\bf a}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+o(n^{-1}),

(32)

where
A(\bm{\theta}_{0})
is the Cox–Snell cumulant matrix in (
28
).

Assumption (C5) is a high-level remainder condition ensuring that the Taylor expansion of the Wallace–Freeman score equation is valid at the
n^{-1}
scale and that expectations may be taken term-wise.

Corollary 3.4

If
\pi(\bm{\theta})\propto|I(\bm{\theta})|^{1/2}
, then
{\bf a}(\bm{\theta})\equiv 0
, so

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE}=o_{p}(n^{-1}),

and the two estimators have the same
O(n^{-1})
bias. If instead
\pi(\bm{\theta})\propto|I(\bm{\theta})|
, then

{\bf a}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}\log|I(\bm{\theta})|,

and minimizing the Wallace–Freeman codelength is equivalent to maximizing Firth’s penalised likelihood
Firth93
in canonical exponential families.

The proof of Corollary
3.4
follows immediately from (
30
).

3.3
Weibull distribution example

We illustrate the general bias formula using the Weibull distribution with density

\displaystyle f(x|k,\lambda)=\frac{k}{\lambda}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k-1}\exp\!\left\{-\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k}\right\},\qquad x>0,

(33)

where
\bm{\theta}=(k,\lambda)
, with
k>0
the shape parameter and
\lambda>0
the scale parameter. For observations
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, the full log-likelihood is

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta})=n(\log k-k\log\lambda)+(k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_{i}}{\lambda}\right)^{k}.

(34)

The expected per-sample Fisher information matrix is

\displaystyle I(\bm{\theta})=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{6(\gamma-1)^{2}+\pi^{2}}{6k^{2}}&\dfrac{\gamma-1}{\lambda}\\[5.16663pt]
\dfrac{\gamma-1}{\lambda}&\dfrac{k^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad|I(\bm{\theta})|=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\lambda^{2}},

(35)

with inverse

\displaystyle I(\bm{\theta})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}6k^{2}&-6(\gamma-1)\lambda\\[3.44444pt]
-6(\gamma-1)\lambda&\dfrac{\{6(\gamma-1)^{2}+\pi^{2}\}\lambda^{2}}{k^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

(36)

where
\gamma
denotes the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

Substituting the corresponding cumulant matrix
A
into the Cox–Snell formula (
27
) yields the first-order bias of the maximum likelihood estimator
TanakaEtAl17
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE})=\frac{1}{n}\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{18k(\pi^{2}-2\zeta(3))}{\pi^{4}}\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{\lambda\left\{72(\gamma-1)k\zeta(3)+6\pi^{2}\bigl(5k+\gamma(-4k+\gamma-2)+1\bigr)+\pi^{4}(1-2k)\right\}}{2\pi^{4}k^{2}}\end{pmatrix}+O(n^{-2}),

(37)

where
\zeta(3)
denotes Apéry’s constant. For the Weibull model, substituting the Cox–Snell expansion for the maximum likelihood estimator and the first-order Wallace–Freeman correction from Theorem
3.3
yields the first-order bias formula. To evaluate the Wallace–Freeman bias correction, assume independent half-Cauchy priors,

\displaystyle\pi(k,\lambda)=\pi_{k}(k)\pi_{\lambda}(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\pi(1+k^{2})}\frac{2}{\pi(1+\lambda^{2})},

(38)

which are often used as weakly informative priors for scale-type parameters
Gelman06
;
PolsonScott12
. Then

\displaystyle{\bf a}(\bm{\theta})=-\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{pen}(\bm{\theta})=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{2k}{k^{2}+1}\\[6.45831pt]
\dfrac{1-\lambda^{2}}{\lambda(\lambda^{2}+1)}\end{pmatrix}.

(39)

Applying Theorem
3.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MML})=\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE})+\frac{1}{n}\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{6}{\pi^{2}}\left\{\dfrac{(\gamma-1)(\lambda^{2}-1)}{\lambda^{2}+1}-\dfrac{2k^{3}}{k^{2}+1}\right\}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\lambda}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\left\{\dfrac{12(\gamma-1)k^{3}}{k^{2}+1}-\dfrac{\{6(\gamma-1)^{2}+\pi^{2}\}(\lambda^{2}-1)}{\lambda^{2}+1}\right\}\end{pmatrix}+o(n^{-1}).

(40)

In particular, the shape parameter of the Weibull distribution is known to be positively biased under maximum likelihood:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{k}_{\rm MLE})=\frac{18k(\pi^{2}-2\zeta(3))}{n\pi^{4}}+O(n^{-2})\approx\frac{1.38\,k}{n}+O(n^{-2}).

(41)

The additional Wallace–Freeman term in (
40
) counteracts this upward shift through the penalty gradient
{\bf a}(\bm{\theta})
, which depends on both the prior and the Fisher-information contribution. For example, when
\lambda=1
, the ratio of the
O(n^{-1})
MLE bias to the corresponding Wallace–Freeman bias for the shape parameter simplifies to

\displaystyle R(k,\lambda=1)=\frac{3(k^{2}+1)(\pi^{2}-2\zeta(3))}{\pi^{2}(k^{2}+3)-6(k^{2}+1)\zeta(3)},

(42)

which is greater than one for all
k>0
. The ratio
R(k,1)
is useful because it reduces the comparison between the two bias expansions to a single dimensionless quantity. This makes it possible to determine directly whether the Wallace–Freeman correction improves on the maximum likelihood estimator at first order: specifically,
R(k,1)>1
implies a smaller
O(n^{-1})
bias under the Wallace–Freeman estimator. More generally, the leading correction term in (
40
) indicates that the Wallace–Freeman estimator typically reduces the first-order upward bias of the Weibull shape estimator over a broad range of parameter values.

\bm{\theta}

W_{n}(\bm{\theta})

Let

n

Define the Fisher–normalised coordinate transform

so that

be a (small) region centered around
{\bf 0}
(in
u

{\rm Vol}(C)

\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle w_{n}(\bm{\theta}):={\rm Vol}(W_{n}(\bm{\theta}))=|J_{n}(\bm{\theta})|^{-1/2}\,{\rm Vol}(C).

Under the regularity assumptions concerning the prior density, the prior probability mass of the region
W_{n}(\bm{\theta})
is

\displaystyle\int_{W_{n}(\bm{\theta})}\pi(\bm{\vartheta})d\vartheta=\pi(\bm{\theta})w_{n}(\bm{\theta})\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right),

where the remainder is uniform for
\bm{\theta}
in compact subsets of the interior of
\Theta
. The
O(n^{-1})

\pi(\bm{\vartheta})
around
\bm{\theta}
; the first-order term vanishes by the centering condition in Assumption (A6)(ii).
The assertion codelength (
43
) is then approximated as

\displaystyle-\log q_{n}(\bm{\theta})=-\log\pi(\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{2}\log|J_{n}(\bm{\theta})|-\log{\rm Vol}(C)+O(n^{-1}).

(44)

We examine the detail codelength next. The expected excess detail codelength for using
\bm{\theta}
instead of
\bm{\vartheta}
is

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{\theta},\bm{\vartheta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\vartheta}}\left\{\log\frac{p({\bf x}|\bm{\vartheta})}{p({\bf x}|\bm{\theta})}\right\}=n\,D_{\rm KL}(p_{\bm{\vartheta}}||p_{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf u}\|^{2}+O(n^{-1/2}),

(45)

for
\bm{\vartheta}=\bm{\theta}+J_{n}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}({\bf u})

Because

is twice continuously differentiable and

U

\bm{\theta}

\bm{\vartheta}

\bm{\vartheta}

\displaystyle\Delta(C)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left\{\|{U}\|^{2}\right\}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),\quad U\sim{\rm Unif}(C).

(46)

For high-rate vector quantisation under the squared-error distortion, the optimal cell shape (in the sense of minimising the normalised second moment) is approached by an optimal lattice quantiser in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose cell shape may be summarised by the normalised second moment
ConwaySloane98

\displaystyle\kappa(C)=\frac{1}{d{\rm Vol}(C)^{1+2/d}}\int_{C}\|{\bf u}\|^{2}du,\quad\kappa_{d}:=\inf_{C}\kappa(C),

for region
C

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\|{U}\|^{2}\right\}=\frac{1}{{\rm Vol}(C)}\int_{C}\|{\bf u}\|^{2}du=d\kappa_{d}{\rm Vol}(C)^{2/d}\left\{1+o(1)\right\},

For a fixed
