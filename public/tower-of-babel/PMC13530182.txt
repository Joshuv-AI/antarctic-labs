Machine learning empowered proactive content caching in O-RAN
Authors: Ayaz Ahmad, Fawad Ahmad, Salman Atif, Adel Aldalbahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proactive caching in cellular networks effectively reduces backhaul load and content delay. Open Radio Access Networks (O-RAN), with their multi-vendor, distributed architecture, offer additional opportunities to cache content at various components. Integrating proactive caching with O-RAN can significantly improve performance, though the process is complex due to the tight coupling between content placement and O-RAN elements. This paper proposes an Intelligent Content-Aware Proactive Caching (ICAPC) mechanism for both published and unpublished videos. In ICAPC, video features are extracted and compressed using a 3D-CNN to generate high-dimensional feature vectors. Videos with similar features are clustered into ‘video classes’ using k-means clustering. The feature vector is mapped into a G-dimensional space, where each value represents the percentage of features from each video class. A support vector machine (SVM) is then trained using these vectors and their corresponding video popularity. For unpublished videos, the trained SVM predicts popularity based on their feature representation. Videos are cached based on descending popularity–first at the RU, then DU, and finally CU. Simulation results show that the proposed method outperforms conventional approaches by achieving higher cache hit ratios and reducing both content latency and backhaul load.

Introduction

In a conventional Radio Access Network (RAN), the system is monolithic, with hardware and software tightly coupled and working together. Proprietary solutions–such as those for radio access, baseband processing, and network management–are provided as a single package by the same vendor, leading to vendor lock-in1,2. Similarly, the equipment used in conventional RAN is typically provided by a single vendor, with little to no interoperability3,4. This lack of interoperability means that equipment from other vendors cannot work seamlessly together, limiting flexibility and increasing costs. The advent of Open RAN has introduced remarkable benefits to cellular networks. Open RAN adopts a modularized system where the RAN stack is disaggregated into different elements5. The O-RAN Alliance has split the RAN stack into modules that operate independently, with separate hardware and software, and these modules are interoperable with components from different vendors. Following the 3GPP RAN split options 2 and 7.26, the O-RAN Alliance divides the RAN stack into three key elements: the O-RAN Central Unit (OCU), the O-RAN Distributed Unit (ODU), and the O-RAN Radio Unit (ORU), as illustrated in Fig. 1. The OCU is responsible for processing the Radio Resource Control (RRC) and Packet Data Convergence Protocol (PDCP) layers7. The Physical (PHY) layer is further divided into PHY-high and PHY-low. The ODU manages PHY-high, while PHY-low is handled by the ORU. Additionally, the Medium Access Control (MAC) layer and Radio Link Control (RLC) layer are managed by the ODU and OCU, respectively. Each element can operate independently and can be virtualized (softwarized) for deployment in various cloud environments, such as the Edge Cloud, Regional Cloud, and Core Cloud as shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Three key O-RAN elements and their deployment across cloud sites.

Three key O-RAN elements and their deployment across cloud sites.

This demonstrates the modularity and flexibility of the O-RAN architecture. The RAN stack can easily be installed at different cloud sites by connecting the elements through transport networks, i.e., xHaul networks8. The deployment depends on requirements such as bandwidth and latency. The cooperation between O-RAN elements depends on several factors, including the location where the O-RAN elements are installed, the computational power of the O-RAN elements–which determines how quickly they process data and complete tasks–and the transport layer network9. This network, which includes xHaul networks, must have sufficient bandwidth and low transmission delay. The inclusion of proactive caching in cellular networks can reduce traffic load on the xHaul, thereby decreasing content delay10,11. This concept can be applied in O-RAN if its elements are equipped with cache memory. In O-RAN, the elements work collaboratively, allowing content requests to be satisfied from the cache memories of the O-RAN elements12–14. Due to the flexible and dynamic association between O-RAN elements, a dynamic content request pattern can be introduced into the network. Content caching requires certain prerequisites, such as predicting the future popularity patterns of both published and unpublished content, which is crucial for effective caching. In this regard, machine learning algorithms play a pivotal role, as they can precisely and accurately predict the popularity of both loaded and unloaded videos. For example, in this context, the 3D-CNN learning algorithm is used to leverage the features of the content to predict its future popularity. Similarly, the decision of which element to cache the content in is also important when designing the content caching policy.

Related work

The notion of content caching in cellular networks has already been explored extensively in the literature. The integration of content caching in Cloud Radio Access Networks (C-RAN), where Remote Radio Heads (RRHs) and the cloud act as caching nodes, has also been studied recently15–22. Additionally, proactive caching in heterogeneous networks has been investigated to minimize latency and maximize network capacity23–27. Similarly, content caching in vehicular networks is nowadays a research-oriented field aimed at improving caching matrix parameters28–30. Content placement in these studies is often based on the popularity prediction of the content26,31. In this regard, the advent of machine learning has significantly improved the accuracy of future content popularity predictions. Deep learning algorithms are used to predict content popularity32,33, while collaborative filtering is employed to maximize the cache hit ratio34,35. Reinforcement learning has been utilized for content placement in cellular networks, although often without considering content popularity36,37. Similarly, deep learning-based content-aware and vision-driven caching/streaming approaches38–43, which includes TrimStream Adaptive Realtime Video Streaming through Intelligent Frame Retrospection in Adverse Network Conditions” and related ViT- and keypoint-based methods44. Inspired by the predictive accuracy of machine learning algorithms and the architecture of C-RAN, the concept can be extended to O-RAN. However, several issues still need to be addressed. The primary challenges include determining what to cache and where to cache it. Additionally, accurately predicting the future popularity of content is crucial. Cooperation between different O-RAN elements for efficient content delivery is another key aspect that needs to be addressed. We have introduced the concept of proactive content caching in O-RAN, particularly for videos, by categorizing them into published videos and unpublished videos–the latter referring to content that is yet to be published. This caching is implemented across the three elements of O-RAN: RU, DU, and CU, and the performance metrics of the caching system in O-RAN are evaluated. Therefore, we have formulated a joint optimization problem to reduce content latency and backhaul load while improving the cache hit ratio (CHR). The problem is constrained by the cache capacities of the RU, DU, and CU.

Back-haul load model

The user can be served by the RU, DU, or CU. The mechanism works as follows: the request is first sent to the RU, and if the requested content is available there, the user is served. Otherwise, the request is forwarded to the DU. If the requested content is available at the DU, it is served from there; otherwise, the request is sent to the CU. The CU has sufficient memory, so it is expected that the user may be served there. If the user is not served, the request is sent to the remote server via the backhaul. If the likelihood of fulfilling the user’s request is not met by any of the cache nodes, the load on the backhaul increases. Therefore, our objective is to design an accurate caching mechanism so that only the most requested published and unpublished content is cached in the RU, DU, and CU, thereby reducing the back-haul load. Extraction of features and formation of feature vector using 3DCNN

Due to the varying nature of the types of video and user preferences, a more sophisticated and complex feature extraction is required. For this purpose, 3D-CNN is the best option to use. 3D-CNN is an extended version of 2D-CNN. In 2D-CNN, only two spatial dimensions–height and width–of the frame are used46,47. On the other hand, in 3D-CNN, a third dimension, the temporal dimension, is also used in addition to the two spatial dimensions. The spatial features are extracted from each frame, while the temporal features are extracted from the sequence of frames. Hence, the relationship between frames over time is also analyzed. This is helpful for event detection in videos. Additionally, 3D-CNN extract deeper feature from video.

Architecture of 3D-CNN

The 3D-CNN architecture comprises three main components: convolutional layers, pooling layers, and fully connected layers. Specifically, the network consists of four convolutional layers with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3 \times 3 \times 3$$\end{document} filters, each followed by a ReLU activation function. Two max-pooling layers with a pooling size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \times 2 \times 2$$\end{document} are applied after the second and fourth convolutional layers to reduce the spatial and temporal dimensions. The extracted features are then passed through two fully connected layers with 512 and 128 neurons, respectively, to generate the final feature representation used for subsequent clustering and popularity prediction.

Feature clustering

The features obtained through 3D-CNN are high-dimensional as they carry a lot of information. Caching content based on these features is very complex, so they need to be simplified. To achieve this, clustering of the features is carried out. In clustering, similar features are grouped together, and clusters are formed. These clusters are then named as video classes, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_c$$\end{document}. For instance, we have feature vectors comprising three features: movement, background color, and pitch of loudness. Videos with similar numeric values in their feature vectors are grouped together to form VCs. For example, Sports VC, Nature VC, and Music VC are examples of video classes. The feature vectors are mapped to a G-dimensional space using SVM, which produces a representation vector for each video i, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_i$$\end{document}. Each element in the representation vector indicates the likelihood or strength of video class. In our framework, the high-dimensional feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{f}_i \in \mathbb {R}^D$$\end{document} obtained from 3D-CNN captures both spatial and temporal characteristics of video contents i. To minimize the complexity, k-means clustering groups similar videos into G video classes (VCs), producing cluster centroids \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c}_g$$\end{document} for g = 1, … , G. The representation vector48,49\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}_i = [r_{i1}, r_{i2}, \dots , r_{iG}]^T$$\end{document}is then formed to quantify the association of video i with each VC. Specifically, each element \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{ig}$$\end{document} is computed as a soft-membership score using a normalized distance-based function:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{ig} = \frac{\exp \left( -\frac{\Vert \textbf{f}_i - \textbf{c}_g\Vert ^2}{\delta ^2}\right) }{\sum _{j=1}^{G} \exp \left( -\frac{\Vert \textbf{f}_i - \textbf{c}_j\Vert ^2}{\delta ^2}\right) },$$\end{document}where δ is a scaling parameter controlling sensitivity. This produces a G-dimensional vector, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{ig} \in [0,1]$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{g=1}^{G} r_{ig} = 1$$\end{document}, giving a probabilistic interpretation. The SVM is then trained using these \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}_i$$\end{document} vectors as inputs and the observed popularity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i(t)$$\end{document} of published videos as outputs, allowing the model to predict the popularity of unpublished videos. In simple terms, k-means defines the clusters, distances from a video to the cluster centroids are converted to normalized scores to form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}_i$$\end{document}, and SVM uses these vectors to map video content to predicted popularity. This unified construction ensures that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}_i$$\end{document} is mathematically consistent, reproducible, and suitable for downstream caching decisions.

In our work, the machine learning model is utilized only to create the predicted popularity values, which are then used as inputs to the optimization problem. The loss function is internal to the machine learning model’s training procedure and does not directly appear in the system-level objective function. However, conceptually, the machine learning loss function ensures accurate prediction, while the optimization objective function uses those predictions to minimize backhaul load/delay. Therefore, let us denote15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} l(\hat{p}_i(t), p_i(t)\big ) = \big (\hat{p}_i(t) - p_i(t)\big )^2 , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}as the instantaneous loss obtained by giving a prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_i(t)$$\end{document} while the actual value is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i(t)$$\end{document}, and define16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_i(T) = \sum _{t=1}^{T} l\big (\hat{p}_i(t), p_i(t)\big ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}as the cumulative loss up to period T. The ML model and the optimization problem are loosely coupled. The ML training process minimizes prediction loss to estimate content popularity, while the optimization problem uses these predicted popularities as inputs to minimize backhaul load and delay and maximize CHR. There is no direct mathematical mapping between the ML loss function and the optimization objective; the ML model does not approximate the optimization solution but supports it by improving popularity estimation.

The Support Vector Machine (SVM) is used to predict the popularity of unpublished videos. For training, a pair consisting of the representation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_i$$\end{document} and the corresponding popularity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i(t)$$\end{document} of the published video i is formed. The selection of the 3D-CNN + k-means + SVM architecture is inspired by the characteristics of the traffic data and the practical constraints of realistic network environments. The 3D-CNN is employed primarily to extract meaningful spatio-temporal features from traffic patterns, rather than to perform direct end-to-end prediction. In practical caching scenarios, the available labeled data are mostly limited and may show non-stationary behavior. Under such conditions, fully end-to-end deep models with a large number of parameters may suffer from overfitting and unstable training. The k-means stage is introduced to structure the extracted feature space into more homogeneous classes, which simplifies the subsequent classification task. Finally, SVM is adopted due to its strong generalization capability in high-dimensional and small-sample settings. By separating feature extraction from final decision learning, the proposed multi-stage framework provides improved training stability and computational efficiency while maintaining robust predictive performance.

The video contents are cached in ORU, ODU, and OCU. The most recently popular videos are cached in the ORU. As their popularity decreases, these videos are shifted to the ODU and then to the OCU. This hierarchical caching mechanism is due to the limited memory available in the ORU, which can store only a small amount of contents. If the relatively less popular content is not shifted to higher-tier elements, there will be insufficient cache memory left for newly popular videos. In addition to this, there is proper cooperation among the three elements for data transfer. For example, if a request is sent to the ORU and the requested content is not available, the request is forwarded to the ODU. If the ODU also does not have the requested content, the request is then forwarded to the OCU. Finally, if the OCU does not contain the requested content, the request is sent to the remote server via the backhaul. An appropriate, accurate, and efficient caching mechanism can reduce the probability of fetching requested video content from the remote server. This reduction decreases the backhaul load, content latency, and cache miss rate, thereby improving the cache-hit ratio (CHR) of the system. The collaborative mechanism for data transfer is illustrated in Fig. 6, and the corresponding algorithm is provided as Algorithm 2. Our proposed mechanism, Intelligent Content-Aware Proactive Caching (ICAPC), is a combination of Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2.

Baseline algorithms

In our experiment, we implement the following baselines in our simulation environment and compare our methods with them.

Least Frequently Used (LFU) algorithm

It is designed to prioritize videos that have been requested multiple times, under the assumption that they are more likely to be requested frequently in the future57–59. Each video in the cache has a counter that tracks the number of requests for that video and records the frequency of its requests. Videos are sorted in descending order in the cache based on their request counts. If two or more items have the same request count, they are sorted by the time they were last accessed. When the cache is full, the video item with the lowest request count is removed. If multiple videos have the same lowest request count, the oldest item among them is removed.

Least Recently Used (LRU) algorithm

The Least Recently Used (LRU) caching algorithm is a simple yet effective caching strategy60,61. It works on the assumption that content recently accessed is likely to be requested again in the near future. In fact, a queue or list is maintained to track the order in which content is accessed, and the size of this queue is equal to the size of the cache. For example, when a video is requested, it is moved to the front of the queue, indicating that it is the most recently used. However, if the video already exists in the cache, no new entry is added; instead, its position is simply updated to the front. On the other hand, if a new video is requested and the cache of the caching node is full, the video at the end of the queue (the least recently used content) is removed to create space for the new video.

Greedy algorithm

A greedy caching algorithm selects the most popular content based on the number of requests. The algorithm always caches the content with the highest number of requests until the cache is full. Greedy caching does not consider future content requests, leading to inefficient cache replacements62..

Local MPC and global MPC

In Model Predictive Control (MPC) caching, the most popular content in the current interval is cached for the next interval63. This algorithm assumes that the videos most requested in the recent past will continue to be highly requested in the future. The local MPC algorithm identifies the most popular content based on the content requests received at the local caching node, while the global MPC algorithm determines the most popular content based on requests received by content providers across the entire network62.

Random caching

A Random caching strategy is a simple video caching method in which video content is cached randomly without considering any specific criteria, such as popularity or frequency of requests64,65. Hence, the video content is randomly selected until the cache’s maximum capacity is reached.

Comparison of systems’s total cache hit ratio

This section compares the overall cache hit ratio (CHR) of seven content caching algorithms. The detailed CHR results for all the caching algorithms, with respect to combined cache size of O-RAN and the number of users, are depicted in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively. In Fig. 7, it is shown that the CHR of the system increases with the cache size. This is because a larger cache size allows more content to be stored in the cache memory, resulting in a higher CHR. Furthermore, it is also evident that ICAPC achieves the highest CHR compared to the other six caching algorithms. This indicates that our method, which is based on load and unloaded caching, accurately predicts user preferences and effectively utilizes the limited cache memory of the caching node. For example, at a combined cache size of 350 GB, the CHR of ICAPC is 90%, outperforming the other algorithms.

Similarly, as shown in Fig. 8, it is depicted that the CHR decreases with an increasing number of users. This is because as the number of users grows, requests for different content also increase, reducing the likelihood of storing all requested content in the limited cache memory. Consequently, the demand exceeds the available memory, leading to more cache misses and a decrease in the cache hit ratio. It is observed that the rate of decrease in CHR with respect to the number of users is lower for ICAPC compared to the other algorithms.

Comparison of total content latency

In cache-enabled O-RAN, content latency significantly affects the performance of content delivery to users. Hence, this section compares the average content latency of various caching methods. The detailed results are depicted in Figs. 9 and 10, which show the variation of content latency with respect to cache size and the number of users, respectively. In Fig. 9, it is clearly shown that the average content latency of cache-enabled O-RAN decreases with an increase in cache size. Similarly, in Fig. 10, the average content latency increases with the number of users. However, the rate of increase for ICAPC is the lowest compared to all other baseline algorithms.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a machine learning-assisted, popularity-based content caching method for both loaded and unloaded videos in O-RAN. In this method, video features are extracted and compressed using a 3D-CNN to form a high-dimensional feature vector. Then, videos with similar features are clustered using k-means clustering, and these clusters are referred to as ’video classes.’ The feature vector is mapped into a G-dimensional space, forming a representation vector. Each numeric value in the representation vector indicates the percentage of the corresponding video class’s features present in the video. Next, a support vector machine (SVM) is trained with the representation vector as input and the popularity of loaded videos as output. Finally, the representation vector of an unloaded video is provided to the SVM, which predicts its popularity. The popularity of both loaded and unloaded videos is sorted in descending order, and the most popular videos are cached first in the RU, then in the DU, and finally in the CU. Extensive simulations indicate that the proposed method outperforms conventional methods in terms of maximizing CHR while minimizing content latency and backhaul load.

Future work

Future work will focus on evaluating the proposed approach using real-world popularity datasets and incorporating social and contextual features to further enhance prediction accuracy. In addition, transformer-based visual encoders, keypoint-aware representations, and multimodal popularity predictors will be thoroughly explored to improve methodological rigor. A direct comparison with TrimStream-style architectures is also suggested, particularly for frame-level caching and adaptive streaming integration in O-RAN.
