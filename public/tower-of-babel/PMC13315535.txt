Protease highly resistant to pelleting improves amino acid digestibility in broiler chickens on pellet form of feed☆
Authors: Xuetao Liu, Songjun Jiao, Ying Zhang, Tiezhao Li, Yu Liu, Yuming Wang, Feng Zhao, Jingjing Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exogenous proteases improve amino acid utilization in broilers, yet enzyme inactivation during feed manufacturing hinders their practical applications in poultry production. This study was conducted to investigate growth performance and nutrient digestibility responses to protease varying in tolerance to pelleting in broiler chicks. Thirteen serine‑type proteases were screened for activity loss using the cold pelleting technique without steam conditioning. Three proteases showing low-tolerance (46.6% of activity loss), moderate-tolerance (29.2% of activity loss) and high-tolerance (12.5% of activity loss) to pelleting were selected for a 5 × 2 factorial feeding trial. The five experimental diets, including normal-protein control (NPC), low-protein control (LPC), and LPC supplemented with protease with low-, moderate- or high-tolerance to pelleting, were provided to day-old chicks as either mash or pellet feed for 14 days. No significant diet × feed form interactions were observed for growth performance, nutrients digestion or standardized ileal amino acids digestibility (SID). Compared with mash feed, pellet feed increased ADG (P < 0.01) and feed intake (P < 0.01) but decreased CP digestibility (P < 0.01) and SID coefficients (P < 0.05). The ADG was compromised in the LPC group relative to the NPC group (P < 0.05) and proteases supplementation restored the impaired growth (P > 0.05). Despite of no interaction between diet × feed form on SID, the magnitude of protease-induced SID improvement negatively correlated with the inherent SID of basal diet (P < 0.05). Regression analyses revealed that low-tolerance protease exerted greater SID benefits in mash diet, whereas proteases with moderate and high tolerance exerted greater SID benefits in pellet diet, as evidenced by the significant differences in regression slope (mash, P = 0.186; pellet, P = 0.002) and intercept (mash, P < 0.001). In conclusion, pelleting improved the growth performance but reduced protein and AA digestion in broiler chicks. In the low-protein regimens, proteases with moderate and high tolerance to pelleting effectively restored growth performance and AA digestibility in pellet feed.

Introduction

Exogenous proteases is considered as a promising nutritional alternative to reduce feed costs and improve the growth performance of animals (de Oliveira Sousa et al., 2025). Two common strategies are available to include exogenous proteases in feed: adding proteases on top of an existing formulation to further improve nutrient efficiency or including proteases in reduced-nutrition diets to lower the inclusion rate of protein feedstuffs. The latter strategy has prominent economic merits for poultry production with rising costs of soybean meals and other protein sources. Low-protein diets, which are formulated to reduce the total CP but meet the requirements for digestible amino acids (AA), have been widely advocated to reduce the consumption of protein feedstuffs and nitrogen excretion (Woyengo et al., 2023). Even though limiting AA were properly balanced, the growth performance of broilers receiving low-protein diets was compromised by reduced CP content (Chrystal et al., 2021; Macelline et al., 2023). Inclusion of exogenous proteases have been reported to improve protein digestibility and restore growth performance in broiler chickens fed with low-protein diets (Angel et al., 2011; McCafferty et al., 2022). A meta-analysis conducted by Lee et al. (2018) demonstrated that protease supplementation improved digestibility coefficients for nearly all individual AA. The protease-driven improvements in AA availability may partially offset inherent AA inadequacies encountered in low-protein diets.

There are vast choices of protease products available on market. Yet their efficacy varied markedly across protease products (Lee et al., 2018; McCafferty et al., 2022; Selle et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2026), complicating proper protease selection for practical application. The inherent amino acid digestibility of basal diet is a critical determinant of protease efficacy in poultry diet. Cowieson and Roos (2013) showed the probability and magnitude of protease-driven improvement in AA digestibility was lowered when inherent AA digestibility of basal diet was higher. Thermostability is also a contributor to protease efficacy. Proteases are more susceptible to thermal inactivation than other feed enzymes owing to self-degradation via autoproteolysis (Guo et al., 2019). Protease activities drop dramatically when the temperature reaches 60∼75°C (Ashraf et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2019). Given that commercial poultry feed manufacturing undergoes conditioning and pelleting at 65∼85°C, thermal sensitivity of proteases leads to significant activity loss in pellet feed. Enzyme coating has been a routine strategy to enhance thermal tolerance. Our prior work on phytases revealed that coating could retain 100% of phytase activity during steam conditioning, while uncoated phytase lost 30% to 50% of its initial activity (Wang et al., 2023). However, both uncoated and coated phytases lost 30% of initial activity after pellet compression (Wang et al., 2023), suggesting that those coating strategies failed to fully protect enzymes against damage during pelleting compression. Furthermore, coating matrix increases the costs of enzyme products and dilutes the active enzyme fraction. Therefore, intrinsic tolerance to pelleting compression is highly desirable for retaining protease activity in pellet feed. This study aimed to assess proteases inherent tolerance against pelleting compression and further verify the efficacy of proteases differing in pelleting tolerance in low-protein diets. The findings of the present work will offer practical references for protease selection and application in low-protein diet regimens.

Experimental design for animal trial

The animal experimental procedure was approved by the Animal Care and Welfare Committee of the Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, under the authorization number #IAS2025-200.

The animal experiment adopted a 2 × 5 completely randomized factorial design. The five dietary treatments were normal-protein diet (NPC), low-protein basal diet (LPC), low-protein basal diet (LPC) supplementing proteases with low-tolerance (LPC+L), moderate-tolerance (LPC+M) and high-tolerance (LPC+H) to pelleting compression, respectively. The three proteases used for animal trials were selected according to their activity loss after cold pelleting. Each experimental diet was provided to broiler chicks in two forms, either mash or pellet feed.

The NPC diet was formulated to meet the nutritional specifications recommended by the Arbor Acres broiler management manual. The inclusion level of each protease was referred to the recommendation of the manufacturers and total protease activity in feed was maintained in the same for all three proteases. Bird management

A total of 600 one-day-old Arbor Acres mixed-sex broilers were obtained from a commercial hatchery and randomly assigned to the 10 treatments blocked with body weight. There were 7 replicates per treatment and 8 birds per replicate. Each group was fed with one of the five experimental diets in either mash or pellet form. Broilers were reared in battery cages, and all management practices followed the management guide recommended by the breeder company. The trial lasted for 14 days. Chicks received ad libitum access to the feed and water throughout the trial. Feed intake and body weight gain were recorded on a per-cage basis.

Total tract nutrient digestibility

Total excreta collection method was used to determine digestibility coefficients of DM, gross energy (GE), CP, starch and crude fat. Excreta from 9:00 AM on Day 11 to 9:00 AM on Day 14 were collected, avoiding contamination from feathers or feed. The excreta were dried in a forced-air oven at 65°C for 72 h and pooled for each replicate cage. Before chemical analyses, feed and excreta samples were finely ground and passed through a 0.42 mm-mesh screen. Dry matter contents in feed and digesta samples were determined according to AOAC method 934.01 (2023). The GE of feed and digesta sample was measured in calories by using a Parr 6400 automatic adiabatic calorimeter (Parr instrument company, Moline, IL, USA). Nitrogen contents were determined via the Kjeldahl method according to AOAC method 954.01 (2023) and CP contents were calculated using a correction factor of 6.25. Starch contents in feed and digesta were determined according to AOAC method 996.11 (2023). Crude fat contents were determined according to AOAC method 920.39 (2023). The digestibility coefficients were calculated on a dry matter basis according to the following equation (Eq. (2)):(2)Nutrientdigestibility,%=(1−Nutrientexcreta×ExcretaoutputNutrientfeed×FI)×100%

Where Nutrientexcreta is the content of nutrient in the excreta; Excreta output is the total output of excreta, Nutrientfeed is the content of nutrient in the feed and FI is the feed intake from D11 to D14.

Ileal digestibility of amino acids

Ileal AA digestibility coefficients were determined using the index method (Kong and Adeola, 2014). On D15, all birds were euthanized with carbon dioxide and digesta from distal ileal section was collected by flushing with deionized water, pooled per cage. Digesta were immediately frozen at −20 °C. All digesta were freeze-dried and finely grounded with mortar. Amino acid contents in feed and digesta samples were determined according to AOAC method 994.12 (2023) with modifications. Approximately 300 mg of diet or digesta was oxidized with performic acid for 16 h, followed by acid hydrolysis with sodium metabisulfite and 8 mol/L HCl at 110°C for 24 h. Contents of 16 AA were determined by ion exchange chromatography using the post-column derivatization with ninhydrin on a Hitachi l-8900 amino acid analyzer (Ibaraki, Japan). Concentration of TiO₂ in the diet and digesta were measured according to the method referenced in Myers et al. (2004).

A nitrogen-free diet, consisting of 59.8% corn starch, 31.5% glucose, 4.2% Solka floc, 1.9% calcium carbonate, 0.3% salt and 0.5% premix and 0.5% TiO2, was used to determine the basal ileal endogenous AA loss (Basal IAAend). The standardized ileal digestibility (SID) coefficients of AA for diets were obtained by correcting the apparent ileal digestibility (AID) of AA for basal endogenous AA loss.(3)BasalIAAend=AANF×(Mdiet/Mdigesta)(4)AID,%=(1−AAdigestaAAdiet×MdietMdigesta)×100%(5)SID,%=AID+(BasalIAAend/AAdiet)×100%

Where Basal IAAend is the basal ileal endogenous loss of an AA in grams per kilogram of DM intake; AANF is the concentration of AA in ileal digesta when fed with nitrogen-free diet; Mdiet is the concentration of TiO2 in diet; Mdigesta is the concentration of TiO2 in ileal digesta; AAdigesta is the concentration of AA in ileal digesta; AAdiet is the concentration of AA in diet.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA using JMP Student Edition 18.2.2 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC) to evaluate main effects of diet, feed form and their interaction. In the absence of significant interaction, main effects were examined separately. When the interaction was significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the LSD-adjusted tests. Linear regression analyses were performed to establish the relationship between the inherent AA digestibility and the AA digestibility response to each protease. The inherent AA digestibility was the SID coefficients of AA in chickens fed with the LPC diet and the AA digestibility response to each protease was the percentage difference in SID coefficients following protease supplementation. The slope and intercept of the best-fit line for each protease were compared to indicate the efficacy difference among proteases. Data were presented as means ± SEM. Significance was considered at P < 0.05 and tendencies were declared at P values between 0.05 and 0.10.

Results

Activity loss after cold pelleting

Cold pelleting-induced activity loss across all 13 tested proteases varied from 8.7% to 46.6%, with a median loss of 30% (Fig. 1). According to their magnitude of activity loss, these proteases were categorized into three groups differing in tolerance to pelleting compression: low-tolerance (activity loss > 34%), moderate-tolerance (21.3% < activity loss ≤ 34%) and high-tolerance (activity loss ≤ 21.3%), respectively. Among the 13 tested proteases, proteases assigned to low-tolerance, moderate-tolerance and high-tolerance groups accounted for 30.7%, 46.2%, 23.1% of the total, respectively. The average activity loss was 45.1% for low-tolerance proteases, 28.1% for moderate-tolerance proteases and 11.8% for high-tolerance proteases. Discussion

In the present study, cold pelleting was used to investigate the adverse effects of pelleting compression on protease activity retention. Cold pelleting is an alternative technique to pellet feed without steam conditioning. The temperature of feed reached 65 to 85°C when passing through the pelleting die (Evans et al., 2020), a range consistent with conventional conditioning temperatures routinely adopted for commercial poultry feed production. Although cold pelleting feed exhibited a lower pellet durability index (PDI) to steam pelleting counterparts; however, diets processed via cold pelleting feed yielded superior growth performance of piglets (Evans et al., 2020). All tested proteases originate from Bacillus except Protease H and belong to serine protease family. Low thermostability of serine‑type proteases has been reported in many studies (Ashraf et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2019; Miao et al., 2023). The protease activity in pellet feed was reduced by 10% to 50% following cold pelleting, revealing pronounced tolerance differences to pelleting across these proteases. Among the tested proteases, the two coated proteases (E and F) and their uncoated counterparts (D) lost 30% of their activities after cold pelleting indicating the applied coating techniques failed to effectively protect protease against pelleting damage. This observation aligns with the result reported by Wang et al. (2023), wherein pelleting compression induced significant phytase activity loss irrespective of coating treatment. Accordingly, high tolerance to pelleting compression is critical for retaining protease activity in pellet feed.

It is well recognized that pellet diets improve growth performance by increasing feed intake and ADG in broilers (Massuquetto et al., 2019; Mohammadi Ghasem Abadi et al., 2019). Superior growth performance with pellet feed was also observed in the present study. Nevertheless, cold pelleting decreased nutrients availability as reflected by lower digestibility of CP (mash, 68.44% vs pellet, 64.31) and AA (TAA: mash, 85.29% vs pellet, 80.23%) in pellet feed. Such compromised protein and AA digestibility is mainly attributed to friction heat generated during pelleting compression. Salazar-Villanea et al. (2022) reported CP and AA digestibility was reduced in over-processed soybean meal. Heat-induced Maillard reactions between AA and carbohydrate (Martins et al., 2000) and protein aggregation (Tang et al., 2009) significantly modify protein structures, which impedes the catalytic action of endogenous digestive proteases, such as trypsin, and decreased the digestibility of protein and AA in pellet feed (Ding et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2009). Despite the negative impacts of pelleting on protein and AA digestibility, improved voluntary feed intake offsets these drawbacks of pelleting on nutrient availability (Jafarnejad et al., 2010; Massuquetto et al., 2019). Therefore, the overall growth performance was superior in birds fed with pellet feed relative to those birds fed with mash feed.

Growth performance was compromised in birds fed with the low-protein diet (LPC) compared to the NPC group and supplementation of the three proteases effectively mitigate the reduction of ADG of birds received LPC diet. These results are consistent with previous studies (Angel et al., 2011; Mahmood et al., 2017). Supplementation of proteases to the LPC diet induce higher digestibility increment for Gly, Asp, Ser, Val and Thr. In the low protein diet regimens, some nonessential AA, such as Gly, become conditional EAA. It has shown that supplementing free glycine and its equivalence Ser in the low protein diet could prevent the reduction of growth performance (Liu et al., 2024; Siegert et al., 2025). The improving digestibility of Gly and Ser with protease supplementation may benefit the growth performance of birds fed with the LPC diet.

Although no significant diet × feed form interactions were observed for growth performance parameters or SID coefficients for AA, the AA digestibility responses to protease supplementation varied considerably between broilers receiving mash feed and pellet feed. Consistent with earlier published data (Cowieson and Roos, 2013), the AA digestibility responses to protease supplementation was negatively correlated to the inherent AA digestibility of basal diet. For broilers receiving mash diet, low-tolerance protease improved AA digestibility by approximately 2.4%, outperforming moderate-tolerance (∼ −0.4%) and high-tolerance proteases (∼ 0.1%). This trend was further supported by higher regression intercept for low-tolerance protease than the moderate-tolerance and high-tolerance proteases (Fig. 2A). Conversely, for birds receiving pellet diet, proteases with moderate and high pelleting tolerance increased AA digestibility by 3.1% and 2.9%, respectively, exceeding a change of −0.3% for low-tolerance protease (Fig. 2B). With a meta-analysis of 25 studies, Cowieson and Roos (2013) found that average protease-mediated improvement in AA digestibility was around 2% when inherent AA digestibility of basal diet reached 90%. Taking 2% as the threshold to indicate the effectiveness of a protease, low-tolerance protease exerted beneficial effects exclusively in mash feed, whereas proteases with moderate and high tolerance were effective in pellet feed. Notably, low-tolerance protease lost AA digestibility response in pellet feed, indicating enzyme inactivation induced by pelleting compression. Unlike low-tolerance protease, proteases with moderate and high tolerance retained their biological activity in pellet feed and exhibited greater improvements in protein and AA digestibility compared with mash feed. The superior AA digestibility responses to moderate- and high-tolerance protease supplementation in pellet feed relative to mash feed may be explained by the reduction in inherent AA digestibility after pelleting. In line with our observations, Salazar-Villanea et al. (2022) reported that protease supplementation restored CP and AA digestibility in moderately overprocessed soybean meal. These results imply that these proteases preferentially target thermally denatured proteins.

Conclusion

In the present study, proteases supplementation improved AA digestibility, particularly for nonessential AA, and restored compromised growth performance of broilers receiving low-protein diet. Intrinsic variation in pelleting tolerance markedly influenced the efficacy of protease for improving AA digestibility in pellet feed. Low-tolerance proteases experienced functional inactivation upon pelleting processing, whereas proteases with the moderate and high pelleting tolerance yielded larger increments in AA digestibility in pellet feed compared with mash feed. The reduction in inherent AA digestibility of pellet versus mash feed partially accounts for superior responsiveness to protease addition in pellet feed. To optimize protease utilization in low-protein diet regimens, both the intrinsic tolerance to pelleting of target enzyme and inherent amino acid digestibility of basal diet should be taken into consideration.
