Towards Safer Large Reasoning Models by Promoting Safety Decision-Making before Chain-of-Thought Generation
Jianan Chen, Zhifang Zhang, Shuo He, Linan Yue, Lei Feng, MinLing Zhang

Abstract
Large reasoning models (LRMs) achieved remarkable performance via chain-of-thought (CoT), but recent studies showed that such enhanced reasoning capabilities are at the expense of significantly degraded safety capabilities. In this paper, we reveal that LRMs’ safety degradation occurs only after CoT is enabled, and this degradation is not observed when CoT is disabled. This observation motivates us to consider encouraging LRMs to make safety decisions before CoT generation. To this end, we propose a novel safety alignment method that promotes the safety decision-making of LRMs before starting CoT generation. Specifically, we first utilize a Bert-based classifier to extract safety decision signals from a safe model (e.g., a CoT-disabled LRM) and then integrate these signals into LRMs’ safety alignment as auxiliary supervision. In this way, the safety gradients can be backpropagated to the LRMs’ latent representations, effectively strengthening the LRMs’ safety decision-making abilities against CoT generation. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method substantially improves the safety capabilities of LRMs while effectively maintaining LRMs’ general reasoning performance.

Introduction

Large reasoning models (LRMs)
(
28
;
6
;
9
;
38
;
31
)
have demonstrated remarkable performance in tackling complex problems by utilizing Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
(
37
)
to enhance their reasoning capabilities. However, recent studies showed that such enhanced reasoning capabilities are at the expense of significantly degraded safety capabilities.
To solve this problem, existing research primarily resorts to safety alignment for LRMs, which performs supervised fine-tuning on manually constructed safe reasoning data
(
15
;
43
)
, so as to enhance the LRMs’ safety capabilities of identifying and
refusing harmful instructions. Nevertheless, these methods hurt general reasoning yet only achieved limited improvements in safety.

In this paper, we empirically reveal that LRMs’ safety degradation occurs
only after
CoT is enabled, and this degradation is not observed when CoT is disabled. As demonstrated in
Figure

1
, when CoT is enabled (CoT-ON), the safety capability of LRMs is very poor.
Conversely, when CoT is disabled (CoT-OFF), remarkable safety capability can be observed. This observation indicates that the safety capability of LRMs degrades as the CoT generation enabled. This observation motivates us to consider:

Can we improve the safety capability of LRMs by promoting LRMs’ safety decision-making
before
CoT generation?

.

To this end, we propose a novel safety alignment method that aligns LRMs’ latent representations with the safety decision signals. Specifically, we first utilize a Bert-based classifier to extract safety decision signals from a safe model (e.g., a CoT-disabled LRM or any other safe LLMs). This lightweight classifier can learn how to make correct safety decisions, instead of merely memorizing the predefined responses of training data. Subsequently, we feed the reliable safety decision signals outputted by the lightweight classifier into LRMs with an auxiliary linear head.
In this way, the safety gradients can be backpropagated to the LRMs’ latent representations, effectively strengthening LRMs’ safety decision-making abilities against CoT generation.

As illustrated in
Figure

2
, vanilla LRMs exhibit high reasoning but low safety, whereas safety-aligned LRMs exhibit low reasoning with relatively high safety. In contrast, LRMs trained with our proposed method achieve both impressive reasoning and safety capability. This because our method allows LRMs to preserve general reasoning performance for benign queries while directly rejecting harmful queries.

We conduct a systematic empirical evaluation, and the experimental results demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms various baselines across different benchmarks, as measured by attack success rate. Concurrently, we also evaluate the general reasoning capability of LRMs trained with our method, and the results show comparable reasoning performance to the original LRMs.

2
Related Work

Large Reasoning Models & Chain-of-Thought.

The evolution from Large Language Models (LLMs) to Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) marks a paradigm shift from reliance on statistical pattern matching to the capability for multi-step reasoning
(
9
;
28
;
6
;
38
)
. The chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting technique
(
37
)
fundamentally enables this shift toward structured reasoning by verbalizing intermediate steps, which yields substantial gains on complex tasks. However, the linear nature of CoT constrains its application to problems requiring exploration of alternatives or backtracking
(
39
)
. To overcome this rigidity, subsequent research has introduced non-linear reasoning topologies. Tree of Thoughts (ToT)
(
39
)
and Graph of Thoughts (GoT)
(
2
)
formalize reasoning as a deliberative search through a space of intermediate steps, enabling essential capabilities like lookahead and backtracking for global planning. In a parallel direction, Self-Consistency
(
35
)
introduces a probabilistic ensemble approach, sampling multiple diverse reasoning paths and aggregating their answers to mitigate single-path variability and improve robustness.
Complementarily, reasoning can be improved via training-time interventions, ranging from self-training loops such as STaR
(
40
)
to process supervision that provides step-level feedback for intermediate reasoning (e.g., process-supervised reward models
(
19
)
and process-based feedback studies
(
34
)
), rather than supervising only final outcomes.

LLM & LRM Safety.

Ensuring the safety of LLMs is a foundational deployment requirement. Standard practice employs post-training alignment paradigms such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and safety fine-tuning
(
1
;
25
;
33
)
. However, these defenses are often circumvented by sophisticated adversarial and jailbreaking attacks that exploit the models’ instruction-following nature
(
36
;
44
;
22
)
. The advent of LRMs amplifies these concerns by introducing novel vulnerabilities inherent in their reasoning processes. The capacity for structured deduction can be manipulated to bypass safeguards (e.g., via reasoning-enabled jailbreaks) or to conceal harmful objectives within intermediate steps
(
18
;
42
)
. Consequently, research is increasingly focused on process-oriented safety
(
15
;
32
;
43
)
, shifting the focus from merely evaluating final outputs to scrutinizing the integrity of the reasoning chain itself. However, these process-oriented approaches primarily intervene during the reasoning chain. In contrast, our work identifies a more fundamental vulnerability: safety degradation in LRMs occurs only after CoT is enabled, suggesting that it is crucial to make safety decisions before the CoT generation begins.

3
Methodology

In this section, we propose our method that promotes safety decision-making before CoT generation, and we call this method
PreSafe
. Specifically, we first extract safety decision signals from a safe model into a Bert-based classifier, which serves as a parametric representation of the safety decision capability. Then, we use the Bert-based classifier to provide safety supervision for LRMs by introducing an auxiliary linear head on top of the reasoning model and simultaneously performing safety alignment of latent representations and standard safety alignment training. Notably, this linear head is only used during training, and will be discarded in the inference stage. In other words, only a single LRM trained by our method without any additional modules will be used for inference.

3.1
Extracting Safety Decision Signals

Firstly, we feed multiple queries into a safe model (e.g., a CoT-disabled LRM or any other safe LLMs) and collect the corresponding safety decisions from the safe model. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a set of user queries where
N
is the number of queries in the set. For each qeury
\bm{x}_{i}
, we obtain a binary safety decision signal
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
according to whether the safe model rejects the query
\bm{x}_{i}
. Specifically, if the safe model refuses
\bm{x}_{i}
, we can collect the corresponding safety decision as signal
y_{i}=1
, otherwise
y_{i}=0
. In this way, we can obtain a binary classification dataset for recording safety decision patterns, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cls}}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Given the collected dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cls}}
, we would like to train a binary classifier
J_{\psi}
that can learn how to make correct safety decisions, instead of merely memorizing the predefined responses of user queries.
J_{\psi}
is instantiated as a pre-trained Bert
(
8
)
followed by a linear classification layer. When processing an input query
\bm{x}_{i}
, the Bert-based classifier
J_{\psi}
encodes it into a sentence-level representation
h_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i})
, and then computes a scalar logit
g_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i})
. By applying a sigmoid function
\sigma
on the logit
g_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i})
, we can obtain the refusal probability for the query
\bm{x}_{i}
:

\displaystyle J_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i})=\sigma(g_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i}))=\sigma(\bm{w}^{\top}h_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i})+\bm{b}),

(1)

where
\bm{w}
and
\bm{b}
are the parameters of the linear classifier layer.
The training objective of
J_{\psi}
is to fit the collected dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cls}}
, by using then binary cross-entropy loss
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{judge}}(\psi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}}(J_{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i}),\,y_{i}).

(2)

After training with the above objective, the Bert-based classifier
J_{\psi}
can be an effective classifier that outputs a desirable safety decision probability
J_{\psi}(\bm{x})
about whether the input query
\bm{x}
should be refused.

3.2
Alignment of Safety Decisions

In the safety alignment stage, we leverage the well-trained safety decision classifier
J_{\psi}
to output the refusal probabilities of a set of queries, which will be utilized as safety supervision signals for the unsafe LRM
R_{\theta}
. Specifically, given a set of
{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}=\{\bm{x}^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
, for each query
\bm{x}^{\prime}_{j}
, we can obtain a refusal probability
p^{\prime}_{j}\in(0,1)
. In this way, we collect a probability-labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prob}}=\{(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{j},p^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
. It is noteworthy that the scale of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prob}}
could be much larger than
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cls}}
, which means that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prob}}
can provide more safety decision supervision in the safety alignment stage. This advantage is actually because we have trained the classifier
J_{\psi}
, which has learned how to make correct safety decisions and can generalize to much more unseen data. If we directly use
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cls}}
collected from the safe model for safety alignment, the collected safety decision signals would be limited, because it is difficult to collect a very large scale of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cls}}
, given that repeatedly executing LRM inference is time-consuming.

Then, in order to better absorb the safety decision capability by learning from the collected probability-labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prob}}
, we introducce a linear head
H_{\phi}
on top of the unsafe LRM
R_{\theta}
.
Specifically, for each query
\bm{x}_{j}
, we use
R_{\theta}
to run a forward pass to collect the last-layer representation
h_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
before CoT generation, where
L
is the input length and
d
is the dimension. After that, we impose masked mean pooling on
h_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{j})
to obtain a sentence-level representation
z_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Finally, the linear head
H_{\phi}
maps
z_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{j})
to a scalar logit
g_{\phi}(\bm{x}_{j})
, and the sigmoid function
\sigma
further transforms
g_{\phi}(\bm{x}_{j})
into a probability
s_{H}(\bm{x}_{j})
predicted by the model:

\displaystyle p_{j}=\sigma(g_{\phi}(\bm{x}_{j}))=\sigma(H_{\phi}(z_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{j}))).

(3)

To promote the safety decision-making of the unsafe LRM
R_{\theta}
, we need to fine-tune the unsafe LRM
R_{\theta}
by fitting the probability-labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prob}}
according to the binary cross-entropy loss
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}(\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{M}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{M}\mathrm{\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}}(p_{j},p_{j}^{\prime}).

(4)

Finally, we employ standard supervised fine-tuning as the primary behavioral alignment task, training the model to output correct answers with CoT for benign queries while directly refusing harmful ones before CoT. The final objective of our proposed PreSafe method combines both
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}(\theta)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}(\theta,\phi)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PreSafe}}(\theta,\phi)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}(\theta)+\lambda_{\mathrm{align}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}(\theta,\phi),

(5)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{align}}>0
controls the degree of strengthening the safety decision-making capability. By optimizing the unsafe LRM
R_{\theta}
with the above objective,
R_{\theta}
not only maintains its original reasoning performance, but also obtains enhanced safety performance, because its latent representations are reshaped to better encode the safety decision capability.

Inference Phase.

It is noteworthy that the Bert-based classifier
J_{\psi}
and the linear head
H_{\phi}
introduced on the top of
R_{\theta}
are only used in the training phase. In other words, in the inference phase, we deployed a single LRM trained by our proposed PreSafe method that has both strong reasoning and safety capabilities, where no additional inference-phase components or overhead would be incurred. In practice, this is also the most practical setting that we can frequently encounter in real-world scenarios.

4
Experiments

In this section, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our method PreSafe through extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks that reflect both the safety robustness and general reasoning capabilities of LRMs. In addition to comparisons with state-of-the-art baselines, we provide detailed analyses of the method’s internal mechanism and its impact on benign usability.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Models.
To systematically assess the impact of PreSafe, we selected six distinct reasoning models for evaluation. These include the DeepSeek-R1-Distill series (Distill-Qwen-7B/14B and Distill-Llama-8B)
(
9
)
, the Qwen3 series (4B/8B)
(
38
)
, and Skywork-OR1-7B
(
10
)
. We conducted a comparative analysis of the safety performance for each model before and after the application of PreSafe. The teacher models employed and details are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

Training Data.

The training dataset consists of adversarial variants derived from 11 high-risk intent categories and benign queries. Specifically, the dataset comprises approximately 5,000 harmful queries and 3,000 benign queries. The training data for the BERT-based classifier amounts to roughly half of this volume. All data were generated and augmented via GPT-5
(
28
)
to cover diverse adversarial attack styles, followed by deduplication and randomization. Details regarding the data generation process are provided in Appendix
B
.

Evaluation.

We evaluate model safety on two complementary benchmark sets. First, we use JailbreakBench
(
3
)
as a unified jailbreak evaluation suite that provides standardized evaluation prompts and protocols. Second, to enable a fair comparison with stronger methods, we additionally evaluate on two widely adopted safety datasets: StrongReject
(
29
)
(313 policy-violating prompts) and WildJailbreak
(
16
)
(we use the 250-prompt subset selected by
15
in our main experiments).

Attacks and Defenses.
For the jailbreak attacks on JailbreakBench, we use Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG)
(
44
)
, Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement (PAIR)
(
4
)
, and the hand-crafted jailbreak prompts from JailbreakChat (JBC). We compare our method against a set of representative defense baselines provided in JailbreakBench, including SmoothLLM
(
27
)
, Perplexity Filter
(
13
)
, Synonym Substitution, Remove Non-Dictionary, and Erase-and-Check
(
17
)
. In addition, we include two stronger safety-alignment baselines: SafeChain
(
15
)
and R2D
(
43
)
.

Metrics.

We employ the Attack Success Rate (ASR) for safety evaluation. It is calculated as the proportion of malicious inputs that the model fails to refuse. A lower ASR indicates better safety performance.

LLM-as-a-Judge.

In this paper, we select DeepSeek-V3.2
(
20
)
as the judge model to assess ASR, leveraging its broad knowledge coverage and robust capability to ensure reliable and authoritative judgment. The prompts given to DeepSeek-V3.2 are shown in Appendix
C.1
.

Experimental Configuration.

All models are fine-tuned with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
11
)
and optimized employing the AdamW optimizer
(
23
)
. More detailed parameter settings are available in Appendix
A.3
.

4.2
Main Results

Table

1
summarizes the ASR of PreSafe in six reasoning models of the backbone under three representative JailbreakBench transfer attacks (PAIR, GCG, and JBC), together with two widely used safety benchmarks (StrongReject and WildJailbreak). Refusals are judged by DeepSeek-V3.2 following the JailbreakBench evaluation protocol. Overall, PreSafe consistently reduces ASR across different models (DeepSeek, Qwen3, and Skywork) and parameter scales, suggesting that promoting safety decision-making before chain-of-thought (CoT) generation yields robust benefits.

Under PAIR, the base models exhibit high ASR (44.4%–69.1%), whereas PreSafe suppresses ASR to near-zero or low single digits across all backbones (0.0%–3.7%). Similarly, on GCG, PreSafe achieves low ASR in most cases (0.0%–6.1%). While the Perplexity Filter can be competitive on GCG for certain backbones, PreSafe remains comparable in magnitude and improves further on several settings. On JBC, where prompts are more natural and thus harder to detect via perplexity anomalies, PreSafe maintains very low ASR (0.0%–3.0%), while the Perplexity Filter is often close to the undefended baseline. Taken together, these results indicate that PreSafe’s gains do not rely on detecting specific string artifacts, but instead stem from improving the model’s safety decision capability.

On StrongReject, PreSafe reduces ASR from 26.0%–67.0% (base model) to 2.9%–7.1% across the evaluated backbones, generally surpassing classic preprocessing defenses and also outperforming SafeChain in this evaluation setting. On the more challenging WildJailbreak subset, PreSafe still provides substantial absolute reductions compared to the undefended baseline (from 51.2%–64.4% down to 14.0%–24.8%), though non-trivial residual ASR remains, reflecting the difficulty of jailbreak under strong attacks and aggressive decoding. In contrast, some stronger baselines exhibit less consistent behavior on this benchmark (e.g., R2D achieves low ASR on certain transfer attacks but remains high on WildJailbreak for multiple backbones).

Across diverse backbones and attack methods, PreSafe yields consistent and substantial reductions in ASR, establishing a clear and robust advantage over existing defenses on established benchmarks like StrongReject and WildJailbreak. These results validate the core thesis of our work: by promoting safety decision-making before the CoT generation, our method addresses the LRMs’ safety degradation that arises from enabling CoT.
Unlike post-hoc filtering or other safety alignment methods, PreSafe enhances LRMs’ safety capability to recognize and refuse a broader spectrum of harmful queries.

4.4
Detailed Analysis

To characterize where PreSafe modifies the backbone, we compare the base model parameters with the parameters after training.
Concretely, we load the original checkpoint and the checkpoint after safety alignment with our methods, and compute a relative update magnitude for each weight tensor
\bm{W}
:

r(\bm{W})=\frac{\lVert\bm{W}^{(1)}-\bm{W}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\bm{W}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}+\epsilon}.

(6)

We group tensors by transformer layer index and by module type (MLP: gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj, attention: q/k/v/o_proj), and report the mean
r(\bm{W})
within each group.

Figure

5
visualizes
\log_{10}r(\bm{W})
as layer-wise heatmaps for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B/LLama-8B.

As shown in
Figure

5
, the update distribution is highly localized. The largest changes concentrate in the MLP expansion projections (gate_proj and up_proj), whereas the MLP output projection (down_proj) and attention projections (q/k/v/o_proj) remain comparatively stable. This pattern indicates that PreSafe does not broadly perturb the pretrained backbone but selectively modulates specific feed-forward pathways responsible for feature transformation. We attribute this sparse update pattern to the training signal introduced by the linear head. During PreSafe, the linear head is trained to predict safety scores from the model’s latent representations before CoT generation. The backpropagated gradients thus encourage the backbone to encode richer safety-relevant information into these early representations. The dominance of updates in gate_proj and up_proj is consistent with this goal, as these layers gate and amplify features for downstream processing. Conversely, the relative stability of down_proj indicates that the projection back to the residual stream is largely preserved, which is consistent with maintaining the model’s generation and reasoning behavior. Finally, the updates are more pronounced in deeper layers, suggesting that safety decision signals extraction primarily reshapes higher layers closer to the output.

4.5
Over-Refusal Evaluation

Most models exhibit pronounced over-refusal tendencies following safety alignment. In this part, we examine whether our method induces similar over-refusal behaviors. We evaluate the base models used throughout this paper, including the DeepSeek-R1-Distill series (Distill-Qwen-7B/14B and Distill-Llama-8B), the Qwen3 series (4B/8B), and Skywork-OR1-7B, and compare them with models trained by SafeChain and R2D.

To quantify the trade-off between benign usability and harmful refusal, we sample 500 benign queries from Alpaca
(
30
)
and use all StrongReject queries as the harmful set. We first report the refusal rate on benign and harmful requests. However, these two numbers move in opposite directions and are not directly comparable across methods. Therefore, we additionally compute an
F1
score by treating
refusing harmful requests
as the positive class. Under this definition, the harmful refusal rate corresponds to recall, while benign refusal contributes to false positives and thus lowers precision. In other words, this
F1
score summarizes how well a method refuses harmful requests without unnecessarily refusing benign ones, providing a single scalar measure of the balance of safety and usability.

As shown in
Table

2
, SafeChain reduces benign refusal but provides limited protection against harmful requests. R2D achieves high harmful refusal, but often does so at the cost of substantially increased benign refusal, indicating notable over-refusal. In contrast, our method achieves a superior balance: it consistently attains high harmful refusal while maintaining relatively low benign refusal, which is also reflected by consistently higher
F1
scores across all evaluated backbones. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our method still incurs non-trivial benign over-refusal in some settings. Reducing this residual over-refusal to further improve usability without compromising safety capability is an important direction for future work.

4.6
Ablation Studies

Figure 6:
Performance analysis of direct distillation.

In this section, we isolate the contributions of the two key components in PreSafe. Specifically, we conduct two targeted ablations: (i) removing the extracting safety decision signals step, which eliminates decision-level supervision and naturally reduces the objective to direct distillation, and (ii) removing the linear head by setting
\lambda_{\text{align}}=0
. All experiments are conducted on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. More details are shown in Appendix
A.5
.

Decision Signals Extraction.

We first ablate the safety decision signals extraction step. Unlike PreSafe, which obtains decision-level safety supervision via a Bert-based classifier, removing this stage leaves us without an explicit safety decision signal. As a result, the training objective naturally reduces to direct distillation. We match next-token distributions and distill the output distribution of a safer teacher into the LRM. Concretely, we treat the same base model under CoT-OFF decoding as the teacher
p_{\text{off}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x})
and the CoT-ON model as the student
p_{\text{on}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x})
. The student is trained by minimizing the token-level KL divergence between teacher and student next-token distributions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\;\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\text{off}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x},\bm{y}_{<t})\,\|\,p_{\text{on}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x},\bm{y}_{<t})\big)\big],

where
p(\cdot\mid\bm{x},\bm{y}_{<t})
denotes the next-token distribution at position
t
.
As shown in
Figure

6
, the direct distillation model yields only limited improvements in safety robustness across both benchmarks, and remains consistently worse than our method.
This suggests that matching output distributions alone is insufficient to reliably transfer the teacher’s safety decision capability, whereas explicitly extracting a safety decision signal provides more effective supervision.

Linear Head.
We ablate the contribution of the linear head
H_{\phi}
by setting the alignment weight
\lambda_{\text{align}}
in our training objective to zero.
From an optimization perspective,
\lambda_{\text{align}}
controls the strength of the linear head
H_{\phi}
supervision relative to the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) objective. When
\lambda_{\text{align}}=0
, the objective reduces to standard SFT under our safety alignment recipe, i.e., without the additional alignment signal that shapes latent representations before CoT generation. As shown in
Table

3
, our full method achieves lower ASR than the
\lambda_{\text{align}}=0
variant on both StrongReject and WildJailbreak.
This comparison indicates that incorporating the linear head supervision provides an additional effective training signal beyond SFT, improving robustness against jailbreak attempts.

Conclusion

In this paper, we identified a critical vulnerability in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), where the generation of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) significantly suppresses the model’s intrinsic safety capabilities. To address this, we proposed a novel framework aimed at promoting the model’s safety decision-making before CoT generation starts, thereby mitigating the adverse impact of CoT generation. By leveraging a Bert-based classifier to guide an auxiliary linear head, our method effectively backpropagates safety supervision into the LRM’s latent representations during the safety alignment training. Our method drives the model to capture risk signals within its hidden states before CoT generation starts, thereby mitigating the suppression effect of reasoning chains. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method significantly reduces the attack success rate against various attacks without compromising reasoning capability.

Impact Statements

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Large Reasoning Models’ safety. The publicly available jailbreak datasets utilized in our experiments are deployed strictly within a controlled environment for the sole purpose of safety evaluation. This protocol ensures that no new jailbreak datasets are generated and prevents potential misuse. Furthermore, the data employed for training is intended exclusively for bolstering model safety. It does not introduce additional ethical concerns.

CoT-OFF Capability Gap

Although the introduction motivates PreSafe by the safety potential of a model’s CoT-OFF state, an important practical concern is whether disabling CoT generation preserves the model’s general capabilities. Table

First, it empirically supports our premise that CoT-OFF can behave qualitatively differently from the standard CoT-enabled decoding regime, and thus can be exploited as a distinct operating point for extracting safety decision signals. Second, it highlights that directly deploying CoT-OFF as the final inference mode would incur a non-negligible utility cost, making it unsuitable as a standalone defense. We analyzed the decoding parameters of PreSafe to evaluate their robustness under various generation conditions. As illustrated in
