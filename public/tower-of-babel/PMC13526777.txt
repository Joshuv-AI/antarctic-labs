Public responses about air quality in the world’s ten most populous countries
Authors: Noah Lim, Alessandro Del Ponte, Lina Ang, Wei Jie Seow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how people view and react to outdoor air quality is crucial to effectively managing and responding to air pollution, which is a major threat to global public health. Using a quota sample of 10,618 respondents from 1,372 localities across the world’s ten most populous countries (Bangladesh, Brazil, China, Indonesia, India, Mexico, Nigeria, Pakistan, Russia, and the United States), we assessed the association between exposure to fine particulate matter with diameter ≤ 2.5 micrometers (PM2.5) and people’s knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, practices, and concern about air quality. Overall, we find an inverse association between air pollution exposure and knowledge about air quality, and a positive association between air pollution exposure, practices, attitudes, and concern about air quality. We also identified significant interactions with demographic characteristics such as age, sex, education, place of residence, political ideology, and marital, employment, or smoking status, which can inform targeted interventions among vulnerable populations.Subject terms: Risk factors, Environmental impact, Social sciences

Introduction

Ambient (outdoor) air pollution is a leading global environmental risk factor, accounting for an estimated 4.2 million deaths worldwide and contributing to a broad range of adverse health outcomes, including mental health issues1,2, cognitive decline3,4, and overall mortality5. Fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 μm or less (PM2.5) is a major contributor to these health effects6. Air pollution are often higher in densely populated areas, where emissions from transportation, residential heating, urbanization, and industrial activities are concentrated. Air pollutants, such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), tend to increase with population density, resulting in substantial health and mortality costs7. Therefore, ambient air pollution is often higher in densely populated cities, where traffic emissions and intensive land use contribute to substantially higher PM2.5 concentrations compared to less densely populated suburban or rural areas. The systematic variation in air pollution raises the question of whether individuals perceive and respond to air quality differently depending on their level of exposure. Understanding the relationship between air pollution exposure and public knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and protective practices attitudes is important, as both perceived risk and preparedness for poor air quality episodes can shape individual behavior and, altimately, public health outcomes.

People’s knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, practices, and emotions regarding air quality (in short, KAPPE) have received considerable research attention because of their relevance to public health. De Pretto and colleagues used a convenience sample of about 300 residents of the Kuala Lumpur area in Malaysia to study the relationship between information about air quality amid haze episodes and knowledge, attitudes, and practices about air quality8. They found a positive relationship between information about air quality and better (i.e., more protective) practices8. A study in Coyhaique, Southern Chile, on about 300 residents found a positive association between average daily PM2.5 concentrations and health risk perceptions attributable to air pollution9. Park and colleagues10 and Ryan and colleagues11, conducted studies involving approximately 40 residents of North Carolina and 100 residents of Ohio (including 40 adolescents) and found that wearing an air pollution monitor improved knowledge, awareness, and practices about air quality. Huang and colleagues recruited 371 participants from Southern Taiwan to study the factors influencing the adoption of air pollution protective measures, and found a positive relationship between knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and protective practices about air quality12. In a cross-national study, Del Ponte and colleagues13 used diverse national samples (totaling about 1250 participants) in China, India, and the United States, recruited from SoJump.com14 and MTurk15 to develop a knowledge questionnaire about air quality. Del Ponte and colleagues also conducted a longitudinal study using a nationally diverse sample from the United States (n = 212) and found that exposure to wildfire episodes was associated with an 8% increase in knowledge scores16.

Previous studies have also examined demographic differences in air quality knowledge. These studies generally reported a positive association between knowledge about air quality and age17, male sex8, education13,18,19, household income13,20, and respiratory symptoms16,20. However, in Bangladesh, people who do not have a bachelor’s degree demonstrated greater knowledge about air quality21.

To date, existing studies have largely focused on case studies or convenience samples drawn from individual cities, limited regions within a country, or a small number of countries. In contrast, overarching global patterns could be studied systematically and on a large scale in a cross-country framework. Examining overarching global associations between PM2.5 and KAPPE is important because, although national air pollution policies exist, cities and subnational jurisdictions often design and implement their own consequential pollution-control measures (e.g., London’s, New York City’s, or Singapore’s congestion pricing policies). As a result, two megacities in distant countries, such as New Delhi and Jakarta, are more similar to each other in terms of air pollution profiles than Jakarta is to Yogyakarta, despite both being in Indonesia.

In this paper, we address this research gap by assessing the association between PM2.5 concentration and KAPPE scores in 1372 localities across the world’s ten most populous countries—India, China, the United States, Indonesia, Pakistan, Nigeria, Brazil, Bangladesh, Russia, and Mexico, which account for 57% of the world’s population22 and more than 60% of global CO2 emissions23. We conducted an online survey of 10,618 respondents from the ten countries, using validated scales on air quality KAPPE across all countries13. Finally, we assess the associations between KAPPE scores as well as PM2.5 concentrations and demographic characteristics, both within and across countries.

Survey respondents were recruited using quota sampling to approximate national representativeness by age and sex, with minor deviations in China, Mexico, and Russia for the latter. The sample also approximates national representativeness by age in the younger and middle-aged groups, which constitute the majority of our sample. Some limitations in age representativeness arose because the Qualtrics survey company faced challenges in recruiting participants aged over 60, leading to an oversampling of younger adults in certain countries. The best representativeness by age was achieved in Bangladesh, Brazil, Nigeria, Pakistan, Russia, and the United States. Approximately 90% of the study population resides in urban areas. An outlier is the United States, where around two-thirds of the respondents live in suburban areas. In addition, in all countries, the proportion of respondents who do not have a bachelor’s degree is higher than the national benchmark (all ps < 0.001). Given that education is often associated with several KAPPE measures in previous work13,16,24, we reweighed the study population using national benchmarks to achieve representativeness by education. Finally, the associations between KAPPE measures were positive for all relationships (except for the association between knowledge and concern about air quality, which is n.s.; Supplementary Table 2). The largest Pearson’s correlation coefficients are between attitudes and risk perceptions (r = 0.541 p < 0.001); risk perceptions and concern about air quality (r = 0.541, p < 0.001); and attitudes and concern about air quality (r = 0.480, p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the smallest ones were the associations between each measure and knowledge about air quality (attitudes: r = 0.085; perceptions: r = 0.040; practices: r = 0.037, all ps < 0.001).

Association between PM2.5 concentrations and KAPPE scores

Air quality knowledge, assessed using the 10-item air quality knowledge (AQIQ) questionnaire (see “Survey Instruments” in the Methods section for details), varied across demographic groups. Participants living in areas with higher PM2.5 concentrations tended to have lower knowledge scores (Table 2). However, the magnitude of this association was modest: each 50 μg/m3 increase in PM2.5 concentration was associated with a 0.2-point reduction in the knowledge score (p < 0.05). The point estimates were largely unchanged after adjustment for demographic covariates. Specifically, we found that females, respondents who do not have a bachelor’s degree, people under 40 years of age, current smokers, and conservatives knew less about air quality. For example, being female was associated with a 0.17-point lower knowledge score (p < 0.01).

Using multivariable linear regression, we found a positive and marginally significant association between air quality attitudes and PM2.5 concentrations (Table 2). Moreover, we found that living in rural areas, being single, or not currently being employed, were associated with lower support for air pollution prevention and control measures, whereas conservative political ideology was associated with greater support for these measures (all ps < 0.01).

We found no statistically significant association between PM2.5 concentration and air quality perceptions (Table 2). This lack of association was likely driven by opposing patterns across countries. For example, participants in India perceived air pollution as a serious threat, consistent with the country’s among-the-highest PM2.5 concentrations globally (Table 3). Conversely, participants in the United States perceived air pollution as less threatening, reflecting its comparatively low levels of ambient PM2.5. However, this pattern was not consistent across countries. In Bangladesh and Nigeria, despite relatively high ambient PM2.5 concentrations, fewer participants perceived air pollution as a serious threat to health (Table 3). We found that females and individuals with more conservative political ideologies reported stronger perceptions of the sources and health risks of air pollution (both ps < 0.01), whereas smokers, people who are single, not currently employed, or those who live in rural areas reported weaker perception (all ps < 0.05).

For air quality practices, we found a positive association between PM2.5 concentrations and more frequent adoption of protective behaviors during periods of poor air quality after adjustment for potential confounders (Table 2). For example, each 50 μg/m3 increase in PM2.5 concentrations was associated with a 1.7-point increase in the practice score (p < 0.01). Females and people without a bachelor’s degree were more likely to engage in protective practice, whereas those living in rural areas were less likely to do so (all ps < 0.01). When examined by country, the top three countries with the highest PM2.5 concentrations—Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan—were also those in which respondents reported the higher use of protective practices during periods of poor air quality (Table 3). On the contrary, respondents from Russia and the United States, where PM2.5 concentrations were the lowest, reported the least frequent adoption of protective behaviors among the ten most populous countries.

Finally, in terms of concern about air quality, we found a positive and statistically significant association between PM2.5 concentration and concern (worry, anxiety, and fear) about air quality. Females (p < 0.01), younger people (p < 0.05), and conservatives reported greater concern about air quality, whereas people living in rural areas, singles, those who were not currently employed, and current smokers reported lesser concern (all ps < 0.01). Among individual countries, we found that participants in India and Pakistan reported the highest level of concern, consistent with the higher PM2.5 concentrations observed in these countries (India: 72.85 μg/m3; Pakistan: 58.47 μg/m3), whereas participants in the United States reported the lowest level of concern, consistent with the comparatively lower PM2.5 concentrations observed in the country (7.58 μg/m3).

Interactions with demographic characteristics

We found statistically significant interactions between PM2.5 concentrations and demographic characteristics on KAPPE (Fig. 2). For air quality knowledge, we found that the inverse association between PM2.5 concentrations and air quality knowledge was stronger among younger people (p < 0.01), participants without a bachelor’s degree (p < 0.05), participants who were not currently employed (p < 0.01), and current smokers (p < 0.05). The positive association between higher PM2.5 concentrations and attitudes towards air pollution prevention and control was more pronounced among current smokers (p < 0.01), conservatives (p < 0.01), people living in rural areas (p < 0.01), and younger people (p < 0.05). For air quality perceptions, statistically significant interactions were observed for rural residence (p < 0.01) and conservative political ideology (p < 0.05). The positive relationship between PM2.5 concentrations and air quality protective practices was stronger among females, participants without a bachelor’s degree, participants who were not currently employed, younger people, conservatives, current smokers, people living in rural areas, and singles (all ps < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for concern about air quality, although the interaction with sex was not statistically significant. In the Supplementary Information file, Supplementary Figs. 3–12 depict all the interaction coefficients between PM2.5 concentration and demographic characteristics in relation to KAPPE for each country.

Discussion

Outdoor air pollution remains one of the leading environmental threats to public health worldwide. Understanding how people perceive, respond to, and protect themselves from air pollution is essential for designing effective public health interventions and policies. Using nationally comparable data from the world’s ten most populous countries, spanning lower-middle-, upper-middle-, and high-income economies, we found that respondents living in localities with higher PM2.5 levels knew less about air quality but reported greater concern about air quality and more frequent adoption of protective behaviors. However, ambient PM2.5 concentrations were not significantly associated with perceptions of pollution sources and health risks, and were only marginally positively associated with attitudes towards air pollution prevention and control.

These results corroborate and extend previous research on the relationship between air pollution and KAPPE, which has largely relied on smaller, geographically localized samples: Del Ponte and colleagues found a positive relationship between exposure to wildfire episodes and knowledge scores in the United States16; Park and colleagues, and Ryan and colleagues, found that wearing air quality monitors improved knowledge, awareness, and practices about air quality in the United States10,11; Boso and colleagues found a positive association between PM2.5 concentrations and perceptions about the health risks of air pollution in Chile9. At the same time, the present study complements and extends existing literature by linking objectively measured ambient PM2.5 concentrations to KAPPE, rather than relying solely on perceived air pollution. For instance, Chen and colleagues found that perceptions of PM2.5 air pollution were associated with negative emotions, greater health risk perceptions, and more protective practices about air quality in South Korea25; Claeson and colleagues found a similar association between air pollution perceptions and health risk perceptions in Sweden26. Matching respondents with objectively measured ambient air pollution levels is advantageous because people often substantially underestimate their actual exposure to air pollution27–29.

We also examined the demographic predictors of KAPPE, which allowed us to identify vulnerable subpopulations. Existing studies found that females are more likely to be exposed to higher levels of air pollution30. We found that females engaged in more air quality protective practices, are more aware, and worried more about it, although they knew less about air quality. Zhang and colleagues found that the subjective well-being of younger respondents and those with fewer years of education is more likely to be affected by poor air quality31. Here, we show that younger adults knew less about air quality, although they worried more and were more aware of it. In the present study, education was not a statistically significant predictor of stronger perceptions of pollution sources and the health risks associated with air pollution, although previous work found a positive correlation between education and risk perceptions about air quality32. Participants with more conservative political ideology demonstrated greater support for air pollution prevention and control measures, stronger air pollution risk perceptions, more frequent protective practices, and greater concern about air quality, despite having lower levels of air quality knowledge. Further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying these associations. In contrast, people living in rural areas, smokers, and those who were not currently employed tended to have lower scores across all KAPPE domains. Previous research in Texas also found that people living in rural areas perceived air pollution as a less pressing issue compared to urban dwellers33, a finding consistent with research from China showing that rural residents generally perceive better air quality compared to urban residents34. The findings for smokers are consistent with those reported in a study conducted in Northeast China during a haze episode in 2013, which found a similar negative relationship between smoking, perceptions, and practices about air quality35.

However, we found important differences in the associations between ambient PM2.5 concentrations and KAPPE across different demographic groups. Although vulnerable subpopulations tended to have lower KAPPE scores overall, many demographic groups reported more frequent adoption of protective behaviours and greater concern about air quality in localities with higher ambient PM2.5 concentrations. However, younger people, people without a bachelor’s degree, those who were not currently employed, and smokers knew even less despite being exposed to higher PM2.5 concentrations. Previous work has not investigated interactions between PM2.5 concentrations and demographic factors on KAPPE. However, previous studies in the United States have shown that decreases in PM2.5 exposure would reduce mortality among low-income White participants and both low- and high-income Black participants36, and that exposure to air pollution disproportionately impacts marginalized subpopulations37. A third study found that the adverse association between air pollution and cognitive performance was more pronounced among participants with lower socioeconomic status and those residing in less affluent neighborhoods38.

Overall, key strengths of this study include its cross-country design, nationally representative samples by age and sex in most participating countries, the comprehensive assessment of KAPPE domains, the rich demographic data, and the assignment of objectively measured ambient PM2.5 concentrations to individual participants rather than relying solely on perceived air pollution. Together, these features allow us to examine a substantial variation in PM2.5 levels and diverse socio-demographic contexts, providing broader generalizability of the findings. However, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the study precludes adjustment for unobserved time-varying factors and limits the ability to infer causality. Future research could exploit panel data to explore temporal trends and better isolate causal relationships between air pollution and the different KAPPE measures. In addition, a limitation of conducting a worldwide KAPPE survey is that we were unable to elucidate country-specific nuances. Furthermore, because each respondent in a locality is assigned the same 3-year average PM2.5, the study cannot fully capture individual-level exposure to PM2.5 or track short-term peaks in PM2.5 concentrations, which may be particularly relevant to emotions and behaviors. Finally, the desired survey representativeness by sex and age was achieved in most but not all countries due to difficulties encountered by the Qualtrics survey company in recruiting certain demographic groups (particularly older participants) in some countries. In addition, when interpreting the association between PM2.5 concentrations and KAPPE, as well as its interaction with demographic characteristics, it is important to note that the manuscript does not establish causal relationships, and alternative mechanisms are possible. For example, populations that are more informed or pay closer attention to air quality may be more likely to advocate for cleaner air. In addition, given the nature of the study, the results provide a global snapshot but may be influenced by long-standing policy responses at local, regional, and national levels, as well as media coverage of air pollution, which can fluctuate seasonally with weather patterns.

From a policy perspective, considering the relative magnitude of the associations between PM2.5 concentrations and KAPPE is crucial. For knowledge and attitudes, the estimated associations with PM2.5 concentrations are modest, whereas the associations with protective practices and, especially, worry, are more pronounced. Notably, despite having lower levels of technical knowledge about air quality, people living in high-pollution settings reported relatively frequent adoption of protective behaviors. Therefore, policymakers could focus their efforts on spreading protective practices and ensuring that the public does not underestimate the health risks of air pollution, particularly in parts of the world where resources are limited.

More broadly, the present study highlights the need for targeted interventions to enhance public engagement with air quality issues (see review of effective intervention strategies by Giles and colleagues39). Previous studies have demonstrated the success of educational programs, such as those implemented in schools40, but persistent budget constraints worldwide compel policymakers to prioritize resources where they are most needed. In this respect, the present study offers practical insights into which subpopulations may require greater support. Even in resource-rich contexts, programs could prioritize vulnerable groups, such as individuals who do not have a bachelor’s degree and younger cohorts, by developing accessible educational materials and awareness campaigns tailored to their specific needs. Efforts to address sex disparities in KAPPE are also critical. Given the observed variation in practices, policymakers could consider leveraging high practice rates in heavily polluted areas to foster broader behavioral changes and community-led initiatives. Furthermore, the positive relationship between air quality knowledge and practices suggests that investments in education about air quality can help increase engagement in protective practices to mitigate the adverse effects of air pollution. By designing interventions that account for socio-demographic heterogeneities, policymakers can more effectively promote equitable responses to air quality challenges.

Methods

Ethics

We obtained ethics approval from the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (NUS-IRB: LS-19-135E). Respondents were provided with an online participant information sheet with details of the study before deciding to participate. Upon clicking a consent button, they could start filling out the anonymous online survey in their respective languages.

Survey platforms

As in previous work by Lim and colleagues using the same dataset, we recruited participants online through the Qualtrics survey platform between April and August 2021 across nine countries—Bangladesh, Brazil, Indonesia, India, Mexico, Nigeria, Pakistan, Russia, and the United States41. In contrast, recruitment in China was conducted through face-to-face interactions (N = 452) facilitated by a collaborator from the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Institute of Urban Environment, located in Xiamen, China, and the SoJump.com platform (N = 419)14 during the period from June to September 2021. The Qualtrics survey platform could not be used in China due to its noncompliance with the China Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL), which came into effect in October 2020.

Survey respondents

To be eligible for participation, respondents were required to meet two criteria: (i) be at least 18 years old and (ii) have resided in their respective country for a minimum of 1 year. A total of 15,017 respondents were initially recruited. After excluding 31% of participants because they failed quality control (e.g., speedsters; suspected bots; and suspicious IP addresses), the final study sample comprised 10,618 respondents as follows (response rate in percent): Bangladesh (1095; 85%), Brazil (1054; 63%), China (871; NA), Indonesia (1170; 81%), India (1075; 52%), Mexico (1052; 73%), Nigeria (1054; 84%), Pakistan (1053; 84%), Russia (1059; 70%), and the United States (1135; 57%). We performed balance tests using t-tests on demographic characteristics to compare participants who were included versus excluded. We found no statistically significant differences in sex, age, smoking status, or employment status. In contrast, excluded participants were more likely to live in rural areas, be single, and were less likely to have a bachelor’s degree and report conservative political ideology (Supplementary Table 3).

Survey instruments

The questionnaire included demographic information, such as age, sex, education level, marital status, place of residence, employment status, political ideology, smoking status, as well as KAPPE. Knowledge was assessed using the air quality knowledge (AQIQ) questionnaire developed by Del Ponte and colleagues, which is composed of 10 multiple-choice items, including 5 on general knowledge, 4 on the air quality index (AQI), and 1 on best practices13. Each correct response was awarded a score of 1, resulting in a knowledge score ranging from 0 to 10 for each respondent. It is worth noting that the AQI is implemented differently across countries. We therefore adapted the AQI items to ensure accuracy in each country. The exact question wording is provided in the Supplementary Information file. Details about the measurement properties of the knowledge items are in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. Attitudes about air quality were measured using five items measured on a five-point scale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.69), ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree,” which was adapted from Rajper and colleagues42. Perceptions about air quality were measured using nine items on a five-point scale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81), also adapted from Rajper and colleagues42. Four of these questions measured perceptions of health risks related to air pollution (scale from “no risk” to “very high risk”); the remaining five questions measured the perception of how certain activities (e.g., smoking) contribute to air pollution (five-point scale from “no pollution” to “a lot of air pollution”). Practices about air quality were measured with an 8-item scale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.71) adapted from Qian and colleagues24 that assesses the frequency of adopting best practices (four-point scale from “never” to “always”). Emotions about air pollution were measured using three items (anxious, worried, and afraid) on a five-point scale ranging from “not at all” to “very” (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88). All Cronbach’s alphas by country are included in Supplementary Table 4. Higher scores on the attitude, knowledge, perception, and practice scales indicate greater support for air pollution prevention and control measures, greater air quality knowledge, stronger perceptions of pollution sources and associated health risks, and more frequent adoption of protective behaviors, respectively. Higher emotion scores indicate greater concern about air quality. Henceforth, the emotion domain of the KAPPE framework will be referred to interchangeably as worry or concern throughout this paper. The questionnaire was administered in each country’s local language.

Measuring exposure to PM2.5 concentration

We defined exposure to PM2.5 as mean exposure for the years 2019, 2020, and 2021 at the smallest geographic administrative unit available (e.g., city, county, province: see Supplementary Data 1 for the full list): respondents in the same unit were assumed to share the same average exposure to PM2.5. We calculated annual average PM2.5 measurements of ground-level concentrations, which were synthesized from satellite data and ground monitoring stations43. According to Van Donkelaar and colleagues, annual global satellite-based estimates of PM2.5 were obtained using monthly data combining satellite retrievals of aerosol optical depth, chemical transport modeling, and ground-based measurements to allow for the characterization of seasonal and episodic exposure, as well as aid air-quality management43. The Pearson’s correlation coefficients between PM2.5 concentrations for each of the three years are r = ≥ 0.98 (Supplementary Table 5), indicating high temporal stability. Therefore, using the three-year average provides a robust measure of PM2.5 exposure over the study period.
