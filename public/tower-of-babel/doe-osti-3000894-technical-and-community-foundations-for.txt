Executive Summary
The goal of this project (hereafter referred to as the “umbrella project”) was to establish cross-cutting
technical foundations for carbon dioxide removal (CDR) accounting decisions. These cross-cutting and
science driven technical foundations are necessary to increase trust in standards-setting and protocol
development. According to key stakeholders in the industry, establishment of trusted standards is
necessary to unlock and catalyze investment in the CDR industry as well as commercialization and
deployment of verified, accepted high quality CDR technologies. Additionally, our umbrella project
served as the lead coordinator of the cohort of three additional CDR pathway-focused projects funded
under this award. In this role, we brought together the project teams to share insights, industry
feedback, and approaches to CDR accounting standards development.

Our project’s most significant external-facing accomplishment was the development of the “Net Flux
Framework” for CDR project accounting-and associated publications, workshops, and report describing
this framework. The Net Flux Framework (NFF) puts forward and highlights the need for a carbon
accounting methodology that is distinct from Life Cycle Assessment (LCA); and that is intended for
measuring fluxes out of the atmosphere, as opposed to changes in emissions from the current status
quo. The NFF discussion has been influential, shaping the conversation around carbon accounting for
CDR, especially amongst LCA practitioners, NGOs, and some early-stage registries and CDR projects.
While major CDR registries did not necessarily adopt NFF to inform their protocols, we did find broad
consensus that the NFF is the most appropriate tool for determining the true “carbon removal” impact
of CDR projects.

Background and Project Objectives
The project aimed to support consistency, rigor, and comparability in the nascent (CDR) measurement,
reporting and verification (MRV) industry, increasing trust in the value of durable CDR and facilitating
new project starts and larger CDR volumes. Through significant industry engagement, the project was
intended to establish cross-cutting technical, regulator, and governance foundations for potential future
MRV standards-setting to catalyze investment in the CDR industry as well as commercialization and
deployment of verified, accepted high quality CDR technologies.

This project was intended to develop an Umbrella MRV Framework that enables CDR stakeholders —
including projects, buyers, standard setters, policy makers, communities, and scientists — to reference a
shared, foundational understanding of what rigorous MRV for different durable CDR pathways should
include, as well as the largest sources of uncertainty for each approach. The framework aimed to
incorporate new CDR pathways in the future, reflect evolving scientific understanding. It was intended
to reduce the uncertainty and complexity currently faced by stakeholders and inform the development
and evaluation of pathway-specific MRV protocols. To facilitate this vision, our project deliverables
included a build-out of CarbonPlan’s Verification Confidence Levels tool (VCL tool) to reflect findings
from projects across the TCF MRV portfolio. The framework was used as the basis of a DACS worked
example that surfaces industry data, informs key dimensions of DACS quantification uncertainty, and
demonstrates how the framework could be used by practitioners.

Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), and
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) aimed to advance this work through deep-dive analysis,
general provision of technical expertise in the framework development process, and support for
convenings of scientists, industry experts, non-governmental organizations and regulators. The lead
industry partner, CarbonPlan, played a cross-cutting coordination role among the industry partners,
national labs, CDR buyers, CDR companies, and other stakeholders to develop the framework, build an
interactive tool to share the framework with the public, and facilitate framework uptake.

To achieve these goals, we developed 6 tasks to structure our work:
Task 1.0: Develop Geophysical MRV Frameworks for MRV Best Practices
Task 2.0: MRV National Lab Cohort Coordination
Task 3.0: Demonstrate and Refine MRV Best Practices using DACS Worked Examples
Task 4.0: Governance

Task 6.0: Engage industry; Measure impact

Project Results and Discussion
We met our major milestones aimed at engaging with the CDR community: understanding positions,
aligning stakeholders, and reporting results. We also met our technical milestones related to developing
geophysical MRV frameworks and demonstrating these frameworks using DACS worked examples and
several examples of biomass carbon removal and storage (BiCRS) and mineralization projects
contributed by the cohort projects and from our own project team. We worked through these examples
using published data on carbon and energy balances of every process step.

We updated the project scope and modified our communication strategy in Winter/Spring 2025. For
example, in lieu of releasing a website, we pivoted to a publication and a culminating report. We used
the publication and report also as mechanisms for collaboration with the cohort projects, where we
shared our vision and collaborated on the results.

Governing principles:

  LCA best practices are designed to quantify and understand environmental impacts, they were
 not developed to be tied directly to incentive structures

  The geophysical framework for MRV must be designed explicitly with market participant
 responses to incentives in mind (avoid LCA best practices that create perverse incentives)

  Actual CDR should be differentiated from GHG mitigation to enable purchasers to pay
 exclusively for CDR if they choose

Initial conclusions:

  It is possible for a net-emitting process to capture and store biogenic or air-derived CO2, yet not
 achieve net CDR

  Working through large numbers of examples, particularly informed by industry partners'
 experiences with real projects, is essential to testing our framework

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Our most significant accomplishments on this project were the development and dissemination of the
Net flux Framework, through engaging with the external community and through our publications. To
develop this framework, we first needed to survey the landscape understand how projects were being
evaluated and how we might address limitations in the current approaches– this took time. Our largest
workshop brought together key actors in the voluntary market, and allowed us to further understand
the core challenge, which was that people were working with different definitions of CDR and working
toward different goals. This realization helped us move toward more productive conversations-the goal
of the CDR project needs to be articulated before the carbon accounting framework can be chosen. Our
publication in One Earth in 2024 garnered a lot of attention in the community and created a blueprint
for companies looking toward realizing net flux goals, including one company that explicitly referenced
our work as the foundation for their accounting methodology. Through convening with the cohort
projects, we were also able to understand the broader landscape of industry needs and gathered
feedback on the benefits and drawbacks of the different accounting approaches. We did not achieve
consensus across the cohort or the industry, nor did we satisfy the goals of all of the industrial partners
– achieving consensus at the current state was not an achievable goal. But we have influenced the
conversations in industry around what project outcomes should qualify as CDR and put forward a
framework for increasing transparency and the effectiveness of communication of project goals. We
discussed this framework with some of the most prominent stakeholders in the voluntary carbon
markets (buyers, projects, registries).

We hosted two workshops, >>20 cohort engagement meetings, and attended dozens of meetings with
industry and other stakeholders. Informed by these engagements, we have one published work
product, and four are forthcoming:

Path Forward: Temporal Accounting, Co Product Allocation and Finding
Middle Ground
Based upon our meetings with industry partners and the cohort projects, we identified key areas of
agreement and challenges that remain.
All cohort projects, for example, agreed upon the need to separately account for and report carbon
removals from avoided and mitigated emissions.

Interviews with industry (buyers, registries) reveal frustration with the continued wide range of carbon
accounting protocols, and the need to identify areas of highest discrepancy. We attempted to do this in
our final report, but more work needs to be done to flag and communicate the most impactful areas of
discrepancy with industry so the issues can be addressed by (e.g. buyers and registries).

The cohort projects, particularly BiCRS and mineralization focused projects, identified temporal
decisions in carbon accounting as being extremely challenging and in need of further study and
consensus building. The Umbrella project has been primarily focused over the past two years on “what”
should be accounted for, but the next big question surrounds “when” the removal should be counted or
credited. For example, some approaches like forest biomass burial may perform a costly activity (forest
biomass removal, processing, and burial) and want to be compensated in the short term, when the
majority of the carbon removal will occur over the following decades as the trees re-grow. One path
forward to address these questions is to couple large scale integrated assessment modeling of temporal
fluxes to help inform temporal accounting decisions.

We also found that there continues to be disagreement amongst registries and LCA practitioners into
how emissions are allocated to co-products. Net flux accounting is straightforward in this regard, the
carbon removal project takes on the full emissions burden of the requisite facility. However, we received
pointed feedback that existing LCA procedures, e.g. mass, energy, or economic allocation should be
followed. As a path forward, it would be instructive to compare different co-product allocation
approaches across different project types to quantitatively to understand the accounting decision
impacts and inform decision making.

Finally, we feel it may be helpful for the industry to identify middle ground-between the more stringent
net flux accounting approach and traditional LCA approaches. For example, “allowances” to net flux
accounting could be made for small scale projects or nascent industries. One such allowance could be to
document, but not penalize small scale projects for production emissions from conversion of feedstocks
that are by-products or wastes from existing industrial processes.
