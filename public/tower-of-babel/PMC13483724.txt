Conserved enhancer association of piRNAs and the implication in germ cell fate surveillance

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe PIWI-piRNA pathway is crucial for protecting genomic integrity from derepressed transposon invasion in developing germ cells after genome-wide epigenetic erasure. While extensive mouse studies show that pre-pachytene piRNAs contribute to establishing the epigenetic landscape, little is known about human piRNAs during embryonic fetal germ cell development. In this study, we collect fetal testes of gestational week 12–22 abortuses from Taiwan, to investigate human piRNA profiles correlating to the time window of epigenetic landscape establishment together with those generated from USA samples.ResultsWe identify numerous human piRNAs in second-trimester fetal testes that corresponded to small nucleolar RNAs. Remarkably, a substantial proportion of human fetal testicular piRNAs map to super-enhancers and typical enhancers. This piRNA-enhancer association is a highly conserved phenomenon across diverse amniote species, including humans, mice, and chickens.ConclusionsNotably, many of the pre-pachytene germline piRNAs mapped to enhancers usually active in various somatic lineages. We propose a conserved biological role for these piRNAs: they actively regulate enhancer activity and modulate enhancerRNA (eRNA) turnover. Background

Germ cells carry the burden of maintaining genetic and epigenetic integrity during their complex development as migrating primordial germ cells, differentiating prospermatogonia, and completing meiosis. Primordial germ cells must undergo epigenomic reprogramming in order to preserve developmental potential throughout germ cell differentiation [1]. This process is accompanied by a derepression of mobile DNA elements such as transposons, which comprise a large fraction of the genome and threaten genome integrity.

Germ cells possess an evolutionarily conserved genome defense system to repress transposons via post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) as well as transcriptional gene silencing (TGS) pathways. The PIWI clade of proteins is a subfamily of Argonaute nucleases and highly-conserved RNA-binding nuclear proteins with RNA-binding and catalytic cleavage activity [2]. Small non-coding RNAs known as PIWI-interacting RNA (piRNA) bind PIWI proteins to form the piRNA-induced silencing complex (piRISC) and act as guides for gene-specific silencing. The piRISC can silence complementary sequences post-transcriptionally via cleavage of target mRNA in the cytoplasm or transcriptionally by directing repressive de novo DNA and H3K9me3 methylation machinery to the corresponding target region [3, 4].

The biosynthesis of piRNAs is initiated by cleavage of a long single-stranded piRNA precursor transcript, which is usually transcribed by RNA polymerase II from a discrete highly repetitive genomic locus called a piRNA cluster (reviewed in [5]). PIWI proteins and associated factors such as mitochondrial phospholipase D/Zucchini (MitoPLD/Zuc) and PNLDC1 then mediate stepwise cleavage [6, 7]. The resulting pre-piRNAs then undergo 2’-O-methylation at the 3’ end via HENMT1, which prevents further trimming [8–10]. These mature piRNAs may carry out downstream transcriptional repression, or mediate secondary piRNA biogenesis through the ping-pong cycle, which amplifies piRNA pools for more efficient transcriptional repression. In the mammalian ping-pong cycle, piRISCs composed of PIWIL2 (HILI/MILI) and antisense piRNAs bind and cleave sense piRNA precursors. The sense piRNAs are loaded into PIWIL4 (HIWI2/MIWI2), which are ready to process antisense piRNA precursors for loading into PIWIL2 (HILI/MILI), and so on in a cyclic manner [11]. Additionally, homotypic ping-pong amplification involving only MILI was reported in the mouse pachytene stage [12].

Throughout germ cell development, the expression levels of PIWI and piRNA are tightly regulated in stage- and cell type-specific fashion. In mouse studies, PIWI pathways are classified according to the pachytene stage of meiosis I. Pre-pachytene piRNAs are expressed in pro-spermatogonia and spermatogonia, and are mostly derived from transposable elements. During this period, MILI functionally contributes to both primary and secondary biogenesis, whereas MIWI2 is confined to secondary biogenesis and transfers piRNAs to the nucleus to perform TGS. MIWI2 expression declines in spermatogonia while MILI continues processing both sense and antisense precursor transcripts approximately until the start of meiosis in the postnatal period [13]. First generated in pachytene spermatocytes, pachytene piRNAs are predominantly derived from piRNA clusters outside of protein-coding genes. Both MILI and MIWI are involved in sense and antisense piRNA biogenesis, and predominantly contribute to PTGS [14].

The precise and dynamic timing of PIWI family member expression suggests that the two major types of piRNAs have specific functions in germ cell development. Previous mouse studies reported the expression dynamics over piRNA clusters between prenatal and post-natal testes [15]. Miwi-null mutants undergo spermatogenic arrest while Mili- and Miwi2-null mutants show interrupted meiosis, underscoring the importance of expression levels and timing of PIWI and piRNA for proper germ cell development [16]. Our lab also reported significant shifting of embryonic gonadal piRNA clusters from TE-rich regions to intergenic regions along with the developmental progress in chicken models [17]. While the piRNA guiding function suggests high affinity toward TE-derived regulatory elements, piRNAs that originated from and targeted non-TE-containing sequences imply additional roles of PIWI-piRNA mechanisms. Emerging evidence over sequence identity and genomic colocalization studies suggest that piRNAs may be derived from non-coding RNA precursors such as tRNAs, rRNAs, and snoRNAs [18, 19]. Moreover, it has been reported that the antagonistic transition from H3K27ac to H3K27me3 over meiotic super-enhancers drives the expression of the colocalized piRNA clusters [20]. These non-TE-derived piRNAs implicated a developmental stage-dependent modulation over various regulatory elements. Therefore, the mechanisms and potential roles and underlying mechanisms of non-TE derived piRNAs in spermatogenesis and mammalian germ cell fate maintenance, merit further investigation.

While epigenetic changes such as histone modifications and DNA methylation have been investigated in human fetal germ cells, the expression of piRNAs during mid-gestation after epigenetic erasure in the fetal testis has not been sufficiently characterized due to limitations on available samples. This study comprehensively analyzed piRNAs in human fetal testes and provided novel evidence for the abundance of enhancer-derived or enhancer targeting piRNA sequences, suggesting new functions for piRNAs. Bolstering the evolutionary conserved role of piRNA-enhancer association, we extend our analyses to chicken and mouse with high confidence piRNA sequences. To deduce the novel roles of piRNAs in maintaining germ cell fate, we characterized the neighboring genes associated with differential piRNA-targeted enhancers between wild-type and PIWI-piRNA pathway-compromised mutant mouse lines. Based on our results, we hypothesize that non-TE piRNAs in the spermatogonial cells target enhancers and super-enhancers usually active in somatic lineages, to reduce ectopic or prolonged expression of somatic lineage-determining genes, thus maintaining and reinforcing germ cell identity.

Results

PIWI-piRNA pathway is present in human fetal testes

During germline development in mouse fetal testes, the PIWI family proteins MILI (PIWIL2), MIWI2 (PIWIL4), and Maelstrom (MAEL) are required for the biogenesis of pre-pachytene piRNAs as well as the amplification of secondary piRNAs, which emerge upon the completion of DNA demethylation [21]. In human fetal testes from the developmental window of predicted epigenetic landscape establishment, these PIWI proteins were found to be expressed by a subset of VASA-expressing germ cells, suggesting that piRNA pathway components and the piRISC-mediated silencing mechanism may also be present in human fetal testes (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

To explore the presence of piRNAs in human male germline from GW12 to GW22, small RNAs derived from fetal testes were sequenced and analyzed using our homebuilt bioinformatic pipeline (NTU cohort); a second similar cohort from UCSF was analyzed in parallel (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). Based on the categorization criteria, we identified approximately 1–3% of small RNAs as candidate piRNAs in human fetal testes (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B, Additional file 2: Table S2). Notably, a significant proportion of these piRNA candidates from both NTU and UCSF cohorts are already represented in an existing piRNA database (piRBase V3), providing confidence in our piRNA bioinformatic pipeline (Fig. 1A, Additional file 1: Fig. S2C, Additional file 2: Table S3). Given that piRNA species are expressed in a stage-dependent manner, we anticipated the identification of previous undocumented piRNAs from the less-studied early developmental stages have not been published or included in current piRNA databases.

Ping-pong cycle-based piRNA biogenesis is a hallmark for a significant proportion of pre-pachytene piRNAs in most species [22]. We observed that most of the human gonadal piRNA profiles in both data cohorts exhibited high frequencies of 10 nt overlap at the 5’ end (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D, Additional file 2: Table S5), which occurs as a result of ping-pong amplification between pairable piRNAs [23]. Nucleotide enrichment analysis over these overlapping piRNAs confirmed a 1U10A bias that is indicative of ping-pong cycle activities (Additional file 1: Fig. S2E). Notably, the 1U10A enrichment signal is not as evident in samples before and around GW13, which suggests that primary piRNA biogenesis predominates before 13-week fetuses, whereas ping-pong amplification ramps at later developmental stages, providing stage-dependent evidence of the intrinsic ping-pong amplification mechanism of piRNA biogenesis in human fetal testes.

Annotation of human piRNAs reveals potential roles beyond regulation of repeat elements

By mapping human piRNA candidates to RefSeq and RepeatMasker reference sequences, we found in both cohorts that approximately 50% to 75% of human fetal testicular piRNAs were associated with coding and non-coding genes; however, only few piRNAs were derived from repeat regions (Fig. 1B, Additional file 1: Fig. S2F, Additional file 2: Table S4). This association with genic sequences suggests that human piRNA precursors may be regulated differently from those in mice. Notably, we identified a significant proportion, up to 80%, of piRNAs that could be mapped onto small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) sequences, indicating their unique origin (Fig. 1C). While previous studies have proposed that snoRNAs may be processed into piRNAs [24, 25], our findings suggest that piRNA biogenesis may also act to scavenge excessive or untimely snoRNA accumulation.

To investigate piRISC-mediated gene regulation, we simulated the targeting process by converting cluster-derived piRNAs to their reverse complementary sequences, and mapping them onto genomic regions of potential targets. Interestingly, in addition to less than 1% of piRNAs targeting transposable elements, a significant proportion of piRNAs in both study cohorts were directed toward imprinted genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Furthermore, we observed significant overlap between piRNA targets and transcriptional regulatory elements: ~ 10–40% of piRNAs mapped to putative gene promoters, ~ 10% mapped to any known gene enhancers, and ~ 40–60% mapped to super-enhancers in both cohorts (Table 1). These findings suggest that human embryonic gonadal piRNA clusters from week 12 to week 22 likely contribute to transcriptional regulation and exhibit less correlation with transposable elements.

Super-enhancers play a crucial role in cellular lineage determination, primarily by harboring binding sites for lineage-specific transcription factors and acting as regulatory elements for genes involved in lineage specification [26, 27]. The significant quantity of human fetal testicular piRNAs mapping to super-enhancers prompted us to analyze the cluster-derived piRNA mapping across enhancers that are usually active in defined tissue type based on our curated enhancer regions from primary tissue types. We observed that cluster-derived piRNAs preferentially mapped to enhancers active in adult tissue lineages over those active in the fetal stage. PiRNA candidates identified from NTU and UCSF cohorts mapped similarly across super-enhancers and typical enhancers that are observed to be active in different tissue types (Fig. 2). Assuming the piRNA targeting would eventually lead to repression of the targeted enhancers, the interplay between piRNA and enhancers may contribute to the suppression of somatic or untimely gene expression in the germline.

Cross-species analysis on chicken piRNAs implied the conserved phenomenon of enhancer mapping addition to the regulation of repeat elements

We next sought to examine the evolutionary conservation of piRNA-enhancer interactions we identified in humans and indeed found a similar phenomenon, although less profound, in the chicken piRNA datasets. We used our published chicken spermatogonial datasets and analysis pipelines on chicken embryonic piRNA for cross-species comparisons [17]. The availability of 3’-end 2’-O methylation enrichment over small-RNA sequencing adds legitimacy and confidence to the identification of piRNA species. Curiously, we found chicken germline piRNAs showed significantly lower association with snoRNAs, compared to human fetal testicular piRNAs. As we reported earlier, the proportion of chicken piRNAs associated with transposable elements declines after the blastoderm stage (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A, B) [17], indicating that chicken piRNAs may also contribute to developmental modulations beyond transposon silencing.

We investigated potential piRNA correlations with known tissue-dependent chicken typical enhancers and super-enhancers annotated in the super-enhancer archive version 3.0 (SEA 3.0) database [28]. Similar to human piRNA, the majority of chicken piRNAs that are mapped to enhancers are not derived from transposable elements (Fig. 3, Additional file 1: Fig. S4C–E). We showed that the patterns of chicken piRNA-enhancer complementarity can be roughly grouped into three developmental periods: blastodermal cells (BCs), embryonic germline, and postnatal germline (Fig. 3). The BC piRNAs predominantly mapped antisense to the repeat elements (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A, B) and showed very low overlap with either typical or super-enhancers across tissues (Fig. 3). Interestingly, we found a noticeable elevation of gonadal piRNA mapped to enhancers in newly-hatched chicks (wk0). Together with the concurrent elevation of piRNA targeting toward transposable elements, these observations may reflect increased genome accessibility that accompanies global reprogramming in the fetal germline (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Fig. S4A, B). This observation in chicken concurs with our finding in human piRNAs and suggests that piRNA-enhancer interactions may play a role in male germline development, including potential modulation of enhancers active in early somatic lineages.

Mouse piRNAs show stage-dependent association with tissue-enriched enhancers

Mouse prenatal and postnatal piRNA profiles were used to further evaluate the conservation of piRNA-enhancer interactions. In contrast to human and chicken piRNA profiles, which were inferred based on piRNA sequence characteristics, mouse piRNAs were identified by co-immunoprecipitation with their PIWI family processing and biogenesis proteins (PIWI-IP). We analyzed the genomic origins and targets of a total of 23 PIWI immunoprecipitation mouse testis datasets from the piRBase v3.0 database [29]. The piRNA length range was consistent with those previously reported (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A) [30, 31]. In accordance with what is currently known about piRNA origins, mouse E16.5 prenatal piRNA datasets coinciding with pre-pachytene stages exhibited a reduced number of annotations to known lncRNAs that reside in genic and intergenic regions compared to pachytene, postnatal (P14 +) and adult piRNAs (Additional file 1: Fig. S5B). The large proportions of repeat element targets in pre-pachytene mouse piRNAs diminished after pachytene (Additional file 1: Fig. S5C). In comparison to humans, mice exhibited a lower prevalence of gene-associated piRNAs across all age groups.

Potential enhancer targeting was also observed in mouse piRNAs, which is consistent with our findings in human and chicken gonads (Additional file 1: Fig. S5D). The targeting frequency, indicated by the mapping percentage of antisense piRNA sequences to enhancers, varied across germ cell developmental stages (Fig. 4, Additional file 1: Fig. S5E, F). In general, pre-pachytene piRNAs showed higher enhancer mapping during the prenatal wave of epigenomic reprogramming until before pachytene (E16.5 to P10 datasets). In this context, we observed similar pre-pachytene MIWI2-bound piRNAs and MILI-bound piRNAs in their enhancer mapping result from mouse testes. Notably, those PIWI protein-bound piRNAs are preferentially mapped to adult testis, liver, and brain enhancers. Further analyses suggest that repeat elements may not be the primary source of these enhancer-targeting piRNAs, as demonstrated by the finding that the non-repeat-derived piRNAs mapped to tissue type-enriched enhancers at comparable percentages (Additional file 1: Fig. S5H, I). The piRNA-enhancer association pattern supports our hypothesis that the maintenance of germ cell identity requires prevention of aberrant somatic differentiation or premature activation of adult testes-enriched genes, both of which are especially crucial after epigenomic reprogramming.

Differential piRNA association of enhancers was found in PIWI-piRNA related mutants in mice

To provide functional implications, we examined mouse mutants in primary, secondary, or both stages of piRNA biogenesis, many of which result in defective spermatogenesis [32]. In their absence, it is thus expected that some enhancer elements, particularly those that regulate the expression of genes related to cell lineage determination, could exhibit a significant alteration in piRNA targeting.

We first quantified piRNA antisense sequences that mapped to typical enhancers and super-enhancers from Super Enhancer Database (SEdb) [33] across various PIWI-piRNA pathway mutants (Fig. 5A, B; Additional file 1: Fig. S6A, B). Our comparative analysis, as demonstrated by MILI-bound piRNAs, revealed a general reduction in enhancer mapping in several testis samples from PIWI-piRNA pathway mutant mouse models, including the MiliDAH slicer mutant at E16.5, Tdrd12 KO at P0, and adult Pnldc1 KO, with the exception of Miwi2 KO at E16.5. By contrast, in the MiliDAH slicer mutant, we observed an increase in MIWI2-bound piRNAs that mapped to both typical- and super-enhancers (Fig. 5B). Notably, we also observed a preferential mapping of MILI-bound piRNAs onto super-enhancers in the brain, but not adult testis super-enhancers, in the Tdrd9 KO strain (red dots in Fig. 5B).

We further explored the roles of the potential piRNA-interacting enhancers in lineage development. As it has been shown that enhancers preferentially activate neighboring genes [34], we considered the function of genes adjacent to enhancers that show differential piRNA targeting between wild-type and PIWI mutants. Gene ontology analysis revealed these neighboring genes are involved in cell fate determination, somatic differentiation, and related processes such as migration, development, and regulatory functions (Fig. 6A–D, Additional file 1: Fig. S7A–D). Notably, many gene ontology categories were shared among several mutant-wildtype comparisons, suggesting that different PIWI-piRNA pathway components may have similar overall effects when removed, resulting from an aberrant piRNA landscape on enhancer coverage. For example, Miwi2 KO led to alterations of MILI-bound piRNAs that mapped to the enhancers linked to Blimp1 (also known as Prdm1), Prdm14, and Tfap2c—transcription factors essential for germline specification, and the enhancer proximal to a neurogenesis gene Neurog1 (Fig. 6E, Additional file 1: Fig. S7E), which would theoretically skew the lineage property of prospermatogonia in Miwi2-KO embryonic testes. However, because these master regulators specify germline lineage much earlier in development than the onset of MIWI2 activity, the observations at E16.5 may suggest an alternative possibility of MIWI2-mediated post-specification modulation or lineage maintenance. Consistent with this idea, single cell analysis of DDX4+ germ cells spanning from E10.5 to E16.5 demonstrated the progressive down-regulation of the germ cell specification genes, culminating in their near-complete silencing by E16.5 (Additional file 1: Fig. S7F). Therefore, the piRNA targeting observed at E16.5 likely serves to enforce or fine-tune the repression of these early specifiers as prospermatogonia mature, which may also be implicated in the phenotype of Miwi2 mutants, where germ cells are specified but exhibit defects in later postnatal developmental stages. Likewise, in response to differential amounts of piRNA targeting, the activity of enhancers neighboring Sdc4, Sox1, and Glis1 may also be affected, suggesting a disruption in the proliferation process and cell potency in favor of differentiation (Additional file 1: Fig. S7G). These findings collectively imply that piRNA-enhancer interaction may contribute to modulating features that underpin the development and maintenance of germline lineages.

piRNA sequences exhibited evidence of eRNA targeting

Enhancers are known to recruit RNA synthesis machinery, enabling the uni- or bidirectional transcription of their DNA sequences into non-coding RNA molecules called enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) [35, 36]. eRNAs have been shown to contribute to the activity of their respective enhancers, thereby activating lineage-specific gene pathways and related cellular processes [37, 38]. Expanding our cross-species observations of the relationship between piRNAs and genomic regions of enhancers, we further explored how piRNAs may interact with enhancers to regulate tissue development. Specifically, we deduced reverse complementary sequences of testes piRNAs and demonstrated their mapping result to predicted eRNA sequences (Fig. 7A). We thereby illustrated the potential of testes piRNAs in modulating somatic enhancers via targeting aberrantly expressed eRNAs associated with different somatic lineages (Fig. 7A). The piRNAs that target chromatin associated eRNA transcripts may guide epigenomic enzymes to introduce epigenetic modifications towards the anchoring enhancers that produce piRNA targeting eRNAs. On the other hand, the possibility for piRNAs to target and slice these eRNAs adjacent to or away from enhancer DNAs may also represent the modulatory effect over eRNAs. Notably, pre-pachytene piRNAs showed stronger eRNA targeting potentials in general, as reflected in a much higher percentage of anti-sense mapping (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, piRNAs were prominently mapped to embryonic stem cell-based eRNA regions. Our analysis over 2,309,455 individual candidate eRNA-transcribing regions revealed that many candidate eRNAs showed significant increases or decreases in the number of associated piRNAs from PIWI-piRNA pathway mutants, potentially contributing to the aberrant germ cell functions in these mutant mice (Fig. 7B, Additional file 1: Fig. S8A).

Gene ontology analysis showed that eRNAs-piRNA correlation in PIWI family mutants were related to differentiation regulation or other somatic cell-type specific processes (Fig. 7C, Additional file 1: Fig. S8B). For instance, Tdrd12 KO showed increases in potentially heart-related eRNAs. Notably, Tdrd12 was found to be more highly expressed in testis and heart than other tissues [39]. Sox2 eRNAs were also affected in a similar manner to those of Sox2 enhancers in Miwi2 KO. On the other hand, gene targets of eRNAs with significant decreases showed some cell type specific processes but had more shared regulatory processes, such as transport, translation, RNA modification, mRNA processing, and histone modification (Fig. 7D, Additional file 1: Fig. S8C). This suggests a potential mechanism for enhancer-associated piRNAs in gene regulation, where piRNAs may target eRNAs linked to specific somatic cell regulation and possibly influence lineage determination in a stage-, type-, and context-dependent manner.

Enhancer association of piRNAs correlated with the down-regulation of neighboring genes

To investigate the possible transcriptional consequences of enhancer-associated piRNAs, we performed an integrative analysis of paired small RNA-seq and poly-A+-transcriptomic data generated from THY1+ spermatogonial stem/progenitor cells (SSCs/SPCs) of wild-type and Dnmt3l knockout mice. In the Dnmt3l mutants, which exhibited alteration in their piRNA profiles, we observed a prominent inverse correlation between changes in piRNA targeting super-enhancers (SEs) and the expression levels of neighboring genes. Specifically, genes adjacent to SEs with reduced piRNA targeting were more frequently upregulated (220 out of 2,148 genes) than downregulated (64 out of 2,148 genes). Conversely, genes near SEs with increased piRNA targeting were predominantly downregulated (159 out of 2,388 genes) compared to those that were upregulated (89 out of 2,388 genes) (Fig. 8A). Subsequent gene ontology analysis confirmed that these reciprocally regulated genes are enriched in pathways related to somatic lineage development (Fig. 8B). Together, these findings underpin a functional link between enhancer-directed piRNA targeting and the timely regulation of gene expression relevant to alternative lineage or specific developmental stages.

Correlation of enhancer-associated piRNAs with expression of neighboring genes in THY1+ enriched spermatogonial stem/progenitor cells (SSCs/SPCs) from wild type and Dnmt3l knockout mice. A Left, piRNA expression profile in each replicate. Right, number of upregulated and downregulated genes near SEs with differential piRNA association. B Gene ontology analysis of differentially regulated genes

In conclusion, we found across three vertebrate species that pre-pachytene testicular piRNAs display notable association with somatic enhancers. Many of these piRNA-mapped enhancers may modulate neighboring genes which are important for pluripotency or somatic lineage determinations, suggesting a possible function for pre-pachytene piRNAs in blocking aberrant gene expression to maintain germ cell identity and functions.

Discussion

With this more comprehensive identification of piRNA candidates from human fetal testes in the second trimester of pregnancy after epigenomic reprogramming, we observed a significant proportion of these piRNAs mapping to snoRNAs, suggesting these snoRNAs being the host transcripts of human fetal piRNAs. Furthermore, given that numerous piRNA-related snoRNAs are embedded within introns of genes involved in the translational process—such as those encoding rRNA synthesis and maturation factors, ribosomal proteins, and translational initiation factors (data not shown)—the piRNA biogenesis process itself may constitute one layer of precision control over translational activities during male germ cell development. In addition, we discovered a conserved feature across human, mouse and chicken that prenatal testicular piRNAs can map to enhancers. Notably, as many of these piRNAs mapped to super-enhancers, which are known to drive the expression of genes relevant to somatic lineage determination, we hypothesize that human fetal testicular piRNAs also function in mediating transcriptional silencing of cell lineage specific genes for germ cell fate surveillance.

This study provides one of the first characterizations of human piRNA profiles at different timepoints of fetal testis development after the epigenetic erasure event. Much research on early human development is hampered by limited sample availability. With our interests in the conservation of piRNA’s roles in germline development among vertebrates, the human fetal testes samples independently collected from NTU and UCSF provided a comprehensive overview of human piRNA’s functional implication during the second trimester. While the IRB approved fetal testes collection for abortuses from two hospitals reflects different racial/ethnic varieties and recruitment criteria, we identified the piRNA traits that are common for the two groups, significantly increasing our confidence in the main conclusions.

Our analysis utilized a subtractive method for preferential identification of piRNA candidates to account for the complexity of piRNA biogenesis. The fetal human samples in this analysis showed relatively low piRNA representation, which is likely due in part to the rare germ cell population among heterogeneous cell composition in fetal testes. Potential RNA degradation during the acquisition process on abortus tissues further limited the small RNA availability in contrast with other model organisms. Despite these limitations, both NTU and UCSF cohorts showed consistency in piRNA signatures, origins and potential targets, demonstrating robustness and reproducibility of data. This consistency further suggests that the observed piRNA features are largely independent of inter-individual variation despite differences in sample conditions, as NTU samples were obtained from pregnancies terminated due to developmental abnormalities, whereas UCSF samples were acquired from elective terminations.

Some somatic lineages have more enhancers or a greater total genomic coverage of enhancers, which could result in higher piRNA targeting percentages. To account for this, normalization by combined enhancer length of each somatic tissue was performed. The result showed that piRNA targeting abundance become relatively uniform across enhancers associated with different somatic lineages (Additional file 1: Fig. S11). This suggests that piRNA displays broad suppressive activity rather than strict lineage specificity. Nevertheless, the uneven pattern in the original observation remains biologically significant (Figs. 2, 3 and 4): lineages characterized by extensive genomic coverage of enhancers and potent differentiation programs may require higher piRNA-targeting activity, resulting in dosage-dependent silencing to ensure effective repression. Regarding the cellular origin of these small RNAs, the germ cell-specific expression of core piRNA machinery and the robust cross-species conservation of these mapping patterns strongly support a germ cell origin over a somatic gonadal source. However, we cannot definitely rule out the possibility of small RNA transfer from somatic cells or an indirect somatic contribution to the germline piRNA pool.

Additionally, while the piRNA-enhancer targeting phenomenon itself is conserved between species, there are some differences in specific targeting activity. For instance, our analysis showed that human fetal testicular piRNA candidates exhibit strong sequence match to genic regions instead of TEs or intergenic regions, which were reported in other model species such as mouse, chicken, and drosophila [17, 40, 41]. This finding implies non-canonical roles of human second trimester piRNAs in gene regulation. Interestingly, our results indicate an association between piRNAs and both snoRNAs and imprinted genes. Also, even with the exclusion of the imprinted genes bearing C/D-box snoRNAs [42], enriched piRNA targeting are still observed compared to non-imprinted genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A, B). Further analyses of MILI- and MIWI2-interacting piRNAs on various imprinted loci suggested a potential role for piRNAs in the dosage regulation of imprinted gene expression (Additional file 1: Figs. S3, S9–10). Mapping of piRNAs to imprinted loci was also observed in Dnmt3l and Pnldc1 mutants; however, it should be noted that these particular mutants did not show significant gene expression changes in transcriptomic data (Additional file 2: Table S5).

To further investigate whether piRNAs may contribute to the establishment of genomic imprinting, we mapped MIWI2-associated piRNAs to major imprinting control regions (ICRs) across the genome (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). Although a small number of MIWI2-bound piRNAs were found to map to these ICRs, their abundance was markedly lower than that of piRNAs mapping to transcribed regions at the developmental stages analyzed. While it remains possible that these rare ICR-associated piRNAs could transiently recruit epigenetic modifiers, their low abundance suggests that such interactions are unlikely to represent a major mechanism of imprint establishment. In contrast, trans-acting piRNAs derived from transposable elements and targeting a specific ICR appear to represent a more prominent example of piRNA involvement in imprinting regulation.

Our hypothesis that piRNA-mediated silencing of somatic lineage enhancers may repress untimely pluripotency or somatic program-related gene expression and maintain germ cell identity was formulated based on four premises: that piRNAs play a crucial role in spermatogenesis, that there is evidence for piRNAs associating with and possibly targeting somatic lineage enhancers in the human germline, that enhancers are determinants of cell identity, and that germ cell identity is maintained throughout spermatogenesis. The piRNAs that target chromatin associated eRNA transcripts may guide epigenomic enzymes to introduce epigenetic modifications towards the anchoring enhancers that produce piRNA targeting eRNAs. On the other hand, the possibility for piRNAs to target and slice these eRNAs adjacent to or away from enhancer DNAs may also represent the modulatory effect over eRNAs.

Perspectives and future work

Elucidating the precise transcriptional consequences and gene regulatory effects of enhancer-directed piRNA targeting requires matched small RNA-seq and transcriptome datasets generated from the exact same isolated germ cell populations in both wild-type and piRNA-deficient contexts. Because such paired datasets are currently scarce in public databases, we addressed this gap by analyzing a newly generated dataset derived from THY1+-enriched spermatogonial stem/progenitor cells (SSCs/SPCs) from wild type and Dnmt3l knockout mice (Fig. 8). Collectively, these results, alongside our discovery of piRNA-eRNA antisense mapping, support a regulatory role for enhancer-associated piRNAs. However, definitive causality will require precise functional assays. To this end, future studies will focus on the analysis of the 3D chromatin architecture to assess changes in enhancer-promoter interactions after manipulating specific piRNA species. These targeted experiments, coupled with matched piRNA and transcriptome profiling can be conducted in cultured chicken primordial germ cells (PGCs). Crucially, unlike mouse PGC-like cells and other in vitro systems where piRNA biogenesis is considered compromised, cultured chicken PGCs possess intact piRNA biogenesis machinery, providing an ideal and robust platform for these functional assays.

The piRNA-enhancer targeting event observed in this study may have implications beyond stage-specific transitions throughout male germ cell development. The proposed untimely and aberrant gene expression pattern may contribute to testicular teratoma formation, which is also correlating to suppression of piRNA pathway components [43]. We hypothesize that aberrant open chromatin around somatic enhancers observed in teratoma may result from compromised piRNA suppression machinery [44], leading to dysregulated expression and function of pluripotent and somatic genes.

Additionally, during epigenetic reprogramming, the instability of repetitive sequences such as TEs and ribosomal DNA (rDNA) may result in the generation and accumulation of extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA) derived from these elements. eccDNA is a kind of circular nuclear DNA that is separate from the chromosomes. Its transcripts can come together and form clusters or hubs that contain eRNA-producing enhancers and can activate transcription [45]. The probability of gonadal piRNAs being important for modulating the enhancers residing on eccDNA to counteract their overactivity in tuning somatic lineage differentiations can be further explored in the future.

Conclusion

This study represents one of the first efforts to characterize the piRNA profile in human fetal testes following genome-wide epigenetic erasure, a process that remains poorly understood in humans. Our sequence analysis provided compelling evidence for active PIWI-piRNA pathways during human germline development. Notably, a substantial proportion of human fetal piRNAs were derived from intronic snoRNA sequences, with their host genes being functionally linked to translational control. In addition, both human and mouse testicular piRNAs mapped to various imprinted genes, implicating their involvement in modulating either the male germline-specific imprinting or the quantity of imprinted transcripts. Furthermore, our findings revealed significant mapping of piRNAs to cis-regulatory regions of the genome, particularly super-enhancers related to lineage determination, a phenomenon conserved across humans, chickens, and mice. In mice, we identified a potential mechanism by which piRNAs associate with enhancers active in pluripotent cells through complementary pairing with their nascent eRNAs. These observations suggest that piRNAs play a role in suppressing aberrant expression of somatic lineage determination genes, and underscore the developmental stage-dependent composition of testis piRNAs in regulating RNA and protein composition/quantity necessary for maintaining germ cell fate in preparation for proper spermatogenesis.

Methods

Human fetal testis collection and library construction

Experimental samples were collected from National Taiwan University Hospital and under IRB approval encompassing human fetal testes from abortuses of gestational week (GW) 12 to GW22 due to genetic or other non-gonadal related phenotypic deficits [46]. With RNA samples acquired from the same batch of tissues, the small RNA-seq datasets generated in this study can be accessed in NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE314801 [47]. The UCSF cohort consisted of elective abortuses at GW13-1, GW13-2, GW14, GW15, GW17, GW19.4, GW20.5, and GW21.5 (repurposing the small RNA sequencing datasets described in Reznik et al. [4].

Human small RNA library construction

Human fetal testes small RNAs from NTU cohort were extracted from abortus gonads using miRNeasy Tissue/Cells Advanced Kits (Cat#: 217,684; Qiagen). Library construction was performed using Illumina Truseq Small RNA Sample Prep Kit, with the 2013 version composing all small RNAs, including microRNAs and larger small RNAs falling into piRNA criteria of 24–34-nt long. In the 2014 version of sequencing analysis, we enriched the proportion of piRNA sequencing reads by actively reducing a significant portion of inserting small RNAs below 22 nt via gel excision during sequencing library preparation. Details for the RNA preparation and sequencing analytical platform have been described in Reznik et al. [4].

Bioinformatic analysis of human fetal testis small RNA

We adapted our previous published pipeline and improved our subtractive method to obtain piRNA candidates [17]. The raw small RNA sequencing reads from human fetal gonads were obtained and underwent quality check using FastQC (v0.10.1). Adapter trimming was performed using cutadapt (v1.13). The RefSeq GRCh38 human genome reference was used in this study. In order to obtain only the reads that matched perfectly to the reference genome, we applied perfect match parameters on GRCh38 mapping with the Tophat2 (v2.0.12) commands “-g 1 -N 0 –read-gap-length 0 –read-edit-dist 0 –read-realign-edit-dist 0 –max-insertion-length 0 –max-deletion-length 0” in order to prevent irrelevant sequences from being included [48]. Bowtie (v1.1.1) was then used to filter out rRNA and tRNA sequences obtained from the Rfam database (v14.7). To identify both novel and known miRNAs, miRDeep2 [49] mapper module was used for preprocessing by mapping to the hg38 reference genome, then the output was run through the main miRDeep2 module to obtain both known miRNA that perfectly mapped to miRbase (v22) [50] and predicted miRNAs. The remaining non-annotated reads with lengths between 24 to 34 nucleotides were taken as piRNA candidates. In order to estimate the confidence of piRNA candidates, we aligned them to the piRBase database (v3.0) [29] and compared the number of mapped to unmapped sequences. We applied nucleotide enrichment analysis for the 1U and 1U10A characteristic signature to validate piRNA candidacy and the presence of a ping-pong cycle, respectively.

Human fetal testicular piRNA cluster prediction

The piRNA candidates were loaded into the piRNA cluster prediction tool proTRAC (v2.4.2) [51] to obtain predicted piRNA clusters.

Human fetal testicular piRNA annotation

The piRNA candidates were annotated using the GRCh38 RefSeq database to assess piRNA origin. To assess piRNA targeting, cluster-derived putative piRNAs were reverse complementary mapped to the GRCh38 genome.

Enhancer association analysis

SEdb was used as the enhancer and super-enhancer database [33, 52]. Samples were curated such that only wild type or primary culture cells were included. These enhancers were grouped by tissue types then merged using the bedtools (v2.30.0) to collapse enhancer regions. Enhancer associated piRNA candidates were annotated by having at least 80% overlap (-f 0.8) with the reference enhancer loci.

Chicken germline piRNA analysis

Oxidation treated small RNA sequencing datasets of different chicken germ line stages were obtained from our previous publication (GSE98005) [17], as well as GSE45049 [14] and GSE165330 [53] from NCBI GEO (Additional file 2: Table S1). Raw reads had the adapter sequences trimmed by Cutadapt 3.4 [54], then mapped to the galGal 6 genome assembly (gga6) using Bowtie2. Chicken piRNA candidates were characterized as 3’-end-2’-O methylated, non-rRNA, tRNA, or miRNA-associated small RNA sequences in length of 24–34 nt [17]. Mapping results were converted to a bigwig track of 25nt bins where bins per million were calculated. Genome coordinates of gga6 non-coding RNA (version 22) were obtained from RNAcentral (https://rnacentral.org/) [55]. Chicken enhancer regions (SEA00601) were obtained from Super-Enhancer Archive 3.0 (SEA3.0; http://sea.edbc.org/) [28]. Sequences were intersected with the RepeatMasker database to determine repeat element-containing regions.

Processing of published mouse testis data

Mouse piRNA datasets (Additional file 2: Table S1) from PIWI protein immunoprecipitation (PIWI-IP) experiments of wild-type mouse testis samples were downloaded from the piRBase v3.0 database [29] to form a set of high-confidence piRNA sequences. Sequence tables were downloaded and transformed into fasta files before alignment to the GENCODE GRCm39 mouse genome assembly using Tophat (v2.1.1). In order to obtain only the reads that matched perfectly to the reference, we applied the parameters “-g 1 -N 0 –read-gap-length 0 –read-edit-dist 0 –read-realign-edit-dist 0 –max-insertion-length 0 –max-deletion-length 0” [48]. Only 24–34-nt sequences were retained.

For mutant comparison studies, mouse piRNA datasets from PIWI-IP and small RNA-seq experiments were taken from various sources in the public domain (Additional file 2: Table S1). For PIWI-IP samples, adapter and quality-based (Q20) trimming using cutadapt and 24–34 nt size selection were performed before alignment. For small RNA-seq samples, known rRNA, tRNA, and miRNA regions from RNAcentral [55] were considered not piRNA candidates and removed after preprocessing.

Potential eRNA-producing regions were taken by extending the TSSs listed in eRNAbase [56] wild-type or primary culture samples by 500 nt in both directions. eRNA regions were collapsed using bedtools merge operation, grouped by tissue or cell types, and taken strand into account using the “-s -c 6 -o distinct” parameters. Mutant versus control piRNA-eRNA association comparisons were done the same way as with enhancers.

Feature mapping of published mouse testis PIWI-IP sequencing data

piRNA feature mapping was done using the bedtools intersect module [57]. Each piRNA read was only counted as a potential feature-associated piRNA once (-u) if it had at least 80% overlap (-f 0.8) with at least one feature. This was done to account for piRNAs in border or flanking regions that may still impact their expression, as well as for mismatch tolerance of piRNAs [58].

Both genomic piRNA origins (sense mapping) and targets (antisense mapping) were investigated for repeat and non-repeat associated piRNAs using bedtools intersect. piRNAs originating from TEs were determined by sense piRNA intersection with the RepeatMasker database [59]. Genic, protein-coding mRNA, lncRNA, and intergenic regions were separated from the GENCODE GRCm39 VM33 annotation file.

piRNA-enhancer association testing

Enhancer data for wild type or primary culture samples were downloaded from SEdb 2.0 [33], grouped by tissue, and collapsed using bedtools merge module with default settings. Meiotic and mitotic super-enhancer datasets were provided by Dr. Satoshi Namekawa [20]. Intersection was performed as above to determine the percentage of piRNAs that associate with at least one enhancer.

piRNA-enhancer abundance comparison testing

The piRNA counts for each enhancer element was determined using bedtools intersect using the parameters “-F 0.8 -c”. DESeq2 (v1.42.1) on R (v4.3.3) was used for differential association analysis between knockout and control [60]. The core statistical methods implemented behind DESeq2 include: the negative binomial distribution for modeling the piRNA sequencing read counts; usage of median of ratios for size factor normalization to address differences in sequencing depth; estimation of dispersion parameters for piRNA counts per enhancer with maximum likelihood and apply empirical Bayes shrinkage for these estimates by sharing information across piRNA mapping per enhancer with similar values; fitting into generalized linear models (GLMs) and perform Wald test for identifying differential piRNA mapping to each enhancer between different samples. Significant differences were considered to have adjusted p-values of less than or equal to 0.05 and log2 fold change values of greater than or equal to 1 or less than or equal to −1. Enhancers with significantly different piRNA association were rlog transformed and clustered using the pheatmap R package (v1.0.12) into at least two groups as determined by the gap statistic value (cluster v4.3.0). The SEdb-listed closest active genes to the selected enhancers were input for gene ontology analysis using the cluster Profiler R package (v4.10.1).

Matched small RNA and transcriptome profiling of THY1+ germ cells

A total of 140 male pups at 8 days postpartum (dpp) were used for matched small RNA and transcriptome profiling. For each sequencing replicate, testes from 35 genotype-matched 8 dpp pups (either homozygous Dnmt3l mutants, Dnmt3ltm1Enl, or wild-type control littermates) were pooled. THY1+ spermatogonial stem/progenitor cells (SSCs/SPCs) were enriched from these pooled samples according to our previously published protocols [61, 62]. Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) and assessed for integrity, yielding excellent RNA Integrity Number ranging from 9.8 to 10. Small RNAs of 15–36 nt in length were subsequently purified via polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Small RNA sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina small RNA sample preparation kit (Illumina, SUA) and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2000. The resulting small RNA dataset (GSE313665) [63] was subjected to the same bioinformatic pipeline described above to identify piRNA-like sequences for subsequent analyses. To enable direct correlation analysis, aliquots of the exact same total RNA extracted from these isolated THY1+ cells were concurrently used for poly-A+ RNA sequencing (GSE54411) [61].
