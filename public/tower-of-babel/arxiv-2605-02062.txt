Neural Generative Distributional Regression
Jinhang Chai, Jianqing Fan, Yihong Gu

Abstract
Any continuous conditional distribution of Y Y given X X can be generated from a transform of a known noise distribution U U such as the uniform or normal distribution via Y = g ⁡ ( X , U ) Y=g(X,U) . This paper provides an estimator of such a generative transformation g g by minimizing the empirical energy distance between distributions of Y Y and g ⁡ ( X , U ) g(X,U) , and implements it via neural networks. The estimated distribution can then be readily applied to downstream tasks such as conditional moment estimation, predictive interval construction, and conditional density estimation. By leveraging the representation power of neural networks, the estimator can adaptively exploit low-dimensional structures in a purely algorithmic manner. Theoretically, we establish an oracle inequality attaining the adaptive optimal nonparametric rates. Numerical simulations and real data analysis further demonstrate the practical effectiveness of the proposed method.



Keywords
: Energy Statistics, Neural Networks, Distributional Regression, Generative Model

However, it is still unclear whether these losses can be realized for both sample-efficient and computation-efficient distributional regression in theory. There is some work establishing the statistical rate of convergence for the estimators using CRPS loss, including the isotonic regression
(

, and kernel estimators

; the latter suffers from the curse-of-dimensionality in the covariate dimension

In this paper, we fill the above gap by showing that a neural generative distributional regression method, which uses regression-type loss, with similar computation and implementation as regression, can fully leverage the neural network’s sample efficiency in modeling conditional distributions and further leads to accurate downstream conditional quantities estimation. The core idea, which dates back to the conditional generative adversarial network

and some noise

Once trained, downstream quantities can be efficiently estimated via Monte Carlo sampling from the fitted conditional simulator. While we consider the general kernel function in Appendix
D

(
69
)

We provide a thorough and sharp non-asymptotic analysis of the proposed method. We summarize the key findings below.

•

We establish the non-asymptotic oracle inequalities akin to regression for the proposed estimator. Moreover, in a similar spirit to the conditional moment regression, we show that as long as the conditional quantile function admits the form of a hierarchical composition model
(
3
;
60
;
21
)
, the estimator can get an optimal, no curse-of-dimension rate of convergence in the
L_{2}
distance of the induced CDF.

•

Our error bound offers an explicit dependence on the number of auxiliary noises

•

Although the conditional distribution simulator

only has error guarantees of
L_{2}

This supports its versatility and sample efficiency in downstream estimation tasks.

1.1
Related Works

Our study connects to several active areas of research spanning generative modeling, distributional prediction, and statistical learning theory.

Implicit generative modeling.

The proposed estimator is closely related to implicit generative models implemented via deep neural networks, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs;
27
) and diffusion-based models
(
35
)
.
Both frameworks have proven highly effective for flexible density modeling but face well-documented limitations.
GANs and their variants, such as Wasserstein GANs
(
1
)
, require solving a minimax optimization problem and often suffer from mode collapse, training instability, and sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning
(
70
)
, whereas diffusion models typically entail heavy computational overhead and slow inference due to iterative denoising procedures
(
47
)
.
In contrast, our method offers a lightweight and stable alternative: it preserves the expressiveness of implicit generative models while maintaining computational complexity comparable to standard regression.

Uncertainty quantification and distributional prediction.

Our framework also contributes to the literature on uncertainty-aware prediction, encompassing quantile regression
(
44
;
4
)
, conformal prediction
(
46
;
2
)
, and predictive inference methods
(
17
;
18
)
.
These approaches typically augment point estimators with post-hoc uncertainty measures or calibration procedures.
By contrast, our method models the entire conditional distribution directly, thereby integrating uncertainty learning intrinsically within the estimation process and avoiding the need for separate calibration or conformalization steps.

Energy-based objectives and distributional metrics.

The loss function employed in our estimator is based on the energy distance
(
69
)
, which can be viewed as a specific instance of the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD;
28
), itself belonging to the broader class of integral probability metrics (IPM;
49
).
Energy-based distances provide a flexible nonparametric approach for quantifying discrepancies between probability distributions when the likelihood function is computationally intractable.
A growing line of work has studied the statistical properties of MMD in parametric estimation problems
(
6
;
12
;
51
)
. Our work differs by focusing on the nonparametric setting.
Moreover, our analysis reveals a surprising efficiency property: using as few as two auxiliary samples (
m_{2}=2
) per batch suffices to achieve the same asymptotic accuracy as employing a large number of samples.

Implicit conditional modeling.

Finally, our approach is conceptually related to Engression
(
63
)
, which also adopts an implicit generative framework for conditional distribution estimation. However, their work primarily focuses on the extrapolation capability of the method, considering the regime where auxiliary sample size per batch
m_{2}
tends to infinity, and does not establish nonasymptotic results for nonparametric settings. In contrast, our work develops rigorous statistical theory—including optimal nonasymptotic convergence rates, oracle inequalities, and guarantees for downstream tasks.

2.1
Implicit Conditional Distribution Estimation via Sampling

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the covariate and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
be the response variable. Our primary goal is to understand or predict

based on observed

Y
given
X
. While we can directly estimate probabilistic quantities like the conditional Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) or conditional probability density function (PDF) directly, in this paper, we adopt a sampling-based approach. Given an i.i.d. sample
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\sim\mu_{0}

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(X=x,U)\overset{d}{\approx}Y|X=x\qquad(X,Y)\sim\mu_{0},

(2.1)

for a noise vector

.
We refer to the above problem as the

since it is modeled implicitly via sampling.

2.2

and

(

)

(

(

;

)

(

)

(

We have the following identity unveiling the relationship between the defined quantity (

\displaystyle E^{2}(X,Y)=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{F_{X}(t)-F_{Y}(t)\right\}^{2}dt.

3.1
Distributional Regression Using Energy Loss

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d. sample from
\mu_{0}

is sampled from the distribution

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}(g)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\{2\mathbb{E}_{U}[|g(X_{i},U)-Y_{i}|]-\mathbb{E}_{U,U^{\prime}}[|g(X_{i},U)-g(X_{i},U^{\prime})|]\Big\},

which is the energy distance (
2.1

As the form

However, the exact calculation of
\mathbb{E}_{U}[|g(X_{i},U)-Y_{i}|]
and
\mathbb{E}_{U,U^{\prime}}[|g(X_{i},U)-g(X_{i},U^{\prime})|]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{R}_{n,m}(g)=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\sum_{b=1}^{m_{1}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg(&\frac{2}{m_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{2}}|Y_{i}-g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k})|\\
&~~~~~~~~-\frac{1}{m_{2}(m_{2}-1)}\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}|g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k})-g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k^{\prime}})|\Bigg)\end{split}

3.2
Downstream Estimation via Sampling

Given that our distributional regression estimator can learn the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
using observations, many downstream estimation and inference problems can be further done via first sampling from our distributional regression estimator
\widehat{g}
in (
3.3
) followed by straightforward calculation, which includes point estimations, prediction band construction, conditional density estimation, and conditional score function estimation. Let
U_{1},\ldots U_{k}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mu_{u}
as generated i.i.d. noises used in all the following, where
k
is the user-defined number of noise samples in evaluation.

Conditional Moments.
For given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, one can estimate the conditional expectation
m_{w}^{\star}(x)=\mathbb{E}[w(Y)|X=x]
for a given function
w
. This can be estimated by
\mathbb{E}_{U}[w(\widehat{g}(x,U))]
and computed by generating a sufficiently large sample size
k
and taking the generated sample mean:

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{w}(x)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}w(\widehat{g}(x,U_{l})).

(3.4)

It can also be implemented by numerical integration with the midpoint method.

Conditional Quantile.
One may also be interested in estimating the conditional quantile of
Y
given
X=x
, which can also be viewed as a prediction interval construction. Let
Q^{\star}(\alpha|x)
be the conditional
\alpha
-quantile of
Y
, given by

\displaystyle Q^{\star}(\alpha|x)=\inf_{q}\left\{\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)\sim\mu_{0}}(Y\leq q|X=x)\geq\alpha\right\}.

(3.5)

We use the following empirical quantile estimator based on generated samples of
\widehat{g}(x,U)
to estimate
Q^{\star}(\alpha|x)
, namely,

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}(\alpha|x)=\inf_{t}\Big\{t:\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}{\mathds{1}}\{\widehat{g}(x,U_{l})\leq t\}\geq\alpha\Big\}.

(3.6)

Prediction Interval.

One may also be interested in constructing a prediction interval that ensures both good coverage and small average width. Here, we can use the generated samples to build a more straightforward and covariate-adaptive prediction band. For a pre-determined confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we first calculate the ordered statistics
\{\widehat{Y}_{(l)}(x)\}_{l=1}^{k}
of
\{\widehat{g}(x,U_{l})\}_{l=1}^{k}
, and let

\displaystyle\widehat{l}(x)=\arg\min_{1\leq l\leq k-\lceil\alpha k\rceil}\left(\widehat{Y}_{(l+\lceil\alpha k\rceil)}(x)-\widehat{Y}_{(l)}(x)\right),

(3.7)

the shortest interval with empirical coverage
\alpha
. Then, the prediction band is

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{PI}}_{\alpha}(x):=\left[\widehat{Y}_{\widehat{l}(x)}(x),\widehat{Y}_{\widehat{l}(x)+\lceil(1-\alpha)k\rceil}(x)\right].

(3.8)

The minimum in (
3.7
) is to reduce the length of the interval. The prediction intervals can also be
constructed by using the traditional interval
\left[\widehat{Y}_{\lfloor\alpha k/2\rfloor}(x),\widehat{Y}_{\lceil(1-\alpha/2)k\rceil}(x)\right]
.

Conditional Density Function Estimation.

One can also build a conditional density estimator to estimate the conditional density
p^{\star}(y|x)
on top of our learned conditional sampler
\widehat{g}(x,\cdot)
. For a given smooth hyper-parameter
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and bandwidth
h
, let
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
m
-order kernel function satisfying

\displaystyle K(0)=1,\qquad\int s^{j}K(s)ds=0~~\forall j\in[m-1],\qquad\text{and}~~\int|s^{m}K(s)|ds<\infty.

(3.9)

Typical choices of second-order kernel are uniform, Epanechnikov and Gaussian kernels. The kernel conditional density estimator for given
x
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(y|x)=\frac{1}{kh}\sum_{l=1}^{k}K\left(\frac{y-\widehat{g}(x,U_{l})}{h}\right).

(3.10)

Conditional Score Function Estimation.

5
Numerical Studies

In this section, we present both simulation and real-data experiments to validate the theoretical results and demonstrate the empirical performance of the proposed method.

5.1
Simulation Models

We begin by examining several synthetic data-generating mechanisms designed to evaluate different aspects of the proposed estimator.
Throughout, the covariate
X
is drawn from the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]
, that is,
X\sim\mathcal{U}[-1,1]
. We investigate five models representing distinct conditional structures for

Y\mid X
:
Model 1a is a univariate location–scale model, and Model 1b provides its multivariate extensions.
Models 2a and 2b specify explicit and implicit forms of the conditional distribution, respectively.
Finally, Model 3 is a Gaussian mixture model designed to test the estimator’s ability to capture multimodality.

Model 1a (Univariate location–scale model).

We begin with a heteroskedastic location–scale model of the form

Y=\mu(X)+\sigma(X)U,\quad\mu(X)=\sin(3X),\quad\sigma(X)=1-0.7\cos(4X),

where
U\sim\mathcal{U}[-1,1]
. This model features nonlinear mean and variance structures.

Model 1b (Multivariate location–scale model).

We next extend Model 1a to a five-dimensional covariate
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{5})
.
Two variants are examined:

1.

Additive model:

\mu_{\mathsf{add}}(X)=\sin(3x_{1})+0.5\sin(3x_{2}),\quad\sigma_{\mathsf{add}}(X)=1-0.3\cos(x_{1})-0.2\cos(2x_{3}).

2.

Interactive model:

\qquad\mu_{\mathsf{int}}(X)=x_{1}x_{2},\quad\sigma_{\mathsf{int}}(X)=1-\frac{1}{2}x_{3}x_{4}.

These settings allow us to examine both additive and interaction effects in higher dimensions.

Model 2a (Explicit conditional density).

We then consider a model with an analytically tractable conditional probability density function (PDF):

p_{Y}(t\mid X)=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{v(X)t}{16}\right)\mathbb{I}\{|t|\leq 1\},\quad v(X)=1+X+4X^{2}.

The corresponding conditional cumulative distribution function (CDF), quantile function, and conditional mean are

\displaystyle F_{Y}(t\mid X)

\displaystyle=(t+1)\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{(1-t)v(X)}{32}\Big),

\displaystyle\quad Q(\alpha\mid X)

\displaystyle=\frac{8-\sqrt{64+v(X)\big(16+v(X)-32\alpha\big)}}{v(X)},\quad\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=-\frac{v(X)}{24}.

Model 2b (Implicitly defined conditional distribution).

Here we specify
Y
through a transformation of a latent variable
U
:

Y=g(X,U)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{v(X)U+U^{2}}{4}\right),\quad U\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],

with
v(X)=1+X+4X^{2}
.
The corresponding conditional CDF admits the closed-form expression

F_{Y}(t\mid X)=\frac{-v(X)+\sqrt{v(X)^{2}-16\log t}}{2},\quad t\in[0,e^{-(v(X)+1)/4}],

and the conditional mean is given by

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=2\sqrt{\pi}\Big[\Phi\!\Big(\frac{v(X)}{2\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)-\Phi\!\Big(\frac{v(X)}{2\sqrt{2}}\Big)\Big]e^{v(X)^{2}/16},

where
\Phi
denotes the standard normal CDF.

Model 3 (Gaussian mixture model).

Finally, we include a conditional Gaussian mixture model to capture multimodality:

Y\sim\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\cos(4\pi X),\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\cos^{2}(4\pi X),\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

This design allows us to assess the estimator’s robustness under non-Gaussian and mixture structures.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

We write the population-level counterpart of (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(g)=\mathbb{E}\left[2|g(X,U)-Y|-|g(X,U)-g(X,U^{\prime})|\right].

For any
g,\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)
, define the following two key quantities.

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(g,\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widetilde{g})-\left(\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(\widetilde{g})\right)

\displaystyle\chi_{n}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{n,m}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}({g}).

These two quantities separate the two sources of randomness coming from original samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the generated random variables
\{U_{b,i,k}\}
.
The following two propositions are the key to our main results, where the first uses localization to derive an instance-dependent error bound, and the second unravels the effects of randomly generated random variables.

Proposition A.1
.

Under Condition
4.1
, the following event

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}(g,\widetilde{g})|\leq C\left(\delta_{n,t}^{2}+\delta_{n,t}\|F_{g}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}\right)\ ,\forall g,\widetilde{g}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\delta_{n,t}=NL\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{t}{n}}

occurs with probability at least
1-2e^{-t}
.

Proposition A.2
.

Under Condition
4.1
, the following event

\displaystyle|\chi_{n}(g)|\leq C\left(\frac{\delta_{n,t}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}+\delta^{2}_{n,t}\right)

occurs with probability at least
1-2e^{-t}
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4.1
. It follows from the definition of
\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}
that

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\int(F_{g}(x,t)-F_{\widetilde{g}}(x,t))^{2}dt\mu_{x}(dx)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~+2\int(F_{g}(x,t)-F_{\widetilde{g}}(x,t))(F_{\widetilde{g}}(x,t)-F^{\star}(x,t))dt\mu_{x}(dx)

\displaystyle\geq 0.5\int(F_{g}(x,t)-F_{\widetilde{g}}(x,t))^{2}dt\mu_{x}(dx)-2\int(F^{\star}(x,t)-F_{\widetilde{g}}(x,t))^{2}dt\mu_{x}(dx)

\displaystyle=0.5\|F_{g}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}-2\|F^{\star}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}.

At the same time, it follows from the optimality condition and Proposition
A.1
and
A.2
that for any
\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{G}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widehat{g})+\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widetilde{g})+\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widetilde{g})-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle\leq|\Delta_{n}(\widehat{g},\widetilde{g})|+\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle\leq|\Delta_{n}(\widehat{g},\widetilde{g})|+\mathsf{R}_{n,m}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{n,m}(\widetilde{g})+|\chi_{n}(\widehat{g})|+|\chi_{n}(\widetilde{g})|

\displaystyle\leq 3C\delta_{n,t}^{2}+C^{2}\delta_{n,t}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|F_{\widehat{g}}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{\mathtt{opt}}+2C\frac{\delta_{n,t}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}.

where in the last inequality we used
\delta_{n,t}\|F_{\widehat{g}}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{4C}\|F_{\widehat{g}}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}+C\delta_{n,t}^{2}
. Combining the two inequalities together, we can obtain

\displaystyle 0.25\|F_{\widehat{g}}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(C^{2}+3C)\delta_{n,t}^{2}+2C\frac{\delta_{n,t}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}+\delta_{\mathtt{opt}}+2\|F^{\star}-F_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}.

We can then conclude the proof using triangle inequality and taking
\widetilde{g}
by minimizing the approximation error.

We start off by bounding

To that aim, we need the following claim.

Claim 3

For a fixed

Proof.

We fix

and

as constants throughout this proof.

Denote

\mu_{2}=-\mathbb{E}[|g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k})-g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k^{\prime}})|\Big|X_{i},Y_{i}]

The first term in the above is easy as
|Y_{i}-g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k})|
as independent for

, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{2}{m_{1}m_{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{m_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{2}}(|Y_{i}-g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k})|-\mu_{1})\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp(-\frac{m_{1}m_{2}t^{2}}{8}).

For the second term, we use the standard splitting for proving U-statistic-type concentration inequality.

Let

consecutive non-overlapping pairs. Let

Appendix B
Proof of Energy Identity

Proof.

Let
P,Q
be two general distributions.
For random variables
X\sim P,\ Y\sim Q
, we rewrite the energy statistics as
E(X,Y)=d_{E}(P,Q)
.
We first claim that

\displaystyle d_{E}(P,Q)=2\int\{P(t)-Q(t)\}^{2}dx

(B.1)

where for simplicity of notation, we also use
P
and
Q
to denote the CDFs of
P
and
Q
.

To prove that,
we have from a simple calculation that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P,Y\sim Q}[|X-Y|]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}(x-y)dQ(y)+\int_{x}^{\infty}(y-x)dQ(y)\right)dP(x)

\displaystyle=\int\left(2xQ(x)-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}ydQ(y)+\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)dP(x).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P,Y\sim Q}[|X-Y|]-\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim P}[|X-X^{\prime}|]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y]-\mathbb{E}[X]+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(2x(Q(x)-P(x))-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}y(dQ(y)-dP(y))\right)dP(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y]-\mathbb{E}[X]+2\left(xQ(x)-xP(x)-\int_{-\infty}^{x}y(dQ(y)-dP(y))\right)\Big|_{x=-\infty}^{x=\infty}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2(Q(x)-P(x))P(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2P(x)(P(x)-Q(x))dx.

Similarly, we also have

C_{x}=\frac{1}{h}\int K\left(s\right)\frac{s^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\frac{\partial^{m}F^{\star}(x,\widetilde{y})}{\partial\widetilde{y}^{m}}\Big|_{\widetilde{y}=\xi(y,s)}ds
.
Then by the Lipschitzness of
\partial^{m}F^{\star}(x,y)/\partial y^{m}
.

\displaystyle C_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int K\left(s\right)\frac{s^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\left(\frac{\partial^{m}F^{\star}(x,\widetilde{y})}{\partial\widetilde{y}^{m}}\Big|_{\widetilde{y}=\xi(y,s)}-\frac{\partial^{m}F^{\star}(x,\widetilde{y})}{\partial\widetilde{y}^{m}}\Big|_{\widetilde{y}=y}\right)ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h}\int K\left(s\right)\frac{s^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}l_{f}(hs)ds=\int K\left(s\right)\frac{s^{m}}{(m-1)!}l_{f}ds

Hence
C_{x}
is bounded by a constant
C_{f}
.

For the variance term, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}[\widehat{p}(Y|x)\big|\widehat{g}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{kh^{2}}\int K^{2}\left(\frac{t-y}{h}\right)dF_{\widehat{g}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kh^{2}}\int K^{2}\left(\frac{t-y}{h}\right)dF^{\star}(x,t)+\frac{1}{kh^{2}}\int\frac{2}{h}K\left(\frac{t-y}{h}\right)K^{\prime}\left(\frac{t-y}{h}\right)(F_{\widehat{g}}(x,t)-F^{\star}(x,t))dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{kh}+\frac{1}{kh^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\int(F_{\widehat{g}}(x,t)-F^{\star}(x,t))^{2}dt}{h}}

where we use the integral by parts in the equality and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality in the second inequality.
Hence we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(\widehat{p}(y|X)-p^{\star}(y|X))^{2}dy\Big|\widehat{g}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(\widehat{p}(y|X)-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}(Y|X)\big|\widehat{g},X])^{2}dy\Big|\widehat{g}\Big]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~+2\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}(Y|X)\big|\widehat{g},X]-p^{\star}(y|X))^{2}dy\Big|\widehat{g}\Big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{h^{3}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(F_{\widehat{g}}(X,t)-F^{\star}(X,t))^{2}dt\Big|\widehat{g}\Big]+h^{2m}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Var}[\widehat{p}(Y|X)\big|\widehat{g},X]\Big|\widehat{g}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h^{3}}\delta_{\mathtt{NDRE}}+h^{2m}+\frac{1}{kh}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{\mathtt{NDRE}}}}{kh^{5/2}}

Take
h\asymp\max\{k^{-\frac{1}{2m+1}},(\delta_{\mathtt{NDRE}})^{\frac{1}{2m+3}}\}
, we have that
\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{\mathtt{NDRE}}}}{kh^{5/2}}\leq\frac{1}{kh}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(\widehat{p}(y|X)-p^{\star}(y|X))^{2}dy\Big|\widehat{g}\Big]\lesssim\max\{k^{-\frac{1}{2m+1}},(\delta_{\mathtt{NDRE}})^{\frac{1}{2m+3}}\}^{2m}

which completes the proof of the first part. The proof of the second part is similar to Proposition
4.4
.
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{q}(Y|x)\big|\widehat{g}]-q^{\star}(y|x)

.

\partial^{m}F^{\star}(x,y)/\partial y^{m}

\displaystyle C_{x}

Appendix D
General Kernel function

D.1
Distributional Regression Using Kernel MMD Loss

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d. sample from
\mu_{0}
.
Let
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{B})
be a measurable space and let

k:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable, symmetric, positive definite kernel with associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
We assume
k
is bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}k(y,y)\leq\kappa^{2}<\infty
.

For a fixed covariate value
x
, denote by
P_{Y|X=x}
the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X=x
.
Given a stochastic generator
g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{Y}
and a noise variable
U\sim\mu_{u}
, define the induced conditional distribution

P_{g,x}:=\mathcal{L}\big(g(x,U)\big).

We measure the discrepancy between
P_{g,x}
and
P_{Y|X=x}
using the squared maximum mean discrepancy, defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}_{k}^{2}(P,Q):=\mathbb{E}_{Z,Z^{\prime}\sim P}[k(Z,Z^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}_{W,W^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(W,W^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P,\,W\sim Q}[k(Z,W)].

(D.1)

The
\mu_{u}
-population-level objective is then given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}^{(k)}(g):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{MMD}_{k}^{2}\big(P_{g,X_{i}},\,P_{Y|X=X_{i}}\big).

(D.2)

Expanding the definition and noting that
Y_{i}
is a draw from
P_{Y|X=X_{i}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}^{(k)}(g)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{U,U^{\prime}}\big[k(g(X_{i},U),g(X_{i},U^{\prime}))\big]-2\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[k(g(X_{i},U),Y_{i})\big]\Big\}+C_{n},

(D.3)

where
C_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(Y_{i},Y_{i}^{\prime})
is independent of
g
and can be ignored in optimization.

Thus, minimizing
\mathsf{R}_{n}^{(k)}(g)
corresponds to matching the conditional distribution of
g(X,U)
to that of
Y|X
in the RKHS induced by
k
. We term this approach
kernel distributional regression
.

Empirical Objective.

As in (
3.2
), the expectations in (
D.3
) are generally intractable for complex function classes such as neural networks.
We approximate them via Monte Carlo sampling.

Let
m=(m_{1},m_{2})
and let
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{b,i,k}\}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu_{u}
, independent of
\mathcal{D}
.
We define the empirical objective

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(g)=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\sum_{b=1}^{m_{1}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg(&\frac{1}{m_{2}(m_{2}-1)}\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}k\big(g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k}),\,g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k^{\prime}})\big)\\
&\quad-\frac{2}{m_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{2}}k\big(g(X_{i},U_{b,i,k}),\,Y_{i}\big)\Bigg).\end{split}

(D.4)

We minimize
\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(g)
over the stochastic neural network class

\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d,L,N,\widetilde{d},M,B)
.

Computational Considerations.

As in the energy loss formulation, the quadratic complexity in
m_{2}
suggests choosing a small
m_{2}\geq 2
and a larger
m_{1}
.
The computations across
b\in[m_{1}]
can be parallelized, while those across
k\in[m_{2}]
cannot.
The statistical accuracy depends on the total number of auxiliary samples
m_{1}m_{2}
, making this decomposition computationally advantageous.

Estimator.

Our estimator
\widehat{g}
approximately minimizes (
D.4
) in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(\widehat{g})\leq\inf_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d,L,N,\widetilde{d},M,B)}\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(g)+\delta_{\mathtt{opt}},

(D.5)

where
\delta_{\mathtt{opt}}
denotes the optimization error.

Relation to Energy Loss.

When
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
k(x,y)=\min(x,y)
, the MMD loss (
D.2
) is equivalent to the energy loss in (
3.1
), and admits the representation

\mathrm{MMD}_{k}^{2}(P,Q)=\int(F_{P}(t)-F_{Q}(t))^{2}\,dt,

where
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are cumulative distribution functions.
Thus, the energy loss can be viewed as a special case of kernel MMD regression corresponding to a first-order Sobolev kernel.

D.2
Excess Risk Bound for General Kernel MMD Loss

Let
k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable, symmetric, positive definite kernel with RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and canonical feature map
\phi(y)=k(y,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Throughout this section, we impose the following condition.

Condition D.1
.

There exist constants
\kappa,L_{k}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in[-M,M]}k(y,y)\leq\kappa^{2},\qquad\|\phi(y)-\phi(y^{\prime})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq L_{k}|y-y^{\prime}|,\qquad\forall y,y^{\prime}\in[-M,M].

Moreover,
|Y|\leq M
almost surely and every
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)
satisfies
|g(x,u)|\leq M
for all
(x,u)\in[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]
.

Remark 4
.

It is straightforward to verify that the first-order Sobolev kernel, linear kernel, and Gaussian kernel all satisfy this condition.

For any
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)
, define its conditional kernel mean embedding

\displaystyle\mu_{g}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\phi(g(x,U))]\in\mathcal{H}_{k},

(D.6)

and define the target conditional embedding

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}(x):=\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)\mid X=x]\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

(D.7)

We also write

\displaystyle\|\mu_{g}-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[\|\mu_{g}(X)-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big].

Recall the empirical kernel objective

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(g)=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\sum_{b=1}^{m_{1}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg(\frac{1}{m_{2}(m_{2}-1)}\sum_{r\neq s}k(g(X_{i},U_{b,i,r}),g(X_{i},U_{b,i,s}))-\frac{2}{m_{2}}\sum_{r=1}^{m_{2}}k(g(X_{i},U_{b,i,r}),Y_{i})\Bigg).\end{split}

(D.8)

Its population-level counterpart is

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mu_{g}(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}-2\langle\phi(Y),\mu_{g}(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\Big].

(D.9)

Similarly, define the exact empirical counterpart

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}^{(k)}(g):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\|\mu_{g}(X_{i})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}-2\langle\phi(Y_{i}),\mu_{g}(X_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\Big).

(D.10)

The following identity is the analogue of the energy identity.

Lemma D.1
.

For every
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g^{\star})=\|\mu_{g}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2},

(D.11)

where
g^{\star}
denotes any measurable minimizer of
\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}
over all measurable generators, equivalently any measurable map satisfying
\mu_{g^{\star}}(x)=\mu^{\star}(x)
for
\mu_{X}
-a.e.
x
.

Proof.

Using (
D.7
), for every measurable
m:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
with
\mathbb{E}\|m(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}<\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\phi(Y)-m(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\phi(Y)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|m(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]-2\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\phi(Y),m(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\phi(Y)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|m(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]-2\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mu^{\star}(X),m(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\big].

Taking
m=\mu_{g}
gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g)=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\phi(Y)-\mu_{g}(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|\phi(Y)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big].

Hence

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g^{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\phi(Y)-\mu_{g}(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}-\|\phi(Y)-\mu^{\star}(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mu_{g}(X)-\mu^{\star}(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\big],

where the last equality follows from the orthogonality relation

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\phi(Y)-\mu^{\star}(X),\,\mu_{g}(X)-\mu^{\star}(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\big]=0.

This proves (
D.11
).
∎

For any
g,\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)
, define

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{(k)}(g,\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}^{(k)}(\widetilde{g})-\Big(\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widetilde{g})\Big),

\displaystyle\chi_{n}^{(k)}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{n,\infty}^{(k)}(g).

As before, these two quantities separate the randomness from the original observations and the Monte Carlo noise.

We first record the two key propositions. Their proofs follow the same localization and chaining arguments as in Proposition
A.1
and Proposition
A.2
, after replacing the scalar CDF discrepancy by the Hilbert-space discrepancy
\mu_{g}-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}
. We therefore only use them in the proof of the theorem below.

Proposition D.1
.

Under Condition
4.1
and Condition
D.1
, the following event

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}^{(k)}(g,\widetilde{g})|\leq C\Big(\kappa^{2}\delta_{n,t}^{2}+\kappa\delta_{n,t}\|\mu_{g}-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}\Big),\qquad\forall g,\widetilde{g},

occurs with probability at least
1-2e^{-t}
, where

\displaystyle\delta_{n,t}:=NL\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{t}{n}}.

Proposition D.2
.

Under Condition
4.1
and Condition
D.1
, the following event

\displaystyle|\chi_{n}^{(k)}(g)|\leq C\kappa^{2}\left(\frac{\delta_{n,t}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}+\delta_{n,t}^{2}\right),\qquad\forall g,

occurs with probability at least
1-2e^{-t}
.

We are now ready to state and prove the oracle inequality.

Theorem D.3
.

Suppose Condition
4.1
and Condition
D.1
hold, and let
\widehat{g}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(\widehat{g})\leq\inf_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)}\mathsf{R}_{n,m}^{(k)}(g)+\delta_{\mathtt{opt}}.

(D.12)

Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that, with probability at least
1-4e^{-t}
,

\displaystyle\|\mu_{\widehat{g}}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\left(\inf_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)}\|\mu_{g}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa^{2}\delta_{n,t}^{2}+\kappa^{2}\frac{\delta_{n,t}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}+\delta_{\mathtt{opt}}\right).

(D.13)

Proof.

Fix any
\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{nn}}(d+1,N,L,B,M)
. By Lemma
D.1
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widetilde{g})=\|\mu_{g}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mu_{\widetilde{g}}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

Expanding the square yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(g)-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\|\mu_{g}-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\big\langle\mu_{g}(X)-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}(X),\,\mu_{\widetilde{g}}(X)-\mu^{\star}(X)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{g}-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}-2\|\mu_{\widetilde{g}}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from
2ab\leq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+2b^{2}
. Applying this with
g=\widehat{g}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widetilde{g})\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{\widehat{g}}-\mu_{\widetilde{g}}\|_{2}^{2}-2\|\mu_{\widetilde{g}}-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

(D.14)

On the other hand, by the definition of
\Delta_{n}^{(k)}
,
\chi_{n}^{(k)}
, and the approximate optimality (
D.12
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widehat{g})-\mathsf{R}_{\infty,\infty}^{(k)}(\widetilde{g})
