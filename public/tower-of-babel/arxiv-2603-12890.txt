Study of attractors and fractal functions on the product spaces and Dimensional aspects
Alamgir Hossain

Abstract
. In this paper, the product of the Hausdorff metric on the product space is defined and the equivalency between the product Hausdorff metric and the Hausdorff metric on the product space is established. The finite product of the iterated function systems (IFS) on the product space is considered and the relation between the attractor of the product IFS and the attractors of the co-ordinate IFSs is studied. Dimension bounds of the homogeneous and inhomogeneous attractors on the product space is established. Also, the product fractal interpolation function on the higher dimensional space is constructed.

1.
Introduction

A fractal can be described as an object which exhibits interesting features on a large range of scales. In pure mathematics, the middle third Cantor set, the Sierpinski triangle, the boundary of the Mandelbrot set, and the von Koch snowflake are archetypal examples and, in ‘real life’, examples include the surface of a lung, the horizon of a forest, and the distribution of stars in the galaxy. The term ‘fractal’ was first used by mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot
[
18
]
in 1975. Mandelbrot based it on the Latin fractus, meaning ‘broken’ or ‘fractured’, and used it to extend the concept of theoretical fractional dimensions to geometric patterns in nature. In mathematics, an iterated function system (IFS) is a method of constructing fractal. IFS fractals are more related to set theory than fractal geometry. The notion of dimension is central to fractal geometry. A dimension is a (usually non-negative real) number which gives geometric information concerning how the object in question fills up the space on small scales. There are many distinct notions of dimension and one of the joys (and central components) of the subject is in understanding how these notions relate to each other. One way of constructing new fractal from old is by forming Cartesian products in the higher dimension spaces. Yimin xiao
[
27
]
, J. M. Marstrand
[
19
]
, A. S. Besicovitch and P. A. P. Moran
[
21
]
and J. D. Howroyd
[
14
]
studied some certain results on various dimensions of the product set in the product spaces.

Formally, an IFS is a collection of finite set of contraction mappings on a complete metric space. Hutchinson
[
15
]
in 1981 showed that for a complete metric space
X
, such a system of functions has a unique nonempty compact fixed set S. This set is called an attractor (or some time it is called homogeneous attractor) of an IFS. In
[
6
]
, M. F. Barnsley and S. Demko introduce a new concept of an IFS with condensation set. They have shown that this systems also have a unique fixed set. This set is called inhomogeneous attractor of an IFS with condensation. In general, the inhomogeneous attractor is not similar to homogeneous attractor. But, if the condensation set is empty, then it coincide with homogeneous attractor of an IFS. Nina Snigireva
[
26
]
has given a nice relation between the inhomogeneous and homogeneous attractors of an IFS with condensation set. In the Lemma
2.2
, we proved this relation in a different way, which is not similar to the proof given in
[
26
]
. Indeed, many fractals that occur in practice, for example an attractor of an IFS, are product or, at least, are locally product-like. But in general the attractor in higher dimensional spaces are difficult to study or even to visualize. Thus study of geometrical properties of the attractor of an IFS in a higher dimensions spaces with respect to the co-ordinate IFSs is relevant. P. F. Duvall, L. S. Husch
[
10
]
introduced the product of two hyperbolic IFSs and studied their properties. In
[
4
]
, Rinju Balu and Mathew introduced (n,m)-IFSs, which generate attractors that lie in the product space. Also, in
[
3
]
, R. K. Aswathy and S. Mathew studied the separation properties of the products of IFSs. Interpolating a non-differential function which passes through some specific data points on a plane is very engrossing. M. F. Barnsley in 1986, first introduced the fractal interpolation function in his airtle
[
8
]
. M. A. Navascues
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
studied the approximation theory of the fractal interpolation functions. P. Massopust
[
20
]
defined the fractal hypersurfaces on higher dimension space. M. N. Akhtar, et al.
[
1
,
2
]
studied the box-dimension of an
\alpha
-fractal function.

In this article, we discussed the finite products of hyperbolic IFSs in a different setting. Using the similar idea, we defined the product fractal function on the higher dimension space. Also, we established the approximation properties of the product fractal function. We discussed about some preliminaries and existing results on dimensions in Section

In Section

2.1.
Product metric

Let
(X_{k},d_{k})
for
k=1,2,\ldots,m,
be the metric spaces and
X=\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
, be the Cartesian product of
X_{k}
. Then the product metric on
X
, is denoted by
d_{0}
and is defined by

d_{0}(\vec{x},\vec{y})=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(d_{k}(x_{k},y_{k}))^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}),\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{m})\in X.

If each metric space
(X_{k},d_{k})
is complete then
(X,d_{0})
is also complete (see
[
16
]
). Let
\pi_{k}:X\rightarrow X_{k}
be the projection map on
X_{k}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,m
.

2.2.
Cartesian product of the functions

Let
f_{k}:X_{k}\rightarrow Y_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq m
, be the given functions, then product function from
X=\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
to
Y=\prod_{k=1}^{m}Y_{k}
, is denoted by
f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}
, is defined by

f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}(\vec{x})=(f_{1}(x_{1}),f_{2}(x_{2}),\ldots,f_{m}(x_{m}))\quad\mbox{for}~\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\in X.

If each
f_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq m
, are continuous function, then product function
f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}
is also continuous function with respect to product metric.

2.3.
Hausdorff metric

Let
\delta>0
and
(Y,d)
be a metric space. Recall that the
\delta
-neighbourhood of a subset
A
of
Y
is the set of points within distance
\delta
of
A
, that is
A_{\delta}=\{y\in Y:d(y,a)\leq\delta~\mbox{for some}~a\in A\}.
Let
\mathscr{H}(Y)
denote the collections of all nonempty compact subsets of
Y
. Then the Hausdorff distance (see
[
11
]
) between the sets
A
and
B
in
\mathscr{H}(Y)
, denoted by
h
, is defined by

h(A,B)=\inf\left\{\delta:A\subset B_{\delta}~\mbox{and}~B\subset A_{\delta}\right\}.

If
(Y,d)
is a complete metric space, then
\left(\mathscr{H}(Y),h\right)
is also a complete metric space (see
[
11
,
7
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

[
7
]

If
\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{i\in\Lambda}
,
\left\{D_{i}\right\}_{i\in\Lambda}
are two finite collections of sets in
\left(\mathscr{H}(Y),h\right)
, then

h\left(\cup_{i\in\Lambda}C_{i},\cup_{i\in\Lambda}D_{i}\right)\leq\max_{i\in\Lambda}h(C_{i},D_{i}).

2.4.
Homogeneous attractor

It is known that a mapping
f:Y\rightarrow Y
on a complete metric space
(Y,d)
is called contraction map if there exist a constant
0\leq c<1,
such that

d(f(x),f(y))\leq c~d(x,y)\quad\mbox{ for all}\quad x,y\in Y.

The constant
c
is called the contractivity factor of
f
. If the equality holds in the above inequality, then
c
is called the contraction similarity of
f
. Banach proved the following principle in 1922:

Theorem 2.1
.

[
5
,
17
]
Let
f:Y\rightarrow Y
be contraction mapping on a complete metric space
(Y,d)
. Then the map
f
has a unique fixed point
y_{0}\in Y
. Moreover, for any point
y\in Y
, the sequence
\left\{f^{n}(y)\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges to
y_{0}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A (hyperbolic) iterated function system consists of a complete metric space
(Y,d)
together with a finite set of contraction mappings
f_{i}:Y\rightarrow Y
with respect to the contractivity factors
c_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, where
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now from Lemma
2.1
, we get the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
11
]

Let
\left\{(Y;f_{i}):i=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
be an IFS, where
f_{i}
’s are contraction maps with contractivity factors
c_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. Then the Hutchinson operator
f:\mathscr{H}(Y)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(Y)
defined by
f(B)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(B)
for
B\in\mathscr{H}(Y)
, is also a contraction map with respect to the Hausdorff metric
h
with contractivity factor
c=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\{c_{i}\}
.

If
(Y,d)
is a complete metric space, then by the Theorem
2.1
,
f
has a unique fixed point
B\in\mathscr{H}(Y)
. This fixed point is called an
attractor
or
homogeneous attractor
of the IFS
\left\{(Y;f_{i}):i=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
. Throughout the sequel, we assume that all the metric spaces are complete.

2.5.
Inhomogeneous attractor

Let
\{(Y;f_{i}):i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
be the IFS on
Y
, with contractivity factors
r_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,N
. Let
\mathbb{N}_{N}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{N}^{*}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{N}_{N}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{N}_{N}^{n}
denotes the family of all strings
\textbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{n}
of length
n
with entries
i_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
. Finally, for
\textbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}^{n}
, we write

f_{\textbf{i}}=f_{i_{1}}f_{i_{2}}\cdots f_{i_{n}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad r_{\textbf{i}}=r_{i_{1}}r_{i_{2}}\cdots r_{i_{n}}.

There is an another important way of making contraction mapping on
\mathscr{H}(Y)
, where
(Y,d)
is a complete metric space.

Definition 2.2
.

(see
[
7
,
26
]
)
Let
(Y,d)
be a metric space and
C\in\mathscr{H}(Y)
. Define a set valued transformation
f_{0}:\mathscr{H}(Y)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(Y)
by
f_{0}(B)=C
for all
B\in\mathscr{H}(Y)
. Then
f_{0}
is called a
condensation transformation
and
C
is called the associated
condensation set
.

Note 2.1
.

Observe that a condensation transformation
f_{0}:\mathscr{H}(Y)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(Y)
is contraction map on the space
(\mathscr{H}(Y),h)
, with contractivity factor equal to zero, and it possesses a unique fixed point, namely the condensation set.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\{(Y;f_{i}):i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
be the hyperbolic IFS with contractivity factors
0\leq r_{i}<1
and
f_{0}:\mathscr{H}(Y)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(Y)
be a condensation transformation. Then
\{(Y;f_{i}):i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
is called a hyperbolic IFS with condensation, with contractivity factors
0\leq r_{i}<1
.

Theorem 2.3
.

(see
[
7
,
26
,
9
]
) Let
\{(Y;f_{i}):i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
be a hyperbolic IFS with condensation set
C
and contractivity factors
0\leq r_{i}<1
. Then the transformation
W_{\theta}:\mathscr{H}(Y)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(Y)
defined by

W_{\theta}(B)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{N}f_{i}(B)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad B\in\mathscr{H}(Y)

is a contraction mapping on the complete metric space
(\mathscr{H}(Y),h)
with contractivity factor
r=\max\{r_{i}\}
. Its unique fixed point
F_{C}\in\mathscr{H}(Y)
, obeys

F_{C}=W_{\theta}(F_{C})=\bigcup_{i=0}^{N}f_{i}(F_{C})

and is given by

F_{C}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}W_{\theta}^{n}(B)\quad\mbox{for any}~B\in\mathscr{H}(Y).

This unique fixed point
F_{C}
is called the
Inhomogeneous attractor
(with condensation set
C
). If we take
C=\emptyset
, then the attractor
F_{\emptyset}
coincide with homogeneous attractor of the IFS
\{(Y;f_{i}):i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
K
denote the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
and
(\frac{1}{2},1)
and the three maps are given by

\displaystyle f_{1}(x,y)=(\frac{x}{2},\frac{y}{2});\quad f_{2}(x,y)=(\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{2},\frac{y}{2});\quad f_{3}(x,y)=(\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{4},\frac{y}{2}+\frac{1}{2}).

Let
f_{0}(x,y)=
small circle located inside the triangle. Then Figure

2.6.
Relation between homogeneous and inhomogeneous attractors

Define the orbital set,
O
, by

O=C\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}^{*}}f_{\textbf{i}}(C),

the union of the condensation set
C
, with all images of
C
under compositions of maps in the IFS. The term orbital set was introduced by Nina Snigireva
[
26
]
. This set plays an important role in the structure of inhomogeneous attractors. The following lemma gives a relation in between the homogeneous and inhomogeneous attractors.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
F_{C}
be the inhomogeneous attractor of an IFS
\{(Y;f_{i}):i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
and
F_{\emptyset}
be corresponding homogeneous attractor. Then

F_{C}=O\cup F_{\emptyset}.

Proof.

The proof was found in
[
26
]
(section-3, Lemma-3.9). But here we are giving a proof which is not similar to the existing one.
For any
B\in\mathscr{H}(Y)
, we have
W_{\theta}(B)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{N}f_{i}(B)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle W_{\theta}(B)=

\displaystyle f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(B)=f_{0}(B)\cup f(B)

\displaystyle W_{\theta}^{2}(B)=

\displaystyle W_{\theta}(f_{0}(B))\cup W_{\theta}(f(B))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(f_{0}(B))\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(f(B))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}f_{\textbf{i}}(f_{0}(B))\cup f^{2}(B)

\displaystyle W_{\theta}^{3}(B)=

\displaystyle f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}f_{\textbf{i}}(f_{0}(B))\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}^{2}}f_{\textbf{i}}(f_{0}(B))\cup f^{3}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\bigcup_{p=1}^{2}\mathbb{N}_{N}^{p}}f_{\textbf{i}}(f_{0}(B))\cup f^{3}(B).

Continuing this process, we get for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

W_{\theta}^{n}(B)=f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\bigcup_{p=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{N}_{N}^{p}}f_{\textbf{i}}(f_{0}(B))\cup f^{n}(B).

In the above expression taking limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
, with respect to the Hausdorff metric, we have

F_{C}=f_{0}(B)\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}^{*}}f_{\textbf{i}}(f_{0}(B))\cup F_{\emptyset}=C\cup\bigcup_{\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{N}^{*}}f_{\textbf{i}}(C)\cup F_{\emptyset}=O\cup F_{\emptyset}.

∎

2.7.
Fractal function

Let
I=[a,b]
and
\Delta
:
a=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}=b
be a partition of
I
. Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
be the given interpolation points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Set
I_{n}=[x_{n-1},x_{n}]
for
n=1,2,\ldots,N
. Suppose
L_{n}:I\rightarrow I_{n}
are contraction homeomorphisms such that

(1)

\displaystyle L_{n}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=x_{n-1},~L_{n}(x_{N})=x_{n},

(2)

\displaystyle|L_{n}(x)-L_{n}(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq c_{n}|x-x^{\prime}|\quad\mbox{for all}~x,x^{\prime}\in I,~\mbox{for some}~0\leq c_{n}<1.

Further, assume that
F_{n}:I\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are continuous maps satisfying

(3)

\displaystyle F_{n}(x_{0},y_{0})=y_{n-1},~F_{n}(x_{N},y_{N})=y_{n},

|F_{n}(x,y)-F_{n}(x^{\prime},y)|\leq a_{n}|x-x^{\prime}|\quad\mbox{for all}~x,x^{\prime}\in I,

|F_{n}(x,y)-F_{n}(x,y^{\prime})|\leq b_{n}|y-y^{\prime}|\quad\mbox{for all}~y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},

for some
a_{n},b_{n}\in(-1,1)
. Define the functions
W_{n}:I\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow I_{n}\times\mathbb{R}
by

W_{n}(x,y)=(L_{n}(x),F_{n}(x,y)).

The maps
W_{n}
satisfies the join up condition

W_{n}(x_{0},y_{0})=(x_{n-1},y_{n-1}),\quad W_{n}(x_{N},y_{N})=(x_{n},y_{n})\quad\mbox{for all}~1\leq n\leq N.

The IFS
\{(I\times\mathbb{R};W_{n}):n=1,2,\ldots,N\}
may or may not be hyperbolic with respect to Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Now, we consider a metric
d_{\theta}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is equivalent to the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is defined as follows:

d_{\theta}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\lvert x-x^{\prime}\rvert+\theta\lvert y-y^{\prime}\rvert,

where
\theta
is a positive real number. Then we get the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(see
[
7
]
)
.

If
\theta=\frac{\min\{(1-|c_{n}|):~n=1,2,\ldots,N\}}{\max\{|a_{n}|:~n=1,2,\ldots,N\}}
,
a=\max\{|c_{n}|+\theta|a_{n}|:~n=1,2,\ldots,N\}
and
b=\max\{|b_{n}|:~n=1,2,\ldots,N\}
, then the IFS
\{(I\times\mathbb{R};W_{n}):~n=1,2,\ldots,N\}
defined as above, associated with data set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:~i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
is hyperbolic with respect to the metric
d_{\theta}
with contractivity factor
\lambda=\max\{a,b\}
.

The following is a fundamental result in the theory of fractal interpolation functions:

Theorem 2.4

(Barnsley
[
8
]
)
.

Let
C[I]
, the space of all real-valued continuous functions on
I
, be endowed with supremum norm. That is
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}=\max\left\{|f(x)|:~x\in I\right\}
. Consider the closed subspace

C_{y_{0},y_{N}}[I]:=\left\{f\in C[I]:f(x_{0})=y_{0},~f(x_{N})=y_{N}\right\}.

Then the following holds.

(1)

The IFS
\{(I\times\mathbb{R};W_{n}):n=1,2,\ldots,N\}
has unique attractor
G(g)
which is the graph of a continuous function
g:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
g(x_{i})=y_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq N
.

(2)

The function
g
is the fixed point of the Read-Bajraktarevic (RB) operator
T:C_{y_{0},y_{N}}[I]\rightarrow C_{y_{0},y_{N}}[I]
defined by

(Tf)(x)=F_{n}(L_{n}^{-1}(x),f(L_{n}^{-1}(x))),\quad\mbox{for}~x\in I_{n};~1\leq n\leq N.

Definition 2.4
.

The function
g
is called the fractal interpolation function (FIF) corresponding to the data set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
.

2.8.
Hausdorff, packing and box dimensions

•

Hausdorff dimension:
Let
(Y,d)
be a metric space. Then for a non-empty subset
U
of
Y
, define the diameter
d(U)
of
U
by

d(U)=\sup\{d(x,y):x,y\in U\}.

For
F\subseteq Y
and
\delta>0
, a countable cover
\{U_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
F
is called a
\delta
-cover of
F
with respect to the metric
d
if
d(U_{i})\leq\delta
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
s\geq 0
. For any
\delta>0
, define

H_{\delta}^{s}(F)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d(U_{i})^{s}:\{U_{i}\}~\mbox{be a}~\delta-\mbox{cover of F}\right\},

then the s-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
F
is given by

H^{s}(F)=\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0}H_{\delta}^{s}(F)

and the Hausdorff dimension of
F

[
11
,
7
]
is given by

\displaystyle\dim_{H}F=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{s\geq 0:H^{s}(F)=0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{s:H^{s}(F)=\infty\right\}.

•

Packing dimension:
Let

P_{\delta}^{s}(F)=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d(U_{i})^{s}\right\},

where supremum is taking over all the collection
\{U_{i}\}
of disjoint balls of radii at most
\delta
, with centres in
F
. Let

P_{0}^{s}(F)=\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0}P_{\delta}^{s}(F).

Then s-dimensional packing measure of
F
is given by

P^{s}(F)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}P_{0}^{s}(F_{i}):F\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}(F_{i})\right\}

and the packing dimension of
F

[
11
]
is given by

\displaystyle\dim_{P}F=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{s\geq 0:P^{s}(F)=0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{s:P^{s}(F)=\infty\right\}.

•

Box dimension:
Let
(Y,d)
be a metric space and let
F
be a compact subset of
Y
and
N_{r}(F)
be the smallest number of balls of radius
r
that covers
F
. Then the upper and lower box dimension of
F
is defined by

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}(F)=

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{r}(F)}{-\log r}

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{B}(F)=

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{r}(F)}{-\log r}.

If both the dimensions are equal, then the common value is called the box dimension of
F

[
11
,
7
]
and is denoted by
\dim_{B}F
.

2.9.
Dimension of the product set

In this section some existing results on the dimensions of the product set is given. This results are used in the sequel to prove the main results.

Theorem 2.5
.

(see
[
11
]
)
For any sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}

\dim_{H}E+\dim_{H}F\leq\dim_{H}(E\times F)\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}(E\times F)\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}E+\overline{\dim}_{B}F.

Theorem 2.6
.

(see
[
14
]
)
If
X
and
Y
are two arbitrary metric spaces, then

3.
Homogeneous attractor on the product space and dimension bounds

Let
(X_{k},d_{k})
,
k=1,2,\ldots,m,
be complete metric spaces and
(X,d_{0})
be the product metric space defined as in Section
2
. Let
(\mathscr{H}(X_{k}),H_{k})
,
k=1,2,\ldots,m,
and
(\mathscr{H}(X),H)
be the Hausdorff metric spaces corresponding to the complete metric spaces
(X_{k},d_{k})
,
k=1,2,\ldots,m,
and
(X,d_{0})
respectively. Then
(\mathscr{H}(X_{k}),H_{k})
,
k=1,2,\ldots,m,
and
(\mathscr{H}(X),H)
are also complete metric spaces (see
[
7
]
). Let us define

(4)

\mathscr{H}=\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathscr{H}(X_{k})=\left\{A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots\times A_{m}:A_{k}\in\mathscr{H}(X_{k})~\mbox{for}~k=1,2,\ldots m\right\}.

Since product of compact sets is compact, so all the sets in
\mathscr{H}
are compact. Also
\mathscr{H}
can be treat as a subset of
\mathscr{H}(X)
via the inclusion map. Now we define a new metric
H_{0}
on
\mathscr{H}
by

(5)

H_{0}(A,B)=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k}))^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\mbox{for all}~A=\prod_{k=1}^{m}A_{k},~B=\prod_{k=1}^{m}B_{k}\in\mathscr{H}.

The metric
H_{0}
, we are calling as the product Hausdorff metric on
\mathscr{H}
. If we restrict the Hausdorff metric
H
on
\mathscr{H}(X)
to
\mathscr{H}
, then
H_{0}
is equivalent to
H
(see Theorem
3.1
).
Since each
(\mathscr{H}(X_{k}),H_{k})
is complete so,
(\mathscr{H},H_{0})
is also complete. The following result provides an equivalency between the product Hausdorff metric
H_{0}
and the restriction of the Hausdorff metric
H
on the space
\mathscr{H}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
H^{\prime}
is the restriction of the Hausdorff metric
H
on
\mathscr{H}
, that is
H^{\prime}=H_{|\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H}}
, then
H_{0}
is equivalent to
H^{\prime}
. In particular

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}H_{0}(A,B)\leq H^{\prime}(A,B)\leq H_{0}(A,B)\quad\mbox{for all}~A,B\in\mathscr{H}.

Proof.

Let
A=\prod_{k=1}^{m}A_{k},~B=\prod_{k=1}^{m}B_{k}\in\mathscr{H}
. Define

\displaystyle M=

\displaystyle\left\{\delta:A\subseteq B_{\delta}~\mbox{and}~B\subseteq A_{\delta}\right\}

\displaystyle M_{k}=

\displaystyle\left\{\delta:A_{k}\subseteq(B_{k})_{\delta}~\mbox{and}~B_{k}\subseteq(A_{k})_{\delta}\right\}\quad\mbox{for}~k=1,2,\ldots,m.

Let
\delta_{0}\in M
and
a_{k}\in A_{k}
for some
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}.
Since each
A_{k}
is non-empty, there exists
a_{p}\in A_{p}
for
p=1,2,\ldots,k-1,k+1,\ldots,m
, such that
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})\in A\subseteq B_{\delta_{0}}
. Moreover, there exist
\overline{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m})\in B
such that
d_{0}(\vec{a},\vec{b})\leq\delta_{0}.
Therefore,

\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{m}(d_{n}(a_{n},b_{n}))^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta_{0}.

This implies that
d_{k}(a_{k},b_{k})\leq\delta_{0}.
Therefore,
a_{k}\in(B_{k})_{\delta_{0}}
. Hence it follows that
A_{k}\subseteq(B_{k})_{\delta_{0}}
. Similarly, one can prove that
B_{k}\subseteq(A_{k})_{\delta_{0}}
. Hence
\delta_{0}\in M_{k}
and therefore,

H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k})\leq\delta_{0}.

This is true for all
k=1,2,\ldots,m.
Therefore,

\sum_{k=1}^{m}(H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k}))^{2}\leq m\delta_{0}^{2}.

Hence from (
5
), we get

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}H_{0}(A,B)\leq\delta_{0}.

This is true for all
\delta_{0}\in M
. Hence

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}H_{0}(A,B)\leq H^{\prime}(A,B).

Now, we prove the other inequality. Let
\epsilon>0.
Then by the definition of infimum, there is a
\delta_{k}\in M_{k},
such that

H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k})\leq\delta_{k}\leq H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k})+\epsilon\quad\mbox{for}~k=1,2,\ldots,m.

Let
\delta=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\delta_{k}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})\in A
. Then
a_{k}\in A_{k}
. Now
\delta_{k}\in M_{k},
implies
A_{k}\subseteq(B_{k})_{\delta_{k}}
. Therefore,
a_{k}\in(B_{k})_{\delta_{k}}
. So, there exist
b_{k}\in B_{k}
such that
d_{k}(a_{k},b_{k})\leq\delta_{k}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,m.
Let
\vec{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m})
. Then
\vec{b}\in B
and

d_{0}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(d_{k}(a_{k},b_{k}))^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\delta_{k}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\delta.

This implies that
\vec{a}\in B_{\delta}
. Hence
A\subseteq B_{\delta}
. Similarly, one can prove that
B\subseteq A_{\delta}.
Therefore,
\delta\in M.
Moreover,

H^{\prime}(A,B)\leq\delta=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\delta_{k}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}((H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k}))+\epsilon)^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This is true for each
\epsilon>0
, so, by taking
\epsilon
tends to zero, we get

H^{\prime}(A,B)\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(H_{k}(A_{k},B_{k}))^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}=H_{0}(A,B).

Hence

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}H_{0}(A,B)\leq H^{\prime}(A,B)\leq H_{0}(A,B)\quad\mbox{for all}~A,B\in\mathscr{H}.

This shows that
H_{0}
and
H^{\prime}
are equivalent.
∎

Let
\left\{(X_{k};w_{ki_{k}}):i_{k}=1,2,\ldots,N_{k}\right\}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,m
, be the IFSs with contractivity factors
c_{ki_{k}}<1,
of

Now define the maps

T_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}(\vec{x})=\left(w_{1i_{1}}(x_{1}),w_{2i_{2}}(x_{2}),\ldots,w_{mi_{m}}(x_{m})\right)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\in X,

where

Also equation (

Now for

\displaystyle d_{0}(T_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}(\vec{x}),T_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}(\vec{y}))=

5.

Lemma 5.1

\theta=\frac{\min\{(1-|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}):~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}}{\max\{|a_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}}
,
a=\max\{|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}+\theta|a_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
and
b=\max\{|b_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
, then the IFS
\{(I\times\mathbb{R}^{m};W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}):~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
defined above, associated with the data set
\{((x_{1,i_{1}},x_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,x_{m,i_{m}}),(y_{1,i_{1}},y_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,y_{m,i_{m}}))\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
is hyperbolic with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\theta}
and with contractivity factor
s=\max\{a,b\}
.

Proof.

Proof is similar to Lemma
2.3
. But for the completeness, we prove it here. Let
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}),\vec{x^{\prime}}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{m})\in I
and
\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{m}),\vec{y^{\prime}}=(y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,y^{\prime}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then

\displaystyle\lVert W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x},\vec{y})-W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y^{\prime}})\rVert_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\lVert(L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}),F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x},\vec{y}))-(L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}}),F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y^{\prime}}))\rVert_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\lVert L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x})-L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}})\rVert_{2}+\theta\lVert F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x},\vec{y})-F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y^{\prime}})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\lVert\vec{x}-\vec{x^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}+\theta\left(\lVert F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x},\vec{y})-F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y})\rVert_{2}+\lVert F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y})-F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y^{\prime}})\rVert_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\lVert\vec{x}-\vec{x^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}+\theta|a_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|\lVert\vec{x}-\vec{x^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}+\theta|b_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\lVert\vec{y}-\vec{y^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}+\theta|a_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\right)\lVert\vec{x}-\vec{x^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}+\theta|b_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\lVert\vec{y}-\vec{y^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq a\lVert\vec{x}-\vec{x^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}+\theta b\lVert\vec{y}-\vec{y^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq s\left[\lVert\vec{x}-\vec{x^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}+\theta\lVert\vec{y}-\vec{y^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}\right]=s~\lVert(x,y)-(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rVert_{\theta}.

Now, from given condition, we get
\theta<\frac{1-|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}}{|a_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}}
. So,
|c_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}+\theta|a_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}<1.
Therefore,
a<1
. Also
b<1
. Hence
s<1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Since the IFS
\{(I\times\mathbb{R}^{m};W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}):~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
is contractive. Hence by Banach fixed point theorem it has an unique attractor
G
(say). Now, we will prove that
G
is the graph of a continuous product function from
I
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which interpolates the data set
\{((x_{1,i_{1}},x_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,x_{m,i_{m}}),(y_{1,i_{1}},y_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,y_{m,i_{m}}))\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
.

Let

\mathscr{C}:=\left\{f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}:~f_{k}\in C[I_{k}]\quad\mbox{for}~1\leq k\leq m\right\},

equip with the norm
\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}:=\max\left\{\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}(\vec{x})\rVert_{2}:~\vec{x}\in I\right\}
. Then the following result holds.

Theorem 5.1
.

The norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
on
\mathscr{C}
is equivalent to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{0}
, where the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{0}
on
\mathscr{C}
is defined by

\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{0}:=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In particular,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{0}\leq\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{0}.

Proof.

Since
|f_{k}(x)|\leq\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{\infty}
for all
x\in I_{k}
. Therefore,

\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}(\vec{x})\rVert_{2}=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|{f_{k}}(x_{k})|^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\mbox{for all}~\vec{x}\in I.

So, by taking supremum over all
\vec{x}\in I
, we get

(24)

\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{0}.

To prove the other part, consider
\epsilon>0
, then by the definition of
\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{\infty}
, there exists
x_{j}\in I_{j}
, such that

\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{\infty}-\epsilon\leq|f_{j}(x_{j})|<\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{\infty}\quad\mbox{for}~1\leq j\leq m.

Let
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})
. Since

|f_{j}(x_{j})|\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|{f_{k}}(x_{k})|^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}(\vec{x})\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}.

Therefore,

\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{\infty}-\epsilon\leq\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}\quad\mbox{for all}~1\leq j\leq m.

Hence

\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{\infty}-\epsilon)^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{m}\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}.

This is true for all
\epsilon>0
. Hence by taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the above expression, we get

(25)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{0}\leq\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}\rVert_{\infty}.

Hence from (
24
) and (
25
), we get the required result.
∎

Now, by Theorem
5.1
, we see that
(\mathscr{C},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty})
is a complete space. We define the closed subspace

\mathscr{T}:=\left\{f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}:~f_{k}\in C_{y_{k,0},y_{k,N_{k}}}[I_{k}];~1\leq k\leq m\right\}\quad\mbox{of}~(\mathscr{C},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}).

The following theorem shows the existence of the product fractal function.

Theorem 5.2
.

The attractor
G
of the IFS
\{(I\times\mathbb{R}^{m};W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}):~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
, associated with the data set
\{((x_{1,i_{1}},x_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,x_{m,i_{m}}),(y_{1,i_{1}},y_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,y_{m,i_{m}}))\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
is the graph of the continuous product function
g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}\in\mathscr{T}
.

Proof.

Let us define the RB-operator
T:\mathscr{T}\rightarrow\mathscr{T}
as follows:

(26)

T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(\vec{x})=F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}\left(L^{-1}_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}),f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}(L^{-1}_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}))\right),

where
\vec{x}\in I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
. Now, we prove that
T
is well-defined. Since from the definition of
L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
, we get

L^{-1}_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x})=\left(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),L_{2,i_{2}}^{-1}(x_{2}),\ldots,L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m})\right)\quad\mbox{for all}~\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\in I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(\vec{x})=

\displaystyle F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{m}L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k}),\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k}))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{m}F_{k,i_{k}}\left(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k}),f_{k}(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k}))\right),\quad[\mbox{using (\ref{prod of F}})].

Let
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\in\partial I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
, where
\partial I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
, denotes the boundary of
I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
. Now,
\vec{x}
may be anywhere on the boundary
\partial I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
but we deal with the case, where it is an intersection point. Let
\vec{x}\in I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}\cdots,i_{m}}\cap I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}+1\cdots,i_{m}}\cap I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{q}\cdots,i_{m}}\cap I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{q}+1\cdots,i_{m}}\cap\cdots
(up to a finite number of intersection), where
i_{p}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N_{p}-1\}
and
i_{q}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N_{q}-1\}
. For simplicity, let
x_{p}\in I_{p,i_{p}}\cap I_{p,i_{p}+1}
and
x_{q}\in I_{q,i_{q}}\cap I_{q,i_{q}+1}
. This is possible if only if
x_{p}=x_{p,i_{p}}
and
x_{q}=x_{q,i_{q}}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
p<q
. Here we deal with the following four possible cases.

Case 1. If
x_{p,i_{p}}\in I_{p.i_{p}}
and
x_{q,i_{q}}\in I_{q,i_{q}}
, then

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}

\displaystyle)(\vec{x})=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))),\ldots,F_{p,i_{p}}(L_{p,i_{p}}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}),f_{p}(L_{p,i_{p}}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}))),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,F_{q,i_{q}}(L_{q,i_{q}}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}),f_{q}(L_{q,i_{q}}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}))),\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m})))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}\left(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))\right),\ldots,y_{p,i_{p}},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,y_{q,i_{q}},\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}\left(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}))\right)\right).

Case 2. If
x_{p,i_{p}}\in I_{p.i_{p}}
and
x_{q,i_{q}}\in I_{q,i_{q}+1}
, then

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}

\displaystyle)(\vec{x})=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))),\ldots,F_{p,i_{p}}(L_{p,i_{p}}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}),f_{p}(L_{p,i_{p}}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}))),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,F_{q,i_{q}+1}(L_{q,i_{q}+1}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}),f_{q}(L_{q,i_{q}+1}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}))),\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m})))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}\left(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))\right),\ldots,y_{p,i_{p}},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,y_{q,i_{q}},\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}\left(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}))\right)\right).

Case 3. If
x_{p,i_{p}}\in I_{p.i_{p}+1}
and
x_{q,i_{q}}\in I_{q,i_{q}}
, then

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}

\displaystyle)(\vec{x})=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))),\ldots,F_{p,i_{p}+1}(L_{p,i_{p}+1}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}),f_{p}(L_{p,i_{p}+1}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}))),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,F_{q,i_{q}}(L_{q,i_{q}}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}),f_{q}(L_{q,i_{q}}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}))),\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m})))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}\left(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))\right),\ldots,y_{p,i_{p}},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,y_{q,i_{q}},\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}\left(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}))\right)\right).

Case 4. If
x_{p,i_{p}}\in I_{p.i_{p}+1}
and
x_{q,i_{q}}\in I_{q,i_{q}+1}
, then

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}

\displaystyle)(\vec{x})=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))),\ldots,F_{p,i_{p}+1}(L_{p,i_{p}+1}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}),f_{p}(L_{p,i_{p}+1}^{-1}(x_{p,i_{p}}))),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,F_{q,i_{q}+1}(L_{q,i_{q}+1}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}),f_{q}(L_{q,i_{q}+1}^{-1}(x_{q,i_{q}}))),\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m})))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(F_{1,i_{1}}\left(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}),f_{1}(L_{1,i_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1}))\right),\ldots,y_{p,i_{p}},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\ldots,y_{q,i_{q}},\ldots,F_{m,i_{m}}\left(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}),f_{m}(L_{m,i_{m}}^{-1}(x_{m}))\right)\right).

From above, we see that the values of
T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(\vec{x})
are same in all the four cases. Similarly, all other possibilities can be checked to conclude that
T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(\vec{x})
is well
defined on the boundary of
I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
. Also, from (
12
), we have

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(\vec{x})=\prod_{k=1}^{m}F_{k,i_{k}}\left(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k}),f_{k}(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k}))\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(T_{k}f_{k})(x_{k}).

Therefore,

(27)

T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})=T_{1}f_{1}\times T_{2}f_{2}\times\cdots\times T_{m}f_{m}.

Hence
T
is well-defined. Also, if
\vec{x}=(x_{1,i_{1}},x_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,x_{m,i_{m}})\in I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
, then

\displaystyle T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})(\vec{x})=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{m}F_{k,i_{k}}\left(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k,i_{k}}),f_{k}(L_{k,i_{k}}^{-1}(x_{k,i_{k}}))\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}F_{k,i_{k}}\left(x_{k,0},f_{k}(x_{k,0})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{m}F_{k,i_{k}}\left(x_{k,0},y_{k,0}\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}y_{k,i_{k}}.

So,
T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})
interpolates data points

\{((x_{1,i_{1}},x_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,x_{m,i_{m}}),(y_{1,i_{1}},y_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,y_{m,i_{m}}))\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}.

Next, we prove that
T
is a contraction map on
\mathscr{T}
.
For that, let
f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m},f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m}\in\mathscr{T}
, and
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\in I_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}
. Then from (
20
) and (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\lVert T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})

\displaystyle(\vec{x})-T(f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m})(\vec{x})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|b_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}(L^{-1}_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}))-f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m}(L^{-1}_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}))\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|b_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}-f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq b\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}-f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m}\rVert_{\infty},

where
b=\max\{|b_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}|_{\infty}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}<1
. Now, by taking supremum over all
\vec{x}\in I
, we get

\lVert T(f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m})-T(f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m})\rVert_{\infty}\leq b\lVert f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\cdots\times f_{m}-f^{\prime}_{1}\times f^{\prime}_{2}\times\cdots\times f^{\prime}_{m}\rVert_{\infty}.

This shows that
T
is a contraction map on the complete space
\mathscr{T}
. Hence by Banach fixed point theorem,
T
has an unique fixed point
g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}
in
\mathscr{T}
. Finally, we see that
G
is the graph of the function
g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}
. Let
\tilde{G}=\left\{\left(\vec{x},g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(\vec{x})\right):~\vec{x}\in I\right\}
. Then

\displaystyle\bigcup\left\{W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\tilde{G}):~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}((\vec{x},g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(\vec{x})):~\vec{x}\in I,~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{\left(L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}),F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x},g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(\vec{x}))\right):~\vec{x}\in I,~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\right\}.

Now, since
g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}
is the fixed point of
T
. Hence from (
26
), we get

g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}))=F_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x},g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(\vec{x})).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigcup\left\{W_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\tilde{G}):~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{\left(L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}),g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(L_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}(\vec{x}))\right):~\vec{x}\in I,~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\vec{x},g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}(\vec{x})\right):~\vec{x}\in I\right\}

\displaystyle=\tilde{G}.

Hence by uniqueness of the attractor, we get
G=\tilde{G}
.
∎

Definition 5.1
.

The function
g_{1}\times g_{2}\times\cdots\times g_{m}
is called the product fractal interpolation function (PFIF) corresponding to the IFS
23
, which interpolates the data set

\{((x_{1,i_{1}},x_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,x_{m,i_{m}}),(y_{1,i_{1}},y_{2,i_{2}},\ldots,y_{m,i_{m}}))\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:~1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k};1\leq k\leq m\}
.

Corollary 5.1
.

If
f_{k}
are the FIF corresponding to the IFS
\{(I_{k}\times\mathbb{R};W_{k,i_{k}}):1\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k}\}
which interpolates the data sets
\{(x_{k,i_{k}},y_{k,i_{k}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0\leq i_{k}\leq N_{k}\}
