Multicentric Calcifying Aponeurotic Fibroma of the Upper Extremity With Associated Nerve Compression and Bony Dysplasia
Authors: Yoshiko Toyoda, Samyd S. Bustos, Steven L. Moran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 19-year-old woman with presumed history of lymphatic malformation of her right elbow and wrist with intermittent mild pain, positive Tinel sign at the proximal forearm, elbow contracture, and limited pronosupination presented for a second opinion. She had been diagnosed clinically with a vascular malformation in infancy with subsequent magnetic resonance imaging findings of a fatty tumor versus malformation. She was treated conservatively with splinting for her range of motion limitations for years. Ultimately, magnetic resonance-guided biopsy demonstrated calcifying aponeurotic fibroma (CAF). Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging further demonstrated a dysplastic distal radioulnar joint and fatty muscle atrophy at the proximal forearm. Excisional biopsy of the two lesions demonstrated multicentric CAF. Multicentric CAF is exceedingly rare as are associated nerve compression and bony dysplasia. This case adds to our collective knowledge on CAF to better elucidate the pathogenesis as well as bring greater awareness of this rare tumor to aid practitioners in avoiding misdiagnoses as in our patient.

Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma (CAF) is a rare benign tumor that was first described in 1953 by Keasbey1 in the extremities of four children. She noted an evolutional pattern of an initial diffuse, multicentric proliferation of neoplastic cells in younger children that later developed into more discrete solitary tumor masses in older children.1 Since this initial description, additional case reports have revealed CAF to be most common in male children in their first and second decades of life with no evidence of increased familial prevalence, but it has also been reported in elderly adults.2,3 Clinically, they present as ill-defined, painless, whitish-yellow small masses less than 5 cm in greatest diameter that are nonadherent to skin, usually without associated neurovascular symptoms.2 They often present as solitary lesions, although there have been rare reports of multicentric primary CAF, usually in close proximity to the same limb, such as in the same finger or in adjacent fingers.4 Work-up typically consists of radiographs that may or may not show stippling calcifications and magnetic resonance imaging that typically shows nonspecific findings of an ill-defined nature.4, 5, 6 Biopsy is needed for definitive diagnosis. Local recurrence after surgical excision is cited at 50%, but rarely causes complications or functional deficits, so the recommended treatment is marginal resection.2,6 Malignant transformation is exceedingly rare with only single reports including a mortality with diffuse metastasis with lung fibrosarcoma 6 years after initial presentation.7,8

We herein present a case of multicentric CAF with clearly distinct masses at the wrist and at the anterior elbow with peripheral nerve involvement and associated bony dysplasia leading to range of motion limitations.

Case Report

A 19-year-old, right-handed woman who was a competitive cheerleader with a presumed history of lymphatic malformation of her right elbow and wrist with associated intermittent mild pain, elbow contracture, and limited pronosupination presented for a second opinion. It was initially noted at age 2 weeks that she had a larger right wrist. A clinical diagnosis of lymphatic malformation was made by a specialist at this time. MRI imaging as an infant demonstrated a fatty tumor that this specialist believed was consistent with either a lymphatic malformation or excess fat. She had no other medical history and no pertinent family history.

As the patient grew into her early teenage years, she began to have more pain and elbow contracture and was seen by a different pediatric hand specialist. It was noted that she never had any infections or bleeding on her arm. The examination was notable for inability to fully extend the elbow with a 30° contracture, as well as supination limitation to 5° past neutral. She was neurovascularly intact. Radiographs from this time demonstrated some calcification along the medial aspect of the proximal forearm and radiopacities in the anterior elbow joint space as well as along the distal ulna suspicious for soft tissue calcification as well as negative ulnar variance and ulnar abutment on the distal radial metaphysis causing remodeling. MRI corroborated these findings with three foci of soft tissue abnormalities and punctate hypointensities that correlated with the soft tissue calcifications seen on radiographs that were thought could represent vascular or venolymphatic malformations. Given the presumed diagnosis of a vascular malformation with a relatively mild contracture, this provider believed the benefits of surgical intervention did not outweigh the potential risks of neurovascular injury and bony growth arrest and therefore treated her conservatively with sequential MRI and nighttime extension splints for her elbow contracture, which improved minimally over the next 5 years.

Given the patient’s desire to achieve full elbow extension as a cheerleader and continued intermittent pain, she ultimately sought a second opinion at the Vascular Malformations clinic at our institution. Given the lack of a definitive diagnosis of a vascular malformation and lack of common symptoms such as infection, bleeding, or signs of diffuse overgrowth, an MRI-guided biopsy was performed. This revealed CAF with immunohistochemistry negative for beta catenin and smooth muscle actin (Fig. 1). Hand surgery evaluation at this time demonstrated no signs of overgrowth or evidence of macrodactyly. She had no numbness or weakness. She did report pain directly over the enlarged part of her medial wrist as well as over the proximal brachioradialis and lacertus fibrosus with a positive Tinel sign over the anterior elbow just distal to the antecubital fossa. She was unable to supinate past neutral and had a resting elbow flexion contracture at 30°. Computed tomography and MRI demonstrated a heterogeneously signaling and enhancing ovoid mass adjacent to the distal ulna and an ill-defined enhancing soft tissue mass at the medial elbow containing mineral densities with associated fatty muscle atrophy of the proximal flexors (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, her pain, and concern for nerve involvement with a positive Tinel sign and muscle atrophy, decision was made to pursue surgical excisional biopsy of both wrist and elbow lesions, decompression of any involved nerves, and plan for a staged bony reconstruction to improve her range of motion deficits. The possible use of auto or allograft was discussed with the patient before surgery. The wrist lesion, which was 2.5 × 1.4 × 0.7 cm, lay just ulnar and dorsal to the ulnar neurovascular bundle, intimately involved with the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve (DSBUN). An ill-defined, whitish-yellow, rubbery mass was excised off of the ulnar bone and the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon sheath (Fig. 4). The ulnar shaft did not appear to be involved with no evidence of scalloping or friability. Distally, the mass became more amorphous and unable to be distinguished from the surrounding fatty tissue. Two branches of the DSBUN were involved in the mass and were resected and sent for pathology. One branch was repaired primarily and the other with a 2.5 cm cadaveric nerve allograft.Figure 4Intraoperative photograph of the wrist.

The anterior proximal forearm over the Tinel response was incised, and a decompression of the radial nerve and median nerve were performed. The median nerve did appear to be compressed by the leading (proximal) edge of the flexor digitorum superficialis muscle belly and was also wrapped by an ill-defined sheet/mass that was also whitish-yellow in appearance (Fig. 5). A nerve stimulator was used to confirm that the amorphous wrap extending distally did not involve motor branches. The lesion measured 0.7 × 0.3 × 0.2 cm and was submitted for pathologic evaluation. At 2 months of follow-up, the patient had no pain and her numbness has resolved. She had a small area of dehiscence close to her antecubital fossa, which resolved with conservative treatment.

Discussion

We presented a rare case of multicentric CAF with anatomically distinct masses associated with pain, nerve compression, and bony dysplasia. Multiple primary CAFs in the same patient have been reported before, but they are often in close anatomic proximity, such as within the same finger or in adjacent fingers on the same hand, raising the question of extension of the mass given its amorphous characteristics.4,9 In contrast, our patient had two distinct masses, including one at the medial volar wrist adjacent to the ulna, the ulnar neurovascular bundle, and the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon sheath and the other at the anterior proximal forearm, deep in the flexor pronator muscle bellies wrapped around the symptomatic median nerve.

CAF has been reported in many cases to be intimately associated with nearby neurovascular bundles, particularly at the finger level, but these typically are asymptomatic.3,6,7 In contrast, our patient had pain directly over the wrist mass that was intimately involved with the DSBUN and a positive Tinel sign and evidence of median nerve compression both by the leading edge of the flexor digitorum superficialis muscle belly and by the elbow lesion with associated muscle atrophy in the median-innervated flexor pronator mass proximally. It is unclear whether this fatty atrophy was a result of chronic nerve compression or a feature of CAF, as Keasbey noted in her initial description of this tumor that when returning for a secondary excision for a recurrent tumor, there was “massive replacement of fat and voluntary muscle” secondary to extension of the tumor with subsequent voluntary muscle atrophy.1

Our patient’s associated bony dysplasia was also a rare occurrence, and it is unclear whether this is causally related to the tumor. Most reports of CAF are in otherwise normal anatomy, although there has been a report of a 3-month-old patient who had associated symbrachydactyly.4 Although the pathogenesis of CAF remains elusive, it is thought to originate from aponeurotic tissue at the transition from fibrous connective tissue to fibrocartilage and bone, hence its name.2 There has been a report of a rapidly growing CAF on a chest wall that required extirpation of the affected rib to get clear margins,4 but CAF is generally not thought to involve skeletal bone with numerous reports specifically reporting the lack of bony tumor involvement on radiographs.2, 3, 4, 5,7 Our patient presumably had these tumors for 19 years. It is possible that despite lack of direct bony involvement and despite the small size of the tumor, the chronicity of the mass displacement by these tumors during growth led to changes at the physes leading to the dysplasias that now limit her range of motion.

Molecular genetic testing has begun to shed some light on CAF and other pediatric lipofibromatoses. Puls et al10 identified in nine cases of CAF that the fibronectin 1 (FN1)- epidermal growth factor (EGF) gene fusion may be unique to CAF. The FN1 gene creates an essential component of the extracellular matrix, and the EGF gene is responsible for a transmembranous protein that is predominantly in epithelial cells.10 A more recent study examining 20 patients with classic lipofibromatoses identified four patients with this same FN1-EGF gene fusion.11 Three of the patients also had local recurrences, with two containing CAF-like nodular calcifications.11 The authors postulated that some cases of classic lipofibromatosis may represent early CAF in which the adipocytic component is more prominent.11 This hypothesis corroborates with Keasbey’s initial description of the two-staged evolution of CAF as well as with our patient’s history of having been diagnosed with a “fatty tumor” on MRI in infancy which subsequently developed soft tissue calcifications in her radiographs by 12 years of age.1 Al-Ibraheemi et al11 conclude from their molecular genetic testing of a variety of pediatric lipofibromatoses that lipofibromatosis may have similar morphological patterns but may be genetically heterogeneous and therefore not a single diagnostic entity.11

This less dogmatic way of thinking is perhaps more consistent with the heterogeneous presentation of CAF. Reported immunohistochemistry results vary with reports of positive vimentin, CD99, CD68, S100, WT1, EGF, and muscle-specific actin (50% of cases), smooth muscle actin (50% of cases) and negative to desmin and CD34.4,5 Our patient tested negative for smooth muscle actin, which neither confirms nor denies her histologic diagnosis of CAF. There is much to still be learned about CAF. Despite the rarity of cases, there is much heterogeneity as seen in molecular genetic studies and corroborated by a variety of clinical presentations such as in our patient who presented with a unique constellation of symptoms. We believe that sharing this interesting case adds to the collective knowledge base on CAF to better elucidate the pathogenesis and prognosis as well as bring greater awareness of this rare tumor to aid practitioners in avoiding misdiagnoses as in our patient.
