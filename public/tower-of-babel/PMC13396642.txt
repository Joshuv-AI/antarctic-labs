A qualitative exploration of perspectives on taking lithium supplements
Authors: Madeleine Bloomfield, Lily Chebabo-Manning, Rebecca Strawbridge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Lithium is widely recognized for its benefits as a medication to treat mood disorders. Low-dose lithium has been commercially available for decades as a nutritional supplement, but there is little research on its effects. Emerging evidence suggests that low-dose lithium may offer therapeutic benefits in mood disorders and cognition, with better tolerability and accessibility than higher doses.Objectives:To explore individuals’ willingness to consider taking lithium supplements, and to explore the experiences of those currently taking lithium supplements.Design:Qualitative study.Methods:Two qualitative studies were undertaken. First, a focus group was conducted with those who had not taken lithium supplements before and were concerned about their own or another individual’s cognitive health. Second, individual semi-structured interviews were undertaken with participants who were currently taking lithium supplements. Data from each study were analyzed separately using thematic analysis.Results:Four overall findings emerged. These relate to lithium supplement effects on mood stability, anxiety, and cognition, as well as distrust toward unfamiliar supplements. Participants taking lithium supplements perceived improvements in balancing and enhancing mood, reductions in anxiety and stress, and improved ability to cope with challenging situations. Three-quarters reported perceived improvement in cognition, particularly concentration and memory. Participants from both groups noted that, initially, lithium supplements elicited feelings of unease and wariness, partly due to association with lithium as a medication, as well as lack of information about its supplement form and an unawareness that lithium is a naturally occurring mineral.Conclusion:Results suggest that there may be benefits from lithium supplements. Participants reported a wide range of perceived benefits across mood, anxiety, and cognition, but also displayed skepticism toward lithium supplements. This research is limited by its size and likely lack of generalizability to the wider population; clinical trials are needed to clarify the safety and efficacy of lithium supplements.Keywords: anxiety, cognition, depression, lithium, lithium orotate, mood, supplement

Background

Lithium is an effective medication across the spectrum of affective disorders, the gold standard first-line mood stabilizer in bipolar disorders (BDs), 1 and also effective in unipolar affective disorders. 2 Despite its efficacy within psychiatry and beyond, 3 it is frequently reported as underutilized, 4 with its use declining internationally.5,6 Patients and clinicians often hold stigma or negative perceptions about lithium because of concerns over side effects, long-term health risks, and the consequent need for frequent safety monitoring.7 –10

Lithium is typically prescribed as lithium carbonate (or sometimes lithium citrate), with dosage determined by illness severity, treatment response, and tolerability. Adverse effects include the potential of toxicity, nausea, weight gain, impairment to renal and thyroid function, and tremor, 3 and these appear to be dose-dependent. 11

Dose range categories can be regarded as follows 12 : “High dose” lithium can be considered with serum levels over 0.8 mmol/L (typically corresponding to an elemental dose exceeding 250 mg), used for severe nonresponsive acute mania, aggressive or self-injurious behaviors, or nonresponsive BD, but with significantly increased safety concerns (including acute toxicity 1.2 mmol/L). “Therapeutic” doses are often considered with serum levels of 0.6–0.8 mmol/L (approximately 112–250 mg elemental dose) and are used widely in affective disorders, but still present some long-term risks to renal and thyroid function as well as side effects in the short term. “Low-dose” lithium, at serum levels of 0.2–0.6 mmol/L (around 20–100 mg elemental dose), is mostly well-tolerated, with significantly less adverse effects than higher doses. 13 “Microdose” formulations, such as lithium orotate or lithium aspartate, are available as food supplements and marketed as “nutritional” products. These typically provide 5–20 mg of elemental lithium per serving, below therapeutic levels but higher than environmental intake from food and water, estimated at under 5 mg daily. 12

Evidence for the neuropsychiatric effects of sub-therapeutic lithium doses is limited and varied. 14 Effects of low-dose lithium on manic symptoms have been reported to have minimal benefits. 13 There is mixed evidence for effects of low-dose lithium on depression and anxiety symptoms in BD patients,15 –17 though significant improvements in depressive symptoms have been reported in other populations (e.g., Rinker et al. 18 ). Findings are also mixed for a possible effect on suicidality for both low-dose and environmental trace lithium.15,19,20

Sub-therapeutic lithium doses may have cognitive benefits, even at trace levels. Exposure to higher concentrations of naturally occurring lithium has been associated with reduced dementia rates21,22 while environmental lithium exposure may correlate with lower dementia prevalence. Low-dose lithium has shown significantly improved cognitive performance or reduced decline compared to placebo in patients with Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) 23 and mild cognitive impairment. 24 Conversely, other studies have reported no significant effects on cognition in AD, and recent meta-analyses question lithium’s efficacy for this indication.25,26 Preclinical evidence, however, indicates that lithium orotate (which is available in supplement form) specifically may prevent pathological changes and memory loss in mouse models of AD, 27 suggesting a potential role for this lithium formulation in preventing and treating cognitive decline.

Despite being commercially available for decades, research into lithium supplements remains limited. 28 These supplements are available for purchase over-the-counter, sometimes presented by vendors as potentially providing benefits in mood and cognition. Consequently, supplemental lithium is readily accessible compared to higher-dose formulations, without the need for clinical monitoring or prescriptions, and presents the additional benefit of being safer and more tolerable. Moreover, supplement formulations allow for controlled administration at very low doses, which is difficult to achieve with standard lithium carbonate tablets. It has been proposed that lithium orotate, in particular, may demonstrate greater efficacy and tolerability than equivalent doses of lithium carbonate. 29

Few studies have explored personal perspectives on lithium supplements. A recent survey of 211 adults taking over-the-counter lithium supplements found that users most frequently reported improvements in cognition, followed by anxiety and mood, with mood noted as the greatest area of improvement. 30 The present study sought to expand on previous work through a qualitative exploration of attitudes toward lithium supplements. We aimed to explore the following topics: (1) understanding and knowledge about lithium, including positive and negative associations/feelings; (2) willingness to use lithium for “brain health”; (3) perceived benefits and disadvantages; (4) adverse effects; (5) users’ physical and mental health backgrounds; and (6) perspectives around lithium supplement research study design and participation.

Method

Design

Two distinct qualitative studies were conducted between 2023 and 2025 to explore a range of attitudes toward lithium supplements: one focus group (n = 6) and semi-structured individual interviews (n = 8).

Participants

Participants were recruited via advertisement from the authors' institution and via internal databases of people interested in taking part in affective disorder research, as well as online web advertising for those taking lithium supplements. For the focus group, participants (a) had never taken lithium supplements, (b) had no prior history of lithium use, (c) were between 55 and 75 years, (d) had no present mental health or neurodegenerative diagnoses, and (e) were concerned about either their own or another’s cognitive health and/or decline. For the interviews, participants were over the age of 18 and were taking lithium supplements at the time of the interview. All participants were given detailed information about the study and gave written informed consent. Both studies were granted ethical approval by the Research Ethics Committee at King’s College London (LRS/DP-22/23-33969 and LRS/DP-24/25-45925 for the focus group and interviews, respectively).

Procedure

The focus group was a one-hour online video call led by an academic (RS, PhD) with relevant experience and training. A predetermined topic guide (Supplemental File 1) was used to facilitate the discussion on the following topics: public understanding and knowledge about lithium as a substance, positive and negative associations with lithium, views on taking a supplement to help with memory and “brain health,” and descriptions of possible research studies and feedback considerations from the viewpoint of potential participants. The topic guide was not pre-tested. The researcher was personally known to two of the participants prior to the focus group (see limitations), and participants were aware that the researcher was an academic with an interest in the field.

Interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview schedule consisting of predetermined questions (Supplemental File 2) to guide the discussion, alongside prompts to elicit more detailed responses from participants. Interviews were conducted by a trained postgraduate psychology student (LCM, BSc) online and explored the following topics: background and initial feelings about lithium supplements, benefits of taking lithium supplements, disadvantages of taking lithium supplements, side effects, and physical and mental health history. The interview guide was not pre-tested. The student was not known to participants prior to the interview, and participants were aware that the interviewer was a psychology student. Interview times ranged from 14 to 44 min.

For the focus group, participants were told to use a name different from their own, and voice recordings were destroyed following transcription so that data analysis was anonymous. For the interviews, sessions were voice-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed anonymously. Repeat focus groups or interviews were not conducted. Minor field notes were made during the focus group.

Analysis

Transcripts were analyzed using a thematic approach 31 by the principal coder (LCM), with transcripts from the focus group and the interviews analyzed separately and without participant comment or correction. The analysis adopted a positivist approach, employing a bottom-up process to prioritize the perspectives and emotional experiences of participants, thereby allowing themes to emerge directly from the data. The data were analyzed in concordance with the six stages of thematic analysis as outlined in Refs. 30 to ensure full reflection, transparency, and rigor of the qualitative research process. After multiple reads, initial codes were formed to identify common themes, and the transcript was continually read until data saturation was reached and new codes could no longer be created. The recurring codes were identified and grouped into initial themes and subthemes, forming a hierarchical coding tree reflecting the relationships between concepts. To ensure reliability, these themes were cross-examined by researchers (MB, MSci, and RS). Following this review, the themes were further refined, integrated, and defined, and evidence was derived directly from the data. A thematic model was developed based on repeated and interlinking themes. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) were used to report this research. 32

Results

Focus group

All six participants were between the ages of 55 and 75, and five participants were female (5/6). All participants were from the UK. All but one participant was primarily worried about their own cognitive health.

Two main themes were identified from the analysis: initial concerns and mediating factors. The initial concerns expressed largely originated from a medical concern about supplement safety, which, in turn, inclined individuals to prioritize adjusting their lifestyle over supplement usage. Opinions of others and feeling skeptical about whether the supplements would be effective also influenced participants’ willingness to consider lithium supplementation. Despite the emergence of these initial concerns, participants recognized mediating factors that they believed would potentially mitigate some of these worries. These involved participants’ own concerns around cognitive health, having trust in doctors, and recognizing that prevention is important. Furthermore, participants expressed that increased familiarity with lithium supplements, coupled with the prospect of further research, would enhance their confidence in the efficacy of lithium supplements and their willingness to use them. Theme 1: Initial concerns

The prospect of taking lithium supplements was perceived by participants as both concerning and unfamiliar. Participants expressed uncertainty regarding how lithium supplements work. Four subthemes were identified within this theme.

Opinions of others: Some participants expressed concerns about their families’ potential reactions and the stigma of taking medication, highlighting the importance of others’ opinions. One participant believed that their family would “just throw up their hands in horror” at the idea of them using lithium supplements. Another participant expressed that their family believed using medication was acceptable for physical health but not for mental health and reported being raised to believe that “you should snap out of it, and you should be able to do that without medication.”

Medical concern: All participants raised concerns of feeling uneasy, particularly associated with the long-term use of supplements, the possibility of a “build up” within the body, and the implications of taking them alongside other medications, as well as reflecting on comparisons with medications and other supplements. Some participants reflected that they were especially apprehensive about taking supplements intended for the brain, as opposed to those for physical conditions: “I might be more nervous. . . whereas supplements for my joints. . . I don’t really have a problem.” Lithium supplements were compared to a “safe supplement” like vitamin C, with one participant reflecting that “this doesn’t sound like anything more sinister than a vitamin.”

Theme 2: Mediating factors

Despite participants reporting initial negative concerns surrounding a willingness to consider lithium supplementation, all participants identified mediating factors that they felt could help to mitigate these concerns. This was divided into four subthemes.

Cognitive health concerns: Concerns for cognitive health were acknowledged by participants through experiences of memory problems. Moreover, this anxiety was shown to impact daily life: one participant said that “I find it really quite worrying; I lie awake and wonder you know, is this the beginning of the end.” Although some participants didn’t perceive these issues as enough of a problem yet or that it was a natural part of getting old, they nonetheless expressed their interest in the potential use of lithium supplements “possibly in the future.”

Trust in doctors: When previously discussing medical concerns related to taking lithium supplements, participants acknowledged that engaging in conversations with healthcare professionals would help to mitigate some of these initial concerns. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study to examine perceptions around lithium supplements. We aimed to gain insight into two areas: an understanding of the notion of taking lithium supplements, focusing on those concerned about either their own or someone else’s cognitive decline, and the perception of individuals who currently take lithium supplements. The main findings to emerge were a distrust of unfamiliar supplements and experiences of lithium supplements improving cognition, balancing mood, and reducing anxiety.

Distrust of unfamiliar supplements

Lithium supplements were perceived by participants as an intimidating type of health product, in both the focus group and initially among interview participants before they began taking lithium supplements. This was partly because, unlike familiar vitamins or minerals, lithium was seen to be associated with psychiatric medication. Public and professional concerns regarding toxicity, kidney effects, and the need for careful blood monitoring 33 helped to reinforce this perception. Even with the knowledge that a nutritional dose of lithium in supplements is substantially lower than a dose given in medicine, participants expressed unease. This wariness was compounded by misinformation and a general lack of awareness that lithium is present naturally in water and some foods. Lithium is not officially recognized as an essential nutrient,34,35 though some have suggested it should be considered a ‘micronutrient’. Provisional intake levels of around 1 mg a day for a 70 kg adult have been proposed. 35 Despite this, the minimum requirement for health remains unknown, and consensus regarding the form/source is lacking. Without such clarity, lithium fortification of foods or drinks, or consuming lithium supplements, may trigger understandable safety concerns.

Furthermore, participants expressed that a lack of familiarity and advertisement of lithium supplements also contributed to their feeling uneasy about trying them. Participants who had taken lithium supplements noted that more awareness and education relating to lithium supplements could aid in shifting public opinions. This is mirrored by consumers’ general attitudes toward supplements, which have shifted over time due to broader exposure and marketing. 36 Studies show that awareness and familiarity of supplement advertising tend to correlate with a reduced perception of risk.36,37 Participants from the focus group noted that having guidance and reassurance from a doctor would make them feel more trusting of lithium supplements. It is clear that more education, awareness, and research are needed before lithium supplements become as widely accepted as other food supplements.

Effects on cognition

Most participants who had taken lithium supplements reported an improvement in their perceived cognitive functioning. Participants largely identified enhancements in their concentration and memory. Several studies have investigated the relationship between sub-therapeutic lithium doses and cognition, both in healthy patients and those with related conditions, such as AD and mild cognitive impairment.23,24,38 –40 While meta-analyses show mixed findings and many trials of lithium for cognitive decline use higher doses of lithium, there does not appear to be a clear dose-response effect. 40 Participants in the focus group expressed that their key concern was their cognitive decline and memory (although this was an eligibility criterion), and noted additional concern over how long they would have to take lithium before observing benefits, highlighting worries regarding the robustness of research and evidence supporting lithium supplements in improving or preventing cognitive decline. Given the scant research into lithium’s potential cognitive benefits at microdoses (although see Refs. 23), and the recent suggestions that cognitive improvements may be common with supplemental lithium, 30 as well as the potential role of lithium orotate from preclinical work, 27 clinical research into lithium supplements’ effects on cognition is warranted.

Effects on mood

Participants taking lithium supplements reported improved mood stabilization. All participants who reported mood stabilization as their primary reason for taking lithium supplements noted improvements and felt as though the supplements were helpful. These findings are consistent with our previous research: mood enhancements were found to be the third most common improvement, and mood-related benefits were reported as the greatest improvement noticed by participants in a recent survey. 30 Studies have shown that even a dose as low as 400 µg of lithium significantly improved mood stability and happiness test scores. 41 It is important to clarify that the effects of supplemental or low doses of lithium are not intended to equate to those of high-dose lithium medication in individuals with BD. Instead, they may be beneficial to individuals experiencing subclinical or mild mood symptoms (e.g., without an affective disorder, a mild or treated affective disorder). No participants reported having a BD (as anticipated, since these individuals would be expected to take a higher dose of lithium for affective stability). However, we did not mandate eligibility based on diagnosis in light of inter-individual variability in clinical, physiological, and sociodemographic characteristics. Two participants taking lithium supplements reported a diagnosis of depression, with both attributing an improvement in mood and depressive symptoms to lithium supplements. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that sub-therapeutic lithium doses may be better tolerated and still provide benefits in certain cases of individuals with major depressive disorder.42,43 The findings of this study and our previous research 30 suggest that even lithium at a supplemental dose may have benefits in mood enhancement and balance, but controlled studies are required to clarify this.

Effects on anxiety

The majority of participants taking lithium supplements reported decreased anxiety, lower stress, and an improvement in their ability to cope with challenging situations after beginning lithium supplementation. While two participants did not report improvements in anxiety since taking lithium supplements, both attributed this to a general absence of stressors and anxiety in their lives, rather than lithium supplements being ineffective in combating anxiety-related symptoms. Our recent survey 30 reported similar results, with anxiety being the second most frequently reported improvement and the third greatest improved benefit in people taking lithium supplements. Lithium at therapeutic doses for BD has been reported as helping with comorbid symptoms of anxiety, 17 as well as improving anxiety in treatment-resistant depression, 44 although there is relatively little support for anxiety over and above other indications. Lithium supplements may prove favorable to individuals with or without any clinical diagnoses who are seeking to better manage their anxiety and stress-related symptoms, but further research is needed beyond patient reports to clarify this.

Limitations

This research was conducted on a very small sample and should not be generalized to the wider population without replication, particularly given that our samples were not diverse. We collected limited demographic information, which could have provided further detail into perspectives and experiences—for example, regarding participants’ family history of cognitive decline, which may have provided a contextual basis for the concerns participants had about their own cognitive health, or a more detailed mental health history, as well as participants’ socioeconomic and education statuses. The topic/interview guides were not pre-tested, which is another limitation. Further, we relied on self-reports concerning the dose and formulation of lithium supplements that were being taken by participants, as well as diagnoses. Of note, several participants were taking a higher dose than expected (40–60 mg), potentially altering the extent to which they felt effects compared to others. We had not anticipated participants taking these higher doses, and including them in analyses may have affected our results. However, the only notable difference associated with participants on higher doses was adverse effects, which were reported as more frequent. It is nonetheless encouraging that the present results are consistent with our previous research on individuals’ experiences with lithium supplements. 30 Lastly, despite consideration in planning of content, it is possible that researchers’ perspectives on lithium supplements may have inadvertently influenced participant responses (and by extension overall findings), for example, through the framing and delivery of questions. As two participants in the focus group knew the academic RS, it was initially conceived that another trained academic would lead the focus group to avoid any effect on these participants’ contributions to the focus group, but ultimately, this was not possible. Given the subject matter and predetermined topic guide used during the session, all analyses occurring without this knowledge, and the fact that neither participant worked in the field, it is unlikely to have significantly affected results.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate a need for further research on lithium supplements. Given how widely available and accessible lithium supplements are, it is crucial to clarify their effects on mood, anxiety, and cognition, as well as other potential benefits, in both patients from a variety of disorders (e.g., major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder) and healthy people who are otherwise seeking benefits across these domains. All participants acknowledged the necessity for further research to be conducted. To our knowledge, at the time of writing, no study has empirically examined the benefits of lithium supplements in a controlled trial.

We argue that education and increased awareness should be implemented into public health knowledge regarding the potential benefits of lithium supplements (alongside clarity about the lack of evidence at these doses) and lithium’s existence as a mineral to reduce stigma surrounding both lithium supplements and medication lithium. Healthcare professionals should be made aware of lithium supplements. Through awareness, education, and continued future research, there is potential to reframe public views surrounding lithium supplements from an unfamiliar medication to a nutritional supplement that could help with symptoms of mood, anxiety, and cognitive function.

The present study explored both individuals’ perspectives on their willingness to consider lithium supplementation and the experiences of those who have used lithium supplements. The findings align with existing literature suggesting that lithium supplements may offer benefits in mood stabilization, anxiety, and cognitive decline, while also highlighting a prevailing distrust of unfamiliar supplements. Increased public education, awareness, and research are necessary to improve perceptions of safety and efficacy of lithium supplements. By enhancing the visibility of lithium supplements and expanding evidence on its potential benefits, individuals may be better positioned to make informed decisions and thus utilize lithium supplements to their full therapeutic potential.
