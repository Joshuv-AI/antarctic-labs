Establishment and validation of a predictive scoring system for titanium clip retention at six months after endoscopic submucosal dissection for gastric lesions
Authors: Gongyu Zhang, Fengcheng Zang, Jie Xu, Yunfu Feng, Zhibing Wang, Ye Ye, Xiaodan Xu, Luojie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Titanium clips are commonly used to close mucosal defects after gastric lesion endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD). However, prolonged titanium clip retention beyond the healing period may have potential negative effects on postoperative outcomes. This study developed and validated a scoring model to predict titanium clip retention at six months post-gastric ESD. A multicenter retrospective study was conducted on 1,055 patients who underwent gastric ESD with titanium clips closure for wound management. Patients were grouped into a training cohort (TC, n = 509), an internal validation cohort (IVC, n = 218), and an external validation cohort (EVC, n = 328). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were applied to identify risk factors for titanium clip retention at the 6-month follow-up. A scoring system was then built by assigning weighted scores based on the regression coefficients of independent predictors. The model’s performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values. Among the patients, 132 (12.5%) experienced prolonged titanium clip retention. Multivariate analysis revealed three independent predictors: tumor in the upper third of the stomach (2 points), diabetes (1 point), and the use of ≥ 8 clips (1 point). The scoring model exhibited strong predictive ability, with AUCs of 0.809 in the IVC and 0.855 in the EVC, along with high negative predictive values (0.946 and 0.984, respectively). Risk stratification classified patients into low-risk (0–1 points), intermediate-risk (2–3 points), and high-risk (4 points) groups. The observed retention rates were 3.5%, 14.8%, and 58.8% in the IVC, and 1.6%, 20.8%, and 76.5% in the EVC, respectively. The proposed predictive score, which combines tumor location, diabetic status, and clip number, effectively stratifies the risk of long-term titanium clip retention after ESD. This tool may assist in risk stratification for personalized postoperative surveillance, pending prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58828-w.

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has emerged as a minimally invasive cornerstone in the management of early gastric neoplasms, enabling en bloc resection with reduced morbidity and preserved gastric function compared to traditional surgical approaches1,2. However, like any medical procedure, ESD is not without risks, with postoperative bleeding being the most prevalent complication3–5. Titanium clips are commonly placed after gastric ESD, both for hemostasis of exposed vessels and, in selected patients, for prophylactic closure of the mucosal defect. The benefit of routine prophylactic closure for preventing delayed bleeding is not established in unselected patients6,7, but complete sustained closure reduces delayed bleeding in those at high bleeding risk, for whom validated tools such as the BEST-J score are available8–11. Closure, and therefore some degree of clip retention, is thus most justified in higher-risk patients. Previous studies have reported that titanium clips may remain in the gastric wall beyond the expected healing period6,12. For instance, Kim et al.12 found that 27.1% of hemoclips remained attached at three months after gastric ESD, and 2.5% persisted beyond one year. The long-term implications of such retention are not fully elucidated but may encompass mucosal injury, ulceration, and interference with subsequent endoscopic follow-up13. Furthermore, retained clips may adversely affect post-ESD ulcer scar formation and can limit future diagnostic imaging procedures—particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—due to local susceptibility artifact, which may degrade the image quality of adjacent pancreatobiliary structures. Of note, these titanium clips are MR-conditional at 1.5T and 3.0T and do not pose a magnetic field–induced displacement or heating hazard at clinical field strengths14,15.

The probability of titanium clip retention after ESD is influenced by a complex interplay of factors. Procedural aspects, such as clip design and the number of clips used, lesion characteristics, including size and location in the proximal stomach, and patient-related factors, such as age, gastric motility, and medication use (especially proton pump inhibitors, which can alter the gastric environment and potentially impact clip fate), all play a role16–18. Although previous studies have extensively explored various aspects of ESD, such as bleeding risks and technical improvements1,2,5,19, there is a notable paucity of research specifically focusing on the predictors of prolonged titanium clip retention. Most existing studies have overlooked this crucial aspect, perhaps due to the complexity of isolating the factors contributing to long-term retention amidst the multifaceted nature of ESD. Moreover, the impact of newer ESD techniques, such as traction devices and robotic assistance, on clip retention remains largely uncharted territory20–22.

This gap in the literature is significant, as understanding the predictors of prolonged clip retention is essential for optimizing ESD outcomes. A predictive scoring system tailored to this purpose could assist clinicians in risk stratification for postoperative surveillance. It would enable the identification of patients at high risk of prolonged retention after ESD has been completed, allowing for more informed postoperative surveillance planning and patient counseling. Additionally, it could guide the development of personalized follow-up protocols and enhance patient counseling. Furthermore, delving into the factors associated with clip retention may offer valuable insights into the gastric healing processes post-ESD and the behavior of foreign materials within the gastric environment. However, the decision to place titanium clips is primarily driven by bleeding risk. Validated tools such as the BEST-J score identify high-risk patients who may benefit from prophylactic closure. When closure is performed, clip retention beyond the healing period is a predictable consequence in a subset of patients. The principal trade-off is not safety—these clips are MR-conditional—but potential artifact on subsequent abdominal MRI or MRCP, particularly relevant for patients requiring pancreatobiliary surveillance (e.g., IPMN). Therefore, our retention score is designed as a post-procedural, complementary tool to inform patient counseling and follow-up planning (including MRI timing or elective clip removal).

This study aims to bridge this gap by developing and validating a predictive scoring system for titanium clip retention identified at the 6-month follow-up gastroscopy after gastric ESD. By leveraging a comprehensive set of variables and employing advanced statistical methods, our approach offers a practical tool for predicting prolonged retention in this retrospective cohort compared to previous attempts. Ultimately, this tool has the potential to inform personalized follow-up strategies, but prospective evaluation of clinically meaningful endpoints is required before its routine use can be recommended.

Materials and methods

Patients

A retrospective multicenter analysis was performed on patients undergoing ESD for gastric lesions between January 2020 and December 2024. Subjects from Suzhou BenQ Medical Center and Nanjing First Hospital were randomly allocated into a training cohort (TC) (70%) and an internal validation cohort (IVC) (30%). Patients from three additional hospitals—Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, No. 1 People’s Hospital of Kunshan, and Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital—constituted an external validation cohort (EVC). Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) ESD as the therapeutic intervention; (2) normal preoperative laboratory results (complete blood count, platelet count, coagulation tests), and electrocardiogram. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Non-use of titanium clips for wound closure; (2) Lack of 6-month postoperative gastroscopic follow-up; (3) Incomplete clinical records. Figure 1 outlines the participant selection process. This retrospective multicenter study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University (Approval No: L2026032). Written informed consent for the original endoscopic procedure, including the use of titanium clips and follow-up gastroscopy, had been obtained prospectively from all participants at the time of their procedure. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study followed the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis (TRIPOD) reporting guidelines23.

Endoscopic procedure

Endoscopic management of gastric lesions was carried out by endoscopists possessing different levels of experience, employing established techniques referenced in existing literature24,25. Nonetheless, all operators were highly skilled, each having performed over 5,000 gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures and at least 200 endoscopic mucosal resections (EMR) prior to performing the index ESD procedure included in this study. In this study, an endoscopist was considered experienced after completing 100 or more ESD procedures. Depending on variables such as wound size and location, postoperative wounds were managed according to the operator’s clinical judgment—options included closure with titanium clips, sealing with nylon loops, or electrocoagulation without subsequent closure. Case allocation was not systematically stratified by operator experience, and lesion complexity was comparably distributed across endoscopists of varying skill levels. Still, the possibility of unmeasured selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. All patients were instructed to discontinue the administration of any anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents one week before the operation, as per standard protocol. This same washout protocol was uniformly applied across all five participating centers. No bridging therapy (e.g., low-molecular-weight heparin) was used in any patient. Antithrombotic agents were restarted 24–48 h after ESD, provided that no overt bleeding occurred, although resumption timing was not strictly standardized across all cases. Figure 2 illustrates a case involving ESD of a submucosal tumor (SMT) in the gastric fundus, where the wound was closed using titanium clips. Notably, the clips remained in place at the six-month follow-up. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation. Patients were positioned in a standard left lateral decubitus position, and gastric insufflation was achieved using a CO₂ pump. High-definition single-channel endoscopes (Olympus GIF-Q260J or Sonoscape EG-550) fitted with transparent distal caps were utilized. Dissection was performed using specialized electrosurgical devices (such as the Olympus KD-650 L dual knife or Vedkang Kunpeng knife), connected to an ERBE VIO 200D electrosurgical generator. Two main types of titanium clips were used: a small clip (Nanwei Medical Hexasis Clip, ROCC-D-6–195) with a 10 mm jaw span, and a large clip (Anjieisi, AG-51042-1950-135-16) with a 16 mm jaw span. Both clip models are manufactured from titanium alloy, which is non-ferromagnetic. According to manufacturer specifications and prior testing, these clips are MR-conditional at 1.5T and 3.0T (i.e., they pose no magnetic field–induced displacement or heating hazard at clinical field strengths; however, local susceptibility artifact may degrade image quality of adjacent structures). The cutoff of ≥ 8 clips was prespecified based on clinical practice, approximately corresponding to the 75th percentile of the cohort distribution, and was determined before outcome analysis to avoid data-driven overfitting. Additional instruments included submucosal injection needles and hot biopsy forceps. Complete mucosal defect closure, defined as complete wound margin apposition with no visible residual defect, exposed submucosa, or muscularis propria, was achieved in all patients using only titanium clips placed in a simple interrupted or edge-approximating manner. No adjunct closure methods (e.g., line-assisted clip closure, clip-and-loop purse-string, over-the-scope clips, or endoscopic suturing) were used. Patients in whom complete closure could not be achieved with titanium clips alone were excluded to ensure cohort homogeneity.

Data collection

The patient data collected in this study encompassed sex, age, body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, use of anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications, use of corticosteroids, tumor size, tumor location, pathological type, injury to the muscularis propria, the number and model of titanium clips used, Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection status, and operator experience.

Definitions

The tumor size was primarily determined based on measurements of the postoperative pathological specimens. The pathological type was determined according to the postoperative results. Precancerous lesions mainly included low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia. Early-stage cancer is referred to as cases where cancer cell infiltration is confined to the mucosal layer or submucosal layer of the gastric wall, regardless of the presence or absence of regional lymph node metastasis. Muscularis propria injury denoted the exposure of the muscularis propria after ESD, with partial muscle fiber rupture but without perforation. Titanium clip retention referred to the presence of residual titanium clips at the original surgical site during a follow-up gastroscopy conducted at 6 months after the procedure. The 6-month cutoff was selected because normal gastric mucosal healing after ESD is typically completed within 8–12 weeks26; thus, any clip remaining at 6-month follow-up (i.e., more than double the expected healing period) is unlikely to undergo spontaneous extrusion and represents prolonged retention. Furthermore, the 6-month follow-up gastroscopy is a standard surveillance time point in routine clinical practice, enhancing the real-world applicability of our findings. Operator experience was categorized based on the number of ESD procedures previously performed. An endoscopist was defined as an ESD-novice when they had completed their first 100 ESD procedures, and as an ESD-experienced when they had completed more than 100 ESD procedures27. Tumor location was classified according to the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma (3rd English edition) into the upper, middle, and lower thirds of the stomach28. The upper third comprised the gastric cardia, fundus, and upper body. Two independent researchers assigned the tumor location based on preoperative endoscopic reports and intraoperative endoscopic findings. In case of discrepancy, a third senior endoscopist (with > 10 years of experience in gastric endoscopy) reviewed the original endoscopic images and reports, and the final classification was determined after consensus among the three reviewers.

Postoperative management

Upon completion of the endoscopic tumor resection, the resected specimen was promptly rinsed with physiological saline, and its exact dimensions were documented. In the case of mucosal lesions, the specimen was pinned onto a foam board for tumor measurement and evaluation of resection margins. For SMTs, the tissues were directly placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and subsequently processed through standard paraffin embedding and sectioning. Following the procedure, nasogastric tube drainage was instituted, and patient vital signs were under continuous surveillance. A strict fasting period of 24 to 48 h was enforced postoperatively. During this time, patients received full supportive care, encompassing acid-suppressive therapy, hemostatic agents, intravenous fluids, and nutritional support. Procedure-related adverse events—such as bleeding, perforation, or systemic infection—were monitored closely. Endoscopic re-examination, upright abdominal radiography, or abdominal CT were performed as needed. A gradual dietary advancement from liquids to semi-solid foods was permitted only after the fasting period and upon confirmation of the absence of any complications. All patients were prescribed a standardized postoperative acid-suppressive regimen consisting of oral omeprazole 40 mg once daily for four weeks. This uniform protocol was followed across all participating centers, ensuring no interpatient variability in proton pump inhibitor (PPI) type, dose, or duration.

Follow-up

All patients were scheduled for follow-up gastroscopy at exactly 6 months (allowed window: ±2 weeks; actual deviation ≤ 1 week in most cases) and 12 months postoperatively to assess wound healing and check for the presence of residual titanium clips. For patients with retained clips, endoscopic removal using a snare was attempted. In cases where clip retrieval was endoscopically challenging or the patient was asymptomatic, continued observation and follow-up were recommended. Additionally, annual imaging surveillance with contrast-enhanced abdominal CT and chest CT was required to evaluate for potential lymph node involvement or distant organ metastasis.

Sample size justification

The sample size for model development was determined according to the events per variable (EPV) criterion, a widely accepted approach for logistic regression-based prediction models. In the training cohort, the total number of outcome events (titanium clip retention at six months) was 63. The final multivariate model included three independent predictors. Thus, the EPV was 63/3 = 21, which exceeds the recommended minimum threshold of 10 (and the more conservative threshold of 20) for reliable model development and the avoidance of overfitting. The sample size for the internal validation cohort (n = 218, events = 27) and the external validation cohort (n = 328, events = 42) was considered adequate for stable estimation of model performance metrics.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and proportions, with intergroup comparisons performed using the Chi-square test. Risk factors for titanium clip retention at the 6-month follow-up were initially screened through univariate analysis. Variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < 0.05) were subsequently incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model. A scoring system was developed by normalizing the regression coefficients (β values) derived from the multivariate analysis, following the method described by Sullivan et al.29. The variable with the smallest β coefficient magnitude was used as the reference and assigned 1 point. The remaining variables were assigned points proportional to their coefficients, followed by rounding to the nearest integer. The model was validated using three distinct methods: (1) discrimination was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and quantified by the area under the curve (AUC); (2) goodness-of-fit was evaluated using calibration plots, and (3) clinical utility estimation through decision curve analysis (DCA). Diagnostic accuracy metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, overall accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), were calculated to evaluate model performance. For the observed clip retention rates across risk strata (low, intermediate, high), binomial 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Wilson method. The denominator (number of patients) and the corresponding 95% Wilson CI are reported for each stratum and each cohort. To account for potential clustering of patients within individual endoscopists, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a mixed-effects logistic regression model with endoscopist as a random intercept. The fixed effects included the three independent predictors identified in the primary analysis (tumor location, diabetes, and number of clips). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to quantify the proportion of total variance attributable to between-operator differences. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software environment (version 4.1.0), with a significance level (α) set at 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

This study enrolled a total of 1,055 patients, consisting of 509 in the TC, 218 in the IVC, and 328 in the EVC. The number of patients who had titanium clips retained at the 6-month follow-up gastroscopy after ESD was 63, 27, and 42 in the three groups, respectively. As shown in Table 1, the baseline characteristics of the TC and IVC groups were comparable, with no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05). Supplementary Table 1 presents a comparison of baseline data between the TC and EVC groups, indicating that long-term steroid hormone use was more frequent in the TC group than in the EVC group (6.3% vs. 2.4%, P = 0.011), whereas other baseline characteristics showed no significant differences (P > 0.05). Figure 3 displays the proportion of patients with and without titanium clip retention across the TC, IVC, and EVC cohorts according to tumor location, diabetes status, and the number of clips used. Exploratory analysis of clip-related findings in the retained group. Among patients with titanium clip retention at the 6-month follow-up, we observed delayed gastric ulcers at the clip site in 3.0% (4/132), mild food impaction around the retained clip in 3.0% (4/132), clip-induced mucosal erosion with intermittent abdominal discomfort in 2.3% (3/132), and endoscopic clip removal required due to planned MRI examination in 9.1% (12/132). Regarding the 12 patients who underwent clip removal for MRI, all were scheduled for 3.0T abdominal MRI for surveillance of pancreatic cystic lesions, including intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm in 8 cases and suspected choledocholithiasis in 4 cases. In all cases, the retained clips were located in the gastric fundus or upper body, whereas the region of interest was the pancreatic head/body and distal common bile duct. Clips were removed endoscopically using a snare or grasping forceps, a median of 2 weeks (range 1–4 weeks) prior to MRI, without adverse events. No perforation, overt gastrointestinal bleeding, or gastric outlet obstruction occurred during follow-up. These data were derived from a retrospective chart review without systematic prospective ascertainment or predefined adverse-event criteria; thus, they should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Comparable data for the non-retained group were not systematically collected, as clip-related findings would not have been routinely documented in the absence of a retained clip.

Comparison of characteristics between patients with and without retained titanium clips

Based on the presence or absence of retained titanium clips during the 6-month postoperative gastroscopic follow-up, patients were categorized into a clip retention group and a non-retention group. In the TC, the retention group demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes compared to the non-retention group (46.0% vs. 22.0%, P < 0.001). Additionally, tumors located in the upper third of the stomach were more frequent in the retention group (79.4% vs. 28.0%, P < 0.001). The proportion of patients who received ≥ 8 titanium clips during the procedure was also significantly greater in the retention group (73.0% vs. 45.7%, P < 0.001) (Table 2). A similar pattern was observed in the IVC cohort, as shown in Supplementary Table 2. Compared to the non-retention group, the retention group again had significantly higher rates of diabetes (P = 0.003), upper-third gastric tumors (P < 0.001), and the use of ≥ 8 titanium clips (P = 0.006). These findings were consistent in the EVC cohort. Supplementary Table 3 confirms that the retention group exhibited significantly greater proportions of patients with diabetes, tumors in the upper stomach, and those receiving 8 or more clips relative to the non-retention group (P < 0.05).

Risk factors for retained titanium clips at six months after ESD

Univariate analysis identified diabetes, tumor location in the upper stomach, and the use of ≥ 8 titanium clips during surgery as significant risk factors for clip retention at six months post-ESD (Table 2). These three factors remained independently associated with prolonged clip retention in the multivariate analysis (Table 3). Specifically, the multivariate model demonstrated that patients with upper stomach tumors had a significantly higher risk of clip retention compared to those with middle/lower stomach tumors (OR: 10.07; 95% CI: 5.30–20.47). Similarly, diabetic patients showed increased odds of retention compared to non-diabetic patients (OR: 3.54; 95% CI: 1.91–6.59). Furthermore, the use of ≥ 8 clips during surgery was associated with a higher risk relative to the use of fewer clips (OR: 2.62; 95% CI: 1.41–5.06) (Table 3). As shown in Supplementary Table 4, we further adjusted for clip type in the multivariable model. Clip type was not significantly associated with clip retention, and the effect estimates for the three independent predictors remained essentially unchanged, confirming the robustness of our primary model.

Sensitivity analysis for operator-level clustering

To further assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a mixed-effects logistic regression model with endoscopist as a random intercept. The three independent predictors remained significantly associated with clip retention, with effect sizes comparable to the primary model (upper-third tumor: OR 9.82, 95% CI: 5.12–18.83; diabetes: OR 3.48, 95% CI: 1.85–6.54; ≥8 clips: OR 2.55, 95% CI: 1.36–4.78). The ICC was 0.03, indicating that only 3% of the total variance in clip retention was attributable to between-operator differences. These results suggest that clustering by endoscopist did not materially bias our primary logistic regression model.

Development and validation of a prediction scoring system for retained titanium clips at six months after ESD

Based on the β-coefficients from the multivariate analysis of the TC cohort, a scoring system was developed by assigning points to each of the three independent risk factors. Points were allocated as follows: diabetes (yes = 1, no = 0), tumor location (upper = 2, middle/lower = 0), and number of clips (≥ 8 = 1, < 8 = 0) (Table 3). Figure 4 shows the association between the total score and the proportion of patients with titanium clip retention at six months after ESD.

In the TC, the rates of clip retention for scores of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 were 2.0%, 3.4%, 11.3%, 30.4%, and 57.1%, respectively (Fig. 4). With an AUC of 0.825 (95% CI: 0.771–0.879) (Fig. 5A), the scoring system demonstrated good performance. Calibration proved satisfactory, showing no significant deviation between predicted and actual probabilities (P > 0.05, Fig. 5B), and the DCA curve is displayed in Fig. 5C. The system showed a sensitivity of 69.8%, specificity of 83.0%, and accuracy of 81.3%, with a PPV of 36.7% and NPV of 95.1% (Table 4). In the IVC, a graded increase in clip retention rate was observed across scores: 2.0% (score 0), 4.7% (score 1), 9.3% (score 2), 23.5% (score 3), and 58.8% (score 4) (Fig. 4). The AUC was 0.809 (95% CI: 0.718–0.899, Fig. 5A), and calibration was satisfactory (P > 0.05, Fig. 5B). The DCA is presented in Fig. 5C as well. Sensitivity was 66.7%, specificity 82.7%, and accuracy 80.7%, with a PPV of 35.3% and NPV of 94.6% (Table 4). Similarly, in the EVC cohort, retention rates increased with score: 0% (score 0), 2.9% (score 1), 17.5% (score 2), 24.2% (score 3), and 76.5% (score 4) (Fig. 4). The model yielded an AUC of 0.855 (95% CI: 0.804–0.905, Fig. 5A) and showed satisfactory calibration (P > 0.05, Fig. 5B). The DCA curve is further illustrated in Fig. 5C. Sensitivity reached 92.9%, specificity was 64.0%, and accuracy was 67.7%, with a PPV of 27.4% and NPV of 98.4% (Table 4). Calibration was assessed by fitting a logistic regression model of the observed outcome on the linear predictor (log-odds). As expected by construction, the calibration slope for the training cohort was 1.00 (95% CI: 0.92–1.08), with an intercept of 0.00 (95% CI: −0.31 to 0.31). For the internal validation cohort, the calibration slope was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.85–1.12), and the intercept was − 0.08 (95% CI: −0.52 to 0.36). For the external validation cohort, the calibration slope was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.89–1.10), and the intercept was − 0.03 (95% CI: −0.44 to 0.38). These findings indicate good calibration across all cohorts, with no evidence of systematic overestimation or underestimation of risk. A slope below 1.0 would indicate over-dispersion (predictions too extreme), and a slope above 1.0 would indicate under-dispersion (predictions too moderate); our results demonstrate well-calibrated predictions without overfitting. Using three-tier risk stratification, patients were classified as low-risk (scores 0–1), intermediate-risk (scores 2–3), and high-risk (score 4). In the TC, the observed clip retention rates were 2.7% (8/292; 95% CI: 1.4–5.3%), 20.6% (39/189; 95% CI: 15.3–27.2%), and 57.1% (16/28; 95% CI: 38.9–73.7%), respectively. In the EVC, the rates were 1.6% (3/186; 95% CI: 0.6–4.6%), 20.8% (26/125; 95% CI: 14.6–28.9%), and 76.5% (13/17; 95% CI: 52.2–91.1%), respectively (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study successfully established and validated a novel predictive scoring system for prolonged titanium clip retention at six months following gastric endoscopic submucosal dissection. The model, derived from a large multicenter cohort, identified three independent predictors: tumor location in the upper third of the stomach, the presence of diabetes mellitus, and the use of ≥ 8 clips for defect closure. The scoring system demonstrated robust performance across both internal and external validation cohorts, with high negative predictive values indicating its particular strength in identifying patients at low risk for this long-term complication. This tool provides a practical means for clinicians to stratify patients postoperatively, potentially informing postoperative monitoring protocols and patient counseling. Nevertheless, it should be recognized that prolonged clip retention is not inherently undesirable; it may simply reflect durable closure and effective wound healing, and in asymptomatic patients generally does not require intervention. The high negative predictive value is especially valuable for reassuring both patients and clinicians when the calculated risk is low, which may help alleviate concerns regarding potential long-term foreign body retention. The stratification into distinct risk groups with markedly different retention rates offers a clear framework for clinical decision-making, aligning with the growing emphasis on personalized medicine in therapeutic endoscopy.

The potential mechanisms underlying these identified risk factors remain speculative and warrant further investigation. Regarding tumor location in the upper stomach—assigned the highest score in our model—the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Unlike the antrum, the gastric fundus and cardia exhibit relatively weak peristaltic activity and lower mechanical stress. One speculative hypothesis is that reduced mucosal mobility and lower shear forces in the upper stomach may lead to less clip dislodgement over time, allowing the clips to remain embedded within the healing tissue. Alternatively, anatomical differences in tissue thickness, vascularity, or submucosal fibrosis in the upper stomach may affect the encapsulation process of foreign bodies. However, these possibilities are hypothesis-generating only, and our data do not directly support any specific mechanism. Future histological and biomechanical studies are needed to clarify why proximal gastric location predicts prolonged clip retention. Diabetes mellitus is a well-established risk factor for impaired wound healing and altered tissue repair processes30,31. It is therefore plausible that hyperglycemia-induced microvascular dysfunction, reduced collagen synthesis, or increased susceptibility to infection might disrupt normal clip encapsulation or extrusion. Nevertheless, this remains a hypothesis derived from indirect evidence. The association between higher clip numbers (≥ 8) and prolonged retention may simply reflect confounding by defect size or complexity. One speculative possibility is that multiple adjacent clips could interact as a foreign body cluster, potentially eliciting a different fibrotic response than a single clip22, but this idea is untested in our study. We emphasize that these mechanistic interpretations are hypothesis-generating only and should not be construed as established conclusions. Future prospective studies are needed to elucidate the biological pathways underlying clip retention.

When contextualized within the broader landscape of predictive model development in gastroenterology and endoscopic surgery, our findings contribute to a rapidly expanding field. The integration of machine learning and artificial intelligence into endoscopic practice is becoming increasingly common, with applications ranging from polyp characterization to predicting lymph node metastasis32,33. Our relatively simple, points-based model offers a contrasting yet complementary approach to these complex computational models. While AI-based systems can process vast datasets and identify subtle, non-linear patterns, they often function as “black boxes” with limited interpretability and require significant infrastructural support32,34. Our scoring system provides immediate clinical translatability without requiring specialized software or computational expertise, making it particularly suitable for rapid post-procedural risk assessment at the bedside or in outpatient follow-up visits. This practical advantage is shared by other nomograms developed for various ESD procedures35–37. The methodological strengths of this study merit emphasis. The multicenter retrospective design enhanced the generalizability of our findings across different practice settings and patient populations. The inclusion of both internal and external validation cohorts strengthens the credibility of the model by demonstrating its performance in completely independent datasets, a critical step often overlooked in predictive model development. The sample size of over 1,000 patients provides substantial statistical power for identifying significant predictors, while the consistent performance across validation cohorts suggests reliable model stability.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding, despite our adjustment for numerous clinical and procedural factors. Second, the definition of prolonged retention based on a 6-month threshold, while clinically relevant and aligned with standard surveillance practice, remains arbitrary; longer-term follow-up may provide additional insights. Third, the types of titanium clips were not standardized across participating centers, and clip design characteristics (e.g., shape, jaw span, closure force) could influence retention rates. Fourth, the external validation cohort, although geographically distinct, originated from centers within the same regional healthcare system; validation in more diverse international populations would further strengthen generalizability. Fifth, we used a traditional univariate-then-multivariate selection strategy (P < 0.05 for entry) to identify independent predictors. Although this approach may discard some clinically relevant confounders and potentially inflate the apparent associations of retained variables, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by forcing all prespecified clinically important covariates into the multivariable model. The results remained essentially unchanged, and the final scoring system was identical. Nonetheless, we recognize that this selection strategy is not state-of-the-art for prediction model development, and the possibility of residual confounding or overoptimism cannot be completely excluded. Sixth, our interpretation of prolonged retention as a potentially undesirable outcome is not universally validated. We cannot definitively distinguish between retention due to “excessive” fibrotic embedding versus retention due to simply adequate healing without spontaneous extrusion. Without histological assessment of the clip-tissue interface or long-term functional outcome data, both interpretations remain plausible. Future prospective studies should incorporate patient-reported outcomes, MRI compatibility testing, and endoscopic re-intervention rates to clarify the net clinical impact of prolonged retention. Seventh, the model’s PPV was modest (27–37%), limiting individual prediction. However, the high NPV (94–98%) reliably identifies low-risk patients. The model is best used for risk stratification to guide surveillance. High-risk scores should be interpreted cautiously with individual factors. Eighth, the exploratory clip-related findings were retrospectively identified without systematic monitoring or a comparator group; thus, they are hypothesis-generating only, and prospective studies are needed to determine the net clinical impact of prolonged clip retention. Ninth, bootstrap optimism correction was not performed for the development cohort AUC, though the external validation (AUC 0.855) and an adequate EPV argue against substantial overfitting. Additionally, the recorded total clip number includes clips used for both selective hemostasis and prophylactic defect closure; because retrospective separation was not feasible and the cohort was restricted to complete-closure cases, this predictor should be interpreted as an overall post-procedural marker reflecting both defect extent and procedural practice, rather than a directly modifiable closure target. Finally, although the cutoff of ≥ 8 clips was prespecified based on clinical practice, it was informed in part by the cohort distribution (75th percentile); prospective external validation is still required to confirm the model’s generalizability.

Our findings support a risk-benefit framing for clip closure. In patients at high bleeding risk, in whom complete closure is beneficial, some degree of clip retention is an acceptable and expected consequence. The present score allows the probability of six-month retention to be estimated so that patients can be counseled accordingly, and post-ESD surveillance can be planned. The principal trade-off of retention is not a direct safety hazard—these clips are MR-conditional14,15—but rather the potential for local artifact that may reduce the diagnostic quality of subsequent MRI or MRCP in the upper abdomen. This is most relevant for patients with anticipated long-term pancreatobiliary imaging needs, such as surveillance for intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms or pancreatic cysts. The score is intended for post-procedural use, as the number of clips placed is known only after closure is completed. Two of the three predictors (tumor location and diabetes) are available preprocedurally and can be discussed during informed consent as factors that influence retention likelihood, with the final estimate incorporating the actual clip number after the procedure. We emphasize that the retention score is complementary to existing bleeding-risk scores (e.g., BEST-J) rather than a replacement. The clinical workflow would be: first, use a bleeding-risk score to decide whether prophylactic closure is warranted; second, if closure is performed, apply the retention score to counsel the patient about the likelihood of persistent clips and to plan any future MRI timing or elective clip removal. By identifying patients at high risk for prolonged clip retention after ESD, clinicians can adopt a more targeted approach to postoperative surveillance. For high-risk patients, more intensive endoscopic follow-up may be warranted to monitor for clip-related complications or to plan elective clip removal (e.g., before MRI). For low-risk patients, less intensive follow-up may be sufficient, reducing healthcare utilization and patient burden. The model also provides valuable information for patient counseling, setting appropriate expectations regarding the likelihood of clip retention and addressing concerns about long-term foreign bodies. From a procedural standpoint, awareness of these risk factors might influence technical aspects of ESD closure, such as clip selection and placement strategy in patients with identified risk factors. In this study, we refined our risk stratification into three tiers: low-risk (scores 0–1), intermediate-risk (scores 2–3), and high-risk (score 4). The observed retention rates increased stepwise across these categories: in the TC, 2.7%, 20.6%, and 57.1%; in the IVC, 3.5%, 14.8%, and 58.8%; and in the EVC, 1.6%, 20.8%, and 76.5%, respectively. This three-tier classification provides more clinically actionable information than the previous binary approach. Patients with a score of 3 (intermediate-risk) have a non-negligible retention rate (approximately 15–21%), warranting closer surveillance or discussion of elective clip removal (e.g., before MRI), whereas those with a score of 4 (high-risk) have a markedly elevated risk (> 55–75%) and should be considered for proactive management. The low-risk group (scores 0–1) can be reassured with routine follow-up.

Future research directions should focus on several key areas. Prospective validation of this scoring system in diverse patient populations is essential to confirm its clinical utility and refine risk stratification thresholds. Investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying clip retention, particularly the role of diabetes-impaired healing and the tissue response to multiple clips, could provide biological plausibility for our findings and identify potential therapeutic targets to promote clip extrusion. The development of specialized clip designs that optimize either secure initial apposition or predictable timed release would represent a significant advancement in endoscopic closure technology16. Integration of our clinical prediction model with radiomic or AI-based analysis of preprocedural imaging might further enhance predictive accuracy, creating hybrid models that leverage both clinical and imaging biomarkers. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses evaluating the impact of risk-stratified management strategies on healthcare utilization and patient outcomes would provide valuable evidence for implementing this approach in routine clinical practice.

In conclusion, we have developed and validated a predictive scoring system for prolonged titanium clip retention following gastric ESD. The model stratifies patients into distinct risk categories using postprocedurally available variables and is intended for use after the decision to perform prophylactic closure has been made (e.g., guided by the BEST-J bleeding-risk score). It serves as a complementary decision-support tool to counsel patients about the likelihood of prolonged clip retention, to plan the timing of future MRI examinations, and to consider elective clip removal when clinically indicated. The identification of upper gastric location, diabetes, and multiple clip use as key predictors offers preliminary insights into factors associated with clip retention and may help guide future technical improvements in endoscopic closure devices. Prospective studies are needed to further validate the model and to assess whether its use can improve clinical outcomes, such as reducing complications or optimizing postoperative care, in patients undergoing this increasingly common procedure.
