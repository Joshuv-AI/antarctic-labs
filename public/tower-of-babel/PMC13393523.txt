Incidence of Patulous Eustachian Tube Following Bariatric Surgery: An Observational Study at a Single Center
Authors: Hiram H. Plata‐Huerta, Josefina Alejandra Morales‐Del Angel, Gerardo E. Muñoz‐Maldonado, Luis F. Zorrilla‐Núñez, Oscar A. Ledesma‐Orta, José L. Treviño‐Gonzalez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the incidence of Patulous Eustachian Tube (PET) following bariatric surgery using standardized diagnostic criteria.Study DesignProspective cohort study.SettingTertiary academic medical center in northern Mexico.MethodsSixty‐four adults undergoing Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy were enrolled from April 2024 to June 2025. Otological assessments were conducted preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at 3 months postsurgery. PET diagnosis was determined using the Japan Otological Society (JOS) criteria. The Eustachian Tube Dysfunction Questionnaire‐7 (ETDQ‐7) was administered at each visit. Weight loss parameters were recorded, and correlated with PET outcomes.Results64 enrolled patients, 12 were excluded, and 52 were analyzed. The mean patient age was 39.4 years (SD ± 10.7); 73.1% were women. Mean weight loss at 3 months was 22.8 kg (19.3% of initial body weight). No patients met criteria for definite PET at any time point. The proportion of patients with possible PET increased from 9.6% preoperatively to 23.1% at 3 months (P = .07). ETDQ‐7 scores did not change significantly across time points (median 8‐9; P = .55). No significant correlation was observed between magnitude of weight loss and PET outcomes.ConclusionDespite significant weight loss, no confirmed cases of PET 3 months after bariatric surgery. These findings suggest that previously reported high PET incidence rates may reflect inconsistent diagnostic standards rather than true clinical pathology. Symptom‐driven evaluation rather than routine PET screening may be more appropriate in the early postoperative period. Larger studies with longer follow‐up are needed to assess long‐term risk.

Metabolic and bariatric surgery are evidence‐based and increasingly utilized options for managing obesity and diabetes. 1 , 2 However, rapid weight loss following surgery may lead to complications in various systems, including the otological system, as seen in patulous Eustachian tube (PET). This condition, first described in the 19th century, 3 is characterized by persistent tubal opening due to loss of valvular function, with symptoms including aural fullness, hearing changes, and autophony.4, 5, 6 The Japan Otological Society (JOS) defines “definite PET” as the presence of aural symptoms, response to tubal obstruction procedures, and objective findings. 7

PET occurs due to loss of tissue around the cartilaginous portion of the Eustachian tube. 8 Imaging studies have shown that while the tube lumen remains open, surrounding low‐density tissue shrinks due to weight loss. Ostmann's fatty tissue plays a role in keeping the tube closed, and its reduction is a key factor in PET development. Although weight loss is a frequent cause, about a third of cases are idiopathic. 9 Hormonal changes in pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and estrogen therapy have also been involved. 10 Other contributing factors include conditions that cause nasopharyngeal or peritubal tissue atrophy such as radiotherapy, neurological disorders, craniofacial anomalies, inflammatory diseases, 8 and anorexia nervosa. 11 Despite the growing body of literature linking bariatric surgery to PET, the wide variability in reported incidence (10.5%‐47.3%) likely reflects heterogeneous diagnostic criteria rather than true differences in disease burden. To date, no prospective study from Latin America has applied the JOS standardized diagnostic algorithm to this population. We therefore conducted a prospective cohort study with the objective to evaluate the incidence of PET following bariatric surgery using standardized diagnostic criteria among a sample of adults from northeast Mexico.

Methods

Setting

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted within the bariatric surgery program at University Hospital “Dr. Jose E. González” located in Monterrey, Mexico from April 2024 to June 2025. This study protocol, with committee registry OT24‐00005 was approved by the Ethics and Research Committees of the University Hospital “Dr. José Eleuterio González,” registered with the National Bioethics Commission (CONBIOÉTICA‐19‐CEI‐001‐20160404) and the Federal Commission for the Protection against Sanitary Risks (15‐CI‐19‐039‐067), and all participants provided verbally informed consent.

Participants and Procedures

Adult patients (≥18 years) scheduled for elective bariatric surgery were eligible for participation. Inclusion criteria were meeting established guidelines for bariatric surgery, agreeing to participate, and completing follow‐up assessments. Exclusion criteria included prior otological disease/surgery, PET at baseline, pregnancy, inability to complete questionnaires, or concurrent procedures involving the head or neck. All surgeries were performed by experienced bariatric surgeons using standardized techniques. Gastric bypass procedures followed the Roux‐en‐Y configuration, while sleeve gastrectomy was performed according to standard protocol.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of PET at 3 months postsurgery, diagnosed using the standardized criteria of the JOS. According to these criteria, “definite PET” is defined by the presence of all three of the following: characteristic aural symptoms (eg, autophony, aural fullness, or perception of one's own breathing sounds), symptom relief with tubal obstruction maneuvers (such as the head‐down position or Toynbee maneuver), and objective findings of the tympanic membrane movement synchronous with respiration on otoscopy, ear endoscopy or tympanometry findings. The decision to perform tympanometry was made by the examining otolaryngologist based on the clinical algorithm. “Possible PET” is defined by the presence of either of these 3 components. The JOS criteria offer a standardized diagnostic framework that prioritizes a combination of symptoms, physiological response, and visual findings rather than a single test. 12 Secondary outcomes included changes in scores on the Spanish‐validated version of the Eustachian Tube Dysfunction Questionnaire‐7 (ETDQ‐7), and their correlation with weight loss parameters. The ETDQ‐7 is a validated, symptom‐based instrument consisting of 7 items, with total scores ranging from 7 to 49; higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Although it has been observed that the questionnaire does not effectively differentiate between obstructive Eustachian tube dysfunction (ETD) and PET, we employed it as a standardized instrument for monitoring Eustachian tube‐related symptoms across time points.

Baseline data collection included demographic information, medical history, anthropometric measurements, and comorbidities. Weight loss data were collected by blinded medical examiners at 3 distinct time points. Standardized otoscopy or ear endoscopy examinations were conducted by neurotology with over 10 years of medical experience. During tympanic membrane examination, the patient was positioned in a seated posture using an endoscope or otoscope. The patient was instructed to inhale deeply through the nose while keeping their mouth closed and occluding the contralateral nostril with a finger.

To clarify the sequence of events at the 3 follow‐up time points, clinical evaluations were conducted as follows: during the preoperative assessment, we gathered a comprehensive otological history and performed an examination, which included otoscopy, the JOS criteria, and the administration of the ETDQ‐7. In the immediate postoperative period and at the 3‐month follow‐up, otological evaluations were conducted using the JOS PET criteria, with otoscopy, ear endoscopy, or tympanometry as needed, along with ETDQ‐7 administration. All otological examinations were performed by trained personnel. Following the Japan Otological Society's diagnostic algorithm, all patients were screened for cardinal PET symptoms—autophony, positional aural fullness, and respiratory‐synchronized hearing changes—during each evaluation. Only patients who reported these characteristic symptoms underwent a focused otoscopic examination to confirm or exclude PET by identifying tympanic membrane oscillation with respiration or tympanometry if needed. Otoscopy specifically assessed tympanic membrane movement during respiration, a pathognomonic finding of PET.

Statistical Analysis and Sample Size

Descriptive statistics comprise mean with standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Weight and body mass index (BMI) were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance across different time points. Nonparametric statistics were applied to dichotomous variables, with JOS criteria and PET presentation analyzed using Cochran's Q, and ETDQ7 scores evaluated with the Friedman test. A P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Correlation coefficients for mean standard deviation weight loss were analyzed using Spearman's correlation for PET classification and Pearson's correlation for the ETDQ‐7 score.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp.). Based on prior studies reporting PET incidence rates of 10% to 20% after rapid weight loss, a 15% incidence was assumed for sample size calculation. A total of 50 participants was estimated to provide 80% power to detect this incidence with a 95% confidence interval of ±5%.

Results

Out of the 64 patients initially evaluated for eligibility, 6 were excluded because they had pre‐existing otologic conditions not related to PET, 5 chose not to participate in the follow‐up, and one could not be reached for follow‐up (Figure 1). A total of 52 participants with a mean age of 39.4 years were included, 73.1% were women and 88.5% underwent Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery. Baseline BMI was 44.1. The most frequent comorbidities were hypertension (38.5%), chronic kidney disease (40.4%), and hypercholesterolemia (42.3%) (Tables 1 and 2). At 3‐month follow‐up, participants showed significant weight loss. Mean weight decreased from 119.17 kg preoperatively to 96.36 kg, with a mean loss of 22.8 kg). In parallel, BMI decreased from 44.26 to 35.66, with a mean weight loss of 19.3% (Table 3).

In relation to the JOS diagnostic criteria, no confirmed cases were observed at any of the three measurement points. Based on the Clopper‐Pearson exact method, the one‐sided 95% upper confidence bound for confirmed PET within 3 months postoperatively was 6.8%, indicating that the true incidence is unlikely to be higher under the studied conditions. However, the proportion of patients with compatible findings (“possible PET”) increased from 9.6% at the preoperative and immediate postoperative evaluation to 23.1% at 3 months, with a trend toward statistical significance (P = .07). The ETDQ‐7 questionnaire score showed no significant changes between the 3 time points. The median was 8 at baseline, 8 postoperatively, and 9 at 3 months (P = .55). In accordance with the diagnostic algorithm of the Japan Otological Society, all 52 patients were evaluated for primary PET symptoms at each assessment point. In instances where patients did not report these characteristic symptoms, an otoscopic examination was not conducted. Conversely, among those patients who did report symptoms necessitating an otoscopic examination, the proportion increased from 7.7% (4/52) at baseline and immediately postoperatively, reported symptoms requiring otoscopic examination. At 3‐month follow‐up, this increased to 9/52 patients (17.3%), though this change was not statistically significant (P = .23). Critically, none of the symptomatic patients at any time point demonstrated respiratory‐synchronized tympanic membrane oscillation on otoscopy, or the need of tympanometry. Consequently, none of the symptomatic patients demonstrated respiratory‐synchronous tympanic membrane movement on otoscopy, and therefore, tympanometry was not pursued. As a result, criterion 3 of the JOS diagnostic criteria was not met based on the available objective findings (Table 3).

No significant correlation was found between the percentage of weight loss and PET outcomes. The correlation coefficient with ETDQ‐7 was −0.013 (P = .167), and with JOS criteria was 0.167 (P = .236) (Table 4). No harms or adverse events related to otological assessment procedures were recorded during this study.

Discussion

Our findings challenge the prevailing narrative regarding the incidence of PET following bariatric surgery. While previous studies have reported postbariatric surgery PET incidence rates ranging from 10.5% to 47.3%, our application of the JOS standardized diagnostic criteria yielded zero confirmed cases of PET at 3 months postsurgery, despite achieving substantial weight loss (mean 22.8 kg, 19.3% of initial body weight). The significant discrepancy could arise from a variety of reasons that range from population type, methodological rigor, and sample size across the different studies evaluating this correlation.

Several studies have explored PET in the context of bariatric surgery. Muñoz et al found a 21.28% PET prevalence in 141 patients, with 96.6% reporting autophony; those affected lost more weight, more rapidly. 17 Pascoto et al observed ETD in 26.3% of patients at 3 months, increasing to 47.3% by 6 months postsurgery. 18 Kinasz et al reported PET symptoms in 18.75% of patients, while Yazici et al noted a 10.5% PET incidence and average weight loss of 35.06%. 19 , 20

Other studies have reported variable outcomes. Wu et al found that patients with rapid weight loss had greater symptom improvement despite being at higher PET risk. 21 Alsanad et al reported patulous tube symptoms in 20.22% of 450 postbariatric patients, with no difference in BMI change between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups. 22 Eravci et al used objective testing and found PET in 1 case; however, overall tympanometric measures remained stable. 23 Finally, Alyahya et al found no significant differences in scores between postbariatric patients and controls, suggesting limited impact of surgery on tube function. 24

This wide range of reported incidence rates—from our finding to previous reports as high as 47.3%—points the issue: the heterogeneity in diagnostic methods that may have led to systematic overestimation of true PET prevalence. The reliance on symptom‐based assessments rather than objective diagnostic criteria exemplifies this methodological concern. Kinasz et al noted that symptomatic patients had a lower preoperative BMI (45 kg/m2) than asymptomatic patients (47 kg/m2), suggesting that other factors besides weight loss might cause symptoms. This raises the question of whether rapid weight loss directly causes PET. 19

Our observation that 23.1% of patients met the criteria for “possible PET” at 3 months (compared to 9.6% at baseline) indicates that while some patients may develop symptoms compatible with PET following bariatric surgery, the progression to clinically definite PET is significantly less common than previously reported. This finding is consistent with the natural history of postsurgical physiological adaptations, in which transient symptoms may appear during the acute weight loss phase without necessarily developing into established pathological conditions. Despite achieving substantial and rapid weight loss comparable to previous studies, our cohort showed no confirmed PET cases, suggesting that rapid weight loss alone may not be sufficient to cause a clinically significant PET. The lack of correlation between percentage weight loss and PET outcomes in our study (correlation coefficient −0.013, P = .167) further challenges the presumed dose‐response relationship between the magnitude of weight loss and PET development. This finding is particularly noteworthy given that our patients achieved weight‐loss parameters similar to those reported in studies with high PET incidence rates.

Wu et al. highlighted that patients who lost weight rapidly were more likely to see an improvement in symptoms compared to those who did not experience rapid weight loss. This unexpected result indicates that the connection between weight loss and PET is more intricate than previously thought and may not progress linearly from rapid weight loss to symptom development. 21

The distinction between general ETD and specific PET diagnoses is a crucial point often overlooked in the literature. Smith et al demonstrated that combining multiple Eustachian tube function tests improves diagnostic accuracy, and that recognition of characteristic symptoms may provide superior diagnostic confidence compared with assessments focused on obstructive dysfunction alone. 25 Our ETDQ‐7 scores remained stable throughout the study period, with median scores of 8, indicating that while patients may experience transient otological symptoms, these do not necessarily signify clinically significant dysfunction or progression toward a PET diagnosis. The work by Eravci et al report ETDQ‐7 scores improved in most patients following bariatric surgery, with only 1 patient developing confirmed PET. This finding suggests that bariatric surgery may not inherently predispose patients to ETD and that reports of high dysfunction rates may reflect methodological limitations rather than true pathological processes. Alyahya et al provided additional evidence supporting this view by demonstrating that bariatric surgery patients had lower ETDQ‐7 scores compared to control subjects, with no statistically significant differences in Eustachian tube function between groups. These findings contradict the assumption that bariatric surgery inevitably leads to otological complications. 24

The timing of PET assessment following bariatric surgery appears to be critical for accurate diagnosis. Our 3‐month follow‐up period aligns with the acute weight loss phase, when PET would theoretically be most likely to manifest. However, the absence of confirmed cases during this period suggests that immediate postoperative assessment may be premature, as patients may experience transient symptoms that resolve spontaneously as physiological adaptation occurs. It is worth noting that variations in follow‐up duration across previous studies may complicate direct comparisons, which could potentially lead to discrepancies in the reported incidence rates.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size and short follow‐up period may limit the ability to detect delayed or late‐onset PET cases in this study. Second, the absence of a control group restricts causal inferences regarding the relationship between bariatric surgery and PET development. The presence of such variability can lead to the risk of misclassification bias and lingering confounding effects.

Importantly, although objective assessment is a core component of the JOS diagnostic criteria, objective testing (Item 3) was not universally applied to all the participants. Otoscopic examination was performed only in patients who reported primary PET symptoms, consistent with the JOS diagnostic algorithm. The decision to perform tympanometry was made by the examining otolaryngologist based on the clinical algorithm. This represents a potential limitation and should be addressed in future studies through other systematic and objective evaluations at predefined intervals.

Additionally, this study was carried out at a single tertiary referral center, which follows specific institutional protocols and serves a regional patient population. This context may restrict the applicability of the results to other healthcare environments or populations with different epidemiological characteristics.

The possible overestimation of PET incidence following bariatric surgery carries significant clinical and economic implications for patients. Routine otological screening of post‐bariatric patients based on inflated incidence rates could lead to unnecessary healthcare utilization, patient anxiety, and inappropriate interventions. Our findings suggest that systematic PET screening in the immediate postoperative period may not be justified, and future studies should specifically assess the long‐term risk, given the absence of confirmed cases despite substantial weight loss. The lack of progression from “possible PET” to “definite PET” in our cohort supports a conservative approach to postbariatric otological management. Instead of implementing routine screening protocols based on presumed high incidence rates, clinicians might benefit more from a symptom‐driven approach, reserving detailed otological evaluations for patients with persistent, bothersome symptoms that significantly impact their quality of life. One of the strengths of our study is the use of the JOS criteria, despite the short follow‐up. The discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies highlight the need for larger multicenter studies employing standardized diagnostic criteria and longer follow‐up periods to focus on the long‐term follow‐up to confirm our results.

Future research should focus on identifying specific risk factors that predispose certain patients to the development of PET, as the current paradigm of rapid weight loss as the primary causative factor seems insufficient to explain the observed clinical manifestations. Additionally, exploring the natural progression of otological symptoms after bariatric surgery could provide valuable insights into distinguishing patients prone to persistent dysfunction requiring intervention.

Conclusion

Our findings challenge the prevailing assumption that bariatric surgery leads to PET development. A multi‐institutional or population‐based prospective study would be more suitably positioned to validate these observations, incorporate a broader range of demographic variables, and include image volumetry to establish an association. Our findings suggest that routine screening in the early postoperative period may not be warranted, pending confirmation by larger prospective studies, regardless of the weight loss rate. Instead, clinicians should focus on symptom‐driven evaluations, acknowledging that transient Eustachian tube symptoms might occur without progressing to significant PET.
