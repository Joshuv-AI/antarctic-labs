Assessing stakeholders’ acceptability of implementing the midwifery continuity of care model in China: a convergent mixed-method study
Authors: Chunlan Yu, Sulin Dong, Xiaojiao Wang, Chunxiang Zhu, Hui Min, Xu Qian, Chunyi Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe midwifery continuity of care (MCoC) model, in which a known midwife or small team provides coordinated care throughout pregnancy, birth, and the postnatal period, is recommended internationally. In China, however, maternity care remains predominantly obstetrician-led and fragmented. Successful implementation of MCoC critically depends on stakeholder acceptance, yet evidence on their perspectives in the Chinese context is scarce. This study aimed to assess the acceptability of implementing the MCoC model among maternity care providers and childbearing women in China.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design was adopted. Nationwide cross-sectional surveys were administered to 896 maternity care providers, and focus group discussions were conducted with care providers and childbearing women. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests; qualitative data were analyzed inductively using thematic analysis.ResultsAmong the 896 providers (median age 35 years), 835 (93.2%) expressed willingness to accept and implement the MCoC model in clinical practice. Acceptability varied significantly by stakeholder group (P < 0.05), being highest among midwives (96.7%), followed by obstetric nurses (93.5%) and obstetricians (88.9%). Key facilitators included supportive leadership (95.3%), endorsement from obstetricians (79.8%) and buy-in from pregnant women (77.9%), as well as support from collaborating hospitals (63.0%). Qualitative analysis yielded four main themes: (1) High feasibility of implementing the MCoC model; (2) Women’s unmet needs for relational continuity within current maternity care; (3) Positive expectations toward the MCoC model; and (4) Barriers to implementing the MCoC model.ConclusionsThe acceptability of the MCoC model was high among stakeholders in China. Supportive leadership, targeted education and training, and strategic publicity are crucial for its successful introduction and scale-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09450-1.

Background

The midwifery continuity of care (MCoC) models are defined as approaches in which a known and trusted midwife, or a small team of midwives, acts as the primary caregiver for women and their babies throughout the entire continuum of care, from the antenatal period through labor and childbirth to the postnatal phase [1]. Grounded in a holistic view of childbirth as a transformative life event, this model encompasses: continuity of care; monitoring the physical, psychological, spiritual and social wellbeing of the woman and family throughout the childbearing cycle; providing the woman with individualized education, counseling and antenatal care; attendance during labor, birth and the immediate postpartum period by a known midwife; ongoing support during the postnatal period; minimising unnecessary technological interventions; and identifying, referring, and co-ordinating care for women who require obstetric or other specialist attention [1–4]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the MCoC model in settings with well-functioning midwifery programs [1]. Robust evidence confirms that this model improves clinical outcomes, such as increasing the likelihood of spontaneous vaginal birth and reducing cesarean section rates, without compromising safety [4, 5]. Furthermore, it significantly enhances women’s satisfaction with their experience of antenatal, intrapartum and postpartum care [4, 6–8].

Globally, midwifery is recognized as a cornerstone of high-quality maternal and newborn care [9–13]. This recognition is actively being translated into policy action, as evidenced by WHO’s 2024 framework for transitioning to midwifery models of care and the International Confederation of Midwives’ strategic plan (2024–2026), which prioritizes continuity of midwifery care worldwide [1, 14]. Currently, several high-income countries (e.g., Australia, the United Kingdom) have successfully integrated various midwife-led continuity models into their maternity systems [15, 16]. International studies highlight both the value and the complexities of implementation. Research demonstrates high acceptability among women, midwives, and obstetricians, linking the model to improved job satisfaction and proactive practice among midwives [17, 18]. However, experiences from diverse settings, such as Ethiopia, also reveal significant challenges, including concerns about workload, organization, staff shortages, and varying levels of engagement among midwifery staff [19, 20]. These findings underscore that successful implementation hinges not only on clinical evidence but also on addressing contextual and human resource barriers.

In contrast, maternity care in countries like China remains predominantly obstetrician-led and task-oriented, resulting in fragmented care pathways [4, 21]. Within this model, Chinese midwives are largely confined to intrapartum care in delivery suites, with obstetricians managing antenatal care and obstetric nurses providing postnatal support [22, 23]. This division limits midwives’ capacity to offer the continuous, holistic care defined by the MCoC model and fails to meet women’s needs for individualized, relational continuity. In China, the predominant educational route to becoming a midwife is through post-registration specialization. Most midwives are registered nurses who subsequently complete an additional, specialized training program in midwifery. Conversely, obstetric nurses are registered nurses who work within maternity units but have typically not undergone this formal midwifery specialization; their training and scope of practice center on general nursing care within an obstetric context [24, 25]. Furthermore, direct-entry midwifery education programs (where students train specifically as midwives from the outset) are rare and not yet part of the mainstream education system [25]. This educational framework, coupled with the hospital-based, task-oriented model, has historically constrained the development of a clearly defined, autonomous midwifery role capable of providing continuous care across the perinatal continuum [24, 26].

Recognizing this gap, efforts have been initiated in China to strengthen midwifery and reduce over-reliance on medicalized care [27]. Pioneering studies and pilot programs have demonstrated the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of midwife-led services in this context [28–31], and a midwifery service task list has been developed to clarify the scope of practice for a MCoC model [22]. However, the successful implementation of such an innovative model depends critically on its acceptability among key stakeholders, the clinicians who will deliver it and the women who receive it [32, 33]. Defined as the extent to which an intervention is perceived as appropriate by its users, acceptability is a multifaceted construct shaped by cognitive, emotional, and contextual factors [33]. While evidence on clinical outcomes is emerging, little is known about the acceptability of the MCoC model among different stakeholder groups in China.

To address this knowledge gap, this study assesses acceptability based on a clear operational definition of the MCoC model tailored to the Chinese hospital context. We define it as a woman-centered, midwifery-led model in which a designated midwife or a consistent small team acts as the primary caregiver, providing relational continuity across the antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal periods within the same institution. This model represents a fundamental shift from the current fragmented, obstetrician-led system and aligns with the extended midwives’ roles outlined in an evidence-informed practice framework in China [22]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the acceptability of this defined MCoC model in China from the perspectives of key stakeholders, including maternity care providers and childbearing women. A nuanced understanding of their perspectives, including perceived facilitators and barriers, is essential for informing strategies for effective scaling and sustainable integration into diverse clinical settings.

Methods

Study design

We employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design (Fig. 1). This approach was selected to leverage the complementary strengths of quantitative and qualitative methods in exploring the multifaceted construct of acceptability [34, 35]. The quantitative component consisted of a nationwide cross-sectional survey, designed to provide breadth and generalizability by assessing the level and distribution of acceptability among a large sample of maternity care providers. Concurrently, the qualitative component utilized focus group discussions (FGDs) to provide depth and contextual richness, uncovering the underlying rationales, perceived needs, and nuanced barriers from the perspectives of both providers and service users. A key feature of this design is the independent collection of both datasets, which allows each methodological strand to address the research question from its distinct epistemological position without one unduly influencing the scope or framing of the other. This preserves the unique contributions of both broad patterns and deep meanings.

Data from the two components were analyzed separately following their respective analytical protocols. The findings were then integrated during the interpretation phase, and the integrated results were further discussed in the Discussion section. This integrative process enabled a comprehensive and corroborated understanding of stakeholder acceptability, whereby statistical patterns could be explained, elaborated, and grounded in the qualitative narratives—an interpretive synthesis that neither method could achieve in isolation [34, 35]. The following sections describe each component; their order of presentation reflects a logical flow from the general (survey findings) to the specific (in-depth perspectives) and does not imply a sequential or dependent relationship.

Part one: quantitative study

Study design and setting

In this part, a nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted from April to May 2020, utilizing a self-designed questionnaire.

Participants and sampling

The target population for this phase included hospital managers and maternity care providers with a minimum of five years of experience in maternity settings. This criterion was established to ensure that participants possessed adequate clinical exposure and professional maturity to offer informed perspectives on care models and implementation challenges. The questionnaire was developed using the online Questionnaire Star Platform. To distribute the questionnaire, snowball sampling was employed via WeChat, targeting maternity healthcare providers. This method was selected for its effectiveness in swiftly reaching a geographically dispersed national sample of health professionals during the data collection period, leveraging professional networks to access a population that is typically challenging to reach through random sampling.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was calculated a priori using the formula for estimating a single population proportion in a cross-sectional study: n = Z2a/2 × p (1 − p) /δ2. The parameters were set as follows: the expected proportion (p) of healthcare providers with a favorable attitude toward the MCoC model was estimated at p = 0.33 (33%). This estimate was derived from a pilot survey conducted by our team prior to this national study, which indicated a preliminary coverage rate of midwife-led clinic services of approximately one-third among early-adopter hospitals. A standard 95% confidence level (Za/2 = 1.96) and a relative precision (δ) of 10% of the expected p (i.e., δ = 0.1 × 0.33 = 0.033) were applied. The calculation yielded a minimum sample size of 780 participants. To account for potential invalid or incomplete responses, we aimed to recruit at least 850 participants to ensure adequate statistical power for the primary analysis of overall acceptability.

Survey instrument

The survey questionnaire was developed through a multi-step process to ensure content relevance and clarity. (1) Development of the initial draft: The initial questionnaires were generated based on a comprehensive literature review of the MCoC model and its implementation, as well as the previously developed Chinese midwifery task list via a Delphi expert consensus study [22]. This approach ensured that the questions were grounded in both international evidence and nationally relevant practice norms. (2) Expert review and pilot testing: The draft was reviewed by an expert panel comprising professionals from midwifery, obstetrics, and health services research to evaluate clarity, relevance, and completeness. Subsequently, it was pilot-tested with 20 maternity care providers from various hospital levels (excluded from the main study) to assess question comprehension, appropriateness of response options, and completion time. Feedback from this phase led to refinements in wording and layout to enhance usability, while the core content and topics remained unchanged. (3) Final structure and content: The final questionnaire consisted of three sections (see Supplementary file 1): Section A: Institutional and sociodemographic information; Section B: Current status of midwifery practice (e.g., existence of midwife-led clinics, perceived scope of services); and Section C: Perspectives on the MCoC model, which included: (1) awareness and attitudes toward international midwifery philosophy; (2) acceptability of the MCoC model; (3) perceived facilitators and barriers at system, professional, and client levels; and (4) open-ended suggestions for implementation.

Data collection and quality control

Before proceeding, all participants were presented with an online informed consent form that detailed the study’s aims, the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymity of responses, and the confidentiality of data. Submission of the questionnaire was considered as implied consent. Multiple data quality controls were implemented on the Questionnaire Star Platform: (1) all items were made mandatory to prevent missing data; (2) each Internet Protocol (IP) address was restricted to a single submission to minimize duplicate entries; and (3) logical checks were embedded where applicable. Furthermore, to encourage broad participation across institutions and to reduce clustering of responses from a single hospital, the survey instructions explicitly encouraged only one participant per hospital department to complete the questionnaire. This approach aimed to promote broad institutional representation and enhance the geographic diversity of the sample. All submitted questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and consistency, and all were retained for analysis as no invalid submissions were identified.

Part two: qualitative study

Study design and setting

This segment of the study was conducted at a tertiary obstetrics and gynecology specialty hospital in Shanghai, China. This institution is a specialized women’s hospital that integrates medical healthcare, education, and scientific research. The hospital records an annual birth rate of approximately 10,000. A descriptive qualitative study utilizing FGDs was carried out from January 2020 to April 2020. This site was purposefully selected as a leading institution with early experience in midwifery service innovation, providing rich insights into the dynamics of implementation.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling to capture diverse perspectives critical to the implementation process, including hospital managers, maternity care providers, and pregnant women who underwent regular prenatal checkups. Hospital managers comprised directors of obstetrics and nursing, the head obstetric nurse, and the head midwife. The inclusion criteria for maternity care providers stipulated that they must be obstetricians, midwives, or obstetric nurses with at least five years of experience in the study setting. The inclusion criteria for pregnant women included: (1) being over 18 years of age; (2) agreeing to participate in the study; and (3) having received regular prenatal checkups in the study setting. The exclusion criteria for women included individuals with mental illness or cognitive impairments.

Three separate FGDs were organized to facilitate homogeneous group dynamics and promote open discussion: one with hospital managers (n = 8), one with maternity care providers (obstetricians, midwives, nurses; n = 9), and one with pregnant women (n = 6). Eligible hospital managers and care providers were invited, while pregnant women were recruited from the antenatal clinics. All participants received both verbal and written information regarding the study’s aims and procedures prior to providing informed consent.

Data collection

Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide (Table 1). Each focus group discussion took place in a private conference room at the study hospital and was facilitated by an experienced researcher (CLY) trained in qualitative methods. A co-facilitator (XJW) assisted in managing logistics and took detailed field notes on non-verbal cues and group interactions. At the beginning of each session, the facilitator reiterated the study’s purpose, assured participants of confidentiality, and established ground rules for respectful discussion. Open-ended prompts and follow-up questions were employed to encourage all participants to share their views, clarify meanings, and explore emerging themes in depth, while ensuring that the conversation remained focused on the research topics. All FGDs were conducted in Mandarin, the native language of all participants, audio-recorded with permission, and lasted between 90 and 120 min.

Data analysis

Quantitative study data were statistically analyzed using SPSS Version 26.0. Quantitative variables were reported as proportions or medians. Differences between groups were analyzed using χ2 tests, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. For qualitative data analysis, Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach was adopted, following their proposed six-phase framework: (1) Familiarization with the data: Researchers repeatedly read transcripts while listening to recordings to immerse themselves in the data; (2) Generating initial codes: Systematic, data-driven coding was performed across the entire dataset; (3) Searching for themes: Codes were collated and clustered into potential themes; (4) Reviewing themes: Themes were checked against the coded extracts and the entire dataset for coherence and distinctiveness; (5) Defining and naming themes: The essence of each theme was refined and clearly defined; and (6) Producing the final report: Final themes were selected, illustrated with compelling anonymized extracts, and related back to the research question [36, 37]. The analysis was conducted in Chinese to preserve nuanced meaning, with key quotes translated into English for reporting. The research team held regular discussions to refine themes and ensure interpretive rigor. NVivo 12.0 was utilized to manage the data.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University (OGHEC201951). All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, provided written informed consent, and were assured of data confidentiality and the privacy of their identities.

Results

Part one: quantitative results

Demographic characteristics of the participants

A total of 896 valid questionnaires were collected from 31 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China between April and May 2020. The majority of participants (87.5%, n = 784) were affiliated with tertiary hospitals (Table 2). Among the 896 participants, 425 (47.4%) were from public general hospitals, while 436 (48.7%) were from public maternity hospitals (Table 2). The median age of the participants was 35 (30,40) years, and the median years of work experience was 11 (6,16) years. Of the participants, 31.6% (n = 283) held senior professional titles (e.g., Associate Chief Nurse/Physician or higher), 35.7% (n = 320) had intermediate professional titles (e.g., Nurse/Physician in Charge), and 32.7% (n = 293) held junior professional titles (e.g., Nurse/Resident Physician).

Acceptability of the MCoC model

Among the 896 survey participants, 835 (93.2%) expressed acceptability of the MCoC model and anticipated to its introduction into their hospitals. Conversely, 30 participants (3.3%) held a positive view of the model but indicated that their hospitals might not adopt it. Additionally, 31 participants (3.5%) expressed an unacceptable attitude or remained undecided regarding the model. Of the 896 respondents, 819 (91.4%) were maternity healthcare providers, comprising 126 obstetricians, 570 midwives, and 123 obstetric nurses. The acceptability rates of the MCoC model among midwives, obstetric nurses, and obstetricians were 96.7% (551/570), 93.5% (115/123), and 88.9% (112/126), respectively (P < 0.01) (Table 3). The reasons cited by the 45 participants who did not accept the model included four key aspects: (1) facility-related factors, such as lack of hospital leadership support and the small scale of hospitals; (2) obstetrician-related factors, including the dominance of the routine obstetrician-led maternity care model and potential conflicts with obstetricians’ roles; (3) midwife-related factors, such as insufficient knowledge and skills among midwives, heavy workloads, high uncertainty in childbirth care, a shortage of the midwifery workforce, lower educational levels, and conservative attitudes among midwives; and (4) women-related factors, specifically women’s distrust toward midwives.

Situation of and suggestion for current midwifery care services

A total of 571 participants (63.7%) reported that their hospitals had established midwife clinics, while 325 participants (36.3%) indicated that no midwifery antenatal care services were provided in their hospitals. Among the established midwife clinics, 469 participants (82.1%) stated that midwifery care was delivered by senior midwives from the delivery room and 235 participants (41.2%) noted that midwives primarily provided care for pregnant women in the late trimesters. Regarding the scope of midwifery practice in China, 552 participants (96.7%) suggested that it should encompass both the late pregnancy and childbirth periods; 454 participants (79.5%) indicated it should include the middle pregnancy stage; 489 participants (85.6%) believed it should cover the postpartum stage; and 396 participants (69.4%) stated that it should include the early pregnancy period. In relation to the international midwifery care philosophy that “Midwives are the primary care providers for low-risk pregnant women,” 535 participants (93.7%) expressed supportive attitudes with 408 participants (71.5%) indicating “strongly agree,” and 127 participants (22.2%) indicating “agree.”

Facilitating factors for implementing the MCoC model

A total of 836 participants identified facilitating factors for implementing the MCoC model (Fig. 2), including supportive leadership (n = 797, 95.3%), endorsement from obstetricians (n = 667, 79.8%) and buy-in from pregnant women (n = 651, 77.9%). Additionally, support from collaborating hospitals was reported by 527 participants (63.0%). Furthermore, 22 participants (2.6%) suggested that strengthening the midwifery workforce, clarifying midwives’ roles, functions, and scope of practice, enhancing knowledge and skills, as well as promoting the model, constituted important factors facilitating the implementation of the MCoC model.

Theme 1: High feasibility of implementing the MCoC model

Participants consistently perceived the MCoC model as both rational and achievable within the Chinese healthcare context. This perception of high feasibility was anchored in a strong belief in the capacity of skilled midwives to deliver continuous, professional support across the antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. The consensus highlighted not only theoretical agreement but also practical confidence in integrating the model into existing hospital systems.

Theme 2: Women’s unmet needs for relational continuity within current maternity care

The interviews revealed a foundational gap in the existing maternity care system: pregnant women’s unmet needs for a continuous, trusting relationship with a known caregiver. Participants described experiences of fragmented care, encountering different professionals at each stage, which led to feelings of uncertainty and a lack of personalized support. This perceived deficiency underscored a clear demand for relational consistency that the current obstetrician-led, task-oriented model fails to provide.

Theme 3: Positive expectations toward the MCoC model

In direct response to this identified need, participants expressed strong and positive expectations for the proposed MCoC model. They anticipated that having a designated midwife or team would directly address the deficit in continuity, fostering trust, reducing anxiety, and providing tailored support throughout the antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. Their expectations extended beyond receiving a service to envisioning a transformative care experience centered on a continuous relationship.

Theme 4: Barriers to implementing the MCoC model

Despite recognizing the model’s value, stakeholders identified several interconnected barriers to its implementation, which can be categorized into systemic, professional, and resource-related challenges.

Systemic and resource barriers

The most frequently cited concern was a critical shortage of the midwifery workforce, perceived as a fundamental constraint on delivering continuous care. Participants linked this directly to existing high workloads, questioning how continuity could be sustained without significant staffing increases. This concern was coupled with the practical challenge of ensuring postpartum continuity after hospital discharge, highlighting a gap between the model’s ideal and current community or follow-up infrastructure.

Professional role and capacity barriers

Participants emphasized the need for greater clarity and development at the professional level. They pointed out that midwives’ specific job roles, responsibilities, and qualifications within the MCoC model required clear definition and standardization. Furthermore, there was a consensus that midwives’ knowledge and skills would need targeted enhancement to confidently assume this expanded, continuous care role.

“This care model is women-centered… It is necessary to further ascertain how to implement it in clinical practice. For example, which midwife will be qualified? And what are their detailed job roles and responsibilities?” (MHP07).

Discussion

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods approach to investigate the acceptability of implementing the MCoC model among stakeholders in China. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate the acceptability of this model from the perspectives of both service users and providers in the Chinese context. Overall, our findings indicate a high level of acceptance for the MCoC model among participating healthcare providers, alongside substantial recognition of international midwifery care principles. Midwives expressed the highest level of acceptability. This aligns with international findings, such as those of Hailemeskel et al., where midwives welcomed midwife-led continuity models for their potential to improve care quality, birth outcomes, and their own professional autonomy [19].

Conversely, while still considerable, the quantitative acceptability level among obstetricians was comparatively lower than among midwives. This discrepancy likely stems from differing professional paradigms. Midwives are primarily educated to manage physiological processes and provide holistic, low-intervention care for low-risk pregnancies, whereas obstetricians are specialists in managing pathology and complications [38, 39]. Therefore, their perspectives on a model centralizing midwifery care for low-risk women may naturally vary. It is crucial to frame this not as a conflict but as complementary expertise within a collaborative, woman-centered team. In an effective MCoC model, obstetricians provide essential specialist support when complexities arise, working alongside the midwife to ensure the woman remains at the center of all decision-making.

Our qualitative findings further revealed that pregnant women expressed a clear need for and positive expectations of continuity of midwifery care, consistent with other studies in China and internationally [40–44]. This desire for a continuous, trusting relationship with a known caregiver is foundational to respectful, woman-centered care as defined by the ICM [3]. Although the current scope of Chinese midwives’ practice is often limited primarily to intrapartum care, participants in our study strongly endorsed the philosophy that midwives are the most appropriate care providers for childbearing women [3]. There was a clear consensus that the future scope of midwifery practice should encompass the entire perinatal continuum, from pre-pregnancy to the postpartum period, echoing Renfrew et al.’s comprehensive definition of the practice of midwifery [27]. Pregnant women in our study valued the prospect of continuous, supportive care from midwives, which is known to enhance trust, empowerment, and outcomes [45–48].

The successful implementation of the MCoC model, crucial for improving maternal and neonatal health, depends on a range of facilitators and barriers. Key facilitators identified in our study include supportive hospital leadership, endorsement from obstetricians and pregnant women, a strengthened midwifery workforce with clear roles, and enhanced knowledge and skills. These align with enabling factors identified globally, such as adequate infrastructure, skilled staff, and decisive leadership [9, 49].

Conversely, significant barriers were also highlighted, including a shortage of midwives, heavy workloads, lack of leadership support, potential role conflicts with obstetricians, and gaps in midwives’ knowledge and skills. These findings are consistent with other researchers’ findings [44, 50] and corroborated by our qualitative data, which detailed contextual challenges such as workforce constraints and unclear role definitions. These barriers resonate with findings from our previous meta-ethnography, which identified organizational, cultural, and professional obstacles to providing woman-centered high quality care [51]. Overcoming these barriers requires collective action and professional commitment from all stakeholders [41, 52].

Therefore, to introduce and scale up the MCoC model in China, it is necessary to address these multifaceted barriers through targeted strategies. These should include: (1) implementing high-quality education and training programs to enhance midwives’ competencies; (2) fostering strong, supportive leadership and enabling policy environments at institutional and national levels; and (3) conducting publicity campaigns to promote the model’s benefits among all stakeholders. As maternity care must adapt to local contexts [53], these strategies should be tailored to the specific drivers, needs, and resources within the Chinese healthcare system to empower midwives to practice to their full potential [51] and ultimately improve care for women and newborns.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its convergent mixed-methods design. By integrating a nationwide quantitative survey with in-depth qualitative focus groups, we were able to triangulate findings and develop a comprehensive, multi-layered understanding of acceptability. The quantitative data established the prevalence and distribution of acceptance among maternity care providers across China, while the qualitative data elucidated the underlying rationale behind these patterns from the perspectives of providers, managers, and childbearing women.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, in the quantitative phase relied on snowball sampling. While this facilitated efficient recruitment across diverse regions, it may introduce selection bias, as participants were recruited through referral networks. This could affect the representativeness of the sample and limit the generalizability of the study findings. Second, the qualitative data were drawn from a single tertiary hospital in Shanghai. Although this setting provided rich insights into stakeholder perspectives within a well-resourced context, the transferability of findings to other regions, particularly those with different healthcare resources, cultural norms, or levels of midwifery integration, may be limited. Third, all women interviewed were recruited during the antenatal period, and most were primiparous. Their views, therefore, reflect expectations and perceptions rather than lived experience of receiving continuity of care across the full perinatal journey. This may have influenced the depth of their insights regarding postnatal continuity and the longitudinal relationship with a midwife. Future research should address these limitations by employing probability or stratified sampling strategies in larger-scale surveys, incorporating multiple and diverse study sites (including secondary hospitals and rural settings) in qualitative designs, and following women across the antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum periods to capture evolving experiences. Such studies will further strengthen the evidence base to guide the effective scaling and sustainable implementation of the MCoC model in China.

Conclusion

This study provides important evidence to inform the implementation of the MCoC model in China. The findings demonstrate a high level of acceptability among key stakeholders, including maternity care providers and pregnant women. A clear consensus emerged on the need to expand the scope of midwifery practice to cover the entire perinatal continuum, from pre-pregnancy through to the postpartum period. To translate this supportive attitude into successful practice, focused strategies are essential. These include cultivating supportive institutional leadership, establishing comprehensive education and training programs for midwives, and conducting targeted publicity campaigns to increase awareness and trust among all stakeholders. Addressing the identified facilitators and barriers through these measures will be crucial for the sustainable integration and scaling-up of the MCoC model within the Chinese maternity care system.
