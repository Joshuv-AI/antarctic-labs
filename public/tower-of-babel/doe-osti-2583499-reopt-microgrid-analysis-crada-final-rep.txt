Abstract of CRADA Work:

NREL is the only DOE national laboratory solely dedicated to advancing energy efficiency (EE)
and renewable energy (RE) technologies and applications. Since its inception, NREL has
supported both the public and the private sectors in implementing EE and RE systems and
strategies to lower energy use and to meet remaining energy needs with resources having
minimal environmental impact. NREL is now a sought-after resource for EE, RE, and energy
systems integration expertise.

CU Boulder is a public university located in Boulder, Colorado, with over 30,000 students. CU is
committed to environmental sustainability and is interested in partnering with NREL to explore
opportunities for RE on campus and evaluate an RE microgrid.

The goal of this project is to assess the technical and economic potential for RE generation at CU
and evaluate how RE may be incorporated as part of a microgrid to increase energy resiliency on
campus.

Statement of Work:

Task 1 - Renewable Energy Assessment

NREL will perform a Renewable Energy Optimization (REopt) assessment to evaluate electric
and thermal energy options and storage at CU. The objective of the REopt analysis is to identify
cost-effective opportunities for renewable energy and to help CU understand the potential energy
generation, carbon impact, and economics of RE options. NREL will consider the following
technologies: 1) photovoltaics, 2) off-site wind (if option elected), 3) biomass, 4) landfill gas, 5)
solar hot water, 6) fuel cells, 7) ground source heat pumps, 8) energy storage (thermal and
electric), and 9) the utility grid.

NREL will gather data on existing and planned campus energy systems, current and projected
energy consumption and cost, utility rate tariffs, RE incentives, and available rooftop or land
areas for RE development. NREL will also gather data on potential off-site RE purchases. CU
will support this data collection effort by providing electrical interval data, thermal/fuels demand
data (ideally 15-minute or hourly interval data, alternatively daily/monthly consumption),
example utility bills, data on available roof/land area, and any available data on existing systems
or proposals for relevant systems. CU will provide information on off-site wind proposals they
have received, including the amount of energy to be provided and the power purchase agreement
(PPA) price.

NREL will customize the REopt model to model CU’s existing energy system, including its
existing combined heat and power (CHP) plant. NREL will work with CU to define analysis
constraints such as limits on technology types and sizes, utility interconnection limits, and
energy export limits. NREL will evaluate two ownership options: direct purchase and third-party
financing.

NREL will evaluate the type and size of technologies that minimize the lifecycle cost of energy
to CU under each ownership option. NREL will also evaluate a second goal, such as the type and
size of technologies that achieve 20% carbon reduction at minimum lifecycle cost. For
technologies that do not show up in the optimal solution (i.e., not lifecycle cost-effective), NREL
will report economics on an estimated system size to demonstrate why they were not selected by
the model. NREL will perform up to three sensitivities or what-if scenarios. Examples of what-if
scenarios include variations in load projections, future renewable costs, future utility costs, and
incentive availability.

The result of this analysis will be an initial assessment of the technical and economic viability of
renewable energy and storage. A PowerPoint summary or report (up to 10 pages) describing the
renewable technologies, sizes, capital costs, and lifecycle costs will be provided. This analysis
will help inform CU’s overall energy strategy planning.

Task 1 Results: The REOpt report:

The following recommendations from the (non-published) PowerPoint report are summarized
here:

 • PV, battery, and thermal energy storage are recommended for reducing costs,
 contributing to climate goals, and enhancing resiliency.
 • Existing CHP plants, ground source heat pumps, solar water heating, biomass heating,
 landfill gas, and fuel cells are not found to be cost-effective.
 • For offsetting carbon at the lowest costs, the existing CHP plant should only be run
 during the winter without the steam turbine.
 • CU is likely to meet their 2020 carbon goal based on current trends with no or little
 additional investment required.
 o Assumes continued aggressive load growth management by CU and Xcel grid
 carbon factor projections are accurate.
 • Meeting the 2030 goal of 50% CO2 reduction from the 2005 baseline will be very costly
 o Can be met with PV and biomass heat plant combined with battery and thermal
 energy storage systems, but will cost CU $166M more than business as usual.
 • Consider expanding carbon reduction opportunities beyond the physical boundary of the
 campus, particularly in light of the 2050 75% CO2 reduction goal.
 o Explore potential for renewable gas/biogas contracts for delivery via existing
 natural gas system.
 o Explore off-site renewable project development (e.g., community solar).
 o Transportation electrification can reduce Scope 3 emissions. Although not
 included in the scope of this analysis, perhaps CO2 reductions from other sources
 can be credited toward overall university emissions targets.
 o Investigate future renewable energy and/or energy storage coordination with the
 City of Boulder.

Task 2 - Microgrid Assessment

NREL will conduct preliminary microgrid modeling for a subset of the CU campus, approximately
3 buildings on East Campus. NREL will work with CU to determine critical loads that need to be
sustained in the event of a grid outage. NREL will then use the model built in task 1 to evaluate
how RE and storage technologies can augment existing backup generation to increase the
resiliency of the campus.

REopt will then be used to optimize two microgrid scenarios: 1) sizing the RE and storage systems
for maximum economic benefit while grid-connected and evaluating the resiliency benefits these
systems provide; and 2) sizing the RE systems to sustain an outage of a defined length and
evaluating the economic benefit these systems provide. If sufficient background information is
provided by CU, NREL will include the cost of grid interruptions in the economic analysis.

 4

NREL will compare the lifecycle costs and outage survivability length of the existing baseline
‘diesel only’ emergency backup solution (if applicable) to that of a ‘diesel/RE/storage’
combination that can sustain the critical loads during simulated grid outages. We assume that
diesel generators can only be used during grid outages, while the RE and storage assets can be
used for economic gain during normal operation. NREL will determine the optimal sizing and
dispatching of energy assets that function in both grid-connected and islanded modes. During
normal grid-connected operation, such energy assets would be used for economic purposes,
including offsetting grid purchases, peak demand reduction, and energy arbitrage. In the case of
grid outages, these assets would be used to form a microgrid capable of sustaining the defined
critical load for a defined period of time.

NREL will develop a microgrid conceptual design. This will include evaluating electrical
infrastructure and associated monitoring and control devices, developing electrical
interconnection concepts, and designing operating schemes in both normal utility-connected and
islanded (isolated during utility outages) microgrid conditions. This also includes generating a
rough cost estimate for the microgrid configuration.

This task will result in a PowerPoint summary or report (up to 20 pages) that identifies the type
and size of RE, conventional generation, and storage that support the critical load; the amount of
resiliency the system provides (number of hours it can support the load); the capital and lifecycle
costs of the system; and a preliminary conceptual design.

Task 2 Results: The Microgrid Report:

The following is the executive summary for the (non-published) Word document:

This report describes the technical assessment of a microgrid for three research buildings at the
University of Colorado’s East Research Campus in Boulder, Colorado, and provides several
conceptual microgrid design options.

The buildings in the microgrid have experienced numerous momentary grid disturbances in the
past several years that result in laboratory equipment shutdowns at the utilization level, with
subsequent loss of valuable research data. The buildings have existing standby generators for full
utility outage conditions, but nothing to protect against short-term events.

A request was made to mitigate the transient-level issues. The primary objective of this project is
to provide short-term uninterrupted power to a partial 13.2 kV distribution system that supplies
three research facilities.

As part of a separate effort, NREL evaluated renewable energy technologies that would
minimize the lifecycle cost of energy at the site under normal, grid-connected operation.

Those results were then evaluated to see how they could be integrated with the microgrid
controller to improve the resiliency of research facilities to grid disturbances.

 5

Findings:

As part of a separate, concurrent effort, NREL evaluated energy resources, including existing
generation assets, renewable energy, and energy storage technologies that would minimize the
lifecycle cost of energy for the university under normal, grid-connected operations. NREL’s
REopt™ techno-economic decision support model was used to optimize the university’s energy
systems, and as part of that work, a 1.4MW/3.1MWh Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS)
was determined to be cost-effective.

For the microgrid assessment, NREL evaluated several technologies for mitigating transient-
level events that include energy storage at medium voltage (13.2 kV) as well as at the low
voltage utilization level. The first option that was considered includes electrochemical batteries
in a line-interactive 13.2 kV UPS (uniterruptible power supplies) (with static switch)
configuration. Another 13.2 kV technology uses kinetic energy in the UPS for uninterrupted
power at grid scale. A third option considered a 13.2 kV system with a true on-line UPS,
including batteries, rectifier, inverter, step-down and step-up transformers, and maintenance
bypass, installed on the 13.2 kV feeder. Finally, decentralized, low-voltage systems were
evaluated in conjunction with a medium-voltage BESS for various economic (i.e., peak shaving)
and resiliency benefits. All evaluated options have a reduced carbon footprint compared to
microgrids with only diesel generators.

The initial system could be sized for the Sustainability, Energy and Environmental Community
(SEEC), Sustainability, Energy and Environmental Laboratory (SEEL), and Wind Tunnel
building loads only. With this configuration, the system would provide improved power quality
to the three research buildings while providing economic benefits to the university.

An option could include planning for the ultimate buildout of the distribution feeder that includes
SEEC, SEEL, Wind Tunnel, and other selected buildings, and sizing equipment for this ultimate
capacity. This would be a larger system with a bigger footprint and, depending on the selected
technology, could be better located near the Planning, Design and Construction (PDC) building.
