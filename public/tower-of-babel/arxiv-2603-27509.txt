Uniqueness of bounded solutions to the fuzzy Landau and multiespecies Landau equations
F.-U. Caja-Lopez

Abstract
. We prove conditional uniqueness of weak solutions to the fuzzy Landau equation and the multiespecies Landau system under suitable integrability assumptions. The results are based on explicit stability estimates in the 2 2 -Wasserstein distance for a broader class of nonlinear equations with singular coefficients. Interestingly, this class includes the 2 2 D incompressible Euler equations, the Vlasov–Poisson system, and the Patlak–Keller–Segel model, thereby recovering known uniqueness results within a unified framework. Our approach builds on the stochastic coupling method introduced by Fournier and Guerin for the homogeneous Landau equation in [ 23 ] , which we recast in a more analytic form. In addition, we present an alternative argument based on the symmetrization technique of Guillen and Silvestre [ 30 ] , which was further developed in [ 7 ] , yielding comparable stability estimates.



1.
Introduction

This work is motivated by the study of the Landau equation, which models collisional electron dynamics in plasmas:

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\textnormal{div}_{v}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Phi(v-v_{*})\Big(f(t,x,v_{*})\nabla_{v}f(t,x,v)-f(t,x,v)\nabla_{v_{*}}f(t,x,v_{*})\Big)\,dv_{*},

where
\Phi(z)=|z|^{2+\gamma}\mathbb{P}(z)
and
\mathbb{P}(z)=\mathrm{Id}-\frac{z\otimes z}{|z|^{2}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\langle z\rangle^{\perp}
. The parameter
\gamma
is typically considered in the range
-3\leq\gamma\leq 1
, with the most physically relevant case being
\gamma=-3
, corresponding to Coulomb interactions. The analysis of (
1.1
) is challenging due to its nonlinear, nonlocal, hypoelliptic structure and the presence of singular coefficients.
We study uniqueness and stability of two variants of (
1.1
). First, we consider the
fuzzy Landau equation

(1.2)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\textnormal{div}_{v}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\kappa(x-x_{*})\Phi(v-v_{*})(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{v_{*}})\big(f(t,x,v)f(t,x_{*},v_{*})\big)\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*},

where the kernel
\kappa
encodes the range of spatial interactions. Similarly to (
1.1
), the right-hand side models collisions between particles with position–velocity pairs
(x,v)
and
(x_{*},v_{*})
. The key feature distinguishing (
1.2
) from the classical Landau equation is that collisions are
delocalized
: particles at different spatial locations may interact whenever
\kappa(x-x_{*})>0
. Mathematically, (
1.2
) can be viewed as an intermediate model between (
1.1
) and the spatially homogeneous equation. Note that setting
\kappa=\delta_{0}
formally recovers (
1.1
), suggesting that the study of (
1.2
) may provide insight into the original equation.

The second variant we study is the
homogeneous multiespecies Landau equation
, which describes the evolution of
N
interacting particle species with the spatial dependence removed:

(1.3)

\partial_{t}f^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}(f^{(i)},f^{(j)}),

where the collision operator between species
i
and
j
is given by

q_{ij}(f,g):=\frac{c_{ij}}{m_{i}}\textnormal{div}_{v^{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Phi(v^{i}-v^{j}_{*})\left(\frac{\nabla_{v^{i}}}{m_{i}}-\frac{\nabla_{v^{j}_{*}}}{m_{j}}\right)\big(f(t,v^{i})g(t,v^{j}_{*})\big)\,dv^{j}_{*},

Here,
m_{i}>0
denotes the mass of species
i
, and
c_{ij}
is a physical constant given by

c_{ij}=\frac{|\log\Lambda_{c}|q_{i}^{2}q_{j}^{2}n_{i}n_{j}}{8\pi\varepsilon_{0}^{2}},

where
|\log\Lambda_{c}|
is the Coulomb logarithm, and
\varepsilon_{0}
is the permittivity of vacuum. Let us do a brief literature review on
eqs.

1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
as further motivation.

The Landau equation.
Several works establish local-in-time existence of solutions to the Landau equation for smooth or bounded initial data; see, for instance,
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
. However, global-in-time existence of classical solutions for general initial data remains a major open problem. Moreover, in connection with hydrodynamic limits and the recent works
[
46
,
47
]
, which exhibit implosion singularities for the compressible Euler equations, it is expectable that the Landau equation may also develop singular behavior. This phenomenon has recently been observed in
[
5
]
for
\gamma>\sqrt{3}
.

Global existence of solutions is currently known only in weaker settings. In particular, renormalized solutions exist globally in time
[
43
]
, while global smooth solutions have been established only under restrictive assumptions, such as initial data close to equilibrium
[
32
,
10
,
9
,
16
,
41
,
31
,
13
]
or near vacuum
[
45
,
12
]
. Another line of work establishes regularity and continuation criteria under uniform control of macroscopic quantities such as the local mass, energy, and entropy

M(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\,dv,\quad E(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f|v|^{2}\,dv,\quad H(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\log f\,dv.

The underlying idea in these works is that loss of regularity should be accompanied by a deterioration of these macroscopic quantities; see
[
27
,
37
,
50
,
8
]
for further details. We also mention
[
26
]
, which shows that solutions may be continued as long as they remain bounded.

In contrast, in the space-homogeneous setting, where the initial data depends only on the velocity variable
v
, the well-posedness theory is considerably more complete. Global-in-time existence of
\mathcal{H}
-solutions has been established in
[
52
,
15
,
2
,
56
,
22
]
. The formation of singularities was ruled out in
[
30
]
, where the Fisher information is shown to be monotone decreasing. This result has since been extended to increasingly rough initial data in
[
14
,
39
,
25
,
33
]
.

Delocalized kinetic equations and the fuzzy Landau equation.
The notion of nonlocal collisions in kinetic equations was first introduced by Morgenstern
[
48
]
in the context of the Maxwell–Boltzmann equation. Several related models were subsequently developed, including the Povzner equation
[
49
]
and the Enskog equation
[
4
,
3
,
51
]
. On the other hand, fuzzy kinetic models have been introduced more recently. These were first proposed for the Boltzmann equation in
[
20
,
21
]
, and later extended to the Landau equation in the series
[
18
,
19
,
17
]
. In this setting, the authors develop a variational formulation of (
1.2
), establish global existence of
\mathcal{H}
-solutions, and derive (
1.2
) as the grazing-collision limit of the fuzzy Boltzmann equation, thereby extending the classical results of
[
1
]
. Shortly after the introduction of
eq.

1.2
, global existence and uniqueness of smooth solutions for regular initial data were established in
[
28
]
for
-\sqrt{7}\leq\gamma\leq 0
.

The multispecies Landau equation.

The literature on the multiespecies Landau equation (
1.3
) remains limited. It was first analyzed in the inhomogeneous setting in
[
29
]
, where a spectral gap for the linearized operator was established, together with existence and uniqueness of solutions for smooth initial data close to equilibrium and exponential convergence to the steady state. In a related perturbative framework,
[
42
]
studied a two-species system in which one species is near vacuum and the other near equilibrium, obtaining instantaneous smoothing for the linearized equation.

For general initial data, existence results are currently available only in the homogeneous setting. In the recent preprint
[
40
]
, global existence of smooth solutions is established for interaction potentials in the range
-\sqrt{8}\leq\gamma<-2
, by extending the symmetrization method of
[
30
]
and proving monotonicity of the Fisher information. Independently, monotonicity of the Fisher information was obtained in
[
58
]
using a similar approach. These results are an important motivation for our second main theorem.

1.1.
Main results

Our main contribution is to extend the uniqueness results of
[
22
,
23
]
to the fuzzy Landau equation
eq.

1.2
and the multiespecies Landau equation
eq.

1.3
, establishing explicit stability bounds under suitable integrability assumptions. The main difficulty lies in treating weak solutions without regularity assumptions, thereby accommodating very rough initial data. We also present a novel analytic re-interpretation of the stochastic argument from
[
22
,
23
]
.

Our stability estimates rely on the
2
-Wasserstein distance, which for probability densities
f,g
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

d_{2}^{2}(f,g):=\inf_{\Pi\in\Gamma(f,g)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi(x,y),

where
\Gamma(f,g)
denotes the set of transport plans, i.e., probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
with marginals
f
and
g
. This distance metrizes the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of probability measures with finite second moments, endowed with the topology of weak convergence together with convergence of second moments. See
[
53
]
for background on optimal transport.

We use a weak formulation of
eqs.

1.2
and
1.3
, in which all derivatives are transferred onto test functions. To this end, we introduce

b(z):=\textnormal{div}_{z}\Phi(z)=-2|z|^{\gamma}z.

We begin with the fuzzy Landau equation.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
-3\leq\gamma\leq-2
,
q>\frac{3}{4+\gamma}
, and assume that
\kappa
is bounded. Consider a curve of probability densities
t\in[0,T]\mapsto f(t)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
with
m_{2}(f):=\sup_{[0,T]}\int(|x|^{2}+|v|^{2})f(t)<\infty
and such that
f\in L^{1}_{t}L^{q}_{v}L^{1}_{x}
. We say that
f
is a weak solution of the fuzzy Landau equation
eq.

1.2
if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\varphi f(t)\,dx\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\varphi f(0)\,dx\,dv

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left[-\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\right)+2(\kappa b*f)\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi+\left(\kappa\Phi*f\right):D_{v}^{2}\varphi\right]f\,dx\,dv\,ds,

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
and every
t\in[0,T]
.

We now state our first main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider
f,g
weak solutions of (
1.2
) for
-3\leq\gamma\leq-2
, and let
\sqrt{\kappa}
be bounded and Lipschitz. Assume that
f,g\in L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v}L^{1}_{x}
for
p=\frac{3}{3+\gamma}\in[3,\infty]
. Then, we have

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}d_{2}^{2}\big(f(t),g(t)\big)\leq\omega\left(d_{2}^{2}\big(f(0),g(0)\big)\right),

where
\omega:[0,\infty)\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
is continuous, increasing, and satisfies
\omega(0)=0
. This modulus of continuity
\omega
depends on
T
,
\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v}L^{1}_{x}}
,
\|g\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v}L^{1}_{x}}
, and
\|\sqrt{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
.

In particular, this establishes uniqueness within
L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v}L^{1}_{x}
. See outline of the proof for an explicit expression of
\omega
. We next extend the discussion to the multispecies Landau equation.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
-3\leq\gamma\leq 0
. Consider curves
{t\in[0,T]\mapsto f^{(i)}(t)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
of probability densities, with
m_{2}(f^{(i)}):=\sup_{[0,T]}\int|v^{i}|^{2}f^{(i)}(t)<\infty
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Further assume that, in the case
-3\leq\gamma<-1
, we have
f^{(i)}\in L^{1}_{t}L^{q}_{v^{i}}
for some
q>\frac{3}{4+\gamma}
. We say that
(f^{(i)})_{i=1}^{N}
is a weak solution of the multiespecies homogeneous Landau equation
eq.

1.3
if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi f^{(i)}(t)\,dv^{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi f^{(i)}(0)\,dv^{i}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{c_{ij}}{m_{i}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{m_{i}}\Phi*f^{(j)}\right):D_{v^{i}}^{2}\varphi+\left(\frac{1}{m_{i}}+\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)(b*f^{(j)})\cdot\nabla_{v^{i}}\varphi\right]f^{(i)}\,dv^{i}\,ds,

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and every
t\in[0,T]
.

Next, we present our second main result on the multispecies homogeneous Landau equation.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(f^{(i)})_{i=1}^{N}
,
(g^{(i)})_{i=1}^{N}
be weak solutions of (
1.3
) for
-3\leq\gamma\leq 0
. Assume that
f^{(i)},g^{(i)}\in L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v^{i}}
where
p=\frac{3}{3+\gamma}\in[1,\infty]
. Then there exists
\omega:[0,\infty)\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
continuous, increasing, with
\omega(0)=0
such that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{2}^{2}\big(f^{(i)}(t),g^{(i)}(t)\big)\leq\omega\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{2}^{2}\big(f^{(i)}(0),g^{(i)}(0)\big)\right).

The modulus of continuity
\omega
depends on
T
,
\gamma
,
N
,
\max_{ij}|c_{ij}|
,
\max_{i}|m_{i}|^{-1}
,
\max_{i}m_{2}(f^{(i)})
, and
\|f^{(i)}\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v^{i}}}
,
\|g^{(i)}\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v^{i}}}
.

See the outline of the proof for an explicit expression of
\omega
.

Comparison with existing results.

theorems

1.1
and
1.2
extend the conditional uniqueness results established in
[
22
,
23
]
for the homogeneous Landau equation. We note that in the non-Coulomb case, our result is slightly sharper, since we are able to reach the critical exponent
{p=\frac{3}{3+\gamma}}
, whereas
[
22
]
shows uniqueness in
L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v}
with
p>\frac{3}{3+\gamma}
. Finally, we note the related work
[
24
]
, which establishes an analogue of
theorem

1.1
for a delocalized version of the Boltzmann equation.

Regarding the fuzzy Landau equation, global existence and uniqueness of solutions with smooth initial data was established in
[
28
]
for moderately soft potentials
\gamma\in[-\sqrt{7},0]
. In contrast,
theorem

1.1
accommodates highly irregular initial data and covers the regime of very soft potentials
\gamma\in[-3,-2]
. In relation to the multiespecies equation, well posedness of smooth solutions was shown in
[
40
]
in the range
-\sqrt{8}\leq\gamma\leq 0
. On the other hand,
theorem

1.2
establishes uniqueness of weak solutions within
L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{v^{i}}
and applies to the full range of soft potentials
\gamma\in[-3,0]
.

It is worth noting that analogous conditional uniqueness results have been established for several well-known equations, including the
2
D Euler equation in vorticity form
[
57
]
, the Vlasov–Poisson system
[
44
]
, and the Patlak–Keller–Segel model
[
11
]
. In fact,
theorems

1.1
and
1.2
follow as direct consequences of a general conditional uniqueness result, which recovers these examples as particular cases within a unified framework. We refer to the outline below for further details.

1.2.
Outline of the proof

We present two approaches for estimating the 2-Wasserstein distance in
eqs.

1.2
and
1.3
. The first extends the method of
[
23
]
to a broader class of nonlinear equations with singular coefficients, reinterpreting the original stochastic argument in a more analytic language. The second employs the symmetrization technique introduced in
[
30
]
for the study of Fisher information.

First approach: Coupling method.
The key observation is that
eqs.

1.2
and
1.3
share a common structure. More precisely, both can be written as

(1.4)

\partial_{t}f=\textnormal{div}\left(cf+(b*f)f\right)+D^{2}:\left(Mf+(A*f)f\right),

where
c,b:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A,M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and we denote
{D^{2}:(Mf)=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}(M^{ij}f)}
. We prove
theorems

1.1
and
1.2
by showing a uniqueness criterion for the more general equation
eq.

1.4
under the following assumptions on the coefficients.

(A1)

The matrices
A
,
M
can be expressed as
{A=\sigma\sigma^{T}}
,
{M=\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{m}^{T}}
for some
\sigma,\mathfrak{m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

(A2)

Both
c
and
\mathfrak{m}
are globally Lipschitz.

(A3)

The variable
x
can be decomposed as
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k},x^{\prime})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-dk}
so that

|b(x)|\leq K\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x^{i}|^{-\alpha+1}\right),\qquad|\sigma(x)|\leq K\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x^{i}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}+1}\right),

where
K>0
and
0\leq\alpha\leq d
.

(A4)

The coefficients
b
,
\sigma
satisfy the following local Lipschitz estimate away from
x^{i}=0
:

\frac{|b(x)-b(y)|}{|x-y|}+\frac{|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\leq K\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(|x^{i}|^{-\alpha}+|y^{i}|^{-\alpha}\right)\right),

Our computations will require that the
x^{i}
marginals of
f
satisfy an integrability condition. We denote such marginals as

f^{(i)}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad f^{(i)}(x^{i}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-d}}f(x)\,dx^{-i},

where
dx^{-i}
denotes integration with respect to all variables except
x^{i}
. The notion of weak solution to (
1.4
) is a simple generalization of Definition
1.1
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
0\leq\alpha\leq d
. Consider a curve of probability densities
t\in[0,T]\mapsto f(t)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
m_{2}(f):=\sup_{[0,T]}\int|x|^{2}f(t)<\infty
. Further assume that, in the case
1<\alpha\leq d
, we have
f^{(i)}\in L^{1}_{t}L^{q}_{x^{i}}
for some
q>\frac{d}{d+1-\alpha}
. We say that
f
is a weak solution of (
1.4
) if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi f(t)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi f(0)\,dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\Big(c+(b*f)\Big)\cdot\nabla\varphi+\Big(M+(A*f)\Big):D^{2}\varphi\right]\,dx\,ds,

for any
0\leq t\leq T
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}
.

Now, given two solutions
f(t,x)
,
g(t,y)
of (
1.4
), we estimate
d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))
by constructing a curve of transport plans
\Pi(t)\in\Gamma(f(t),g(t))
, generated by a suitably chosen parabolic equation:

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\Pi=\textnormal{div}_{x,y}\left(\Big(\overline{c}+(\overline{b}*\Pi^{*})\Big)\Pi\right)+D^{2}_{x,y}:\left(\Big(\overline{M}+(\overline{A}*\Pi^{*})\Big)\Pi\right),\\
\Pi(0)=\Pi^{*}(0),\end{cases}

where
\Pi^{*}(t)\in\Gamma(f(t),g(t))
denotes the optimal transport plan and we introduced

(1.6)

\displaystyle\overline{c}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}c(x)\\
c(y)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad\overline{M}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{m}(x)\mathfrak{m}^{T}(x)&\mathfrak{m}(x)\mathfrak{m}^{T}(y)\\
\mathfrak{m}(y)\mathfrak{m}^{T}(x)&\mathfrak{m}(y)\mathfrak{m}^{T}(y)\end{pmatrix},

(1.7)

\displaystyle\overline{b}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}b(x)\\
b(y)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad\overline{A}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(x)\sigma^{T}(x)&\sigma(x)\sigma^{T}(y)\\
\sigma(y)\sigma^{T}(x)&\sigma(y)\sigma^{T}(y)\end{pmatrix}.

Once the existence of a measure valued solution to
eq.

1.5
has been established, we take the test function
{\varphi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}}
, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi(t)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi(t)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4n}}\left|\sigma(x-x_{*})-\sigma(y-y_{*})\right|^{2}\,d\Pi^{*}(t)\,d\Pi(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4n}}|x-y|\left|b(x-x_{*})-b(y-y_{*})\right|\,d\Pi^{*}(t)\,d\Pi(t).

Then the last two terms are estimated by splitting the integral into regions according to the size of
|x^{i}-x^{i}_{*}|
,
|y^{i}-y^{i}_{*}|
and using assumptions
A3
,
A4
. In the end, we essentially show

\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f^{(i)}(t)\|_{L^{p}_{x^{i}}},\|g^{(i)}(t)\|_{L^{p}_{y^{i}}},m_{2}(f),m_{2}(g),1)\Psi\big(d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))\big),

where
p=\frac{d}{d-\alpha}\in[1,\infty]
;
C
depends on
\alpha
,
d
and the constants from assumptions
A2
-
A4
; and

\Psi:[0,+\infty)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty),\qquad\Psi(x):=\max(-x\log x,x)=\begin{cases}-x\log x,&x<e^{-1},\\
x,&x\geq e^{-1}.\end{cases}

This implies uniqueness due to Osgood’s criterion. Moreover, we can explicitly solve the differential inequality to obtain the bound

(1.8)

d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))\leq H\left(d_{2}^{2}(f(0),g(0)),C\int_{0}^{t}\max_{i}(\|f^{(i)}(t)\|_{L^{p}_{x^{i}}},\|g^{(i)}(t)\|_{L^{p}_{y^{i}}},m_{2}(f),m_{2}(g),1)\,ds\right),

where
H(x,y)\geq 0
is the continuous function with
H(0,\cdot)\equiv 0
given by

(1.9)

H(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}x^{e^{-y}},&0\leq x\leq e^{-1},&x\leq e^{-e^{y}},\\
\frac{e^{y-1}}{-\ln x},&0\leq x\leq e^{-1},&x\geq e^{-e^{y}},\\
xe^{y},&e^{-1}\leq x,&x\geq e^{y-1},\\
e^{-\frac{1}{x}e^{-y-1}},&e^{-1}\leq x,&x\leq e^{y-1}.\end{array}\right.

In particular, if we choose the modulus of continuity

(1.10)

\omega(x):=H\Big(x,C\max_{i}\big\{\|f^{(i)}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{x^{i}}},\|g^{(i)}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{x^{i}}},m_{2}(f),m_{2}(g),T\big\}\Big),

then we have the following general result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider
0\leq\alpha\leq d
. Let
f
,
g
be weak solutions to (
1.4
) with
f^{(i)},g^{(i)}\in L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{x^{i}}
for
p=\frac{d}{d-\alpha}\in[1,\infty]
. Then we have

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}d_{2}^{2}\big(f(t),g(t)\big)\leq\omega\left(d_{2}^{2}\big(f(0),g(0)\big)\right),

where the modulus of continuity
\omega
is defined in
eq.

1.10
.

Note that when
b
and
\sigma
are globally Lipschitz we obtain unconditional uniqueness of
eq.

1.4
. This is a particular case of a more general result on nonlinear Fokker-Planck equations. See, for instance,
[
55
]
for a general statement.

Application to fuzzy Landau and multiespecies Landau.

theorems

1.1
and
1.2
are obtained as simple corollaries of the previous result. For the fuzzy Landau equation we have
n=6
,
d=3
,
k=1
and replace
(x^{1},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
by
(v,x)
. Moreover, the role of
c
is taken by
(-v,\mathbf{0})
, we set
M\equiv\mathbf{0}
,
b
becomes
(\mathbf{0},-4\kappa(x)v|v|^{-\gamma})
, and
A
is replaced by a
6\times 6
matrix with all zeroes except for the block
\kappa(x)|v|^{2+\gamma}\mathbb{P}(v)
corresponding to the operator
D^{2}_{v}
. For the choice of
\sigma
, it is convenient to note that
\mathbb{P}(z)=r(z)r(z)^{\perp}
, where

r(z):=|z|^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}z_{2}&-z_{3}&0\\
-z_{1}&0&z_{3}\\
0&z_{1}&-z_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then, it is not hard to see that in this particular example

\displaystyle\left|\sigma(x,v)-\sigma(y,w)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\gamma}(\|\sqrt{\kappa}\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}+1)\left(|x-y|+|v-w|\right)\left(|v|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}+|v|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}+1}+|w|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}+|w|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}+1}\right),

\displaystyle\left|b(x,v)-b(y,w)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\gamma}(\|\kappa\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}+1)\left(|x-y|+|v-w|\right)\left(|v|^{\gamma}+|v|^{\gamma+1}+|w|^{\gamma}+|w|^{\gamma+1}\right).

So we verify assumptions
A1
-
A4
when
-3\leq\gamma\leq-2
with
\alpha=-\gamma
.

For the multiespecies Landau model we consider the equation satisfied by the tensor product
F:=f^{(1)}(t,v^{1})\cdots f^{(N)}(t,v^{N})
, which takes the form (
1.4
) with
c\equiv\mathbf{0}
,

, and

-4\sum_{j=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{c_{1j}}{m_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{m_{1}}+\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)v^{j}|v^{j}|^{-\gamma}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{c_{Nj}}{m_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{m_{1}}+\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)v^{j}|v^{j}|^{-\gamma}\end{pmatrix},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{c_{1j}}{m_{j}m_{1}}|v^{j}|^{2+\gamma}\mathbb{P}(v^{j})&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&\frac{c_{Nj}}{m_{j}m_{N}}|v^{j}|^{2+\gamma}\mathbb{P}(v^{j})\end{pmatrix}.

The verification of the hypothesis is a simple calculus exercise. Finally, it is not hard to see that if

is a weak solution of the fuzzy Landau equation then

is a weak solution of

1.4

theorem

1.3

Other models included in the previous framework.
First we note that
theorem

1.3
can be extended to a broader class of equations. For instance, one may allow the coefficients
c
,
M
,
b
, and
A
to depend on time, replace the convolution terms by more general interactions of the form
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b(x,x_{*})f(x_{*})\,dx_{*}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(x,x_{*})f(x_{*})\,dx_{*}
, or consider the equation on a domain with boundary conditions that preserve mass. This allows us to recover a variety of models as particular cases of

1.4
. Let us briefly list some relevant examples.

The 2D incompressible Euler in vorticity form
models the evolution of the vorticity of a two dimensional inviscid fluid. The equation in the whole plane takes the form

(1.11)

\partial_{t}\omega_{t}+\textnormal{div}(u_{t}\omega_{t})=0,

where
u_{t}
can be expressed using Biot-Savart law as

u_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{\textnormal{BS}}(x-x_{*})\omega(x_{*})\,dx_{*},\qquad K_{\textnormal{BS}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi|x|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-x_{2}\\
x_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, if we assume that vorticity is nonnegative, bounded and integrable then
theorem

1.3
shows uniqueness within the class of solutions satisfying these properties. Uniqueness of bounded solutions to
eq.

1.11
is a classical result which was proven in
[
57
]
using different methods.

The Vlasov-Poisson equation
models the evolution of a distribution of charged particles which interact through the Coulomb force by neglecting collisional effects. The equation takes the form

\partial_{t}f_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+E\cdot\nabla_{v}f=0.

When considering the whole
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the electromagnetic force can be expressed as

E(x)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|^{3}}\rho(x_{*})\,dx_{*},\qquad\rho(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,v)\,dv.

We note that this has exactly the same form as the drift term in the Landau equation. In this setting, uniqueness of bounded solutions was shown in
[
44
]
also using optimal transportation techniques. In fact, the method we present can be seen as an extension of the one used in
[
44
]
. In addition,
theorem

1.3
also applies to the two species Vlasov-Poisson system

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f^{+}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{+}+E\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{+}=0,\\
\partial_{t}f^{-}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{-}-E\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{-}=0,\\
E=\displaystyle{-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|^{3}}\left(\rho^{+}(x_{*})-\rho^{-}(x_{*})\right)\,dx_{*}},\end{cases}\quad\rho^{\pm}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f^{\pm}(x,v)\,dv.

which models the evolution of electrons and ions. To the best of our knowledge, the uniqueness of bounded solutions for the previous two species system has not appeared explicitly in the literature, although it definitely seems expected.

The Patlak-Keller-Segel equation
models the evolution of a population of cells which move due to diffusion and to a chemical attractant. It takes the form

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+\textnormal{div}\left(f\nabla u\right)=\Delta f,\\
-\Delta u+\alpha u=f.\end{cases}

Note that
\nabla u
can be written as
\nabla\Phi*f
, where
\Phi
is the fundamental solution of
-\Delta u+\alpha u
. Since
\Phi
behaves like the fundamental solution of
-\Delta
near
0
, we fall within the assumptions
A1
-
A4
and
theorem

1.3
applies. Uniqueness of bounded solutions for these type of equations was proven in
[
11
]
, and the method we present may be viewed as an extension of the one used in
[
11
]
. Similarly to the Vlasov-Poisson equation
theorem

1.3
would also imply uniqueness of bounded solutions for a multiespecies version of Patlak-Keller-Segel and to Patlak-Keller-Segel on bounded domains with a boundary condition preserving mass. To our knowledge, these extensions of the uniqueness result of
[
11
]
have not appeared in the literature, although they seem expected.

Second approach: Symmetrization method.
We build on the symmetrization technique recently introduced in
[
30
]
, where Fisher information was shown to decrease for the homogeneous Landau equation, leading to a resolution of its well-posedness theory. The key insight is that the derivative of Fisher information can be reformulated in terms of a linear parabolic equation, referred to as the lifted equation. Notably, the
2
-Wasserstein distance admits an analogous lifting property, suggesting the natural question of whether it also exhibits monotonicity properties.

This idea was developed in
[
7
]
in collaboration with Delgadino, Gualdani, and Taskovic. Monotonicity was not obtained due to the absence of a suitable functional inequality. Nonetheless, the method provides a new proof of the conditional uniqueness criterion from
[
22
,
23
]
. In Section
3
we adapt the approach to the fuzzy Landau and the multiespecies Landau equations. Let us now outline the strategy in the case of the homogeneous Landau equation as presented in
[
7
]
, which also captures the main ideas we use for
eqs.

1.2
and
1.3
.

Consider two solutions
f(t,v),g(t,v)
of the homogeneous Landau equation. The main observation is that the time derivative of the
2
-Wasserstein distance
d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))
may be expressed as the derivative of
d_{2}^{2}
along the flow of a linear parabolic equation in double variables. More precisely,

\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))=\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{d}{d\tau}\right|_{\tau=0}d_{2}^{2}(\tilde{F}(\tau),\tilde{Q}(\tau)),

where
\tilde{F}
,
\tilde{G}
are solutions to linear equations in
\mathbb{R}^{6}

\begin{cases}\partial_{\tau}\tilde{F}=Q(\tilde{F})\\
\tilde{F}(0,v,v_{*})=f(t,v)f(t,v_{*})\end{cases},\qquad\begin{cases}\partial_{\tau}\tilde{G}=Q(\tilde{G})\\
\tilde{G}(0,v,v_{*})=g(t,v)g(t,v_{*})\end{cases},

where
Q
is an appropriate elliptic operator on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Therefore, estimating the growth of
d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))
reduces to the study of a linear model. Then the approach to estimate
\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f(t),g(t))
is to replicate the following computation for the heat equation taking into account the structure of
Q
.

Proposition
.

Let
f
and
g
be two nonnegative, unit-mass solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the heat equation
\partial_{t}f=\Delta f
,
\partial_{t}g=\Delta g
. Then
d_{2}^{2}(f,g)
is non-increasing with dissipation

\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f,g)=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{2}u\right|^{2}\rho(x)\,dx\,ds,

where
u
is an optimal solution of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (
1.12
), and
\rho(s,x)
,
0\leq s\leq 1
, denotes the
2
-Wasserstein geodesic joining
f
and
g
, characterized by (
1.13
).

Proof.

We use the dual formulation of the
2
-Wasserstein distance in terms of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation

(1.12)

d_{2}^{2}(f,g)=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(1,x)g(x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(0,x)f(x)\,dx:\partial_{s}u+\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}=0\right\}.

It is well known that an optimal potential
u
generates the
2
-Wasserstein geodesic

(\rho(s,x))_{0\leq s\leq 1}
connecting
f
and
g
through the continuity equation

(1.13)

\partial_{s}\rho+\textnormal{div}(\rho\nabla u)=0,\qquad\rho(0,\cdot)=f,\;\rho(1,\cdot)=g.

See
[
53
]
for more details on this formulation. Differentiating
d_{2}^{2}(f,g)
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(1)\Delta g\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(0)\Delta f\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Delta u\rho\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}-\nabla\Delta u\cdot\nabla u\right)\rho\,dx\,ds=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{2}u\right|^{2}\rho\,dx\,ds,

where we have used Bochner’s formula
\frac{1}{2}\Delta\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}=\nabla\Delta u\cdot\nabla u+\left|D^{2}u\right|^{2}
.
∎

We note that this approach does not cover the Coulomb case
\gamma=-3
. In addition, a rigorous justification of the method requires the function
u
to possess three derivatives. Such regularity can only be ensured when
f
and
g
are of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and locally bounded away from zero. These drawbacks led us to adopt the first approach that we previously described. Nevertheless, we include this symmetrization method as a formal alternative for deriving comparable estimates.

\displaystyle\;C|x-y|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigg[\int_{|z^{i}-x^{i}|\geq\varepsilon}|z^{i}-x^{i}|^{-\alpha}f_{t}^{(i)}\,dz^{i}+\int_{|z^{i}-y^{i}|\geq\varepsilon}|z^{i}-y^{i}|^{-\alpha}f_{t}^{(i)}\,dz^{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;C\left(1-\log\varepsilon\right)\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)|x-y|^{2}.

Putting all the regions together, we have

\int\left|\sigma(x-z)-\sigma(y-z)\right|^{2}f_{t}(z)\,dz\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\left[\left(1-\log\varepsilon\right)|x-y|^{2}+\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d}}\right].

Now we simply pick
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d}}=\min(e^{-1},|x-y|^{2})
.
∎

The proof of estimate
eq.

2.2
from
lemma

2.2
follows the same argument, so we omit it. The last estimate is a bit more involved, so we present its proof separately.

Proof of estimate
eq.

2.3
from
lemma

2.2
.

We must show that for
\alpha\geq 2
we have

\int\left|b(x-x_{*})-b(y-y_{*})\right||x-y|\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}\,d\Pi_{t}\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},\|g_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\Psi\left(\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi_{t}\right).

To simplify notation, we define

I(x,y,x_{*},y_{*}):=|x-y|\left|b(x-x_{*})-b(y-y_{*})\right|.

As before, we split the integral into regions depending on
0<\varepsilon<1
, which is suitably chosen later and omit the
+1
term in the assumptions for simplicity.

Region 1:
R_{1}:=\left\{\min_{i}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|\leq\varepsilon\right\}
.
By symmetry, we can reduce it to
R_{1}=\left\{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|\leq\varepsilon\right\}
, where assumption
A3
shows

\displaystyle\int_{R_{1}}I\,d\Pi_{t}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle+C\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int|x-y|\left(\int\mathds{1}_{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|<\varepsilon}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha+1}f_{t}\,dx_{*}\right)\,d\Pi_{t}

(2.7)

\displaystyle+C\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int|x-y|\left(\int\mathds{1}_{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|<\varepsilon}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha+1}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}\right)\,d\Pi_{t}.

Apply Hölder’s inequality twice with exponents
\left(\frac{2d}{2d-1},2d\right)
,
\left(p=\frac{d}{d-\alpha},\frac{d}{\alpha}\right)
and use
eq.

2.4
:

\displaystyle\int\mathds{1}_{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|<\varepsilon}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha+1}f_{t}\,dx_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\mathds{1}_{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|<\varepsilon}f_{t}^{(1)}\,dx_{*}^{1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}\left(\int|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-\frac{2d(\alpha-1)}{2d-1}}f_{t}^{(i)}\,dx_{*}^{i}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2d}}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{\alpha}{2d}}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1).

The third term
eq.

2.7
is estimated the same way:

\displaystyle\int\mathds{1}_{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|<\varepsilon}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha+1}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\mathds{1}_{|x^{1}-x_{*}^{1}|<\varepsilon}f_{t}^{(1)}\,dx_{*}^{1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}\left(\int|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|^{-\frac{2d(\alpha-1)}{2d-1}}g_{t}^{(i)}\,dy_{*}^{i}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2d}}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{\alpha}{2d}}\max(\|f_{t}^{(1)}\|_{L^{p}},\|g_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1).

Keeping in mind
\alpha\geq 2
, this yields

\int_{R_{1}}I\,d\Pi_{t}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d}}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},\|g_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\left(\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi_{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Region 2:
R_{2}:=\left\{\min_{i}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|\leq\varepsilon\right\}
.
Is symmetric to the previous case.

Region 3:
R_{3}=\left\{\min_{i}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon,\min_{i}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon\right\}
.
Here we use assumption
A4
, which gives

I\leq C\left(|x-y|^{2}+|x_{*}-y_{*}|^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha}\right),

yielding

\displaystyle\int_{R_{3}}I\,d\Pi_{t}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq C\int|x-y|^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha}f_{t}^{(i)}\,dx_{*}^{i}\right)\,d\Pi_{t}

\displaystyle+C\int|x-y|^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha}g_{t}^{(i)}\,dy_{*}^{i}\right)\,d\Pi_{t}

\displaystyle+C\int|x_{*}-y_{*}|^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon}|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha}f_{t}^{(i)}\,dx^{i}\right)\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle+C\int|x_{*}-y_{*}|^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon}|y^{i}-y_{*}^{i}|^{-\alpha}g_{t}^{(i)}\,dy^{i}\right)\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}.

Now
eq.

2.6
shows

\int_{R_{3}}I\,d\Pi_{t}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}\leq C(1-\log\varepsilon)\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},\|g_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi_{t}.

All in all, we have

\int I\,d\Pi_{t}\,d\Pi_{t}^{*}\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},\|g_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\left((1-\log\varepsilon)\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi_{t}+\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d}}\left(\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi_{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Now we may choose
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d}}=\min\left(e^{-1},\int|x-y|^{2}\,d\Pi_{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
∎

2.3.
Existence of the curve of transport plans

In this section we prove
lemma

2.1
in the case
2\leq\alpha\leq d
. That is, the existence of a curve of transport plans
t\mapsto\Pi_{t}\in\Gamma(f_{t},g_{t})
such that
\Pi_{0}=\Pi_{0}^{*}
and

(2.8)

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi\,d\Pi_{t}=\int\left[\Big(\overline{c}+(\overline{b}*\Pi_{t}^{*})\Big)\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\varphi+\Big(\overline{M}+(\overline{A}*\Pi_{t}^{*})\Big):D_{x,y}^{2}\varphi\right]\,d\Pi_{t},

where
\Pi^{*}_{t}\in\Gamma(f_{t},g_{t})
is the optimal transport plan. Several elements of this proof already appeared in
[
22
,
23
]
but we include all the details for the sake of completeness. We use that
eq.

2.8
has a probabilistic counterpart given by the pair of stochastic differential equations

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}c(X_{s})\,ds+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{m}(X_{s})\,dB_{s}

(2.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b(X_{s}-x_{*})f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})\,W(dx_{*},dy_{*},ds),

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}c(Y_{s})\,ds+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{m}(Y_{s})\,dB_{s}

(2.10)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b(Y_{s}-y_{*})g_{s}(y_{*})\,dy_{*}\,ds+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sigma(Y_{s}-y_{*})\,W(dx_{*},dy_{*},ds),

where
B
is an
n
-dimensional Brownian motion, the initial data are chosen with

\textnormal{Law}(X_{0})=f_{0},\quad\textnormal{Law}(Y_{0})=g_{0},\quad\textnormal{Law}(X_{0},Y_{0})=\Pi_{0}^{*},

and
W(dx,dy,dt)=(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})(dx,dy,dt)
is a 3D white noise on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]
independent from
B
with covariance measure
d\Pi_{t}^{*}(x,y)\,dt
. See
[
54
]
for details and definitions on white noise. Most importantly, this means that for any test functions
\varphi(x,y,t)
,
\psi(x,y,t)
, the processes

(\xi_{t}^{i})_{i=1}^{d}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x,y,s)\,W(dx,dy,ds),\quad(\zeta_{t}^{i})_{i=1}^{d}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(x,y,s)\,W(dx,dy,ds)

are square integrable martingales with cross variation structure

\left\langle\xi^{i},\zeta^{j}\right\rangle_{t}=\delta_{ij}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x,y,s)\psi(x,y,s)\,d\Pi_{s}^{*}(x,y)\,ds,

which is all the information required to perform the subsequent computations. Now a simple application of Itô’s formula for a test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
shows that if
X_{t}
,
Y_{t}
are solutions of
eqs.

2.9
and
2.10
then
\Pi_{t}=\textnormal{Law}(X_{t},Y_{t})
is a weak solution of
eq.

2.8
. Therefore, since both equations are analogous, the problem reduces to showing that
eq.

2.9
has a solution and that
\textnormal{Law}(X_{t})=f_{t}
. We divide the proof into various steps. One of the main goals is showing existence of strong solutions through Picard iterations on the mapping

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle:L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\longrightarrow L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right),

\displaystyle\Phi(X)_{t}

\displaystyle:=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}c(X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b(X_{s}-x_{*})f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{m}(X_{s})\,dB_{s}+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})\,W(dx_{*},dy_{*},ds),

where
\Omega
denotes the probability space.

Step 1. Mapping
\Phi
is well defined.
We verify that
\sup_{[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Phi(X)_{t}\right|^{2}\right]<\infty
. Using the global Lipschitz assumption
A2
on
c
,
\mathfrak{m}
shows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Phi(X)_{t}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{0}|^{2}\right]+(t^{2}+t)C\left(1+\sup_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}|^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+C\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int\left|b(X_{s}-x_{*})\right|f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds\right)^{2}\right]+C\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int|\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})|^{2}f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds\right].

We bound the remaining two terms using assumption
A3
and
eq.

2.4
, which show

\int\left|b(X_{s}-x_{*})\right|f_{s}(x_{*}),\int|\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})|^{2}f_{s}(x_{*})\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1),

which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Phi(X)_{t}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{0}|^{2}\right]+C(t^{2}+t)\left(1+\sup_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}|^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{T}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\,ds+C\left(\int_{0}^{T}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\,ds\right)^{2},

showing that
\Phi
is well defined.

Step 2. Continuity estimate on
\Phi
.
Given
X,\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
we estimate

\displaystyle\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi(\tilde{X})_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(c(X_{s})-c(\tilde{X}_{s})\right)\,ds+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mathfrak{m}(X_{s})-\mathfrak{m}(\tilde{X}_{s})\right)\,dB_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int\left(b(X_{s}-x_{*})-b(\tilde{X}_{s}-x_{*})\right)f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int\left(\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})-\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})\right)W(dx_{*},dy_{*},ds).

Using Itô’s formula gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi(\tilde{X})_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(c(X_{t})-c(\tilde{X}_{t})\right)\left(\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi(\tilde{X}_{t})\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathfrak{m}(X_{t})-\mathfrak{m}(\tilde{X}_{t})|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi(\tilde{X})_{t}\right)\cdot\int\left(b(X_{t}-x_{*})-b(\tilde{X}_{t}-x_{*})\right)f_{t}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\int|\sigma(X_{t}-x_{*})-\sigma(\tilde{X}_{t}-x_{*})|^{2}f(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\right].

lemma

2.2
bounds the last two terms:

\displaystyle\int|b(X_{t}-x_{*})-b(\tilde{X}_{t}-x_{*})|f_{t}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\Psi\left(|X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|\right),

\displaystyle\int|\sigma(X_{t}-x_{*})-\sigma(\tilde{X}_{t}-x_{*})|^{2}f(x_{*})\,dx_{*}

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\Psi\left(|X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|^{2}\right).

Also note that, since
\Psi
is increasing and satisfies
a\Psi(a)\leq\Psi(a^{2})
we have for any
a,b\geq 0

a\Psi(b)\leq\max\left\{a\Psi(a),b\Psi(b)\right\}\leq\Psi(a^{2})+\Psi(b^{2}).

Putting all of this together yields

(2.11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi(\tilde{X})_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;C\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\left[\Psi\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi(\tilde{X}_{t})|^{2}\right]\right)+\Psi\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]\right)\right].

Step 3. Continuity of
\Phi
.
The differential inequality
eq.

2.11
suggests that we should have continuity of
\Phi:L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\to L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
. This turns out to be true, although we do not prove it using
eq.

2.11
. Note hat the log-Lipschitz modulus of continuity
\Psi
shows only due to the non-integrability of
|x^{i}-x_{*}^{i}|^{-d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which appears after applying Hölder’s inequality with exponents
\frac{d}{d-\alpha}
,
\frac{d}{\alpha}
on assumption
A4
. Therefore, if we consider a cut-off function

\Theta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\quad 0\leq\Theta\leq 1,\quad\Theta|_{[-1,1]}\equiv 0,\quad\Theta|_{[-2,2]^{c}}\equiv 1,\quad\Theta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\Theta(\varepsilon^{-1}x),

and define a regularized map
\Phi_{\varepsilon}:L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\to L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
where
b(x)
,
\sigma(x)
are replaced by
\Theta_{\varepsilon}(x)b(x)
,
\Theta_{\varepsilon}(x)\sigma(x)
, then we would obtain an estimate of the form

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Phi_{\varepsilon}(X)_{t}-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{X})_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq C_{\varepsilon}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{t}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\Phi_{\varepsilon}(X)_{t}-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{X}_{t})|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]\right),

where
C_{\varepsilon}\nearrow\infty
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Here Gronwall’s inequality clearly implies the continuity of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
since

\mathbb{E}\left[|\Phi_{\varepsilon}(X)_{t}-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{X})_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq M_{\varepsilon}e^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sup_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}|^{2}\right],\quad M_{\varepsilon}:=C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\,ds.

Now we show uniform convergence
\Phi_{\varepsilon}\to\Phi
using Itô’s formula, assumption
A3
and
eq.

2.5

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Phi(X)_{t}-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(X)_{t}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|X_{s}-x_{*}|<2\varepsilon}\left|b(X_{s}-x_{*})\right|f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|X_{s}-x_{*}|<2\varepsilon}\left|\sigma(X_{s}-x_{*})\right|^{2}f_{s}(x_{*})\,dx_{*}\,ds\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\,ds\right)^{2}\right)\varepsilon^{2}.

This proves the continuity of
\Phi:L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\to L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
.

Step 4. Existence of solutions through Picard iterations.
First, we restrict the time interval to ensure that iterations are uniformly bounded. In step 1 we proved an inequality of the form

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Phi(X)_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C_{1}+tC_{2}\sup_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}|^{2}\right],

where both
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
depend on the constants from the assumptions and
C_{1}
additionally depends on
\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{0}|^{2}\right]
,
\|f^{(i)}\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{p}_{x^{i}}}
. Therefore, if we set

\mathcal{B}_{R}:=\left\{X\in L^{\infty}\left([0,\tau];L^{2}(\Omega)\right):\sup_{[0,\tau]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq R\right\},\;\tau=\frac{1}{2C_{2}},\quad R=2C_{1}

then
\Phi:\mathcal{B}_{R}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{R}
, which ensures that the following Picard iterates are uniformly bounded:

X_{t}^{(0)}=X_{0},\quad X_{t}^{(m+1)}=\Phi(X^{(m)})_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq\tau.

If we now define
\rho_{m,\ell}(t)=\sup_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{s}^{(m+\ell)}-X_{s}^{(m)}\right|^{2}\right]
, step 2 implies

\rho_{m+1,\ell}(t)\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\left[\Psi\left(\rho_{m+1,\ell}(s)\right)+\Psi\left(\rho_{m,\ell}(s)\right)\right]\,ds.

Now, setting
\rho_{m}(t)=\sup_{\ell}\rho_{m,\ell}(t)
,
\rho(t)=\limsup_{m}\rho_{m}(t)
, we conclude that

\rho(t)\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\Psi\left(\rho(s)\right)\,ds,

which implies that
\rho(t)\equiv 0
and thus

\lim_{m}\sup_{\ell}\sup_{[0,\tau]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(m+\ell)}-X_{t}^{(m)}|^{2}\right]=0,

which precisely means that
\left(X^{(m)}\right)_{m}
is Cauchy in
L^{\infty}\left([0,\tau];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, so there exists a process
{X\in L^{\infty}\left([0,\tau];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}
such that

\lim_{m}\sup_{[0,\tau]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}-X_{t}^{(m)}|^{2}\right]=0.

Moreover, since
\Phi
is continuous with respect to
L^{\infty}\left([0,\tau];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, we may let
m\to\infty
in
X^{(m+1)}=\Phi(X^{(m)})
to conclude that
X
is a strong solution of
eq.

2.9
in the interval
[0,\tau]
. Finally, note that the choice of
\tau
only depends on the constants from our assumptions, so we may choose
X_{\tau}
as new initial data and iteratively extend the solution to the whole time interval
[0,T]
.

Step 5. Uniqueness of solutions to
eq.

2.9
.
Assume we have two solutions
X,\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
with
X_{t}=\Phi(X)_{t}
,
\tilde{X}_{t}=\Phi(\tilde{X})_{t}
. Then
eq.

2.11
becomes

\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq\int_{0}^{t}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(\|f_{s}^{(i)}\|_{L^{p}},1)\Psi\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}|^{2}\right]\right)\,ds,

which implies that
\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]=0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Step 6. Showing that
\textnormal{Law}(X_{t})=f_{t}
.
A simple application of Itô’s formula shows that
\mu_{t}:=\textnormal{Law}(X_{t})
is a weak solution of

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\textnormal{div}_{x}\left(c\mu_{t}+(b*f_{t})\mu_{t}\right)+D_{x}^{2}:\left(M\mu_{t}+(A_{t}*f_{t})\mu_{t}\right),

which is essentially
eq.

1.4
but with the coefficients frozen to remove the nonlinearity. By definition, we have that
f_{t}
,
\mu_{t}
solve the same linear equation with the same coefficients and the same initial condition. Then, we would immediately have
\mu_{t}=f_{t}
if we are able to apply
[
38
, Theorem B.1]
or
[
6
, Theorems 4.1 and 5.1]
, which provide a uniqueness criterion for weak solutions of

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{*}\mu_{t},

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}^{*}
denotes the formal adjoint of a differential operator. We must verify the following:

(1)

There is a common domain
\mathcal{D}
for
\mathcal{L}_{t}
which is a dense sub-algebra of
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and such that
\mathcal{L}_{t}:\mathcal{D}\longrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(2)

For each
t
,
\mathcal{L}_{t}
satisfies the maximum principle.

(3)

There is a countable subset
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{D}
such that, for any
f\in\mathcal{D}
and
t\geq 0
, there is
\{f_{m}\}_{m}\subset\mathcal{D}_{0}
with
\left\{(f_{m},\mathcal{L}_{t}f_{m})\right\}_{m}
uniformly bounded and converging pointwisely to
(f,\mathcal{L}_{t}f)
.

(4)

The martingale problem associated with
(\mathcal{L}_{t},\delta_{x_{0}})
is well posed for each
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. That is, there is a process such that
X_{0}=x_{0}
and for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
we have that

(2.12)

\varphi\left(X_{t}\right)-\int_{0}^{t}(\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi)(X_{s})\,ds

is a margingale. Moreover, solutions are unique in law.

Point (2) is immediate from the form of the operator

\mathcal{L}_{t}\varphi=\left(c+b*f_{t}\right)\cdot\nabla\varphi+\left(M+A_{t}*f_{t}\right):D^{2}\varphi.

We have already shown (4) as well, since solutions to
eq.

2.12
are represented by solutions of
eq.

2.9
, for which we already have path-wise uniqueness, which implies uniqueness in law. As for (1), from assumption
A3
we have

(2.13)

3.1.
Fuzzy Landau equation

In this section we derive the following result, which can be proven rigorously using techniques from Section
2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f,g
be weak solutions of the fuzzy Landau equation for
-3<\gamma\leq-2
with bounded second moments. Let
p>\frac{3}{3+\gamma}
and assume that
f,g\in L^{\alpha_{p,\gamma}}_{t}L^{p}_{v}L^{1}_{x}
. That is, we have

\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|\tilde{f}_{t}\|_{L_{v}^{p}}^{\alpha_{p,\gamma}}+\|\tilde{g}_{t}\|_{L_{v}^{p}}^{\alpha_{p,\gamma}}\right)\,dt<\infty,\qquad\tilde{f}(v):=\int f(x,v)\,dx,\quad\alpha_{p,\gamma}:=-\frac{\gamma p}{3(p-1)}\in(0,1).

Then we have

d_{2}^{2}(f_{t},g_{t})\leq d_{2}^{2}(f_{0},g_{0})\exp\left\{C\int_{0}^{t}\max(\|\tilde{f}_{t}\|_{L_{v}^{p}}^{\alpha_{p,\gamma}},\|\tilde{g}_{t}\|_{L_{v}^{p}}^{\alpha_{p,\gamma}},1)\,dt\right\},

where
C>0
depends on
\gamma
and
\|\kappa\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}
.

We use the formulation of the
2
-Wasserstein distance in terms of a Hamilton-Jacobi equation:

(3.1)

d_{2}^{2}(f,g)=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{1}(v)g(v)\,dv-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{0}(v)f(v)\,dv:\partial_{s}u_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla u_{s}\right|^{2}=0\right\}.

It is well known that an optimal
u
generates the
2
-Wasserstein geodesic
(\rho_{s})_{0\leq s\leq 1}
connecting
f
and
g
through the continuity equation

(3.2)

\partial_{s}\rho_{s}+\textnormal{div}(\rho_{s}\nabla u_{s})=0,\qquad\rho_{0}=f,\;\rho_{1}=g.

See
[
53
]
for more details on this formulation. Let us denote the collision operator of the fuzzy Landau equation as
q
:

q(f)(x,v)=\textnormal{div}_{v}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\kappa(x-x_{*})\Phi(v-v_{*})(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{v_{*}})\big(f(x,v)f(x_{*},v_{*})\big)\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}.

Simply differentiating in
eq.

3.1
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\int u_{1}\partial_{t}g\,dx\,dv-\int u_{0}\partial_{t}f\,dx\,dv

(3.3)

\displaystyle=-\int u_{1}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}g\right)\,dx\,dv+\int u_{0}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\right)\,dx\,dv

(3.4)

\displaystyle+\int u_{1}q(g)\,dx\,dv-\int u_{0}q(f)\,dx\,dv.

The contribution (
3.3
) coming from the transport term can be controlled directly in terms of the Wasserstein distance. To see this, recall that the Hopf–Lax formula for the Hamilton-Jacobi equation (see Section 5.4.6 of
[
53
]
) gives

u_{0}(x,v)=\phi(x,v)-\frac{|x|^{2}+|v|^{2}}{2},\qquad u_{1}(x,v)=\frac{|x|^{2}+|v|^{2}}{2}-\psi(x,v),

where
\psi=\phi^{*}
is the Legendre transform of
\phi
, which provides the inverse optimal transport map
(\nabla\phi)^{-1}=\nabla\psi
. Then, using the change of variables
(y,w)=\nabla_{x,v}\phi
gives

\displaystyle(\ref{eq:transport_term})

\displaystyle=\int w\cdot\left(y-\nabla_{y}\phi\right)g\,dy\,dw+\int v\cdot\left(x-\nabla_{x}\phi\right)f\,dx\,dv

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{v}\phi\cdot\left(\nabla_{x}\phi-x\right)f\,dx\,dv+\int v\cdot\left(x-\nabla_{x}\phi\right)f\,dx\,dv

\displaystyle=\int\left(\nabla_{x}\phi-x\right)\left(\nabla_{v}\phi-v\right)\,f\,dx\,dv\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{2}^{2}(f,g).

All in all, we have

\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f,g)\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{2}^{2}(f,g)+\int u_{1}q(g)\,dx\,dv-\int u_{0}q(f)\,dx\,dv.

Therefore, we may assume that
f
and
g
satisfy a transport free fuzzy Landau equation

(3.5)

\partial_{t}f=q(f),\qquad\partial_{t}g=q(g)

and estimate
\frac{d}{dt}d_{2}^{2}(f,g)
. The first step is to establish a lifting lemma, which reduces the problem to estimating the evolution of the Wasserstein distance for a linear equation associated with the following
lifted operator
:

Q(F):=\kappa(x-x_{*})\left(\textnormal{div}_{v}-\textnormal{div}_{v_{*}}\right)\Big[\Phi(v-v_{*})\left(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{v_{*}}\right)F\Big].

The operator
Q
acts on functions of
\mathbb{R}^{6}\times\mathbb{R}^{6}
and is degenerate elliptic. It is connected with the Landau collision operator through tensorizaton and the projection in the
x
and
v

(3.6)

q(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}Q(f\otimes f)\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*},\qquad(f\otimes f)(x,v,x_{*},v_{*})=f(x,v)f(x_{*},v_{*}).
