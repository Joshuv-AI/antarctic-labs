Home telemonitoring in high‐risk pregnancies: A Finnish retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tiina Stark, Heidi Kruit, Leena Rahkonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHigh‐risk pregnancies require close monitoring and frequent hospital visits. Home telemonitoring offers a potential alternative to improve access and satisfaction while reducing healthcare use. This retrospective cohort study characterized the clinical course and events during home telemonitoring including home‐based cardiotocography during a pilot program for women with high‐risk pregnancies at an urban tertiary hospital.Material and MethodsThis retrospective three‐year cohort study included 117 singleton pregnancies enrolled in a home telemonitoring program at Helsinki University Hospital between January 2021 and December 2023. Participants were grouped by primary indication: premature rupture of membranes, preeclampsia and/or fetal growth restriction, or other high‐risk conditions. The main outcomes included the course and duration of home telemonitoring, rates of hospital visits and readmissions, the onset of labor or emergency cesarean delivery, and severe complications during home telemonitoring, including maternal or fetal death, placental abruption, prolapse of umbilical cord, placenta previa with bleeding, chorioamnionitis, eclampsia, and worsening of preeclampsia.ResultsThe median gestational age at initiation of home telemonitoring was 32.4 weeks, and the median monitoring duration was 7 days (IQR 2.5–13.5). Thirty‐eight women (32.6%) were readmitted. The median gestational age at delivery was 34.6 (range 25.1–41.4) weeks, and 87.2% (102/117) delivered preterm. The overall cesarean rate was 47.0% (55/117). Spontaneous onset of labor occurred in 11 (9.4%) women during the home telemonitoring period; no home deliveries were recorded. 38 women (32.5%) underwent an emergency cesarean delivery. No maternal or intrauterine fetal deaths occurred. Complications occurred in 36 cases (30.8%). The most common complications were chorioamnionitis (n = 21,17.9%) and worsening of preeclampsia (n = 11, 9.4%). No eclampsia was observed. Two cases of placental abruption (n = 2, 1.7%) and two cases of bleeding due to placenta previa (n = 2, 1.7%) occurred. In all the preceding cases, immediate readmission and prompt management resulted in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.ConclusionsThis pilot study suggests that home telemonitoring represents an acceptable approach for the management of selected high‐risk pregnancies. However, careful patient selection and strict adherence to evidence‐based clinical protocols are essential to ensure safe and effective implementation.Keywords: fetal growth restriction, high‐risk pregnancy, home care, home telemonitoring, perinatal mortality, preeclampsia, preterm rupture of membranes

Key Message

This pilot study suggests that home telemonitoring represents an acceptable alternative for managing selected high‐risk pregnancies when implemented with careful patient selection and adherence to clinical guidelines.

INTRODUCTION

The global prevalence of high‐risk pregnancies, characterized by increased health risks to the pregnant woman, fetus, or both, is on the rise. High‐risk pregnancies encompass complications such as preterm pre‐labor rupture of membranes (pPROM), fetal growth restriction (FGR), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), imminent preterm birth, and hypertensive disorders, including preeclampsia (PE). This increased surveillance leads to antenatal hospital admissions in up to 20% of pregnancies. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Hospital admission due to pregnancy complications is a significant event with considerable impacts, including family burden, psychological stress, and increased healthcare workload and costs. 5 , 6

Home management of high‐risk pregnancies by remote self‐monitoring, an innovative approach to remotely monitor both fetal and maternal conditions from home, has developed as an alternative to hospitalization or frequent outpatient visits. Home telemonitoring has the potential to enhance patient satisfaction and access to care, while simultaneously reducing healthcare costs by decreasing the need for hospital admissions and outpatient clinic visits. 7

Pregnant women constitute a population that is generally well‐acquainted with digital tools, as they are frequent users of the internet, smartphones, and mobile health applications. 8 The integration of eHealth into maternal care can enhance healthcare accessibility, patient engagement, and autonomy. eHealth, defined as the use of information and communication technologies to deliver, manage, and improve health services, encompasses web‐based programs, remote monitoring, teleconsultations, and mobile device–supported care. 9

Obstetric telemedicine is reshaping the way of providing perinatal health care, and it is likely to disperse globally in the future. 7 Telemonitoring platforms enable remote assessment of maternal and fetal well‐being through home‐based cardiotocography (CTG) and wireless monitoring of fetal heart rate and uterine contractions. Pregnant women can also record and transmit blood pressure, urine, blood, and temperature measurements to healthcare providers via secure digital systems.

Studies on home telemonitoring of high‐risk pregnancies, especially those involving telemonitoring implementation using patient‐recorded CTG, are limited. However, a recent multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Bekker et al. demonstrated that telemonitoring may be as safe as conventional inpatient care for the surveillance of high‐risk pregnancies. 10 Another retrospective study by Zizzo et al. involving 400 intermediate‐ or high‐risk pregnancies utilizing remote CTG self‐monitoring concluded that home‐based monitoring is safe and feasible, with maternal and fetal outcomes comparable with or even surpassing those reported with inpatient care. 11 An ongoing observational study in Norway is currently evaluating home telemonitoring; the findings have not yet been reported. 12 Given the novelty of this approach, further data are required to establish its safety in high‐risk pregnancies.

Helsinki University Hospital Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology initiated a pilot program providing high‐quality home telemonitoring for women with high‐risk pregnancies who would otherwise require hospital admission or frequent outpatient visits. The aim of this study was to investigate the outcome of high‐risk pregnancies managed by home telemonitoring during the pilot, and to assess the right indications and eligibility criteria for home telemonitoring. The insights gained from this study are expected to provide valuable guidance for clinicians implementing home monitoring strategies as part of prenatal care in high‐risk pregnancies.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This retrospective three‐year cohort study included pregnant women selected for the newly implemented home telemonitoring pilot program at Helsinki University Hospital (HUS) from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023. The study population consists of patients selected for home telemonitoring at the time of its introduction.

Inclusion criteria home monitoring included age 18 years or older, singleton pregnancy, gestational age (GA) ≥ 22 +0 weeks, and a pregnancy complication requiring maternal or fetal surveillance. Exclusion criteria for home monitoring included multiple gestation, abnormal CTG indicating fetal hypoxia or distress, significantly reduced fetal movements in case of intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, hemorrhage, clinical signs of infection, insufficient proficiency in Finnish, Swedish, or English, and a lack of motivation or capacity to adhere to the home monitoring protocol. Patient selection and monitoring criteria were based on the existing institutional guidelines of the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at Helsinki University Hospital. Each participant received face‐to‐face training conducted by a midwife to ensure safe and correct use of the home telemonitoring devices.

Each participant was provided with a personalized treatment plan tailored to their specific clinical needs. The treatment plan included daily assessments of fetal well‐being via CTG (Pregnabit, Nestmedic S.A., Warshaw, Poland), bedside C‐reactive protein (CRP) testing (QuikRead go CRP, Aidian Oy, Espoo, Finland) routine laboratory blood tests, urine strip analysis, body temperature and blood pressure measurements. Bedside C‐reactive protein (CRP) testing and urine dipstick testing were performed at home as part of the individualized care plan. CRP testing was not routine but was used selectively according to hospital guidelines, in cases of pPROM, to support the monitoring for signs of infection. Women performed home‐based CTG once daily at a scheduled time for real‐time assessment via an internet portal. CTG recordings were obtained using a Doppler ultrasound‐based device (Pregnabit, Nestmedic S.A., Warshaw, Poland), capturing fetal heart rate patterns, uterine contractions, and maternal pulse (Figure 1). Signals were transmitted wirelessly to a tablet and securely transferred via an encrypted channel to the hospital server. CTG analysis was based on visual assessment performed by healthcare professionals in accordance with the international guidelines of the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO). A normal tracing was defined as a baseline fetal heart rate of 110–160 bpm, normal variability with a bandwidth amplitude of 5–25 bpm, absence of repetitive decelerations, and the presence of cyclicity and accelerations.

Fetal heart rate (FHR, green) and maternal heart rate (blue) are shown in the upper panel as beats per minute. Fetal movements (FM) are depicted as purple markers in the middle panel, and uterine contractions (UC) are shown in the lower panel as pressure (mmHg, purple).

Additionally, participants completed a structured daily questionnaire evaluating fetal movements and overall maternal well‐being.

Self‐monitored data were transmitted from the patient to the hospital using a mobile device equipped with a secure telemedicine platform, which served as the primary tool for data transfer and communication. All submitted data were reviewed daily by healthcare professionals. Findings were documented and assessed using the hospital's electronic health record system. Participants had 24/7 access to the hospital's obstetric unit and were encouraged to contact the department at any time. The telemonitoring team, comprising trained midwives, contacted participants daily by phone to review symptoms and self‐monitoring results. Protocolized management included continued expectant care if findings were reassuring, same‐day clinical assessment for concerning but non‐critical findings (e.g., abnormal CTG, elevated blood pressure, or new maternal symptoms), and clinical admission when indicated (e.g., abnormal CTG, hypertension, uterine contractions, antenatal hemorrhage, signs of infection, maternal distress, or technical issues compromising telemonitoring). Follow‐up care, including outpatient visits, ultrasound evaluations, and laboratory testing, was scheduled on an individual basis in accordance with each patient's clinical condition.

The study population was stratified into three subgroups based on the primary clinical indication for home telemonitoring: pPROM, PE, and/or FGR, and other maternal or fetal conditions requiring increased surveillance.

pPROM was diagnosed by clinical examination combined with a positive rapid vaginal insulin‐like growth factor binding protein‐1 dipstick test (ActimProm®, Medix Biochemica, Espoo, Finland). Initial in‐hospital management followed institutional guidelines and involved admission to the obstetric department. This included Group B Streptococcus (GBS) screening using the Xpert® GBS assay (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA), prophylactic antibiotic therapy for the first three days, daily monitoring of CRP and white blood cell count, urine culture, and administration of antenatal corticosteroids when indicated according to the hospital's own treatment guidelines. Corticosteroid therapy was administered in cases at risk of preterm labor between GA 22 +5 and 34 +6 weeks in accordance with national recommendations. 13 Eligibility criteria for home telemonitoring in the pPROM subgroup included absence of clinical signs of infection, a closed cervix with a cervical length of ≥ 20 mm on transvaginal ultrasound, normal CTG registration, and no clinical or sonographic evidence of preterm labor.

PE was diagnosed based on the criteria defined by the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy (ISSHP). 14 Diagnosis required a systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg on at least two occasions, measured four hours apart in previously normotensive women. This had to be accompanied by one or more of the following new‐onset conditions occurring at or after GA 20 weeks: proteinuria ≥ 30 mg/mmol protein (creatinine ratio, ≥ 300 mg/24 h, or ≥ 2+ on a dipstick test), evidence of maternal organ dysfunction (e.g., renal insufficiency, liver involvement, neurological complications, or hematological disturbances) or uteroplacental dysfunction, including FGR or abnormal umbilical artery Doppler velocimetry. FGR was defined as an estimated fetal weight below the 10th percentile for gestational age.

The other indications for home telemonitoring included intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, placenta previa/accreta spectrum, partial placental abruption, fetal supraventricular tachycardia, fetus with congenital heart malformation, and woman with type 1 diabetes mellitus with fetal macrosomia.

The main outcomes of the study were the course of home telemonitoring, including the duration of telemonitoring, rates of hospital visits, readmission, and onset of labor or emergency CD during home telemonitoring. Duration of home telemonitoring was defined as the time under home telemonitoring, excluding periods of rehospitalization but including outpatient follow‐ups. Planned visits were scheduled outpatient appointments according to each patient's individualized follow‐up plan, whereas unplanned visits were emergency consultations initiated by the patient or prompted by a clinical request. Onset of labor during home telemonitoring was defined as regular uterine contractions which lead to cervical change. Emergency CD was defined as CD performed within 4 h of clinical decision. Furthermore, the main outcomes of the study were severe complications during home telemonitoring. Severe complication was defined as maternal or fetal death, placental abruption, prolapse of umbilical cord, placenta previa with bleeding, chorioamnionitis, eclampsia, and worsening of PE.

Chorioamnionitis was diagnosed by an obstetrician based on clinical suspicion. Traditionally, the diagnosis is guided by signs and symptoms indicative of microbial‐associated intrauterine inflammation and bacterial infection, as first described by Gibbs et al. 15 The diagnostic criteria include maternal fever (> 37.8°C) accompanied by at least two of the following: maternal tachycardia (heart rate > 100 beats/min), uterine tenderness, foul‐smelling amniotic fluid or vaginal discharge, fetal tachycardia (heart rate > 160 beats/min), or maternal leukocytosis (white blood cell count > 15 000 cells/mm3). Severe chorioamnionitis was defined as chorioamnionitis associated with either maternal or neonatal death, or the occurrence of maternal sepsis.

Worsening of PE was defined as the need for patient follow‐up in the high‐dependency unit according to hospital guideline and meeting at least one of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) criteria for severe preeclampsia, including systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 110 mmHg, new‐onset cerebral or visual symptoms, hepatic dysfunction (transaminases > 2× the upper limit of normal or severe right upper quadrant/epigastric pain), thrombocytopenia (< 100 × 109/L), renal impairment (creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL or doubling of baseline), or pulmonary edema. 16

The results are presented as numbers (n) with percentages (%), means with standard deviations (SD), or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or ranges, as appropriate. Means were used for normally distributed variables and medians for non‐normally distributed variables. For dichotomous outcomes, numbers and percentages were reported. Missing observations were excluded from the analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 117 women were included in the study. The mean maternal age was 33.2 (±5.2) years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 26.1 (±5.6) kg/m2. The most common indications for telemonitoring were pPROM (67.5%, n = 79/117) and PE/FGR (21.4%, n = 25/117). In the PE/FGR group (n = 25), four women (16%) had isolated FGR, 14 women had PE (56%), and seven women (28%) had a combination of PE and FGR. The other fetal or maternal indications (11.1%, n = 13/117) included intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (6.8%, n = 8), placenta previa/accreta spectrum (0.9%, n = 1), partial placental ablation (0.9%, n = 1), fetal supraventricular tachycardia (0.9%, n = 1), fetus with congenital heart malformation (0.9%, n = 1), and a woman with type 1 diabetes mellitus with fetal macrosomia (0.9%, n = 1). Course of home telemonitoring

The median GA at the initiation of home telemonitoring was 32.4 (IQR 23.7–37.1) weeks. The mean duration of home telemonitoring was 7.0 (IQR 2.5–13.5) days. Home telemonitoring lasted fewer than three days for 33 women (28.2%) and more than 14 days for 26 women (22.2%). A total of 74 women (63.2%) had planned antenatal visits during home telemonitoring, accounting for a total of 172 visits (Table 2). Unplanned visits occurred in 92 women (78.6%) during home telemonitoring, totaling 126 visits (Table 2). A doctor's consultation was required for 76 visits (50%), while the remaining 50% were midwife consultations. During home telemonitoring, 38 women (32.6%) were readmitted, including five women (4.3%) within 24 h (Table 2). Spontaneous onset of labor occurred in 11 (9.4%) women during the home telemonitoring period; no home deliveries were recorded. 38 women (32.5%) underwent an emergency CD. Complications during home telemonitoring

No maternal or intrauterine fetal deaths occurred during home telemonitoring. The total rate of severe complications during telemonitoring was 30.8% (n = 36/117). The most common complications were chorioamnionitis (n = 21, 17.9%) and worsening PE (n = 11, 9.4%) (Table 3). Two cases of placental abruption (n = 2, 1.7%) and two cases of bleeding due to placenta previa (n = 2, 1.7%) occurred (Table 3).

pPROM

In the pPROM group, 25 women (31.6%) were readmitted. The most common reason for readmission was clinical suspicion of chorioamnionitis (n = 23/25, 92%), accounting for 26.6% of the total cohort (n = 23/79). Three women (3.8%, n = 3/79) were readmitted due to the spontaneous onset of labor by regular uterine contractions at home. No home deliveries were recorded. Two cases of bleeding due to placenta previa occurred during home telemonitoring: One case at GA 34 + 4 weeks was admitted urgently and underwent emergency CD; the neonate required a three‐day stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Another woman presented at GA 31 + 2 weeks with a fourth bleeding episode, was re‐hospitalized, and later had elective CD at GA 33 + 0 weeks. In both cases, because of prompt management, maternal and neonatal outcomes were favorable.

PE and/or FGR

In the PE/FGR group, 11 women (44.0%) were readmitted. The most common reason for readmission was worsening of preeclampsia (7/11, 63.6%). No cases of eclampsia occurred. Two cases of placental abruption occurred. In the first case, the woman was called in for evaluation due to an abnormal CTG during home telemonitoring; at readmission, she experienced vaginal bleeding and underwent emergency CD at GA 27 + 6 weeks. The neonate had an Apgar score < 7 at 5 min and required a 20‐day stay in the NICU. In the second case, vaginal bleeding began at home during telemonitoring at GA 35 + 5 weeks, leading to immediate hospital readmission and emergency CD. The neonate also had an Apgar score < 7 at 5 min but did not require NICU admission. In both cases, rapid management ensured favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Other

Among other indications for monitoring, three women (23.1%) were readmitted: two for pPROM and one due to abdominal pain. No obstetric complications were observed.

Obstetric and neonatal outcomes

The median gestational age at delivery was 34.6 (25.14–41.42) weeks. The preterm delivery rate was 87.2% (n = 102/117). Overall, 73.5% (n = 86/117) of all neonates were admitted to NICU, with the median duration of stay of 4.0 (IQR 0–9.5) days. The total CD rate was 47.0% (n = 55/117). Ten cases (8.5%) of severe maternal post‐partum complications unrelated to home monitoring occurred (Table 4): Six women with PE required intensive care for 24 h after delivery; one of them developed HELLP syndrome. Two women had massive post‐partum hemorrhage resulting in emergency hysterectomy, one with placental abruption at 28 + 1 gestational weeks and one with placenta accreta spectrum at 34 + 6 weeks. Two cases of post‐partum psychosis occurred (Table 4). In our study, one case of neonatal death occurred 20 days after birth and was unrelated to the telemonitoring setting; the infant had congenital heart disease.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective cohort study characterized the clinical course and events during home telemonitoring and evaluated outcomes of a pilot program for women with high‐risk pregnancies at an urban tertiary hospital. This study specifically focused on events occurring during the home telemonitoring period. This pilot program, the first of its kind in Finland, contributes to the limited data on remote monitoring in this group. Our findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that home telemonitoring in high‐risk pregnancies appears to be an acceptable alternative for managing selected high‐risk pregnancies. 10 , 11

Our study cohort demonstrated maternal age and BMI comparable to those reported in earlier studies. 10 , 11 The main indications for home telemonitoring were pPROM and PE/FGR, in line with previous reports. Our pilot program applied strict exclusion criteria, similar to Bekker et al., whereas Zizzo et al. used minimal exclusion criteria, which may partly explain differences in outcomes between studies. 10 , 11 In our cohort, home telemonitoring was initiated at a slightly earlier gestational age, with a shorter monitoring duration and a higher readmission rate compared with prior studies. In our study, the mean duration of home telemonitoring was 7.0 days. In Becker study the mean duration of inclusion in the study was 11–12 days. 10 Most readmissions occurred among women with pPROM or PE/FGR.

Although baseline maternal demographics were similar, our cohort represented a higher obstetric risk profile in terms of pregnancy‐related complications and gestational characteristics. A greater proportion of deliveries in our study were preterm (72% vs. 56%), and telemonitoring was initiated at an earlier GA (31.9 vs. 34 weeks) compared to the study of Bekker et al. 10 The prevalence of GDM in our study was 20.5%, reaching 25% in the pPROM subgroup, and severe preeclampsia requiring high‐dependency unit follow‐up post‐partum occurred in six women. Consequently, the higher CD and NICU admission rates likely reflect earlier gestational age at delivery and a greater burden of clinically significant obstetric complications rather than differences in baseline maternal characteristics alone.

Compared with Bekker et al., 10 both CD and NICU admission rates were higher in our study, which is consistent with the lower gestational age at delivery and the higher proportion of preterm births in our cohort, as well as the higher prevalence of conditions such as GDM and severe preeclampsia. By contrast, Zizzo et al. 11 reported a similar CD rate but a substantially lower proportion of preterm births, further highlighting the differences in patient characteristics between cohorts and overall obstetric risk between the study populations.

No maternal deaths occurred in our cohort, consistent with previous studies on home telemonitoring. 10 , 11 The maternal severe complication rate was 8.5%, with severe eclampsia being the most common complication. Six women with preeclampsia required follow‐up in the high‐dependency unit after delivery according to hospitals' guidelines. No cases of eclampsia occurred.

In our cohort, no intrauterine fetal deaths occurred, in line with Zizzo et al., 11 whereas Bekker et al. reported one fetal death in a fetus with trisomy 21 and growth restriction. 10 One neonatal death occurred in our study, involving an infant with congenital heart disease, considered unrelated to telemonitoring. Similarly, Zizzo et al. reported one neonatal death due to pulmonary hypoplasia, and Bekker et al. described two cases following pulmonary hypoplasia after pPROM and placental abruption, respectively. 10 , 11

Chorioamnionitis was the most frequent complication in our study, affecting over one quarter of women with pPROM, consistent with previous reports. 11 , 17 , 18 No cases of severe chorioamnionitis, such as sepsis or maternal or neonatal death, occurred, likely reflecting early detection and prompt admission to the obstetric unit. All chorioamnionitis cases were mild to moderate and managed promptly in hospital. A meta‐analysis of 1608 cases across seven cohort studies by Williamson et al. suggests potential benefits of outpatient management of pPROM, with longer latency from membrane rupture to delivery. 16 In our cohort, the overall median gestational age at delivery for women with pPROM was similar to that reported by Zizzo et al. 10

The incidence of placental abruption is approximately three times higher in women with PE and approximately 10‐fold in women with pPROM compared with normal pregnancies. 18 , 19 In our study, two cases of placental abruption occurred in the PE/FGR group. Both cases were immediately readmitted for emergency CD, and at least in one case, early detection and intervention were enabled by the home monitoring. Zizzo et al. reported two cases of placental abruption in the pPROM group, and Williamson et al. reported the incidence of 6.4% of placental abruption in pPROM outpatients. 10 , 16 Bekker et al. reported one neonatal death due to placental abruption in the home telemonitoring group, while the overall incidence of placental abruption was not specified. 10

The strengths of this study include its focus on a high‐risk obstetric population who may particularly benefit from digital care. The cohort was heterogeneous, including women with diverse pregnancy complications requiring intensified maternal and fetal surveillance, and the use of comprehensive electronic health records enhances data reliability. Clear definitions of clinical conditions, such as pPROM and preeclampsia, improve reproducibility. Importantly, this study provides novel data on home telemonitoring, including CTG surveillance, in a high‐risk population—a setting with limited prior evidence—offering insights into safety and clinical management.

The main limitation of the study is the retrospective design, which introduces potential biases and limits causal inference. Additional limitations include the modest sample size, although acceptable for a pilot evaluation of a novel care mode, the restriction to Finnish‐, Swedish‐, or English‐speaking women, and the absence of socioeconomic and detailed demographic data, all of which may affect generalizability and introduce selection bias. Patient enrollment was based on clinical judgment within existing hospital protocols, which may have been influenced by resource availability, potentially introducing selection bias. Information bias is possible due to reliance on routine clinical records, and residual confounding cannot be excluded, as unmeasured factors, such as disease severity, comorbidities, or patient engagement, may have influenced both selection and outcomes. Differences between our cohort and prior studies likely reflect institutional practices and case mix rather than research‐driven selection, and we consider this a strength. Earlier gestational age at inclusion, higher proportions of preterm deliveries, and greater burden of obstetric complications provide valuable information on the external applicability of home telemonitoring and may guide refinement of local eligibility criteria and clinical pathways. Compared with standard outpatient care, home telemonitored women had 24/7 direct access to the obstetric unit, likely improving timely care and safety. This contextual advantage reinforces the clinical relevance and potential benefit of home telemonitoring in high‐risk pregnancies.

Finally, we acknowledge not assessing the cost implications of home monitoring, although previous research suggests it can be cost‐effective, reducing hospital admissions and overall obstetric care expenditure. 10

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this pilot study suggests that home telemonitoring represents an acceptable alternative for managing selected high‐risk pregnancies. Nonetheless, careful patient selection and strict adherence to evidence‐based clinical guidelines remain essential to ensure the safe and effective implementation of home telemonitoring in high‐risk pregnancies.
