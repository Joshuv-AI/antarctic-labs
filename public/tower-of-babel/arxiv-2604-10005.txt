What Happens When Institutional Liquidity Enters Prediction Markets: Identification, Measurement, and a Synthetic Proof of Concept https://x.com/DaedalusRsch
Shaw Dalen

Abstract
Prediction markets are starting to look less like crowd polls and more like electronic markets. The central question is therefore no longer only whether these markets forecast well, but what happens when institutional liquidity enters: do spreads tighten, does price discovery improve, and do those gains actually reach the traders who are slowest to react when information arrives? This paper offers a research design for answering that question. It defines a broad market-quality lens, separates the main channels through which institutional liquidity enters, and maps the identification problems that arise in live venue data. It also uses a synthetic microstructure laboratory as a proof of concept for the measurement pipeline. The main lesson of the synthetic exercise is deliberately narrow. Market-maker coverage, liquidity incentives, and automation do not have to work through the same channel; average liquidity gains do not have to translate into equal gains for all traders; and the sharpest welfare losses are most likely to appear in shock states, when slower takers receive the least pass-through of tighter quoted markets. The synthetic results are useful because they stress-test the design, not because they settle the live empirical question.

The problem this paper focuses on is more specific and more contemporary. Once institutional liquidity enters prediction markets through maker programs, exchange-run incentives, and automated quoting, the market can look easier to trade on average while still becoming harsher for slower or less informed traders. A narrower quoted spread does not automatically mean that every participant actually receives better execution. The key economic question is therefore simple: when institutional liquidity enters prediction markets, who really gets the benefit?

A concrete example helps. Imagine a contract on whether a CPI release will come in above consensus. Before the announcement, market-maker support and automated quoting may compress the displayed spread. At the release time, however, some participants react almost instantly while others arrive late. The same market can therefore look more liquid in the book and yet become less forgiving for slower takers exactly when information arrives. This paper is organized around that tension.

The market that now matters in practice is also richer than the one emphasized in the earliest forecasting literature. The U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission has recently treated prediction markets and event contracts as an explicit regulatory object

This paper answers the resulting question in a deliberately scoped way. It develops a research design with a synthetic proof of concept: the aim is to define the relevant outcome family, separate the channels through which institutional liquidity enters, clarify what the live empirical paper must measure, and demonstrate that the pipeline can be implemented end-to-end before private or venue-level data are attached.

Contribution and scope.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it argues that prediction markets should be evaluated with a joint market-quality lens spanning liquidity, information efficiency, forecast quality, internal consistency, and trader-level welfare. Second, it replaces an omnibus institutionalization dummy with a channel-based treatment map: market-maker coverage, liquidity-incentive eligibility, automation/API intensity, and participant-composition shifts. Third, it states the live-data empirical agenda explicitly, including staggered-adoption identification, mediator measurement, richer heterogeneity, and welfare decomposition. The synthetic laboratory serves only to illustrate the estimands, tables, and figures that such a live paper should ultimately deliver.

What public data can already tell us.

Even without account-level data, a live public-data paper can say a great deal about quoted spreads, effective spreads, realized spreads, pass-through, and several forms of internal consistency. What it cannot cleanly sign on its own are informed-trader rents and genuine risk-sharing gains. Bringing that distinction forward helps clarify both the ambition and the limits of the empirical agenda.

2
Institutional Liquidity, Treatment Channels, and Hypotheses

Prediction markets as electronic markets.

A modern event contract is a binary claim paying $1 if a specified event occurs and $0 otherwise. Unlike equities, event contracts have bounded payoffs and deterministic convergence to 0 or 1 at settlement. They are also unusually shock-driven: macro releases, debate performances, court rulings, injuries, and protocol announcements can all move beliefs discontinuously. For that reason, market quality in prediction markets cannot be assessed from one dimension alone. Spread, depth, resiliency, price impact, forecast calibration, and cross-contract consistency all matter.

Why institutional liquidity entry must be decomposed.

The phrase “institutional participation” is too broad for causal work. To study institutional liquidity entry, the live empirical paper should instead distinguish at least four separable channels:

1.

Designated market-maker coverage.
Formal maker status, quoting obligations, or program enrollment.

2.

Liquidity-incentive eligibility.
Exchange subsidy rules for displayed orders or resting quotes.

3.

Automation/API intensity.
The ability and willingness of participants to quote or react through public or private infrastructure.

4.

Participant-composition shifts.
A change in the share of professional, systematic, or cross-venue-capable flow.

The first three are candidate
entry channels or treatments
; the fourth is better viewed as a
mediator or equilibrium outcome
. This distinction is important. A convincing live paper should estimate what maker designation, incentive eligibility, and automation access do separately, and then ask whether they work through a reweighting of participant composition.

Conceptual trade-off.

The microstructure intuition is standard. More institutional liquidity can tighten spreads, replenish depth, and reduce transitory price impact
[
13
,
24
,
27
,
4
,
31
,
15
,
16
]
. Modern algorithmic participation can also improve liquidity in other electronic markets
[
17
,
29
]
. But the same infrastructure can sharpen toxicity filters and concentrate information rents against less-informed traders. Prediction markets should therefore exhibit a familiar tension between better average execution and more selective extraction in shock states.

Hypothesis 1

(Liquidity)
.

Market-maker coverage, liquidity incentives, and automation intensity improve average displayed liquidity, but the magnitude need not be the same across channels.

Hypothesis 2

(Efficiency)
.

At least some institutional-liquidity channels improve price discovery and internal consistency, but forecast-quality gains may be weaker than execution-quality gains.

Hypothesis 3

(Selection)
.

Execution-quality improvements can coexist with worse shock-state adverse selection for slower takers.

Hypothesis 4

(Heterogeneity)
.

The gains from institutional liquidity vary with information density, hedgeability, resolution clarity, time-to-resolution, and participant mix, not just with coarse event categories.

3
Empirical Agenda and Identification

Takeaway.
This section is not about methodological ornament. Its purpose is to make clear where the live paper will be hardest to identify and what must be measured before any claim about institutional liquidity is credible.

The empirical agenda is most persuasive when stated explicitly.

Step 1: build a modular market panel.

The live paper should combine market metadata, order-book snapshots, trade prints, resolution outcomes, and treatment metadata from venue documentation or platform-side records
[
23
,
20
,
34
,
33
]
. The key difference from the earlier draft is that maker designation, liquidity-incentive eligibility, and automation proxies should be encoded separately.

Step 2: identify staggered treatment correctly.

Because the treatment channels are likely to be staggered and heterogeneous, the main live estimators should not rely on a single off-the-shelf two-way-fixed-effects regression. The natural toolkit is now the staggered-adoption difference-in-differences literature: decomposition diagnostics
[
14
]
, interaction-weighted event studies
[
36
]
, and group-time average treatment effects
[
6
]
. The live paper should pair those with matched controls, randomization-style placebo dates where feasible, and cluster-robust inference, ideally including wild bootstrap variants when the effective number of treated clusters is small
[
7
,
8
,
26
]
.

Step 3: define richer heterogeneity.

The prior category split — politics, macro, sports, crypto — is too coarse on its own. A stronger paper should study heterogeneity by information density, resolution ambiguity, hedgeability, time-to-resolution, and the availability of related markets or cross-venue hedges. Those dimensions are more economically interpretable than raw category labels.

Step 4: take welfare seriously.

The best version of the paper will not stop at spread and price impact. It will measure how gains from institutional liquidity are distributed across maker profits, taker execution, informed-trader rents, and inventory-risk sharing. That requires trader segmentation or at least credible proxies for trader type, order size, and speed. This is where venue-level identifiers or internal tags would be especially valuable.

Step 5: measure participant-composition shifts as mediators.

Participant-composition changes should not be coded as a primitive treatment if they are themselves caused by maker programs, incentives, or automation. Instead, the live paper should measure them directly and treat them as mediators or equilibrium outcomes. Useful proxies include the share of message traffic with high cancel-to-trade ratios, the share of quote updates that replenish within a short latency window after best-price moves, the fraction of flow synchronized across related markets, the prevalence of cross-venue basis reactions after public news, and the share of volume accounted for by accounts with repeated multi-market quoting behavior.

These proxies are informative but highly endogenous. High cancel-to-trade ratios, fast replenishment, and synchronized cross-market reactions can reflect professional liquidity, but they can also reflect venue rules, event intensity, or latent order-flow toxicity. The live paper therefore needs a contamination strategy as well as a proxy list: compare proxy behavior inside and outside scheduled-news windows, validate against markets with known differences in automation access, exploit abrupt program-eligibility thresholds when available, and report robustness across “strict” and “broad” mediator definitions. The goal is not to claim that any single proxy cleanly identifies professionalism, but to show whether several imperfect measures move together in ways consistent with a participant-composition channel.

A practical validation hierarchy would separate
core
proxies from
supplemental
proxies. Core proxies are the ones closest to continuous quoting technology, such as repeated multi-market quoting, short-latency replenishment, and cross-market synchronization immediately after public news. Supplemental proxies include cancel-to-trade ratios, message intensity, and cross-venue basis reactions, which are informative but more easily contaminated by venue mechanics and temporary toxicity. In the live paper, the strictest specifications should begin with the core set, then show how conclusions move when the broader proxy bundle is layered in.

Step 6: classify shock states rather than using one black-box indicator.

The paper’s main welfare claim is inherently state dependent, so the live design should distinguish at least three shock classes:
scheduled public news
(for example, macro releases or known debate times),
semi-scheduled or quasi-public events
(such as court windows or lineup announcements with uncertain timing), and
unscheduled breaking news
. Those classes differ both in adverse-selection mechanics and in proxy contamination risk. Scheduled shocks are the cleanest environment for testing how institutional-liquidity channels affect pass-through because the event clock is known in advance. A CPI release market is the natural example: everyone knows when the information will arrive, but not everyone can react at the same speed. Breaking-news windows are economically important, but they are also where mediator proxies, participant composition, and toxicity become hardest to disentangle.

Step 7: write down the robustness checklist before touching live data.

A minimal live-data robustness set should include: alternative treatment timestamps; strict versus broad mediator definitions; exclusion of scheduled macro windows; matched-market subsamples; alternative quote-sampling frequencies; placebo treatment dates; and contract-family fixed effects. Writing this checklist into the design paper makes the eventual live study look less like an ex-post robustness search and more like a pre-analysis blueprint.

Step 8: make the identification map explicit.

Figure
1
summarizes the live-paper logic: maker programs, incentive eligibility, and automation act as channels through which institutional liquidity enters; participant-composition shifts operate as mediators; shock states and venue mechanics affect both mediator proxies and outcomes; and welfare is identified primarily through pass-through and execution-cost objects rather than through fully observed surplus decomposition.

4
Synthetic Proof-of-Concept Laboratory

Role of the synthetic section.

The synthetic panel is kept for one narrow purpose: to show that the proposed measurement stack, treatment decomposition, tables, figures, and welfare language can be implemented coherently end-to-end before private or venue-level data are attached. The simulator still encodes economic structure — latent belief dynamics, order-book outcomes, shock states, and multiple institutional-liquidity channels — but its comparative statics are designed rather than discovered. It should therefore be read as a pipeline sanity check rather than a source of external validity.

4.1
Latent belief process

Each market
m
belongs to a category and a richer attribute vector capturing information density, resolution clarity, and hedgeability. The latent log-odds belief evolves as

x_{m,t+1}=x_{m,t}-\kappa_{m}(x_{m,t}-x^{*}_{m})+\sigma_{m}\varepsilon_{m,t+1}+J_{m,t+1},

(1)

where
J_{m,t+1}
captures news shocks. Final outcomes are drawn once per market from the anchor probability. Observed probabilities are then contaminated by microstructure noise whose variance declines with automation intensity and rises with resolution ambiguity.

4.2
Channels of institutional liquidity entry

The synthetic panel separately assigns a market-maker activation time, a liquidity-incentive activation time, an automation-intensity path that ramps up after API-style adoption, and an endogenous professional-share proxy that responds to the first three channels. This is a better match to the live agenda because it prevents all institutional changes from being collapsed into one treatment dummy.

4.3
Synthetic outcomes

Spread, effective spread, depth, price impact, no-arbitrage gaps, and forecast-quality statistics are generated from the latent state, the shock process, and the modular channels through which institutional liquidity enters. The synthetic design deliberately allows some channels to help execution much more than calibration. It also allows quality gains to depend on hedgeability, information density, and resolution clarity.

Panel dimensions.

The synthetic panel contains 320 markets and 57,600 market-time observations. Its purpose is illustrative rather than evidentiary, so the panel is calibrated to generate richer variation across information density, hedgeability, and resolution ambiguity than the earlier bundle-only design.

5
Measurement

Takeaway.
If the paper cares about what institutional liquidity does, it cannot stop at quoted spreads. A usable live paper has to track execution quality, forecast quality, internal consistency, and who actually receives the gains.

The paper tracks five outcome families: liquidity, information efficiency, forecast quality, internal consistency, and welfare incidence.

5.1
Execution and efficiency metrics

For execution price
P_{j}
, midpoint
M_{j}
, and trade sign
d_{j}\in\{+1,-1\}
,

\displaystyle{\rm EffectiveSpread}_{j}

\displaystyle=2d_{j}(P_{j}-M_{j}),

(2)

\displaystyle{\rm RealizedSpread}_{j,\Delta}

\displaystyle=2d_{j}(P_{j}-M_{j+\Delta}),

(3)

\displaystyle{\rm AdverseSelection}_{j,\Delta}

\displaystyle={\rm EffectiveSpread}_{j}-{\rm RealizedSpread}_{j,\Delta}.

(4)

Quoted spread, best-price depth, and short-horizon price impact complete the standard microstructure stack. In the live paper, those measures should be sampled at multiple quote frequencies because prediction-market books can look materially different at event time, calendar time, and trade time.

5.2
Forecast quality and internal consistency

For forecast quality, with outcome
Y_{m}\in\{0,1\}
and probability
p_{m,t}
,

{\rm Brier}_{m,t}=(p_{m,t}-Y_{m})^{2}.

(5)

The live paper should supplement Brier with expected calibration error (ECE), where contracts are binned by implied probability and the average absolute gap between forecast probability and empirical resolution frequency is computed across bins.

Prediction markets also have a distinctive internal-consistency problem. In plain terms, a market can look liquid and still disagree with itself. At least three no-arbitrage or coherence objects are worth separating. First,
complementary-book gaps
compare the sum of YES and NO prices for the same contract to one. Second,
simplex gaps
compare the sum of mutually exclusive and exhaustive contracts on the same event family to one. Third,
semantic-dispersion gaps
compare prices for semantically matched contracts across venues, after adjusting for fee structure and resolution rules. The live paper should not collapse those into a single number unless it first shows that they move together empirically.

6
Estimands and Estimation Protocol

The paper draws a clear line between
descriptive recovery inside the synthetic lab
and
causal identification in the live paper
. Inside the synthetic proof-of-concept, I estimate descriptive two-way-fixed-effects regressions of the form

Y_{m,t}=\alpha_{m}+\tau_{t}+\beta_{\rm MM}{\rm MM}_{m,t}+\beta_{\rm LIP}{\rm LIP}_{m,t}+\beta_{\rm API}{\rm API}_{m,t}+\Gamma^{\prime}X_{m,t}+\varepsilon_{m,t},

(6)

with
X_{m,t}
including shock state and local volatility. In the real-data paper, the same equation should be replaced by estimators suited to staggered adoption and treatment heterogeneity
[
14
,
36
,
6
]
.

Pass-through as a formal welfare estimand.

The most tractable welfare object with public data is not total surplus but
pass-through
. For trader group
g
, define

PT_{g}\;\equiv\;\frac{-\widehat{\Delta{\rm EffectiveSpread}}_{g}}{-\widehat{\Delta{\rm QuotedSpread}}},

(7)

where both numerator and denominator are treatment-induced improvements, so
PT_{g}=1
means full conversion of quoted-spread gains into effective-spread gains for group
g
, while
PT_{g}<1
means incomplete pass-through. A second object is the shock wedge,

SW_{g}\;\equiv\;PT_{g}^{\rm shock}-PT_{g}^{\rm calm},

(8)

which captures whether the same group loses pass-through exactly when adverse selection is most likely to bite. This is the natural bridge between spread regressions and welfare claims.

What public data can and cannot identify.

With public data only, the live paper can estimate quoted spreads, effective spreads, realized spreads, pass-through, and several forms of internal consistency. It can partially recover maker-side outcomes through realized spread and inventory-style proxies. By contrast, informed-trader rents and genuine risk-sharing gains usually require venue-side or account-level data to sign cleanly. Treating those objects as bounded or partially identified is therefore a feature of disciplined scope, not a weakness of the design.

8
Welfare Incidence and Why It Matters

Takeaway.
Better average liquidity does not automatically mean better trader welfare. The central welfare question is whether the gains from institutional liquidity are actually passed through to the traders who are slow, small, or weakly hedged.

The paper’s most important substantive claim is a welfare claim: displayed-liquidity improvements need not translate one-for-one into trader surplus. For the live paper, the natural accounting identity is

\Delta W=\Delta CS_{\rm takers}+\Delta\Pi_{\rm makers}+\Delta IR_{\rm informed}+\Delta RS_{\rm risk\,sharing},

(9)

where the terms denote taker execution quality, maker profits, informed-trader rents, and genuine risk-sharing gains.

That identity is best viewed as an accounting target rather than a fully operational estimator stack. With public market data alone,
\Delta CS_{\rm takers}
is the most tractable component because it can be approximated from execution prices, effective spreads, and timing. Maker profits may be partially recoverable from realized spreads and inventory proxies, but informed rents and risk-sharing gains are much harder to sign cleanly without account-level labels, strategy traces, or inventory data. The live paper should therefore distinguish between components that can be estimated directly, components that can only be bounded, and components that remain qualitative.

Because the current proof-of-concept lacks live trader identifiers, it uses illustrative archetypes instead: small slow takers, small fast takers, large hedged traders, and informed-like traders. Table
6
and Figure
6
therefore focus on pass-through rather than levels alone. In the synthetic design, calm-state benefits are highly uneven, and slow takers can lose them entirely in shock states. That is the closest synthetic analogue to the claim that institutional liquidity may finance average spread compression through more selective extraction from slow flow when information arrives.

Interpretation.

The welfare illustration now does something stricter than the earlier bundle-only version. In calm states, slow takers receive only a small fraction of the pass-through enjoyed by faster or better hedged traders. In shock states, the synthetic slow-taker cell is actually worse under the high bundle than under the low bundle, even while large hedged and informed-like traders continue to benefit. This is still only an illustration, but it makes the welfare logic sharper: average spread compression and depth growth can coexist with a redistribution of execution quality away from the slowest flow. In the live paper, this should motivate trader-level pass-through estimates rather than welfare claims built from quoted spreads alone.

What the public-data version should report first.

A disciplined public-data paper should begin with four objects: average quoted-spread compression, effective-spread compression, pass-through by trader-size or speed proxy, and the shock wedge in pass-through. Only after those have been established should it move to partial maker-profit recovery or more speculative statements about informed rents. This ordering keeps the welfare section anchored in what the data can actually bear.

10
Conclusion

Prediction markets are moving into a more professional, electronically intermediated regime. Studying that transition requires more than asking whether prices are accurate on average. It requires following the joint movement of liquidity, information efficiency, forecast quality, internal consistency, and trader-level welfare, while treating maker designation, incentive design, automation intensity, and participant-composition shifts as distinct empirical objects.

The main value of the current paper is to organize that agenda into a concrete empirical blueprint. The synthetic proof of concept shows what the tables and figures look like, how moderator and mediator analysis fit together, and why the strongest live claims will likely concern execution and shock-state incidence rather than dramatic calibration gains. Just as importantly, the paper makes the measurement burden explicit: which objects are tractable with public data only, which mediator proxies are likely to be contaminated, which consistency gaps are prediction-market-specific, and which robustness checks the live version should treat as non-negotiable.

Three points should remain central. First, institutional liquidity is the core object of interest, while maker programs, incentives, and automation are best understood as the channels through which it enters. Second, the first and clearest gains from institutional liquidity are more likely to appear in execution quality than in forecasting performance. Third, the most important welfare question is whether those gains are actually passed through to the traders who are slowest, smallest, or least able to hedge when information arrives.
