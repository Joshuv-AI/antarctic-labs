 ABSTRACT
The task of resolving discontinuities between thermal cross sections and fast cross sections in AMPX pro-
cessing is part of a broader project to provide guidance on the potential effect of unknown thermal neutron
scattering law (TNSL) data, primarily from the standpoint of criticality safety. The primary goal of this
work is to deliver code fixes in AMPX that increase confidence that SCALE libraries provide physically
correct cross sections across the full range of neutron energies encountered in transport applications. This
report discusses the resolution of two sources of discontinuity. The first issue occurs when the Bragg edges
in an evaluated nuclear data file do not extend to 5 eV. The second issue concerns parameters chosen dur-
ing the processing of the nuclear data library—specifically, the parameter that defines the energy at which
the thermal data ends and the fast nuclide data begins. This report also discusses a third issue, which is re-
lated to the calculation of probability distributions from a densely gridded double-differential cross section.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Neutron cross sections on target materials are a necessary component of neutronics simulations. The cross
sections used in most neutron transport codes come from an evaluated library, such as ENDF/B-VIII.0 (Brown
et al. 2018). The cross section evaluation compiles the best available experimental measurements with the
best available physical models to provide a single, comprehensive cross section library that is suitable for a
wide range of applications.
Different evaluation methodologies must be employed for different ranges of neutron energies. At low
(e.g., thermal) neutron energies, the scattering of the neutron is affected by the atomic and molecular prop-
erties of the target material. At these energies, the thermal neutron scattering law (TNSL) treatment is ap-
propriate. For neutron energies above thermal energies, referred to as epithermal or fast, only the nuclear
properties of the target influence the neutron scattering behavior.
Regardless of evaluation methodology, the transport codes require a neutron cross section for each material
over the full energy range. Processing codes such as AMPX (Wiarda et al. 2022) translate the cross sec-
tions from the physics-oriented evaluated libraries into the formats compatible with the transport codes’
use of the cross sections. In particular, the SCALE system’s continuous-energy (CE) transport codes re-
quire that the neutron cross section be given in complete double-differential representation across the full
range of incident energies. The processing codes must then join the thermal cross section data to the fast
neutron data for each target material.
It is worth noting that many nuclides describe the energy region immediately above the thermal region via
resonance parameters so that the cross section is computed from a formalism such as the Reich–Moore
R-matrix formalism. There are ongoing efforts (Chapman and Arbanas 2025) to properly account for res-
onances that occur in the traditional thermal energy range (i.e., below 10 eV); while that method will be
important for this transition from the thermal region to the epithermal region, it is not yet adopted in cur-
rent evaluated libraries, and so is beyond the scope of this report.
In this work, some specific aspects of the thermal–fast transition as well as the effects of different decisions
on transport quantities of interest such as keff are discussed, focusing on elucidating and updating the pro-
cedure followed by the AMPX processing code for the SCALE code system’s cross section libraries. This
work was conducted as part of a broader project to assess the potential impacts of TNSL on the simula-
tion of critical systems. This report focuses specifically on the factors that need to be mitigated during the
processing of the transport library by AMPX. Those issues are described in the sections that follow.

 2. EARLY END OF BRAGG EDGE DATA
The ENDF-6 format manual (Brown 2023) indicates that Bragg edge data for coherent elastic scattering
should be specified up to 5 eV. Bragg edge data stopping short of 5 eV are prominent in beryllium metal;
in the ENDF/B-VIII.0 file for that material (Brown et al. 2018), Bragg edges are reported up to only about
1.5 eV.

 Figure 1. Comparison of the coherent elastic scattering cross section in Be metal in ENDF/B-VIII.0
 and ENDF/B-VIII.1.

As a result, the coherent elastic cross section is calculated to be zero beyond that point, as shown in Fig-
ure 1. This issue is resolved in the ENDF/B-VIII.1 file (Nobre et al., in press), which reports Bragg edges
up to 5 eV, and thus AMPX calculates the evaluator-intended coherent elastic cross section up to 5 eV.
The elastic cross section in the thermal energy region is the sum of the coherent and incoherent elastic
scattering contributions. The elastic cross section for Be metal for energies ranging from thermal to ep-
ithermal energies (0.1 eV—20 keV) is shown in Figure 2. The cross section for ENDF/B-VIII.0, for which
Bragg edge energies did not extend to 5 eV, drops to a very low value until the short collision time approx-
imation for the incoherent elastic scattering becomes active between 5 and 10 eV. The ENDF/B-VIII.1
evaluation removes this issue, and the cross section retains a reasonable magnitude throughout this energy
region.

 3. PROCESSING CODE-SPECIFIED THERMAL CUTOFF
As described in the introduction, the neutron cross section for a material is accounted for by different phys-
ical processes depending on the energy of the incident neutron. As the energy of the incident neutron tran-
sitions from the thermal region, where the TNSL physics is appropriate, into the fast energy region (such
as the immediately adjacent resolved resonance region), decisions must be made to treat this transition ap-
propriately. In the SCALE code system and the AMPX processing code, a thermal cutoff parameter spec-
ifies the energy at which the TNSL treatment is stopped (i.e., cut off) and the fast treatments begin. Be-
fore SCALE 6.3, this cutoff value was often 5 eV. In SCALE 6.3 and beyond, the default cutoff value has
been raised to 10 eV. Transport codes such as KENO allow users to specify a different thermal cutoff value
through input parameters.
Another wrinkle in the SCALE 6.3 development history is that the SCALE CE libraries were migrated
from a legacy binary format into a new format based on HDF5. In the course of preparing the ENDF/B-
VIII.1 SCALE libraries, a routine comparison between XML-based and HDF5-based CE libraries found
an unexpected effect on test case keff that was not due to the file formats themselves but rather differences
in the preparation of the thermal cross sections.
The C-12 in graphite, as shown in Figure 3, illustrates the issues that can arise based on the preparation of
the CE library, including the loading of the CE library by the transport code. The intended cross section
for transport should stop the thermal treatment at the cutoff energy specified by the user code—with the
intent that the fast (nonthermal) nuclide data would be used above that energy. However, for both a 5 eV
and a 10 eV thermal cutoff, Figure 3 shows that the fast data are not properly followed in this case. The
intended cross section at the thermal cutoff energy should align with the fast (nonthermal) cross section
independently of the thermal cutoff energy specified at loading time.
Focusing on the curve corresponding to loading the cross section with 5 eV thermal cutoff (the black curve),
it is tempting to consider that the correct cross section because it appears to proceed smoothly from ther-
mal energies to fast energies without discontinuities. However, that appearance of continuity arises be-
cause of an early step in the CE library processing. In that step, pieces of the cross section from the ther-
mal region are spliced together with pieces from the fast cross section. For C-12 in graphite with the 5 eV
thermal cutoff, there is a gap between 5 eV from the thermal data and 100 eV from the fast data, and linear
interpolation is performed in between those energies. The correct procedure should fully stop the thermal
treatment of the cross section at the specified cutoff energy, and proceed directly to the fast cross section
treatment at that cutoff energy.
The correct procedure has now been implemented in both the AMPX code and processing scripts, which
are undergoing further validation and code review.

 4. DENSE GRIDS FOR ACCURATE DOUBLE-DIFFERENTIAL CROSS SECTIONS
Before this project was initiated, an update was made to SCALE (to be released in SCALE version 6.3.3)
in which the adaptive grid algorithm for computing d2 σ/dE ′ dΩ from S (α, β) was improved. This im-
provement is especially important for cryogenic moderator materials, and it also improves the treatment
of the cross section in the short collision time regime.
The improvement of the grid for incoherent inelastic scattering was found to increase the computation time
in AMPX’s y12 module by a significant amount for the affected materials. This was judged to be accept-
able to ensure that the cross sections are now computed with correct values.
This effort also aligned with maintenance work in AMPX to use only the modern version of its jamaican
module, which transforms the complete double-differential representation of the cross section into the
probability distributions marginalized for angle and conditional on exit energy that are actually used in
CE transport codes.
The modern jamaican was found to take an inordinately long time (several days, as opposed to mere min-
utes as before) to compute probability distributions from particularly dense double-differential cross sec-
tions.
The approach to solution began by determining whether the criteria for considering two different energy–
angle distributions to be equivalent to each other are too strict. In particular, these equivalence criteria de-
termine whether the domain mesh is fine enough to allow linear interpolation between mesh points. If the
criteria are too strict, then functionally similar points in the distribution may be filling out the mesh more
densely than truly necessary.
To provide an illustrative example, an energy–angle distribution, produced by the module y12, is shown
for ENDF/B-VII.1 H-in-H2 O for a few exit energies that were observed to be rather close to each other.
Intuitively, it would seem that the middle exit energy should be close enough that linear interpolation be-
tween these curves is viable. However, the criteria as implemented deem these exit energy values to be too
far apart from each other to allow the middle exit energy to be removed.
The investigation concluded that the criteria implemented in the y12 module are acceptable, and necessary
to produce accurate double differential cross sections.
The inordinately long run times in the jamaican module were resolved and returned to reasonable run
times, even for very dense double differential cross section grids, by porting the corresponding code from
the older, Fortran-based implementation of jamaican.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
This work was undertaken to resolve and elucidate discontinuities in processed neutron cross sections at
the transition from thermal to fast neutron energies, as observed in the SCALE transport codes.
This report presents two major findings from this effort. The first category of discontinuity can only be re-
solved by improving the evaluated TNSL. The example of Be metal is a prominent case, and the updated
Be metal TNSL file from ENDF/B-VIII.1 improves that from ENDF/B-VIII.0 by extending the specifica-
tion of Bragg edge energies up to 5 eV, as intended in the ENDF format. This allows the transport codes to
use the cross section as intended but has a minimal effect on criticality for the cases considered.
The second category of discontinuity involves decisions made by the processing code in the preparation of
cross sections for transport. The SCALE transport codes include a thermal cutoff parameter exposed to the
users. However, it was discovered that the HDF5 version of the CE ENDF/B-VIII.0 library distributed with
SCALE 6.3 is not compatible with the flexibility of the thermal cutoff parameter. Furthermore, assump-
tions made during the course of processing, such as to join the end of the thermal region to some point in
the fast region, were found to have a significant effect on the processed cross sections and criticality. The
authors have implemented code that resolves these decisions to join the thermal cross section to the fast
cross section at the actual point of the thermal cutoff rather than assuming a linear interpolation over a gap
in energies.
Finally, another aspect of ensuring correct double-differential cross sections and their translation into CE
probability distributions is discussed. That investigation concluded that the algorithms for constructing the
double differential cross section grids in y12 have acceptable criteria. The jamaican module was subse-
quently modified in order to accommodate particularly dense double differential cross sections, allowing
jamaican to successfully compute energy-angle distributions for the SCALE CE libraries in a reasonable
run time.
The AMPX procedure for preparing SCALE CE libraries has been updated to consistently follow ther-
mal cross sections below the thermal cutoff and fast cross sections above the thermal cutoff. As discussed
above (see Figure 3, the correct procedure can produce cross sections that appear to have more severe dis-
continuities than those produced by the incorrect procedure. One consideration for future work to improve
the smoothness of the cross sections is to incorporate the uncertainties in the cross section. For exam-
ple, the uncertainty in the reported fast cross section may allow a small variation in that cross section that
would connect more smoothly with the processed thermal cross section.

 APPENDIX A. EXAMPLES OF CORRECTED CROSS SECTIONS
Some examples of the cross sections spanning from the thermal energies to epithermal energies are shown
in Figures A.1–A.4.
Each figure compares the cross section obtained via the incorrect procedure to the cross section obtained
via the now correct procedure.
To reiterate, it is tempting to notice that the incorrect procedure yields cross sections that appear to be
smoother than those obtained with the correct procedure. Despite the appearance of smoothness, that smooth-
ness is an artifact of interpolating over a gap in energies that appears in between the end of the thermal
evaluation and the beginning of the fast nuclide evaluation for certain reactions (namely, elastic scattering).
Above the designated thermal cutoff, the system must be described by the fast data. The previous proce-
dure’s interpolation produced a hybrid of the thermal and fast data which, unfortunately, is inconsistent
with the intent of either the thermal or fast evaluation.
The best resolution of this situation will be to work towards methods for simultaneously evaluating thermal
and fast data (Chapman and Arbanas 2025).
Until the methods for such simultaneous evaluation are put into practice, the processing codes must follow
the data appropriate for each energy region.
