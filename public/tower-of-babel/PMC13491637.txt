Epicardial fat is associated with prevalent heart failure and adverse cardiac remodeling in atrial fibrillation
Authors: Alexander P Hamel, Taylor Orwig, Bao Ngoc Tran, Bella Vogt, Ethan Senser, Shruti Hegde, Lara Kovell, Jung Ae Lee, Matthew W Parker, Timothy Fitzgibbons, David D McManus, Khanh-Van T Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) frequently coexists with cardiometabolic conditions such as diabetes and heart failure (HF), which increase morbidity and mortality. While body mass index (BMI) is widely used for risk stratification, regional adiposity measures including epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) and EAT indexed to body surface area (iEAT) may better reflect local cardiac fat burden and its association with conditions of adverse cardiac remodeling.MethodsWe identified 348 participants from an AF registry with EAT area quantified from a pre-ablation CT scan. Echocardiographic parameters and clinical covariates were collected from the electronic medical record. Adiposity measures were analyzed in tertiles. Multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, and race assessed associations between adiposity and outcomes. ROC curves were used to compare predictive performance. Multivariable linear regression analysis assessed echocardiographic parameters, including left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction, LV dimensions, LV mass, left atrial anteroposterior diameter, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure.ResultsAmong 348 participants, higher BMI, EAT and iEAT were each associated with older age and increased prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidity. In multivariable models, the highest tertiles of EAT and iEAT were each independently associated with heart failure [EAT OR 4.83 (95% CI 1.82–12.84); iEAT OR 4.40 (95% CI 1.70–11.43)]. ROC analysis showed that iEAT best discriminated heart failure (AUC = 0.74), while BMI best discriminated diabetes (AUC = 0.69). In adjusted models, all three adiposity metrics were significantly associated with higher LV mass; EAT and iEAT were additionally linked to higher pulmonary artery systolic pressure.ConclusionIn patients with atrial fibrillation, epicardial adiposity measures were more strongly associated with prevalent heart failure than BMI, possibly due to its association with adverse cardiac remodeling. In contrast, BMI remains a stronger predictor of diabetes. Incorporating epicardial fat may enhance risk stratification for heart failure in patients with atrial fibrillation beyond BMI alone. Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia, projected to affect 12.1 million people in the United States by 2030, with an annual cost of $22 billion. It is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1–3] AF often coexists with cardiometabolic conditions such as diabetes mellitus and heart failure (HF), both of which independently worsen patient outcomes [4, 5]. While AF and HF share overlapping pathophysiologic pathways, including atrial and ventricular remodeling, systemic inflammation, and neurohormonal activation, accurately identifying AF patients at highest risk for HF remains an unmet clinical need [6, 7].

Obesity, commonly assessed using body mass index (BMI), is a strong predictor of both diabetes and HF [8]. Elevated BMI reflects overall adiposity and is associated with insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and hemodynamic stress [9, 10]. However, BMI is limited as an adiposity measure because it does not distinguish between fat and lean mass or capture the regional distribution of adipose tissue [11]. In particular, it does not account for ectopic fat depots such as epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), which may directly influence myocardial structure and function [12].

EAT is a visceral fat depot located between the myocardium and the visceral pericardium. Due to its proximity to the heart and shared microcirculation, EAT can exert local effects through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), fibrogenic factors (e.g., TGF-β), and lipotoxic mediators [13–15]. These bioactive substances contribute to myocardial fibrosis, left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, left atrial (LA) dilation, elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressures (PASP), and impaired diastolic function, factors that characterize adverse cardiac remodeling and may promote HF development in patients with AF [16–20] EAT can be further indexed to body surface area (iEAT) to limit the effects of body size variation [21].

Prior studies have evaluated the relationships between BMI, epicardial adipose tissue, and cardiometabolic outcomes. However, few studies have directly compared systemic and regional adiposity measures within the same atrial fibrillation cohort or evaluated their relative associations with both heart failure and diabetes. In addition, the role of iEAT as a normalized measure of cardiac fat burden has not been well characterized. Furthermore, the extent to which these adiposity measures relate to echocardiographic markers of adverse cardiac remodeling and provide discriminatory value beyond traditional clinical risk factors remains incompletely understood.

In this study, we evaluated the relative performance of BMI, EAT, and iEAT in predicting prevalent diabetes and heart failure among patients with AF. Furthermore, we investigated the relationship between epicardial adiposity and echocardiographic parameters of cardiac remodeling, including left ventricular mass, left atrial size, pulmonary artery systolic pressure, and diastolic function.

Methods

Study population

We analyzed data from participants enrolled in a prospective AF registry at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center (UMMC) between 2011 and 2017 (Fig. 1).

Study cohort flow and analytic framework. 348 participants who were enrolled in the atrial fibrillation (AF) registry had complete data on body mass index (BMI) and epicardial adipose tissue (EAT). BMI was stratified into tertiles, and clinical characteristics were compared across groups. EAT was indexed to body surface area (BSA) to calculate indexed EAT (iEAT), which was also categorized into tertiles for analysis. Patients without EAT measurements were excluded from iEAT-based analyses

Data for this study were derived from an AF registry of participants who underwent pre-ablation cardiac CT imaging between 2011 and 2017, we included 348 patients who had complete data for BMI, EAT quantification, and echocardiographic measurements. Participants were included if they had EAT data available. No additional exclusions were made beyond the availability of EAT data required for analysis. This represents a selected cohort with available EAT data. The study flow is depicted in Fig. 1. This was a cross sectional study using previously collected clinical and imaging data. No interventions were assigned, and the study did not meet the criteria for a clinical trial. The study was approved by the UMass Institutional Review Board (IRB#00003865), and informed consent was obtained by each participant in the study.

Clinical variables

Demographic data and clinical characteristics such as, age, self-reported race, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, HF, coronary artery disease, stroke, obstructive sleep apnea, and peripheral vascular disease were abstracted from the electronic medical record. HF in this cohort includes both HFrEF and HFpEF, as determined by clinician diagnosis at the time of assessment, and classification reflects real-world clinical documentation rather than guideline-adjudicated phenotyping. The CHA₂DS₂-VASc score was calculated for each patient using established clinical criteria [16].

EAT quantification

EAT was quantified from CT scans acquired using a Siemens Somatom Definition Flash 128 dual-source CT scanner as previously described [17]. Contrast-enhanced images were obtained using standardized acquisition protocols. EAT was defined as adipose tissue located between the myocardium and visceral pericardium, segmented within a predefined Hounsfield unit (HU) range [20, 22]. Manual tracing was performed in a standardized four-chamber equivalent view to delineate EAT compartments around the LA, LV, and right ventricle (RV). Inter- and intra-observer reproducibility for EAT measurement were high (r = 0.89 and 0.95, respectively). EAT was then indexed [23] to BSA to derive iEAT. BSA was calculated using the Mosteller formula: BSA (m²) = √(height [cm] × weight [kg] / 3600). iEAT was derived as iEAT (cm²/m²) = EAT area / BSA.

Echocardiographic measurements

Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography was performed according to American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [21, 24]. Echocardiography measurements were collected from images obtained using the ie33 xMatrix (Philips North America, Bothell, WA, USA) and Voluson S6 (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) ultrasound systems. All measurements were performed in the TomTec Arena v2.20 (TOMTEC Corporation USA, Chicago, IL, USA). We measured LA AP diameter, LV internal diameters in diastole (LVIDd) and systole (LVIDs), posterior wall thickness (PWT), interventricular septal thickness (IVSTd), early ( E) and active (A) components of the mitral inflow velocity for patients in sinus rhythm, early diastolic mitral annular tissue Doppler velocity (e’), and calculated LV mass using the formula: LV mass = 0.8 × [1.04 × ((LVID + PWT + IVSTd)³ − (LVID)³)] + 0.6 g. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was calculated using the tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity and an estimation of right atrial pressure based on the inferior vena cava size and collapsibility [21].

Baseline characteristics of study participants stratified by iEAT

Baseline characteristics for the full study cohort were described in Supplemental Table 1. For continuous variables, one-way analysis of variance was used based on the distribution of data. For categorical variables, chi-squared tests were applied. Multiple comparison-adjusted p-values were reported where applicable. We conducted multivariable logistic regression models to assess associations between adiposity measures and two outcomes of interest: HF and diabetes. Tertile-based modeling was selected a priori to allow for potential non-linear relationships between adiposity measures and outcomes and to enhance clinical interpretability by comparing groups with relatively low, intermediate, and high adiposity burden. In addition, given the modest sample size and the skewed distributions of EAT and iEAT, categorization into tertiles reduced sensitivity to extreme values. The lowest tertile served as the reference group. Three models were built: (1) unadjusted, (2) Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and race, and (3) Model 2 additionally adjusted for BMI when EAT or iEAT was the predictor or iEAT with BMI was the predictor. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess the independent associations of BMI, EAT, and iEAT with each echocardiographic parameter. Each adiposity measure was modeled separately as the independent variable, and each echocardiographic parameter was modeled as the dependent variable. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and chronic kidney disease.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), defined as the area under the plot of sensitivity (y-axis) against 1 − specificity (x-axis). AUC values were interpreted using established cutoffs: 0.5 indicating no discrimination, 0.7–0.8 acceptable discrimination, and > 0.8 good discrimination [25]. For each model, optimal cutoff values with corresponding sensitivity and specificity determined and reported in Supplemental Tables 3 and 4 [26].

Analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.13) and relevant statistical packages including panda, numpy, scipy, seaborn, and matplotlib. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess normality of continuous variables. As many variables departed from normality, we report both mean (± SD) in main tables and median (IQR) in supplemental tables as a sensitivity analysis. To address potential multiple comparisons, Holm–Bonferroni-corrected P-values were reported for pairwise tertile comparisons (Supplemental Table 5). Adiposity measures were additionally modeled as continuous variables in sequential clinical models to confirm the robustness of tertile-based findings (Supplemental Table 6).

Results

Cohort characteristics

We analyzed baseline clinical and cardiometabolic characteristics stratified by BMI, EAT, and iEAT among 348 participants. Across BMI tertiles (Table 1), participants in the highest tertile had significantly greater EAT (10.15 ± 4.80 cm² vs. 6.36 ± 3.19 cm², P < 0.0001) and iEAT (4.29 ± 2.02 cm²/m² vs. 3.23 ± 1.64 cm²/m², P = 0.0001) compared to those in the lowest tertile. The highest BMI group also showed a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus (33.6% vs. 12.9%, P = 0.0003), hypertension (90.5% vs. 65.5%, P < 0.0001), and obstructive sleep apnea (56.0% vs. 25.9%, P < 0.0001). A non-significant trend toward increased heart failure prevalence was noted (19.0% vs. 8.6%, P = 0.0518), while no differences were observed for blood pressure or history of stroke, CAD, or PVD. There was a borderline difference in sex distribution (P = 0.051), with more males in the lowest BMI tertile.

Stratification by unindexed EAT area (Table 2) demonstrated consistent associations with adverse cardiometabolic profiles. Higher EAT tertiles were associated with greater iEAT values (P < 0.0001), older age (P = 0.042), and higher BMI (P = 0.0002). The highest EAT tertile had a significantly higher prevalence of CAD (30.2% vs. 13.8%, P = 0.0074), dyslipidemia (81.9% vs. 56.9%, P = 0.0002), diabetes (31.0% vs. 13.8%, P = 0.0044), heart failure (23.3% vs. 6.0%, P = 0.0012), and hypertension (91.4% vs. 61.2%, P < 0.0001) compared to those in the lowest tertile. Higher EAT was also associated with increased rates of COPD (P = 0.0146), anemia (P = 0.0177), and obstructive sleep apnea (P = 0.0107). CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores increased across EAT tertiles (P < 0.0001), indicating higher thromboembolic risk in those with greater EAT burden. No differences in sex, race, or blood pressure were observed across EAT strata.

When participants were stratified by iEAT tertiles (Table 3), higher iEAT was associated with older age (P = 0.0018), greater BMI (P = 0.0961), and higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores (P < 0.0001). Individuals in the highest iEAT tertile exhibited significantly higher prevalence of dyslipidemia (80.2% vs. 57.8%, P = 0.001), CAD (29.3% vs. 13.8%, P = 0.0138), heart failure (22.4% vs. 6.0%, P = 0.0018), and hypertension (89.7% vs. 61.2%, P < 0.0001) compared to those in the lowest tertile. Additionally, anemia (P = 0.0058) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (P = 0.0056) were more common with increasing iEAT. Notably, BMI, sex, race, and blood pressure were not significantly different across iEAT tertiles.

Association with heart failure

In multivariable logistic regression models, higher tertiles of BMI, EAT, and iEAT were each associated with increased odds of heart failure. In unadjusted models, the highest tertile of BMI was associated with a 2.48-fold increased odds of heart failure compared to the lowest tertile (OR 2.48, 95% CI 1.12–5.51, P = 0.026, Table 4).

Association with diabetes

In unadjusted models, individuals in the highest tertile of BMI had significantly higher odds of diabetes compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR 3.41, 95% CI 1.75–6.63, P < 0.001, Table 5).

This association remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, and race (Model 1 OR 4.12, 95% CI 2.05–8.27, P < 0.001) and after further adjustment for iEAT (Model 2 OR 3.78, 95% CI 1.84–7.75, P < 0.001), suggesting BMI is independently associated with diabetes risk.

EAT was also associated with diabetes. In fully adjusted models (Model 2, adjusted for age, sex, race and BMI), individuals in the highest EAT tertile had over twofold increased odds of diabetes (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.10–4.36, P = 0.025). However, the association was not significant for the intermediate tertile. In contrast, iEAT showed more modest and inconsistent associations. In unadjusted models, both intermediate and highest tertiles were significantly associated with increased diabetes risk (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.06–4.09, P = 0.033 for both). However, these associations were attenuated in adjusted models. Only the intermediate tertile remained statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 2 OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.00–4.03, P = 0.050), while the highest tertile was not statistically significant (OR 1.76, 95% CI 0.87–3.55, P = 0.115). ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of BMI, EAT, and iEAT for identifying individuals with diabetes (Fig. 2).

Among the three metrics, BMI demonstrated the highest area under the curve (AUC = 0.69), indicating superior discriminatory performance. EAT showed moderate predictive ability with an AUC of 0.62, while iEAT had the lowest predictive value (AUC = 0.59).

Additionally, to assess the incremental value of epicardial adiposity measures beyond established clinical risk factors, we compared the discrimination of sequential models for prevalent heart failure (Fig. 3). The baseline clinical model (Model 1), which included age, sex, BMI, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and chronic kidney disease, demonstrated an AUC of 0.70. Addition of EAT (Model 2) resulted in a modest increase in discrimination (AUC = 0.75), while inclusion of iEAT (Model 3) yielded a similar improvement (AUC = 0.75). Discussion

In this analysis of patients with AF, we examined the associations of three adiposity metrics, BMI, EAT, and iEAT, with prevalent HF, diabetes, and echocardiographic indices of cardiac remodeling. Our findings demonstrate that while BMI was most strongly associated with diabetes, iEAT and EAT showed stronger cross-sectional associations with prevalent HF and structural cardiac abnormalities. These results suggest important differences between systemic and regional adiposity measures in their relationships with systemic and local disease burden among individuals with AF.

We found that both iEAT and EAT were significantly associated with prevalent HF in unadjusted and adjusted models. Participants in the highest tertile of iEAT had over fourfold increased odds of HF compared to those in the lowest tertile, and this association remained robust after adjusting for age, sex, race, and BMI. A similar pattern was observed with EAT. These findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that EAT may exert paracrine and mechanical effects on the myocardium due to its anatomical proximity and shared microcirculation with underlying cardiac structures, though our data do not establish a mechanistic or causal link [27–29]. Unlike BMI, which reflects generalized adiposity, epicardial fat measures directly quantify local cardiac fat burden and may more accurately capture adipose-mediated myocardial stress [30]. Furthermore, the attenuation of the BMI and HF association after inclusion of iEAT in the regression model may suggest shared variance or confounding by regional epicardial fat rather than mediation.

In contrast, BMI remained the strongest correlate of diabetes across all models, with effect sizes exceeding those observed for EAT and iEAT. This finding is consistent with the established role of total body and abdominal adiposity in the development of insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and altered glucose metabolism. This pattern supports the idea that while epicardial fat may reflect aspects of metabolic dysfunction, its association with diabetes is weaker than traditional systemic measures like BMI in this cohort.

We find that the discriminatory power of BMI for heart failure is modest, reflecting its inability to capture the heterogeneity in adipose tissue distribution and metabolic activity [31]. Differences in abundance and activity among fat deposits––including brown, beige and white adipose tissue––may play a critical role in cardiometabolic risk in ways not reflected by BMI [32].

ROC analyses further illustrated the differential predictive performance of the adiposity measures. iEAT and EAT yielded the higher AUC than BMI, suggesting regional adiposity measures are more closely associated with myocardial dysfunction, whereas systemic adiposity is more strongly related to metabolic disease. This pattern mirrors other disease processes such as hepatic steatosis whereby indexed liver fat deposition more strongly predicts metabolic dysfunction than does BMI [33]. These findings highlight the need to align adiposity measures with relevant biological pathways, distinguishing regional from systemic effects. Nonetheless, the overall discriminative ability of all measures was modest, emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation and further investigation.

Importantly, epicardial fat demonstrated significant associations with several echocardiographic markers of adverse remodeling. Higher EAT and iEAT were associated with increased LV mass, greater LA diameter, higher PASP, and higher E/e′ ratio suggestive of impaired diastolic function. The relationship between iEAT and increased LA size further aligns with the AF phenotype, where atrial stretch and fibrosis contribute to arrhythmogenesis and progressive atrial myopathy [34, 35]. While these findings are consistent with prior hypotheses regarding the relationship between epicardial fat and myocardial structure, they remain observational and may be influenced by unmeasured confounders, including HF subtype or severity, AF burden, valvular disease, and medical therapy. However, the stronger association of epicardial fat with these structural indices, relative to BMI, may suggest a localized impact of epicardial fat on cardiac remodeling, independent of overall body size or composition.

The findings also underscore the heterogeneity of fat depots and their varying contributions to disease processes. Whereas BMI captures a composite of lean and fat mass, EAT and iEAT provide direct quantification of a fat depot implicated in myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, and mechanical compression. Thus, inclusion of iEAT or EAT in cardiovascular risk assessment models may enhance the identification of patients at risk for HF in patients with AF. Additionally, EAT measurement is likely to have utility in patients with atrial fibrillation who are already undergoing cardiac CT imaging, such as for ablation planning, where it can be derived without additional cost or testing. In this setting, increased EAT or iEAT may help identify individuals at higher risk of heart failure and adverse cardiac remodeling beyond traditional clinical risk factors.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this study was retrospective and observational in design, and heart failure was assessed as a prevalent rather than incident outcome; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of patients with atrial fibrillation undergoing CT for ablation planning at a single center, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of these findings to broader AF populations, non-ablation cohorts, or individuals without AF. We anticipate that the observed associations may be most directly applicable to patients with established AF, though they may also provide insight into earlier-stage AF and potentially to non-AF populations. Third, although multivariable models were adjusted for key demographic and clinical covariates, factors related to heart failure severity or phenotype, treatment, valvular disease, and atrial fibrillation burden were not fully captured, raising the possibility of residual confounding and precluding reliable stratification by HF subtype. Finally, epicardial fat was quantified using a single-slice, area-based approach rather than volumetric assessment, which may introduce measurement variability and may not fully reflect total epicardial fat burden.

This study adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that EAT is not merely a passive bystander but may play an active role in adverse myocardial remodeling. Our findings suggest that iEAT, in particular, may serve as a more specific imaging biomarker than BMI for identifying high-risk AF patients susceptible to developing HF and structural heart disease. These findings support the need for prospective studies using volumetric fat quantification and incident HF outcomes to clarify the temporal and clinical significance of epicardial fat in AF.
