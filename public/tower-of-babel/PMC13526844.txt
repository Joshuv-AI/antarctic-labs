A manufacturability-informed topology framework for AI-guided design of fibrous network materials
Authors: Yunhao Yang, Jing Ren, Leitao Cao, Xuankai Zhang, Chen Huang, Xinquan Jiang, Shengjie Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing fibrous network materials that are simultaneously high-performance and manufacturable remains a fundamental challenge due to the complex coupling between topology, mechanics, and fabrication constraints. Here, we introduce the Regular Fibrous Network Framework, a manufacturability-informed and physics-consistent artificial intelligence framework that bridges digital topology, mechanical prediction, and physical realization. Within this framework, the Topology-Preserving Network Construction algorithm formalizes Eulerian circuit continuity for single-fiber fabrication and transforms digital topologies into knitting- and three-dimensional-printing-compatible architectures. An automated finite-element-analysis pipeline and a physics-inspired graph neural network accurately capture nonlinear J-type and C-type load-displacement behaviors, while a reinforcement learning module performs inverse design within minutes, achieving approximately 50% higher strength and approximately 20% lower mass compared with initial designs. Extending the framework with QuadriFlow-based surface mapping enables direct projection of optimized two-dimensional networks onto curved three-dimensional geometries. This approach is experimentally validated through stereolithography and fused deposition modeling. By integrating manufacturability constraints, physics-inspired learning, and artificial-intelligence-driven optimization into a unified pipeline, the proposed framework provides a generalizable paradigm for knittable, printable, and programmable fibrous network materials, offering a pathway toward autonomous and high-efficiency design of architected materials across length scales.

Introduction

Network-structured materials are ubiquitous across scales, from molecular assemblies and fibrous architectures to biological tissues and engineered devices1–4. Examples such as extracellular matrices, spider silk webs, woven textiles, biomedical scaffolds, and lattice frameworks illustrate how complex connectivity and looped topology enable multichannel load transfer and nonlinear mechanical behavior5–9. Yet, designing networks that are simultaneously manufacturable, predictable, and optimizable remains a fundamental challenge10–12. Existing approaches often separate mathematical design from fabrication feasibility, lack a unified structure-property description under process constraints, and face exponentially growing design complexity13,14. Existing generative frameworks typically consider fabrication constraints only after generation, which can restrict design space coverage when exploring feasibility boundaries15,16.

Graph theory provides a natural language for describing fibrous networks, capturing both local connectivity and global load paths that can be directly linked to knitting, winding, or additive-manufacturing processes17–20. However, current frameworks rarely integrate manufacturability with mechanical functionality21,22. In nature and engineered systems, topological constraints dictate performance: spider webs combine radial and spiral paths for high specific strength and localized damage tolerance23, collagen and tendon networks exhibit nonlinear strain stiffening through heterogeneous node distributions24–26, and woven fabrics achieve directional stiffness via ordered pathways27,28. Engineered lattices and architected metamaterials similarly employ multiloop connectivity to realize lightweight strength and recoverability9,29–31. While generative AI has recently reshaped the discovery of atomic-scale materials and pixel-based metamaterials, extending this data-driven success to macroscopic networks constrained by the continuous paths of manufacturability remains a missing piece of the design puzzle.

Here, we introduce the Regular Fibrous Network framework (REFINe)—a unified, manufacturability-informed, and physics-consistent AI framework that bridges digital topology, mechanical prediction, and physical realization. Using graph theory, REFINe formalizes the topological rules that ensure Eulerian circuit continuity for single-fiber fabrication and incorporates Topology-Preserving Network Construction (TOPNet), a graph-based algorithm that transforms digital networks into knitting- and printing-compatible architectures while preserving connectivity. Within REFINe, an automated 3D finite element (FE) pipeline and a physics-inspired graph neural network (GNN) enable accurate topology-mechanics mapping through decoupled response representations, reproducing J-type and C-type nonlinear mechanical behaviors32. Coupled with a reinforcement-learning (RL) optimization module, the framework performs inverse design of high-performance fibrous networks within minutes, achieving ~50% higher strength and ~20% lower mass. Finally, a QuadriFlow-based surface mapping method extends optimized 2D topologies onto 3D geometries of varying curvature, experimentally validated by 3D-printed prototypes33.

The REFINe framework establishes a general paradigm for knittable, printable, and programmable fibrous networks, uniting manufacturability constraints, physics- inspired learning, and AI-driven optimization into a single closed-loop pipeline (Fig. 1). Results

Manufacturability rules governing topological feasibility

Within the REFINe framework, manufacturability is the foundational constraint linking digital topology and fabrication reality. Although regular fibrous network structures can, in theory, assume numerous topological configurations, only a limited subset can be realized through knitting or additive manufacturing. REFINe specifically targets continuous-path processes, including continuous fiber laying, and continuous fused deposition modeling, where uninterrupted fiber deposition eliminates joint-induced weak points (see Supplementary Note 3.6 for manufacturing details). These processes impose strict geometric and physical limits that determine whether a designed network can be physically fabricated. To formalize these constraints, we developed a graph-theoretic description that defines the topological feasibility boundary of regular fibrous networks (Fig. 1).

Topology-preserving strategy for network construction

Building upon the manufacturability constraints defined within the REFINe framework, we developed TOPNet—a graph-based algorithm that transforms digital designs into knittable and printable fibrous networks. In the first step, Eulerian base-unit generation, a square grid serves as the structural template. Five equally spaced control points are defined along each edge, and their planar coordinates form a ten-dimensional vector representing local deformation. By applying identical displacement patterns to all four edges, the grid undergoes a topology-preserving geometric transformation that preserves node connectivity and ensures all vertices have even degrees \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(d\left(v\right)\equiv 0\mathrel{{\mathrm{mod}}}2\right)$$\end{document}dv≡0mod2, satisfying the Eulerian condition required for single-fiber fabrication; this topology preservation enables the entire network to be deposited in a single uninterrupted cycle.

The second step, regular tiling, scales this base unit into larger networks. Uniform tiling arranges identical units in an n × n array, whereas hybrid tiling combines units with varied parameters to generate heterogeneous structures, while still preserving the even-degree condition required for an Eulerian circuit, because every base unit is topology-preserving (see Supplementary Note 1). It should be noted that during mesh assembly, adjacent cells share common boundaries: the right edge of the left cell coincides with the left edge of the right cell, while the bottom edge of the upper cell overlaps with the top edge of the lower cell. Although this overlap makes the corresponding nodes appear to have odd degrees geometrically, these overlapping edges are precisely identified and separated into two distinct edges during subsequent deformation analysis (final degrees remain ≤8). As a result, all nodes retain even degrees in the topological sense, thereby satisfying the Eulerian topology and manufacturability (Fig. 2).

During fabrication, overlapping fibers are joined by thermal bonding or adhesion, forming anchors that stabilize the structure. The anchor-point regulation step modulates the number and distribution of these junctions without disrupting the continuous knitting path. By projecting fibers onto a stacking matrix and adjusting the ten deformation parameters, TOPNet controls local overlap frequency, thereby tuning anchor density to meet desired stiffness or flexibility.

To adapt the network to different geometries, boundary fitting maps outer nodes to a predefined contour and repositions interior nodes using Laplacian smoothing. This maintains topological integrity while enabling the network to conform to curved or irregular shapes, extending the versatility of REFINe to customized designs.

Finally, continuous path planning determines the fiber trajectory for manufacturing. Without preserving Eulerian topology, the deformed networks would contain numerous odd-degree vertices, requiring computationally expensive algorithms such as Guan’s route problem34 algorithm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{O}}}}\left({n}^{3}\right)$$\end{document}On3 to identify traversable paths. Exploiting the topology-preserving characteristics of regular fibrous network structures, the Canonical Grid Mapping (CGM) routine algorithm in TOPNet directly maps the canonical grid path to its deformed counterpart, transforming exhaustive traversal into structured sequence generation. This reduces complexity to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{O}}}}\left(n\right)$$\end{document}On and provides a single, uninterrupted path suitable for both knitting and 3D printing.

Automated multiscale simulation linking topology to mechanics

To bridge digital topology and mechanical performance, the REFINe framework incorporates an automated multiscale simulation pipeline that establishes a direct mapping from network geometry to mechanical response. The modeling targets physically realizable structures: node-consolidated TPU networks fabricated via continuous-path manufacturing (fused filament fabrication, automated fiber laying), where fibers are bonded at junctions to form integral architectures. In the topology verification step, a line-intersection algorithm detects all fiber junctions within a 10 cm × 10 cm normalized domain, while redundant or invalid loops are automatically removed using self-intersection detection and the Shoelace-area filter. The cleaned geometry is converted into a 2D constraint sketch that preserves connectivity but minimizes redundant line segments through colinear-point simplification, ensuring smooth topology transfer for 3D modeling.

During 3D modeling and meshing, the sketch is extruded into a volumetric solid via a straight-sweep algorithm and discretized with adaptive C3D8H hexahedral elements. Mesh resolution dynamically adapts to local geometric complexity, maintaining surface-interior continuity essential for capturing realistic stress gradients. Boundary constraints are then applied automatically: the top and bottom 10% of nodes are coupled to reference points through Coupling Constraints, ensuring homogeneous load transmission during deformation.

The uniaxial tensile simulation employs a Newton–Raphson solver for large-strain nonlinear deformation, combining a Mooney–Rivlin hyperelastic model with plasticity to represent the mechanical behavior of thermoplastic polyurethane-like (TPU-like) elastomers. Adaptive stabilization suppresses local instability and guarantees numerical convergence. Entire simulations are executed using Python-based automation, providing reproducible, operator-independent modeling for hundreds of regular fibrous network structure configurations generated by TOPNet.

Compared with conventional beam-element networks, the 3D solid-element approach improves geometric fidelity and numerical stability. Beam models typically diverge beyond 5–10% strain, whereas the 3D implementation accurately reproduces deformation up to 150% strain. As shown in Fig. 3b, the simulations capture density-dependent deformation modes: selective fiber stretching in low-density networks, uniform elongation and necking in medium densities, and central fiber bundling with peripheral extension in high densities. Digital image correlation with 3D-printed TPU samples confirms a structural similarity index of 0.92, notably higher than that of mass-spring analogs (0.76; Supplementary Figs 6 and 7).

The comparison between simulated and experimental load–displacement curves (Fig. 3c) further validates model accuracy, achieving R2 ≈ 0.94 in the 0–200 N regime. Overestimation at higher loads (R2 = 0.79 overall) arises from complex plasticity and fracture phenomena that represent <1% of the data, and thus negligibly affect downstream training of the physics-inspired GNN within REFINe. Out of 900 randomly generated structures, 855 produced stable FE results (Fig. 3e, f). Even at identical densities, different base-unit arrangements yielded markedly distinct strengths: late-percolating networks (step >0.2) carried 15–50 N, whereas early-percolating networks (step <0.1) reached 250–275 N—a maximum difference of 18×.

Distinct topologies also exhibited unique load–strain signatures. Loop-rich networks displayed J-shaped curves-low initial stiffness followed by strain stiffening-analogous to soft biological tissues such as skin. Simulation results (Fig. 3g) attribute this to loosely connected short fibers that allow topological reconfiguration before full alignment along the loading axis. In contrast, fiber-bundle-dominated networks exhibited C-shaped behavior, with high initial stiffness and later softening (Fig. 3h), reflecting pre-aligned fibers that form efficient but rigid load paths. While intermediate responses naturally exist across this continuum, these two characteristic extremes serve as interpretable descriptors for discussing how topology governs mechanical response. Such architectures, reminiscent of metallic elasticity, are promising for high-stiffness, low-strain structural applications such as aerospace and precision components.

The GNN employs a dual-path learning architecture that decouples the mechanical prediction into shape and magnitude components. Each fibrous network generated by the TOPNet algorithm is represented as an undirected graph where nodes correspond to fiber junctions and edges represent segments. Meanwhile, load–displacement curves from FE simulations are decomposed into dimensionless shape vectors and amplitude scalars through polynomial basis projection. Two parallel GNNs are trained to regress these components independently and are recombined to reconstruct the full mechanical response curve. This physics-guided separation associates curve morphology and strength level with distinct structural descriptors, improving interpretability and applicability. This split mirrors classical dimensional analysis, where a dimensionless shape function is scaled by a stiffness-dependent factor.

The correlation matrix (Fig. 4b) illustrates how topological features (see Supplementary Table 3 for detailed definitions) influence the load capacity at 150% strain (LC150, measured under axial tension of the fibrous network at 150% strain). Connectivity-related metrics show strong positive correlations: total fiber length (S₁), box-counting fractal dimension (S₂), and total node count (S₃) all exhibit r = 0.5, while number of 4-cycles (S₄) shows r = 0.4, indicating that higher path redundancy increases stress distribution efficiency. The edge-betweenness Gini coefficient (S₅, quantifying inequality in load-path utilization) exhibits weaker positive correlation (r = 0.2), suggesting limited contribution from load-path uniformity. Conversely, geometric and structural features display negative correlations with LC150: coefficient of variation of pore area (S₆, r = −0.2), mean fiber length (S₇), radius of gyration (S₈), and maximum edge betweenness (S₉) (all r = −0.3), implying that larger structural elements and key bottlenecks weaken mechanical strength. Similarly, average shortest path length from loading boundary (S₁₀, r = −0.4), boundary fiber ratio (S₁₁, r = −0.4), and maximum pore area ratio (S₁₂, r = −0.5) show stronger negative correlations, revealing that elongated load paths, peripheral clustering, and large voids significantly reduce network stability.

However, single features only partially explain strength variation. The most correlated feature—total fiber length (S₁)—yields (R2 = 0.25), accounting for only 25% of LC150 variance. This confirms that regular fibrous network structures’ mechanics arise from multi-feature coupling, which the GNN effectively captures. Feature cross-correlation also highlights hierarchical relationships, such as between total node number (S₃) and 4-cycle count (S₄) (r = 0.8), reflecting that closed loops serve as independent indicators of connection density, and between mean fiber length (S₇) and boundary fiber ratio (S₁₁) (r = 0.8), suggesting geometric bias toward edge alignment.

Based on FE-derived curve morphology, the dataset is divided into two characteristic hardening modes (Fig. 4c–e), which represent two end-member patterns along a continuous spectrum rather than a strict binary separation. Here, k₁ and k₂ represent the characteristic slopes of the first and second mechanical stages, respectively. These correspond to networks with initially loose fibers that gradually align and twist under loading. Reinforcement learning for inverse design of fibrous networks

To enable performance-driven inverse design, the REFINe framework integrates a RL module coupled with the physics-inspired GNN predictor, allowing direct optimization of fibrous-network topologies within manufacturable design space. The RL action space embeds topology-preserving deformation constraints inherited from the TOPNet algorithm, ensuring that all generated networks remain topologically continuous and fabrication-ready.

While TOPNet can generate networks at any grid density, a 3× 33×3 configuration serves as a sufficient representative volume element (Supplementary Note 3). For most practical applications—ranging from high-performance fabrics and soft robotic lattices to tissue-engineering scaffolds—optimization aims to achieve high strength at minimal weight. As shown in Fig. 5a, each regular fibrous network with fixed density (n = 3) is parameterized by ten deformation variables, each ranging from −0.50 to +0.50 (step = 0.01), yielding 10110 possible combinations. Exhaustive exploration of this vast space is infeasible; therefore, a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm is employed to operate directly in the continuous ten-dimensional action space of deformation parameters. Two optimization modes were examined (Fig. 5c). In Loss-Weight Mode, both strength enhancement and weight reduction were pursued simultaneously. The training reward trajectory exhibited three phases: an exploratory stage (0–1800 steps) near zero reward; a middle stage (1800–2600 steps) showing stepwise gains from ≈1 to 6; and a final convergence phase (2600–3000 steps). The optimized structures achieved a 46% increase in load capacity (57 → 83 N) and a 19% reduction in mass (5.9 → 4.8 g). To elucidate the optimization dynamics, the full RL trajectory was projected into topological-feature space (Fig. 5d–f). The node count increased by ≈5–18%, generating additional load-transfer pathways; non-monotonic oscillations in Loss-Weight Mode reflect trade-offs between light-weighting and reinforcement, whereas Fixed-Weight Mode exhibited smoother incremental growth. Overall, these changes reveal that the RL agent autonomously evolves networks toward denser connectivity, shorter fibers, and more compact architectures—structural signatures associated with enhanced mechanical efficiency.

Despite differences in convergence patterns, both optimization modes ultimately converge toward stronger, denser, and more efficient network topologies. A controlled comparison with random search, Bayesian optimization, and CMA-ES confirmed that SAC achieves the highest peak solution quality and the fastest convergence among the benchmarked optimizers under the same evaluation budget (Supplementary Note 5). These results demonstrate that the REFINe framework, by coupling physics-inspired prediction with RL-based exploration, enables data-efficient, fabrication-feasible, and performance-guided inverse design of fibrous networks, reducing design cycles from months to minutes.

While the REFINe framework primarily operates in a two-dimensional design space, many real-world applications require conformal 3D architectures. Typical examples include shoe uppers that must conform to a curved last and biomedical scaffolds that replicate complex anatomical surfaces. To meet these requirements, REFINe integrates surface-mapping techniques that convert optimized 2D network topologies generated by the TOPNet algorithm into continuous, manufacturable 3D geometries.

Discussion

This study establishes REFINe, a unified manufacturability-informed and physics-consistent framework that bridges digital topology, mechanical response, and physical fabrication of fibrous network materials. By integrating the TOPNet algorithm, REFINe formalizes the topological boundaries of manufacturable design through Eulerian circuit continuity, algebraic connectivity, and geometric precision, ensuring that generated architectures remain compatible with single-fiber knitting and additive manufacturing. This elevates manufacturability from a post-generation feasibility check to a construction principle at the design stage, imposing explicit boundaries on the design space.

Coupling the REFINe framework with an automated FE simulation pipeline and a physics-inspired GNN enables accurate mapping from network topology to decoupled mechanical behavior, capturing both J-type and C-type nonlinear load–displacement modes. Through reinforcement learning, REFINe performs inverse design of lightweight, high-strength architectures within minutes—achieving ~50% strength enhancement and ~20% weight reduction relative to baseline configurations. Experimental validation using 3D-printed prototypes indicates the predictive reliability of the framework, demonstrating agreement between simulations and physical tests. Extending REFINe with QuadriFlow-based surface mapping further enables the translation of optimized 2D designs onto complex 3D geometries such as biomedical scaffolds and wearable architectures while maintaining topological continuity and manufacturability.

Beyond fibrous networks, REFINe provides a generalizable foundation for AI-assisted, topology-driven materials design, where graph-based representation, physics-inspired machine learning, and reinforcement-guided optimization are integrated into a single digital-to-physical loop. As the simulation boundary conditions and learning objectives are extended to accommodate additional loading modes such as shear and compression, this approach lays the groundwork for autonomous, scalable, and fabrication-aware design of architected materials across disciplines, bridging the gap between theoretical topology and practical manufacturing in next-generation programmable matter.

Methods

Network generation and topological control

The construction of regular fibrous network structures within the REFINe framework was achieved through a graph-based generative process consisting of five key stages: Eulerian base-unit generation, regular tiling, anchor-point regulation, boundary fitting, and continuous path planning. This workflow ensures that the resulting structures are both topologically continuous and manufacturable using single-fiber weaving or additive printing. REFINe is based on a Python 3.8 environment and NetworkX 2.8 graph theory library.

In the Eulerian base-unit generation, a square grid was used as the template, and five equally spaced control points were placed along each edge. A geometric deformation was applied to one edge and replicated to the remaining three edges, ensuring rotational symmetry and maintaining even node degrees across the structure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{d}}}}\left(v\right)\equiv 0\,{{\mathrm{mod}}}\,2$$\end{document}dv≡0mod2. The planar displacement coordinates of all topological control points form a ten-dimensional encoding sequence vector.

During regular tiling, the base unit was expanded into larger networks through matrix-based replication. Uniform tiling generated periodic lattices, while checkerboard tiling combined base units with different parameters to form hybrid architectures. A normalization factor was applied to maintain consistent dimensions across different network densities.

Anchor-point regulation adjusted the density of fiber intersections. The algorithm detected overlaps between fiber segments by representing each network edge as a two-dimensional line segment and employing cross-product-based geometric algorithms to determine intersection relationships between segment pairs, then modified local displacements of control points to tune stacking levels, thereby balancing structural integrity and material efficiency.

In the boundary fitting stage, network nodes along the perimeter were mapped onto target contours, while internal nodes were repositioned using Laplacian smoothing or radial expansion. This allowed the network to conform to complex geometries without breaking topological continuity.

Finally, the CGM algorithm was developed to generate efficient continuous paths. Leveraging the structural isomorphism of the regular fibrous network structures, the predefined path of the canonical square grid was directly mapped onto the deformed structure. The resulting trajectory provided a continuous, single-fiber manufacturing path with linear computational complexity.

FE modeling and automated simulation analysis

To establish an automated mapping from network topology to mechanical response, Abaqus 2024 was controlled via custom Python scripts, enabling fully automated processing from model generation to data extraction.

In the topology verification stage, the system first analyzed the geometric contours of the network. A computational-geometry algorithm identified line-segment intersections to detect closed loops and disconnected regions, while the Shoelace formula was used to calculate polygonal areas and remove invalid micro-loops. The validated contours were normalized to a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(10\times 10\right)$$\end{document}10×10 unit domain, and a self-intersection check ensured geometric consistency.

During 2D sketch generation, the verified contours were geometrically optimized. A collinearity simplification algorithm detected and removed redundant points while preserving structural integrity. Each closed loop was automatically reconnected at its endpoints, forming a continuous, constraint-ready sketch for 3D modeling.

The 3D modeling and meshing stage employed a straight-sweep method to extrude the 2D sketch into a solid model with a uniform thickness of one unit. The mesh was discretized using C3D8H eight-node reduced-integration hexahedral elements, with an adaptive meshing strategy balancing computational efficiency and local accuracy (see Supplementary Note 2). All elements were defined as deformable bodies to capture large-strain behavior.

For boundary constraint definition, the system automatically identified geometric boundaries and constructed load-transfer mechanisms. The top and bottom 10% of the model height (Y-direction) were defined as boundary zones, and reference points were established at their centroids. A surface-based coupling was preferentially used to link these regions with their reference points; if unavailable, a node-based constraint was applied as a fallback, directly assigning displacement or rigid-body boundary conditions. This automated constraint scheme ensured consistent loading conditions across networks of varying geometries.

The uniaxial tensile analysis adopted a static, nonlinear method to simulate large deformation. The load factor was set to 1.0 and divided into 20 equal increments. Material behavior was modeled using a Mooney–Rivlin hyperelastic formulation with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left({C}_{1}=6.0\right)$$\end{document}C1=6.0, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left({C}_{2}=3.0\right)$$\end{document}C2=3.0, Poisson’s ratio of 0.495, and density of 1160 kg m⁻³, complemented by experimental plastic fitting. Local instabilities were stabilized using an energy-dissipation method with parameter ALLSDTOL = 0.05 to ensure numerical convergence. In the data extraction stage, the system automatically detected simulation completion by monitoring the ODB file size; when the file size remained unchanged for three consecutive checks without file locks, the analysis was deemed finished. Reaction force data (RF2) were extracted from the “BOTTOM/REFBOT” region and displacement data (U2) from the “TOP/REFTOP” region. Time, displacement, and force data were exported as standardized CSV files for subsequent machine-learning model training. The complete set of constitutive, geometric, boundary-condition, and numerical-stabilization assumptions, together with quantitative FEA-experiment comparisons, are documented in Supplementary Note 3.

Performance evaluation and experimental validation

The overall performance of the REFINe framework was evaluated across four quantitative dimensions: topological stability, design flexibility, computational efficiency, and mechanical response characteristics.

Topological stability was verified through the Eulerian condition and connectivity analysis. For each generated network, node-degree parity was checked to ensure that all nodes satisfied the Eulerian rule \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(d\left(v\right)\equiv 0\,{{\mathrm{mod}}}\,2\right)$$\end{document}dv≡0mod2. Network connectivity was further examined using graph-theoretic algorithms to identify disconnected components and ensure that the structure could be traversed continuously by a single fiber path, confirming manufacturability.

Design flexibility was assessed by analyzing the parameter space of the ten-dimensional encoding vector. The diversity of possible configurations was quantified under a 10 cm2 standardized design domain, and hybrid architectures were generated through checkerboard tiling of distinct base units. Anchor-point regulation tests were conducted by systematically varying encoding parameters to quantify changes in anchor density, structural similarity, and material usage.

Computational efficiency was benchmarked by comparing the CGM algorithm with the conventional Guan’s route method across density parameters (n = 3–10). Key metrics included path-generation time, total path length, traversal steps, and number of overlapping points.

RL-based optimization framework

To enable inverse design of fibrous network topologies from target mechanical properties, a RL optimization framework was developed within the REFINe system. The framework employs a ten-dimensional vector of isomorphic deformation parameters as optimization variables, each ranging from −0.50 to +0.50. Optimization is performed using a SAC algorithm operating in a continuous action space, with embedded geometric constraints to ensure topological connectivity and manufacturability during exploration.

Fabrication and application of three-dimensional regular fibrous network architectures

To demonstrate the manufacturability of optimized topologies, three-dimensional (3D) regular fibrous network architectures were fabricated using a surface-mapping technique that transforms two-dimensional (2D) networks into geometries conforming to complex curved surfaces. The workflow consisted of four stages: mesh preprocessing, encoding and mapping, geometric deformation, and model export.

During mesh preprocessing, the input 3D models were reconstructed into regular quadrilateral meshes using the QuadriFlow algorithm. Parameters were adjusted for boundary weight, sharp-edge threshold, and iteration count to balance smoothness and geometric fidelity. The face number ranged from 100 to 20,000, depending on model complexity, covering both engineering (e.g., shoe last) and biomedical (e.g., lung surface) geometries. The resulting mesh topology was analyzed using the NetworkX library to extract node-degree distributions and connectivity statistics.

In the encoding and deformation stage, the system parsed vertex and face data from the quadrilateral mesh and applied the optimized regular fibrous network encoding sequence to control local geometric variations. Surface normals were computed for each face using vector cross-products, and nodal positions were adjusted according to tangential and normal displacement vectors derived from the encoding. Additional interpolated nodes were inserted along each edge to preserve fiber continuity and accurately fit surface curvature.

The export stage converted the graph structure into a printable solid model. Nodes and edges were rendered as spheres and tubular segments, respectively, and merged into a single mesh. A global scaling transformation was applied to ensure size consistency before exporting the final structure as an OBJ file suitable for additive manufacturing.

Two representative applications were demonstrated. For the lung scaffold, a 3D anatomical lung model was meshed using QuadriFlow, encoded with the optimized regular fibrous network sequence, and deformed to match the lung surface. The structure was fabricated via photopolymer 3D printing and coated with an electrospun fiber layer, forming a composite scaffold with time-controlled degradation. For the footwear prototype, a 3D shoe last was meshed into 300 quadrilateral faces, mapped with the regular fibrous network structures topology optimized under the Fixed-Weight Mode, and printed using TPU 95 A via fused deposition modeling. The resulting fiber-reinforced mesh formed a single integrated upper structure suitable for fabrication and geometric-fidelity assessment.

Experimental validation included structural integrity and functional performance tests. The lung scaffold underwent degradation testing in aqueous environments to monitor timed fiber dissolution and internal network exposure. The footwear prototypes were fabricated and evaluated for structural integrity.

Statistics and reproducibility

Sample size was not predetermined by statistical methods, as the dataset size was governed by the computational cost of generating and simulating regular fibrous networks rather than by hypothesis testing. For the n = 3 case, 900 networks were generated by TOPNet and simulated via the automated FE pipeline; 855 yielded converged load–displacement curves and were retained, while 45 non-converged cases were excluded because their incomplete curves cannot serve as valid mechanical descriptors. No other data were excluded. Randomization is not applicable, since this study involves no group allocation; each network is deterministically reconstructed from its ten-dimensional encoding vector, and all simulations and 3D-printed tensile tests were performed under identical boundary and environmental conditions. Blinding is likewise not applicable, as the entire workflow, including topology generation, FE simulation, GNN prediction, and RL optimization, was executed through fully automated Python scripts whose outputs depend solely on the input encoding vector and are independent of operator judgment. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets at a 7:2:1 ratio with a fixed random seed. Reproducibility is supported by the agreement between simulations and experiments (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}\approx 0.94$$\end{document}R2≈0.94 within 0–200 N) and by more than eight repeated RL runs converging to comparable high-performance topologies.
