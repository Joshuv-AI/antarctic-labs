Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of immune and metabolic pathways in broiler chickens supplemented with Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor
Authors: Oluwaseun D. Adeyemi, Sarayu Bhogoju, Thyneice Taylor-Bowden, Goodness B. Olakanmi, Agnes Kilonzo-Nthenge, Xiaofei Wang, Samuel N. Nahashon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The overuse of antibiotics in poultry production has raised global concerns on antimicrobial resistance thereby increasing the need for effective and sustainable alternatives such as probiotics. Although previous research reveals that Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor supplementation improves performance metrics in broiler chickens, the molecular mechanisms contributing to these outcomes remain unclear. The goal of this study was to assess the transcriptomic and metabolomic responses of broiler chickens fed diets supplemented with L. reuteri, S. coelicolor, or their combination. A total of 240-day-old broiler chicks were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments and raised under standard commercial conditions for eight weeks. Liver and intestine samples collected at weeks 4 and 8 were subjected to RNA isolation, sequencing and differential gene expression analysis. Transcriptome profiling revealed unique gene expression patterns associated with each probiotic treatment and organ. Functional enrichment analyses highlighted the modulation of key immune pathways including cytokine signaling and lymphocyte activation, as well as metabolic pathways related to lipid processing and electrolyte balance. Metabolomics revealed that the combination treatment produced the largest unique number of detected metabolites. Together, we demonstrate that the combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor induced a synergistic transcriptional and metabolomic response that affects both immune regulation and nutrient metabolism. These findings provide insight into the host–probiotic interaction at the molecular level and support the potential use of targeted probiotics as functional feed additives in poultry.

Introduction

The global poultry industry continues to face significant challenges in balancing productivity with animal health, especially as antimicrobial resistance intensifies. Previously, antibiotics have been used to promote growth and control disease in broiler production. However, their overuse has contributed to the emergence of resistant bacterial strains while prompting stricter regulations and a shift toward alternative solutions such as the use of probiotics (Abreu et al., 2023; Hafez and Attia, 2020). Probiotics are live microorganisms that provide health benefits to the host and in recent times, they have emerged as a promising alternative to antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) in poultry production. Species from the genera Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium and Streptomyces have shown potential for improving gut health, enhancing immune responses, and promoting better growth efficiency (Bhogoju et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2024; Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Particularly, Lactobacillus reuteri, is a well-characterized probiotic that naturally inhabits the gastrointestinal tract of chickens and other animals. It produces a range of antimicrobial compounds including reuterin, reutericyclin and organic acids which help maintain microbial balance and suppress pathogenic bacteria (Mu et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2023). Similarly, Streptomyces coelicolor, a soil-dwelling actinobacterium, is known for its capacity to produce diverse secondary metabolites such as actinorhodin and undecylprodigiosin, many of which have antibacterial, immunomodulatory, or metabolic effects (Schlimpert and Elliot, 2023). Although the use of Streptomyces coelicolor as a probiotic in poultry is relatively novel, it has been widely applied in aquaculture (Usman Dawood Butt et al., 2023) and its potential as a functional feed additive make it a great candidate for exploration in poultry.

Our previous study evaluated L. reuteri and S. coelicolor supplementation in broiler diets and reported improvements in feed efficiency, growth performance, and carcass traits (Bhogoju et al., 2021). These findings suggest that the combined probiotic formulation may exert synergistic effects through complementary modulation of immune, metabolic, and gut health-related pathways, contributing to improved host performance (Konieczka et al., 2022). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these phenotypic changes remain to be fully understood. Since transcriptomic profiling, through RNA sequencing, offers a high-resolution approach to explore host responses especially at the gene expression level, analyzing transcriptional changes in tissues involved in immune and metabolic functions will help to uncover how probiotics treatment modulates key biological pathways. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that probiotics modulate host physiology through regulation of cytokine signaling, antioxidant defense systems, gut barrier integrity, and microbial metabolite-associated pathways (Truong et al., 2024; Yosi and Metzler-Zebeli, 2023). Based on our previous study, we hypothesized that combined supplementation with L. reuteri and S. coelicolor would exert synergistic effects on immune- and metabolism-related pathways compared to single-strain supplementation. Therefore, we investigated the effects of L. reuteri, S. coelicolor, and their combination on broiler chickens’ metabolome and transcriptome with a focus on interleukins, immune, and metabolic pathways.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All animal handling procedures and experimental protocols were carried out in compliance with the ethical standards set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Tennessee State University, USA (Animal Welfare Assurance #A4472-01). All efforts were made to minimize discomfort and distress to the animals throughout the study.

Probiotic encapsulation and preparation

The probiotic strains Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor were cultured and encapsulated as previously described by Bhogoju et al. (2021). Briefly, inoculated cultures of Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15mins and gently mixed with 5% sterile soy protein and 0.2% alginate solution. To promote the formation of microcapsules and ionic cross linking, the resulting protein solution was added in a dropwise manner to sterile 1 M CaCl2 solution and left to settle for 30 min at room temperature followed by centrifugation and storage at 4 °C until incorporation into feed.

Experimental design and dietary treatments

A total of 240-day-old broiler chicks were procured from a commercial hatchery (Aviagen, Huntsville, AL, USA). Birds were assigned to one of four treatment groups consisting of 60 birds per treatment via a completely randomized design. Each treatment had three replicates with 20 birds per replicate pen. The treatment groups were as follows: Control (standard broiler diet with no probiotic supplementation); L. reuteri (100 mg/kg of encapsulated L. reuteri); S. coelicolor (100 mg/kg of encapsulated S. coelicolor) and a combination of both probiotic (50 mg/kg each of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor). Encapsulated probiotics were added to the basal diet using wheat middlings (1% of total feed weight) as a carrier to ensure uniform distribution. Animal husbandry and sample collection

The birds were reared under standard commercial conditions in battery cages. Environmental temperature and lighting were adjusted according to brooding guidelines. Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the 8-week trial.

At weeks 4 and 8, tissue (liver and intestine) samples were collected from a 20% of the birds (n = 6 per treatment) for transcriptomic analysis. Birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation followed by exsanguination. Intestine and liver tissues were dissected under sterile conditions, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C until RNA extraction.

RNA isolation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. High-quality RNA samples (RIN ≥ 7.0) were used for library preparation using the appropriate Ion Total RNA-Seq Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA libraries were subsequently sequenced on the Ion Torrent Personal Genome Machine (PGM) platform, generating single-end reads with a modal read length of approximately 150–200 bp.

RNA sequencing data processing and normalization

Raw RNA-seq data from chicken liver and intestine samples were processed to quantify gene expression levels. The expression data were normalized and presented as Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (RPKM). Each treatment group included biological replicates collected at different time points (week 4 and week 8), and the RPKM values were used for subsequent analyses.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize the overall variance in gene expression profiles across treatment groups. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to visualize sample clustering. Treatment group labels were assigned based on sample names, and the PCA plot was generated using the ggfortify package in R with ellipses drawn around treatment clusters to highlight group separations.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing treatment groups using log2 fold change (FC) and p-values derived from statistical tests. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 and a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Six pairwise comparisons were conducted across treatments and for each comparison, the number of significant DEGs was recorded. RPKM values for all replicates (weeks 4 and 8) were also averaged to obtain mean expression values per gene across treatments for visualization purposes using heatmaps, volcano plot, MA plots and Upset plot for DEG overlaps.

All differential expression analyses were performed separately for liver and intestine samples to assess tissue-specific gene regulation patterns in response to treatments.

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using gProfiler while visualization of enriched terms and gene expression patterns was carried out using ggplot2 in R (v4.4.3).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Gastrointestinal tract (GI) and plasma samples were collected from broiler chickens supplemented with Lactobacillus reuteri and/or Streptomyces coelicolor, alongside control groups. Metabolites were extracted and analyzed using liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (QTOF-MS). Data were acquired in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes to maximize metabolite coverage.

Raw spectra were processed for peak detection, alignment, and feature extraction. Differential ions were identified between treatments and controls, and metabolites were annotated against the KEGG database. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KEGG assignments, and results were visualized in R (v4.4.3).

Results and discussion

Principal component analysis (PCA) of gene expression profiles in intestinal and liver samples

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to assess the global gene expression patterns in intestinal and liver tissues following different probiotic treatment interventions. The PCA plot with mean points and error bars (Fig. 1A) revealed a distinct separation between intestinal (IC, Itrt1, Itrt2, Itrt3) and liver samples (LC, Ltrt1, Ltrt2, Ltrt3) along the first principal component (PC1) with the intestinal treatment groups (Itrt1, Itrt2, Itrt3) clustered on the positive side of PC1, while the liver treatments (Ltrt1, Ltrt2, Ltrt3) were positioned on the negative side. This highlights tissue-specific gene expression profiles that reinforce the fundamental biological differences between the intestine, which is primarily involved in nutrient absorption and gut immunity, and the liver, which plays key roles in metabolism, detoxification, and systemic immune regulation. Moreover, within each tissue, it was observed that variations exist with the different probiotic treatment. Notably, intestinal samples from chickens fed with a combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor (Itrt3) exhibited a greater gene expression shift away from their controls (IC), thereby suggesting a stronger transcriptional response to the cocktail probiotic treatment. As shown in Fig. 1B, individual replicates with confidence ellipses were visualized to observe within-group dispersion. PC1 accounted for 66.91% of the total variance, which reflects the influence of tissue type on gene expression while PC2 explained 16.12% and captures within-tissue variations as influenced by probiotic treatment. All liver samples irrespective of treatments formed a tight cluster distinct from intestinal samples further highlighting tissue-specific gene expression. Within each cluster, gene expression patterns were influenced by treatments, particularly, intestine samples from chicken fed S. coelicolor (Itrt2) and a combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor (Itrt3) shows noticeable gene expression pattern from the control group (IC). Liver samples from chicken fed either L. reuteri (Ltrt1) or S. coelicolor (Ltrt2) or a combination of both (Ltrt3) also showed clear gene expression patterns from the control group (LC), with liver samples from the combination group (Ltrt3) forming a distinct subgroup along PC2.

Biologically, these PCA patterns suggest that both tissue type and treatment significantly influence gene expression profiles. The separation between liver and intestine reflects their specialized physiological roles while the observed shifts within each tissue type indicate that the L. reuteri (Ltrt1) or S. coelicolor (Ltrt2) or a combination of both (Ltrt3) modulate immune response, metabolic pathways, or stress-related gene expression in a treatment-specific manner. Moreover, the confidence ellipses in Fig. 1B highlight the consistency of the treatment responses within groups. Tighter ellipses as seen in intestinal samples from chicken fed a combination of both probiotic (Itrt3) suggest homogeneous biological responses, while more dispersed ellipses as seen in intestinal samples from chickens fed L. reuteri (Itrt1) indicate variability in how individuals respond to the treatment, possibly due to differential activation of immune or metabolic pathways.

A total of 3,410 significantly unique DEGs were commonly expressed in both the intestine and liver, while 2,665 DEGs were unique to either the intestine or liver, respectively (Fig. 2a). Further analysis of DEGs by treatment comparisons showed varying degrees of gene regulation (Fig. 2b). Across all treatment conditions, DEGs from the comparison between intestine samples from chicken fed a combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor versus control (IT3vsC) were the highest, with 2,290 upregulated and 1,858 downregulated genes. Other comparisons, such as LT1vsC (liver samples from chickens fed L. reuteri compared to control) and IT2vsC (intestine samples from chickens fed S. coelicolor compared to control), also showed robust differential expression patterns, although at a lower magnitude. Notably, the balance between upregulated and downregulated genes varied between comparisons, suggesting treatment-specific regulatory mechanisms. These findings highlight the dynamic transcriptional landscape influenced by both organ-specific context and treatment-specific stimuli, with notable differences in the scale and directionality of gene expression changes across comparisons.

Upset plots were generated to visualize the intersections among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across liver and intestine samples from various treatment groups respectively (Fig. 2c&d). The analysis revealed that gene expressions were influenced by probiotic treatment. In liver samples, LT1vsC comparison exhibited the largest number of unique differentially expressed genes (n = 583), suggesting that L. reuteri triggered a distinct gene expression pattern that was not shared with other probiotic treatments. This set of unique DEGs reflects treatment-specific regulatory effects that may involve pathways that are uniquely sensitive to the bioactive compounds present in L. reuteri. Similarly, S. coelicolor and the combination of both probiotic also displayed subsets of unique DEGs not overlapping with other comparisons, although with smaller gene counts (n = 376 and 316 respectively). The unique genes found in each treatment show that, although some pathways respond similarly to probiotics, each probiotic formulation activates its own distinct set of genes. This means that the different probiotic strains influence the bird's gene expression in their own specific way, depending on the strains used and how they interact with the host. On the other hand, a higher number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were observed across all treatment comparisons in the intestine compared to the liver. The Upset plot (Fig. 2d) shows that the largest intersection involved over 1,400 shared DEGs, indicating a strong response to probiotic supplementation across multiple treatments in the gut. Additionally, each treatment comparison, such as IT3vsC and IT1vsT3, displayed unique sets of DEGs not shared with other groups. Among all these treatment comparisons, the T3vsC group exhibited the largest set of differentially expressed genes (n = 1466), indicating that a combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor had the most substantial impact on intestinal gene expression compared to the individual treatments. The large number of DEGs unique to this group (T3) further emphasizes its increased modulation of gut-associated pathways, including immune activation and nutrient metabolism. Moreover, the increased gene activity in the intestine may be associated with the direct interaction between probiotics and the gut epithelium, where live microorganisms can directly stimulate immune receptors, alter nutrient absorption pathways, and influence local metabolic processes (Mazziotta et al., 2023). Unlike the liver, which responds to probiotics indirectly through systemic signaling, the intestine serves as the primary contact site, resulting in a broader and more immediate transcriptomic response. Furthermore, the unique gene set expression identified in the combination group suggests a synergistic relationship between L. reuteri and S. coelicolor and reinforces the hypothesis that probiotic synergy exerts distinct transcriptional influences beyond the additive effects of individual strains.

The heatmap in Fig. 3b presents the scaled expression profiles of the top 50 PCA-contributing genes across liver (Ltrt1, Ltrt2, Ltrt3, LC) and intestinal (Itrt1, Itrt2, Itrt3, IC) samples from chicken fed L. reuteri (trt1), S. coelicolor (trt2) or a combination of both (trt3). Notably some of the genes including VWA3A, NAV2, WDR6, AIG1, PEX11A, MRPS14, DDX54, TRMT12, C1ORF74, RPB6, FLAD1, POLB, CEP63, ABHD3, VTG1, TCF1, LYPLAL1, ADH6, AK3, FNBP4 are not well-characterized in chickens although they may be involved in basic cell processes like protein translation, cell cycle regulation (MRPS14, POLB), lipid or body metabolism (VTG1 yolk protein precursor; ABHD3 hydrolase) and signaling or transcriptional regulation (TCF1). However, their precise role in chickens requires further characterization as this will give insight into the influence of probiotic treatments in chicken immunity and metabolism.

Some other functional genes have been identified with differential expression of these genes revealed across probiotic treatment, tissue-type and time of sample collection. For instance, TVB which encodes the tumor virus locus B receptor serves as the cellular entry receptor for avian leukosis virus (ALV) subgroup B and while variants of tvb confer resistance to ALV infection they also share structural similarity with TNF receptor family death receptors and can activate caspase-mediated apoptosis upon viral entry (Lee et al., 2017). It was observed that TVB gene was significantly upregulated in intestine and liver samples from chicken fed L. reuteri and a combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor respectively. Although chickens in our study were uninfected, variation in TVB expression may reflect antiviral immune preparedness or mitochondrial stress responses. Interestingly, THBS4 an extracellular matrix protein, involved in tissue remodeling, cell adhesion, and potentially immune cell recruitment and signaling (Lv et al., 2016) was markedly downregulated in probiotic-treated intestinal tissues compared to the control intestine, where it was upregulated. The observed downregulation of THBS4 in probiotic-treated intestines suggests a reduced need for extracellular matrix remodeling and immune cell adhesion, therefore highlighting the anti-inflammatory and barrier-stabilizing effects of Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor. Contrarily, the upregulation of THBS4 in the control intestine may indicate a higher baseline level of epithelial turnover or low-grade immune activation driven by endogenous microbiota or environmental stress. Also, DHFR, a central enzyme in folate and nucleotide metabolism, was upregulated in intestinal and liver tissues from chicken fed S. coelicolor and in liver tissues from chicken fed L. reuteri. This suggests increased immune cell renewal and epithelial turnover, which is usually associated with high DHFR activity (Zhang, et al., 2023). Finally, FOXK1, a transcription regulator implicated in the integration of metabolic and immune-related gene networks, exhibited significant upregulation in liver tissues from chickens fed the combined treatment of Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor compared to the individual probiotic treatments and control group. This highlights the synergistic potential of multi-strain probiotics and suggests enhanced modulation of hepatic pathways involved in both energy metabolism and immune signaling.

IL17F was significantly upregulated in intestinal samples from broiler chickens fed Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor with a significant downregulation observed in chickens the combined supplementation of Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor. However, IL17RA exhibited a pronounced upregulation in chickens fed the combined supplementation of Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor compared to other treatments. Also, IL17RD was significantly upregulated in intestine sample from chicken fed either S. coelicolor or a combination of both probiotic candidates suggesting that S. coelicolor is a suitable probiotic for broiler chicken either individually or when used in combination with another species.

Overall, the increased expression of the IL-17 family which are key mediators of mucosal immunity, promoting the recruitment of neutrophils and enhancing epithelial defense mechanisms against microbial invasion suggests an augmented gut immune surveillance response stimulated by probiotic treatments (Huang, 2021).

Although there was no significant difference between the pattern of upregulation or downregulation of the IL-12 signaling axis (IL12RB1 and IL12RB2) in the liver samples from treated group compared to the untreated group, IL-18 and IL-18R1, which synergize with IL-12 to induce IFN-γ production (Ihim et al., 2022), were upregulated in liver samples from chickens fed the combined supplementation of Lactobacillus reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor thereby indicating systemic enhancement of innate immune functions. Similarly, L. reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor supplementation induced a mild upregulation of IL10R2 and IL22RA1 in intestinal tissues with the combination group stimulating a more significant upregulation. Therefore, we can conclude that the supplementation of broiler chicken with a combination of L. reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor will modulate anti-inflammatory signaling and enhance mucosal repair. Interestingly, IL-6, a cytokine pivotal to acute phase responses and metabolic regulation (Tanaka et al., 2014), exhibited elevated expression primarily in the intestinal samples of the chickens fed L. reuteri particularly at week 8, suggesting a potential link between metabolic–immune axis activation and the time exposure of probiotic treatments. Moreover, the expression of IL-34 and IL-41, both involved in macrophage differentiation and tissue homeostasis, was upregulated in the liver tissue of chicken fed a combination of L. reuteri and Streptomyces coelicolor, further supporting enhanced immunological balance.

Collectively, these expression patterns suggest that the combined supplementation of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor orchestrated a more robust and balanced immune gene expression profile that enhanced mucosal immunity via IL-17 signaling while modulating systemic innate immune pathways through IL-12 and IL-18 axes.

Probiotic supplementation enhances key functional pathways

To gain insight into the biological pathways modulated by probiotic supplementation, Gene Ontology (GO) and upstream regulator analyses were performed on differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The general enrichment terms mainly involved cytoskeletal organization and cellular transport processes. Notably, circulatory system processes, regulation of actin filament-based processes, and regulation of establishment of protein localization were among the top enriched categories. These suggest that probiotic treatments, especially the combination of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor, influence cellular structure by modulating actin filament dynamics and circulatory system processes, which may strengthen epithelial integrity and improve barrier function in the gut. Furthermore, the enrichment of pathways related to protein localization and phosphorylation indicates the active regulation of protein trafficking and post-translational modifications, essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and adaptive responses to microbial stimuli. Within immune-specific pathways, significant enrichment was observed in terms related to lymphocyte activation, leukocyte migration, and chemotaxis and T-cell activation. Key adaptive immunity processes such as antigen receptor-mediated signaling and positive regulation of cytokine production were also prominently enriched thus highlighting the role of probiotics in modulating both innate and adaptive immunity in broiler chickens. Interestingly, high enrichment of immune effector processes suggests that probiotic treatments in broiler chickens not only activate immune cells, but they also enhance the functional mechanisms by which these cells eliminate pathogens, modulate inflammation, and maintain tissue homeostasis. These findings align with the upregulation of IL-17 family genes observed in the gene expression data, suggesting a consistent immune activation profile.

Enriched metabolic terms were largely associated with membrane transporter activity, including transmembrane transporter complexes and sodium ion transmembrane transporters, suggesting enhanced nutrient absorption and electrolyte balance regulation. Pathways related to lipid metabolism (cholesterol metabolic process) were also significantly enriched, indicating a possible shift in hepatic lipid processing and metabolic reprogramming in response to probiotic supplementation. Similarly, upstream analysis identified several transcription factors and growth factors as key regulators of the observed gene expression profiles. Noteworthy regulators included GATA-3 and HFH2 (FOXD3), GATA-3 is implicated in the regulation of Th2-mediated immune responses, and its significant enrichments suggests its contribution to the upregulation of cytokines essential for epithelial defense and immune tolerance in the presence of probiotic treatments, while HFH2 plays a pivotal role in maintaining epithelial integrity and controlling inflammatory gene expression. Therefore, the supplementation of broiler chicken with L. reuteri and S. coelicolor contributes to the enhanced regulation of mucosal immunity and gut homeostasis responsible for intestinal health.

Compared to the untreated control (ChCt), all probiotic treatments resulted in an increased metabolite diversity. Among the individual strains, L. reuteri (ChPT1) and S. coelicolor (ChPT2) yielded 250 and 240 unique metabolites, respectively, while the combination of both probiotics (ChPT3) produced the highest count (≈260 unique metabolites). Overall, the combination treatment (ChPT3) generated the richest metabolite profile, suggesting a potential synergistic effect of L. reuteri and S. coelicolor in modulating the metabolome of broiler chickens. This is supported by the emphasis of several research on the benefits of multi-strain probiotics in modulating the metabolites of chickens (Dibakoane et al., 2025; Halder et al., 2025; Hossain et al., 2024).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis also revealed that the metabolites identified were distributed across diverse biological pathways, with marked differences in their representation (Fig. 6b). Majority of the metabolites were assigned to the broad categories of metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, which together accounted for the highest counts (>25 metabolites each). Other enriched categories included biosynthesis of cofactors, ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis, and purine metabolism also had more than 10 metabolites mapped. Additional pathways with fewer metabolites include those involved with amino acid metabolism (lysine degradation, thiamine metabolism), lipid metabolism (fatty acid degradation, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism), and xenobiotic biodegradation (dioxin degradation, naphthalene degradation). These findings are consistent with previous reports that probiotic interventions in chicken contribute to the remodeling of both central energy metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis which results in the production of bioactive molecules containing antimicrobial or immunomodulatory properties (Idowu et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the shared and unique metabolites across treatments were examined using an UpSet plot (Fig. 6c). Most of the metabolites were shared among multiple groups, with the largest overlap consisting of 212 metabolites common to S. coelicolor (ChPT2), L. reuteri (ChPT1), and the combination treatment (ChPT3) obtained through positive ion mode. A second major intersection included 138 metabolites shared between the probiotic combination (ChPT3) and the control (ChPct). Smaller sets of shared metabolites (ranging from 45 to fewer than 10) were distributed across various combinations of treatments, reflecting treatment-specific metabolite expression. On the treatment level, S. coelicolor (ChPT2) and L. reuteri (ChPT1) showed broad overlaps with the combination treatment (ChPT3), suggesting that the metabolic output of the combined probiotic intervention encompassed features from both single strains. Nonetheless, each probiotic treatment retained unique metabolites, highlighting distinct metabolic contributions.

Interestingly, the mode of metabolite acquisition uncovered a distinct set of features particularly including the top 50 metabolites which were only present in the negative ion mode and could not be matched to existing entries in standard metabolomics databases (Fig. 6d). The absence of annotations suggests that these metabolites may be novel compounds. Moreover, their consistent detection and differential abundance among treatments also indicates that they contribute to the unique metabolic fingerprints induced by probiotic supplementation. This observation aligns with the limitation of untargeted metabolomics, where microbial metabolism frequently yields rare or uncharacterized compounds not captured in current reference libraries. From a functional perspective, these unidentified metabolites may be biologically relevant and require future research.

Conclusion

This transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct gene expression patterns between the liver and intestine of broiler chickens supplemented with probiotics. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) showed clear tissue-specific separation, with intestinal samples from chickens fed L. reuteri + S. coelicolor (T3) clustering apart from the control group on the positive side of PC1 and PC2, indicating a strong transcriptional shift induced by this treatment. Interestingly, among the top 50 genes contributing to sample clustering, a significant proportion were uncharacterized in the chicken genome, highlighting a critical knowledge gap in poultry functional genomics that warrants further investigation. Treatment effects were also tissue-specific with L. reuteri + S. coelicolor inducing a higher number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the intestine compared to the control, suggesting that the gut, being the primary site of microbial interaction, responds robustly to probiotic intervention. On the other hand, liver samples from chicken fed L. reuteri (T1) had a higher number of DEGs relative to the control, highlighting potential systemic metabolic regulation. Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis revealed that probiotic supplementation stimulated key immune-related pathways, including cytokine signaling and immune effector functions. In addition, metabolic pathways such as lipid metabolism and transporter activity, essential for nutrient absorption and electrolyte balance, were significantly enriched. These findings suggest a dual role of the probiotics in modulating both host immunity and metabolic efficiency.

Overall, all probiotic treatments in this study demonstrated beneficial modulation of immune and metabolic pathways. However, S. coelicolor (T2), especially when used in combination with L. reuteri, showed enhanced transcriptomic effects in the intestine, indicating a synergistic benefit from probiotic co-supplementation. These insights provide a molecular basis for the observed phenotypic benefits of probiotics in poultry and reinforce the potential of targeted probiotic strategies as functional feed additives to improve gut health and systemic physiology in broiler production.
