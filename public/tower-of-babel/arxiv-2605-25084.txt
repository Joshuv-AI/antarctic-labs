Safe Trajectory Tracking of the Stefan Problem with Second-Order Moving Boundary Dynamics
Shumon Koga, Miroslav Krstic

Abstract
This paper considers a safe trajectory tracking of the Stefan problem with a second-order moving boundary dynamics. The model is given by a parabolic Partial Differential Equation (PDE) defined on a time-varying domain of moving boundary governed by a second-order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) associated with the Neumann boundary condition. A feedforward control is designed by a series expansion approach to solve the inverse Stefan problem under given reference trajectory of the moving boundary, and the convergence of infinite series is proven. A trajectory tracking controller is derived based on an energy-shaping, which ensures the safety of the model constraint in the closed-loop system. The closed-loop system is also shown to be globally exponentially stable with respect to the tracking error by performing PDE backstepping transformation and Lyapunov analysis. Numerical simulation illustrates an effective tracking performance of the proposed method under a sinusoidal reference trajectory. Code is released at https://github.com/shumon0423/StefanTracking_ACC2026.git .



I
INTRODUCTION

The Stefan problem is a classical mathematical model representing physical phenomena of thermal phase change, such as melting or solidification, which is widely studied in many applications, including sea ice melting
[
35
]
, additive manufacturing
[
39
]
, continuous casting
[
3
]
, electrosurgery
[
33
]
, and cell therapy
[
37
]
. The Stefan problem comprises a partial differential equation (PDE) for the temperature profile, defined over a time-varying spatial domain whose moving boundary is governed by an ordinary differential equation (ODE). This modeling approach, where the spatial domain grows or shrinks, is also known as a free boundary problem and has been incorporated even beyond thermal dynamics for applications such as modeling chemical reactions in batteries
[
32
]
, biological cell growth
[
13
]
, and option pricing
[
28
]
.

Although the Stefan problem has been studied for more than a century, the geometric nonlinearity arising from the coupling between the PDE and the moving boundary presents an inevitable challenge. Consequently, ongoing work has focused on studying the solution of the Stefan problem using state-of-the-art methods such as Bayesian optimization
[
42
]
and physics-informed machine learning
[
41
]
. Control design for the Stefan problem has been developed over the last two decades using several approaches, such as optimal control
[
16
]
, model predictive control
[
12
]
, geometric control
[
26
]
, and enthalpy-based control
[
30
]
. The PDE backstepping method, a systematic approach for designing boundary controllers and observers for many classes of PDEs and delay systems
as summarized in a recent survey paper
[
38
]
,
has been successfully applied to the Stefan problem, ensuring global exponential stability
[
19
]
and validated in physical experiments
[
25
]
. Several extensions of the backstepping design for the Stefan problem are provided in
[
21
]
. Other classes of PDEs with moving boundaries have also been handled by the backstepping method, including traffic shockwaves
[
44
]
, piston dynamics
[
5
]
, and neuron growth
[
8
]
.

Trajectory tracking for the Stefan problem, which aims to follow a desired behavior of the moving boundary, has also been studied in a few works. A pioneering work on motion planning by series expansion
[
10
]
provides a solution for the reference temperature of a nonlinear Stefan problem for an a priori given moving boundary as a function of time. Trajectory tracking control designs to stabilize the Stefan system at a reference solution have been proposed using enthalpy-based feedback
[
31
]
and a flatness-based approach
[
11
]
. Moreover,
[
14
]
proposed an extremum-seeking control for the Stefan problem to drive the system to an unknown optimum by tracking a reference trajectory that includes sinusoidal perturbations on a cost map. However, in all those works, stability analysis has been performed under assumptions on either closed-loop performance or a static reference. The reasons for these assumptions are twofold: first, the requirement that a physical condition being satisfied under closed-loop control, and second, the spatial domain discrepancy between the system and reference profiles. As shown in
[
11
]
, once the reference error PDE state is defined to maintain consistent conditions at the moving boundary, the error dynamics includes a time derivative of the reference state at the boundary. This term cannot be bounded in standard
L_{2}
or
H_{1}
norms for the classical Stefan problem with first-order moving boundary dynamics, as the velocity gap is equivalent in norm to the Neumann boundary value.

Ensuring that constraints are satisfied in control systems has recently been studied under the notion of safety and Control Barrier Function (CBF)
[
2
]
.
For trajectory tracking, combining a CBF-based safety filter with a preset planning and low-level control architecture was addressed in
[
1
]
. Based on an energy-shaping approach and passivity
[
29
]
, an energy-based safety constraint and its associated CBF to preserve passivity were proposed in
[
36
]
for handling kinematic constraints in robotic systems. Several extensions combining passivity with CBF-based safety have since been developed
[
7
,
6
]
.

Safe or constrained control of PDE systems has also been studied in several works. Early work by
[
9
]
proposed a model predictive control approach that incorporates state and input constraints through a modal decomposition of a parabolic PDE. Input constraints under saturation for unstable parabolic PDEs have been handled using linear matrix inequalities
[
27
,
43
]
. A Lyapunov-based constraint guarantee for PDEs has been addressed for fluid dynamics
[
18
]
and batteries
[
34
]
. The safe control design by a high-order CBF for PDEs was initially proposed for the Stefan problem with actuator dynamics in
[
23
]
as well as an event-triggered mechanism
[
22
]
, and later for hyperbolic PDEs in
[
40
]
. A learning-based approach for the safe control of PDEs was proposed in
[
17
]
using a neural operator
[
4
]
.

This paper is the first to provide a rigorous proof of safety and tracking stability of the Stefan problem with a trajectory tracking control. We consider the Stefan problem with second-order moving boundary dynamics, proposed in
[
24
]
, to represent thermal inertia in the phase change dynamics. The feedforward control is designed following the series expansion method, and convergence of the infinite series under the second-order moving boundary dynamics is also shown. The trajectory tracking control is then designed via energy-shaping with respect to the energy function of the reference solution, and the safety condition is proven to hold by maximum principle. Based on the reference PDE state proposed in
[
11
]
, the stability analysis in the tracking error is employed using a Lyapunov approach and a PDE backstepping transformation. Numerical simulation is performed with a sinusoidal reference trajectory.

Stefan Problem with Second-Order Moving Boundary

We consider the one-phase Stefan problem modeling the dynamics of the temperature profile
T(x,t)
defined on the time-varying domain

at time

, described by (see Fig.

\displaystyle T_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\alpha T_{xx}(x,t),\hskip 5.69054pt\textrm{for}\hskip 5.69054ptt>0,\hskip 5.69054pt0<x<s(t),

(1)

\displaystyle-kT_{x}(0,t)

\displaystyle=q_{\rm c}(t),\hskip 5.69054pt\textrm{for}\hskip 5.69054ptt>0,

(2)

\displaystyle T(s(t),t)

\displaystyle=T_{\rm m},\hskip 5.69054pt\textrm{for}\hskip 5.69054ptt>0,

(3)

with initial conditions

s(0)=s_{0}
and
T(x,0)=T_{0}(x),\forall x\in(0,s_{0}],

where

The classical one-phase Stefan problem, studied widely in literature including
[

Following

]

\displaystyle\varepsilon\ddot{s}(t)

\displaystyle=-\dot{s}(t)-\beta T_{x}(s(t),t),

(4)

and

denotes the relaxation time
in phase transformation and

The validity of the Stefan model (

\displaystyle T(x,t)\geq

\displaystyle T_{{\rm m}},\quad\forall x\in(0,s(t)),\quad\forall t>0,

(5)

\displaystyle 0<s(t)<

\displaystyle L,\quad\forall t>0.

(6)

The first condition implies that the liquid phase is not frozen from the heat inlet. The second condition implies that the material is not entirely in one phase. These physical conditions are also required for the existence and uniqueness of solutions under the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

0<s_{0}<L
,
T_{0}(x)\in C^{1}([0,s_{0}];[T_{\rm m},+\infty))
with
T_{0}(s_{0})=T_{\rm m}
.

Assumption 2
.

.

We prove the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

With Assumptions
1
-
2
, if

is a bounded piecewise continuous non-negative heat function, i.e.,

\displaystyle q_{{\rm c}}(t)\geq 0,\quad\forall t\geq 0,

then there exists a unique classical solution for the Stefan problem (
1
)–(
4
), which satisfies (
5
), and

\displaystyle\dot{s}(t)\geq 0,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(8)

Proof.

By applying the maximum principle for parabolic PDEs and Hopf’s lemma, it holds that

, which leads to

\varepsilon\ddot{s}(t)\geq-\dot{s}(t).

Applying the Gronwall’s inequality yields
\dot{s}(t)\geq v_{0}e^{-\varepsilon^{-1}t}
.
Thus, with Assumption
2
, (
8
) holds

∎

III
CONTROL DESIGN

III-A

Feedforward Control

Obtaining the temperature profile solution
T^{\rm r}(x,t)
for a known moving boundary
s_{\rm r}(t)
is known as the inverse Stefan problem
[
15
]
. Once the solution to the inverse Stefan problem is obtained, the feedforward control
q_{\rm c}^{r}(t)
can be derived from the boundary condition in (
10
). Among several approaches, we employ the series expansion method in
[
10
]
. Namely, we formulate the reference temperature profile as

\displaystyle T^{\rm r}(x,t)=T_{\rm m}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}(t)}{n!}(x-s_{\rm r}(t))^{n},

(13)

where
a_{n}(t)
for
n=0,1,\dots
are time-varying coefficients to be determined. Substituting (
13
) into the boundary conditions (
11
) and (
12
) provides the following initial coefficients:

\displaystyle a_{0}(t)=0,\quad a_{1}(t)=-\frac{1}{\beta}(\varepsilon\ddot{s}_{\rm r}(t)+\dot{s}_{\rm r}(t)).

(14)

Moreover, substituting (
13
) into the reference PDE (
9
) gives a recursive update for
a_{n}(t)
where
n\geq 2
:

\displaystyle a_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}(\dot{a}_{n-2}(t)-\dot{s}_{\rm r}(t)a_{n-1}(t)).

(15)

Similarly to
[
10
]
, to ensure the convergence of the series solution, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

The reference trajectory
s_{\rm r}(t)
is Gevrey of order
(d-1)
for some
d\in[1,2]
, i.e., there exist constants
M>0
and
R>0
such that the following bound holds

\displaystyle\big|s_{\rm r}^{(m+1)}(t)\big|\leq M\frac{m!^{d}}{R^{m}}

(16)

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where
s_{\rm r}^{(m)}(t):=\frac{{\rm d}^{m}}{{\rm d}t^{m}}s_{\rm r}(t)
.

We prove the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

With Assumption
3
, it holds

\displaystyle\big|a_{n}^{(m)}(t)\big|\leq MF^{n-1}GH_{n,m}

(17)

where
G:=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\varepsilon+R\right)
,
H_{n,m}=\frac{1}{R^{n+m}}\frac{((n+m)!)^{d}}{(n!)^{d-1}}
, and

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\frac{RM+\sqrt{R^{2}M^{2}+16\alpha R}}{4\alpha}.

(18)

Proof.

We prove (
17
) holds for all
m
by induction on
n=0,1,\dots
. The detailed steps of this proof follow the methodology in
[
10
]
. For the base case
n=0
, the inequality holds for all
m
since
a_{0}(t)=0
from (
14
). For
n=1
, taking the
m
-th derivative of the second condition of (
14
) and applying (
16
) leads to

\displaystyle|a_{1}^{(m)}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq MG\frac{(m+1)!^{d}}{R^{m+1}},

(19)

thus, (
17
) holds for
n=1
.

Next, we prove (
17
) for
n\geq 2
, assuming as the induction hypothesis that it holds for
n-1
and
n-2
. Taking the bound of (
15
) and applying the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\big|a_{n}^{(m)}(t)\big|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\big|a_{n-2}^{(m+1)}(t)\big|+\big|(\dot{s}_{\rm r}a_{n-1})^{(m)}\big|\right).

(20)

Following the derivations in
[
10
]
by applying the induction hypothesis to the terms on the right-hand side, we derive the following bound:

\displaystyle\big|a_{n}^{(m)}(t)\big|\leq

\displaystyle MF^{n-3}GH_{n,m}\frac{R}{\alpha}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{MF}{n}\right).

(21)

Finally, by choosing
F
such that the final factor equals 1, we obtain a quadratic equation for
F

18

∎

Proposition 2
.

The radius of convergence of the infinite series (
13
) is given by
R/F
. Namely, if

\displaystyle R\geq F\sup_{t\geq 0}|s_{\rm r}(t)|,

(22)

then the series (
13

\displaystyle|T^{\rm r}(x,t)-T_{\rm m}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MGR}{F\left(R-F(s_{\rm r}(t)-x)\right)},

\displaystyle|T^{\rm r}_{x}(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MGR}{(R-F(s_{\rm r}(t)-x))^{2}}.

(24)

Proof.

The bounds are found by applying the triangle inequality to the series (

) and its spatial derivative, followed by applying the coefficient bound from (

\displaystyle|T^{\rm r}(x,t)-T_{\rm m}|\leq MGF^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{F}{R}(s_{\rm r}(t)-x)\right)^{n}.

(25)

Under the condition in (

is less than 1, leading directly to the bound in (

Applying the series solution of the reference temperature (

\displaystyle q^{r}_{\rm c}(t)=-k\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}(t)}{n!}(-s_{\rm r}(t))^{n}.

Assumption 4

The reference trajectory

satisfies

\displaystyle s_{\rm r}(0)>0,\quad\dot{s}_{\rm r}(t)\geq 0,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}s_{\rm r}(t)=\bar{s}_{\rm r}<L.

(27)

Assumption 5

The series solution (

III-B

Energy-Shaping Tracking Control with Safety Guarantee

Let the total energy be defined as

\displaystyle E(t)=\int_{0}^{s(t)}(T(x,t)-T_{\rm m})dx+\frac{k}{\beta}(\varepsilon\dot{s}(t)+s(t)).

(28)

Taking the time derivative of (
28
) along the system’s solution yields the energy conservation law
\dot{E}(t)=q_{\rm c}(t)
.
Let
E_{\rm r}(t)
be the reference energy defined by

\displaystyle\hskip-6.25958ptE_{\rm r}(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s_{\rm r}(t)}(T^{\rm r}(x,t)-T_{\rm m})dx+\frac{k}{\beta}(\varepsilon\dot{s}_{\rm r}(t)+s_{\rm r}(t)).

(29)

We design an energy-shaping controller as

\displaystyle q_{\rm c}(t)=q_{\rm c}^{\rm r}(t)-c\left(E(t)-E_{\rm r}(t)\right),

(30)

where
c>0
is a control gain and
q_{\rm c}^{\rm r}(t)
is the feedforward control from (
26
).
To ensure safety, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 6
.

The initial energy satisfies

\displaystyle E(0)\leq E_{\rm r}(0)+\frac{1}{c}q_{\rm c}^{\rm r}(t)e^{ct},\quad\forall t\geq 0,

(31)

\displaystyle E_{\rm r}(t)+(E(0)-E_{\rm r}(0))e^{-ct}<\frac{k}{\beta}\bar{s}_{\rm r},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(32)

Proposition 3
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
hold. Then the closed-loop system, consisting of the Stefan dynamics (
1
)–(
4
), the tracking control (
30
), and the feedforward control (
26
), satisfies the safety condition (
5
) and

\displaystyle s_{0}<s(t)\leq\bar{s}_{\rm r},\quad\dot{s}(t)\geq 0,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(33)

Proof.

Substituting the controller (
30
) into the energy conservation law yields a first-order linear ODE for the energy error
E(t)-E_{\rm r}(t)
, whose solution is

\displaystyle E(t)-E_{\rm r}(t)=(E(0)-E_{\rm r}(0))e^{-ct}.

(34)

From Lemma
1
, (
5
) is guaranteed if

. Using (
30
) and (
34
), this safety requirement becomes
q_{\rm c}^{\rm r}(t)-c(E(0)-E_{\rm r}(0))e^{-ct}\geq 0
, which gives (

and non-negativity of the temperature profile require

from (

).
∎

IV
Stability Analysis

This section presents the main theoretical result: the exponential stability of the closed-loop trajectory tracking error.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
hold. Consider the closed-loop system of (
1
)–(
4
) with the tracking control (

\Phi(t):=\int_{0}^{s(t)}\big(T(x,t)-T^{r}(x-(s(t)-s_{\rm r}(t)),t)\big)^{2}dx+(s(t)-s_{\rm r}(t))^{2}+(\dot{s}(t)-\dot{s}_{\rm r}(t))^{2}

IV-B

Lyapunov Analysis

We consider the following Lyapunov function candidate
V(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s(t)}w(x,t)^{2}dx+X(t)^{\top}PX(t)
,
where
P\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix satisfying the Lyapunov equation
P(A+BK)+(A+BK)^{\top}P=-Q
,
for a given positive semidefinite matrix
Q=\lambda\diag(1,\kappa)
with
\lambda,\kappa\geq 0
.
Taking the time derivative of
V
along with (
39
)–(
42
)
owns a cross-term
Xw_{x}(s(t),t)
, which can be canceled by choosing the vector
D
as
D=-\frac{2}{\alpha}PB
.
As the solution to the Lyapunov equation can be explicitly obtained with the matrix
Q=\lambda\diag(1,\kappa)
and
(A,B,K)
, an explicit form of
D
can be given as
D=\lambda\beta\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{c}&\frac{\varepsilon}{c(1+c\varepsilon)}+\frac{\kappa}{1+c\varepsilon}\end{array}\right]^{\top}
.
Applying Young’s, Cauchy-Schwarz, and Poincaré’s inequalities to all the terms except for
\int_{0}^{s(t)}w_{x}(x,t)^{2}dx
and
X^{\top}QX
in the time derivative of the Lyapunov function, one can show that there exist positive constants
M_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
that are independent on parameters
(\lambda,c)
such that
\dot{V}\leq-\alpha\left(\frac{1}{4}-M_{1}|D|\right)\int_{0}^{s(t)}w_{x}(x,t)^{2}dx-(\lambda-M_{2}-M_{3}|D|)|X|^{2}+M_{4}\dot{s}(t)V
holds.
Therefore, by first choosing
\lambda\geq 4M_{2}
and then selecting a sufficiently large gain
c\geq c^{*}
to make
|D|
small enough satisfying
|D|<\min\left\{\frac{1}{8M_{1}},\frac{M_{2}}{M_{3}}\right\}
, we can ensure that the first two terms in the inequality of
\dot{V}
are negative definite. This guarantees the existence of a constant
M_{5}>0
such that
\dot{V}\leq-M_{5}V+M_{4}\dot{s}(t)V
.
The final term
M_{4}\dot{s}(t)V
in is handled by applying the conditions
\dot{s}(t)\geq 0
and
s_{0}<s(t)<L
shown in Proposition
3
, similarly to the procedure established in
[
19
]
, which leads to the exponential decay of
V(t)
. By norm equivalence between
(w,X)
and
(u,X)
, the exponential decay of
\Phi(t)
is proven, which completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
