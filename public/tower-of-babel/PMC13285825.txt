Responsive neurostimulation (RNS) programming
Authors: Philip M. McCarthy, David E. Burdette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Programming of RNS is an iterative process involving a logical progression of patient-specific electrocorticographic biomarker detections that inform stepwise progression of stimulation parameters. In this paper, we discuss the identification and detection of specific pro-ictal, pre-ictal, ictal onset, and seizure body biomarkers as they relate to optimal timing of neurostimulation. General principles underlying different stimulation paradigms are reviewed, and a practical progression of stimulation paradigms is presented with the goals of maximizing the acute seizure-disruptive effects of stimulation as well as the long term neuromodulatory effects, which translate to sustained improvement in seizure frequency and severity.

Introduction

In the setting of drug-resistant epilepsy [1], our attention turns toward surgical options. The highest rates of post-procedure seizure freedom are with resective and ablative surgeries; however, if a patient is not a candidate for or is uninterested in a destructive procedure, neurostimulation in the form of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), and responsive neurostimulation (RNS) comes to the fore. Of these US FDA-approved technologies, RNS is unique due to its closed loop stimulation paradigm and the lack of restriction regarding targets for brain stimulation. This manuscript will focus on optimization of RNS stimulation.

The discussions in this manuscript will include seizure biomarkers and their utilization in the programming of RNS. “Biomarker” is a perhaps over-utilized and under-validated term in the epilepsy space [2], so we will focus on potentially targetable (within the constraints of RNS detector parameters) and validated biomarkers. As we identify biomarkers for targeting detection, we remain cognizant of two over-arching phenomena that define the antiseizure effectiveness of RNS: acute disruptive [3] and long-term neuromodulatory effects [4,5]. These effects are predicated on the ability to link effective stimulation parameters to appropriate biomarkers.

Acute disruptive effects are directly seizure-targeted stimulations that disrupt or “break up” the rhythmic oscillations of the epileptogenic network. By contrast, the long-term neuromodulatory effects result from stimulation of non-ictal epileptic biomarkers. Stimulation of non-ictal biomarkers can promote network remodeling and, colloquially, “train the brain” to fire in a fashion incompatible with seizures. This manuscript aims to explore the vagaries of identifying appropriate biomarkers for stimulation and to provide a theoretical framework for optimal manipulation of stimulation parameters. We will focus on identifying biomarkers, designing appropriate stimulation paradigms, and utilizing a safe combination of the two.

Identification and detection of biomarkers for stimulation

Epilepsy surgery centers have gravitated away from intracranial recordings with subdural strips and grids, which have broad spatial, albeit superficial, coverage. Rather, they have moved toward stereotactically-placed depth electrodes that allow for broader sampling of deeper targets and definition of the thalamocortical epileptogenic networks. With both techniques we are met with a conundrum. Are the contacts that demonstrate ictal onsets within the seizure onset zone, or are they within an area of propagation? Similar biomarkers characterize ictal onsets within specific cortical regions whether the onset was initial or propagated [6]. Some ictal onsets, however, have greater specificity for the onset zone (e.g., ictal fingerprinting) [7] whereas others do not (e.g., low voltage fast activity) [6]. This conundrum is of particular relevance when planning destructive surgical solutions, but it is also important for targeting of RNS leads. Neurostimulation outside of the ictal onset zone/network is likely to produce less robust acute disruptive and neuromodulatory effects. We will focus on biomarkers that are well-vetted in the resective epilepsy surgery space, for they are the ones that will have the greatest specificity for the ictal onset network and should provide the most robust biomarkers for RNS detection.

Biomarkers for rational targeting of RNS detection include pro-ictal biomarkers (appearing hours to days before seizure-onset), pre-ictal biomarkers (appearing minutes before seizure onset), ictal onset biomarkers (electrographic changes at seizure onset including “chirps’” or fingerprints), seizure body biomarkers, and terminal frequency biomarkers (dominant frequency components of the resolving ictal discharge). These biomarkers have varying degrees of RNS validation. For those with little available RNS data, we turn to stereo-electroencephalographic (sEEG) literature as it relates to the seizure onset zone for resection. In those situations when the seizure onset zone or network is eloquent or too broad to accommodate a destructive procedure, it will become a target for neuromodulation. RNS utilizes three detection paradigms - line length, area, and bandwidth detectors, the practical features of which are well-described elsewhere and beyond the scope of this manuscript [8]. Rather, this section focuses on detectable electrographic monitoring biomarkers [9], through which the acute disruptive and long-term neuromodulatory effects can be realized.

Pro-ictal electrographic biomarkers, multidien rhythms, and the long term neuromodulatory effect

There is a burgeoning literature regarding ideal timing of RNS stimulation as it relates to the intrinsic multidien (multi-day) fluctuations between times of low and high seizure risk [10]. Evidence suggests that concentrating stimulation to the rising phase from low to high risk is more likely to produce beneficial long term neuromodulatory effects [11]. With an eye to the future, the mantra of RNS is that we are playing the long game. Unlike destructive procedures where antiseizure success or failure is rapidly evident, neuromodulatory devices in general and the RNS in particular affect a more gradual and progressive reduction in seizure frequency and severity.

Anderson and colleagues provide insight into the ideal timing of neurostimulation within that multidien cycle of epileptogenicity [11]. They compared RNS super responders (>90% seizure reduction) to intermediate and low responders. Over the course of multidien fluctuations between high and low seizure-risk states, stimulation in super-responders was concentrated in the rising phase from low to high seizure risk. Contrastingly, stimulation in the low and intermediate responders was concentrated at times of high risk. The implication is that well-timed interictal stimulation drives neuromodulatory improvement - not just random stimulation, but stimulation targeting biomarkers specific for increasing brain excitability, which will have greater expression during times of increasing seizure risk. Unfortunately, it is unclear what biomarker(s) are specific for this rising phase from low to high seizure risk. How might we actualize this temporal pattern of stimulation?

Pro-ictal biomarkers are expected to vary by patient and targeted brain region, but recent reports shed light on potential pro-ictal biomarker targets. Dallmer-Zerbe and colleagues evaluated longer term changes in brain dynamics comparing EEG characteristics at 10 a.m. on the day after implantation and then at regular intervals for the ensuing 1–12 days until the first seizure occurred during sEEG monitoring in a cohort of 35 patients weighted toward temporal lobe epilepsy. They differentiated between pre-ictal (minutes) and pro-ictal (days) biomarkers [12]. In brief, they identified a slow increase in gamma (30–100 Hz) power that was positively correlated with spike and high frequency oscillation (HFO) rates over the 1–12 days leading up to the first seizure – a change that was not seen in the minutes prior to seizure onset. These changes were confined to the seizure-onset zone whereas the phenomenon of critical slowing down [13], a measure of network stability, affected a broader network that included healthy cortex. Although the phenomenon of critical slowing down is not amenable to RNS detection, gamma bursts offer a detectable means of focusing stimulation to the pro-ictal state.

The spike and high frequency oscillation (HFO) combination has been described as better correlated with epileptogenic regions than either phenomenon (spike or HFO) by itself [14,15]. This spike-HFO combination represents a detectable multi-component biomarker [9] specific to the pro-ictal state, the rising phase between low and high seizure risk. The sampling rate of the RNS device does not allow detection of activities >125 Hz; however, in the Dallmer-Zerbe study, HFOs were assessed in the 80–126 Hz range due to confinement of their EEG sampling rate to 256 Hz [12]. Thus, HFO-spikes offer a biomarker, the stimulation of which may promote neuromodulation through pro-ictal stimulation (Fig. 1). Pre-ictal electrographic biomarkers and the acute disruptive effect

When identified and detected, pre-ictal biomarkers allow pre-ictal application of disruptive stimulation. Looking toward the resection literature and identification of resectable epileptogenic tissue, examples of pre-ictal biomarkers include increases in 80–500 Hz high frequency oscillations (HFOs) that have been described up to 30 min before a seizure [16]. The sampling rate of the RNS system does not allow detection of oscillations above 125 Hz, and activities in the 80–125 Hz range are typically low in amplitude, consistent with a 1/fα distribution (i.e., pink noise) [17], so this potential biomarker is difficult to operationalize. Pre-ictal spiking varies between patients whereby some exhibit increased and some decreased pre-seizure spiking [12]. Nevertheless, resection of brain tissue exhibiting interictal gamma spikes (spikes with intermixed/overriding gamma) and pre-ictal spiking have been shown to be predictors of surgical outcome [14,15]. It is reasonable to conclude that gamma-spikes present a targetable pre-ictal biomarker for responsive stimulation in those patients exhibiting this phenomenon.

Ictal onset electrographic biomarkers and the acute disruptive effect

Ictal onset biomarkers form the foundation for imposing an acute disruptive effect on seizures. Seven common electrographic ictal onset biomarkers have been well-described [6]. Low-voltage fast activity, low-frequency high-amplitude periodic spikes, sharp activity at ≤13 Hz, spike-and-wave activity, burst of high-amplitude polyspikes, burst suppression, and delta brushes represent well-characterized ictal biomarkers that are eminently detectable by RNS although these ictal onset biomarkers do not necessarily distinguish between seizure-onset and propagated activity [6]. Maximal acute disruptive effects on seizures will rely upon rapid and accurate detection of these electrographic patterns. Testament to the detectability of certain biomarkers like low voltage fast activity, the “chirp” (an intense narrow frequency band appearing quickly from the background on a spectrogram) has been described in animal models [18] and as a component of the recently described ictal fingerprint [7,19]. Seizure body electrographic biomarkers and seizure detection after implantation

When the RNS is implanted, detection is activated without stimulation. The initial detection algorithm targets the body of a seizure with a goal of capturing the seizure onset within the window of a Long Episode electrocorticogram (ECoG) storage, the Long Episode consisting of a predefined duration of sustained detection (often 30 s). Detection of the body of the seizure is designed with specificity rather than sensitivity in mind and typically uses a line length or area under the curve algorithm to detect increases in electrographic activity across a broad spectrum of frequencies and amplitudes. Once seizures are captured and the electrographic onset is evident, the detection algorithm is fine-tuned to capture the earliest ictal or pre-ictal change (Fig. 2).

Termination biomarkers as determinant for low frequency stimulation frequency

Terminal frequencies are of little utility for detection and timing of stimulation; nevertheless, they are of use in identifying alternative stimulation frequencies when the typical high frequency stimulation paradigms prove to be ineffective or poorly tolerated. This is discussed in the atypical stimulation paradigm section below.

Summary of appropriate biomarkers for responsive neurostimulation

The goals of RNS are to maximize the acute disruptive and long term neuromodulatory effects of responsive neurostimulation. Identification and targeting of biomarkers follow a natural progression after RNS implantation. Initial detection targets seizure body electrographic biomarkers. Once seizures have been captured and seizure onsets identified, attention turns to detection of seizure onset electrographic biomarkers and fine-tuning of seizure body detectors (to assure ongoing capture of ictal ECoGs). The final progression is detection of pro-ictal biomarkers to promote neuromodulation and detection of pre-ictal biomarkers to maximize acute disruptive effects of stimulation.

Stimulation

Electrical stimulation of neural structures forms the basis for neurostimulation in the treatment of epileptic seizures; however, over 12 million permutations of RNS stimulation parameters exist. Optimal parameters will vary by patient, stimulation target, and time (optimal stimulation parameters early in the neuromodulatory process will likely differ from ones late in the process).

Our knowledge of optimal stimulation parameters is focused upon the acute disruptive effects of neural stimulation, and we currently have little insight into ideal stimulation parameters that promote long term neuromodulatory effects. Recent investigations into stimulation parameters optimal for producing transitions from high risk seizure states (more long episodes [seizures and seizure-prone states] per hour and more interictal epileptiform activities per hour) to low risk seizure states demonstrate that charge density, stimulation frequency, and stimulation duration have different effects depending upon the stimulation site (mesial temporal versus neocortical) and the seizure risk state (high versus low) [20,21]. Current technology does not allow for seizure risk-state-dependent differences in stimulation parameters, and there is lack of data to understand this variability as it related to thalamocortical or thalamic-only stimulation.

Overriding principles for acute disruptive effects

Within the confines of RNS stimulation parameters, there is a therapeutic peak such that for a given stimulation frequency and location, charge densities below that peak evoke suboptimal disruption of the network. Charge densities above the peak increase the likelihood of afterdischarges and exhibit reduced efficacy. Empirically, we find that as we titrate the charge density, we reach an earlier therapeutic peak with high frequency stimulation relative to low frequency stimulation and an earlier therapeutic peak with cortical stimulation than with thalamic stimulation (Fig. 4). How an individual will respond to different stimulation parameters is empirically determined although we may gain some insight from stimulation performed during intracranial monitoring. Stimulation pathway

Bipolar stimulation (adjacent anode and cathode) produces current flow between adjacent contacts yielding a more confined electrical field whereas monopolar stimulation with all contacts being of the same polarity (cathodal or anodal) produces broader tissue activation [22]. A suggested therapy protocol [23] by NeuroPace recommends initial stimulation pathways based on implantation target (mesial temporal versus neocortical) and type of stimulating electrode (depth versus strip), but there are no published outcomes to define uniform recommendations. In fact, recent data demonstrates variable effectiveness of bipolar and monopolar stimulation depending upon the location (mesial temporal versus neocortical) and seizure risk state [23]. Regarding thalamic stimulation and by way of comparison, the SANTE trial of open loop anterior thalamic nucleus stimulation utilized initial monopolar (cathodal) stimulation [24] whereas the recently concluded NAUTILUS trial of centromedian-targeted stimulation for idiopathic generalized epilepsy utilized initial bipolar stimulation (personal experience).

Stimulation dose (charge density)

The “dose” of the applied current is represented by surface charge density, a measure of the number of electrons introduced into the neural tissue and a function of the surface area of the contact, the amperage of the current, and the pulse width per phase of the applied current. Charge density is restricted to <25 μC/cm2, well-below charge densities that produce tissue damage. With the RNS device, the charge density is calculated such that the programmer has immediate insight into the stimulation dose. For stimulation to produce an effect, it must produce an action potential; therefore, very low charge densities will have no effect. Once the action potential threshold is exceeded, we expect some network-disruptive effect to occur. As we titrate the charge densities, more action potentials are induced within the electrical field producing more disruptive influence on synaptically connected oscillating networks via action potentials propagating anterograde and retrograde from the site of stimulation. These desired effects continue to increase with sequential increases in charge density until we approach an apex, after which, further increases in charge density produce worsening of seizure control. The mechanisms underlying the worsening of seizure control are incompletely understood but are likely determined by network dynamics [21] and, at least in part, the threshold for after-discharge production.

Frequency of the applied stimulation

The interplay between frequency and charge density as they affect the RNS therapeutic curve is best understood for high frequency (200 Hz) cortical stimulation due to the volume of available experience insofar as most patients in the RNS pivotal trial and in subsequent case series were stimulated at 200 Hz [25,26]. However, smaller case series have demonstrated safety and efficacy of low-frequency stimulation (<10 Hz) [27]. Less data is available for mid frequencies between 20 and 100 Hz although there is a suggestion that mid-frequency monopolar stimulation of mesial temporal structures may be beneficial in the low-risk state and deleterious in the high-risk state, whereas when applied in a bipolar pattern in the high-risk state, it may be beneficial [23].

We can derive some insight regarding the therapeutic peak from effects of frequency on afterdischarges and seizures induced during intracranial mapping. Animal data demonstrates that higher frequency, longer duration, longer pulse width, and higher amperage stimulation are more likely to produce afterdischarges [28]. When controlling for amount of electricity delivered (i.e., progressively higher frequencies delivered with progressively lower pulse widths), there was a U-shaped dose-response curve with lowest thresholds for ADs between 50 and 200 Hz and the highest thresholds with stimulation >500 Hz [29]. In humans, data is more limited due to confinement of stimulation paradigms to low frequency (typically 1 Hz) for corticocortical evoked potentials and to 50 Hz for functional testing. Nevertheless, both low and high frequency stimulation can induce clinical seizures with high frequency stimulation more likely to do so and lower frequency stimulation less epileptogenic but more specific for the epileptogenic zone [30].

Stimulation location

The above literature is cortico-centric. There is a dearth of literature regarding afterdischarges and seizures due to thalamic stimulation. There are some reports of after-discharges with stimulation of ventrolateral nucleus in baboons [31] and nucleus medialis dorsalis in monkeys [32]. Nevertheless, thalamic stimulation can rarely exacerbate seizures. For example, one SANTE trial “outlier” experienced a marked increase in seizure frequency upon initiation of 5 V (V), 145 Hz open loop stimulation of the anterior thalamic nucleus but improved when stimulation was held and then reintroduced at 4 V 145 Hz [24].

Identifying the optimal stimulation paradigm

Randomized controlled data comparing stimulation paradigms is lacking; however, a plethora of empirical data informs effective cortical responsive neurostimulation [25] and open loop thalamic stimulation targeting the anterior nucleus of the thalamus [24]. Initial RNS cortical stimulation parameters utilize short burst (100–200 msec) and high frequency (200 Hz) stimulation administered in a bipolar, lead-to-lead, or cathodal stimulation pathway. Initial thalamic stimulation most often targets 125 Hz [17,33,34] or 142.5 Hz (personal experience, NAUTILUS trial). Initial charge densities are 0.5 μC/cm2, and these are increased in 0.5–1.0 μC/cm2-intervals at 3-month intervals to identify the therapeutic peak. The three-month intervals minimize the confounding effects of multidien variability in seizure frequency [10]. If clinical response is adequate at or before the high frequency therapeutic peak, further adjustment is not required. If, however, the response is inadequate, then adjustment of stimulation pathway or engaging atypical stimulation paradigms are considered. When identifying alternative stimulation frequencies, two methods have been described: 1. Terminal frequency stimulation [35], 2. Disharmonic low frequency stimulation [36] wherein we drive the network to cycle at frequencies incompatible with seizures.

Terminal frequency stimulation – utilization of a single stimulation frequency

The consistency between onset frequencies in seizures arising from the same network in an individual patient forms the foundation for RNS. Similarly, brain dynamics produce consistent frequencies as nonconvulsive seizures within that same network approach termination. Sobayo and Mogul evaluated these dynamics in a model of chronic rat limbic epilepsy with recording and stimulating leads in bilateral hippocampi and the left anteromedian thalamic nucleus [35]. Using empirical mode decomposition, they identified for each rat's seizures the terminal frequencies and found them to be consistent for individual rats but variable between rats (ranging from 7 to 300 Hz with the majority at 7 or 15 Hz). Ictal network stimulation at the terminal frequency was significantly more effective at terminating the seizure than was stimulation with other frequencies, but only when stimulation targeted structures exhibited high coherence at the terminal frequency. Extrapolating these findings to humans, if we can identify a resonant terminal frequency between contacts or leads and focus stimulation between those leads at that frequency, an effective low frequency stimulation paradigm emerges. The optimal charge density will be determined empirically, but a reasonable starting point is the charge density at which the preceding high frequency stimulation had to be abandoned. We will have more freedom to increase the charge density than we did with high frequency stimulation (Fig. 4). Unfortunately, the terminal frequency is not always readily identifiable when seizures outlast the recording window of the RNS electrocorticograms. Dysharmonic low frequency stimulation – utilization of more than one frequency

If driving the network to oscillate at the terminal frequency is helpful, then sequentially driving the network at dysharmonic low frequencies may prove uniquely disruptive. A recent approach was described by Geffrey and colleagues. They described two cases, one with bilateral corticothalamic RNS devices and one with idiopathic generalized epilepsy with a single RNS targeting bilateral centromedian nuclei of the thalamus. Neither patient responded to high frequency stimulation; however, both became seizure free utilizing a dysharmonic stimulation paradigm whereby burst 1 consisted of a 5-s, 5 Hz burst followed immediately by 5 s of 2 Hz stimulation for 5 s [36].

Summary of optimization of stimulation parameters for responsive neurostimulation

Ideal stimulation parameters vary by cortical or subcortical target, underlying pathology, network dynamics, and likely other factors yet to be identified. The overriding goal is to apply adequate charge density to exceed the action potential threshold and then titrate to efficacy and tolerability with an eye toward not exceeding the therapeutic peak, i.e., maximizing seizure disruption while minimizing seizure exacerbation. Initial stimulation parameters should be high frequency and adhere to the suggested therapy protocol [23]. Titration should proceed in a stepwise fashion until either adequate response or the therapeutic peak is reached. The RNS System Suggested Therapy Protocol [23] provides specific steps in the progression of charge density. If the therapeutic peak is inadequately effective or if adverse effects occur, then transition to an alternative stimulation paradigm such as low frequency stimulation is appropriate as is reassessment of the biomarkers that have been targeted for detection. Ultimately each patient will define their individual detection and stimulation paradigms.

Safety – balancing efficacy and tolerability

Limitations to titration of charge density are the potential for seizure exacerbation at high charge densities and the potential for untoward effects when the acutely disruptive impulses produce symptoms. The potential for seizure exacerbation is addressed above in terms of the therapeutic peak and the need to avoid charge densities beyond that peak.

Regarding stimulation-induced side effects, RNS is generally well-tolerated; however, when stimulation occurs within or near eloquent cortex, eloquent white matter tracts, or thalamic first order relay nuclei, stimulation may produce untoward symptoms. These effects are particularly evident in the thalamus where first order nuclei (e.g., ventroposterolateral and ventroposteromedial nuclei) are near higher order nuclei (e.g., centromedian nucleus) thereby allowing moderate charge densities to produce sensory effects from impingement of the stimulation field onto first order thalamic relay nuclei.

Potential stimulus-induced adverse effects can often be predicted with mapping during stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) if the sEEG leads target the same region as the RNS contacts. Often the RNS-targeted higher order thalamic nuclei were not previously sampled by sEEG or the trajectory of the RNS lead differs from that of the sEEG (e.g., longitudinal approach hippocampal depth following sEEG sampling with orthogonal approach depth leads). In these cases, strategies are required to mitigate the adverse effects.

A practical approach to mitigation of stimulus-induced adverse effects is to identify the contact(s) producing the adverse effect [38]. This is done by stimulating in a monopolar pathway the contact being assessed (cathode) with the can (ferule) being the anode. Threshold for sensory perception can then be assessed, and the most sensitive contacts altered in one of three ways: 1. Reduce the stimulation frequency; 2. Reduce the charge density; 3. Change the stimulation pathway (from monopolar to bipolar or vice versa); 4. Remove the contact from the stimulation paradigm [38]. Switching from high to low frequency has been shown to be safe and effective in both cortical [27] and thalamic [17] contacts.

Safety – battery life

The battery life for RNS model 320 with short burst (100–200 ms), high frequency stimulation is over a decade. Higher charge densities, longer stimulation durations, higher stimulation frequencies, and higher stimulation rates combine to reduce battery life [39]. High stimulation rates are often a sign of over-sensitive detection paradigms and are readily identified through the trending data for “Therapies.” When daily therapies exceed 6000 or when no multidien rhythm is evident in therapy administration, consider fine-tuning the detection parameters. When there is both high therapy burden and lack of multidien variation, battery life is at risk, and appropriate changes need to be made. The battery-draining effects of over-detection are particularly salient with thalamic stimulation where more prolonged stimulation durations are commonly utilized. Exceptional circumstances – status epilepticus

With status epilepticus, the emphasis is on maximizing the acute disruptive effects of stimulation while minimal attention is paid to long term neuromodulation. More rapid changes are made since these patients are usually being simultaneously monitored with scalp electrodes. Challenges arise when the ictal signature changes with level of sedation. Nevertheless, the concept of therapeutic peak still applies. Cortical stimulation will need to be applied judiciously to avoid exceeding the therapeutic peak. A case series of RNS in super refractory status epilepticus in 10 patients describes a mix of high and low frequency cortical and thalamic stimulation paradigms [40]. Similarly with stimulation paradigms outside of the setting of status epilepticus, higher charge densities are tolerated in the thalamus relative to cortex and with low relative to high frequency stimulation.

Lead implantation strategy

The above observations regarding optimization of detection and stimulation parameters are predicated on the assumption that the implanted leads are targeting key hubs or nodes in the epileptogenic network. When leads are mis-targeted, detection is delayed, pro- and pre-ictal biomarkers may be inevident, and stimulation is less effective. Neurostimulation administered too many degrees of separation from the epileptogenic network produces poorer outcomes [41]. However, when key nodes of the epileptogenic network are accurately identified, basic principles for lead targeting emerge.

Seizures arising from a single discrete network

When seizures arise from one discrete network, the most common and effective approach is to target the seizure onset network with one lead and to target a site of early propagation with the other. Examples include a longitudinal hippocampal lead paired with either a lead longitudinally targeting the ipsilateral parahippocampal gyrus or a strip overlying the inferior ipsilateral temporal neocortex [42].

Seizures arising from two discrete networks

In this situation the most common and effective strategy is to target the predicted (if no intracranial monitoring was performed) or proved (with intracranial monitoring) most active node in each network. For example, left and right hippocampal longitudinal depth electrodes are commonly used for independent left and right mesial temporal lobe seizures [42].

Seizures arising from a unilateral broad network

The term, broad network, has been applied to seizures that have near-simultaneous onsets in at least 6 recording contacts (often in disparate leads) [43]. The two most common approaches to RNS targeting are to stimulate the opposite poles (antipodes) of the cortical network - a process that is colloquially described as “goal-posting” the network. Ma and colleagues reported a case series of 30 patients treated with this approach such that the antipodal leads were 1–11.5 cm (cm) apart (median 2.7 cm for strips and 3.4 cm for depths) achieving a median 75.5% seizure reduction [43]. Stimulation pathways varied such that the majority employed lead-to-lead although others used bipolar and cathodal paradigms [43]. An alternative approach is to rotate the antipodes by 90° and target the most robust cortical node and an in-network thalamic nucleus [17]. Such corticothalamic approaches require accurate identification of in-network higher order thalamic nuclei. At times, the appropriate thalamic nucleus is evident (e.g., anterior nucleus of the thalamus for limbic epilepsies [44] or pulvinar for posterior quadrant onsets [45]); however, optimal choice of thalamic target may require hypothesis-driven sampling of higher order thalamic nuclei and cerebro-cerebral evoked potentials to identify the ideal thalamic target [46,47].

Seizures arising from bihemispheric broad and/or multifocal networks

Networks of this ilk are rife in the setting of developmental epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) and well-characterized in Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome (LGS) [48]. Case reports and case series have most often reported a single RNS targeting bilateral centromedian nuclei of the thalami (CMT) to reduce seizures and improve functioning and quality of life, particularly in pediatric LGS patients [39,49,50]. A smaller case series reported improvement with single RNS devices targeting bilateral pulvinar in two patients with posteriorly dominant discharges and DEE [51]. An investigation of bilateral corticothalmically-targeted RNS devices is under way [52]. In this prospective cohort, each RNS targets ipsilateral CMT with a depth lead and ipsilateral premotor cortex/caudal middle frontal gyrus with a strip lead [48]. The results from this NIH-sponsored trial will help determine whether a single LGS solution exists and, if not, which patients are most likely to benefit from the bilateral thalamocortical stimulation approach. In the less network-diverse setting of idiopathic generalized epilepsy, case series as well as the recently reported NAUTILUS trial are pointing toward a single RNS device solution targeting bilateral CMT [33,34,53].

Limitations

This manuscript has a number of limitations borne of publications bias due to lack of negative publications and lack of prospective data. The authors’ approach to programming when adherence to the RNS System Suggested Therapy Protocol produces inadequate results is guided by their experience and interpretation of the available data. There is a lack of uniformity between centers. Oversight from NeuroPace Field Engineers typically produces consistency in programmed detection parameters. Different centers, though, have different levels of comfort with higher charge densities and transitions to low frequency stimulation. Two particular limitations bear more scrutiny.

Biomarkers for stimulation

This manuscript represents an attempt to meld variously-timed, sEEG-identified, seizure-associated biomarkers that, when detected, may produce a condition conducive to maximizing the acute disruptive and long term neuromodulatory effects of responsive neurostimulation. Although there is data that addresses the impact of stimulation timing on these effects, there is no prospective data proving the described biomarkers to be the ideal targets for optimization of stimulation timing. Further, it is unknown whether the ideal timing of stimulation during the up phase from low seizure risk to high seizure risk is epiphenomenal (contacts within the epileptogenic network are more likely than contacts within propagated hubs/nodes to evidence pro- and pre-ictal biomarkers) or is cause and effect. Future research is required definitively to confirm the targets for and utility of pro- and pre-ictal stimulation.

Optimization of stimulation paradigms

The available literature for brain stimulation is largely empirical and driven by results from pivotal trials, in which stimulation is biased toward high frequency. Alternative stimulation strategies including mid and low frequency are largely based upon case series. Formal, protocol-driven stimulation paradigms neglect the interindividual vagaries of epileptogenic network behaviors, so identification of concrete stimulation progressions is difficult. Future clinical investigations could focus on groups of patients with similar neuropathological and electrophysiological findings to identify common successful approaches; however, the authors suspect that the most fruitful insights will arise from signal processing of recorded pre-ictal and ictal biomarkers to identify the most effective stimulation paradigms in a fashion analogous to the work of Sobayo and Mogul [35].
