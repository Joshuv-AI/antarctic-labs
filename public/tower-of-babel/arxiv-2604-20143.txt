Machine learning moment closure models for the radiative transfer equation IV: enforcing symmetrizable hyperbolicity in two dimensions
Juntao Huang

Abstract
This is our fourth work in the series on machine learning (ML) moment closure models for the radiative transfer equation (RTE). In the first three papers of this series, we considered the RTE in slab geometry in 1D1V (i.e. one dimension in physical space and one dimension in angular space), and introduced a gradient-based ML moment closure [ 1 ] , then enforced the hyperbolicity through a symmetrizer [ 2 ] , or together with physical characteristic speeds by learning the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix [ 3 ] . Here, we extend our framework to the RTE in 2D2V (i.e. two dimensions in physical space and two dimensions in angular space). The main idea is to preserve the leading part of the classical P N P_{N} model and modify only the highest-order block row. By analyzing the structural properties of the P N P_{N} model, we show that its coefficient matrices are symmetric and admit a block-tridiagonal structure. Then we use this property to introduce a block-diagonal symmetrizer for the ML moment model and derive explicit algebraic conditions on the closure blocks which guarantee the symmetrizable hyperbolicity of the resulting ML system. These conditions lead to a natural parametrization of the closure in terms of a symmetric positive definite matrix together with symmetric closure blocks, which can be learned from data while automatically enforcing symmetrizable hyperbolicity by construction. The numerical results show that the proposed framework improves upon the classical P N P_{N} model while maintaining hyperbolicity.



Keywords:
radiative transfer equation; moment closure; machine learning; symmetrizable hyperbolicity; spherical harmonics; neural networks

These works have shown the promise of ML closures for kinetic equations, but they also indicate that careful design is needed to ensure that the learned closure preserves the structural properties of the underlying PDE. For example, if the learned closure breaks hyperbolicity, the resulting system can lose long-time stability and generate unphysical solutions, even if it fits the training data well. Hence, it is important to incorporate structure preservation into the ML closure design.

In our previous work, we proposed a structure-preserving ML moment closure framework for the RTE in slab geometry
[
1
,
2
,

. In the first work
[

, motivated by the exact free-streaming closure, we proposed to learn the gradient of the unclosed moment rather than the moment itself

[

, we enforced the hyperbolicity by constructing a symmetrizer for the ML closure system. In the third work
[
3

, we made use of the lower Hessenberg structure of the coefficient matrix to approximate the eigenvalues by neural networks with additional control of characteristic speeds. This framework was extended to the BGK equations in
[
47
]
and the linear Boltzmann equation with uncertainties in
[
48
]
.
Together, these works showed that accurate ML closures can be built only when the neural network architecture is carefully designed to preserve the structural properties of the PDE.

The purpose of this paper is to extend our framework from the radiative transfer equation (RTE) in slab geometry, namely the 1D1V setting (one physical dimension and one angular dimension), to the RTE in the 2D2V setting (two physical dimensions and two angular dimensions). This extension is highly nontrivial. In the 1D case, the hyperbolicity of the moment system is determined by the eigenstructure of a single coefficient matrix. In 2D, however, one must require that any linear combination of the coefficient matrices in the
x
- and
y
-directions is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}
, which is a substantially stronger and more delicate condition.
In addition, the algebraic structures of the coefficient matrices in the 2D system become much more complicated than in 1D. In the 1D setting, moments are ordered by the degree of the Legendre polynomial basis, and the coefficient matrix has a lower Hessenberg form, making it possible to construct closures by modifying only the last row. In 2D, however, moments are organized by both degree and order of the spherical harmonics basis, and the coefficient matrices exhibit a block structure indexed by degree. The resulting coupling pattern is much more intricate: each moment interacts with multiple neighboring moments within the same degree as well as across adjacent degrees. As a consequence, the closure strategies developed in 1D do not directly extend to 2D.

In this work, we address these challenges by first identifying the structural properties of the real-valued
P_{N}
system in 2D. We show that the coefficient matrices of the
P_{N}
model are symmetric and block tridiagonal, with blocks indexed by degree. This structure makes it possible to preserve the leading part of the
P_{N}
system and modify only the highest-order block row, which forms the foundation of our ML closure construction. We then introduce a block-diagonal symmetrizer and derive explicit algebraic conditions on the closure blocks that ensure symmetrizable hyperbolicity of the resulting system. This perspective leads to a natural parametrization of the ML closure in terms of a symmetric positive definite matrix together with symmetric closure blocks, which can be represented by neural networks while automatically enforcing the required structural constraints by construction.
Our ML moment closure is trained through a residual-based loss function for the highest-order moment equation. Numerical experiments are conducted on a series of tasks which clearly show that the proposed method can improve on the classical
P_{N}
model when trained on kinetic reference data.

3

4

5

2
The RTE and the
P_{N}
model in 2D

In this section, we begin from the RTE and derive the two-dimensional

P_{N}
system. Then we analyze the structural properties of the
P_{N}
system, which will later guide the ML closure construction. In particular, we will see that the coefficient matrices in the
P_{N}
system are symmetric and admit a block-tridiagonal structure after grouping the moments by polynomial degree.

We consider the RTE in two spatial dimensions with isotropic scattering:

\partial_{t}\psi+\Omega_{x}\partial_{x}\psi+\Omega_{y}\partial_{y}\psi=\sigma_{s}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\psi(t,x,y,\Omega^{\prime})\,d\Omega^{\prime}-\psi\right)-\sigma_{a}\psi,

(2.1)

for
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Omega=(\Omega_{x},\Omega_{y},\Omega_{z})\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The angular variable
\Omega
can also be parameterized by

\Omega=(\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos\varphi,\,\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\sin\varphi,\,\mu),\qquad\mu\in[-1,1],\ \varphi\in[0,2\pi).

(2.2)

Here
\psi=\psi(t,x,y,\Omega)
is the specific intensity,
\sigma_{s}=\sigma_{s}(x,y)\geq 0
is the scattering cross section, and
\sigma_{a}=\sigma_{a}(x,y)\geq 0
is the absorption cross section. The collision operator on the right hand side is isotropic, since the scattering redistributes particles uniformly in all directions.

We assume the problem is planar, so that the coefficients are independent of the third spatial variable and the solution is even with respect to
\mu=\Omega_{z}
:

\psi(t,x,y,\mu,\varphi)=\psi(t,x,y,-\mu,\varphi).

This symmetry is preserved by (
2.1
), since both transport coefficients
\Omega_{x}
,
\Omega_{y}
and the isotropic collision operator are even in
\mu
.

To derive the
P_{N}
system, we first introduce a set of real-valued orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. Define the real-valued spherical harmonics by

\displaystyle\Phi_{l}^{0}(\mu,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=N_{l}^{0}P_{l}(\mu),\qquad l\geq 0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}}(\mu,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2}\,N_{l}^{m}P_{l}^{m}(\mu)\cos(m\varphi),\qquad 1\leq m\leq l,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}(\mu,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2}\,N_{l}^{m}P_{l}^{m}(\mu)\sin(m\varphi),\qquad 1\leq m\leq l.

(2.5)

Here
P_{l}
and
P_{l}^{m}
denote the Legendre and associated Legendre polynomials and the constants
N_{l}^{m}:=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}
. By the orthogonality of the (associated) Legendre polynomials in
\mu
and the orthogonality of
\{1,\cos(m\varphi),\sin(m\varphi)\}
in
\varphi
, the family

\bigl\{\Phi_{l}^{0},\ \Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}},\ \Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}:l\geq 0,\ 1\leq m\leq l\bigr\}

(2.6)

forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
; see the details in Theorem
A.1
in the appendix. In addition, the parity relations
P_{l}(-\mu)=(-1)^{l}P_{l}(\mu)
and
P_{l}^{m}(-\mu)=(-1)^{l+m}P_{l}^{m}(\mu)
imply

\Phi_{l}^{0}(-\mu,\varphi)=(-1)^{l}\Phi_{l}^{0}(\mu,\varphi),\qquad\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}/\mathrm{s}}(-\mu,\varphi)=(-1)^{l+m}\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}/\mathrm{s}}(\mu,\varphi).

(2.7)

Hence, for an even function
\psi
of
\mu
, we introduce the truncated space

V_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\Bigl(\{\Phi_{l}^{0}:0\leq l\leq N,\ l\ \text{even}\}\cup\{\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}},\ \Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}:0\leq l\leq N,\ 1\leq m\leq l,\ l+m\ \text{even}\}\Bigr),

(2.8)

and approximate
\psi
by

\psi_{N}(t,x,y,\Omega)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq l\leq N\\
l\ \mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}R_{l}^{0}(t,x,y)\,\Phi_{l}^{0}(\Omega)+\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq l\\
l+m\ \mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\Bigl(R_{l}^{m}(t,x,y)\,\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)+I_{l}^{m}(t,x,y)\,\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}(\Omega)\Bigr),

(2.9)

where the coefficients are the moments of
\psi
in the corresponding basis functions:

R_{l}^{0}=\langle\psi,\Phi_{l}^{0}\rangle,\qquad R_{l}^{m}=\langle\psi,\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}}\rangle,\qquad I_{l}^{m}=\langle\psi,\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}\rangle,

(2.10)

with
\langle\cdot\rangle
the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
For each degree
l
, the number of moments is
l+1
. We order them as
\bm{u}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{l+1}
:

\bm{u}_{l}=\begin{cases}(R_{l}^{0},\,R_{l}^{2},\,I_{l}^{2},\,\dots,\,R_{l}^{l},\,I_{l}^{l})^{T},&l\ \text{even},\\[3.00003pt]
(R_{l}^{1},\,I_{l}^{1},\,R_{l}^{3},\,I_{l}^{3},\,\dots,\,R_{l}^{l},\,I_{l}^{l})^{T},&l\ \text{odd},\end{cases}

(2.11)

and collect all the moments in

\bm{u}=(\bm{u}_{0},\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{1}{2}(N+1)(N+2)}.

(2.12)

For
l\geq 0
, let
V_{l}\subset V_{N}
denote the span of the basis functions of degree
l
. Using the recursion relations for associated Legendre polynomials together with the trigonometric identities,
one finds that multiplication by
\Omega_{x}=\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos\varphi
and
\Omega_{y}=\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\sin\varphi
changes the degree only from
l
to
l\pm 1
and the order only from
m
to
m\pm 1
. Hence, we have the degree coupling property

\Omega_{x}V_{l}\subset V_{l-1}\oplus V_{l+1},\qquad\Omega_{y}V_{l}\subset V_{l-1}\oplus V_{l+1}.

(2.13)

Projecting (
2.1
) onto
V_{N}
yields the real-valued
P_{N}
system

\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\mathcal{A}\,\partial_{x}\bm{u}+\mathcal{B}\,\partial_{y}\bm{u}=\mathcal{Q}\bm{u},

(2.14)

where the coefficient matrices
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are generated by the transport terms
\Omega_{x}
and
\Omega_{y}
:

(\mathcal{A})_{\alpha\beta}=\langle\Omega_{x}\Phi_{\beta},\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle,\qquad(\mathcal{B})_{\alpha\beta}=\langle\Omega_{y}\Phi_{\beta},\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle.

(2.15)

Here
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}(x,y)
is the diagonal collision matrix derived from absorption and isotropic scattering on the right hand side of (
2.1
):

\mathcal{Q}=\operatorname{diag}(Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{N}),\qquad Q_{0}=-\sigma_{a},\qquad Q_{l}=-(\sigma_{a}+\sigma_{s})I_{l+1},\quad 1\leq l\leq N.

(2.16)

By the definition of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
in (
2.15
), one can easily show that they are symmetric matrices. Moreover, by the degree coupling property in (
2.13
), the degree ordering
\bm{u}=(\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{N})^{T}
gives the block tridiagonal form

\mathcal{A}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&A_{0}^{T}\\
A_{0}&0&A_{1}^{T}\\
&A_{1}&0&A_{2}^{T}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&A_{N-2}&0&A_{N-1}^{T}\\
&&&&A_{N-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{B}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{0}^{T}\\
B_{0}&0&B_{1}^{T}\\
&B_{1}&0&B_{2}^{T}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&B_{N-2}&0&B_{N-1}^{T}\\
&&&&B_{N-1}&0\end{pmatrix},

(2.17)

with

A_{l},B_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{(l+2)\times(l+1)},\quad 0\leq l\leq N-1.

(2.18)

Equivalently, for
1\leq l\leq N-1
the moment of degree
l
satisfies

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{l}+\frac{1}{2}A_{l-1}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{l-1}+\frac{1}{2}A_{l}^{T}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{l+1}+\frac{1}{2}B_{l-1}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{l-1}+\frac{1}{2}B_{l}^{T}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{l+1}=Q_{l}\bm{u}_{l}.

(2.19)

This indicates that the evolution of the moments of degree
l
only depends on the moments of degree
l-1
,
l
, and
l+1
. The explicit form of the blocks
A_{l}
and
B_{l}
can be derived from the recursion relations for associated Legendre polynomials. We omit the details here since they are not needed for the ML closure, and refer the readers to
[
49
]
for more details.

Therefore, the
P_{N}
system (
2.14
) is hyperbolic with the coefficient matrices
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
in (
2.17
) symmetric and block tridiagonal. This is the starting point for the ML closure construction in the next section.

3
Hyperbolic ML moment closure

Our ML closure idea is to preserve the head of the real-valued
P_{N}
system and modify only the last block row. This is equivalent to modifying only the evolution equation for the highest-degree moment
\bm{u}_{N}
while keeping the equations for
\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{N-1}
unchanged. The motivation is that the evolution equations of the lower-degree moments are exact and only approximation are introduced in the highest-degree moment equation in the
P_{N}
system. We therefore introduce the matrices for the ML closure as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&A_{0}^{T}\\
A_{0}&0&A_{1}^{T}\\
&A_{1}&0&A_{2}^{T}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&A_{N-2}&0&A_{N-1}^{T}\\
G_{0}&G_{1}&\cdots&G_{N-2}&G_{N-1}&G_{N}\end{pmatrix},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\textrm{ML}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{0}^{T}\\
B_{0}&0&B_{1}^{T}\\
&B_{1}&0&B_{2}^{T}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&B_{N-2}&0&B_{N-1}^{T}\\
K_{0}&K_{1}&\cdots&K_{N-2}&K_{N-1}&K_{N}\end{pmatrix},

where the matrices
G_{j},\ K_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(j+1)}
for
j=0,\dots,N
are closure terms to be learned from data. Generally, they may depend nonlinearly on the moment vector
\bm{u}
, which will be approximated by neural networks. The corresponding ML moment closure system is

\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}\partial_{x}\bm{u}+\mathcal{B}^{\textrm{ML}}\partial_{y}\bm{u}=\mathcal{Q}\bm{u}.

(3.2)

The
P_{N}
system (
2.14
) is symmetric hyperbolic, with symmetrizer given by the identity matrix. To preserve the symmetrizable hyperbolicity for the new system (
3.2
), we seek a block-diagonal symmetrizer

\mathcal{S}=\operatorname{diag}(I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{N-1},H),\quad H\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}.

(3.3)

Here
I_{r}
denotes the
r\times r
identity matrix and
H
is a symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix to be determined. The requirement is that
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}
and
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{B}^{\textrm{ML}}
are both symmetric. To achieve this, we compute

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&A_{0}^{T}\\
A_{0}&0&A_{1}^{T}\\
&A_{1}&0&A_{2}^{T}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&A_{N-2}&0&A_{N-1}^{T}\\
HG_{0}&HG_{1}&\cdots&HG_{N-2}&HG_{N-1}&HG_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.4)

The upper left
N\times N
block of
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}
is exactly the same as that of the original
P_{N}
matrix
\mathcal{A}
, hence it is already symmetric. Therefore, to make
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}
symmetric, it is sufficient to compare the last block row and the last block
column, which leads to the following conditions for the closure terms. First, we have

HG_{j}=0,\quad 0\leq j\leq N-2.

(3.5)

which implies that
G_{j}=0
for
0\leq j\leq N-2
due to the invertibility of
H
.
Second, we have

HG_{N-1}=A_{N-1},

(3.6)

or equivalently,

G_{N-1}=H^{-1}A_{N-1}.

(3.7)

And lastly, we have

HG_{N}=(HG_{N})^{T}.

(3.8)

Applying the same argument to
\mathcal{B}^{\textrm{ML}}
, we have

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{B}^{\textrm{ML}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{0}^{T}\\
B_{0}&0&B_{1}^{T}\\
&B_{1}&0&B_{2}^{T}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&B_{N-2}&0&B_{N-1}^{T}\\
HK_{0}&HK_{1}&\cdots&HK_{N-2}&HK_{N-1}&HK_{N}\end{pmatrix},

(3.9)

and therefore

K_{0}=\cdots=K_{N-2}=0,\qquad K_{N-1}=H^{-1}B_{N-1},\qquad HK_{N}=(HK_{N})^{T}.

(3.10)

We summarize the characterization of the hyperbolicity constraints for the ML closure as follows:

Theorem 3.1
.

The ML moment closure model (
3.2
) with the coefficients
given by (
3.1
) is symmetrizable hyperbolic if and
only if

G_{j}=0,\qquad K_{j}=0,\qquad 0\leq j\leq N-2,

(3.11)

and any one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

1.

There exists an SPD matrix
H
such that

G_{N-1}=H^{-1}A_{N-1},\qquad K_{N-1}=H^{-1}B_{N-1},\qquad HG_{N}=(HG_{N})^{T},\qquad HK_{N}=(HK_{N})^{T}.

(3.12)

2.

There exist an SPD matrix
H
and symmetric matrices
M_{x}
and

M_{y}
such that

G_{N-1}=H^{-1}A_{N-1},\qquad K_{N-1}=H^{-1}B_{N-1},\qquad G_{N}=H^{-1}M_{x},\qquad K_{N}=H^{-1}M_{y}.

(3.13)

3.

There exist an SPD matrix
H
and symmetric matrices
M_{x}
and

M_{y}
such that

G_{N-1}=HA_{N-1},\qquad K_{N-1}=HB_{N-1},\qquad G_{N}=HM_{x},\qquad K_{N}=HM_{y}.

(3.14)

In the implementation, we use the formulation
(
3.14
).

Proof.

The vanishing conditions for
G_{j}
and
K_{j}
with
0\leq j\leq N-2
, together with (
3.12
), are exactly the relations obtained above by comparing the last block row and last block column of
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{ML}}
and
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{B}^{\textrm{ML}}
.

Condition (
3.13
) is just a rewriting of (
3.12
), if we set

M_{x}:=HG_{N},\qquad M_{y}:=HK_{N},

(3.15)

then
M_{x}
and
M_{y}
are symmetric when
HG_{N}
and
HK_{N}
are symmetric, and conversely (
3.12
) follows from
(
3.13
) by substituting
HG_{N}=M_{x}
and
HK_{N}=M_{y}
.

Finally, (
3.13
) and (
3.14
)
are equivalent after the change of variable
\widetilde{H}=H^{-1}
and the
relabeling
\widetilde{H}\mapsto H
, since the inverse of an SPD matrix is also
SPD.
∎

To guarantee the constraints of hyperbolicity in (
3.14
), we may set

H=LL^{T}+\varepsilon I,

(3.16)

where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}
is a lower triangular matrix and
\varepsilon>0
is a small constant to ensure the positive definiteness of
H
. Then
H
is always an SPD matrix for any
L
. To define
M_{x}
and
M_{y}
, we take the symmetric part of arbitrary matrices
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
:

M_{x}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{x}+L_{x}^{T}),\qquad M_{y}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{y}+L_{y}^{T}),

(3.17)

Note that
L
,
L_{x}
, and
L_{y}
will be taken as nonlinear matrix-valued functions of the moment vector
\bm{u}
:

L=L(\bm{u}),\qquad L_{x}=L_{x}(\bm{u}),\qquad L_{y}=L_{y}(\bm{u}),

(3.18)

which will be parameterized by multilayer perceptrons (MLPs). With this parametrization, the hyperbolicity constraints are automatically satisfied for any choice of the parameters in
L
,
L_{x}
, and
L_{y}
. Therefore, the closure terms
G_{N-1}
,
K_{N-1}
,
G_{N}
, and
K_{N}
can be learned from data without worrying about the loss of hyperbolicity. The neural network architecture is illustrated in Fig.

.

M_{y}
, respectively. With this architecture, the hyperbolicity constraints in (
3.14
) are automatically satisfied for any choice of the parameters in the MLPs.

To derive the loss function, we consider the difference between the exact evolution and the closed evolution. We can write down the exact evolution of the highest-order moment
\bm{u}_{N}
(without closure) as:

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{N}+\frac{1}{2}A_{N-1}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N-1}+\frac{1}{2}A_{N}^{T}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N+1}+\frac{1}{2}B_{N-1}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N-1}+\frac{1}{2}B_{N}^{T}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N+1}=Q_{N}\bm{u}_{N}.

(3.19)

We replace this with the closed evolution

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{N}+\frac{1}{2}G_{N-1}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N-1}+\frac{1}{2}G_{N}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N}+\frac{1}{2}K_{N-1}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N-1}+\frac{1}{2}K_{N}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N}=Q_{N}\bm{u}_{N}.

(3.20)

Compare the two equations, we can define the loss function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}={}

\displaystyle\Bigl\|(G_{N-1}-A_{N-1})\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N-1}+G_{N}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N}-A_{N}^{T}\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{N+1}

(3.21)

\displaystyle+(K_{N-1}-B_{N-1})\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N-1}+K_{N}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N}-B_{N}^{T}\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{N+1}\Bigr\|^{2}.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments to investigate the performance of our ML moment closure.
We first illustrate the training and evaluation procedure. We then begin with a simple single-mode sine-wave experiment that serves as a calibration test for the training pipeline and for the choice of retained order. We then move to a family of random multi-mode sine-wave initial conditions, which is the main in-family generalization test. Finally, we consider training on multi-mode data with varying material parameters in order to test robustness with respect to both the initial condition and the medium.

4.1
Training and Evaluation Procedure

In this part, we describe the training and evaluation procedure for the ML closure model. First, to generate the training data, we make use of a
P_{N}
solver based on StaRMAP
[
49
]
, a second-order staggered-grid method implemented in MATLAB. Here, we run the solver at a sufficiently large moment order to obtain a good approximation to the kinetic solution. For each trajectory, we save the moments
\bm{u}_{l}
for
0\leq l\leq{N+1}
at the specified time steps.
Then, we preprocess the training data to compute the spatial derivatives
\partial_{x}\bm{u}_{l}
and
\partial_{y}\bm{u}_{l}
for
l=N-1,N,N+1
using a second-order finite difference method.

Then, we train the ML closure model by minimizing the residual-based loss in (
3.21
) on the processed reference trajectories. The input to the network is the moment vector
(\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{N})
, while the derivatives of
\bm{u}_{N-1}
,
\bm{u}_{N}
, and
\bm{u}_{N+1}
are used inside the loss to evaluate the mismatch in the last retained block.

For the hyperbolic model, the network consists of one branch generating an SPD matrix
H(\bm{u})
and two branches generating symmetric matrices
M_{x}(\bm{u})
and
M_{y}(\bm{u})
. These outputs determine the learnable closure blocks through the structural formulas in (
3.14
), so the architecture enforces the symmetry and positivity constraints by construction.

In the numerical experiments, we use the mini-batch AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
and a batch size of 1024. The MLP architecture consists of 2 hidden layers with 64 neurons in each layer, if not specified otherwise. We train for 1000 epochs and select the checkpoint with the smallest validation loss for evaluation.

4.4
Task 3: a family of multi-mode sine wave solutions with different material parameters

The previous two tasks keep the material coefficients fixed and only vary the initial condition. This setting is useful for testing whether the ML closure can improve on the linear
P_{N}
baseline within one homogeneous medium, but it does not yet address a more important practical question: whether the ML closure can remain accurate across different scattering and absorption regimes. The purpose of this third task is therefore to enlarge the training distribution in two directions at once: (1) the initial data are still taken from the random multi-sine family (
4.2
), so that the spatial profiles remain diverse; (2) the homogeneous material parameters are now also varied from one training trajectory to another, by sampling both
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{s}
.

To generate the training data for this task, we follow the same procedure as in Task 2 to run the StaRMAP solver for the
P_{50}
model on the time interval
[0,1]
with snapshots at
t=0,0.1,\dots,1
. However, instead of fixing the material parameters in Task 2, we now sample them from prescribed ranges for each random multi-sine realization. In particular, we take
\sigma_{a}\in[0,1]
and
\sigma_{s}\in[1,10]
to cover a range of absorption and scattering regimes. We take the random seeds
0,1,\dots,99
to generate a total of
100
trajectories with different multi-sine initial conditions and different material parameters.

In practice, storing all 100 seeds with each seed containing 11 snapshots of the full
P_{50}
moments and their derivatives can exceed the available local storage, especially when the retained order
N

We therefore replace the one-shot data generation pipeline by a streaming selection procedure: after generating one seed, we compute the score of the corresponding snapshot file based on the magnitude and spatial variation of the omitted degree block
\bm{u}_{N+1}

P_{N}
closure residual:

s=\|\bm{u}_{N+1}\|+\|\nabla\bm{u}_{N+1}\|.

(4.3)

Hence we keep the files whose next order moment is both large in magnitude and rapidly varying in space, since these are precisely the regions where the closure model is expected to matter most. Maintaining a storage budget of
K
files is then straightforward: after each newly processed seed, we recompute the scores of the currently available derivative files, keep the top
K
files, and delete the rest. This yields an online top-
K
selection rule that never requires the full
0,\dots,99
dataset to be stored at once.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we extended our previous work on ML closures for the RTE in 1D1V to the 2D2V setting. We introduced a block-diagonal symmetrizer and derived explicit algebraic conditions for the symmetrizer to ensure the hyperbolicity of the closed moment system. We then designed a new ML closure model that is guaranteed to satisfy these algebraic conditions by construction. We performed a series of numerical experiments to test the performance of the ML closure. The results show that the ML closure consistently improves over the linear
P_{N}
model for a family of multi-sine initial conditions with fixed material parameters, and also generalizes to new trajectories within the same family. Future work includes testing the ML closure on a broader family of benchmark tests such as heterogeneous checkerboard media, and exploring more structural properties of the ML closure system such as rotational invariance and realizability-preserving properties. The ultimate goal is to design a closure model that can robustly improve over the linear
P_{N}
model across a wide range of initial conditions and material regimes.

Appendix A
Properties of Real Spherical Harmonics

Theorem A.1

(Real spherical harmonics form an orthonormal basis)
.

Let

N_{l,m}:=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}},\qquad l\geq 0,\quad 0\leq m\leq l,

and define

\Phi_{l}^{0}(\mu,\varphi):=N_{l,0}P_{l}(\mu),

and, for
1\leq m\leq l
,

\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}}(\mu,\varphi):=\sqrt{2}\,N_{l,m}P_{l}^{m}(\mu)\cos(m\varphi),\qquad\Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}(\mu,\varphi):=\sqrt{2}\,N_{l,m}P_{l}^{m}(\mu)\sin(m\varphi).

Then the family

\bigl\{\Phi_{l}^{0},\ \Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{c}},\ \Phi_{l}^{m,\mathrm{s}}:\ l\geq 0,\ 1\leq m\leq l\bigr\}

is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

Proof.

We first derive the integration formula for the surface integral on the sphere with the variables
(\mu,\varphi)
. With the spherical coordinates
(\theta,\varphi)
with
\Omega=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)
, the surface integral of a function
f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
can be written as

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f(\Omega)\,d\Omega=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f(\theta,\varphi)\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi.

(A.1)

Now with the change of variable
\mu=\cos\theta
, we have
d\mu=-\sin\theta\,d\theta
, so that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f(\Omega)\,d\Omega=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f(\theta,\varphi)\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}f(\mu,\varphi)\,d\mu\,d\varphi.

We now check the orthonormality in the following cases:

1.

The orthogonality of (
\Phi_{l}^{0}
,
\Phi_{l^{\prime}}^{m,\mathrm{c}}
), (
\Phi_{l}^{0}
,
\Phi_{l^{\prime}}^{m,\mathrm{s}}
), and (
\Phi_{l^{\prime}}^{m,\mathrm{c}}
,
\Phi_{l^{\prime}}^{m,\mathrm{s}}
) can be easily checked by the orthogonality of the trigonometric functions of
\varphi
.

2.

The orthonormality of
\Phi_{l}^{0}
and
