RECLAIM: Cyclic Causal Discovery Amid Measurement Noise
Muralikrishnna G. Sethuraman Faramarz Fekri

Abstract
Uncovering causal relationships is a fundamental problem across science and engineering. However, most existing causal discovery methods assume acyclicity and direct access to the system variables—assumptions that fail to hold in many real-world settings. For instance, in genomics, cyclic regulatory networks are common, and measurements are often corrupted by instrumental noise. To address these challenges, we propose RECLAIM, a causal discovery framework that natively handles both cycles and measurement noise. RECLAIM learns the causal graph structure by maximizing the likelihood of the observed measurements via expectation-maximization (EM), using residual normalizing flows for tractable likelihood computation. We consider two measurement models: (i) Gaussian additive noise, and (ii) a linear measurement system with additive Gaussian noise. We provide theoretical consistency guarantees for both the settings. Experiments on synthetic data and real-world protein signaling datasets demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed method.



1
Introduction

Understanding cause-effect relationships among variables is a central problem in science and
engineering
(
30
;
14
)
. Causal models provide a mechanistic
view of a system, enabling prediction of its behavior under unseen interventions. Causal
relations are typically encoded as a directed graph (DG), where edges represent causal
dependencies, and learning them is naturally posed as a graph structure learning problem.

Causal discovery methods broadly fall into three categories: (i)
constraint-based
,
(ii)
score-based
, and (iii)
hybrid
. Constraint-based methods

(
37
;
40
)
identify causal graphs consistent
with the conditional independence constraints observed in data, but scale poorly as the
number of required independence tests grows exponentially. Score-based methods

(
17
)
instead optimize a scoring function
such as the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) over the space of graphs; greedy search
is typically employed since the search space grows super-exponentially with the number of
nodes. Hybrid methods
(
41
;
36
)
combine elements of
both approaches.

With few exceptions, most causal discovery methods rely on two key assumptions: (i) the
underlying causal graph is acyclic (a directed acyclic graph, or DAG), and (ii) the system
variables are directly observed. While these assumptions simplify the learning problem, they
are often violated in practice. Variables of interest are frequently
latent
—for
instance, a person’s beliefs cannot be measured directly, but surveys provide noisy proxies
of the underlying state. Furthermore, feedback loops are prevalent in biological
systems
(
13
)
, and such systems often exhibit structured measurement
noise such as “dropout.” Imposing these assumptions in such settings can lead to
misleading causal conclusions, limiting the practical applicability of existing methods.

To address these challenges, we propose
Reclaim
, a novel causal discovery framework
that learns latent causal structure while jointly modeling directed cycles and measurement
noise. We consider two measurement noise models: (i) Gaussian additive noise, and (ii) a
linear measurement system with Gaussian noise. Using interventional data, we first estimate
the noise distribution parameters, then employ an expectation-maximization (EM) procedure
to learn the causal graph via likelihood maximization.

Causal discovery from indirect measurements.

Under indirect measurements, causal discovery broadly splits into two paradigms.

Causal representation learning
(CRL) aims to jointly recover latent variables and their causal structure from high-dimensional observations under unknown transformations. Identifiability has been studied under parametric assumptions such as linear models
(
38

8

and polynomial transformations
(
1

, as well as in fully nonparametric settings

Causal discovery under measurement noise

16

31

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we address two central limitations in causal discovery: (i) inability to handle measurement error, and (ii) restriction to acyclic graphs. Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce
Reclaim
, a differentiable causal discovery framework that natively handles nonlinear cyclic relationships in the presence of additive Gaussian measurement error.

•

We consider two measurement noise models: (i) Gaussian additive noise and (ii) linear measurement systems, and provide a consistent estimator for the noise variance when appropriate interventions are available.

•

We establish that exact maximization of the proposed score function under appropriate interventions identifies the interventional Markov equivalence class of the ground-truth graph.

•

We conduct extensive experiments comparing
Reclaim
against the state-of-the-art causal discovery methods on both synthetic and real-world datasets, and show that increasing the number of measurements results in improved recovery performance.

2.1
Structural Causal Model

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E})
denote a directed graph with vertex set
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\}
and edge set
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
. Let
\mathbold{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
be the associated random (endogenous) variable. The edge
(X_{i},X_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
denotes a directed edge originated from the node
X_{i}
to the node
X_{j}
, with slight abuse of notation we also denote this edge as
X_{i}\to X_{j}
. Following
5
;
26

i\in\mathcal{X}
, we have the following
structural equation
:

X_{i}=f_{i}(\text{pa}_{\mathcal{G}}(X_{i}))+Z_{i},

(1)

where
f_{i}
, called the
causal mechanism
, encodes the functional dependency between the parents and the children.
\text{pa}_{\mathcal{G}}(X_{i})\triangleq\{X_{j}\in\mathcal{X}\mid X_{j}\to X_{i}\in\mathcal{E}\}
denotes the
parent set
of a node
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
.
The set of variables
\mathbold{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
represent the
exogenous noise
within the system. We assume that the exogenous noise variables are independent of each other, i.e.,
Z_{i}\perp Z_{j}
for
i,j\in[d]
and
i\neq j
. This assumption, also known as
causal sufficiency
, excludes hidden confounding and selection bias. Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})
be the combined causal mechanism, combining
eq.

1
for all
i\in[d]
, we obtain the following:

\mathbold{X}=f(\mathbold{X})+\mathbold{Z}.

(2)

However, it is not guaranteed that
eq.

2
has a solution, primarily due to the (potential) presence of cycles in
\mathcal{G}
. As a result, restrictions are necessary on the causal mechanism to ensure the system attains equilibrium (see
appendix

A
for a detailed discussion). To that end, building on
33
, we make the following assumption on the map
(\text{id}-f):\mathbold{X}\mapsto\mathbold{Z}
, which we call the
forward map
.

Assumption 1
.

The forward map
(\text{id}-f):\mathbold{X}\mapsto\mathbold{Z}
is a
diffeomorphism
1
1

1

A function

is a diffeomorphism if
f^{-1}
exists, and both
f
and
f^{-1}
are differentiable.
.

Finally, the SCM provides us with a probability density for the exogenous noise variables, which we denote as
p_{Z}
. Given
p_{Z}
, we can obtain the probability density of the endogenous variables using the change of variable property of probability densities. That is,

p_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbold{X})=p_{Z}\big((\text{id}-f)(\mathbold{X})\big)\big|J_{(\text{id}-f)}(\mathbold{Z})\big|,

(3)

where
J_{(\text{id}-f)}(\mathbold{X})
is the Jacobian matrix of the forward map evaluated at
\mathbold{X}
.

Interventions.

We consider
surgical interventions

(
26
)
within our framework. Under surgical interventions, when a subset of nodes
\mathcal{X}_{I}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
are (surgically) intervened, we obtain a mutilated graph, denoted as
\text{do}(I)(\mathcal{G})
, where all the incoming edges to the intervened nodes are removed (see
fig.

1
). We identify an intervention by the index set
I\subseteq[d]
of the nodes intervened in
\mathcal{X}
. Given a set of intervened nodes
\mathcal{X}_{I}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, let
\mathbf{U}\in\{0,1\}^{d\times d}
be a diagonal masking matrix with
U_{kk}=1

0

eq.

2

\mathbold{X}=\mathbf{U}f(\mathbold{X})+\mathbf{U}\mathbold{Z}+\mathbold{C},

(4)

where
\mathbold{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the values of the intervened nodes. Consequently, under the intervention
I
, the probability density of the observations is given by

p_{\text{do}(I)(\mathcal{G})}(\mathbold{X})=p_{C}(\mathbold{X}_{I})p_{Z}\big(\mathbold{Z}_{[d]\setminus I}\big)\big|J_{(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)}(\mathbold{Z})\big|,

(5)

where
p_{C}
denotes the interventional density, and
\mathbold{Z}_{[d]\setminus I}=\big[(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)(\mathbold{X})\big]_{[d]\setminus I}
. In our experiments, we assume that the intervened nodes are sampled from a distribution with known variance
\sigma_{\mathcal{I}}^{2}
.

2.2
Measurement System

In practice, the causal variables
\mathcal{X}
are latent and only indirectly observed. They often pass through a measurement system, yielding
measured
variables
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{p}\}
(with
\mathbold{Y}
being the corresponding random variable). Each
Y_{j}
represents a coarsened measurement of
\mathbold{X}
given by,

Y_{j}=g_{j}(\mathbold{X},\varepsilon_{j}),\quad j=1,\ldots,p,

(6)

where
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p})
denotes the
coarsening mechanism
(also referred as the
measurement channel
), and
\mathbold{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d})
represents any external factor that can affect the measurement system. We assume that
p\geq d
(otherwise, identifiability is ill-posed), and
\varepsilon_{i}\perp\varepsilon_{j}
, for
i,j\in[p]
and
i\neq j
. This results in a combined measurement graph, denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{m}
, whose vertex set contains both the latent variables
\mathcal{X}
and the measured variables
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
, see
fig.

1
.

In our work, we deal with two different types of measurement system:

1.

Gaussian additive noise
: In this case,
p=d
,
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
, and

Y_{i}=X_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

(7)

Given the latent variables
\mathbold{X}
, the density of the measurements is also Gaussian, i.e.,
\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbold{x},\mathbf{D}_{\varepsilon})
, where
\mathbf{D}_{\varepsilon}=\text{Diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{d}^{2})
. We refer to
p_{Y|X}(\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{X})
as the
measurement channel density
.

2.

Linear measurement system with Gaussian noise
: Similar to the previous setting, we have
\varepsilon_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbold{0},\sigma_{j}^{2})
. However, in this case,
p
can be larger than
d
, i.e.,
p\geq d
, and

Y_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{ji}X_{i}+\varepsilon_{j}.

(8)

The matrix
\mathbf{A}=[A_{ji}]
, with
i\in[d]
and
j\in[p]
, is referred to as the
measurement matrix
, and is assumed to be known and full (column) rank. Given the latent variables
\mathbold{X}
, the conditional density of
\mathbold{Y}
is again Gaussian, i.e.,
\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\mathbold{x},\mathbf{D}_{\varepsilon})
, where
\mathbf{D}_{\varepsilon}=\text{Diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{p}^{2})
.

3
RECLAIM Causal Discovery Framework

Let
\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi}
represent the parameters of the latent variable density
p_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbold{X})
and the conditional measurement density
p_{Y|X}(\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{X})
, respectively. For an interventional experiment
I\subseteq[d]
, we aim to learn the causal graph structure
\mathcal{G}
by maximizing the likelihood of the observed variables. However, this maximization is generally intractable, since

p(\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi},I)=\int p_{\text{do}(I)(\mathcal{G})}(\mathbold{X}\mid\mathbold{\theta})\,p_{Y|X}(\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{X},\mathbold{\phi})\,d\mathbold{X}

does not admit a closed-form solution in general.

To address this intractability, RECLAIM adopts an EM-based approach that maximizes a tractable lower bound on
\log p(\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi})
.
We now describe the framework in detail, beginning with defining the score function to be maximized, followed by an overview of the EM-based optimization procedure. We then detail the technical components of the expectation step: estimating the measurement process parameters, computing the latent likelihood, and sampling latents conditioned on the observations. Finally, we establish the theoretical guarantees of RECLAIM, showing that exact maximization of the score function recovers the true causal structure up to Markov equivalence.

3.1
Score Function

Given a family of interventions
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and a causal graph
\mathcal{G}
, we define the
score function
as the regularized log-likelihood of the observed variables under each interventional regime. Following
32
, this takes the form:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})\triangleq\sup_{\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{Y}\sim p^{(k)}}\log p(\mathbold{Y}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi},I_{k})-\lambda|\mathcal{G}|,

(9)

where
p^{(k)}
denotes the data-generating density under the
k
-th interventional experiment
I_{k}
, and
\lambda|\mathcal{G}|
is an
\ell_{0}
-type sparsity penalty on the edge set of
\mathcal{G}
.

In practice, we assume access to a collection of finite samples per interventional experiments,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}=\{\{\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. Thus, the expectation in
eq.

9
is replaced with sample mean. Finally, we maximize the score function over the space of graphs
\mathcal{G}
resulting in the following score function:

\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}})\triangleq\sup_{\mathcal{G},\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi},I_{k})-\lambda|\mathcal{G}|.

(10)

3.2
Optimizing The Score Via Penalized Expectation-Maximization

As discussed earlier, the intractability of
eq.

10
stems from integrating out the latent variables, precluding a closed-form solution. To circumvent this, we adopt the penalized EM framework of
9
. Beginning from an initial guess
\mathbold{\theta}^{0}
for the latent SCM parameters (encompassing both the neural network weights and the graph adjacency), the algorithm alternates between the two steps below until a convergence criterion is met:

E-step:

Given the current parameter estimate
\mathbold{\theta}^{t}
and the observations, form the surrogate objective
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbold{\theta}\mid\mathbold{\theta}^{t})
as the expected complete-data log-likelihood:

\mathcal{Q}(\mathbold{\theta}\mid\mathbold{\theta}^{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}\sim p(\cdot\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbold{\theta}^{t},\hat{\mathbold}{\phi},I_{k})}\Big[\log p\big(\mathbold{X},\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\hat{\mathbold}{\phi},I_{k}\big)\Big].

(11)

where
\hat{\mathbold}{\phi}
denotes the estimated parameters of the measurement channel (see
section

3.3
for details), and

\log p\big(\mathbold{X},\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\hat{\mathbold}{\phi},I_{k}\big)=\log p_{\text{do}(I_{k})(\mathcal{G})}(\mathbold{X}\mid\mathbold{\theta})+\log p\big(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{X},\hat{\mathbold}{\phi}\big).

(12)

M-step:

Update the parameters by solving the penalized maximization of the surrogate:

\mathbold{\theta}^{t+1}=\arg\max_{\mathbold{\theta}}\;\mathcal{Q}(\mathbold{\theta}\mid\mathbold{\theta}^{t})-\lambda\,\mathcal{R}(\mathbold{\theta}),

(13)

where
\mathcal{R}(\mathbold{\theta})
is a sparsity-promoting regularizer on the graph.

The maximization in the M-step is carried out with stochastic gradient ascent. A key observation is that the surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
serves as a lower bound (up to a constant) on the marginal log-likelihood:

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{k}}\log p\!\left(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{\theta},\hat{\mathbold}{\phi},I_{k}\right)\;\geq\;\mathcal{Q}(\mathbold{\theta}\mid\mathbold{\theta}^{t})-\text{const}.

(14)

Consequently, each M-step improves a valid lower bound on the log-likelihood of the observed-data rather than optimizing it directly. A formal derivation of
eq.

14
and a convergence analysis are deferred to
appendix

B
.

3.3
Estimation of Measurement System Parameters

Computing the surrogate objective
\mathcal{Q}
in
eq.

11
requires evaluating the
measurement channel density, whose parameters are a priori unknown and must therefore be
estimated from data. In the two measurment processes under consideration (Gaussian additive noise and linear measurement system), the unknown parameters correspond to the variances of the additive noise,
which fully identify the measurement channel.
Since the latent
variables
\mathbold{X}
are not directly observed, estimating these parameters requires additional
structure on the data-generating mechanism. We formalize this as the following condition on
the intervention family
\mathcal{I}
.

Condition 2

(Measurement Channel Identifiability)
.

An intervention family
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is
measurement channel identifiable

if, for each node
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists an intervention
I_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
such that

X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{I_{k}}
.

Intuitively,
2
requires that every latent
variable is targeted by at least one intervention, ensuring that its variance is known under
that interventional regime and can therefore be used to identify the measurement noise. We
now show that this condition is sufficient to identify the measurement parameters for each
of the three noise models under consideration.

3.3.1
Gaussian Additive Noise.

Since
\mathbold{X}
and
\mathbold{\varepsilon}
are independent, the marginal variance of
Y_{i}
decomposes as

\sigma_{Y_{i}}^{2}=\sigma_{X_{i}}^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2}
, where
\sigma_{Y_{i}}^{2}
can be estimated
directly from observations. While
\sigma_{X_{i}}^{2}
is generally unknown under observational
data,
2
guarantees the existence of an
intervention
I_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
under which
X_{i}
is intervened upon, fixing its variance to the
known interventional variance
\sigma_{\mathcal{I}}^{2}
. The measurement noise variance is then
recovered as
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sigma_{Y_{i}}^{2}-\sigma_{\mathcal{I}}^{2}
.

3.4
Computing Likelihood of Latent Variables

From
eqs.

11
and
12
, computing the surrogate loss function
\mathcal{Q}
requires evaluating log likelihood of the latent variables,
\log p_{\text{do}(I_{k})(\mathcal{G})}(\mathbold{X}\mid\mathbold{\theta})
. We describe how this is computed by detailing the SCM parameterization and the associated log-likelihood.

Modeling the Causal Mechanism.

We model each causal mechanism
f_{i}
in
eq.

1
using a contractive neural network, that is, a neural network with Lipschitz constant less than one, which is enforced during training by rescaling layer weights by their spectral norm
(
3
)
. Contractivity guarantees, via the Banach fixed point theorem
(
28
)
, that the map
(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)
is invertible (ensuring that
1
is satisfied) and the log-determinant of its Jacobian is well-defined.

To prevent spurious self-loops and encourage sparsity, we introduce a dependency mask

\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{d\times d}
with zero diagonal, whose entries are treated as Bernoulli
random variables sampled via the Gumbel-softmax distribution
(
21
)
,
\mathbf{M}\sim p_{M}(\mathbf{M}\mid\mathbold{\theta})
.
The causal mechanism then takes the form:

[\mathrm{F}_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{X})]_{i}=[\mathrm{NN}_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbf{M}_{\ast,i}\odot\mathbold{X})]_{i},

(17)

where
\mathbf{M}_{\ast,i}
denotes the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{M}
. and the sparsity penalty is given by

\mathcal{R}(\mathbold{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{M}\sim p_{M}(\cdot\mid\mathbold{\theta})}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{1}
.

Log-Determinant of the Jacobian.

Given the contractive neural network parameterization in
eq.

17
, the log-likelihood in
eq.

11
requires evaluating the log-determinant of the Jacobian of
(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)
. Naïve computation of the log-determinant of the Jacobian function has a computation cost that is
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
. However, since the causal mechanism
f
is contractive, the log-determinant admits the convergent power series expansion
(
3
)
:

\log|\det J_{(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)}(\mathbold{X})\big|=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{J^{m}_{\mathbf{U}f}(\mathbold{X})\Big\}.

(18)

The terms in the power series only depend on the trace of the powers of the Jacobian matrix, and hence reduces the computation cost to
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
. However, Truncating this series introduces bias, which we correct using the Russian roulette estimator
(
10
)
, and reduce the per-iteration cost using the Hutchinson trace estimator
(
19
)
. This results in the final unbiased estimator given by
eq.

29
in
section

D.2
. We defer the details to
section

D.2
.

3.5
Sampling Latents Given the Observations

With the measurement parameters
\hat{\mathbold}{\phi}
estimated and the latent likelihood in hand, the remaining challenge in evaluating
\mathcal{Q}
is computing the posterior expectation over
\mathbold{X}
. Since the nonlinear causal mechanisms preclude a closed-form posterior, we employ
Sampling Importance Resampling
(SIR)
(
35
)
to draw approximate posterior samples.

We draw
S
proposal samples
\{\mathbold{x}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
from a Gaussian proposal

q(\mathbold{X}\mid\mathbold{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbold{\mu}_{y},\mathbf{D}_{\varepsilon})
, where the proposal mean
\mathbold{\mu}_{y}

depends on the measurement model:

\mathbold{\mu}_{y}=\begin{cases}\mathbold{y}&\text{additive Gaussian and dropout,}\\
(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbold{y}&\text{linear measurement system.}\end{cases}

(19)

Each sample is then assigned an importance weight proportional to:

w^{(s)}\propto\frac{p(\mathbold{x}^{(s)}\mid\mathbold{\theta})\,p(\mathbold{y}\mid\mathbold{x}^{(s)},\mathbold{\theta})}{q(\mathbold{x}^{(s)}\mid\mathbold{y})},

(20)

and a final set of samples is obtained by resampling from
\{\mathbold{X}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}

according to the normalized weights
\{\tilde{w}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we introduced RECLAIM, a differentiable causal discovery framework that can jointly handle cyclic causal graphs, interventional data, and measurement noise in a unified probabilistic framework. Unlike existing methods that assume acyclicity or direct observation of system variables, RECLAIM operates on coarsened measurements across two noise settings: additive Gaussian, and linear measurement systems. We establish consistency of the graph estimator in the large-sample regime and demonstrate through experiments that RECLAIM outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, with particular gains in cyclic and high-noise regimes.

A key limitation is the reliance on interventional data for measurement noise parameter
estimation—insufficient interventional coverage can degrade both noise estimation and
graph recovery. Future directions include relaxing this requirement, extending support to
soft and unknown interventional targets, non-Gaussian exogenous noise, and jointly
learning the measurement system from data.

Solvability of Cyclic SCMs

Consider the cyclic SCM defined below:

(21)

In general, it is not guaranteed that

. In order to see why this is true, note that, we can view
eq.

21
as the unique fixed point (if it exists) to the following discrete time dynamical system:

\mathbold{X}(t+1)=f\big(\mathbold{X}(t)\big)+\mathbold{Z},

(22)

where
\mathbold{X}(t)
denotes the value of the random variable
\mathbold{X}
at time
t
. In the following example, we illustrate how the choice of

dictates whether or not
eq.

22
attains a unique fixed point.

Example A.1
.

Consider

Appendix B
Convergence of EM algorithm

Here, we provide the convergence analysis of RECLAIM’s EM-based score maximization algorithm. Let
\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)}=(\mathbold{\theta}^{(t)},\hat{\mathbold{\phi}})
. For ease of notation, we focus on a single interventional experiment
I_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
. However, the final result would still hold even when we consider the sum of likelihood over all the interventional experiments. Our analysis relies on the convergence of the EM algorithm
(
43
;
15
)
, with the crux of it relying on establishing that the total log-likelihood of the observed variables either increases or stays the same in each iteration of the algorithm. That is,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta}^{t+1},I_{k})\geq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta}^{t},I_{k})

(23)

To that end, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k})

Taking expectation with respect
\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}
on both side, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)};\mathbf{\Theta}^{t}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)};\mathbf{\Theta}^{t}}\log p(\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k})}_{=Q(\mathbold{\theta}|\mathbold{\theta}^{t})}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)};\mathbf{\Theta}^{t}}\log p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta},I_{k}).

(24)

The first term on the RHS in the above equation is nothing but
Q(\mathbold{\theta}|\mathbold{\theta}^{t})
. This is maximized in the M-step, i.e.,
Q(\mathbold{\theta}|\mathbold{\theta}^{t+1})\geq Q(\mathbold{\theta}|\mathbold{\theta}^{t})
. On the other hand,

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)};\mathbf{\Theta}^{t}}\log\frac{p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{t+1},I_{k})}{p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{t},I_{k})}=\\
-D_{KL}\Big(p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{t},I_{k})\big\|p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{t+1},I_{k})\Big)\leq 0.

Thus,

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)};\mathbf{\Theta}^{t}}\log p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{t+1},I_{k})\\
\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)};\mathbf{\Theta}^{t}}\log p(\mathbold{X}^{(k,\ell)}\mid\mathbold{y}^{(k,\ell)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{t},I_{k}).

(25)

From combining
eqs.

24
and
25
, we can see that at the end of the M-step
eq.

23
is satisfied. Thus, RECLAIM reaches a stationary point of the optimization objective.

|J_{i}|=d-1

\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A})\triangleq(\mathbf{A}_{-i})_{J_{i},\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-1)\times(d-1)}

is invertible.

Let
\mathbold{t}
be such that
\mathbf{A}_{-i}^{\top}\mathbold{t}=0
for some
i\in[d]
. Then,

\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\mathbold{t}_{J_{i}}+\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\mathbold{t}_{[p]\setminus J_{i}}=0,

where
\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{A})
denotes the submatrix of
\mathbf{A}
formed by rows not included in
J_{i}
. Since
\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A})
is invertible, we have

\mathbold{t}_{J_{i}}=-\Big(\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\Big)^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\mathbold{t}_{[p]\setminus J_{i}}.

Thus, every admissible
\mathbold{t}
has to have the following form:

\mathbold{t}(\mathbf{A},\mathbold{u})=\begin{bmatrix}-\Big(\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\Big)^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{\top}\mathbold{u}\\
\mathbold{u}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbold{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-d}.

Moreover,

\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{-1}=\frac{\text{adj}(\mathbf{M}_{i}(\mathbf{A}))}{\det(\mM_i(\mA))}

Thus, the each coordinate of
\mathbold{t}(\mathbf{A},\mathbold{u})
are: linear in
\mathbold{u}
, divided
\det(\mM_i(\mA))
, and multiplied by polynomial functions of entries of
\mathbf{A}
. Upon taking Hadamard product with itself, each entry of
\mathbold{t}^{\odot}
is a polynomial function of
\mathbold{u}
and the entries of
\mathbf{A}
divided by the determinant of the minor matrices.

Suppose we choose
p
such vectors
\mathbold{t}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbold{t}^{(p)}
(each possibly corresponding to different column of
\mathbf{A}
). The determinant of the resulting
\mathbf{T}_{2}
,
\det(\mT_2)
is also polynomial in
\mathbold{u}^{(\ell)}
, for
\ell\in[p]
; and polynomial in entries of
\mathbf{A}
along with a division of the determinant of the minor matrices. That is,

\det(\mT_2)=\frac{\text{Polynomial in }\mathbf{A},\mathbold{u}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbold{u}^{(p)}}{\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}\det(\mM_\ell(\mA))^{k_{\ell}}}

Let us define

D(\mathbf{A})\triangleq\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}\det(\mM_\ell(\mA))^{k_{\ell}}

and

P(\mathbf{A},\mathbold{u}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbold{u}^{(p)})\triangleq D(\mathbf{A})\cdot\det(\mT_2).

Note that, since the minor matrices are invertible

P(\mathbf{A},\mathbold{u}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbold{u}^{(p)})=0\iff\det(\mT_2)=0.

In
Part 1
, we showed the existence of an
\mathbf{A}_{0}
and
\mathbold{u}_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbold{u}_{0}^{(p)}
for which the function
P(\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbold{u}_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbold{u}_{0}^{(p)})\neq 0
. Thus the polynomial
P
is non-trivial. Hence, from
lemma

C.1
, the zero set of
P
has zero Lebesgue measure. Thus,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
—which is a subset of the zero set of
P
—has zero Lebesgue measure.
∎

, and

. Then, under
C.8
,
C.9
,
C.10
,

and
C.13

, we have that
\hat{\mathcal{G}}\equiv_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{G}^{\ast}
. That is,
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
is
\mathcal{I}
-Markov equivalent to
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}
.

Proof.

The proof is a direct extension of Theorem 2 in
32
, with
lemma

C.16
substituting
lemma

C.15
. Which we present here for self-containment.

It is sufficient to show that for
\mathcal{G}\notin\mathcal{I}\text{-MEC}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})
, the score function of
\mathcal{G}
is strictly lower than the score function of
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})>\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G})
. Since
\mathcal{G}\notin\mathcal{I}\text{-MEC}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})
and
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})
(by
C.8
), there must exist subsets of nodes
A,B,D\subseteq\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
such that either:

A\mathop{\perp}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma}B\mid D\quad\text{and}\quad A\mathop{\not\perp}_{\mathcal{G}^{\ast}}^{\sigma}B\mid D,

(C1)

or

A\mathop{\not\perp}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma}B\mid D\quad\text{and}\quad A\mathop{\perp}_{\mathcal{G}^{\ast}}^{\sigma}B\mid D,

(C2)

If no such subsets exist, then
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}
impose the same
\sigma
-separation constraints and thus induce the same set of distributions. This would imply that
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{I}\text{-MEC}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})
, contradicting our assumption. Since
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})
, in the case of (
C1
), it must be true that
\bm{A}\not\perp_{p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}}\bm{B}\mid\bm{D}
(
C.9
). Therefore
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}
doesn’t satisfy the general directed Markov property with respect to
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{I}}
and hence
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})
. For (
C2
), if
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})
, then from
C.9
, it must be true that
\bm{A}\not\perp_{p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}}\bm{B}\mid\bm{C}
. However,
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})
, and
C.5
implies that
\bm{A}\perp_{p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}}\bm{B}\mid\bm{C}
, resulting in a contradiction. Therefore,
p_{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})
. Morevover, from
C.7
we can conclude that
p_{Y,\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\notin\mathcal{M}_{Y,\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})
.

For convenience, let

\eta(\mathcal{G}):=\inf_{\mathbold{\theta}}D_{KL}(p_{Y,\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\|p_{Y,\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi}^{\ast})).

Note that

\eta(\mathcal{G})=\inf_{\mathbold{\theta}}D_{KL}(p_{Y,\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}\|p_{Y,\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid\mathbold{\theta},\mathbold{\phi}^{\ast}))\geq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{M}_{Y,\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})}D_{KL}(p_{Y}^{(k)}\|p_{Y})>0,

where we use
lemma

C.16
for the final inequality. Thus, from Lemma
C.14

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})-\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G})=\eta(\mathcal{G})+\lambda(|\mathcal{G}|-|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|).

Following
7
, we now show that by choosing
\lambda
sufficiently small, the above equation is stictly positive. Note that if
|\mathcal{G}|\geq|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|
then
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})-\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G})>0
. Let
\mathbb{G}^{+}:=\{\mathcal{G}\mid|\mathcal{G}|<|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|\}
. Choosing
\lambda
such that
0<\lambda<\min_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}^{+}}\frac{\eta(\mathcal{G})}{|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|-|\mathcal{G}|}
we see that:

\displaystyle\lambda<\min_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}^{+}}\frac{\eta(\mathcal{G})}{|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|-|\mathcal{G}|}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\lambda<\frac{\eta(\mathcal{G})}{|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|-|\mathcal{G}|}\quad\forall\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}^{+}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\lambda(|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|-|\mathcal{G}|)<\eta(\mathcal{G})\quad\forall\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}^{+}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 0<\eta(\mathcal{G})+\lambda(|\mathcal{G}|-|\mathcal{G}^{\ast}|)=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})-\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G})\quad\forall\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}^{+}.

Thus, every graph outside of the general directed Markov equivalence class of
(\mathcal{G}^{\ast})^{\mathcal{I}}
has a strictly lower score.

\mathbf{V}

\mathbold{t}=\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbold{u}

\mathbold{t}

\mathbf{T}_{2}

\mathbf{T}_{2}

\mathbf{T}_{2}

D.2
Log-determinant of the Jacobian Computation

Computing the log-determinant of the Jacobian computation poses a significant challenge when evaluation the log-density of the latent variables. To overcome this issue, we exploit the power-series expansion of
\log(1 - x)
to obtain the following:

\log|\det J_{(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)}(\mathbold{X})\big|=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{J^{m}_{\mathbf{U}f}(\mathbold{X})\Big\}.

(28)

This brings the complexity of down to
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
. Further improvement can be made by using
Hutchinson trace estimator

(
19
)

\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{J^{m}_{\mathbf{U}f}(\mathbold{X})\Big\}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W}}\Big[\mathbf{W}^{\top}J^{m}_{\mathbf{U}f}(\mathbold{X})\mathbf{W}\Big],

where
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{W}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}]=\mathbf{I}
. The above estimator only depends on a vector-Jacobian product which can be efficiently computed using an auto-differentiation library (such as PyTorch), often reducing the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(d)
. In practice, the power series is truncated to a finite number of terms. This, however, introduces bias in the log-determinant of the Jacobian estimate. To improve on this, following
10
, the series cut-off
n
is randomly sampled,
n\sim p_{N}
, where
p_{N}
is a distribution over natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
. The individual terms in
eq.

28
are then reweighted by the inverse probability of ending there. Finally, we have the following unbiased estimator:

\log|\det J_{(\text{id}-\mathbf{U}f)}(\mathbold{X})\big|=-\mathbb{E}_{n,\mathbf{W}}\Bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{W}^{\top}J^{m}_{\mathbf{U}f}(\mathbold{X})\mathbf{W}}{m\cdot P(N\geq n)}\Bigg].

(29)

D.3
RECLAIM and Baselines Code Details

RECLAIM.

Our framework was built using the
Pytorch
library in Python and the code is provided as a part of the supplementary materials.

We follow the setup of
33
, employing neural networks (NNs) with dependency masks parameterized by a Gumbel-softmax distribution. The log-determinant of the Jacobian is computed using a power series expansion combined with the Hutchinson trace estimator. To mitigate bias from truncating the power series expansion, the number of terms is sampled from a Poisson distribution, as detailed in
sections

3

. The final objective is optimized using the Adam optimizer
(
22
)
.

The learning rate in all our experiments was set to

10^{-3}
for all the experiments. The models were trained and evaluated on NVIDIA RTX6000 GPUs.
