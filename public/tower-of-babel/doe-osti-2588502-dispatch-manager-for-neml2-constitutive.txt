Abstract
This report describes the extended capabilities of the NEML2 constitutive modeling library, in-
cluding a flexible and efficient work dispatching system designed to leverage both CPU and GPU
resources. This enhancement addresses one of the primary computational challenges in large-
scale simulations: the ability to distribute and execute batches of material model evaluations
across heterogeneous computing devices. The new dispatch system introduces a modular set
of dispatcher and scheduler classes that coordinate the flow of data and execution between de-
vices. The dispatcher is responsible for efficiently packaging work, managing device-specific
memory operations, and synchronizing results. This modularity allows for extensibility, making
it straightforward to integrate additional computing backends in the future. From an implementa-
tion standpoint, the dispatcher system interfaces seamlessly with NEML2’s existing models. They
handle device-aware tensor operations, optimize memory transfers, and support asynchronous
execution when applicable. This design ensures that batches of material points can be evaluated
concurrently, substantially improving throughput compared to previous single-device or serial
implementations. These improvements not only enhance the raw performance of NEML2 but also
improve its usability in multiscale and high-fidelity simulations, where the simultaneous evalua-
tion of large material point batches is critical. Benchmarks included in the report demonstrate the
system’s scalability, highlighting its effectiveness when leveraging modern GPU architectures.

1 Introduction
Modern computational mechanics simulations increasingly demand the evaluation of large num-
bers of material points under complex constitutive models. These evaluations are often the com-
putational bottleneck, particularly in multiscale simulations, inverse problems, or uncertainty
quantification studies where thousands or millions of material states must be updated repeatedly
at each timestep. While GPUs offer substantial raw compute power, efficiently utilizing hetero-
geneous resources (both CPUs and GPUs) requires careful coordination.
 Previously, NEML2’s material model evaluations were primarily designed for single-device or
sequential execution, which limited scalability and left available hardware underutilized. With-
out an intelligent system for batching and distributing workloads, users had to manually set up
device-specific runs or accept significant overhead from poor memory layouts, inefficient batch-
ing, and underfilled compute queues.
 The dispatcher system was introduced to solve these challenges by providing a modular, ex-
tensible framework that automates the preparation, scheduling, and routing of work across avail-
able devices. It abstracts away device-specific details while ensuring that large batches of evalu-
ations are executed in parallel, with optimized memory movement, proper synchronization, and
minimal idle time on compute resources. By integrating this system, NEML2 can now handle
complex workflows efficiently and transparently, improving both raw throughput and ease of
use in high-performance computing environments.
 The newly introduced NEML2 work dispatcher system, hereinafter also interchangeably re-
ferred to as the dispatcher system, is structured around three core components that together
enable efficient, scalable material model evaluations on heterogeneous devices.
Data loaders and generators These components are responsible for preparing and providing the
input data needed for batch evaluations. They handle tasks like constructing initial conditions,
assembling material point states, and generating synthetic or preprocessed data batches. The
design abstracts data preparation away from the computational routines, ensuring that the work
schedulers and dispatchers receive data in a uniform, device-agnostic format. Specialized data
loaders can accommodate both simple cases (e.g., uniform initial states) and complex use cases
(e.g., varying boundary or loading conditions across batches).
Work schedulers The schedulers determine how batches of work are divided and assigned to
available compute resources. They act as intermediaries between the data loaders and the device-
specific dispatchers. Schedulers are responsible for gathering information about available devices
(CPUs, GPUs), assessing their current loads, and distributing batch segments accordingly. The
scheduler logic is designed for flexibility, supporting strategies such as round-robin dispatch-
ing, load balancing, or user-specified allocation rules. This ensures that the overall system can
adapt to both homogeneous and heterogeneous computing environments, maximizing resource
utilization.
The work dispatcher The work dispatcher is responsible for managing the assignment and rout-
ing of work batches across different execution devices. Unlike simple device-specific executors,
the work dispatcher itself does not perform the material model computations. Instead, it focuses
on orchestrating how batches of work – pre-prepared and scheduled – are handed off to lower-

level device-specific functors for actual execution. The work dispatcher defines the core interface
and lifecycle for dispatch operations, including methods to: Set up and initialize the dispatcher
with the required computational context and resources; Divide and route work batches accord-
ing to device availability or user configurations; Track and manage results, ensuring that once
computations are completed, the gathered outputs are properly aligned and assembled.
 Together, these components form a modular and extensible system that significantly enhances
the computational efficiency of NEML2, especially in large-scale simulations requiring simultane-
ous material updates across devices. The clear separation of concerns between data preparation,
scheduling, and dispatching also makes the system adaptable to future hardware or algorithmic
advances.
 This report presents the overall design of each core component as well as the corresponding
implementation details and choices.

2 Implementation
2.1 Data loaders and generators
The data loading and generation system in NEML2 plays a foundational role in preparing batches
of material point data for evaluation across heterogeneous computing devices. This system en-
sures that data is not only efficiently structured for downstream scheduling and dispatching but
also flexible enough to accommodate a wide range of use cases, from synthetic data generation
to preloaded input datasets.
WorkGenerator At the core of this system is the abstract WorkGenerator base class, which de-
fines the general interface and lifecycle for generating batches of work. This class provides virtual
methods that derived generators override to implement specific data production strategies. By
decoupling the data generation logic from the scheduler and dispatcher components, NEML2 en-
sures modularity and simplifies the integration of new data sources or generation patterns. The
WorkGenerator handles responsibilities like batch size definition, data format standardization,
and consistency checks, ensuring that all downstream components can assume a uniform batch
interface.
FixedSizeWorkGenerator FixedSizeWorkGenerator is a key derived class specializing in cases
where the total number of work items and the batch size are known and fixed in advance. This
class provides utilities to partition the total dataset into fixed-size batches and ensures that all
generated batches comply with the expected size and shape. By managing internal counters and
offsets, it guarantees that the full dataset is covered systematically, which is particularly useful
for reproducible simulation runs and controlled benchmarking scenarios.
SliceGenerator The SliceGenerator class is a specialized implementation of the FixedSizeWork-
Generator designed to generate data by slicing an existing dataset or tensor. It takes as input
a large tensor and produces smaller, fixed-size slices that serve as independent work batches.
This approach is particularly efficient for applications where a large dataset has been preloaded
into memory or generated by an upstream process, and only segmented portions need to be dis-
patched for computation. The SliceGenerator ensures that slices are consistently aligned with
batch boundaries, preserving data integrity and maximizing memory locality.
TensorLoader TensorLoader is another derived class of FixedSizeWorkGenerator that focuses
on loading work batches directly from provided tensors. Unlike SliceGenerator, which cuts up a
larger tensor, TensorLoader works by assembling batches from distinct tensor blocks or arrays.
This makes it well-suited for cases where the input data comes from multiple sources or pre-
constructed tensors, allowing the loader to collate them into coherent batches ready for schedul-
ing. It includes mechanisms for validating tensor shapes and types, ensuring compatibility with
NEML2’s batching and evaluation system.
ValueMapLoader Finally, the ValueMapLoader extends FixedSizeWorkGenerator to handle cases
where input data is provided as key-value mappings, typically representing structured or labeled
datasets. This class translates user-specified or file-provided mappings into batched tensor in-
puts, ensuring that all necessary variables are present and correctly formatted. It is especially

ANL-25/22                                       3

useful when working with datasets where different material points or simulation conditions are
represented as discrete labeled entries, enabling flexible and targeted data assembly for evalua-
tion.
 Together, these components form a powerful and adaptable data preparation layer for NEML2,
allowing users to flexibly generate, load, and manage work batches for complex material simu-
lations. Their modular design supports the seamless integration of new data formats and work-
flows, ensuring that the NEML2 dispatching system can be easily extended to meet future com-
putational demands.

2.2 Work schedulers
The work scheduler system in NEML2 plays a central role in determining how batches of ma-
terial model evaluations are divided and assigned to available computing resources. Positioned
between the data generators and the device-specific dispatchers, the scheduler orchestrates work-
load distribution, ensuring balanced resource usage, efficient execution, and compatibility with
various computational environments, including parallel and distributed systems.
WorkScheduler At the heart of the scheduling system is the abstract WorkScheduler base class.
This class defines the general interface and control flow for managing how incoming work batches
are assigned to computing devices. It includes virtual methods that derived schedulers override
to implement specific scheduling strategies. The base class is responsible for:

 • Accepting batches of work generated by the data loaders,
 • Deciding how to partition and allocate these batches, and
 • Passing the allocated segments to the appropriate dispatcher objects for execution.

This architecture cleanly separates the policy of “how to distribute work” (handled by the sched-
uler) from the mechanics of “how to execute work” (handled by the dispatcher), making it straight-
forward to introduce new scheduling strategies without modifying the underlying execution en-
gines.
 The work scheduler in NEML2 provides the abstraction layer responsible for deciding how
batches of work are partitioned and routed to different devices. Its design follows a clear sep-
aration of interface and implementation: the public API exposes high-level methods such as
schedule_work, dispatched_work, completed_work, and wait_for_completion, while device-
and policy-specific scheduling strategies are delegated to pure virtual functions (schedule_work_impl,
dispatched_work_impl, completed_work_impl, and all_work_completed). This separation
ensures that new scheduling algorithms can be introduced by subclassing without modifying the
base logic for synchronization, tracing, or user interaction.
 Internally, the scheduler enforces thread safety and coordination through a combination of a
mutex and a condition variable. The schedule_work method is blocking: it attempts to obtain
a valid device–batch pair through the subclass’s schedule_work_impl, and if none is available,
waits until work becomes schedulable. Conversely, the completed_work method updates the
scheduler’s state and notifies all waiting threads, enabling dependent calls to resume as soon as

                                                  4                                     ANL-25/22

new resources are available. The wait_for_completion method leverages the same mechanism
to block until all outstanding work has finished, ensuring proper synchronization at the end of a
dispatch cycle.

 Together, these design choices make the work scheduler both robust and extensible. The com-
bination of abstract scheduling policies, synchronization primitives, and optional tracing support
allows the dispatcher system to scale from simple single-device workflows to complex heteroge-
neous environments, while maintaining transparency and debuggability for end users.

SimpleScheduler The SimpleScheduler is the most basic implementation of the WorkScheduler.
It is designed for single-device systems or environments where all work is dispatched to a single
target (e.g., CPU-only or GPU-only runs). This scheduler partitions the incoming work batches
into equally sized segments and assigns all segments to the same dispatcher. Its simplicity makes
it an excellent choice for development, testing, or production runs where device heterogene-
ity and parallel distribution are not a concern. Despite its simplicity, it still leverages NEML2’s
batching and tensor operations to achieve good computational efficiency.

SimpleMPIScheduler The SimpleMPIScheduler extends the basic scheduling framework to distributed-
memory parallel environments using MPI (Message Passing Interface). This scheduler is designed
to operate in multi-rank simulations where each MPI rank acts as an independent worker han-
dling its local portion of the total workload. The scheduler ensures that work batches are divided
and assigned according to the rank’s local context, enabling efficient parallel scaling across dis-
tributed systems. Integration with MPI allows NEML2 to support large-scale simulations where
the overall computational load must be shared across many nodes, with minimal coordination
overhead between ranks.

StaticHybridScheduler The StaticHybridScheduler is a more advanced scheduler that supports
static partitioning of workloads across heterogeneous devices within a single node or system —
typically CPUs and GPUs working together. This scheduler allows users to predefine how much
of the total workload should be allocated to each type of device. For example, a user might assign
70% of the batches to the GPU and 30% to the CPU, balancing the computational throughput
and memory constraints of each device. Once the partitioning is set, the scheduler consistently
applies this allocation across incoming batches, ensuring reproducible and balanced execution.
This scheduler is particularly useful in hybrid systems where both CPU and GPU resources need
to be used effectively without dynamic runtime adjustments.

 Together, these schedulers form a robust and extensible system for distributing computa-
tional workloads in NEML2. Whether running on a single CPU, leveraging GPUs, scaling across
MPI clusters, or coordinating hybrid hardware setups, the scheduler layer ensures that material
model evaluations are distributed effectively, maximizing performance and resource utilization.
The modular design also allows for the easy addition of future schedulers, such as dynamic load
balancers or adaptive hybrid strategies, to further enhance NEML2’s scalability and versatility.

2.3 Work dispatchers

The work dispatcher in NEML2 is the central component responsible for managing the lifecycle
of work batches generated by the WorkGenerator and scheduled by the WorkScheduler. In ad-
dition to simply launching tasks, the dispatcher encapsulates preprocessing, postprocessing, and
reduction logic, ensuring that the entire workflow—from raw input to final reduced output—can
be orchestrated through a unified interface. The dispatcher is templated on input and output
types, allowing it to flexibly adapt to different workloads, while maintaining thread safety and
optional tracing support.
 The dispatcher can operate in two distinct modes: synchronous and asynchronous. The
header file WorkDispatcher.h defines clear interfaces for both modes, giving instantiations flex-
ibility to optimize device-specific execution paths while maintaining a consistent outward-facing
API.
 The thread pool itself is initialized with care to ensure compatibility with underlying libraries
like PyTorch’s linear algebra routines, and it supports optional per-thread initialization via a user-
provided functor. Event tracing hooks are integrated throughout the dispatch loop — spanning
thread initialization, task execution, and reduction—-providing fine-grained insight into perfor-
mance characteristics during asynchronous execution.
 By supporting both synchronous and asynchronous modes, the dispatcher balances usability
and performance: synchronous mode offers a straightforward execution path for small or se-
quential workloads, while asynchronous mode unlocks parallelism across CPUs and accelerators
with explicit coordination through the scheduler. This dual design ensures that the dispatcher
can scale seamlessly from simple test problems to demanding production simulations.

2.3.1 Interaction with Data Loaders and Work Schedulers

The Work Dispatcher operates downstream of two major systems:

 • Data Loaders and Generators (such as WorkGenerator, FixedSizeWorkGenerator, and its
 derived classes)
 • Work Schedulers (such as SimpleScheduler, SimpleMPIScheduler, and StaticHybridSched-
 uler)

Once the data loaders prepare batches of work — typically in the form of structured tensors or
mapped values — the scheduler determines how these batches are partitioned across available
devices (CPUs, GPUs, or a hybrid mix). The dispatcher then steps in to carry out the actual
execution of these assigned tasks.
 This separation of concerns ensures that the scheduler only needs to decide what goes where,
leaving the dispatcher responsible for how the work is launched, how memory is managed, and
how results are collected. The modular design ensures that improvements or changes in the
dispatching logic (e.g., adding support for new devices) can be implemented without altering the
scheduling or data loading systems.

2.3.2 Operation Modes: Synchronous vs. Asynchronous
The NEML2 Work Dispatcher supports two principal operation modes, giving users and devel-
opers flexibility in managing execution latency, throughput, and resource utilization:
Synchronous Mode In synchronous mode, all dispatching occurs sequentially in the main thread.
Each batch of work is drawn from the generator, optionally preprocessed, executed via the pro-
vided do_work functor, and then postprocessed before being stored in the results buffer. The
scheduler is queried for the next device and batch size, but it is not notified of intermediate
dispatches or completions, since the sequential execution eliminates the need for inter-thread
coordination. Once all batches have been processed, the dispatcher either returns the raw re-
sults or applies the user-provided reduction function. This mode is simple, deterministic, and
avoids the complexity of concurrency, making it suitable for debugging, lightweight workloads,
or environments where additional threads may not be desirable.
Asynchronous Mode In asynchronous mode, the dispatcher creates a dedicated thread pool, with
each thread bound to a specific device. Work batches are placed into per-device task queues,
from which worker threads continuously draw tasks. For each batch, a task is assembled that in-
cludes preprocessing, execution, postprocessing, and result collection. The dispatcher notifies the
scheduler both when tasks are dispatched and when they complete, ensuring proper accounting
and coordination across devices. The main thread continues dispatching tasks until all work has
been scheduled, then waits for completion signals before triggering the reduction step. This de-
sign allows overlap between computation, data movement, and scheduling, thereby maximizing
throughput on heterogeneous architectures such as multi-GPU systems.

2.3.3 Modularity and Extensibility via Functors
A key feature of the Work Dispatcher design is its modularity, achieved through the use of func-
tors — small callable objects that encapsulate the core computational task to be applied to each
batch. Instead of hardcoding the execution logic, the dispatcher accepts various functors that
define what operation should be performed on the data.
 This design has several benefits:

 • Extensibility: New operations (e.g., evaluating a new material model or applying a new
 update rule) can be plugged into the dispatch system simply by providing new functor
 definitions, without modifying the dispatcher internals.
 • Separation of Concerns: The dispatcher focuses solely on managing the flow and execution,
 while the functors define the specific computations.
 • Reusability: The same dispatcher can execute a wide variety of tasks, depending on the
 functors it is provided, making the system adaptable to different use cases, including for-
 ward evaluations, derivative calculations, or diagnostic runs.

 The WorkDispatcher base class exposes templated dispatch methods that accept these func-
tors, making it straightforward to plug in both predefined and user-defined operations.

2.4 Event tracing and visualization
Work scheduling and dispatching can get complicated quickly as more sophisticated scheduling
strategies are used, and asynchronous dispatching further builds upon such complexity. Bench-
marking and profiling can give us a rough idea on whether the scheduler and dispatcher are
coordinating as expected, by examining the wall time against theoretical bounds. However, such
approach is less ideal especially in hybrid computing environments.
 In addition to benchmarking and profiling, NEML2 offers the capability of logging events
related to scheduling and dispatching. The events are logged into a trace file in the Chrome trace
format. The trace file is essentially a JSON array of events.
 Event tracing is tightly integrated into the scheduler through the TracingInterface. When
tracing is enabled, each major scheduling operation emits structured JSON events that capture
details such as the assigned device and batch size. These events are logged in Chrome’s trace
format, making it possible to visualize scheduling and dispatching timelines. Tracing is applied
consistently to all phases —– scheduling, dispatch, completion, and final synchronization — pro-
viding users with fine-grained insight into how workloads are distributed and executed across
heterogeneous devices.
 To enable event tracing, inside the input file, set Schedulers/*/trace_file to the path of
the output trace file. While the work dispatcher and scheduler are coordinating the tasks, key
events are continuously written to the trace file. After all calculations are complete, the trace file
can be visualized using third-party tools such as Chrome trace viewer, Perfetto, and Speedscope.

3 Numerical Experiments
3.1 The optimal dispatching batch size

The spatial (e.g., memory) and temporal (wall-clock time) efficiencies of a NEML2 calculation de-
pends on a variety of factors, including the compute device’s peak FLOP and memory bandwidth,
the coordination among different compute devices, operation complexity, and implementation of
operators on each compute device. Due to the inherent space-time trade-offs, there usually exists
an optimal work dispatching strategy. Similarly, given a model, there exists an optimal dispatch-
ing batch size (DBS) for each type of device. However, it is challenging, if possible, to analytically
determine the optimal DBS, and oftentimes the optimal DBS has to be estimated based on some
numerical experiments.
 The SimpleScheduler discussed in Section 2.2 can be used to design a series of numerical ex-
periments to estimate the optimal DBS. Consider a single-crystal crystal plasticity model, where
the crystals are isotropic in the elastic regime, and follow a power-law slip rule with Voce slip
hardening. The kinematics and rotation are updated in an implicit, fully coupled fashion. Refer
to [1] for a complete description of the constitutive model and its update algorithm.
 A total of 131 072 batches of constitutive updates are performed in each trial, with different
compute device and DBS. In each trial, the constitutive model is first evaluated twice to warm up
the cache and trace the initial profiling function graph. Next, the model is evaluated 10 times with
the same input. The total wall-clock time is recorded for each trial. During the model evaluation,
CPU and CUDA memory usages are queried every 10 ms, and the maximum allocated memory
is recorded for each trial. Both metrics are summarized in Figure 3.1.
 As expected, the peak memory allocation is approximately the same on CPU and CUDA, and
it scales linearly with the DBS. First, as the DBS increases, the total wall-clock time decreases on
both devices, meaning that the average throughput for model evaluation is increasing. The CPU
reaches its optimal DBS somewhere between 16 384 and 32 768, and the CUDA device reaches its
optimal DBS at approximately 16 384. Beyond their respective optimal DBS, the total wall-clock
time increases, translating to a decrease in the average throughput for constitutive update.
 In practice, the DBS should be chosen depending on the memory constraint. When there’s
no constraint on memory consumption or if sufficient memory is available before reaching the
optimal DBS, the optimal DBS should be used as the practical dispatching batch size for the
corresponding compute device. When the calculation is memory-bound, a smaller DBS should
be used to reduce memory consumption, at the cost of a reduced throughput.
 Once the DBS for each compute device is determined, it is recommended to further fine tune
the work scheduling and dispatching by playing with the parametrized StaticHybridScheduler. In
this case, we consider three compute devices: one CPU and two CUDA devices. Since evaluating
the model on CUDA is much faster than evaluating on CPU, it is of our interest to understand
how the hybrid dispatching to the less efficient device affects overall run time. Figure 3.2 sum-
marizes the wall-clock time for each trial with different DBS to CPU, while the DBS to the two
CUDA devices remains unchanged. When the CPU DBS is relatively small, the overall run time
only increases slightly with DBS. However, as the CPU DBS keeps increasing, the overall model
evaluation throughput is significantly degraded.

 As shown in Figure 3.3, the execution time (blue tracks) of each dispatch is roughly the same

them with the specified batch size 16 382. After the first dispatch, the work scheduler determines
that the CUDA devices are fully occupied, and the next 8192 batches of constitutive update are
dispatched to CPU. While the CPU is performing the calculations, the CUDA devices completed
their tasks and reached back to the scheduler for more work. After a few more dispatches to
CUDA, all tasks have been fully scheduled, and so the CUDA devices remain idle, and all threads
are set to wait for the CPU to complete its task.
 Therefore, in practice, it is advised that numerical experiments should be carried out to de-
termine both the per-device DBS and the hybrid dispatching strategy.

3.3 Partial differential equation: Crystal plasticity finite element
The NEML2 work dispatchers can also be used in the context of solving partial differential equa-
tions, to either accelerate the constitutive model evaluation or to overcome memory constraints.
In this example, the same single-crystal crystal plasticity model is used as part of a crystal plas-
ticity finite element simulation in MOOSE [2]. The simulation contains 100 incremental load
steps with approximately 64 000 constitutive updates per step. Wall-clock time corresponding to
various major routines are recorded and plotted in Figure 3.6.

Figure 3.6: Comparison of wall-clock time for major routines of a MOOSE CPFE simulation using

 Wall-time is attributed to four major routines: (i) residual and Jacobian evaluation, and sys-
tem assembly, (ii) constitutive update, (iii) solving the system of equations, and (iv) data transfer
across compute devices. Using NEML, the vast majority of wall-clock time is attributed to the
constitutive update. Using NEML2, such attribution is dramatically reduced, regardless of the
compute device. On CPU, the constitutive update sees approximately 2x acceleration; on GPU,
the constitutive update is further accelerated by more than 400 times. It is worth noting that a
data marshaling step becomes necessary when using NEML2 in MOOSE, which marshals the non-
contiguous material storage into a contiguous data structure which can be vectorized by NEML2.
The cost associated with the marshaling step (purple in Figure 3.6) could be non-negligible de-
pending on the size of the problem, and for inexpensive constitutive models, such cost could
impose a significant overhead.

4 Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we introduced and demonstrated a new dispatch manager for the NEML2 constitu-
tive modeling library, designed to efficiently coordinate material model evaluations across hetero-
geneous computing resources. By separating responsibilities among data loaders, schedulers, and
dispatchers, the framework achieves both modularity and extensibility, enabling straightforward
integration of new devices and workflows. The design supports synchronous and asynchronous
execution, incorporates event tracing for performance analysis, and provides clear abstractions
for user-defined computational tasks.
 Numerical experiments highlight the significant performance improvements enabled by the
new dispatch system. Optimal dispatch batch sizes were identified for both CPUs and GPUs,
showing the importance of balancing throughput with memory constraints. Benchmarks of crys-
tal plasticity updates demonstrated that NEML2 achieves more than a twofold speedup over its
predecessor on CPUs, and GPU acceleration further reduces execution times by several orders of
magnitude. In the context of MOOSE-based finite element simulations, the dispatcher dramati-
cally reduced the cost of constitutive updates, shifting the computational bottleneck away from
material model evaluation.
 Looking forward, there are several avenues for further development. First, adaptive schedul-
ing strategies that dynamically respond to runtime conditions could improve efficiency in hetero-
geneous environments. Second, reducing or overlapping data marshaling costs will be critical for
scaling to large simulations where memory movement becomes a dominant factor. Finally, ex-
panding the dispatcher framework to support emerging accelerator architectures (such as AMD
GPUs or specialized AI hardware) would further broaden the applicability of NEML2 in high-
performance computing.
 Overall, the dispatcher system represents an important step toward making NEML2 a versa-
tile, scalable, and production-ready tool for advanced multiscale and multiphysics simulations.
