Intranasal Biodegradable Nanomedicine for Epilepsy Management: Targeting the Brain Beyond the Blood-Brain Barrier
Authors: Sadaf Jamal Gilani, Ahlam Mansour Sultan, Samar Zuhair Alshawwa, Md Rizwanullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy is a common chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures arising from abnormal neuronal hyperexcitability and hypersynchronous electrical activity within the brain. Despite advances in antiseizure medications, effective epilepsy management remains challenging because of pharmacoresistance, limited blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, inadequate intracerebral drug accumulation, and systemic toxicity. Moreover, currently available therapies primarily provide symptomatic seizure control without addressing the fundamental pathological processes involved in epileptogenesis, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal degeneration. Intranasal administration has emerged as an attractive non-invasive approach for direct brain targeting, enabling therapeutic agents to circumvent BBB-associated transport barriers and access the central nervous system through the olfactory and trigeminal neural pathways. In this context, biodegradable nanomedicine platforms have gained considerable attention due to their favorable biocompatibility, controlled drug release, enhanced mucosal permeation, and potential for targeted intracerebral delivery. This review provides a comprehensive and critical overview of recent advances in intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine for epilepsy, integrating current knowledge on disease pathophysiology, biological and pharmaceutical barriers, nose-to-brain transport mechanisms, biodegradable nanoparticle platforms, and emerging functionalization strategies. Importantly, the review critically evaluates the current evidence, distinguishing encouraging preclinical findings and discusses the major translational challenges that continue to hinder clinical implementation. Finally, future perspectives are highlighted to identify opportunities for developing safer, more effective, and clinically translatable therapies for epilepsy management.

Introduction

Epilepsy is among the most common chronic disorders of the central nervous system, affecting over 70 million people globally and imposing a substantial medical, social, and economic burden on healthcare systems.1 The disorder is defined by recurrent, unprovoked seizures that arise from excessive, hypersynchronous electrical activity within neuronal networks of the brain.2 Epilepsy comprises a heterogeneous group of disorders with multiple underlying causes, such as genetic factors, traumatic brain injury, cerebrovascular disease, neurodegenerative conditions, infections, tumors, and developmental abnormalities.3 In addition to recurrent seizures, many patients experience cognitive deficits, psychiatric comorbidities, neurobehavioral dysfunction, social stigma, and reduced quality of life.4 Despite significant advances in neuropharmacology and clinical management, achieving sustained seizure control continues to be challenging for a significant proportion of individuals with epilepsy.5

Current epilepsy management primarily relies on antiseizure medications (ASMs), which suppress neuronal hyperexcitability by modulating ion channels, neurotransmitter systems, and synaptic transmission.6 Although numerous ASMs are clinically available, approximately 30% of patients continue to experience uncontrolled seizures despite appropriate pharmacotherapy, while many responders develop dose-dependent adverse effects, including sedation, cognitive dysfunction, hepatotoxicity, mood disturbances, and systemic toxicity.7,8 Moreover, conventional ASMs predominantly provide symptomatic seizure suppression rather than addressing the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms involved in epileptogenesis and disease progression.9 The therapeutic efficacy of many ASMs is further compromised by poor BBB permeability, limited intracerebral drug accumulation, extensive hepatic metabolism, pharmacokinetic variability, and efflux transporter-mediated drug extrusion.10 Consequently, the development of more efficient brain-targeted therapeutic strategies remains an urgent clinical priority.

The BBB represents one of the most formidable biological barriers limiting effective central nervous system (CNS) drug delivery. Structurally, the BBB consists of tightly connected endothelial cells supported by astrocytes, pericytes, and basement membrane components that collectively regulate molecular transport between systemic circulation and neural tissues. Although essential for maintaining cerebral homeostasis and neuroprotection, the BBB severely restricts the penetration of many therapeutic agents into the brain.11,12 In epilepsy, BBB dysfunction, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, gliosis, and transporter overexpression further complicate therapeutic delivery and contribute to disease progression and pharmacoresistance. Overexpression of efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp), multidrug resistance-associated proteins (MRPs), and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) can significantly reduce intracerebral ASM concentrations, thereby limiting therapeutic efficacy.13,14 These complex pathological and biological barriers highlight the need for innovative delivery systems capable of enhancing brain-targeted drug transport.

The intranasal route has gained considerable attention as a non-invasive approach for overcoming BBB-associated transport limitations. Anatomically, the nasal cavity communicates directly with the central nervous system through the olfactory and trigeminal pathways, enabling administered drugs to bypass systemic circulation and directly access CNS tissues. Intranasal administration additionally avoids hepatic first-pass metabolism, improves drug bioavailability, enables rapid therapeutic onset, and reduces systemic adverse effects.15,16 However, efficient nose-to-brain delivery remains challenging because of several physiological barriers, including mucociliary clearance, enzymatic degradation within the nasal cavity, short formulation residence time, and poor epithelial permeation of many therapeutic agents. Collectively, these challenges have stimulated increasing interest in the development of nanotechnology-enabled intranasal drug delivery platforms for the treatment of neurological disorders.17

Among emerging nanomedicine approaches, biodegradable nanoparticles (NPs) have emerged as highly promising delivery platforms owing to their excellent biocompatibility, sustained drug-release characteristics, and ability to enhance brain-specific drug targeting. Biodegradable NPs safeguard encapsulated therapeutic agents against premature enzymatic or chemical degradation, improve mucosal permeation, increase nasal retention, and promote direct neuronal transport to epileptogenic brain regions.18,19 Furthermore, NPs can improve intracerebral drug retention, minimize systemic toxicity, and potentially overcome pharmacoresistance associated with efflux transporter activity.20 Advanced biodegradable nanomedicine platforms additionally enable multifunctional therapeutic approaches involving co-delivery of anticonvulsants, neuroprotective agents, anti-inflammatory compounds, antioxidants, and gene-based therapeutics. Continued advancements in surface functionalization, stimuli-responsive systems, and precision nanomedicine are further expanding the therapeutic potential of biodegradable NPs for epilepsy management.21–23

Despite growing interest in intranasal drug delivery and nanomedicine for neurological disorders, a comprehensive review specifically focused on biodegradable nanomedicine for epilepsy remains limited. Previous reviews have primarily discussed intranasal brain-targeted drug delivery or nanocarrier-based therapeutic systems in a broader context, with comparatively less emphasis on integrating the pathophysiology of epilepsy, biological and pharmaceutical barriers, biodegradable nanoplatforms, advanced surface engineering approaches, translational limitations, and future directions for clinical implementation within a unified perspective.10,24–26 To address this knowledge gap, this review critically examines the application of intranasal biodegradable NPs in epilepsy management. Specifically, it discusses the pathophysiological basis of epilepsy; biological and pharmaceutical barriers that limit conventional therapy; intranasal nose-to-brain transport mechanisms; biodegradable nanoparticle platforms; advanced functionalization strategies; and the major translational challenges associated with clinical application. Furthermore, the review critically distinguishes promising preclinical advances from the currently available clinical evidence and highlights emerging opportunities, including multifunctional nanomedicine, artificial intelligence-assisted nanoplatforms, and precision therapeutic approaches, to provide a balanced perspective on future clinical translation.

Review Methodology

The literature included in this narrative review was identified through systematic literature searches of major electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search focused on English-language publications using combinations of keywords such as epilepsy, intranasal drug delivery, nose-to-brain delivery, biodegradable nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, lipid-based nanoparticles, brain targeting, and nanomedicine. Peer-reviewed original research articles, review articles, and relevant preclinical and clinical studies related to intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine for epilepsy management were reviewed and synthesized. Irrelevant and duplicate publications were excluded, and priority was given to recent, high-quality studies.

Pathophysiological Basis of Epilepsy and Implications for Brain-Targeted Therapy

Epilepsy is a complex neurological disorder characterized by recurrent seizures resulting from excessive and synchronized neuronal discharges within the brain.27 The pathogenesis of epilepsy involves multifactorial molecular and cellular alterations, including excitatory-inhibitory neurotransmitter imbalance, ion channel dysfunction, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial impairment, synaptic remodeling, and BBB disruption. These interconnected pathological processes collectively contribute to neuronal hyperexcitability, seizure propagation, and chronic epileptogenesis, thereby presenting substantial challenges for effective therapeutic intervention.28 A comprehensive understanding of these mechanisms is essential for the rational development of advanced brain-targeted nanomedicine systems for epilepsy therapy.

One of the hallmark mechanisms underlying epileptogenesis is the disruption of the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. Glutamate serves as the principal excitatory neurotransmitter in the CNS, whereas γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) mediates inhibitory signaling. Excessive activation of glutamatergic receptors, particularly N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors, promotes sustained neuronal depolarization, calcium influx, and abnormal neuronal firing.29,30 Elevated intracellular calcium activates several downstream signaling cascades associated with neuronal injury, mitochondrial dysfunction, and synaptic plasticity.31 Simultaneously, impaired GABAergic neurotransmission resulting from interneuron loss, receptor dysfunction, or altered ion channel activity weakens inhibitory control and further amplifies neuronal hyperexcitability.32 These excitatory-inhibitory imbalances lower seizure thresholds and facilitate the establishment of hyperexcitable neuronal networks.

Ion channel abnormalities also contribute significantly to epilepsy pathophysiology. Voltage-gated sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride channels regulate neuronal membrane excitability and action potential generation. Mutations or dysregulation of these channels alter neuronal firing dynamics and increase susceptibility to spontaneous seizures.33 Abnormal sodium channel activation prolongs depolarization, whereas impaired potassium channel activity delays membrane repolarization and promotes repetitive neuronal firing.31,34 In addition, calcium channel dysfunction enhances neurotransmitter release and neuronal synchronization, thereby facilitating seizure propagation and network hyperexcitability.35

Neuroinflammation has emerged as a critical driver of epileptogenesis and seizure recurrence. Activated astrocytes, microglia, and infiltrating immune cells release pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which modulate synaptic transmission, alter ion channel activity, and increase neuronal excitability.36 Activation of inflammatory pathways, including nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling, further amplifies chronic neuroinflammatory responses and contributes to neuronal injury.37 Persistent neuroinflammation also promotes gliosis, leukocyte infiltration, and BBB dysfunction, thereby establishing a self-perpetuating cycle between inflammation and seizure activity.38 Major pathophysiological mechanisms underlying epilepsy and corresponding therapeutic targets are illustrated in Figure 1. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are closely associated with epilepsy progression. Excessive neuronal firing during seizures markedly increases the production of reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen species, overwhelming endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms and causing lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and mitochondrial injury.39 Mitochondrial dysfunction impairs ATP synthesis and calcium homeostasis, further exacerbating neuronal hyperexcitability and neuronal apoptosis.40 In addition, oxidative stress intensifies neuroinflammatory signaling and contributes to synaptic remodeling and neurodegeneration within epileptogenic regions.41

Structural and synaptic remodeling further contribute to the formation of epileptic neuronal circuits. Hippocampal sclerosis, neuronal loss, mossy fiber sprouting, reactive gliosis, and dendritic reorganization are commonly observed in chronic epilepsy, particularly temporal lobe epilepsy.42 These pathological alterations create recurrent excitatory networks that facilitate synchronized neuronal firing and seizure propagation. Astrocytic dysfunction additionally impairs glutamate clearance and extracellular potassium buffering, thereby intensifying neuronal excitability and network instability.43

Importantly, BBB impairment represents a critical factor contributing to both the development of epilepsy and reduced treatment efficacy. Seizure activity can compromise the integrity of endothelial tight junctions, leading to increased vascular permeability, which facilitates the infiltration of serum proteins, inflammatory mediators, and peripheral immune cells into the cerebral parenchyma. This disruption of BBB integrity promotes persistent neuroinflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and altered homeostasis of the neuronal microenvironment.44 Moreover, upregulated expression of efflux transporters such as P-gp, MRPs, and BCRP significantly reduces intracerebral concentrations of ASMs, thereby limiting therapeutic efficacy.45

Collectively, the multifactorial pathophysiology of epilepsy underscores the necessity for innovative therapeutic strategies that can enhance brain-targeted drug delivery, enhance neuronal uptake, and overcome biological transport barriers. In this context, biodegradable nanomedicine systems designed for intranasal delivery offer significant potential to enhance treatment outcomes and overcome limitations associated with conventional epilepsy therapy.

Experimental Models for Preclinical Evaluation of Epilepsy

Experimental epilepsy models are essential for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy, brain-targeting capability, pharmacokinetics, and safety of emerging intranasal nanomedicine systems before clinical translation. Experimental models reproduce key pathological features of epilepsy, including neuronal hyperexcitability, recurrent seizures, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal degeneration, thereby providing valuable platforms for assessing both antiseizure and disease-modifying therapeutic strategies.46

Among chemically induced models, the pentylenetetrazole model is one of the most widely used for evaluating anticonvulsant activity, seizure susceptibility, and antiepileptogenic effects. The kainic acid and pilocarpine models closely mimic temporal lobe epilepsy by inducing status epilepticus, hippocampal neuronal loss, gliosis, and chronic spontaneous recurrent seizures, making them particularly useful for investigating disease progression and neuroprotective interventions. In addition, electrical kindling models progressively induce permanent seizure susceptibility through repeated subthreshold electrical stimulation and are widely employed to investigate epileptogenesis, pharmacoresistance, and long-term therapeutic efficacy.47,48

It should be noted that no single experimental model fully replicates the complexity and heterogeneity of human epilepsy. Consequently, different models are selected according to the specific objectives of individual studies. Collectively, these complementary models provide robust preclinical evidence for evaluating the therapeutic potential and translational feasibility of intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine for epilepsy management.

Current Treatment Strategies and Their Limitations

Current epilepsy management primarily focuses on seizure suppression through pharmacological, surgical, dietary, and neuromodulatory interventions. Although these therapeutic approaches have significantly improved clinical outcomes for many patients, substantial limitations remain regarding long-term efficacy, brain-targeted drug delivery, systemic toxicity, and pharmacoresistance. Importantly, most currently available therapies primarily provide symptomatic seizure control rather than targeting the underlying mechanisms associated with epileptogenesis, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal degeneration.49 Accordingly, the identification and development of clinically effective, brain-directed therapeutic approaches with improved safety profiles remain a high priority for the management of epilepsy.

ASMs and Their Limitations

ASMs remain the first-line therapeutic approach for epilepsy management. These agents suppress neuronal hyperexcitability by modulating ion channels, enhancing GABA-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission, inhibiting glutamatergic excitatory signaling, or regulating synaptic vesicle release.50 Although currently available ASMs can effectively control seizures in many patients, approximately one-third of individuals continue to experience uncontrolled seizures despite appropriate pharmacotherapy.51 Furthermore, long-term ASM administration is frequently associated with dose-dependent adverse effects, including sedation, cognitive dysfunction, hepatotoxicity, psychiatric disturbances, dizziness, gastrointestinal complications, and teratogenicity, which can substantially impair patient quality of life and treatment adherence.6

Another major limitation of conventional ASMs is their inability to adequately penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and achieve therapeutically effective intracerebral concentrations. Efflux transporter overexpression, particularly P-gp, MRPs, and BCRP, can further reduce drug accumulation within epileptogenic brain regions and contribute to pharmacoresistance.52 Additionally, many ASMs exhibit poor aqueous solubility, rapid systemic clearance, extensive hepatic metabolism, and significant pharmacokinetic variability, thereby limiting therapeutic efficiency. Importantly, most ASMs suppress seizure symptoms without preventing disease progression or modifying epileptogenic mechanisms.53

Surgical and Neuromodulation Approaches

Surgical intervention may provide therapeutic benefit for selected patients with focal epilepsy who remain refractory to pharmacological treatment. Procedures such as temporal lobectomy, corpus callosotomy, and laser interstitial thermal therapy can reduce seizure frequency by removing or disconnecting epileptogenic brain tissue.54 However, epilepsy surgery is highly invasive and suitable only for carefully selected patients with well-localized seizure foci. Surgical complications, including cognitive impairment, neurological deficits, infection, and postoperative recurrence, additionally limit widespread applicability. Moreover, many patients are not eligible candidates because of multifocal seizure origins or localization within functionally critical brain regions.55

In addition to pharmacological treatment, several neuromodulation-based interventions, such as vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation, and responsive neurostimulation, are increasingly used in the management of drug-resistant epilepsy. These techniques modulate abnormal neuronal activity through electrical stimulation and may reduce seizure frequency in selected patient populations.56 Nevertheless, neuromodulatory interventions remain invasive, expensive, and technologically complex, while therapeutic outcomes vary substantially among patients. Device-related complications, surgical risks, battery replacement requirements, and incomplete seizure control continue to restrict their broader clinical utility.57

Dietary and Emerging Therapeutic Approaches

Dietary interventions, particularly ketogenic and modified Atkins diets, have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in selected patients with refractory epilepsy by altering cerebral energy metabolism and neuronal excitability.58 Despite encouraging clinical outcomes, long-term adherence to restrictive dietary regimens remains challenging because of gastrointestinal intolerance, nutritional deficiencies, metabolic disturbances, and poor patient compliance. Furthermore, therapeutic responses vary considerably among individuals, limiting universal applicability.59

Emerging therapeutic approaches, including gene therapy,60 stem cell therapy,61 neuroprotective agents,62 anti-inflammatory therapeutics,63 and precision medicine strategies,64 are increasingly being explored to address the complex pathophysiology of epilepsy. Although these approaches demonstrate promising preclinical potential, most remain in early developmental stages and face substantial translational challenges associated with safety, efficacy, regulatory approval, and long-term clinical validation.

Unmet Clinical and Pharmaceutical Challenges

Despite the availability of multiple therapeutic strategies, several major unmet clinical and pharmaceutical challenges continue to limit effective epilepsy management. Poor brain-targeted drug delivery, BBB-associated transport restrictions, pharmacoresistance, systemic toxicity, frequent dosing requirements, and inadequate intracerebral drug retention within epileptogenic brain regions continue to compromise sustained therapeutic outcomes.65 In addition, substantial interpatient variability in disease etiology, seizure type, neuroinflammatory status, and therapeutic responsiveness complicates treatment optimization. Importantly, currently available therapies primarily focus on seizure suppression rather than disease modification or prevention of epileptogenesis.66 These limitations emphasize the need for innovative brain-directed drug delivery technologies that can enhance intracerebral drug accumulation, minimize systemic adverse effects, overcome biological barriers, and enable multifunctional therapeutic approaches.

Biological Barriers in Epilepsy Therapy

The effective treatment of epilepsy remains substantially limited by several biological barriers that restrict the delivery of ASMs to epileptogenic brain regions. Although numerous ASMs are clinically available, their therapeutic efficacy is often compromised by restricted BBB permeability, seizure-induced neurovascular dysfunction, efflux transporter-mediated drug resistance, and impaired intracerebral drug accumulation. These interconnected barriers not only reduce brain bioavailability of ASMs but also contribute to persistent neuronal hyperexcitability, neuroinflammation, pharmacoresistance, and disease progression. Consequently, overcoming these biological limitations has become a major focus in the development of advanced brain-targeted therapeutic systems for epilepsy management. BBB and Drug Transportation

The BBB is a highly specialized neurovascular structure responsible for preserving the physiological environment of the brain by tightly regulating the movement of molecules between the bloodstream and the CNS. At the structural level, the BBB is primarily formed by brain microvascular endothelial cells that are joined by specialized tight junction complexes, comprising claudin proteins, occludin, and zonula occludens proteins. The endothelial layer is further reinforced by surrounding astrocytic end-feet, pericytes, extracellular basement membrane components, and a variety of membrane transport systems, all of which contribute to the formation of a highly selective permeability barrier.67,68

The molecular transport of substances across the BBB is mediated through several distinct transport mechanisms, including passive lipophilic diffusion, paracellular transport, receptor-mediated transcytosis, transporter-mediated influx, carrier-mediated transport, and adsorptive-mediated transcytosis.69 Small lipophilic molecules can diffuse directly across endothelial membranes, whereas hydrophilic compounds and macromolecules generally require specialized transport systems. Among these, solute carrier transporters play a crucial role in delivering essential metabolites, including glucose, amino acids, and various ions, into the cerebral microenvironment.70 However, the restrictive physicochemical characteristics of the BBB limit the penetration of most therapeutic agents, with nearly all macromolecules and approximately 98% of small-molecule drugs exhibiting inadequate BBB permeability.71 Consequently, many ASMs fail to achieve therapeutically effective intracerebral concentrations within epileptogenic regions.

Seizure-Induced BBB Disruption and Neuroinflammation

Recurrent seizures induce profound structural and functional alterations in the BBB that contribute to epileptogenesis and disease progression. Seizure-associated excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, angiogenesis, and inflammatory signaling disrupt endothelial tight junction integrity, thereby increasing BBB permeability and altering the neurovascular microenvironment. Elevated extracellular glutamate activates NMDA receptors and promotes calcium-mediated endothelial dysfunction, resulting in vascular leakage and neuronal injury.72

Neuroinflammation plays a central role in this pathological process. Activated microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, and infiltrating immune cells release pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and HMGB1, which further amplify inflammatory cascades and neuronal hyperexcitability.73 Persistent activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, such as NF-κB and TLR4-mediated signaling, contributes to chronic epileptogenesis.74 Simultaneously, anti-inflammatory microglial responses attempt to restore tissue homeostasis through phagocytosis, trophic factor release, and debris clearance; however, prolonged inflammatory activation ultimately favors neuronal injury and seizure recurrence.75

Importantly, BBB impairment and neuroinflammatory processes interact in a bidirectional manner, creating a vicious cycle that promotes disease progression. Disruption of BBB integrity facilitates the infiltration of serum proteins, pro-inflammatory mediators, together with circulating immune cells, into the cerebral parenchyma, thereby enhancing neuronal hyperexcitability and further exacerbating BBB dysfunction.76

Efflux Transporter-Mediated Drug Resistance

Overexpression of efflux transporters within the BBB represents another major obstacle limiting effective epilepsy therapy. ATP-binding cassette transporters, particularly P-gp, MRPs, and BCRP, actively transport many ASMs from the brain back into systemic circulation, thereby significantly reducing intracerebral drug concentrations. Increased expression of these transporters is strongly associated with pharmacoresistant epilepsy and substantially contributes to therapeutic failure.77

Seizure activity itself can induce transporter overexpression through glutamate-mediated signaling and inflammatory pathways. Elevated extracellular glutamate activates NMDA receptor signaling in brain capillary endothelial cells, resulting in enhanced P-gp expression and increased drug efflux activity.78 Furthermore, inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress pathways additionally regulate transporter function, thereby linking neuroinflammation with ASM pharmacoresistance.79 Cytochrome P450 enzymes expressed within BBB endothelial cells further contribute to reduced intracerebral drug availability by accelerating local drug metabolism.80

Blood-Cerebrospinal Fluid Barrier (BCSFB)

In addition to the BBB, the BCSFB also contributes to restricted CNS drug delivery. The BCSFB is primarily located within the choroid plexus and consists of epithelial cells connected by tight junctions that regulate molecular exchange between blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Although capillaries within the choroid plexus are fenestrated, tight junctions present in the choroid plexus epithelium selectively limit molecular transport into the CSF and subsequently into the brain parenchyma.81 Similar to the BBB, the BCSFB expresses multiple efflux transporters and metabolic enzymes that further restrict drug entry into the CNS. Alterations in BCSFB integrity during epilepsy may additionally influence inflammatory signaling, CSF composition, and intracerebral drug distribution.82 Consequently, both the BBB and BCSFB collectively represent major biological barriers limiting ASM bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy in epilepsy treatment.

Collectively, these interconnected biological barriers severely hinder efficient drug delivery to epileptogenic brain regions and substantially contribute to pharmacoresistance and poor seizure control. Therefore, the development of advanced therapeutic platforms capable of bypassing or overcoming these barriers is essential for improving epilepsy management. In this context, intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine systems have emerged as promising strategies for enhancing nose-to-brain drug transport, improving intracerebral drug accumulation, and overcoming limitations associated with conventional epilepsy therapy.

Pharmaceutical Barriers in the Treatment of Epilepsy

Despite significant advances in antiseizure pharmacotherapy, several pharmaceutical barriers continue to compromise the effectiveness of epilepsy management. Current ASMs primarily provide symptomatic seizure suppression and do not effectively prevent epileptogenesis or disease progression. Furthermore, adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions, pharmacoresistance, medication non-adherence, economic limitations, and restricted healthcare accessibility collectively reduce therapeutic efficacy and negatively affect patient quality of life. These interconnected barriers frequently contribute to inadequate seizure control, persistent neurological dysfunction, and increased clinical burden. Medication-Related Barriers

Long-term ASM therapy is frequently associated with dose-dependent adverse effects that negatively influence treatment adherence and therapeutic outcomes. Common adverse reactions include sedation, dizziness, fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, mood disturbances, gastrointestinal complications, hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and weight alterations.83 Chronic ASM exposure may additionally impair memory, concentration, and psychomotor performance, thereby reducing social functioning and overall quality of life. Adverse drug reactions remain one of the most common causes of medication discontinuation and non-adherence in epilepsy patients.84

Polytherapy is commonly required in refractory epilepsy and patients with multiple comorbidities; however, simultaneous administration of multiple ASMs substantially increases the risk of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic drug-drug interactions.85 Many ASMs are metabolized through cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme systems and can either induce or inhibit hepatic metabolism, thereby altering plasma drug concentrations and therapeutic efficacy.86 These interactions may lead to reduced seizure control, increased systemic toxicity, or unpredictable adverse effects.

Approximately one-third of patients with epilepsy develop pharmacoresistant epilepsy, in which seizures remain uncontrolled despite appropriate diagnosis and optimized ASM therapy. Pharmacoresistance is associated with multiple mechanisms, including altered drug targets, neuronal network remodeling, genetic polymorphisms, enhanced drug metabolism, and overexpression of efflux transporters such as P-gp, MRPs, and BCRP within the BBB. These mechanisms significantly reduce intracerebral ASM accumulation and contribute to persistent neuronal hyperexcitability.87

Patient-Related Barriers

Medication non-adherence is one of the most common causes of preventable seizures and treatment failure in epilepsy. Multiple factors contribute to poor adherence, including forgetfulness, adverse drug reactions, high treatment costs, complicated dosing schedules, psychological stress, and patient perceptions regarding treatment inefficacy. Poor adherence significantly increases the risk of uncontrolled seizures, emergency hospitalization, psychosocial dysfunction, and misclassification as pharmacoresistant epilepsy.88

Insufficient patient education regarding epilepsy pathophysiology, long-term treatment requirements, medication safety, and seizure management often promotes negative beliefs toward ASMs. Misconceptions regarding adverse effects, fear of long-term toxicity, and social stigma may encourage patients to self-adjust doses or discontinue therapy without medical supervision.89 Furthermore, inaccurate classification of seizure type and epilepsy syndrome may lead to inappropriate ASM selection, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy or even aggravating seizure activity in certain epilepsy subtypes. Delayed diagnosis and incorrect therapeutic decisions, therefore, remain important barriers to effective epilepsy management.90

Healthcare System and Economic Barriers

The economic burden associated with lifelong epilepsy treatment represents a major obstacle. Newer-generation ASMs, long-term clinical monitoring, diagnostic procedures, and hospitalization substantially increase healthcare costs and reduce treatment accessibility. High out-of-pocket expenses frequently contribute to poor medication adherence and treatment discontinuation.91 Limited availability of ASMs, delayed medication supply, inadequate insurance coverage, and restricted access to specialized neurological care further compromise epilepsy treatment outcomes. Inconsistent drug availability may force patients to switch formulations or interrupt therapy, thereby increasing seizure recurrence risk and disease progression. Additionally, inadequate physician-patient communication, short consultation times, and limited provider expertise may contribute to suboptimal therapeutic planning and patient mistrust.92

Collectively, these pharmaceutical, patient-related, and healthcare system barriers significantly impair seizure control and therapeutic effectiveness in epilepsy management. Therefore, overcoming these limitations remains essential for improving long-term epilepsy outcomes. In this context, advanced nanotechnology-based drug delivery approaches, including biodegradable nanomedicine and intranasal brain-targeted drug delivery platforms, are increasingly recognized as effective strategies for improving brain-specific drug distribution, increasing drug bioavailability, minimizing systemic adverse effects, and circumventing many of the challenges related to the conventional ASMs therapy.

Intranasal Nose-to-Brain Drug Delivery: A Promising Strategy for Epilepsy Therapy

Intranasal nose-to-brain drug delivery has emerged as a promising non-invasive strategy for improving CNS targeting in epilepsy therapy. Conventional oral and parenteral administration routes are frequently limited by poor BBB permeability, extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism, systemic toxicity, and inadequate intracerebral drug accumulation. These limitations substantially compromise the therapeutic efficacy of many ASMs, particularly during chronic treatment. Several anatomical and physiological features of the nasal cavity make it an attractive route for rapid drug delivery to the CNS. The nasal mucosa is richly vascularized and covered by a relatively permeable epithelial layer that supports efficient absorption of administered therapeutics. Anatomically, the nasal cavity comprises three principal regions: the vestibular, respiratory, and olfactory compartments. Among these, the olfactory region plays a pivotal role in direct brain targeting because olfactory sensory neurons establish a neuronal connection with the olfactory bulb. In addition, branches of the trigeminal nerve distributed throughout the respiratory epithelium provide an additional neural route linking the nasal cavity with CNS structures, including the brainstem and spinal cord. These neural pathways provide a unique opportunity to bypass BBB-associated transport restrictions and directly deliver therapeutic agents into the brain.94,95

Following intranasal administration, therapeutic agents can access the brain through two principal neuronal pathways: the olfactory and trigeminal pathways. The olfactory pathway facilitates drug movement across the olfactory epithelium, after which the molecules are transported to the olfactory bulb and subsequently distributed to deeper regions of the brain. This pathway includes transcellular transport through epithelial cells, paracellular diffusion between epithelial tight junctions, and intracellular neuronal transport via axonal trafficking along olfactory neurons. Intracellular transport generally occurs through endocytosis-mediated uptake followed by axonal movement, whereas extracellular diffusion through perineural and perivascular channels enables rapid drug distribution into CSF and brain parenchyma (Figure 4A).96,97

The trigeminal pathway also contributes significantly to nose-to-brain drug transport. The respiratory epithelium is innervated by branches of the trigeminal nerve, which extend to the brainstem through the ophthalmic and maxillary divisions. Following absorption through this neuronal pathway, therapeutic agents may be delivered to multiple regions of the CNS through a combination of intracellular axonal trafficking and extracellular diffusion (Figure 4B). Although trigeminal transport is generally slower than olfactory transport, it supports broader intracerebral drug distribution and prolonged CNS exposure.98

Intranasal administration offers several important advantages for epilepsy therapy. First, it bypasses hepatic first-pass metabolism and gastrointestinal degradation, thereby improving drug bioavailability and reducing metabolic loss. Second, rapid absorption through the highly vascularized nasal mucosa enables faster onset of pharmacological action, which is particularly beneficial during acute seizure episodes. Third, direct brain targeting minimizes systemic drug exposure and may reduce peripheral adverse effects associated with long-term ASM therapy. Moreover, the intranasal route is a painless and minimally invasive mode of drug administration that can be easily self-administered, thereby enhancing treatment adherence and overall patient convenience.99,100

Although intranasal drug delivery offers several therapeutic benefits, it remains challenging because of several physiological and pharmaceutical limitations. The efficiency of drug absorption within the nasal cavity is compromised by mucociliary clearance, continuous mucus turnover, enzymatic metabolism within the nasal environment, and the short residence time of pharmaceutical formulations. Furthermore, the olfactory region occupies only a small portion of the nasal surface area, thereby limiting direct neuronal transport capacity. Drug physicochemical properties, including molecular weight, lipophilicity, charge, and stability, additionally influence nasal permeation and brain targeting efficiency.101,102

These challenges have stimulated the development of nanotechnology-enabled intranasal drug delivery platforms as a promising strategy for improving nose-to-brain drug transport. Biodegradable NPs enhance formulation stability by shielding encapsulated therapeutic agents against enzymatic degradation, extending their retention within the nasal cavity, enhancing mucosal permeation, and facilitating controlled intracerebral drug release. Various NPs have demonstrated enhanced brain bioavailability and improved anticonvulsant efficacy in preclinical studies. Surface functionalization with mucoadhesive polymers, targeting ligands, and permeation enhancers can further improve neuronal uptake and brain-targeting efficiency.103

Biodegradable Nanomedicine Platforms for Intranasal Epilepsy Therapy

The selection of an appropriate nanocarrier system is a critical determinant of the safety, efficacy, and clinical translatability of nanomedicine for epilepsy management. Among the various nanomedicines investigated for brain-targeted drug delivery, biodegradable nanoparticles have attracted considerable attention because they degrade into biocompatible byproducts that can be metabolized or eliminated from the body, thereby minimizing the risk of long-term tissue accumulation and chronic toxicity.104,105 Figure 5 illustrates the schematic representation of the major classes of biodegradable NPs employed for epilepsy management, along with their representative examples, key advantages, and associated limitations. Conventional ASMs frequently exhibit limited therapeutic efficacy because of poor BBB permeability, rapid systemic clearance, enzymatic degradation, and inadequate intracerebral drug accumulation.6 Moreover, chronic systemic administration of ASMs is often associated with dose-dependent toxicity, pharmacokinetic variability, and reduced patient adherence.106 Consequently, there is growing interest in developing advanced drug delivery systems capable of improving brain-targeted therapeutic efficiency while minimizing systemic adverse effects. The rationale for employing biodegradable nanomedicine in intranasal epilepsy therapy is primarily based on its ability to facilitate direct drug transport from the nasal cavity to the brain through olfactory and trigeminal neural pathways. Due to their nanoscale dimensions, biodegradable NPs can efficiently interact with the nasal epithelium, enhance mucosal permeation, and improve neuronal uptake while bypassing BBB-associated transport limitations.10 Furthermore, nanocarriers can encapsulate therapeutic agents within protective biodegradable matrices, thereby shielding drugs from enzymatic degradation and premature systemic elimination within the nasal cavity. This protective capability is particularly important for unstable therapeutic molecules and biomacromolecules exhibiting poor physiological stability.107

Another important advantage of biodegradable nanomedicine platforms is their ability to improve the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of ASMs. Encapsulation within NPs can prolong nasal residence time, sustain drug release, and maintain therapeutically effective intracerebral drug concentrations over extended periods. Enhanced brain retention may improve seizure suppression while reducing dosing frequency and minimizing systemic drug exposure. Consequently, biodegradable nanosystems may reduce peripheral adverse effects commonly associated with long-term ASM administration, including hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, cognitive dysfunction, and gastrointestinal complications.108,109

Biodegradable nanomedicine systems also offer significant potential for improving brain-targeting efficiency and overcoming pharmacoresistance. Surface engineering approaches such as mucoadhesive modification, ligand functionalization, and permeation enhancement can improve NPs’ interaction with the nasal mucosa and facilitate receptor-mediated neuronal transport. Additionally, NPs may bypass efflux transporter-mediated drug extrusion and improve intracellular drug accumulation within epileptogenic brain regions where conventional formulations often fail to achieve therapeutic concentrations.110 These properties are particularly relevant in pharmacoresistant epilepsy, where inadequate intracerebral drug exposure remains a major cause of therapeutic failure.

Overall, biodegradable nanomedicine platforms represent a promising therapeutic strategy for intranasal epilepsy therapy by improving brain targeting, enhancing bioavailability, prolonging therapeutic retention, and overcoming BBB-associated delivery limitations. Continued advances in nanomedicine engineering and intranasal delivery technologies may facilitate the development of safer and more effective brain-targeted therapies for epilepsy management. Nevertheless, these advantages have largely been demonstrated in preclinical studies, and further clinical investigations are required to confirm their long-term safety, efficacy, and translational potential in patients with epilepsy.

Lipid-Based Biodegradable NPs for Intranasal Delivery in Epilepsy

Liposomes

Liposomes are phospholipid vesicles composed of one or more lipid bilayers surrounding an aqueous core, enabling encapsulation of both hydrophilic and lipophilic therapeutic agents. Compared with other lipid-based nanosystems, liposomes offer greater versatility in delivering biomacromolecules, peptides, and gene therapeutics due to their unique aqueous core-lipid bilayer architecture.122 Their structural similarity to biological membranes additionally supports efficient interaction with neuronal tissues following intranasal administration.123

In epilepsy therapy, liposomal formulations of different ASMs have demonstrated improved therapeutic efficacy and intracerebral drug delivery compared with conventional formulations in experimental models.113–115 Beyond conventional ASM delivery, liposomes are increasingly being explored for multifunctional therapeutic applications, including delivery of siRNA, neuroprotective agents, and anti-inflammatory therapeutics targeting epileptogenic pathways.124 Electro-responsive liposomes capable of stimulus-triggered drug release during seizure-associated neuronal activity have also emerged as innovative therapeutic systems.125

Despite these advantages, liposomal formulations may exhibit limited physical stability, premature drug leakage, and manufacturing complexity during large-scale production.126 In addition, long-term safety and reproducibility following chronic intranasal administration remain insufficiently characterized. Nevertheless, continued advances in lipid engineering and responsive nanomedicine technologies are expected to strengthen the translational potential of liposomal systems for epilepsy therapy.

SLNs

SLNs have emerged as promising lipid-based biodegradable nanocarriers for intranasal delivery because of their excellent biocompatibility, physical stability, and ability to enhance brain-targeted drug delivery.127 Structurally, SLNs consist of solid biodegradable lipid matrices stabilized by surfactants, enabling efficient encapsulation of therapeutic agents and protection against chemical or enzymatic degradation. Compared with liposomes, SLNs generally provide greater physicochemical stability and lower drug leakage because of their rigid solid lipid matrix.128 Their lipidic architecture promotes interaction with nasal epithelial membranes and facilitates direct nose-to-brain transport through olfactory and trigeminal pathways.129

Preclinical studies involving ASM-loaded SLNs have demonstrated improved anticonvulsant efficacy and enhanced intracerebral drug retention compared with conventional formulations.116 Beyond antiseizure drug delivery, SLNs offer a versatile platform for delivering antioxidant and anti-inflammatory therapeutics that may counteract oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory pathways involved in epileptogenesis.130 Furthermore, enhanced intracellular drug accumulation may partially overcome efflux transporter-mediated pharmacoresistance within epileptogenic brain tissues.131

However, the highly crystalline lipid matrix of SLNs may restrict drug loading capacity and increase the likelihood of drug leakage or expulsion during storage. These limitations motivated the development of NLCs, which possess more flexible lipid matrices and improved drug-loading capability. In addition, challenges related to long-term physicochemical stability and the reproducible large-scale manufacturing of SLNs limit the broader clinical application of SLN-based epilepsy therapeutics.132

NLCs

NLCs were introduced as an advanced lipid-based drug delivery platform to address the inherent limitations of SLNs, particularly their relatively low drug-loading capacity and the possibility of drug expulsion during storage. In contrast to SLNs, NLCs are formulated by combining solid and liquid lipids, which generates a partially disordered lipid matrix with greater structural flexibility. This unique matrix facilitates higher drug incorporation, enhances formulation stability, and minimizes the likelihood of drug leakage during storage when compared with conventional SLNs.133,134

In epilepsy therapy, NLC formulations of different ASMs have demonstrated improved therapeutic performance and prolonged seizure suppression in experimental models.117–120 Their flexible lipid architecture supports efficient incorporation of poorly water-soluble ASMs while maintaining favorable physicochemical stability. In addition, NLCs have shown potential for co-delivery of neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory agents targeting oxidative stress and neuroinflammation associated with epileptogenesis.118

Compared with SLNs, NLCs generally exhibit superior formulation flexibility, improved drug retention, and reduced risk of drug leakage during long-term storage. Surface engineering approaches involving mucoadhesive polymers or targeting ligands may further enhance neuronal uptake and intracerebral drug delivery following intranasal administration.135 Nevertheless, formulation complexity, lipid oxidation, and scalability challenges continue to limit broader clinical translation of NLC-based therapeutics.136

Nanoemulsion

Nanoemulsions are colloidal lipid-based delivery systems composed of nanosized oil droplets dispersed within aqueous phases and stabilized by surfactants or co-surfactants. Owing to their small droplet size, high surface area, and favorable membrane interaction properties, nanoemulsions have emerged as promising carriers for intranasal epilepsy therapy, particularly for poorly water-soluble ASMs.137 Compared with conventional emulsions, nanoemulsions generally exhibit improved kinetic stability, enhanced drug solubilization, and efficient mucosal dispersion following intranasal administration.138

In epilepsy therapy, nanoemulsions of topiramate have demonstrated enhanced brain delivery and improved anticonvulsant efficacy in experimental models compared with conventional formulations.121 Their lipidic composition facilitates efficient incorporation of lipophilic drugs while supporting rapid drug absorption through the nasal mucosa.137 In addition, incorporation of mucoadhesive polymers or in situ gelling systems may further prolong nasal residence time and improve therapeutic retention within epileptogenic brain regions.

Another important advantage of nanoemulsions is their relatively simple fabrication process and favorable scalability potential compared with more structurally complex nanosystems.139 However, thermodynamic instability, surfactant-associated toxicity, phase separation during long-term storage, and variability in droplet-size distribution remain important challenges limiting clinical translation.140 Nevertheless, nanoemulsions remain attractive lipid-based platforms for intranasal epilepsy therapy because of their formulation simplicity, drug-solubilization capability, and translational feasibility.

Polymer-Based Biodegradable NPs

Polymer-based biodegradable NPs provide greater structural tunability, multifunctional engineering capability, and controlled drug-release behavior compared with many lipid-based systems. Biodegradable polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), chitosan, polycaprolactone (PCL), gelatin, and alginate have been extensively investigated for intranasal epilepsy therapy because of their favorable biocompatibility and pharmaceutical versatility.141 In addition to conventional ASMs delivery, polymeric nanosystems are increasingly being explored for the delivery of neuroprotective agents, antioxidants, and peptide therapeutics targeting epileptogenic pathways.142–144 Among these systems, polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs), polymeric micelles (PMs), dendrimers, and hydrogels represent the most extensively investigated polymer-based platforms for brain-targeted epilepsy therapy. Polymer-Based Biodegradable NPs for Intranasal Delivery in Epilepsy

PNPs

PNPs have demonstrated considerable potential for improving intranasal delivery of ASMs because of their favorable biocompatibility, formulation flexibility, and tunable physicochemical properties. Biodegradable polymers such as PLGA, chitosan, gelatin, alginate, and PCL have been widely investigated for the fabrication of brain-targeted nanosystems.141 Compared with many lipid-based carriers, PMs generally provide greater structural stability and multifunctional engineering capability.

Preclinical studies involving oxcarbazepine,145 carbamazepine,146 lamotrigine,147 and amisulpride148 encapsulated PNPs have demonstrated prolonged anticonvulsant activity and improved therapeutic performance compared with conventional formulations. PLGA-based PNPs have attracted particular attention because of their biodegradability, regulatory acceptance, and pharmaceutical versatility,141 whereas chitosan-containing formulations exhibit favorable interaction with the nasal epithelium.154 Surface functionalization using targeting ligands, mucoadhesive polymers, or permeation enhancers may further improve neuronal uptake and intracerebral delivery following intranasal administration.155

Beyond conventional ASM delivery, PNPs are increasingly being explored for multifunctional therapeutic applications involving antioxidants, neuroprotective compounds, peptides, and gene therapeutics targeting epileptogenic mechanisms.142–144 Nevertheless, formulation reproducibility, polymer-associated cytotoxicity, large-scale manufacturing complexity, and long-term safety evaluation remain important translational challenges limiting broader clinical application.

PMs

PMs are self-assembled nanosystems formed from amphiphilic biodegradable block copolymers possessing hydrophobic cores and hydrophilic shells.156 Their unique architecture makes them particularly suitable for encapsulating poorly water-soluble ASMs while improving formulation solubility and colloidal stability. Compared with conventional PNPs, PMs generally provide more efficient incorporation of lipophilic therapeutic agents because of their hydrophobic core structure.157

In epilepsy therapy, PMs loaded with clonazepam149 and lamotrigine150 have demonstrated improved therapeutic availability and prolonged seizure suppression in experimental models. Functionalization using polyethylene glycol (PEG), poloxamers, or targeting ligands may further enhance colloidal stability and neuronal uptake following intranasal administration.157 In addition, PMs have shown promise for the delivery of neuroprotective compounds and multifunctional therapeutics targeting oxidative stress and neuroinflammation associated with epileptogenesis.158,159

Despite these advantages, PMs may exhibit instability following dilution in physiological fluids, leading to premature drug leakage and reduced therapeutic consistency. Furthermore, large-scale manufacturing reproducibility and long-term storage stability remain important translational concerns.160 Nevertheless, because of their efficient solubilization capability and formulation flexibility, PMs remain promising nanosystems for intranasal epilepsy therapy.

Dendrimers

Dendrimers are highly branched nanoscale macromolecules possessing a well-defined three-dimensional architecture and multiple surface functional groups that enable efficient drug conjugation and multifunctional surface engineering. Compared with many conventional polymeric nanosystems, dendrimers provide superior structural precision and high loading capacity, making them attractive carriers for targeted therapeutic delivery applications. Among various dendrimer families, poly(amidoamine) dendrimers have been most extensively investigated for neurological applications because of their favorable formulation versatility and controllable surface characteristics.161,162

In epilepsy therapy, dendrimer-based systems have demonstrated improved therapeutic stability, enhanced neuronal targeting, and reduced seizure severity in experimental models.151 Functionalization with PEG, targeting ligands, or peptides may further improve intracellular delivery and neuronal specificity following intranasal administration.163 In addition, dendrimers exhibit considerable potential for the delivery of siRNA, peptides, and nucleic acid therapeutics targeting the CNS, thereby extending their application beyond conventional seizure suppression toward disease-modifying therapeutic approaches.164

Despite these advantages, several challenges continue to limit the clinical translation of dendrimer-based therapeutics. Highly cationic dendrimers may induce membrane destabilization and cytotoxicity, whereas biodegradation kinetics and long-term safety profiles remain insufficiently characterized. Furthermore, manufacturing reproducibility and regulatory standardization continue to represent important translational barriers. Nevertheless, dendrimers remain promising multifunctional nanoplatforms for advanced brain-targeted epilepsy therapy.165

Polymeric Hydrogels

Polymeric hydrogels are three-dimensional, hydrophilic polymeric networks that can absorb large amounts of water while maintaining structural integrity. Because of their favorable biocompatibility, mucoadhesive properties, and in situ gelling capability, hydrogel-based systems have emerged as promising platforms for intranasal drug delivery. Compared with conventional liquid formulations, polymeric hydrogels can prolong nasal residence time and reduce rapid mucociliary clearance, thereby improving therapeutic retention within the nasal cavity.166,167

Various biodegradable polymers, including chitosan, poloxamers, carbopol, alginate, and gelatin, have been investigated for the fabrication of intranasal hydrogel systems. Thermoresponsive and pH-responsive hydrogels are particularly attractive because they undergo sol-to-gel transition under physiological nasal conditions, enabling sustained retention and controlled therapeutic release following administration.168,169 In epilepsy therapy, hydrogel formulations containing oxcarbazepine152 and topiramate153 have demonstrated improved anticonvulsant efficacy and enhanced brain delivery in experimental models compared with conventional formulations.

Another important advantage of polymeric hydrogels is their ability to serve as multifunctional delivery matrices for the incorporation of NPs, nanoemulsions, micelles, or liposomes, thereby combining the advantages of nanomedicine with prolonged intranasal retention.169 However, variability in gelation behavior, limited mechanical stability, potential nasal irritation, and challenges in reproducible large-scale manufacturing remain important translational concerns. Nevertheless, polymeric hydrogels represent promising adjunctive platforms for improving intranasal brain-targeted epilepsy therapy.

Polymer-Lipid Hybrid Nanoparticles (PLHNPs)

PLHNPs have emerged as advanced biodegradable nanocarriers for intranasal delivery by integrating the structural advantages of PNPs with the membrane affinity and biocompatibility of lipid-based systems.170 Typically, PLHPNs consist of a biodegradable polymeric core surrounded by a lipid shell, thereby combining the controlled-release behavior and structural stability of polymeric matrices with the enhanced biological interaction and mucosal permeation properties of lipid carriers.171 This hybrid architecture improves drug retention, minimizes premature drug leakage, and enhances brain-targeting efficiency following intranasal administration.170

In epilepsy therapy, PLHNPs have demonstrated substantial potential for improving the delivery and therapeutic performance of ASMs. The lipid shell facilitates interaction with nasal epithelial membranes, whereas the polymeric core supports sustained intracerebral drug release.172 Incorporation of mucoadhesive polymers such as chitosan may further enhance nasal retention and neuronal uptake.173 Recent preclinical investigations have shown promising therapeutic outcomes using PLHNPs. Youssef et al developed phenytoin loaded lecithin-chitosan PLHNPs for pentylenetetrazole-induced seizures and reported enhanced nose-to-brain transport, reduced systemic accumulation, and complete seizure suppression after intranasal delivery.174 Similarly, zopiclone-loaded PLHNPs formulated with chitosan and lipid excipients demonstrated improved brain-targeting efficiency, prolonged nasal residence, enhanced anticonvulsant activity, and reduced oxidative stress compared with conventional formulations.175

Another important advantage of PLHNPs is their multifunctional therapeutic capability. Surface functionalization using PEG, targeting ligands, or cell-penetrating peptides may further improve neuronal specificity and intracellular uptake.176 In addition, PLHNPs may help overcome pharmacoresistance by enhancing intracellular drug retention within epileptogenic brain tissue. Although several translational challenges remained for widespread clinical application, such as formulation complexity, long-term physicochemical stability, large-scale manufacturing reproducibility, and insufficient chronic safety evaluation.177 Nevertheless, owing to their structural versatility, controlled-release behavior, and enhanced brain-targeting capability, PLHNPs represent promising next-generation nanoplatforms for intranasal epilepsy therapy. Biodegradable PLHNPs for Intranasal Delivery in Epilepsy

Collectively, lipid-based and polymer-based biodegradable nanosystems exhibit complementary pharmaceutical advantages for intranasal epilepsy therapy. Lipid-based carriers generally provide superior membrane interaction and biocompatibility, whereas polymeric systems offer greater structural tunability, controlled release capability, and multifunctional engineering flexibility. PLHNPs integrating both lipidic and polymeric components may therefore provide greater translational potential by combining efficient mucosal interaction with enhanced formulation stability and drug-loading capacity. Nevertheless, large-scale manufacturing reproducibility, long-term safety, regulatory standardization, and clinical validation remain major barriers limiting clinical translation.

Advanced Functionalization Strategies for Intranasal Nanomedicine

The therapeutic performance of intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine systems is strongly influenced by their physicochemical properties and surface characteristics. Although nanosystems can improve nose-to-brain drug delivery, several physiological barriers, including mucociliary clearance, enzymatic degradation, limited epithelial permeability, and efflux transporter activity, continue to restrict therapeutic efficiency.180 Consequently, advanced functionalization strategies have been extensively explored to enhance nasal retention, improve neuronal uptake, increase brain-targeting specificity, and optimize intracerebral drug accumulation. Surface engineering and stimuli-responsive modifications have therefore emerged as critical approaches for improving the translational potential of nanomedicine-based epilepsy therapy.181

Mucoadhesive functionalization represents one of the most widely investigated strategies for improving intranasal drug delivery. The nasal cavity possesses an efficient mucociliary clearance mechanism that rapidly removes foreign particles and reduces formulation residence time. Surface modification using mucoadhesive polymers such as chitosan, hyaluronic acid, carbopol, and thiolated polymers can significantly prolong NPs retention within the nasal cavity through electrostatic interaction with negatively charged mucin glycoproteins. Enhanced mucoadhesion improves epithelial contact time, facilitates drug absorption, and increases transport through olfactory and trigeminal pathways. Furthermore, certain mucoadhesive polymers, particularly chitosan, can transiently open epithelial tight junctions and enhance paracellular permeation of therapeutic agents.154,182

Ligand-mediated targeting has also gained considerable attention for its potential to improve neuronal uptake and brain specificity. Surface conjugation with targeting ligands can facilitate receptor-mediated transport across epithelial and neuronal barriers. These ligands interact with specific receptors expressed on nasal epithelial cells, olfactory neurons, or brain endothelial cells, thereby promoting intracellular uptake and enhanced intracerebral distribution. Such active targeting approaches may significantly improve therapeutic accumulation within epileptogenic brain regions while minimizing systemic drug exposure.183,184

Stimuli-responsive nanomedicine systems have further expanded the therapeutic capabilities of intranasal delivery platforms. These “smart” nanosystems can modulate drug release in response to physiological triggers such as pH, temperature, enzymatic activity, oxidative stress, electrical stimulation, or inflammatory microenvironments associated with epileptogenic tissue. Stimuli-responsive NPs enable site-specific and controlled drug release, thereby improving therapeutic precision and minimizing off-target toxicity.185,186 Electro-responsive and oxidative stress-responsive NPs have shown particular promise for epilepsy applications because seizure-associated neuronal activity and oxidative stress can serve as endogenous triggers for drug release.187,188

Surface charge optimization additionally plays a crucial role in determining NPs interaction with the nasal mucosa and neuronal tissues. Positively charged NPs generally exhibit enhanced mucoadhesion and epithelial interaction because of electrostatic attraction with negatively charged mucosal surfaces. However, excessive cationic charge may induce mucosal irritation and cytotoxicity, necessitating careful optimization of NPs composition and surface properties.137,189 Similarly, particle size critically influences mucosal permeation, intracellular trafficking, and neuronal transport, with smaller NPs generally demonstrating improved brain penetration and intracellular uptake.190

Collectively, advanced functionalization strategies significantly enhance the therapeutic efficiency of intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine systems by improving nasal retention, epithelial permeation, neuronal uptake, and brain-targeting specificity. Continued advances in surface engineering, ligand conjugation, and stimuli-responsive nanotechnology are expected to accelerate further the clinical translation of intranasal nanomedicine for epilepsy therapy.

Challenges, Limitations, and Translational Barriers

Despite substantial advances in biodegradable nanomedicine for epilepsy therapy, several scientific, technological, and translational challenges continue to hinder successful clinical implementation. Although biodegradable NPs have demonstrated improved brain targeting, enhanced intracerebral drug accumulation, prolonged seizure suppression, and reduced systemic toxicity in preclinical studies, their translation from experimental systems to clinically approved therapeutics remains limited. Major barriers include physiological limitations associated with nose-to-brain delivery, NPs-related toxicity concerns, formulation instability, manufacturing complexity, regulatory uncertainty, and insufficient clinical validation. Addressing these challenges is essential for advancing biodegradable nanomedicine toward clinically effective epilepsy therapy.

Physiological and Biological Limitations

The nasal cavity presents several physiological barriers that restrict efficient nose-to-brain transport of biodegradable NPs. Rapid mucociliary clearance, continuous mucus turnover, enzymatic degradation, and limited residence time significantly reduce drug absorption and intracerebral delivery efficiency.191 In addition, the olfactory region responsible for direct neuronal transport occupies only a small fraction of the nasal cavity, thereby limiting effective drug deposition. Interindividual variability in nasal anatomy, mucosal thickness, pathological conditions, and administration techniques can further contribute to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes.192 Furthermore, BBB-associated efflux transporters, including P-gp, MRPs, and BCRP, may continue to restrict intracerebral drug accumulation even after NPs-mediated transport.193

Safety and Toxicological Concerns

Although biodegradable NPs are generally designed to degrade into biocompatible byproducts, long-term safety remains an important concern, particularly in chronic neurological disorders such as epilepsy requiring prolonged therapy. Repeated intranasal administration may induce local mucosal irritation, ciliotoxicity, epithelial damage, neuroinflammation, or neuronal toxicity depending on NPs composition, surface charge, and excipient selection.194,195 Positively charged NPs, while beneficial for mucoadhesion and cellular uptake, may exhibit increased cytotoxicity due to excessive interaction with epithelial membranes.196 Moreover, insufficient understanding of long-term biodistribution, NPs accumulation, immunogenicity, and metabolic fate remains a major limitation for clinical translation. Comprehensive toxicological evaluation and long-term safety assessment are therefore essential before regulatory approval.

Translational and Manufacturing Challenges

Despite promising preclinical outcomes, several translational barriers continue to limit the clinical applicability of biodegradable nanomedicine platforms for epilepsy therapy. One major challenge involves large-scale manufacturing reproducibility and Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant production. Many NPs systems demonstrating favorable experimental efficacy are prepared using complex laboratory-scale methods that may be difficult to scale industrially.197 Batch-to-batch variability in particle size, drug-loading efficiency, surface properties, and release behavior can significantly affect formulation stability, therapeutic consistency, and regulatory approval. Liposomal and PLHNPs systems often require multistep fabrication procedures, increasing manufacturing complexity and cost, whereas certain PNPs and SLNs may offer comparatively better scalability and physicochemical stability.198

Long-term formulation stability also remains a significant concern. Lipidic NPs may undergo lipid oxidation, polymorphic transitions, or drug leakage during storage, while polymeric NPs may exhibit aggregation or hydrolytic degradation. In addition, sterilization, lyophilization, and packaging procedures can alter NPs integrity and therapeutic performance.199 Therefore, development of standardized, scalable, and reproducible manufacturing strategies remains essential for successful clinical translation.

Clinical Predictability and Regulatory Considerations

Although intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine has demonstrated encouraging therapeutic potential in experimental epilepsy models, its clinical predictability remains uncertain. Rodent intranasal models may overestimate nose-to-brain transport efficiency because rodents possess proportionally larger olfactory regions compared with humans. Furthermore, interspecies differences in nasal anatomy, mucociliary clearance, and airflow dynamics can substantially influence NPs deposition and transport behavior, limiting direct extrapolation of preclinical findings to clinical settings.200

Nasal-device dependency additionally represents an important translational challenge, as drug deposition can vary depending on spray characteristics, formulation viscosity, administration technique, and patient-specific anatomical differences.201 Moreover, regulatory approval remains challenging because of the lack of standardized protocols for NPs characterization, biodistribution analysis, biodegradation assessment, and long-term safety evaluation. Consequently, future clinical translation will require standardized regulatory frameworks, predictive large-animal studies, scalable manufacturing approaches, and well-designed clinical trials to establish the safety and reproducibility of intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine for epilepsy therapy.202

Pharmacokinetic, Biodistribution, and Targeting Limitations

Although biodegradable NPs can enhance brain-targeted drug delivery, achieving precise and reproducible intracerebral biodistribution remains a major challenge. NPs transport across biological barriers is strongly influenced by physicochemical parameters, including particle size, surface charge, morphology, hydrophobicity, and biodegradation kinetics.203 Minor alterations in NPs composition may substantially affect nasal deposition, mucosal permeation, neuronal uptake, systemic distribution, and drug release profiles.204 Consequently, optimization of NPs physicochemical properties remains critical for achieving efficient and reproducible therapeutic outcomes.

Another major limitation involves an incomplete understanding of NPs biodistribution, intracellular trafficking, metabolic fate, and long-term clearance mechanisms within the CNS. Following intranasal administration, a significant fraction of NPs may undergo systemic absorption rather than direct neuronal transport, thereby reducing brain-targeting efficiency and increasing off-target exposure. Additionally, NPs aggregation, opsonization, and premature drug leakage may further compromise therapeutic precision.205 Limited penetration into deep brain regions and insufficient accumulation within epileptogenic foci also remain important barriers for effective seizure control.206 Advanced surface-functionalization strategies, ligand-mediated targeting, and stimuli-responsive nanosystems may partially overcome these limitations; however, further mechanistic investigations are necessary to fully optimize brain-specific NPs delivery.

Overall, although several biological, technological, and regulatory challenges remain unresolved, biodegradable nanomedicine platforms possess substantial potential to transform epilepsy therapy by improving brain targeting, enhancing therapeutic efficacy, and overcoming limitations associated with conventional antiseizure drug delivery. Furthermore, differences between experimental animal models and human epilepsy, together with the limited number of clinical studies, make it difficult to directly translate encouraging preclinical outcomes into clinical practice.

Future Perspectives and Emerging Trends

Biodegradable NPs-based drug delivery systems are expected to play a promising role in the future management of epilepsy by improving brain targeting, enhancing therapeutic efficacy, and reducing systemic toxicity. Although current ASMs primarily provide symptomatic seizure suppression, emerging nanomedicine strategies increasingly aim to modulate the underlying pathological mechanisms associated with epileptogenesis, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal degeneration. Continued advances in biomaterials engineering, precision nanomedicine, and intranasal delivery technologies are therefore anticipated to significantly expand the therapeutic potential of biodegradable NPs for epilepsy therapy.

Stimuli-Responsive and Smart Nanomedicine Systems

One of the most promising emerging trends involves the development of stimuli-responsive or “smart” biodegradable NPs capable of site-specific and controlled drug release. These advanced nanosystems can respond to endogenous triggers such as oxidative stress, inflammatory mediators, pH alterations, enzymatic activity, or abnormal electrical activity associated with seizure episodes. Such responsive behavior enables selective drug release within epileptogenic brain regions while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target toxicity.22 Smart nanocarriers may additionally improve therapeutic precision, prolong intracerebral drug retention, and reduce dosing frequency, thereby enhancing long-term epilepsy management.17

Surface-Functionalized and Targeted NPs

Surface engineering strategies are expected to further improve the brain-targeting efficiency of biodegradable NPs. Functionalization with targeting ligands such as transferrin, lactoferrin, apolipoproteins, antibodies, aptamers, and cell-penetrating peptides can facilitate receptor-mediated transport across biological barriers and enhance neuronal uptake.207 Similarly, mucoadhesive and permeation-enhancing surface modifications may improve nasal residence time and intranasal nose-to-brain transport efficiency. These actively targeted nanosystems may also help overcome efflux transporter-mediated pharmacoresistance by enhancing intracellular ASM accumulation within epileptogenic brain regions.182

Gene Therapy and RNA-Based Nanotherapeutics

Gene-based therapeutics represent another rapidly evolving direction in epilepsy nanomedicine. Biodegradable NPs are increasingly being explored for the delivery of siRNA, mRNA, antisense oligonucleotides, CRISPR/Cas systems, and microRNA modulators to epileptogenic brain tissue. These approaches may enable disease-modifying interventions targeting abnormal ion channel expression, neuroinflammatory signaling, synaptic dysfunction, and epileptogenic gene networks. Importantly, NPs-mediated delivery can protect nucleic acids from enzymatic degradation and improve intracellular transport, thereby enhancing therapeutic stability and efficacy.208,209

Multifunctional and Combination Nanomedicine Approaches

Future biodegradable nanomedicine systems are expected to increasingly incorporate multifunctional therapeutic strategies capable of simultaneously targeting multiple pathological pathways involved in epilepsy. Advanced nanosystems may co-deliver ASMs together with anti-inflammatory agents, antioxidants, neuroprotective compounds, neuromodulators, or gene therapeutics to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects. Such combination approaches may not only improve seizure suppression but also reduce neuronal injury, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and disease progression.210,211 Multifunctional NPs, therefore, hold considerable promise for shifting epilepsy treatment from symptomatic management toward disease-modifying therapy.

AI and Precision Nanomedicine

The integration of AI, machine learning, and computational modeling is expected to significantly accelerate the development of biodegradable NPs systems for epilepsy therapy. AI-assisted approaches may facilitate rational NPs design through predictive optimization of particle size, surface charge, drug-loading efficiency, biodegradation kinetics, and targeting specificity.212 In addition, precision nanomedicine approaches based on patient-specific genetic profiling, seizure phenotyping, and biomarker-guided therapy may support individualized treatment strategies and improve therapeutic outcomes.213

Theranostic and Biomimetic NPs

Emerging theranostic and biomimetic NPs also represent promising future directions in epilepsy therapy. Theranostic NPs combining therapeutic and diagnostic functions may enable simultaneous drug delivery, seizure monitoring, and real-time therapeutic assessment.214 Similarly, bioinspired NPs such as exosome-based carriers, cell membrane-coated NPs, and biomimetic NPs possess inherent biocompatibility, immune evasion capability, and enhanced BBB penetration properties.215 These advanced NPs systems may significantly improve intracerebral targeting and therapeutic precision while reducing systemic toxicity.

Overall, biodegradable NPs-based nanomedicine represents a promising frontier for next-generation epilepsy therapy. Although several biological, technological, and regulatory challenges remain unresolved, continued advances in nanotechnology, biomaterials engineering, neuroscience, and precision medicine are expected to accelerate the development of safer, more effective, and potentially disease-modifying therapies for the management of epilepsy.

Conclusion

Epilepsy remains a major neurological disorder requiring safer and more effective therapeutic strategies because conventional ASMs are frequently limited by pharmacoresistance, systemic toxicity, poor brain targeting, and inadequate disease modification. Biological barriers, including the BBB, efflux transporter-mediated drug extrusion, neuroinflammation, and restricted intracerebral drug distribution, continue to compromise therapeutic efficacy and long-term seizure management. In this context, intranasal drug delivery has emerged as a promising non-invasive approach to bypass BBB-associated transport limitations and enhance CNS drug delivery via the olfactory and trigeminal pathways. Biodegradable nanomedicine platforms offer significant advantages for intranasal epilepsy therapy owing to their favorable biocompatibility, controlled-release behavior, enhanced mucosal interactions, and capacity for targeted intracerebral drug delivery. Furthermore, multifunctional nanosystems may facilitate delivery of anticonvulsants, neuroprotective agents, antioxidants, peptides, and gene therapeutics while potentially overcoming pharmacoresistance and reducing systemic adverse effects. Although encouraging preclinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine, robust clinical evidence remains limited, and its routine clinical application has yet to be established. Despite encouraging preclinical findings, important translational challenges involving manufacturing scalability, formulation reproducibility, long-term safety, regulatory standardization, and clinical predictability must still be addressed. Future progress will depend on well-designed clinical trials, standardized manufacturing protocols, comprehensive long-term safety evaluation, and harmonized regulatory frameworks to establish the safety, efficacy, and reproducibility of these nanomedicine platforms. Addressing these evidence gaps and translational barriers will be essential for realizing the full clinical potential of intranasal biodegradable nanomedicine in epilepsy management.
