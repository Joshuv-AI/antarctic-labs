First record of the forensically important genus Protopiophila Duda (Diptera: Piophilidae) from South Korea and an integrative framework for forensic identification
Authors: Sangjin Han, Taemin Kang, Seung‐Gyu Lee, Seunggwan Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe family Piophilidae is of significant forensic importance due to its association with late‐stage decomposition; however, the Korean piophilid fauna remains poorly studied, lacking comprehensive morphological and molecular data. This study reports the first record of the genus Protopiophila Duda from South Korea. Five male specimens were collected and identified as Protopiophila contecta (Walker) based on morphological characters. We provide the first detailed illustrations and descriptions of the previously poorly documented male internal genitalia and a new identification key to the South Korean piophilids. To complement the morphological data, mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) barcodes were generated and analyzed. The neighbor‐joining tree showed that the four Korean specimens formed a distinct, well‐supported clade, clearly separated from the congeners P. latipes and P. litigata. This study establishes an integrative framework for identifying P. contecta, adding a fourth forensically relevant piophilid species to the Korean entomofauna. These morphological and molecular data provide a crucial reference for future forensic investigations in the region.Keywords: carrion flies, COI, DNA barcoding, forensic entomology, Piophilidae, Protopiophila contecta, South Korea

INTRODUCTION

Piophilidae (Diptera: Tephritoidea), commonly known as “skipper flies” because of the characteristic jumping behavior of third‐instar larvae [1], is a small acalyptrate family of yellow to shiny black flies with a little over 100 described species in about 24 genera worldwide [2, 3]. Most species are saprophagous; adults occur on various kinds of decomposing organic matter where they feed and oviposit [4, 5]. Several species are closely associated with vertebrate carrion and are therefore regarded as forensically important indicators [6], together with the well‐studied groups, such as blow flies (Calliphoridae) and flesh flies (Sarcophagidae). Unlike these typical carrion flies, however, piophilids tend to colonize carcasses later: adults are usually collected during the bloated and decay stages of decomposition [7, 8, 9], whereas larvae are most abundant in the advanced [10] and dry/remains stages [11, 12]. This distinct temporal pattern makes them potentially valuable complementary indicators in medico‐legal investigations [13].

Despite this potential, piophilids remain poorly studied in South Korea. The few available records are derived from forensic casework, in which larvae recovered from human remains were identified using COI barcoding because no specialist and no regional keys were available for reliable larval identification. These studies revealed only three species of Piophilidae from Korean forensic material—Piophila casei (Linnaeus, 1758), Parapiophila vulgaris (Fallén, 1820), and Stearibia nigriceps [14] [15, 16]. However, their primary aims were to document forensic cases and to expand the barcode reference library, rather than to provide detailed taxonomic treatments of Korean material, and they therefore added little morphological information for either larvae or adults. Consequently, there is still no comprehensive morphological account of the Korean piophilid fauna, and both routine and forensic identifications continue to rely heavily on molecular data. Establishing an integrative morphological and molecular framework for the group is therefore a prerequisite for the wider and more confident use of piophilids as forensic indicators in this region.

Protopiophila Duda, 1924, originally erected as a subgenus of Piophila Fallén, 1810, is now treated as a distinct genus characterized by scutum with four dorsocentral setae and two postpronotal setae [17, 18]. The genus currently comprises about 13 described species, occurring in all biogeographical regions [3]. Species of Protopiophila exploit a range of substrates from vertebrate carrion to bone‐related resources [19, 20]. For example, the Nearctic P. litigata Bonduriansky, 1995 is a highly specialized “antler fly”, whose larvae develop in shed cervid antlers [5, 19], whereas congeners such as P. latipes [14] and P. contecta [21] are typically associated with small vertebrate carcasses and have repeatedly been recorded from carrion, including human remains, indicating clear forensic relevance [19, 22]. Nevertheless, detailed morphological information—especially on the male internal genitalia of P. contecta—remains fragmentary, and molecular data for the genus are extremely sparse: to date, publicly accessible COI sequences are available only for P. latipes and P. litigata, leaving P. contecta without any DNA barcode reference.

In this study, we report the first record of Protopiophila from South Korea and add one more forensically important species, Protopiophila contecta, to the Korean forensic entomofauna. We provide the morphological description of this species from Korea, including photographs and illustrations of the adult habitus, and the first detailed documentation of the male internal genitalia. Because of the lack of regional expertise and the recognized difficulty of identifying piophilid larvae, we also generated a COI barcode for the Korean specimens. To our knowledge, this represents the first COI sequence for P. contecta and thus extends the very limited molecular coverage of Protopiophila. These data will provide a crucial reference for accurate future molecular identifications of P. contecta from forensic material in South Korea and elsewhere. In addition, we present a key to the South Korean piophilids, thereby establishing a combined morphological and molecular foundation for the routine use of piophilids as forensic indicators in the region. The key and barcode reference may support medicolegal investigations involving late‐stage decomposition.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection and examination

Adult specimens were collected in South Korea during surveys of pollinator diversity by sweeping flowers, during an insect‐diversity survey in a post‐wildfire restoration area using Malaise traps, and by sweeping over a rotting heap of food waste. Collected specimens were preserved in 99% ethanol and stored at −20°C. Specimens examined in this study are deposited in the following institutions: Seoul National University Laboratory of Evolution and Phylogenomics (SNUE), Seoul, Republic of Korea, National Institute of Biological Resources (NIBR), Incheon, Republic of Korea. In SNUE, the number of deposited materials is represented as SNUExxxxx, where ‘x’ represents a numerical digit.

Adults were first identified to Piophilidae and then to Protopiophila using available higher‐level keys and diagnoses [17, 18, 20, 23]. Species‐level identification as P. contecta was based on comparison with published diagnoses and redescriptions, including external characters and male terminalia [18, 20] and was subsequently corroborated by COI barcoding.

Samples were prepared for photography by transferring specimens from 99% ethanol into microporous specimen capsules. Capsules were subsequently immersed in 98% of hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) to slowly remove ethanol from specimens [24, 25]. Specimens were then pinned, and habitus images were taken with a Canon EOS 90D camera with an MP‐E 65 mm F2.8 1‐5x Macro Photo lens (Canon, Tokyo, Japan) attached to StackShot 3X macro rail (Cognisys, Traverse City, MI, USA). Photos were taken in a Fotodiox Pro LED Studio‐in‐a‐box (Fotodiox, Inc., USA) with a StackShot 3X controller.

Morphological terminology follows Cumming & Wood [26]. Genital dissections were conducted by gently pressing the very front part of the abdomen with a dissection needle to separate it from the thorax. Male abdomens were placed in 500 μL of 10% KOH in 2 mL microtubes and heated at 60°C for 3 h to soften and clear the tissues. The material was then transferred to 500 μL of 8% acetic acid for a few seconds, rinsed in distilled water, rinsed again in absolute ethanol, and finally placed in glycerin for dissection. The epandrium was first removed with two dissection needles, then sternites 6–8 were separated from the genitalia. All dissected male genital structures were kept in genitalia micro vials. Male genital structures were photographed with a Tucsen Dhyana 400 DC sCMOS camera (Tucsen, Fuzhou, Fujian, China) on a Leica M205 FCA stereoscopic microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) in glycerol. Focus stacking for habitus and genitalia structures was performed with Helicon Focus version 7.6.1. Genitalia were illustrated in tandem by Clip Studio Paint Pro ver. 3.0.4 (Celsys Inc., Tokyo, Japan) to clarify minute structures together with labelling. Dissection tools included two dissection needles made by combining a 0.20 mm fine insect pin at the tip of a wooden chopstick.

DNA barcoding

Genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit (Cat. #69504; QIAGEN, Venlo, The Netherlands). A partial 658‐base pair region of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene was amplified with the primers [LCO1490 5′‐GGT CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G‐3′ (forward)] and HCO2198 [5′‐TAA ACT TCA GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA‐3′ (reverse)] [27]. PCR amplification of this region was performed using AccuPower® PCR Premix (BIONEER Corporation, Daejeon, Korea) in a total volume of 20 μL: 0.5 μM each primer, 1.0 ng of genomic DNA from four specimens, and Ultrapure™ Distilled Water (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 95°C for 5 min for denaturation, 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, 48°C for 40 s, 72°C for 40 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Amplicons were visualized on 1.5% (wt/vol) agarose gels stained with SYBR™ Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA), then purified and sequenced in both directions by Bionics (Seoul, Korea). Raw sequences were trimmed and assembled by Geneious Prime 2024.0.4 (https://www.geneious.com). Obtained sequences were deposited in GenBank (Table 1).

Bionomics

Adults are typically associated with decomposing protein‐rich substrates such as rotten meat [20], and larvae have been collected from the bone‐marrow cavities of human corpses in Japan, highlighting the forensic importance of this species [22]. In the present study, a single male was collected by sweeping inflorescences of Castanea crenata during a survey of pollinating insect diversity. Although flower visitation by piophilids is rarely documented, there are historical records of such behavior, such as Piophila australis reported from flowers of Cotoneaster in Sydney gardens [37]. At first, we hypothesized that P. contecta might similarly act as a rarely recorded flower visitor, much like some calliphorids [38, 39, 40]. However, in the present case, a heap of food waste and garbage was present directly beneath the tree, which had already attracted numerous saprophagous flies, including Hermetia illucens (Linnaeus, 1758). Therefore, we interpret this specific record as an incidental by‐catch rather than definitive evidence of regular flower visitation or pollination. Another single male was collected by Malaise trap during an insect‐diversity survey in a forest restoration area recovering from wildfire. Lastly, three males were collected by sweeping over a large, rotting heap of mixed food waste. This waste pile, located at a forest edge near a commercial restaurant area in Mt. Odaesan, consisted primarily of discarded vegetable and fruit matter. However, it had accumulated over an extended period, emitting a highly pungent, foul odor characteristic of advanced decomposition and attracting other saprophagous and coprophagous Diptera, including Sepsidae spp., Prosthiochaeta bifasciata, and Hermetia illucens. While P. contecta is a known necrophagous species recovered from human remains [22], its presence on long‐standing food waste indicates that this species is also attracted to environments simulating advanced decay or complex decomposing organic matter. From a forensic perspective, this observation may be relevant to scene interpretation. It suggests that nearby mixed food waste may influence the local abundance or activity of P. contecta and should therefore be considered during scene interpretation.

Remarks

The terminology applied to male genital structures in Piophilidae has long been inconsistent, particularly with respect to the pregonite and postgonite. Different authors have used these terms in conflicting ways or have not labeled the structures, which has led to confusion [3, 18, 41, 42, 43, 44]. For the sake of clarity, we here treat the laterally projecting processes of the hypandrium as a distinct structure and refer to them as the “hypandrial lobes”.

P. contecta can be readily distinguished from its forensically important Holarctic congener, P. latipes, by the dorsally pruinose anepisternum and entirely yellow legs (Figure 1C), whereas P. latipes has a completely glossy anepisternum and a hind femur blackish apically.

This species was reported in a list of dipteran species from Sǎo Miguel Island in the Azores Archipelago of Portugal in the Atlantic Ocean [45]. However, this record is omitted in a later publication that contains a new list of arthropods of the Azores Archipelago and a study of piophilids in Portugal, making this record dubious [46, 47]. If that is the case, a South Korean specimen is the northernmost record of this species.

This study reports the first South Korean record of the genus Protopiophila, represented by Protopiophila contecta, a species regarded as forensically important within Piophilidae. The five specimens were assignable to P. contecta using the combination of external diagnostic characters provided in previous taxonomic treatments [18, 20], and we here document these characters in detail for Korean material. With this addition, four species in four genera of Piophilidae are now known from South Korea. All four piophilid species currently recorded from South Korea have been documented from carrion or medicolegal contexts and are thus of potential forensic relevance. However, their practical evidential value remains constrained by the limited availability of developmental and regional baseline data. Future work should prioritize controlled developmental studies and the description of immature stages of P. contecta so that this species can be more reliably incorporated into practical time of colonization (TOC) or minimum postmortem interval (PMImin) estimation in Korea.

Adult piophilids are, in general, relatively easy to identify to species using external characters alone, without the need to dissect the small and complex male genitalia [48]. Consistent with this, P. contecta can readily be identified by its distinctive combination of dense dorsal pruinosity on the anepisternum, entirely yellow hind femur, and the presence of two strong setae on the dorsoapical surface of the epandrium. Nevertheless, we provide the first detailed documentation of the internal male genitalia of P. contecta to facilitate future morphological work and to improve comparability in the interpretation of these characters. Because male internal genitalia may provide important diagnostic characters for species‐level differentiation in Piophilidae, clarifying and standardizing the terminology of these structures improves reproducibility and reduces ambiguity in future identifications. Although this is primarily a taxonomic contribution, it also supports forensic practice by facilitating more consistent species determinations and by providing a stable framework to which future morphological and molecular datasets can be linked.

Although DNA barcoding is increasingly used in forensic entomology, it depends on the availability of comprehensive reference libraries and requires laboratory infrastructure, time, and financial resources. Morphology‐based identification therefore remains an essential component of forensic entomology, particularly where molecular identification is impractical or where reliable barcode references are unavailable. In South Korea, forensic investigations have so far relied heavily on COI barcoding for the identification of unknown larvae collected from human remains, including all previous records of Piophilidae [15, 16]. In those cases, larvae were assigned to species solely on the basis of sequence similarity to reference barcodes, without accompanying morphological descriptions or taxonomic treatment. Such an approach is only possible when reliable reference sequences already exist, and it leaves identifications vulnerable when novel or poorly documented taxa are encountered. It is also time‐consuming and costly, and it does not by itself provide information on larval morphology, which is crucial for routine casework.

By contrast, in many other countries the main forensically important species are supported by an extensive body of data, including detailed descriptions of adult, larval and pupal morphology as well as rich molecular reference libraries [49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54]. Korean forensic entomology is still in its infancy in this respect, and every additional species‐level record accompanied by solid morphological and molecular information significantly strengthens the national framework. P. contecta is currently known only from the Oriental [21] and Australasian regions [40, 55], the Ryukyu Islands and Honshu in Japan [20], and now South Korea, and no larval or pupal stages have been described. Documenting the immature morphology of such regionally important, yet neglected, species will be essential for providing critical information to future forensic investigations and for reducing the current over‐reliance on molecular data alone.

To complement the morphological data, we generated the first publicly available COI sequences for P. contecta and analyzed it together with publicly available sequences of the congeners P. latipes and P. litigata, using Liopiophila varipes as an outgroup in a neighbor‐joining (NJ) tree. As expected, P. latipes and P. litigata each formed well‐defined conspecific clusters (Figure 3). In contrast, the four sequences of P. contecta obtained in this study formed a distinct, well‐supported clade (Figure 3), clearly separated from the congeners P. latipes and P. litigata. This clustering confirms the morphological identification of the Korean specimens and shows the utility of COI barcoding for separating P. contecta from other congeners. These data provide a robust reference point for future molecular identifications of P. contecta from forensic material in South Korea and elsewhere.
