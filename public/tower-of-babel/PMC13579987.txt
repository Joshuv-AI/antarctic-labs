Association between hypertension and sepsis-related complications: a retrospective nationwide inpatient sample database cross-sectional study
Authors: Jing Wang, Yinglan Huang, Zhifen Xie, Jianping Yu, Lele Xiao, Xiaolan Liu, Zifeng Wang, Jianlong Zhu, Meirong Shen, Li Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis remains a significant global health challenge, with high morbidity and mortality rates. The interplay between hypertension, a prevalent comorbidity, and sepsis outcomes has been the subject of ongoing debate. This study aimed to investigate the incidence of sepsis-related complications in individuals with and without hypertension via a large nationwide inpatient database.MethodsA retrospective observational analysis was conducted via the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database from 2010 to 2019. Background

Sepsis, characterized by life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection, continues to pose a significant global health challenge [1]. Despite advancements in critical care medicine, sepsis-associated morbidity and mortality rates remain alarmingly high, necessitating further investigation into risk factors, prognostic indicators, and improved management approaches [2]. Recent data indicate that sepsis affects an estimated 20–30 million individuals worldwide annually [3–5], with mortality rates equating to one death every 3–4 s [6]. The impact of sepsis is particularly severe in regions with high infectious disease prevalence and in populations with compromised immunity or underlying chronic conditions [7].

Hypertension has emerged as a prominent comorbidity and global health concern [8]. Hypertension affects approximately 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years globally and contributes to 9.4 million annual deaths [9], and its relationship with cardiovascular events such as strokes, heart attacks, and heart failure is well documented. It compromises vascular health through direct endothelial cell damage and indirectly by triggering inflammatory responses and promoting atherosclerosis [10]. Moreover, hypertension may disrupt immune system function and balance [11]. In sepsis pathogenesis, acute vascular endothelial dysfunction plays a pivotal role in enhancing vascular permeability, activating coagulation cascades, promoting tissue edema, and impairing vital organ perfusion [12–15]. A comprehensive epidemiological study revealed an association between preexisting vascular disease and sepsis risk, suggesting that endothelial dysfunction is a potential physiological link [16].

The interplay between hypertension and sepsis outcomes remains controversial. While some studies have indicated increased mortality and complications in hypertensive sepsis patients, other studies have reported no significant correlation or even suggested a protective effect [17–19]. Currently, comprehensive large-scale national database analyses examining the relationship between hypertension and sepsis outcomes are lacking. This study aimed to investigate the incidence of sepsis-related complications in individuals with and without hypertension via a large, publicly accessible national inpatient database in the United States. By exploring these associations and enhancing our understanding of the hypertension‒sepsis relationship, we aspire to contribute valuable insights that will inform clinical practice and guide the management of sepsis patients with preexisting hypertension.

Methods

Data source

This study leveraged the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database, a component of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project overseen by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). As the most comprehensive all-payer inpatient database in the United States since 1998, the NIS annually captures a stratified sample representing approximately 20% of inpatient admissions from over 1,000 hospitals nationwide. This rich dataset provides researchers with crucial insights into hospitalization patterns, resource utilization, and healthcare trends.As this research employed de-identified public data, it was deemed exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patient population

We conducted a retrospective observational analysis using NIS data spanning from 2010 to 2019. The study population comprised adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with sepsis in U.S. hospitals identified through the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10-CM) codes(Additional file2: Table S1). According to established statistical principles and the literature [20], when the proportion of missingness is below 5% and the missingness mechanism can be reasonably assumed to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), the bias introduced by complete case analysis (listwise deletion) is negligible and does not significantly impair statistical power. Consequently, we opted for a direct exclusion approach for these patients (n = 106,828;2.60%),whose essential baseline demographic or hospital-level variables were incomplete (Additional file 1:Figure S1). The final cohort was stratified into two groups: patients with hypertension (n = 2,409,370) and those without hypertension (n = 1,528,964). Hypertension was identified using ICD-9-CM codes (401.x, 405.x) and ICD-10-CM codes (I10, I15) [21].

Outcome assessment

We analyzed the hypertension cohort’s characteristics based on demographics (age, sex, race, income, insurance type), hospital attributes (admission type, bed size, teaching status, location, region), renal replacement therapy (RRT), and comorbidities. The selection of comorbidities was informed by their previously established significance as risk factors for adverse events following sepsis [22]. The primary outcomes included sepsis-related complications: pulmonary embolism, septic shock, deep vein thrombosis, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, heart failure, acute respiratory failure (ARF), acute kidney injury (AKI), arrhythmia, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. ARF was identified using validated ICD-9-CM codes (518.81, 518.82, 518.85) and ICD-10-CM codes (J96.00-J96.02, J96.20-J96.22, J96.90-J96.92, J95.821, J95.822, J80) [23–25]; AKI was defined by ICD-9-CM code 584.x and ICD-10-CM code N17 [26, 27]. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, length of stay (LOS), and total hospitalization costs.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), with visualizations created in GraphPad PRISM version 5.3 (San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. We used the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous comparisons and the chi-square test for categorical variables.To assess the independent association between hypertension and each sepsis-related complication, we constructed separate binary logistic regression models using a forced-entry (enter) method, including all covariates listed in Table 1a priori based on clinical knowledge and literature [22, 28–30]. The full covariate set comprised patient demographics (age group, sex, race, income, insurance type), hospital characteristics (admission type, bed size, teaching status, location, region, RRT), and a composite indicator of total comorbidities (0, 1, 2, ≥ 3).(Additional file 3 : Table S2).

Importantly, as this is a retrospective observational study using administrative claims data, all reported associations are purely statistical and cannot be interpreted as causal relationships. Unmeasured confounding (e.g., severity of illness at sepsis onset, laboratory values, medication adherence) and residual bias may persist despite multivariable adjustment. Therefore, no inference of a “protective” or “harmful” effect is supported by our study design. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

Prevalence of hypertension among sepsis patients

From 2010 to 2019, 3,938,334 sepsis cases were identified in the NIS database. Compared with that in the previous year, the incidence of hypertension continuously increased (from 53.17% to 64.62%) but barely decreased in 2019. The overall ten-year incidence of hypertension in patients with sepsis was 61.18%. (Fig. 1)

Patient demographics and comorbidity characteristics

The two groups of patients showed statistically significant differences in demographic data and hospital-related characteristics, as shown in Fig. 2. Patients with hypertension were more likely to be male, elderly, and White and had a lower likelihood of being hospitalized in a small bed. Patients in the hypertension group had a median age of 69.51 years (IQR 60–81 years), which was significantly greater than the median age of 58.64 years (IQR 44–74 years) in the non-hypertensive group (P < 0.001). The age distribution revealed that 41.12% of patients in the hypertension group were aged 75 years or older, which was significantly greater than the 24.28% reported in the non-hypertension group (P < 0.001). In terms of sex distribution, there was a significant difference between the groups, with males comprising 50.24% of the hypertension group and 49.9% of the non-hypertension group (P < 0.001). Similarly, the racial distribution did show disparities; the hypertension group had a greater proportion of black patients (14.95%) than did the non-hypertensive group (11.42%), whereas the percentages of Hispanic and other racial groups were lower (P < 0.001). Furthermore, patients in the hypertension group had a significantly greater prevalence of multiple comorbidities than did those without hypertension (P < 0.001). In fact, 73.38% of patients in the hypertension group had three or more comorbidities, contrasting sharply with the 56.33% observed in the non-hypertension group. Significant differences in RRT utilization were observed between the two groups. The proportion of patients receiving RRT was substantially higher in the hypertensive group (12.10%) compared to merely 4.24% in the non-hypertensive group, a difference achieving high statistical significance (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

The incidence of comorbidities among sepsis patients is presented in Fig. 3. Fluid and electrolyte disorders were highly prevalent, affecting 56.54% of the patients, followed by metastatic cancer (27.32%) and chronic pulmonary disease (23.51%). The incidence rates of lymphoma and uncomplicated diabetes were 19.29% and 18.00%, respectively. Other common comorbidities included congestive heart failure (17.79%), deficiency anemia (15.95%), peripheral vascular disorders (15.59%), and liver disease (13.98%). The incidence of comorbidities in almost 60% of hypertension patients is significantly greater than that in nonhypertensive patients. Notably, certain comorbidities, such as congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, and renal failure, were more prevalent among hypertensive patients. Congestive heart failure affected 29.54% of patients in the hypertension group, which was significantly greater than the 14.01% reported in the non-hypertensive group. The complications and outcomes in patients with sepsis

The incidence rates of complications related to sepsis are presented in Fig. 4. Notably, acute respiratory failure emerged as the most prevalent complication, with an occurrence rate of 23.80%, followed closely by heart failure at 22.31%. Arrhythmia was the third most common complication, with a rate of 10.21%. Other complications included gastrointestinal hemorrhage (2.96%), disseminated intravascular coagulation (1.81%), acute kidney injury(1.19%), deep vein thrombosis (1.18%), pulmonary embolism (0.91%), and ARDS (0.66%). Sepsis shock and gangrenous complications were the least common, with rates of 0.57% and 0.16%, respectively. Patients with hypertension experienced higher incidences of certain complications than did those without hypertension. Specifically, heart failure occurred more frequently among hypertensive patients (29.62% vs. 14.62%; OR = 2.46, 95% CI: 2.45–2.47, P < 0.001), as did acute respiratory failure (10.75% vs. 9.36%, P < 0.001) and arrhythmia (1.0% vs. 0.8%, P < 0.001). In contrast, the incidences of septic shock and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) were lower in the hypertension group, indicating an observed inverse association (which should not be interpreted as causal) between preexisting hypertension and these specific severe complications (Table 4). Despite these increased risks for some complications, the median length of stay (LOS) for patients in the hypertension group was slightly shorter, averaging 8.65 days (IQR 3–10 days) compared with 9.28 days (IQR 3–11 days) in the nonhypertension group (P < 0.001). Additionally, the median total hospital charge for hypertensive patients was significantly lower at $92,201.60 (IQR $25,228.00–$99,876.00) than at $102,218.26 (IQR $22,886.00–$101,802.00) for those without hypertension (P < 0.001). Patients with hypertension were less likely to use Medicare and self-pay but were more likely to pay through private insurance. Interestingly, the in-hospital mortality rate was also lower for hypertensive patients, at 11.93%, than for nonhypertensive patients, at 13.33% (P < 0.001). These findings suggest that, among sepsis patients, hypertension may be associated with a reduced in-hospital mortality rate.

Discussion

This study undertook a comprehensive health economic analysis of sepsis patients. To our knowledge, this is the first objective examination of the effects of hypertension on sepsis patients from 2010 to 2019. Our findings revealed an associational increase in the prevalence of hypertension among sepsis patients from 53.17% in 2010 to 64.62% in 2019 (Fig. 1), highlighting a complex interplay that should be interpreted as correlational, not causal. Several factors may explain this consistent increase in incidence rates during the study period. The growth of the elderly population has contributed to this trend. According to a report by the Population Reference Bureau, the percentage of individuals aged 65 and older in the United States reached 16.9% in 2020, a 34% increase since 2010 [31].

In our study, we observed that patients in the hypertension cohort were significantly older than those in the nonhypertensive cohort were. Notably, the proportion of elderly patients aged 75 years and above was considerably greater in the hypertension group than in the control group, indicating that older sepsis patients are more prone to hypertension. This age-related disparity aligns with established correlations between age and hypertension incidence. Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) illustrate a clear trend in hypertension incidence as age increases: it is 22.4% among individuals aged 18–39 years, 54.5% for those aged 40–59 years, and 74.5% for individuals aged 60 years and older [32].

Moreover, patients in the hypertension group presented a significantly greater prevalence of various comorbidities than did those without hypertension did, corroborating findings from previous studies. Aging and chronic conditions—such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, and cancer—are acknowledged risk factors for sepsis. Additionally, the likelihood of sepsis risk escalates with the accumulation of chronic medical conditions [16].

The overall in-hospital mortality rate for sepsis patients is 12.49%. Within this cohort, the mortality rates were 11.93% for patients with hypertension and 11.33% for those without hypertension, which is consistent with findings from earlier studies [33]. Interestingly, within the hypertensive group, the incidence rates of pulmonary edema, shock, deep venous thrombosis (DVT), gastrointestinal bleeding, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) were lower than those in the non-hypertensive group. Conversely, rates of heart failure, renal insufficiency, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and arrhythmias were elevated in the hypertensive group. These observations could be speculated to be associated with several underlying mechanisms, but given the observational nature of our data, such speculation requires confirmation in future mechanistic studies, such as the effects of preadmission anti-hypertensive therapy, immune modulation, and alterations in vascular reactivity.

Our findings revealed that patients in the hypertension group presented a greater incidence of arrhythmias and heart failure. This may be due to several factors. On the one hand, the increased cardiac pressure load associated with hypertension may contribute to this increase. Chronic pressure overload on the heart can result in structural adaptations, such as myocardial hypertrophy and ventricular remodeling, which increase the risk of arrhythmias and heart failure [34]. As noted by Suvila and Niiranen (2022), this pressure burden becomes even more pronounced in hypertensive patients during sepsis due to heightened physiological demands, thereby further increasing cardiovascular complication risks [35]. On the other hand, dysregulated neurohormonal mechanisms play a significant role. Sepsis activates both the sympathetic nervous system and the renin‒angiotensin‒aldosterone system (RAAS), which are already disrupted in individuals with hypertension [36, 37]. Angriman et al. (2023) reported that hypertensive individuals may experience an exaggerated response, increasing the likelihood of arrhythmias and heart failure during sepsis [38]. Additionally, patients in the hypertension group presented a greater likelihood of AKI, potentially linked to the pharmacological effects of antihypertensive medications. Research has shown that the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) is associated with lower short-term mortality, albeit with a greater risk of acute kidney injury (AKI). This apparent contradiction can be explained by the vasodilatory effects of these medications on both the renal afferent and efferent arterioles [39].

Septic patients are frequently affected by coagulation disorders, placing them at elevated risk for thrombotic complications, which can manifest as widespread microvascular involvement, including disseminated intravascular coagulation and venous thromboembolism (DVT or pulmonary embolism). The coagulation cascade is activated during sepsis by innate immune cells such as neutrophils and monocytes, leading to clot formation primarily in the microcirculation, a process termed immunothrombosis [40]. Key players in this process include endothelial cells, platelets, and neutrophils, which interact with tissue factors and the coagulation cascade to play critical roles in host defense and thrombosis formation [41]. Chronic arterial hypertension is also associated with endothelial cell damage and dysfunction, which supports the hypothesis that it may increase the risk of sepsis through similar mechanisms. However, our study suggests a divergent conclusion. Notably, the incidences of septic shock, ARDS, DIC, and DVT were lower in the hypertension group than in the control group, suggesting that hypertension may confer a protective effect against these severe sepsis complications. This paradoxical finding could stem from the effects of chronic antihypertensive therapy.

Recent evidence has shed light on the multifaceted potential associations of antihypertensive therapies in reducing sepsis-related complications. A pioneering study by Mortensen et al. (2012) suggested that ACE inhibitors and ARBs might be associated with reduced sepsis-related adverse events, attributing these associations to their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and endothelial function-enhancing properties [42, 43]. This proposed mechanism may offer a hypothesis to explain the reduced incidence of certain adverse outcomes in hypertensive patients, as supported by subsequent research. Several studies have reinforced this view, indicating that prior use of ACEIs or ARBs among hypertensive individuals correlates with lower rates of severe complications in sepsis [44, 45]. Similarly, research by Li et al. (2018) emphasized the potential of ACEIs and ARBs to modulate immune responses and mitigate inflammation, thus being associated with better sepsis outcomes [46, 47]. Moreover, angiotensin II is recognized for its potent proinflammatory properties, which promote tissue growth and remodeling. Numerous studies have demonstrated that inhibiting angiotensin II substantially decreases the levels of proinflammatory mediators and oxidative stress markers across various inflammatory models. The application of ACE inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor blockers to disrupt angiotensin II activity has shown potential associations with improved outcomes for patients with inflammatory conditions [48–50].

While direct investigations into the impact of hypertension on immune modulation remain scarce, emerging studies suggest intricate interactions between hypertension and the immune system. For example, research by WG et al. (2020) suggested that T cells and their cytokines play crucial roles in experimental hypertension, with stimuli such as angiotensin II promoting the infiltration of T cells and macrophages in the renal and vascular regions. Cytokines such as interleukin-17 and tumor necrosis factor-α are known to drive renal and vascular dysfunction, leading to sodium retention and heightened vascular resistance. Oxidative stress in dendritic cells generates reactive oxygen species that activate T cells, exacerbating hypertension. Targeting immune activation could mitigate end-organ damage and complications associated with hypertension [51]. Regarding hemodynamic stability, the Beta-blockers-in-sepsis trial (2017) underscored the efficacy of β-blockers in reducing episodes of hypotension and shock during sepsis [52]. This aligns with more recent findings by Smith et al. (2022), which indicated that β-blockers, alongside other antihypertensive agents that enhance cardiovascular function, might play critical roles in sustaining hemodynamic stability in septic patients [53, 54].

Studies have shown that the release of endogenous catecholamines during sepsis may stimulate glycolysis through β-adrenergic activation, resulting in increased lactate production [55]. Lactate serves as a key indicator reflecting tissue hypoperfusion and is closely associated with sepsis prognosis; early blood lactate levels and their trends are critical [56]. As early as 2012, the “Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC)” guidelines incorporated lactate as a quantitative measure of tissue perfusion in sepsis management [57]. In 2016, major critical care organizations in Europe and the U.S. released Sepsis 3.0, which included blood lactate in the diagnostic criteria for septic shock [1]. Recent investigations have confirmed the involvement of lactate in regulating both innate and acquired immune responses, along with its posttranscriptional modification functions, which directly influence sepsis development [58–60]. By blocking β-adrenergic receptors, β-blockers may inhibit this process, thereby reducing the rates of glycolysis and lactate generation [61].

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.First, due to the retrospective nature of the NIS database, we can only establish observational associations, not causal relationships.Second, a major limitation is the lack of in-hospital pharmacological data. We were unable to determine which specific antihypertensive medications patients were receiving, nor the timing, dosage, or adherence. Consequently, any speculation about the role of ACEIs, ARBs, or β-blockers remains hypothetical.Third, residual confounding is likely present. Although we adjusted for patient demographics, hospital characteristics, and a composite comorbidity index, we could not account for severity of illness at sepsis onset (e.g., SOFA score, qSOFA, lactate levels, vasopressor use), nor for physiological parameters (blood pressure, heart rate) or laboratory values (creatinine, bilirubin). Patients with hypertension might have been diagnosed earlier or managed differently, introducing detection or lead-time bias.Fourth, the use of ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes carries inherent limitations. Coding errors, misclassification, and variations in coding practices across hospitals are possible. The NIS database does not capture outpatient diagnoses or medications prior to admission, nor does it record lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, diet, physical activity) that could confound the hypertension-sepsis relationship.Fifth, the NIS lacks longitudinal follow-up post-discharge; therefore, we could not assess long-term outcomes such as rehospitalization or post-sepsis mortality.Finally, the observed inverse association between hypertension and certain complications (e.g., septic shock, DIC) might be influenced by survivor bias or by differential admission criteria, which we could not fully adjust for.

Conclusions

In conclusion, hypertension is associated with the prognosis of sepsis patients, influencing both the occurrence of complications and overall clinical outcomes. Our findings highlight the importance of individualized management approaches that consider the presence of hypertension and associated comorbidities. Further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying the observed associations between hypertension and sepsis outcomes, as current findings are associational and do not imply direct protective effects of hypertension itself and to develop targeted therapeutic strategies that optimize outcomes for this high-risk population.
