Extraosseous osteochondroma of the hypothenar region: a case report and literature review
Authors: Saied Besharaty, Mahmood Akhavan Tafti, Amir Pasha Amel Shahbaz, Milad Gholizadeh, Shahab Sheikhalishahi, Hesameddin Jafarinasab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteochondroma (OC) is the most common benign bone tumor, yet hand involvement is uncommon and extraosseous presentations are rarer still. The hypothenar compartment is an exceptionally unusual site, and lack of corticomedullary continuity can mimic soft-tissue and parosteal lesions. We report an extraosseous OC of the hypothenar region with adjacent ulnar artery thrombosis, provide a focused literature synthesis, and advance a microtrauma-related pathogenesis.Case presentationA 24-year-old Afghan male construction worker presented with a 10-year history of a slowly enlarging, firm, minimally painful mass in the left hypothenar region. Examination showed a bony-hard, immobile mass without neurovascular deficit. Radiographs and MRI demonstrated a 4.0 × 3.5 × 2.0 cm well-circumscribed ossified soft-tissue lesion with no corticomedullary continuity and no malignant features. En bloc excision was performed; intraoperatively a thrombosed ulnar artery was noted adjacent to the mass, with nerves preserved. Histopathology revealed a thin hyaline cartilage cap with abrupt endochondral ossification to mature trabecular bone containing marrow elements and no cytologic atypia, confirming extraosseous OC. Recovery was uneventful; at two months the wound was well-healed with full range of motion and no evidence of recurrence.ConclusionsThis case exemplifies a rare presentation of OC in the hypothenar compartment, extending the recognized anatomic spectrum of extraosseous OC and highlighting the potential for palmar soft tissues to harbor osteocartilaginous lesions without corticomedullary continuity. Clinicoradiologic and pathologic correlation was decisive, as an orderly hyaline cartilage cap with endochondral ossification to mature trabecular bone established osteochondroma and distinguished it from the principal ossified extraosseous mimics, particularly BPOP/Nora lesion, soft-tissue chondroma, myositis ossificans, and surface osteosarcoma. The coexisting ulnar artery thrombosis, in the setting of repetitive manual loading, supports a biologically plausible link between microtrauma, local vascular injury, metaplastic chondrogenesis, and endochondral ossification in soft tissue. The literature synthesis indicates excellent outcomes after complete excision with no recurrence across short to midterm follow up. Clinically, firm palmar masses without corticomedullary continuity should prompt consideration of OC, confirmation by histology when imaging is indeterminate, and treatment with complete excision including the cartilage cap.Keywords: Extraosseous osteochondroma, Case report, Hypothenar mass, Endochondral ossification

Introduction

Osteochondroma (OC) is the most common benign bone tumor, typically arising from the metaphyseal regions of long bones such as the femur and tibia during skeletal growth. Its occurrence in the hand is rare, accounting for less than 5% of all reported OCs, and solitary lesions in the metacarpals are exceptionally uncommon [1, 2]. Although often asymptomatic, OCs of the hand can be clinically significant because of the compact anatomy and high functional demands of this region. These lesions can lead to cosmetic deformity, pain, restricted range of motion, or neurovascular compression, depending on their size and location [3]. Palmar involvement, particularly in the hypothenar region, is exceedingly rare and poses a diagnostic challenge, not least because extraosseous presentations without obvious cortico-medullary continuity can mimic a range of soft-tissue and parosteal entities.

Here we describe an unusual case of a solitary, extraosseous OC presenting as a firm, painless mass in the hypothenar region of the left palm, without demonstrable cortico-medullary continuity to the adjacent metacarpal and without a pedunculated morphology, thereby deviating from the typical metaphyseal pattern [4]. The anatomically atypical, palmar location and extraskeletal configuration render preoperative diagnosis challenging and justify a focused appraisal of mimics encountered in this compartment. In this setting, the principal prepathology differential diagnosis for an ossified extraosseous palmar mass included bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP/Nora lesion), soft-tissue chondroma, myositis ossificans, and, less likely, surface osteosarcoma. More common hand masses and anatomic mimics were considered clinically but were less compatible with the radiographic and MRI appearance. This diagnostic context required careful integration of history, examination, radiographic findings, MRI features, and subsequent tissue diagnosis. Finally, given the patient’s long-standing construction work and repetitive hypothenar loading, we advance a concise mechanistic hypothesis linking micro-trauma to metaplastic chondrogenesis and subsequent endochondral ossification in this region.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old Afghan male presented to the outpatient clinic on March 2, 2025, with a complaint of a slowly enlarging mass located over the medial border of the left palm on the palmar side, corresponding anatomically to the hypothenar region. He reported long-standing occupational exposure to repetitive manual tasks (e.g., lifting, hammering, forceful gripping), implying chronic hypothenar loading and probable micro-trauma. The patient first noticed the mass approximately ten years ago as a small, painless nodule. Over the years, the lesion gradually increased in size. The swelling was associated with dull, aching, minimal pain that was typically aggravated by excessive or repetitive hand activities and relieved with rest. There was no history of night pain, fever, systemic symptoms, or significant functional limitations in his daily activities. The patient denied any history of trauma, prior injury, or overuse specific to the region. There were no associated symptoms such as numbness, paresthesia, muscle weakness, or vascular compromise. The hand maintained full range of motion and preserved strength and dexterity. The recent noticeable increase in the size of the mass, along with cosmetic concerns, prompted the patient to seek medical evaluation. His past medical, surgical, and drug histories were unremarkable, and there was no relevant family history.

Local examination of the left hand revealed a firm, non-tender, immobile mass measuring approximately 4.0 cm × 3.5 cm × 2.0 cm, located over the hypothenar eminence on the ulnar aspect of the palm. The mass was bony hard in consistency with an irregular surface, and was fixed to the underlying structures, with no mobility in either the coronal or sagittal planes. The patient reported intermittent tenderness over the hypothenar region during the past six months, particularly after prolonged or strenuous hand activity. There was no local rise in temperature, and no signs of inflammation such as erythema or swelling were present. The overlying skin was intact with no evidence of scars, sinuses, dilated veins, or discoloration. No regional lymphadenopathy was detected. A thorough general examination revealed no similar swellings elsewhere in the body. Neurological examination of the upper limb was within normal limits, and the range of motion of the wrist and fingers was preserved without restriction or discomfort. No intrinsic hand muscle weakness was identified on clinical examination. To further evaluate the lesion and establish a definitive diagnosis, plain radiography and MRI of the left hand were ordered.

Investigations and treatment

Plain radiographs of the left hand, including oblique, posteroanterior, and lateral views, demonstrated a well-circumscribed, corticated ossified mass within the ulnar palmar soft tissues of the hypothenar region, adjacent to the fifth metacarpal. The lesion showed lobulated margins and internal trabecular ossification, but no visible pedicle, cortical attachment, or corticomedullary continuity with the adjacent metacarpal or carpal bones. There was no periosteal reaction, cortical destruction, fracture, or other aggressive osseous change (Fig. 1). MRI further characterized the lesion as a lobulated, well-defined extraosseous soft-tissue mass centered in the hypothenar compartment. The mass demonstrated intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and predominantly high, heterogeneous signal intensity on T2-weighted images, with internal low-signal foci corresponding to ossified components and a thin peripheral cartilage-cap-like component. The lesion was clearly separated from adjacent osseous structures, with no cortical or medullary continuity. It displaced nearby soft tissues without infiltration, surrounding edema, or MRI features suggestive of malignancy (Fig. 2). Taken together, the imaging findings favored a benign ossified extraosseous osteocartilaginous lesion; however, because of the atypical palmar location and absence of conventional corticomedullary continuity, histopathologic confirmation was required.

After informed surgical consent was obtained, the patient underwent excision under general anesthesia. The left upper limb was prepared and draped in a sterile fashion; neither tourniquet nor tumescent infiltration was used. A direct longitudinal incision was made over the hypothenar region, centered on the palpable mass. Careful dissection through the subcutaneous tissue exposed the lesion, which was mobilized from the surrounding soft tissues. Intraoperatively, a thrombosed ulnar artery was identified adjacent to the mass, while the regional nerve structures were explored and preserved. The lesion was excised en bloc and sent for histopathological examination. The wound was closed in layers with nylon sutures, and immediate postoperative radiographs confirmed complete excision of the hypothenar mass without residual bony fragments (Figs. 3, 4, 5).

Differential diagnosis and clinically relevant mimics

The principal prepathology differential diagnosis for a well-circumscribed ossified extraosseous hypothenar mass included bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP/Nora lesion), soft-tissue chondroma, myositis ossificans, and, less likely, surface osteosarcoma. Other common hand masses and anatomic mimics, including ganglion cyst, accessory ossicle or bone cyst, giant cell tumor of the tendon sheath, and lipoma, were considered clinically but were less compatible with the radiographic and MRI appearance. BPOP was an important mimic because it may present in the hands or feet as an ossified or calcified juxtacortical mass lacking medullary continuity; however, its typical histology includes disorganized cartilage, irregular “blue bone” trabeculae, and a spindle-cell component [5]. Soft-tissue chondroma was also considered because of its predilection for the hands and feet, but histopathologic distinction was required [6]. Myositis ossificans was less likely because the lesion lacked a subacute painful course and the typical zonal maturation pattern [7]. Surface osteosarcoma was considered unlikely because there was no cortical destruction, aggressive periosteal reaction, permeative change, or rapid growth [8]. Ganglion cyst, accessory ossicle or bone cyst, giant cell tumor of the tendon sheath, and lipoma were regarded as broader clinical mimics rather than true imaging-based differentials, given the ossified and corticated extraosseous nature of the lesion and the absence of cystic MRI features, a typical accessory ossicle location, an intraosseous cystic pattern, hemosiderin-related signal change, or macroscopic fat [9–12]. Overall, clinicoradiologic-pathologic correlation remained essential for definitive diagnosis in this anatomically atypical lesion [13].

Pathology

The pathological images of the patient presented in Fig. 6 demonstrate histologic features consistent with the diagnosis of OC. In Fig. 6 (a and b), a hyaline cartilage cap overlies trabecular bone with abrupt endochondral ossification at the cartilage to bone interface, representing a sudden transition from cartilage to bone that is characteristic of OC and supports the diagnosis [4, 14]. A thin fibrous perichondrial band covers the cap, which supports a non-articular origin because articular cartilage lacks perichondrium [15]. By contrast, soft-tissue chondroma usually shows lobules of mature hyaline cartilage with variable calcification but lacks organized endochondral ossification into mature trabecular bone with marrow elements, which helps to exclude it here. Taken together, these features confirm an osteochondromatous lesion arising in an extraosseous location.

In clinicoradiologic-pathologic correlation, the resected lesion demonstrated a thin hyaline cartilage cap with orderly endochondral ossification producing mature trabecular bone with interposed marrow elements and no cytologic atypia, a constellation that is diagnostic of a benign osteochondromatous lesion. The sections lacked spindle-cell stroma, irregular “blue-bone” trabeculae, and nuclear pleomorphism, thereby excluding BPOP. Consistently, the decade-long indolent course, sharply corticated margins, absence of aggressive periosteal reaction, and the apparently orderly endochondral ossification zone on imaging further favored OC rather than BPOP. A pure soft-tissue chondroma is unlikely given the extensive endochondral ossification with mature bony trabeculae beneath a thin cartilage cap. Myositis ossificans is disfavored by the absence of a zonal maturation pattern histologically and by the chronic, minimally painful evolution. Among the broader clinically considered mimics, there were no cystic or gelatinous contents or a definable cyst wall to support a ganglion cyst; similarly, there was no permeative growth, cortical destruction, malignant osteoid, or high-grade cytologic atypia to support surface osteosarcoma. Taken together, these findings establish OC arising in an extraosseous location.

Outcome and follow-up

At the first postoperative follow-up, one week after discharge, the surgical wound demonstrated appropriate healing without signs of infection, dehiscence, or abnormal discharge. The patient reported minimal pain and was able to resume daily activities without difficulty, exhibiting no functional limitations. A second follow-up visit was conducted two months postoperatively. Clinical evaluation revealed a well-healed surgical incision over the hypothenar region, with no signs of infection. The patient exhibited full range of motion in all digits, with normal hand alignment. Follow-up radiographs of the hand demonstrated no evidence of residual lesion or recurrence, with normal bony and soft tissue appearance. Postoperative clinical photographs are shown in Fig. 7. At six months, the patient remained asymptomatic and fully functional, performing regular work and daily tasks without difficulty. Examination by inspection and palpation revealed no palpable mass or induration, no intrinsic weakness, and no neurologic deficit; given the absence of symptoms and a normal physical examination, additional radiographs were not obtained. There has been no clinical evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

OC is the most common benign bone tumor, accounting for 20–50% of all benign bone neoplasms and 9% of all bone tumors. It typically arises from the metaphyseal region of long bones, especially around the knee and proximal humerus. Involvement of the bones of the hand is rare, representing less than 5% of all reported cases [10, 16, 17], In even rarer instances, OCs may occur in extraosseous locations, such as soft tissues without direct bone continuity, posing diagnostic challenges due to their atypical presentation [13]. In contemporary usage, extraosseous and extraskeletal OC denote the same entity; when arising adjacent to joints it is often termed para-articular OC, and many authors use soft-tissue OC synonymously [18, 19].

OCs are generally asymptomatic and discovered incidentally. However, when they occur in small bones or atypical locations, they may present with symptoms due to mass effect, such as pain, cosmetic deformity, limitation of joint motion, or compression of adjacent neurovascular structures [20]. In this case, the patient presented with a gradually enlarging, firm, immobile mass over the hypothenar region, with minimal pain exacerbated by hand activity but no neurological deficits or vascular compromise. Although no preoperative clinical evidence of vascular compromise was present, color Doppler ultrasonography might have further evaluated ulnar arterial continuity and could potentially have identified the adjacent thrombosed segment before surgery. The absence of overlying skin changes, systemic symptoms, and no discrete acute trauma helped narrow the differential diagnosis while remaining consistent with the history of repetitive occupational micro-trauma described in the Case Presentation.

Histology provided the decisive arbitration among pre-pathology differentials and, together with the lesion’s chronic indolent course and benign imaging features, supports a final diagnosis of extraosseous OC in the hypothenar region, an atypical location where lack of cortico-medullary continuity is recognized. Early follow-up in our patient has shown no recurrence to date, whereas BPOP, the closest preoperative mimic, is notorious for high local recurrence rates (up to ~ 50%) [21].

Solitary OCs of the hand are uncommon, and palmar involvement is particularly rare; most reported hand lesions arise on the dorsal aspect of phalanges or metacarpals [3]. Extraosseous OCs are rarer still in the upper limb, with only isolated reports around the wrist, including palmar wrist presentations, and exceedingly sparse descriptions within the ulnar palmar compartment; recent clinicopathological work further underscores the rarity of para-articular OC and the need for histologic confirmation [22, 23]. Notably, explicit reports of extraosseous OC arising within the ulnar palmar compartment are, to our knowledge, exceedingly scarce. The available literature on upper-limb para-articular/extraskeletal presentations largely centers on the wrist region and seldom provides comprehensive, multimodality documentation of absent cortico-medullary continuity together with detailed clinicopathologic correlation. Against this background, the present case represents an uncommon hypothenar presentation of extraosseous OC, with intraoperative and histologic confirmation and the additional finding of adjacent ulnar artery thrombosis, a finding that resonates with the vascular vulnerability described in hypothenar hammer syndrome but, to our knowledge, has not been co-reported with hypothenar extraosseous OC. These features underscore the clinical imperative to consider osteochondromatous pathology in slow-growing, firm palmar masses despite atypical location and imaging non-continuity, and they motivate the mechanistic hypothesis outlined below.

Mechanistic hypothesis

We propose that this extraosseous OC arose through metaplastic chondrogenesis of extraosseous soft-tissue mesenchymal cells in the hypothenar region, a mechanism long invoked for extraskeletal chondro-osseous lesions in which peri-/para-articular connective tissues undergo cartilaginous metaplasia [18, 24]. Repetitive micro-trauma and sustained mechanical loading related to the patient’s construction work represent plausible triggers that, via local inflammation, drive chondrogenic differentiation and endochondral ossification beneath a thin hyaline cartilage cap; an interpretation consonant with prior reports of trauma-linked para-articular OCs and soft-tissue chondromas [25, 26]. The resultant lesion’s lack of cortico-medullary continuity with adjacent bone is compatible with extraskeletal/para-articular variants and with the histologic pattern observed in our case. As a biologically plausible, non-exclusive alternative, classic OCs frequently reflect EXT1/EXT2-related disturbances in heparan-sulfate biosynthesis, whereas the genetic basis of extraskeletal counterparts remains insufficiently defined [27]. Finally, the adjacent ulnar artery thrombosis aligns with the pathophysiology of hypothenar hammer syndrome from repetitive blunt impacts [28]; while likely secondary to mass effect, such localized ischemia/inflammation could further potentiate metaplastic chondrogenesis in predisposed soft tissues. Surgical excision is the treatment of choice for symptomatic lesions or those causing functional or cosmetic impairment [29]. Complete resection, including the cartilaginous cap and periosteum, is necessary to minimize recurrence. A concise literature review was compiled to contextualize this case (Table 1). Reported extraosseous OCs typically present as slow-growing, firm masses. Imaging most often showed a well-circumscribed soft-tissue ossified lesion without corticomedullary continuity, and histology consistently demonstrated a hyaline cartilage cap with orderly endochondral ossification. All patients underwent surgical excision (marginal or en bloc) with symptom resolution; across the available midterm follow-up, no recurrences were reported, including in the present case.
