Dentin tubule penetration of a calcium silicate–based sealer after removal of calcium hydroxide or N-acetylcysteine medicaments: an in vitro study
Authors: Sude Coşar, Merve Sarı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate, using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), the dentin tubule penetration of a calcium silicate–based root canal sealer after removal of calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) or N-acetylcysteine (NAC) intracanal medicaments with needle irrigation or EDDY sonic activation.MethodsRoot canals of 128 extracted single-rooted mandibular premolars were prepared with ProTaper Next. Canals received Ca(OH)2 (Kalsin) or (NAC) for 7 days. Medicaments were removed using conventional needle irrigation or EDDY sonic activation. Specimens were obturated with a calcium silicate–based sealer Bioserra using the single-cone technique. CLSM quantified residual medicament and sealer penetration into dentin tubules. Statistical analyses were performed using the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests, with the significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsNeither the type of intracanal medicament nor the activation technique significantly affected the residual medicament amount (p > 0.05). However, the type of medicament significantly influenced sealer penetration, with NAC showing greater dentin tubule penetration than Ca(OH)₂ (p < 0.05). The activation technique did not significantly influence sealer penetration (p > 0.05). Sealer penetration was highest in the coronal third and lowest in the apical third (p < 0.05).ConclusionWithin the limitations of this in vitro study, NAC enhanced the dentin tubule penetration of a calcium silicate–based sealer compared with Ca(OH)₂. The activation technique did not affect sealer penetration or residual medicament.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08809-2.

Background

The primary goal of root canal treatment is to eliminate microorganisms from the root canal system and subsequently obturate the canal to achieve a fluid-tight seal [1]. Although chemomechanical preparation substantially reduces the microbial load, complete eradication is not always achieved. Therefore, intracanal medicaments are recommended to supplement root canal disinfection [2].

Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) is the most commonly used intracanal medicament because its high pH confers antibacterial activity and enables neutralization of endotoxins. It also sustains an alkaline environment within the root canal system, suppressing microbial metabolism and promoting tissue healing [3]. However, its action can be buffered by dentin, efficacy is limited against certain bacterial species, and complete removal from canal walls is often difficult [4]. Residual Ca(OH)2 may hinder penetration of sealers into dentin tubules, increase apical leakage, and compromise the quality of the root canal filling [5].

Recently, N-acetylcysteine (NAC)—an antioxidant and mucolytic agent—has emerged as an alternative intracanal medicament [6]. Studies indicate that NAC exhibits antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Proposed mechanisms include inhibition of bacterial cysteine utilization and reactions of its thiol group with bacterial cell proteins [7]. By scavenging reactive oxygen species, NAC can protect pulp cells [8], and it shows activity against pathogens such as Enterococcus faecalis (E. Faecalis) by disrupting disulfide bonds [7]. Moon et al. [9] reported that NAC demonstrated greater antibiofilm efficacy than Ca(OH)2 and 2% CHX gel against biofilms of Actinomyces naeslundii, Lactobacillus salivarius, Streptococcus mutans, and E. faecalis. Notably, NAC retains its effectiveness even in the presence of dentin, an advantage over Ca(OH)2 [10].

Conventional needle irrigation (CNI) alone may be insufficient to remove intracanal medicaments, particularly materials that can penetrate dentinal tubules, such as Ca(OH)2. To enhance irrigant efficacy, various activation systems have been advocated [11]. EDDY (VDW, Munich, Germany) is a sonic activation tip made of flexible polyamide to preserve canal morphology; it is noncutting, sterilized, and single-use. The tips are size 25/0.04 and are used with an air scaler operating at 5–6 kHz [12]. The effectiveness of EDDY compared with CNI has drawn particular attention. Elgazzar et al. [13] evaluated irrigant penetration depth and reported that EDDY provided superior irrigant delivery relative to CNI. This enhanced hydrodynamic activation promotes more effective irrigant distribution throughout the root canal system and contributes to the removal of residual intracanal medicament, especially in the apical third [14].

Bioserra (Dentac, Istanbul, Turkey), the novel sealer investigated in the present study, is a ready-to-use, premixed calcium silicate–based root canal sealer supplied in a syringe with single-use tips for convenient clinical application. Its formulation contains calcium silicate, zirconium oxide, and aluminum. Calcium silicate–based sealers achieve sealing by forming an interfacial hydroxyapatite layer through alkaline pH and Ca²⁺ release, thereby establishing a chemical bond with dentin. Owing to their hydrophilic and biocompatible nature, they are capable of setting in the presence of tissue fluids. Furthermore, their low surface tension and nano-/microscale particle size facilitate homogeneous distribution into both main and accessory root canals [15].

Although several studies have examined dentin tubule penetration of root canal sealers after Ca(OH)2 dressing, no study to date has evaluated either the residual intracanal medicament or sealer penetration following NAC use. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate, using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), the dentin tubule penetration of a calcium silicate–based root canal sealer after removal of Ca(OH)2 or NAC with needle irrigation or EDDY sonic activation. Methods

Sample size calculation and ethical approval

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich-Heine-University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The study design included four experimental groups based on the intracanal medicament and activation technique. Sample size estimation for differences in sealer penetration among groups was performed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Cohen’s f was used as the standardized effect size; assuming f = 0.2516 [16], α = 0.05, and a power of 0.80, a total of 128 extracted teeth were required.

The study protocol was approved by the Hatay Mustafa Kemal University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee on April 29, 2024 (Decision No. 22), and the methodology was conducted in accordance with the PRILE 2021 guidelines.

Tooth selection and preparation

The study included mandibular premolars with a single root and a single canal. These teeth were extracted from periodontally healthy individuals aged 18 to 45. All extractions were carried out for orthodontic purposes. Suitability was confirmed radiographically. Specimens were screened for oval canal morphology; those with a buccolingual-to-mesiodistal ratio > 1.5 were excluded. Teeth were examined under 4× magnification using a surgical loupe (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany). Teeth with immature apices, resorption, fractures/cracks, or restorations were excluded. After cleaning, specimens were stored in 0.1% thymol solution until use.

Root canal preparation

Crowns were sectioned to standardize root length at 15 mm. Working length (WL) was established 1 mm short of the apical foramen with a #10 K-file (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan). A glide path was created to WL using ProGlider 16/0.02 (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Canal shaping was performed with ProTaper Next X1 (17/0.04) and X2 (25/0.06) (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) instruments, following a crown-down technique. Files were used with a pecking motion of ≤ 3 mm amplitude, and each file prepared no more than three canals. Irrigation was performed using 30-G needles (Medic, Shanghai Carelife, China) kept 2 mm short of WL. During canal preparation, a total of 15 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (Cerkamed, Stalowa Wola, Poland) was used, with 5 mL delivered to each third of the root canal (coronal, middle, and apical). This was followed by a final rinse with 5 mL of distilled water.To remove the smear layer, 5 mL of 17% EDTA (Werax Dental, Izmir, Turkey) was applied for 1 min, followed by 5 mL distilled water and 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl. A final rinse with 5 mL distilled water was performed, and canals were dried with paper points (Diadent, Cheongju, Korea).

Group allocation and intracanal medicament application

Following chemomechanical preparation, specimens were randomly assigned to two groups according to the intracanal medicament—calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) or (NAC)—(n = 64).

Intracanal medicament placement

For the Ca(OH)2 group, Kalsin (Promida, Konya, Turkey) was used; for the NAC group, NAC powder (Aromel, Izmir, Turkey) was mixed 1:1 with distilled water. For CLSM analysis, 0.1% Rhodamine B (Isolab, Istanbul, Turkey) was added to each mixture. After evaporating the liquid phase, the medicaments were delivered with a #25 Lentulo spiral (Jensen Dental, North Haven, USA) to 2 mm short of WL. Access cavities were sealed with a cotton pellet and Cavit-G (3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). Specimens were stored at 37 °C and 100% humidity for 7 days. To verify intracanal placement and apical extent, a wax plug had been positioned at the root apex prior to the procedure and was removed after medicament placement. The extent of the medicament reaching the apex was then assessed through this opening. Since NAC is non-radiopaque, its distribution could not be evaluated radiographically; therefore, its presence in both the apical and coronal regions was confirmed using this method.

Removal of intracanal medicaments

At this stage, each intracanal medicament group was further divided into two subgroups according to the irrigation protocol (CNI or EDDY sonic activation) (n = 32). In all experimental groups, removal of the intracanal medicament was performed by enlarging the apical preparation with ProTaper Next X3 (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) under irrigation with 2.5% NaOCl to ensure effective chemomechanical cleaning.

Needle irrigation group

Canals were irrigated using 30-G needles positioned 2 mm short of the WL. A total of 15 mL of 2.5% NaOCl was used, followed by 5 mL distilled water and 5 mL of 17% EDTA for 1 min. Irrigation was then completed with an additional 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl and 5 mL distilled water. Finally, canals were dried with sterile paper points.

EDDY activation group

Canals were irrigated with 30-G needles positioned 2 mm short of the WL using a total of 15 mL of 2.5% NaOCl, followed by 5 mL distilled water. Subsequently, 5 mL of 17% EDTA was activated with a #25/0.04 EDDY sonic tip (VDW, Munich, Germany) for 20 s, repeated three times (total activation time: 1 min). After EDTA removal with 5 mL distilled water, 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl was similarly activated in three 20-second cycles (total 1 min). Final irrigation was performed with 5 mL distilled water, and canals were dried with paper points.

Root canal obturation

After completion of the irrigation protocols, X3 master cones (ProTaper Next; Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) were fitted to the WL with tug-back. For CLSM, fluorescein (Isolab, Istanbul, Turkey) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and mixed with the calcium silicate–based sealer (Bioserra; Dentac, Istanbul, Turkey) at 0.1% (w/v). The sealer was delivered with a #30 Lentulo spiral (Jensen Dental, North Haven, USA) to 1 mm short of WL. Master cones lightly coated with the sealer were placed, and canals were obturated using the single-cone technique. Excess gutta-percha was removed at the canal orifice with a heated plugger, and the access cavities were temporarily sealed (Cavit-G; 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). Obturation quality was verified radiographically. Specimens were stored for 7 days at 37 °C in a humid environment.

Sectioning and CLSM analysis

Transverse sections approximately 1 mm thick were obtained at 3, 6, and 9 mm from the root apex, perpendicular to the long axis of the root. The section surfaces were polished with silicon carbide papers (3 M, Maplewood, USA) to ensure smoothness. Samples were examined under a Zeiss LSM 510 CLSM (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany). Excitation wavelengths were 543 nm for Rhodamine B and 488 nm for Fluorescein. Images were acquired at ×4 magnification with a 512 × 512 pixel resolution. All measurements were performed by a single blinded examiner in a randomized order and repeated twice to ensure reliability. The length of the canal perimeter and area measurements were obtained using the proprietary analysis software of the confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM Image Browser, Version 4.2.0; Carl Zeiss, Germany). Image calibration was automatically defined by the system based on the microscope’s hardware parameters and acquisition settings; therefore, no manual calibration using an external reference scale was required. The canal perimeter was traced using the “Freehand ROI” (region of interest) tool, and the software automatically converted pixel-based data into real-world measurements expressed in micrometers (µm). All analyses were conducted under standardized magnification and resolution settings. The length of the canal wall infiltrated by the sealer was measured and divided by the total canal wall perimeter to calculate the percentage of dentin tubule penetration of the sealer [17] (Fig. 1). The obtained measurements were recorded within the software and exported in.csv format for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data were non-normally distributed, nonparametric tests were used. Intrarater reliability was evaluated with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The effects of intracanal medicament type and irrigation/activation technique on residual medicament percentage and sealer penetration were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences among canal thirds (coronal, middle, apical) for residual medicament and sealer penetration were assessed with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed—when indicated—by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni adjustment. In addition to sectional analyses, an overall (total) value was calculated for each specimen by combining the coronal, middle, and apical measurements using the median of the three sections. These overall values were then compared between medicament groups and irrigation groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Effect size (r) was calculated for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 384 sections from 128 specimens were analyzed for residual medicament and sealer penetration. Intrarater agreement was acceptable; the Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed no difference between repeated measurements (p > 0.05), so mean values were used for analysis.

Effect size analysis revealed a negligible effect of medicament type on residual medicament (r = 0.0575) and a large effect on sealer penetration (r = 0.5591). For irrigation technique, a small-to-moderate effect was observed for residual medicament (r = 0.2376), whereas the effect on sealer penetration was negligible (r = 0.0326) (Table 1).

When the data from all canal thirds were combined to obtain overall values, medicament type (Ca(OH)₂ or NAC) did not significantly affect the amount of residual medicament (p = 0.5305). However, sectional analysis revealed a significant difference in the middle third when CNI was used, where the NAC group exhibited higher residual medicament than the Ca(OH)₂ group (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Regarding irrigation techniques, the overall analysis showed that EDDY resulted in significantly lower residual medicament compared to CNI (p = 0.0093). This finding was consistent with the coronal third, where EDDY produced significantly less residual medicament than CNI in both Ca(OH)₂ and NAC groups (p < 0.05). In the middle third, no difference was observed between irrigation techniques in the Ca(OH)₂ group (p > 0.05), whereas EDDY was superior to CNI in the NAC group (p < 0.05). In the apical third, no significant technique-related differences were detected in either medicament group (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Discussion

Intracanal medicaments are commonly used to control infection during root canal treatment. However, because of the complex anatomy of the root canal system, complete removal is difficult; residues can adversely affect sealer–dentin adhesion and long-term sealing ability [4]. Moreover, residual medicament may hinder sealer penetration into dentin tubules, compromising obturation quality and potentially contributing to treatment failure [5].

Although several studies have examined dentin tubule penetration of root canal sealers after Ca(OH)2 dressing, the evidence base remains limited. To date, no study has evaluated either the residual intracanal medicament or sealer penetration following NAC use. Therefore, this study aimed to assess, using CLSM, the dentin tubule penetration of a calcium silicate–based sealer (Bioserra) after removal of Ca(OH)2 or NAC with different irrigation protocols.

CLSM minimizes artifacts because it requires no additional specimen processing and, with fluorescent dyes, permits clear discrimination between sealer and dentin [18]. Residual intracanal medicament was visualized with 0.1% Rhodamine B [19]. However, the use of Rhodamine B with calcium silicate–based sealers has drawbacks [20]. Donnermeyer et al. [21] reported—using TotalFill BC Sealer and BioRoot RCS—that Rhodamine B can leach into the surrounding medium and overestimate true sealer penetration, thereby compromising CLSM reliability. This effect has been attributed to Rhodamine B’s high affinity for Ca²⁺ released from calcium silicate sealers, allowing the dye to dissociate from the material and migrate freely within dentinal tubules. Accordingly, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was used to label the calcium silicate–based sealer for penetration analysis. Employing two spectrally distinct fluorophores thus enabled CLSM-based differentiation of the distributions of residual medicament and sealer within dentinal tubules. Fluorescein was incorporated at a concentration of 0.1% (w/v), in line with previous CLSM-based studies [20, 22]. Although such low concentrations are widely accepted and considered unlikely to significantly affect the physicochemical properties of endodontic sealers, the potential influence on viscosity, flow, and setting behavior cannot be completely excluded.

CLSM analyses were based on the mean sealer penetration percentage, a metric reported to have high reliability in the literature [23]. Sealer penetration into dentin tubules was quantified using the method described by Gharib et al. [17]. In this method, on CLSM images the circumferential length of sealer-positive canal wall is measured, divided by the total canal perimeter, and multiplied by 100 to yield the penetration percentage Although the penetration percentage is a useful parameter for assessing the circumferential spread of sealer along the canal wall, because it reflects only surface distribution and includes very short, shallow incursions, it may overestimate true dentin tubule penetration. Moreover, when used alone, this method is limited to illustrating the distribution of the sealer along the canal wall and does not adequately reflect other critical aspects such as penetration depth or interfacial surface area.

In the literature, the antimicrobial effect of Ca(OH)2 generally manifests within 1–4 weeks. Siqueira et al. [24] emphasized that, in infected teeth, a minimum of 7 days is required for the efficacy of a Ca(OH)2 dressing. Accordingly, to achieve effectiveness while avoiding potential adverse effects of prolonged exposure on dentin, the medicament application period in this study was set to 7 days [25].

The single-cone technique, which pairs NiTi rotary file–matched gutta-percha cones with a sealer, is described as a rapid and clinically feasible obturation approach [26]. De-Deus et al. [27] reported that warm vertical compaction and Thermafil techniques achieve deeper dentin tubule penetration than lateral compaction. Türkyılmaz et al. [28] compared lateral compaction and the single-cone technique and found comparable levels of dentin tubule penetration, emphasizing that performance depends primarily on the sealer’s properties. These divergent findings indicate no consensus regarding the influence of obturation technique on sealer penetration. Therefore, to ensure methodological standardization and minimize extraneous variables, we used the single-cone technique with master file–matched gutta-percha and a calcium silicate–based sealer.

This study compared the effects of Ca(OH)2 and NAC on residual medicament and sealer tubule penetration. Because no prior head-to-head investigation of these two medicaments was identified, interpretation was confined to our dataset. The overall analysis demonstrated that medicament type did not significantly influence residual medicament, despite the presence of a significant difference in the middle third under the needle irrigation protocol, and H01 was accepted. Irrigation technique showed a significant overall effect, with EDDY providing better removal than CNI, although this superiority was not observed uniformly across all canal thirds, and H02 was rejected.

Donnermeyer et al. [14] and Lu Shi et al. [29] reported that activation methods such as EDDY and passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) outperform CNI in the apical third, while yielding comparable results coronally. Usta et al. [30] ranked SWEEPS as the most effective and CNI as the least, and Arıcan et al. [31] likewise found EDDY/PUI superior to other approaches. In the present study, although no consistent superiority of EDDY over CNI was observed across all canal thirds, the overall analysis demonstrated that EDDY resulted in significantly lower residual medicament compared to CNI. This suggests that cumulative evaluation of all canal levels may reveal advantages of activation techniques that are not consistently detectable at individual sectional levels. The use of straight, single-canaled teeth likely permitted more homogeneous irrigant distribution, thereby limiting the incremental advantage of activation methods. In addition, a relatively large apical preparation may have increased irrigant exchange and velocity, enhancing the performance of CNI.

With respect to sealer tubule penetration, the NAC groups exhibited consistently higher values than Ca(OH)2 across the coronal, middle, and apical thirds, and H03 was rejected. This finding is also consistent with previous reports emphasizing the difficulty of completely removing Ca(OH)2, as residual deposits may persist on canal walls and hinder sealer penetration [32, 33]. The enhanced sealer penetration observed in the NAC groups may be attributed to its chemical interaction with dentin. NAC contains a thiol (-SH) group capable of disrupting disulfide bonds [7, 8, 34] within the organic components of the smear layer and dentinal matrix, thereby facilitating smear layer modification and increasing dentinal tubule accessibility.

In addition, Ghatole et al. [35] reported that the use of NAC as a final irrigant resulted in higher bond strength compared to chlorhexidine, suggesting that NAC may render the dentin surface more favorable for adhesion and improve the bonding of root canal materials. Similarly, Palaniswamy et al. [36] demonstrated that NAC reduces extracellular polysaccharide production, which may further enhance the adhesion of root canal materials to canal walls.

Rajakumaran et al. [37] reported that 20% NAC used as an irrigant removed the smear layer as effectively as EDTA while causing less reduction in dentin microhardness. Sunny et al. [38] showed that NAC produced the greatest sealer penetration in the coronal third, with comparable results to EDTA in the middle and apical thirds. Shenvi et al. [39] further reported that NAC better preserved dentin mineral integrity than EDTA. Taken together, these features suggest that NAC may provide more favorable surface conditioning—by effectively removing the smear layer, inducing less microstructural alteration of dentin, and preserving mineral integrity—thereby facilitating sealer dentin tubule penetration. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the use of NAC as an intracanal medicament remains limited, and further studies under varying clinical conditions are warranted.

In the present study, no significant differences in sealer penetration percentage were detected between EDDY and CNI after removal of either NAC or Ca(OH)2, and H04 was accepted. Because no analogous investigation has evaluated NAC in this context, interpretation is necessarily limited to the Ca(OH)2 literature. Uslu et al. [40] evaluated dentin tubule penetration of root canal sealers in distal roots of mandibular molars using CLSM and found no significant difference between EDDY and CNI. By contrast, Aydın et al. [41] reported higher penetration with EDDY than with CNI in mandibular premolars. Galler et al. [42] reported that EDDY nearly doubled penetration depth compared with CNI. The heterogeneity of the literature indicates that the effect of activation on sealer dentin tubule penetration cannot be accounted for by physical (hydrodynamic) principles alone, but is likely multifactorial. Factors such as residual intracanal medicament, morphologic variability, canal diameter, shaping protocols, sealer properties, and heterogeneity of measurement methods also influence sealer dentin tubule penetration. Therefore, irrigation activation techniques should not be expected to deliver uniformly superior performance under all conditions.

Sealer penetration was greatest in the coronal third and lowest in the apical third; therefore, hypothesis H05 was rejected. Similar findings have also been reported in the literature. Gülmez et al. [43] in mandibular premolars, and Yılmaz et al. [44] in curved root canals, reported —using CLSM— greater sealer penetration in coronal sections and lower penetration in apical sections. Although NAC demonstrated greater sealer penetration overall, no significant differences were observed in the apical third, which represents the most clinically critical region of the root canal system. This finding may be attributed to anatomical and structural limitations of the apical region, including reduced dentinal tubule density, increased sclerosis, and limited irrigant accessibility [45]. Additionally, age-related dentin sclerosis and secondary dentin formation are among the factors that adversely affect apical penetration [46]. Therefore, the clinical relevance of the observed differences should be interpreted with caution, particularly with respect to the apical third.

It should be emphasized that dentin tubule penetration of root canal sealers does not directly reflect antimicrobial efficacy or clinical success. Although deeper penetration may contribute to the entombment of residual microorganisms and potentially improve the sealing ability of the root canal system [47], it cannot be considered a substitute for bacterial elimination. A limitation of the present study is that the specimens did not contain a microbial biofilm, which does not fully replicate clinical conditions. The presence of biofilms may influence the behavior, penetration, and removal efficiency of intracanal medicaments. NAC, in particular, has been reported to exhibit mucolytic and antibiofilm properties by disrupting extracellular polymeric substances and disulfide bonds within biofilms [6, 48]. Therefore, its performance under infected conditions may differ from that observed in this study.

An important limitation of our study is that only premolars with straight, single canals were included. This sampling choice restricted our ability to appraise the potential advantages of irrigation-activation techniques in complex canal anatomies. The literature indicates that complex morphologies—such as curved canals or internal resorption—can substantially affect irrigation efficacy and, particularly in the apical third, hinder irrigant penetration [29, 31]. In such anatomies, the removal of intracanal medicaments may be more challenging, and the distribution and effectiveness of irrigants and sealers may differ significantly compared to straight canals. Therefore, the relative performance of NAC and Ca(OH)2 observed in this study may not be directly reproducible under more complex clinical conditions. Accordingly, we consider that findings obtained in straight canals may not be directly generalizable to the complex canal anatomies encountered in clinical practice. Including teeth with complex morphologies in future studies would enable a more comprehensive appraisal of the real-world clinical performance of irrigation techniques.

Because we used only one calcium silicate-based sealer and a single-cone obturation technique, the generalizability of our findings is limited: the tested material cannot represent the broader range of calcium silicate- or resin-based sealers, and the design did not permit assessment of obturation-technique effects on sealer penetration. Notably, dentinal tubule penetration likely varies among sealers according to their composition and physicochemical properties.

Because no intracanal medicament–free control group was included, it was not possible to determine whether the tested medicaments enhanced sealer penetration or hindered it to varying degrees. Therefore, the absence of a baseline condition limits the ability to interpret the absolute effect of intracanal medicaments on dentinal tubule penetration and restricts the broader clinical interpretation of the findings.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, medicament type did not significantly affect residual intracanal medicament overall, although a section-specific difference was observed in the middle third when needle irrigation was used. Irrigation technique significantly affected residual medicament overall, with EDDY demonstrating superior cleaning performance compared to needle irrigation; however, this effect was not consistent across all canal thirds.

By contrast, medicament type influenced dentinal tubule sealer penetration, with NAC yielding greater penetration than Ca(OH)2. The activation technique did not significantly influence sealer penetration. However, sealer penetration varied according to canal level, being highest in the coronal third and lowest in the apical third.
