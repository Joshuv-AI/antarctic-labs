Brightenings AnD Polarity Inversion Tracking (BADPIT) method for studying solar active region evolution before major solar flares
A.Augustin André-Hoffmann \orcid 0009-0005-2036-4199, M.B.Marianna B. Korsós \orcid 0000-0002-0049-4798, A.Alexander Nindos \orcid 0000-0003-0475-2886, S.Spiros Patsourakos \orcid 0000-0003-3345-9697, M.K.Manolis K. Georgoulis \orcid 0000-0001-6913-1330, R.Robertus Erdélyi \orcid 0000-0003-3439-4127

Abstract
This study investigates the relationship between extreme ultraviolet (EUV) transient brightenings (TBs) and the onset of GOES X-class solar flares in active regions (ARs). We introduce the Brightenings AnD Polarity Inversion Tracking (BADPIT) method that detects TBs across multiple SDO/AIA channels. To identify TBs, we impose two independent thresholds: a 3- σ \sigma intensity-based criterion and a power-law divergence approach. We apply the BADPIT method to datasets of a flaring and a non-flaring AR for 24 hours as a pathfinder to a comprehensive statistical study for a complete performance verification: the flare-productive AR 11429 and the quiescent AR 13186, both sharing a similar Hale sunspot classification. Preliminary results are encouraging: significantly more TBs are detected in the flaring AR, with up to five times more 3- σ \sigma thresholded TBs, while power-law thresholded events were frequent only in the flaring AR and mostly absent in the non-flaring AR. We find that both the power-law threshold method and the 3- σ \sigma method can be useful diagnostic tools for distinguishing between imminent flaring or not, several hours before the onset of major solar flares.

Introduction

Many properties of solar active regions have been deemed important in describing the pre-flare state of flaring regions, in par with significant progress in predicting major solar flares in the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) era
(
15
, see, for more details,)
.
The SDO mission has provided continuous observations of the solar atmosphere through the Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA;
36
) and of the photospheric magnetic field through the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI;
45
).

Most studies aiming to identify signatures of imminent flaring in ARs rely on the evolution of magnetic fields
(see e.g.
6
;
34
;
13
;
28
;
27
;
48
;
26
, and references therein)
.
In particular, strong and sheared polarity inversion lines (PILs) have been statistically linked to flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs)
(see, e.g.
38
;
20
;
50
;
46
;
54
;
25
;
21
, and references therein)
.
The PIL is a key region for the storage and release of magnetic free energy.
Strong-field, highly sheared, and extended PILs are closely associated with
resistive (i.e., via magnetic reconnection) formation of magnetic flux ropes that will subsequently erupt
(
17
;
43
, see, for example)
.
The build-up of magnetic shear and electric currents along the PIL makes it a favourable site for magnetic reconnection, which is a fundamental driver of eruptive activity
(
44
;
53
)
.

To further improve the identification of the pre-flare/pre-eruptive phase in active regions, as well as refine existing flare prediction methods, an important complement to magnetic-field-based precursors is provided by extreme ultraviolet (EUV) observations.
Several studies have addressed EUV small-scale brightening prior to flares
(see e.g.
49
;
16
;
24
;
31
;
30
, and references therein)
, while others have investigated the occurrence of coronal dimmings
(see e.g.
19
;
23
, and references therein)
.
Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that eruptive flares may be initiated by internal magnetic reconnection between a small-scale flare-triggering field and the overlying sheared arcade.
These small-scale reconnection events, often manifesting as lower solar atmospheric pre-flare brightenings, are thought to act as triggers for flares
(
39
;
8
;
32
;
2
;
7
;
3
;
33
;
37
;
29
;
35
;
11
;
10
;
31
, e.g.,)
.
Alternatively, as suggested by
57
, observed brightenings may simply reflect general photospheric and chromospheric dynamics and restructuring, such as emerging active regions, bald patches, sunspot moats, or light bridges, and may not be directly linked to subsequent energetic events.

In recent years, an increasing number of machine- and deep-learning studies have focused squarely on flare prediction.
These studies have leveraged the large-scale, high-cadence datasets of SDO/AIA
(
41
;
22
;
1
;
56
;
35
;
51
)
.
However, the AIA data used in these models is subject to significant spatial degradation over time
(
12
, see )
, with this downscaling partly hindering the identification of small-scale features that may be critical to understanding the underlying flare initiation mechanisms.
In summary, observational and theoretical/modelling work suggests that magnetic reconnection may play a key role in forming pre-eruptive magnetic configurations and triggering flares, with transient brightenings (TBs) serving as a
“smoking gun”
of these reconnections events.

8
reported transient precursor brightenings around PILs between 2 and 50 min before a flare’s impulsive phase.
Work such as this motivate us to investigate the evolution of brightenings in such regions over an extended, 24-hour interval prior to major flaring.
Our objective is to develop a method to determine whether there are systematic differences in the low/intermediate-activity between non-flaring (no flares) and flaring (X-class flares) ARs based on the characteristics of their TBs and how are the TBs distributions correlated with the PIL area.

To enable brightening detection around the PIL, we introduce the Brightenings AnD Polarity Inversion Tracking (BADPIT) method, which identifies and characterises small TBs using time-series analyses of HMI and AIA observations.
We aim to study the pre-flare evolution of active regions over a 24-hour period and further investigate whether the evolution of TBs near the PIL is connected to upcoming energetic events.
The proposed methodology follows a similar hypothesis as recently put forward by
10
.
They analysed 2-hour intervals during 3 intervals: the flaring interval and the pre- and post-event associated intervals.
They suggested that, prior to flaring, TBs form large clusters near the future flare site, concentrated around strong-gradient PILs.
In contrast to their topological approach, our work focuses on quantifying the evolution of the TB population and tracking its long-term behaviour over a substantial observational period.

This work is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we introduce the dataset used in our analysis.
Section
3
describes the key components of the BADPIT method for identifying small-scale TBs.
Section
4
shows the results obtained with the method on a test pair of ARs exhibiting flare activity levels typical of flaring (period prior an X-class flare) and non-flaring (no C-class or above flare) ARs.
Finally, Section
5
presents the conclusions and outlines some future directions of research.

The 94 Å channel observes plasma with temperatures from several hundred thousand Kelvin (
\sim

The 131 Å wavelength provides insight into both high-temperature flaring plasma (
\sim

\sim

The 171 Å channel is ideal for tracing the structure of coronal loops at approximately 0.6 MK, while 193 Å captures hotter, ambient coronal plasma around 1.5 MK and is useful for examining coronal holes and hotter flare plasmas.

0.01-0.1

By combining observations from HMI magnetograms with observations from multiple AIA channels covering atmospheric layers from the photosphere to the corona, we are able to track the evolution of TBs across a broad range of heights and temperatures, at the same time monitoring the magnetic activity underneath them.
This multi-channel approach enables us to identify the optimal combination of wavelengths for detecting TBs in the vicinity of the PIL.

3
Methodology

The BADPIT method tracks changes in TB activity using AIA observations over a continuous 24-hour period.
The spatial location of the detected TBs is also compared with the polarity inversion area (PIA), namely the PIL and its surroundings.
In a distinct way than that of the
10
study, we quantify and track the evolution of TBs and the TB population over selected 24-hour intervals.

To test the BADPIT method, we have selected two active regions, namely NOAA ARs 13186 and 11429. In this work,
the two ARs will serve as archetypes of flaring (the former hosted GOES C- and M-class flares occurring during the observed interval and was followed by two X-class flares in the next hour) and non-flaring configuration (the latter hosted no flares stronger than C1.0 during our interval as well as in the preceding and following 48 hours).
AR 11429, observed in early March 2012, was incompatible with Hale’s polarity law and exhibited a conspicuous
\delta
-spot magnetic configuration, indicating a complex magnetic structure that is highly likely to produce major flares and eruptions
(
14
)
.
It was also the solar source of a few major space weather events.
Among multiple C- and M-class flares, this AR hosted two X-class flares and produced two fast CMEs on March 7, 2012, with speeds exceeding 2000 km/s
(
42
)
.
The events occurred within an hour of each other and started right after the end of the interval we studied, as previously mentioned.
Using multi-wavelength data from SDO and STEREO, alongside nonlinear force-free magnetic field extrapolations,
9
identified weakly twisted magnetic flux systems interpreted as pre-eruptive magnetic flux ropes.
These structures formed during flaring events hours before the eruptions and eventually evolved into the cores of the two CMEs.

52
revealed by a differential emission measure analysis the presence of hot plasma (
\log T=6.8-7.1
) in both the eastern and western parts of AR 11429, where the CMEs originated from.
These magnetic flux ropes displayed gradual heating and upward motion before the eruptions. Therefore, this highly active AR 11429 serves as an ideal candidate to investigate the evolution of pre-flare brightenings.

Conversely, AR 13186, observed in mid-January 2023, exhibited no flaring activity (no C-class flares or above in the 48 hours preceding and following nor during the chosen interval).
Remarkably, however, it shared a similar Hale-class (
\beta\gamma\delta
) as the chosen flaring AR (11429), providing a contrasting case with a similar sunspot class.

A notable byproduct of this study is a new algorithm that was developed to achieve a satisfactory and sufficient desaturation of the AIA images.
This is an essential step to ensure the quality and validity of the input data to the BADPIT method.

AIA images may be contaminated by artefacts originating from the design of the AIA telescope, which includes both an entrance filter mesh and a focal-plane filter mesh as part of its optical system.
These components are intended to block visible light and off-band EUV radiation but also introduce optical diffraction effects as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Due to the mounting angles of the meshes (40° and 50°), characteristic diffraction patterns appear as four arms around saturated regions, oriented in the focal plane at 50°, 40°,
-40
°, and
-50
°
(
18
)
.
Saturation refers to pixels with intensities at or above 16000
\text{DN}\cdot$\mathrm{s}$^{-1}

(
47
, data number per second,)
.

In addition to saturated pixels, we account for two related types of artefacts: First, saturation trails, namely vertical streaks aligned along the detector’s
y
-axis, flagged to capture secondary effects from bright pixel blooming (see area 3 in Fig.
1
).
Second, diffraction arms, namely linear features aligned with axis defined by the telescope’s Point Spread Function (PSF), as described in the AIA PSF Characterisation and Image Deconvolution documentation
(
18
)
.
These diffraction patterns are flagged along their respective axes (see areas 2 in Fig.
1
).

To conservatively capture saturation effects, as well as saturation trails and diffraction patterns, and stay consistent with the approach by
47
, we flag all pixels with intensities above 12000
\text{DN}\cdot$\mathrm{s}$^{-1}
as saturated (see area 1 in Fig.
1
).
Once all problematic pixels are identified in the EUV observations, they are replaced by values derived via linear interpolation in time.
Due to the way brightenings are detected (through thresholding methods), we add a sinusoidal component to the interpolated light curves.
This prevents the corrected points from triggering the brightening detection procedure (see below) and thus helps avoid the processing of false data.

The method described here will not have any effect on persistently bad pixels, even in cases where they would have a value over the saturation threshold.
Because they would not affect our study due to the detection criteria that will be presented in Section
3.2
, we do not try to detect and correct such artefacts.

Our desaturation procedure reduces the risk of false detections and ensures that the detected brightenings correspond to physically meaningful transient activity.
Nevertheless, caution is advised as the desaturation process itself can introduce new artefacts, such as emission spikes.
In Figure
2
, for example, the spike observed around
18000~\text{DN}\cdot$\mathrm{s}$^{-1}
(black curve) originates from saturation of the original images, while the spike around
9500~\text{DN}\cdot$\mathrm{s}$^{-1}
(red curve) results from the desaturation process and the change of intensity from the pixels affected by saturation effects (presented previously) to lower values from the areas surrounding the saturated regions and creating a surge as observed in figure
2
.
These newly-created artefacts can be observed on figure
1b
, mainly as dark patches on the PSF axis.

One might consider that we solved one problem only to generate another.
However, these newly created artefacts are ignored by our brightening detection method due to the criteria we apply (explained in Section
3.2
).
Thus, they do not compromise our analysis.
Still, caution is invariably advised when employing desaturation methods: a careful investigation should always precede their application to evaluate their potential effects on the study.

3.2
Brightening detection on the AIA observations

After the desaturation process, brightenings in active regions can be identified.
Brightenings are short-lived, localised events that are significantly brighter than their surrounding background.
They can also be visualised in sequences of difference images such as in Fig.
3
, where they appear as rapidly changing, coherent patches of pixels.

BADPIT detects intensity peaks in the light curves of each pixel within the field of view (FOV).
Brightenings are then defined as spatially coherent patches of high-intensity pixels.
To ensure that only physically meaningful events are detected and to minimise the likelihood of including artefacts into the analysis, we introduce two additional constraints, based on the technical limitations of the SDO/AIA observations (12-second cadence and
\sim 1.5^{\prime\prime}
spatial resolution):

•

Spatial scale: Detected patches must span an area larger than 3 contiguous pixels (i.e., greater than
$5.67\text{\times}{10}^{5}$~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}$^{2}
).

•

Temporal scale: Detected events must persist for more than five consecutive frames (i.e., at least one minute).

These criteria restrict the analysis to brightenings that exhibit both spatial structure and sufficient temporal evolution to be confidently characterised as transient activity.
While smaller-scale events may indeed occur, the risk of ignoring them is deemed lower than the risk of including them in the analysis.
Moreover, difference images may reveal both real brightenings and background noise fluctuations, as shown in Fig.
3
.
By applying the spatial and temporal filters described above, we isolate only the relevant TBs.

Once we ensure that only the most statistically significant and morphologically consistent brightenings remain, we apply two methods to accurately identify brightenings’ populations, namely:

•

3-
\sigma
threshold method

This intensity threshold (3 times the standard deviation; 3-
\sigma
) is determined based on the Gaussian distribution of every pixels’ intensity to remove the noise from our signal.
This 3-
\sigma
threshold corresponds to a high-confidence criterion for detecting significant brightenings relative to the background
(
40
, see, for example)
.
It is used here to help us better understand small-scale transient activity and behaviour.
However, since this threshold is determined from the full-period distribution of intensities, it is not directly applicable to a potential future attempt of near real-time forecasting.

•

Power-law fit based threshold method

This second threshold is globally defined, meaning it is not pixel-based.
It is spatially and temporally invariant for a given AR throughout the observation interval and is based on the histogram of the intensities of the AR over the entire period.
We compared the observed density distribution of the intensity histogram with other distributions frequently considered in similar studies, such as the lognormal or exponentially modified Gaussian
(
12
, see )
.
However, the distribution appears to better follow a power-law (
y=ax^{-\alpha}
; see Figs.
4a
and
4b
).

A clear difference in the power-law index,
\alpha
, already provides qualitative information about the ARs: the non-flaring AR exhibits a steeper, “softer” distribution with a large absolute
\alpha
value, while the flaring AR shows a flatter, “harder” one with a smaller absolute
\alpha
value.
This implies that intense, persistent emission occurs more frequently in the flaring AR, whereas the non-flaring AR is dominated by weaker emission patterns.

Our aim here is to use a power-law fit as a tool to define a threshold for “strong” brightenings.
To achieve this, we set the threshold at the intensity where the histogram of the data begins to deviate from the fitted distribution at high intensities.
We identify two configurations in the residuals, as shown in Figure
4
, that serve as qualitative criteria for defining deviation values: a significantly increased spread in the intensity distribution (Fig.
4a
and a high-intensity frequency rollover (Fig.
4b
)).
With this method, we identify brightenings that are significantly stronger than the background small-scale activity in the AR and depart from an otherwise self-similar intensity distribution, represented by a power-law.

Utilizing the above, we found that the combination of the 3-
\sigma
and power-law threshold methods provides valuable insight into TB activity and effectively filters out both nearby quiet Sun regions and low-activity areas within the active region.
We will return to this discussion in Section
4
.
Distinct from the pixel-based 3-
\sigma
method, the power-law detection method captures generally stronger TBs, corresponding to the brightest TBs detected by the 3-
\sigma
method.
The 3-
\sigma
method captures a broader range of localised events, including both stronger and weaker events.
Therefore, the two threshold identification methods can be considered complementary.

3.3
Analysis of Magnetic Field Observations

To examine the connection between the locations of TBs and the area of the PIL, we use LoS magnetic field data to generate masks around the PILs, as in the case of AR 11429 shown in Fig.
5a
(magnetogram) and Fig.
5b
(1600 Å), following the methodology presented in
46
.
LoS magnetograms are sufficient here since we restrict our analysis to ARs within central meridian distances of
\pm 45\degree
as mentioned in Section
2
.

We define a region called the polarity inversion area (PIA), which encompasses the PIL and extends into its immediate surroundings by
8"
(16 SDO/HMI pixels).
The value was chosen empyrically to allow a good overlap between both polarities of the AR magnetic field.
The broader PIA provides a useful indication of where magnetic reconnection associated with the PIL takes place, and thus, where TBs are most likely to occur.
The PIA detection algorithm operates by generating two masks based on where the absolute value of the magnetic field exceeds a preset threshold of
\pm 150
G
(
46
)
.
These masks are then dilated by 16 pixels (
9.6^{\prime\prime}
), and the regions where the two expanded masks overlap identify areas where opposite polarities are sufficiently close to each other, thereby defining the PIA.
An example of the resulting PIA mask is shown for AR 11429 in Figure
5
and represented by the red contour over the LoS magnetic field and the 1600 Å image.
Additionally, another mask, that of the entire AR, is also derived from HMI magnetograms.
This AR mask, shown with orange contour in Figure
5
, highlights areas with magnetic field strength of over
30~$\mathrm{G}$
(see Fig.
5a
) to ensure that only stronger than quiet-Sun fields are associated with the AR.
This mask is then applied to the corresponding AIA observations (for example, see Fig.
5b
for its application in the 1600 Å data) to isolate magnetically active areas relevant to our study through visual inspection.
The value of
30~$\mathrm{G}$
was empirically chosen so as to separate quiet-Sun from active-region fields.

4
Testing the brightening detection method

In this section, we test whether our BADPIT method can identify notable differences within a 24-hour window between the quiescent, non-flaring AR 13186 and the highly dynamic, flaring AR 11429.
Our goal is to analyse AR behaviour prior to and leading up to significant X-class flaring events.
For AR 13186, we randomly selected a 24-hour interval of flare-quiet activity when the AR was located near the disk centre.
For AR 11429, we selected a period free of X-class flares immediately before two such flares occurred, starting from 00:02 on March 7, 2012.
Therefore, we analysed the 24-hour period beginning at 00:00 on March 6, 2012.
The chosen interval for the flaring AR is not free of M-class flares that are sometimes also considered as major flares, however in this case study our focus is on the differences between a flare-quiet configuration and an X-class flare-imminent one.
A more comprehensive analysis is currently underway and will examine these differences over a broader and finer flare classification scale than the exploratory case study presented here.

5
Conclusion

Our main objective in this study is to enhance our understanding of the small- to medium-scale EUV events, TBs, occurring in ARs around the PIL, and their possible link to the development of major flares, especially GOES X-class flares in this study.
TBs are short-lived, localised events that appear significantly brighter than their immediate surroundings.
These brightenings tend to form large clusters near the site of future flare ribbons, typically concentrated along strong-gradient polarity inversion lines and in regions of enhanced magnetic energy density
(
10
)
.
To specifically investigate whether the evolution of small-scale TB activity in the vicinity of the PIA can serve as a promising indicator of imminent flaring within a given 24-hour window, we developed the Brightenings AnD Polarity Inversion Tracking (BADPIT) method.

To implement the BADPIT methodology, we developed a new algorithm that successfully desaturates AIA images.
Our simple desaturation method may be useful for other researchers working with SDO/AIA data, particularly in the 94 Å, 131 Å, 171 Å, 193 Å, and 1600 Å channels.
To favour the observation of TBs around the PIA we focused our attention on the 94 Å data that showed clusters more concentrated in the PIA than other channels, which aligns with the analysis of
35
, who concluded that the 94 Å AIA EUV filter contains the largest number of parameters with flaring discriminant power.

The BADPIT method tracks TB activity across multiple SDO/AIA EUV channels using two threshold methods:

•

3-
\sigma
threshold method: A pixel-based threshold defined as three times the standard deviation above the mean of a given pixel’s light curve.
This method assumes the intensity signal follows a Gaussian distribution, such that 99% of values fall within the 3-
\sigma
interval, making deviations statistically significant.

•

Power-law threshold method: A global thresholding approach that identifies only high-intensity brightenings, using a spatially and temporally invariant threshold independent of the pixel’s background intensity.
The threshold is defined as the separation value at which the intensity histogram deviates from the power-law fitted distribution.
However, the determination of the power-law threshold appears not to work satisfactorily on channels other than 94 Å.

To investigate the TB activity within the strong magnetic gradient area of the ARs, we followed the method described by
46
to identify the PIA using the corresponding line-of-sight SDO/HMI magnetograms (see Fig.
7
).
We applied the BADPIT methodology to two ARs with distinctly different levels of flaring activity: AR 11429, which produced several strong flares, and AR 13186, which produced none (see Table
2
).
For each region, we analysed a continuous 24-hour observation window.
In the case of the flaring region (AR 11429), the 24-hour window was selected to cover the period leading up to a couple of X-class flares.
Based on this analysis, we found the following:

•

While the spatial distribution of high-intensity brightenings i.e., resulted from the application of the power-law method, remains consistent across the two analysed active regions in different AIA channels, the 3-
\sigma
brightenings follow patterns adhering to the attributes of each channel.
They appear evenly scattered across the active region in the 1600 Å observations, follow loop-like structures in the 171 Å channel, and are concentrated within the PIA in the 94 Å channel.
Since the 94 Å channel shows the strongest concentration of 3-
\sigma
brightenings within the PIA, we deemed that 94 Å observations are most relevant for identifying TBs associated with strong flares.

•

When counting the number of TBs, we identify approximately five times as many in the flaring active region compared to the non-flaring one, using the 3-
\sigma
threshold method on 94 Å data (see Fig.
8
).
Furthermore, the power-law threshold method demonstrates even stronger discriminating power in separating TB activity between the two regions, based on the number of detected TBs with no TBs detected in the non-flaring configuration (see Fig.
8
).

•

We also examined the cumulative evolution of brightening intensity over the 24-hour analysis windows, based on 94 Å data.
This comparison clearly differentiates the two active regions, revealing an order-of-magnitude difference in cumulative intensity when applying the 3-
\sigma
method (Fig.
9
).

Overall, we find that the combination of image desaturation, dual thresholding methods, and comparison with the PIA acts to significantly distinguish between a flaring and a non-flaring solar active region.
This advancement improves the diagnostic capability of the BADPIT method to identify the pre-eruptive state of active regions by capturing elevated TB activity over 24-hour periods.

To fully evaluate the robustness and general applicability of BADPIT, further work is needed on a larger, statistically significant sample of both flaring and non-flaring active regions.
Future efforts will also focus on refining the power-law threshold level to account for the gradual decrease in AIA sensitivity as the instrument continues to age.
