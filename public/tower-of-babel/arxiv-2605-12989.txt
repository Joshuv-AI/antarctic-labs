A sharp lower bound on the self-intersections of fold singularities
Joshua Drouin, Liam Kahmeyer

Abstract
. For an oriented surface S S , the singular set of a fold map f : S → ℝ 2 f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2} is a collection of smooth curves, also known as fold singularities. We construct a sharp lower bound on the number of self-intersections of such fold singularities. This is done by first establishing a sharp lower bound on the number of self-intersections of the boundary of a surface immersed in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . We then construct a sharp lower bound for the number of self-intersections of the singular set of a simple stable fold map of a surface to ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} by viewing the connected components of the singular set as the boundary components of smaller surface components, and invoking the previously constructed lower bound for the number of self-intersections of an immersed boundary.

1.
Introduction

The relationship between fold singularities and manifolds with boundary has been explored and developed by numerous authors. In
[
1
]
, Eliashberg described necessary conditions for a codimension 0 map to have prescribed fold singularities. In the even dimensional case, this prescription splits the manifold into two submanifolds with boundary such that the submanifolds have equal Euler characteristic. Therefore, in our study of singularities, it is necessary to examine manifolds with boundary. In particular, Guth
[
3
]
investigated immersions of surfaces with a singular boundary component, and he constructed a sharp lower bound on the number of self-intersections of the immersed boundary.

Additional contributions include the works of Gromov, Pignoni, M. Yamamoto, T. Yamamoto, and Ryabichev. In
[
2
]
, Gromov finds lower bounds on the topological complexity of the singular set of generic smooth maps. In
[
8
]
, Pignoni connected the genus of a surface with its orientability and the properties of the singular set of a map of the surface into the plane. In
[
14
]
, M. Yamamoto studied the winding numbers of boundaries of immersed surfaces, and in
[
15
]
, he uses the work of Eliashberg to calculate the number of singular set components of fold maps between closed oriented surfaces. In
[
16
]
, T. Yamamoto determines certain conditions for which a degree-
d
stable map to the plane or
2
-sphere admits a prescribed singular set, and in
[
17
]
he extends the work of Pignoni by examining weakly-admissibly homotopic maps of surfaces to the plane. In
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
, Ryabichev extends Eliashberg’s
h
-principle to first smooth maps of surfaces with cusps and folds, and second to arbitrary generic smooth maps of smooth manifolds.

Unless otherwise stated, all manifolds and maps between them are assumed to be smooth. While the main results of this paper pertain to fold singularities, the proofs we present rely on the immersion of surfaces with boundary immersed into the plane. First, we construct a lower bound on the number of self-intersections of immersed boundaries, and then we utilize this result to construct a lower bound on the number of self-intersections of fold singularities. We begin by generalizing a result of Guth
[
3
, Proposition 1]
to surfaces with multiple boundary components. Recall, a surface is called
non-planar
if every connected component has genus
g\geq 1
.

Theorem
5.12
.

Let
S
be an oriented, compact, non-planar, not necessarily path-connected, surface with boundary
\partial S
. Suppose that
\partial S
has
k
path components. If
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an immersion, then
f(\partial S)
has at least
4-k-\chi(S)
self-intersections. This estimate is sharp.

In order to state a lower bound on the number of self-intersections of fold singularities, we recall the following definitions.

Definition 1.1

(Singular Points)
.

Let
M
be an
m
-dimensional manifold and
N
be an
n
-dimensional manifold, with
m\geq n
. Also, let
f:M\rightarrow N
be a smooth map. We say that
x\in M
is
regular
if at point
x
, we have
\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{ker}(df))=m-n
. A point
x\in M
is
singular
if it is not regular. We denote the set of all singular points of a map by
\Sigma
, and the image of
\Sigma
by
f(\Sigma)
. We note that
\Sigma\subset M
and
f(\Sigma)\subset N
.

For this paper, we consider 2-dimensional manifolds, typically denoted by
S
, that are mapped to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
via
f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
where
f
only admits fold points. Recall that a
fold point
for
f
is a point
p
in
S
, with coordinate representation
p=(x_{1},x_{2})
, such that
{f(p)=f(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1},\pm x_{2}^{2})}
. We refer to all such maps as
fold maps
, and, in particular, we are interested in simple fold maps.

Definition 1.2

(Simple Fold Maps)
.

Similar to Saeki
[
11
]
and Sakuma
[
12
]
, we call a smooth map
f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
a
simple fold map
if it is a fold map such that for every singular point
x\in S
, the connected component of
f^{-1}(f(x))
containing
x
intersects the singular set of
f
only at
x
.

Definition 1.3

(Simple Stable Fold Maps)
.

A simple fold map is said to be
stable
if and only if the image of the singular set is an immersion with only normal (transverse double point) crossings.

Unless otherwise stated, we assume that all simple fold maps are stable. To this end, we omit the word “stable” when referring to simple stable fold maps, unambiguously calling them simple fold maps. Moreover, outlined in full detail in Section
6
, simple fold maps induce a splitting on a surface, wherein we view the surface as a collection of disjoint path components and where the fold singularities coincide with the boundaries of these path components.

Definition 1.4

(Induced Splitting)
.

Let
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a simple fold map of an oriented closed surface
S
. Let
S_{+}
(respectively,
S_{-}
) denote the closure in
S
of the set of points
x\in S
such that
d_{x}f
is a linear map preserving (respectively, reversing) orientation. We denote the
splitting
of
S
induced via
f
by
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
. Furthermore, we denote the number of components of
S_{+}
(respectively,
S_{-}
) by
\#|S_{+}|
(respectively,
\#|S_{-}|
).

The fold singularities are thus viewed as
\Sigma=S_{+}\cap S_{-}
, and the number of components of
\Sigma
is denoted by
|\Sigma|
. Surfaces with a prescribed splitting can also be viewed as graphs. More precisely, we associate a bipartite graph
G
with
f
where the vertices of
G
correspond to the interiors of the path components
S_{+}
and
S_{-}
. An edge in
G
between two vertices exists if and only if the path components share a common fold boundary. We are now in a position to state the main results.

Theorem
7.1
.

Let
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a simple fold map with splitting
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
that has no planar components whose fold singular set is
\Sigma=S_{+}\cap\,S_{-}
. If
\left|\#|S_{+}|-\#|S_{-}|\right|=n
, then the number of self-intersections of fold singularities has a lower bound given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\geq 4\max\left\{\#|S_{+}|,\#|S_{-}|\right\}-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)

\displaystyle=2(\rho(G)+1+n)-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)

where
|\Sigma|
is the number of path components of fold-singularities, and
\rho(G)
is the number of edges in a spanning tree of the graph
G
corresponding to
f
.

We also prove that this lower bound is sharp when
g
,
|\Sigma|
,
\#|S_{+}|
, and
\#|S_{-}|
are considered an
admissible combination
. Discussed in greater detail in Section
7.3
, an admissible combination of
g
,
|\Sigma|
,
\#|S_{+}|
, and
\#|S_{-}|
satisfies the conclusion of the following lemma.

Lemma
7.4
.

Let
S
be a closed, oriented surface of genus
g
. If
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a simple fold map with splitting
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
that has no planar components whose fold singular set, denoted by
\Sigma=S_{+}\cap S_{-}
, is nonempty, then the following are true:

(i)

g\geq 2

(ii)

g>|\Sigma|\geq 1

(iii)

|\Sigma|\geq\#|S_{+}|+\#|S_{-}|-1\geq 1

(iv)

|\Sigma|-g\equiv 1\mod 2

Theorem
7.6
.

Let
g
,
|\Sigma|
,
\#|S_{+}|
, and
\#|S_{-}|
be an admissible combination. If
S
is a closed, oriented surface with genus

, then there exists a simple fold map

with splitting

\Sigma=S_{+}\cap S_{-}
, such that the number of self-intersections of fold singularities is

\Delta_{\Sigma}=2(\rho(G)+1+n)-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)

where
|\Sigma|
is the number of path components of fold-singularities,
\rho(G)
is the number of edges in a spanning tree of the graph

, and
\left|\#|S_{+}|-\#|S_{-}|\right|=n
.

Finally, Theorem

and Theorem

can be restated in terms of the homology of the surface

yielding the following refinement of Gromov’s
[
2
, Section 2.1]
homological lower bound.

Corollary
7.8
.

Let
S
be a closed orientable surface and
f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
a simple fold map. Then,

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq 2(\rho(G)+n)+\frac{1}{2}|H_{*}(S)|-|\Sigma|

where
|\Sigma|
is the number of path components of fold-singularities,
\rho(G)
is the number of edges in a spanning tree of the graph

.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we define the notion of inner and outer components of boundaries of surfaces immersed in the plane, as well as review the winding number of a curve in the plane. We dedicate Section
3
to recall a renowned formula of Whitney, state its relevance to our paper, and provide examples of computing the winding number and verifying Whitney’s formula for specific curves of interest. Next, in Section
4
we define positive isotopy and concordance of curves, again providing examples relevant to this paper. In Section
5
, we state and prove a generalized version of Guth’s theorem. The purpose of Section
6
is to recall how weighted bipartite graphs are associated to simple stable fold maps. Finally, in Section
7
, we state and prove the main results of this paper. The paper concludes with two applications of our main results: a detailed example and a refinement of Gromov’s homological lower bound.

Codimension Zero Immersions

We begin by examining codimension zero immersions of an oriented
n
-manifold with boundary into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The specific focus of this paper will be the case
n=2
. Unless otherwise stated, we let
S
be an oriented, compact, connected manifold of dimension
n
with boundary
\partial S
, and
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a codimension zero, orientation preserving, immersion such that the restriction
f|_{\partial S}
is a codimension one self-transverse immersion of
\partial S
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note,
\partial S
may or may not be connected.

2.1.
Outer and inner components of the immersed boundary

The immersed submanifold
f(\partial S)
inherits a canonical coorientation. Namely, it is cooriented by the vector field
df(\nu)
, where
\nu
is an outward normal vector field in
TS
over
\partial S
. The submanifold
f(\partial S)
also inherits an orientation such that the normal coorientation of
f(\partial S)
followed by the orientation of
f(\partial S)
agrees with the standard orientation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1

(Inner and Outer points)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{k}
denote connected components of
\partial S
. Let
U(\gamma_{i})
denote the unique unbounded connected component of the complement to
f(\gamma_{i})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
x\in\partial U(\gamma_{i})
be a point that is not a double point of
f|_{\partial S}
. We say that
x
is
outer
if the coorientation of
f(\gamma_{i})
at
x
points in the interior direction of
U(\gamma_{i})
. Otherwise, we say that the point
x
is
inner
.

Definition 2.2

(Inner and Outer immersed boundaries)
.

We say that the entire immersed boundary component
f(\gamma_{i})
is
outer
if all non-double points of
\partial U(\gamma_{i})
are outer. Otherwise, we say that
f(\gamma_{i})
is
inner
. Note, that an inner component may have both inner points and outer points.

Let
p_{i}\in\gamma_{i}
such that
f(p_{i})
is a non-double point. We choose and fix a reference point
f(p_{i})
for each path component
f(\gamma_{i})
in such a way that if
f(\gamma_{i})
is an outer component, then
f(p_{i})
is outer, and if
f(\gamma_{i})
is an inner component, then
f(p_{i})
is inner. There is an alternate definition regarding whether a point is inner or outer.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
p_{i}
is a point on
\gamma_{i}
such that
f(p_{i})
is not a double point and the
n^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
f(p_{i})
assumes the minimum value. Then
f(p_{i})
is
outer
if the coorientation of
f(\gamma_{i})
at
f(p_{i})
agrees with
-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}
. Otherwise,
f(p_{i})
is
inner
.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}
be a point on
\gamma_{i}
such that the
n^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
f(p_{i})=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
attains a minimum value. Consider the
(n-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
\mathcal{P}
containing the point
f(p_{i})
that is parallel to the coordinate hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. The hyperplane
\mathcal{P}
splits
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into two regions:
R_{1}=\{(y_{1},\cdots y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~y_{n}>x_{n}\}
and
R_{2}=\{(y_{1},\cdots y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~y_{n}<x_{n}\}
. Since
x_{n}
is a minimum value for the
n^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
f(p_{i})
, we necessarily have that
f(\gamma_{i})\subset R_{1}\cup\mathcal{P}
and
f(\gamma_{i})\cap R_{2}=\emptyset
. We also note that
R_{2}\subset U(\gamma_{i})
.

Now, suppose the coorientation of
f(\gamma_{i})
at
f(p_{i})
agrees with
-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}
. In this case, the coorientation of
f(\gamma_{i})
points into the interior of

and thus points into the interior of
U(\gamma_{i})
, making
f(p_{i})
an outer point. Next, suppose the coorientation of
f(\gamma_{i})
at
f(p_{i})
agrees with
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}
. In this case, the coorentation of
f(\gamma_{i})
at
f(p_{i})
points away from the interior of
R_{2}
and therefore away from the interior of
U(\gamma_{i})
, making
f(p_{i})
an inner point.

2.2.
The Gauss Map and the Winding Number

Given an orientation preserving immersion
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a compact oriented manifold
S
of dimension
n
, we have seen that the boundary
\partial S
inherits a canonical orientation, while the immersed boundary
f(\partial S)
inherits a canonical coorientation. Let
\nu\colon\thinspace\partial S\to S^{n-1}
be a coorienting unit vector field over
f(\partial S)
. We say that
\nu
is a
Gauss map
. Since both
\partial S
and
S^{n-1}
are oriented and
f
is orientation preserving, the degree of the Gauss map
\nu
is well-defined. We emphasize that we do not assume that
\partial S
is path-connected, and define the degree of
\nu
as the algebraic number of preimages of a regular point of
\nu
.
We call the degree of
\nu
the
winding number
of the immersed boundary
\partial S
and denote it by
w(\partial S)
.

The winding number allows us to compare invariants of a manifold to the invariants of its boundary. The following theorem is a version of the Poincaré-Hopf Theorem, see
[
6
, Section 6]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

The winding number
w(\partial S)
equals the Euler characteristic
\chi(S)
of the manifold
S
.

3.
The Whitney Formula

The Whitney formula
[
13
]
allows us to relate the winding number of a closed, connected, oriented, immersed curve
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with its self-intersections. We will parameterize
\gamma
as
\gamma(t):S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by defining
S^{1}
as
[0,1]/\sim
, where
0
and
1
are identified. The base point,
\gamma(0)
is chosen following the conventions of Section
2.1
. That is, if
\gamma
is an outer (respectively, inner) curve, then
\gamma(0)
is an outer (respectively, inner) point.

A
self-intersection point
, or
double point
, of
\gamma(t)
is defined to be a pair
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<1
such that
\gamma(t_{1})=\gamma(t_{2})
. We emphasize that a generic curve
\gamma
is self-transverse, and has no triple self-intersection points, i.e., there are no distinct triples of points
t_{1},t_{2}
and
t_{3}
such that
\gamma(t_{1})=\gamma(t_{2})=\gamma(t_{3})
. We say that a self-intersection point
x=\gamma(t_{1})=\gamma(t_{2})
is
positive
if the ordered pair of vectors
(\dot{\gamma}(t_{1}),\dot{\gamma}(t_{2}))
forms a positive basis for
T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Otherwise the self-intersection point is
negative
. Note, the sign of a self-intersection point of
\gamma
essentially depends on the orientation of
\gamma
as well as on the base point
\gamma(0)
.

Let
n^{+}=n^{+}(\gamma)
denote the number of positive self-intersection points of
\gamma
, and
n^{-}=n^{-}(\gamma)
the number of negative self-intersection points. Additionally, let
i^{+}=i^{+}(\gamma)
equal 1 if
\gamma
is an outer curve and 0 if
\gamma
is an inner curve. Similarly, let
i^{-}=i^{-}(\gamma)
equal 1 if
\gamma
is an inner curve and 0 if
\gamma
is an outer curve. Lastly, let
w(\gamma)
denote the winding number of
\gamma
. With this notation, we may finally state the Whitney formula.

Theorem 3.1

(Whitney
[
13
]
)
.

If
\gamma
is a 1-dimensional, closed, connected, oriented, immersed curve with
n^{+}
positive self-intersection points and
n^{-}
negative self-intersection points, then the winding number of
\gamma
is
w(\gamma)=i^{+}-i^{-}+n^{-}-n^{+}
.

We comment that in Whitney’s paper
[
13
]
, the notions of positive and negative for self-intersections are opposite that of ours in this context. Since the winding number is additive over connected components, Whitney’s formula immediately gives the following.

Let
\gamma

where
I^{+}
(respectively,
I^{-}

N^{+}
(respectively,
N^{-}

4.
Positive Isotopy and Concordance of Curves

Recall that every oriented immersed curve
\gamma\colon\thinspace S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is canonically cooriented by a vector field such that, at every point on
\gamma
, the coorientation followed by the orientation forms a positive basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Definition 4.1

(Positive Isotopy)
.

Let
\gamma_{i}\colon\thinspace S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be two cooriented curves, for
i=0,1
. We say that
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
are
positively isotopic
if there is an immersion
f\colon\thinspace S^{1}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
f|_{S^{1}\times\{i\}}=\gamma_{i}
, and the coorientations of
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
agree with
df(\frac{\partial}{\partial t})
, where

5.
An extension of the Guth theorem/Pinon theorem

In an unpublished preprint, Guth determines the minimal number of self-intersection points of the boundary of an immersed surface. These immersions are considered
normal
, if they are smooth, generic maps, such that the double points of the immersed boundary intersect transversely.

Theorem 5.1

(Guth
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose

is a compact, oriented surface of genus

, with a single boundary component. If

is a normal immersion
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

has at least
2g+2
self-intersections. Moreover, for each
g
, there exists an immersion
f_{g}
such that
f_{g}(\partial S)
has exactly
2g+2
self-intersections.

We note that the Euler characteristic
\chi(S)
of a surface of genus

with one boundary component is
1-2g
. Thus, the estimate in the Guth theorem can also be written as
2g+2=3-\chi(S)
.

We now wish to extend this to a surface with multiple boundary components, and, to do this, we will utilize a result from Haefliger. Let
S
be a compact surface with boundary. The surface
S
as well as its boundary may or may not be path-connected. Let
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an immersion. Then, by
[
4
, Theorem 3]
, the winding number
w
of
f|\partial S
satisfies the condition
\chi(S)=w(f|\partial S)
. Recall, the generalized Whitney formula in Equation
1
. Using Haefliger’s result, we may rewrite the generalized Whitney formula as:

(2)

\chi(S)=I^{+}-I^{-}+N^{-}-N^{+}

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
S
be a compact, oriented, non-planar surface of genus
g
with
k\geq 1
boundary components and let
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a normal immersion. Then
N^{+}\geq N^{-}
and
N^{+}\equiv N^{-}\mod 2
.
Moreover, the number

\Delta=N^{+}+N^{-}+N^{+}_{ij}+N^{-}_{ij}
of self-intersection points of
f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is even.

Proof.

Since
I^{-}=k-I^{+}
and
\chi(S)=2-2g-k
,
the generalized Whitney formula, Equation

, may be re-written as

N^{+}-N^{-}=2g+2I^{+}-2.

Since

and

. Finally, we recall that

∎

5.1.

In order to extend Guth’s theorem to surfaces with multiple boundary components, we need to analyze numerous cases. These cases depend on both the number of immersed outer boundaries and whether they are embedded or not. We begin with the case where there is at least one immersed outer boundary with self-intersections. While not called an immersed outer boundary by Guth, if the surface has only one boundary component, then it must be an immersed outer boundary and have at least one negative self-intersection.

If

If

Definition 5.6

(Inner and Outer Arcs)
.

Given an immersed boundary component
\gamma
, a
boundary arc
of
\gamma
is defined as a maximal section of
\partial U(\gamma)
between self-intersection points of
\gamma
that contains no self-intersections. Recall the definition of inner and outer from Section
2.1
. A boundary arc will be denoted as an
inner arc
, respectively an
outer arc
, if all points on the boundary arc are inner points, respectively outer points. Note, a boundary arc cannot have both inner and outer points. Furthermore, we emphasize that if a curve is an inner curve then it must have at least one inner arc.

Definition 5.7

(Bounded Arcs)
.

Let
\gamma
be an immersed boundary with
k
connected components
\gamma_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. An inner arc
\alpha
on
\gamma_{i}
is said to be
bounded
if for every point
x\in\alpha
, every ray starting at
x
, pointing in the direction opposite the coorientation of
\gamma_{i}
toward infinity, passes through an outer arc. In particular, this outer arc is not on
\gamma_{i}
.

Remark 5.8
.

We emphasize that for an inner arc to be bounded, there must be a bounding outer arc on a
different
connected component. Recall an inner arc on
\gamma_{i}
is comprised of only inner points, and, by Definition
2.1
, inner points are those that exist on
\partial U(\gamma_{i})
. Therefore, for any inner point
x
on
\gamma_{i}
, the ray starting at
x
, pointing in the direction opposite the coorientation of
\gamma_{i}
toward infinity, does not intersect any other point of
\gamma_{i}
. If such an intersection exists, then
x
is not on
\partial U(\gamma_{i})
and is not an inner point.

Lemma 5.9
.

Suppose
S
is an orientable compact surface with
k>0
boundary components immersed into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. All inner arcs of the immersed boundary components of
S
must be bounded.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that an arc
\alpha
of
\gamma_{i}
is unbounded. Then, there exists a point
x\in\alpha
such that the ray from
x
to infinity passes through no outer arcs. We then have two cases to consider: either the ray passes through no arcs, or it passes through only inner arcs. If the ray passes through no arcs, then the compactness of
S
is contradicted since this implies that every point of the unbounded region is the image of a compact surface. If the ray passes through only inner arcs, then consider the last inner arc the ray passes through. We then apply the same argument as before, contradicting the compactness of
S
.
∎

We are now in a position to finish our case of only immersed inner boundaries. Inner curves must have at least one inner arc, and each inner arc must be bounded. Thus, there must be at least two immersed inner boundaries that have at least one outer arc each. We will now show that for an inner curve to have both inner arcs and outer arcs, it must contain at least one negative self-intersection.

Remark 6.4

(Weights on the Graph)

A simple fold map

,

Lemma 6.5
.

If
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a simple fold map of an oriented closed surface with a splitting
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
as above, then the following are true:

(i)

\chi(S)=\chi(S_{+})+\chi(S_{-})

(ii)

\chi(S_{+})=\chi(S_{-})

(iii)

\#|S_{+}|-\#|S_{-}|=\displaystyle\sum_{i}g_{+}^{i}-\displaystyle\sum_{j}g_{-}^{j}

where
g_{+}^{i}
, respectively
g_{-}^{j}
, is the genus of each path component
S_{+}^{i}
, respectively
S_{-}^{j}
, and
1\leq i\leq\#|S_{+}|
and
1\leq j\leq\#|S_{-}|
.

Proof.

Part (
i
) is immediate since
\chi(S)=\chi(S_{+})+\chi(S_{-})-\chi(\Sigma)
and
\Sigma
is a collection of circles.

Part
(ii)
is the application of a result of Èliašberg
[
1
, Theorem B]
to our specific case and constructions. Èliašberg proved that for a one-dimensional submanifold
V
of a closed oriented surface
S
, there exists an immersion
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\Sigma=V
if and only if
V
splits
S
into two submanifolds
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, such that
\partial S_{1}=\partial S_{2}
and
\chi(S_{1})=\chi(S_{2})
. In our context, we have an immersion
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
with singular set
\Sigma
if and only if
\Sigma=\partial S_{+}=\partial S_{-}
and
\chi(S_{+})=\chi(S_{-})
.

Part
(iii)
is a consequence of part
(ii)
. Indeed, since
\chi(S_{+})=\chi(S_{-})
we see:

\displaystyle\chi(S_{+})

\displaystyle=\chi(S_{-})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{i}\chi(S_{+}^{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\chi(S_{-}^{j})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 2(\#|S_{+}|)-|\Sigma|-\sum_{i}2g_{+}^{i}

\displaystyle=2(\#|S_{-}|)-|\Sigma|-\sum_{j}2g_{-}^{j}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\#|S_{+}|-\#|S_{-}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}g_{+}^{i}-\sum_{j}g_{-}^{j}

∎

To construct a minimum for
\Delta_{\Sigma}
, we now examine the path components
\{S_{+}^{i}\}
and
\{S_{-}^{j}\}
of the splitting. Note, each path component is an oriented surface with boundary immersed via
f
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus, using methods from Section
5
, we may examine the count,
\Delta_{+}^{i}
, respectively
\Delta_{-}^{j}
, of self-intersections of the boundaries of each path component
f(S^{i}_{+})
, respectively
f(S^{j}_{-})
.

Lemma 6.6
.

If
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a simple fold map with splitting
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
and fold singular set
\Sigma=S_{+}\cap S_{-}
, then the number of self-intersections of the fold singularities is equal to the sum of all self-intersections of the boundaries divided by two.

\Delta_{\Sigma}=\sum_{i}\Delta_{+}^{i}=\sum_{j}\Delta_{-}^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i}\Delta_{+}^{i}+\sum_{j}\Delta_{-}^{j}\right)

Proof.

Let us first observe that the number of self-intersections of
f(\Sigma)
, denoted by
\Delta_{\Sigma}
, is equal to the sum of the self-intersections of each of the path components of
f(\Sigma)
. Each path component of
f(\Sigma)
is represented by an edge
(e_{i}^{j})_{m}
in the graph
G
. Therefore,
\Delta_{\Sigma}
is the sum of
n_{+}((e_{i}^{j})_{m})
and
n_{-}((e_{i}^{j})_{m})
across all edges:

\Delta_{\Sigma}=\sum_{i,j,m}n_{+}((e_{i}^{j})_{m})+n_{-}((e_{i}^{j})_{m})

Second, we examine the self-intersections of boundaries. If two path components share a boundary, i.e.
S_{+}\cap S_{-}\neq\emptyset
, we call them
adjacent
path components. Given two adjacent path components
S_{+}^{i}
and
S_{-}^{j}
denote
\partial_{i}^{j}
as the sum of all self-intersections of all boundary path components between
S_{+}^{i}
and
S_{-}^{j}
.

\partial_{i}^{j}=\sum_{m=1}^{|e_{i}^{j}|}n_{+}((e_{i}^{j})_{m})+n_{-}((e_{i}^{j})_{m})

Recall that
\Delta_{+}^{i}
represents the number of self-intersections of
all
boundaries on the path component
S_{+}^{i}
. For a fixed
i
let
J
be an indexing set representing all path components
S_{-}^{j}
that are adjacent to
S_{+}^{i}
. Summing across all boundaries between all adjacent path components, we have that:

\Delta_{+}^{i}=\sum_{j\in J}\partial_{i}^{j}

A analogous equation can also be made for
\Delta_{-}^{j}
. From here, it is clear to see that by summing across all
\Delta_{+}^{i}
we have calculated
\Delta_{\Sigma}
.
∎

The following lemma extends the results of Theorem
5.12
to the self-intersections of fold singularities.

Lemma 6.7
.

If
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a simple fold map with splitting
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
that has no planar components whose fold singular set is
\Sigma=S_{+}\cap\,S_{-}
, then the number of self-intersections of fold singularities has a lower bound given by

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq\max\left\{\sum_{i}2g_{+}^{i}+2,\sum_{j}2g_{-}^{j}+2\right\}

where
g_{+}^{i}
, respectively
g_{-}^{j}
, is the genus of each path component
S_{+}^{i}
, respectively
S_{-}^{j}
.

Proof.

The path components of the splitting
S^{i}_{+}
and
S^{j}_{-}
are each oriented surfaces with boundary immersed via
f
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Recall,
\Delta_{+}^{i}
, respectively
\Delta_{-}^{j}
, denotes the count of self-intersections of the boundaries of
S^{i}_{+}
, respectively
S^{j}_{-}
. By Theorem
5.12
, we have a lower bound for the number of self-intersections of the boundaries of
S^{i}_{+}
and
S^{j}_{-}
:

\Delta_{+}^{i}\geq 2g_{+}^{i}+2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta_{-}^{j}\geq 2g_{-}^{j}+2

Utilizing this fact and Lemma
6.6
, we see:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Delta_{+}^{i}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\Delta_{-}^{j}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i}2g_{+}^{i}+2

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j}2g_{-}^{j}+2

Thus,
\Delta_{\Sigma}
must be greater than or equal to both of its lower bounds. Hence, we can clearly see that:

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq\max\left\{\sum_{i}2g_{+}^{i}+2,\sum_{j}2g_{-}^{j}+2\right\}

∎

7.
The Main Theorems

We are now equipped to state and prove the main results of the paper.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
f\colon\thinspace S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a simple fold map with splitting
S=S_{+}\cup S_{-}
that has no planar components whose fold singular set is
\Sigma=S_{+}\cap\,S_{-}
. If
\left|\#|S_{+}|-\#|S_{-}|\right|=n
, then the number of self-intersections of fold singularities

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\geq 4\max\left\{\#|S_{+}|,\#|S_{-}|\right\}-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)

\displaystyle=2(\rho(G)+1+n)-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)

where
|\Sigma|
is the number of path components of fold-singularities, and
\rho(G)
is the number of edges in a spanning tree of the graph
G
corresponding to
f
.

Proof.

Recall, each path component of the splitting
S_{+}^{i}
, respectively
S_{-}^{j}
, is an immersed surface with boundary. Let
g_{+}^{i}
,
\chi(S_{+}^{i})
, and
k_{+}^{i}
denote the genus, Euler characteristic, and number of boundary components of
S_{+}^{i}
respectively, and let
g_{-}^{j}
,
\chi(S_{-}^{j})
, and
k_{-}^{j}
denote the analogous properties of
S_{-}^{j}
. We note that
\sum_{i}k_{+}^{i}=\sum_{j}k_{-}^{j}=|\Sigma|
, since these boundaries are the fold singularities. Furthermore, note that for each path component
S_{+}^{i}
, respectively
S_{-}^{j}
, we have
2g_{+}^{i}+2=4-k_{+}^{i}-\chi(S_{+}^{i})
, respectively
2g_{-}^{j}+2=4-k_{-}^{j}-\chi(S_{-}^{j})
. Also, by Lemma
6.5
,
\chi(S_{+})=\chi(S_{-})
and
\chi(S)=\chi(S_{+})+\chi(S_{-})
. Thus, Lemma
6.7
may be restated as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\sum_{i}2g_{+}^{i}+2,\sum_{j}2g_{-}^{j}+2\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sum_{i}(4-k_{+}^{i}-\chi(S_{+}^{i})),\sum_{j}(4-k_{-}^{j}-\chi(S_{-}^{j}))\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{4(\#|S_{+}|)-|\Sigma|-\chi(S_{+}),4(\#|S_{-}|)-|\Sigma|-\chi(S_{-})\right\}

\displaystyle=4\max\left\{\#|S_{+}|,\#|S_{-}|\right\}-|\Sigma|-\frac{1}{2}\chi(S)

Finally, recall that
\rho(G)
is the number of edges in a spanning tree of the graph
G
corresponding to
f
and, for bipartite graphs associated with the splitting of a surface, is one less than the total number of path components of the splitting; that is
\rho(G)=\#|S_{+}|+\#|S_{-}|-1
. Therefore, if
\left|\#|S_{+}|-\#|S_{-}|\right|=n
we can see that
2(\rho(G)+1+n)=4\max\left\{\#|S_{+}|,\#|S_{-}|\right\}
. Thus, we conclude

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq 2(\rho(G)+1+n)-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)

∎

Corollary 7.2
.

If

that has no planar components whose fold singular set is

Suppose the surface

, we see:

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq 2(\rho(G)+1+n)-(\chi(S)/2+|\Sigma|)=2(g+0)-((1-g)+(g-1))=2g.

∎

7.1.
Relating Sharp Lower Bound to the Homology of the Surface

Let
|H_{*}(S)|
be the sum of Betti numbers of the closed orientable surface
S
. In
[
2
, Section 2.1]
, Gromov proved the following inequality

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq\frac{1}{2}|H_{*}(S)|-|\Sigma|

We wish to compare our result to Gromov’s.
Note, for closed, oriented surfaces of finite genus
g
, we have
|H_{*}(S)|=2g+2

\displaystyle\chi(S)=2-2g

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 2g+2+\chi(S)=4

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|H_{*}(S)|+\chi(S)=4

Substituting, we see

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\geq 2(\rho(G)+n+1)-(\frac{1}{2}\chi(S)+|\Sigma|)

\displaystyle=2\rho(G)+2n+2-\frac{1}{2}(4-|H_{*}(S)|)-|\Sigma|

\displaystyle=2(\rho(G)+n)+\frac{1}{2}|H_{*}(S)|-|\Sigma|

This yields the following refinement of Gromov’s homological lower bound.

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
S
be a closed orientable surface and
f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
a simple fold map. Then,

\Delta_{\Sigma}\geq 2(\rho(G)+n)+\frac{1}{2}|H_{*}(S)|-|\Sigma|

where
|\Sigma|
is the number of path components of fold-singularities,
\rho(G)
is the number of edges in a spanning tree of the graph
G
corresponding to
f
,
\Delta_{\Sigma}
is the number of self-intersections of fold singularities, and
|H_{*}(S)|
denotes the sum of the Betti numbers of
S
.
