Bacterial phospholipid selective quaternary phosphonium polymers with potent antibacterial activity
Authors: Tianjing Wang, Xiangchen An, Fengming Dong, Hongying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCationic polymers hold strong promise for treating bacterial infections. Most reported cationic antibacterial polymers incorporate nitrogen-containing moieties, including primary, secondary, or quaternary amine group, whereas phosphonium-based antibacterial polymer was less reported and their structure–activity relationships have not been systematically elucidated. In this work, a series of polymers bearing different quaternary phosphonium side chains (CP-n) were synthesized. Among these polymers, the polymer with tripropylphosphine side group (brief named as CP-3) shown best antibacterial activity (MIC = 2 μg/mL against S. aureus) and highest selectivity index (SI = 100). Mechanistic study further demonstrated that, compared with the zwitterionic phosphocholine (DOPC) in mammalian membranes, CP-3 can selectively bind to the anionic bacterial-specific phospholipids phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) through electrostatic interactions, thereby effectively disrupt bacterial membrane integrity. Due to the unique bacterial phospholipids targeting ability and membrane damaging mechanism, CP-3 demonstrates rapid bactericidal kinetics with low tendency to induce bacterial resistance. Notably, CP-3 effectively inhibits S. aureus biofilm formation and disrupts mature S. aureus biofilms. Furthermore, CP-3 demonstrated strong therapeutic potential in MRSA-induced lung infection and peritonitis. Hence, this work provides a new strategy for the design of phosphonium-based antibacterial polymers.Keywords: Antibacterial polymer, Bacterial resistance, Bacterial biofilm, Phospholipids selective, Poly (α-amino acids)

Introduction

Bacterial infections, including those caused by antibiotic-resistant organisms, impose a substantial burden on global health and economies, with drug-resistant pathogens accounting for an estimated 4.95 million deaths associated with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in 2019, including 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to AMR [[1], [2], [3]]. However, the rate of antibacterial innovation has failed to match the accelerating rise in bacterial resistance. The WHO reviews and independent pipeline audits consistently report a scarcity of truly novel, first-in-class agents advancing against priority pathogens, thereby widening the gap between clinical need and available therapies [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Beyond acquired resistance in planktonic populations, biofilm-associated infections are intrinsically tolerant to a wide range of antibiotics owing to extracellular-matrix shielding nutrient limitation with reduced metabolic activity and persisted-bacterial cell formation and these features complicate eradication and drive chronic relapsing disease [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Collectively, there is an urgent need for novel antibacterial agents with a reduced propensity for bacterial resistance development and effective bacterial biofilm eradication ability.

The bacterial cytoplasmic membrane performs essential functions in barrier integrity, energy transduction, nutrient transport, and stress sensing, and is therefore a critical determinant of viability [[13], [14], [15]]. Phospholipids are the principal constituents of membranes [16,17]. Notably, the phospholipid composition of bacterial membranes differs markedly from that of mammalian cell membranes [18,19]. Quantitatively, negative charged 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) constitutes roughly 20–25% of total phospholipids in Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and can reach 50–60% in Gram-positive species like Staphylococcus aureus [[20], [21], [22]]. Owing to the high levels of POPG, bacterial membranes generally display obvious negative charge. In sharp contrast, the exoplasmic leaflet of mammalian plasma membranes is enriched in zwitterionic choline-headgroup phospholipids, predominantly phosphatidylcholine (DOPC) and sphingomyelin, yielding an approximately neutral net surface charge [[23], [24], [25]]. Considering this distinctive difference in membrane charge of bacteria and mammalian cells, some cationic antimicrobial polymers have been designed to target anionic bacterial membranes, achieving good antibacterial activity while exhibiting low cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. According to the published literature, most cationic antibacterial polymers have nitrogen-containing moieties, including primary, secondary amines, and quaternary ammonium groups [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. In addition, sulfonium-based polymers have also shown promising antibacterial and antibiofilm activity [[42], [43], [44]]. However, phosphonium-based antibacterial polymers were less reported [36,45]. There is still a lack of systematic studies on how to regulate the structure of phosphonium groups to modulate their antibacterial properties and biocompatibility.

In this study, a series of cationic polymers with different quaternary phosphonium side chains was synthesized via N-carboxy anhydride (NCA) ring-opening polymerization (ROP) followed by post-polymerization modification, and their structure–activity relationships were systematically elucidated. Among the synthesized polymers, CP-3 (bearing tripropylphosphine side group) demonstrated the best antibacterial activity and highest selectivity index. Mechanistic studies showed that CP-3 can selectively bind to the anionic phospholipids POPG on bacterial membranes, resulting in bacterial membrane disruption, rapid bactericidal kinetics, and low propensity to induce bacterial resistance. In addition, CP-3 effectively inhibits bacterial biofilm formation and disrupts mature biofilms. Finally, the in vivo antibacterial efficacy of CP-3 was validated in both local and systemic bacterial infection models.

Experimental section

Synthesis of cationic polymers (CP-n) with quaternary phosphonium side chains

PCLG (43.3 mg, containing 0.175 mmol of chloro groups) was dissolved in DMF (2.0 mL). NaI (78.5 mg, 0.525 mmol) was dissolved separately in acetonitrile (1.0 mL). The two solutions were combined in a round-bottom flask, followed by the addition of trimethylphosphine (51.5 μL, 0.35 mmol). The reaction mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 48 h. After completion, most of the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. An aqueous NaCl solution (1.0 M, 2.5 mL) was then added, and the suspension was stirred at room temperature for 3 h, followed by purification in deionized water for 3 days. Finally, the product (CP-1) was isolated as a white powder by lyophilization (yield: 57%). CP-2, CP-3, and CP-4 were synthesized according to the same procedure.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) measurement

MICs of the CP-n and antibiotics (vancomycin or norfloxacin) against bacteria were determined by the broth microdilution method. Bacterial cultures were incubated in MH broth at 37 °C overnight. The bacteria were washed with PBS two times and adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU mL−1. Then, the bacterial solution was incubated with each agent at 37 °C for 24 h. The MIC was the lowest concentration with no increase in OD600. Broth with bacteria but without drug served as the negative control. Each MIC measurement was performed in triplicate.

The MBC assay was conducted according to previously described literature. After the MIC assay, 100 μL bacterial solution from each selected well of the 96-well plate was spread evenly onto the surface of MH agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by colony counting. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no colonies were observed.

Zeta potential test

The bacteria were cultured at 37 °C overnight. The bacteria were washed twice with PBS, and the suspension was adjusted to OD600 = 0.5. The polymer solutions with different concentrations were incubated with the bacterial suspension in microcentrifuge tubes for 30 min. The suspension was removed by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in PBS. 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was transferred to the sample well, and the zeta potential was determined using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Mouse lung infection

The infection model was established according to previously reported procedures, with minor modifications. Briefly, female ICR mice (18–22 g) were rendered neutropenic by intraperitoneal cyclophosphamide (150 mg/kg on day −5 and 100 mg/kg on day −3). On day 0, mice were anesthetized and intratracheally instilled with 50 μL of MRSA suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL). At 24 h post-infection, mice were randomized into three groups and given a single intratracheal dose of PBS, CP-3 (1 mg/mL), or vancomycin (1 mg/mL). At 24 h post-treatment, lungs were harvested, homogenized, serially diluted, and plated on MH agar, colonies were counted after incubation at 37 °C for 14 h to quantify bacterial burden (CFU per gram of lung). Representative lung tissues were further processed for histopathological evaluation using standard staining protocols. For survival analysis, infected mice were treated as above and monitored daily for up to 8 days, with survival recorded.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CP-n

The synthetic route to of polymers with varying quaternary phosphonium salt groups (brief named as CP-n) is shown in Scheme 1. Briefly, PCLG was first prepared by ring-opening polymerization (ROP) of γ-(6-chlorohexyl)-L-glutamate (CLG-NCA, Fig. 1A) and the phosphonium monomers were subsequently introduced onto the pendant chloroalkyl groups via post-polymerization modification. As shown in Fig. 1B, the peak at 0.89 ppm was assigned to the methyl protons of the hexylamine segment, while the peaks at 4.72, 4.19, 3.57, 2.56, 2.23, 1.81, and 1.50 ppm were attributed to the protons of the γ-(6-chlorohexyl)-L-glutamate units, confirming the successful preparation of PCLG. On the basis of the 1H NMR spectrum (Fig. 1B), the degree of polymerization (DP) of CLG in PCLG was calculated to be 23 from the integral ratio of characteristic peak b to characteristic peak a. According to gel permeation chromatography (GPC) test (Fig. 1D), PCLG exhibits a number-average molecular weight of 4800 g mol−1 and a narrow molecular weight distribution (Đ = 1.21). CP-n bearing various quaternary phosphonium functional groups were obtained through a one-step reaction of the PCLG pendant chlorohexyl groups with different phosphonium monomers. In the 1H NMR spectra (Figures S1-S3, 1C), the appearance of new proton resonance peaks f, h, and k, characteristic of the phosphonium monomers, demonstrated the successful grafting onto the CP-n backbone. The quantitative analysis of the 1H NMR spectra indicated complete conversion of the pendant chlorohexyl groups, with modification degrees of 100%. GPC result (Fig. 1D) also confirmed that CP-n were successful synthesized with a narrowly distributed molecular weight (Đ<1.2). The ClogP values of the CP-n polymers were measured to evaluate their hydrophobicity. As shown in Table S1, the ClogP values increase from −3.48 (CP-1) to 1.28 (CP-4) as the polymer structure changes. This trend indicates that CP-4 possesses a highest hydrophobicity. In addition, a polymer bearing quaternary ammonium side chains was also synthesized and used as a control in the subsequent experiments (Fig. S4).

In vitro antibacterial activity and selectivity index

The antibacterial activity of CP-n was evaluated by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values against various bacterial strains including clinically isolated multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus. As shown in Fig. 2A, CP-1, CP-2, and CP-3 exhibited certain antibacterial activity against the tested bacteria. In addition, the structure of the side-chain substituents had a pronounced effect on their antibacterial performance. When the alkyl chain length of the side groups increased, the antibacterial activity of the CP-n was markedly enhanced. Among all the polymers, CP-3 (bearing tripropylphosphine side group) demonstrated the best antibacterial activity, with MIC values of 2 μg/mL and 4 μg/mL against S. aureus and E. coli, respectively. CP-3 also displayed potent activity against clinically antibiotic-resistant strains, with MIC values of 2 μg/mL against MRSA and MDR S. aureus. The superior antibacterial performance of CP-3 may be attributed to the long hydrophobic alkyl chain of tripropylphosphine group, which can effectively interact with the phospholipid tails in the bacterial membrane, thereby inducing physical disruption of the membrane [35,46]. However, further increasing the alkyl chain length from propyl to butyl groups led to poor water solubility of the resulting polymer (CP-4), which consequently precipitated in water (Fig. S5), thereby exhibiting reduced antibacterial activity. In addition, to demonstrate the application potential of phosphorus-containing polymers, we synthesized the quaternary ammonium analogue of CP-3 and denoted it as CN-3. As shown in Table S2, CP-3 showed superior antibacterial activity, better cytocompatibility, and a higher selectivity index than CN-3, further confirming the promising potential of phosphorus-based polymers for antibacterial applications.

To further verify the antibacterial activity of CP-n, bacterial growth curves were recorded after incubation with CP-n at varying concentrations. As shown in Fig. 2B–D, growth of S. aureus was completely inhibited by CP-3 at a concentration of 2 μg/mL, whereas CP-1 and CP-2 exhibited no inhibitory effect on S. aureus at the same concentration. CP-3 also exhibited significant inhibition of E. coli growth at a concentration of 4 μg/mL (Fig. 2E). In addition, a zone of inhibition assay was performed. As demonstrated in Fig. 2F and G, compared with PBS group, obvious inhibition zones against S. aureus and E. coli were observed in CP-3 treated groups at concentrations of 2× and 4× MIC, further confirming the potent antibacterial ability of CP-3.

Antibacterial agents are inevitably exposed to blood in practical applications. Therefore, the hemolytic activity of the CP-n was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2H, all polymers exhibited low hemolytic activity, with hemolysis levels remaining below 50% even at the high concentration of 2 mg/mL. The selectivity index (SI) was defined as HC50/MICS. aureus, providing a quantitative measure of bacterial versus mammalian cell selectivity, whereby higher SI values indicate a more favorable activity profile. As demonstrated in Fig. 2I, among all the polymers, CP-3 exhibited the highest selectivity index of 1000 against S. aureus. We further assessed the biocompatibility of CP-n through MTT assay. As illustrated in Fig. 2J, with increasing hydrophobicity of the side-chain groups, the cytotoxicity of the polymers showed a slight increase. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of CP-1, CP-2, and CP-3 toward HUVEC cells were 278, 254, and 179 μg/mL, respectively. Subsequently, the SI was calculated through IC50/MICS. aureus. As shown in Fig. 2K, compared with CP-1 and CP-2, CP-3 displayed the highest selectivity index of 89.5 against S. aureus, further demonstrating its satisfied selectivity ability toward bacteria. Owing to its potent antibacterial activity and highest bacterial selectivity index, CP-3 was selected as the optimal antibacterial polymer for further investigation.

Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of phosphonium groups in disrupting bacterial cells and exhibiting antibacterial activity [47]. However, challenges persist regarding their stability under physiological conditions. Thus, we investigated the antibacterial activity of CP-3 under different physiological conditions (fluctuating pH, abundant proteases, and a high-salt microenvironment). As shown in Fig. 2L–N, CP-3 exhibited stable antibacterial activity under the different microenvironmental conditions, with its MIC and MBC values remaining unchanged. In sharp contrast, the natural antimicrobial peptide melittin showed markedly reduced antibacterial activity after pretreatment with trypsin or proteinase K, with its MIC value increasing from 5 μg/mL to 50 μg/mL. Additionally, as shown in Fig. S6, the MIC of CP-3 against S. aureus remains unchanged after treatment with 10% fetal bovine serum, further demonstrating its stable antibacterial performance. In summary, CP-3 exhibits potent antibacterial activity, highest selectivity index, and stable antibacterial efficacy under complex environmental, highlighting its strong potential as an antibacterial agent for practical applications (Fig. 2O).

Antibacterial mechanism assay

It has been reported that cationic polymers exert bactericidal effects primarily by compromising bacterial membrane integrity [48,49]. The antibacterial mechanism of CP-3 was first examined by zeta-potential measurements. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, the bacterial surface potential was progressively shifted toward positive values with increasing CP-3 concentrations, indicating that cationic CP-3 can effectively binding to negatively charged bacteria surface through electrostatic interactions. Membrane depolarization was then assessed using DiSC3(5) as a membrane-potential probe. As shown in Fig. 3C and D, fluorescence intensity was markedly increased in a concentration-dependent manner, demonstrating that CP-3 induced bacterial membrane depolarization, which mainly attributable to insertion of hydrophobic side chains into the bilayer and consequent structural disruption [50]. Bacterial membrane damage was evaluated by quantifying the release of intracellular constituents. Following exposure to CP-3 across a range of concentrations, substantial amounts of DNA, ATP, K+, and protein were detected in the bacterial supernatants (Fig. 3E–H).

To further confirm the membrane-disruption mechanism of CP-3, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed. As demonstrated in Fig. 3I and J, clear edges and intact surfaces were observed for PBS-treated bacteria. In sharp contrast, CP-3 treated bacteria were characterized by pronounced membrane collapse and deformation with concomitant cytoplasmic leakage. These morphological features are consistent with CP-3 exerting bactericidal activity through membrane disruption. Live/dead staining assay was also conducted to confirm the membrane‐lysis ability of CP-3. SYTO 9 labels nucleic acids in cells with either intact or damaged membranes, whereas PI penetrates only compromised membranes, binds DNA, and yields red fluorescence [51]. Strong red fluorescence was observed after 2 min of co-incubation of CP-3 and bacteria (Fig. 3K), further demonstrating the rapid and effective bacterial membrane-lytic ability of CP-3. Taken together, the above results suggest that the antibacterial mechanism of CP-3 mirrors that of many natural or synthetic AMPs, the positively charged CP-3 engages the negatively charged bacterial membrane through electrostatic interactions, leading to membrane disruption, permeabilization, and subsequent lysis (Fig. 3L).

In contrast to normal mammalian cells, whose plasma membranes are rich in zwitterionic phospholipids such as DOPC, bacterial cytoplasmic membranes contain higher levels of the anionic lipid POPG (Fig. 4A and B) [16,52]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the cationic CP-3 can disrupt the integrity of bacterial membrane through binding to negatively charged POPG. CP-3 was co-incubated with POPG and DOPC at varying molar ratios to assess phospholipids-dependent changes in antibacterial activity. Bacterial viability was quantified using the resazurin sodium salt assay, in which resazurin (blue) is metabolically reduced by live cells to fluorescent resorufin (pink) in the presence of bacterial growth. As shown in Fig. 4C, with increasing fractions of POPG in the mixed solution, a gradual increase in MIC value was detected of CP-3. When the phospholipid to CP-3 molar ratio was 10:1, the MIC values of CP-3 against S. aureus and E. coli increased by 8- and 16-fold, respectively (Fig. 4D and E). However, after co-incubation of DOPC with CP-3, the antibacterial activity of CP-3 showed no change (MIC values consistently remained at 2 μg/mL). The above result indicated that CP-3 can effectively bind to POPG on bacterial membranes, thereby exhibiting strong bacterial membrane-lytic activity.

In order to confirm the interaction between CP-3 and phospholipid, Cy5–CP-3 was incubated with different concentrations of POPG or DOPC, and fluorescence changes were recorded. As shown in Fig. 4F, an obvious blue shift (∼8 nm) of the emission maximum (λem) was observed for Cy5–CP-3 in POPG mixed solution relative to water. It was inferred that, upon co-incubation with POPG, the Cy5 moiety conjugated to CP-3 was relocated from a less polar environment to the more polar surface of POPG. Moreover, as the concentration of POPG liposomes was increased, the fluorescence intensity of Cy5–CP-3 was markedly decreased, which was consistent with Dexter quenching mechanism [53]. Since Dexter quenching occurs only when the donor and acceptor are sufficiently close to allow orbital overlap, effective binding of Cy5–CP-3 to POPG was thereby confirmed. In contrast, no changes in either λem or fluorescence intensity were detected when Cy5–CP-3 was incubated with DOPC over a range of concentrations, indicating the weak interaction between CP-3 and DOPC (Fig. 4G). To further verify the binding ability between CP-3 and phospholipids, ITC assay was conducted. As indicated in Fig. 4H and I, CP-3 displayed a strong interaction with POPG (Kd = 6.21 × 10−5 M). In contrast, the interaction between CP-3 and DOPC was weak and did not produce a measurable Kd. Taken together, the strong interactions of CP-3 with bacterial-specific phospholipids POPG, combined with its weak affinity for DOPC, are the main reasons for its potent antibacterial activity and low cytotoxicity (Fig. 4J). Considering the differences in lipid compositions between E. coli and S. aureus, we used different lipid ratios of POPE and POPG to simulate the inner membrane of E. coli and the cell membrane of S. aureus for dye leakage assays. As shown in Fig. S7, CP-3 rapidly induced dye leakage from liposomes mimicking the S. aureus membrane structure within 30 min. In contrast, CP-3 showed limited dye leakage in the E. coli mimicking liposomes, demonstrating its weaker disruptive ability. These results further support the observation that the polymer exhibits stronger antibacterial activity against S. aureus and slightly weaker activity against E. coli due to its limited effect on the inner membrane.

Owing to the distinctive membrane lytic mechanism, the time kill kinetics of CP-3 against bacterial were characterized. As shown in Fig. 4K and L, compared with the clinically used antibiotics vancomycin and norfloxacin, CP-3 exhibits rapid bactericidal kinetics, resulting in a 100% killing rate within 30 min. Compared with intrinsic resistance, acquired resistance, often driven by the excessive use of antibiotics and other antimicrobials, represents the greater clinical threat [54,55]. Accordingly, the potential of CP-3 to induce bacterial resistance under continuous exposure was investigated. Vancomycin and norfloxacin were used as control groups. Cultures were maintained for 20 sequential passages at subinhibitory (sub-MIC) concentrations of CP-3 or antibiotics, with MIC values measured at each passage. As displayed in Fig. 4M and N, after 20 serial passages under continuous exposure, CP-3 did not induce resistance in either S. aureus or E. coli, whereas the MICs of vancomycin and norfloxacin increased to 16-fold and 8-fold, respectively. In addition, we found that CP-3 treated S. aureus and E. coli did not exhibit cross-resistance to common antibiotics. In contrast, S. aureus treated with vancomycin showed moderate resistance to teicoplanin and daptomycin, while E. coli treated with norfloxacin demonstrated a certain level of cross-resistance to ampicillin (Fig. S8). Owing to the potent antibacterial activity, rapid time–kill kinetics, and low tendency to induce bacterial resistance, CP-3 could be considered as a potential antibacterial candidate.

In vitro antibacterial biofilm ability

Apart from the acquired resistance of planktonic bacteria, bacterial biofilms are also a major driver of antimicrobial tolerance [56]. It has been reported that biofilm-embedded bacteria display up to a 1000-fold increase in antimicrobial tolerance relative to planktonic cells [57,58]. The ability of CP-3 to inhibit bacterial biofilm formation was first evaluated (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5C, both vancomycin and CP-3 effectively suppressed biofilm formation at 2× MIC. Subsequently, the ability of CP-3 to disrupt mature biofilms was evaluated, which is generally more difficult than biofilm inhibition (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 5D, the amount of biomass was significantly reduced with a biomass removal rate of 89.2%, whereas vancomycin exhibited negligible biofilm disruption activity.

The biofilm elimination ability of CP-3 was further verified by three-dimensional confocal laser scanning microscopy (3D-CLSM). As shown in Fig. 5E, intact biofilm architecture with strong fluorescence signals persisted in the PBS and vancomycin groups, while CP-3 treated group resulted in predominant red fluorescence with only scattered residual signals, indicative of extensive disruption. Consistently, quantitative analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in biofilm thickness after CP-3 treatment compared with PBS and vancomycin (Fig. 5F). SEM assay also revealed that, relative to the dense and continuous matrices observed in the PBS and vancomycin groups, biofilms were conspicuously removed following CP-3 treatment (Fig. 5G). Finally, enumeration of residual bacteria within the biofilm showed that CP-3 reduced viable counts by over 80.2% (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CP-3 can effectively inhibit bacterial biofilm formation, disrupt mature bacterial biofilms, and kill bacteria within biofilms, indicating good potential for the treatment of biofilm associated infections.

In vivo antibacterial activity

Due to the potent in vitro antibacterial activity of CP-3, the therapeutic efficacy of CP-3 was investigated in a MRSA induced mouse lung infection. Mice were immunosuppressed via intraperitoneal cyclophosphamide administration on days −5 and −3. On day 0, MRSA was delivered intratracheally to establish pneumonia, followed by intratracheal CP-3 treatment on day 1. After 24 h, the lung tissue was collected (Fig. 6A). H&E staining showed extensive alveolar disruption and septal thickening in the PBS group, consistent with severe lung injury (Fig. 6B). By comparison, CP-3 and vancomycin treatment largely preserved alveolar architecture and airspace integrity, indicating markedly reduced infection-induced tissue damage. The antibacterial efficacy was quantified using the standard plate dilution method (Fig. 6C). Compared to the PBS treated group, CP-3 significantly reducing the bacterial load by approximately 2.71- log CFU, indicating its excellent antibacterial ability in vivo. In addition, intratracheal administration of CP-3 at the therapeutic dosage caused no obvious pathological changes in lung tissues of uninfected healthy mice compared with PBS, suggesting good local pulmonary compatibility (Fig. S9).

The degree of pulmonary inflammation serves as a key measure of lung tissue recovery. The pulmonary expression of IL-6 and TNF-α was analyzed by immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 6D–G, compared with the PBS group, both CP-3 and vancomycin treatment markedly reduced the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α. The survival ratio of the mouse was monitored over an 8-day period. As shown in Fig. 6H, CP-3 markedly improved survival, yielding a 90% survival rate compared with 20% in the PBS group. The in vivo safety of CP-3 was also assessed. As shown in Fig. 6I, the mice showed a slight increase in body weight during treatment, and serum biochemical indices remained within normal physiological ranges at the end of dosing (Fig. 6J–K). Collectively, these results suggest favorable in vivo biocompatibility and safety of CP-3.

Next, the therapeutic potential of CP-3 in mouse peritonitis was conducted, which is much more challenging. Mice were immunosuppressed with cyclophosphamide and then challenged by intraperitoneal injection of 200 μL of MRSA (Fig. 7A). After four consecutive treatments, bacterial burdens in major organs, blood, and peritoneal fluid were quantified. As shown in Fig. 7B–H, both vancomycin and CP-3 resulted in marked reductions in bacterial loads relative to PBS. Survival over a 15-day period was assessed. As depicted in Fig. 7I, CP-3 significantly prolonged survival, with an 80% survival rate at day 15 compared with 20% in the PBS group.

To assess the in vivo biocompatibility of CP-3, the body weight of the mice was monitored. As demonstrated in Fig. 7J, no significant weight loss was observed in CP-3 treatment group, indicating a negligible systemic toxicity of CP-3. Additionally, hematoxylin–eosin staining of major organs after different treatments was also conducted and no obvious histopathological abnormalities was observed in the CP-3 treated group (Fig. 7K). Finally, serum biochemical analyses were performed to further evaluate the in vivo safety of CP-3. As shown in Fig. S10, no abnormalities were detected in the biochemical parameters of CP-3 treated mice, further supporting the favorable in vivo safety profile of CP-3. The above results demonstrated that CP-3 is a promising antibacterial agent for the treatment of bacterial infection with low toxicity in vivo.

Conclusion

In this study, a series of polymers with different quaternary phosphonium side chains were synthesized and evaluated for the treatment of bacterial infections. Among the synthesized polymers, the optimal polymer CP-3 (bearing tripropylphosphine side group) shown best antibacterial ability in vitro. CP-3 was found to bind anionic bacterial phospholipids POPG preferentially rather than the neutral phospholipids DOPC present in mammalian membranes. This phospholipids selectivity ability of CP-3 was considered a principal driver of its potent antibacterial activity and low cytotoxicity. In addition, CP-3 effectively inhibits bacterial biofilm formation and disrupts mature bacterial biofilms. In MRSA induced mouse lung infection and peritonitis, CP-3 shown significant therapeutic ability while exhibiting negligible in vivo toxicity. This work provides a novel strategy for development of phosphonium-based polymer with potent antibacterial ability.
