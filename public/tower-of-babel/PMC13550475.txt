Progressive fusion and guidance-aware denoising for robust multimodal sentiment analysis
Authors: Ziyu Zheng, Xinfeng Ye, Sathiamoorthy Manoharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid growth of digital media, multimodal sentiment analysis has attracted increasing research attention. However, existing approaches largely emphasize fusion strategies while overlooking the noise and redundancy inherent in real-world multimodal data, which limits the representational capacity of models. To address this issue, we propose a Progressive Fusion and Guidance-Aware Denoising Framework (PFGAD). It first learns joint multimodal representations using progressive fusion. Next, it applies a variational information bottleneck to remove noise that is irrelevant to the task. Finally, it uses a Guidance-Aware Denoising Module to make up for any information lost during denoising. This design achieves a good balance between informativeness and robustness. Our method demonstrates notable improvements on CMU-MOSI and CMU-MOSEI. On CMU-MOSI, it achieves Corr (0.814) and Acc-2 (87.5), which corresponds to a 1.75% relative gain in Corr and a 0.5%-point increase in Acc-2 over the strongest prior result. On CMU-MOSEI, it achieves Corr (0.786) and F1 (86.7), yielding a 0.77% relative gain in Corr and a 0.2%-point improvement in F1.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, with the unprecedented growth of digital media, we have witnessed an explosive increase in multimodal data. Analyzing such data is critical in domains such as human–computer interaction, healthcare, and personalized recommendation systems. Multimodal Sentiment Analysis (MSA)1–3 has emerged as an important topic in this context. Its aim is to integrate heterogeneous signals from different modalities and infer human emotions. In the domain of MSA, however, one of the fundamental challenges is how to achieve accurate fusion of heterogeneous and diverse multimodal information4.

To cope with this challenge, a large amount of work has been devoted to design effective fusion strategies. The key idea is to build a joint representation space where information from different modalities can interact and complement each other. Early efforts explored tensor-based fusion5–7, which explicitly models higher-order interactions among modalities. Subsequent research introduced graph-based approaches8–10, enabling more flexible modeling of relationships between modality-specific features. Recently, the wide adoption of attention-based mechanisms11–13 allows the model to dynamically focus on the most informative signals across modalities.

These studies demonstrated that well-designed fusion strategies can significantly improve the performance of models. However, they often rely on an implicit assumption that modality signals are reliable and strongly associated with sentiment. In practice, this assumption is often violated, as the collected signals are easily interfered with by noise and redundancy. For example, audio may be distorted by background interference14, video may suffer from occlusion or frame loss15, and text frequently contains colloquial expressions or transcription errors16. These task-irrelevant noise may mislead the model during feature learning, thus weakening its representations and reducing predictive performance.

Although some studies17–19 have attempted to address this issue through denoising modality representations, most of these approaches rely on information compression and thus provide only implicit constraints. Specifically, these methods rely on applying general regularization to the representation, while task semantics are conveyed only indirectly through the supervised loss. As a result, the model becomes highly sensitive to the degree of compression: overly strong compression may discard useful task-relevant information, whereas insufficient compression leaves noise and redundancy largely untouched, making it difficult to maintain a balance between informativeness and robustness and ultimately undermining overall performance.

Building upon these observations, we propose a Guidance-Aware Denoising model, which leverages two auxiliary branches to provide stable, comprehensive guidance during training. The framework consists of two core components: the Progressive Fusion Module (PFM) and the Guidance-Aware Denoising Module (GADM). PFM is designed to address insufficient cross-modal interaction during the fusion stage by obtaining joint multimodal representations in a text-centric and progressive manner, where visual and acoustic signals are gradually integrated with textual semantics. GADM first applies the Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB) to the joint representation to remove task-irrelevant noise, yielding a denoised primary branch. As discussed earlier, because this denoising process lacks explicit task awareness, it may fail to reliably preserve task-relevant information. Therefore, GADM introduces two auxiliary branches: a reference branch from the original joint representations, providing comprehensive but potentially noisy information, and a stabilized reference branch derived from the primary branch’s parameters, providing smoother and more stable signals. These branches complement each other, offering comprehensive yet robust signals to the primary branch. Notably, they are employed only during training and removed at inference, preventing noise from affecting the final predictions. By combining these two modules, PFGAD achieves a good balance between informativeness and robustness, and experiments on two widely used MSA datasets show consistent improvements over recent state-of-the-art methods in multiple metrics.

Related work

Multimodal sentiment analysis

MSA aims to infer human emotions by integrating heterogeneous signals across modalities, thereby enabling comprehensive affect modeling for human–computer interaction, opinion mining, and personalized recommendation. Against the backdrop of rapidly growing multimodal data, this task has become increasingly salient and technically demanding.

Early studies in MSA placed strong emphasis on fusion strategies. Zadeh et al.5 introduced the Tensor Fusion Network (TFN), which explicitly models high-order interactions across modalities through tensor outer products. This design significantly improved performance compared to simple concatenation or addition methods. However, TFN suffers from high dimensionality and heavy computational overhead, making it less scalable in practice. To alleviate these limitations Liu et al.6 proposed a low-rank decomposition approach that reduces computational cost while maintaining the expressive power of tensor-based fusion.

Another line of research has investigated graph-based fusion, which emphasizes structured modeling of both inter- and intra-modal relationships. Rahman et al.20 proposed MAGCN, which maps different modality features as nodes and employs graph convolutions to capture dependencies within and across modalities. Ghosal et al.21 introduced DialogueGCN, which applies graph attention networks in conversational sentiment recognition to identify the most influential speaker nodes and modality features for emotion prediction. Zhang et al.22 developed MMHG, which models text, visual, and acoustic signals as heterogeneous nodes and leverages heterogeneous GNNs to capture complex cross-modal interactions.

In parallel, extensive research has explored attention-based fusion, which uses attention mechanisms to dynamically assign weights and adaptively emphasize the importance of different modalities and their interactions. Tsai et al.23 proposed the Multimodal Transformer, introducing cross-modal attention to handle unaligned modality sequences, which has been regarded as a milestone in this line of work. Hazarika et al.24 combined convolutional networks with a multimodal Transformer to capture both local and global cross-modal interactions through attention mechanisms. Xue et al.12 further designed a multi-level attention network that performs information selection and aggregation within and across modalities.

Although these studies have demonstrated that sophisticated fusion strategies can improve model accuracy, a critical issue that has remained underexplored is the reliability of the input data. In real-world scenarios, the collected signals often contain task-irrelevant noise and redundancy, which undermines representation quality and leads to performance degradation. To address this problem, we adopt VIB to filter out task-irrelevant information, thereby enabling the learning of more robust representations.

Noise-robust learning

Given the problem of redundant and noise information in multimodal representations, some researchers have used an approach based on information theory. Tishby et al.25 first introduced the Information Bottleneck (IB) method, which posits the existence of an optimal intermediate representation z between the input x and the label y. The goal of z is to retain as much information as possible that is relevant to y while discarding task-irrelevant redundancy. Formally, the IB objective can be expressed as:

However, the computational intractability of mutual information in IB has hindered its direct application. Recently, the VIB18 was introduced, which employs variational approximation and parameterizes the bottleneck model with neural networks to make IB practically realizable. This advancement has enabled the application of IB in deep learning and inspired a series of subsequent studies. Mai et al.17 proposed applying IB constraints to both unimodal and joint representations in order to learn minimal sufficient representations at both levels. Jeon et al.26 proposed the IB-GAN framework, which combines the IB with generative adversarial networks (GANs) and employs IB constraints to learn disentangled latent representations that separate task-relevant and task-irrelevant information. Pan et al.27 further extended IB by imposing constraints on different dimensions of the representation, enabling the model to capture independent semantic factors.

Above studies have extended the IB framework in various ways and achieved encouraging results. However, their strategies essentially rely on constraining the representations to approximate a prior distribution. The effectiveness of such methods is highly sensitive to the constraint strength: overly strong constraint may suppress task-relevant information, whereas insufficient regularization fails to effectively remove noise and redundancy. This instability makes it difficult for the model to reliably converge to a minimal sufficient representation. In some cases, it may even inadvertently discard useful information during the denoising process, ultimately leading to further performance degradation. To address this problem, we introduces auxiliary branches during denoising, thereby better maintaining the balance between informativeness and robustness.

Next, the three unimodal representations are fed into the PFM to perform cross-modal fusion, integrating complementary information across modalities. The PFM is text-centered and progressively integrates the visual and acoustic modalities with the textual modality to form a joint representation . As a result, serves as a comprehensive joint embedding that captures correlated information from all modalities.

During the whole denoising process, two auxiliary branches are introduced: the reference branch provides comprehensive semantic information from the original representations, and the stabilized reference branch constructed using the exponential moving average (EMA) of the parameters of the primary branch to deliver smoother and more stable signals. These two branches collaboratively provide auxiliary signals to the primary branch, helping it maintain a good balance between informativeness and robustness. They are employed only during training, while inference relies solely on the denoised primary branch.

Progressive fusion module

PFM aims to fuse unimodal representations into an informative joint representation. Although simple operations such as addition and concatenation can also combine multimodal features, they mainly aggregate different modalities at the feature level and lack an explicit mechanism for modeling inter-modal dependencies30. As a result, direct fusion may fail to capture important cross-modal relations, limiting the model’s ability to learn interactions across different modality sequences.

In multimodal sentiment analysis, text usually provides the most explicit semantic content and affective context31, while visual and acoustic modalities offer complementary behavioral and paralinguistic cues. These non-textual cues can be ambiguous when interpreted independently and therefore need to be grounded in textual semantics for more accurate interpretation32. Based on this consideration, we adopt a text-centered progressive fusion strategy, where text is used as the semantic anchor to progressively integrate visual and acoustic information.

Specifically, PFM integrates multimodal signals in two stages. In the first stage, we design our fusion strategy in a text-centered manner. Specifically, bidirectional cross-attention is performed between the text–visual and text–acoustic modality pairs: one modality serves as the query while the other provides the keys and values, and then the roles are reversed. The representations from both directions are subsequently concatenated. In the second stage, we apply bidirectional cross-attention to the two intermediate representations obtained from the first stage and then concatenate the bidirectional outputs to produce the final joint multimodal representation. Although this branch is capable of suppressing noise in the joint representation through the VIB mechanism, it is essentially a prior-based global regularization: it controls only the overall amount of information, without indicating which subspaces should be preserved for the target. Therefore, adjusting the hyperparameter β only alters the overall degree of compression, without enabling the selective preservation of information that is critical for the task. Moreover, the optimal β is often non-transferable across different data distributions or noise conditions and tends to be highly sensitive to training stability, making it difficult to reliably distinguish task-relevant from irrelevant information and often leading to the inadvertent removal of sentiment cues during denoising.

These branches provide auxiliary learning signals during the primary branch’s denoising process to assist its learning. Experiments

Datasets and metrics

We evaluate our model on two widely used multimodal sentiment analysis datasets, CMU-MOSI and CMU-MOSEI. Following prior work33, we adopt the same evaluation protocols to ensure a fair and consistent comparison with existing methods.

Datasets The CMU-MOSI8 dataset contains 2,199 opinion video clips collected from YouTube. Each clip is manually segmented and annotated with sentiment labels on a 7-point scale ranging from –3 (strongly negative) to +3 (strongly positive). The dataset provides aligned text, audio, and visual modalities. We use 1,284 utterances for training, 229 utterances for validation, and 686 utterances for testing.

The CMU-MOSEI1 dataset contains 23,453 annotated video segments from more than 5,000 speakers on YouTube. Each segment is labeled with sentiment on a 7-point scale ranging from –3 (strongly negative) to +3 (strongly positive). The dataset provides aligned text, audio, and visual modalities, and also includes categorical emotion annotations. We use 16,265 utterances for training, 1,869 utterances for validation, and 4,659 utterances for testing.

Metric We evaluate our model using multiple metrics. Although the task is originally formulated as regression, it can also be converted into a classification problem for evaluation. For classification, we report 7-class accuracy (Acc-7), binary accuracy (Acc-2), and weighted F1. For regression, we report mean absolute error (MAE) and Pearson correlation (Corr).

Implementation details

For the textual modality, we use BERT-base-uncased to encode each utterance into context-dependent word embeddings, following the standard setting where each token is represented as a 768-dimensional vector. For the visual modality, we employ the OpenFace toolkit to extract frame-level features, including facial landmarks, head pose, facial action units, and eye gaze. For the acoustic modality, we use Librosa to extract frame-level low-level descriptors such as MFCCs, pitch, and energy.

All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU using the PyTorch framework. The batch size is set to 128, and the hidden dimension of the model is fixed at 128. During training, the Adam optimizer is employed, and model hyperparameters are tuned via grid search. The initial learning rate is set to and scheduled using exponential decay with ExponentialLR. To mitigate overfitting, an early stopping strategy is applied with a patience of 8.

The results show that PFM achieves the best overall performance among these fusion variants. This is because PFM explicitly models text–visual and text–acoustic interactions before output the final joint representation. Module ablation We validate the effectiveness of each module in the pipeline by using them independently. Specifically, to evaluate the effectiveness of PFM, we remove GADM and compare PFM with three baseline fusion methods, using the resulting fused representation directly for prediction. Concretely, we test Concat (labelled ’Concat only’), which concatenates the three modality embeddings, element-wise addition (labelled ’ElemAdd only’), which sums the three embeddings element-wise, and element-wise multiplication (labelled ’ElemMul only’), which multiplies the three embeddings element-wise. The results show that PFM achieves the best overall performance among these fusion variants. This is because PFM explicitly models text–visual and text–acoustic interactions before output the final joint representation. By using text as the semantic anchor, PFM can organize non-textual cues into a more informative and sentiment-relevant representation. This indicates that progressive fusion can better capture cross-modal interactions and integrate complementary cues for semantic fusion. Nevertheless, PFM alone remains suboptimal, suggesting that fusion by itself is insufficient to fully remove noise and redundancy from multimodal inputs.

To evaluate the effectiveness of GADM, we apply it to multiple basic fusion methods, including Concat, ElemAdd, and ElemMul. The results show that adding GADM improves the performance of the corresponding fusion-only baselines. This suggests that GADM can enhance fused representations through guidance-aware denoising, where the auxiliary reference branches help the model suppress task-irrelevant noise while preserving useful sentiment cues. However, its effectiveness is still limited by the informativeness of the input joint representation. When the fused features lack sufficient cross-modal interactions, denoising alone cannot fully recover sentiment-discriminative cues or compensate for insufficiently enriched multimodal representations. These observations motivate the combination of PFM and GADM to jointly enhance semantic integration and representation robustness.

Table 3 compares our method with MISA, MMIM, and Self-MM in terms of model size, computational complexity, and inference-time efficiency. In terms of wall-clock performance, our method reports a latency of 24.17 ms per batch and a throughput of 2647.38 samples/s, which is slightly worse than the baselines under the same setting. This gap can be attributed to FLOPs quantify arithmetic operations but do not fully capture practical factors such as operator fusion, parallelism, and memory-access behavior, which can materially affect GPU utilization and end-to-end latency. Overall, our results suggest that our method matches prior baselines in model size and theoretical compute, while incurring a modest runtime overhead in latency and throughput under the evaluated configuration.

Visualization of sentiment polarity category distribution

To assess representation differences among the branches in GADM, we visualize sentiment polarity embeddings of the reference, stabilized reference, and primary branches using t-SNE44, as shown in Fig. 3. In the reference branch, positive and negative samples exhibit substantial overlap in the central region, and their class-wise covariance contours also overlap noticeably. Neutral samples are mainly distributed in the transitional area between the two polarities and overlap with both sides. This indicates a relatively mixed neighborhood structure in the embedded space, with a weak separability trend across sentiment categories. In the primary branch, positive samples are largely concentrated on the left, while negative samples are more clustered on the right, with neutral samples remaining still in between. Compared with the reference branch, the overlap between class-wise covariance contours is reduced and the main regions of different categories become more distinguishable, suggesting that the primary branch strengthens sentiment-discriminative structure. Meanwhile, neutral samples still lie in the transitional band, reflecting their closer proximity to the “boundary” in semantic space; therefore, a certain degree of natural overlap with the two polarities is expected. The stabilized reference branch shows a smoother and more regular global structure than the primary branch, yet the overlap around the transitional region remains evident. As it is obtained via an EMA of the primary branch parameters, it tends to suppress short-term representation variations and provides a lower-variance reference signal during training. Meanwhile, this smoothing can make boundary regions less sharp, which is consistent with our ablation results: using only the stabilized reference branch as the auxiliary signal yields smaller gains and can be slightly worse than configurations involving the reference branch on boundary sensitive metrics such as Corr and Acc-2.

Conclusion

In this work, we address the robustness challenge in multimodal sentiment analysis caused by noisy and redundant signals and propose PFGAD, a framework composed of a Progressive Fusion Module and a Guidance-Aware Denoising Module. The Progressive Fusion Module fuses multimodal signals in a text-centric manner through a progressive process, enabling more effective integration of information. The Guidance-Aware Denoising Module introduces reference and stabilized reference branches that assist the primary branch during denoising when the model is trained. This design helps the model preserve task-relevant information while improving generalization, thereby achieving a good balance between informativeness and robustness. Experimental results on the CMU-MOSI and CMU-MOSEI datasets demonstrate that PFGAD consistently outperforms recent state-of-the-art methods across multiple metrics and exhibits strong capability in handling noisy, colloquial, and mixed emotional signals.
