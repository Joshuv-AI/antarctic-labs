Optimizing Nurse‐Involved Non‐Pharmacological Interventions for Postoperative Pain: A Scoping Review
Authors: Jihyung Son, Sunghwa Na, Hyun Joo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo identify the types of non‐pharmacological interventions, nurses' roles and outcome measures used for postoperative pain management in inpatients.DesignA scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines.MethodsEligible studies were those involving adult surgical inpatients receiving nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions, with outcome measures assessed using pain‐assessment tools. Introduction

Postoperative pain remains one of the most common and distressing complications following surgery, with approximately 80% of patients reporting moderate to severe pain despite advances in surgical techniques and analgesic protocols (Bekele et al. 2024; Gan 2017; Institute of Medicine (US) Committee on Advancing Pain Research, Care, and Education 2011). As surgical procedures continue to increase worldwide (Meara et al. 2015), this persistently high prevalence highlights a critical unmet need in postoperative pain. According to the International Association for the Study of Pain, pain is defined as ‘an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage’ (Raja et al. 2020). Although diverse pain management strategies are routinely implemented in clinical practice, inadequate postoperative pain control remains a sustained concern for both clinicians and patients. Poorly controlled postoperative pain is not merely a sensory experience; it is associated with delayed functional recovery and psychological distress, increased morbidity, prolonged hospitalization and greater economic burden. In addition, suboptimal pain management may negatively influence patient satisfaction and increase the risk of postoperative complications, thereby affecting both individual health outcomes and healthcare system efficiency (Jain et al. 2015; Lovich‐Sapola et al. 2015).

In 2016, a clinical practice guideline emphasized the importance of integrating non‐pharmacological interventions with pharmacological treatments to enhance postoperative pain management (Chou et al. 2016). Within this multimodal framework, non‐pharmacological strategies complement medication rather than replace it and have gained increasing attention as feasible and patient‐centered approaches to pain relief (Pain Management Best Practices Inter‐Agency Task Force 2019; Tsegaye et al. 2023). Interventions such as music, massage, cold and heat therapy, behavioural therapy, mindfulness, relaxation, and pain‐coping skills training through education are generally simple to implement and can be applied throughout the perioperative phase (Mota et al. 2021; Ruano et al. 2022; Small and Laycock 2020). Growing concerns regarding opioid‐related adverse effects and opioid stewardship have further reinforced the role of non‐pharmacological strategies in acute postoperative pain management (Small and Laycock 2020).

Nurses play a central role in postoperative pain prevention and management. As healthcare professionals who provide continuous, patient‐centered bedside care throughout the perioperative period and spend the greatest amount of time with patients, nurses are uniquely positioned to coordinate pain management efforts (Niyonkuru et al. 2025; van Veen et al. 2024). Through ongoing assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation and evaluation of pain, nurses contribute substantially to optimizing postoperative recovery (Tsegaye et al. 2023). In clinical practice, nurses implement both pharmacological and non‐pharmacological strategies, with non‐pharmacological interventions easily delivered at the bedside and integrated into routine nursing care (van Veen et al. 2024).

Despite growing interest in non‐pharmacological approaches, existing reviews have primarily focused on specific surgical procedures or distinct patient populations, such as paediatric patients or individuals with opioid tolerance (Fan and Chen 2020; Hargett et al. 2022; Llerena et al. 2024; Mota et al. 2021; Ruano et al. 2022). Consequently, evidence exploring these interventions across various surgical settings while explicitly examining nurses' roles during the perioperative period remains limited. Furthermore, given the multidimensional nature of pain, diverse outcome measures have been used to evaluate intervention effectiveness; however, these outcome measures have not been comprehensively examined in studies of nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological postoperative pain management (Scher et al. 2020).

Therefore, in this scoping review, we focused on studies published from 2016 onward, following the release of the 2016 clinical practice guideline that highlighted the importance of non‐pharmacological interventions as part of multimodal postoperative pain management (Chou et al. 2016). We aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions for postoperative pain management among adult inpatients in general wards and to inform their implementation in clinical practice.

Aims

In this scoping review, we aimed to identify and map nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions for postoperative pain management in surgical inpatients. The review questions were developed using the PCC (Population, Concept and Context) framework (Peters et al. 2024).

The research questions were as follows:

What types of nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions have been used for postoperative pain management?

Methods

Design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al. 2024). Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist (Appendix S1). A review protocol was not formally registered for this scoping review.

Search Strategy

The search was conducted across five databases: PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and Web of Science. The search strategy was structured according to the PCC framework used to define the eligibility criteria. Both controlled vocabulary and free‐text keywords were used in each database, including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in PubMed, Emtree in Embase and CINAHL Headings in CINAHL. The Cochrane Library was searched across all available databases using relevant keywords. The search covered studies published between January 1, 2016, and November 30, 2025. The full search strategy is detailed in Appendix S2. The PubMed search query, presented as a representative example, incorporated MeSH terms and keywords related to non‐pharmacological interventions (e.g., aromatherapy, music and relaxation), surgical timing (e.g., postoperative, postsurgical and perioperative), pain (e.g., acute pain and postoperative pain) and inpatient settings.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The eligibility criteria were determined based on the PCC framework (Peters et al. 2024). The population included adult inpatients aged 18 years and older who had undergone surgery. The concept focused on nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions for managing postoperative pain across the perioperative period. The context was limited to general wards in hospital settings. Studies were included if postoperative pain was assessed using any validated or self‐reported pain assessment scale and if they were published from 2016 onward.

The exclusion criteria encompassed studies targeting outpatients and those without nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions. Additionally, studies were excluded if the intervention was delivered outside general wards, such as in preoperative outpatient clinics, intensive care units, post‐anaesthesia care units or post‐discharge settings. Studies not published in English or Korean were also excluded. Reviews, editorials, dissertations, conference proceedings, case studies, continuing education unit articles, protocols and trial registry records were additionally excluded.

Study Selection

The search strategy was validated by a professional librarian. All identified records were exported to EndNote 21, and duplicates were removed. Two researchers (J.S. and S.N.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the records according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, full‐text articles were retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (H.J.L.). Consistent with the methodological guidance for scoping reviews, no formal quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted, as the aim of this review was to map the available evidence rather than evaluate methodological quality.

Data Extraction and Charting

Three data extraction templates were developed using Microsoft Excel to capture key characteristics of the included studies. Two reviewers independently extracted data using these templates. The templates included general characteristics, intervention characteristics and the nurses' roles (Table 1). The extracted data were summarized and synthesized narratively. The findings were subsequently grouped into thematic categories based on similarities in intervention characteristics, outcome measures and nurses' roles. These thematic categories were used to organize the results of the review.

In analysing nurses' roles, nurse involvement was operationally defined as cases in which nurses were explicitly identified as participating in any stage of the intervention (e.g., planning, implementation or evaluation). In cases where the intervention personnel were not explicitly specified, studies were included only when the intervention was clearly described as part of nursing care and the study authors were identified as nurses. This approach was adopted to minimize the misclassification of intervention involvement. In addition, the types of non‐pharmacological interventions and nurses' roles were summarized using graphical representations.

Results

A total of 2579 records were identified through searching across the five databases: 495 from PubMed, 1186 from Embase, 177 from CINAHL, 349 from Cochrane and 372 from Web of Science. In addition, 12 studies were identified through other sources, including hand‐searching (n = 4) and reference list screening (n = 8). After removing 601 duplicates, 1990 studies remained for title and abstract screening. The screening process was conducted by two reviewers, and 1892 records were excluded based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Ninety‐eight full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility by the same two reviewers. Following full‐text review, 75 articles were excluded due to ineligible participants (n = 2), ineligible intervention (n = 28), ineligible study design (n = 43) or absence of pain‐assessment scale (n = 2).

A range of non‐pharmacological strategies was identified across studies as summarized in Figure 2. Cold therapy was the most frequently reported intervention, followed by acupressure and education. Other single‐component interventions comprised aromatherapy, dry‐heat application, warm menthol‐oil sitz bath, massage, reflexology and gum chewing. Multiple‐component interventions combined two or more non‐pharmacological strategies, with specific components varying across studies. Education and cold therapy were used both as standalone interventions and as components within multiple‐component or protocol‐based approaches (Andujo et al. 2023; Zou et al. 2023). In several studies, interventions were delivered collaboratively with other healthcare professionals, including medical doctors (n = 7), pharmacists (n = 2) and case managers (n = 1). Six studies reported the use of intervention‐support devices, including a Bluetooth‐capable speaker (Hill et al. 2020), a Quick Response (QR) code‐based education system (Zou et al. 2023), an infrared lamp device (Roma et al. 2023) and video‐assisted education materials in three studies (Biricik et al. 2024; Jeon and Park 2016; Turhan Damar and Bilik 2022).

Outcome Measures

Outcome measures are summarized in Table 2. Pain intensity was assessed in all included studies and represented the primary outcome across the literature. The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was the most frequently used instrument (n = 12), followed by the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) (n = 8). Other tools including the Verbal Rating Scale (Balaban and Yayla 2025; Stallings Welden et al. 2021), the Short‐Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF‐MPQ) (El‐Saidy et al. 2018), and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) pain subscale (Biricik et al. 2024; Turhan Damar and Bilik 2022) were used less frequently. In one study, a 0–10 scale was used without specifying whether it corresponded to a validated tool (Lindner et al. 2023).

Beyond pain intensity, several categories of secondary outcomes were reported. These included analgesic‐related outcomes (n = 9), such as opioid consumption; psychological outcomes (n = 7), including anxiety, depression and stress; functional outcomes (n = 7), such as mobility and physical function; and clinical indicators of postoperative recovery (n = 8), including patient comfort and length of hospital stay.

Nurses most frequently participated in the implementation stage (n = 20). During this stage, nurses delivered the interventions and guided patients in performing the intervention procedures. Nurses were also involved in the evaluation stage (n = 13), where they assessed pain using standardized tools or facilitated patient self‐report. Nurses were less frequently involved in the planning stage (n = 7). In these studies, nurses participated in organizing the intervention process or preparing intervention components. Only one study reported nurses' involvement across all three stages of the intervention process (Turhan Damar and Bilik 2022).

Evaluation time points were primarily short‐term, with the most studies assessing outcomes during the immediate postoperative period or during hospitalization.

Discussion

This review provides an overview of nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions for postoperative pain management across diverse surgical populations. Three key findings emerged from the included studies. First, a wide range of non‐pharmacological interventions was identified, with simple single‐component strategies being most frequently implemented in clinical settings. Second, nurses were primarily involved in the implementation and evaluation stages of interventions, while their participation in the planning stage remained limited. Third, outcome evaluation predominantly focused on pain intensity, with relatively fewer studies examining broader recovery‐related outcomes.

A notable proportion of included studies focused on obstetric procedures, particularly caesarean section and episiotomy. Previous research has similarly reported that non‐pharmacological pain management strategies are frequently investigated in obstetric populations, partly due to the high prevalence of postoperative discomfort and the emphasis on minimizing pharmacological exposure during the postpartum period (Dantas Varella Dutra et al. 2019; Gondim et al. 2025). This concentration suggests that the current evidence base may be more applicable to obstetric populations than to other surgical groups, highlighting the need for future research across a broader range of surgical contexts.

The included interventions varied in their structure, ranging from single‐component to multiple‐component approaches, with single‐component interventions being more frequently applied in postoperative pain management. Single‐component interventions may be more feasible within routine clinical workflows and may therefore be more readily adopted in nursing practice. Previous research has similarly suggested that interventions requiring minimal resources and training are more likely to be implemented consistently in busy clinical environments (Rogers et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2023). In contrast, multiple‐component interventions may provide broader benefits by addressing multiple aspects of pain and recovery simultaneously (Kasicki et al. 2025; Skivington et al. 2021). These findings highlight the importance of considering both feasibility and comprehensiveness when integrating non‐pharmacological interventions into clinical practice.

In addition, protocol‐based interventions were identified across both single‐ and multiple‐component approaches. In this context, protocolization appears to function as a structured framework that supports consistent and systematic delivery of non‐pharmacological interventions rather than representing a specific therapeutic modality. Standardized protocols may therefore facilitate the integration of non‐pharmacological strategies into routine clinical care and help ensure greater consistency in intervention delivery across healthcare settings (Department of Health (Ireland) 2025).

Several studies also incorporated digital tools, such as QR code‐based educational materials and video modules, to support the delivery of non‐pharmacological interventions. The integration of technology‐mediated approaches may facilitate standardized patient education while allowing patients to access information repeatedly at their own pace. Such approaches may also support patient engagement and improve the efficiency of nursing interventions within busy clinical environments.

Pain, as a subjective and multidimensional experience, requires accurate and comprehensive assessment to guide clinical decision‐making and evaluate the effectiveness of pain management strategies (Raja et al. 2020). In the included studies, pain intensity was consistently assessed, most commonly using validated unidimensional instruments such as the VAS and NRS. While these tools are widely recommended and predominantly used in clinical practice (Crisman et al. 2024), they primarily capture a single dimension of pain. Given the complex nature of postoperative pain, incorporating multidimensional assessment tools may provide a more comprehensive understanding of patients' pain experiences (Crisman et al. 2024; van Boekel et al. 2017). However, only a limited number of studies used instruments that captured broader aspects of pain, such as the SF‐MPQ, or functional or condition‐specific measures including the WOMAC pain subscale. This pattern highlights that the sensory, affective and evaluative dimensions of pain may remain underrepresented in current research. Future studies may therefore benefit from incorporating multidimensional pain assessment approaches to better capture the complexity of postoperative pain experiences.

In addition to pain intensity, several studies evaluated broader outcomes including psychological, functional and clinical indicators of recovery. These findings suggest a growing recognition that postoperative recovery extends beyond pain reduction alone and encompasses multidimensional aspects of patient well‐being (Giordano et al. 2023; Rajabiyazdi et al. 2021). Expanding outcome frameworks to include diverse recovery indicators may therefore support a more comprehensive evaluation of non‐pharmacological pain management strategies.

Another important observation was that evaluation time points in the included studies were predominantly short‐term, focusing on the immediate postoperative period or during hospitalization. Only a limited number of studies extended follow‐up beyond discharge, with assessments conducted at varying time points up to several months postoperatively. While these timeframes provide insights into early postoperative recovery, they may not fully capture the longer term effects of non‐pharmacological interventions, including their potential influence on persistent postoperative pain or long‐term quality of life. Consistently, previous research has highlighted the importance of assessing pain at specific time points and monitoring changes over time to better understand intervention effects (Choi et al. 2024).

The findings of this review revealed an uneven distribution of nurses' roles across intervention stages. Although nurses were involved in planning, implementation and evaluation stages, their participation was predominantly concentrated in implementation and evaluation. This pattern may reflect existing clinical structures in which nurses are more commonly positioned as implementers of predefined protocols rather than active contributors to intervention design. Institutional factors such as workload, time constraints and hierarchical decision‐making processes may also influence nurses' opportunities to participate in planning activities. However, effective postoperative pain management requires nurses to engage across all stages of care, including planning, implementation, and evaluation, in order to deliver individualized and patient‐centered interventions (Taşcı and Göktaş 2023; Yaban 2019). Limited involvement in the planning stage may reduce opportunities to incorporate bedside insights into intervention design and may hinder alignment across intervention stages. Strengthening nurses' participation in planning processes may therefore contribute to more integrated and patient‐centered postoperative pain management strategies.

Practice Implications

The findings of this review have several implications for clinical practice, healthcare policy and nursing education. First, healthcare organizations should develop standardized protocols or clinical roadmaps to guide the implementation of non‐pharmacological interventions in postoperative care. Such structured approaches may reduce unwarranted variability in nursing practice and facilitate the integration of non‐pharmacological strategies into existing clinical workflows, thereby improving both nurse adherence and patient outcomes (Harrison et al. 2019). At the healthcare policy level, institutional support is required to formally recognize nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions as an integral component of postoperative pain management. This includes adequate staffing, resource allocation, and the incorporation of these interventions into pain management guidelines and care standards. Policy‐level support may ensure that non‐pharmacological strategies are implemented as routine elements of care rather than perceived as additional nursing tasks. Within clinical pathways, non‐pharmacological interventions can be systematically incorporated into perioperative care processes. Clearly defining nurses' roles across planning, implementation and evaluation stages may enhance continuity of care and promote more coordinated pain management strategies. Finally, nursing education should emphasize the development of competencies related to non‐pharmacological pain management, including intervention planning, implementation and outcome evaluation. Strengthening these competencies may support nurses' active participation across all stages of care and promote the consistent application of evidence‐based non‐pharmacological interventions in clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered. First, this review followed established methodological guidance for scoping reviews; however, no formal review protocol was registered prior to conducting the study. This may limit the transparency regarding prospectively defined methodological decisions. Second, the search was restricted to peer‐reviewed journal articles, excluding grey literature such as dissertations and conference proceedings. While this ensured the methodological quality of the evidence included, it may have introduced publication bias. Third, identifying nurse involvement was challenging in some studies that lacked explicit descriptions of personnel. In such cases, roles were inferred based on the study context and author information, which may have introduced potential for misclassification bias in categorizing nurses' roles. Fourth, this review focused on mapping the characteristics of non‐pharmacological interventions and nurse involvement rather than evaluating their effectiveness. Therefore, the findings provide an overview of the existing evidence rather than definitive conclusions regarding the effectiveness of these interventions. Fifth, most included studies evaluated outcomes during the immediate postoperative period or during hospitalization. The limited follow‐up periods restrict understanding the long‐term effects of nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions on postoperative recovery. Finally, the included studies were geographically concentrated in a limited number of countries, particularly Turkey and the United States, which may restrict the global applicability of the findings. Differences in healthcare systems, clinical practices and nursing roles across regions should therefore be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

This scoping review highlights the diversity and clinical relevance of nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions for postoperative pain management across surgical settings. To strengthen clinical practice, these interventions should be systematically integrated into routine perioperative care through standardized protocols. Clear delineation of nurses' roles across planning, implementation and evaluation stages, supported by adequate institutional resources, may further facilitate their effective implementation. In addition, the use of multidimensional pain assessment tools should be encouraged to better capture the complexity of postoperative pain and recovery.

Future research should expand the evidence across diverse surgical populations and healthcare settings, strengthen nurses' involvement across all stages of intervention development and delivery, and incorporate longer follow‐up periods to better understand sustained outcomes. Furthermore, the use of multidimensional outcome measures should be advanced to reflect the comprehensive impact of non‐pharmacological interventions. By addressing these priorities, nurse‐involved non‐pharmacological interventions have the potential to contribute to more integrated, patient‐centered, and sustainable perioperative care models, ultimately improving postoperative recovery and supporting the global advancement of postoperative pain management.
