Strep pyogenes infection causing rapid onset orbital cellulitis, superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, and necrotizing scleritis with dehiscence of extraocular muscle following strabismus surgery
Authors: Michael E Gray, Eniolami Dosunmu, Roxana Y Rivera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report an infrequent but potentially devastating complication following strabismus surgery and its approach to management.ObservationsA 5-year-old girl underwent bilateral medial rectus recessions for esotropia. She quickly developed progressive orbital cellulitis that was confirmed on computed tomography along with non-occlusive thrombosis of the superior ophthalmic vein. She was treated with intravenous antibiotics, additional surgery (which found a dehisced medial rectus muscle), and anticoagulation. Cultures grew Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A). Ultimately the patient made a full recovery.Conclusion and importanceAlthough orbital cellulitis is a known complication following strabismus surgery, surgeons should be aware of additional secondary complications that can occur and have a low threshold for imaging and repeat surgical intervention.Keywords: Dehisced muscle, Group A Streptococcus, Ophthalmic vein thrombosis, Orbital cellulitis, Strabismus surgery, Streptococcus pyogenes

Case report

A 5-year-old female without significant past medical history underwent uncomplicated bilateral medial rectus recessions for partially accommodative esotropia. The patient was prepped in a standard manner for strabismus surgery using 5% ophthalmic betadine, with a drop placed on the ocular surface following by cleansing of the eyelids, eyelashes, and periocular face. A standard fornix conjunctival incision was used for the surgery. The muscles were recessed and directly secured to sclera using 6-0 polyglactin 910 suture and the conjunctiva was closed with 7-0 mild chromic gut sutures. She was discharged home on topical tobramycin-dexamethasone drops in both eyes. On postoperative day one, she was noted to have a swollen left upper eyelid with mild purulent discharge, but the conjunctiva was not definitely dehisced. The patient was started on oral clindamycin and topical polymyxin B/trimethroprim drops. Topical tobramycin-dexamethasone was continued in just the right eye. On post-operative day two there was worsening periorbital swelling and restricted ocular motility of the left eye. Visual acuity could not be obtained. A relative afferent pupillary defect was noted on the left. The patient was sent to the emergency department for urgent imaging and admission for broad spectrum intravenous antibiotics. Orbital computed tomography (CT) with contrast showed asymmetric left-sided periorbital soft tissue swelling and left-sided proptosis. There was abnormal fat stranding surrounding the left globe with involvement of the left retrobulbar fat. No definite abscess was reported. Also noted was hypoenhancement of the left superior ophthalmic vein, concerning for nonocclusive thrombus (Fig. 1). In addition, there was moderate mucosal thickening of the left maxillary and ethmoid sinuses. The patient was started on intravenous vancomycin, clindamycin and piperacillin-tazobactam.

Given the imaging findings and the rapidity of worsening clinical findings including progressive eyelid swelling, discharge, and severe pain, later on post-operative day two the patient was taken back to the operating room with pediatric ophthalmology and oculoplastics. An examination under anesthesia was performed. Intraocular pressure was elevated in the left eye to 25 mmHg using Tonopen®, compared to 12 mmHg in the right eye. The left eyelids were erythematous and edematous, with proptosis and marked conjunctival injection. The fornix conjunctival surgical wound was dehisced with pus and exposure of the underlying sclera (Fig. 2). The exposed sclera appeared necrotic. The pus was collected and sent for gram stain and culture. Forced ductions exam showed restriction of both adduction and abduction. Posterior segment exam was normal with clear vitreous, normal appearing optic nerve and retina. No scleral depression was performed given concern for necrotic sclera. The eye was gently rotated to expose the medial area, but the medial rectus muscle was not visible. Copious amounts of purulent material were drained from the medial retrobulbar space. Eventually, with the help of the oculoplastic surgeon, the medial rectus muscle was found further back in the retrobulbar space and brought forward. It was noted to be very friable with adherent purulent material. It was carefully secured with 5-0 Mersilene (non-absorbable) suture, but not reattached to the globe given the scleral changes and concern for a scleral perforation. The sutures, which were approximately 3-4cm long, were tucked under the conjunctiva superiorly and inferiorly with the muscle resting on the globe approximately 11mm from the limbus (at the location of the intended recession). The sutures were left long to allow for easy retrieval if needed at a future time. A lateral canthotomy and cantholysis were performed due to concern for elevated intraocular pressure and risk of progression to orbital compartment syndrome, even post-operatively. The orbital space was rinsed with vancomycin and ceftazidime, and the conjunctiva was sutured closed as much as the tissue allowed. The eye was irrigated with ophthalmic Betadine and subconjunctival injections of vancomycin and ceftazidime were performed.

Cultures grew Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A). She quickly improved after surgery and was ultimately discharged home on oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid for 3 weeks. She was also started on anticoagulation with the help of hematology due to the superior vein thrombosis. Repeat CT of the orbits 6 weeks later showed resolved left orbital inflammation and resolved superior ophthalmic vein thrombus, at which time anticoagulation was stopped.

On follow-up ophthalmic exams the initial significant adduction deficit improved with time. Approximately 6 weeks post-surgery she was noted to have an exposed Mersilene suture (Fig. 3). On return to the operating room, the exposed sutures were cut and the conjunctival wound was closed. No further manipulation of the muscle was performed. At the time of this surgery she was given intravenous antibiotic for prophylaxis and oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid for 1 week. On most recent follow-up 6 months after her initial surgery, she had full motility of both eyes, equal acuity with correction (20/40 in each eye), no relative afferent pupillary defect, an intermittent esotropia of 3 prism diopters and intermittent left hypertropia of 4 prism diopters in distance, and at near intermittent esotropia of 9 prism diopters and intermittent left hypertropia of 4 prism diopters. Her conjunctiva showed minimal scarring nasally with no suture exposure (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Orbital cellulitis following strabismus surgery is a known but infrequent complication, with reported incidence between 1 in 1100 and 1 in 1900 and perhaps higher.1, 2, 3 Staphylococcus aureus is the most common causative agent in post strabismus infections.2,3 Group A streptococcus has been known to cause rapid onset postoperative orbital infection.4, 5, 6 While superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis can be a complication of orbital cellulitis, this specific complication following strabismus surgery is unusual. Of note the CT scan did not report any definite abscess, but during her emergent surgery there was a significant pocket of pus in the medial orbit surrounding the medial rectus. It should be noted that although no abscess was noted on CT, the operating surgeon was concerned based on radiographic findings of retrobulbar fat stranding and proptosis. Also of interest is that the muscle, once found, was not directly sutured back to the globe given concerns of necrotic sclera. However, the muscle clearly reattached itself during the healing process as evidenced by her return of full adduction and overall good alignment, highlighting the importance of placing it in the intended location.

The etiology of this child's rapid infection in unclear, but we suspect eye rubbing and poor hand hygiene may have contributed based on parental report, although Group A strep is not part of the normal skin flora. We doubt sinus disease as the etiology as the CT showed only mild paranasal sinus disease, the patient did not have any sinus symptoms, there was no evidence of bony erosion on the scans or in the OR, and strep pyogenes is not a typical sinus pathogen. However, while the sinus findings were felt to most likely be reactive to the infectious and inflammatory process of the orbit, we cannot conclusively say that she did not have sinusitis that was contributory. In any case of orbital infection, it is important to evaluate for possible secondary complications such as superior vein thrombosis as this entity can lead to even more severe consequences such as cavernous sinus thrombosis and its sequalae. Despite this patient's initial post-operative course, she has subsequently done very well, as she maintains good acuity, alignment, and appearance. The decision to order imaging and return to the operating room promptly upon evidence of rapid clinical deterioration was crucial and resulted in the good outcome. Surgeons should recognize and counsel families on signs of post-operative strabismus surgery infections, have a low threshold for imaging and intervention in clinical deterioration, and be mindful of rare but potentially devastating secondary complications such as occurred in this patient.
