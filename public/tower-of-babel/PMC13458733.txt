Strengthening the EGFR blockade for those lung cancers that need it most
Authors: Shuo Shi, Petros Christopoulos
Published: 2026

Activating mutations of the epidermal growth factor receptor gene (EGFR) were a milestone discovery that marked the onset of precision medicine for Thoracic Oncology approximately twenty years ago (1). Today, EGFR+ disease still remains the most active subset of oncogene-driven non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with rapid drug development and the highest rate of new drug approvals spanning the entire spectrum of modern therapeutics, from next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) to bispecific antibodies and antibody drug conjugates (ADC) (2). Besides the relatively high frequency of EGFR mutations in NSCLC, ranging from 15% among Western to >40% in Asian patients, this extraordinary level of research activity is also fuelled by the great heterogeneity of activating EGFR alterations, which is most prominent in the special subset of “atypical” (aka “rare” or “uncommon”) variants, i.e., excluding exon 19 deletions (del19), p.L858R and exon 20 insertions (ex20ins), with hundreds of unique examples described in the literature so far (3). Apart from huge structural variability, these are also characterized by lower sensitivity to available EGFR TKI and associated with worse prognosis than classical EGFR mutations, which remains a major unmet need today despite the general therapeutic progress of the last decade (4).

In this context, Tomasini et al. investigated the third generation (3G) EGFR TKI lazertinib combined with the EGFR/Mesenchymal–Epithelial Transition (MET)-bispecific antibody amivantamab as treatment for 105 NSCLC patients with atypical EGFR mutations, including 56% p.G719X, 26% p.S768I, 23% p.L861Q, and ≤2 previous lines of therapy in Cohort C of the CHRYSALIS-2 phase 2 study (NCT04077463) (5). Doses were 240 mg orally once daily for lazertinib, and 1,050 mg (1,400 mg if ≥80 kg) for amivantamab i.v. once weekly for the first 4 weeks and subsequently once every 2 weeks. Almost half of the participants were treatment-naive (49/105 or 46.7%), while the rest had previously received mostly first/second-generation (1G/2G) EGFR TKI (43/105 or 41%) and/or platinum-based chemotherapy (13/105 or 12.4%). The combination showed good efficacy in the overall population with an objective response rate (ORR) of 52% [95% confidence interval (CI): 42–62%], median duration of response (mDoR) of 14.1 months (95% CI: 9.5–26.2), and median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 11.1 months (95% CI: 7.8–17.8), while the median overall survival (mOS) was immature and not estimable yet (NE; 95% CI: 22.8–NE) after a median follow-up of 16.1 months. Outcomes among treatment-naive patients were even better with an ORR of 57% (95% CI: 42–71%), mPFS of 19.5 months (95% CI: 11.2–NE), mDoR of 20.7 months (95% CI: 9.9–NE), and mOS NE (95% CI: 26.3–NE). There was no difference in efficacy by the presence of solitary vs. compound atypical EGFR mutations, or according to their structural class [P-loop and αC-helix compressing (PACC; n=38) vs. classical-like (n=14), vs. T790M-like (n=3) (3)], or based on the tumors’ protein 53 gene (TP53) status (mutated vs. wild-type), while patients with coexisting EGFR del19, p.L858R, or ex20ins were excluded (5). Moreover, there were no new safety signals, with grade ≥3 adverse events (AEs) in 70% of patients and discontinuation of any agent in 28%. Most frequent AE were rash (in 67% of all patients, grade ≥3 in 13%), paronychia (67% overall, grade ≥3 in 5%), hypoalbuminemia (59% overall, grade ≥3 in 8%), and infusion-related reactions (56% overall, grade ≥3 in 4%), while pulmonary embolism was detected in 15% (grade ≥3 in 8%) (5).

Despite the phase 2 character of CHRYSALIS-2, these results from cohort C reported by Tomasini et al. are very important for several reasons. First, they establish amivantamab/lazertinib as the regimen with the best survival outcomes reported so far for NSCLC with atypical EGFR mutations, if we compare with the readout of previous studies employing other drugs in a similar setting (Table 1). Even though the ORR has been comparable or even better with some EGFR TKI, e.g., 71.1% with afatinib for patients in the first line (Table 1), amivantamab/lazertinib remain superior due to the longer PFS and OS, which underline the importance of combined EGFR blockade for durability of response. Biochemically, the combination of amivantamab/lazertinib can inhibit EGFR more potently than TKI monotherapies, e.g., afatinib or osimertinib, due to the combined intracellular (by the TKI) and extracellular (by amivantamab) effects. Such a stronger, combined EGFR blockade appears to directly address the root problem of atypical EGFR variants, whose higher risk is attributable to lower TKI sensitivity (caused by the aberrant kinase domain structure hindering docking of the drug), rather than intrinsically stronger oncogenic signalling, as happens for example in the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)+ disease with Echinoderm Microtubule-Associated Protein-Like 4 (EML4)-ALK variant 3 compared other ALK fusion variants (6,7). The particular strength of amivantamab/lazertinib was also evident in the similar context of the phase 3 MARIPOSA trial for NSCLC with classical EGFR mutations, where the same combination could prevent the emergence of acquired on-target (i.e., secondary EGFR mutations) and off-target resistance (i.e., other escape pathways, like MET amplifications) more effectively than osimertinib (8). With the results of Tomasini et al., this notion of higher potency for amivantamab/lazertinib compared to TKI monotherapies is now extended to other EGFR alterations and a new key principle is revealed: that TKI/antibody combinations could become a universally preferred treatment for EGFR-driven NSCLC regardless of the molecular disease subtype, as in the cohort C of the CHRYSALIS-2 study a similar and very good efficacy was observed regardless of the exact EGFR variant (PACC or other, single or compound) and TP53 co-mutation status (5). Thus, the strong synergy between amivantamab and lazertinib could provide a new “one-size-fits-all“ standard of care for NSCLC with atypical EGFR mutations, improving and simplifying our clinical algorithms, which currently prioritize either afatinib for most (e.g., p.G719X), or osimertinib for the remaining few “classical-like“ rare EGFR variants (e.g., p.L861Q) based on structural considerations and clinical evidence (9-11), as CHRYSALIS-2 suggests that amivantamab/lazertinib is superior to both (Table 1). This is further supported by a retrospective comparison of the CHRYSALIS-2 results with real-world data (RWD) from patients treated in the first line presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Annual Congress in 2024, which showed a significantly longer time-to-treatment-discontinuation (TTD) for amivantamab/lazertinib (median 14.0 months, 95% CI: 9.7–NE) compared to osimertinib (9.6 months, 95% CI: 3.4–23.2), or afatinib (median 4.5 months, 95% CI: 3.2–NE) (5). Such leveraging of RWD to create synthetic control arms and guide clinical decision-making becomes imperative for patients with rare molecular alterations, whose small numbers preclude multi-arm randomized prospective comparisons (12).

Main downside of the stronger EGFR inhibition is skin toxicity, which can be very bothersome, especially for younger women, who represent a typical patient profile for EGFR mutated NSCLC. Indeed, rash and paronychia were the most frequently reported EGFR-related adverse events by Tomasini et al. (5), occurring in 67% (13% grade ≥3) and 67% (5% grade ≥3) of patients respectively, followed by stomatitis in 30% (2% grade ≥3) and diarrhea in 23% (no grade ≥3). Furthermore, there were hypoalbuminemia (59% overall, 8% grade ≥3) and peripheral edema (36% overall, 3% grade ≥3) as MET-related AEs, infusion-related reactions in 56% (4% grade ≥3), venous thromboembolism in 30% (10% grade ≥3), discontinuation of both or one of the two drugs in 7% or 28% of patients, respectively, as well as one treatment-related death (1%) (5). However, for our own daily clinical practice important new knowledge and additional tools are already available to mitigate this problem in ways inaccessible for the CHRYSALIS-2 investigators. First and foremost, the prophylactic regimen established by the successful COCOON global phase 2 study (NCT06120140) with antibiotics, nail antiseptics and long-acting skin hydration could reduce dermatologic problems as well as the discontinuation rate of amivantamab/lazertinib by ca. 50% in a randomized prospective comparison (13). In addition, subcutaneous instead of intravenous administration of amivantamab could reduce infusion-related reactions of amivantamab by approximately 2/3 and—in combination with prophylactic anticoagulation, which is obligatory in this setting—also suppress the rate of thromboembolism to <10%, as demonstrated by the randomized phase 3 PALOMA-3 study (14). None of these measures were available for patients treated within the CHRYSALIS-2 study, which means that the risk-to-benefit ratio of amivantamab/lazertinib for our patients today would be even more favourable than that reported by Tomasini et al. The merit of amivantamab/lazertinib in this setting is further underlined by the updated CHRYSALIS-2 readout recently presented at ASCO 2026, which showed an extraordinary median OS exceeding 40 months in the first-line, which was significantly and more than twice longer than the OS with any other physician-selected EGFR TKI in a comparison with retrospective real-world Flatiron data: 41.0 (95% CI: 27.7–NE) vs. 15.2 (95% CI: 11.2–24.8) months (15). These results clearly stand out above any other outcomes for NSCLC with atypical EGFR mutations reported to date (Table 1), and therefore we can only wish that amivantamab/lazertinib may become available for our patients despite the phase 2 character of CHRYSALIS-2, similar to NSCLC with classical EGFR alterations (del19/L858R), for which the same regimen represents a new standard of care since 2024 based on the results of the pivotal phase 3 MARIPOSA trial (16).

Further developments in NSCLC with classical EGFR mutations suggest the combination of EGFR TKI with chemotherapy as highlighted by the FLAURA2 study results could represent another potential path forward (17). Indeed, a single-arm phase 2 study is ongoing in Germany to prospectively test this approach (FLAURARE, EudraCT Nr. 2024-516790-78-00). Main advantages of this option are the limited toxicity, as pemetrexed is tolerated relatively well and the platinum administration is limited to the first 3 months only; the fact that all patients can get chemotherapy circumventing the problem of high attrition comprising approximately 30% between the first and second treatment lines in EGFR-driven NSCLC (18); as well as the retained activity of chemotherapy in situations of EGFR variants with lower TKI sensitivity and the occasional tumours with co-occurring additional drivers besides EGFR (3,19). However, at least for TKI-sensitive tumours, experience from FLAURA2 shows that the additional chemotherapy, despite the clear early benefit, does not increase the rate of complete responses, cannot modify the profile of acquired resistance and is therefore unlikely to produce a plateau of survival curves (20). Novel EGFR TKI with enhanced sensitivity for atypical EGFR mutations represent another important strategy to improve patient outcomes, however these compounds are still emerging and their results very limited. No conclusions are yet possible for zipalertinib with immature published data from only 8 first-line patients so far (Table 1), while the firmonertinib first-line trials [phase 2 in China (NCT05548348); randomized global phase 3 ALPACCA (NCT07185997)] are ongoing, and clinical results from Blossom Hill therapeutics (BH)-30643, a novel reversible, mutant-selective macrocyclic pan-EGFR inhibitor with excellent biochemical properties, are still pending (21). Several ADCs, like the Trophoblast Cell Surface Antigen 2 (TROP2)-directed datopotamab deructecan (NCT06350097, TROPION-Lung14), and the EGFR/Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 3 (HER3)-directed izalontamab brengitecan (NCT06838273, PANKU-Lung04) are currently in phase 3 trials alongside osimertinib as first-line treatments for NSCLC with classical EGFR mutations and could thus become an additional future therapeutic option in combination with TKI for tumours with atypical EGFR mutations, as well.

Nonetheless, it remains questionable today whether cure of metastatic lung cancer can ever be achieved using molecularly targeted drugs and/or chemotherapy only. We believe that for this, novel immunomodulatory strategies to harness the microenvironment of EGFR-driven tumours will be essential, as PD-(L)1 inhibitors are not active against them (22). Ultimately, we perceive our best prospects in the arduous fight against NSCLC with atypical EGFR mutations to rely on the rational integration of novel targeted drugs (Table 1) with next-generation immunotherapeutics, such as mRNA vaccines, TCR-T cells, and multispecific antibodies, including amivantamab (23,24), whose advent in the first-line alongside lazertinib would therefore pave the way for a brighter future by addressing what these tumours have always needed most: a stronger EGFR blockade (5).
