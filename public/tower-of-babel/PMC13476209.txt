Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir for Acute Hepatitis C Virus Infection in Individuals with a History of Prior Hepatitis C Infection
Authors: Christoph Boesecke, Kimberly Workowski, Stuart C. Gordon, Barbra Cave, John Marcinak, Michael G. Miller, Dimitri Semizarov, Anne Welhaven, Axel Baumgarten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn a phase 3b study, glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (G/P) for 8 weeks was well tolerated and effective in treatment-naive adults with acute hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. Here we analyze the efficacy and safety of G/P in adults with acute HCV infection with a history of prior HCV infection.MethodsParticipants with acute HCV were stratified by presence of prior HCV infection. Efficacy was assessed in the intention-to-treat population (ITT; all patients receiving ≥ 1 G/P dose) and modified ITT virologic failure population, which excluded non-virologic failures (mITT-VF). Safety (adverse events [AEs] and laboratory values) was assessed, as well as viral kinetics.ResultsAmong 286 participants, 52 (18.2%) had ≥ 1 prior HCV infection and 234 (81.8%) did not. High-risk characteristics in those with prior infection versus those without: human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) coinfection, 92.3% versus 40.2%; unprotected male–male sexual activity, 78.8% versus 63.7%; and current/recent injection drug use, 23.1% versus 12.4%, respectively. Introduction

In 2022, there were an estimated 50 million individuals living with hepatitis C virus (HCV) worldwide, with approximately 1 million new infections registered annually [1, 2]. Increased rates of acute HCV infections have been observed in populations at high risk of loss to follow-up and reinfection post-HCV cure, such as men who have sex with men (MSM) and people who inject drugs (PWID) [3–6]. Recent evidence suggests the speed of HCV transmission among MSM may be faster than other known HCV transmission routes. Comparing two HCV epidemics in Lyon, France revealed a distinct epidemic among MSM that is doubling ten times faster than the ongoing epidemic among non-MSM [7]. PWID remain an important group for HCV transmission; a modeling study suggested that 43% of new HCV infections could be prevented over a 12-year period if the risk of HCV acquisition associated with injection drug use and shared paraphernalia was removed [8].

In some instances, treatment in high-risk groups is deferred until HCV infection is chronic, contributing to viral transmission and the loss of patients from care. Thus, immediate intervention is recommended by the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases/Infectious Diseases Society of America, the European Association for the Study of the Liver, and the European AIDS Clinical Society guidelines, and is necessary as a strategy to reduce HCV incidence, improve continuity of care, as well as reduce morbidity and mortality associated with progression to chronic HCV infection [9–12].

Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) have been shown to be highly effective in curing chronic HCV infections [13], and several DAA-based regimens have been evaluated for the treatment of acute HCV infection [14–17]. Glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (G/P) is approved globally for the treatment of chronic HCV infection and was approved in the USA for the treatment of acute HCV infections in June 2025 [18]. Previously, we have reported that an 8-week regimen of G/P was well tolerated and highly effective in a phase 3b study of 286 participants with acute HCV infection [19]. This study included both participants without prior HCV infection and those with one or more previously cured infection.

Reinfections remain a concern in people with ongoing HCV exposure risk factors, especially among PWID and MSM. These risk factors can be a barrier to treatment in some settings, as there may be reluctance among healthcare teams to provide HCV treatment to those with high risk of reinfection, in addition to reimbursement restrictions [20]. Some MSM have increased risk of HCV reinfection after successful HCV treatment, due to high-risk sexual activity, such as having unprotected sex with multiple partners or men who have unprotected sex with men, notably when combined with the sharing of drug paraphernalia [21, 22]. Reinfections can occur quickly with the median time from initial infection to reinfection being 1.9 years, and 1.1 years to a second reinfection [23]. Further, the reinfection rate following treatment for a primary infection has been reported to be between 10 and 15 per 100 person-years, with increased risk among those with injection drug use and certain sexual behaviors, such as “chemsex” and “slamsex” [24, 25]. Regardless of whether there is a new acute infection or a reinfection, prompt detection and treatment are necessary to impact transmission of HCV and improve continuity of care.

In patients with HCV infection, the distinction between a late relapse of a current infection versus a reinfection can be challenging, especially among high-risk populations [26]. The distinction is relevant, as retreatment of a relapse may require a revised treatment strategy from the initial strategy, while a reinfection can be treated as a treatment-naive case. The use of first-line regimens has the potential to reduce patient burden in cases of reinfection, as they are typically shorter in duration and contain fewer active components compared with second-line regimens [9, 10, 12]. Reinfection can be confirmed if the current infection is caused by a distinct strain from the prior infection; however, tools to assess this are not available to all patients [26]. While the risk of recurrence after achieving sustained virologic response at 12 weeks post-treatment (SVR12) with DAAs is low, the increased risk in populations of interest is largely driven by reinfection rather than late relapse [27].

Here we analyze the efficacy and safety of G/P, and HCV viral kinetics, in adults with acute HCV infection who had a history of previously cured HCV infection in a single-arm, prospective phase 3 study, including those with high-risk characteristics, such as HCV–human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) coinfection, high-risk sexual activity, and injection drug use.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Data were derived from a post hoc subgroup analysis of a multicenter, single-arm, prospective phase 3 study of G/P in adults with acute HCV infection who were treatment naive for the current infection (NCT04903626) [19].

The trial protocol upon which this post hoc analysis was conducted was approved by the independent ethics committees or institutional review boards at all the trial centers. The trial was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki; all participants provided written informed consent.

The study design and participant population of this trial have been previously published [19]. Eligible participants were adults and adolescents, ≥ 12 years of age, with acute HCV infection and no prior treatment for the current infection. Eligibility required a physician diagnosis of acute HCV infection, quantifiable HCV RNA at screening, and meeting ≥ 1 of 4 acute criteria (criterion A–D), described in Table 1. Participants could not have previously received treatment for their current HCV infection and did not have a history of liver decompensation. Prior HCV infection was determined on the basis of participant self-reporting of history of prior infection at study entry and review of medical records by study staff. Participants who reported a previous HCV infection that had been treated and cured prior to the acute infection were eligible. Investigators excluded participants with chronic HCV while ensuring those with acute HCV were included.

Endpoints

The primary efficacy endpoint was achievement of SVR12, defined as HCV RNA less than the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) 12 weeks after the last dose of G/P in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, defined as all patients receiving ≥ 1 dose of G/P. Participants were seen at screening; baseline (day 1); weeks 2, 4, and 8; and post-treatment weeks 4 and 12. Real‐time reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction was utilized to quantify plasma HCV RNA for SVR12 assessment. Plasma HCV RNA levels were determined for each sample collected by the central laboratory using the Roche COBAS® AmpliPrep/COBAS® TaqMan® Quantitative HCV Test, version 2.0. The LLOQ for this assay (regardless of genotype) was 15 IU/mL. The key secondary endpoint was achievement of SVR12 in the modified ITT population, defined as all participants in the ITT population excluding those who did not achieve SVR12 for reasons other than virologic failure (mITT-VF; i.e., excluding those with HCV reinfection, those not achieving SVR12 owing to premature discontinuation of G/P, and those missing HCV RNA data in the SVR12 window after backward imputation).

Safety outcomes included adverse events (AEs), serious AEs, AEs leading to discontinuation of G/P, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and total bilirubin levels during treatment and at the final treatment visit. AEs were coded using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 27.0. AEs of hepatic decompensation/failure included events such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, esophageal variceal bleeding, hepatorenal syndrome, and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. ALT and total bilirubin levels were graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.03 (grades 1–4), with changes from baseline grade to the worst and final grades during treatment summarized. Post-nadir ALT > 3 × upper limit of normal (ULN) with total bilirubin > 2 × ULN was also assessed.

Viral kinetics, stratified by prior infection status, were assessed as an exploratory endpoint.

Statistical Analysis

For primary and secondary efficacy endpoints, the number and percentage of participants achieving the endpoint were calculated along with a two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) using Wilson’s score method, for subjects with a history of prior HCV infection and without. Safety endpoints were summarized with the numbers and percentages of participants.

SAS version 9.4 software was used for the statistical analyses.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In the ITT population (n = 286), 52 participants (18.2%) had ≥ 1 prior HCV infection and 234 (81.8%) had no prior HCV infection. Baseline demographic and disease characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Overall, the majority of participants were male, MSM, non-cirrhotic, and infected with HCV genotype 1; 47.9% had current or recent illicit drug use, and 49.7% had HCV–HIV coinfection. Baseline demographics and disease characteristics were generally balanced between those with and without prior HCV infection, except for HIV coinfection (92.3% among those with prior infection versus 40.2% among those without), unprotected sexual activity with other males (78.8% versus 63.7%), and current or recent injection drug use (23.1% versus 12.4%). Baseline ALT levels were generally numerically lower in those with prior HCV infection with a median 73.5 U/L (interquartile range: 44.5, 154.5) compared with median 155.5 U/L (interquartile range: 61.0, 302.0) in those without prior HCV infection. Among the 144 participants without HIV coinfection, 58 (40.3%) had use of any medication for pre-exposure prophylaxis to HIV infection. Among these 58 participants, 2 (3.4%) had a history of prior HCV infection. Among the 86 participants without HIV coinfection and without pre-exposure prophylaxis use, 2 (2.3%) had a history of prior HCV infection. Efficacy

Among the 52 participants in the ITT population who had prior HCV infection, 51 (98.1%; 95% CI 89.9–99.7) achieved SVR12 (Fig. 1). One participant (1.9%) did not achieve SVR12 owing to premature discontinuation of G/P. Among the 234 participants in the ITT set who had no prior HCV infection, 224 (95.7%; 95% CI 92.3–97.7) achieved SVR12. Reasons for not achieving SVR12 were post-treatment reinfection in two participants (0.9%), premature discontinuation of G/P in four participants (1.7%), and missing SVR12 data in four participants (1.7%). There were no participants with virologic failure. In the mITT-VF population, among the 51 participants with prior HCV infection, the SVR12 rate was 100% (95% CI 93.0–100), and among the 224 with no prior infection, the SVR12 rate was also 100% (95% CI 98.3–100).

Viral Kinetics

Among participants in the ITT population with data available and prior HCV infection, the percentage of participants with HCV RNA less than the LLOQ was 84.3% (43/51) at study week 2, 94.0% (47/50) at week 4, 100% (43/43) at week 8, and 98.1% (51/52) at the final treatment visit (Supplementary Fig. 1). Similar results were seen in participants with no prior infection, where the rates were 74.1% (163/220), 95.6% (216/226), 100% (207/207), and 99.1% (230/232) at weeks 2, 4, 8, and final visit, respectively. Further, the results were comparable between the ITT and mITT-VF populations.

Safety and Tolerability

A greater percentage of participants with prior HCV infection experienced at least one AE (75.0%) compared with those with no prior HCV infection (56.4%) (Table 4). AEs that occurred in ≥ 5% of participants in either group were nasopharyngitis, diarrhea, asthenia, and fatigue. The rates of nasopharyngitis, diarrhea, and asthenia were numerically higher in those with prior HCV infection. Serious AEs occurred in 5.8% of participants with prior HCV infection and 3.0% of those without, and AEs leading to G/P discontinuation occurred in 0% and 0.4%, and AEs leading to G/P interruption in 1.9% and 0.9%, respectively. No participant in either group had an AE of hepatic decompensation/hepatic failure. There were no deaths in the study. Discussion

G/P was highly effective, safe, and well tolerated when used to treat individuals with acute HCV infection acquired after previously achieving SVR12 with treatment for a prior HCV infection. These results were observed in a population of reinfected participants that included those with high-risk sexual practices, such as having unprotected sex with multiple partners or men who have unprotected sex with men, and current or recent injection and illicit drug use. Nearly all the participants with prior infection were living with HIV coinfection.

In this study, the SVR12 rates achieved with G/P for acute infection were high regardless of prior infection status, with ITT SVR12 rates of 98.1% in those with prior infection and 95.7% in those without, and no virologic failures. The SVR12 rates achieved with G/P in participants with acute infection were similar to the rates from G/P for chronic HCV infection [28–31]. Additionally, ≥ 94% of participants had HCV RNA less than the LLOQ at week 4 of treatment or later, regardless of prior infection status. Prompt viral suppression is important because elevated HCV viral load can increase the risk of HCV transmission [32, 33]. One epidemiologic analysis of nearly 7500 individuals with chronic HCV infection identified high viral load, young age, and HIV coinfection as indicators of increased HCV transmission [32], so rapid viral load reduction and eradication may limit infectivity and transmission to others, even while an individual is receiving therapy. Of note, prior HCV infection status did not have an impact on viral kinetics, as the proportions of participants with HCV RNA less than the LLOQ did not differ between the groups at points from week 2 to the final treatment visit.

DAAs have been found to be generally well tolerated in the acute HCV setting [14–16]. In this study, no new safety signals were identified, and the safety profile of G/P was similar in those with and without prior HCV infection. Further, the safety profile of G/P observed in the acute HCV setting was comparable to that observed with G/P for the treatment of chronic HCV infection [34]. Consistent with other studies evaluating DAAs for acute HCV infection, ALT and bilirubin levels decreased over the course of treatment, regardless of prior infection status [16, 35]. Additionally, the ALT and bilirubin elevation profiles were similar between those with prior infection and those without, suggesting that prior infection was not a risk factor for liver test abnormalities while on treatment. Of interest, it appeared that baseline ALT elevations were lower in participants with prior infection compared with those with no prior infection. The reason for this is not known, yet it can be speculated that this is due to a lower inflammatory response to a subsequent HCV infection in those with a prior infection compared with a more robust response to an initial exposure of a first infection. However, imbalances between the two groups in acute HCV infection inclusion criteria (such as criterion C, which includes ALT elevation) and other baseline factors, such as estimated duration of acute infection, could be confounding factors in this observation.

There are some limitations to this analysis. First, as a post hoc subgroup analysis, this study was not designed to compare the efficacy and safety of G/P between the two groups, and the number of participants with prior infection was relatively small. Secondly, the primary clinical trial that this analysis was based on was a single-arm study with no control arm, so there is the potential for factors other than G/P to contribute to outcomes. However, viral load and laboratory outcomes are not expected to be impacted by the placebo effect. Further, as there is no previously established standard of care for acute HCV infection and, due to the high cure rates with DAAs, it is considered unethical to use a placebo arm. As such, a control arm would have had limited value. Another limitation is that the prior infection status was determined on the basis of patient self-reporting and review of medical records, so there was the potential for misclassification. Two patients had reported prior infections despite testing negative for anti-HCV antibody at baseline but positive for HCV RNA and core antigen. This may have been due to seroreversion, which can occur with heightened frequency over time in patients who are HIV-positive (one of the two participants who appeared to have seroreversion was HIV-positive) [36]. Lastly, the selection criteria for the clinical trial may contribute to selection bias, and the study population may not be fully representative of patients in the real world, which may include more difficult to reach patients and those with poor adherence to treatment.

Conclusions

This study supports that individuals with acute HCV infection who have a history of prior HCV infection can be successfully treated with G/P, regardless of their history of one or more prior HCV infection or the most recent prior treatment regimen used. The high SVR12 rates observed in participants with previous HCV infection suggest that these individuals can be viewed as treatment naive when selecting a treatment plan, instead of needing a more complicated retreatment approach. These data, including the reduced viral load observed after treatment initiation, which may help reduce infectiousness, support the immediate treatment of acute HCV infection in adults who may have a reinfection or may be at high risk of reinfection. Individuals at risk of reinfection should be tested at least yearly for HCV RNA (HCV RNA testing is recommended because a positive HCV-antibody test is expected regardless of current infection status), and following behaviors associated with increased risk of HCV exposure, so that treatment can be promptly initiated if the individual is viremic [9, 10]. Risk of reinfection in high-risk populations should not deter testing and treatment without delay, a strategy that can help reduce onward HCV transmission and loss to care.
