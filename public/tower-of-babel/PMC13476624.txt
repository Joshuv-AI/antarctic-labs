Automated computed tomography quantification of iliofemoral venous arc length demonstrates left-sided predominance and sex-specific variation
Authors: Pauline M Berens, Benjamin C Liu, Arash Fereydooni, Shipra Arya, Christopher P Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn treatment planning for iliac venous obstruction, iliofemoral venous anatomy is central to procedural planning, but population-level reference data on venous arc length are limited. Arc length, the distance measured along the three-dimensional vessel centerline, reflects the longitudinal course a device must traverse, and is therefore relevant to stent length selection and implant coverage. We used automated computed tomography segmentation and centerline generation to characterize iliofemoral venous arc length and evaluated variation by laterality, age, and sex.MethodsIn this retrospective study, a machine learning (nnU-Net-based) pipeline was used to segment iliofemoral vessels from participant computed tomography scans. Three-dimensional centerlines were generated from these segmentations, and iliofemoral arc length was measured from the iliocaval confluence or aortic bifurcation to the common femoral venous confluence or common femoral bifurcation (encompassing common iliac, external iliac, and common femoral veins/arteries). After quality control and exclusion of patients <25 years, bilateral venous centerlines were available in 711 patients. Right-left arc length differences were compared within patients using paired t-tests. Arterial arc lengths were quantified for comparison. Multivariable linear regression evaluated the relationship between arc length and core covariates such as age, height, and sex.ResultsAmong adults with bilateral venous measurements, iliofemoral venous arc length was 14.8 mm greater on the left side than the right [95% confidence interval (CI), 13.9-15.6; P < .001] corresponding to +7.6% side difference. Height was strongly positively associated with venous arc length (+8.2 mm/10 cm; 95% CI, 7.1-9.3; P < .001). After adjusting for age and height, females had significantly longer venous arc length than males (+5.9 mm; 95% CI, 3.5-8.4; P < .001), with a more pronounced effect on the left side than the right (+7.9 vs +4.4 mm; P < .001). Increased age was associated with a modest decrease in venous arc length (−1.1 mm/decade; 95% CI, −1.8 to −0.4; P = .002).ConclusionsIn this large-scale morphometric study, iliofemoral venous arc length demonstrates meaningful variation by laterality, height, age, and sex, with left-sided predominance and longer height-adjusted venous arc length in females. These population-level reference data suggest that laterality and sex-specific differences may be relevant when considering venous intervention planning, device working length, and stent coverage decisions.Keywords: Femoral vein, Computed tomography, Machine learning, Anatomy

In the treatment of nonthrombotic iliac vein lesions (NIVLs), May-Thurner syndrome, and other forms of chronic iliac venous obstruction, anatomic characterization focuses heavily on vessel diameter and degree of compression.1 Although these characteristics are critical for proper stent sizing, contemporary practice additionally emphasizes the importance of appropriate extent of stent coverage.2 Proper iliac venous length measurements and extent of coverage are essential to minimize protrusion into the inferior vena cava (IVC) beyond the confluence, which has been associated with increased risk of contralateral deep venous thrombosis.3,4

Consensus guidelines recommend the use of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) study in conjunction with fluoroscopy for intraoperative anatomic assessment of venous length and diameter.5,6 Nevertheless, computed tomography (CT) remains useful in preprocedural planning due to its wide availability and ability to characterize anatomy in an expeditious, noninvasive manner.7 Although CT-derived luminal measurements are not interchangeable with IVUS study for intraprocedural sizing, CT-based centerline analysis may still provide useful preprocedural information regarding longitudinal vessel course and anticipated working length.

Arc length reflects the distance along the vessel centerline, and CT-derived arc length may serve as a noninvasive preprocedural proxy for longitudinal vessel course, informing device working length, delivery system reach, and implant coverage. However, population-level data characterizing iliofemoral venous length, and how this varies by laterality and between demographic groups are limited. Quantitative measurements of iliofemoral venous length derive from a single imaging series of 22 patients with venous obstruction and a few small cadaveric studies.2,8,9 Although manual measurement of geometric metrics is time-consuming and lacks standardization, the advent of machine learning allows for consistent derivation of metrics at scale, to better characterize anatomy and variation in large cohorts. The purpose of this study is to provide large-scale, standardized quantitative reference data, including population-level percentile values and sex effects to inform contemporary device design and procedure planning.

In this retrospective analysis, we used a machine learning-based automated pipeline to perform CT-based segmentation of the iliofemoral vasculature, extracted centerlines, and quantified centerline arc length in adults. We then evaluated right-left laterality differences in iliofemoral venous arc length and associations of arc length with age, height, and sex. We hypothesized that iliofemoral venous arc length would differ by laterality, with a longer course on the left, and would vary by sex independent of body height, and that arterial arc length would provide a contrasting internal comparator.

Methods

Study design and cohort

We retrospectively reviewed patients at our institution who underwent contrast-enhanced CT scans for any indication. Contrast timing was not protocoled to a dedicated arterial or venous phase but was intentionally variable across the cohort. This allowed for opacification of both the arterial and venous systems for clear anatomic delineation and reflected the broad range of clinical indications for which the scans were obtained. We included patients across five age groups (0-20, 21-40, 41-60, 61-80, and over 81 years), with approximately 200 patients/age group. Key demographics including sex, age, race, body mass index (BMI) and height were obtained from the electronic medical health record. This study was approved by the Stanford University institutional review board (eProtocol No. 70872). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

For this analysis, we included only adults aged ≥ 25 years to focus on differences in arc length in the adult population and avoid differences related to changing pediatric body size. Patients with previous vascular reconstruction or device implantation in the areas of interest (such as IVC, iliac veins, femoral veins, aorta, iliac arteries, and femoral arteries) were excluded.

Automated vessel segmentation

A deep learning-based pipeline was developed to enable automatic segmentation of CT images to detect the IVC, infrarenal aorta, and bilateral iliofemoral arteries and veins. In this context, the term “pipeline” refers to an automated, multistep computational workflow in which the output of each stage—image preprocessing, anatomic identification, and deep learning segmentation—serves as the input to the next, enabling segmentation to be performed consistently and at scale without manual tracing. This pipeline utilizes the nnU-Net framework for biomedical image segmentation,10 and was trained and validated on the TotalSegmentator dataset.11 The pipeline was further fine-tuned for these anatomic regions using a custom training set of manually annotated CT scans segmented by vascular surgery residents using ITK-SNAP software (Penn Image Computing and Science Laboratory; n = 30). Vessel segmentations underwent morphological postprocessing to resolve discontinuities in three-dimensional structure.

Centerline extraction

Centerlines were extracted from these segmentations using a method described in the Vascular Modeling Tool Kit (VMTK; Orobix Srl), where segmentations were first converted to surface meshes.12 Subsequently, a Voronoi-based skeletonization method was used to generate a path from the inlet to outlet of the surface mesh representative of the centerline.13,14 The inlet was seeded at the iliocaval confluence or aortic bifurcation, and the outlet at the common femoral venous confluence or the common femoral arterial bifurcation. The path was then smoothed with a cubic Hermite spline and sequential Fourier smoothing to remove signal noise, resulting in a final centerline representative of the physiological vessel path15 (Fig 1). The centerline extraction and smoothing methods applied here have been previously validated and described in several vessel geometry studies.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Centerline coordinates were used to calculate arc lengths in millimeter, which were reviewed for physiological suitability and excluded in cases of incomplete vessel segmentation or centerline extraction. For each participant, four centerlines were attempted: left iliofemoral vein, right iliofemoral vein, left iliofemoral artery, and right iliofemoral artery. To compare within-patient differences, patients were only included in the analysis if they had adequate centerlines bilaterally for a given vessel type.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Python (module: statsmodels). Patients with bilateral arc length measurements for each vessel type were included in the analysis, which was performed separately for veins and arteries. For the primary analysis, arc length differences were compared by laterality using within-patient paired two-tailed t-tests (right-left, testing whether mean difference differed from zero). Results are reported as mean difference with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and Cohen dz effect size, computed as the mean right-left difference divided by the standard deviation of the paired difference.17 Percent differences are expressed relative to the paired-cohort mean arc length for that vessel type.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate the relationship between bilateral mean arc length (average of right and left per patient for each vessel type) and core covariates, including age (years), height (cm), and sex using heteroskedasticity-consistent 3-robust standard errors.18 Missing height was imputed using the sex-specific median for the primary adjusted analysis.19 Complete-case height models were performed as sensitivity analyses. Similar side-specific models were used to evaluate associations between laterality and core covariates. In secondary analyses, BMI (kg/m2) and race (reference = White) were added as covariates to the multivariable models to evaluate the influence of body habitus and race. Two-sided P < .05 was used for statistical significance.

Arterial comparison

For participants with both venous and arterial data (right, n = 714; left, n = 724; bilateral, n = 675), veins were shorter than arteries on both sides. Mean venous arc length was 194.0 ± 13.9 mm, whereas mean arterial arc length was 211.0 ± 18.7 mm, with veins 17.0 mm shorter than arteries (95% CI, 16.0-17.9; P < .001). There was a more prominent arteriovenous length difference on the right side (+28.3 mm; 95% CI, 27.2-29.4; P < .001) than left side (+6.4 mm; 95% CI, 5.3-7.5; P < .001) (Table IV). Multivariable models explored the associations of core covariates and the arterial-minus-venous arc length difference, with positive coefficients indicating a larger difference between vessel types. Increased age was associated with a larger arteriovenous difference, reflecting the combination of increased arterial length and modest venous shortening with age (Fig 5). Female sex was associated with a smaller difference between arterial and venous length.

Sensitivity analysis

In complete case analyses excluding patients with missing height, multivariable regression results were consistent with primary height-imputed analyses. Age remained negatively associated with venous arc length (−1.2 mm/decade; 95% CI, −2.0 to −0.5), and height remained positively associated with venous arc length (+8.2 mm/10 cm; 95% CI, 7.1-9.3). Height-adjusted female-male difference in venous arc length was similar (+6.2 mm; 95% CI, 3.7-8.7). These findings indicate minimal influence of missing height data.

Discussion

In this CT-based analysis of adults aged ≥25 years, we observed that iliofemoral venous arc length demonstrated consistent and clinically relevant asymmetry, with substantially longer iliofemoral veins on the left side than the right. This left-sided predominance likely reflects intrinsic pelvic anatomic asymmetries, as the iliocaval confluence sits anterior to the right of the spine, leading to a longer course of the left iliofemoral vein from the bifurcation to the groin.20,21 In contrast, the aorta sits anterior and to the left of the spine, with right common iliac artery crossing anterior to the left common iliac vein, contributing to the well-described left common iliac vein compression seen in May-Thurner anatomy.22 From a venous intervention standpoint, these findings are relevant because arc length serves as an anatomic proxy for intravascular working length and could influence stent coverage decisions.

In covariate models, the strongest association was observed between height and venous arc length, consistent with expected body size scaling. After height adjustment, females had notably longer iliofemoral veins compared with males, with a more pronounced effect on the left side. Notably, these differences were apparent only in height-adjusted models, emphasizing the importance of accounting for the strong effect of stature. There was no meaningful sex-based difference in arterial arc length, suggesting that sex-related variation in vessel length may be specific to the venous system. One possible explanation is that sex-based differences in pelvic morphology, such as wider pelvic width in females, alter the venous course more than the arterial course. This effect may be more apparent on the left side because the left iliofemoral vein follows a longer path to the rightward iliocaval confluence, potentially amplifying underlying pelvic anatomic differences. This sex-laterality difference is particularly interesting with regard to May-Thurner syndrome, which is more common in females.23 The relationship between left-sided venous length and compression remains uncertain and warrants further study in disease-specific cohorts. Previous work has shown differences in iliac venous diameters by sex.24 The finding of sex-based iliofemoral length difference provides additional anatomic context that may help explain sex-based differences in treatment and outcomes of iliac venous stenting.25,26

Arterial arc length measurements provided an internal comparator. Interestingly, age had divergent associations by vessel type, with arterial arc length increasing slightly with age and venous arc length decreasing slightly. As a result, the arterial-minus-venous arc length difference became larger with increasing age, a pattern also reflected in multivariable models of vessel-type differences. Although small in magnitude, these effects represent interesting findings which could reflect differential remodeling in veins and arteries across the lifespan.

Another notable finding was the broad distribution of iliofemoral vessel lengths across the population, with a substantial spread in venous arc length values between the 5th and 95th percentiles. This variation appears larger than would be expected from differences in body height alone, suggesting that although height is an important determinant of vessel length, overall stature does not fully reflect vascular anatomy. Differences in pelvic geometry, lower-extremity proportion and vessel course could all contribute additional variability. Clinically, these findings reinforce the importance of vascular imaging rather than assuming a typical working length based on patient height. Although skeletal pelvic dimensions were not measured in this study, differences in pelvic bony morphology such as hip width or level of the vascular bifurcations relative to the lumbosacral spine may influence vessel course.27,28 Future work could examine whether such landmarks, which are also readily measured on routine imaging, serve as suitable surrogates for venous arc length.

The positive association between BMI and venous arc length is notable. Although BMI is an imperfect surrogate for body fat or central adiposity, it is possible that greater body habitus provides some anatomic constraint which alters vessel course or promotes elongation. Whereas there was no significant association between race and iliofemoral venous arc length, this finding is limited by the small sample size of many subgroups, including those of Black, Pacific Islander, and Native American race.

This large-scale study provides automated, standardized quantification of venous arc length, offering reference values for contemporary management of NIVLs, in which careful attention to stent coverage and geometry is increasingly emphasized. From a procedural standpoint, arc length may provide an anatomically grounded proxy for intravascular working length. Collectively, these findings suggest a “one-size-fits-all” assumption regarding iliofemoral anatomy may be an oversimplification, particularly when considering laterality and sex in venous procedures. Even after accounting for height, vessel length varies substantially across the population, supporting the need for patient-specific imaging when planning venous interventions, estimating device working length, and planning device coverage. The divergent age-related trends in venous and arterial arc length emphasize the importance of system-specific device designs for venous interventions, which are often adapted from arterial-based technologies.

Although CT-based arc length measurements are useful for noninvasive preprocedural planning and population-level anatomic characterization, they should be used as complements, rather than replacements for intraprocedural modalities such as venography and IVUS study for assessment of length, diameter, and/or degree of compression. The principal value of CT in this study is its ability to generate standardized reference data across a large population, for which intraprocedural modalities are often impractical. Vessel diameter and degree of compression were not included in this analysis as venous caliber on cross-sectional CT can be influenced by transient factors such as volume status, positioning and respiration.29 In addition, axial CT sections are not always orthogonal to the vessel centerline, especially in cases of vessel angulation or tortuosity, which can lead to distorted measurements.30 In contrast, centerline-derived arc length characterizes the longitudinal vessel course. Previous work using CT imaging in chronic iliofemoral venous obstruction has shown centerline arc length measurements have high fidelity with intraprocedural imaging.7 We are not suggesting that all patients should necessarily undergo CT imaging. However, as CT imaging is increasingly used as an adjunct for preoperative planning—especially when duplex visualization can be limited in this region—there is opportunity to evaluate anatomic metrics, which can be extracted in a scalable way.31

Because we measured the entire iliofemoral region from the iliocaval confluence to the common femoral confluence, these arc length values represent full iliofemoral coverage rather than the length which should be stented in any individual case. In focal NIVLs, only a portion of this segment is typically stented, whereas in extensive post-thrombotic disease, the entire region may be covered.32, 33, 34 Current dedicated venous stents are available in lengths up to 160 mm, so complete coverage of this area with a mean length of 194 mm would require overlapping stents.35,36

As CT scans were obtained for a broad range of clinical indications rather than specifically for venous disease, this cohort should be regarded as an anatomic reference sample rather than a symptomatic or intervention-indicated population. This was a single-center retrospective cohort, and patients undergoing CT might not represent a healthy general population. Some variables which might plausibly influence pelvic venous anatomy, such as parity, were not available in this chart-derived data set. In addition, variations in contrast timing and image quality could affect vessel visualization, potentially leading to heterogeneity in segmentation and centerline extraction despite quality control measures.

Future studies should evaluate the associations of arc length and other complementary geometric metrics such as diameter, compression, and tortuosity with procedural outcomes, complications, and device performance in disease-specific cohorts.
