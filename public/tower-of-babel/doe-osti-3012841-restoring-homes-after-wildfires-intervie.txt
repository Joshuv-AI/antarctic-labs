Summary
This document summarizes the key themes from our interviews with 10 remediation companies
regarding common approaches used to remove odor and contaminants from homes impacted
by fire and smoke damage Remediation companies are in agreement that thorough cleaning is
the first step. Cleaning and removing contaminants from homes impacted by wildfires may be
sufficient to eliminate smoke odor, which is the most important step when it comes to reducing
exposure to any residues that may pose health risks to residents. Remediation companies
differ in their approaches to addressing odors, such as using ozone generators, hydroxyl
generators, chlorine dioxide treatment, thermal fogging and applying sealants. Their opinions of
what works and why differs. There is little consensus on efficacy, safety, or application
protocols. For example, some view ozone very favorably, while others would prefer using other
methods. The influence of insurance companies in their coverage for some methods but not
others also impact the decision about the approaches used. Remediation companies differ in
their view on laboratory testing for contaminants, which may be partly influenced by insurance
companies as well.

Based on our knowledge about the transformation and fate of smoke contaminants, 1 we
applaud the remediation companies for their strong emphasis on cleaning. However, we also
know that cleaning cannot eliminate 100% of the contaminants because some are surface-
bound and/or deeply adsorbed into porous materials. The introduction of various chemical
approaches to mitigate odors would transform residual contaminants to other compounds, such
as oxygenated byproducts, some of which may be more toxic than their precursors. On the
other hand, remediation companies are finding that these methods are helpful at removing
odors, citing success in most cases and very few callbacks other than reasons that can be
easily explained, such as customers that had opted to not clean a certain part of the home
initially but found that to be a source of smoke odor later.

From these interviewees, we gathered that the methods used to mitigate odors are important
parts of the toolbox for restoring homes after a wildfire. We see that there is a need to better
understand how smoke contaminants are transformed when ozone, hydroxyl, and other
reactive chemicals are added following proper cleaning. There are many cases where opinions
differ in terms of the efficacy of various approaches. There are also differences in terminologies
used, as well as the explanation of how different approaches work. Better understanding and
more clarity in communicating about these different approaches to mitigate odors is needed.
The role of laboratory testing for contaminants also needs to be explored further, which is not
only driven by the difference in opinions among remediation companies, but also by insurance
companies and their influence on the work scope. In addition, we see a need to evaluate the
potential benefits from increased ventilation, filtration, and air cleaning after the cleanup is
completed that may provide some additional protection for residents when they return to their
homes after the cleanup is completed.

1. Background
Following wildfire events, outdoor air containing wildfire smoke, soot, and ash can enter into
homes, resulting in indoor air and surface contamination that require professional cleanup.
Homes with wildfire smoke contamination will generally be accompanied by a noticeable smoke
odor. Residents will often seek out fire and smoke damage remediation companies to remove
this odor and restore their homes.

There is general guidance 2,3,4 from emergency response and public health agencies and
organizations on what residents should be aware of when cleaning up their home. When
involving a professional restoration company, a technical guide 5 published by the Institute of
Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification (IICRC), the Restoration Industry
Assessment (RIA), and the Cleaning Industry Research Institute (CIRI) outline the common
steps involved. Homeowners seeking professional services are advised to check references,
ask about certification, call their insurance agent, and ask for a cost estimate and work
schedule before signing a contract with a remediation company. Technical guides and industry
standards for wildfire impact assessments and remediation exist, 6,7 but they are not easily
accessible to homeowners. The use of marketing language and different terminologies to
describe various remediation approaches, such as “green cleaning” and “odor neutralizing",
also add to the confusion of the general public.

Motivated by the large number of homes impacted by recent wildfires, USEPA funded
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Berkeley Lab) to gather information on how
remediation companies remove smoke and other contaminants from homes exposed to
wildfires. From interviews with remediation companies our goal is to synthesize the common
remediation approaches being used. We asked questions about their on-the-ground
experiences and observations about the effectiveness of different approaches. From these
interviews and our knowledge of the scientific literature, 8 we identified knowledge gaps that
would help improve the overall outcome of remediation.

2. Key Findings from Interviews
Based on the interview transcripts, we summarized the key findings below. Where possible, we
selected some example quotes from interviewees to highlight their opinions. We developed the
key findings to outline consensus that were agreed upon by our interviewees, and also areas
where opinions differ. As we are driven by the goal to identify knowledge gaps, we placed our
attention on comments made by interviewees where future research has the potential to

improve the overall outcomes, including approaches that can reduce long-term health impacts
of occupants living in homes impacted by wildfire smoke.

2.1 Health concerns and odor removal are key motivations for
 remediation

Health is a big concern for residents seeking remediation services. Even for customers who are
seeking these services primarily to remove visible soot and smoke odor, they are also worried
about any potential health impacts from exposure to wildfire smoke if contaminants were to
linger in their home.

The "smell of smoke is the… secondary benefit"; they are "actually looking for the toxins from the
smoke to be removed, so… The hydrocarbons, the unburnt fuels, the soot, the char. We want
those things to be gone.

"99% of the time" one of the first questions asked is "how healthy is the environment". "Wildfire is
bad, it's like a health impact. That already is in people's head"

Interviewees often associated their concerns about toxic contaminants when fires involved
burning of structures, building materials, and their contents. Many interviewees (7) mentioned
heavy metals as a concern and often calling out lead specifically. Asbestos was also mentioned
by 4 interviewees, as well as “hydrocarbons” that could be carcinogenic (2 interviewees). Some
interviewees mentioned specific toxins, like arsenic and dioxin. If interviewees had a concern
about lead and/or asbestos, some would rely on laboratory testing to guide their work. But there
are differences among companies in how testing is conducted and whether it is conducted
routinely or not.

Some interviewees (3) discussed sharing safety information about the cleaning products with
customers, such as by providing the safety data sheet that details their chemical composition
and possible hazards. Remediation companies use a range of cleaners and other products

                                                              Restoring Homes After Wildfires | 8

described as degreasers, deodorizers, sealants, and disinfectants. When applied according to
instructions, remediation companies see little concern with the cleaning products they use,
aside from some customers who have sensitivity to chemicals.

Remediation companies take worker safety seriously. They described the various ways to
minimize exposure of their workers to contaminants during remediation. They discussed the
importance of personal protective equipment (PPE), such as gloves, boots, eye and respiratory
protection. One specific challenge that was pointed out by an interviewee was coverage by
insurance companies of supplies like respirator cartridges, or the lack of it.

All interviewees were familiar with the IICRC’s S500 and S700 standards. It is also broadly

large wildfires that impact lots of homes, it will always be the case that a wide range of cleaning
approaches would be applied depending on the conditions of the homes and their contents,
and intensity and extent of the damage caused by the wildfires and smoke.

Some common approaches that were mentioned by interviewees include:
 • HEPA vacuuming and wiping surfaces are standard initial steps to remove loose soot and
 ash. Some interviewees mentioned using dry sponges to pick up soot and ash without
 smearing it.
 • Using specialized cleaning agents like degreasers to remove smoke residues from

 surfaces. Others also mention applying cleaners that have peroxide and disinfectants to
 clean surfaces.
 • Other physical methods include "sandblasting, soda blasting" and "ice blasting (dry ice)"
 which has the additional benefit that it "takes care of odor too, and no residuals".

The decision of whether contents of the home are packed out for treatment or not will depend
on a number of factors, such as the extent of the damage, and what is found in the home (rugs

contents to their facility for cleaning. Others mentioned working with companies that specialize
in a particular kind of cleaning, such as laundromats for treating textiles. Insurance companies
can also influence the decision on whether certain contents are replaced or cleaned. There are
certain types of home goods, such as most electronics, where replacement often makes more
sense than cleaning.

2.3 Smoke-exposed insulation often needs to be removed
Remediation companies expressed their restoration-first mindset, as opposed to demolition.
Some view it as a more environmentally friendly approach compared to sending everything to a
landfill. Others suggested that restoring the home the hard way by thorough cleaning not only
enabled occupants to keep more of their possessions, but it is also one way to get people back
in their homes as quickly as possible and help the community recover faster.

During home assessments, “I'll go check the crawl space as well to see if there's any ash.
Typically in a wildfire, wind-driven soot and ash will be driven into the attic space, and you can
see beneath the vents piles of ash on top of everything.”

On the other hand, removal of wall insulation will be decided on a case-by-case basis. For
structural fires, smoke can travel into wall cavities because of high heat and pressurization
inside the home. But that is less common for that to happen in homes that were exposed to
wildfire smoke. Remediation companies described looking for visual cues to judge whether the
wall cavities were significantly impacted by smoke or not, such as if there is any sign of soot
coming out of electrical outlets on the wall.

2.4 Remediation companies use many methods to mitigate odor
Many different approaches are used to address smoke odor. Remediation companies
expressed varying opinions on what is effective that were developed from past experience.
Companies often have a preferred treatment method or a combination of them that they would
apply first as the primary treatment. If odor persists after the first treatment round, additional
treatment method(s) are then used to help eliminate odor.

“Negative pressure air machines” (i.e. blowers that induce air movements) and air scrubbers
(filtration devices to remove particulate matter, and sometimes gaseous pollutants as well) are
used universally by remediation companies. Some interviewees described setting up negative
pressure air machines as the first step to flush the house with clean air. During the cleaning
process, running the air scrubbers are helpful to capture soot and ash that can become
airborne. Two interviewees mentioned using air scrubbers with activated carbon (or “carbon
vapor filter”) to control odor. Air scrubbers are often described as being used in conjunction with
the hydroxyl generators.

The two other common pieces of equipment used in the fire restoration industry are ozone

interviewees). Some companies have a preference for releasing either ozone or hydroxyl as
part of their process, and there are also companies that utilize both. Other methods mentioned
to remove smoke odor include chlorine dioxide treatment, thermal fogging, and applying
sealants or “encapsulation”.

2.5 Varying opinions on ozone and hydroxyl applications
Some interviewees prefer using hydroxyl generators over ozone generators, citing concerns
about potential damages to building materials from ozone. They also viewed the need to keep
the property vacated during ozone treatment as a downside, because that would prevent
workers from being able to clean the home while ozone is being applied. It also keeps residents
away from their property for longer periods of time. Some remediation companies generally
view hydroxyl generators as a safer alternative to ozone.

"We rarely use [ozone generators] because the people have to leave the house, and there can be
issues with it degrading rubbers and plastics."

“I really try not to use ozone. If I do use ozone, it’s in a vacant home where there’s no one
present.”

Aside from concerns about potential damages to materials like rubbers and plastics, some
remediation companies also expressed some concerns about ozone creating odor after
treatment. This potential concern has only been associated with ozone and not with the
hydroxyl generators, based on the experiences of interviewees. These observations may be
indicative of reactive chemistry creating odorous compounds that were not present before the
ozone treatment.

"Ozone has a very distinctive smell, and some people have complained about a change in odor
after an extended ozone soak."

                                                              Restoring Homes After Wildfires | 13

“And you might smell the ozone. Later on, if you use it too much, it might create an odor.”

Some interviewees stated that they prefer to use ozone generators for smoke odor removal,
due to their speed and effectiveness in removing odor. There are also remediation companies
stating that they use both ozone and hydroxyl in combination.

“I do own hydroxyl generators. We typically don't use them. I don't believe they're nearly as
effective as an ozone. You can get the same result with an ozone in 24 hours that you'll get from a
hydroxyl in 7-10 days.”

“I use hydroxyl and ozone. And we'll use them sometimes in combination, put two hydroxyl
machines and an ozone machine.”

There were differences among remediation companies in how ozone is applied. This may be
attributed to the fact that no two jobs are identical, but it could also be due to the preference of
the remediation companies interviewed. From the interviews, it appears that these are largely
based on rules of thumb or experience. One interviewee mentioned that because ozone is
heavier than air, they would place the ozone machine high up on a ladder, allowing ozone
generated to fall and fill the space. More commonly, remediation companies would use fans to
recirculate the air and promote mixing. The duration of ozone treatment also varies. Some
interviewees would run the ozone machine for just a few hours, pointing out that they would set
the timer on the ozone generators. Others believe that a few hours are insufficient and would
run the ozone generators for 1-2 days. There is one interviewee suggesting that ozone
treatment is usually a 3 to 5-day process, which is longer than the typical practices described
by most other remediation companies.

Remediation companies tend to use hydroxyl generators for at least a day, and more
commonly for a few days. Hydroxyl generators are often used concurrently while cleaning is
happening.

“The hydroxyl needs to run inside the rooms we're going to be cleaning at least 24 hours before
we clean the room.”

“The hydroxyl machines take a little bit longer it's not as fast a process as the ozone, so usually
you would want the hydroxyl machines in there for 2-3 days.”

In reviewing the hydroxyl generator that are used, we learned that in most cases, they work by
photocatalytic oxidation (PCO). In PCO, hydroxyl radicals and other highly reactive species are
formed on the catalyst surface, and no hydroxyl radicals are released into the air. PCO works
by oxidation of VOCs in contact with a titanium dioxide (TiO2) catalyst when irradiated with
ultraviolet (UV) light. In theory, this reaction initiates a series of sequential oxidation steps to
form final inorganic products such as carbon dioxide (CO2) and water. However, partially
oxidized byproducts, such as formaldehyde and acetaldehyde, are often emitted from PCO

                                                              Restoring Homes After Wildfires | 14

during this process 9.

One interviewee described the hydroxyl generators they use would generate a hint of the same
smell as ozone. This suggests that reactive chemical species are being released into the air, a
process that is more similar to ozone generators than PCOs. In that specific case, the
interviewee described running their hydroxyl generators starting from day one of the project and
continuously for weeks inside the house.

Multiple interviewees stated that hydroxyl generators require sufficient moisture in the air to
work effectively. One interviewee raised the same concern when using ozone generators. This
is surprising for us to learn as we are not aware of humidity as a factor that can significantly
impact the performance of PCO or ozone generators.

“The limitation with hydroxyl is it does work better with higher humidity, and we're in a low
humidity environment here, so we generally have to raise the humidity some. We use a humidity
booster.”

“The other issue is that a lot of people are just putting ozone machines in a house. And the
relative humidity is below 40%. It needs a moisture content, or the relative humidity, of 40 to 60%.”

2.6 Chlorine dioxide not used commonly but reported as effective
Several interviewees called out chlorine dioxide (ClO2) for its effectiveness, but it is not used
universally. Two interviewees stated that their companies do not use this method. Some
companies described ClO2 mainly reserved for cases with more extensive smoke
contamination, or as a “last line of defense”. Application of ClO2 is described as straightforward,
where the product is placed in water and the gas is released. Remediation companies that use
ClO2 would use several packets in larger homes.

Opinions about safety of ClO2 are mixed. While one interviewee believed ClO2 to be safe to
use, another interviewee described it as more hazardous and requires more worker safety
protection than ozone. The use of ClO2 in homes as a deodorizer through oxidation involve
many complex reactions 10 that will require more studies to determine its effectiveness.

"We use ClO2 quite a bit as a gas agent to molecularly break down smoke odors. It's a lot safer to
use. Once it goes from the liquid to the gas phase and it evaporates, and then ventilates itself out.
It doesn't have any residue."

“Way worse than ozone. You can't be in there for 24 hours. And then, even when you open up the
building, you have to be in a respirator, and you got to let the building air out.”

2.7 Occasional use of thermal fogging and encapsulation for wildfire
 smoke
Thermal fogging is not commonly used for homes impacted by wildfire smoke. Even though it is
a method that has been applied for decades, most remediation companies interviewed
expressed that it is more suitable for fire-damaged buildings in structural fires or is used to treat
more persistent odor such as in heavy smokers’ homes.

“I typically try to stay away from thermal fogging because there are certain applications where it
could really work, but wildfire is definitely not one.”

The process of thermal fogging involves vaporizing a solvent to form a hot, pressurized fog that
can penetrate structures and materials in the same way that the original smoke did.
Remediation companies view this as an effective method for the right situation. Some would
use thermal fogging as a “good first step to reduce some odor”, while others would use it as a
final step in their process.

One often stated criticism against thermal fogging is that the solvent used may be scented and
is masking the odor. It could be covering up for incomplete or inadequate cleaning. On the
other hand, several interviewees describe lingering odor perceived only by the most sensitive
persons, and sometimes described as a placebo or psychological, could benefit from
introducing a different scent in the home. In those cases, thermal fogging could be one way to
achieve that. Alternatively, one interviewee mentioned using essential oils to address what they
described as “psychological odor”.

Separate from thermal fogging, encapsulation is another final step that some remediation
companies would apply to prevent odor from returning. Interviewees described spraying sealant
onto wood framing, such as in the attic, as a way to lock in any remaining odor.

2.8 Laboratory testing viewed as valuable but adds cost and time
Laboratory testing may be conducted to guide the cleaning of homes, but it is not always
performed. Testing results can be used to decide how to clean different components of the
home, and whether cleaning was effective at reducing the level of contaminants found. One
interviewee pointed out the guidance document from the American Industrial Hygiene
Association on environmental assessments related to wildfires. But testing adds costs and time
to the job. Some interviewees cited that it could take several weeks to perform testing and wait
for the laboratory results. The process would also add a few thousands of dollars to the job. In
some cases, the decision to test or not may be decided according to whether it is requested by
insurance companies, and whether they will cover the testing costs or not.

One interviewee felt strongly about the value of hiring a third-party environmental testing
company to conduct an assessment as a way to guide and evaluate their cleaning approaches.
Testing may involve collecting surface samples using wipes and tape lifts, settled dust samples
using micro-vacuum, as well as aerosols and VOCs samples. Laboratory analysis would report
the presence of char, ash, and soot, and identify specific contaminants.

2.9 Influence from insurance companies on remediation scope
Insurance companies have significant influence over the scope of work and the methods used.
This is especially the case for remediation companies that are program vendors who receive
jobs directly from the insurance companies. For example, interviewees described the use of
ozone generators and hydroxyl generators as established line items in insurance billing
programs, so they are the most commonly used methods to mitigate odor.

2.10 Callbacks are infrequent but can happen for multiple reasons
Remediation companies sometimes get callbacks for wildfire cases, but it is not very common,
indicating that most jobs are completed successfully. Some interviewees cited cases where
they were called in because prior efforts by another company did not completely remove the
contaminants. Inexperience of some of the out-of-the-area contractors responding to large-
scale events, such as the L.A. wildfires, was cited as a factor contributing to this. There are also
cases where homeowners initially opted for not to clean certain parts of their home, such as the
garage. At a later time, odor returned and the remediation company was called back. Similarly,
if the attic and substructure was not properly cleaned, smoke odor may return to the home at a
later time.

Off gassing from wood was cited as a reason why smoke odor returns, which would happen
even if it has been cleaned. Applying a sealant or “encapsulation” was described as one
approach to mitigate recurring odor. Other interviewees raised the issue of receiving callbacks
due to debris from nearby demolition and/or reconstruction, which kicks up contaminants that
can re-enter into already cleaned homes.

getting dug up. so, we're telling people, change your HVAC filters more frequently, get yourself
