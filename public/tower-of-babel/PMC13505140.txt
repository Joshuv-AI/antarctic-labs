Adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the Turkish version of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire
Authors: Dilek Sakallı, Suzan Havlioğlu, Turkan Karaca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aimed to test the validity and reliability of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire, which is specifically designed for nurses, in Turkish.MethodsThe study conducted between May and July 2025, participating nurses were asked to complete the ‘Sociodemographic Information Form’ and the ‘Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire’. Considering a potential 10% data loss, the aim was to survey 165 nurses. A total of 164 nurses who completed all the survey items were included in the study sample. Those who did not consent to participate, had worked at the hospital for less than six months or incompletely answered the survey items were excluded from the study.ResultsBased on the confirmatory factor analysis, the modified three-factor model demonstrated acceptable-to-good fit (χ²/df = 1.544, RMSEA = 0.058, NFI = 0.904, CFI = 0.963, IFI = 0.964, and TLI = 0.953).ConclusionThe validity and reliability tests conducted in this study determined that the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire is a valid and reliable measurement tool for the Turkish population. It is also believed that the scale will contribute significantly to improving the nursing workforce by enabling nurse managers to better understand these factors in management processes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The concept of thriving in the workplace has been studied for decades. Spreitzer and colleagues (2005) defined thriving as a psychological state characterised by two dimensions: vitality and learning [1]. Essentially, employees thrive when they feel energised at work and continuously acquire and develop knowledge and skills [2]. It is also important to distinguish thriving from related concepts such as resilience, flourishing and subjective well-being [1].

In healthcare, particularly nursing, relatively little is known about thriving in the workplace. Recent research has primarily focused on the increasing burnout rates among healthcare professionals [3–5]. However, a growing body of research has recently begun to examine factors associated with nurses’ workplace thriving [6–10].

Thriving has also revealed stress levels, burnout and workforce-related issues in Turkey [4, 11, 12] and globally [13]. Research has also indicated that these issues have deteriorated, especially since the outbreak of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic [14, 15]. The chronic stress and heavy workloads experienced by physicians, nurses and other clinical staff, particularly in healthcare settings, can eventually lead to burnout. Burnout is characterised by decreased job satisfaction, emotional exhaustion, and reduced personal accomplishment [7, 16], all of which pose a serious threat to psychological well-being.

Zhu and colleagues (2021) found that authentic leadership is a positive predictor of thriving [8]. They also found that factors such as workplace mindfulness, organisational justice and years of experience have a positive impact. In contrast, they found that workplace violence is negatively associated with thriving [6]. Yun and colleagues (2022) found that high-performance work systems were positively associated with both nurse resilience and thriving [9]. Similarly, Shen et al. (2024) examined resilience and reported that nurses’ workplace thriving partially mediates the relationship between resilience and job performance [10].

A review of the literature shows that the tools used have mainly aimed to capture the dimensions of thriving related to vitality and learning only. However, it is also important to define the cognitive, emotional and behavioural dimensions of thriving. Additionally, as the data collection instruments are not specific to the nursing work environment, many questions relating to factors unique to thriving in nursing remain unanswered. While existing tools measure vitality and learning, none specifically capture the unique workplace context of nursing. Therefore, this study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire (THINQ), originally developed by Arnetz et al. (2025).

Methods

Research type

This study is methodological research aimed at establishing the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire.

Population and sample of the study

The study population consists of 800 nurses working at an University Hospital in Sanlıurfa. Based on the guideline that the sample size for validity and reliability studies should ideally be ten times the number of items [18], the minimum sample size required for the 15-item ‘Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire’ was calculated to be 150 participants. Allowing for a potential 10% data loss, the aim was to survey 165 nurses. The individuals included in the study were selected using purposive sampling, and those who agreed to participate in the study voluntarily and had been working as nurses for at least six months were included in the study. A total of 164 nurses participated in the study, which is approximately 11 times the number of items. The adequacy of the sample size was additionally supported by the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value of 0.861, which indicated that the sample was sufficient for factor analysis. Furthermore, the participant-to-item ratio in the present study was approximately 11:1, exceeding the commonly recommended minimum ratios for psychometric validation studies.

Data collection

The research data was collected by researchers using the face-to-face interview technique. The data collection process for the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire took place in two stages. First, the scale items were translated from English to Turkish and linguistic validity was established. Once linguistic equivalence had been ensured, the scale was reviewed by academic nurses to assess content validity. Based on the experts’ feedback on content validity, the final version of the scale was prepared.

In the second stage, between May and July 2025, participating nurses were asked to complete the ‘Sociodemographic Information Form’ and the ‘Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire’. The average time taken to complete the questionnaires was approximately 10–15 min.

A researcher-prepared Sociodemographic Information Form was used to collect participants’ demographic and professional characteristics.The Sociodemographic Information Form included questions regarding participants’ age, gender, educational status, type of institution, years of professional experience, years worked in the current unit, unit of employment, job position, working schedule, average working hours, voluntary choice of profession, job satisfaction, perceived professional competence, participation in professional activities such as congresses, training sessions, or seminars during the past year, and whether professional development was supported in the workplace.

The present study used the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire (THINQ), originally developed and psychometrically evaluated by Arnetz et al. (2025), and aimed to assess its Turkish linguistic and psychometric validity. The scale consists of 15 items divided into three sub-dimensions: individual resources (three items), job resources (six items) and interpersonal resources in the work environment (six items). Each item is rated using a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 1 (very poor) to 10 (very good) [17]. Reliability estimates for all three factors exceeded 0.80, indicating good internal homogeneity.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using AMOS, SPSS 25 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences). The personal information of the participating nurses was presented as numbers and percentages. To ensure linguistic validity, the group translation and back-translation technique was employed. Expert opinions were sought to calculate the content validity indexes (CVI) for the scale items, in order to assess content validity. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to evaluate the construct validity of the scale. Prior to this, the suitability of the scale was examined using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, total and min-max) of the item scores were first provided to determine the reliability of the scale. Item analyses were conducted to assess internal consistency, test-retest and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated for the entire scale and all subdimensions. In statistical decisions, p < 0.05 is accepted as an indicator of significant difference.

Ethical considerations

Written permission to use the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire was obtained via email from the original authors who developed the instrument. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Ethics Approval No. 2016/882). Institutional permission was also obtained from the healthcare institution where the study was conducted.

The purpose and procedures of the study were explained to all participating nurses, and the principle of voluntary participation was upheld. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participants were informed that they could leave the study at any time. The consent process and written consent form were reviewed and formally approved by the University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee. The authors got permission for questionnaire adaptation.

Participants were instructed not to include identifying information, such as their names or surnames, on the data collection forms. In this way, anonymity and confidentiality were ensured throughout the study process. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and publication ethics were followed in our study.

Linguistic validity

A language equivalence study was first conducted to test the linguistic validity of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire. Using the translation–back translation method, three independent language experts translated the scale items from English to Turkish. The Turkish version of the scale was then revised based on feedback from ten subject-matter experts. After the Turkish translation was completed, three experts (who expert in fundamentals of nursing, management nursing and public health nursing) translated the scale back into its original language. The researchers compared the initial and final English versions of the scale, and finalised it. A pilot study was conducted with ten volunteer nurses to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness of the translated items. Following completion of the questionnaire, participants were asked to provide verbal feedback regarding item wording, clarity, and ease of understanding. Based on the feedback obtained during the pilot testing process, no revisions were required, as all items were considered understandable and linguistically appropriate by the participants. The nurses who participated in the pilot study were excluded from the main study sample.

Based on the nurses’ responses, it was determined that none of the items were unclear, thus ensuring the scale’s linguistic equivalence. The ten nurses who participated in the pilot study were excluded from the research sample.

Content validity

Following the establishment of the linguistic equivalence of the scale for the validity and reliability study, expert opinions from academic nurses were sought in order to assess the content validity. To calculate the Content Validity Index (CVI) for each item, the experts were asked to rate each item on a 4-point scale (4 = Very Appropriate, 3 = Quite Appropriate, 2 = Somewhat Appropriate and 1 = Not Appropriate). Expert evaluations were assessed using the Davis technique. According to this method, the CVI for each item was determined by dividing the number of experts who rated an item as 3 or 4 by the total number of experts. A CVI value of 0.80 is considered the minimum acceptable threshold [18]. As all items had a CVI value above 0.80, none were removed from the scale. All items demonstrated I-CVI values above the recommended minimum threshold of 0.80, indicating adequate agreement among experts and supporting the content validity of the Turkish version of the scale. All items were reviewed based on expert feedback, and the final version of the scale was created.

The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated for each item using the Davis technique. All items demonstrated I-CVI values above the recommended threshold of 0.80, ranging from 0.90 to 1.00. The scale-level content validity index based on the average method (S-CVI/Ave) was calculated as 0.96, indicating excellent content validity for the Turkish version of the THINQ. Construct validity

The construct validity of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire was evaluated. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the original three-factor structure of the scale. Suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) coefficient and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The KMO coefficient was found to be 0.861. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 1265.165, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis.

Therefore, the analysis was theory-driven rather than exploratory. The initial three-factor CFA model demonstrated a suboptimal fit to the data (χ² = 271.048, df = 87, χ²/df = 3.115, RMSEA = 0.115, CFI = 0.848, NFI = 0.794, IFI = 0.850, and TLI = 0.816). Consequently, modification indices and theoretical relationships between items were carefully examined.

Correlated error terms were added only between items belonging to the same latent dimension and demonstrating conceptual overlap. For clarity, the correlated residuals corresponded to the following item pairs: e1–e2 (M1–M2), e3–e5 (M3–M5), e7–e8 (M7–M8), e9–e12 (M9–M12), and e10–e11 (M10–M11). Specifically, covariance between e1–e2 was allowed because both items addressed closely related aspects of interpersonal support within the nursing work environment. Covariance between e3–e5 reflected overlap in perceptions of workplace relationships and communication. Correlations between e7–e8 and e9–e12 were permitted because these item pairs addressed similar aspects of organizational and work-related resources. Finally, covariance between e10–e11 was considered theoretically justifiable because both items referred to opportunities for professional growth and support within the workplace. These modifications were based on both statistical evidence and theoretical consistency rather than solely on modification indices. After the modifications, the model fit improved substantially and demonstrated acceptable-to-good fit indices (χ² = 126.571, df = 82, χ²/df = 1.544, RMSEA = 0.058, GFI = 0.906, NFI = 0.904, CFI = 0.963, IFI = 0.964, and TLI = 0.953). These findings indicate that the modified three-factor model adequately represented the underlying construct of thriving in nursing (Fig. 1). Although the sample size was relatively modest, the participant-to-item ratio exceeded 10:1 and the KMO value (0.861) indicated adequate sampling adequacy for factor analytic procedures.

Reliability analyses

To determine the reliability of the study, test–retest reliability, internal consistency, and item–total score correlation coefficients were evaluated. For the test–retest analysis, 30 volunteer nurses were randomly selected from the study sample and reassessed three weeks after the initial administration of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire. The test–retest analysis demonstrated acceptable temporal stability for the total scale, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.736 (95% CI: 0.44–0.87, p < 0.001). In addition, a paired samples t-test was conducted to compare the mean scores obtained from the two administrations, and no statistically significant difference was found between the measurements (t = 1.312, p = 0.180). These findings indicate that the Turkish version of the scale has strong temporal stability over time. Although the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval was 0.44, the ICC value remained within the acceptable range for test–retest reliability in scale adaptation studies, supporting the temporal stability of the instrument.

Examination of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire revealed that the item-total score correlations ranged from 0.30 to 0.72 (Table 3). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Interpersonal Resources subscale was 0.854, for the Work Resources subscale was 0.826, for the Individual Resources subscale was 0.811, and for the total scale was 0.900. McDonald’s omega coefficients were also calculated to provide additional evidence of internal consistency. The omega coefficients were 0.795 for the Interpersonal Resources subscale, 0.820 for the Work Resources subscale, 0.714 for the Individual Resources subscale, and 0.902 for the total scale. These findings further support the reliability of the Turkish version of the THINQ and are generally consistent with the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients obtained in the present study.

Discussion

In this study, the aim was to adapt the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire to the Turkish population. As a Turkish adaptation study conducted to measure the factors contributing to professional development among nurses, the results confirmed that the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire consists of 15 items and three dimensions. None of the items have been removed from the original Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire. The Turkish version, whose validity and reliability have been established, consists of three sub-dimensions and 15 items, just like the original.

Discussion of validity findings

In this study, the content and construct validity findings demonstrated that the Turkish version of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire is a valid measurement tool for nurses in Türkiye. The high CVI values indicated strong agreement among experts, supporting the cultural and linguistic appropriateness of the translated items. Similar findings have been reported in previous scale adaptation studies in nursing and health sciences [19, 20]. The strong agreement observed in this study may be related to the careful translation process and the involvement of experts experienced in nursing and scale development.

The construct validity results supported the original three-factor structure of the scale. The CFA findings were consistent with previous studies emphasizing the multidimensional nature of thriving. The KMO and Bartlett’s test results confirmed that the sample was adequate for factor analysis [21]. Overall, the CFA fit indices indicated that the Turkish version adequately represented the theoretical structure proposed in the original THINQ study.

The CFA fit indices obtained in this study were within acceptable or good fit ranges [22–24]. These findings are similar to those reported in previous psychometric validation studies. Minor differences in fit indices compared with other studies may be associated with cultural differences, sample characteristics and variations in healthcare settings. Overall, the consistency between the present findings and previous studies supports the cross-cultural applicability and validity of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire. Although the model modifications improved fit indices, correlated error terms were introduced based on modification indices and theoretical considerations. Therefore, the possibility of model overfitting cannot be completely excluded. Given the relatively modest sample size (n = 164), future studies should cross-validate the factor structure in larger and independent samples. Nevertheless, the number of modifications was limited, and all modifications were theoretically justified and restricted to items within the same latent dimensions.

Discussion of reliability findings

The reliability findings demonstrated that the Turkish version of the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire has strong internal consistency. Item–total score correlations ranged from 0.30 to 0.72, indicating that all items contributed adequately to the overall structure of the scale. Although item M14 demonstrated an item–total correlation at the minimum acceptable threshold (0.30), it was retained because the value remained within the acceptable range reported in the psychometric literature. Furthermore, deleting the item did not lead to a meaningful increase in the overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Retaining the item was also considered important for preserving the conceptual integrity and original factor structure of the scale. Similar findings have been reported in previous psychometric studies, where item–total correlations above 0.30 were considered evidence of sufficient reliability [23]. The relatively high correlations observed in this study suggest that the items consistently measure the same construct and support the multidimensional structure of the scale.

Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subdimensions ranged from 0.811 to 0.854, while the coefficient for the total scale was 0.900, indicating high internal consistency [25]. These findings are consistent with the original study, in which Cronbach’s alpha values for all factors were also reported above 0.80. The similarity between the present findings and the original study supports the reliability of the Turkish adaptation. Minor differences in alpha values may be related to cultural characteristics, sample composition and differences in working environments among nurses. Overall, the findings indicate that the Turkish version of the scale is a reliable instrument for assessing thriving in nursing.

In addition to Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega coefficients were calculated to provide further evidence of internal consistency. The omega values were generally consistent with the alpha coefficients and supported the reliability of both the total scale and its subdimensions. These findings strengthen the reliability evidence for the multidimensional structure of the THINQ.

Compared to other scales designed for nurses, the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire (THINQ) offers a more thorough and profession-specific evaluation of nurses’ professional lives. The scale stands out for its integrated approach to the individual, interpersonal and organisational resources that support nurses’ professional development. While other scales assess the work environment, job commitment, and quality of work life, they typically focus on organisational characteristics such as managerial support and autonomy. However, they do not directly evaluate dimensions of individual vitality, learning, and personal development. THINQ offers a more comprehensive evaluation framework by integrating these dimensions through a holistic approach. Consequently, this scale can contribute to a more comprehensive assessment of factors supporting professional development and psychological well-being for nursing managers and researchers.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, it was conducted in a single university hospital, which limits the generalisability of the findings. Data were collected using self-report measures, which may have been influenced by response biases such as social desirability. The cross-sectional design prevents causal or predictive interpretations from being made. Furthermore, convergent and discriminant validity with related constructs (e.g. burnout and job satisfaction) were not examined. Finally, the test–retest analysis was conducted with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the strength of the reliability evidence. Additionally, although the CFA model modifications were theoretically justified, they may carry a risk of overfitting, and cross-validation in an independent sample is recommended.

Practical implications for nursing management

The Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire (THINQ) is a practical tool that allows nurse managers to systematically assess and monitor their staff’s well-being. It can be used to identify units or departments where levels of thriving are low and determine which of the three areas — individual, interpersonal or work resources — require improvement. This allows managers to implement targeted strategies, such as improving team communication, increasing support mechanisms or enhancing professional development opportunities.

THINQ can also be used to evaluate the effectiveness of managerial or organisational interventions. By applying the scale before and after initiatives such as training programmes, staffing adjustments or well-being interventions, managers can objectively assess changes in thriving levels among nurses. Regular use of the scale enables continuous monitoring, helping to detect early signs of decreased well-being and allowing data-driven decisions to be made to improve job satisfaction, staff retention and the quality of the overall work environment.

Additionally, while the scale was developed for nurses, it could potentially be adapted and tested in related groups, such as student nurses or midwives, in future studies.

Conclusion

The validity and reliability analyses conducted in this study indicate that the Thriving in Nursing Questionnaire is a valid and reliable measurement tool for use with the Turkish population. The findings confirmed that the scale consists of three dimensions and 15 items, which is consistent with the original structure and is supported by the literature. The scale is designed to evaluate the factors that contribute to the professional development of nurses. It could help improve the nursing workforce by enabling nurse managers to better understand these factors in management processes.
