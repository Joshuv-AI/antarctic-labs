Numerical study of viscous and rarefaction effects on choked flow in two-dimensional convergent conical nozzle
Authors: Vivekamanickam Koothan Venkateswaran, Unai Fernandez Gamiz, Ana Boyano, Jesus Maria Blanco, Alberto Peña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, a numerical investigation of gas flow through a convergent conical nozzle operating under low-pressure conditions, with an emphasis on choked mass flow behaviour with viscous and rarefaction effects, is conducted. Simulations are performed over a range of pressure ratios spanning the continuum and slip-flow regimes under no-slip, slip, and inviscid wall boundary conditions. At high pressure ratios, all the formulations yield comparable core-flow accelerations, with deviations below 1%, thus indicating near-continuum behaviour. As the pressure ratio decreases, rarefaction effects become increasingly important and lead to discernible differences in flow structure and mass flow rate. Compared with the no-slip model, the slip-flow formulation predicts mass flow rates approximately 3% higher because of reduced wall shear, whereas the inviscid solutions provide deviations of approximately 1% compared with the slip-flow at the lowest pressure ratios. These results highlight the necessity of modelling for accurate prediction of choked flow characteristics in microscale nozzle applications in comparison to the one-dimensional equation.

Introduction

Engineers and researchers treat gas behaviour using different modelling approaches depending on whether the flow is continuous, transitional, or rarefied. Parameters such as density, viscosity, and velocity change with changes in pressure. However, under certain conditions, the changes in the length of the pipe, density, viscosity, and velocity are negligible. If treated as constants, countless terms in the Navier–Stokes equations can be discarded. To expand closed-form clarifications to real-world complications, researchers should make defensible decisions1. In most pipes and valves, choked flow occurs because the flow of gas at the initial velocity reaches sonic conditions2. Choked flow occurs when the flow velocity reaches the local speed of sound at the minimum cross-sectional area of a duct or nozzle. Under these conditions, pressure disturbances cannot propagate upstream, and the mass flow rate becomes independent of downstream pressure variations. In a purely convergent nozzle, choking is achieved without the formation of shock waves and is governed by the sonic conditions at the throat. The flow is typically modelled as adiabatic; viscous effects, when considered, introduce irreversibility and energy dissipation but do not cause choking1. The mass flow rate is vital for analysis in the choked regime and does not increase any further with a decrease in downstream pressure, as pressure waves cannot travel upstream anymore because of sonic speeds3.

In gas dynamics studies, the inlet and the outlet pressure ratio reaches a particular value; for example, in a valve, the flow of gas on the continuum scale for a Knudsen number less than one, whether in a variable cross section or a straight cross section, is choked because of the phenomenon of the sonic region at the throat position for the nozzle cross section4. Interaction between the shock wave and boundary layer, which is the interaction between the viscous flow at the bottom (wall) and the inviscid flow from the outside (core), is inevitable in the flow within an inlet5,6. Theoretical and numerical predictions revealed a linear relationship between the area disturbed by a normal shock wave and the running time in a choked flow7. When the viscosity of a fluid becomes zero, the flow becomes inviscid. The continuum flow regime is modelled by the compressible Navier–Stokes equations with the ideal gas equation and boundary conditions that express the continuity of velocity and temperature between the wall and fluid. The compressible Navier–Stokes equations are the governing conservation laws for energy, mass, and momentum. These laws are written under the assumption that the fluid is Newtonian8. The Navier–Stokes equations also assume that the fluid flows according to the Fourier law of diffusion. Hence, “q” represents the heat flux vector and is related to the temperature gradient q=-K ▿ T, where “K” represents the thermal conductivity as a function of the temperature9,10.

Hence, if the mass of the gas passes through a cross section of the tube per unit time, then the discharge “Q” must be a constant along the tube. The convergent conical nozzle type was of interest in this study because it is a frequent and common exhaust nozzle configuration. In real flows with viscous and thermal heat conduction phenomena, the deviation causes the inviscid flow assumption to be practical for the boundary layer. As the boundary layer thickness increases, the influence of the fluid or stream from the critical cross section changes. This is because the structure and shape of the wall change in the minimal cross section \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S_{min}}$$\end{document}. Hence, in this study, the mass flow rate is obtained from the critical cell region, and a comparison is performed. This rate indicates the distance by which the object is displaced to showcase the effects of the boundary layers in the inviscid flow. However, this approach leads to inaccuracy in the main equation, namely, Equation 3. Both viscosity and thermal conductivity affect the flow rate. An increase in viscosity decreases the flow rate, and an increase in heat transfer increases the flow rate. The main purpose of this study is to understand the viscous and rarefaction effects in comparison to the theoretical 1D Inviscid equation for choked flow conditions because theoretical comparisons with the inviscid formula have often been lacking in previous studies, which motivates this detailed study. The 1D inviscid formula does not capture viscous or rarefaction effects and does not capture 2D, 3D or axisymmetric effects. To achieve this aim, a convergent conical nozzle with a random ideal gas of argon is considered. In this study, two-dimensional Navier–Stokes simulations with slip/jump wall models are conducted to quantify the effects of viscosity and rarefaction on mass flow rates under choked conditions in convergent conical nozzles. No-slip, slip and inviscid boundary conditions are systematically compared across pressure ratios that span continuum to slip regimes, and viscous vs. rarefaction contributions to mass flow and wall shear are isolated. The results of this study reveal the conditions under which 1-D inviscid formulas remain acceptable and slip corrections are required for accurate choked-flow prediction. This study fills the gap between idealized 1-D theory and practical 2-D CFD for low-pressure and small-scale nozzles by providing information on contour regimes and sensitivity to wall modelling.

Flow equation

The flow structure of the regular reflection region is obtained from the Navier–Stokes simulations at the specified Reynolds number, Prandtl number, and Knudsen number. The Reynolds number is calculated on the basis of the diameter of the nozzle (0.001 m), with a Reynolds range of 32.92 to 1645, and the mean free path is determined.

The sonic speed is V=279.19 m/s, the static temperature is T =225 K, and the static pressure is p=100 to 5000 Pa. The model is assumed to have a constant surface temperature of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_0$$\end{document} =273 K. The Reynolds number used in this study is much lower than those of conventional large-scale nozzles. This low value arises from the low-pressure conditions, small throat diameter, and moderate flow velocities considered. Such Reynolds numbers are common in micronozzle and rarefied gas flows, where viscous and rarefaction effects strongly influence the flow. Under these conditions, the viscous forces are comparable to the inertial forces, which leads to thicker boundary layers and increased momentum diffusion. The resulting Reynolds number is also consistent with the corresponding Knudsen numbers, thus placing the flow in the slip or transitional regime rather than the high-Reynolds-number continuum regime typical of conventional nozzles.

Numerical simulations

The numerical simulations were performed using the Ansys Fluent; the procedure is described in the Ansys manual49. A density-based solver uses a coupled algorithm, not a segregated algorithm. This choice is fundamental and directly affects convergence, especially for the flow in the nozzle. A density-based solver uses a coupled algorithm because density, pressure, velocity, and energy are strongly linked through compressibility.

The flow in the convergent conical nozzle is assumed to be compressible, steady-state, and axis symmetric. By using the ideal gas law, the viscous and rarefaction effects are evaluated for the total energy equation. For simplicity, the flow is modelled as single-phase flow. This assumption is reasonable because the gases involved have similar physical and thermodynamic properties and no phase separation or interfacial effects are expected under the operating conditions. Consequently, the single-phase approach captures the essential flow behaviour while remaining computationally efficient. Boundary conditions

To evaluate the rarefaction and viscous effects, the three boundary conditions that are considered are the velocity slip and temperature jump, no slip, and inviscid wall conditions. The speed of the fluid at the wall region is null under no-slip boundary conditions; however, under slip boundary conditions, relative movement occurs between the wall and the fluid. The viscous laminar model is chosen so that low-pressure boundary slip and viscous heating can be applied. In the estimation of the boundary of the fluid flow, the Knudsen number plays a vital role. In general, the flow at the border of a flow field, i.e., the channel walls, is fixed in space, and the fluid in direct contact is assumed to be non-movable. This condition is the no-slip boundary condition, which means that there is no relative movement (slip) between the wall and the fluid layer that is in direct contact. The Kn value is crucial for understanding the regime for which the CFD is valid. According to the value of Kn, the flow can be divided into four categories of flow regimes, namely, the continuum flow (Kn < 0.001), slip flow (0.001< Kn < 0.1), transition flow (0.1< Kn < 10), and free molecular flow (Kn > 10)48,53–55.

The slip regime is said to range between these two extremes. Under no-slip boundary conditions, the fluid speed at the wall is assumed to be zero, whereas under slip boundary conditions, the wall and fluid move relative to some value.

The no-slip condition represents the classical continuum limit where viscous momentum transfer at the wall is dominant. Viscous effects and rarefaction effects

When a fluid moves from one point to another, it interacts with its surroundings and is characterized by collisions and shear forces. Each collision and shear event converts energy from kinetic to thermal energy. This non-reversible energy conversion is fundamentally an energy loss to the flow stream and is called fluid friction. Fluid flow with friction is called viscous flow, which means that the viscosity of the fluid is nonzero. Viscosity is a measure of the ability of a fluid to resist shear forces. The effects of rarefaction become significant when the mean free path of the molecules and the micro channel characteristics are studied. During these time periods or occurrences, non-continuum effects such as local thermal non-equilibrium occur. In this section, the focus on slip flow with the effects of rarefaction is vital. In this regime, convectional Navier–Stokes simulations can be conducted by applying the velocity slip boundary condition. This zone has been extensively investigated by the research community because of its importance in fluid mechanics.Fig. 7Comparison of the numerical results along the axis for the three boundary conditions analyzed.

Conclusions

In this study, a detailed analysis of viscosity and rarefaction was performed on a two-dimensional convergent conical nozzle for a monatomic gas, argon, under ideal gas conditions. The choked flow of the monatomic gas (argon) in a conical convergent nozzle was validated and analysed. The effect of the Knudsen number was evaluated, and the results were compared to the λ values. However, the equation for λ 2 with a Kn value of 0.0008 yielded a value greater than λ 1. Hence, λ 1 and λ 2 were comparatively analysed, and the results showed that λ 2 was higher than λ 1. The two types of mean free paths (λ) were used to define the Knudsen numbers and evaluate the accuracy of the 1D inviscid equation to evaluate the relation of the mass flow rate determined at the critical cross section of the conical convergent nozzle. This study enables us to understand the theoretical and computational values of the flow rate choked at the critical cell region. This finding will enable us to use the conditions for the choking case and to determine mass flows for various gases in the critical section of the convergent conical nozzle in upcoming studies. In this work, a detailed study was performed to compare the viscous and rarefaction effects with the theoretical values for the mass flow rates under choking conditions. The differences in error were 13% and 14% between the inviscid and 1D equation for the slip and no-slip conditions, respectively. However, importantly, the study of neighbouring cells is also meant to consider the mass flow between them. Development of dimensionless correction or a correlation factor will provide more advantages with higher datasets apart from the above mentioned error datas. In future work, we will focus on three key directions. First, the present analysis will be extended to different gases, including other monoatomic, diatomic, triatomic, and polyatomic species (e.g., neon, nitrogen, and other gases), to assess the influence of molecular structure on rarefaction effects and critical mass flow rates,along with this the shear stress analysis comparison will be done. Second, geometric effects will be investigated using reduced outlet diameters, varied throat areas, and refined near-throat cell resolution, with validation against additional experimental data and discharge coefficient evaluation. Third, multidimensional effects will be examined by comparing 2.5D and fully 3D simulations, including the interaction of shock structures at larger nozzle divergence angles and their effects on critical flow behaviour, along with this an real time printed nozzle will be made to understand the real time effects to support more advantages to the simulations to be made.
