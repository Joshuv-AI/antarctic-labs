Abstract
. Platelets expire within five days. Blood banks face uncertain daily demand and must balance ordering decisions between costly wastage from overstocking and life-threatening shortages from understocking. Reinforcement learning (RL) can learn effective ordering policies for this Markov decision process (MDP) , but the resulting neural policies remain black boxes, hindering trust and adoption in safety-critical domains. We apply COOL-MC , a tool that combines RL with probabilistic model checking and explainable RL, to verify and explain a trained policy for the MDP on platelet inventory management inspired by Haijema et al. By constructing a policy-induced discrete-time Markov chain (which includes only the reachable states under the trained policy to reduce memory usage), we verify PCTL properties and provide feature-level explanations. Results show that the trained policy achieves a 2.9% stockout probability and a 1.1% inventory-full (potential wastage) probability within a 200-step horizon, primarily attends to the age distribution of inventory rather than other features such as day of week or pending orders. Action reachability analysis reveals that the policy employs a diverse replenishment strategy, with most order quantities reached quickly while several are never selected. Counterfactual analysis shows that replacing medium-large orders with smaller ones leaves both safety probabilities nearly unchanged, indicating that these orders are placed in well-buffered inventory states. This first formal verification and explanation of an RL platelet inventory management policy demonstrates COOL-MC ’s value for transparent, auditable decision-making in safety-critical healthcare supply chain domains.



1.
Introduction

Platelets are essential for treating patients with serious conditions such as cancer
[
150
]
and bleeding
[
6
]
.
Since platelets are obtained exclusively through voluntary donations and have a very short shelf life of only about five days, managing their inventory is particularly challenging
[
128
]
.
Hospitals must repeatedly decide how many units to order from regional blood centers and how to allocate them across patients, all under uncertain future demand
[
28
]
.
Each ordering decision directly affects the availability of supply in subsequent periods. Ordering too many units leads to costly wastage of a scarce resource, while ordering too few risks critical shortages that can endanger patients.
This sequential decision-making problem, combined with stochastic demand and strict expiration constraints, makes platelet inventory management a compelling safety-critical application for
reinforcement learning (RL)

[
145
,
55
,
56
,
32
]
.

This platelet inventory management problem can be modeled as a
Markov decision process (MDP)

[
81
]
, in which an RL agent repeatedly observes the current state of the environment, chooses an action, and receives a penalty signal
[
145
,
113
]
.
The state describes the current day of the week, pending orders, and the number of platelet units in stock at each remaining shelf life.
The action determines how many new units to order, and the environment penalizes the agent for each unit that expires unused and for each unit of demand that cannot be fulfilled.
Through repeated interactions, the agent learns a
memoryless policy
that maps each observed state to an order decision, thereby minimizing the total penalty over time.
Modern RL algorithms typically represent this policy using a
neural network (NN)

[
119
]
, enabling them to generalize across large state spaces and outperform traditional optimization methods
[
71
]
.

However, RL policies can exhibit
unsafe behavior

[
71
]
such as systematically under-ordering on certain days, leading to dangerous shortage periods
[
145
]
, as penalties are often not enough for complex safety requirements
[
146
]
, and NN-based policies are additionally
hard to interpret
because the internal complexity of NNs hides the reasoning behind each decision
[
27
]
.
In the platelet setting, this opacity is particularly problematic: blood bank managers need to understand
why
a policy orders a certain quantity on a given day and whether it accounts for the age distribution of stock.
Without such understanding, adoption of RL-based ordering policies in practice remains challenging
[
32
]
.

To resolve the issues mentioned above, formal verification methods like
probabilistic model checking

[
24
]
, which exhaustively analyzes all possible behaviors of a stochastic system to compute exact probabilities of satisfying formally specified properties, has been proposed to reason about the safety of RL policies
[
151
,
85
,
34
,
80
]
and
explainable RL methods
to interpret trained RL policies
[
118
,
76
,
75
,
74
]
.

COOL-MC
addresses both of these challenges by combining RL with probabilistic model checking and explainability
[
71
,
76
,
75
,
74
]
: after learning a policy via RL, it constructs only the reachable state space induced by that policy, yielding a
discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC)
amenable to probabilistic model checking and explainable RL methods
[
71
]
.
By constructing only the reachable states under the trained policy rather than enumerating the full state space, this approach mitigates the curse of dimensionality that renders classical methods such as full-MDP model checking intractable as the number of states grows
[
104
,
71
]
.

On the verification side, PCTL properties formalize safety-relevant questions about the policy’s behavior, such as “Does the policy reach stockout with a probability of less than 5%?” or “Does the policy eventually place a large order?”
Probabilistic model checking then evaluates each of these properties on the induced DTMC, returning both a yes-or-no verdict on whether the property is satisfied and exact quantitative values, such as probabilities and expected steps, that characterize the specified policy behavior
[
86
,
71
]
.

On the explainability side, feature pruning removes individual state observation features and measures the resulting change in the policy’s behavior via probabilistic model checking
[
75
]
, thereby revealing which inventory characteristics drive ordering decisions.
Additionally, state labeling in the induced DTMC via explainability methods such as feature-importance permutation ranking
[
35
]
enables identification of which state features most strongly influence the policy’s decisions across different ordering trajectories
[
76
]
.
Action labeling annotates each state with the ordering level chosen by the policy, enabling formal queries to detect specific failure modes, for example, whether the policy consistently orders too few units along paths that eventually lead to a stockout.
Beyond explanation, counterfactual analysis enables targeted what-if interventions, such as replacing a specific ordering action with a simpler one or increasing storage capacity, and then re-verifying the model to assess the impact on safety outcomes without retraining the policy.

To date, however,
COOL-MC
’s capabilities have not been applied to the verification and explanation of RL policies for platelet inventory management.

In this paper, we demonstrate that
COOL-MC
can be applied to the platelet inventory management MDP inspired by Haijema et al.
[
81
]
as a case study.
This MDP models the daily ordering planning problem for a regional blood bank in the North-East Netherlands, where the objective is to minimize the combined cost of platelet shortages and full-inventory issues (potential wastage) under stochastic daily demand.
States represent the day of the week, pending orders, and the age-structured inventory, actions correspond to ordering levels, and penalties penalize shortages and wastage with a 5:1 cost ratio.

Using
COOL-MC
, we train a
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
policy
[
132
]
on the platelet inventory MDP and construct its induced DTMC to verify and explain the learned policy ordering behavior with the approaches mentioned above.

Our analysis reveals properties of the learned policy that are not captured by standard reward-based evaluation.
The trained policy achieves a 2.9% stockout probability and a 1.1% inventory-full (potential wastage) probability within a 200-step horizon, primarily attends to the age distribution of inventory rather than other features such as day of week or pending orders.
Feature-importance permutation ranking confirms this picture at the per-state level, showing that inventory age classes dominate the policy’s action selection across ordering trajectories.
Action labeling reveals that the policy employs a diverse replenishment strategy, with most order quantities reached quickly while several are never selected.
Counterfactual analysis shows that replacing medium-large orders with smaller ones leaves both safety probabilities nearly unchanged, indicating that these orders are placed in well-buffered inventory states.

Our main contribution
is the demonstration of
COOL-MC
as a tool for platelet inventory management applications, combining RL with probabilistic model checking and explainability to provide the first formal analysis of a platelet inventory management RL policy, including verified safety properties, feature-level explanations via pruning and permutation ranking, action-level behavioral profiling, and counterfactual what-if analysis of the trained RL ordering policy.

3.
Background

First, we introduce
Markov decision processes (MDPs)
as the formal framework for sequential decision-making. We then describe probabilistic model checking for verifying policy properties, RL, and different explainability methods for explaining their RL policy decisions.

3.1.
Probabilistic Systems

A
probability distribution
over a set
\displaystyle X
is a function
\displaystyle\mu\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
with
\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)=1
. The set of all distributions on
\displaystyle X
is
\displaystyle Distr(X)
.

Definition 1

(MDP)
.

A
MDP
is a tuple
\displaystyle M=(S,s_{0},Act,Tr,rew,AP,L)

where
\displaystyle S
is a finite, nonempty set of states;
\displaystyle s_{0}\in S
is an initial state;
\displaystyle Act
is a finite set of actions;
\displaystyle Tr\colon S\times Act\rightarrow Distr(S)
is a partial probability transition function and
\displaystyle Tr(s,a,s^{\prime})
denotes the probability of transitioning from state
\displaystyle s
to state
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
when action
\displaystyle a
is taken;

\displaystyle rew\colon S\times Act\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a reward function;

\displaystyle AP
is a set of atomic propositions;

\displaystyle L\colon S\rightarrow 2^{AP}
is a labeling function.

We represent each state
\displaystyle s\in S
as a vector of
\displaystyle d
features
\displaystyle(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
, where
\displaystyle f_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The available actions in
\displaystyle s\in S
are
\displaystyle Act(s)=\{a\in Act\mid Tr(s,a)\neq\bot\}
where
\displaystyle Tr(s,a)=\bot
means that action
\displaystyle a
is not available in state
\displaystyle s
.
In our setting, we assume that all actions are available at all states.

Definition 2

(DTMC)
.

A
discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC)
is a tuple
\displaystyle D=(S,s_{0},Tr,AP,L)

where
\displaystyle S
,
\displaystyle s_{0}
,
\displaystyle AP
, and
\displaystyle L
are as in Definition
1
,
and
\displaystyle Tr\colon S\rightarrow Distr(S)
is a probability transition function.

In many practical settings, the agent does not have direct access to the underlying state
\displaystyle s\in S
of the MDP. Instead, the agent receives an observation that may represent a transformed view of the true state. We formalize this through an observation function.

Definition 3

(Observation)
.

We define the observation function
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\colon S\rightarrow O
as a function that maps a state
\displaystyle s\in S
to an observation
\displaystyle o\in O
.
An observation
\displaystyle o\in O
is a vector of features
\displaystyle(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
where
\displaystyle f_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The observed features may differ from the exact state features.

A policy operates on observations rather than on the underlying states directly.
In our setting, the environment is fully observable, so the observation function
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
maps each state feature directly to its corresponding observation feature, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
is the identity on the state features.

Definition 4
.

A
memoryless deterministic policy
\displaystyle\pi
for an MDP
\displaystyle M
is a function
\displaystyle\pi\colon O\rightarrow Act
that maps an observation
\displaystyle o\in O
to action
\displaystyle a\in Act
.

Applying a policy
\displaystyle\pi
to an MDP
\displaystyle M
with observation function
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
yields an
induced DTMC

\displaystyle D^{\pi}
where all non-determinism is resolved: for each state
\displaystyle s
, the transition function becomes
\displaystyle Tr(s,s^{\prime})=Tr(s,\pi(\mathbb{O}(s)),s^{\prime})
.
The induced DTMC fully characterizes the observable behavior of the policy: the states visited, the transitions taken, and the probabilities of all outcomes.
The interaction between the policy and environment is depicted in Figure
1
.

.

3.2.
Probabilistic Model Checking

Probabilistic model checking
enables the verification of quantitative properties of stochastic systems.

COOL-MC
uses
Storm

[
86
]
internally as its model checker, which can verify properties of both MDPs and DTMCs.
Among the most fundamental properties are
reachability
queries, which assess the probability of a system reaching a particular state.
For example, one might say: “The reachability probability of reaching an unsafe state is 0.1.”

The general workflow for model checking is as follows (see also Figure
2
).
First, the system is formally modeled using a language such as
PRISM

[
124
]
.
Next, the property of interest is formalized in a temporal logic.
Using these inputs, the model checker verifies whether the property holds or computes the relevant probability.

Figure 2
.
General model checking workflow
[
86
]
. The system is formally modeled, the requirements are formalized, and both are input to a model checker such as
Storm
, which verifies the property.

The
PRISM
language
[
124
]
describes probabilistic systems as a collection of
modules
, each containing typed
variables
and guarded
commands
.
A state of the system is a valuation of all variables, and the state space
\displaystyle S
is the set of all possible valuations.
Each command takes the form

\texttt{[action]}\ g\rightarrow\lambda_{1}:u_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}:u_{n}

where
\displaystyle g
is a Boolean guard over the variables, each
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}
is a probability, and each
\displaystyle u_{j}
is an update that assigns new values to the variables.
When the system is in a state satisfying
\displaystyle g
, the command can be executed: update
\displaystyle u_{j}
is applied with probability
\displaystyle\lambda_{j}
, transitioning the system to a new state.
For an MDP, each enabled command in a state corresponds to a distinct probabilistic choice, and the nondeterministic selection among enabled commands is resolved by a policy.
Listing
1
shows a simplified MDP in
PRISM
.
The model declares a single module with a state variable
s
ranging over integer identifiers.
Each command specifies a transition: a guard on the current state, an action label, and a probabilistic update.
For instance, from state
\displaystyle s=0
, taking action
a0
leads to state
\displaystyle s=1
with probability
\displaystyle 0.7
and to state
\displaystyle s=2
with probability
\displaystyle 0.3
.
States
\displaystyle s=2
and
\displaystyle s=3
are absorbing terminal states corresponding to different labels.
A reward structure assigns a reward of
\displaystyle+1
to the
\displaystyle s=2
state.
Additionally,
PRISM
supports
atomic labels
that annotate states with propositions.
These labels are defined using
label
declarations, which assign a name to a Boolean condition over the state variables.
For example,
label "goal" = s=2;
marks all states where
\displaystyle s=2
with the proposition
goal
.
Such labels serve as the atomic propositions referenced in PCTL properties.

COOL-MC
allows automated user-specified state labeling of
PRISM
models.
For a complete formal treatment of the
PRISM
semantics, we refer to
[
124
]
.

Properties are specified using PCTL
[
82
]
, a branching-time temporal logic for reasoning about probabilities over paths.
In this paper, we use two path operators.
The
eventually
operator
\displaystyle\lozenge\,\varphi
(also written
\displaystyle F\,\varphi
) states that
\displaystyle\varphi
holds at some future state along a path.
The
until
operator
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\;\mathcal{U}\;\varphi_{2}
states that
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}
holds at every state along a path until a state is reached where
\displaystyle\varphi_{2}
holds.
A bounded variant
\displaystyle\lozenge^{\leq B}\,\varphi
restricts the eventually operator to paths of at most
\displaystyle B
steps.
A PCTL property has the form

P_{\sim p}(\psi)

where
\displaystyle\psi
is a path formula (such as
\displaystyle\lozenge\,\varphi
or
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\;\mathcal{U}\;\varphi_{2}
),
\displaystyle\sim
is a comparison operator (
\displaystyle<
,
\displaystyle\leq
,
\displaystyle\geq
,
\displaystyle>
), and
\displaystyle p\in[0,1]
is a probability threshold.
Beyond checking whether a property is satisfied,
Storm
can compute the exact probability, denoted
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\psi)
.
For example,
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 200}\;\textit{empty})
computes the exact probability of reaching a stockout within 200 steps.

In addition to probability queries, PCTL supports
expected time
queries of the form
\displaystyle T_{=?}(F\;\varphi)
, which compute the expected number of steps to first reach a state satisfying
\displaystyle\varphi
.
For instance,
\displaystyle T_{=?}(F\;\texttt{"pr}_{14}\texttt{"})
returns the expected number of steps until the policy first selects an order of 14 units.
Such queries enable temporal characterization of the policy’s ordering behavior, revealing how quickly different actions or inventory configurations are encountered during system evolution.
We refer to
[
82
,
24
]
for a detailed description and additional PCTL operators.

3.3.
Reinforcement Learning

The standard goal of RL is to learn a policy
\displaystyle\pi
for an MDP that maximizes the expected accumulated discounted reward
[
27
]

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{N}\gamma^{t}\cdot rew(s_{t},a_{t})\right],

where
\displaystyle\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor,
\displaystyle rew(s_{t},a_{t})
is the reward at time step
\displaystyle t
, and
\displaystyle N
is the episode length.

3.4.
Explainability Methods

Explainability methods aim to make trained RL policies understandable
[
118
]
.
Global methods interpret the overall policy’s behavior
[
136
]
, while local methods explain a policy’s decision-making in individual states.
In this work, we use four complementary methods:
feature pruning
,
feature permutation importance
,
action labeling
, and
counterfactual action replacement
, each combined with PCTL model checking on the induced DTMC.

Consider a neural network policy operating on observations with
\displaystyle d
input features and
\displaystyle|Act|
output actions, encoding a function
\displaystyle g\colon\mathbb{Q}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{|Act|}
.
The function
\displaystyle g
is parameterized by a sequence of weight matrices
\displaystyle\vec{W}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{W}^{(k)}
with
\displaystyle\vec{W}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}\times d_{\ell-1}}
for
\displaystyle\ell=1,\dots,k
.
Feature pruning eliminates all outgoing connections from a particular input neuron by setting
\displaystyle\vec{W}^{(1)}_{j,i}=0
for all
\displaystyle j
, effectively removing the
\displaystyle i
-th input feature
\displaystyle f_{i}
from the policy’s decision-making
[
75
]
.
By measuring the resulting change in the policy’s behavior (e.g., degradation in stockout probability on the induced DTMC), we quantify the importance of each feature.

Feature permutation importance
[
35
]
offers a complementary, model-agnostic measure of feature relevance.
For a given observation
\displaystyle o=\mathbb{O}(s)
with features
\displaystyle(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
, the values of a single feature
\displaystyle f_{i}
are randomly permuted across observations while all other features remain fixed.
The policy is then re-evaluated on the permuted inputs, and the change in action selection is measured.
A large change indicates that the policy’s decisions depend strongly on
\displaystyle f_{i}
.
Unlike feature pruning, which permanently modifies the network weights, permutation importance leaves the policy intact. It can be computed per state, providing a local importance ranking that can be used to label states in the induced DTMC for further PCTL analysis.

Action labeling annotates each state in the induced DTMC with the action selected by the trained policy
[
78
]
.
These labels become atomic propositions that can be referenced in PCTL properties, enabling formal queries over the policy’s decision behavior, such as detecting whether certain ordering levels are consistently chosen before reaching undesirable states.

Counterfactual action replacement modifies the policy by substituting one action with another across all states where it is selected, and then reconstructs and re-verifies the induced DTMC
[
74
]
.
This allows targeted what-if analysis that isolates the effect of specific ordering decisions on safety outcomes. For example, one can assess how the probabilities of stockout and wastage change if all medium- and large-order quantities are replaced with smaller ones, without retraining the RL policy.

4.
Methodology

Our methodology proceeds in four stages.
First, we encode the platelet inventory management MDP
[
81
]
in the
PRISM
modeling language, defining states, actions, transition dynamics, and penalty structures that capture the daily ordering planning problem (§
4.1
).
Second, we train a deep RL policy using PPO to learn an ordering strategy that minimizes the combined cost of shortages and outdating (inventory-full) (§
4.2
).
Third, we verify the trained policy on its induced DTMC by applying PCTL queries to assess safety properties such as stockout probability and to characterize ordering trajectories (§
4.3
).
Fourth, we explain the policy’s decision-making through feature pruning, feature-importance permutation ranking, action labeling, and counterfactual action replacement, combined with PCTL queries on the induced DTMC (§
4.4
).
Finally, we describe the limitations of our methodology (§
4.5
).

4.1.
MDP Encoding

We encode the platelet inventory MDP inspired by Haijema et al.
[
81
]
in the
PRISM
modeling language. This MDP models the daily ordering planning problem for a regional blood bank in the North-East Netherlands, with the objective of minimizing the combined cost of platelet shortages and outdating.
The model parameters are derived from operational data of approximately 144 platelet pools demanded per week
[
81
]
, following the aggregation and downsizing procedure described therein.

Our
PRISM
encoding uses a two-phase structure per day that differs from Haijema et al.’s original formulation: in phase 0, the controller makes a nondeterministic ordering decision; in phase 1, stochastic demand is realized and inventory is updated. Haijema et al. model each day as a single decision epoch in which the production decision and demand realization occur implicitly within one transition. Our two-phase separation explicitly encodes this interleaving of controller actions and nature’s moves within
PRISM
’s modeling framework, introducing the additional state variables
\displaystyle\mathit{pend}
and
\displaystyle\mathit{ph}
(see below).
The MDP cycles indefinitely through the weekly schedule,
matching the infinite-horizon formulation of Haijema et al.

4.1.1.
State space

The state is represented by 8 features: the day of the week
\displaystyle d\in\{0,\ldots,6\}
(Monday through Sunday), five age-structured inventory features
\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots,x_{5}\in\{0,\ldots,S_{\max}\}
where
\displaystyle x_{r}
denotes the number of aggregated units with
\displaystyle r
days of residual shelf life, a pending order feature
\displaystyle\mathit{pend}\in\{0,\ldots,S_{\max}\}
representing units ordered today that become available the next morning (this feature is part of our
PRISM
encoding to explicitly track the one-day lead time; it is not a separate state feature in Haijema et al.’s original formulation), and a phase feature
\displaystyle\mathit{ph}\in\{0,1\}
.
The total available inventory is
\displaystyle\mathit{tinv}=\sum_{r=1}^{5}x_{r}
. Following the downsizing procedure of Haijema et al., one aggregated unit corresponds to 4 real platelet pools, and
\displaystyle S_{\max}=30
.

4.1.2.
Actions

The action set consists of 31 ordering levels
\displaystyle\{\mathit{pr}_{0},\mathit{pr}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{pr}_{30}\}
, where action
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{k}
orders
\displaystyle k
aggregated units. On weekdays (Monday–Friday), all actions satisfying
\displaystyle\mathit{tinv}+k\leq S_{\max}
are available; on weekends, only
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{0}
(no ordering) is enabled.
The chosen order amount is stored in
pend
and is
not
available to serve same-day demand, matching the specification of Haijema et al. that ordering is released at the end of the day and becomes available the next morning
[
81
]
.
In phase 1, the environment executes a single demand-realization action
\displaystyle\mathit{dm}
.

4.1.3.
Demand and transition dynamics

Demand on day
\displaystyle d
follows a Poisson distribution with day-dependent rate
\displaystyle\lambda_{d}
, truncated to the range
\displaystyle\{0,\ldots,K_{\max}\}
with
\displaystyle K_{\max}=20
and renormalized. The daily rates (in aggregated units) are
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Mon}}=6.5
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Tue}}=5.0
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Wed}}=8.0
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Thu}}=5.0
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Fri}}=6.5
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Sat}}=1.75
, and
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{Sun}}=3.25
, corresponding to the weekly demand pattern of
\displaystyle[26,20,32,20,26,7,13]
pools divided by the aggregation factor of 4.
The Poisson probabilities are computed inline using
PRISM
formulas via unnormalized weights
\displaystyle w_{k}=\lambda_{d}^{k}/k!
and normalization
\displaystyle p_{k}=w_{k}/\sum_{j=0}^{K_{\max}}w_{j}
, ensuring probabilities sum to exactly 1 for any parameter values.

Given demand realization
\displaystyle b
, the transition encodes three simultaneous operations:

(1)

FIFO issuing:
Demand is served oldest-first. The remaining demand after consuming age classes
\displaystyle 1
through
\displaystyle r
is
\displaystyle\max(0,\,b-\sum_{i=1}^{r}x_{i})
, and the leftover stock at age
\displaystyle r
is
\displaystyle\max(0,\,x_{r}-\max(0,\,b-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}x_{i}))
.

(2)

Outdating and aging:
Any leftover
\displaystyle x_{1}
(oldest stock) is discarded. The remaining stock shifts down one age class:
\displaystyle x_{r}^{\prime}=\text{leftover}_{r+1}
for
\displaystyle r=1,\ldots,4
.

(3)

Delivery:
Today’s pending order arrives as fresh stock:
\displaystyle x_{5}^{\prime}=\mathit{pend}
.

The day then advances (
\displaystyle d^{\prime}=(d+1)\bmod 7
) and the phase resets to 0.

4.1.4.
Penalty structure

Penalties are defined as state-based expected costs at phase 1, computed over the demand distribution using formulas:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{shortage}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{K_{\max}}p_{k}\cdot\max(0,\,k-\mathit{tinv}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{outdating}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{K_{\max}}p_{k}\cdot\max(0,\,x_{1}-k).

The combined cost reward is
\displaystyle C_{S}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{shortage}]+C_{O}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{outdating}]
, where
\displaystyle C_{S}=5
and
\displaystyle C_{O}=1
reflect the 5:1 shortage-to-outdating cost ratio of Haijema et al.

4.1.5.
Labels

States are labeled with atomic propositions to enable PCTL property specification such as
empty
(
\displaystyle\mathit{tinv}=0
),
full
(
\displaystyle\mathit{tinv}=S_{\max}
), and
weekend
(
\displaystyle d\geq 5
).

4.1.6.
Summary

The encoded
PRISM
MDP is formally defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle S=\{0,\ldots,6\}\times\{0,\ldots,S_{\max}\}^{5}\times\{0,\ldots,S_{\max}\}\times\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathit{Act}=\{\mathit{pr}_{0},\mathit{pr}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{pr}_{30}\}\cup\{\mathit{dm}\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathit{rew}(s)=C_{S}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{shortage}\mid s]+C_{O}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{outdating}\mid s]\quad\text{if }\mathit{ph}=1.

4.2.
RL Policy Training

We train a deep RL policy
\displaystyle\pi
on the platelet inventory MDP using PPO.
The policy network is a feedforward neural network with three fully connected hidden layers of 256 neurons.
The agent is trained for 25,000 episodes with a learning rate of
\displaystyle\alpha=3\times 10^{-4}
, discount factor
\displaystyle\gamma=0.99
, batch size 64, and a maximum episode length of 200 steps.

4.3.
Policy Verification

For the trained policy
\displaystyle\pi
,
COOL-MC
constructs the induced DTMC
\displaystyle D^{\pi}
by exploring only the reachable state space under
\displaystyle\pi
(see Algorithm
1
).
Starting from the initial state
\displaystyle s_{0}
, the construction proceeds as a depth-first traversal.
At each visited state
\displaystyle s
, the policy selects an action
\displaystyle a=\pi(s)
, and all successor states
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
with
\displaystyle Tr(s,a,s^{\prime})>0
are added to the induced DTMC along with their transition probabilities.
The procedure recurses into each successor that has not yet been visited and is relevant for the property under verification.
This incremental approach offers two key advantages.
First, it constructs only the fragment of the full MDP that is actually reachable under
\displaystyle\pi
, which can be substantially smaller than the complete state space, mitigating the state explosion problem.
Second, it creates an induced DTMC rather than an MDP, since all nondeterminism is resolved by
\displaystyle\pi
, reducing the number of states and transitions.

4.4.
Policy Explanation

Verification alone establishes what the policy does (e.g., the probability of reaching stockout) but not
why
it makes particular ordering decisions.
To address this, we apply four explainability methods: feature pruning, feature-importance permutation ranking, action labeling, and counterfactual action replacement.

4.4.1.
Feature pruning

Feature pruning
[
75
]
provides a global sensitivity analysis of the trained neural network policy.
For each input feature
\displaystyle f_{i}\in\{d,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots\}
, all outgoing connections from the corresponding input neuron are set to zero, effectively removing
\displaystyle f_{i}
from the policy’s decision-making.
The induced DTMC is then reconstructed under the pruned policy, and the PCTL query of interest (e.g.,
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 200}\;\textit{empty})
) is re-computed.
The change in probability relative to the unpruned baseline quantifies the extent to which the policy relies on each feature for shortage avoidance.
A large increase in stockout probability upon pruning a feature indicates that the policy critically depends on that feature; a negligible change suggests redundancy.
This analysis reveals, for instance, whether removing the weekday

\displaystyle d
causes the policy to fail to build stock on specific days, such as Fridays.

4.4.2.
Feature-importance permutation ranking

Feature permutation importance
[
35
]
complements pruning with a local, per-state measure of feature relevance.
For each state observation
\displaystyle o
in the induced DTMC, the values of a single feature
\displaystyle f_{i}
are randomly permuted across observations while all other features remain fixed, and the policy is re-evaluated on the permuted inputs.
A large change in action selection indicates that the policy’s decision in state
\displaystyle s
depends strongly on
\displaystyle f_{i}
.
Each state is labeled with the feature whose permutation most frequently changes the policy’s action (e.g.,
imp_d
,
imp_x1
, …,
imp_ph
).
These labels are injected into the induced DTMC, enabling PCTL queries such as
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 10}\;\texttt{imp\_x1})
, which computes the probability of reaching a state where the oldest inventory class is the most important feature, and combined queries such as
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge\;(\texttt{friday}\wedge\neg\texttt{imp\_d}))
, which detects whether the policy ignores the day-of-week on the critical pre-weekend order day.

4.4.3.
Action labeling

Action labeling annotates each state in the induced DTMC with the action selected by the trained policy
[
78
]
, using the notation
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{k}
for the order of
\displaystyle k
aggregated units (where
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{0}
denotes no order and
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{30}
denotes maximum order).
These labels become atomic propositions that can be referenced in PCTL, enabling formal queries directly over the policy’s ordering behavior.
For example,
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 200}\;\mathit{pr}_{30})
quantifies the probability that the policy resorts to maximum ordering within 200 steps, while
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge\;(\mathit{pr}_{0}\wedge\texttt{monday}))
detects the reachability probability of no ordering on Mondays.

4.4.4.
Counterfactual action replacement

Counterfactual action replacement modifies the policy by substituting one action with another across all states where it is selected, and then reconstructs and re-verifies the induced DTMC
[
74
]
.
This allows targeted what-if analysis that isolates the effect of specific ordering decisions on safety outcomes without retraining the policy.
For example, one can assess how stockout and wastage probabilities change if all medium-large orders (
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{14}
, ordering 14 units) are replaced with smaller orders (
\displaystyle\mathit{pr}_{6}
, ordering 6 units).
By re-verifying both
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 200}\;\textit{empty})
and
\displaystyle P_{=?}(\lozenge^{\leq 200}\;\textit{full})
on the counterfactual DTMC, we quantify the trade-off between shortage risk and wastage risk induced by the action change.

4.5.
Limitations

Several limitations should be noted.
First, the platelet inventory MDP uses aggregated demand data from a single regional blood bank in the Netherlands
[
81
]
.
The demand patterns, Poisson rates, and cost parameters are specific to this setting; policies verified on this MDP may not directly transfer to other hospitals or regions with different demand profiles.
Second,
COOL-MC
requires a discrete action space, which is naturally satisfied by the platelet MDP’s integer ordering levels.
However, the state space grows combinatorially with
\displaystyle S_{\max}
and the number of age classes.
While the induced DTMC mitigates this by constructing only the reachable states under a single policy, sufficiently large inventory capacities or finer shelf-life discretizations could still pose scalability challenges.
Third, the
PRISM
model’s Poisson demand is truncated at
\displaystyle K_{\max}=20
and renormalized, which slightly distorts the tail of the demand distribution.

5.
Experiments

In this section, we apply the methodology described above to the platelet inventory MDP.
We first describe the experimental setup, including the hardware environment and software tools used.
We then present our analysis in two parts: Part A covers the training of the PPO agent and the construction and baseline verification of the induced DTMC, establishing the policy’s stockout and inventory-full probabilities.
Part B provides a detailed behavioral analysis of the trained policy, progressively applying permutation importance, feature pruning, action labeling, combined labeling, parameter sensitivity, and counterfactual action replacement to build a comprehensive picture of the policy’s ordering behavior and its driving factors.
The experiments are intended to illustrate the range of analyses that
COOL-MC
enables for inventory management settings.

5.1.
Setup

We executed our experiments in a Docker container with 16 GB RAM and an AMD Ryzen 7 7735HS processor, running Ubuntu 20.04.5 LTS.
For model checking, we use
Storm
1.7.0.
Implementation details are provided in the accompanying source code at
https://github.com/LAVA-LAB/COOL-MC/tree/sepsis

6.
Discussion

This section discusses the capabilities that
COOL-MC
demonstrates in this case study and reflects on the implications of our findings for platelet inventory management.

6.1.
From Aggregate Cost to Structural Understanding

Standard RL evaluation of inventory management policies reduces performance to a single scalar: expected cost per period, combining shortage and outdating penalties.
This aggregate metric confirms whether a policy is cost-effective but does not reveal
how
it achieves that outcome, such as how it balances the asymmetric costs of shortages (
\displaystyle C_{S}=5
) and full inventories (
\displaystyle C_{O}=1
).

COOL-MC
moves beyond this aggregate view by constructing the
induced DTMC for PCTL queries, feature pruning, and counterfactual
analysis. These reveal, for instance, that six order quantities are
never selected, that the freshest inventory features
\displaystyle x_{4}
/
\displaystyle x_{5}
are critical for
stockout avoidance, while the oldest stock
\displaystyle x_{1}
governs the inventory-full
prevention, and that replacing
\displaystyle\texttt{pr}_{14}
with

\displaystyle\texttt{pr}_{6}
in
\displaystyle 11.8\%
of states leaves both safety probabilities
unchanged, transforming a single reward number into a structured
characterization of the policy’s behaviour.

6.2.
COOL-MC as a Pre-Deployment Analysis Tool for Inventory Policies

For blood bank operations,
COOL-MC
’s analysis pipeline could support several practical workflows.
First, before deploying a new ordering policy, managers could verify that it meets safety requirements.
Second, when evaluating policy changes, the feature pruning profile could confirm that the new policy attends to the relevant inventory features.
Third, MDP parameter sweeps could assess policy robustness under anticipated demand changes (e.g., seasonal variation, pandemic-related disruptions) without requiring retraining for each scenario.

6.3.
Additional COOL-MC Capabilities

In this work, we focused on PPO training, PCTL verification on induced DTMCs, feature pruning, temporal feature-importance permutation ranking, and action labeling with parameter sensitivity.
However,
COOL-MC
offers additional capabilities that could further enrich the analysis of platelet inventory policies.

COOL-MC
’s feature abstraction and feature remapping
capabilities
[
71
]
allow analysis of policy
behaviour when state features are coarsened. In an inventory context, this could aggregate adjacent stock levels to simulate different measurement precision levels, quantifying how much safety degrades when the blood bank operates with coarser inventory tracking.

COOL-MC
also supports robustness verification through adversarial state-observation perturbations
[
73
,
72
]
.
By perturbing the inventory observation fed to the policy and re-verifying the induced DTMC, one can assess whether small counting errors or measurement inaccuracies in the recorded stock levels cause the policy to make substantially different ordering decisions.
In a blood bank setting, this could reveal whether the policy is robust to minor inventory discrepancies arising from manual counting or delayed database updates.

7.
Conclusion

This paper demonstrated the application of
COOL-MC
to the platelet inventory management MDP inspired by Haijema et al., providing the first formal probabilistic verification and explanation of an RL-trained ordering policy for this domain.

Several directions for future work emerge from this study. These include applying
COOL-MC
to other healthcare MDPs
[
145
,
138
]
and integrating domain-expert knowledge into the analysis.
Furthermore, leveraging policy-agnostic test suite methods, such as MPTCS
[
30
]
, to identify critical initial states can focus bounded reachability verification on the most challenging regions of the state space.
