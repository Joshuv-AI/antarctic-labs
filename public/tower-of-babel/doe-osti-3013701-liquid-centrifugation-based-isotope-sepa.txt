 Liquid centrifugation-based isotope separation of 7Li, 37Cl and D

1. Overview
 Liquid centrifugation is an emerging method to separate isotopes.1 By spinning a liquid
containing target isotopes (e.g., pure chemical or solution), the heavier isotope will be enriched at
the outer part of a centrifuge, and the lighter ones will be enriched at the inner part of a centrifuge.
The separation capability is positively correlated with rotation speed, outer radius and mass
difference between isotopes to separate. This award targets to understand whether liquid
centrifugation is effective in separating isotopes important for nuclear fission technologies. The
results include the following three sections: 1) Effects of solute concentration on isotope separation;
2) Preliminary progress of building a low-speed countercurrent centrifuge, and 3) Analysis on
possible candidates for 7Li and 37Cl. The award trained one research scientist, one Ph.D. student,
one master student, and one undergraduate on liquid centrifugation and isotope measurements.

2. Effects of solute concentration on isotope separation
 2.1 Introduction
 The Einstein relation is a fundamental principle in transport theory, which connects diffusivity
(D) and mobility (u) under an external field as D/u = RT.2 This principle has been widely used to
describe important transport phenomena, such as for ions in electrolytes, electrons/holes in
semiconductors, and movement of microparticles in fluids. The validity of Einstein relation roots
from Boltzmann distribution and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem3. Although breakdown of the
ER has been observed in systems with strong interactions, such as concentrated liquid electrolytes4,
5
  , solids6, 7, and supercooled water8, 9, it is generally believed to be valid in dilute systems, such as
ion transport in dilute electrolytes and electron transport in semiconductors 5, 10.
 In developing models for liquid centrifugation of isotope separation, we studied if the Einstein
relation is valid at different solute concentrations. Surprisingly, when we use O-18 as a tracer, we
surprisingly observe that the Einstein Relation breaks down substantially in pure water and diluted
solution under centrifugation, where the centrifugal mobility of H218O tracers decreases such that
D/u >> RT in pure water (Fig. 1). This breakdown disappears with the addition of various solutes
above a critical concentration (ct) (Fig. 1). We also find that ct is proportional to the square of the
partial molar density of the solute (𝜕𝜌soln /𝜕𝑐𝑖 where 𝜌soln is the solution density and 𝑐𝑖 is the
concentration of solute i), such as 0.9 mM for CsI, 3.0 mM for MgSO4, 350 mM for EtOH, and
6.0 M for H218O itself. The experimental results can also be accurately described by a two-phase
phenomenal model.

 Such a breakdown of the ER under a centrifugal field has not been reported before to the
best of our knowledge. While the origin is unclear, the phenomenon’s dependence on partial molar
density makes us suspect that it may be related to the following two mechanisms: 1) The hydrogen
bond network in H2O, where heavier solutes more easily disrupt the network vibrations, and thus
a lower concentration of solutes is needed to break the network and restore the ER. 2) Long-range
dipole correlations of the water molecules, which inhibit the free motion of solutes, and heavier
solutes impart a greater internal stress on the water structure and thus break up the correlations.

3 Development of a liquid centrifuge prototype
3.1 Background and overview
 Gas centrifugation used countercurrent flow to amplify isotope separation. Previously we
simulated isotope separation in a liquid centrifuge with countercurrent flow.11 The results show
that isotope separation can also be amplified by increasing the height of a centrifuge since more
countercurrent exchange is enabled. For example, in the case that only had countercurrent flow
and no inlet/outlet flow (F = 0) (Fig. 5), ln() scales with the centrifuge height linearly. At
centrifuge height (h) of 1 m and countercurrent flow (L) of 15 g hr-1, ln() is 0.189, 0.295, and
0.425 for vo = 400, 500, and 600 m s-1, respectively, where  is the separation factor, and vo is the
rotation velocity at the outer peripheral. In contrast, with no countercurrent flow, ln() is only
0.029, 0.045, and 0.064 for vo = 400, 500, and 600 m s-1. Therefore, a countercurrent configuration
with a height of 1 m will result in an amplification factor of 6-7 compared to the case without the
countercurrent design.

Figure 5. The performance characteristics for a range of centrifuge conditions were simulated. (a) shows
the separation factor (per neutrons difference, n) vs. centrifuge height for a range of peripheral velocities.

 In this project we target to build a prototype of such liquid centrifuge with countercurrent
flow. Due to the early termination of the project, we only finished part of the prototype, and this
section will discuss design principle and progress so far.
 The project evolved through two primary phases. The initial focus was on achieving high
rotational speeds and characterizing rotor dynamics, vibration modes, and stabilization strategies
in a dry system without liquid inside. During this phase, the centrifuge was operated using
frequency-based control, and stable operation was achieved at frequencies approaching 7-8 kRPM
in favorable configurations. These results demonstrated that additively manufactured components

 7

and relatively simple bearing architectures could sustain high-speed rotation, establishing a
baseline level of mechanical feasibility.
 During the second phase, the emphasis shifted toward liquid integration and continuous
operation. The goal of this phase was to develop a system capable of introducing and extracting
liquid from the centrifuge while rotating, with countercurrent and separation functionality. This
phase included iterative prototype design and experimental testing of various flow phenomena,
ultimately combining to create an intended system with an inlet flow source and two outlet flows.
This phase exposed a new class of challenges related to friction, inertia, sealing, and
manufacturability. Although the prototype did not yet achieve successful spinning operation with
liquid flow by the end of the project, the work completed during this second phase clarified several
fundamental limitations that must be addressed in future iterations. Fig. 6 shows the exploded CAD
design of liquid centrifuge assembly for phase II. The design shows the primary rotating
components left-to-right in order of assembly: top end cap (with nozzles connecting rotor to
stationary-rotating flow equipment), connector with inlet and outlet channels, upper filter disk,
rotor, lower filter disk, and bottom end cap.

3.2 Prototype design
 3.2.1 Material Selection and initial device design
 We use resin-based 3D printing to make the centrifuge due to considerations of time,
accessibility, and iteration speed. Resin printing allowed for rapid prototyping and parallel
development, enabling the team to continue designing and testing other aspects of the system while
prints were in progress. Tough 2000 resin was selected as the primary material for printed
components due to its relatively high toughness and resistance to brittle failure compared to
standard resins. This choice was particularly important for components subjected to centrifugal
loading and vibrational stress.

 The initial prototype was conceived as a mechanically forgiving system whose primary
purpose was to explore the upper limits of rotational speed achievable with resin-printed
components while maintaining stability through critical frequencies.

 8

 We started with an empty centrifuge design without any liquid inside. The mechanical
architecture of the first prototype relied heavily on compliance and damping to accommodate
imperfections inherent to additive manufacturing. At the top of the centrifuge, a magnetic bearing
was mounted to a small metal shelf and submerged in oil (Fig. 7a). This bearing was designed to
be relatively stiff while still allowing motion in the horizontal plane, enabling the rotor to self-
center during operation. A corresponding magnet was rigidly mounted in the top end cap of the
rotor, creating an attractive force that aligned the rotor with the stationary bearing above. The oil
bath surrounding the bearing served to damp vibrations and suppress high-frequency oscillations,
although it introduced practical challenges related to oil level control and leakage.

Figure 7. (a) Magnetic bearing arrangement used in the original prototype, showing the stationary magnet
mounted to the metal shelf through a jack for fine height adjustment to control the attractive force, and the

 The rotor itself was a hollow cylindrical structure chosen for its favorable balance
characteristics and ease of fabrication. The absence of internal flow paths simplified the geometry
and reduced the likelihood of print defects, allowing the team to focus on dynamic behavior rather
than fluid integration. The rotor was connected at its lower end to a DC motor, which was
supported by a cross-like structure printed in PLA. This structure provided a simple and
lightweight means of holding the motor in place while allowing some flexibility in alignment.
 At the bottom of the motor, the design incorporated a bearing system intended to replace
the needle-point pivots used in earlier centrifuge designs (Fig. 7b). This system consisted of a small
metallic ball that rotated on a carved brass plate. The brass plate was mounted on four small springs
housed within a rectangular container filled with oil. This arrangement allowed limited motion in
the horizontal plane while providing viscous damping through the oil. The combination of spring
compliance and oil damping was intended to absorb vibrational energy and reduce sensitivity to
misalignment, particularly during passage through critical frequencies.

 3.2.2 Testing results and limitations
 Such a prototype exhibited several distinct critical frequencies, observed at approximately
510, 1530, and 2550 RPM, although the exact values varied depending on rotor geometry and
assembly details. At these frequencies, oscillations and vibrations increased noticeably, but the
compliant and damped architecture generally prevented catastrophic instability. By carefully
ramping through these regimes, stable operation at higher frequencies could be achieved.

 9

 In favorable configurations, the centrifuge reached operating frequencies on the order of
800-950 Hz. Achieving these speeds was a significant milestone, as it demonstrated that resin-
printed components, when combined with appropriate stabilization strategies, could sustain the
rotational regimes required for meaningful centrifugal forces. These results established a baseline
level of mechanical feasibility and provided confidence that further development toward liquid
operation was justified.
 The first architecture revealed multiple important limitations. The reliance on oil-damped
magnetic bearings introduced practical challenges related to leakage, maintenance, and
reproducibility. Controlling oil levels proved difficult, and even small leaks could compromise
damping performance or contaminate other components. Additionally, the magnetic alignment
system, while effective for dry operation, was incompatible with the introduction of fluid interfaces
at the top of the centrifuge. Any attempt to route liquid through the top end cap would have
interfered with the magnetic bearing and oil bath, making the existing architecture unsuitable for
continuous liquid operation. These limitations motivated a fundamental reassessment of the
mechanical architecture as the project transitioned toward liquid integration in the second
prototype.

 3.2.3 Second design
 The transition from the first prototype to the second design was driven by the need to
integrate liquid into the centrifuge while preserving the performance gains achieved during high-
speed dry testing. This transition required a shift in design priorities away from compliance and
self-alignment and toward rigidity, integration, and controllable fluid interfaces. One of the most
significant challenges in liquid centrifuge design is the interface between stationary and rotating
components. Introducing liquid into a rotating system requires either direct mechanical contact,
which increases friction and wear, or a specialized interface such as a rotary union. The initial
design was effective in dry systems but left no practical way to introduce liquid, therefore it was
necessary to fundamentally alter the design.
 The second design prioritized a rigidly mounted rotary union at the top of the centrifuge
(Fig. 6 and 8). This component provided a direct and conceptually straightforward means of
routing liquid between the stationary laboratory frame and the rotating centrifuge. However, the
use of a rotary union imposed new constraints on alignment, friction, and mass distribution. Unlike
the magnetic bearing, which could accommodate misalignment through compliance, the rotary
union required precise geometric alignment to avoid excessive friction and binding. To
accommodate the rotary union, the top end cap of the centrifuge was redesigned to be rigidly
attached to the rotating portion of the union. This eliminated the need for self-aligning features at
the top of the system and simplified the fluid routing geometry.
 At the same time, this updated design sought to preserve as much of the initial prototype’s
stabilization strategy as possible at the bottom of the centrifuge. The bottom end cap and motor
support structure remained conceptually similar, with the motor held by a PLA-printed cross
structure. This approach compromised between known stabilizing elements and accommodating
the new requirements for liquid integration. Theoretical considerations and earlier fluid
experiments suggested that at least two inlets and two outlets would be desirable to enable
symmetric flow paths and eventual countercurrent operation. This requirement drove the redesign
of the top end cap to include multiple nozzles and internal connectors, further increasing its
complexity and mass.

 3.2.4 Testing results and path forward
 New challenges of the second design became apparent through testing. Even before
introducing liquid, the presence of the rotary union altered the dynamic behavior of the centrifuge
through greater resistance to rotation and the motor appeared to stall more readily, even under
conditions in which the initial prototype had operated smoothly. These observations suggested that
friction within the rotary union, combined with increased inertial loading from the more complex
rotor and the addition of liquid, was placing significantly higher demands on the motor.
 At this stage, it became clear that the initial prototype’s performance metrics could not be
assumed to carry over to the liquid-capable design. High rotational speed, which had been
achievable in a dry, compliant system, now had to be re-worked under substantially more
demanding conditions. This realization underscored the importance of treating the second
prototype not as a refinement of the earlier design, but as a fundamentally new system with its own
constraints and failure modes.
 The current inability to achieve stable spinning with the rotary union installed ultimately
prevented meaningful testing of liquid operation and internal flow behavior within the second
prototype. Nevertheless, the early observations from this configuration provided insight into the
challenges of combining high-speed rotation with rigid fluid interfaces. These insights set the stage
for more detailed analysis of rotor design, motor limitations, and fluid integration challenges.
 At the end of the project, the system exists in a partially functional state. The pump and
external fluid routing through the rotary union and top end cap operate reliably in isolation,
demonstrating that the basic fluid interface concept is sound. However, the centrifuge has not been
successfully spun with the rotary union installed, and no testing has been conducted with liquid
present under rotation.
 The most significant blocking issues are the excessive friction and alignment sensitivity
introduced by the rotary union and the complete blockage of internal rotor flow paths due to resin

 11

printing limitations. These issues prevent evaluation of countercurrent flow and separation
behavior and must be addressed before further progress can be made. Future development of the
liquid centrifuge should proceed in a staged and performance-aware manner. The immediate
priority is updating the rotor design to ensure the internal flow paths remain open after printing.
This redesign may require larger channel diameters, alternative printing orientations, or a
reconsideration of fabrication methods altogether. A functional rotor is required for experimental
progress in liquid centrifuge functionality.
 Once a viable rotor is available, the system should be tested incrementally, beginning with
dry spinning using the rotary union to establish a new baseline for rotational performance. Only
after stable dry operation is achieved should liquid be introduced, initially without attempting
countercurrent flow. This staged approach will help isolate the contributions of friction, inertia,
and fluid loading to overall performance.
 Countercurrent flow and separation experiments should be deferred until the mechanical
and fluidics subsystems demonstrate reliable operation under rotation. At that stage, insights from
the Spring 2025 fluid experiments and theoretical models can be revisited with the benefit of a
more robust mechanical platform. The long-term objective remains a continuous-operation liquid
centrifuge capable of meaningful separation, and achieving this goal will require disciplined
iteration grounded in the lessons documented in this report.

that liquid lithium metal at 180 oC have a much higher cD of 51.7 × 10-5 mol/m/s, which is on par
with gas centrifugation. On the other hand, given the high separation factor in chemical separation
of lithium isotopes, centrifugation-based separation is unlikely to reach cost as low as chemical
exchange-based separation, such as the COLEX process and the crown ether-based separation.
