A physics-regularized machine learning approach for predicting time-temperature-transformation curves in alloys: application to uranium-based alloys
Authors: Sunidhi Garg, Jishnu Bhattacharyya, Vineet V Joshi, Sean R Agnew, Prasanna V Balachandran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA physics-regularized machine learning (ML) approach is developed for predicting time-temperature-transformation (TTT) diagrams from alloy composition in uranium (U)-based systems. To ensure physically realistic C-curve shape while maintaining predictive accuracy, four loss function formulations are systematically evaluated: (1) baseline mean squared error (MSE), (2) MSE regularized with a semi-empirical nucleation-based model, (3) MSE regularized with shape constraints that enforce characteristic C-curve convexity, and (4) a hybrid approach that combines both semi-empirical regularization and shape constraints with MSE. Models that incorporate shape-constrained loss functions generate TTT predictions exhibiting a physically consistent C-curve form and demonstrate good agreement with experimental isothermal transformation data. Post hoc model explanation using global feature importance and partial dependence plots reveals systematic changes in the learned composition-TTT relationships as a function of the loss function formulation. The optimized ML model, which used a hybrid loss function, was applied to previously unexplored U-Mo-X ternary systems. This analysis identified U-Mo-Pt, U-Mo-Au, U-Mo-Ta, U-Mo-W, and U-Mo-Fe as candidate alloys that exhibit enhanced kinetic stability against γ-to-α phase decomposition during isothermal holds compared to the U-Mo binary baseline. Furthermore, TTT nose time predictions from the optimized ML model are compared with those from a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model using MSE loss. The developed physics-regularized ML framework offers potential for accelerating alloy discovery in U-based systems, where traditional TTT experimental determination is time and resource-intensive.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Time-temperature-transformation (TTT) diagrams capture the kinetics of isothermal solid-state phase transformations through characteristic C-shaped curves. These diagrams show the time needed to achieve a specific volume fraction of transformation at various isothermal temperatures. The nose of the TTT diagram marks the temperature with the fastest transformation rate. TTT diagrams are valuable in physical metallurgy because they link processing parameters, such as temperature, time, and cooling rate, to microstructure evolution, which determines mechanical properties and alloy performance. However, experimentally generating TTT diagrams requires extensive isothermal heat treatment studies across multiple temperatures and hold times, making the process time-consuming and resource-intensive.

Computational methods have been developed to predict time-temperature-transformation (TTT) diagrams prior to synthesis and characterization. Bhadeshia introduced a physics-inspired approach grounded in the Russell’s nucleation theory1, integrating fundamental thermodynamic principles, such as nucleation barrier, activation energy, driving force, with empirically fitted parameters to model TTT curve shapes2. Although this method effectively captures the characteristic C-curve topology, its applicability is restricted by the availability of experimental data and the necessity of extensive parametrization for each alloy system. Moreover, the validity of this technique has been extensively tested in ferrous alloys, such as steels2,3, and its application to non-ferrous alloys is limited or largely unexplored.

Machine learning (ML) offers an alternative approach that addresses some of these limitations by learning composition-transformation relationships directly from experimental databases. In contrast to physics-based models, which require explicit parametrization of nucleation kinetics for each alloy family via model calibration, ML algorithms can model complex, nonlinear relationships between alloy composition and TTT curve characteristics without imposing restrictive functional forms. Malinov et al.4 developed an artificial neural network (ANN)-based ML model for predicting TTT diagrams for titanium alloys. Although the authors do not explicitly discuss their choice of loss function, their model description suggests the use of a method that minimizes the difference between experimentally measured and ANN-predicted targets during training. Kundu et al.5 developed an ANN model for predicting the TTT diagrams of steels. Rather than utilizing the entire TTT diagram, the authors divided the curve into upper and lower halves and selected two temperatures as outputs. Mean squared error (MSE) was employed as the loss function to train the ANN models. Recently, Huang et al.6–8 introduced a methodology that integrates multiple ML techniques to predict the TTT diagram of stainless steels, high-alloy steels, and low-alloy steels. The authors used three statistical metrics, namely mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and correlation coefficient, to evaluate the prediction accuracy of the trained ML models. Hernández-Flores et al.9 developed an ANN-based approach to predict the TTT diagrams of Ni-Al alloys. The authors used RMSE as the loss function to train the ANN model. Although these studies demonstrate the potential of ML for predicting TTT diagrams, model training has relied solely on standard statistical loss functions, such as MSE, MAE, or RMSE. These functions minimize prediction error but fail to incorporate physical constraints on TTT curve morphology or the thermodynamic principles governing phase transformation kinetics.

This study develops physics-regularized ML models to predict complete TTT curves for binary and ternary uranium (U) alloys by employing custom loss functions. The design of U-based nuclear fuels requires consideration of multiple factors, including phase stability, burnup characteristics, and mechanical properties, which vary depending on reactor design and application. Many research and test reactors are transitioning from highly enriched uranium (HEU) fuels to low-enriched uranium (LEU) fuels to mitigate nuclear proliferation risks while maintaining performance comparable to HEU fuels10. HEU fuels are those with a greater than 20% proportion of the naturally occurring fissile isotope, U-235, which is also the least abundant uranium isotope. One method to convert HEU to LEU fuel involves increasing the uranium density within the fuel. This is necessary because, as previously reported, each atom of U-235 in LEU must be accompanied by nearly four atoms of U-23811. Of the three allotropic forms of U, the body-centered cubic γ-phase (stable above 1440 K) exhibits favorable irradiation characteristics and thermal stability, which are critical during both processing and in-reactor use12. In contrast, the orthorhombic α-phase (stable up to 939 K) and the tetragonal β-phase (stable between 939 K and 1440 K) are unsuitable as fuel phases due to inadequate irradiation properties. Among U-based alloys, the U-molybdenum (U-Mo) binary alloy system is of particular interest because it stabilizes the desired metastable γ-phase13,14. This metastable γ-phase can undergo eutectoid decomposition to orthorhombic α-U and intermetallic -phases (UMo) upon heat treatment. Studies have shown that introducing small amounts of a ternary element to this alloy (U-Mo-X, where X is the ternary element) can further stabilize the γ-phase and extend the nucleation time for the α-phase14. Certain 4d and 5d transition metals form solid solutions with the γ-U phase, and narrowing the selection of metals and their compositions can expedite the development of optimal LEU fuels15. Predictive modeling of TTT diagrams in the U-Mo-X composition space has the promise to enable systematic identification of alloying additions that extend the incubation time of the γ- to α-phase transformation and slow eutectoid decomposition, thereby minimizing the need for exhaustive experimental characterization of each candidate ternary system.

Johns and Burkes investigated the effect of incorporating a ternary element into U-Mo alloys by predicting the nose of the TTT curves using ANN16. Their models used the weight percentages of U, Mo, and the ternary element, along with the isothermal temperature from TTT curves, as input variables. The only output was the time corresponding to the nose of the TTT curve. Forty-two experimentally determined TTT curves from various literature sources were used to develop the ANN models. Consistent with previous studies, MSE was used as the loss function for model training. Although this work demonstrated the feasibility of ML for U-based alloy systems, it was limited to predicting a single point (the nose) rather than the entire TTT curve and did not include mechanisms to enforce physically realistic TTT curve topology. The present study addresses these limitations through three methodological advancements: (1) physics-regularized loss functions that integrate Bhadeshia’s semi-empirical model2 and geometric constraints such as C-curve convexity to ensure physically plausible predictions, a capability not present in previous ML-based TTT models; (2) complete TTT curve prediction for compositionally unexplored U-Mo-X ternary systems that await experimental validation, enabling computational screening of candidate alloying additions; and (3) quantitative uncertainty estimates for all predictions, addressing a limitation explicitly acknowledged by Johns and Burkes in their ANN approach. Predictions are compared with a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model that predicts the TTT nose time using MSE loss, following the approach of Johns and Burkes16. This model serves as a baseline for evaluating the physics-regularized TTT predictions.

Dataset curation

The TTT dataset for multiple U-Mo-X alloys was curated from the work of Johns and Burkes16, who compiled 42 U-Mo-X-based TTT curves from four primary sources, each employing different methods to measure these curves20–23. In this context, X represents chromium (Cr), niobium (Nb), rhenium (Re), ruthenium (Ru), or zirconium (Zr), with varying weight percentages. The U-10Mo and U-12Mo curves measured by Peterson et al. were excluded from further analysis due to previously reported discrepancies in the behavior of U-12Mo compared to U-10Mo24. Among the remaining 40 curves, the U-8Mo TTT curve was measured by three separate groups, resulting in 38 distinct alloys included in this study. The U-10Mo TTT curve measured by Repas et al. was retained, as recent literature demonstrates agreement between their reported nose time and temperature and those reported in other studies25. The complete list of these 38 alloys is provided in Table S1 in the Supplementary Information (SI). Some discrepancies are anticipated in the reported TTT curves, as different experimental techniques and conditions were employed to measure the TTT curves and synthesize the U-Mo-X alloys. The temperature range for all TTT curves is 440 C to 560 C. To establish relationships between alloy composition and TTT curves, ten elemental properties for each element (U, Mo, Cr, Nb, Re, Ru, Zr) were used as descriptors: atomic radius, number of valence electrons, electronegativity, melting point, thermal conductivity, bulk modulus, density, polarizability, heat of fusion, and the ratio of volume to number of atoms in a unit cell (V/Z)26,27. Each elemental descriptor was multiplied by its respective weight percentage in the alloy, excluding uranium, and averaged over the total composition. An additional descriptor, the number of alloy elements (), was included to indicate whether the alloy is binary (two elements, U-Mo) or ternary (three elements, U-Mo-X). For each of the 40 TTT curves, a vector of 11 input descriptors was constructed to represent the alloy composition and the corresponding time-temperature data for the TTT curve.

Feature extraction

A curated dataset comprising 38 distinct alloys and 11 input descriptors was analyzed using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC)28. To remove redundancy, principal component analysis (PCA), a linear dimensionality reduction technique, was applied to the elemental descriptors29–31. The resulting principal components (PCs) are linear combinations of the elemental descriptors, each with distinct weights (loadings). PC1 accounts for the largest variance in the dataset, with subsequent PCs capturing progressively less variance. It is important to note that the ten elemental descriptors remain constant across all temperature-time pairs within a given TTT curve because composition does not change with temperature. Consequently, temperature is included as an additional independent input descriptor, along with PC1, PC2, PC3, and , to capture temperature-dependent transformation kinetics within each TTT curve. Future research could benefit from incorporating thermodynamic descriptors that depend on both composition and temperature, such as the Gibbs energy difference between the α- and γ-phases.

Machine learning with custom loss function

A quantitative relationship between alloy composition and the corresponding TTT curve is established using ML models. A bagging ensemble learning approach is implemented32 with two ML models: (1) least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)33 and (2) support vector regression (SVR)34,35, referred to as eLASSO and eSVR, respectively. The bootstrap resampling method generates multiple replicas of the input data through sampling with replacement36. The non-linear eSVR model with a radial basis function kernel demonstrates superior performance compared to the linear eLASSO model and is therefore selected for subsequent analysis. The eLASSO performance plot for the training and test datasets is provided in Fig. S3 in the SI. The final prediction from the eSVR method is a probability distribution characterized by the ensemble mean and standard deviation. The ensemble comprises 25 bootstrap samples. Bayesian optimization is employed to optimize the hyperparameters, (C, ε ) for the SVR method and (σ ) for the RBF kernel37, by minimizing the ten-fold cross-validation error. The final predictions from the eSVR approach yield a probability distribution for time, given the three principal components (PCs), , and the temperature from the TTT curves. The eSVR code utilizes mean squared error () as the default loss function to assess model performance during hyperparameter optimization. For this study, the loss function is modified as described below to ensure conformity with the characteristic C-curve topology of the TTT curve. The code for the eSVR model is implemented in R. The generalization capability of the trained eSVR models was subsequently assessed using an independent test set and predictions on previously unseen compositions (see “ML model training and performance” and “Testing ML prediction of TTT curves on known alloys”).

Loss function

In ML models, loss functions quantify the difference between predicted and experimentally obtained time values during training and guide the iterative optimization of model parameters. A custom loss function approach is used in this work, where this function is modified. Standard statistical loss functions, such as MSE, RMSE, and MAE, prioritize prediction accuracy but do not account for physical plausibility. To address this limitation, a custom loss function is developed that integrates physics-based regularization terms with prediction error. This approach ensures that predicted TTT curves display physically realistic features, including C-curve morphology and alignment with nucleation theory. This concept is inspired by physics-informed ML approaches, which incorporate domain knowledge from governing physical laws into the learning process using custom loss functions, network architectures, or training constraints38. The loss function for training SVR models comprises three terms: (1) mean squared error , which quantifies prediction accuracy on experimental data; (2) a semi-empirical model regularization term that penalizes deviations from Bhadeshia nucleation-based predictions; and (3) a shape constraint term that enforces the characteristic C-curve topology. Post hoc model explanation

This study employs two types of model-agnostic post hoc explanation techniques to provide insights into the learning patterns of the “black box” eSVR models: (i) global feature importance (GFI) and (ii) partial dependence plots (PDPs).

Global feature importance (GFI)

The goal of GFI is to calculate the importance of individual descriptors relative to others in predicting the output of the trained ML models. GFI is quantified by randomly permuting the values of a specific descriptor across all samples in the dataset, while keeping the values of other descriptors unchanged. This process intentionally breaks the association between the selected descriptor and the target variable40. The trained ML model is then applied to the permuted dataset, and the resulting increase in prediction error indicates the importance of the descriptor. In general, descriptors critical to accurate prediction exhibit large performance drops when permuted, while uninformative descriptors show minimal change. In this study, GFI values are calculated for each input descriptor (PC1, PC2, PC3, , and temperature) to identify which descriptor most strongly influences TTT curve predictions. Partial dependence plots (PDP)

Partial dependence plots (PDPs) characterize the marginal effect of individual descriptors on model predictions by averaging predictions over the distribution of all other descriptors41. To construct PDPs, each of the five input descriptors (PC1, PC2, PC3, , and temperature) is selected and varied across a grid of 50 evenly spaced values spanning their observed ranges in the training data. For each grid value, a simulated dataset is created by setting the target feature to that value for all training samples, while keeping the other features at their original values. The ensemble of trained SVR models then generates predictions for each simulated dataset. The PDP at each grid point represents the mean prediction averaged first across all training samples (marginalizing over other features) and then across all ensemble members. This procedure reveals how the average predicted transformation time varies with each descriptor, independent of the effects of the other descriptors42.

Gaussian process regression (GPR)

The GPR model uses Bayesian inference to learn the distribution of functions, f, that is most likely to have generated the observed data43. This involves computing the posterior distribution of f given the data, using Bayes’ theorem17. The GPR model assumes that the function f is chosen from a Gaussian process i.e. the prior distribution of f is multivariate Gaussian with mean function and covariance function . The covariance function measures the correlation between two points in the input space , and is defined using kernel functions. Once the posterior distribution of f has been learned, the model can make predictions at new test points by computing the posterior predictive distribution. The MATLAB function fitrgp with the Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) squared exponential kernel function, ardsquaredexponential44, with a separate length scale for each predictor, , and signal standard deviation was employed: . Post hoc model explanation

After analyzing model performance and predictions, the trends learned by the four trained eSVR models are examined to assess the impact of loss function modifications. Two model-agnostic post hoc explanation techniques are employed: GFI and PDP. GFI analysis indicates that all five input features (PC1, PC2, PC3, , and temperature) are important across all loss function variants, although their relative rankings vary with different regularization terms (detailed results are shown in Fig. S4 in the SI). Because GFI does not reveal clear trends distinguishing the loss function formulations, the focus shifts to PDPs, which offer more interpretable insights into how each feature influences predicted transformation kinetics and how these relationships change under physics-based constraints.

In Fig. 4, the PDPs for predicted time (hr) versus normalized temperature (C) across all four eSVR models are shown. The overall trend exhibits a U-shaped curve, consistent with expectations for a TTT curve. In Fig. 4a,b, the curves display a U-shape within the intermediate temperature range but deviate at both lower and higher temperature values. Incorporating the term into the loss function corrects this deviation, as demonstrated in Fig. 4c,d.

The absolute loading values across all elemental descriptors are similar for PC1 (see Table S2 in SI). This result indicates that PC1 captures variance related to correlated changes across all elemental properties. Consequently, PC1 does not distinguish the contributions of individual elemental properties. The PDPs shown in Fig. 5a,b reveal that models without the loss term are characterized by a dominant maximum at PC1≈0.5. In contrast, incorporating the loss term alters how the model interprets the role of PC1. Similarly, the PDPs for predicted time versus normalized PC2, which accounts for 3.6% of the variance, are shown in Fig. 6. The curvature in the lower PC2 value range changes when the term is added to the loss function, as shown in Fig. 6c,d. PC2 is primarily influenced by thermal conductivity, polarizability, and the ratio of unit-cell volume to the number of atoms in a unit cell (V/Z), each exhibiting absolute loadings greater than 0.4. Negative loadings for thermal conductivity and polarizability indicate that alloying elements with high values in these properties reduce the overall contribution to PC2. This reduction is favorable, as the PDP plots in Fig. 6c,d demonstrate a delayed nucleation time for the α-phase, thereby enhancing γ-phase stability. Conversely, the V/Z descriptor shows a positive loading, indicating that higher V/Z values contribute more to PC2. Therefore, the competition between thermal conductivity, polarizability and V/Z determines the value of PC2. The PDP plots in Fig. 6c,d further show that elevated PC2 values decrease the nucleation time of the α-phase, consequently reducing γ-phase stability. Further, as seen in Fig. 2a,c, the Mo content decreases in U-Mo alloy series as the PC2 values increases indicating decrease in predicted time and destabilization of γ phase with decreasing Mo content. Additionally, Zr alloyed U-Mo-X alloys also lie in the elevated PC2 range as shown by black points in Fig. 2a,c, indicating lower γ-phase stability as reported in the experimental literature21,47.

Predicted time generally decreases as PC2 increases, with a slight increase observed in the low temperature region for (a) and (b), where the loss function term is absent. PC3 captures 1.5% of the variance and is primarily influenced by thermal conductivity and density with loading values being greater than 0.4 for each. Negative loadings for thermal conductivity and positive loadings for density indicate that they are competing descriptors in determining the final PC3 value. A higher thermal conductivity and lower density will reduce the PC3 value. The key takeaway from the PDP analysis is that enforcing C-curve shape constraints through the term fundamentally alters the learned marginal effects of composition (as represented by PC1, PC2, and PC3) and temperature on transformation kinetics.

Testing ML prediction of TTT curves on known alloys

Following the analysis of loss function modifications in the PDP, the next step is to evaluate which loss function formulation produces the most physically realistic TTT predictions for compositions outside the training set. Four eSVR models generate complete TTT curves for three U-Mo-X ternary alloys: U-9.8Mo-0.2Ni, U-9.8Mo-0.2Cr, and U-9.8Mo-0.2Co. These compositions are excluded from both training and test datasets but have been experimentally characterized in the literature25,48, enabling direct validation of model performance and assessment of the effectiveness of different loss function terms. Peterson et al.25 performed ex-situ neutron diffraction measurements on samples heat-treated at three temperatures (450 C, 500 C, and 525 C) for two durations (20 and 100 h), yielding discrete phase fraction data instead of continuous TTT curves. These results demonstrate that Ni and Co additions accelerate γ-phase decomposition at all tested temperatures, whereas Cr shows transformation kinetics similar to or slightly slower than those of binary U-10Mo. Therefore, model validation assesses whether predicted TTT curves exhibit physically realistic C-curve topology and whether the relative transformation kinetics among the alloys are consistent with these experimental findings.

The predicted TTT curves for the three U-9.8Mo-0.2X alloys (where X = Ni, Cr, and Co), along with the U-10Mo baseline, are shown in Fig. 7. The uncertainties in the time predictions arise from the ensemble learning approach used in this study. Loss functions that exclude the C-curve shape, such as in Fig. 7a and in Fig. 7b, fail to reproduce the characteristic C-shape of the TTT curves. Conversely, Fig. 7c,d, which incorporate the C-curve shape constraint through the term, largely preserve the characteristic shape of the TTT-curves, despite some local irregularities. This comparison indicates that the term is crucial for producing physically plausible TTT predictions on previously unseen compositions. Based on these results, all subsequent analyses use the eSVR model trained with the full loss function ( + + ). Although produces only modest differences compared to + alone, its inclusion ensures that predictions remain consistent with semi-empirical nucleation theory and enforce geometric constraints. The eSVR model trained using the hybrid ++ loss function effectively capture the relative ordering in U-9.8Mo-0.2X system, where X = Ni, Co, and Cr (Fig. 7c). The predicted curves exhibit close similarity, with the primary difference observed in the 500–520 C range. These results indicate that small ternary additions (0.2 wt%) induce modest shifts in TTT behavior rather than substantial changes. Error bars increase at lower temperatures because the training dataset has fewer experimental measurements in this temperature range.

The predicted TTT curves for U-9.8Mo-0.2X (where X= Ni, Cr, Co) from the eSVR models employing (a) , (b) +, (c) +, and (d) + loss functions. Each plot also includes predicted TTT curve for U-10Mo alloy as the baseline for comparison.

The predicted TTT curves for U-10Mo and U-8Mo exhibit inflection points near both the lower and upper bounds of the temperature range (440–560C). This trend can be attributed to two primary factors. (1) Limited data coverage at these temperature extremes in the training dataset reduces model accuracy outside the nose region. (2) The curvature loss term is applied using only a single calibration composition (U-10Mo), which may not sufficiently constrain the C-curve shape for alloys with markedly different transformation kinetics.

The U-Mo-Ti ternary alloy system has been experimentally investigated and compared with the baseline U-10Mo alloy. Experimental results demonstrate that the U-8Mo-Ti alloy reduces the γ-phase stability relative to U-10Mo binary alloys22. The predicted TTT curve for U-8Mo-Ti, obtained using the loss function, is shown in Fig. S8b in the SI. Consistent with experimental observations, the predicted TTT curve shifts leftward, indicating that Ti addition decreases γ-phase stability compared to U-10Mo (see Fig. S7 in the SI) and U-9Mo (see Fig. S8b in the SI) binary alloys. Furthermore, Fig. S8b in the SI shows good agreement between predicted and experimentally measured TTT curves at higher temperatures, while discrepancies increase at lower temperatures. The error bars for the predicted TTT curves are also larger at lower temperatures than at higher temperatures. Due to the inherent experimental variability in TTT measurements across different research groups, the primary value of the current physics-regularized eSVR framework lies in its ability to accurately capture compositional trends in γ-phase stability within the U-Mo-X alloy system, rather than achieving precise quantitative agreement with individual experimental measurements.

The predicted nose times for U-9.8Mo-0.2Ni, U-9.8Mo-0.2Co, and U-9.8Mo-0.2Cr (orange, magenta, and blue squares, respectively) are nearly identical. These three alloys are also predicted to exhibit higher nose temperatures compared to the U-10Mo baseline. In contrast, the predicted nose time for U-8Mo-Ti (green squares) is substantially lower than those predicted for U-9Mo and U-10Mo, as well as the experimental values. Predictions on previously unexplored U-Mo-X alloys

The trained eSVR model with hybrid loss function is employed to predict the TTT curves for 24 U-Mo-X alloys not previously reported in the literature. The investigated alloys have general compositions U-9Mo-X and U-8Mo-2X, where X represents the ternary element. Out of these 24 alloys, 12 of these compositions have X = Ta, Pt, Hf, Ge, Ga and Au and were selected based on their solubility in the γ-phase of uranium, as derived from equilibrium phase diagrams49. Predictions for these 12 alloy compositions, are compared with those for baseline alloys U-8Mo, U-9Mo, and U-10Mo. A representative set of ternary elements (X) in the remaining 12 U-Mo-X alloys was chosen due to their frequent presence as elemental impurities in uranium ore concentrates50. These alloys also have the same general compositions U-9Mo-X and U-8Mo-2X, where X = W, V, Fe, Mg, Cu, and Al. Among these six ternary systems, V-, Fe- and Al-alloyed U-Mo-X compositions have been studied in the literature previously but none of these studies report the TTT curves for the alloys.47,51,52. Storhok et al.47 discussed the effect of ternary and quaternary alloying of Cr, Mo, Nb, Ru, V, and Zr on γ-phase stability of U-Zr, U-Nb, and U-Mo-based alloys and their effect on properties such as the transformation kinetics, transformation temperature, hot hardness, and corrosion resistance; however, no TTT curves were reported. More specifically, addition of V was reported to be detrimental to the γ-phase stability of U-Mo base alloys. Nicholson et al.51 investigated the influence of Fe, Al, Ru, Cr, and Nb alloying on the microstructure of dilute U-Mo-based alloys and estimated the phase ratio for these ternary alloys. Although no TTT curves were provided, the phase ratio was observed to decrease in Fe- and Al-containing U-3.6 (at%)Mo alloys. Slattery and Miller52 measured and compared the creep strength of uranium alloys alloyed with Mo, V, Zr, Cr, Al, and Ti. They evaluated properties including creep moduli, maximum strain rate, and deflection rate for these alloys; however, no TTT curves were reported.

Comparisons between alloys containing X = Ta, Pt, Hf, and Au and the baseline alloys are shown in Fig. 9a,b. Benchmarking the performance of the physics-regularized eSVR model against single-point predictions

A Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model is developed to benchmark the physics-regularized eSVR framework against traditional single-point prediction methods by predicting TTT nose features, particularly the time at the lowest transformation rate. The input to this model is PC1, PC2, PC3, and corresponding to each alloy and the output is the nose TTT time. Temperature is excluded as an input descriptor in this model, unlike in the physics-regularized eSVR model above, where the TTT temperature serves as an input variable. The TTT curve data for 42 alloys presented in the Johns and Burkes paper16 are also utilized in this model. A 90:10 split is used to generate the training and test split comprising of 37 and 5 points, respectively. The automatic relevance determination (ARD) kernel is used for the GPR model and the optimized hyperparameter values are given in Table S3 in the SI. The GPR model performs well on both training and test sets with an value of 0.97 and 0.95, respectively. The parity plot of experimentally measured nose and GPR predicted nose is given in Fig. 10a. The blue dashed line represents the points where experimentally measured and predicted values align. The GPR-predicted nose time closely matches experimentally reported values along the blue dashed line for both the training and test sets. Unlike the previous method by Johns and Burkes, the GPR approach provides associated uncertainty estimates for the predicted TTT nose time, as noted in the paper. To further analyze the relationship between predicted nose time and alloy composition, and to compare these with experimental trends, both experimental and predicted nose times are plotted against varying Mo and Zr content in U-Mo and U-Mo-X alloys in Fig. S10 in the SI. The GPR predictions accurately reflect experimentally observed trends: increasing Mo content systematically extends nose time in binary alloys, whereas Zr additions reduce nose time, indicating accelerated transformation kinetics. This observation aligns with the reported destabilizing effect of Zr on the metastable γ-phase.

Conclusion

This study establishes a quantitative relationship between U-based alloy compositions and their TTT diagrams using physics-regularized ML models. Within a defined temperature range and by representing alloy composition with elemental descriptors, these models achieve strong predictive accuracy for TTT times. After initial dataset curation, PCA is applied to eliminate descriptor redundancy and reduce data dimensionality. To improve generalizability, four variations of the eSVR model loss function are evaluated: (1) baseline mean squared error (MSE), (2) MSE regularized with a semi-empirical nucleation-based model, (3) MSE with shape constraints enforcing characteristic C-curve convexity, and (4) a hybrid approach combining both semi-empirical regularization and shape constraints with MSE. The coefficient of determination () values for training and test datasets decrease as additional terms are added to the baseline MSE loss function, with a notable reduction upon inclusion of the curvature term that enforces the C-shape of the TTT curves. Post hoc model explanations based on PDPs reveal trends learned by the trained models and map elemental descriptors to the predicted time-value trends via PCA loadings. Ultimately, the eSVR model integrating both semi-empirical regularization and shape constraints is used to predict TTT curves for U-9.8Mo-0.2Ni, U-9.8Mo-0.2Cr, U-9.8Mo-0.2Co, and U-8Mo-Ti, none of which are present in the training or test datasets. Models incorporating semi-empirical regularization and curvature-loss function terms, despite lower values, produce C-shaped predicted TTT curves that are compared with experimentally reported trends. The models accurately capture the qualitative trend associated with nose time but systematically overestimate nose temperature. This model is also applied to predict TTT curves for 24 previously unexplored alloys, out of which the ternary elements for 12 of these alloys are selected based on the solubility of the ternary element with the γ-phase uranium, identifying Pt-, Ta-, and Au-alloyed U-Mo compositions as candidates for stabilizing the γ-phase. The ternary elements for the other 12 U-Mo-X alloys are selected based on their frequent presence as elemental impurities in uranium ore concentrates. These predictions indicated that W- and Fe-alloying have a stabilizing effect on the γ-phase. The predicted nose time values for these new alloys obtained with the physics-regularized hybrid model are benchmarked against a GPR model that predicts nose time solely using the MSE loss function. The findings from this study demonstrate the importance of a constraint-based ML framework for preserving the C-shape of predicted TTT curves. Incorporating these constraints improves alignment with experimental results and enables accelerated alloy design through TTT curve predictions. The predictions await experimental validation.
