Strategic analysis of electronic prescriptions in Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Amene Ghaderi, Bahar Shirdel, Sareh Shakerian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to examine the electronic prescription programme in Iran by thoroughly identifying the key strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats (SWOT) associated with its implementation and utilisation among healthcare stakeholders.DesignA thematic analysis was adopted, with data systematically examined through the SWOT analytical framework.SettingHospital, pharmacy, specialised clinic, insurance affiliated clinic in Iran.ParticipantsParticipants consisted of four groups involved in implementing the electronic prescription programme, including physicians, pharmacists, nurses and hospital administrative employees. Moreover, research participants were selected using a purposive sampling approach and sampling continued until data saturation was reached.MethodsSemistructured interviews were employed for data collection. The data analysis steps were systematically executed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis approach. Within the study’s conceptual framework based on SWOT analysis, themes and codes were categorised into positive (strengths, opportunities) and negative (weaknesses, threats) drivers. In addition, the method proposed by Lincoln and Guba was employed to endorse the data credibility.ResultsOverall, data saturation was reached with 25 participants and two major themes and four minor themes. The ‘strengths’ theme comprised patient safety, economic efficiency, information sharing, remote access and drug list accessibility. The ‘weaknesses’ theme encompassed internet and electricity dependency, resistance to implementation, numerous platforms and diverse insurer schemes and frequent changes in insurance company tariffs. The ‘opportunities’ theme consisted of health system networking, clarifying processes, eliminating drug intermediaries, improving health service quality, enhancing data banks, expediting drug monitoring and improving technological infrastructures in the health field. Ultimately, the ‘threats’ theme involved confidential information access and misuse, damaged drug barcodes, incorrect data recording, patient dissatisfaction and government financial burden.ConclusionsDue to the fact that the challenges and benefits associated with this system vary across countries depending on their technical and non-technical capacities and infrastructures, the findings of the current research can contribute to promoting the system within our country.Keywords: eHealth, Prescriptions, Pharmacists

Introduction

Health information technology, particularly electronic prescriptions, is regarded as the foundation of advancements in health service quality.1 According to the definition provided by the Iranian Medical System Organisation, an electronic prescription refers to an ‘electronic document whose all components are individually produced and recorded by an authorized prescriber through authorized systems or software’.2 An electronic prescribing system can be a stand-alone system or merged with an electronic health record (EHR) system.3 The primary motivation for launching an electronic prescription system was to mitigate preventable medical errors by creating software to assess drug interactions and other potential issues.3

A review study by Salmon J W demonstrated that the use of electronic prescribing mitigated medication errors to less than one-seventh of their previous level. Moreover, it resulted in cost savings due to improved patient outcomes.4 Based on Khammarnia’s study exploring the rate of medical errors in Iran between 2005 and 2019, error rates in the medication process, including prescribing, recording and administration, were reported to be 31%, 27% and 35%, respectively.5

A scoping review indicates that e-prescribing offers benefits such as time efficiency, convenience and improved legibility, although barriers like system design and inadequate training persist in primary care settings.6 The results of the other review study indicated that improper implementation of electronic prescribing systems may lead to the emergence of new errors that adversely affect operational effectiveness, escalate medication costs and compromise patient safety.7 To address these challenges, stakeholders suggest implementing forcing functions, prescription tracking technologies and enhanced training, with overall positive experiences reported.6 7

Physicians and pharmacists believe that changes in workflow, familiarity with technology and time commitment may overshadow the potential advantages of electronic prescribing. Hence, to ensure the success of this system, project administrators must monitor factors that contribute to user satisfaction or dissatisfaction.8 In order to extend the adoption of electronic prescriptions among physicians, it is necessary to implement strategies at both the technological and healthcare system levels.8 9

History in Iran

As acknowledged by the Food and Drug Administration, administering an electronic prescription system is a priority for the Iranian Ministry of Health, Treatment and Medical Education. In 2015, a multidisciplinary working group composed of medical informatics experts was formed, and the electronic prescription pilot project was first launched in Yazd province with nine physicians. Gradually, the online prescription issuance plan expanded nationwide. On average, 80% of this plan has been implemented in each province. In 2020, in accordance with clause (a) of Article 74 of the Sixth Development Plan Law, the Ministry of Health was obliged to provide electronic health services. Moreover, all healthcare facilities and associated organisations were mandated to use electronic prescriptions. Additionally, based on Note 17 of the 2020 Budget Law, basic health insurers were obligated to issue electronic prescriptions based on the national ID number, personal information, physician’s medical education number and other required data. As of December 2021, paper prescriptions were no longer accepted. It is worth mentioning that the COVID-19 pandemic was another factor that prompted the Social Security Organisation to eliminate health insurance booklets at that time, giving rise to the recognition of electronic prescriptions as a legal form for prescribing medication. Generally, healthcare centres in Iran use three separate electronic prescribing systems: ‘Electronic Prescription’ and ‘Dinad’ provide services to outpatients covered by the Social Security Organisation and health insurance, and ‘Shafa’ provide services to all inpatients. For individuals not covered by these insurers, physicians resort to paper prescriptions.10

Undoubtedly, the implementation of e-prescription programme entails both benefits and drawbacks across different levels of the health system. SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats) framework is an effective tool for strategic planning and management within organisations. This tool enables managers to assess the capabilities and deficiencies of organisational resources, market opportunities and external threats.11 Due to the fact that the challenges and benefits associated with e-prescribing programme implementation can vary significantly across countries based on geographical location and differing technical and non-technical capacities, it is essential to conduct a comprehensive study of this programme, which is relatively new in Iran. This examination should focus on identifying the specific challenges and obstacles that hinder effective implementation, thereby facilitating targeted improvements and reforms.

Employing SWOT analysis serves as an effective method for assessing the various dimensions of the programme. In this context, the present study was designed and implemented to examine the dimensions of the programme within the framework of SWOT analysis. The insights gained from this analysis can provide valuable guidance to policymakers in enhancing the programme’s effectiveness and overall impact.

Methods

Research setting and participants

The current study was conducted in the city of Saqqez, Kurdistan Province, Iran, in 2023, after obtaining ethical approval and ensuring confidentiality. Kurdistan Province is situated in western Iran, bordering Iraq.12

The study settings included hospitals, clinics and pharmacies that met two inclusion criteria: long-term implementation of the electronic prescription system (at least 2 years) and being under contract with the Social Security Organisation. These settings were selected using maximum variation purposeful sampling. Locations were chosen that would provide access to diverse stakeholders in terms of organisational role (physicians, pharmacists, nurses and programme-related staff), as well as work experience and level of engagement with the system. The aim of this variation was to obtain information-rich cases from different aspects of the electronic prescription system implementation, from the perspective of a wide range of involved actors.

Participants consisted of four groups involved in implementing the electronic prescription system, including physicians, pharmacists, nurses and hospital administrative employees. Moreover, research participants were selected using a purposive sampling approach, and sampling continued until data saturation was reached.13

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and individuals of the public were not engaged in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Research design

The current research was conducted by using qualitative thematic analysis. Qualitative thematic analysis deals with interpreting and reporting obtained from qualitative data. This method employs a back-and-forth method to identify meaning units, codes, subthemes and the main themes.14–16

The conceptual framework of the present study is based on the SWOT framework, as illustrated in figure 1. This framework clearly delineates the negative drivers that can potentially harm the system, which include internal weaknesses as well as external threats beyond the internal environment. Conversely, the positive drivers that contribute to the improvement and enhancement of the system encompass internal strengths and external opportunities. This framework facilitates a more comprehensive analysis of the current situation, aiding in the identification of strengths and weaknesses, and lays the groundwork for leveraging opportunities and countering threats (figure 1).11

Data collection

Semistructured face-to-face interviews were employed for data collection. The interviews took place at the participants’ workplaces after performing necessary coordination and obtaining participants’ informed consent.

Before conducting the interview by AG, to get appropriate answers, the applied research objectives and key concepts (strategic analysis: SWOT) were explained and clarified to the interviewees.

Each interview lasted approximately 45 min to 1 hour. Participants were free to enter and exit the study at any time. In addition, interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ informed consent. To ensure participants’ confidentiality and protect their content, a code was assigned to each participant.

Data analysis

The analysis was conducted based on a conceptual framework developed in alignment with the SWOT approach. This analysis was performed in a recursive manner to ensure that the initial codes were accurately incorporated within the established framework (figure 1). Additionally, the data analysis steps were systematically executed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework, facilitating a comprehensive exploration of the data.14

The coding process was independently performed by three coders in three phases: open coding (ie, line-by-line extraction of initial labels from the transcripts), axial coding (ie, grouping codes into categories and subcategories and identifying inter-relationships) and selective coding (ie, identifying the core category and constructing the final analytical framework). To assess intercoder reliability, 20% of the data (five interviews) was randomly selected. Cohen’s kappa was calculated for each of the three coder pairs and the average was reported as a reliability index. Disagreements were resolved through negotiation sessions among all three coders, referencing the transcripts and theoretical framework, until full consensus was reached.17

Interviews were recorded by the researcher AG, and their texts were manually transcribed verbatim by AG. The first phase (familiarisation with the data) was achieved through multiple readings of the transcripts (SS, AG and BS). In the next phase, the text was organised into meaningful concepts, and initial open codes were extracted from the data, encompassing all concepts. Subsequently, after establishing a deep connection between the text and the codes, initial themes and subthemes were extracted. In this process, the researchers, through an iterative method using an induction and deduction process, sought to identify relationships between concepts and codes, extracting themes at both positive and negative factors the organisation (axial coding). Themes and codes were then placed under the SWOT framework of strengths, weaknesses, threats and opportunities (selective coding). Ultimately, the findings were interpreted. The analysis was conducted manually and using Atlas software V.8. The relationship between codes and themes for each section was presented in tables.

Reliability and validity

The method proposed by Lincoln and Guba was employed in this study to provide and endorse the data credibility.

To elevate credibility, the researcher immersed themselves deeply in the data, taking detailed and comprehensive notes of the interviews. Sufficient time (6 months) was allocated for data collection and interpretation. To enhance transferability, thick description was employed, including a detailed account of the research context, participant characteristics (maximum variation sampling), data collection and analysis procedures, and direct interview excerpts. This allows readers to compare their own context with the studied sample and judge the transferability of the findings.

To ensure dependability, participants with a wide range of experiences, ages, genders and academic backgrounds were selected. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached and the most appropriate semantic unit was identified. Additionally, the recorded interviews were listened to multiple times (by SS, AG and BS). A strong rapport was established with the interviewees, and sufficient time was allocated to this stage. After extracting codes and themes, their accuracy was verified by discussing the extracted themes with several research participants. Reflexivity was examined at three levels: personal, interpersonal and methodological. The interviewer, though employed in healthcare, had no direct involvement with the electronic prescription programme, thus holding no preconceptions that could influence the research. Participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality, which enabled them to express critical opinions freely. Methodologically, an iterative inductive–deductive approach was used alongside Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis to ensure coding validity. Intercoder reliability was assessed using both qualitative and quantitative methods. Finally, the codes were reviewed through member checking and their accuracy was confirmed.18

Discussion

In this study, participants’ perspectives were explored through the SWOT framework, which allowed us to identify both the positive drivers (strengths and opportunities) and the negative drivers (weaknesses and threats). These findings provide a structured basis for discussing the factors that facilitate or hinder the implementation process.

The positive drivers

The theme of strengths

From the perspective of all stakeholders, the most frequent codes explored in this section can be categorised as time-saving and accelerated services, avoid prescribing the wrong drug and prevention of potential misuse of health insurance booklets. Numerous studies have been conducted in this area, and the findings of the current research align with them. Zadeh P E and Bouraghi H also highlighted the key benefits of electronic prescribing, such as mitigated saving healthcare costs and increased prescribing accuracy.7 19 In a systematic review, Osmani F reported a relative risk reduction in medication error rates ranging from 13% to 93%, while Zadeh P E reported a reduction of 83%.7 20

The findings of studies conducted by Bouraghi H, Camacho E M, Boonstra A, Oktarlina R Z, Cusack C M and Ammenwerth E revealed that electronic prescribing has been associated with a reduction in medication errors (such as drug name, dosage and treatment duration).19 21–25

In a study, Lanham A E highlighted innovations in electronic prescribing, there is potential to add value and improve prescribing.26 Lina’s research indicated that 81% of respondents found the electronic prescribing system easy to use, more time-efficient and safer.27 The findings of Tiffany’s study, conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, also demonstrated improvements in the prescribing process, safety and security, and also increased convenience for patients during the pandemic.28

The prescription of medications within the primary care system, particularly in health houses staffed by non-physician personnel, presents significant challenges, including inadequate expertise and high patient volumes, which can contribute to the incidence of medication errors.29 Yazdi F B stated in the scoping review that the adoption of electronic prescribing systems enhances primary healthcare quality by providing access to drug information, reducing human errors and improving patient monitoring and follow-up.6

Despite the considerable advantages of electronic prescribing, inadequate implementation of these systems can generate unintended consequences. Evidence suggests that such circumstances may introduce new categories of errors that diminish workflow efficiency, elevate medication costs and compromise patient safety. While the occurrence of certain unintended mistakes in electronic prescribing appears unavoidable, empirical data indicate that approximately 9% of electronic prescriptions contain medication errors.7 23 30

The theme of opportunities

The most frequent themes associated with the opportunities of electronic prescribing in this study include improved health service quality; the potential for sharing patient records across different departments (networking); remote access to patient information.

The opportunities identified in this study align with numerous pieces of evidence worldwide. In this regard, Ashley has introduced opportunities such as the adoption of new technology, strong competition, an improved image of healthcare and increased health service quality.26 Klinis S reported opportunities gained from the use of an information technology system in the health system, including compatibility with existing systems like mobile devices, remote data access, data storage and data synthesis.31 Furthermore, findings from a number of review studies indicate that demographic disparities in technology adoption and use constitute critical barriers to the successful implementation of e-prescribing systems. This consequently leads to missed implementation opportunities and ultimately undermines the overall effectiveness of such systems.7 9 23

The negative drivers

The theme of weaknesses

The following frequent codes were identified in the study findings: shortcomings in technical infrastructures; the impossibility or disobedience of electronic prescription registration in the private sector and unskilled staff.

The weaknesses observed in the current study are consistent with numerous findings in the literature. In line with the findings of our study, Moghani N B highlighted that the main challenges in implementing electronic prescribing were unstable internet connections and the complexity of multiple insurance schemes.31

Cusack C M’s study findings refer to the levels of physician’s acceptance and resistance.23 Oktarlina R Z considered compatibility with healthcare centres and healthcare workers as major obstacles.22

One of the key findings of this study was the lack of staff proficiency in using electronic prescribing systems and the need for targeted training. This aligns with Boonstra’s research, which reported that electronic prescribing increased medication errors and, in some cases, prolonged patient waiting times due to typing mistakes, system failures and forgotten codes.21 Tiffany also reported that the most significant weakness was the patients’ and medical staff’s requirement for training.27

Moreover, the lack of access to computer systems by some physicians has culminated in an increase in prescriptions for uninsured drugs.21 Finally, Zadeh P E also demonstrated factors contributing to potential errors were categorised into four main groups, including human, technical, interactive and organisational.7

As emphasised by other researchers, increasing physicians’ adoption of such systems requires coordinated interventions at both the technological and health system levels. In particular, user-friendly design and supportive organisational policies are essential to facilitate physicians’ acceptance and adaptation in practice.9 32 In line with this, Lina’s study demonstrated that the majority of respondents considered their EHR systems specifically effective for prescribing medications (88%).26

The theme of threats

Among the most significant issues in this theme are the following subthemes: access to confidential information and the potential misuse of patient data and legal issues and the government financial burden, etc.

Aligning with the present study findings, Ashley’s study results demonstrated that data breaches caused by cyberattacks, poorly designed platforms, data recording errors and financial burdens were among the system’s threats.25 Klinis S identified concerns related to confidentiality and malpractice, system failures, natural and human-made crises, and technological, systemic and equipment-related crises.30 Rasmi and Caitlin’s findings considered the biggest barriers to be related to high costs.22 23

Tiffany delves into the concepts of insecure systems and the criteria by which a system is deemed insecure.27 Warren highlights significant barriers to implementation, including costs, lack of vendor support, patient privacy concerns, system errors and legal issues.3

Among the benefits of the study, several unique findings can be highlighted, including the clarifying processes, reducing tax evasion, eliminating drug intermediaries, effective monitoring of the country’s drug needs.

Through appropriate policymaking and proper programme implementation, challenges such as damaged drug barcodes due to power outages and internet disruptions, numerous tariffs of insurers and multiple platforms are expected to be prevented. Additionally, implementing such measures may significantly enhance service quality and further strengthen stakeholder trust.

Limitations

One of the main limitations of most qualitative studies is the subjective expression, which may make people’s opinions different in the same situation. A person with an average infrastructure may have the same opinion as others in another place with an excellent infrastructure. To more rigorously examine the successful implementation of such programmes and to clarify their dimensions and effectiveness, future research should employ complementary approaches, including systematic reviews, time-series analyses and randomised controlled trials. These methodologies can generate stronger evidence and broader generalisability, thereby supporting more effective policy and practice.

Another limitation is that people’s views were affected by working conditions. So, it is often dependent on the improvement of technical and electrical facilities. This issue caused the interviewees to refrain from expressing the benefits at the national level.

One of the other limitations of this investigation is that it was conducted in a single province, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions. Iran’s geographical, cultural and climatic diversity creates significant variation in socio-economic conditions and technological access. This heterogeneity influences programme implementation, producing different levels of resistance and compliance and leading to context-specific outcomes. Effective implementation requires attention to local infrastructural and cultural factors, supported by regional studies that inform policymakers.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicated that the strengths of the implementation programme comprised reduced prescribing errors, misreading of prescriptions, mitigated drug interactions, drug dosage, facilitated work processes and time saving, rapid and remote access to patient records and previous medications, and connection with other departments and information sharing, while the weaknesses included shortcomings in technical infrastructures, the programme’s internet and electricity dependency, the impossibility or disobedience of electronic prescription registration in the private sector and unskilled staff and the associated challenges with equipment usage, and the presence of multiple platforms and the diversity of insurer schemes. The most frequent codes identified within the ‘opportunities’ theme involved expediting drug monitoring by the organisation and satisfying the country’s drug needs, improving health information networking, helping decrease bureaucracy, clarifying processes, eliminating drug intermediaries and black market, reducing tax evasion, improving digital health data banks and improving the quality of health services, whereas the most critical findings in the ‘threats’ theme were the government financial burden, access to pharmacies’ financial resources, the potential for misuse of patient data and access to confidential information (security, hackers, crimes).

Overall, despite the presence of internal and external factors that may lead to negative outcomes, the programme yielded notable positive results. By distinguishing between internal and external sources of both challenges and benefits, the analysis provides a balanced understanding of the programme’s current position and informs strategies for sustainable improvement. In the realm of developing and implementing electronic prescription infrastructures, it is imperative to foster constructive interactions among all stakeholders and technology providers to create highly acceptable platforms. Similarly, policymakers must be cognisant of their roles in this domain and take effective measures to establish policies aimed at improving health outcomes.
