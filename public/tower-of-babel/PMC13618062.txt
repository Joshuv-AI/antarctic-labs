Anharmonic and surface effects on thermodynamic properties of Ag, Al, and Cu nanostructures

Hua Xuan Dat, Nguyen Thi Thao, Ho Khac Hieu

Abstract

We present a theoretical approach combining the statistical moment method (SMM) and a bonding-energy model to investigate the size-, shape-, and temperature-dependent thermodynamic properties of Ag, Al, and Cu nanostructures. Analytical expressions are derived for the mean square displacement (MSD), thermal expansion coefficient, isobaric heat capacity, and Debye temperature. The results demonstrate that as the characteristic size decreases, the MSD, thermal expansion coefficient, and isobaric heat capacity increase, whereas the Debye temperature decreases. As the size increases, these thermodynamic quantities asymptotically approach their corresponding bulk values. Notably, at high temperatures, we observe pronounced nonlinear variations in nanostructures smaller than 5 nm, highlighting the critical role of anharmonic effects in lattice vibrations. Furthermore, the thermodynamic properties exhibit a strong shape dependence governed by the dimensionless shape factor that quantifies the effect of nanoparticle geometry on the surface-atom fraction. Regular tetrahedral particles show the greatest size-dependent variations due to their high shape factor, while thin films exhibit the lowest. This confirms that the surface area fundamentally drives the thermodynamic behavior of these nanomaterials. This study provides valuable theoretical insights for the design and optimization of nanostructure architectures tailored for specific practical applications.

Introduction

In recent years, metal nanoparticles have become an important research focus in modern materials science due to their superior physical and chemical properties compared to bulk materials. As the material size decreases to the nanometer scale, the surface area-to-volume ratio increases sharply, leading to significant changes in electronic structure, surface energy, and atomic bonding. As a result, metal nanoparticles exhibit many outstanding properties, such as high catalytic activity, strong photoabsorption, superior electrical and thermal conductivity, and good compatibility with various application environments. These properties offer many opportunities for applications in chemical catalysis, biosensors, nanoelectronics, plasmonic materials, heat transfer, energy storage, and biomedicine. However, due to their high surface energy and lower thermal stability compared to bulk materials, metal nanoparticles also have limitations, including agglomeration, surface restructuring, and property degradation at high temperatures.

Among metal nanosystems, silver (Ag), copper (Cu), and aluminum (Al) are materials of particular interest due to their wide application potential and relatively low fabrication costs. Ag nanoparticles stand out with their strong surface plasmon resonance, high antibacterial properties, and good catalytic activity, making them widely used in optical sensors, antibacterial materials, and biomedical applications. Cu nanoparticles have high electrical and thermal conductivity, making them suitable for nanoelectronic components, electrochemical catalysts, and efficient heat transfer materials. Meanwhile, Al nanoparticles have low density, high energy density, and strong reactivity, making them suitable for applications in nanofuels, energy materials, and energy storage technologies. In addition, Ag–Cu nanosystems and metal alloy nanoparticles exhibit superior properties, including structural stability, catalytic activity, and the ability to tune optoelectronic properties through nanoparticle size and shape. The performance and stability of metal nanosystems depend closely on thermodynamic properties such as melting temperature, Debye temperature, specific heat capacity, MSD, and thermal expansion coefficient (TEC), which are strongly influenced by size, shape, and surface effects at the nanoscale.

Many previous studies have shown that the thermodynamic properties of metal nanoparticles depend strongly on particle size and shape. As particle size decreases to the nanometer range, the surface atomic ratio increases rapidly, leading to a decrease in the average bonding energy, which, in turn, results in a significant decrease in the melting temperature, Debye temperature, and consolidation energy of the nanosystem. Based on the modified Gibbs–Thomson model, Ansari showed that the melting temperature, Debye temperature, and specific heat capacity of metal nanoparticles vary strongly with size due to surface energy and finite-size effects. Similarly, Kareem and Mawllod also showed that many thermodynamic quantities, such as entropy, heat capacity, and melting temperature of nanoparticles, change significantly when particle size decreases below a few tens of nanometers. Besides size, the geometric shape of nanoparticles also plays a particularly important role in thermal stability and phase transition mechanisms. Recent studies have shown that spherical, cubic, polyhedral, or wire-shaped nanoparticles have different surface atomic densities and crystal face distributions, leading to significant differences in melting temperature and lattice vibration properties. Delgado-Callico et al. discovered a “universal sign” in the melting of metal nanoparticles, showing that the melting mechanism strongly depends on the shape and surface structure of the nanoparticles. Meanwhile, Ansari developed theoretical models describing the simultaneous dependence of Debye temperature, melting entropy, and melting enthalpy on the size and shape of metal nanomaterials. Recent studies on Ag–Cu nanoclusters also indicate that changes in shape and composition can significantly alter the structural, vibrational, and thermodynamic properties of the nanosystem.

Besides the melting temperature and Debye temperature, other thermodynamic quantities, such as MSD, isobaric heat capacity, and TEC, have also received attention recently. Thermodynamic vibration models show that the specific heat capacity and TEC of nanomaterials generally increase with decreasing size due to increased thermal vibration of surface atoms. In particular, Tam et al. showed that the MSD of nanoparticles increases sharply with decreasing size or with increasing temperature, reflecting increased atomic mobility and decreased structural stability at the nanoscale. In addition, Rigaut and Calvo also demonstrated that the Debye temperature and “breathing mode” vibration of metal nanoparticles strongly depend on the shape and composition of the nanosystem.

Besides theoretical and simulation studies, many experimental works have also confirmed the strong dependence of thermodynamic properties on the size and structure of metal nanoparticles. One of the earliest and most important experimental studies was conducted by Rupp and Birringer, in which the specific heat capacity of crystalline nanomaterials was measured over the temperature range 150–300 K. The results showed that the specific heat capacity of the nanosystem increased significantly compared to that of bulk materials, due to the increased density of vibrational states in the surface region and the influence of low-energy vibrational modes. Subsequently, Qian et al. studied the influence of crystal lattice microstrains on the thermal properties of Cu nanocrystals. Experimental results showed that the presence of microstrains significantly altered the nanosystem's heat capacity and thermal stability by altering the lattice structure and atomic bonding energy. More recently, Bogatyrenko and Kryshtal used in situ TEM diffraction coupled with electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) to determine the TEC of Ag and Cu nanoparticles. The results showed that the TEC of the nanoparticles differed significantly from that of the bulk material and depended strongly on particle size and surface state.

Despite significant advances, most current research still focuses primarily on investigating individual thermodynamic parameters or considering only the influence of size or shape on a specific nanosystem. Some theoretical models are simple and easy to apply, but do not fully capture the simultaneous effects of size, shape, and temperature on nanoparticles' thermodynamic properties. Meanwhile, molecular dynamics simulations, although highly accurate, often require high computational costs and are heavily dependent on the inter-atomic interaction potential. Therefore, developing a theoretical model capable of simultaneously investigating the influence of size, shape, and temperature on the thermodynamic properties of Ag, Cu, and Al nanoparticles remains a scientifically and practically significant problem. In this work, we propose a theoretical model that allows the simultaneous determination of the effects of temperature, shape, and size on the thermodynamic properties of nanoparticles. The main idea is that we develop a new theoretical approach by combining the SMM with a size- and shape-dependent bonding energy model to build a unified computational framework for nanoparticles. In this framework, the simultaneous influence of temperature, size, and shape is incorporated by varying the atomic coordination environment, bonding energy, and lattice vibrational characteristics. On this basis, important thermodynamic properties, including MSD, TEC, isobaric heat capacity, and Debye temperature, are systematically investigated on Ag, Al, and Cu nanoparticles of different sizes and shapes.

It should be noted that the nanoparticle morphologies considered in this work are idealized and prescribed geometries. In real metallic nanoparticles, particularly for tetrahedral and octahedral structures, the morphology may be metastable and can evolve with temperature as surface atomic diffusion becomes activated. Experimental observations on Pt nanoparticles have demonstrated that tetrahedral and octahedral structures can undergo progressive surface reconstruction upon heating and eventually transform toward more stable equilibrium-like morphologies at temperatures below the bulk melting point. Therefore, the present calculations do not describe temperature-induced shape reconstruction. Instead, they describe the temperature dependence of the thermodynamic properties for a given fixed morphology characterized by its shape factor λ. This approximation is appropriate for investigating the intrinsic shape effect within the analytical model, but it should be kept in mind when interpreting the calculated results at elevated temperatures. The present model is not intended to predict the size-dependent equilibrium morphology of metallic nanoparticles. In particular, Marks decahedral and FCC truncated-octahedral motifs, which may compete with icosahedral structures depending on particle size, are not explicitly considered in the present set of prescribed geometries.

Physically, the increase in MSD at small sizes stems from the characteristic surface effect of the nanosystem. As particle size decreases, the surface atomic ratio increases rapidly, reducing the average atomic coordination number and softening lattice vibrations, thereby increasing the atom's thermal vibration amplitude. Comparison with the results of Sadaiyandi shows that the current model fits well in terms of the variation trend and the quantitative value of MSD, especially in the medium- and large-sized regions. Small differences appear in the very small region, which may be related to differences in the bonding-energy model and the way the shape factor is defined. The research results show that MSD is closely related to the thermal stability, atomic diffusion capacity, and melting temperature of metal nanoparticles. This has important implications for the applications of Ag, Cu and Al nanoparticles in catalysis, sensors and nanoelectronic components.

The results also show that TEC depends significantly on nanoparticle shape. For all three materials, tetrahedral structures generally yield the largest TEC values, followed by octahedral and icosahedral structures, while nanowires and nanofilms yield significantly lower TEC values. This reflects differences in the structural stability and the distribution of atomic coordination numbers in each geometry. Polyhedral structures contain many atoms at edges and vertices with low coordination numbers, which degrade local bonding energy and enhance atomic thermal vibrations, thereby increasing the coefficient of thermal expansion. Conversely, in nanowires and nanofilms, the arrangement of atoms along crystal planes is more stable, limiting thermal vibrations and reducing the system's thermal expansion. Physically, the increase in TEC at small sizes mainly stems from the surface effects characteristic of nanomaterials. As particle size decreases, the proportion of surface atoms increases rapidly, reducing the average atomic coordination number and weakening the effective atomic bonds. This leads to a softening of lattice vibrations and an increase in the amplitude of atomic thermal vibrations, thereby increasing the thermal expansion of the nanosystem. For very small particles, atoms at edges, vertices, and surfaces vibrate significantly more strongly than atoms in the crystal core due to their unsaturated bonding environment and lower coordination number. In addition, Al exhibits a larger TEC than Ag and Cu under the same size conditions because it has weaker atomic bonds and a lower Debye temperature, leading to stronger atomic thermal vibrations. Comparison with experimental results shows that the current model reproduces the trend in TEC variation with nanoparticle size and shape for Cu nanoparticles, especially in the medium- and large-size regions. However, significant differences remain in the very small size range below about 5–10 nm, especially for Ag nanoparticles across the entire investigated region. The results in were investigated at 300 K, so the influence of anharmonic oscillations does not dominantly govern the nanosystem. Therefore, the difference between theory and experiment in this case mainly stems from structural factors and model limitations, rather than from strong anharmonic effects. First of all, most theoretical models assume that nanoparticles have an ideal crystal structure, a perfect geometric shape and uniform atomic distribution. These factors can significantly alter the local atomic coordination number and bond stiffness at the nanosurface. In addition, the difference between theory and experiment may also be related to experimental conditions such as lattice defects, deformation, surface state, or interactions between nanoparticles and the surrounding environment. Meanwhile, the current theoretical model assumes that nanoparticles are free, have an ideal crystal structure, and does not fully consider these external influences. This is an important reason for the difference between theoretical and experimental results in the very small region.

The calculated results are compared with previous theoretical models. It can be seen that, for all three materials, the Debye temperature decreases sharply as the particle size decreases to a few nanometers and gradually converges to bulk material values as the size increases. This trend becomes particularly pronounced in the size range below approximately 10 nm, where the slope of the curves changes significantly due to the dominant influence of surface effects on lattice vibrations and atomic thermal dynamics. A noteworthy feature is that the Debye temperature depends strongly on the nanoparticles' geometry. The theoretical curves indicate that nanofilms and nanowires consistently exhibit the highest Debye temperatures, followed by octahedral and tetrahedral structures, while spherical and icosahedral structures exhibit lower Debye temperatures. For Cu and Al, this difference is particularly pronounced in the sub-5 nm size region, where the Debye temperature of nanofilms is significantly higher than that of spherical structures. This reflects the inherent difference in lattice vibrational stiffness between nanostructures with different anisotropies. Physically, the Debye temperature is directly related to the effective lattice stiffness and the system's characteristic vibrational frequency. As particle size decreases, the average atomic coordination number decreases due to a sharp increase in the surface atom ratio, reducing the effective bonding energy between atoms. This leads to lattice vibrational softening and a decrease in the characteristic vibrational frequency, thereby reducing the nanoparticle's Debye temperature. However, the degree of this decrease depends strongly on the nanoparticle's shape.

For anisotropic structures such as nanofilms and nanowires, atoms maintain a periodic arrangement along preferred crystal orientations, thereby preserving atomic bond stiffness along certain vibrational directions. Therefore, these structures exhibit considerably higher Debye temperatures than near-spherical or polyhedral nanoparticles, which contain a larger fraction of atoms at edges and vertices. In contrast, regular icosahedral and spherical nanoparticles possess a high density of low-coordinated surface atoms, particularly at edges, vertices, and highly curved surface regions, whose proportion increases markedly with decreasing particle size. These atoms experience unsaturated bonding environments and reduced coordination numbers, resulting in enhanced thermal vibrations and local lattice softening. Consequently, the effective bond stiffness is weakened, leading to a reduction in the Debye temperature. The present model accounts for the dominant average effect of this non-bulk coordination through the shape-dependent effective bonding energy, but does not explicitly resolve the distinct FCC/HCP/twin local environments or the associated fivefold strain energy. This reduces the Debye temperature of the nanosystem compared to structures with higher symmetry and stronger bonds. Comparison with previous theoretical results shows that the current model reproduces both the variation trend and the quantitative value of the Debye temperature quite well. The results are in particularly good agreement with the models of Xiong et al. and Zhang et al. in the medium- and large-size regions. However, in the very small region below about 3 nm, certain discrepancies still exist between the models. Therefore, more advanced experimental techniques will be needed in the future to verify the above results.

The main reason stems from differences in the way surface bonding energy, average atomic coordination number, and the influence of geometric shape in each theoretical model. Some previous models used the isotropic oscillation assumption and therefore did not fully describe the influence of phonon anisotropy in low-dimensional structures such as nanowires and nanofilms. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations by Fedorov et al. show that surface atomic restructuring and local vibrations at the boundary region can significantly affect the heat capacity of very small nanoparticles, while analytical thermodynamic models often do not fully account for these effects. Therefore, further experimental results are needed to verify these theoretical models.

More broadly, previous atomistic studies have shown that the finite-temperature behavior of Ag and Cu clusters and Ag–Cu nanoalloys may involve competition between different structural motifs, structural transformations, chemical ordering, and thermally induced rearrangements. These studies provide complementary atomistic insight into nanoscale thermodynamics and structural evolution, whereas the present analytical treatment focuses on the systematic dependence of MSD, TEC, isobaric heat capacity, and Debye temperature on size, morphology, and temperature for prescribed nanostructures.

Conclusion

In this work, we formulated a theoretical framework combining the SMM with a bonding energy model to study the thermodynamic properties of Ag, Al, and Cu nanoparticles. We have derived analytical expressions for quantities such as MSD, TEC, isobaric heat capacity, and Debye temperature as functions of shape, size, and temperature. This formalism was systematically applied to nanoparticles ranging from 2 nm to 40 nm, 100 K ≤ T ≤ 1000 K across diverse morphologies, including spheres, regular tetrahedra, octahedra, icosahedra, nanowires, and thin films.

The results show that the thermodynamic properties of nanoparticles strongly depend on size, shape, and temperature. Specifically, as the particle size decreases, the MSD, TEC, and isobaric heat capacity exhibit a pronounced increase, whereas the Debye temperature correspondingly decreases. As particle size increases, the values of thermodynamic properties gradually approach those of bulk materials. At high temperatures, anharmonic effects significantly influence the thermodynamic properties of nanoparticles, especially MSD and TEC. This indicates that surface and anharmonic effects in lattice vibrations are indispensable for investigating the thermodynamic properties of nanoparticles smaller than 5 nm. Morphologically, the thermodynamic properties exhibit the strongest size-dependence in regular tetrahedral nanoparticles due to their high surface-to-volume ratio, whereas thin films demonstrate the weakest dependence. The robust agreement of our SMM calculations with both MD simulations and experimental data confirms the validity and reliability of our theoretical approach.

From an application-oriented perspective, the present results provide theoretical guidance for tailoring the thermal behavior of metallic nanostructures through control of size and morphology. The predicted variations in isobaric heat capacity may be useful for identifying structures with different heat-storage characteristics, while the size- and temperature-dependent TEC and MSD provide information relevant to thermal expansion and atomic thermal motion. These quantities alone do not determine the performance of a specific device, which also depends on thermal transport, interfaces, mechanical stability, and environmental conditions. Therefore, the present framework is best regarded as a low-cost theoretical screening tool for selecting candidate nanoparticle sizes and morphologies prior to detailed atomistic simulations and experimental evaluation.
