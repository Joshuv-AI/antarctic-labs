Distinct intracellular and extracellular maturation of goose astrovirus 2 capsid protein and the potential of virus-like particles as a novel subunit vaccine
Authors: Anping Wang, Zhi Wu, Li Liu, Qingkang Zhou, Huipeng Lu, Yuting Cheng, Wenfeng Jia, Rui Zhu, Shanyuan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Goose astrovirus 2 (GAstV-2) is the primary causative agent of gosling gout in China, causing severe economic losses, yet no commercial vaccines are available. Virus-like particles (VLPs) represent a promising subunit vaccine platform, but the capsid maturation mechanism and VLP immunogenicity of GAstV-2 remain unknown. Herein, we expressed the GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein utilizing the baculovirus/insect cell system and systematically characterized its intracellular and extracellular maturation dynamics. Western blot analysis with domain-specific antibodies revealed distinct maturation pathways: intracellular processing yielded predominantly mature core (38/42 kDa) and spike (30/34 kDa) proteins, whereas extracellular products retained more precursor proteins (90/75 kDa) and processing intermediates. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the ORF2 protein self-assembled into morphologically intact ~30 nm VLPs, with intracellular VLPs (iVLPs) associated with membranous structures and extracellular VLPs (eVLPs) containing additional ~10 nm ring-like assembly intermediates. Notably, although iVLPs and eVLPs exhibited distinct protein compositions, both preparations triggered robust antibody production and neutralizing activity comparable to the inactivated vaccine. VLPs elicited a balanced Th1/Th2 cellular response, marked by elevated IL-4 and IFN-γ levels, whereas the inactivated vaccine primarily induced a Th2-type response. Upon viral challenge, both iVLPs and eVLPs significantly reduced viral shedding with protective efficacy equivalent to the inactivated vaccine. This study elucidates the differential maturation mechanism of the GAstV-2 capsid protein and establishes a VLP-based platform that provides a promising vaccine candidate for the control of gosling gout.

Introduction

Goose astrovirus (GAstV) is a non-enveloped, single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus of the Avastrovirus genus, with virions of approximately 30 nm displaying a star-like morphology (1). Since 2015, an infectious disease characterized by visceral and articular gout in goslings has broken out in multiple goose-rearing regions of China, causing substantial economic losses. Pathogenic studies have demonstrated that this disease is primarily caused by GAstV (2, 3). GAstV comprises two distinct genotypes, GAstV-1 and GAstV-2, which exhibit marked divergence at the genetic level, sharing merely 57.9% nucleotide sequence identity and only 42.6% homology in their open reading frame 2 (ORF2)-encoded capsid protein (4–6). In recent years, GAstV-2 has emerged as the predominant strain responsible for gosling gout outbreaks in China and has become the primary focus of disease control efforts (7, 8).

Currently, no commercial vaccines are available for GAstV, and control strategies rely mainly on biosecurity measures. However, GAstV displays strong environmental resistance and can be vertically transmitted (9, 10), rendering these measures insufficient. Conventional inactivated and live attenuated vaccine development has been hindered by the difficulty of cultivating GAstV in vitro, as the virus replicates poorly in cell lines and relies primarily on goose embryos (11). Therefore, developing novel vaccine strategies independent of virus culture has become an urgent priority. Virus-like particles (VLPs) are nanoscale hollow structures formed by self-assembly of viral structural proteins, containing no viral genetic elements. These particles preserve authentic conformational epitopes of the source virus and potently elicit both humoral and cellular immune responses (12, 13). Research on astrovirus VLPs remains relatively limited. Early studies utilized the baculovirus system to prepare human astrovirus type 1 (HAstV-1) VLPs (14), and another study employed a poxvirus system to express HAstV-2 VLPs (15). Our laboratory previously prepared GAstV-1 VLPs for the first time, demonstrating induction of high antibody levels and inhibition of viral shedding (16). However, research on GAstV-2 VLPs remains absent.

Successful VLP construction depends on understanding of the maturation mechanism of viral structural proteins. The astrovirus capsid protein is the sole structural component determining antigenicity, receptor recognition, and induction of neutralizing antibodies (17). Post-translational processing differs markedly among astroviruses. Classical HAstVs undergo multi-step maturation: an intracellular caspase-mediated cleavage converts the 90 kDa precursor (VP90) into a 70 kDa intermediate (VP70), followed by extracellular trypsin digestion that yields the mature structural components VP34 (34 kDa, core protein) and VP27 (27 kDa, spike protein) (18–20). In contrast, the HAstV MLB clade produces a spike protein of approximately 30 kDa (21), whereas HAstV VA1 accomplishes full processing of its capsid proteins entirely within the intracellular compartment without reliance on extracellular trypsin, thereby generating core protein VP33 and spike protein VP38 (22, 23). These examples demonstrate that even within the same virus family, maturation mechanisms and structural protein characteristics are highly diverse. Research on the maturation processing of avian astrovirus (AAstV) capsid proteins remains limited. Structural analysis of the turkey astrovirus type 2 (TAstV-2) spike protein revealed marked differences in its core fold compared to that of HAstV-8 (24, 25), suggesting that the structural characteristics of AAstV capsid proteins differ from those of HAstVs. Our previous research on GAstV-1 identified mature core protein at approximately 40/43 kDa and spike protein at approximately 25/27 kDa (16). However, the GAstV-2 capsid maturation mechanism is entirely uncharacterized, including the processing pattern of the ORF2 precursor, the molecular weights of mature core/spike domains, and the subcellular localization of processing.

Therefore, we utilized the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus/insect cell platform to express GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein, systematically analyzed its expression profile, proteolytic processing dynamics, and self-assembly capability into VLPs. Using domain-specific antibodies, we characterized the protein composition and maturation status of intracellular and extracellular VLPs. Finally, we evaluated the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of these VLPs in goslings. This study aims to establish a technical platform for the generation of GAstV-2 VLPs, elucidate the maturation processing mechanism of its capsid protein, and lay the groundwork for advancing subunit vaccine candidates against this pathogen.

Materials and methods

Cells, virus, protein and antibodies

Chicken hepatocellular carcinoma (LMH) cells were cultured in LMH-specific complete medium (HyCyte, Beijing, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Sf9 insect cells were maintained in Sf-900™ II SFM medium (Gibco, United States) at 27 °C in static or suspension culture (150 r/min). The GAstV-2 YC20 strain (GenBank: MW536497) was propagated in 11-day-old goose embryos (26). The viral titer was determined as 104.5 TCID50/mL in LMH cells. Full-length GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein was prokaryotically expressed and purified to 100 μg/mL as described (27). Mouse monoclonal antibody (mAb) A10D12 specific to GAstV-2 ORF2 was prepared in our laboratory. The B-cell linear epitope recognized by mAb A10D12 is located at amino acid residues 36–45 (36YKPQKLPMKA45) (27).

Construction of recombinant GAstV-2 baculovirus

The complete ORF2 gene was amplified using primers G2-ORF2-F (5’-TAAGGATCCATGGCAGACAGGGCGGTGGC-3′) and G2-ORF2-R (5’-ATACTCGAGTCACTCATGTCCGCCCTTCTC-3′), and inserted into pFastBac1. Recombinant bacmid was generated in DH10Bac and transfected into Sf9 cells to produce recombinant baculovirus rBac-GAstV2-ORF2, which was amplified to high-titer P3 working stock. Viral titers were quantified through plaque formation assays.

Preparation of polyclonal antibodies

Primers targeting the ORF2 core domain (amino acids 80–402) and spike domain (amino acids 424–666) of GAstV-2 YC20 were designed: G2-core-F (5′-GCTGATATCGGATCCGAATTCGGGAATTCAACAGACACACTCGA-3′), G2-core-R (5′-TTGTCGACGGAGCTCGAATTCTGTATTCACATTTGGCTGTGTTAGC-3′), G2-spike-F (5′-GCTGATATCGGATCCGAATTCATGCAGGTTACTCCCTCGCTT-3′), and G2-spike-R (5′-TTGTCGACGGAGCTCGAATTCGTCAGAGGTCTTGAGCGAGACTG-3′). Target fragments were amplified, inserted into pET-30a, and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) with 0.5 mmol/L IPTG induction at 27 °C. Recombinant proteins were purified by Ni-NTA chromatography (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), formulated with Freund’s adjuvant, and used to immunize BALB/c mice (30 μg/dose, 2-week intervals, three doses). Anti-core polyclonal antibodies (pAb α-core) and anti-spike polyclonal antibodies (pAb α-spike) were collected 1 week after the final boost (Figure 1A).

Evaluation of GAstV-2 ORF2 protein expression in sf9 cells

Sf9 cells were infected with rBac-GAstV2-ORF2 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 and incubated at 27 °C with shaking (150 r/min). At 3–7 days post-infection (dpi), culture supernatant was harvested by centrifugation (3,000 × g, 15 min) and ultracentrifuged (200,000 × g, 2 h) to obtain extracellular fraction. Cell pellets were washed, subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles, and centrifuged (15,000 × g, 10 min) to obtain intracellular soluble fraction. Both fractions were analyzed by Western blot (WB) using pAb α-core, pAb α-spike, and mAb A10D12.

Production of GAstV-2 virus-like particles

Sf9 cells were infected with rBac-GAstV2-ORF2 (MOI = 5). At 4 dpi, culture supernatant was harvested by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min) and ultracentrifuged (200,000 × g, 2 h) for extracellular VLPs (eVLPs). Cell pellets were washed, disrupted by freeze–thaw, and centrifuged (15,000 × g, 10 min); the soluble lysate was retained for intracellular VLPs (iVLPs). Both fractions were layered onto 20–60% sucrose gradients and ultracentrifuged (200,000 × g, 3 h). Gradient fractions were collected, diluted with PBS, and pelleted (200,000 × g, 3 h) to obtain purified iVLPs and eVLPs. Protein concentration was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. As a control, GAstV-2 YC20 virions from allantoic fluid were processed in parallel using identical methodology.

Western blot and indirect immunofluorescence assay

For WB, samples were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with specific antibodies (mAb A10D12 at 1:1,000, pAb α-core at 1:100, pAb α-spike at 1:100), followed by HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, United States). For IFA, infected cells were fixed with methanol, blocked with 5% skim milk, and stained with pAb α-spike (1:1,000) and FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:200; Jackson ImmunoResearch).

Electron microscopy analysis

Sf9 cells were infected with rBac-GAstV2-ORF2 at an MOI of 5 and maintained at 27 °C for 72 h. Infected cells were fixed with glutaraldehyde/osmium tetroxide, embedded in Epon 812, sectioned (70 nm), and stained with uranyl acetate/lead citrate. For negative staining, purified VLPs and native virions were stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid and examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Animal experiment

Sixty one-day-old goslings (GAstV-2 antigen/antibody negative) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 15): iVLPs, eVLPs, β-propiolactone-inactivated GAstV-2 virions (positive control), or PBS (negative control). Goslings were immunized intramuscularly (10 μg protein emulsified with MONTANIDE ISA 206 adjuvant) at 3 days of age and boosted at 14 days. Serum samples were collected weekly. Fourteen days post-booster, all birds were challenged orally with 0.3 mL GAstV-2 YC20 allantoic fluid. Cloacal swabs were collected every 3 days until 15 days post-challenge. Viral RNA was extracted from cloacal swabs and quantified by qPCR targeting the ORF1b gene as described (28).

Serological assays

GAstV-2-specific antibodies were detected by indirect ELISA (iELISA) using recombinant ORF2 protein or purified GAstV-2 virions as coating antigens. Neutralizing antibodies were measured by virus neutralization test (VNT) in LMH cells using the Reed-Muench method. Serum IFN-γ and IL-4 levels were quantified using commercial goose-specific ELISA kits (Milbio, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were carried out with GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., United States). Values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05.

Results

Successful expression and proteolytic processing of GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein in sf9 cells

To generate rBac-GAstV2-ORF2, the complete ORF2 gene of GAstV-2 YC20 strain was inserted into pFastBac1. IFA results (Figure 1B) showed distinct specific green fluorescence in the cytoplasm of infected cells, confirming efficient capsid protein expression. WB analysis (Figure 1C) using pAb α-spike detected multiple specific bands in the culture supernatant of infected cells, with molecular weights of approximately 90 kDa, 75 kDa, 70 kDa, 45 kDa, and 34 kDa. In the cell lysate, the predominant bands were approximately 60 kDa, 34 kDa, and 30 kDa. No corresponding bands were detected in control cells. Given that the theoretical molecular weight of full-length ORF2 protein is approximately 90 kDa, these results indicate that the expressed ORF2 precursor protein underwent extensive proteolytic processing both intracellularly and extracellularly.

Differential intracellular and extracellular maturation processing of the GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein

To characterize the maturation dynamics of ORF2 protein, expression products in culture supernatants (sup) and soluble cell lysates (sol) were analyzed using domain-specific antibodies: mAb A10D12 targeting the N-terminal linear epitope (aa 36–45), pAb α-core targeting the core domain, and pAb α-spike targeting the spike domain (Figure 1A).

WB analysis with mAb A10D12 revealed distinct spatiotemporal features in ORF2 protein processing. In the sup, a predominant band of approximately 90 kDa was detectable from 3 dpi. At 4 dpi, an additional band of approximately 75 kDa appeared. By 5 dpi, the 90 kDa band was almost absent, with only a faint 75 kDa band remaining. No A10D12-reactive bands were detected in the sol at any time point (Figure 2A). As the A10D12 epitope resides in the N-terminal region of ORF2, these results indicate that the N-terminal region undergoes gradual extracellular hydrolysis but is rapidly cleaved or sequestered intracellularly.

Sf9 cells were infected with the recombinant baculovirus rBac-GAstV2-ORF2 at a multiplicity of infection of 5. Culture supernatants (sup) and soluble cell lysates (sol) were collected daily from 3 to 7 days post-infection (dpi) and analyzed by Western blot using three domain-specific antibodies. (A) Detection with monoclonal antibody A10D12 targeting the N-terminal linear epitope (aa 36–45) of ORF2. (B) Detection with α-core polyclonal antibody targeting the core domain. (C) Detection with α-spike polyclonal antibody targeting the spike domain.

Analysis using pAb α-core showed a time-dependent processing pattern in the sup. At 4 dpi, a predominant band of approximately 70 kDa was observed. By day 5, this band was accompanied by a series of diffuse bands ranging from 42 to 70 kDa. In contrast, the sol exhibited clear predominant bands of approximately 38 and 42 kDa at 5–6 dpi (Figure 2B). These results indicate that extracellular processing generates partially digested core-containing intermediates, whereas intracellular processing yields fully matured core proteins through more precise proteolytic cleavage.

Detection with pAb α-spike revealed persistent bands of approximately 30 and 34 kDa in the sup from 3 dpi onwards, along with larger intermediates (38–60 kDa). In the sol, 30/34 kDa bands were the main products at 3–4 dpi, expanding to 38–75 kDa by 5 dpi, with no signal detected at 7 dpi (Figure 2C). These results suggest that the spike domain is cleaved early intracellularly into stable products, whereas extracellularly it remains associated with incompletely processed precursors.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein follows markedly different maturation pathways intracellularly versus extracellularly, yielding mature core proteins of 38/42 kDa and spike proteins of 30/34 kDa.

Self-assembly of the GAstV-2 ORF2 protein into virus-like particles

To verify VLP formation, infected sf9 cells and culture products were examined by TEM. In the cytoplasm of infected cells, numerous round, electron-dense particles of approximately 30 nm were observed. These particles were morphologically similar to native astroviruses and often clustered around intracellular membranous structures (Figure 3A), suggesting that VLP assembly is associated with the endomembrane system. No such particles were found in uninfected cells. Analysis of purified products revealed that iVLPs (derived from cell lysate supernatant) contained morphologically uniform particles of approximately 30 nm in diameter (Figure 3B). In contrast, eVLPs (from culture supernatant) contained both 30 nm particles and distinct ring-like structures approximately 10 nm in diameter (Figure 3C), potentially representing assembly intermediates. These results demonstrate that GAstV-2 ORF2 protein efficiently self-assembles into morphologically intact VLPs in the baculovirus/insect cell system.

Structural protein composition of GAstV-2 VLPs

To characterize the protein composition and maturation status of GAstV-2 VLPs, purified iVLPs, eVLPs, and native GAstV-2 virions were analyzed by WB using mAb A10D12, pAb α-core, and pAb α-spike. WB detection with mAb A10D12 showed that eVLPs contained bands of approximately 90 and 75 kDa consistent with native virions, whereas no bands were detected in iVLPs (Figure 4A). This result indicates that the N-terminal region is partially or completely removed in iVLPs, but retained in eVLPs as intact or processed intermediates. Detection with pAb α-core revealed a clear band of approximately 38 kDa in iVLPs matching native virions, while eVLPs showed a predominant band of approximately 70 kDa with fainter 38/45 kDa bands (Figure 4B). These results demonstrate that iVLPs contain fully processed core proteins resembling mature virions, whereas eVLPs retain largely unprocessed or partially processed precursors. Detection with pAb α-spike showed that both iVLPs and eVLPs contained specific bands of approximately 30 and 34 kDa along with 90/75 kDa precursor bands, indicating that the spike domain is correctly assembled. Notably, native virions exhibited slight molecular weight differences (31/36 kDa) compared to VLPs (Figure 4C), possibly reflecting variations in post-translational modifications or protease cleavage precision between insect cells and goose host cells.

Purified intracellular VLPs (iVLPs, derived from cell lysates), extracellular VLPs (eVLPs, derived from culture supernatants), and native GAstV-2 virions (purified from goose embryo allantoic fluid) were analyzed by Western blot using three domain-specific antibodies. (A) Detection with monoclonal antibody A10D12 targeting the N-terminal linear epitope (aa 36–45) of ORF2. (B) Detection with polyclonal antibody α-core targeting the core domain. Collectively, these results indicate significant differences in structural protein composition and maturation status between iVLPs and eVLPs. iVLPs predominantly contain mature core protein (38 kDa) and fully cleaved spike protein (30/34 kDa) without N-terminal epitopes, closely resembling native virions in core protein maturity. eVLPs retain more unprocessed precursors (90/75 kDa) with incomplete core protein processing, yet maintain N-terminal integrity similar to native virions.

GAstV-2 VLPs induce robust humoral immune responses in goslings

To assess the immune response elicited by GAstV-2 VLPs, goslings were immunized with purified iVLPs, eVLPs, inactivated GAstV-2 virions (positive control), or PBS (negative control), followed by a booster immunization at 14 days (Figure 5A). GAstV-2-specific antibodies were measured by iELISA using recombinant ORF2 protein or purified virions as coating antigens, and neutralizing titers were determined by VNT. When the prokaryotically expressed ORF2 recombinant protein was used as the coating antigen (Figure 5B), the serum OD450 values in all three immunized groups were significantly higher than the negative control group at 7 days post-primary immunization (p < 0.001), with no significant differences among the three groups (p > 0.05). Antibody levels in all three immunized groups increased further post-booster, with no statistically significant difference compared to pre-booster levels or among groups (p > 0.05). Results using purified virions as coating antigen (Figure 5C) were highly consistent, confirming the reliability of both detection systems. VNT results demonstrated that neutralizing antibody titers in the iVLP, eVLP, and inactivated virus groups were comparable at both 7 days post-primary and 7 days post-booster immunization, with no significant differences among groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 5D). All three immunogens elicited markedly elevated neutralizing responses following the booster dose. These results demonstrate that both iVLPs and eVLPs induce robust specific humoral immune responses in goslings, with antibody levels and neutralizing activity comparable to those induced by the traditional inactivated vaccine. No significant difference in humoral immunogenicity was observed between the two VLP preparations.

GAstV-2 VLPs elicit a Th1/Th2 mixed-type cellular immune response in goslings

To evaluate the cellular immune responses induced by GAstV-2 VLPs, the serum concentrations of IL-4 and IFN-γ were measured using goose-specific ELISA kits. IL-4 levels (Figure 6A) were significantly higher in all three immunized groups than the negative control at 14 days post-primary immunization (p < 0.01), with no significant differences among immunized groups (p > 0.05). IL-4 levels in all immunized groups increased further post-booster, remaining comparable among groups (p > 0.05). These results indicate that iVLPs, eVLPs, and inactivated virus all effectively induce Th2-type immune responses with comparable potency. IFN-γ levels (Figure 6B) showed no significant differences between the three immunized groups and the negative control group at 14 days post-primary immunization (p > 0.05). However, at 14 days post-booster, iVLP and eVLP groups exhibited significantly elevated IFN-γ compared to the negative control (p < 0.01), whereas the inactivated virus group showed only a slight, non-significant increase (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that both VLP preparations effectively induce Th1-type immune responses following booster immunization, while the traditional inactivated vaccine has limited capacity to elicit Th1-type responses. Collectively, these results demonstrate that GAstV-2 iVLPs and eVLPs simultaneously activate Th1-type (IFN-γ) and Th2-type (IL-4) cellular immune responses, exhibiting a balanced Th1/Th2 mixed-type immune profile, in contrast to the Th2-biased response induced by the traditional inactivated vaccine.

Cytokine responses induced by GAstV-2 VLPs in goslings. (A,B) Concentrations of interleukin-4 (IL-4) (A) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (B) were measured using goose-specific ELISA kits. Serum samples were measured from immunized goslings at 14 days and 28 days post immunization. Data are presented as mean cytokine concentrations ± SD. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

GAstV-2 VLPs confer effective immune protection against viral challenge in goslings

To evaluate the protective efficacy of GAstV-2 VLPs, all goslings were challenged orally with GAstV-2 YC20 strain at 14 days post-booster immunization. Because GAstV-2 primarily causes severe gout and mortality in goslings within 2 weeks of age, the goslings in this study were already 1 month old at the time of challenge, which is within the tolerance period for GAstV-2. Consequently, no typical gout symptoms or deaths were observed in any group, including the negative control. qPCR results (Figure 7) showed that viral loads in cloacal swabs from the negative control group increased significantly at 3 dpc, peaked at 6 dpc, and declined slowly, with viral shedding still detectable at 15 dpc. In contrast, low-level viral shedding was detected in the iVLP, eVLP, and inactivated virus groups only at 3–6 dpc, with viral loads significantly lower than the negative control at all-time points (p < 0.001). By 9 dpc, viral loads in all three immunized groups were almost undetectable. No significant differences were observed among the three immunized groups at any time point (p > 0.05). These results demonstrate that both iVLPs and eVLPs significantly inhibit GAstV-2 replication and shedding, effectively shortening the duration of viral shedding, with protective efficacy comparable to the traditional inactivated vaccine.

Discussion

This study represents the first systematic characterization of the expression profile, maturation processing dynamics, and VLP self-assembly capacity of the GAstV-2 ORF2 capsid protein in a baculovirus-driven insect cell platform. Through immunization and challenge studies in goslings, we validated that the resultant VLPs exhibit robust immunogenicity and effective protective efficacy against GAstV-2 infection. The results reveal that the GAstV-2 ORF2 protein, successfully expressed in sf9 cells, undergoes extensive post-translational proteolytic processing via markedly distinct intracellular and extracellular maturation pathways. Specifically, intracellular processing yields predominantly mature core (38/42 kDa) and spike (30/34 kDa) protein forms, whereas extracellular products retain a higher abundance of precursor proteins (90/75 kDa) alongside various proteolytic intermediates. Notably, despite the significant differences in structural protein composition and maturation status between iVLPs and eVLPs, both VLP preparations induced high levels of humoral immune responses and Th1/Th2 mixed-type cellular immune responses in goslings, significantly inhibiting viral shedding after challenge, with protective efficacy comparable to that of the traditional inactivated GAstV-2 vaccine.

The maturation processing mechanisms of astrovirus capsid proteins are known to be highly divergent across the family (29, 30), and our findings further enrich this diversity map. Previous studies showed that turkey astrovirus spike structure differs from human astrovirus (24). Similarly, we found that GAstV-2 and GAstV-1 also differ: their core proteins are similar in size (38/42 kDa vs. 40/43 kDa), but GAstV-2 spike protein (30/34 kDa) is larger than GAstV-1 spike (25/27 kDa) (16). This mirrors the difference between classical human astroviruses (VP27) and VA-type strains (VP38), suggesting that genetic diversity among astrovirus genotypes is primarily concentrated within the spike domain. These results show that even closely related astroviruses can have different capsid maturation patterns. These differences likely arise from three factors. First, low ORF2 sequence similarity between GAstV-1 and GAstV-2 may change protease cutting sites. Second, the spike domain itself is highly variable, directly affecting final protein size. Third, different genotypes may respond differently to insect cell proteases. Notably, our data suggest that GAstV-2 undergoes extensive intracellular maturation without requiring exogenous trypsin, similar to our previous observations for GAstV-1 (16). This observation raises the possibility that intracellular maturation may represent a shared feature among avian astroviruses, although additional studies in other avian astroviruses will be required to confirm this hypothesis. In contrast, classical human astroviruses require both internal caspase cutting and external trypsin activation. This fundamental distinction has important implications for understanding tissue tropism and pathogenic mechanisms across the Astroviridae family (31, 32). Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, because all experiments were performed using the baculovirus/insect-cell expression system, the maturation profile observed here may not fully reflect the proteolytic processing that occurs during authentic GAstV-2 infection in goose cells. Validation using goose-derived cells and natural infection models will therefore be important to establish the physiological maturation pathway. Second, the precise N- and C-terminal cleavage sites of the mature core and spike proteins were not determined. Future mass spectrometry analyses will be required to accurately define these cleavage events. Finally, the cellular protease(s) responsible for ORF2 maturation remain unknown. Although our data support extensive intracellular processing of the capsid protein, the underlying molecular mechanism has yet to be established. Future studies combining protease inhibitor screening, proteomic analyses, and genetic approaches will be necessary to identify the host protease(s) involved and to clarify the molecular basis of GAstV-2 capsid maturation.

The complexity of extracellular processing products observed in this study was far greater than that of intracellular products, with multiple intermediate bands ranging from 90 to 30 kDa. This is highly similar to the complex processing pattern observed when expressing HAstV-1 ORF2 in the baculovirus system (14), but differs from the single 87 kDa band detected when expressing HAstV-2 ORF2 in the poxvirus system (15). This difference highlights the significant influence of the expression system on the processing of astrovirus structural proteins. Furthermore, the ring-like structures approximately 10 nm in diameter observed in eVLP samples are highly similar to the ring structures produced by HAstV-1 in insect cells (14). These structures were previously speculated to be assembly intermediates or subunit forms of the capsid, which could be dissociated from intact VLPs upon EDTA treatment and reassembled into complete VLPs upon Mg2+ supplementation, suggesting they may represent a reversible intermediate state of capsid assembly (31). Herein, we report the first observation of similar structures in the GAstV-2 system. This observation is morphologically consistent with previous findings in human astrovirus and raises the possibility that a conserved reversible assembly intermediate may exist during astrovirus capsid biogenesis (33). Nevertheless, whether the approximately 10 nm ring-like structures represent reversible assembly intermediates remains to be experimentally verified. Functional studies, including EDTA/Mg2+-mediated interconversion assays, will be valuable to further evaluate this hypothesis.

The differences in protein composition and maturation status between iVLPs and eVLPs may reflect different stages in the dynamic process of astrovirus capsid assembly. Classical human astrovirus studies show that VP90 precursor first binds to cell membranes to form immature particles, then caspase cleavage releases these particles into the cytoplasm as VP70, which is finally secreted outside the cell (34–36). Similarly, our iVLPs appear to be mature particles that completed intracellular processing but were not yet released, while eVLPs were secreted particles that may have undergone further processing outside the cell. Our electron microscopy results support this: iVLPs were mainly found in the cytoplasm attached to membranes, whereas eVLPs were collected from the culture medium. Importantly, both iVLPs and eVLPs triggered similar humoral immune responses in goslings despite their different protein compositions. This finding confirms that both types retain the critical conformational epitopes necessary to stimulate a robust protective immune response. These results hold important implications for the scalable production of VLP-based vaccines. Purifying eVLPs directly from the culture supernatant is a simpler and more cost-effective process, making it the optimal strategy for large-scale production. Future work could further improve eVLP quality, such as by enriching specific particle types or treating with proteases to promote maturation.

Vaccine development against GAstV-2 is still at an early stage, largely constrained by the technical bottleneck of inefficient in vitro viral propagation, which has severely hampered the advancement of traditional inactivated vaccines. Consequently, genetically engineered vaccines have emerged as the primary focus of research. Recombinant live-vector vaccines using Newcastle disease virus or duck enteritis virus can trigger immune responses (37, 38), but they face drawbacks such as interference from pre-existing vector immunity and inherent biosafety concerns. mRNA vaccines hold theoretical promise (39), but their high cost and strict storage needs limit their use in livestock farming. The GAstV-2 VLPs developed here offer distinct advantages: they induce high levels of humoral immunity comparable to inactivated vaccines and, importantly, stimulate a Th1/Th2 mixed-type cellular response difficult to achieve with inactivated vaccines. Furthermore, VLP immunization significantly inhibits viral shedding, with protective efficacy comparable to traditional vaccines. However, our animal study had one major limitation due to the age-related disease pattern of GAstV-2 (40). The goslings we challenged were 1 month old, an age when they are relatively resistant to the virus. This precluded the direct assessment of protection against disease symptoms and mortality. While lower viral shedding is a useful indicator of protection, the link between reduced shedding and clinical protection requires further validation. Future research should evaluate the protective efficacy of VLPs in highly susceptible young goslings. Efforts should be directed toward optimizing the immunization strategy, including exploring alternative delivery routes, evaluating novel adjuvant combinations, and refining vaccination schedules. Additionally, evaluating the protective effect of maternal antibodies in offspring goslings from VLP-immunized breeder geese and establishing prevention and control strategies based on maternal immunization should be considered. In addition, it should be noted that all VLPs evaluated in the present study were produced using the baculovirus/insect-cell expression system. Although this platform is widely used for VLP production, differences in host-cell proteases and post-translational modification pathways may influence capsid maturation and consequently affect VLP composition and quality. Nevertheless, both intracellular and extracellular VLPs elicited humoral and cellular immune responses, as well as protection against viral shedding, comparable to those induced by the experimental inactivated vaccine. These findings indicate that the baculovirus-derived VLPs possess sufficient antigenic integrity and immunogenicity to serve as promising vaccine candidates, while further validation in goose-derived expression systems would strengthen these conclusions.

In conclusion, we established a baculovirus-based platform for GAstV-2 VLP production and characterized the distinct intracellular and extracellular maturation pathways of the capsid protein. Our results demonstrate that both iVLPs and eVLPs are highly immunogenic, eliciting robust humoral responses and a balanced Th1/Th2 cellular immune profile that effectively reduces viral shedding to levels comparable with the experimental inactivated vaccine. VLPs represent a safe, scalable, and promising vaccine candidate for gosling gout prevention. This work lays a solid foundation for the development of next-generation GAstV-2 subunit vaccines and provides valuable insights into the molecular biology of avian astroviruses.
