Molecule-well in platinum-zeolite engineers molecular adsorption for highly selective hydrogenation
Authors: Xiaocheng Lan, Shunxin Fan, Zhixiang Huang, Mengjiao Huai, Dehuai Liu, Tiefeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal-zeolite composites have emerged as a promising class of heterogeneous catalysts, while the atomic-level understanding of their internal microenvironments remains limited. Here, we integrate controlled synthesis, catalytic evaluation, and atomistic modeling to resolve and quantify the three-dimensional (3D) microenvironments formed by platinum encapsulated within MFI zeolites. Using unsaturated aldehyde hydrogenation as a model reaction, we identify two distinct access modes—channel-access and well-access—that govern how reactant molecules approach Pt active sites. In particular, the well-access configuration forms a molecule-well that imposes steric constraints favoring C=O bond adsorption, thereby enabling highly selective hydrogenation to unsaturated alcohol. These findings establish a direct structure–function relationship between the geometry of the zeolite-confined space and catalytic performance, offering a molecular-level framework for rational catalyst design.

Introduction

Metal-zeolite composites, which involve fixing metal species inside the zeolite framework, have rapidly emerged as a new class of heterogeneous catalysts1–3. These composites are particularly promising due to their unique performance, which often outperforms traditional supported metal catalysts. Specifically, the ability to engineer both the metal active sites and the zeolite micropores to control molecular diffusion and reaction makes the metal–zeolite composites highly attractive, enabling the modulation of reaction routes toward desired products4–6. Building on this concept, significant efforts have been made to optimize these materials for key reaction processes, such as dehydrogenation7–10, hydrogenation11, and hydroformylation12–14.

It is evident that the location and morphology of metal species—including single sites, clusters, and nanoparticles (NPs)—play a crucial role in determining the catalytic performance of metal–zeolite composites2,3. Although significant progress has been made in the synthesis, characterization, and catalysis of metal–zeolite composites, the following fundamental challenges remain unresolved: (1) constructing and identifying a uniform and well-defined microenvironment for metal sites induced by zeolite micropores, (2) quantitatively describing the geometric and confinement effects of this microenvironment on reactant molecules, particularly in three-dimensional (3D) space, and (3) elucidating the structure-performance relationships governed by the microenvironment in catalysis.

Herein, we identify the 3D microenvironment at the atomic level and unravel its structure-performance relationships for catalysis. Platinum-zeolite composites, with clusters or NPs encapsulated inside purely siliceous MFI zeolite, were prepared based on the developed controllable one-pot synthesis technology3,13,15. The hydrogenation of unsaturated aldehydes (UALs) was chosen as a model reaction, as the products are widely used and highly valuable in the fine chemicals industry16. Previous studies have largely focused on whether metal encapsulation or porous overcoating enhances selectivity, for example, by promoting C=O hydrogenation16,17. In many cases, encapsulation is treated as a relatively spatial uniform confinement effect. In the present work, we observe that even when metal species are encapsulated within the same MFI framework, drastically different selectivities can arise depending on the precise spatial relationship between the metal site and the surrounding channel architecture. The Pt-cluster@S-1 shows low unsaturated alcohol (UOL) selectivity (<5%) for 3-methylcrotonaldehyde (3-mCRAL) hydrogenation, whereas Pt-NP@S-1 achieves >80% selectivity. Moreover, the selectivity strongly depends on the substrate molecular size of UALs.

By combining DFT calculations, control experiments, and in situ FTIR surface-reaction studies, we revealed that silicate-1 (S-1) encapsulated clusters and NPs can respectively construct two distinct 3D microenvironments that allow reactant molecules to access the Pt active sites: the so-called channel-access and well-access. In the case of well-access, the concept of a molecule-well arises. The molecule-well differs fundamentally from earlier proposed pore-mouth or intersection effects, which are typically discussed in the context of reactions occurring on zeolite acid sites. These effects are related to the pore architecture, which directly influences which molecules can access or react at the intrinsic zeolitic active sites (e.g., skeletal isomerization near pore entrances)18,19. In such cases, the zeolite provides both the confinement environment and the adsorption/activation sites. In contrast, in our system, the adsorption and activation sites are metallic Pt sites, with the zeolite framework primarily serving as a spatially confining environment. The molecule-well creates unique steric hindrance for UAL molecules of specific sizes, steering them toward a typical adsorption configuration through the C=O bond and thereby facilitating highly selective conversion to unsaturated alcohols (UOLs).

Results and discussion

Morphology of platinum species encapsulated inside silicate-1

The catalysts were prepared by one-pot synthesis to confine the Pt species inside the S-1 framework, followed by a calcination or reduction process15. This strategy is effective for controlling the particle size of the encapsulated metal species. Samples directly reduced in H2 flow (denoted as Pt-cluster@S-1) contain subnanometer Pt clusters within the MFI framework (Fig. 1a–e). The HAADF-STEM images indicated that the Pt clusters are mainly located in the sinusoidal channels (Fig. 1e). This observation is consistent with previous works showing that highly dispersed metal species tended to be fixed in the sinusoidal channels during the one-pot synthesis process8,13,15. Samples that were calcined in air at 550 °C and then reduced in H2 flow (denoted as Pt-NP@S-1) contain encapsulated Pt NPs with a mean particle size of 3.6 nm (Fig. 1f–j). The Pt NPs are fixed inside the MFI framework, spanning more than 6 straight channels. The Pt loading was determined to be 1.3 wt%. The location of Pt species was determined by probe reactions using substrates with different molecular sizes (1-hexene and cyclooctene), where cyclooctene, with a molecular size larger than the MFI pore openings, cannot access the internal micropores to contact encapsulated Pt sites. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and N2 adsorption-desorption characterizations confirm the crystal structure of purely siliceous MFI for the Pt-cluster@S-1 and Pt-NP@S-1 catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). TG analysis revealed that no exothermic peaks are observed in either Pt-cluster@S-1 or Pt-NP@S-1, indicating that all organic species were completely removed during the calcination and/or reduction processes (Supplementary Fig. 2). CO-FTIR analysis was employed to characterize the Pt species within the S-1 framework (Supplementary Fig. 3). CO stretching frequencies below 2100 cm−1 are assigned to metallic adsorption sites on Pt NP surfaces. Specifically, well-coordinated (WC) Pt sites exhibit CO adsorption bands in the range of 2080–2098 cm−1, while under-coordinated (UC) Pt sites are characterized by bands at 2060–2075 cm−120. For Pt-cluster@S-1, the CO peak centered at 2070 cm−1 indicates the presence of subnanometer Pt clusters with a significant proportion of UC sites (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The stronger CO adsorption on UC Pt sites leads to a characteristic red-shift in the CO stretching frequency. During CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD), CO molecules adsorbed on more coordinated Pt sites exhibit weaker binding and desorb more readily compared to those on less coordinated sites. Thus, the CO peak gradually decreases and shifts to lower wavenumbers with the increase of temperature. Notably, a distinct CO peak at ~2060 cm−1 remains even at 200 and 300 °C. The presence of this peak at a relatively low wavenumber further confirms the presence of subnanometer Pt species, as these species have a higher proportion of UC sites. In contrast, Pt-NP@S-1, which primarily consists of larger Pt NPs with a higher proportion of WC sites, initially displays a CO peak centered at 2080 cm−1 v (Supplementary Fig. 3a). But upon heating to 200 °C, this peak diminishes, leaving only a minor peak corresponding to CO adsorbed on the edge or corner sites of the larger Pt NPs.

Catalytic performance and molecule-well of platinum-zeolite

The Pt NPs supported on the external surface of S-1 (Pt/S-1, with an average size of 4.6 nm, Supplementary Fig. 4) were first evaluated in the hydrogenation of 3-mCRAL. The Pt/S-1 catalyst achieved only ~20% selectivity to 3-methylcrotyl alcohol (3-mCROL) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3), consistent with previous studies showing that C=C hydrogenation is thermodynamically favored over C=O hydrogenation on Group VIII metals21–23. Compared with UALs containing larger substituent groups, such as cinnamaldehyde (CAL), achieving high selectivity to UOL in 3-mCRAL hydrogenation is more challenging22,24,25. This can be attributed to the steric effect of the large phenyl group in the vicinity of the C=C bond. To achieve high selectivity to UOL for small UALs like 3-mCRAL, a confinement strategy was proposed. The characterization results revealed that the Pt clusters were well encapsulated inside the channel of S-1. Considering the commensurate pore size of S-1 (0.5–0.6 nm) with the molecular size of 3-mCRAL (4.0 × 6.4 × 8.5 Å), our promising approach is to use the Pt-cluster@S-1 as a confinement catalyst to achieve highly selective hydrogenation of 3-mCRAL to 3-mCROL. However, reaction evaluation showed that the selectivity to 3-mCROL was low as <5% (Fig. 2b). We further evaluated the Pt-NP@S-1 catalyst (encapsulated Pt NPs with a mean size of 3.6 nm) for the hydrogenation of 3-mCRAL, which unexpectedly achieved a high selectivity of >80% to 3-mCROL.

Time-dependent rate data and catalytic performance at different reaction temperatures for Pt-cluster@S-1 and Pt-NP@S-1 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. For the Pt-cluster@S-1 catalyst, the UOL selectivity in 3-mCRAL hydrogenation remained consistently low (<5%) over the investigated temperature range. In contrast, the Pt-NP@S-1 catalyst exhibited much higher UOL selectivity, although the UOL selectivity slightly decreased with increasing reaction temperature. Activation energy analysis indicated that the two catalysts possess comparable apparent activation energies (~60 kJ mol−1). However, the product distribution clearly demonstrates that Pt-NP@S-1 exhibits a strong preference for C=O hydrogenation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5e, Pt-cluster@S-1 displays a low TOFC=O/TOFC=C ratio (<0.5), whereas Pt-NP@S-1 exhibits a much higher ratio (>2.8). Catalytic recyclability tests were further conducted to evaluate catalyst stability (Supplementary Fig. 6). The product selectivity remained essentially unchanged over multiple cycles, while a slight decrease in activity was observed. ICP analysis of the spent catalysts showed that the Pt content remained at ~1.3 wt%, indicating negligible Pt leaching during the recycling tests.

Many studies have demonstrated that modifying the electronic properties of Pt via bimetallic formation (e.g., with Zn or Fe) or through electron-donating supports (e.g., carbon-based materials) can significantly influence adsorption and reaction pathways16,17. In our system, however, both Pt-NP@S-1 and Pt-cluster@S-1 feature Pt species encapsulated within the MFI framework. From a support perspective, the surrounding Si–O framework is chemically similar in both catalysts, and thus comparable electronic metal–support interactions might be expected. Nevertheless, the two catalysts exhibit strikingly different catalytic behaviors, which is difficult to rationalize solely by electronic effects. The non-porous reference catalyst (Pt/SiO2) was prepared to decouple confinement and electronic effects. The Pt/SiO2 catalyst, with a mean Pt particle size of ~1.7 nm, was evaluated in 3-mCRAL hydrogenation (Supplementary Fig. 7). This catalyst also exhibited low selectivity to UOL (<15%).

The H2 activation pathway (homolytic or heterolytic dissociation) also showed an effect on the selectivity to UOL, and heterolytic dissociation was linked to enhanced selectivity for polar C=O hydrogenation11. Homolytic dissociation is typically associated with contiguous metal sites in metal particles, whereas heterolytic dissociation is often proposed to occur at metal–base pairs and single-atom catalyst11,29. In our catalysts (Pt/S-1, Pt-cluster@S-1, and Pt-NP@S-1), structural characterization indicates that Pt species predominantly exist as clusters or NPs with adjacent Pt atoms, rather than as isolated single atoms. Furthermore, considering also that Pt-cluster@S-1 exhibits low C=O hydrogenation selectivity, a dominant heterolytic pathway appears less likely in our system. The H2–D2 isotope exchange experiments (Supplementary Fig. 8) show that all three catalysts exhibit weak temperature dependence for H2–D2 exchange, with very low apparent activation energies (5.0 kJ/mol). Such behavior is characteristic of metallic surfaces where H2 activation is facile and typically proceeds via homolytic dissociation29,30.

The particle size effect has been reported in the selective hydrogenation of UAL, but this phenomenon was mainly observed in the hydrogenation of CAL, which contains a large substituent, phenyl, on the C=C bond31–33. The proposed explanation is that, over larger metal particles, steric hindrance from the aromatic ring prevents the C=C bond from parallel adsorption on flat metal surfaces, thereby enhancing selectivity to cinnamyl alcohol (COL). Notably, this particle size effect typically requires metal particles to grow from <4 nm to 6–10 nm17,34, which is larger than those in Pt-NP@S-1. In addition, our experiments showed that the Pt/S-1 catalyst with 4.6 nm particles exhibited low selectivity to 3-mCROL. These observations indicated that the significant difference in selectivity between Pt-cluster@S-1 and Pt-NP@S-1 cannot be attributed solely to Pt particle size.

To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we investigated the hydrogenation of various UALs over the Pt-NP@S-1 catalyst (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 4). The molecular sizes of these UALs were comparable to the pore size of S-1, particularly for M1, M2, and M3, which had simple methyl or ethyl substituents. However, M1 and M2 exhibited low selectivity towards UOLs. Analysis of the relative hydrogenation rates of C=O versus C=C bonds revealed that, over Pt-NP@S-1, the C=C bonds in M1 and M2 were more readily hydrogenated than the C=O bonds. In contrast, for M3, M4, and M5, the reaction activity ratio (rC=O/rC=C) exceeded 5.5, indicating a strong preference for C=O hydrogenation.

The significant differences in catalytic performance of Pt species with different particle sizes toward various reactant molecules indicate that the observed reaction behaviors cannot be solely attributed to the confinement effect of the zeolite pores. Instead, the unique reaction environment likely originates from specific interactions between the Pt species and the 3D framework of S-1. To investigate this, we used Material Studio to construct models of Pt-cluster@S-1 and Pt-NP@S-1, aiming to reveal the local environment around the Pt active sites (Fig. 2d–f). For Pt-cluster@S-1 (Fig. 2d, e), subnanometer Pt clusters of ~0.8 nm were anchored within the sinusoidal channels (S1). The length of the sinusoidal channel between adjacent straight channels (C2 and C3) was comparable to the Pt cluster size. This geometry exposed Pt active sites along the wall surfaces of the straight channels (C2 and C3), a configuration referred to as channel-access (Fig. 2e).

For Pt-NP@S-1, Pt NPs with a particle size of 3.6 nm were fixed within the framework of S-1. A thin slice was extracted from the 3D model (Fig. 2f, g) to highlight the local regions around the surface Pt sites, specifically regions A and B. The straight channel (denoted as neck I) between adjacent sinusoidal channels, such as S2 and S3, was formed by two layers of 10-membered rings (10MR) of the MFI framework, with a length of 7–8 Å. Similarly, the sinusoidal channel (denoted as neck II) between adjacent straight channels, such as C6 and C7, was also constructed by two layers of 10MR of the MFI framework, with a comparable length of 7–8 Å. For both neck I and neck II (Fig. 2g), one of the channel ports is blocked by the Pt NPs, allowing reactant molecules to access the Pt active sites exclusively through the opposite port of the neck. These structural units, comprising Pt NPs and the surrounding MFI framework, function like a molecule-well and are referred to as well-access.

It is worth noting that the hydrogenation of UALs on Pt sites through well-access exhibited high sensitivity to molecule geometry. The molecular dimensions of M1 and M3 are approximately 4.2 × 5.5 × 9.9 and 4.0 × 6.4 × 8.5 Å, respectively. The volumes of the substituent groups attached to the C=C bond were also calculated (Supplementary Table 5). Both M1 and M3 had identical substituents at R3 and R4, and the main difference between them was in the substituents at R1 and R2. However, it remains challenging to directly correlate either the overall molecular dimensions or the substituent volumes with the observed differences in reaction behaviors of M1 and M3.

Theoretical calculations and in situ FTIR experiments for adsorption configuration verification

To further investigate the catalytic behavior over the Pt active sites through channel-access and well-access, we carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations to examine various access modes for the adsorption of reactant molecules. Based on the characterization results, a Pt13 cluster confined within the sinusoidal 10MR channels of the MFI zeolite was used as a model for the channel-access environment in Pt-cluster@S-1, denoted as Pt@channel (Supplementary Fig. 9). For Pt-NP@S-1, it is challenging to directly construct a theoretical MFI model encapsulating a 3–4 nm Pt NP. Instead, a Pt13 cluster positioned at the intersection of the sinusoidal and straight channels was used to represent the well-access environment in Pt-NP@S-1, denoted as Pt@well (Supplementary Fig. 10).

The UAL molecules exhibited different adsorption configurations on the metal surface, including η4-trans, η4-cis, σC=C, πC=C, σC=O, and atopC=O (Fig. 3a). It is well accepted that the adsorption of UAL molecules via the C=C bond is energetically favorable on the Pt surface35,36. Consequently, hydrogenation of the C=C bond occurs preferentially over that of the C=O bond, leading to the formation of SALs or SOLs37,38. To verify this, we first calculated the adsorption of M1 and M3 on the Pt(111) surface (Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). The adsorption energy was generally lower when the molecule M1 or M3 adsorbed exclusively through the C=C bond (η4-trans, η4-cis, and σC=C configurations) compared to adsorption via the C=O bond (σC=O and atopC=O configurations), indicating that the C=C bond is preferentially adsorbed and hydrogenated over the C=O bond. For the Pt@channel model, the σC=C and πC=C adsorption configurations of M1 and M3 exhibited lower adsorption energy than the σC=O and atopC=O configurations (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 13, 14), consistent with the reaction results showing less than 5% UOL selectivity over the Pt-cluster@S-1 catalyst. The higher selectivity observed over Pt/S-1 compared to Pt-cluster@S-1 could be ascribed to the change in relative adsorption energies. DFT calculations (Fig. 3b) show that while the adsorption energies of σC=C and πC=C are significantly lower on the Pt@channel model than on Pt(111), those of σC=O and atopC=O remain similar. Consequently, under the Pt@channel model conditions, the preferential adsorption of C=C bonds is more likely, which could reasonably explain the lower UOL selectivity observed over Pt-cluster@S-1.

We further calculated the adsorption of M1 and M3 over the Pt@well models (Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Figs. 15–18). For the M1 molecule, the adsorption energies of σC=C and πC=C were comparable to or even lower than that of σC=O (Fig. 3c, Pt@well-A and Pt@well-B), suggesting that C=C hydrogenation remains favored over C=O hydrogenation for M1 with well-access. Surprisingly, for the M3 molecule, only the σC=O and atopC=O configurations were stable (i.e., had negative adsorption energies), whereas the η4-cis, σC=C, and πC=C configurations had positive adsorption energies (Fig. 3b, Pt@well-A and Pt@well-B). This indicates that the molecule-well efficiently inhibits C=C bond adsorption for M3, thereby promoting high selectivity to 3-mCROL. The relative hydrogenation activity toward C=O and C=C bonds was correlated with the corresponding adsorption energies obtained from DFT calculations (Supplementary Fig. 19). Catalysts predicted by DFT calculations to preferentially stabilize C=O adsorption indeed show higher experimental selectivity toward C=O hydrogenation.

In contrast, for well-access, the Pt sites are located at the bottom of the well. Adsorption via the C=C bond causes significant molecular distortion, as the molecule length exceeds the well diameter. Pt13 cluster positioned at the intersection of channels was used to represent the well-access environment in Pt-NP@S-1. In order to minimize the influence of particle size effects in this model, the particle size effect on the adsorption energies was further evaluated by additional DFT calculations. Specifically, models of Pt13 and Pt55 were constructed without considering the zeolite effect, and additionally, Pt55@well-access models were created for considering Pt-zeolite interfaces (Supplementary Figs. 21–23). The adsorption energies of different 3-mCRAL adsorption configurations were calculated on these metal surfaces, including η4, σC=C, πC=C, σC=O, and atopC=O (Supplementary Fig. 24). For the models without the zeolite structure, Pt particles of different sizes showed similar trends, where the adsorption energy was generally lower for the C=C bond (η4 and σC=C configurations) compared to adsorption via the C=O bond (σC=O and atopC=O configurations). Compared to Pt55 and Pt(111), the adsorption energies of the corresponding adsorption configurations on the Pt13 were generally lower. For models with the zeolite structure, Pt55@well-access exhibited a similar trend to Pt13@well-access. Notably, only the σC=O and atopC=O configurations were stable, with negative adsorption energies, whereas the σC=C and πC=C configurations had positive adsorption energies.

In situ FTIR experiments were carried out to verify the adsorption properties over the Pt-cluster@S-1 and Pt-NP@S-1. The spectra of 3-mCRAL adsorbed on the samples were analyzed in Fig. 4a. The gas-phase 3-mCRAL exhibited two features at 1635 cm−1 and in the range of 1350–1500 cm−1, which were assigned to the vibrations of ν(C=C) and δ(-CH3) in the methyl group, respectively24,39,40. Additionally, the typical split vibration of ν(C=O) around ~1700 cm−1 was observed due to the conjugation effect of ν(C=C). After 3-mCRAL adsorbed onto the catalyst surfaces, the characteristic peaks of ν(C=C) and ν(C=O) shifted to lower wavenumbers, with the C=O stretch ν(C=O) shifting to the region of 1630–1670 cm−1 and the C=C stretch shifting to 1610 cm−1. The temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) experiments results (Supplementary Fig. 25) showed that the adsorption strength of these samples for 3-mCRAL follows the order of Pt-cluster@S-1 > Pt-NP@S-1 > blank S-1. These results are consistent with the DFT calculations. Pt-NP@S-1 primarily contains well-access Pt sites (Pt@well model) for 3-mCRAL. In order to directly verify the different adsorption modes and characteristics of 3-mCRAL on the Pt-cluster@S-1 and Pt-NP@S-1 catalysts, we further conducted surface reactions using in situ FTIR (Fig. 4b–d). For Pt-cluster@S-1, the band intensity at 1681 cm−1 (ν(C=O)) and 1610 cm−1 (ν(C=C)) gradually decreased upon the introduction of hydrogen, while the band intensity at 1710 cm−1 (corresponding to saturated aldehyde by the hydrogenation of C=C bond) increased, indicating the hydrogenation of the adsorbed C=C bond and the conversion of 3-mCRAL to SAL. A prominent band appeared at 2060 cm−1, corresponding to CO adsorbed on Pt. The intensity of this band increased with reaction time, which was attributed to the decarbonylation of 3-mCRAL. The decarbonylation of UAL primarily occurred via the η4 adsorption mode during gas-phase hydrogenation, requiring the co-adsorption of both C=C and C=O bonds24,41,42. In contrast, the Pt-NP@S-1 catalyst exhibited a markedly different evolution of the FTIR spectra. No formation of ν(C=O) at 1710 cm−1 and no formation of Pt-CO at 2060 cm−1 via η4 adsorption and decarbonylation was observed. Only a decrease in the ν(C=O) of 3-mCRAL at 1681 cm−1 was observed. Collectively, the in situ surface reaction suggested that the adsorption mode of 3-mCRAL on Pt-cluster@S-1 primarily involves η4, σC=C, and πC=C, while adsorption via the C=C bond was significantly inhibited on Pt-NP@S-1.

Topology effect of metal species and zeolite framework, and expanding analysis

The characterization results indicate that Pt clusters in the Pt-cluster@S-1 catalyst are predominantly confined within the sinusoidal channel, where most Pt active sites are accessible through channel-access. In contrast, for the Pt-NP@S-1 catalyst, the spatial distribution of Pt NPs within the 3D MFI framework is likely to be uncertain. As Pt species aggregate from clusters to NPs, not all Pt sites necessarily adopt the well-access configuration; some may remain accessible through channel-access. Although theoretical calculations clearly show that well-access significantly inhibits the adsorption of the C=C bond in M3 due to steric hindrance, the relative proportion of well-access versus channel-access sites remains unclear during the aggregation of Pt species from clusters to NPs.

To investigate the effect of Pt aggregation from clusters to NPs, we further prepared Pt-NP@S-1 catalysts with varied Pt particle sizes. Pt species tend to aggregate under high-temperature conditions, particularly in air15. As the calcination temperature (T) increased (catalyst denoted as Pt-NP@S-1-T), the particle size of Pt increased, and its distribution became broader (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Fig. 5b). The average Pt particle size increased from 0.8 to 3.5 nm. Catalytic evaluation revealed that selectivity toward C=O hydrogenation increased with increasing Pt particle size (Fig. 5a). The rC=O/rC=C ratio increased from 0.33 to 0.9 and further to 5.6 as the mean particle size of Pt increased from 0.8 to 2.6 nm and then to 3.5 nm. To the best of our knowledge, experimentally determining a reliable topological structure of Pt NPs within the MFI framework, particularly the local interactions between the metal NPs and the MFI framework in Pt-NP@S-1, remains highly challenging. To address this, we employed a probabilistic statistical approach to explore the underlying rationale.

Specifically, we constructed an MFI model with a Pt NP of x nm in diameter randomly positioned within the MFI framework, denoted as Model-x (Fig. 5c–e). The channel mouths (CMs) located on the (010) or (100) planes, which are directly connected to the Pt active sites, were labeled as An (Fig. 5c). A spherical probe was initiated at each An and allowed to traverse toward the Pt active sites (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 27, and Supplementary Note 1). If the probe reached a Pt atom without an escape route, the CM was classified as well-access, such as A6. In contrast, if an escape route existed, the CM was classified as channel-access, such as A14 and A17. For model-x, the fraction (pw) of CMs with well-access was quantified (Supplementary Fig. 27). The molecular size of the UAL molecules showed a typical width of 4.0 × 6.0 Å (Supplementary Table 5). Therefore, the probe radius was initially set to 2.4 Å.

To reduce uncertainties arising from the random distribution of the Pt NP, the Pt NP was systematically displaced within the framework along the a, b, and c directions with specific step sizes (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 28). Each displacement generated a new model, and pw was calculated accordingly. This process was repeated for models with different Pt particle sizes, and the resulting statistical data are shown in Fig. 5g. When the Pt NP size was relatively small (e.g., <1 nm), pw values exhibited a uniform distribution between 0 and 1. This suggests that when small clusters are randomly distributed within the MFI framework, the catalyst does not preferentially form either well-access or channel-access Pt sites. However, structural characterization confirms that in Pt-cluster@S-1, Pt clusters are selectively located within the sinusoidal channels, where most Pt active sites are accessed through channel-access. As the particle size increased, the distribution of pw gradually shifted toward the range of 0.5–1. Although pinpointing the exact position of large Pt NPs within the MFI framework is more challenging than locating Pt clusters, probabilistic statistical analysis suggests that increasing Pt particle size promotes the formation of well-access sites.

To further investigate the impact of different molecular sizes on the determination of well-access and channel-access sites, we conducted a probe radius sensitivity analysis for the pw distribution. We calculated the pw distribution as a function of Pt particle size for probe radii of 2.0, 2.2, and 2.6 Å (Supplementary Fig. 29). For the probe radius of 2.0 Å, as the particle size increased, the pw distribution tended to approach ~0.5. While for probe radii of 2.2 and 2.6 Å, the distribution of pw gradually shifted toward the range of 0.5–1 as the particle size increased. The sensitivity analysis of the probe radius indicates that probe radii in the range of 2.2–2.6 Å (corresponding to larger molecules) yield consistent results, showing that increasing the Pt particle size promotes the formation of well-access sites. Additionally, as the molecular size increases, a higher proportion of well-access sites is formed. These statistical findings are consistent with the catalytic behavior observed during reaction evaluation: with increasing Pt particle size, the selectivity for C=C hydrogenation of M3 notably decreases, leading to a higher selectivity to the UOL product.

To further strengthen the generalizability of the molecule-well concept in governing molecular adsorption and reaction orientation, we expanded our study to an additional reaction of system: the hydrogenation of an unsaturated nitro compound to oxime (Supplementary Table 13). Complementary DFT calculations were also performed to identify the adsorption properties (Supplementary Figs. 30–31). The catalytic results show that Pt-NP@S-1 exhibits a much higher selectivity (86%) toward oxime than Pt-cluster@S-1 (53%). To understand this, we systematically calculated the adsorption energies of different UN adsorption configurations on the metal surface, including σC=C, πC=C, η3(-NO2), and atop(-NO2) (Supplementary Fig. 30). For the Pt@channel model, both C=C and –NO2 group adsorption are feasible, and thus C=C hydrogenation is not significantly suppressed over Pt-cluster@S-1. In contrast, for the Pt@well model, the σC=C and πC=C configurations exhibit positive adsorption energies due to steric constraints, and only η3(-NO₂) and atop(-NO2) configurations are favorable. These results further support that the confined environment can selectively modulate adsorption modes depending on molecular functionality.

We also extended the concept to another metal, namely Ir, for which Ir-cluster@S-1 and Ir-NP@S-1 were prepared and evaluated for 3-mCRAL hydrogenation (Supplementary Figs. 32–34 and Supplementary Table 14). The hydrogenation results show that Ir-NP@S-1 exhibits a much higher selectivity (91.4%) toward UOL than Ir-cluster@S-1 (44.9%). Correspondingly, adsorption calculations on Ir sites, including σC=C, πC=C, σC=O, and atopC=O configurations, reveal trends remarkably similar to the Pt-zeolite system. For the Ir@well model, only σC=O and atopC=O configurations are stable with negative adsorption energies, whereas σC=C and πC=C configurations are unfavorable.

Supplementary Table 15 summarizes the representative catalysts for selective UAL hydrogenation, including electronic modification and confinement modification for differentiate substrate UALs. Higher UOL selectivity is generally more readily achieved for larger substrates such as CAL than for smaller molecules. The present work provided new perspectives on tailoring metal location, identifying the structure of confined metal sites, and correlating these structural features with reaction pathways in metal–zeolite catalytic systems.

In summary, Pt-zeolite composites create distinct 3D microenvironments that regulate molecular access to Pt sites, specifically through channel-access and well-access configurations. The concept of a molecule-well, characterized by unique steric hindrance, effectively guides reactant molecules toward the C=O bond, leading to high selectivity in the hydrogenation of unsaturated aldehydes. Given the existence distinct zeolite framework topologies, it should be possible to construct tailored molecule-wells that selectively accommodate specific reactant adsorption geometries, thereby tuning reaction pathways to achieve high catalytic performance. This strategy opens up new avenues for designing efficient heterogeneous catalysts for a range of important reactions.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

The catalyst was synthesized via a one-pot method reported in the literature15. First, the Pt(EDA)2Cl2 precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.24 g of PtCl2 in 10 mL of aqueous solution containing 1 mL of ethylenediamine (EDA), with stirring until complete dissolution. The Pt@S-1 composite was then synthesized hydrothermally using a gel with the molar composition: TEOS: TPAOH: H2O: Pt(EDA)2Cl2 = 1:0.4:35:2.25 × 10−3. The mixture was aged and crystallized under hydrothermal conditions at 170 °C for 3 days. The resulting solid was recovered by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with ultrapure water, and dried overnight at 100 °C. Two types of catalysts were obtained from the dried sample (Pt@S-1) via different post-treatments: (1) Pt-cluster@S-1: the as-synthesized solid was directly reduced under a pure H2 flow (60 mL/min) at 550 °C for 5 h. (2) Pt-NP@S-1-T: the solid was first calcined in air at T = 350, 450, or 550 °C for 6 h, followed by reduction under 40% H2/N2 (50 mL/min) at 400 °C for 4 h. Ir-cluster@S-1 and Ir-NP@S-1 were synthesized using the same one-pot method as for Pt, with PtCl2 replaced by IrCl3·3H2O.

For comparison, Pt/S-1 was prepared via incipient wetness impregnation. Blank S-1 zeolite was synthesized following the same procedure as for Pt@S-1, but without the addition of the Pt(EDA)2Cl2 complex. H2PtCl2·6H2O aqueous solution was used as the Pt precursor, which was impregnated onto the S-1 support, followed by ultrasonication for 2 h. The sample was then dried at 100 °C for 12 h, calcined at 400 °C for 2 h, and finally reduced under 40% H2/N2 (50 mL/min) at 400 °C for 2 h.

Characterizations

Powder XRD patterns were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5418 Å). Powder samples were pressed onto a single-crystal Si substrate using a glass slide. Data were recorded over a 2θ range of 5°–90° with a scanning rate of 5° min−1. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using an Autosorb-iQ2-C surface area and pore analyzer (Quantachrome Instruments, USA). Prior to measurement, samples were degassed at 200 °C under vacuum. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was applied to determine specific surface areas, with the relative pressure (P/P₀) range set to 0.4–0.6, and data points selected using the Micropore BET Assistant. Micropore surface area, external surface area, and pore volume were calculated using the t-plot method. The actual metal loadings in the catalysts were quantified using ICP-OES (iCAPQ, Thermo, Waltham, USA). TGA was performed on a TGA/DSC1/1600HT instrument under an oxygen atmosphere with a flow rate of 50 mL/min. The temperature was ramped from 30 to 750 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. In situ CO and 3-mCRAL adsorption IR spectra were collected using a Thermo Nicolet Nexus 470 FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT-A) detector. Approximately 10 mg of catalyst was pressed onto a tungsten mesh and mounted in a IR cell. Prior to measurement, the catalyst was pre-reduced in H2 at 500 °C for 1 h. 3-mCRAL adsorption was carried out at 11.5 kPa and 40 °C for 30 min, followed by evacuation at the same temperature until the pressure dropped below 5.0 × 10−3 Pa. After the sample was fully chemisorbed with 3-mCRAL molecules, the gas-phase and physically adsorbed 3-mCRAL were removed under continuous evacuation. Then, 10 kPa of H2 was introduced into the IR cell, followed by in situ FTIR detection. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) were performed using an FEI Titan Cubed G2 300 transmission electron microscope operated at 300 kV, equipped with a probe aberration corrector. Prior to imaging, samples were ultrasonically dispersed in ethanol for 15 min and dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid. Particle size distributions were obtained by analyzing over 100 individual particles. The H2–D2 exchange reaction were performed in a vacuum batch reactor, where a mixture of H2 and D2 was introduced, and the evolution of H2, D2, and HD was monitored in situ by mass spectrometry.

Catalytic evaluation

The selective hydrogenation of unsaturated aldehydes was carried out in a high-pressure batch reactor under liquid-phase conditions. For the hydrogenation of trans-3-(2-furyl)acrylaldehyde and 3-(2-furyl)-2-methyl-2-propenal, the reaction was performed in 30 mL of isopropanol as the solvent, using 0.2 g of o-xylene as an internal standard and 0.2 mmol of unsaturated aldehyde as the substrate. Zero point two grams of catalyst was loaded into the reactor. The reaction was conducted at 80 °C under a H2 pressure of 3 MPa. For the hydrogenation of 3-methyl-2-butenal, 2-pentenal, and 2-methyl-2-pentenal, the reaction was performed in 30 mL of cyclohexane as the solvent, with 0.120 mL of n-hexanol as the internal standard and 0.345 mL of the corresponding aldehyde as the substrate. Zero point two grams of catalyst was similarly used, and the reaction conditions were 80 °C and 3 MPa H2 pressure in a high-pressure batch reactor. For the hydrogenation of unsaturated nitrocompound, the reaction was performed in 24 mL of ethanol and 6 mL of H2O as the solvent, with 0.1 g of reactant and 0.2 g of catalyst were used, and the reaction conditions were 80 °C and 3 MPa H2 pressure. The reaction products were analyzed using a gas chromatograph equipped with a Super-Wax capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.5 μm) and a flame ionization detector. Computational calculations

The molecule adsorption was studied by DFT calculations using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4)43,44. Electron-ion and electron–electron interactions were described with the projected augmented wave (PAW) method45,46 and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional47, respectively. Van der Waals (vdW) interactions were incorporated using the DFT-D3 method. The Brillouin zone was sampled only on the Gamma point for geometry optimization. To balance computational efficiency with chemical accuracy, calculation settings were adopted from previous studies13,14: a plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 300 eV was used to describe the Kohn–Sham wavefunctions, and all structures were considered fully optimized when the residual force on each atom was less than 0.03 eV Å−1.
