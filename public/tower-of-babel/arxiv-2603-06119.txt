Abstract
. Serrin’s symmetry theorem shows that the classical overdetermined torsion problem forces the domain to be a ball. Extending this rigidity statement to merely Lipschitz (and more generally rough) domains in the weak formulation has been a long-standing and challenging problem, recently resolved by the authors in [ 12 ] . In this paper we address the corresponding question in the anisotropic setting: Given a uniformly convex C 2 , γ C^{2,\gamma} anisotropy H H , we study the overdetermined problem for the anisotropic Laplacian Δ H ​ u = div ⁡ ( H ⁡ ( ∇ u ) ​ D ​ H ​ ( ∇ u ) ) \Delta_{H}u={\rm div}\big(H(\nabla u)\,DH(\nabla u)\big) on a bounded indecomposable set of finite perimeter Ω \Omega . Assuming the Ahlfors–David regularity of ∂ ∗ Ω \partial^{*}\Omega and a global β \beta -number square-function bound (a weak uniform rectifiability hypothesis), we prove that a weak solution exists if and only if Ω \Omega is a translate and dilation of the Wulff shape, in which case the solution is unique and explicit. In particular, the result applies to Lipschitz domains. While our approach follows the rough-domain strategy of [ 12 ] at a high level, the key Laplacian-specific ingredients exploited there have no direct analog for Δ H \Delta_{H} , necessitating the development of new ideas and techniques.

1.
Introduction

Let
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a convex,
1
-homogeneous positive function, smooth away from the origin, and define the dual function
H_{*}
as

H_{*}(x):=\sup\{x\cdot y:\ H(y)=1\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and

K:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ H_{*}(x)\leq 1\}.

For a set
E
of finite perimeter, we define the anisotropic perimeter corresponding to
H
by

P_{H}(E):=\int_{\partial^{*}E}H(-\nu_{E})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1},

where
\nu_{E}
is the measure-theoretic outer unit normal of
E
.
Equivalently, this is the anisotropic perimeter with surface tension
F(\nu):=H(-\nu)
; with this definition,
the Wulff minimizer for
P_{H}
(namely, the minimizer of
P_{H}
at a given volume) is the reflected body
-K
.

Define the energy integrand and the anisotropic Laplacian as

V(\xi):=\frac{1}{2}H(\xi)^{2},\qquad\Delta_{H}u:={\rm div}\bigl(DV(\nabla u)\bigr)={\rm div}\bigl(H(\nabla u)\,DH(\nabla u)\bigr),

and consider the following anisotropic overdetermined system:

Find

u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=-\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\varphi\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx,\qquad\forall\,\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\mathbf{c}=\frac{|\Omega|}{P_{H}(\Omega)}
.

In terms of the anisotropic Laplacian,
the system can be equivalently restated in distributional form as

u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad u=0\ \text{a.e.\ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\qquad\Delta_{H}u=\mathbf{c}H(-\nu)\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\,dx.

The foundational case where
K=B
(the Euclidean unit ball) and
\Omega
is of class
C^{2}
was first investigated by Serrin

[
24
]
, who proved that a solution exists if and only if
\Omega
is a ball. Later, Weinberger
[
31
]
provided an alternative proof. Building on Weinberger’s approach, several other
methods have been developed; see, for instance,
[
4
,
6
,
20
]
as well as the recent manuscript
[
5
]
.

When both
\Omega
and
K
are sufficiently smooth so that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
(i.e.
u
is a classical solution),

[
7
]
and
[
30
]
independently showed that
\Omega
must be homothetic to
K
, namely it coincides with
K

up to a translation and a dilation. These arguments refine the techniques introduced in
[
4
]
and
[
31
]
,
respectively.

1.1.
Serrin’s theorem in general domains

In 1992, Vogel
[
29
]
showed that if
\Omega
is a
C^{1}
domain admitting a solution to Serrin’s overdetermined problem
(for the Laplacian or for certain special degenerate operators), then
\Omega
must be of class
C^{2}
and Serrin’s theorem applies.
In his approach Vogel assumed the boundary behavior

u(x)\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad|\nabla u|(x)\to\mathbf{c}\ \text{ uniformly as }x\to\partial\Omega,

and used the Alt–Caffarelli-type free boundary regularity theory to deduce the
C^{2}
-regularity of
\Omega
.

Later, in the classical case
K=B
, Berestycki raised the question of what happens if the domain is
C^{2}
except at a potential corner, and
u
is a strong
solution everywhere except at that corner. This was resolved in
[
22
]
using an adapted moving plane
method that avoids the singular point, sparking interest in extending Serrin’s theorem to a broader class of domains. Specifically,

[
15
, Question 7.1]
asked the following in the case
K=B
:

Does Serrin’s theorem hold if
\Omega
is merely Lipschitz and
u
solves the equation under the weak formulation?

This question was recently answered in
[
12
]
for an even broader class of domains. Recall that a measurable set

\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
finite perimeter
if the distributional gradient of its characteristic function

\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued Radon measure
D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}
with finite total variation, i.e.

|D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}|(\mathbb{R}^{n})<\infty
.
By the Lebesgue–Besicovitch differentiation theorem, for
|D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}|
-a.e.
x
,

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}(B_{r}(x))}{|D\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}|(B_{r}(x))}=-\nu_{x},\qquad|\nu_{x}|=1.

The set of such points is the
reduced boundary

\partial^{*}\Omega
, and
\nu_{x}
is the measure-theoretic outer unit normal.
By De Giorgi’s structure theorem,
\partial^{*}\Omega
is
(n-1)
-rectifiable. Moreover,
\Omega
can be adjusted on a null
set so that
\overline{\partial^{*}\Omega}=\partial\Omega
.

A set
E
of finite perimeter is
indecomposable
if for any
F\subset E
of finite perimeter satisfying

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}E)=\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}F)+\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}(E\setminus F)),

either
|F|=0
or
|E\setminus F|=0
. For further details, see
[
1
]
and
[
19
, Sections 12 & 15]
.

In
[
12
]
, it was proven (via geometric measure theory techniques) that if a bounded, indecomposable set of finite
perimeter
\Omega
satisfies, for some
A>0
, the measure-theoretic condition

(1.1)

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(B_{r}(x)\cap\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq Ar^{n-1}\quad\text{for $\mathscr{H}^{n-1}$-a.e.\ }x\in\partial^{*}\Omega\text{ and }r\in(0,1),

along with

(1.2)

u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=-\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\varphi\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx\quad\forall\,\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then
\Omega
must be a ball. Notably, Lipschitz domains satisfy (
1.1
), thereby resolving

[
15
, Question 7.1]
.

More recently,
[
11
,
10
]
presented two alternative approaches for Lipschitz domains: One based on non-tangential limits and harmonic analysis techniques, and another relying on refined regularity theory for weak solutions of Alt–Caffarelli-type free boundary problems. We stress that all three proofs (those in
[
12
]
,
[
11
]
, and
[
10
]
) depend in an essential way on structural properties specific to the Laplacian.

The paper
[
11
]
also proposes Conjecture 1.4, formulating an anisotropic Serrin-type conjecture for rough domains. However, the solvability of regularity problems for second-order divergence-form elliptic operators in general Lipschitz domains remains open, except in settings where the local Lipschitz constant is sufficiently small (e.g., in
C^{1}
domains; see
[
9
]
). Consequently, the strategy in
[
11
]
to extend Serrin’s theorem from the Laplacian to general anisotropic operators in Lipschitz domains faces intrinsic limitations and requires strong additional assumptions on both the geometry of the domain and the structure of the operator.

In the present paper, we instead pursue the geometric-measure-theoretic strategy of
[
12
]
.
A central difficulty is that several identities and rigidity steps used there rely on the special
structure of the Laplacian; developing replacements for these ingredients is the main
new contribution of this work.

1.2.
Main result

To formulate our result, we first recall (a variant of) the
\beta
-number introduced by P. Jones
[
21
]
: For each
x\in\partial^{*}\Omega
and
r>0
, define

(1.3)

\beta(x,r):=\inf_{P}r^{1-n}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B_{r}(x)}\frac{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(z,P)}{r}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z),

where
P
ranges over all affine hyperplanes intersecting
B_{r}(x)
.
Our main theorem states as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded indecomposable set of finite perimeter and assume there exist constant
A_{1},A_{2}\geq 1
such that

(1.4)

\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}\beta(x,s)^{2}\,\frac{ds}{s}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x)\leq A_{1}

and that, for
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
-a.e.
x\in\partial^{*}\Omega
and every
r\in(0,1)
,

(1.5)

A_{2}^{-1}r^{n-1}\leq\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(B_{r}(x)\cap\partial^{*}\Omega)\leq A_{2}r^{n-1}.

Let
K
be a bounded uniformly convex body whose boundary is of class

C^{2,\gamma}
for some
\gamma>0
small, and let
H
be the corresponding Wulff potential.
Then
\Omega
admits a solution
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to

(1.6)

u=0\quad\text{a.e.\ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\qquad\Delta_{H}u=\mathbf{c}H(-\nu)\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\,dx,

in the sense of distributions, if and only if, up to a translation,

\text{$\Omega$ is homothetic to $K$ }\quad\text{ and }\quad u(x)=\frac{r^{2}-H_{*}^{2}(x)}{2n}\quad\text{for some $r>0$.}

Remark 1.2
.

Theorem
1.1
implies, in particular, an anisotropic version of Serrin’s theorem for any domain
\Omega
whose boundary
satisfies (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) above. Note that Lipschitz domains, or even uniformly
n
-rectifiable sets, do satisfy these assumptions, see e.g.
[
13
]
and

[
14
, Proposition III.4.2]
. See also
[
25
,
2
]
for the application of
\beta
-type numbers in characterizing the rectifiability of sets.

We note that the assumptions on
K
imply that
H
is of class
C^{2,\gamma}
and uniformly convex away from the origin.

Remark 1.3
.

We say that a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has finite
(n-1)
-dimensional upper Minkowski content if there exists
M>0
such that

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{|\mathcal{N}_{r}(\partial\Omega)|}{r}\leq M<\infty,

where, for
r>0
, the
r
-neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
is

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\partial\Omega):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ {\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(x,\partial\Omega)\leq r\,{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,diam\,}}(\Omega)\right\}.

Note that, via a Besicovitch covering, one can directly conclude from (
1.5
) that
\partial\Omega
has a finite
Minkowski content with
M=M(n,A_{2})
.

Remark 1.4
.

When there are finitely many cusps along
\partial\Omega
, our method can still be applied, even though the lower bound in the
Ahlfors regularity assumption (
1.5
) does not necessarily hold. Indeed, this lower bound is only used to
control the number of “bad” balls (see the last conclusion in Proposition
A.2
and its application in the proof of
Lemma
2.2
). Thus, when there are finitely many cusps, one can directly cut off a suitable neighborhood of the cusps in the argument.

The manuscript is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we establish the volume identity (
2.14
) by following Weinberger’s original approach. The primary challenge
arises from the nonlinearity of the operator, which restricts the regularity of the solutions: the original proof relies on global
higher-order differentiability properties that are not guaranteed in this setting. To be specific, if
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)

were known, the proof could be adapted from
[
12
]
with minor technical adjustments. However, while local
W^{2,2}

regularity is available in our setup, its global counterpart remains unclear, even in Lipschitz domains.

To address this issue, we exploit the geometric properties encoded by the
\beta
-number (
1.4
)
(see Proposition
A.2
in the appendix). In addition, we prove a vanishing property for the Hessian of
u

(see Item (5) in Lemma
2.1
). These results enable us to prove the validity of the crucial estimate (
2.3
),
which would follow directly from the chain rule under the assumption
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
.

In Section 3, we analyze Green’s function for the linearized operator and conclude Theorem
1.1
via classical

P
-function theory.

\frac{u(x+\varepsilon x)}{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{a}(x)\,(-\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+},\qquad\frac{u(x-\varepsilon x)}{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{a}(x)\,(\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+}.

Therefore

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{u(x+\varepsilon x)-u(x-\varepsilon x)}{2\varepsilon}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathbf{a}(x)}{2}\Big((-\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+}-(\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+}\Big)=-\frac{\mathbf{a}(x)}{2}\,(\nu_{x}\cdot x),

since for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
(-s)_{+}-s_{+}=-s
.
By dominated convergence (recall
|\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\leq C
) and
\mathbf{a}(x)H(-\nu_{x})=\mathbf{c}
, we obtain

(2.18)

-\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\to\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Step 4: Expand
\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}
and split the RHS.

We compute

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x)-(1-\varepsilon)\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x)-\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x)+\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2}.

Accordingly,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u(x))\cdot\frac{\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x)+\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2}\,dx}_{=:R_{1}(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u(x))\cdot\frac{\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x)-\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2\varepsilon}\,dx}_{=:R_{2}(\varepsilon)}.

Step 5: Limit of
R_{1}(\varepsilon)
.

Since
\nabla u((1\pm\varepsilon)x)\to\nabla u(x)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
DV(\nabla u)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, dominated convergence yields

R_{1}(\varepsilon)\to\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla u\,dx.

Testing (
1.6
) with
\varphi=u
(by density in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
) gives

\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla u\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u\,dx,

hence

(2.19)

R_{1}(\varepsilon)\to\int_{\Omega}u\,dx.

Step 6: Treat
R_{2}(\varepsilon)
via Lemma
2.2
.

By Lemma
2.2
(with
\alpha_{\pm\varepsilon}(x)=\nabla u((1\pm\varepsilon)x)
), we have

\displaystyle R_{2}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\frac{DV(\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x))-DV(\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x))}{2\varepsilon}\,dx+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,\frac{{\rm div}\!\big(DV(\nabla u((1+\varepsilon)x))\big)-{\rm div}\!\big(DV(\nabla u((1-\varepsilon)x))\big)}{2\varepsilon}\,dx+o(1),

where we integrated by parts and used
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Since

{\rm div}(DV(\nabla u((1\pm\varepsilon)x)))=(1\pm\varepsilon)\,\Delta_{H}u((1\pm\varepsilon)x),

we get, by choosing
\varepsilon
as the sequence taking the limit inferior in Lemma
2.2
,

(2.20)

R_{2}(\varepsilon)=-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\Delta_{H}u((1+\varepsilon)x)-(1-\varepsilon)\Delta_{H}u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2\varepsilon}\,dx+o(1).

Step 7: Compute the integral in (
2.20
).

Set

J(\varepsilon):=\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\Delta_{H}u((1+\varepsilon)x)-(1-\varepsilon)\Delta_{H}u((1-\varepsilon)x)}{2\varepsilon}\,dx.

Using (
1.6
), i.e.

\Delta_{H}u=\mathbf{c}H(-\nu)\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\,dx,

a change of variables gives

\displaystyle J(\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{\mathbf{c}}{2\varepsilon}\Bigg[\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\partial^{*}\Omega}(1+\varepsilon)\,u(x)\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\partial^{*}\Omega}(1-\varepsilon)\,u(x)\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Bigg[-\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}(1+\varepsilon)\,u(x)\,dx+\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}(1-\varepsilon)\,u(x)\,dx\Bigg].

Boundary part.

Since dilation preserves normals and
d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
scales by
(1\pm\varepsilon)^{-(n-1)}
, we can rewrite the boundary term as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{c}}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\Bigg[\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\partial^{*}\Omega}(1+\varepsilon)\,u(x)\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x)-\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\partial^{*}\Omega}(1-\varepsilon)\,u(x)\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)\,H(-\nu_{x})\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x),

where we set

(2.21)

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2-n}u((1+\varepsilon)^{-1}x)-(1-\varepsilon)^{2-n}u((1-\varepsilon)^{-1}x)}{2\varepsilon}.

Volume part.

Since
0\in\Omega
, for
\varepsilon>0
small we have
\Omega\subset(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega
and
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega\subset\Omega
.
Using that
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, we obtain

\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u\,dx.

Hence the volume part equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Bigg[-\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}(1+\varepsilon)\,u\,dx+\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}(1-\varepsilon)\,u\,dx\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Bigg[-(1+\varepsilon)\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx+(1-\varepsilon)\int_{\Omega}u\,dx\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx+\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega\setminus(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx.

Now, if
x\in\Omega\setminus(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega
, then
x\in\Omega
but
(1+\varepsilon)x\notin\Omega
.
Therefore the segment
[x,(1+\varepsilon)x]
meets
\partial\Omega
, and hence

{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(x,\partial\Omega)\leq|(1+\varepsilon)x-x|=\varepsilon|x|\leq\varepsilon\,{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,diam\,}}(\Omega).

This shows

\Omega\setminus(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega\subset\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega).

Using the boundary linear growth
u(x)\leq C\,{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(x,\partial\Omega)
(Lemma
2.1
(2) and the fact
u=0
outside),
we get
u\leq C\varepsilon
on
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega)
, therefore

\int_{\Omega\setminus(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx\leq C\varepsilon\,|\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega)|.

Since
|\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega)|=O(\varepsilon)
(by (
1.5
)),

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega\setminus(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx=O(\varepsilon)\to 0.

Therefore the whole volume part equals

-\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega}u\,dx+o(1)=-\int_{\Omega}u\,dx+o(1),

since
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\Omega\uparrow\Omega
and
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
.

Putting boundary + volume together.

We have shown

(2.22)

J(\varepsilon)=\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\psi_{\varepsilon}\,H(-\nu)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega}u\,dx+o(1).

Step 8: Boundary limit for
\psi_{\varepsilon}
.

Fix
x\in\partial^{*}\Omega
. Note that

(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}x=x-\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}x,\qquad(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}x=x+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}x.

Applying Lemma
2.1
(4) with
r=\frac{\varepsilon}{1\pm\varepsilon}
and
z=\mp x
, we obtain

\frac{u((1+\varepsilon)^{-1}x)}{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{a}(x)\,(\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+},\qquad\frac{u((1-\varepsilon)^{-1}x)}{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{a}(x)\,(-\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+}.

Since
(1\pm\varepsilon)^{2-n}\to 1
, it follows from (
2.21
) that

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)\to\frac{\mathbf{a}(x)}{2}\Big((\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+}-(-\nu_{x}\cdot x)_{+}\Big)=\frac{\mathbf{a}(x)}{2}\,(\nu_{x}\cdot x).

By dominated convergence and
\mathbf{a}(x)H(-\nu_{x})=\mathbf{c}
we conclude

(2.23)

\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\psi_{\varepsilon}\,H(-\nu)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}.

Step 9: Conclude the limit of
R_{2}(\varepsilon)
.

Combining (
2.20
), (
2.22
), and (
2.23
) gives

(2.24)

R_{2}(\varepsilon)\to-\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Omega}u\,dx.

Step 10: Finish the identity.

Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
2.16
) and using (
2.17
), (
2.18
), (
2.19
), and (
2.24
),
we obtain

-n\int_{\Omega}u\,dx+\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}=2\int_{\Omega}u\,dx-\frac{\mathbf{c}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}.

Therefore,

(n+2)\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\mathbf{c}^{2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}.

Finally, by the divergence theorem for sets of finite perimeter (with
\nu_{x}
the outer normal),

\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}(\nu_{x}\cdot x)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\Omega}{\rm div}(x)\,dx=n|\Omega|.

This proves (
2.14
).
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

By Lemma
2.1
(3), we may assume without loss of generality that
\Omega
is open (replacing it by its
open representative
\mathring{\Omega}=\{u>0\}
).

Set

\mathcal{A}(x):=D^{2}V(\nabla u(x))\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in\Omega,

and define the linearized operator

L_{\mathcal{A}}:={\rm div}(\mathcal{A}\nabla\cdot).

Since
K
is uniformly convex and
\partial K\in C^{2,\gamma}
, the matrix
D^{2}V
is bounded and uniformly elliptic on

\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
; hence
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
is bounded and uniformly elliptic in
\Omega
(defining
\mathcal{A}
arbitrarily
on the negligible set
\{\nabla u=0\}
if needed).

3.1.
Green function and harmonic measure for
L_{\mathcal{A}}

Let
\{\Omega_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an exhausting sequence of smooth open sets with

\Omega_{k}\subset\subset\Omega_{k+1}\subset\subset\Omega,\qquad\bigcup_{k}\Omega_{k}=\Omega.

Fix
x\in\Omega
. For
k
large enough so that
x\in\Omega_{k}
, let
G_{x,k}
be the unique weak solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x,k}=-\delta_{x},&\text{in }\Omega_{k},\\[2.0pt]
G_{x,k}=0,&\text{a.e.\ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega_{k}.\end{array}\right.

By the maximum principle,
\{G_{x,k}\}
is monotonically increasing in
k
, hence we can define

G_{x}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}G_{x,k}\qquad\text{pointwise in }\Omega\setminus\{x\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
satisfy the assumptions in Theorem
1.1
and let
u
solve (
1.6
).
Fix
x\in\Omega
and let
G_{x}
be the Green function constructed above. Then:

(1)

There exist
\rho=\rho(x,\Omega)>0
and
M=M(x,\Omega)>0
such that

(3.1)

0\leq G_{x}\leq Mu\qquad\text{in }\Omega\setminus B_{\rho}(x).

In particular, since
u
is Lipschitz and vanishes on
\partial\Omega
in the trace sense,

G_{x}(y)\leq C\,{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(y,\partial\Omega)\qquad\forall\,y\in\Omega\setminus B_{\rho}(x).

(2)

Extending
G_{x}
by
0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, the distribution
L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x}
is a Radon measure
supported on
\partial^{*}\Omega\cup\{x\}
. Moreover, there exists a bounded Borel function

\alpha_{x}:\partial^{*}\Omega\to[0,\infty)
such that

(3.2)

L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x}=\alpha_{x}\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega-\delta_{x}\qquad\text{in }\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and

(3.3)

\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\alpha_{x}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}=1.

Proof.

Step 1: Comparison with
u
.

Choose
\rho=\rho(x,\Omega)>0
so that
\overline{B_{\rho}(x)}\subset\subset\Omega
.
By the strong maximum principle,
u>0
in
\Omega
, hence
u
has a positive minimum on
\partial B_{\rho}(x)
.
Since
G_{x,k}
is smooth in
\Omega_{k}\setminus\{x\}
and
G_{x,k}\to 0
on
\partial\Omega_{k}
, we may choose

M=M(x,\Omega)>0
so large that for all
k
large enough,

G_{x,k}<Mu\quad\text{on }\partial B_{\rho}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad G_{x,k}\leq Mu\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{k}.

Inside
\Omega_{k}\setminus B_{\rho}(x)
we have

L_{\mathcal{A}}(Mu-G_{x,k})=ML_{\mathcal{A}}u-L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x,k}=M(-1)-0<0,

so by the maximum principle
G_{x,k}\leq Mu
in
\Omega_{k}\setminus B_{\rho}(x)
.
Letting
k\to\infty
gives (
3.1
).

Step 2: Measure representation.

Extend
G_{x}
by
0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Then
G_{x}\in W^{1,2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\})
and

L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x}
is a distribution supported in
\partial\Omega\cup\{x\}
.
Using the linear growth bound in (1) and standard Caccioppoli-type estimates for uniformly elliptic divergence-form operators,
one obtains that for every
y\in\partial\Omega
and all
r>0
small,

|L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x}|\bigl(B_{r}(y)\bigr)\leq C\,r^{n-1}.

In particular,
L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x}+\delta_{x}
is a finite Radon measure with
(n-1)
-dimensional growth, hence it is absolutely
continuous with respect to
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega
. This yields (
3.2
) for some bounded

\alpha_{x}\geq 0
.

Finally, testing (
3.2
) against the constant function
1
gives

0=\langle L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x},1\rangle=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\alpha_{x}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}-1,

which proves (
3.3
).
∎

Remark 3.2

(Representation via the boundary measure
\alpha_{x}\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
)
.

Fix
x\in\Omega
, let
\Omega_{k}\subset\subset\Omega
be a smooth exhaustion as above, and let
G_{x,k}
be the Green function for

L_{\mathcal{A}}
in
\Omega_{k}
with a zero Dirichlet condition. Define the boundary measure

d\omega_{x,k}(y):=-\mathcal{A}(y)\nabla G_{x,k}(y)\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{k}}(y)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(y)\qquad(y\in\partial\Omega_{k}),

where
\nu_{\Omega_{k}}
is the
outer
unit normal of
\Omega_{k}
. Then
\omega_{x,k}
is a probability measure and, for every

v\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
satisfying
L_{\mathcal{A}}v=0
in
\Omega
, one has the Green representation

v(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}v\,d\omega_{x,k}.

Indeed, since
G_{x,k}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{k}
, Green’s identity gives

0=\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{A}\nabla v\cdot\nabla G_{x,k}\,dx=v(x)+\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}v\,\mathcal{A}\nabla G_{x,k}\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{k}}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1},

hence
v(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}v\,d\omega_{x,k}
.
Moreover, extending
G_{x,k}
by
0
outside
\Omega_{k}
, one has the measure identity

L_{\mathcal{A}}G_{x,k}=\omega_{x,k}-\delta_{x}\quad\text{in }\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

so letting
k\to\infty
and using Lemma
3.1
(2) yields

\omega_{x,k}\rightharpoonup\alpha_{x}\,\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega
.
Thus, whenever
v
admits an approximate boundary trace
v^{*}
on
\partial^{*}\Omega
, then

v(x)=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}v^{*}(y)\,\alpha_{x}(y)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(y).

(A closely related argument in the Laplacian case can be found in
[
12
, Section 3]
.)

3.2.
A maximum principle for
H(\nabla u)

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded set satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
1.1
, and let
u
solve
(
1.6
). Then

\sup_{\Omega}H(\nabla u)\leq\mathbf{c}.

Proof.

Fix
e\in K
(equivalently,
H_{*}(e)\leq 1
). Since
u\in W^{2,2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
and

{\rm div}(DV(\nabla u))=-1
in
\Omega
, we may differentiate the equation in distributions as follows.
Take
\psi\in C_{c}^{2}(\Omega)
and test (
1.6
) with
\varphi=\partial_{e}\psi
; since

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{e}\psi\,dx=0
, we obtain

0=\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla(\partial_{e}\psi)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{e}\!\bigl(DV(\nabla u)\bigr)\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx.

Using
\partial_{e}(DV(\nabla u))=D^{2}V(\nabla u)\,\nabla(\partial_{e}u)=\mathcal{A}\,\nabla(\partial_{e}u)
a.e., we conclude that

v_{e}:=\partial_{e}u
is a weak solution of

L_{\mathcal{A}}v_{e}=0\qquad\text{in }\Omega.

Fix
x\in\Omega
and let
\alpha_{x}
be given by Lemma
3.1
(2).
Applying Remark
3.2
to the
L_{\mathcal{A}}
-harmonic function
v_{e}=\partial_{e}u
, we obtain

(3.4)

v_{e}(x)=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}v_{e}^{*}(y)\,\alpha_{x}(y)\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(y),

where
v_{e}^{*}
denotes the approximate boundary trace on
\partial^{*}\Omega
.
By Lemma
2.1
(4), for
\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
-a.e.
y\in\partial^{*}\Omega
the interior gradient has the approximate
limit
\nabla u(y)=-\mathbf{a}(y)\nu_{y}
, hence

v_{e}^{*}(y)=\nabla u(y)\cdot e=-\mathbf{a}(y)\,\nu_{y}\cdot e.

Since
e\in K
, we have
(-\nu_{y})\cdot e\leq\sup_{z\in K}(-\nu_{y})\cdot z=H(-\nu_{y})
, and Lemma
2.1
(4) gives

\mathbf{a}(y)H(-\nu_{y})=\mathbf{c}
. Hence

v_{e}^{*}(y)\leq\mathbf{a}(y)\,H(-\nu_{y})=\mathbf{c}\qquad\text{for $\mathscr{H}^{n-1}$-a.e.\ }y\in\partial^{*}\Omega.

Plugging this into (

) and using (

) yields

v_{e}(x)\leq\mathbf{c}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\alpha_{x}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}=\mathbf{c}.

Since

and

were arbitrary, we conclude

and taking the supremum over

for all

.

3.3.
Conclusion via the
P
-function

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemma
2.1
(3), we may assume that
\Omega
is open. Define the
P
-function

P(x)=H(\nabla u(x))^{2}+\frac{2}{n}u(x)=2V(\nabla u(x))+\frac{2}{n}u(x)\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in\Omega.

Step 1: Subharmonicity of
P
.

Since
u\in W^{2,2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
and
L_{\mathcal{A}}u={\rm div}(\mathcal{A}\nabla u)={\rm div}(DV(\nabla u))=-1
in
\Omega
, a standard
computation (Weinberger-type identity) gives

(3.5)

L_{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(V(\nabla u)\bigr)=\bigl|\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}D^{2}u\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\bigr|^{2}\qquad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega,

hence

L_{\mathcal{A}}P=2\bigl|\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}D^{2}u\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\bigr|^{2}-\frac{2}{n}.

At each point, set
B:=\mathcal{A}^{1/2}D^{2}u\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}
, which is symmetric. Then

{\rm tr}(B)={\rm tr}(\mathcal{A}D^{2}u)=\Delta_{H}u=-1,

and by Cauchy–Schwarz,

|B|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n}\,{\rm tr}(B)^{2}=\frac{1}{n}.

Therefore
L_{\mathcal{A}}P\geq 0
in
\Omega
in the weak sense.

Step 2: Maximum principle and sharp bound for
P
.

Fix
\eta>0
and consider the superlevel set
\{u\geq\eta\}\subset\subset\Omega
. Since
P
is
L_{\mathcal{A}}
-subharmonic,
the weak maximum principle yields

{\rm\,esssup\,}_{\{u\geq\eta\}}P={\rm\,esssup\,}_{\partial\{u\geq\eta\}}P.

On
\partial\{u\geq\eta\}
we have
u=\eta
, and by Proposition
3.3
,

H(\nabla u)\leq\mathbf{c}
. Hence

P\leq\mathbf{c}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\eta\qquad\text{on }\partial\{u\geq\eta\}.

Letting
\eta\downarrow 0
gives

(3.6)

P\leq\mathbf{c}^{2}\qquad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega.

Step 3: Rigidity from the volume identity.

Using
{\rm div}(DV(\nabla u))=-1
in
\Omega
and
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have

\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\int_{\Omega}DV(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla u\,dx=\int_{\Omega}2V(\nabla u)\,dx.

Therefore,

\int_{\Omega}P\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(2V(\nabla u)+\frac{2}{n}u\right)\,dx=\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\frac{n+2}{n}\int_{\Omega}u\,dx.

By Lemma
2.3
,
\frac{n+2}{n}\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=\mathbf{c}^{2}|\Omega|
, hence

\int_{\Omega}P\,dx=\mathbf{c}^{2}|\Omega|.

Together with (
3.6
) this forces

P\equiv\mathbf{c}^{2}\qquad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega,

and consequently
L_{\mathcal{A}}P\equiv 0
. In particular, equality holds in the Cauchy–Schwarz step, so

B=\mathcal{A}^{1/2}D^{2}u\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}
must be a scalar multiple of the identity matrix, namely

\mathcal{A}^{1/2}D^{2}u\,\mathcal{A}^{1/2}=-\frac{1}{n}I,

or equivalently,

D(DV(\nabla u))=\mathcal{A}D^{2}u=-\frac{1}{n}I\qquad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega.

As shown for instance in
[
7
, (5.17)–(5.19)]
, this rigidity implies that, up to a translation,

u(x)=\frac{r^{2}-H_{*}^{2}(x)}{2n}

for some
r>0
, and hence
\Omega=\{u>0\}
is homothetic to
K
.
∎

Appendix A
A covering lemma under ADR with a
1/|\log r|
gain at selected scales

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded set of finite perimeter, with reduced boundary
\partial^{*}\Omega
.
For
r>0
and a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write

\mathcal{N}_{r}(E):=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ {\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(z,E)\leq r\}.

We also denote by
\omega_{n-1}
the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the unit ball in an linear hyperplane,
i.e.
\omega_{n-1}=\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(B_{1}\cap P)
for any linear hyperplane
P
.

For
x\in\partial^{*}\Omega
and
r>0
define the
\beta
-number

(A.1)

\beta(x,r):=\inf_{P}\;r^{1-n}\!\!\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x,r)}\frac{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}}(z,P)}{r}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z),

where the infimum runs over all affine hyperplanes
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assume the global
\beta
-square bound

(A.2)

\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}\beta(x,s)^{2}\,\frac{ds}{s}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(x)\ \leq\ A_{1}<\infty.

Definition A.1

(Ahlfors–David regularity (ADR))
.

We say that
\partial^{*}\Omega
is
Ahlfors–David regular
if there exist
0<r_{\rm ADR}\leq 1
and constants

0<c_{\rm ADR}\leq C_{\rm ADR}<\infty
such that

(A.3)

c_{\rm ADR}\,r^{n-1}\ \leq\ \mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x,r))\ \leq\ C_{\rm ADR}\,r^{n-1}\quad\forall\,x\in\partial^{*}\Omega,\ \ 0<r<r_{\rm ADR}.

Proposition A.2

(ADR covering with density control and
1/|\log r|
gain on
\beta
-bad)
.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
and let
\theta:(0,1)\to(0,1)
be any function with
\theta(\eta)\downarrow 0
as
\eta\downarrow 0
.
Then there exist
r_{0}=r_{0}(\Omega,\eta,{\rm ADR})\in(0,r_{\rm ADR})
and a Borel set
F_{\eta}\subset\partial^{*}\Omega
such that, for all
x\in F_{\eta}
and
0<s\leq r_{0}
,

(A.4)

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\setminus F_{\eta})\ \leq\ \theta(\eta)\,\eta^{\,n-1},\qquad\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x,s))}{\omega_{n-1}s^{n-1}}\in\Big(1-\tfrac{\eta}{10},\,1+\tfrac{\eta}{10}\Big).

Moreover, there exists a sequence
r_{j}\downarrow 0
such that for each
j
there is a Besicovitch covering

\mathcal{B}_{r_{j}}=\{B(x_{h},r_{j})\}_{h}

of
\mathcal{N}_{r_{j}/2}(\partial\Omega)
, with centers
x_{h}\in\partial^{*}\Omega
, enjoying the following properties:

(1)

If
x_{h}\in F_{\eta}
and
\beta(x_{h},r_{j})\leq\eta
, then

\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},r_{j}))}{\omega_{n-1}r_{j}^{n-1}}\in(1-\eta,\,1+\eta).

(2)

Let
N_{\beta}(r_{j}):=\#\{h:\ \beta(x_{h},r_{j})>\eta\}
. Then

N_{\beta}(r_{j})\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,\eta^{-2}\,A_{1}\ \frac{1}{|\log r_{j}|}\ r_{j}^{-(n-1)}.

(3)

For each
0<r\leq r_{0}
, define

D_{r,\eta}:=\Big\{x\in\partial^{*}\Omega:\ \Big|\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x,r))}{\omega_{n-1}r^{n-1}}-1\Big|>\eta\Big\}.

Let
N_{\rm dens}(r):=\#\{h:\ x_{h}\in D_{r,\eta}\}
for a maximal
r/5
-separated net
\{x_{h}\}\subset\partial^{*}\Omega
. Then

N_{\rm dens}(r)\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,\theta(\eta)\ r^{-(n-1)}.

Consequently,

\#\Big(\mathcal{B}_{r_{j}}\setminus\{B(x_{h},r_{j}):\ x_{h}\in F_{\eta},\ \beta(x_{h},r_{j})\leq\eta\}\Big)\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,r_{j}^{-(n-1)}\!\left(\frac{\eta^{-2}A_{1}}{|\log r_{j}|}\ +\ \theta(\eta)\right).

To prove the proposition, we first establish the following preliminary result:

Lemma A.3

(Lipschitz dependence on the center)
.

Assume (
A.3
). Fix
r\in(0,r_{\rm ADR})
. For all
x,y\in\partial^{*}\Omega
with
\delta:=|x-y|\leq r/2
,

(A.5)

\Big|\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\!\big(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x,r)\big)-\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\!\big(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(y,r)\big)\Big|\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,\frac{\delta}{r}\,r^{n-1}.

Equivalently, for

f_{r}(x):=\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x,r))}{\omega_{n-1}r^{n-1}},

we have

|f_{r}(x)-f_{r}(y)|\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,\frac{|x-y|}{r}.

Proof.

Let
\mu:=\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\llcorner\partial^{*}\Omega
. For Borel sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has

|\mu(A)-\mu(B)|\leq\mu(A\triangle B)
, hence

\Big|\mu(B(x,r))-\mu(B(y,r))\Big|\leq\mu\big(B(x,r)\triangle B(y,r)\big).

If
\delta=|x-y|\leq r/2
, then

B(x,r)\triangle B(y,r)\subset\{z:\ ||z-x|-r|<\delta\}=B(x,r+\delta)\setminus\overline{B(x,r-\delta)}.

By the upper ADR bound,

\mu\big(B(x,r)\triangle B(y,r)\big)\leq\mu(B(x,r+\delta))-\mu(B(x,r-\delta))\leq C_{\rm ADR}\big((r+\delta)^{n-1}-(r-\delta)^{n-1}\big),

and the last difference is bounded by
C(n)\,\delta\,r^{n-2}
. This gives (
A.5
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
A.2
.

Step 1: An Egorov set for density.

For
x\in\partial^{*}\Omega
let
f_{r}(x)
be as in Lemma
A.3
.
By De Giorgi’s structure theorem
[
19
, Theorem 15.9]
,
f_{s}(x)\to 1
as
s\downarrow 0
for

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}
-a.e.
x\in\partial^{*}\Omega
.
Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
and define

A_{m}:=\left\{x\in\partial^{*}\Omega:\ \sup_{0<s\leq 1/m}|f_{s}(x)-1|\leq\tfrac{\eta}{10}\right\},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

Then
A_{m}\uparrow\partial^{*}\Omega
up to a null set, hence

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\setminus A_{m})\downarrow 0
as
m\to\infty
.
Choose
m
so large that

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\setminus A_{m})\ \leq\ \theta(\eta)\,\eta^{\,n-1}.

Set
F_{\eta}:=A_{m}
and
r_{0}:=1/m
. This gives (
A.4
).

Step 2: Pointwise control of the number of
\beta
-bad centers.

Fix
s\in(0,r_{0})
. Let
\{x_{h}\}\subset\partial^{*}\Omega
be a maximal
s/5
-separated set, and set

\mathcal{B}_{s}=\{B(x_{h},s)\}
.
Write
S_{s}:=\{h:\ \beta(x_{h},s)>\eta\}
.
If
z\in B(x_{h},s/10)
then
B(x_{h},s)\subset B(z,2s)
, and therefore

(A.6)

\beta(x_{h},s)\ \leq\ 2^{n}\,\beta(z,2s).

The balls
\{B(x_{h},s/10)\}_{h\in S_{s}}
are disjoint; by the lower ADR bound,

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},s/10))\geq c_{\rm ADR}(s/10)^{n-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\eta^{2}\,\#S_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h\in S_{s}}\beta(x_{h},s)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h\in S_{s}}\frac{2^{2n}}{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},s/10))}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},s/10)}\beta(z,2s)^{2}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C(n,{\rm ADR})\,s^{-(n-1)}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\beta(z,2s)^{2}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z).

That is,

(A.7)

N_{\beta}(s):=\#S_{s}\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,s^{-(n-1)}\,\eta^{-2}\!\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\beta(z,2s)^{2}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z).

Step 3: Selecting scales with a
(1/|\log r|)
-gain.

For
k\geq 1
, let
I_{k}=[2^{-k-1},2^{-k}]
. Integrating (
A.7
) on
I_{k}
and changing variables
t=2s
, we get

\int_{I_{k}}N_{\beta}(s)\,\frac{ds}{s}\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,2^{(n-1)k}\,\eta^{-2}\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\int_{2^{-k}}^{2^{-k+1}}\beta(z,t)^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z).

Define

a_{k}:=\int_{\partial^{*}\Omega}\int_{2^{-k}}^{2^{-k+1}}\beta(z,t)^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(z),

so that
\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}\leq A_{1}
by (
A.2
).
Given
K\in\mathbb{N}
large, choose
k\in\{K/2,\dots,K\}
such that
a_{k}\leq 2A_{1}/K
(by averaging).
Then, by averaging
N_{\beta}(s)
over
I_{k}
with respect to
ds/s
, there exists
s_{k}\in I_{k}
such that

N_{\beta}(s_{k})\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{I_{k}}N_{\beta}(s)\,\frac{ds}{s}\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,2^{(n-1)k}\,\frac{\eta^{-2}A_{1}}{K}.

Since
s_{k}\in[2^{-k-1},2^{-k}]
, one has
2^{(n-1)k}\leq C\,s_{k}^{-(n-1)}
and also
K\sim k\sim|\log s_{k}|
, hence

N_{\beta}(s_{k})\ \leq\ C(n,{\rm ADR})\,s_{k}^{-(n-1)}\,\frac{\eta^{-2}A_{1}}{|\log s_{k}|}.

Choosing a sequence
K\to\infty
and setting
r_{j}:=s_{k}
yields (
2
).

Step 4: Counting density-bad centers.

Fix
r\in(0,r_{0}]
and define

D_{r,\lambda}:=\{x\in\partial^{*}\Omega:\ |f_{r}(x)-1|>\lambda\}.

By Lemma
A.3
, there exists
C_{0}=C_{0}(n,{\rm ADR})
such that

|f_{r}(x)-f_{r}(y)|\leq C_{0}|x-y|/r
whenever
|x-y|\leq r/2
.

Let
\{x_{h}\}\subset\partial^{*}\Omega
be a maximal
r/5
-separated net, and suppose
x_{h}\in D_{r,\eta}
.
We may assume that
C_{0}\geq 10
so that,

0<\rho=\frac{\eta}{2C_{0}}r\leq\frac{r}{20}\,r.

Then for any
y\in\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},\rho)
,

|f_{r}(y)-1|\geq|f_{r}(x_{h})-1|-|f_{r}(x_{h})-f_{r}(y)|>\eta-\eta/2=\eta/2,

so
B(x_{h},\rho)\cap\partial^{*}\Omega\subset D_{r,\eta/2}
.
Since
\rho\leq r/20
, the balls
\{B(x_{h},\rho)\}
are pairwise disjoint. By the lower ADR bound,

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}\!\big(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},\rho)\big)\ \geq\ c_{\rm ADR}\,\rho^{n-1}\ \geq\ c(n,{\rm ADR})\,(\eta r)^{n-1}.

Summing over
x_{h}\in D_{r,\eta}
gives

N_{\rm dens}(r)\cdot c(n,{\rm ADR})\,(\eta r)^{n-1}\ \leq\ \mathscr{H}^{n-1}(D_{r,\eta/2}).

By (
A.4
), on
F_{\eta}
one has
|f_{r}-1|\leq\eta/10\leq\eta/2
for all
r\leq r_{0}
, hence

D_{r,\eta/2}\subset\partial^{*}\Omega\setminus F_{\eta}
. Therefore,

\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(D_{r,\eta/2})\leq\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\setminus F_{\eta})\leq\theta(\eta)\,\eta^{n-1},

which yields (
3
).

Step 5: Item (
1
) and the final count.

If
x_{h}\in F_{\eta}
and
\beta(x_{h},r_{j})\leq\eta
, then (
A.4
) implies

\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(\partial^{*}\Omega\cap B(x_{h},r_{j}))}{\omega_{n-1}r_{j}^{n-1}}\in\Big(1-\tfrac{\eta}{10},\,1+\tfrac{\eta}{10}\Big)\subset(1-\eta,1+\eta),

which is (
1
). Combining (
2
) and (
3
) yields the concluding estimate.
∎
