Sequential densities of rational languages
Alexi Block Gorman, Dominique Perrin

Abstract
We introduce the notion of density of a rational language with respect to a sequence of probability measures. We prove that if ( μ n ) (\mu_{n}) is a sequence of Bernoulli measures converging to a positive Bernoulli measure μ ¯ \overline{\mu} , the sequential density is the ordinary density with respect to μ ¯ \overline{\mu} . We also prove that if ( μ n ) (\mu_{n}) is a sequence of invariant probability measures converging in the strong sense to an invariant probability measure μ ¯ \overline{\mu} , then the sequential density of every rational language exists for this sequence.



1
Introduction

The density of rational languages with respect Bernoulli measures
has been widely studied (see
[
3
]

for references).
It has been recently shown that it exists in the more general setting of invariant probability measures
[
4
]
.
In
[
7
]
, another generalization of the Bernoulli measure case was studied, namely the density of rational languages with respect to a sequence of Bernoulli measures.
This density is shown in
[
7
]
to always exist.

In this paper, we introduce the notion of sequential density of a rational language with respect to a sequence
of probability measures, thereby considering a setting that is more general than both

[
4
]
and
[
7
]
.
We prove a result which describes the sequential density in the case of a sequence of Bernoulli measures
converging to a positive measure (Theorem
4.1
).
This also provides a direct proof of Lynch’s result in this setting.

We also prove the existence of the sequential density of every rational language,
with respect to a sequence
\mu
of invariant probability measures converging strongly
to an invariant probability measure (Theorem
5.1
). This generalizes
the main result of
[
7
]
in which only Bernoulli measures are considered.
The proof uses the main result of
[
4
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
,
we define the basic notions of symbolic dynamics and probability theory used in the paper.
In Section
3
, we define sequential densities.
We prove Theorem
4.1
in Section
4

and Theorem
5.1
in
Section
5
.
We give examples in Section
6

that demonstrate in which cases this density might not exist, or for which the density
does
exist, but does not agree with the density of limiting measure to which the sequence converges.
Finally, we consider in Section
7

the notion of combinatorial density, which is a particular case of sequential density.

2
Preliminaries

We provide a short introduction to key notions coming from symbolic dynamics
(see
[
6
]
for more details).

2.1
Shift spaces

Let
A
be finite set. We denote by
A^{*}
the set of words on the alphabet
A

and by
A^{+}
the set of nonempty words.
A
prefix code
is a subset of
A^{*}
which does
not contain a proper prefix of any of its elements.
Symmetrically, a
suffix code
is a subset of
A^{*}
which does
not contain a proper suffix of any of its elements.

We consider the set
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
of two-sided infinite sequences of elements of

A
as a topological space with the usual topology induced by the distance function

d(x,y)=2^{-r(x,y)}
, where
r(x,y)=\min\{|n|\mid n\in\mathbb{Z},x_{n}\neq y_{n}\}
.
For
x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
i<j
, we denote
x_{[i,j)}=x_{i}\cdots x_{j-1}
.
For
w\in A^{*}
, we denote
[w]=\{x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}\mid x_{[0,|w|)}=w\}

the cylinder with basis
w
.

A
shift space
on
A
is a closed and shift-invariant subset of
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Equivalently,
X

is a shift space if there is a set
W
of words on
A
, which we
call the set of
forbidden blocks
,
such that
X
is the set of sequences
x
without any occurrence of any word in
W
.

The language of
X
, denoted by
\mathcal{L}(X)
, is the set of words
w

which appear in some element of
X
.
We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)
the set of words of length
k
in
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
A
shift of finite type
is a shift space defined by a finite set of forbidden blocks.
A shift space
X
is called
irreducible
if for every
u,w\in\mathcal{L}(X)

there exists
w
such that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Let
X
be a shift space and let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Let
u\mapsto\langle u\rangle

be a bijection from the set
\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)
onto an alphabet
A_{k}
. Let
\varphi_{k}\colon X\to A_{k}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be
the map defined by
y=\varphi_{k}(x)
if
y_{n}=x_{[n,n+k)}
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The image
\varphi_{k}(X)
of
X
under
\varphi_{k}
is a shift space on
A_{k}
called
the
k
-block presentation
of
X
.

2.2
Rational languages

A language on the alphabet
A
is a subset of
A^{*}
.
A finite automaton
\mathcal{A}=(Q,i,T)
on the alphabet
A
is given by a map
(q,a)\mapsto q\cdot a

from the finite set
Q\times A
into
Q
. The map extends in a natural way to a right action of
A

on
Q
. The language
recognized
by the automaton
\mathcal{A}
is the set

L=\{w\in A^{*}\mid i\cdot w\in T\}.

A
rational language
is a language which can be recognized by some finite automaton.

A language
L
is
recognized
by a morphism
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M

from
A^{*}
to a monoid
M
if
L=\varphi^{-1}(N)
for some
N\subset M
.
A language is recognizable if and only if it can be recognized by a morphism
onto a finite monoid.

The language of a shift of finite type is a rational language.
As an equivalent definition, a language is rational if it can be obtained from
the finite subsets of
A^{*}
by a finite number of unions, products (concatenations), and (kleene) star, where
the star of a language
L
is

L^{*}=\{w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}\mid w_{i}\in L,n\geq 0\}.

For example, the language
L=ab\{a,b\}^{*}
of words on the alphabet
\{a,b\}

beginning with
ab
is rational.

A finite monoid
M
is
aperiodic
if there is an integer
n\geq 1
such that

m^{n}=m^{n+1}
for every
m\in M
.
A rational language is
aperiodic
if it can be recognized by a morphism into a finite aperiodic monoid.
For example, the language
(a^{2})^{*}
is rational, but one can show that it is not aperiodic.

Rational languages are definable in the monadic second order logic of the integers with addition,
and aperiodic languages in the corresponding first-order logic (see
[
8
]
).

2.3
Probability measures

We introduce probability measures on shift spaces
(see
[
5
]
for a more complete introduction).

A
probability measure
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a map
\mu
assigning to every Borel subset
U\subseteq A^{\mathbb{Z}}
a real number
\mu(U)
which is
\sigma
-additive

(that is,
\mu(\cup_{n\geq 0}U_{n})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mu(U_{n})
for every sequence
(U_{n})
of pairwise disjoint
Borel sets
U_{n}
) and
\mu(X)=1
.

It follows from
\sigma
-additivity that a measure is
monotone
; that is,

U\subset V
implies
\mu(U)\leq\mu(V)
and that, if

U_{1}\subset U_{2}\subset\ldots
, then
\mu(\cup_{i\geq 1}U_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu(U_{i})
.
For
w\in A^{*}
, we denote
\mu(w)=\mu([w])
.

For each
n\geq 0
, the map
\mu_{n}\colon A^{n}\to[0,1]

defined by
\mu_{n}(w)=\mu(w)
is a probability measure on
A^{n}
called the
n
-th
marginal distribution

of
\mu
.
For a set
W\subset A^{*}
,
we denote by
\mu(W)
the sum
\sum_{w\in W}\mu(w)
, which is a real number or
+\infty
.

A probability measure
\mu
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is
invariant
if
\mu(S^{-1}U)=\mu(U)

for every Borel set
U
.
An invariant probability measure is
ergodic
if for every Borel set
U
invariant under the shift, one has
\mu(U)=0
or
\mu(U)=1
. By the Birkhoff ergodic theorem, an invariant measure

\mu
is ergodic if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu(U\cap S^{-i}V)=\mu(U)\mu(V)

(2.1)

for every pair
U,V
of Borel sets.

Two distinct ergodic measures
\mu,\nu

on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
are
mutually singular
, that is, there
exists a Borel set
U
such that

\mu(U)=\nu(X\setminus U)=0
. Indeed,
let
f
be a measurable function such
that
\int fd\mu\neq\int fd\nu
. Let

U
be the set of
x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(x)

converges to
\int fd\nu
. Then

\mu(U)=\nu(X\setminus U)=0
.

An invariant probability measure
\mu
is
mixing
if (
2.1
) holds without taking the average, that is, if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(U\cap S^{-n}V)=\mu(U)\mu(V)

(2.2)

for every pair
U,V
of Borel sets.

We consider the space of probability measures on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
as a topological
space with the
weak-
*
topology
, in which
\mu_{n}\to\mu

if
\mu_{n}(U)\to\mu(U)
for every Borel set
U
. By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem,
this space is compact. The limit of a sequence of invariant measures is invariant
and the limit of a sequence of ergodic measures is ergodic.

A sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of probability measures on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}

converges in the
strong sense
to a measure
\overline{\mu}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
N\geq 1

such that for every Borel set
U
and every
n\geq N
, one has

|\mu_{n}(U)-\overline{\mu}(U)|\leq\varepsilon
.

A Bernoulli measure is a probability measure
\mu
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}

such that
\mu(uv)=\mu(u)\mu(v)
for every
u,v\in A^{*}
.
A Bernoulli measure is ergodic. If
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}

is a convergent sequence of Bernoulli measures, the limit
is a Bernoulli measure. Indeed, for every
u,v\in A^{*}
,
one has
\mu(uv)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(uv)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(u)\mu_{n}(v)=\mu(u)\mu(v)
.

A
Markov measure
is a measure defined by a stochastic
A\times A
-matrix
P

called its transition matrix and a vector
\pi
, called its
initial vector
,
by

\mu(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})=\pi_{a_{0}}P_{a_{0}a_{1}}\cdots P_{a_{n-2}a_{n-1}}

for
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in A
.
If
\pi P=\pi
, the measure is invariant. An invariant Markov measure defined by
an irreducible matrix
P
is ergodic.

A
k
-step Markov measure
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a measure
\mu
such that
\mu(U)=\nu(\varphi_{k}(U))

for every Borel set
U
, and
for some Markov measure
\nu
on the
k
-block presentation
\varphi_{k}(A^{\mathbb{Z}})
.

The
support
of a probability measure
\mu
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the set of words
w

such that
\mu(w)>0
.
For every irreducible shift of finite type,
there is an ergodic measure supported by
X
, called
its measure of maximal entropy.

Let
M
be an irreducible matrix with left eigenvector
v
and right eigenvector
w

for the dominant eigenvalue
\lambda
, with the normalization
v\cdot w=1
.
The
measure of maximal entropy

\mu

on the shift of finite type defined by
M
is the Markov measure with initial vector
\pi_{i}=v_{i}w_{i}

and transition matrix
P_{ij}=(w_{j}/\lambda w_{i})M_{i,j}
(see
[
6
, chapter 13]
).

Example 2.1

Let
X
be the shift of finite type defined by the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\
1&1&1\\
1&1&1\end{bmatrix}.

Its dominant eigenvalue is
\lambda=(3+\sqrt{5})/2
.
The corresponding left and right eigenvectors
v
and
w
, normalized by
v\cdot w=1
, are

v=\frac{1}{2\lambda-3}\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda-2&1\end{bmatrix},\quad w=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-2\\
1\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\mu_{2}
is the Markov measure defined by

\pi=\frac{1}{2\lambda-3}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-2&\lambda-2&1\end{bmatrix},\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\lambda}&0&\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\\
\frac{\lambda-2}{\lambda}&\frac{1}{\lambda}&\frac{1}{\lambda}\\
\frac{\lambda-2}{\lambda}&\frac{1}{\lambda}&\frac{1}{\lambda}\end{bmatrix}.

3
Sequential densities

Let
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of probability measures
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Given a language
L
on the alphabet
A
, the
sequential density

of
L
with respect to
\mu
is

\delta_{\mu}(L)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu_{i}(L\cap A^{i})

whenever the limit exists. We say that
L
has a density in the strong
sense if the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(L\cap A^{n})
exists, and not only
in average.
When the sequence
(\mu_{n})
is constant, we meet the usual notion of density (see
[
3
]
).

Let us list a few elementary properties of sequential densities, which are actually the same for ordinary densities.
If
L
has a sequential density with respect to
\mu
, then
0\leq\delta_{\mu}(L)\leq 1
.

The sequential density if
finitely additive
; that is, if
L,L^{\prime}
are disjoint languages
having a sequential density (resp. in the strong sense), then

L\cup L^{\prime}
has a sequential density (resp. in the strong sense).

The sequential density is
monotone
, that is, if
L
and
L^{\prime}
have sequential densities (resp. in the
strong sense) and
L\subset L^{\prime}
, then
\delta_{\mu}(L)\leq\delta_{\mu}(L^{\prime})
.

The following result is from
[
7
]
.

Theorem 3.1

If
\mu
is a convergent sequence of Bernoulli measures on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
the sequential density of every rational language exists. If
L
is aperiodic,
the density exists in the strong sense.

This result from
[
7
]
is stated
for
A=\{a,b\}
.
This is not really a restriction
since every alphabet can be encoded with two letters.
Moreover, it is assumed that
\mu_{n}(a)

does not converge to
1
.
Exchanging

a
and
b
allows us to always satisfy this hypothesis.
Finally, various hypotheses
are stipulated concerning the convergence
of the sequence
\mu_{n}(a)
. The various
cases (which appear in the example below)
cover all possibilities, and therefore
there is no need to mention these
cases in the statement.

We first give an example illustrating Lynch’s theorem. For two positive real functions
f
and
g
,
we write
f\ll g
if for every
\epsilon>0

there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f(n)\leq\epsilon g(n)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq n_{0}}
.
We write
f\sim g
if
f(n)/g(n)
converges to
1
.

Example 3.2

Let
A=\{a,b\}
and
L=A^{*}aA^{*}
. We have

\delta_{\mu}(L)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $\mu_{n}(a)\ll n^{-1}$,}\\
1-e^{-c}&\mbox{if $\mu_{n}(a)\sim cn^{-1}$,}\\
1&\mbox{if $n^{-1}\ll\mu_{n}(a)$.}\end{cases}

(3.1)

We shall obtain these values below in Example
4.2
. Note that,
in the second case, the sequential density
exists, but fails to be equal to
\delta_{\overline{\mu}}
.
Indeed, in this case,
\overline{\mu}(a)=0
and therefore
\delta_{\mu}(L)\neq\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L)=0.

The following example illustrates the case
where the limit exists only in average.

Example 3.3

Let
L=(A^{2})^{*}
be the set of words of even length. Then,

\mu_{i}(L\cap A^{i})=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $i$ is even,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Therefore, the sequence
\mu_{i}(L\cap A^{i})

does not converge. One has of course

\delta_{\mu}(L)=1/2
for every sequence
of probability measures
\mu
.

4
A formula for sequential densities

The following result covers Theorem
3.1

when
\mu
converges to a positive measure.
It also proves a stronger property, since we prove that the sequential
density not only exists, but is equal to the density with respect to the limit measure
\overline{\mu}
(we have seen in Example
3.2
that this can fail
to hold if
\overline{\mu}
is not positive).
We give a direct proof.

Theorem 4.1

Let
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of Bernoulli measures on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. If
\mu
converges
to a positive Bernoulli measure
\overline{\mu}
, then for every rational language
L\subset A^{*}
, the sequential
density of
L
is equal to its density with respect to
\overline{\mu}
.
The same holds for densities in the strong sense for an aperiodic language
L
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(Q,i,T)
be a deterministic automaton recognizing
L
.
Since
L
is a finite union of languages
recognized by
(Q,i,\{t\})
for
t\in T
, we may assume that
T=\{t\}
.
Without loss of generality,
we may also assume that every
q\in Q
lies on a path from
i
to
t
. For a Bernoulli measure
\nu
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
let
P(\nu)
be the stochastic
Q\times Q
-matrix
defined by

P(\nu)_{p,q}=\sum_{a\in A,p\cdot a=q}\nu(a).

(4.1)

Since
\overline{\mu}
is positive, the matrix
P(\overline{\mu})
is irreducible.
Since
\mu
converges to
\overline{\mu}
, we may assume that all matrices
P_{n}=P(\mu_{n})
have
period
p
equal to the period of
P(\overline{\mu})
. We may assume
p=1
and therefore
that each
P_{n}
is primitive.

Let
\lambda_{n,k}
,
1\leq k\leq d
be the distinct eigenvalues of
P_{n}
and let
\gamma_{n,k}
be the multiplicity
of
\lambda_{n,k}
in the minimal polynomial of
P_{n}
. We may assume that
\lambda_{n,1}=1
. Since
P_{n}

is primitive, we have
\gamma_{n,1}=1
.

Using the Jordan normal form of
P_{n}
, we may write

P_{n}=U\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&J_{n,2}&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\ddots\\
0&0&\cdots&J_{n,d}\end{bmatrix}U^{-1}

where for
2\leq k\leq d
,
J_{n,k}
is the Jordan block of
P_{n}
corresponding to
\lambda_{n,k}
.
The columns of the matrix
U
are generalized eigenvectors of
P_{n}
.
In particular, the first column is the vector
v
with all components equal to
1

and its first row is the stochastic vector
w_{n}
such that
w_{n}P_{n}=w_{n}
.
Since
P_{n}
converges to
P(\overline{\mu})
, the vectors
w_{n}
tend to the
stochastic vector
w
such that
wP(\overline{\mu})=w
.

Since
P(\overline{\mu})

is primitive, there is
\epsilon>0
such that all eigenvalues
\lambda_{n,k}
for
k\geq 2
satisfy
|\lambda_{n,k}|<1-\epsilon
.
So each
J_{n,k}^{n}
tends to
0
, and therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}^{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}vw_{n}=\lim P(\overline{\mu})^{n}.

This implies that
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L)

for every rational language
L
.
∎

The following result is due, for a single Bernoulli measure, to Schützenberger
[

]

(see also

The statement uses elementary notions for finite monoids (see
[
2
]
). In particular, a finite monoid has a unique minimal ideal, which is the union of the minimal right ideals of
M
, and also the union of the minimal left ideals. For every minimal right ideal
R
and every minimal left ideal
L
, the intersection
R\cap L
is a finite group.
These groups are all isomorphic.
If
M

G
is trivial.

Corollary 4.3

Let
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of Bernoulli measures on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
converging to a positive
Bernoulli measure
\overline{\mu}
. Let
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M

be a morphism onto a finite monoid. Let
K
be the minimal ideal of
M
. Set
\nu=\delta_{\mu}\circ\varphi^{-1}
.
Then, for every
m\in M
, one has the following.

1.

\nu(m)>0
if and only if
m\in K
.

2.

For every
m\in K
, one has

\nu(m)=\frac{\nu(mM)\nu(Mm)}{d},

(4.2)

where
d=\Card(mM\cap Mm)
.

If
M
is aperiodic, then
d=1
and
\delta_{\mu}(L)
exists in the strong sense.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
, we have
\delta_{\mu}=\delta_{\overline{\mu}}
.
Since the above result holds for a positive Bernoulli measure, the statement follows.
∎

5
Convergence in the strong sense

Theorem 5.1

Let
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of invariant probability measures converging in the strong sense
to an invariant probability measure
\overline{\mu}
. Then, for every rational language
L
,
the sequential density of
L
exists and

\delta_{\mu}(L)=\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L)
. If
L
is aperiodic, the sequential density exists in the strong sense.

We use the following result, established in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 5.2

Let
\mu
be an invariant probability measure. Every rational language
L
has a density
with respect to
\mu
. If
L
is aperiodic, the density exists in the strong sense.

We also use the following elementary result from real analysis (see
[
1
, Section 8.20]
).
We say that a sequence
(u_{m,n})
of real numbers converges to
a
if for every
\varepsilon>0
,
there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|u_{m,n}-a|\leq\varepsilon
whenever
m,n\geq N
.
If the sequence converges to
a
,
then the limits
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{m,n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}u_{m,n}

also converge to
a
.
The converse is also true with the additional hypothesis of uniform convergence.

Lemma 5.3

Let
(u_{m,n})_{m,n\geq 0}
be a sequence of real numbers. If

(i)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{m,n}=a
,

(ii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{m,n}
exists uniformly in
m
,

then
(u_{m,n})
converges to
a
.

Proof
of Theorem
5.1
.

Set

u_{m,n}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mu_{n}(L\cap A^{i}).

We have by Theorem
5.2
,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{m,n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\overline{\mu}(L\cap A^{i})=\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L).

On the other hand,

\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{m,n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mu_{n}(L\cap A^{i})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\overline{\mu}(L\cap A^{i}),

uniformly in
m
since the sequence
(\mu_{n})
converges strongly.
Therefore, by Lemma
5.3
, the sequence
(u_{m,n})
converges to
\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L)
.

If
L
is aperiodic, set
v_{m,n}=\mu_{n}(L\cap A^{m})
. By Theorem
5.2
, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{m,n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\overline{\mu}(L\cap A^{m})=\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L),

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{m,n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\overline{\mu}(L\cap A^{m})

uniformly in
m
. By Lemma
5.3
, the sequence
v_{m,n}
converges
to
\delta_{\overline{\mu}}(L)
.

Theorem
5.1
is, however,
of limited application.
In fact, a sequence of ergodic measures never
converges in the strong sense unless
it is eventually constant.
Indeed, let
\mu_{n}
be a sequence of ergodic measures
converging in the strong sense to
\overline{\mu}
. If
\mu_{n}\neq\overline{\mu}

for all
n\geq 0
,
the measures
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
being mutually singular, the sequence
(\mu_{n})

cannot converge in the strong sense to
\overline{\mu}
.

7
Combinatorial density

Let
X
be a shift space on the alphabet
A
. The
combinatorial density

of a language
L\subset A^{*}
is the limit

\delta_{X}(L)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Card(L\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}(X))}{\Card(\mathcal{L}_{i}(X)}

provided the limit exists.

The combinatorial density is a particular case of sequential density.
For each
n\geq 0
, let
\mu_{n}
be
a probability measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that its
n^{th}
marginal distribution for
w\in A^{n}
is the following:

\mu_{n}(w)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Card(\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)}&\mbox{if $w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then,

\mu_{n}(L)=\frac{\Card(L\cap\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)}{\Card(\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)}.

Therefore, the combinatorial density of
L
is equal to its sequential density with respect to
the sequence
(\mu_{n})
.

The following shows that, when they exist, the combinatorial density
of right ideals is its density with respect to some probability measure on
X
.

Proposition 7.1

Let
X
be a shift space such that
\delta_{X}(uA^{*})
exists for each
u\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. There is a probability measure
\mu
on
X
such that
for every
u\in\mathcal{L}(X)
one has

\delta_{X}(uA^{*})=\mu(u).

Proof.

One has for every
u\in A^{*}

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}\delta_{X}(uaA^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Card(uaA^{*}\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}(X))}{\Card(\mathcal{L}_{i}(X))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sum_{a\in A}\Card(uaA^{*}\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}(X))}{\Card(\mathcal{L}_{i}(X))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Card(uA^{*}\cap A^{i})}{\Card(\mathcal{L}_{i}(X))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{X}(uA^{*}).

Since
\delta_{X}(A^{*})=1
, this shows that there is a unique Borel probability measure on
X

such that
\mu([u])=\delta_{X}(uA^{*})
for every
u\in\mathcal{L}(X)
.
∎

Proposition 7.2

Let
X
be a Sturmian shift on
A=\{0,1\}
. For every
u\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, one has

\delta_{X}(uA^{*})=\mu(u),

where
\mu
is the (unique) invariant probability measure on
X
.

Proof.

Let
x\in A^{-\mathbb{N}}
be the unique right-special sequence in
X^{-}
.
The integer

\Card(uA^{*}\cap\mathcal{L}_{n}(X))-1
is equal to the number of right-special words
of length at most
n
beginning with
u
. Since all right-special words are suffixes of
x
,
it is also equal to the number
f_{n}(x,u)

of occurrences of
u
in
x_{(-n,0]}
. Thus

\delta_{X}(uA^{*})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1+f_{n}(x,u)}{n+1}=\mu(u).

∎
