Unveiling the quality profiles of honey from selected districts of South Wollo, Ethiopia
Authors: Wubalem Alebachew, Destaw Worku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study was conducted to assess the physico-chemical characteristics of honey collected from selected districts of the South Wollo Zone of Amhara region, Ethiopia. Thirty honey samples from modern hives were collected across two harvesting seasons and three different agro-ecologies. The result revealed that, the average values for major quality parameters were 19.14 meqkg-1 for the free acidity (FA), 17.43% for the moisture content (MC), 0.09% for the ash content (AC), 3.13 for the pH and 5.49 mg/kg for hydroxyl-methyl-furfural (HMF), respectively. The results for the highland region showed significant influences of harvest season on ash and HMF contents of honey samples from Dessie Zuria district at p < 0.05 significant levels. In midland agro-ecology, season had a significant effect only on FA and HMF content at p < 0.05 significant levels. In the lowland agroecology, only HMF levels were influenced by season at p < 0.05. In general, no quality parameters varied significantly between harvesting seasons at p > 0.05, except ash content and HMF content at p < 0.05. Honey from the first season had the highest ash content (0.13 ± 0.0), while honey from the second season had a higher HMF content (9.25 ± 1.2). In general, the physico-chemical properties of the honey samples were within the range specified for established honey quality standards.Keywords: Honey bee, Quality parameters, Harvesting season, Agro-ecologiesSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Zoology

Introduction

Honey is a natural sweet substance produced by honeybees from plant nectar or secretions (Codex Alimentarius 2001). Its composition and characteristics vary significantly based on geographical and botanical origins, floral sources, and the nectar utilized by bees1. Research shows that honey’s functional properties for human health largely depend on its floral source, high osmolarity, and antibacterial properties. These qualities, along with its antioxidant and antibacterial activities, contribute to its effectiveness in wound dressing2. Ethiopia ranks first in Africa and third globally in beeswax production, while leading Africa and ranking 10th worldwide in honey production. This high production potential is due to its diverse agroclimatic conditions and rich biodiversity, which support abundant honeybee flora and numerous colonies3.

Ethiopia is reported to have over 10 million bee colonies, with 5 to 7.5 million hived and the rest wild3. According to CSA and agricultural surveys (2014), the leading honey and beeswax-producing regions are Oromia (41%), Amhara (22%), SNNPR (21%), and Tigray (5%). Therefore, it is expected that there will be good potential for honeybee production practices in northern Ethiopia, particularly in eastern Amhara3.

Honey quality, crucial for both local and international markets, is often overlooked by producers and processors in developing countries like Ethiopia . As a result, thousands of tons of honey produced annually are crude, poorly managed, and visually unappealing4. The harvesting season, associated climatic factors, and postharvest handling procedures are what cause its market fluctuations5. But as of now, no information is recorded about the characteristics of honey collected in eastern Amhara, especially in certain parts of South Wollo Zone. On the other hand, it is very beneficial to be aware of the honey production area’s quality standards. The data can be used by interested parties and policymakers to guide future sector development initiatives.

The highest level of hydroxyl methyl furfural (HMF) value (4.48) was found in lowland honey, with a significant difference (P < 0.05) compared to midland and highland honey. However, the difference between lowland honey and either of the other two areas was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

In the minor harvesting season (June), all parameters across the agro-ecological zones showed significant differences (P < 0.05), except for pH. Midland honey exhibited the highest FA value (29.67), which was significantly greater (P < 0.05) than that of the other samples. The lowest FA (15.97) was recorded in lowland honey, though the difference between lowland and highland honey (16.60) was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The lowest MC was observed in lowland honey (15.67), which differed significantly (P < 0.05) from the other samples. Although midland honey had the highest MC (18.28), no significant difference (P > 0.05) was found between it and highland honey (17.82). Midland honey also showed the highest AC at 0.13, significantly higher (P < 0.05) than the other samples. While the AC of lowland honey (0.04) exceeded that of highland honey (0.03), the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The lowest HMF content was detected in midland honey (3.53), which differed significantly (P < 0.05) from the other samples. Conversely, the highest HMF content was recorded in lowland honey (13.00), though the difference between lowland and highland honey (11.21) was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The chemical composition of honey depends on factors such as botanical origin, season, environmental conditions, production methods, and storage6–8. In this study, the honey’s moisture content averaged 17.59%, 17.27%, and 17.43%, with seasonal ranges of 12–20% (Table 4), complying with national and international standards (≤ 21% per CAC, 2001; EU, 2002; QSAE, 2005). The MC observed in this study was considerably lower than those reported in Ethiopia’s more humid southwestern regions. This variation can be attributable to the semi-arid and sub-humid climatic conditions, which is typical to South Wollo Zone of Ethiopia9. Moreover, the consistency of MC across seasons is explained by the deliberate timing of harvests in November-December and May-June, which coincide with post-rainfall dry periods. These intervals provide ideal conditions for bees to ripen and seal the nectar. Given honey’s hygroscopic, its MC is strongly influenced by the ambient humidity during extraction3. Ethiopian honey exhibits notable regional variations in MC, which range from 18.3% to 22.03%9–15 across the various geographical regions of Ethiopia. These moisture levels surpass those recorded in the current investigation.

Conversely, lower values were reported in northeastern (11.87–16.7%)3 and southwestern (16.5–20%)16 Ethiopia, respectively. These variations stem from factors like nectar source, climate, and harvesting methods17,18. The uniformity of MC across altitudinal gradients observed in this study suggests that beekeepers in both lowland and highland districts of South Wollo predominantly harvest fully ripened, capped honeycombs, thereby reducing the influence of local humidity3. Scientifically, such variations are explained by honey’s hygroscopic nature, whereby its moisture level is primarily regulated by the relative humidity of the surrounding agro-ecology and the botanical composition of nectar sources available during each flow season.

The AC in this study averaged 0.09% (range: 0.01–0.55%), meeting international standards (≤ 0.6% for CAC, EU, and QSAE). The progressive increase in AC from the lowland (0.04%) to the midland (0.20%) and highland regions reflects underlying differences in soil composition and botanical diversity. In South Wollo Zone, highland areas are characterized by mineral-rich basaltic volcanic soils, in contrast to the sedimentary and alluvial deposits typical of lower elevations. Furthermore, the presence of deep-rooted melliferous plant species in highland ecosystems likely facilitates the transfer of minerals from the soil into floral nectar3. The observed mineral enrichment in highland honey is consistent with contemporary research identifying mineral concentration as a distinctive “geochemical fingerprint,” underscoring the interaction between local soil properties and the mineral uptake capacities of specific nectar-producing flora across altitudinal gradients17. Previous studies reported higher ash contents: 0.2% in Central Ethiopia11, 0.23% in Arba Minch Zuria12, 0.42% in Jimma, Bale, Kefa, and North Shewa9, 0.29% in Bale Zone14, and 0.25% in Kembata Tambaro15. Conversely, Nega et al.18 reported an AC range of 0.09–0.26% in honey from East Gojjam, Amhara, Ethiopia, higher at the lower limit but lower at the upper limit compared to this study. Similarly, AC ranges of 0.08–0.3%16, 0.17–0.2%13, 0.16–0.48%19, and 0.12–0.49%3 were reported in honey from various Ethiopian regions. The relatively lower ash levels observed in this study, compared with those reported for honey from regions such as Jimma and Bale, Ethiopia9, suggest that the predominant floral sources in South Wollo, particularly Acacia and Eucalyptus, are naturally characterized by lower mineral content than the forest-based flora typical of southwestern Ethiopia. These variations can be attributed to factors such as soil types, mineral concentrations in nectar, botanical origins20.), and practices including harvesting methods, beekeeping practices, processing and handling21,22.

The FA in this study ranged from 10 to 45 meq/kg, with an overall mean of 19.14 meq/kg, meeting the national and international quality standards (≤ 50 for CAC, ≤ 40 for EU and QSAE), though two samples (44 and 45) exceeded 40 meq/kg. The higher FA observed in midland regions (21.55 meq/kg) and during the minor flow season (29.67 meq/kg) may be attributed to the influence of specific floral sources whose nectars are naturally rich in organic acids or exhibit elevated glucose oxidase activity. These variations likely arise from the complex interaction between local soil mineral composition and the botanical characteristics of nectar-producing plants unique to particular districts17. Although two samples exceeded the EU/QSAE permissible limit of 40 meq/kg, the absence of corresponding increases in HMF and MC suggests that the elevated acidity is of natural origin rather than a result of fermentation. This is most plausibly linked to specific botanical sources, such as Guizotia abyssinica and related oilseed species, which are known to produce nectar with inherently higher organic acid levels3. Previous findings have reported FA values ranging from 26.8 meq/kg to 46.6 meq/kg across various regions of Ethiopia12,9,18. Higher mean values, reaching 57.66 and 58.33 meq/kg, have been documented in the Bale and Bench Sheko zones13,14, respectively. In contrast, considerably lower FA levels, 16.3 meq/kg, 13.39 meq/kg, and 7–10 meq/kg were reported in Gedebano Gutazer Wolene, Kembata Tambaro, and South-West forest regions of Ethiopia10,11,15, respectively. These variations are largely attributable to differences in soil characteristics, floral nectar sources, and bee species. Elevated FA values may also result from sugar fermentation processes, which can adversely affect honey quality and safety11,17,15,23.

This study found average HMF levels of 5.49 mg/kg (ranging from 0 to 37.8 mg/kg), which remain comfortably within both national and international standards (≤ 60 CAC, ≤ 40 EU/QSAE). The marked increase in HMF concentrations observed during the minor flow season (9.25 mg/kg), compared to the major season (1.74 mg/kg), is largely driven by prevailing thermal conditions. In South Wollo Zone, the minor harvest period coincides with peak temperatures in May and June, which often exceed 25–30 °C. Such conditions facilitate the acid-catalyzed dehydration of fructose into HMF, a temperature-dependent kinetic process described by Tarapoulouzi et al.,24. This thermally induced acceleration is particularly pronounced in lowland and midland areas, where heat intensity is greater than in the relatively cooler highland zones3.

Previous studies have reported HMF concentrations ranging from 9.45 to 14.8 mg/kg12,25,9,14. Substantially higher levels, 46.53–67.68 mg/kg and 23.31–59.03 mg/kg, have been documented in Bench Sheko and North-Eastern Ethiopia3,13, respectively. In contrast, lower mean values of 2.63 mg/kg and 3.42 mg/kg have been reported in Central and Southern Ethiopia, respectively11,15. Such variation in HMF content is primarily influenced by differences in climatic conditions, moisture levels, and storage practices11,16,24,26,27.

The present study revealed pH values of 3.12 and 3.14 for seasons one and two, respectively, with a mean of 3.13. These values fall within internationally accepted quality standards (3.4–6.1, CAC). This relatively high acidity serves as an important quality indicator, contributing to honey preservation by inhibiting microbial growth28. The comparatively lower pH values observed in this study, particularly when contrasted with those of previous studies9,10,12–14,16,18 reported in various regions of Ethiopia, may reflect the distinctive botanical composition of South Wollo’s predominant melliferous flora. Plant species such as Becium grandiflorum and various Acacia species are known to produce nectar with inherently elevated organic acid content. Moreover, the consistency of pH values across seasons, despite temperature fluctuations, suggests that the observed acidity is primarily an intrinsic property of the nectar source rather than a result of fermentative changes during storage. This could be likely due to differences in geographic origin, shelf life, microorganism growth, and fermentation processes24,28–30.

Conclusions

The findings of this study confirms that honey produced in the South Wollo Zone complies with both national and international quality standards and exhibits inherent resistance to spoilage due to its favorable pH and MC. Nonetheless, its physicochemical characteristics are strongly influenced by altitude and seasonal variation. In particular, the elevated temperatures during the minor harvest season (May-June) in lower-altitude areas tend to increase HMF concentrations, while variations in mineral composition impart a distinctive “geochemical fingerprint” linked to dominant floral sources such as Acacia and Eucalyptus. Priority should be given to reducing HMF levels to meet the European honey quality standards, particularly for stakeholders aiming to export and enhance its market competitiveness. This issue is especially critical for beekeepers, as HMF formation increases under excessive heat and during prolonged or improper storage. To safeguard both quality and marketability, beekeepers and processors must adopt improved post-harvest practices, including storage in cool, dark environments to limit thermally induced HMF accumulation. Likewise, minimizing heat exposure during extraction and filtration is essential to preserve honey’s natural chemical integrity. Finally, coordinated efforts among authorities and stakeholders are needed to deliver targeted training on standardized grading, handling, and packaging, coupled with enhanced transport logistics to ensure timely delivery of premium-quality honey to international markets.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in South Wollo Zone (Fig. 1), one of the 11 zones in the Amhara region of Ethiopia, lies between 10.20° and 11.71° N latitude and 38.41° and 40.02° E longitude, covering a total area of 17,067.45 km2. The administrative center of the zone is Dessie town. The zone comprises 20 districts, but, Tehulederie (Midland), Kalu (Lowland), and Dessie Zuriya (Highland) were specifically selected for this study due to their beekeeping potential, accessibility, and proximity to honey marketing and processing routes. The area receives an average annual rainfall of 1162 mm, with a monthly minimum temperature of 12.6 °C and a monthly maximum temperature of 26.4 °C31.

Map of the study area.

Sampling techniques and sample size

A purposive stratified random sampling method was employed to select the sampling locations. Districts were first classified based on their agroecological locations, after which the sampling sites were selected using a random sampling technique. Fifteen representatives Kebeles were selected: five from the midlands (Tehuledere), five from the lowlands (Kalu), and five from the highlands (Dessie Zuria). In total, thirty (30) honey samples were collected, with ten samples from each agroecological zone. The sampling framework involved the collection of 10 independent samples (2 samples from each kebele) per agro-ecology per season, rather than utilizing a repeated-measures design. All samples were obtained from modern (frame) hives during two harvesting seasons: October-November (the major harvesting season) and June-May (the minor harvesting season). To preserve physicochemical integrity, the collected honey samples were immediately sealed in sterile, airtight, food-grade plastic containers to prevent contamination and moisture absorption (Fig. 2). The samples were subsequently stored in a cool, dark environment under refrigeration at − 20 °C. These measures minimized exposure to light and heat, which could otherwise alter the physicochemical properties of honey. Quality analyses were conducted within a defined timeframe to ensure sample integrity, and transportation for laboratory analysis was completed within 48 h to reduce post-harvest acidity fluctuations and HMF accumulation induced by thermal or light exposure. The collected honey samples were predominantly derived from melliferous plant species such as Eucalyptus globulus, Rumex spp., Guizotia abyssinica, Acacia, Vernonia spp., and Croton macrostachyus.

Quality measurement procedures

The quality composition of the honey samples, including moisture content (MC), pH, acidity, HMF, mineral content, and ash-was determined using the Harmonized Methods of the International Honey Commission (HMIHC) [32]. The analysis of the honey’s quality characteristics was conducted at the Holeta Bee Research Centre, an agricultural research institute located in Oromia, Ethiopia.

Moisture content (MC)

The moisture content of the honey sample was determined by an Abbe digital refractometer thermostated at 20°C. The refractometer was calibrated by using distilled water, after which the honey’s refractive index was determined from a standard table. In taking reading, the sample of the honey was evenly spread over the surface of the prism provided with the refractive index being recorded after two minutes. Each sample had three repeat measurements; the average of the three repeats provided one value for the corresponding moisture content.

pH

The pH of honey was determined using a digital pH meter (Inolab, Germany). 10 g of honey sample were mixed with 75 ml of distilled water in a 250 ml beaker. Then, after agitating this mixture and submerging the pH electrode in the same, the measurement of pH was taken.

Free acidity (FA)

Ten grams of honey were weighed using an electronic balance, and transferred to a conical flask, to which 75 ml of distilled water was added after rinsing the utensil. Phenolphthalein is used as an indicator during titration. The solution was titrated with 0.1 M NaOH from a burette until a persistent pink color appeared, indicating the endpoint. The free acidity was calculated to one decimal place using the following formula:

Milliequivalents, or millimoles of acid/kg honey = mL of 0.1 M NaOH × 10.

Mineral (ash)

After burning the honey sample in an electric muffle furnace (Lenton Thermal Designs, England), the amount of ash was determined. The ash dish was first heated to ashing temperature in an electrical furnace, then cooled to room temperature in a desiccator, and weighed to 0.001 g (M2). Subsequently, two drops of olive oil were then added to the prepared ash dish to prevent frothing, after 10 g of honey sample had been weighed to the nearest 0. 001 g (M0). The water was drained off and the ashing process was started without loss (by foaming and overflowing) at a low heat, raised to 350–400° C with an electrical device. The sample was charred on a hot plate before being placed in the furnace. Following the initial ashing with a hot plate, the dish was heated for an hour at 550° C in a muffle furnace that had been preheated. After being cooled in the desiccator, the ash dish was weighed. Until a constant weight was reached, the ashing process was carried out (M1). These samples were preserved until the time of analysis (for one week) in refrigerator under 20 °C. Estimation of hydroxyl methyl furfural (HMF)

The reagents enlisted below, required to estimate the HMF content in honey samples were prepared as follows:

Carrez Solution I: 15 g of potassium hexacyanoferrate (II) trihydrate K4[Fe(CN)6] 3H2O was dissolved in distilled water and the volume was made to 100 ml.

Carrez Solution II: 30 g of zinc acetate Zn (CH3COO)2. 2H2O was dissolved in distilled water and the volume was made to 100 ml.

Sodium bi-sulphate solution (0.20%): − 0.20 g of solid sodium bi-sulphate (NaHSO₄) was dissolved in distilled water and the volume was made to 100 ml. Five grams of honey sample was taken approximately and diluted in 25 ml water and then poured into a volumetric flask. Then 0.5 ml of Carrez solution I was mixed with 0.5 ml of Carrez solution II and made up the volume. Then the solution was filtered through the filter paper and the first 10 ml of filtration was rejected. Five ml of sample was pipetted out in two test tubes and 5 ml of water was added to the first test tube and mixed well. Five ml of 0.2% NaHSO₄ solution was added to the second test tube and mixed well for reference solution (Table 5).

Statistical analysis

The performance differences were tested and analyzed using the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) in the SAS general linear model (GLM) procedure. Mean comparisons were conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) test at the 5% significant level. P value < 0.05 was considered as significant.
