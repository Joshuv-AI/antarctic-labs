History of Presentation

A 42-year-old man presented to us with intermittent fever for the last 2 months. He had also noticed unintentional weight loss and easy fatiguability for the last 2 months. There were no symptoms or complaints that would help localize the symptoms. He denied orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, cough, arthralgia, or rash. He had been extensively evaluated at a primary health care hospital and had received antipyretics and empirical intravenous antibiotics for 2 weeks at an outside hospital but with no relief.Take-Home Messages•Cardiac myxomas are a rare cause of pyrexia of unknown origin.•Surgical resection is the only definitive treatment and has excellent prognosis.

On examination, he was afebrile and hemodynamically stable with blood pressure of 102/70 mm Hg and a pulse rate of 102 beats/min, with SpO2 of 98% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed a low-pitched, end-diastolic sound, which is likely to be tumor plop at the left lower sternal border. Rest of the examination was unremarkable.

Past Medical History

No significant past medical history.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included myxoma, thrombus, angiosarcoma, lymphoma, and pseudotumor.

Investigations

Routine blood investigations revealed an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/h (normal range 0-20 mm/h) and C-reactive protein level of 23 mg/L (normal: <5.0 mg/L). Complete blood count and renal and liver function tests were within normal limits. Blood culture, urine culture, chest radiograph, antinuclear antibody and rheumatoid factor were all negative. Ultrasonography of the abdomen was also normal. Twelve-lead electrocardiography revealed low-voltage complexes with incomplete right bundle branch block. Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography revealed right atrial and right ventricular dilation with a large, heterogeneous, mobile intracardiac mass attached to the interatrial septum and prolapsing into the right ventricular outflow tract during diastole (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). Left ventricular systolic function was normal. Coronary angiography showed normal coronary arteries with feeding vessels from the left circumflex artery (LCx) and the right coronary artery (RCA) to the right atrium mass (Figure 2, Videos 3 and 4). Management

The patient was diagnosed with right atrial myxoma and underwent surgical excision with tricuspid valve repair by De Vegas annuloplasty (Figures 4 and 5). Outcome and Follow-Up

Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography showed normal left ventricular function with mild tricuspid regurgitation and mild right-ventricular dysfunction.

Discussion

Cardiac tumors are rare, and once diagnosed, they are marked by an alarmingly poor clinical course. Secondary cardiac tumors are 30 times more frequent than primary cardiac tumors.1 While primary cardiac tumors are very rare (0.0017% to 0.028%), they portend poor prognosis when present. Benign tumors comprise the vast majority of all primary cardiac tumors, accounting for nearly 90%. Among the benign tumors, myxoma and rhabdomyoma comprise 50% of all benign tumors in adults and children, respectively, whereas sarcomas are the most common malignant tumor of the heart.2

Myxomas are the most common benign tumors of the heart and are commonly seen in middle-aged women. The most common location of myxoma is the left atrium (60% to 80%), followed by right atrium, left ventricle, and lastly the right ventricle in that order.3 Most of the cases (90%) are sporadic and present as an isolated mass in the left atrium attached to the interatrial septum. Multiple myxomas, recurrent tumors, atypical locations, and myxomas when associated with cutaneous markers should suggest a syndromic association and must be investigated as they may occur with one of the familial syndromes.4

Clinically, cardiac myxomas classically present with a triad of obstructive symptoms, embolic phenomena, and constitutional manifestations. Constitutional symptoms—including fever, weight loss, malaise, and anemia—are mediated by cytokine release, particularly interleukin-6, and may dominate the clinical picture.5 In some patients, these inflammatory manifestations precede or entirely overshadow cardiac symptoms, leading to diagnostic confusion with chronic infections, inflammatory disorders, or occult malignancy.

Right atrial myxomas, although less common than left atrial lesions, pose a particular diagnostic challenge. Unlike left atrial myxomas, they often do not produce early valvular obstruction or characteristic auscultatory findings. Instead, patients may present with nonspecific systemic symptoms, features of right-sided heart failure, pulmonary embolism, or—rarely—isolated prolonged fever without any overt cardiac manifestations, as observed in the present case. Such myriad presentations frequently lead to extensive evaluation for fever of unknown origin and delayed diagnosis.3

Transthoracic echocardiography remains the initial imaging modality of choice for suspected cardiac myxoma, allowing detection of intracardiac masses and assessment of size, mobility, attachment, and hemodynamic consequences. Transesophageal echocardiography further improves spatial resolution and is particularly useful in defining right atrial lesions and their relationship to surrounding structures.6 In our patient, echocardiography was pivotal in identifying a right atrial mass, prompting further diagnostic evaluation.

Cardiac CT served as a valuable complementary modality, providing additional tissue characterization and anatomical detail. CT demonstrated a well-defined hypodense lesion with mild postcontrast enhancement and focal calcification, features consistent with benign myxomatous tissue and helpful in differentiating myxoma from thrombus or malignant cardiac tumors.7 CT also aided in delineating tumor extent and attachment, facilitating surgical planning.

Coronary angiography is not routinely required in all patients with suspected cardiac myxoma but may be useful in selected cases, particularly in middle-aged or older patients, to exclude coexistent coronary artery disease and to assess tumor vascularity. Previous studies have shown that atrial myxomas commonly derive their blood supply from the sinoatrial nodal branch of the RCA (100%), followed by the atrioventricular nodal branch of the RCA (80%), and the atrial branch of the LCx (40%). Huang et al8 described a characteristic “sea anemone”–like appearance of tumor vascularity on coronary angiography, reflecting the friable and highly vascular nature of atrial myxomas.8 In the present case, feeding vessels arose from both the sinoatrial nodal branch of the RCA and the LCx, with no evidence of concomitant coronary artery disease.

A review of previously published cases highlights the rarity yet clinical importance of right atrial myxomas presenting predominantly with fever. Across reported cases, fever—often prolonged and unexplained—was a dominant or initial symptom, frequently leading to extensive diagnostic workups before echocardiographic detection of the tumor. Importantly, surgical excision resulted in complete resolution of fever in nearly all cases, underscoring the inflammatory origin of symptoms and the curative potential of surgery.9

Surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for cardiac myxomas and is generally curative, with excellent long-term outcomes when performed promptly. Early surgery is crucial given the unpredictable risk of embolization, hemodynamic compromise, and sudden cardiac death. Recurrence is rare in sporadic cases but more common in familial or syndromic forms, necessitating long-term follow-up.10

This case highlights 2 critical learning points: 1) the rarity and diagnostic challenge of right atrial myxomas; and 2) their ability to present solely as prolonged fever without overt cardiac symptoms. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and consider early echocardiographic evaluation in patients with persistent fever of unknown origin, as timely identification and surgical management can be both diagnostic and life-saving.

Conclusions

Right atrial myxomas are an uncommon but important cause of pyrexia of unknown origin, often masquerading as chronic infection or inflammatory disease. Prompt surgical excision is both curative and lifesaving, leading to rapid resolution of symptoms and prevention of fatal complications.
