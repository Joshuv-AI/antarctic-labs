 ABSTRACT

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) particle fuels are a key technology for many advanced reactor designs, as
evidenced by broad industry interest and the significant number of TRISO-based reactor designs proposed
for a wide range of applications. The success of TRISO particle fuels is rooted in both the inherent safety
provided by local defense-in-depth containment of radioactive fission products and in the fuel
performance and qualification data packages produced over the past two decades by the US Department
of Energy’s (DOE’s) Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program. While
these data form a foundation for the licensing of TRISO fuel, evolving reactor designs have unique fuel
design requirements compared with the nominal high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR). In
addition, strictly interpreted, the AGR Program results qualify fuel within the as-fabricated AGR
envelope of fuel properties rather than the broader AGR fuel specification space. A potential solution that
has been suggested is a follow-on integral fuel irradiation using fuel with a broader range of as-fabricated
properties to complement and expand existing AGR qualification data. This report collects these as-
fabricated properties across the AGR campaigns, examining the most impactful parameters within TRISO
fuel specifications to target for expansion. Strategies for targeted synthetic distributions of selected
parameters are evaluated to maximize statistics in the unexplored corners of the fuel specification.
Opportunities to combine parameter variations are also explored to minimize the number of fuel variants
that must be fabricated while maintaining conservatism in performance.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 FUNDAMENTALS OF TRISO

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) particle fuels possess a layered structure designed to enable a fully
refractory core for higher operating temperatures and to provide local containment of fission products
(FPs), thus simplifying the balance of reactor design, with the inherent trade-off of a more complex and
thus more expensive fuel to fabricate and characterize [1]. While particle fuels have a long history, a
significant milestone in TRISO development was the German Arbeitsgemeinschaft Versuchsreaktor
(AVR) Program, which demonstrated high performance for particle fuels in a pebble bed reactor design
from the 1960s to the 1980s [2]. This experience led to the development of the Fort Saint Vrain Nuclear
Power Plant in the US, which operated from 1979 to 1989 [3, 4]. The fuel for this reactor was fabricated
by General Atomics, establishing US TRISO fabrication capabilities and developing the uranium carbide
+ uranium oxide (UCO) kernel type. Based on the results of these developments, the Advanced Gas
Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program was launched in the US in the early 2000s
to further develop TRISO technologies and to provide a qualification database to support domestic reactor
designers and fuel vendors in achieving regulatory approval for TRISO-based reactor designs [5]. The
success of the AGR Program has since led to the creation of many private companies seeking to
commercialize TRISO fuel technology with a variety of particle-fuel-based reactor designs. One of the
key lessons learned has been that the performance of TRISO fuels is inherently linked to the quality of the
fuel fabrication. This has led to a focus on quality control over the statistical distribution of key fuel
properties and quality assurance via characterization of those properties [6, 7]. For a given reactor design,
the bounds on these properties and the statistical tests that they must pass are generally provided in a fuel
specification [8].

The typical TRISO particle design standardized through the AGR Program consists of a central uranium-
bearing kernel in a ceramic form approximately 350–500 μm in diameter [9, 10]. In the AVR Program,
gel spheres were fabricated using an external gelation process, whereas in the AGR Program and most
modern TRISO fabrication processes, an internal gelation process is used [2]. In either case, gel spheres
are converted to kernels via thermochemical processing steps, resulting in a nearly fully dense uranium

 1

ceramic that is roughly spherical. All coating layers of TRISO particles are deposited in sequence using
fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition (FBCVD). The first coating is an approximately 50% dense
carbon buffer layer that is typically ~100 μm thick. This layer accommodates kernel swelling under
irradiation, acts as a plenum for fission gases, and prevents FP recoils from damaging the following
structural layers [11]. The second coating is a near fully dense inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) layer that is
typically ~40 μm thick. This layer protects the kernel from attack by chlorine gas, which is a by-product
of the following silicon carbide (SiC) layer deposition, and protects the SiC from chemical attack by
specific FPs (e.g., palladium) [11]. The third coating is the SiC layer, which is typically ~35 μm thick.
The SiC layer is the primary barrier to the release of FPs that are not contained within the kernel and
provides the primary mechanical strength of the particle [11]. The fourth and final coating is the outer
pyrolytic carbon (OPyC) layer, which is again near fully dense carbon and typically ~40 μm thick. The
OPyC acts as a soft protective layer on the SiC to prevent mechanical damage during particle handling or
within the final fuel form [11]. The final result is a layered particle of ~800–1,000 μm diameter. These
particles are combined with matrix material (typically graphitic matrix, although some newer designs are
pursuing SiC matrix) to produce fuel forms containing thousands of particles each with size and shape
depending on reactor design.

1.2 AGR PROGRAM HISTORY

The AGR Program has consisted of a series of irradiation experiments designed to mature TRISO fuel
technology from the laboratory to industrial scale while providing qualification data from integral fuel
irradiations to reduce barriers to entry for private companies seeking to build TRISO-based reactor
designs. The AGR-1 experiment consisted of approximately 298,000 TRISO particles in 72 graphitic
matrix cylindrical compacts. The fuel for the AGR-1 experiment was fabricated by BWXT (uranium
kernels) and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) (particle coatings and compacts), and samples were
irradiated at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) in the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR). The irradiation
spanned approximately 3 years, during which compacts experienced burnups from 11.3 to 19.6 %FIMA
and time-average volume-average (TAVA) temperatures of 955°C–1,136°C [12]. Of the 298,000 particles
irradiated in AGR-1, there were no observed full TRISO failures and four SiC failures observed in reactor
[13].

The following AGR-2 irradiation shifted fuel fabrication partially to the industrial scale, as particles were
fabricated with larger diameter kernels at BWXT and then upgraded and compacted into final fuel forms
at ORNL before irradiation in ATR at INL. Approximately 133,000 TRISO particles were irradiated in
this experiment, of which 114,000 had heterogenous UCx+UO2 kernels similar to AGR-1 and 19,000 had
UO2 kernels similar to what was used in the AVR Program, but at different irradiation conditions than
typically used with UO2 kernels. Irradiation for AGR-2 spanned 9.3 to 12.2 %FIMA and TAVA
temperatures of 1,032°C–1,252°C. Fission gas monitoring in the first three irradiation cycles showed no
signs of TRISO coating failure; however, issues with the monitoring system prevented further data
collection during irradiation [14]. Based on following post-irradiation examination (PIE), it was
concluded that for the UCO TRISO, there were ≤ 4 full TRISO failures (in which hermeticity is lost and
fission gases can escape) and ≤ 10 combined TRISO and SiC failures (in which some FPs diffuse through
the failed SiC, but particle hermeticity is maintained through at least one intact pyrolytic carbon [PyC]
layer) [15]. Similarly, for the UO2 TRISO, there were no TRISO failures and 5 SiC failures observed.
Although these failure rates were higher than those observed in AGR-1, they are still significantly lower
than the historic design specification of ≤ 2E−4 at 95% confidence [15].

The AGR-3/4 experiment is unique within the AGR Program. It was designed to provide data on the
mobility of FPs within the fuel following particle failure rather than focusing on shifting from laboratory
to industrial scale fuel fabrication and establishing fuel qualification data. To achieve this, particles and
compacts were fabricated at ORNL. Each compact included approximately 1,900 “driver fuel” particles,

 2

which were standard TRISO particles similar to those from the AGR-1 experiment, as well as 20
designed-to-fail (DTF) particles in the compact centerline [16]. These DTF particles consisted of a kernel
with only a single PyC coating layer; they were intended to fail in the first stage of irradiation, providing a
known source of FP diffusion. The intent of the AGR-3/4 irradiation was not to provide qualification
data, and therefore on-line monitoring of particle failure was not performed and would be convoluted in
any case due to the presence of DTF particles. The ~91,000 driver fuel particles were irradiated from 4.85
to 15.27 %FIMA at TAVA temperatures of 854°C–1,363°C [17]. Additional PIE and analysis of
irradiated AGR-3/4 driver fuel particles could possibly yield some insights into performance. Whether
such an effort is worthwhile largely depends on the additional design space encompassed by this fuel
relative to the other AGR irradiation campaigns.

The AGR-5/6/7 experiment represents the culmination of the AGR Program, as all stages of fuel
fabrication were performed at the industrial scale by BWXT. A total of ~570,000 particles in 194
compacts were irradiated in ATR at INL from 5.66 to 15.26 %FIMA at TAVA temperatures of 756°C–
1,313°C [18]. Unfortunately, issues with the design of Capsule 1 in the AGR-5/6/7 experiment led to
external chemical attack on the particles in that capsule, limiting the usefulness of fuel performance data
from those particles [19]. This left approximately 262,000 particles in AGR-5/6/7 that were unaffected by
this issue. Post-irradiation examination of these particles to enumerate failures and identify mechanisms is
ongoing, but when complete, these data will likely be combined with those from prior AGR irradiations
to form a unified whole.

1.3 EPRI TOPICAL REPORT

In the interim (as AGR-5/6/7 PIE data are gathered), a topical report on the performance of UCO TRISO
fuels was published by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) [20]. This report is based on the
results of the AGR-1 and AGR-2 experiments, as those data were what was published at the time. This
report concluded that:

 The kernels and coatings of the UCO TRISO-coated fuel particles tested in AGR-1 and
 AGR-2 exhibited property variations and were fabricated under different conditions and
 at different scales, with remarkably similar excellent irradiation and accident safety
 performance results. The ranges of those variations in key characteristics of the kernels
 and coatings are reflected in measured particle layer properties provided in Table 5-5
 from AGR-1 and AGR-2. UCO TRISO-coated fuel particles that satisfy the parameter
 envelope defined by these measured particle layer properties in Table 5-5 can be relied
 on to provide satisfactory performance.

This conclusion directly matches one of the primary objectives of the AGR Program to provide
qualification data for TRISO fuel fabricated from the laboratory to the industrial scale irrespective of
where the fuel was fabricated and establishing an AGR standard TRISO design. However, the ranges
presented in the referenced Table 5-5 in the EPRI topical report (reproduced here in Table 1) are
substantially narrower than the fuel specifications used for any of the AGR experiments, even though
those specification ranges were set based on known technical bases [11]. This is a natural consequence of
the AGR Program experimental design and the methodology used in the EPRI topical report. The bounds
on the fuel specifications in the AGR experiments were set based on a combination of historical
precedence from prior irradiation performance results, modeling of fuel performance, and the practical
limits of fabricability. Generally, the actual fuel properties landed near the middle of the specification
ranges with some statistical distribution. This was an intentional choice made early in the program to
ensure successful irradiation, as based on prior mixed results with particle fuels the AGR irradiations
needed to succeed in demonstrating reliability. In contrast, the ranges in the EPRI topical report were
based on the actual properties of the irradiated fuel from the AGR-1 and AGR-2 experiments. While the

 3

same bases used to develop the AGR specification bounds still apply, the EPRI topical report takes the
conservative position of only accepting fuel property ranges for which there are integral fuel irradiation
data from the AGR Program as qualified. This was driven largely by the response by the US Nuclear
Regulatory Commission (NRC), which reiterated the above quote from the EPRI topical report, then
further clarified point in NUREG-2246, which generalized advanced reactor fuel qualification and states:

 The assessment framework particularly emphasizes the identification of key fuel
 manufacturing parameters, the specification of a fuel performance envelope to inform
 testing requirements, the use of evaluation models in the fuel qualification process, and
 the assessment of the experimental data used to develop and validate evaluation models
 and empirical safety criteria.

In other words, fuel qualification is driven by actual performance data and understanding of fuel
behavior (the fuel performance envelope), not simply by meeting a generic specification. However, this
poses a challenge for particle fuel fabricators, as due to the statistical penalty that comes with proving a
mean value and dispersion extrema with 95% confidence, fabricating TRISO particles that meet the
ranges and critical limits set forth in the EPRI topical report would require tighter property distributions
than those of the fuel fabricated for the AGR irradiations and significantly tighter property distributions
than those allowed by the AGR fuel specifications. Given the already high cost of fabricating TRISO
fuels, this added hurdle would likely pose a significant challenge to economical particle fuel reactor
deployment by negatively impacting yield.

1.4 TARGETED SUPPLEMENTAL IRRADIATION CONCEPT

Although the eventual addition of finalized AGR-5/6/7 irradiation performance data to the AGR-1 and
AGR-2 data may somewhat widen the confidence bounds on fuel properties for TRISO performance, the
fundamental challenge of limited data near the edges of the AGR fuel specification space will remain.
One solution to this challenge that has begun to gain traction in the particle fuel community is the
possibility of a follow-on irradiation to the AGR Program intended to demonstrate the performance of
particles with properties in the unexplored regions of the AGR specification space. In addition, such an
effort could also undertake limited exploration beyond those bounds for select properties of interest for
some reactor designs. These excursions would need to be limited to maintain an achievable scope for an

 4

irradiation campaign, but investigation of some key parameters outside the AGR ranges may be
worthwhile. Most notably, significantly larger diameter kernels potentially containing uranium nitride
(UN) are likely to be of interest for microreactor designs that require higher uranium loading than
traditional 425 μm diameter UCO kernel TRISO can provide [22].

Given this context, this report is intended to outline a nominal fuel design for such a follow-on irradiation
with the objective of expanding the qualified range of UCO TRISO to the full AGR specification space.
Such a fuel design should meet several requirements to be effective in this mission. First, it should
effectively extend out to both the high and low edges of the specification space for each property of
interest such that when combined with the existing AGR qualification data, the full breadth of the AGR
specification space is covered, accounting for the statistical distribution of the properties. Second, the
interplay of proposed variations in the values of different properties on composite fuel performance must
be considered using the underlying knowledge of the specification bases [11]. Third, the proposed fuel
design must be practically fabricable and characterizable and within a scope such that a realistic
irradiation test and associated PIE may be performed. To meet these requirements, what is proposed
herein is a series of eight fuel variants combining parameter variations by layer into conservative worst-
case scenarios to achieve a manageable scope for an integral irradiation campaign while effectively filling
in the gaps in current irradiation data for TRISO fuels meeting the nominal AGR specification. Although
such an irradiation would be more complex than the prior AGR irradiation campaigns due to the number
of fuel variants, this added complexity in experimental design may be mitigated by reduced PIE scope, as
detailed examination of irradiated fuel behavior will be less important than bulk demonstration of
sufficiently low particle failure fractions.

 2. UCO KERNEL PARAMETERS OF INTEREST

2.1 KERNEL DIAMETER

Diameter is arguably the kernel parameter of greatest interest to industry due to the potential for
increasing fuel uranium loading by increasing kernel size while presumably keeping nominal coating
layer thicknesses the same. As shown in Figure 1, the AGR irradiation campaigns included two nominal
kernel diameters with their own specification limits. The AGR-1 fuel and AGR-3/4 driver fuel both had
nominal kernel diameters of 350 μm with an allowable range on the mean of 340 μm (red dashed line) to
360 μm (blue dashed line) and critical limits for no more than 1% of the kernels at 300 μm and 400 μm
(black dashed lines). The AGR-2 UCO fuel and AGR-5/6/7 fuel had nominal kernel diameters of 425 μm
with an allowable range on the mean of 415 μm (red dot-dashed line) to 435 μm (blue dot-dashed line)
and critical limits for no more than 1% of the kernels at 375 μm and 475 μm (black dot-dashed lines).

In terms of demonstrating performance within the full AGR specification space, the populations of
kernels irradiated in the AGR experiments broadly cover the space spanning the dispersion limits on

 6

kernel diameter. If marginal further expansion of irradiation data for kernel diameter were desired, kernels
with a mean diameter of ~390 μm would effectively fill the existing gap between irradiation data for
350 μm and 425 μm diameter UCO kernels, while kernels with a mean diameter of ~475 μm would
represent a modest step toward larger kernels for improved fuel loading. In both cases, the distribution of
kernel diameters would overlap with the distribution of kernel diameters in previously irradiated UCO
TRISO, giving good assurance of good performance over the full range. Establishing qualification data
for significantly larger particles (e.g., TRISO particles with 800 μm diameter kernels) would represent a
substantial step away from the existing irradiation database and may need to be considered as a separate
experiment. Given that the existing irradiation database for UCO TRISO already covers the AGR
specification space reasonably well, including different kernel diameters as a variable in an irradiation
campaign intended to expand qualification data to the full AGR specification space is likely not a priority
unless it is generally deemed to be so by the particle fuel industry.

2.2 KERNEL ASPECT RATIO

Based on the experience of the AVR Program with oblate spheroid kernels due to gel sphere deformation
in an external gelation process, kernel aspect ratio was included as a specified parameter in each of the
AGR irradiation campaigns [11]. However, the specific mechanism leading to kernels with a high aspect
ratio is linked to the external gelation process, and the internal gelation process used by the AGR Program
for kernel synthesis did not suffer from this issue [29]. There are still deviations from sphericity
observable in the AGR kernels; however, these deviations are generally localized facets and points that
are less well captured by the relatively crude measurement of aspect ratio [11]. Given the basis for kernel
asphericity specification being concerns over local stress concentration in the coating layers, more
localized measurements of kernel curvature may be more fitting. However, direct specification of kernel
sphericity may not be necessary from a fuel performance perspective so long as the kernels are round
enough to fluidize well during buffer deposition. This is because deviations from sphericity in the kernel
have been found to not propagate significantly into the coating layers. Rather, deviations from sphericity
originating in the buffer layer have been found to propagate through the following coating layers and are
the primary source of local variations in curvature that may act as stress concentration points in the
structural coating layers [30].

2.3 UCO KERNEL CHEMISTRY

Relative to the UO2 kernels that were used in the AVR Program, the UCO kernels predominantly used in
the AGR Program enable improved performance at higher burnup at the cost of more complex fabrication
and potentially lower retention of FPs within the kernel [11]. This kernel chemistry was first used in
TRISO for Fort Saint Vrain and fabricated at General Atomics [3]. The improved performance at elevated
burnup for UCO kernels relative to UO2 kernels is a function of the presence of uranium carbide within
the heterogenous mixture of UO2, UC2−x, and UC. Regardless of the carbide phase stoichiometry, this
effect occurs by gettering of oxygen released by fission of uranium in oxide form, converting original
uranium carbide into new uranium oxide. This prevents the buildup of free oxygen in TRISO particles
with UCO kernels so long as there is sufficient uranium carbide present, mitigating the production of CO
by reaction of liberated oxygen with the carbonaceous buffer layer, which can result in overpressurization
of TRISO particles with UO2 kernels at high burnup [31]. The amount of uranium carbide necessary to
achieve this beneficial effect may be calculated based on the intended fuel burnup of a given reactor
design, and generally it is not desirable to greatly exceed this amount, as great carbon inclusion makes the
sol–gel process more difficult and retention of some FPs is lower in the carbide phase.

In the AGR Program, the fraction of uranium carbide was set intentionally high (~25% carbide phase)
compared with the necessary amount for the intended burnup (~5.5%) [31]. This decision was likely
driven by uncertainty in the distribution of uranium carbide within the population of kernels since only

 7

bulk chemical analysis methods were used to determine the relative ratios of uranium, carbon, and oxygen
present in kernels. In addition to this limitation in measuring the distribution of uranium carbide within
UCO kernels, bulk chemical analysis was sensitive to variations in carbide phase stoichiometry and to the
presence of unreacted free carbon in the kernels. Enhanced characterization methods developed since the
start of the AGR Program based on automated image analysis of optical microscopy images taken of
hundreds of UCO kernel cross sections have removed these limitations, enabling more direct
measurement of the carbide phase abundance on individual kernels [32]. This improved capability opens
the door to more precise specification of UCO kernel chemistry that is more closely connected to the
performance basis and that does not require the level of conservatism in the original AGR UCO kernel
specifications. The distribution of carbide phase observed in UCO kernels from the AGR Program is
shown in Figure 2 along with lower (red) and upper (blue) bounds on the mean for AGR-1 and AGR-3/4
(dashed) as well as AGR-2 and AGR-5/6/7 (dot-dashed). These data were not measured during fuel
fabrication, but have been subsequently measured on archived fuel samples [33]. Based on these values,
the typical standard deviation in carbide phase fraction was approximately 2.5%. Given the minimum
carbide phase fraction calculated by McMurray et al. for typical HTGR conditions of 5.5%, a mean
carbide phase fraction of 13% would be 3 standard deviations above this nominal minimum threshold for
in-reactor performance, resulting in a very small fraction of kernels below that limit. Allowing for some
additional conservatism due to the uncertainties in the calculated minimum carbide phase fraction, this
may be rounded up to a 15% carbide phase fraction, which conveniently matches with the lower bound of
the AGR-1 specification. These low-C UCO kernels would have significantly less carbide phase than the
AGR UCO kernels, potentially resulting in easier fabrication and reduced mobility of some FPs [11].
Thus, if UCO kernel stoichiometry is to be considered as a variable in a follow-on irradiation, it is
recommended that carbide phase content in this range (~15%) be targeted to lower the total carbide
content while maintaining a conservatively sufficient amount for intended HTGR burnup levels across the
kernel distribution. UCO kernels with higher carbide content near the upper bound of the AGR
specification space could also be pursued, but this is likely only worthwhile if there are ultra-high burnup
applications that would warrant the greater difficulty in fuel fabrication. A nominal distribution of carbide
phase for UCO kernels with a mean carbide phase fraction of 15% and a standard deviation of 2.5% based
on prior AGR experience is shown for reference in Figure 2.

2.4 KERNEL DENSITY

Minimum kernel density has consistently been a specified parameter for TRISO fuels in general and the
AGR Program in particular; however, there is no known failure mode for TRISO particles due to low-
density kernels. From a reactor optimization standpoint, higher-density kernels are desirable to maximize
uranium loading and to reduce the occurrence of fractured kernels leading to abnormally shaped TRISO
particles. However, any such particles on fractured kernels should either be removed during material
upgrading for size and shape or be identified during characterization of particle asphericity/aspect ratio.
Thus, specification of kernel density is relevant as a fabrication target for optimization of reactor design
and fabrication efficiency but is not a parameter of interest for fuel performance testing.

2.5 KERNEL IMPURITY CONTENT

The allowable impurity content within the fuel kernels is included in each of the AGR campaign fuel
specifications. For most elements considered, a limit of < 100 ppm was used, with the exception of
phosphorus and sulfur, which were given limits of < 1,000 ppm. However, as shown in Table 3, the actual
content of these impurities was far below these limits. These elemental impurities may be introduced due
to contamination during fabrication, but they may also result from trace elements present in the uranium
feedstock. Sourcing of high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) for fuel fabrication is an initial
challenge for first-of-a-kind designs, making tight limits on elemental impurities undesirable. Thus, in a
follow-on irradiation to establish acceptable performance in the full AGR specification space, the use of
deliberate salting of the sol–gel broth with impurities targeting the specified limits may be worthwhile.

2.6 INTERPLAY OF KERNEL PARAMETERS

The combination of the kernel parameters discussed herein into a test fuel for an irradiation campaign
requires consideration of the complexity added to fabrication steps. Salting of the sol–gel broth with
impurities could be performed on some or all of the kernel batches produced without significantly
affecting subsequent layer deposition steps, as the size and density of the kernels, which are the primary
factors influencing fluidization, will not significantly vary. Similarly, if multiple carbide phase contents
were to be included, separate kernel batches with the desired properties could be blended together in
composite, and the resulting mix of high and low carbide content kernels could be coated simultaneously
with minimal variation between them.

In contrast, exploring variations in kernel diameter and shifting to a new kernel chemistry (e.g., UN
kernels) would multiply the scope of the test matrix and require fully separated deposition of all the
coating variants to be considered. With this in mind, it is proposed that a follow-on irradiation to establish
performance at the margins of the AGR specification space focus on low carbide phase UCO kernels with
salted impurities near the AGR specification limits and a singular mean kernel diameter near the nominal
425 μm target used in the AGR-2 and AGR-5/6/7 irradiations. If significantly larger diameter kernels
and/or UN kernels are of substantial interest to industry to improve volumetric uranium loading,
evaluation of that fuel design would likely be best achieved with a singular series of coating layer
property targets rather than the following test matrix for coating properties which is designed to augment
the existing AGR irradiation data.

Consideration of kernel aspect ratio or local curvature variation is likely lower priority due to the
predominance of internal gelation routes for kernel fabrication and the limited impact of kernel
asphericities on variations in local curvature that could lead to stress concentrations in the structural

 10

coating layers. However, this is predicated on kernels remaining intact through the coating process, as
fractured kernels can introduce very strong asphericities [34].

 3. BUFFER PARAMETERS OF INTEREST

3.1 BUFFER THICKNESS

The interparticle variation in buffer layer thickness in TRISO particles is greater than that of the other
three coating layers due to the greater overall thickness of the layer and the more rapid deposition rate
entailed by deliberately low density. The distribution of buffer layer thicknesses within composites for the
AGR-1 baseline and all variants, the AGR-2 UCO TRISO, and the AGR-3/4 driver fuel is shown in
Figure 3. Also shown in dashed lines are the upper limit on the mean thickness (blue), the lower limit on
the mean thickness (red), and the critical lower limit for 1% of the population (black). The thickness of
the buffer layer is comparable between each of these irradiation campaigns, with the mean landing
slightly above the nominal target of 100 μm with an average standard deviation of approximately 8 μm.
Buffer thickness distribution data for AGR-1 and AGR-3/4 were taken from fuel fabrication reports,
while data for AGR-2 were from unpublished analysis performed at ORNL on archived fuel samples [23,
24, 25, 26, 27]. Raw data for AGR-5/6/7 particle buffer layer thickness are in the process of being added
to this analysis, but the reported mean thickness of 100.4 μm is comparable [28].

The set of irradiated TRISO particles from the AGR Program leaves notable gaps at the edges of the
specification space, particularly at the lower end. Shown in Figure 3 are nominal synthetic distributions
with low (red) and high (blue) buffer thickness centered on the limits on the mean from the AGR
specifications and with standard deviations equal to what was observed in the AGR fuel. In practical
terms, irradiation data of lower buffer thicknesses are likely of greatest interest for near-term TRISO
reactor deployment, as they are more directly linked to potential particle failure mechanisms through
insufficient accommodation of kernel swelling and gaseous FPs, and generally reducing the thickness of
coating layers is preferred to increase volumetric uranium loading.

3.2 BUFFER DENSITY

The other specified parameter for the buffer layer in AGR TRISO was the mean layer density. This is a
difficult parameter to measure, as the buffer layer is fragile and is deposited onto kernels before
measurement. The current best method used within the AGR Program for buffer density measurement is
mercury porosimetry on interrupted coating runs, in which layer deposition is stopped on a batch of
particles after the buffer layer is deposited. The average weight of those particles and the volume of a
several gram sample of particles are then measured, and the average buffer layer density is then
determined by subtracting the known average kernel weight and density and dividing the calculated
average buffer layer mass by the calculated average buffer layer volume [11]. This method has several
key limitations. First, it requires interrupted coating runs or hot sampling. Second, it measures only the
mean buffer density while providing no data on the interparticle distribution. Third, it generates mixed
HALEU/mercury waste, which is challenging to dispose of.

Due to the constraints of the measurement method, the available data on buffer density for AGR TRISO
are limited. Interrupted coating runs using kernel sizes and buffer deposition parameters identical to the
AGR-1 and AGR-3/4 driver fuel had a mean buffer density of 1.10 g/cm3 [23], interrupted coating runs
from AGR-2 UO2 TRISO had a mean buffer density of 0.99 g/cm3 (AGR-2 UCO TRISO buffer density
was not measured) [15], and hot-sampled AGR-5/6/7 TRISO had a mean buffer density of 1.03 g/cm3
[28]. These values do not fully span the specified ranges for buffer density of 0.88–1.18 for AGR-1 and
AGR-3/4 or 0.95–1.15 g/cm3 for AGR-2 and AGR-5/6/7.

If further study of the impact of buffer density on TRISO fuel performance is to be performed, it would be
worthwhile to pursue alternative methods of measuring the relative buffer density such that that the

 12

property distribution for the actual fuel can be measured instead of just the mean value for fuel fabricated
under similar conditions. If such a method is developed, results from it could likely be used to look back
to prior irradiations by applying it to archived AGR fuel samples.

3.3 INTERPLAY OF BUFFER PARAMETERS

Considering the performance bases of buffer thickness and density specification [11], there are two worst-
case combinations at the edges of the AGR specification space that would be reasonable selections for
additional targeted irradiation. First, particles with mean thickness at the lower limit of the AGR
specification and mean density at the upper limit of the AGR specification would present the greatest
possibility of particle failure by minimizing the ability of the buffer layer to sufficiently accommodate
kernel swelling and the production of fission gases. Second, particles with mean thickness at the upper
limit of the AGR specification and buffer density at the lower limit of the AGR specification would
present the greatest risk of particle failure due to high buffer shrinkage under irradiation combined with
stronger stitching between the buffer and IPyC layers, potentially leading to tearing of the IPyC and
exposure of the SiC layer to reactive FPs [11].

Although both buffer variants have merit for consideration, the first combination of thin, high-density
buffer is likely of greatest importance. The first reason for this is that existing AGR irradiation data tend
toward the higher side of the allowed range of buffer thickness. The second is that in general, reactor
designs will prefer thinner layers to increase effective uranium loading. The third is that lower-density
buffer is more difficult to fabricate due to its fragility.

4.3 IPyC ANISOTROPY

Unlike IPyC layer thickness and density, which are specified with upper and lower limits on the mean and
upper and lower critical regions, IPyC layer anisotropy has only been specified with an upper limit on the
mean and an upper critical region. This is because the basis for specification of IPyC layer anisotropy is
minimization of anisotropic irradiation-induced shrinkage to avoid internal fracture, and there is no
negative performance outcome associated with the IPyC layer being too isotropic [11]. In the AGR-1,
AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 campaigns, the anisotropy of the IPyC and OPyC layers was specified in terms of
the Bacon anisotropy factor, whereas in the AGR-5/6/7 specification, an updated metric of the layer
diattenuation was used [11]. In Figure 6, the distributions of measured diattenuation values for each AGR
campaign are shown along with the upper limit on the mean (blue dashed line) and the critical region limit
(black dashed line) with all values converted to diattenuation for ease of comparison. All IPyC anisotropy
data was taken from characterization reports generated at ORNL [23, 24, 25, 26, 35, 27, 36].

The spread of diattenuation values in the AGR data covers a relatively broad range, particularly when the
AGR-5/6/7 data are included, as that fuel had an average diattenuation approaching the specified upper
limit on the mean. As PIE of AGR-5/6/7 is ongoing, these data were not included in the EPRI topical
report. Once these data are factored into consideration of the proven range of performance for TRISO
IPyC layer diattenuation, further irradiation testing of high-diattenuation IPyC may be unnecessary.
However, a nominal distribution of such a fuel with a mean diattenuation at the upper limit on the mean
and a standard deviation based on the prior AGR fuels is shown in blue in Figure 6 for reference.

4.4 INTERPLAY OF IPyC PARAMETERS

Based on the above survey of irradiated IPyC layer properties for UCO TRISO from the AGR Program,
the primary parameter that warrants further irradiation to demonstrate performance at the bounds of the
fuel specification is the IPyC layer thickness. However, if an irradiation of low and high IPyC thickness
particles is to be performed, the other IPyC parameters that influence the failure mechanisms potentially
associated with variations in IPyC thickness should be modulated in concert. This naturally leads to two
variants of IPyC to be irradiated.

First, particles with IPyC at the lower limit on the mean of layer thickness should be coupled with density
at the lower limit as well. For both parameters, the basis driving the specification of lower limits is
controlling layer permeability [11]. Excessive permeability of the IPyC layer may lead to increased
chlorine infiltration during SiC deposition, resulting in uranium dispersion from the kernel into the buffer
layer. Under irradiation, excessive permeability of the IPyC layer may lead to increased degradation of
the SiC layer by reactive FPs [11]. Combining low IPyC thickness with low IPyC density represents the
highest permeability of that layer and the worst-case scenario for these failure mechanisms.

Conversely, particles with IPyC at the upper limit on the mean of layer thickness should be coupled with
density and anisotropy at the upper limit as well. Excessively thick IPyC may increase internal stresses
and thus the probability of IPyC cracking under irradiation, exposing the SiC layer to FP attack [11]. This
mechanism couples with IPyC at the upper limit of diattenuation, as anisotropic shrinkage of the IPyC
will also contribute to internal stresses within the layer [11]. Because density and diattenuation of the PyC
layers are generally linked, targeting density near the upper limit on the mean is a natural fit. There is
some evidence that SiC layer failure is reduced by strong interstitching with the IPyC layer, preventing
debonding during irradiation and allowing the IPyC layer to exert beneficial compressive stress onto the

 16

SiC layer [11]. The inclusion of particles with IPyC layer density at the upper limit will test the
performance implications of this, as they will have reduced interlayer stitching.

Grain size of the SiC layer in the AGR irradiation campaigns was not quantitatively specified. Rather, a
visual standard for unacceptably coarse SiC was provided along with a visual standard of “good” SiC.

5.4 INTERPLAY OF SiC PARAMETERS

While the ranges of SiC layer thicknesses (34.8–36.2 μm) and densities (3.196–3.209 g/cm3) identified to
reliably provide satisfactory performance in the EPRI topical report [21] are very narrow, substantial
relaxation of the bounds placed on industrial fuel fabricators may be realized if the AGR-3/4 driver fuel
performance can be considered in setting the lower limit on SiC layer thickness and the lack of a
performance basis for placing upper limits on SiC layer thickness and density is accepted. This may be
sufficient to render the SiC layer of TRISO particles industrially fabricable with acceptable efficiency;
however, if an irradiation is to be performed to broaden the database for UCO TRISO, then at least one
variant of SiC will necessarily be present. Given this, the highest priority should be SiC at the lower
limits of layer thickness and density with a relatively coarse microstructure at the upper limits of average
SiC grain area. This combination will result in relatively mechanically weak SiC with the highest
probability of layer fracture due to stresses during fabrication or irradiation, as well as an upper bounding
case on diffusive FP release during irradiation based on historic experience [11]. Of less interest would be
a second variant of SiC at the upper limits of layer thickness and density with a relatively fine
microstructure, as there are no negative in-reactor performance issues speculated to exist for this
combination [11].

 6. OPyC PARAMETERS OF INTEREST

6.1 OPyC THICKNESS

The distributions of OPyC layer thickness included in the AGR irradiations are shown in Figure 10 along
with the lower (red dashed line) and upper (blue dashed line) limits on the mean and critical lower limit
(black dashed line) from the AGR specifications. OPyC thickness distribution data for AGR-1 and AGR-
3/4 were taken from fuel fabrication reports, while data for AGR-2 were from unpublished analysis
performed at ORNL on archived fuel samples [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Reported mean OPyC thickness and
standard deviation was used to generate a synthetic normal distribution for AGR-5/6/7 IPyC layer
thickness [28]. The data shown for AGR-2 UCO TRISO are based on the measurement of layer
thicknesses in archived material at ORNL, not the fuel acceptance data gathered originally at BWXT.
There is some discrepancy between these values, as the sample measured at ORNL was found to have a
mean OPyC layer thickness of 41.2 μm with a standard deviation of 1.9 μm, whereas the published
acceptance data from BWXT have a mean OPyC layer thickness of 43.4 μm with a standard deviation of
2.9 μm [15]. The EPRI topical report used the published acceptance data in determining the range of
OPyC layer thicknesses that may be relied upon to provide satisfactory performance, resulting in a range
of 39.1–44.3 μm [21]. Once the AGR-5/6/7 data are included in consideration of proven TRISO fuel
parameters, the relatively thin OPyC layer of that fuel (mean thickness of 35.0 μm) should result in a
sufficiently broad range of qualified thicknesses for efficient industrial TRISO fabrication.

From a fuel performance perspective, the primary function of the OPyC layer is to protect the SiC layer
from mechanical damage during handling with secondary functions of acting as a last barrier to gaseous
FP release in cases of SiC failure, mitigation of transmitted mechanical stresses due to matrix dimensional
changes, and presumed transmission of beneficial compressive stress to the SiC layer (supported by
modeling) [11], but PIE commonly shows gaps between the SiC and OPyC layers) [13, 15, 16]. Each of
these functions are dependent on the OPyC layer being sufficiently thick, with the only hypothesized

 20

failure linked to overly thick OPyC being increased internal irradiation-induced stresses resulting in layer
fracture. However, even in the case of such hypothetical fracture, the OPyC layer would still perform its
primary function of protecting the SiC layer from mechanical damage while failing in its less critical
secondary functions. This rationale is behind the inclusion of a lower critical limit on OPyC thickness in
the AGR specifications with no corresponding upper critical limit.

Thus, in terms of additional irradiation performance data, OPyC with a thickness near the lower specified
limit from the AGR specification is of the greatest interest, with a nominal distribution shown in red in
Figure 10. Once the full performance data from AGR-5/6/7 are available, it is possible that an even lower
mean OPyC layer thickness could be targeted, as that fuel was already below the nominal AGR
specification limit. A nominal distribution of OPyC with a thickness near the upper specified limit from
the AGR specification is also shown in Figure 10 in blue but is of substantially less interest.

6.2 OPyC DENSITY

The distributions of OPyC layer densities measured for the AGR fuels are shown in Figure 11 along with
the lower (red dashed line) and upper (blue dashed line) limits on the mean and the lower and upper
critical limits (black dashed lines). Compared with other parameters, the range of OPyC densities
irradiated through the AGR Program is notably tightly clustered compared with the allowed range in the
specification, as reflected in the suggested range of 1.895–1.914 g/cm3 provided in the EPRI topical
report [21]. OPyC density distribution data for AGR-1 and AGR-3/4 were taken from fuel fabrication
reports [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Reported mean OPyC densities and standard deviations were used to generate
synthetic normal distributions for AGR-2 and AGR-5/6/7 IPyC layer densities [28, 15].

 21

The basis for the specification of an upper limit on OPyC layer density is inconclusive. One theoretical
concern is that high PyC density is generally associated with high anisotropy, which is linked to
anisotropic shrinkage and increased internal stresses. However, a separate specification exists for OPyC
layer anisotropy, so indirect control of this parameter via an upper limit on layer density is unnecessary.
As noted in the Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Specification Technical Bases report, modeling of fuel
performance with PARFUME has found a negligible dependence of SiC failure on OPyC density [11].
The basis for the specification of a lower limit on OPyC layer density is arguably stronger, with primary
concerns of increased FP release from particles with failed SiC due to greater OPyC layer permeability
and enhanced bonding between OPyC and matrix material leading to greater risk of OPyC fracture and
potential transmission of undesirable stresses to the SiC layer [11].

Given this context, irradiation of TRISO particles with OPyC density near the lower limit specified by the
AGR Program would be of greatest interest in expanding the irradiation database for this parameter. A
nominal distribution for such a fuel is shown in red in Figure 11. The alternate case of OPyC near the
upper limit specified by the AGR Program is shown in blue in Figure 11 but is of lesser importance.

6.4 INTERPLAY OF OPyC PARAMETERS

Consideration of the bases for the specification of OPyC layer parameters yields two combinations that
could be irradiated to establish performance at the bounds of the AGR specification space. First, particles
with thin, low-density OPyC would capture the primary failure mechanisms considered for the layer by
minimizing mechanical strength to protect the SiC layer from external damage and maximizing the
permeability of the layer to increase matrix bonding and potentially allow enhanced release of gaseous
FPs in particles with failed SiC.

The second combination to be considered would be particles with thick, high-density OPyC with
correspondingly high anisotropy. The nominal concern for such particles would be higher internal stresses
due to excessive layer thickness and anisotropic shrinkage resulting in layer fracture and removal of
beneficial compressive stresses from the SiC as well as the final barrier to gaseous FP release for particles
with failed SiC. The impact of this failure mode should be considered less critical, as the OPyC in this
case would still have executed its primary function of protecting the SiC from external mechanical
damage. Thus, this potential variant is of less interest for targeted irradiation.

 7. PROPOSED INTEGRAL FUEL IRRADIATION TEST MATRIX

7.1 NOMINAL EIGHT VARIANT TEST MATRIX

In the preceding section, the combined impact of variations in particle properties is considered on a per
layer basis; however, the properties and performance of each layer in TRISO particles impact the

 23

performance of the other layers as well. Thus, any kernel or layer variants that are included in a test
matrix for irradiation should be included in combination with each other variant to demonstrate
performance under their combined effects unless a strong case can be made from the understanding of the
underlying bases for the specification of these parameters as to why they will not impact one another. The
nominal bounds for each parameter are the limits of the AGR fuel specifications, focusing this effort on
fully mapping that space, not breaking new ground. The variants of the kernel and each layer proposed in
the preceding section are:

  Kernels
 o Low carbide phase kernels with a target diameter near 425 μm deliberately salted with
 elemental impurities at levels near the AGR specification limits.
  Buffer
 o Thin, high-density buffer to test accommodation of kernel swelling and fission gases.
 o Thick, low-density buffer to test high irradiation-induced shrinkage and strong bonding to
 the IPyC layer.
  IPyC
 o Thin, low-density IPyC to test permeability and high shrinkage.
 o Thick, high-density, high-anisotropy IPyC to test anisotropic densification and high
 internal stresses with poor SiC layer interstitching.
  SiC
 o Thin, low-density, coarse-grained SiC to test low mechanical strength and presumed high
 layer permeability.
  OPyC
 o Thin, low-density OPyC to test low mechanical strength with higher layer permeability and
 increased matrix bonding.
 o Thick, high-density, high-anisotropy OPyC to test anisotropic densification and high
 internal stresses.
As an example of how parameter variations for the kernel and each coating layer may interact, consider
low carbide phase kernels with thin, high-density buffer; thick, high-density, high-anisotropy IPyC; thin,
low-density SiC; and thin, low-density OPyC. In this hypothetical case, CO(g) production in the kernel due
to low carbide phase combined with kernel swelling and fission gas production could overpressurize the
particle due to the reduced plenum provided by the buffer layer. Meanwhile, high anisotropic internal
stresses in the IPyC could trigger fracture of that layer, exposing the already thin and mechanically weak
SiC layer to chemical degradation by FP attack, resulting in rupture of the SiC layer due to internal gas
pressure. Finally, the ultimate barrier to FP release, the OPyC layer, could have been strongly bonded to
the matrix due to high permeability, which, coupled with its low mechanical strength, could result in full
TRISO failure. To be clear, this combination of parameters at the bounds of, but still within, the AGR
specification space is not anticipated to result in this combined particle failure; however, confidence in
fuel performance over the full span of properties encompassed by the AGR specification requires
demonstration that even in such a worst-case scenario, the fuel still performs with statistically sufficient
rigor.

In the proposed list of combinations of parameters for each layer, there are three layers with two sets of
parameters. If all of these were retained for an irradiation campaign, the result would be eight total fuel
variants, as given in Table 4. Demonstrating a 1E−4 failure rate or better with 95% confidence would
require 48,000 particles with one particle failure allowed or 63,000 particles with two particle failures
allowed. Inclusion of such numbers of particles of each of the eight variants would correspond to 384,000
or 504,000 particles in total, on the same rough order as the AGR-5/6/7 irradiation.

7.2 LOWEST-PRIORITY VARIANTS TO CONSIDER FOR REMOVAL

Removal of some variants from the proposed test matrix would have a significant impact, allowing for
greater performance statistics for the remaining variants, broader testing across different irradiation
conditions, or reduced total scope and cost. Based on the discussed bases for the specification of each
parameter [11] in the preceding sections and the existing irradiation performance database provided by
the AGR Program for UCO TRISO [13, 15], the lowest-priority variants that could be removed are thick,
low-density buffer and thick, high-density OPyC.

The design space of thick, low-density buffer is likely of least interest for reactor design in terms of the
buffer layer. Additional buffer layer thickness reduces the overall uranium loading of the particles, which
most reactor designs wish to increase if possible. Additionally, very-low-density buffer is difficult to
fabricate, handle, or characterize due to the fragility of the material. The established AGR data on buffer
thickness and density combined with additional irradiation data on thinner, higher-density buffer should
provide ample space for efficient fabrication of TRISO fuel to suit a broad range of reactor designs.

Similarly, additional irradiation data on thick, high-density OPyC is likely of lesser interest. Again,
thicker coating layers negatively affect overall uranium loading of the fuel, and the existing irradiation
data on OPyC density combined with additional data for lower-density OPyC should provide ample space
for efficient TRISO fabrication. Additionally, the technical basis for an upper limit on either OPyC layer
thickness or density is relatively weak, as there are not significant failure modes anticipated based on
irradiation experience or modeling for the upper limits of these parameters.

Removing one of these parameters from the proposed test matrix would result in a factor of two reduction
in the overall scope, and removing both would result in a factor of four reduction. If a scientific consensus
may be reached on these parameters being unnecessary to consider for further irradiation, then the
simplified test matrix could be used to reduce the scope of the potential irradiation campaign or increase
the performance statistics and broaden the range of irradiation conditions considered.

7.3 POTENTIAL EXPANSIONS TO THE AGR SPECIFICATION SPACE

The above test matrix is designed with the goal of demonstrating acceptable UCO TRISO performance at
the bounds of the AGR specification space that have not been adequately explored by the existing AGR
irradiation datasets. Expansion of the demonstrated performance regime beyond the bounds of the AGR
specification is possible but would represent a significant undertaking. Due to the complex interplay of
kernel and layer parameters in fuel performance, shifting one parameter significantly outside the range of
prior irradiations would require at least consideration of the potential impact of changing that parameter
on the performance implications of other affected parameters. Such an effort would be more akin to the
early AGR irradiations, demonstrating acceptable performance near the center of a nominal specification.

The parameters likely to be of greatest interest to industry in moving beyond the bounds of the AGR
specification space involve increasing uranium loading, typically either by substantially increasing the
kernel diameter or by shifting to a UN kernel to take advantage of that material’s higher uranium density.
Both concepts may be worth integral fuel irradiation campaigns similar to the AGR Program to
demonstrate continued strong performance for TRISO particles with substantially different kernels;
however, these first irradiations would likely be best served to focus on maintaining other parameters near
the center of their nominal specifications, with secondary irradiations testing the bounds of the design
space to follow as warranted.

 8. CONCLUSION

The AGR Program has been highly successful in demonstrating the performance of TRISO fuel in
prototypical HTGR conditions; however, the tight distributions of as-fabricated fuel parameters have led
to a qualified fuel space that is substantially narrower than the original AGR specification space. This
poses a potential challenge to the effective use of the AGR data in support of commercial TRISO reactor
designs, as industrial fabrication of particle fuel to those tight distributions will likely be challenging. One
solution that has been proposed is a targeted follow-on irradiation to the primary AGR mission focused
on demonstrating continued fuel performance in the edges and corners of the fuel specification. Based on
review of existing as-fabricated data for AGR fuel in conjunction with the current understanding of the
bases for specification, a list of key parameters to consider for targeted variation has been identified.
These parameters can be combined on a layer-by-layer basis to provide realistic combinations of cross-
layer parameters that capture worst-case scenarios on a per layer level based on current understanding of
the mechanisms in play. Depending on downselection from these parameter sets, this would result in 2–8
variants for irradiation.
