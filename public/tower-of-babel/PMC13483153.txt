Association between early postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord and postoperative C5 nerve root dysfunction after anterior cervical decompression and fusion: a retrospective imaging cohort study using standardized measurements
Authors: Haoteng Chen, Fei Liu, Shiyu Zhuo, Yuanxiang Lin, Changzhen Jiang, Tong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundC5 nerve root palsy is a challenging complication following anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF) or anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) with a reported incidence of 1–5% (1). While posterior displacement of the spinal cord with resultant nerve root tethering is a leading hypothesis, high-quality evidence quantifying early postoperative cord migration and its association with C5 palsy risk, based on standardized imaging, remains scarce.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between early postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord, measured using a standardized magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) protocol, and the occurrence of symptomatic C5 palsy, and to explore its potential discriminative value with postoperative C5 palsy.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted. Patients who underwent single- or double-level ACDF/ACCF for cervical spondylotic myelopathy between January 2019 and December 2023 were included. An initial cohort was identified via surgical log review. Patients with eligible pre- and early postoperative (3 days to 6 weeks) MRI were enrolled. The distance from the anterior surface of the spinal cord to the posterior vertebral body line at the C4/5 and C5/6 levels was measured independently and in a blinded manner by two radiologists. The primary outcome was postoperative symptomatic C5 palsy. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to explore the discriminative ability.ResultsOne hundred patients were ultimately included, with a C5 palsy incidence of 10.0% (10/100). The C4/5 posterior migration distance was significantly greater in the C5 palsy group than in the non-palsy group (3.42 ± 0.47 mm vs. 2.04 ± 0.76 mm, P < 0.001). Multivariable analysis showed that, after adjusting for age and preoperative intramedullary high signal, C4/5 posterior migration was an independent risk factor for C5 palsy (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.89, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.35–6.20, P = 0.006). ROC analysis revealed that the C4/5 posterior migration distance predicted C5 palsy with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86. The optimal cut-off value was 2.85 mm (sensitivity 80.0%, specificity 84.4%).ConclusionThis retrospective exploratory study suggests that greater early postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord at the C4/5 level may be associated with an increased risk of symptomatic C5 nerve root palsy following anterior cervical decompression and fusion. Although a migration distance of 2.85 mm demonstrated preliminary discriminative ability in this cohort, the small number of C5 palsy events and the retrospective single-center design substantially limit the robustness and external applicability of the findings. Therefore, the identified threshold should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires validation in larger prospective multicenter studies before clinical application.

Introduction

Anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF/ACCF) is widely considered a standard surgical treatment for single- or two-level cervical disc disease and selected cases of cervical spondylotic myelopathy, whereas posterior decompression approaches are more commonly applied in multilevel cervical myelopathy and severe cervical spondylosis, effectively relieving compression on the spinal cord and nerve roots [1, 2]. However, postoperative complications persist, among which symptomatic C5 nerve root palsy garners particular attention due to its distinctive clinical presentation and unpredictable prognosis [3, 4].Characterized by new-onset or worsened weakness of the deltoid and/or biceps brachii muscles postoperatively, Previous studies have reported that postoperative C5 nerve root palsy/radiculopathy occurs more frequently after posterior cervical decompression procedures than after anterior approaches. This difference may be related to the greater posterior displacement (“drift”) of the spinal cord after posterior decompression, which can increase traction on the anatomically short and relatively fixed C5 nerve root [5–7]. In clinical practice, postoperative C5 dysfunction may also present with radicular pain and sensory symptoms with or without motor weakness, and some authors therefore prefer the broader term “C5 radiculopathy”.While most patients experience partial or complete neurological recovery, its occurrence significantly prolongs hospital stays, increases healthcare costs, and severely impacts patients’ quality of life and confidence in rehabilitation [8].The pathogenesis of C5 palsy is multifactorial and not fully elucidated. Leading hypotheses include intraoperative direct injury, nerve root tethering, segmental spinal cord ischemia or injury, ischemia involving segmental radicular arteries supplying the spinal cord, and reperfusion injury [9, 10]. Among the proposed mechanisms, the most widely accepted hypothesis is that postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord increases traction on the anatomically vulnerable C5 nerve root.Due to its relatively short, horizontal course and limited mobility near the intervertebral foramen, the C5 nerve root may be subjected to excessive mechanical stretching after decompression-induced posterior cord shift, potentially resulting in traction-related injury and microvascular ischemia [11–13]. In the present study, the term “posterior displacement” was used to describe the segmental posterior drift of the spinal cord observed on postoperative MRI, rather than true anatomical migration. This theory posits that, due to its anatomical features—specifically its relatively short and straight course and a fixed dural sleeve near the foramen—the C5 nerve root is more susceptible to traction than other nerve roots when the spinal cord drifts posteriorly following decompression [9, 14].Multiple studies on posterior cervical surgery have found a correlation between the degree of posterior displacement of the spinal cord and the incidence of C5 palsy [15].However, for anterior cervical surgery, studies investigating this relationship are relatively fewer and yield inconsistent conclusions [16, 17].

Previous research has several limitations [18]. Firstly, most studies are retrospective with limited sample sizes, resulting in insufficient statistical power [19].Secondly, there is a lack of standardization in measuring the key variable—“posterior displacement of the spinal cord.” Inconsistent measurement levels, reference lines, and imaging timepoints (postoperative day) make comparisons across studies difficult. For instance, Pennington et al. [10] .found cord migration not to be an independent predictor for C5 palsy after posterior surgery, but the measurement methodology and surgical approach differ from anterior procedures. Third, many studies analyzed multiple potential risk factors concurrently without clearly isolating the independent effect of cord migration itself. Finally, there is a paucity of exploratory imaging-based studies evaluating the association between quantitative spinal cord migration measurements and postoperative C5 palsy risk [20].

Given these limitations, this study aimed to address the following key questions through a methodologically rigorous retrospective cohort study: (1) Is early postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord, measured using a standardized method, independently associated with the occurrence of C5 palsy following ACDF/ACCF? (2) If associated, is there a potentially discriminative exploratory threshold? (3) What is the predictive performance of this measurement? To this end, we employed a “surgical log review” method to construct an unbiased cohort, established strict imaging inclusion criteria and a blinded measurement protocol, and utilized multivariable statistical modeling to control for confounders, aiming to provide a more standardized exploratory imaging analysis.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (Approval No.: 2026-041), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study population

The study population was identified using the “surgical log review” method. The hospital’s electronic medical record system was searched to identify all patients who underwent elective, primary, single- or double-level ACDF/ACCF for cervical spondylotic myelopathy or radiculopathy in our spine surgery department between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023.

Inclusion criteria

① Age ≥ 18 years; ② Surgical level included C4/5 and/or C5/6; ③ High-quality preoperative (within 3 months before surgery) and early postoperative (3 days to 6 weeks after surgery) cervical 3.0T MRI scans, including sagittal and axial T2-weighted sequences, were available; ④ Complete clinical data, including detailed preoperative neurological assessment and at least 3 months of postoperative follow-up records.

Exclusion criteria

① History of previous cervical spine surgery; ② Etiology of trauma, tumor, infection, or inflammatory disease; ③ Concomitant severe ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) or diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH); ④ Severe metal artifacts on postoperative MRI or incomplete scan coverage precluding measurement; ⑤ Pre-existing C5 nerve root motor deficit (Medical Research Council grade < 5).

Data collection

Clinical data

Two researchers not involved in image analysis extracted data from electronic medical records in a blinded fashion. The collected data included: ① Demographics: age, sex; ② Medical history: symptom duration, smoking history, diabetes mellitus, hypertension; ③ Preoperative neurological function: modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score; ④ Surgical details: surgical level(s), procedure (ACDF or ACCF), implant type and size, use of anterior plate, intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) results; ⑤ Postoperative complications: focused on new-onset or worsened unilateral/bilateral deltoid/biceps weakness. In the present study, postoperative C5 nerve root palsy was defined as the motor-dominant subtype of C5 nerve root dysfunction, characterized by new-onset unilateral or bilateral deltoid and/or biceps brachii muscle weakness ≥ 1 grade according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, without concomitant worsening of lower limb symptoms, confirmed by the attending surgeon’s clinical diagnosis and follow-up [4].

Imaging analysis

All imaging measurements were performed on a dedicated medical imaging workstation (RadiAnt DICOM Viewer) according to a pre-established standardized protocol.

Posterior displacement of the spinal cord distance: On preoperative and early postoperative mid-sagittal T2-weighted MRI, at the level of the C4/5 and C5/6 intervertebral discs, the perpendicular distance from the anterior-most point of the spinal cord to a line connecting the posterior-superior cortices of the corresponding inferior vertebral body (C5 or C6) was measured (Fig. 1). To ensure reproducibility, each level was measured three times, and the average was calculated. The posterior migration distance was calculated as the difference between postoperative and preoperative values for that level (unit: mm, recorded to the nearest 0.1 mm). A positive value indicated posterior migration.Spinal Cord Cross-Sectional Area (CSA): On preoperative and postoperative axial T2-weighted images at the disc center level, the spinal cord boundary was manually traced, and the CSA (unit: mm²) was computed automatically by the software. The postoperative CSA change rate was calculated: (Postop CSA - Preop CSA) / Preop CSA × 100%.Other Imaging Parameters: The minimum C4/5 foraminal width (axial T2) and the presence of pre-existing intramedullary T2 hyperintensity at C4/5 were recorded.Inter- and Intra-rater Reliability: All measurements were performed independently by two senior spine surgeons (R1, R2). Four weeks later, R1 repeated measurements on a random sample of 30 patients. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated to assess reliability.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation(SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage) and compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

To explore factors potentially associated with C5 palsy, univariate analysis was first performed to screen potential variables (P < 0.2). Subsequently, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection to avoid overfitting. Selected variables were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Internal validation of the final model was performed using the Bootstrap method (1000 repetitions).

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

Initial screening identified 132 patients eligible for surgery. Twelve patients without qualified postoperative MRI and 20 with missing key clinical or imaging data were excluded, resulting in 100 patients for final analysis. The mean age was 57.5 ± 9.2 years, and 52 (52.0%) were male. All patients underwent successful surgery, with a mean follow-up of 14.2 ± 6.5 months. Postoperative symptomatic C5 palsy occurred in 10 patients (10.0%), unilateral in 8 and bilateral in 2. The median time to palsy onset was postoperative day 3 (range: 1–7 days). The C5 palsy and non-palsy groups showed no significant differences in baseline characteristics including age, sex, symptom duration, smoking history, diabetes, preoperative mJOA score, number of surgical levels, or surgical approach (P > 0.05). However, the proportion of patients with preoperative C4/5 intramedullary T2 hyperintensity was significantly higher in the C5 palsy group (60.0% vs. 26.7%, P = 0.035). Details are shown in Table 1.

Imaging measurement reliability and results

Measurement of posterior displacement of the spinal cord showed excellent reliability. The intra-rater ICC for C4/5 migration distance was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.89–0.97), and the inter-rater ICC was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.85–0.95).

Primary analysis revealed that the C4/5 posterior migration distance was significantly greater in the C5 palsy group (3.42 ± 0.47 mm) than in the non-palsy group (2.04 ± 0.76 mm; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). In contrast, the difference in posterior migration distance at the C5/6 level was not statistically significant between the two groups (2.21 ± 0.68 mm vs. 1.98 ± 0.71 mm, P = 0.212). The postoperative spinal cord CSA change rate was also higher in the C5 palsy group (20.1% ± 11.2% vs. 14.7% ± 9.5%, P = 0.092), though this did not reach statistical significance.

Risk factor analysis for C5 nerve root palsy

Univariate analysis identified preoperative C4/5 intramedullary hyperintensity, C4/5 posterior migration distance, and CSA change rate as variables potentially associated with C5 palsy (P < 0.2). LASSO regression selected four variables for the multivariable model: age, preoperative hyperintensity, C4/5 migration distance, and CSA change rate.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, after adjusting for age and preoperative intramedullary hyperintensity, C4/5 posterior migration distance remained independently associated with C5 palsy (aOR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.35–6.20, P = 0.006) (Table 2). This suggests that each 1-mm increase in C4/5 posterior migration distance was associated with a 2.89-fold increase in the odds of C5 palsy. Preoperative intramedullary hyperintensity showed a trend towards significance (aOR = 4.12, 95% CI: 0.88–19.35, P = 0.074). Age and CSA change rate were not significant in the multivariable model. Internal validation via Bootstrap for the final model yielded a corrected C-statistic of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.80–0.95), suggesting preliminary discriminatory ability within this cohort (Fig. 3).

Exploratory analysis of discriminative ability

ROC curve analysis showed that the C4/5 posterior migration distance demonstrated exploratory discriminative performance for postoperative C5 palsy (95% CI: 0.76–0.96) (Fig. 4). The optimal cut-off value determined by the Youden Index was 2.85 mm. At this threshold, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value for predicting C5 palsy were 80.0%, 84.4%, 34.8%, and 97.6%, respectively.

Discussion

This methodologically standardized retrospective cohort study systematically evaluated the association between early postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord—measured via standardized MRI—and the risk of C5 nerve root palsy in the context of anterior cervical surgery. Our key finding is that greater posterior displacement of the spinal cord at the C4/5 level, particularly values exceeding approximately 2.85 mm in this cohort, may be associated with an increased risk of symptomatic C5 palsy, and this metric demonstrates promising discriminative ability in this exploratory analysis (AUC = 0.86). This finding provides preliminary imaging-based observations potentially consistent with the “nerve root tethering” hypothesis for anterior approaches [9, 10] and proposes a preliminary quantitative threshold for future risk-stratification research.

Our results align with and extend findings from posterior surgery literature. Multiple studies on posterior decompression have indicated that wider laminectomies or larger opening angles lead to greater cord migration, increasing C5 palsy risk [15, 22]. Radcliff et al. [9] found that patients with C5 palsy after posterior surgery had greater laminectomy width and more pronounced cord drift at C5. Our study confirms that in anterior surgery, despite a different decompression mechanism (direct removal of anterior pathology), the spinal cord also undergoes posterior displacement. Our radiological findings may support the traction-related mechanism of postoperative C5 palsy. Greater posterior displacement of the spinal cord at the C4/5 level may increase mechanical tension on the anatomically short and relatively fixed C5 nerve root. Excessive traction may subsequently contribute to nerve root dysfunction through direct mechanical stress and possible microvascular ischemia. This suggests the “tethering” mechanism may be a common pathophysiological process across surgical approaches [14]. Compared with anterior cervical surgery, posterior decompression procedures generally produce greater posterior spinal cord displacement after decompression, which may partly explain the higher reported incidence of postoperative C5 palsy/radiculopathy in posterior approaches. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that clinically relevant posterior spinal cord displacement may also occur after anterior cervical surgery [17, 23].

The 2.85 mm risk threshold identified in this study may warrant further investigation. Baba et al. [20] previously reported that a posterior migration distance of 3.6 mm after posterior surgery was associated with C5 palsy. Our slightly lower threshold may reflect biomechanical and anatomical differences between anterior and posterior approaches. Anterior surgery restores intervertebral height via disc space distraction and cage implantation, potentially altering local cervical curvature and foraminal morphology, making the nerve root more sensitive to a similar degree of posterior migration. Furthermore, our study focused on early postoperative MRI (3 days to 6 weeks), a period considered the “critical window” for C5 palsy onset [21].Prospective studies have shown dynamic changes in spinal cord position early after surgery. Our measurement timeframe encompasses this high-risk period, ensuring that the results more accurately reflect morphological changes directly associated with clinical symptoms.

In the multivariable analysis, preoperative C4/5 intramedullary T2 hyperintensity showed a trend towards being a risk factor (OR = 4.12, P = 0.074). This aligns with findings by Chiba et al. [15] and Hasegawa et al. [14], who observed that C5 palsy patients often have signal changes in the corresponding cord segment. This may suggest that pre-existing chronic spinal cord injury (e.g., edema, gliosis) renders the cord more vulnerable to the mechanical traction or hemodynamic changes occurring postoperatively. However, its effect was attenuated after adjusting for migration distance, suggesting that cord migration may be the more direct and critical pathogenic event, while preoperative hyperintensity might lower the cord’s tolerance to traction by affecting its biomechanical properties or microcirculatory reserve [24].

This study has several methodological strengths. First, the “surgical log review” method and strict imaging inclusion criteria minimized selection bias. Second, the standardized blinded measurement protocol and high ICC values ensured the reliability of the core variable data. Third, we employed LASSO regression for variable selection combined with Bootstrap internal validation, enhancing the internal stability of the exploratory statistical model. These design features may improve the methodological consistency of the present exploratory analysis [19, 25].

Nevertheless, limitations exist. First, the retrospective design cannot establish causality. Second, single-center data and a moderate sample size may constrain the external validity of the conclusions [26].Third, with such a low event count, ROC analysis is prone to overfitting, which can lead to unstable estimates of the area under the curve and the optimal cut-off value. Therefore, the reported threshold of 2.85 mm should be interpreted as an exploratory finding that requires external validation in larger, prospective multicenter cohorts before any clinical application.Fourth, although we controlled for multiple confounders, unmeasured confounding variables, such as the specific decompression width or degree of intervertebral distraction, may persist. Fifth, we did not incorporate advanced imaging sequences (e.g., diffusion tensor imaging) to assess changes in white matter tract integrity.

Although the present findings may provide preliminary imaging-based insight into the potential mechanism of postoperative C5 palsy, the current evidence remains insufficient to support routine clinical decision-making based solely on postoperative spinal cord migration measurements. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating larger cohorts, longer follow-up durations, and advanced imaging modalities such as diffusion tensor imaging are warranted to further validate these findings and clarify the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.

Conclusion

This retrospective exploratory study suggests that greater early postoperative posterior displacement of the spinal cord at the C4/5 level may be associated with an increased risk of symptomatic C5 nerve root palsy following anterior cervical decompression and fusion. Although a migration distance of 2.85 mm demonstrated preliminary discriminative ability in this cohort, the small number of C5 palsy events and the retrospective single-center design substantially limit the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the identified threshold should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires validation in larger prospective multicenter studies before clinical application.
