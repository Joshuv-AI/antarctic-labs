Central Limit Theorems for Outcome Records in Disordered Quantum Trajectories
Lubashan Pathirana

Abstract
We prove annealed central limit theorems for finite pattern counts in the measurement record of discrete-time quantum trajectories generated by repeated measurements in a disordered environment. Under summable mixing assumptions on the environment and an annealed trace-norm forgetting property for the associated non-selective channel cocycle, we first establish the CLT under the annealed law determined by the dynamically stationary state. This part applies to general disordered quantum instruments, and in particular is not restricted to the perfect-measurement regime, and it complements the law of large numbers established in [ EMP25 ] for the same disordered setting and provides a disordered counterpart of the homogeneous CLT in [ AGS14 ] . We then introduce a coupling-based notion of admissibility for initial states and show that the same Gaussian limit extends to every admissible initial law, with unchanged centering and asymptotic variance. In the perfect-measurement setting, we further identify a general condition ensuring admissibility for every initial state, and hence obtain a universal annealed central limit theorem. We also provide practical sufficient criteria for this condition and verify the assumptions in a broad family of examples, including disordered walk-type models generated by finite group actions.



1
Introduction

The statistics of repeated measurement outcomes of a quantum system are modeled by a
quantum instrument

\mathcal{V}

[
DL70
]
.
Given a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a finite outcome set
\mathcal{A}
, a quantum instrument is a family
\mathcal{V}:=\{\mathcal{T}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
of completely positive, trace-non-increasing linear maps
\mathcal{T}_{a}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

\Phi\;:=\;\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{T}_{a}\quad\text{is trace-preserving}.

(1.1)

Here
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the algebra of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Thus
\Phi
is completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP), i.e. a
quantum channel

[
Wat18
]
.

A particularly important case is the
perfect-measurement
regime, where each recorded outcome
a\in\mathcal{A}
is associated with a single Kraus operator:

\mathcal{T}_{a}(\rho)=V_{a}\rho V_{a}^{\dagger}.

More generally, a recorded outcome may combine several microscopic alternatives, in which case

\mathcal{T}_{a}(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{r(a)}V_{a,j}\rho V_{a,j}^{\dagger}.

This
imperfect-measurement
regime arises naturally from mixed probe preparations, degenerate readout, detector misidentification, or coupling to unmonitored degrees of freedom; see, e.g.,
[
ABBP25
,
SDSR12
]
. For example, if
\eta=(\eta_{i,j})
is a left-stochastic confusion matrix linking an ideal microscopic outcome
j
to a reported outcome
i
, then

\mathcal{T}_{i}(\rho)=\sum_{j}\eta_{i,j}\,V_{j}\rho V_{j}^{\dagger}.

The quenched and annealed path-space constructions below apply to this general setting. Later, for the final universal transfer theorem, we impose additional structure in the perfect-measurement regime.

Given an initial state
\rho_{0}\in\states
and an outcome string
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
, the corresponding posterior state after
n
repeated measurements is

\rho_{n}:=\frac{(\mathcal{T}_{a_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{a_{1}})(\rho_{0})}{\operatorname{Tr}\left((\mathcal{T}_{a_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{a_{1}})(\rho_{0})\right)},

(1.2)

whenever the denominator is nonzero. By Born’s rule
[
Bor26
]
, the probability of observing the finite outcome string
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
is

\operatorname{Tr}\left((\mathcal{T}_{a_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{a_{1}})(\rho_{0})\right).

(1.3)

These finite-dimensional distributions define, by the Kolmogorov extension theorem, a unique probability measure,
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}}
, on the outcome space
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is the product
\sigma
-algebra, such that for every cylinder set

\{a_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{a_{n}\}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\times\cdots

one has

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}}\!\left(\{a_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{a_{n}\}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\times\cdots\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left((\mathcal{T}_{a_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{a_{1}})(\rho_{0})\right).

(1.4)

Thus
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}}
is the law of the measurement record associated with the initial state
\rho_{0}
.

Let

A_{n}:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{A},\qquad A_{n}(\bar{a})=a_{n},

be the canonical coordinate maps. For
L\in\mathbb{N}
, we also write

A_{1:L}:=(A_{1},\dots,A_{L}):\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{A}^{L}

for the block-coordinate map. The associated posterior-state process
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is then defined
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}}
-almost surely by

\rho_{n}(\bar{a}):=\frac{(\mathcal{T}_{A_{n}(\bar{a})}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{A_{1}(\bar{a})})(\rho_{0})}{\operatorname{Tr}\left((\mathcal{T}_{A_{n}(\bar{a})}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{A_{1}(\bar{a})})(\rho_{0})\right)}.

Thus
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is the
outcome process
, while
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is the corresponding
quantum trajectory
.

1.1
Disordered Quantum Trajectories

We now allow the instrument to depend on an external disorder parameter.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
be a probability space equipped with a
\pr
-preserving transformation
\theta:\Omega\to\Omega
.
For each
\omega\in\Omega
, let

\mathcal{V}_{\omega}=\{\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}

be a quantum instrument on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, so that each
\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}
is completely positive and trace-non-increasing and

\Phi_{\omega}:=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}

is trace-preserving for
\pr
-almost every
\omega
.
We assume that for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the map

\omega\longmapsto\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}

is
\mathcal{F}
-Borel measurable as a map into the finite-dimensional vector space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
. Equivalently, for each
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the map

\omega\longmapsto\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)

is
\mathcal{F}
-Borel measurable.
The probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
is called the
disorder dynamical system
or the
base system
.
A realization
\omega\in\Omega
is called a
disorder realization
or
disorder configuration
.

Fix a measurable initial state
\rho_{0}:\Omega\to\states
.
For a fixed disorder realization
\omega\in\Omega
, the
k
-th measurement uses the instrument
\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}
.
The corresponding quenched cylinder probabilities are

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}\!\left(\{a_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{a_{n}\}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\times\cdots\right):=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\left(\mathcal{T}_{a_{n};\theta^{n}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{a_{1};\theta(\omega)}\right)\!\bigl(\rho_{0}(\omega)\bigr)\right).

Again, by the Kolmogorov extension theorem, this defines a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}
on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma)
, called the
quenched outcome law
at environment (or at disorder realization)
\omega
.
The map

\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}})

is measurable; see
Lemma

A.3
.
We refer to
[
EMP25
]
for a detailed proof of the construction of quenched measures.
Although
[
EMPS25
,
EMP25
]
are written in the perfect regime, this construction uses only the instrument axioms and therefore extends verbatim to the imperfect regime.

With the canonical coordinates
A_{n}
, define the quenched posterior-state process by

\rho_{n}^{\omega}(\bar{a}):=\frac{\left(\mathcal{T}_{A_{n}(\bar{a});\theta^{n}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{A_{1}(\bar{a});\theta(\omega)}\right)\!\bigl(\rho_{0}(\omega)\bigr)}{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\left(\mathcal{T}_{A_{n}(\bar{a});\theta^{n}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{A_{1}(\bar{a});\theta(\omega)}\right)\!\bigl(\rho_{0}(\omega)\bigr)\right)},

(1.5)

for
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}
-almost every
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\sigma(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
.
Then for
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\geq 0
,

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}(A_{n+1}=a\mid\mathcal{H}_{n})=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n+1}(\omega)}(\rho_{n}^{\omega})\right),\qquad\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}\text{-a.s.},

and on
\{A_{n+1}=a\}
,

\rho_{n+1}^{\omega}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n+1}(\omega)}(\rho_{n}^{\omega})}{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n+1}(\omega)}(\rho_{n}^{\omega})\right)}.

Hence, for each fixed
\omega
, the posterior-state process is a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain, and the outcome process is a hidden Markov process with hidden process
(\rho_{n}^{\omega})_{n\geq 0}
.

Averaging over the disorder yields the
annealed law

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{0}}(d\omega,d\bar{a}):=\pr(d\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{0}(\omega);\omega}(d\bar{a})

(1.6)

on
(\Omega\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma)
. Let
\varsigma
denote the left shift on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and define the skew product

\tau(\omega,\bar{a}):=(\theta\omega,\varsigma\bar{a}).

(1.7)

Under
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{0}}
, the coordinate process
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is the measurement record in the disordered environment.

Outcome Frequencies.

Let
A_{n}:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{A}
be the canonical coordinate maps,
A_{n}(\bar{a})=a_{n}
, and let
\varsigma
denote the left shift on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The sequence
\bar{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the
measurement record
.
For a fixed block
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
, define the cylinder set

C_{\boldsymbol{b}}:=\{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}:a_{1}=b_{1},\dots,a_{m}=b_{m}\}.

(1.8)

The number of occurrences of the pattern
\boldsymbol{b}
with starting index at most
n
is

N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{a}):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{a}),\qquad\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{a}):=\mathbf{1}_{C_{\boldsymbol{b}}}(\varsigma^{k-1}\bar{a}).

We call
(N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b}})_{n\geq 1}
the
pattern counting process
associated with
\boldsymbol{b}
in the measurement record,
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
the
pattern indicator
at time
k
, and
\frac{1}{n}N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
the
empirical pattern frequency
.

The goal of this paper is to establish a
central limit theorem for the fluctuations of the empirical pattern frequencies

\frac{1}{n}N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
, complementing the
law of large numbers
obtained in
[
EMP25
]
. In
[
EMP25
]
, the authors work under the assumptions that

1.

the base system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
is ergodic, and

2.

a unique
dynamically stationary state
exists.

Although
[
EMP25
]
formulates the disordered model in the perfect regime, that is, with one Kraus operator per recorded outcome, the construction of the quenched and annealed laws and the corresponding ergodic arguments rely only on the instrument axioms: each component
\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}
is completely positive and trace-non-increasing, and
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}
is trace-preserving.
Thus, the LLN extends verbatim to the imperfect-measurement regime considered here.

Dynamically Stationary State.

A random initial state
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}:\Omega\to\states
is called
dynamically stationary
for the sequence of instruments
\left(\mathcal{V}_{\theta(\omega)},\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{2}(\omega)},\ldots\right)
if, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Phi_{\omega}^{(n)}\!\left({\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega)\right)\;=\;\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{n}(\omega)),

(1.9)

where

\Phi_{\omega}^{(n)}=\Phi_{n;\omega}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{1;\omega},\qquad\Phi_{k;\omega}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{k}(\omega)}.

(1.10)

In
[
EMP25
]
, the following Law of Large Numbers for
N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
was proved under the existence and uniqueness of the dynamically stationary state.

Theorem 1.1

(LLN for Outcome Frequencies
[
EMP25
]
)
.

Assume that
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta\right)
is an invertible, ergodic,
\pr
-preserving dynamical system, and let
\omega\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{\omega}
be a random instrument on a finite outcome set
\mathcal{A}
.
Suppose there exists a
unique
dynamically stationary state
{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega)
for the sequence of measurements
\left(\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{1}(\omega)},\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{2}(\omega)},\ldots\right)
.
Then:

1.

The skew shift
\tau
is a
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}
-measure-preserving ergodic transformation.
Furthermore, for any
\tau
-invariant set
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma
(i.e.
\tau^{-1}(\Gamma)=\Gamma
) we have
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}(\Gamma)=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\Gamma)\in\{0,1\}
, for any initial state
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
.

2.

For every (random) initial state
\vartheta
, for
\pr
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, and for
\mathbb{Q}_{\vartheta;\omega}
-almost every
\bar{a}\in\outcomes
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{a})}{n}\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{Q}_{{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega);\omega}(C_{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega).

where
C_{\boldsymbol{b}}
is as defined in (
1.8
).

1.2
Assumptions and Main Result

Fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
, where
\theta

\pr
-preserving, and consider the measurement sequence

\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}\bigr)_{k\geq 1}.

Write

\Phi_{k;\omega}:=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{k}(\omega)},\qquad k\geq 1,

and for
k,n\geq 1
,

\Phi^{(n)}_{k;\omega}:=\Phi_{k+n-1;\omega}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{k;\omega},\qquad\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}:=\Phi^{(n)}_{1;\omega}.

Since
\theta

\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma\!\bigl(\omega\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{j}(\omega)}:\,j\leq k\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{F}^{k}:=\sigma\!\bigl(\omega\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{j}(\omega)}:\,j\geq k\bigr).

(1.11)

Their
\rho

\alpha
-mixing coefficients are

\rho(n):=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{\pr}(\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{F}^{k+n}),\qquad\alpha_{\pr}(n):=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{\pr}(\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{F}^{k+n}),

(1.12)

where, for sub-
\sigma

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}

\rho_{\pr}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\sup\Bigl\{|\mathrm{Corr}_{\pr}(U,V)|:\,U\in L^{2}(\mathcal{A}),\ V\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}),\ U\neq 0,\ V\neq 0\Bigr\},

and

\alpha_{\pr}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\sup\Bigl\{|\pr(A\cap B)-\pr(A)\pr(B)|:\,A\in\mathcal{A},\ B\in\mathcal{B}\Bigr\}.

It is known that

\alpha_{\pr}(n)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\,\rho(n)

for all
n

[
Bra07
]
.

We impose the following standing assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The environment
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
is an invertible, ergodic,
\pr
-preserving dynamical system.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\pr}(n)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty.

Next, we assume the existence of a dynamically stationary state together with annealed trace-norm forgetting for the non-selective cocycle.
For
n\geq 1
and a measurable initial state
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
, define

\beta_{n}(\vartheta):=\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[\norm{
\Phi^{(n)}_\omega(\vartheta(\omega))-\s(\theta^n\omega)
}_{1}\right].

Assumption 3
.

There exist a dynamically stationary state
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}:\Omega\to\states
and a deterministic sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq[0,\infty)
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty,

and for every measurable initial state
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
,

\beta_{n}(\vartheta)\leq r_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

.

A stronger, but often more natural, sufficient condition is

\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[\sup_{\rho\in\states}\norm{
\Phi^{(n)}_\omega(\rho)-\s(\theta^n\omega)
}_{1}\right]\leq r_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Also note that if
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}
exists and satisfies the conditions in

Theorem 2

(Annealed CLT for
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}
)
.

Suppose
1
,
2
and
3
hold, and let
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}
be the unique dynamically stationary state. Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
, and define

\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left[\delta N_{1}^{\boldsymbol{b}}\right]=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(C_{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega).

Then the covariance series

\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left[(\delta N_{1}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}})^{2}\right]+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left[(\delta N_{1}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}})(\delta N_{1+k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}})\right]

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}}\bigr)\underset{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}{\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{2}),\qquad n\to\infty.

Annealed Outcome Laws and Admissibility

To transfer
2
to more general random initial states, we compare the corresponding annealed outcome laws via coupling.
For probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, we write
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
for the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
.
For a random initial state
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
, define its annealed outcome law on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma)
by

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta}:=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}}^{-1},\qquad\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta}(E)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{Q}_{\vartheta(\omega);\omega}(E)\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega),\quad E\in\Sigma,

(1.13)

where
\pi_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}}(\omega,\bar{a})=\bar{a}
. Since
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
depends only on the outcome sequence, convergence in distribution under
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}
is equivalent to convergence under
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}^{\mathrm{out}}
.

Definition 1

(Admissible initial state relative to a reference law)
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
. Let
\vartheta,\eta:\Omega\to\states
be random initial states. We say that
\vartheta
is
admissible for
\boldsymbol{b}
relative to

if there exists

\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}\in\Pi\!\bigl(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta},\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\eta}\bigr)

such that, writing
(\bar{A},\bar{B})
for the canonical coordinate processes on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}}\!\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigl(\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{B})-\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{A})\Bigr)\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(1.14)

This notion is formulated purely at the level of annealed outcome laws and is exactly what is needed to transfer the CLT from the stationary initial state to a general one.

,
2
and

Sufficient Conditions for Universal Admissibility

We now give instrument-level sufficient conditions, in the perfect-measurement regime, under which every random initial state is admissible for every pattern.
Thus, throughout this subsection, we assume that for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\,\cdot\,)=V_{a;\omega}(\,\cdot\,)V_{a;\omega}^{\dagger}\qquad\text{$\pr$-a.s.}

Informally, the condition below requires an orthonormal basis
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
such that each Kraus operator
V_{a;\omega}
acts on basis vectors by sending them to scalar multiples of basis vectors, with target label independent of
\omega
, and in such a way that images of distinct basis vectors remain orthogonal. Moreover, after a fixed number
L
of steps, any two such label evolutions can be coupled so that the terminal labels agree with probability at least
\varepsilon>0
, uniformly in the starting time and disorder realization.

Formally:

Condition (A)
.

Let
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Assume:

(A.1)

Basis-state preserving structure.

For each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a deterministic map

f_{a}:\{1,\dots,d\}\to\{1,\dots,d\}

such that for
\pr
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
and every

1.3
Related Literature

The literature on quantum trajectories naturally separates into a continuous-time theory and a discrete-time repeated-measurement theory.
For continuous-time quantum trajectories, one may consult the general stochastic Schrödinger equation and continuous-measurement framework developed in

,
Gis84
,
Car93

Hol01

Pel08

]
.
Within the discrete-time finite-outcome setting, early ergodic results for measurement records and trajectories, that is, for the posterior-state process, appear in

KM04

.

For homogeneous repeated measurements, fluctuation and invariant-measure results were developed further in

,
BFPP19
,

,

]
.
Closely related path-space questions for repeated measurements were also studied from the viewpoint of entropy production in

]
, while repeated quantum non-demolition measurements and their continuous-time limits were analyzed in
[
BBB13
]
.
More recently, imperfect-measurement versions of the trajectory theory were developed in
[
ABBP25
]
.

The present work belongs to the disordered branch of the subject.
Section 1.2 of
[
EMP25
]
places this model within the broader literature on disordered open quantum dynamical systems and repeated interactions, including
[
BJM08
,
PP09
,
BCSŻ09

.
Our contribution differs in emphasis from the invariant-measure theory of the posterior-state process: we focus instead on fluctuation theory for finite block counts in the measurement record under disorder.

More precisely, our theorem complements the law of large numbers established in
[

]
. It may be viewed as a disordered analogue, at the level of the measurement record, of the central limit theorems obtained in the homogeneous setting in

Let

be any invertible ergodic base system, and let

. Since

2.2
Environment Examples

Now we proceed to construct genuinely disordered examples.
For this, we shall first consider environments for which
1
and
2
are natural.

I.i.d. Instrument Environments.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be finite and
\dim(\mathcal{H})=d<\infty
.
Let
\mathsf{CP}_{\leq}
denote the set of all completely positive trace–nonincreasing linear maps on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, equipped with the Borel
\sigma
–algebra induced by a finite-dimensional parametrization (for example via Choi matrices). Define

\mathsf{Instr}:=\Bigl\{(\Phi_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\in(\mathsf{CP}_{\leq})^{\mathcal{A}}:\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Phi_{a}\text{ is trace--preserving}\Bigr\},

equipped with the induced subspace Borel
\sigma
–algebra.
Then
(\mathsf{Instr},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{Instr}))
is a standard Borel space.

Fix a probability law
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Instr})
and let

\Omega:=\mathsf{Instr}^{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{Instr})^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}},\qquad\pr:=\mu^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}},

and define the left shift

\theta\bigl((\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigr):=(\omega_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Then
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
is invertible, measure-preserving, and ergodic.
In particular, Assumption
1
holds.
Let

X_{n}(\omega):=\omega_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\text{ for }\qquad\omega=(\omega_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\Omega

denote the coordinate process.
Then
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is i.i.d. with common law
\mu
, and the instrument applied at time
n
is

\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{n}\omega}=X_{n}(\omega)=\omega_{n}.

Hence

\alpha_{\pr}(n)=\rho_{\pr}(n)=0,\qquad n\geq 1,

so Assumption
2
holds.

For each fixed
a\in\mathcal{A}
, let

e_{a}:\mathsf{Instr}\to\mathsf{CP}_{\leq},\qquad e_{a}\bigl((\Phi_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\bigr):=\Phi_{a}.

Then the
a
-th component process

\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n}\omega}=e_{a}(X_{n}(\omega)),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

is itself i.i.d. with common law

\mu_{a}:=\mu\circ e_{a}^{-1}.

In particular, for each fixed
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the random maps
\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n}\omega}
all have the same distribution.

Markov-driven Instrument Environments.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a strictly stationary Markov chain on a measurable state space
(E,\mathcal{E})
with stationary law
\pi
and transition kernel
P
.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr)
be the canonical path space

\Omega:=E^{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{E}^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}},

with
\pr
the stationary Markov measure induced by
(\pi,P)
, and let
\theta
be the left shift.
Then
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
is invertible and measure-preserving.
If the chain is ergodic, then
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
is ergodic, hence Assumption
1
holds.

Assume that the instrument at time
n
is a measurable function of the Markov symbol, i.e. there exists a measurable map

F:E\to\mathsf{Instr}

such that

\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{n}\omega}=F(X_{n}(\omega)),

where
X_{n}
is the coordinate projections as above.
For each fixed
a\in\mathcal{A}
, let

e_{a}:\mathsf{Instr}\to\mathsf{CP}_{\leq},\qquad e_{a}\bigl((\Phi_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\bigr):=\Phi_{a}.

Then

\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n}\omega}=e_{a}\!\bigl(F(X_{n}(\omega))\bigr),

so for each fixed
a
, the component process
(\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n}\omega})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a measurable image of the stationary process
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and is therefore strictly stationary.
In particular, for each fixed
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the one-dimensional law of
\mathcal{T}_{a;\theta^{n}\omega}
does not depend on
n
.

Assumption
2
is not automatic for general ergodic Markov chains.
A convenient sufficient condition is geometric mixing of the driving chain; for example, one may assume

\exists\,C<\infty,\ r\in(0,1)\ \text{ such that }\ \rho_{\pr}^{X}(n)\leq Cr^{n},\qquad n\geq 1,

(2.1)

where
\rho_{\pr}^{X}(n)
denotes the maximal correlation coefficient computed with respect to the past/future
\sigma
–algebras generated by
(X_{k})
.
Under (
2.1
), Assumption
2
holds for the instrument process as well.
Indeed, the instrument-level past/future
\sigma
–algebras are sub–
\sigma
–algebras of those generated by
(X_{n})
, and therefore, by monotonicity of
\alpha_{\pr}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\rho_{\pr}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the instrument-level mixing coefficients are bounded above by those of the driving chain.
In particular, if
E
is finite and
P
is irreducible and aperiodic, then the stationary Markov chain
(X_{n})
is mixing with exponential rate (see, e.g.,
[
Bra05
, Theorem 3.2]
), so the above geometric mixing condition holds for the driving chain, and hence the induced instrument environment satisfies Assumption
2
and also satisfies that
\rho_{\pr}(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Deterministic Instruments

Deterministic instruments are included in the present framework as a degenerate special case: one may either take a one-point base system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
, or more generally any invertible ergodic base and assume that
\omega\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{\omega}
is constant. In the latter case, the disorder-mixing coefficients satisfy

\alpha_{\pr}(n)=\rho_{\pr}(n)=0,\qquad n\geq 1,

so
2
holds trivially.

These classes are only representative sources of environments for which
1
and
2
are natural.
They are not intended to be exhaustive: many other disordered environments, beyond the i.i.d., deterministic, and Markov-driven settings considered here, also fit our framework whenever the corresponding ergodicity and mixing conditions can be verified.

3
Preliminaries and Notation

In this section, we collect the notation and basic objects used throughout the article.
We also record several conventions concerning probability spaces, product measures, mixing coefficients, and convergence in distribution.

3.1
Matrices and Superoerators

Throughout, the finite-dimensional Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
We denote by
\mathbb{M}_{d}
the set of
d\times d
complex matrices and equip
\mathbb{M}_{d}
with the trace norm
\|X\|_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(|X|\right)
, where
|X|=\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
.
The Hilbert–Schmidt inner product is

\langle A|B\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(A^{\dagger}B\right),\qquad A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}.

The state space is

\states:=\{\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{d}:\ \rho\geq 0,\ \operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho\right)=1\},\qquad\states^{\mathrm{o}}:=\{\rho\in\states:\ \rho>0\}.

We write
\partial\states=\states\setminus\states^{\mathrm{o}}
for the boundary of rank–deficient states and denote by
\eta(\rho)
the smallest eigenvalue of
\rho\in\states^{\mathrm{o}}
.

Let
\mapspace
denote the space of linear maps
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{d}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
equipped with the
1\!\to\!1
operator norm

\norm{\phi}_{1\to 1}=\sup\{\norm{\phi(X)}_{1}:\ \norm{X}_{1}\leq 1\}.

A superoperator
\phi
is
positive
if it preserves the PSD cone and
strictly positive
if it maps every non-zero PSD matrix to a positive definite one.

Throughout, we fix an arbitrary full–rank reference state
\rho_{*}\in\states
.
The projective action of
\phi\in\mapspace
on
\states
is defined by

\phi{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}X:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\phi(X)}{\norm{\phi(X)}_{1}},&\phi(X)\neq 0,\\[5.16663pt]
\rho_{*},&\phi(X)=0.\end{cases}

(3.1)

3.2
Contraction Coefficient

Definition 3.1

(Contraction coefficient)
.

For
\phi\in\mapspace
define

\mathrm{c}(\phi):=\sup_{A,B\in\states}\mathrm{d}\!\left(\phi{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}A,\phi{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}B\right),

where the projective metric on
\states
is

\mathrm{d}(A,B)=\frac{1-m(A,B)m(B,A)}{1+m(A,B)m(B,A)},\qquad m(A,B):=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0:\ \lambda B\leq A\}.

The following facts about
\mathrm{d}
and
\mathrm{c}(\phi)
are used later without proof.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
MS22
, Lemma 3.5, Lemma 3.8]
,
[
RS25
, Lemma 4.4]
)
.

3.3
Probability Spaces and Product Measures

Let

be a probability space, where
\mathcal{F}
is the
\sigma
-algebra of events and
\mu
is a probability measure on
(X,\mathcal{F})
.
A map
\theta:X\to X
is called
\mu
-probability preserving
(or
ppt
for short) if
\theta
is
\mathcal{F}
-measurable and

\mu(\theta^{-1}(E))=\mu(E)\qquad\text{for all }E\in\mathcal{F}.

When
\theta
is bijective and
\theta^{-1}
is also
\mathcal{F}
-measurable, we say that
\theta
is an
invertible
ppt.
A ppt
\theta
is called
ergodic
if for every
E\in\mathcal{F}
with
\theta^{-1}(E)=E
we have
\mu(E)\in\{0,1\}
.
An equivalent characterization of ergodicity of a
\mu
-ppt is that members
E
of
\mathcal{F}
with
\mu(\theta^{-1}(E)\triangle E)=0
are those with
\mu(E)\in\{0,1\}
.
Here
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference.
We refer the reader to
[
Wal00
]
for further characterizations of ergodicity.

We denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\,\cdot\,]
the expectation with respect to
\mu
.
When there is no ambiguity, we write simply
\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,]
. For
U,V\in L^{2}(\mu)
we write

\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(U,V):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[(U-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}U)(V-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}V)\right],\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(U):=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(U,U).

For
1\leq p<\infty
and a sub-
\sigma
-algebra

, we define

L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\mu):=\left\{f:X\to\mathbb{R}\text{ (or }\mathbb{C}\text{)}:f\text{ is }\mathcal{A}\text{-measurable and }\int_{X}|f|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty\right\},

\norm{f}_{L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\mu)}:=\left(\int_{X}|f|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/p}.

For
p=\infty
we set

L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\mu):=\left\{f:X\to\mathbb{R}:f\text{ is }\mathcal{A}\text{-measurable and }\esssup_{x\in X}|f(x)|<\infty\right\}.

When
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{F}
we simply write
L^{p}(\mu)
in place of
L^{p}(\mathcal{F},\mu)
.

If
X
is a topological space, we denote by
\dummyborel\left(X\right)

\sigma
-algebra

-algebra containing all open subsets of

A measurable space

\tau

such that

coincides with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\tau

\mathcal{X}=\mathscr{B}_{\tau}(X).

In this case, we often simply write
\mathcal{X}=\dummyborel\left(X\right)
.
We refer to
[
Kal21
]
for results on probability spaces (or more generally on measure spaces).

Measurable Functions.

Given two measurable spaces

and

(or simply

Product Spaces.

Given measurable spaces

and
(Y,\mathcal{G})

X\times Y,\qquad\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{G}:=\sigma(A\times B:\ A\in\mathcal{F},\ B\in\mathcal{G}).

If
\mu
and
\nu

For
n

We refer to Halmos

]
for background.

If

Probability Kernel

Given two measurable spaces

and
(Y,\mathcal{Y})

Convergence in Distribution.

Let

and

We write

(weak convergence of measures, see

In particular for

(3.2)

Skew-Product Trajectory

4.1
Proof of

With the preliminaries above, we are now ready to prove

We start be re-stating

See

The existence of a dynamically stationary state under
1
and condition 1 above is established in
[

, Theorem 1]
.
Thus there exists a random state
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}:\Omega\to\states
such that

\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}\left(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega)\right)=\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}\left(\theta^{n}(\omega)\right)\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, it is shown there that
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega)
is strictly positive for
\pr
-almost every
\omega
.
We also note that the existence of such a state does not require ergodicity of the base (see, e.g.,
[
PW25
, Lemma A.5]
).

To prove uniqueness, we use the reducibility theory of
[
ES24
]
.
Recall that, in the sense of
[
ES24
]
, a random orthogonal projection
P
is
reducing
for the cocycle
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}
if there exists a (possibly random) constant
C(\omega)>0
such that

\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}\bigl(P(\omega)\bigr)\leq C(\omega)\,P\bigl(\theta^{n}(\omega)\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\pr\text{-almost surely}.

(4.1)

We show that every nonzero reducing projection is equal to
\mathbb{I}_{d}

\pr
-almost surely.
This implies that
\mathbb{I}_{d}
is the unique nonzero minimal reducing projection, and hence uniqueness follows from
[
ES24
, Theorem 1(c)]
.

So let
P
be a nonzero reducing projection.
By (
ESP
), for
\pr
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
there exists
N_{0}(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}
is strictly positive for all
n\geq N_{0}(\omega)
.
Hence, whenever
P(\omega)\neq 0_{d}
, we have

\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}\bigl(P(\omega)\bigr)>0\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N_{0}(\omega).

Combining this with (
4.1
), we obtain

0<\Phi^{(n)}_{\omega}\bigl(P(\omega)\bigr)\leq C(\omega)\,P\bigl(\theta^{n}(\omega)\bigr),\qquad n\geq N_{0}(\omega).

Since
P(\theta^{n}(\omega))
is an orthogonal projection, this forces

P(\theta^{n}(\omega))=\mathbb{I}_{d}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N_{0}(\omega)

whenever
P(\omega)\neq 0_{d}
. Indeed, if
Q
is an orthogonal projection and
0<X\leq cQ
for some
c>0
, then
Q=\mathbb{I}_{d}
: the support of the strictly positive matrix
X
is the whole space, so the range of
Q
must be the whole space as well.

Now consider the event

E:=\{\omega\in\Omega:0_{d}\neq P(\omega)\neq\mathbb{I}_{d}\}.

If
\omega\in E
, then
P(\omega)\neq 0_{d}
, and therefore there exists
N_{0}(\omega)
such that

P(\theta^{n}(\omega))=\mathbb{I}_{d}\notin E\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N_{0}(\omega).

Thus every
\omega\in E
visits
E
only finitely many times under forward iteration of
\theta
. By the Poincaré recurrence theorem,
\pr(E)=0
. Therefore,

P(\omega)\in\{0_{d},\mathbb{I}_{d}\}\qquad\text{for }\pr\text{-almost every }\omega.

Set

A:=\{\omega\in\Omega:P(\omega)=0_{d}\},\qquad B:=\{\omega\in\Omega:P(\omega)=\mathbb{I}_{d}\}.

Also define

F:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists N(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }P(\theta^{n}(\omega))=\mathbb{I}_{d}\ \forall n\geq N(\omega)\right\}.

The set
F
is

F=\bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{n\geq N}\{\omega:P(\theta^{n}(\omega))=\mathbb{I}_{d}\}.

We also have that

-invariant. Indeed, if
\omega\in F

N

for all

for all
n\geq\max\{1,N-1\}
thus

.
Therefore
\pr(\theta^{-1}(F)\triangle F)=0
and thus ergodicity of
\theta
we get
\pr(F)\in\{0,1\}
.
Moreover, from the previous argument, we have
\{\,\omega:P(\omega)\neq 0_{d}\,\}\subseteq F

\pr
-almost surly (i.e. modulo null sets).
Since
P
is nonzero,
\pr\{\omega:P(\omega)\neq 0_{d}\}>0
, hence
\pr(F)>0
and it follows that
\pr(F)=1
.

We now show that
\pr(A)=0
. If
\omega\in A\cap F
, then
P(\omega)=0_{d}
and, by definition of
F
, there exists
N(\omega)
such that

P(\theta^{n}(\omega))=\mathbb{I}_{d}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N(\omega).

In particular,
\theta^{n}(\omega)\notin A
for all
n\geq N(\omega)
. Thus every
\omega\in A\cap F

\theta

Since

\pr(A)=0
. Therefore,

P(\omega)=\mathbb{I}_{d}\qquad\text{for }\pr\text{-almost every }\omega.

Thus, every nonzero reducing projection is equal to
\mathbb{I}_{d}

\pr
-almost surely. In particular,
\mathbb{I}_{d}
is the unique nonzero minimal reducing projection.
Therefore
[
ES24
, Theorem 1(c)]
yields uniqueness of the dynamically stationary state

Now we prove the absolute continuity assertion.
Let
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
be an arbitrary random initial state. For each
\omega
,
\vartheta(\omega)
is a density matrix, so

Since
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega)
is strictly positive for
\pr
-almost every
\omega

\eta(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))

\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega)\geq\eta(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))\,\mathbb{I}_{d},\qquad\text{so}\qquad\mathbb{I}_{d}\leq\eta(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))^{-1}\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega),

\mathbb{Q}_{\vartheta(\omega);\omega}(E)=\langle\vartheta(\omega)|\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\omega}(E)\rangle.

Since

\mathbb{Q}_{\vartheta(\omega);\omega}(E)\leq\eta(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))^{-1}\langle\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega)|\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\omega}(E)\rangle=\eta(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))^{-1}\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(E).

\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[\mathrm{c}(\Phi^{(n)})\right]\leq C_{p}\,n^{-p},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

where

is the contraction coefficient introduced in

.

Using the forward and backward filtrations
(\mathcal{G}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(\mathcal{G}^{k})_{k\geq 1}
, we define the
\alpha
–mixing profile of the skew-product system, for a given (random) initial state
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
, by

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}}(n):=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}}\!\left(\mathcal{G}_{k},\,\mathcal{G}^{k+n}\right),

(4.5)

where for sub–
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma
,

\alpha_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\sup\left\{\left|\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}(A\cap B)-\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}(A)\,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}(B)\right|:A\in\mathcal{A},\ B\in\mathcal{B}\right\}.

(4.6)

Remark 4.1
.

In the skew-product space
(\Omega\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta})
it is convenient to view the annealed mixing profile as the
\alpha
–mixing coefficient of a concrete process. Define the process
Z:=(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
by

Z_{n}(\omega,\bar{a}):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{n}(\omega)},&n\leq 0,\\
\left(\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{n}(\omega)},\,A_{n}(\bar{a})\right),&n\geq 1.\end{cases}

where
A_{n}(\bar{a})=a_{n}
is the canonical outcome coordinate.
Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sigma(Z_{j}:\,j\leq k)=\mathcal{G}_{k},\qquad\sigma(Z_{j}:\,j\geq k+n)=\mathcal{G}^{k+n}.

Hence, in the notation of
Section

3
,

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}}(n)=\alpha_{Z,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}}(n).

In other words,
\overline{\alpha}(n)
is precisely the
\alpha
–mixing coefficient of the process
Z
under the annealed law
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}
.

We now obtain the first key estimate on the mixing profile of the skew-product.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\pr,\theta)
satisfy
1
, and let
(\mathcal{V}_{\theta^{n}(\omega)})_{n\geq 1}
satisfy
3
.
Then for the unique dynamical stationary state
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}
(from
3
), consider the annealed law
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}
on
\Omega\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then for all
n\geq 2
, and
m\in\{1,\ldots n-1\}

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\;\leq\;8\,\alpha_{\pr}(n-m)+r_{n-1}+2r_{m}

where
\overline{\alpha}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(n)
and
\alpha_{\pr}(n)
are as defined in
eq.

4.5
and
eq.

1.12
resp.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 2
and
k\geq 1
. Let
E\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
,
F\in\mathcal{F}^{k+n}
,
A\in\sigma(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k})
and
B\in\sigma(\pi_{k+n},\pi_{k+n+1},\ldots)
. Consider the rectangles
E\times A
and
F\times B
in
\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma
. We must bound

\Delta:=\left|\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}\left((E\times A)\cap(F\times B)\right)-\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(E\times A)\,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(F\times B)\right|.

By definition of the annealed law,

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(E\times A)=\int_{\Omega}1_{E}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A)\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega),

and similarly for the other terms. A direct expansion gives

\Delta\leq|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|,

where

I_{1}:=\int_{\Omega}1_{E\cap F}(\omega)\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega),

and

I_{2}:=\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}\!\left(U(\omega),V(\omega)\right),

with

U(\omega):=1_{E}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A),\qquad V(\omega):=1_{F}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B),

and by construction,
0\leq U(\omega)\leq 1
and
0\leq V(\omega)\leq 1
for all
\omega
.

We first consider
|I_{1}|
and prove that it is bounded above by
r_{n-1}
where
r_{n-1}
as in
3
.

We use the POVM representation and
Lemma

3.5
to factor the joint probability of
A
and
B
. Since
A\in\sigma(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k})
, there exists a finite set
S\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{k}
such that

A=\bigsqcup_{\boldsymbol{a}\in S}\pi_{1,\ldots,k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{a}),\qquad\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}).

Since
B\in\sigma(\pi_{k+n},\pi_{k+n+1},\ldots)=\varsigma^{-k}((\sigma(\pi_{n}\,\pi_{n+1},\ldots)))
we have that
B=\varsigma^{-k}(C)
for some
C\in\sigma(\pi_{n},\pi_{n+1},\ldots)
.
By the duality relation between
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}
and the POVM
\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\omega}
,

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A\cap B)=\innerproduct{
\s(\omega)
}{
\mbQ^*_\omega\left(
A\cap\varsigma^{-k}(C)
\right)
}.

Using the decomposition of
A
and applying
Lemma

3.5
with
n=k
and
S=C
, we obtain

\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\omega}\left(\pi_{1,\ldots,k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{a})\cap\varsigma^{-k}(C)\right)=\left(\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{\boldsymbol{a};\omega}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)\right),

for
\pr
–almost every
\omega
. Summing over
\boldsymbol{a}\in S
gives

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A\cap B)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in S}\innerproduct{
\s(\omega)
}{
\left(\mcT^{(k)}_{\pmb a;\omega}\right)^\dagger\left(
\mbQ^*_{\theta^k(\omega)}(C)
\right)
}.

Let

\widetilde{T}_{S;\omega}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in S}\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{\boldsymbol{a};\omega},\qquad K_{\omega}(A):=\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A)=\innerproduct{
\s(\omega)
}{
\mbQ^*_\omega(A)
}.

Then

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A\cap B)=\innerproduct{
\widetilde T_{S;\omega}(\s(\omega))
}{
\mbQ^*_{\theta^k(\omega)}(C)
}.

Whenever
K_{\omega}(A)>0
, we define the posterior state using
Equation

3.1
as follows.

\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\widetilde{T}_{S;\omega}(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))}{K_{\omega}(A)}\in\states.\,&K_{\omega}(A)>0,\\
\rho_{\ast}&K_{\omega}(A)=0.\end{cases}

If
K_{\omega}(A)=0
, the event
A
has zero quenched probability and the contribution to
I_{1}
from such
\omega
is zero.
Thus, in all cases,

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A\cap B)=K_{\omega}(A)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega);\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C),

and by definition,

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A)=K_{\omega}(A).

Now, note that

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B)=\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}\left(\varsigma^{-k}(C)\right)=\innerproduct{
\s(\omega)
}{
\mbQ^*_\omega\left(\varsigma^{-k}(C)\right)
}.

Applying
Lemma

3.5
with
n=k
and
S=C
, but now summing over all possible
k
–step prefixes, we obtain

\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\omega}\left(\varsigma^{-k}(C)\right)=\left(\Phi^{(k)}_{\omega}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)\right).

Hence

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B)=\innerproduct{
\Phi^{(k)}_\omega\left(\s(\omega)\right)
}{
\mbQ^*_{\theta^k(\omega)}(C)
}=\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi^{(k)}_{\omega}(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega));\,\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C).

In particular, the difference appearing in
I_{1}
can be written as

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B)=K_{\omega}(A)\,\left(\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega);\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)-\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi^{(k)}_{\omega}(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega));\,\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)\right).

Since
B\in\sigma(\pi_{k+n},\pi_{k+n+1},\ldots)
, there exists

C\in\sigma(\pi_{n},\pi_{n+1},\ldots)
such that

B=\varsigma^{-k}(C).

Since
C\in\sigma(\pi_{n},\pi_{n+1},\ldots)=\varsigma^{-(n-1)}(\Sigma)
,
there exists
C^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that

C=\varsigma^{-(n-1)}(C^{\prime}).

Fix
\omega\in\Omega
and a state
\rho\in\states
.
Then

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho;\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)=\mathbb{Q}_{\rho;\theta^{k}(\omega)}\left(\varsigma^{-(n-1)}(C^{\prime})\right)=\innerproduct{\rho}{\mbQ^*_{\theta^k(\omega)}\left(\varsigma^{-(n-1)}(C')\right)}.

We now apply
Lemma

3.5
with
\omega
replaced by
\theta^{k}(\omega)
,
n
replaced by
n-1
, and
S=C^{\prime}
, and then sum over all
(n-1)
-step prefixes.
This yields

\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}\left(\varsigma^{-(n-1)}(C^{\prime})\right)=\left(\Phi_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{\theta^{k+1}(\omega)}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}(C^{\prime})\right).

Hence

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho;\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)=\left\langle\Phi_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{\theta^{k+1}(\omega)}(\rho)\,\Big|\,\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}(C^{\prime})\right\rangle.

The POVM element
\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}(C^{\prime})
is positive and bounded above by the identity, so for any pair of states
\rho,\sigma\in\states
,

\left|\mathbb{Q}_{\rho;\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)-\mathbb{Q}_{\sigma;\theta^{k}(\omega)}(C)\right|\leq\norm{
\Phi_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{\theta^{k+1}(\omega)}(\rho-\sigma)
}_{1}.

Substituting
\rho=\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega)
and
\sigma=\Phi^{(k)}_{\omega}(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))
into this bound and integrating over
\omega
yields

|I_{1}|\leq\int_{\Omega}\norm{
\Phi_{\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{\theta^{k+1}(\omega)}
\left(\rho_{A,\s}(\omega)-\Phi^{(k)}_\omega(\s(\omega))\right)
}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega),

Since
\Phi^{(k)}_{\omega}(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega))=\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{k}\omega)
, the previous estimate may be rewritten as

|I_{1}|\leq\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\|\Phi^{(n-1)}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}\bigl(\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega)\bigr)-\Phi^{(n-1)}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}\bigl(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{k}\omega)\bigr)\Bigr\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega).

By dynamical stationarity of
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}
,

\Phi^{(n-1)}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}\bigl(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{k}\omega)\bigr)=\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)),

and hence

|I_{1}|\leq\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\|\Phi^{(n-1)}_{\theta^{k}(\omega)}\bigl(\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\omega)\bigr)-\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega))\Bigr\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega).

Now define a shifted random initial state by

\vartheta_{A,k}(\omega):=\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}(\theta^{-k}(\omega)),\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

Since
\theta
is invertible and
\rho_{A,\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}
is measurable,
\vartheta_{A,k}
is measurable as a map
\Omega\to\states
. Using the change of variable
\omega_{k}=\theta^{k}(\omega)
and the
\pr
-invariance of
\theta
, we obtain

|I_{1}|\leq\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\|\Phi^{(n-1)}_{\omega_{k}}\bigl(\vartheta_{A,k}(\omega_{k})\bigr)-\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{n-1}(\omega_{k}))\Bigr\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\pr(\omega_{k}).

Therefore, by
3
,

|I_{1}|\leq r_{n-1}.

We now estimate
I_{2}
.
Fix
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
.
Recall that

U(\omega)=1_{E}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(A),\qquad V(\omega)=1_{F}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B),

where
E\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
,
F\in\mathcal{F}^{k+n}
,
A\in\sigma(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k})
, and

B\in\sigma(\pi_{k+n},\pi_{k+n+1},\dots)
.
Choose a deterministic reference state
\rho_{\ast}\in\states
, and define

\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\omega):=\Phi^{(m)}_{\theta^{-m}(\omega)}(\rho_{\ast}),\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

Since
\rho_{\ast}
is deterministic, the random state
\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\omega)
depends only on the instruments at times
-m+1,\dots,0
.

Define

U^{\prime}(\omega):=1_{E}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\omega);\omega}(A).

Then
U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, since
E\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, the event
A
depends only on the first
k
outcome coordinates, and
\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\omega)
depends only on the instruments up to time
0
.
Next, since
B\in\sigma(\pi_{k+n},\pi_{k+n+1},\dots)
, there exists
S\in\Sigma
such that

B=\varsigma^{-(k+n-1)}(S).

Using the duality relation and
Lemma

3.5
, we obtain

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega);\omega}(B)=\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi^{(k+n-1)}_{\omega}(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\omega));\,\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}(S)=\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}(\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega));\,\theta^{k+n-1}(\omega)}(S),

where in the last step we used the dynamical stationarity of
\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}
.

Accordingly, define

V^{\prime}(\omega):=1_{F}(\omega)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\theta^{k+n-1}\omega);\theta^{k+n-1}\omega}(S).

Then
V^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{k+n-m}
, because
F\in\mathcal{F}^{k+n}
, the state

\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\theta^{k+n-1}\omega)
depends only on the instruments at times

k+n-m,\dots,k+n-1
, and the quenched law

\mathbb{Q}_{\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\theta^{k+n-1}\omega);\theta^{k+n-1}\omega}(S)

depends only on the instruments from time
k+n
onward.

We now compare
U
with
U^{\prime}
. Since
0\leq\mathbb{Q}_{\omega}^{*}(A)\leq\mathbb{I}_{d}
, the duality formula gives

|U(\omega)-U^{\prime}(\omega)|=1_{E}(\omega)\,\left|\innerproduct{\s(\omega)-\rho_\ast^{(m)}(\omega)}{\mbQ_\omega^* (A)}\right|\leq 1_{E}(\omega)\,\norm{\s(\omega)-\rho_\ast^{(m)}(\omega)}_{1}.

Since

\rho_{\ast}^{(m)}(\omega)=\Phi^{(m)}_{\theta^{-m}(\omega)}(\rho_{\ast}),

the
\pr
-invariance of
\theta
and
3
imply

\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[|U-U^{\prime}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[\norm{\s(\omega)-\rho_\ast^{(m)}(\omega)}_{1}\right]\leq r_{m}.

Similarly,

|V(\omega)-V^{\prime}(\omega)|\leq 1_{F}(\omega)\,\norm{
\s(\theta^{k+n-1}\omega)
-
\rho_\ast^{(m)}(\theta^{k+n-1}\omega)
}_{1},

and again, by
\pr
-invariance of
\theta
and
3
,

\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[|V-V^{\prime}|\right]\leq r_{m}.

We now decompose the covariance:

I_{2}=\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U,V)=\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})+\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U-U^{\prime},V)+\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U^{\prime},V-V^{\prime}).

Since
0\leq U^{\prime},V,V^{\prime}\leq 1
, we have

|\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U-U^{\prime},V)|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[|U-U^{\prime}|\right],\qquad|\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U^{\prime},V-V^{\prime})|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pr}\!\left[|V-V^{\prime}|\right].

Moreover,
U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
V^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{k+n-m}
, so by
Lemma

3.4
with
p=q=\infty
,

|\mathrm{Cov}_{\pr}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})|\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}(n-m).

Combining these estimates yields

|I_{2}|\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}(n-m)+2r_{m}.

Finally, together with the bound
|I_{1}|\leq r_{n-1}
, we obtain

\Delta\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}(n-m)+r_{n-1}+2r_{m}.

Since the right-hand side is independent of
k
,
E
,
F
,
A
, and
B
, taking the supremum and using a monotone class argument gives

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}(n-m)+r_{n-1}+2r_{m},

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of

Proof.

Set

X_{k}:=\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}},\qquad k\geq 1.

Then
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of centred random variables on the skew-product space
(\Omega\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}})
.
Under
1
and with the existence and uniqueness of

(from

Theorem

yields that
\tau
is
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}

By definition,

\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}=\delta N_{1}^{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\tau^{k-1},\qquad k\geq 1,

hence

Since
\tau
preserves

.

For every

.
In particular,
X_{1}\in L^{2+\delta}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}})
for any

.

Now, let
\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)
denote the
\alpha
–mixing coefficients of the process
X
under
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}
as defined in

:

\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n):=\sup_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left(\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}),\,\sigma(X_{k+n},X_{k+n+1},\ldots)\right).

We compare these with the skew-product coefficients
\overline{\alpha}(n)
defined in
eq.

4.5
.
Since

\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\omega,\bar{a})=\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{k}=b_{1},\ldots,a_{k+m-1}=b_{m}\}}(\bar{a}),

each
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
depends only on the environment through
\omega
and on the outcome coordinates
a_{k},\ldots,a_{k+m-1}
.
In particular, for each
k

\sigma\left(\delta N_{1}^{\boldsymbol{b}},\ldots,\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k+m-1},\qquad\sigma\left(\delta N_{k+n}^{\boldsymbol{b}},\delta N_{k+n+1}^{\boldsymbol{b}},\ldots\right)\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{k+n},

where
\mathcal{G}_{j}
and
\mathcal{G}^{j}
are the past and future
\sigma
–fields on the skew product introduced in the beginning of this section.
Since
X_{k}
is obtained from
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
by subtracting the constant
\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}}
, we have

\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})=\sigma(\delta N_{1}^{\boldsymbol{b}},\ldots,\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}})\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k+m-1},

and similarly for the future
\sigma
–fields. By monotonicity of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients,

\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\leq\sup_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left(\mathcal{G}_{k+m-1},\mathcal{G}^{k+n}\right)=\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n-m+1),\qquad n>m-1.

Set

\zeta:=\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}\in(0,1).

By
2
and
3
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl[\alpha_{\pr}(j)\bigr]^{\zeta}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}^{\zeta}<\infty.

Let
\ell:=|\boldsymbol{b}|
. From the previous step, for every
n>\ell-1
,

\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\leq\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n-\ell+1).

Now fix
r\geq 2
. Applying
Lemma

4.2
with

q:=\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor\in\{1,\dots,r-1\},

we obtain

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(r)\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}\!\left(r-\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+r_{r-1}+2r_{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}.

Since
r-\lfloor r/2\rfloor\geq\lfloor r/2\rfloor
and
\alpha_{\pr}
is nonincreasing,

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(r)\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+r_{r-1}+2r_{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}.

Substituting
r=n-\ell+1
, we get for all
n\geq\ell+1
,

\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\leq 8\,\alpha_{\pr}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-\ell+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+r_{n-\ell}+2r_{\lfloor(n-\ell+1)/2\rfloor}.

Since
0<\zeta\leq 1
, the map
x\mapsto x^{\zeta}
is subadditive on
[0,\infty)
, and therefore

\bigl[\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\bigr]^{\zeta}\leq 8^{\zeta}\left[\alpha_{\pr}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-\ell+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\right]^{\zeta}+r_{n-\ell}^{\zeta}+2^{\zeta}r_{\lfloor(n-\ell+1)/2\rfloor}^{\zeta}.

Summing over
n\geq\ell+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}\bigl[\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\bigr]^{\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq 8^{\zeta}\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}\left[\alpha_{\pr}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-\ell+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\right]^{\zeta}+\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}r_{n-\ell}^{\zeta}+2^{\zeta}\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}r_{\lfloor(n-\ell+1)/2\rfloor}^{\zeta}.

Now each integer
j\geq 1
occurs as

\left\lfloor\frac{n-\ell+1}{2}\right\rfloor

for at most two values of
n
, so

\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}\left[\alpha_{\pr}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-\ell+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\right]^{\zeta}\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl[\alpha_{\pr}(j)\bigr]^{\zeta}<\infty,

and similarly,

\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}r_{\lfloor(n-\ell+1)/2\rfloor}^{\zeta}\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}^{\zeta}<\infty.

Also,

\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}r_{n-\ell}^{\zeta}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}^{\zeta}<\infty.

Hence

\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{\infty}\bigl[\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\bigr]^{\zeta}<\infty.

Since the finitely many terms
1\leq n\leq\ell
do not affect convergence, it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl[\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\bigr]^{\zeta}<\infty.

That is,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\right]^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty.

We have therefore verified that the stationary sequence

X_{k}:=\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}},\qquad k\geq 1,

is centred, belongs to
L^{2+\delta}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}})
, and has

\alpha
-mixing coefficients satisfying

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\alpha_{X,\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}(n)\right]^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty.

Hence, by the central limit theorem for stationary
\alpha
-mixing sequences
(
Theorem

4.1
), the covariance series

\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left[X_{1}^{2}\right]+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}\!\left[X_{1}X_{1+k}\right]

converges absolutely and defines a finite number

\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{2}\in[0,\infty)
. Moreover,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{b}}\right)\underset{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}}{\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{2}).

If
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{2}=0
, then the limit law is the Dirac mass at
0
, and the
convergence is convergence to
0
in probability as well
(see
Lemma

4.4
). This is precisely the claimed statement.
∎

We now record the classical central limit theorem for stationary
\alpha
-mixing sequences that will be used below.
The version stated here is
[
Bra07
, Theorem 10.7]
, originating in
[
Ibr62
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(CLT for stationary
\alpha
–mixing sequences)
.

Let
X:=(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a strictly stationary sequence of centered real-valued random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
.
Suppose that there exists
\delta>0
such that
X_{1}\in L^{2+\delta}(\mu)
and

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\alpha_{X,\mu}(n)\right]^{\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}}<\infty,

(4.7)

where
\alpha_{X,\mu}(n):=\alpha_{X}(n)
denotes the
\alpha
–mixing coefficient of the process
X
under
\mu
.
Then the covariance series

\sigma^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[X_{1}^{2}\right]\;+\;2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[X_{1}X_{1+n}\right]

(4.8)

converges absolutely and defines a finite number
\sigma^{2}\in[0,\infty)
.
Moreover,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\ \underset{\mu}{\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}}\ \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(4.9)

Here we allow
\sigma^{2}=0
, in which case the limit law is the Dirac mass at
0
, and convergence holds in probability as well.

Remark 4.3
.

In many references, the conclusion is stated only for
\sigma^{2}>0
.
The degenerate case
\sigma^{2}=0
then follows immediately once one knows that

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\!\left(n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)\to\sigma^{2}.

Indeed, if
\sigma^{2}=0
, then the normalized sums converge to
0
in
L^{2}
, hence also in probability and in distribution. We record this in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a strictly stationary, centered sequence of real-valued random variables on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
, with
X_{1}\in L^{2}(\mu)
. Write

\gamma(k):=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(X_{1},X_{1+k})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X_{1}X_{1+k}],\qquad k\geq 0,

and assume that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\gamma(k)|<\infty.

Define

\sigma^{2}:=\gamma(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma(k),

which is well-defined by absolute summability. Then, with
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})\longrightarrow\sigma^{2}\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

In particular,
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
. If moreover
\sigma^{2}=0
, then

\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\mu),

and hence also in probability.

Proof.

Since
(X_{n})
is centered and strictly stationary,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[S_{n}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X_{i}X_{j}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma(|i-j|).

Grouping terms by lag
k:=|i-j|
, we obtain the standard variance decomposition

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})=n\,\gamma(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\,\gamma(k),

so that

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(S_{n})=\gamma(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k).

(4.10)

We now pass to the limit
n\to\infty
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By absolute summability of
(\gamma(k))_{k\geq 1}
, there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{k>K}|\gamma(k)|<\varepsilon.

Split the sum in (
4.10
) as

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k)+\sum_{k=K+1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k).

For the finite part,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma(k),

since
(1-k/n)\to 1
for each fixed
k
and the sum is finite.

For the tail, we use
0\leq 1-k/n\leq 1
to obtain the bound

\left|\sum_{k=K+1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k)\right|\leq\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}|\gamma(k)|<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }n\geq K+1.

Combining these estimates, we find

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma(k)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, it follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\gamma(k)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma(k).

Returning to (
4.10
), we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\gamma(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma(k)=\sigma^{2},

as claimed.
If
\sigma^{2}=0
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\left|\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\longrightarrow 0,

so
\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\mu)
and hence in probability.
∎

5
Coupling Space and Proof of
4

Recall that for a fixed pattern
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
the block indicator

\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{(A_{k},\dots,A_{k+m-1})=\boldsymbol{b}\}},

depends only on the outcome sequence
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and therefore does not depend on the choice of initial state of the system, even though its distribution does.
In order to compare these distributions for different (random) initial states, it is natural to place the corresponding outcome processes on a common probability space by means of a coupling of their annealed path measures.
This is the role of the coupling space introduced in this section.

5.1
Abstract Coupling Space

We fix the outcome path space
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is the product
\sigma
-algebra generated by cylinder sets.
Given two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma)
, a
coupling
of
(\mu,\nu)
is any probability measure
\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}
on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma\otimes\Sigma)
such that

\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}(A\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}})=\mu(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\times B)=\nu(B)\qquad\forall\,A,B\in\Sigma.

We denote by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the set of all such couplings.
It is well known that
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is nonempty for all
\mu,\nu
(for instance
\mu\otimes\nu\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
).

In the present setting, for each random initial state
\vartheta:\Omega\to\states
we have the annealed measure
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}
on
(\Omega\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{F}\otimes\Sigma)
and its
annealed outcome law

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta}:=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}}^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}),

where
\pi_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}}(\omega,\bar{a})=\bar{a}
is the projection onto the outcome coordinate.
Since all block-counting observables depend only on the outcome sequence, the relevant objects for coupling and CLT transfer are the outcome laws
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}
and
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta}
on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma)
.

Fix any
\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}\in\Pi(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}},\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta})
.
We regard
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}},\Sigma\otimes\Sigma,\widehat{\mathbb{Q}})
as a

for the two outcome laws, with coordinate projections

\bar{A}^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})}(\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\prime}):=\bar{a},\qquad\bar{A}^{(\vartheta)}(\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\prime}):=\bar{a}^{\prime},\qquad\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}.

We also introduce the time-coordinate maps

A_{k}^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})}(\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\prime}):=a_{k},\qquad A_{k}^{(\vartheta)}(\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\prime}):=a_{k}^{\prime},\qquad k\geq 1.

Thus
(A_{k}^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})})_{k\geq 1}
and
(A_{k}^{(\vartheta)})_{k\geq 1}
are the two coupled outcome processes, with the correct marginals:

\mathrm{Law}_{\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}}\!\left((A_{k}^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})})_{k\geq 1}\right)=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}},\qquad\mathrm{Law}_{\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}}\!\left((A_{k}^{(\vartheta)})_{k\geq 1}\right)=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta}.

On this coupling space, we define the pattern indicators by

\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})}(\bar{a},\bar{a^{\prime}}):=\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}\left(A^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})}(\bar{a},\bar{a^{\prime}})\right)=\mathbf{1}_{\{(A_{k}^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})},\dots,A_{k+m-1}^{(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})})=\boldsymbol{b}\}},

and similarly

\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\vartheta)}(\bar{a},\bar{a^{\prime}}):=\delta N_{k}\left(A^{(\vartheta)}(\bar{a},\bar{a^{\prime}})\right):=\mathbf{1}_{\{(A_{k}^{(\vartheta)},\dots,A_{k+m-1}^{(\vartheta)})=\boldsymbol{b}\}},

and the corresponding block-counting processes

N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})},\qquad N_{n}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\vartheta)}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\vartheta)}.

By construction, for each
k
the law of
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\rho_{\mathsf{ss}})}
under
\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}
coincides with the law of
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
under
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\rho_{\mathsf{ss}}}^{\mathrm{out}}
, and the law of
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b};(\vartheta)}
under
\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}
coincides with the law of
\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}
under
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{out}}
.

The admissibility condition introduced in

may be viewed as a quantitative hypothesis on the existence of such a coupling with controlled discrepancy between these pattern indicators.

\vartheta
is admissible for the pattern

\lambda_{\omega}(i,j):=\begin{cases}m_{\omega}(i),&\text{if }i=j,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha_{i}(\vartheta(\omega))-m_{\omega}(i))(\alpha_{j}(\eta(\omega))-m_{\omega}(j))}{r_{\omega}},&\text{if }i\neq j\text{ and }r_{\omega}>0,\\[10.0pt]
0,&\text{if }i\neq j\text{ and }r_{\omega}=0.\end{cases}

A direct computation shows that
\lambda_{\omega}
is a coupling of the probability vectors
(\alpha_{i}(\vartheta(\omega)))_{i=1}^{d}
and
(\alpha_{j}(\eta(\omega)))_{j=1}^{d}
:
For fixed
\omega\in\Omega^{\ast}
, write

a_{i}:=\alpha_{i}(\vartheta(\omega)),\qquad b_{i}:=\alpha_{i}(\eta(\omega)),\qquad m_{i}:=\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\},\qquad r:=1-\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}.

Since
m_{i}=\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\}
, we have

(a_{i}-m_{i})(b_{i}-m_{i})=0\qquad\text{for every }i.

If
r>0
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{\omega}(i,j)=m_{i}+\frac{a_{i}-m_{i}}{r}\sum_{j\neq i}(b_{j}-m_{j}).

Using
\sum_{j=1}^{d}(b_{j}-m_{j})=r
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{\omega}(i,j)=m_{i}+\frac{a_{i}-m_{i}}{r}\bigl(r-(b_{i}-m_{i})\bigr)=m_{i}+(a_{i}-m_{i})=a_{i}.

Thus the first marginal of
\lambda_{\omega}
is
(\alpha_{i}(\vartheta(\omega)))_{i=1}^{d}
.
The verification of the second marginal is identical by symmetry.
If
r=0
, then
a_{i}=b_{i}=m_{i}
for all
i
, so
\lambda_{\omega}
is supported on the diagonal and both marginals are again correct.

Moreover, for each fixed
(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{2}
, the map

\omega\longmapsto\lambda_{\omega}(i,j)

is measurable, since it is obtained from the measurable maps

\omega\longmapsto\alpha_{i}(\vartheta(\omega)),\qquad\omega\longmapsto\alpha_{j}(\eta(\omega))

by finitely many measurable operations.

Using the kernels from
(A)
(A.2), we now define a unified block-coupling kernel
\Lambda
so that off the diagonal it agrees with the given coupling
\kappa
, while on the diagonal it is replaced by the diagonal self-coupling.
This ensures that, once the hidden states agree, all subsequent coupled blocks coincide almost surely.

For
\omega\in\Omega^{*}
,
n\geq 0
, and
(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{2}
, define

\Lambda^{(n)}_{\omega}(i,j;\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}^{L}\times\mathcal{A}^{L})

by

\Lambda^{(n)}_{\omega}(i,j;C):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{A}^{L}}P^{(n)}_{\omega,i}(u)\,\mathbf{1}_{C}(u,u),&\text{if }i=j,\\[10.0pt]
\kappa^{(n)}_{\omega;i,j}(C),&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Here
\kappa^{(n)}_{\omega;i,j}(\,\cdot\,)=\kappa_{\theta^{n}\omega;i,j}(\,\cdot\,)

Then

is a coupling of
P^{(n)}_{\omega,i}
and
P^{(n)}_{\omega,j}
.
Indeed, if

, this is exactly
(A)
(A.2), while if

, the measure

is the diagonal coupling of

with itself.

Moreover, when

\Lambda^{(n)}_{\omega}\Bigl(i,j;\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{A}^{L}\times\mathcal{A}^{L}:\ f_{u}(i)=f_{v}(j)\}\Bigr)\geq\varepsilon

by

(A.2).
Since

the map

We now iterate the block-coupling kernel
\Lambda
and construct the full coupled process via the Ionescu–Tulcea theorem.
Set

\omega\in\Omega

For

and

.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(U^{(1)}=u^{(1)},\dots,U^{(R)}=u^{(R)}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{x_{0},y_{0}}\lambda_{\omega}(x_{0},y_{0})\prod_{r=1}^{R}\Lambda^{((r-1)L)}_{\omega}(x_{r-1},y_{r-1};\,\{u^{(r)}\}\times\mathcal{A}^{L})\,\pr(d\omega).

\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl((A_{1},\dots,A_{RL})=(u^{(1)}\cdots u^{(R)})\bigr)=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\vartheta}\bigl((A_{1},\dots,A_{RL})=(u^{(1)}\cdots u^{(R)})\bigr).

We now proceed to prove the remaining claims.
Define the block coalescence time

R_{*}:=\inf\{r\geq 0:\ X_{r}=Y_{r}\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}.

We show that once the hidden labels agree, they remain equal almost surely.
If

, then by the definition of
\Lambda
, the conditional law of

Hence

Therefore

It follows by induction that once the labels meet, they never separate. In particular,

\{R_{*}>r-1\}=\{X_{r-1}\neq Y_{r-1}\},\qquad r\geq 1.

For
r\geq 1
, let

\mathcal{H}_{r-1}:=\sigma\left((\omega,X_{0},Y_{0}),(U^{(1)},V^{(1)}),\dots,(U^{(r-1)},V^{(r-1)})\right).

On the event

\{R_{*}>r-1\}=\{X_{r-1}\neq Y_{r-1}\},

the conditional law of
(U^{(r)},V^{(r)})
given
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
is

\Lambda^{((r-1)L)}_{\omega}(X_{r-1},Y_{r-1};\cdot)=\kappa^{((r-1)L)}_{\omega;X_{r-1},Y_{r-1}}(\cdot).

Since

X_{r}=f_{U^{(r)}}(X_{r-1}),\qquad Y_{r}=f_{V^{(r)}}(Y_{r-1}),

(A)
(A.2) implies

\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{r}=Y_{r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1})\geq\varepsilon\qquad\text{on }\{R_{*}>r-1\}.

Therefore

\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{r}\neq Y_{r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1})\leq 1-\varepsilon\qquad\text{on }\{R_{*}>r-1\}.

Using that

\{R_{*}>r\}=\{R_{*}>r-1\}\cap\{X_{r}\neq Y_{r}\},

we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(R_{*}>r)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{R_{*}>r-1\}}\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{r}\neq Y_{r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon)\,\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(R_{*}>r-1),\qquad r\geq 1.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(R_{*}>0)\leq 1
, induction yields

\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(R_{*}>r)\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{r},\qquad r\geq 0.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[R_{*}]=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}(R_{*}>r)\leq\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(1-\varepsilon)^{r}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

Now, define

T_{\mathrm{out}}:\outcomes\times\outcomes\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

by

where

\bar{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots),\qquad\bar{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots)\in\outcomes.

T
is measurable, since for each

and each set

is measurable in
\Sigma\otimes\Sigma
.
Now suppose that
X_{r}=Y_{r}=i
.
Then, by the definition of
\Lambda
, the next block is coupled by the diagonal coupling of

with itself.
Hence

U^{(r+1)}=V^{(r+1)}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\text{-a.s. on }\{X_{r}=Y_{r}=i\}.

It follows that

X_{r+1}=Y_{r+1}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\text{-a.s. on }\{X_{r}=Y_{r}=i\}.

Iterating this argument, once the hidden labels meet, all subsequent blocks coincide
almost surely. Thus, on the event
\{R_{*}=r\}
,

U^{(s)}=V^{(s)}\qquad\text{for all }s\geq r+1,

and therefore, by concatenation,

A_{n}=B_{n}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq rL+1.

Equivalently,

T_{\mathrm{out}}(\bar{A},\bar{B})\leq LR_{*}+1\qquad\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\text{-a.s.}

Since

\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\circ(\bar{A},\bar{B})^{-1},

we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}}[T_{\mathrm{out}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[T_{\mathrm{out}}(\bar{A},\bar{B})]\leq L\,\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[R_{*}]+1\leq\frac{L}{\varepsilon}+1.

In particular,
T_{\mathrm{out}}<\infty
,
\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}
-almost surely, and

\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(A_{n}=B_{n}\text{ for all }n\geq T_{\mathrm{out}}\bigr)=1.

Finally, fix

and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
.
For every

, the tails of
\bar{A}
and

Hence, for every

\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}
and dividing by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{B})-\delta N_{k}^{\boldsymbol{b}}(\bar{A})\bigr)\right|\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}}[T_{\mathrm{out}}]}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Thus

is admissible for every pattern
\boldsymbol{b}

relative to
\eta
.

2^{\mathcal{A}}
, and the state space
\states
with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\dummyborel\left(\states\right)
.
The product space
\mathcal{A}\times\states
is equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra
2^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right)
, which coincides with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of the product topology.

We recall the projective action of an instrument component.
For each
\omega\in\Omega
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\rho\in\states
we set

\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}\rho:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)}{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)\right)},&\text{if }\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)\right)>0,\\[4.30554pt]
\rho_{\star},&\text{if }\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)\right)=0,\end{cases}

(A.1)

where
\rho_{\star}\in\states
is some fixed reference state.
The choice of
\rho_{\star}
on the zero-trace set does not affect any of the outcome distributions.

Lemma A.1
.

Fix
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then the map

\Omega\times\states\ni(\omega,\rho)\longmapsto\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}\rho\in\states

is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\states\right))
-measurable.

Proof.

Fix
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
is finite-dimensional, the space
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a finite-dimensional complex vector space.
Choose a basis
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, where
N=d^{2}
, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
be the corresponding coordinate
functionals, so that

\rho=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\ell_{r}(\rho)\,E_{r},\qquad\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

Each
\ell_{r}
is linear and therefore continuous.

For
(\omega,\rho)\in\Omega\times\states
, linearity of
\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}
gives

\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\ell_{r}(\rho)\,\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(E_{r}).

For each
r
, the map

\omega\longmapsto\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(E_{r})

is
(\mathcal{F},\dummyborel\left(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\right))
-measurable by the standing measurability assumption on the instrument, and the map

\rho\longmapsto\ell_{r}(\rho)

is
(\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\mathbb{C}\right))
-measurable. Hence

(\omega,\rho)\longmapsto\ell_{r}(\rho)\,\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(E_{r})

is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\right))
-measurable for each
r
. Summing over finitely many

, we conclude that

X(\omega,\rho):=\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)

is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\right))
-measurable as a map
\Omega\times\states\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Since the trace map
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\,\cdot\,\right):\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, the function

t(\omega,\rho):=\operatorname{Tr}\left(X(\omega,\rho)\right)

is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\mathbb{R}\right))
-measurable.
Let

A:=\{(\omega,\rho)\in\Omega\times\states:\ t(\omega,\rho)>0\}.

Then
A\in\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right)
.

On
A
, define

Y(\omega,\rho):=\frac{X(\omega,\rho)}{t(\omega,\rho)}.

The map

f:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\times(0,\infty)\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),\qquad f(Z,s):=\frac{Z}{s},

is continuous, hence Borel measurable.
Since

(\omega,\rho)\longmapsto(X(\omega,\rho),t(\omega,\rho))

is measurable and takes values in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\times(0,\infty)
on
A
, it follows that
Y
is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right))|_{A}
-measurable.

Now define
\widetilde{Y}:\Omega\times\states\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by

\widetilde{Y}(\omega,\rho):=\begin{cases}Y(\omega,\rho),&(\omega,\rho)\in A,\\[2.15277pt]
\rho_{\star},&(\omega,\rho)\notin A,\end{cases}

where
\rho_{\star}\in\states
is the fixed reference state from (
A.1
). Since
\rho_{\star}
is constant and
A
is measurable,
\widetilde{Y}
is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\right))
-measurable.

By construction,

\widetilde{Y}(\omega,\rho)=\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}\rho\qquad\text{for all }(\omega,\rho)\in\Omega\times\states.

Moreover,
\widetilde{Y}(\omega,\rho)\in\states
for every
(\omega,\rho)
: if
t(\omega,\rho)>0
, then
\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}(\rho)\geq 0
and normalization gives a state; if
t(\omega,\rho)=0
, then
\widetilde{Y}(\omega,\rho)=\rho_{\star}\in\states
.
Since
\states
carries the subspace Borel
\sigma
-algebra inherited from
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, it follows that

(\omega,\rho)\longmapsto\mathcal{T}_{a;\omega}{{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}}\rho

is
(\mathcal{F}\otimes\dummyborel\left(\states\right),\dummyborel\left(\states\right))
-measurable.
∎

Lemma B.1
.

Assume

(A)
(A.1) holds with

(A)
(A.2) holds with

and

Using

q_{k\ell}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{\alpha}{d},

\Phi_{\omega}(\rho)=(1-\alpha)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle e_{i},\rho e_{i}\rangle\,\rho^{(i)}+\frac{\alpha\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho\right)}{d}\,I_{d}=(1-\alpha)D(\rho)+\frac{\alpha\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho\right)}{d}\,I_{d}

\Phi_{\omega}(\rho)=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}q_{k\ell}|e_{k}\rangle\langle e_{\ell}|\rho|e_{\ell}\rangle\langle e_{k}|=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}q_{k\ell}\,\langle e_{\ell},\rho e_{\ell}\rangle\,\rho^{(k)}.

Using

q_{k\ell}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{\alpha}{d},

we get

\Phi_{\omega}(\rho)=(1-\alpha)\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\langle e_{\ell},\rho e_{\ell}\rangle\,\rho^{(\ell)}+\frac{\alpha}{d}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\langle e_{\ell},\rho e_{\ell}\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\rho^{(k)}\right).

\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\langle e_{\ell},\rho e_{\ell}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho\right)

\sum_{k=1}^{d}\rho^{(k)}=I_{d}
, this becomes

\Phi_{\omega}(\rho)=(1-\alpha)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle e_{i},\rho e_{i}\rangle\,\rho^{(i)}+\frac{\alpha\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho\right)}{d}\,I_{d}=(1-\alpha)D(\rho)+\frac{\alpha\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho\right)}{d}\,I_{d}

where

Note that for the operator

we have that

Thus for any random states

(A.2).

(A.1) holds.

Lemma B.2
.

Lemma B.3
.

Assume
1
and
2
