On the Optimum Secrecy Outage Probability and Ergodic Secrecy Rate over Wireless Channels Thanks: This study was funded by the AID CIEDS project SEPHYTEL.
Clément Leroy 1 , Tarak Arbi 1 , Benoit Geller 1 , Olivier Rioul 2

Abstract
We study the secrecy of wireless channels in the presence of an eavesdropper, where the channels are random and the transmitter only has knowledge of the channel statistics. We investigate the optimal input distribution with respect to several secrecy metrics: the Secrecy Outage Probability ( SOP ), defined as the probability that the coding rate r r exceeds the instantaneous secrecy rate; the Ergodic Secrecy Rate ( ESR ), defined as the expected secrecy rate over channel realizations; and the Ergodic Positive Secrecy Rate ( EPSR ), defined as the expected value of the positive part of the secrecy rate. We introduce two partial orderings for random channels: the uniformly less noisy order and the less noisy on average order. We show that when the main channel is uniformly less noisy than the eavesdropper channel, the optimal input distribution is a non-precoded Gaussian input for both the SOP and the EPSR . Furthermore, we show that the same input distribution is optimal for the ESR when the less noisy on average order holds. In addition, similar optimality results for the SOP and the EPSR are obtained for single-transmit-antenna channels without requiring any channel ordering assumptions. Closed-form expressions of the secrecy metrics are derived for special cases of Rayleigh fading channels.



I
Introduction

The wireless wiretap channel involves a transmitter (Alice) that emits a signal carrying a message intended to be reliably received by a legitimate receiver (Bob) while remaining confidential from an eavesdropper (Eve). Wyner’s seminal work
[
1
]
on the degraded wiretap channel demonstrated that secrecy can be achieved through channel coding at the transmitter, with a rate up to the secrecy capacity. Csiszár and Körner
[
2
]
derived the secrecy capacity for all kinds of wiretap channels, proving that it can be written as

C_{S}=\max_{U-X-Y,Z}\left[I(U;Y)-I(U;Z)\right],

(1)

where
I
denotes the Shannon mutual information, and
U-X-Y,Z
represents a Markov chain in which
U
is an auxiliary variable,
X
is the transmitted signal, and
Y
and
Z
are the signals received by Bob and Eve, respectively, as illustrated in Figure
1
. The maximum in (
1

X
:
\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{P}_{T}
, where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation.

Khisti and Wornell

U\equiv X
and

\displaystyle C_{S}=\max_{0\preceq\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}}\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}})}{\det(\mathbf{I+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}})}\right),

(2)

where

\mathbf{G}

[
5
,
6
]
. The I-MMSE formula
[
18
]
is used in

However, the Gaussianness of the optimal input distribution was established under the idealized and unrealistic assumption that both the legitimate channel and the eavesdropping channel are perfectly known to all parties involved in the communication. A more realistic approach is to consider that Alice only has statistical knowledge of the channels, which are modeled as random variables. This setting encompasses imperfect channel estimation, partial channel state information, and knowledge of the statistics of a fast-fading channel. Additionnaly to the secrecy capacity, the secrecy outage probability is a popular metric of secrecy in such a communication model. To the best of our knowledge, no exact expression for the secrecy capacity or the secrecy outage probability has yet been proposed in the literature for this case. Nevertheless, some bounds have been established on the secrecy capacity for channels whose randomness comes from non prefect channel estimation
[
7
,
8
]
.

Most previous works focus on achievable secrecy rates or achievable low secrecy outage probability, typically assuming a Gaussian input that is either non-precoded
[

11
,
12
,
10

or only combined with artificial Gaussian noise
[

15

14

[

from the perfect knowledge of the instantaneous channel state by the transmitter and the receiver.

In this paper, we investigate the optimization of three secrecy metrics involving the channel state randomness with respect to the input distribution
p_{(U,X)}
under an average power constraint. We show that for some large classes of wiretap channels, what we call the
Ergodic Positive Secrecy Rate

II
System model

II-A

Communication model

We consider a MIMOME (Multiple-Input, Multiple-Output, Multiple-Eavesdropper) wireless channel: the transmitter (Alice), the legitimate receiver (Bob) and the eavesdropper (Eve) have repectively
N_{A}
,
N_{B}
and
N_{E}
antennas. Alice transmits a message
u
belonging to an arbitrary set
\mathcal{U}
, encoded into a signal
\mathbf{x}
, which is modeled as a complex random vector in
\mathbb{C}^{N_{A}}
. Bob observes the vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{B}}
, given by:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w}_{B},

(3)

while Eve observes the vector
\mathbf{z}
in
\mathbb{C}^{N_{E}}
:

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w}_{E},

(4)

where
\mathbf{w}_{B}
and
\mathbf{w}_{E}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) circularly symmetric complex Gaussian noise vectors with covariance matrix
\mathbf{I}
. The matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{B}\times N_{A}}
(respectively,
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{E}}\times N_{A}
) is a random matrix with distribution
p_{\mathbf{H}}
(respectively,
p_{\mathbf{G}}
). The distributions
p_{\mathbf{H}}
and
p_{\mathbf{G}}
represent the channel statistics. As the instantaneous channel states are unknown to the transmitter, the random variables
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{G}
are independant to
u
and
\mathbf{x}
.

II-B

Secrecy metrics

The secrecy of the communication system can be quantified using different metrics, each being relevant in various scenarios:

1)

the
Ergodic Secrecy Rate
(
ESR
), defined as

\textit{ESR}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right];

(5)

2)

the
Ergodic Positive Secrecy Rate
(
EPSR
), defined as

\textit{EPSR}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\left(I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right)^{+}\right];

(6)

where
(x)^{+}=\max(0,x)
;

3)

the
Secrecy Outage Probability
(
SOP
), defined for all non-negative real numbers
r
as

\textit{SOP}(r)\triangleq\mathbb{P}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})<r\right].

(7)

The
ESR
represents an achievable secrecy rate when both Bob and Eve have perfect channel state information, while Alice remains unaware of it. Thus, the maximum
ESR
coincides with the secrecy capacity in this specific scenario. This can be shown using the Csiszár and Körner secrecy capacity expression by considering that the channel output includes both the received signal and the instantaneous channel state:

\displaystyle C_{S}

\displaystyle=\max_{p_{u,\mathbf{x}}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{H})-I(u;\mathbf{z},\mathbf{G})\right]

(8)

\displaystyle=\max_{p_{u,\mathbf{x}}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{H})+I(u;\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{H})-I(u;\mathbf{G})-I(u;\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{G})\right]

(9)

\displaystyle=\max_{p_{u,\mathbf{x}}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{H})-I(u;\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{G})\right]

(10)

\displaystyle=\max_{p_{u,\mathbf{x}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]\right].

(11)

The
EPSR
corresponds to an achievable secrecy rate over slow-fading channels with feedback, where the transmitter employs an on-off power control strategy, transmitting only when the instantaneous secrecy rate is positive. This strategy is used in
[
11
]
to show that the secrecy capacity expression established for the slow-fading wiretap channel with feedback is indeed achievable.

The
SOP
reflects the probability of information leakage to the eavesdropper when transmission occurs at a fixed secrecy rate
r
. This metric is commonly used when the eavesdropper channel is unknown to the legitimate transmitter. It quantifies the probability that secure communication fails, i.e., that the instantaneous secrecy rate falls below the target rate
r
.

II-C

Partial ordering of channels

The legitimate channel is fully characterized by the distribution
p_{\mathbf{H}}
of the channel matrix
\mathbf{H}
, while the eavesdropper channel is fully characterized by the distribution
p_{\mathbf{G}}
of
\mathbf{G}
. In
[
16
]
, Körner and Marton introduced two partial orderings for noisy memoryless channels with the same input alphabet: the
less noisy
order and the
more capable
order. Bergmans later introduced in
[
17
]
the notion of
degraded
channels. These three orderings have since become classical tools for comparing communication channels. In this work, we extend these definitions to the setting of random fading channels and we propose two ways to partially order ergodic wireless channels.

•

Less noisy on average: in
[
16
]
, a channel
X\rightarrow Y
is said to be less noisy than
X\rightarrow Z
when
I(U;Y)\geq I(U;Z)
for every channel
U\rightarrow X
. We define the
less noisy on average
order as the
less noisy
order on the channels
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{H})
and
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{G})
. Therefore, an ergodic channel given by
\mathbf{H}
is said to be
less noisy on average
than an ergodic channel given by
\mathbf{G}
when:

\displaystyle\forall u\rightarrow\mathbf{x},I(u;(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{y}))\geq I(u;(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{z})),

(12)

which can also be written as:

\displaystyle\forall u\rightarrow\mathbf{x},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{z})\right].

(13)

From (
13
), we see that the inequality defining the
less noisy on average
order is the average over all instantaneous channel states of the inequality defining the
less noisy
order.

•

Uniformly less noisy: we define the
uniformly less noisy
order as the
less noisy
order on the channels
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{z}
for every possible instantaneous channels
(\mathbf{H}_{0},\mathbf{G}_{0})
. Therefore, an ergodic channel given by
\mathbf{H}
is
uniformly less noisy
than an an ergodic channel given by
\mathbf{G}
when:

\displaystyle\forall(\mathbf{H}_{0},\mathbf{G}_{0}),\forall u\rightarrow\mathbf{x},I(u;\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}_{0})\geq I(u;\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}_{0}).

(14)

•

Degraded: an ergodic channel given by
\mathbf{G}
is a degraded version of an ergodic channel given by
\mathbf{H}
when
u-(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{H})-(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{G})
forms a Markov chain.

It is straighforward to prove that the
degraded
order is stronger than the
uniformly less noisy
order, which is itself stronger than the
less noisy on average
order. In other words, if an ergodic channel
\mathbf{G}
is a degraded version of an ergodic channel
\mathbf{H}
, then
\mathbf{H}
is both uniformly less noisy and less noisy on average than
\mathbf{G}
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{H}
is uniformly less noisy than
\mathbf{G}
, then it is also less noisy on average. In this paper, we focus on the
uniformly less noisy
order and the
less noisy on average
order. The
degraded
order is mentioned primarily because it is often easier to verify in practice whether the eavesdropper channel is a degraded version of the legitimate channel than to check whether either of the other two conditions holds.

Secrecy Outage Probability (

III-A

MIMOME channel

In this subsection, we study the general MIMOME channel, where Alice, Bob, and Eve are each equipped with an arbitrary number of antennas. We show that when the legitimate channel is
uniformly less noisy
than the eavesdropper channel, both the minimum
SOP
and the maximum

SOP

EPSR

Theorem 1
.

Let

\mathbf{H}

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(u,\mathbf{x})}\\

(15)

Note that in

Lemma 1

Given that the instantaneous wireless channel

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(u,\mathbf{x})}\\

\displaystyle=I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z})

(16)

Proof of Lemma
1

Since the instantaneous channel

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(u,\mathbf{x})}\\

(17)

III-B

SIMOME channel

In this subsection, we prove that the optimal input distribution for the
SOP
and the
EPSR
is still a non-precoded Gaussian distribution when
N_{A}=1
and both receivers have an arbitrary number of antenna. This scenario is refered to as a SIMOME (Single Input, Multiple Output, Multipe Eavesdropper) channel. Contrary to the MIMOME case, no order between channels is necessary to obtain the optimality of the non-precoded Gaussian input.

Theorem 3
.

Let
r
be a non-negative real number. If
N_{A}=1
(SIMOME scenario), the input distribution
p_{(u,x)}
minimizing the Secrecy Outage Probability under the average power constraint
\mathcal{P}_{T}
is the one for which
u\equiv x
and
x
is a complex Gaussian random variable with variance
\mathcal{P}_{T}
; that is:

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(u,x)}\\
\mathbb{E}[|x|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{P}_{T}\end{subarray}}\textit{SOP}(r)=\mathbb{P}\left[\log\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{P}_{T}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^{2}}{1+\mathcal{P}_{T}\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert^{2}}\right)<r\right],

(29)

where
\mathbf{h}
(resp.
\mathbf{g}
) is the legitimate (resp. eavesdropper) channel matrix of dimension
1\times N_{B}
(resp.
1\times N_{E}
).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Minimizing the
SOP
is equivalent to maximizing

\displaystyle 1-\textit{SOP}(r)=\mathbb{P}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\geq r\right].

(30)

We now simplify the SIMOME expression into a SISOSE (Single Input, Single Output, Single Eavesdropper) equivalent expression using basic matrix transformations. Let us first define
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{h}}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{g}}
as unitary matrices such that the first column of
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{h}}
is
\frac{\mathbf{h}}{\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert}
and the first column of
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{g}}
is
\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert}
. We have:
\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{h}}.(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert{}0...0)^{T}
and
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{g}}.(\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert{}0...0)^{T}
. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=I(u;\mathbf{h}x+\mathbf{w}_{B})-I(u;\mathbf{g}x+\mathbf{w}_{E})

(31)

\displaystyle=I(u;\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{h}}.(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert{}0...0)^{T}x+\mathbf{w}_{B})-I(u;\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{g}}.(\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert{}0...0)^{T}x+\mathbf{w}_{E})

(32)

\displaystyle=I(u;(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert{}0...0)^{T}x+\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{h}}^{T}.\mathbf{w}_{B})-I(u;(\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert{}0...0)^{T}x+\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{g}}^{T}.\mathbf{w}_{E})

(33)

\displaystyle=I(u;\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert x+w_{B})-I(u;\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert x+w_{E}),

(34)

where
w_{B}
and
w_{E}
are unit-variance circularly symmetric complex Gaussian noise.

By
[
21
]
, we know that if
\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert\geq\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert
, then

\displaystyle\max_{p_{(u,x)}}I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})=I(x_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y})-I(x_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z}),

(35)

where
x_{\text{g}}
is a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian random variable with variance
\mathcal{P}_{T}
.

Besides, if
\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert\leq\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert
, then by the Data-Processing Inequality (DPI), for all
p_{(u,x)}
, we have

\displaystyle I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\leq 0.

(36)

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\geq r\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[(I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\geq r)\cap(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert\geq\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[(I(x_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y})-I(x_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z})\geq r)\cap(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert\geq\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert)\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[I(x_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y})-I(x_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z})\geq r\right].

(38)

To conclude the proof, we are left to notice that

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z})=\log\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{P}_{T}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^{2}}{1+\mathcal{P}_{T}\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert^{2}}\right).

(39)

∎

The reasoning used to prove Theorem
2
can be directly extended to derive the optimal
EPSR
for the SIMOME channel.

Theorem 4
.

If
N_{A}=1
(SIMOME scenario), the input distribution
p_{(u,x)}
that maximizes the Ergodic Positive Secrecy Rate under the average power constraint
\mathcal{P}_{T}
is the one for which
u\equiv x
and
x
is a complex Gaussian random variable with variance
\mathcal{P}_{T}
; that is:

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(u,x)}\\
\mathbb{E}[|x|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{P}_{T}\end{subarray}}\textit{EPSR}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}\left[\log\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{P}_{T}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^{2}}{1+\mathcal{P}_{T}\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert^{2}}\right)^{+}\right].

(40)

Proof.

The proof for Theorem
4
is the same as the proof for Theorem
2
but using the result of Theorem
3
instead of Theorem
1
.
∎

IV
Ergodic Secrecy Rate (
ESR
) optimization

IV-A

MIMOME channel

In this subsection, we study the general MIMOME channel: Alice, Bob and Eve all have an arbitrary number of antenna. We show that when the legitimate channel is
less noisy on average
than the eavesdropper channel, the maximal
ESR
is achieved by a non-precoded Gaussian input.

Theorem 5
.

If the legitimate channel is less noisy on average than the eavesdropper channel, then the ESR is maximized by a non-precoded Gaussian input distribution:

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(u,\mathbf{x})}\\
\mathrm{cov}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\end{subarray}}\textit{ESR}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G})}\right)\right].

(41)

Proof of Theorem
5
.

We can decompose the
ESR
as

ESR

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]

(42)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y}|u)-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}|u)\right].

(43)

As the legitimate channel is
less noisy on average
than the eavesdropper channel, the second term in (
43
) is non negative and is zero when
u\equiv\mathbf{x}
. Thus, maximizing the
ESR
reduces to the maximization of the first term in (
43
).

Then, as the legitimate channel is
less noisy on average
than the eavesdropper channel, the
ESR
is non negative for every input. From (
43
), we obtain for every
u\rightarrow\mathbf{x}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z})\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y}|u)-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}|u)\right].

(44)

Inequality (
44
) is exactly the concavity caracterization of the fonction

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z})\right],

(45)

whose expression can further be decomposed using the Pinsker formula
[
22
,
23
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y}_{\text{g}})+D(\mathbf{w}\,\|\,\mathbf{w}_{\text{g}})-D(\mathbf{y}\,\|\,\mathbf{y}_{\text{g}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z}_{\text{g}})+D(\mathbf{w}\,\|\,\mathbf{w}_{\text{g}})-D(\mathbf{z}\,\|\,\mathbf{z}_{\text{g}})\right]

(46)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{y}_{\text{g}})-I(\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}};\mathbf{z}_{\text{g}})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[D(\mathbf{z}\,\|\,\mathbf{z}_{\text{g}})-D(\mathbf{y}\,\|\,\mathbf{y}_{\text{g}})\right],

(47)

where the subscript "
g
" indicates that the variable is Gaussian with the same covariance as the original random variable. When the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
of
\mathbf{x}
is fixed, the first term in (
47
) is constant, so the second term is concave in the input distribution
p_{\mathbf{x}}
. Moreover, both
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[D(\mathbf{z}\,\|\,\mathbf{z}_{\text{g}})\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[D(\mathbf{y}\,\|\,\mathbf{y}_{\text{g}})\right]
are 0 when
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}}
, which is their minimum. Therefore,
p_{\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}}}
is a stationnary point of the second term in (
47
). Since this term is also concave in the input distribution, the maximum of the
ESR
is achieved with
p_{\mathbf{x}_{\text{g}}}
.

The straightforward derivation of the mutual informations with Gaussian inputs concludes the proof.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
5
, the
less noisy on average
order plays a central role. This assumption is essential and cannot be relaxed, as demonstrated by the counterexample in Section
VI-B
, where the order fails and the maximum
ESR
is not achieved by a non-precoded Gaussian input.

IV-B

SIMOME channel

Unlike the optimization of the
SOP
and the
EPSR
, where reducing the problem to the SIMOME case leads to a unique general solution, the optimization of the
ESR
is not significantly simplified in this scenario.

Theorem 6
.

Let us consider the case
N_{A}=1
(SIMOME). Let
c_{x|u}
denote a fixed precoder distribution (the distribution of
x
given
u
), and let
p_{(u,x)}=p_{u}c_{x|u}
and
q_{(u,x)}=q_{u}c_{x|u}
be two input distributions sharing the same precoder, such that
\mathbb{E}\!\left[|x|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{P}_{T}
. Denote by ESR
(p_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})
(respectively, ESR
(q_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})
) the ESR obtained with the input distribution
p_{(u,x)}
(respectively,
q_{(u,x)}
) and the channel statistics
p_{\mathbf{h}}
and
p_{\mathbf{g}}
. Exactly one of the following two statements holds:

(i)

For all channel statistics
p_{\mathbf{h}}
and
p_{\mathbf{g}}
,

\textit{ESR}(p_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})=\textit{ESR}(q_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}}).

(48)

(ii)

There exist channel statistics
p_{\mathbf{h}}
and
p_{\mathbf{g}}
such that

\textit{ESR}(p_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})<\textit{ESR}(q_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}}).

(49)

In Theorem
6
, statement
(i)
corresponds to the trivial case in which the distributions
p_{(u,x)}
and
q_{(u,x)}
yield the same overall behavior. This occurs, for example, when
p_{(u,x)}=q_{(u,x)}
.

Moreover, since the distributions
p_{(u,x)}
and
q_{(u,x)}
play symmetrical roles in statement
(ii)
of Theorem
6
, they can be interchanged. Thus, no single distribution maximizes the
ESR
in general, as the optimal distribution strongly depends on the channel statistics. In particular, the Gaussian input is not optimal in every scenario. Two illustrative examples are provided in Section
VI-A
: one where BPSK modulation outperforms Gaussian coding, and another where Gaussian coding achieves better performance.

Besides, as SIMOME channels are a special class of MIMOME channels, Theorem
6
also shows that no unique distribution is optimal for all MIMOME channel statistics.

Before proving Theorem
6
, we introduce several quantities and establish properties that will be useful in the proof.

For any distribution
d\in\{p_{(u,x)},q_{(u,x)}\}
, we define the function
I_{d}
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as:

I_{d}(\gamma)\triangleq I_{d}(u;\sqrt{\gamma}x+w),

(50)

where
w
is a Gaussian noise with unit variance. The parameter
\gamma
is commonly referred to as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). We also denote by
\Delta
the difference function:

\Delta(\gamma)\triangleq I_{p}(\gamma)-I_{q}(\gamma).

(51)

The random variables
u-x-y
form a Markov chain, so the mutual information can be decomposed as:

I_{p}(\gamma)=I_{p}(x;y)-I_{p}(x;y|u).

(52)

From (
52
), it follows that
I_{p}
is infinitely differentiable, as it is the difference of two infinitely differentiable functions
[
18
,
19
]
. Therefore, the function
\Delta
is also infinitely differentiable. Its first and second order derivatives are denoted by
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, respectively. The function satisfies the following properties, which are stated in Lemma
2
.

Lemma 2
.

\Delta(0)=\Delta^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow+\infty}\Delta^{\prime}(\gamma)=0.

(53)

Proof of Lemma
2
.

When
\gamma=0
, the output
y
is always 0, so both mutual informations
I_{p}
and
I_{q}
are zero. Hence, we have:

\Delta(0)=0.

(54)

The derivative
\Delta^{\prime}
can be computed using the
I-MMSE
formula
[
18
]
(the factor
\frac{1}{2}
disappears since the random variables are complex):

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(\textit{MMSE}_{p}(x|\gamma,y)-\textit{MMSE}_{p}(x|\gamma,y,u)\right)-\left(\textit{MMSE}_{q}(x|\gamma,y)-\textit{MMSE}_{q}(x|\gamma,y,u)\right).

(55)

So:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\left(\textit{MMSE}_{p}(x|\gamma=0)-\textit{MMSE}_{p}(x|u)\right)-\left(\textit{MMSE}_{q}(x|\gamma=0)-\textit{MMSE}_{q}(x|u)\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=\left(\textit{MMSE}_{p}(x|\gamma=0)-\textit{MMSE}_{q}(x|\gamma=0)\right)-\left(\textit{MMSE}_{p}(x|u)-\textit{MMSE}_{q}(x|u)\right)

(57)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[|x|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[|x|^{2}\right]\right)-\left(\textit{MMSE}_{c}(x|u)-\textit{MMSE}_{c}(x|u)\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=0.

(59)

Moreover, using this general bound on the
MMSE

[
19
]
:

\textit{MMSE}(x|\gamma,y,u)\leq\textit{MMSE}(x|\gamma,y)\leq\frac{1}{\gamma},

(60)

we obtain by the triangular inequality:

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow+\infty}|\Delta^{\prime}(\gamma)|\leq\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{4}{\gamma}=0,

(61)

so
\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow+\infty}\Delta^{\prime}(\gamma)=0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The precoder
c_{x|u}
is fixed, and two distributions
p_{(u,x)}=p_{u}c_{x|u}
and
q_{(u,x)}=q_{u}c_{x|u}
are given such that
\mathbb{E}\left[|x|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{P}_{T}
. We construct channel distributions
p_{\mathbf{h}}
and
p_{\mathbf{g}}
such that (
48
) or (
49
) holds.

Now, assume that
\Delta
is concave on all of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then,
\Delta^{\prime}
is a non-increasing function on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Since
\Delta^{\prime}(0)=\Delta^{\prime}(+\infty)
, it follows that
\Delta^{\prime}=0
and
\Delta
is constant. Moreover,
\Delta(0)=0
, so
\Delta=0
, which implies that for all
\gamma\geq 0
:

I_{p}(u;\sqrt{\gamma}x+w)=I_{q}(u;\sqrt{\gamma}x+w).

(62)

So for any channels statistics
p_{\mathbf{h}}
and
p_{\mathbf{g}}
, we have:

ESR

\displaystyle(p_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}\left[I_{p}(u;\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert x+w)-I_{p}(u;\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert x+w)\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}\left[I_{q}(u;\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert x+w)-I_{q}(u;\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert x+w)\right]

(64)

\displaystyle=\textit{ESR}(q_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}}).

(65)

Thus, if
\Delta
is concave on all of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, then statement
(i)
holds. We now assume that statement
(i)
does not hold and proceed to prove statement
(ii)
. Since both statements cannot be true for the same distributions
p_{(u,x)}
and
q_{(u,x)}
, this is sufficient to show that exactly one statement is always true.

When
(i)
does not hold,
\Delta
is not concave on all of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. So there exists a non-negative real number
\gamma_{0}
such that
\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\gamma_{0})>0
. By continuity of
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime}
, there exists an interval
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
over which
\Delta
is strictly convex and strictly monotone.

Let
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
be two numbers in
\mathcal{J}
. We denote by
\bar{\gamma}
the mean of these two numbers, defined as:

\bar{\gamma}\triangleq\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{2}.

(66)

Let
\gamma^{*}
be the number in
\mathcal{J}
such that

\Delta(\gamma^{*})=\frac{\Delta(\gamma_{1})+\Delta(\gamma_{2})}{2}.

(67)

If
\Delta
is increasing over
\mathcal{J}
, we define a channel
\mathbf{h}_{0}
such that
\bar{\gamma}<\lVert\mathbf{h}_{0}\rVert^{2}<\gamma^{*}
. If
\Delta
is decreasing over
\mathcal{J}
, we define
\mathbf{h}_{0}
such that
\bar{\gamma}<\lVert\mathbf{h}_{0}\rVert^{2}
. These constructions for both monotonicities are illustrated in Figure

.

.

We now construct
{\mathbf{h}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
with finite support and whose square norm lies in
\mathcal{J}
. We define the channel distribution
p_{\mathbf{h}}
to have a single mass point, while
p_{\mathbf{g}}
has two mass points of equal weights. This models a non-varying main channel and an eavesdropper channel that can take only two distinct states. We choose the two mass points of

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, and the single mass point of

\displaystyle\textit{ESR}(p_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})-\textit{ESR}(q_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}\left[\left(I_{p}\left(u;\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert x+w\right)-I_{p}\left(u;\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert x+w\right)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}}\left[\left(I_{q}\left(u;\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert x+w\right)-I_{q}\left(u;\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert x+w\right)\right)\right]

(68)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{p}(\lVert\mathbf{h}_{0}\rVert^{2})-\frac{I_{p}(\gamma_{1})+I_{p}(\gamma_{2})}{2}\right)-\left(I_{q}(\lVert\mathbf{h}_{0}\rVert^{2})-\frac{I_{q}(\gamma_{1})+I_{q}(\gamma_{2})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta(\lVert\mathbf{h}_{0}\rVert^{2})-\frac{\Delta(\gamma_{1})+\Delta(\gamma_{2})}{2}

\textit{ESR}(p_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})<\textit{ESR}(q_{(u,x)},p_{\mathbf{h}},p_{\mathbf{g}})

IV-C

Bounds on the maximum ESR

In the general case, without the
less noisy on average
order assumption, the maximum
ESR
is difficult to derive. However, bounds can be established.

Theorem 7
.

The maximum ESR at a fixed covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
for
\mathbf{x}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle C_{S}^{-}\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{u,\mathbf{x}}\\
\mathrm{cov}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\end{subarray}}\textit{ESR}\leq C_{S}^{+},

(76)

with

\displaystyle C_{S}^{-}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}\right)\right]+\max_{0\preceq\mathbf{K}_{M}\preceq\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{M}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{M}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}\right)\right],

(77)

and

\displaystyle C_{S}^{+}=\int\log(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger})(p_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{A})-p_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{A}))^{+}d\mathbf{A}.

(78)

Corollary 2
.

In Theorem
7
,
C_{S}^{+}
can also be written using a conditional expectation:

\displaystyle C_{S}^{+}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G})\in\mathcal{A}^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}\right)\Big|(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G})\in\mathcal{A}^{2}\right],

(79)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{\mathbf{A}:p_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{A})\leq p_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{A})\}.

(80)

Proof of Theorem
7
.

We first prove that
C_{S}^{-}\leq\max\textit{ESR}
, then we prove that
\max\textit{ESR}\leq C_{S}^{+}
.

We prove that
C_{S}^{-}\leq\max\textit{ESR}
by designing an input distribution
p_{(u,\mathbf{x})}
such that
C_{S}^{-}=\textit{ESR}(p_{(u,\mathbf{x})})
. We decompose the signal
\mathbf{x}
into two components:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{I}+\mathbf{x}_{M},

(81)

where we choose
\mathbf{x}_{I}
and
\mathbf{x}_{M}
to be Gaussian and independant. The covariance of
\mathbf{x}_{M}
is denoted by
\mathbf{K}_{M}
so
0\preceq\mathbf{K}_{M}\preceq\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
. We also choose
u\equiv\mathbf{x}_{I}
. This is similar to an artificial noise scheme
[
13
]
as
\mathbf{x}_{I}
bears all the information and
\mathbf{x}_{M}
is an additionnal Gaussian noise sometimes called a
mask
. However, it is to be noted that in our proof,
\mathbf{x}_{M}
is not assumed to lie in the null space of
\mathbf{H}
, as the legitimate channel is unknown to the transmitter. Therefore, the mutual information difference obtained with the defined input distribution and given instantaneous channels
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{G}
is:

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{M}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{M}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}\right)

(82)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{M}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{M}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger})}\right).

(83)

Averaging over all instantaneous channels
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{G}
and maximizing over all possible
\mathbf{K}_{M}
yields
C_{S}^{-}\leq\max\textit{ESR}
.

Now, we upperbound
\max\textit{ESR}
to show that
\max\textit{ESR}\leq C_{S}^{+}
. We have:

\displaystyle\max\textit{ESR}

\displaystyle=\max\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]

(84)

\displaystyle=\max\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]

(85)

\displaystyle=\max\int I(u;\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A})p_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{A})d\mathbf{A}-\int I(u;\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{A})p_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{A})d\mathbf{A}

(86)

\displaystyle=\max\int I(u;\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A})(p_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{A})-p_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{A}))d\mathbf{A}

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\int\max I(u;\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A})(p_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{A})-p_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{A}))^{+}d\mathbf{A}

(89)

where (

\mathbf{y}

\mathbf{z}
have the same distribution when

, (

) follows from the non negativity of mutual information and (

.

The lower bound

are obtained applying Theorem

to the SIMOME scenario. The upper bound
C_{S,\text{SIMOME}}^{+(1)}
however is specific to the SIMOME case. We have:

\displaystyle\max\textit{ESR}

\displaystyle=\max\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]

(100)

\displaystyle=\max\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]

(101)

\displaystyle=\max\int\frac{d}{d(a^{2})}\left(I(u;\mathbf{y}|\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert=a)\right)\mathbb{P}[\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert\geq a]da-\int\frac{d}{d(a^{2})}\left(I(u;\mathbf{z}|\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert=a)\right)\mathbb{P}[\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert\geq a]da

where (

) comes from the fact that
\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d(a^{2})}\left(I(u;\mathbf{y}|\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert=a)\right)

Rayleigh fading channels

V-A

Degraded MIMOME Rayleigh fading channels

In this subsection, we consider the special case of degraded MIMOME Rayleigh fading channels. The legitimate channel is Rayleigh distributed, so each coefficient of the matrix follows
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{H}}^{2})
. The eavesdropper channel is
\mathbf{G}
and can be expressed as a cascade of two Rayleigh channels: the first one is
\mathbf{H}
and the second one is
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}
, whose coefficient follows
\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\sigma_{\mathbf{G}}}^{2})
. As the eavesdropper channel is degraded compared to the legitimate channel, channel
\mathbf{H}
is also less noisy than channel
\mathbf{G}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{H}\tilde{\mathbf{G}}
. The
SOP
, the
ESR
and the
EPSR
can be computed in this case.

From Theorems
1
,
2
and
5
, the
SOP
, the
EPSR
and the
ESR
are optimized by the Gaussian input when the input covariance
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
is fixed. We can now derive the input covariance matrix that maximizes the secrecy metrics.

First, we derive the maximum
ESR
and
EPSR
. For all fixed instantaneous channel state
(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G})
,
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}
forms a Markov chain and:

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})-I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z})=I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}).

(109)

The mutual information is always non negative, so in this case, the
EPSR
is equal to the
ESR
as the term in the expectancy is never forced to zero by the positive part operator. From
[
3
]
, we know that when
\mathbf{x}
is Gaussian, the right hand side of (
109
) is concave in the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
. Thus the function

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G})}\right)

(110)

is concave in
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
and the average over all instantaneous channel state
(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G})
is also concave in
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
. This means that the
ESR
is concave in
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
.

From
[
24
]
, we know that the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{I}
maximizes both
\mathbb{E}\left[\log(\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{H}))\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\log(\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{G}))\right]
. Thus, this input is a stationnary point of the
ESR
. As the
ESR
is a concave function, the maximum is achieved by this input. This leads to

\displaystyle\textit{ESR}^{\star}=\textit{EPSR}^{\star}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G})}\right)\right].

(111)

We now optimize the
SOP
and show that the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{I}
also minimizes the
SOP
.

We apply the eigenvalue decomposition to
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
, and we obtain
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
for some unitary matrix
\mathbf{U}
and some diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}
. As the channels are Rayleigh distributed, the distribution of the secrecy rate is the same whether
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
or
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{D}
. Thus, we can restrict the search of an optimal covariance matrix to diagonal covariance matrices.

Let
D_{1}\triangleq Diag(d_{1},...,d_{N_{A}})
be an optimal covariance matrix whose trace is equal to the average power contraint
\mathcal{P}_{T}
. As the channels are Rayleigh faded, no direction preveils on another, so for any
i\in\{1,...,N_{A}\}
,
D_{i}\triangleq Diag(d_{i},d_{i}+1,...,d_{N_{A}},d_{1},...,d_{i-1})
is also optimal. Let
p_{\text{g}}^{(i)}
denote the Gaussian input with covariance matrix
D_{i}
. We construct a new distribution
\tilde{p}
by time sharing: the time is equally shared among the inputs
p_{g}^{(i)}
. We have

\displaystyle\textit{SOP}(\tilde{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{A}}\frac{\textit{SOP}\left(p_{\text{g}}^{(i)}\right)}{N_{A}}=\textit{SOP}\left(p_{\text{g}}^{(1)}\right).

(112)

The covariance matrix of the input
\tilde{p}
is the average of all
D_{i}
’s, which is
\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{I}
. Therefore, by Theorem
1
,
\textit{SOP}(\tilde{p})\geq\textit{SOP}(p^{\star})
, where
p^{\star}
is the Gaussian input with covariance
\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{I}
. As
\textit{SOP}\left(\tilde{p}\right)=\textit{SOP}(p_{\text{g}}^{(1)})
and
p_{\text{g}}^{(1)}
is minimal,
p^{\star}
is also minimal. We have:

\displaystyle\textit{SOP}^{\star}=\mathbb{P}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G})}\right)<r\right].

(113)

V-B

Less noisy on average MIMOME Rayleigh fading channels

In this subsection, we study the special case of
less noisy on average
MIMOME Rayleigh fading channels. The legitimate channel
\mathbf{H}
and the eavesdropper channel
\mathbf{G}
are Rayleigh distributed, so each coefficient of the matrix follows respectiveley
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{H}}^{2})
and
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{G}}^{2})
. The order assumption imposes that for every input
u\rightarrow\mathbf{x}
:
\mathbb{E}\left[I(u;\mathbf{y})-I(u;\mathbf{z})\right]\geq 0
. This case happends with different set of parameters
(N_{A},N_{B},N_{E},\sigma_{\mathbf{H}},\sigma_{\mathbf{G}})
. For instance, when
N_{B}\geq N_{E}
and
\sigma_{\mathbf{H}}\leq\sigma_{\mathbf{G}}
, the eavesdropper channel is equivalent to a degraded version of the legitimate channel, as shown in
[
10
]
. The channel order also holds when
N_{A}=1
and the complementary distributive function (CCDF) of
\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert
is always greater than the CCDF of
\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert
, as the channel can then be expressed with equivalent degraded SISO channels.

The
less noisy on average
order is more general than the
degraded
order studied in the previous subsection. However, we show that the optimal
ESR
has the same mathematical expression as in the more specific case.

Assuming the
less noisy on average
channel order, we compute the maximum
ESR
using a similar approach to the optimal
SOP
in the degraded Rayleigh fading case. Note that the approach that was used for the maximum
ESR
in the degraded case cannot be replicated as it requires to write the secrecy rate as a single mutual information that is concave in the Gaussian input covariance matrix.

By Theorem
5
, the maximum
ESR
is achieved by a Gaussian input with covariance
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
. We now search the
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}}
that maximizes the
ESR
.

As detailled in the previous subsection, we can restrict our search to diagonal matrices. Let
D_{1}\triangleq Diag(d_{1},...,d_{N_{A}})
be a covariance matrix whose trace is equal to
\mathcal{P}_{T}
and which maximizes the
ESR
. As in the previous subsection, construct
D_{i}
and
p_{\text{g}}^{(i)}
. As the channels are Rayleigh faded, no direction preveils on another, so
D_{i}
also maximizes the
ESR
. We construct
\tilde{p}
by time sharing: the time is equally shared among the inputs
p_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\textit{ESR}(\tilde{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{A}}\frac{\textit{ESR}\left(p_{\text{g}}^{(i)}\right)}{N_{A}}=\textit{ESR}\left(p_{\text{g}}^{(1)}\right).

(114)

The covariance matrix of the input
\tilde{p}
is the average of all
D_{i}
’s, which is
\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{I}
. Therefore, by Theorem
5
,
\textit{ESR}(\tilde{p})\leq\textit{ESR}(p^{\star})
, where
p^{\star}
is the Gaussian input with covariance
\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{I}
. As
\textit{ESR}\left(\tilde{p}\right)=\textit{ESR}(p_{\text{g}}^{(1)})
and
p_{\text{g}}^{(1)}
is optimal,
p^{\star}
is also optimal. We have for less noisy on average MIMOME Rayleigh channels:

\displaystyle\textit{ESR}^{\star}=\textit{EPSR}^{\star}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{G}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}}{N_{A}}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G})}\right)\right].

(115)

\mathbf{h}

1/2

VI-B
2
Non-precoded Gaussian input

We first consider a Gaussian input without precoding. Its covariance matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b^{\ast}&c\end{pmatrix},

(131)

where
a,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{C}
. By Sylvester’s criterion, the matrix
\mathbf{K}
must satisfy
a\geq 0
,
c\geq 0
, and
ac\geq|b|^{2}
. In addition, the power constraint imposes
a+c=\mathcal{P}_{T}
. We denote by
\beta=\Re(b)
the real part of
b
.

When the eavesdropper channel realization is
\mathbf{g}_{1}
, the secrecy rate is

R_{s}^{(1)}=\log(1+a)-\log(1+\mathcal{P}_{T}+2\beta),

(132)

while for
\mathbf{g}_{2}
, it becomes

R_{s}^{(2)}=\log(1+a)-\log(1+\mathcal{P}_{T}-2\beta).

(133)

The quantity
\min(R_{s}^{(1)},R_{s}^{(2)})
is maximized when
\beta=0
, in which case both secrecy rates coincide and reduce to

R_{s}=\log(1+a)-\log(1+\mathcal{P}_{T})\leq 0.

(134)

This implies that no non-precoded Gaussian input can achieve a zero
SOP
, and in fact the
SOP
is at least
\frac{1}{2}
.

Moreover, the
ESR
is given by

ESR

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{s}^{(1)}+R_{s}^{(2)}\right)

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the optimization of three secrecy metrics for wireless channels under the assumption that only channel statistics are available at the transmitter. We show that, when the legitimate channel is
uniformly less noisy
than the eavesdropper channel, both the Secrecy Outage Probability (
SOP
) and the Ergodic Positive Secrecy Rate (
EPSR
) are maximized by a non-precoded Gaussian input. The same input distribution also maximizes the Ergodic Secrecy Rate (
ESR
) under the weaker condition that the main channel is
less noisy on average
than the eavesdropper channel.

Reducing the problem to SIMOME channels still yields the same optimal input distribution for the
SOP
and the
EPSR
, regardless of the channel ordering. However, in the absence of assumptions on the number of transmit antennas or on channel ordering, no unique general input distribution maximizes the
ESR
. This implies that
ESR
optimization must be carried out on a case-by-case basis, depending on the specific statistical characteristics of the channels.

In MIMOME channels, assumptions on channel ordering render certain functions involved in the optimization of the secrecy metrics concave, thereby enabling tractable optimization. In SIMOME channels, the optimization of the
SOP
and the
EPSR
relies on the fact that a non-precoded Gaussian input achieves the maximum instantaneous secrecy rate whenever Bob’s channel is stronger than Eve’s, and on the fact that SIMO channels can be totally ordered. Scenarios in which Eve’s channel is stronger are irrelevant in this context, since both the
SOP
and the
EPSR
only capture how large the secrecy rate is when it is positive, rather than how negative it becomes otherwise. For some channels, the non-precoded Gaussian input is not optimal, which shows that the channel ordering assumptions cannot be simply relaxed. Characterizing more general conditions under which the non-precoded Gaussian input is optimal remains an open problem.
