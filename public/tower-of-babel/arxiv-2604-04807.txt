Rank-Based Sparse Regression in Principal Components Space under Measurement Error
Long Feng

Abstract
We study high-dimensional regression in principal components space when the predictors are observed with additive measurement error and the response errors may be heavy-tailed. The starting point is the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -penalized principal-components estimator of 22 , which enjoys a blessing-of-dimensionality phenomenon under predictor contamination but senstive for heavy-tailed data or outliers. We replace the squared loss by a Wilcoxon-type rank loss and then apply a one-step adaptive reweighting scheme to reduce the shrinkage bias of the initial ℓ 1 \ell_{1} fit. The resulting procedure combines robustness to heavy-tailed response errors with the contamination geometry induced by the empirical principal-components basis. Our main theorem gives a prediction bound for the fixed- λ \lambda second-stage fitted mean. Simulations show that the rank-based procedure is competitive under Gaussian noise and substantially more stable under heavy-tailed errors, especially when predictor contamination is present.



Keywords:
principal components regression; measurement error; robust high-dimensional regression; Wilcoxon rank regression.

1
Introduction

Sparsity has been the organizing principle of high-dimensional regression for nearly three decades. The modern literature begins with the lasso of
25
and includes basis-pursuit and Dantzig-selector formulations, together with a large class of folded-concave refinements such as SCAD and MCP; see, among many others,
7
,
5
,
11
,
29
, and
3
. In most of this literature, sparsity is imposed in the original coordinate system of the predictors. That viewpoint is natural and often effective, but it is not the only meaningful structural assumption. Whenever the regression function is expressed after a linear transformation of the covariates, sparsity can also be imposed in the transformed basis. The practical value of such transformed-space sparsity depends on the scientific problem and on how well the transformed coordinates align with the signal.

Principal components provide the most classical transformed basis for regression. Building on that idea,
14
studied
\ell_{1}
-penalized regression in the empirical principal-components space and showed that it can materially improve on classical principal component regression. Related empirical evidence appears in
8
, while
21
provided a broader theoretical analysis of sparse regression in principal-components space under nonsparse signal configurations. These papers emphasize an important distinction: principal-components sparsity is not the same as classical principal component regression, which keeps only a small number of leading components and may miss predictive directions carried by lower-variance components.

A second line of work studies regression with error-contaminated covariates. Measurement error has long been a central topic in statistics; see the monograph of
6
. In high dimensions, most available procedures operate in the original predictor space and correct the estimating equations or loss function by explicitly using the covariance structure of the contamination. Representative examples include the nonconvex approach of
15
, the matrix-uncertainty selector of
19
, and the convex CoCoLasso procedure of
9
. These methods are designed for coefficient estimation in the presence of noisy predictors and are powerful when the contamination model is sufficiently well specified. They are less directly informative when the predictive model is built in a transformed space, or when one wishes to understand the effect of contamination without assuming access to a reliable estimate of the measurement-error covariance.

This issue was recently revisited by
22
, who analyzed
\ell_{1}
-penalized regression in the empirical principal-components space built from contaminated predictors. Under weak sparsity of the regression signal in the latent principal-components basis, they showed that the prediction error can attain the clean-data minimax rate up to an additional perturbation term depending on the eigengap and the contamination level. Their theory also reveals a blessing-of-dimensionality phenomenon: when the number of predictors is large, the price paid for measurement error can diminish rather than increase. That finding is conceptually different from the usual sparse measurement-error regression theory in the original coordinate system.

The estimator of
22
, however, is a classical
\ell_{1}
-penalized least-squares procedure. Squared loss is statistically efficient under light-tailed Gaussian errors, but it can be unstable under heavy-tailed response contamination. Robust high-dimensional variable-selection methods have therefore been developed around alternative loss functions, including quantile and least-absolute-deviation losses
(
2
;
4
;
28
;
12
)
, Huber-type losses
(
10
;
16
;
23
)
, minimum-distance and other robustified criteria
(
17
;
1
;
18
)
, and rank-based procedures. Particularly relevant for the present paper is the Wilcoxon-type rank method of
27
, which combines a tuning-free first-stage rank lasso with a second-stage adaptive bias-reduction step and enjoys both robustness and high efficiency.

The purpose of this paper is to combine these two ideas. We study rank-based sparse regression in empirical principal-components space when the predictors are contaminated by additive measurement error. Our first-stage estimator replaces the squared loss of
22
by the Wilcoxon rank loss while retaining the same empirical principal-components geometry. A second, adaptively weighted stage then follows the local-linear approximation principle of
30
and
27
. The resulting procedure is intended to be robust to heavy-tailed response errors, while preserving the predictor-contamination structure that drives the blessing-of-dimensionality phenomenon in the principal-components model.

In this paper, we provide a two-stage rank-based procedure for sparse regression in principal-components space under measurement error. The theory shows that the second-stage fitted mean inherits the same eigengap-dependent contamination term as in the least-squares analysis of
22
, but improves the estimation term to the oracle order associated with the active principal components. Numerical results demonstrate that the rank-based procedure is especially advantageous under heavy-tailed errors and contaminated predictors.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the model, the rank-based pilot estimator, and the second-stage adaptive refinement. Section
3
presents the assumptions and the main prediction theorem. Section
4
reports simulation results and Section
5
gives a real data application. Section
6
concludes with a brief discussion. Technical proofs are collected in the appendix.

2
Methodology

2.1
Principal-components-space model under contaminated predictors

Write the singular-value decomposition of the latent design matrix as

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

where
m=\min(n,p)
,
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
have orthonormal columns, and
\mathbf{D}
is diagonal with nonincreasing nonnegative entries. Following
22
, we parameterize the latent mean vector in the principal-components basis of
\mathbf{X}
:

\bm{y}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\gamma}^{*}+\bm{\varepsilon},\qquad\bm{\theta}^{*}=n^{-1/2}\bm{\gamma}^{*}.

The vector
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is the weakly sparse parameter of interest. Working on the
\bm{\theta}
-scale is convenient because the natural penalty level is then of order
\sqrt{\log m/n}
.

The latent predictor matrix is unobservable. Instead, we observe

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{W},

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a measurement-error matrix. Let

\mathbf{Z}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}

be the singular-value decomposition of
\mathbf{Z}
, with columns ordered by decreasing singular values. We denote the
i
th row of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
by
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}
and write

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=\sqrt{n}\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}=\sqrt{n}\,\widehat{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}.

All estimators below are computed in this empirical principal-components basis.

Following
22
, a natural benchmark estimator in the empirical principal-components basis is the
\ell_{1}
-penalized least-squares estimator

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\bigl\|\bm{y}-\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\theta}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\right\},\qquad\widehat{\bm{y}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\lambda)=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\lambda).

This estimator exploits weak sparsity in the empirical principal-components space and, in the least-squares framework, leads to the blessing-of-dimensionality phenomenon emphasized by
22
. In particular, the effect of predictor contamination enters through the perturbation of the empirical principal-components basis, and the resulting prediction error is controlled by an eigengap-dependent term.

However, the criterion above is still based on squared loss, and therefore its performance may deteriorate when the model errors are heavy-tailed or when a small fraction of the responses exhibits unusually large fluctuations. This limitation is especially relevant in high-dimensional problems with contaminated predictors, where one would like to retain the principal-components representation of the signal while reducing the sensitivity of the estimation step to extreme observations. These considerations motivate us to replace the least-squares loss by a rank-based loss and to combine it with a bias-reduction step.

2.2
Stage 1: the rank-lasso pilot

Define the pairwise rank loss

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\Big|(y_{i}-y_{j})-(\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j})^{\top}\bm{\theta}\Big|.

(1)

The first-stage estimator is

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}(\lambda_{0})=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})+\lambda_{0}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\right\}.

(2)

This is a convex optimization problem and can be written as a linear program by introducing the usual slack variables for each pairwise residual. The associated fitted mean is

\widehat{\bm{y}}^{(0)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}(\lambda_{0}).

For the pilot penalty level we adopt the simulated calibration of
27
. Conditionally on
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
, generate a random permutation
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})^{\top}
of
(1,\ldots,n)
and define

\bm{\xi}=2\bm{r}-(n+1)\mathbf{1}_{n},\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{\circ}=-\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\bm{\xi}.

Let
G_{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{\circ}\|_{\infty}}
denote the conditional distribution function of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{\circ}\|_{\infty}
given
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
. For fixed constants
c>1
and
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
, set

\widehat{\lambda}_{0}=c\,G^{-1}_{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{\circ}\|_{\infty}}(1-\alpha_{0}).

(3)

The theoretical development below only requires that
\lambda_{0}\asymp\sqrt{\log m/n}
, but (
3
) provides a convenient data-driven calibration in practice.

2.3
Stage 2: adaptive reweighting and HBIC selection

As in
27
, the rank-lasso pilot can be bias-corrected through a local-linear approximation to a folded-concave penalty. Let
p_{\lambda}(t)
be a folded-concave penalty such as SCAD
(
11
)
or MCP
(
29
)
, and let
p_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)
denote its derivative on
(0,\infty)
. The only properties used in the theory are

\text{$p_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)$ is decreasing,}\qquad p_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)\geq a_{1}\lambda\ \text{for }0<t<a_{2}\lambda,\qquad p_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)=0\ \text{for }t>a_{2}\lambda,

(4)

for some constants
a_{1}>0
and
a_{2}>1
. The SCAD penalty function is given by

p_{\lambda}(|\beta|)=\lambda|\beta|\,I(0\leq|\beta|<\lambda)+\frac{a\lambda|\beta|-(\beta^{2}+\lambda^{2})/2}{a-1}\,I(\lambda\leq|\beta|\leq a\lambda)+\frac{(a+1)\lambda^{2}}{2}\,I(|\beta|>a\lambda),

for some
a>2
. The MCP function has the form

p_{\lambda}(|\beta|)=\lambda\left(|\beta|-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2a\lambda}\right)I(0\leq|\beta|<a\lambda)+\frac{a}{2}\lambda^{2}\,I(|\beta|\geq a\lambda),

for some
a>1
. In practice, these two popular choices lead to similar performance.

Given the pilot estimator, define adaptive weights

w_{j}(\lambda)=p_{\lambda}^{\prime}\big(\left\lvert\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{(0)}\right\rvert\big),\qquad j=1,\ldots,m.

The second-stage estimator is

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}(\lambda)\left\lvert\theta_{j}\right\rvert\right\}.

(5)

For fixed weights this is again a convex weighted
\ell_{1}
rank-regression problem.

To choose
\lambda
in practice, let
\Lambda_{1}
be a finite grid. For each
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}
, compute
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}(\lambda)
and its support

A^{(1)}_{\lambda}=\{j:\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{(1)}(\lambda)\neq 0\}.

Then refit the unpenalized rank loss on that support,

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}_{\lambda}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\theta_{(A^{(1)}_{\lambda})^{c}}=0}\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}}),

and define the high-dimensional BIC criterion

\mathrm{HBIC}_{1}(\lambda)=\log\!\Big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}\big(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}_{\lambda};\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\big)\Big\}+|A^{(1)}_{\lambda}|\,\frac{\log\log n}{n}\log m.

(6)

The practical second-stage penalty level is
\widehat{\lambda}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}\mathrm{HBIC}_{1}(\lambda)
, and the corresponding fitted mean is

\widehat{\bm{y}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{HBIC}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}(\widehat{\lambda}).

3
Theory

3.1
Assumptions

Assumption 3.1

(Centering and coherence)
.

The response and the principal-components bases are centered in the sense that
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\bm{y}=0
,
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\bm{0}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}=\bm{0}
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{U}>0
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq k\leq m}\sqrt{n}|U_{ik}|\leq C_{U},\qquad\max_{1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq k\leq m}\sqrt{n}|\widehat{U}_{ik}|\leq C_{U}

with probability tending to one.

Assumption 3.2

(Heavy-tailed response errors)
.

The variables
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are independent and identically distributed, independent of
\mathbf{W}
, and have median zero. Their density
f_{\varepsilon}
is continuous. Let

g(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{\varepsilon}(u)f_{\varepsilon}(u+t)\,du,

which is the density of
\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
at
t
. There exist constants
g_{0}>0
,
L_{g}>0
, and
r_{0}>0
such that

g(0)\geq g_{0},\qquad|g(t)-g(0)|\leq L_{g}|t|,\qquad|t|\leq r_{0}.

No finite-variance assumption is imposed on
\varepsilon_{i}
.

Assumption 3.3

(Sub-Gaussian measurement errors)
.

The latent design matrix
\mathbf{X}
is fixed throughout. The rows of
\mathbf{W}
are independent and identically distributed mean-zero
\tau_{W}^{2}
-sub-Gaussian random vectors. In addition, the squared row norms
\|\mathbf{W}_{i\cdot}\|_{2}^{2}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, are sub-exponential with parameters of the same order as in
22
. The matrix
\mathbf{W}
is independent of
\bm{\varepsilon}
. Let

c_{W}=p^{-1}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{W}_{1\cdot}\|_{2}^{2}.

Let

\tau_{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\qquad A=\left\{k\in[m]:|\theta_{k}^{*}|\geq\tau_{\star}\right\}.

We focus on the nontrivial case
A\neq\varnothing
.
Let
\lambda_{0}\asymp\sqrt{\log m/n}
and define

\Delta_{n,\kappa}=\frac{C_{\Delta}}{\kappa}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

(7)

where
C_{\Delta}
depends only on the sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential constants in Assumption
3.3
. Further let

\eta_{n}=\sqrt{|A|}\,\lambda_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{n}}+R_{q}^{1/2}\tau_{\star}^{1-q/2}+\Delta_{n,\kappa}\frac{\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(8)

Every active eigenvalue of
p^{-1}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
is assumed to be simple and separated from the rest:

\kappa=\min_{j\in A}\min_{\ell\neq j}\left|\zeta_{j}\left(\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right)-\zeta_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right)\right|>0,

(9)

where
\zeta_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the
i
th largest eigenvalue. When defining the columns of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
, choose the sign of
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}
so that
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{u}_{j}\geq 0
for every
j\in A
.

Assumption 3.4

(Signal structure, active eigengap, and tuning)
.

For some fixed
q\in[0,1]
and radius
R_{q}>0
,

\sum_{k=1}^{m}|\theta_{k}^{*}|^{q}\leq R_{q}.

In addition,
\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/n
is bounded away from zero and infinity, where
\bm{y}^{*}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\gamma}^{*}=\sqrt{n}\,\mathbf{U}\bm{\theta}^{*}
.
We also assume

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)=o(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\log n}{\kappa^{2}p}=o(1).

(10)

For sufficiently large constants
C_{\beta}>0
and
C_{\lambda}>0
, the stage-2 penalty level
\lambda
satisfies

\min_{j\in A}|\theta_{j}^{*}|\geq a_{2}\lambda+C_{\beta}\eta_{n},\qquad\lambda\geq C_{\lambda}\eta_{n}.

(11)

3.2
Main result

The first result controls the pilot estimator needed to construct the second-stage adaptive weights.

Theorem 3.1

(First-stage prediction bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
hold. Let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
be any solution of (
2
) with tuning parameter
\lambda_{0}\asymp\sqrt{\log m/n}
. Then, with probability at least
1-C_{1}m^{-C_{2}}
,

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq C|A|\lambda_{0}^{2}+CR_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}+\frac{C\,\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right).

(12)

Moreover,

\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq C\Big(\sqrt{|A|}\,\lambda_{0}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\Big),\qquad\bm{v}_{A}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A})\bm{y}^{*},

(13)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{A}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{A}^{\top}
.

Let

\bar{\bm{\theta}}=\begin{pmatrix}n^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\bm{y}^{*}\\[3.00003pt]
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}

be the oracle target obtained by truncating to the active empirical principal-components subspace. The corresponding oracle estimator is

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\theta_{A^{c}}=0}\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}}).

(14)

To state the oracle reduction precisely, define the pilot separation event

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)=\left\{\min_{j\in A}\left\lvert\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{(0)}\right\rvert>a_{2}\lambda,\quad\max_{k\in A^{c}}\left\lvert\widehat{\theta}_{k}^{(0)}\right\rvert<a_{2}\lambda\right\},

(15)

and the oracle off-support score event

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{there exists }\bm{\Delta}^{(o)}\in\partial\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\text{ with }\bm{\Delta}_{A}^{(o)}=\bm{0},\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{A^{c}}^{(o)}\right\rVert_{\infty}<a_{1}\lambda\end{array}\right\}.

(16)

The next theorem is the deterministic oracle-reduction step. It shows that once the pilot separation event and the oracle score event occur at the same fixed penalty level
\lambda
, the weighted second-stage objective has exactly the same minimizers as the truncated oracle objective.

Theorem 3.2

(Second-stage oracle equivalence)
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
hold, and let the penalty derivative satisfy (
4
). On the event

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda),

the minimizer set of the weighted second-stage problem (
5
) coincides with the minimizer set of the oracle problem (
14
). In particular, every second-stage minimizer is an oracle minimizer, and every oracle minimizer is also a second-stage minimizer.

Lemma
C.3
in the appendix shows that the signal-strength and tuning clause (
11
) in Assumption
3.4
implies

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)\big)\to 1.

Theorem 3.3

(Prediction error of the fixed-
\lambda
second-stage fitted mean)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
hold and let the penalty derivative satisfy (
4
). Then the fixed-
\lambda
second-stage fitted mean

\widehat{\bm{y}}^{(1)}(\lambda)=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}(\lambda)

satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{y}}^{(1)}(\lambda)-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=O_{P}\left\{\frac{|A|}{n}+R_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}+\frac{\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)\right\}.

(17)

In particular, relative to the first-stage rank-lasso rate, the estimation term improves to the oracle order
|A|/n
, while the predictor-contamination contribution remains unchanged.

The bound in (
17
) decomposes naturally into an estimation term and a contamination term. The estimation part
|A|/n+R_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}
is the oracle rate associated with the truncated active principal-components model. The last term is the perturbation price paid for using the empirical principal-components basis extracted from contaminated predictors. Theorem
3.3
therefore shows that the second-stage rank procedure preserves the blessing-of-dimensionality structure identified by
22
, while replacing the first-stage lasso estimation term by its oracle analogue.

4
Numerical Experiments

We compare two convex procedures. The first is the
\ell_{1}
-penalized least-squares estimator in empirical principal-components space from
22
, which we label
L1PCR
. The second is the second-stage Wilcoxon rank estimator (
5
), which we label
RPCR
to match the legends in the figures. Here we only choose the MCP penalty
(
29
)
in our simulation studies.

We consider the same settings as
22
.

Model 1.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be an
n\times p
matrix with independent
N(0,1)
entries, and let
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
be the left and right singular-vector matrices of
\mathbf{M}
. With
m=\min(n,p)
and
A=\{1,\ldots,7\}
, define

\mathbf{X}=a\mathbf{U}_{A}\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}+b\mathbf{U}_{A^{c}}\mathbf{V}_{A^{c}}^{\top},\qquad a=\Big(0.9\,\frac{np}{7}\Big)^{1/2},\qquad b=\Big(0.1\,\frac{np}{m-7}\Big)^{1/2}.

Set

\bm{\theta}^{*}=(0.483,0,0.029,0.019,0,0.126,0.009,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},\qquad\bm{\gamma}^{*}=\sqrt{n}\,\bm{\theta}^{*}.

Thus the signal is concentrated on the first seven principal components, which is the same sparse principal-components design used by
22
.

Model 2.

With the same preliminary random matrix
\mathbf{M}
, let
A=\{m-5,\ldots,m\}
and define

\mathbf{X}=a\mathbf{U}_{A}\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}+b\mathbf{U}_{A^{c}}\mathbf{V}_{A^{c}}^{\top},\qquad a=(\kappa p)^{1/2},\qquad b=(2\kappa p)^{1/2}.

All coordinates of
\bm{\theta}^{*}
are set to
0.003
except the last six, which are
0.009
,
0.125
,
0.003
,
0.019
,
0.029
, and
0.482
. Again
\bm{\gamma}^{*}=\sqrt{n}\,\bm{\theta}^{*}
. This model is more challenging because the important signals lie near the bottom of the principal-components spectrum and the coefficient vector is only weakly sparse.

As in
22
, the observable design is
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{W}
. We consider three contamination regimes:
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{0}
(no measurement error), independent measurement error with
\bm{W}\sim N(0,\mathbf{I}_{p})
, and correlated measurement error with
\bm{W}\sim N(0,\mathbf{\Sigma})
and
\Sigma_{ij}=0.5^{|i-j|}
.

For every configuration we report the average prediction error
n^{-1}\|\widehat{\bm{y}}-\bm{y}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
over 1,000 Monte Carlo replications. The response errors are generated as follows: (i)
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,1)
; (ii)
\varepsilon_{i}\sim t_{3}/\sqrt{3}
so that the
t_{3}
law is standardized to unit variance; and (iii)
\varepsilon_{i}=\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}/\sqrt{10.9}
with
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\sim 0.9N(0,1)+0.1N(0,100)
, which yields a standardized mixture-normal distribution. The latter two settings represent increasingly heavy-tailed response contamination.

Figures
1
and
2
summarize the effect of increasing the number of predictors. The most stable qualitative feature is the same one emphasized by
22
: under predictor contamination, the average prediction error decreases as
p
grows. This is visible for both L1PCR and RPCR in both models and across all three response-error distributions. In other words, the blessing-of-dimensionality phenomenon persists after the squared loss is replaced by the rank loss.

The distinction between the two methods is driven primarily by the tail behavior of the response errors. When there is no measurement error, RPCR is uniformly competitive and typically more accurate than L1PCR, with especially pronounced gains under the standardized
t_{3}
and standardized mixture-normal errors. Once predictor contamination is introduced, the Gaussian setting becomes more nuanced: L1PCR often retains a modest efficiency advantage under normal errors, particularly in the contaminated cases of both models. This is unsurprising because least squares is well aligned with a light-tailed Gaussian response model.

The pattern changes markedly under heavy-tailed errors. Under standardized
t_{3}
errors, RPCR typically improves on L1PCR in the contaminated designs, and the advantage becomes clearer as
p
increases. Under standardized mixture-normal errors, the gains are substantial. Across both models and under both independent and correlated measurement error, the rank-based method yields visibly smaller prediction error, with the gap generally widening at larger dimensions. The improvement is especially strong in Model 2, where the signal is weaker and concentrated near the lower end of the spectrum.

6
Discussion

This paper studies prediction in high-dimensional errors-in-variables regression when the signal is sparse in the empirical principal-components space. We propose a rank-based two-stage estimator that combines an
\ell_{1}
-penalized Wilcoxon pilot with a refitted second stage to reduce shrinkage bias while retaining the same eigengap-controlled contamination term as in the least-squares framework. Both the theory and the simulations show that the proposed method is especially advantageous under heavy-tailed errors, where it is substantially more stable and accurate than least-squares-based procedures.

Several extensions merit further investigation. One natural direction is to extend the present analysis beyond linear models to high-dimensional generalized linear models
(
26
;
13
)
, where the interaction among nonlinear links, rank-based loss functions, and predictor contamination would require new theory. It would also be of interest to study contaminated high-dimensional regression under other transformed predictor bases, such as principal-component-guided sparse regression
(
24
)
, so that the structural assumption can better adapt to the geometry of the signal. These questions are left for future work.

Proposition A.1

(Jaeckel representation)
.

Let
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})^{\top}
be any residual vector. Denote by
R_{i}(\bm{r})
the rank of
r_{i}
among
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(R_{i}(\bm{r})-\frac{n+1}{2}\Big)r_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|r_{i}-r_{j}|.

Consequently, minimizing
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})
is equivalent to minimizing Jaeckel’s dispersion with Wilcoxon scores computed from the empirical principal-components design
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
.

Proof.

Let
r_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq r_{(n)}
be the order statistics. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(R_{i}(\bm{r})-\frac{n+1}{2}\Big)r_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(k-\frac{n+1}{2}\Big)r_{(k)}.

A direct summation-by-parts argument gives

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(k-\frac{n+1}{2}\Big)r_{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(r_{(j)}-r_{(i)})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|r_{i}-r_{j}|.

Dividing by
n(n-1)
only rescales the objective and does not change the minimizer.
∎

Lemma A.1

(Tail bounds for weak sparsity)
.

Let
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}|^{q}\leq R_{q}
with
q\in[0,1]
. For any
\tau>0
, define

B(\tau)=\{k:|a_{k}|\geq\tau\}.

Then

|B(\tau)|\leq R_{q}\tau^{-q},\qquad\sum_{k\notin B(\tau)}a_{k}^{2}\leq R_{q}\tau^{2-q},\qquad\sum_{k\notin B(\tau)}|a_{k}|\leq R_{q}\tau^{1-q}.

Proof.

The cardinality bound follows from
|B(\tau)|\tau^{q}\leq\sum_{k}|a_{k}|^{q}
. The other two bounds follow from

\sum_{k\notin B(\tau)}|a_{k}|^{r}=\sum_{k\notin B(\tau)}|a_{k}|^{q}|a_{k}|^{r-q}\leq\tau^{r-q}\sum_{k\notin B(\tau)}|a_{k}|^{q},

with
r=2
and
r=1
, respectively.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Local quadratic curvature)
.

Let
\xi=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
, where
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are independent copies of
\varepsilon
. Under Assumption
3.2
, there exist constants
0<c_{\ell}<c_{u}<\infty
and
t_{0}\in(0,r_{0}]
such that for all
|t|\leq t_{0}
,

c_{\ell}t^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\big\{|\xi-t|-|\xi|\big\}\leq c_{u}t^{2}.

Proof.

Define
\phi(t)=\mathbb{E}\{|\xi-t|-|\xi|\}
. Since the density of
\xi
is
g
, we have
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)=2g(t)
for
|t|<r_{0}
. By symmetry of
\xi
,

\phi(t)=2\int_{0}^{|t|}(|t|-s)g(s)\,ds.

Using the Lipschitz bound on
g
, choose
t_{0}
so that
g_{0}-L_{g}t_{0}\geq g_{0}/2
and
g_{0}+L_{g}t_{0}\leq 3g_{0}/2
. Then for
|t|\leq t_{0}
,

\frac{g_{0}}{2}t^{2}\leq\phi(t)\leq\frac{3g_{0}}{2}t^{2}.

This gives the result.
∎

Lemma A.3

(Score concentration)
.

Let

\mathbf{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j})\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}).

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, there exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}>C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{n}}\bigg)\leq 2m^{-C_{2}}.

Proof.

Condition on
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
. For each coordinate
k
, the kernel

h_{k}\big((\varepsilon_{i},\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}),(\varepsilon_{j},\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j})\big)=(\widetilde{U}_{ik}-\widetilde{U}_{jk})\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})

is bounded by
2C_{U}
under Assumption
3.1
. Hoeffding’s concentration inequality for bounded U-statistics therefore yields

\mathbb{P}\big(|S_{n,k}|>t\mid\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{cnt^{2}}{C_{U}^{2}}\Big)

for some universal constant
c>0
. A union bound over
k=1,\ldots,m
gives the claim.
∎

Lemma A.4

(Permutation representation of the score)
.

Let
R_{i}
be the rank of
\varepsilon_{i}
among
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
, and define

\eta_{i}=2R_{i}-(n+1),\qquad\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})^{\top}.

Then

\mathbf{S}_{n}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\bm{\eta},\qquad\mathbf{S}_{n,A}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\bm{\eta}.

Conditional on
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
, the vector
\bm{\eta}
is a uniform random permutation of

(-(n-1),-(n-3),\ldots,n-3,n-1)^{\top}.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\right)=\frac{n(n+1)}{3}\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\right).

(18)

Proof.

For each
i
,

\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})=(R_{i}-1)-(n-R_{i})=2R_{i}-(n+1)=\eta_{i}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j})\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\bm{\eta}.

Restricting the coordinates to
A
gives the same formula for
\mathbf{S}_{n,A}
.

Because the errors are i.i.d. with continuous density, every ordering of
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
is equally likely. Hence
\bm{\eta}
is a uniform random permutation of the deterministic centered score vector displayed above. Its coordinates have mean zero, marginal variance
(n^{2}-1)/3
, and pairwise covariance
-(n+1)/3
. This is exactly the matrix formula in (
18
).
∎

Lemma A.5

(Restricted score norm)
.

Let

\mathbf{S}_{n,A}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i,A}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j,A})\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}).

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, there exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}>C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{|A|}{n}}\bigg)\leq 2e^{-C_{2}|A|}.

Proof.

Condition on
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
and write

\mathbf{B}_{A}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{S}_{n,A}=\mathbf{B}_{A}\bm{\eta}

by Lemma
A.4
. Since
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}=\bm{0}^{\top}
, the centering projector in (
18
) acts as the identity on the column space of
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\mathbf{B}_{A}\mathbb{E}(\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{B}_{A}^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n+1)}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}_{A}\mathbf{B}_{A}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n+1)}{3}\cdot\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\mathrm{tr}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{4(n+1)}{3(n-1)^{2}}|A|\leq C\frac{|A|}{n}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\right)\leq C\sqrt{\frac{|A|}{n}}.

Next, the map
f(\bm{z})=\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{A}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}
is convex and Lipschitz with constant

\|\mathbf{B}_{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{n(n-1)}\leq\frac{C}{n^{3/2}}.

Since
\bm{\eta}
is a random permutation of a vector with entries in
[-(n-1),n-1]
, the standard concentration inequality for convex Lipschitz functions on the symmetric group yields

\mathbb{P}\Big(\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}>\mathbb{E}(\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{U}})+t\,\Big|\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\Big)\leq 2\exp(-cnt^{2})

for all
t>0
, where
c>0
is universal. Taking
t=C\sqrt{|A|/n}
and then integrating out
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
proves the claim.
∎

Lemma A.6

(Pairwise design identity)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
satisfy
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=\bm{0}^{\top}
, and let
\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}
denote its
i
th row. Then

\sum_{i\neq j}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})^{\top}=2n\,\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}.

Consequently, for any
\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\big\{(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})^{\top}\bm{\delta}\big\}^{2}=\frac{2}{n-1}\,\bm{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\,\bm{\delta}.

In particular, when
\mathbf{X}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
or
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{A}
,

\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\big\{(\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j})^{\top}\bm{\delta}\big\}^{2}=\frac{2n}{n-1}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

Expand the square and use
\sum_{i}\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{0}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}+\bm{x}_{j}\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}-\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}-\bm{x}_{j}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=2(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-2\sum_{i\neq j}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}

\displaystyle=2(n-1)\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+2\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=2n\,\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}.

The stated consequence follows by contracting with
\bm{\delta}
on both sides.
∎

\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\Big(|\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}+v_{A,i}-v_{A,j}-t_{ij}|-|\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}+v_{A,i}-v_{A,j}|\Big).

At this point we separate the argument into a “clean” rank-regression part and a deterministic approximation-error part. For the clean part we use Knight’s identity

|u-v|-|u|=-v\,\mathrm{sgn}(u)+2\int_{0}^{v}\big\{\mathbf{1}(u\leq s)-\mathbf{1}(u\leq 0)\big\}\,ds.

Applied with
u=\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
and
v=t_{ij}
, this identity isolates the empirical score term
-\bm{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{n}
and a nonnegative curvature remainder. The shift
v_{A,i}-v_{A,j}
is then handled by adding and subtracting the clean increment

|\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}-t_{ij}|-|\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}|.

Using Lemma
A.2
for the quadratic curvature, Lemma
A.6
to rewrite the average of
t_{ij}^{2}
, and Lemma
A.7
to control the approximation error created by
\bm{v}_{A}
, we obtain on an event of probability at least
1-C_{1}m^{-C_{2}}
,

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}+\bm{\delta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})-\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bar{\bm{\theta}};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\geq-\bm{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{n}+\frac{c}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{8}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}-C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(27)

The interpretation is simple: the first term is stochastic, the second is curvature, and the last two terms are the price of localization and projection bias.

Lemma
A.3
gives
\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lambda_{0}/4
on the same event once
\lambda_{0}\asymp\sqrt{\log m/n}
. Insert (
27
) into (
26
). Since
\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{A^{c}}=\bm{0}
, the usual decomposability inequality gives

\left\lVert\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\bar{\bm{\theta}}+\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A^{c}}\right\rVert_{1}.

Also,

|\bm{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{n}|\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{\lambda_{0}}{4}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{c}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\bm{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{n}|+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{8}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{0}\Big(\left\lVert\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\bar{\bm{\theta}}+\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}\Big)+C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\lambda_{0}}{8}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{1}+\lambda_{0}\big(\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A^{c}}\right\rVert_{1}\big)+C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

After grouping the
A
and
A^{c}
coordinates, we arrive at

\frac{c}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A^{c}}\right\rVert_{1}\leq C\lambda_{0}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A}\right\rVert_{1}+C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(28)

This is the usual basic inequality for an
\ell_{1}
-penalized estimator: the inactive coordinates are controlled by the active coordinates plus the approximation error.

Because
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=n\mathbf{I}_{m}
,

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Also,
\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{|A|}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}
. Ignoring the nonnegative term
(\lambda_{0}/2)\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{A^{c}}\right\rVert_{1}
in (
28
) gives

c\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq C\lambda_{0}\sqrt{|A|}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}+C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

A Young inequality yields

\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}\leq C\Big(\sqrt{|A|}\,\lambda_{0}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\Big),

(29)

which is exactly (
13
).

For the prediction error, decompose

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bar{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(30)

Now use the definition of
\bm{v}_{A}
:

\bm{v}_{A}=(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}+(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}.

Hence

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}\left\lVert(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\left\lVert(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(31)

By Lemma
A.8
,

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C\,\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right).

By Lemma
A.1
and the definition
A=\{k:|\theta_{k}^{*}|\geq\tau_{\star}\}
,

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}_{A^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq R_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}.

Substituting these bounds into (
31
) shows that

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq CR_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}+\frac{C\,\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right).

Combining this with (
29
) and (
30
) yields

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq C|A|\lambda_{0}^{2}+CR_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}+\frac{C\,\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right),

which is (
12
).
∎

Appendix C
Oracle lemmas for the second stage

Lemma C.1

(Oracle rate on the truncated model)
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}
be any oracle estimator in (
14
). Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
,

\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{2}=O_{P}\Big(\sqrt{|A|/n}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\Big).

(32)

Consequently,

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=O_{P}\Big(\frac{|A|}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Big).

(33)

Proof.

Set
\bm{\delta}_{o}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}
. Because the oracle optimization is restricted to
A
, we have
(\bm{\delta}_{o})_{A^{c}}=0
. As in the first-stage proof, write

t_{ij}^{(o)}=(\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{o}.

Applying the same Knight-identity decomposition as before, but now without any penalty term, yields the basic oracle inequality

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}+\bm{\delta}_{o};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})-\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bar{\bm{\theta}};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\geq-\bm{\delta}_{o}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{n,A}+\frac{c}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(34)

where
\mathbf{S}_{n,A}
is the restriction of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
to the active coordinates. The first term is the empirical score term, the second is the local quadratic curvature, and the last term is again the approximation error caused by replacing
\bm{y}^{*}
with its empirical projection
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}\bm{y}^{*}
.

Since
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}
minimizes the restricted objective, the left-hand side of (
34
) is nonpositive. Therefore

\frac{c}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}+C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Because
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=n\mathbf{I}_{m}
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Hence

c\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}+C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(35)

Lemma
A.5
gives
\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}=O_{P}(\sqrt{|A|/n})
. Insert this into (
35
) and apply the elementary inequality
ab\leq\eta a^{2}+(4\eta)^{-1}b^{2}
with
a=\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}
,
b=\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{n,A}\right\rVert_{2}
, and a sufficiently small fixed
\eta>0
. We obtain

\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=O_{P}\Big(\frac{|A|}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Big),

which is equivalent to (
32
). Finally, (
33
) follows immediately from

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\delta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

∎

Lemma C.2

(Off-support oracle score)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
, there exists a subgradient vector

\bm{\Delta}^{(o)}\in\partial\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})

with
\bm{\Delta}_{A}^{(o)}=\bm{0}
such that

\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{A^{c}}^{(o)}\right\rVert_{\infty}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|A|+\log m}{n}}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\right).

(36)

Proof.

Let

\bm{\delta}_{o}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}},\qquad\bm{x}_{ij}=\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{j},\qquad\xi_{ij}=\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j},\qquad v_{ij}=v_{A,i}-v_{A,j}.

Because the oracle problem is a convex minimization over the affine space
\{\bm{\theta}:\theta_{A^{c}}=0\}
, there exists at least one KKT subgradient
\bm{\Delta}^{(o)}
with
\bm{\Delta}_{A}^{(o)}=\bm{0}
. Except on a null event of pairwise residual ties, its inactive coordinates can be written as

\Delta_{k}^{(o)}=-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}x_{ij,k}\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\big(\xi_{ij}+v_{ij}-\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{o}\big),\qquad k\in A^{c}.

For a radius
r>0
, define

\mathcal{B}(r)=\{\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\delta_{A^{c}}=0,\ \left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}\leq r\},

and for
k\in A^{c}
set

G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta})=-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}x_{ij,k}\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\big(\xi_{ij}+v_{ij}-\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta}\big).

Then
\Delta_{k}^{(o)}=G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta}_{o})
. Condition on
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
(equivalently on the measurement-error sample): then
\bm{x}_{ij}
,
v_{ij}
, and the radius chosen below are fixed, while all remaining randomness comes from the i.i.d. response errors.

Choose

r_{n}=M\Big(\sqrt{|A|/n}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\Big)

with a sufficiently large fixed constant
M>0
. By Lemma
C.1
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\delta}_{o}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})\big)\to 1.

Hence it is enough to bound
\sup_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})}\max_{k\in A^{c}}|G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta})|
.

The kernel class

\Big\{(i,j)\mapsto x_{ij,k}\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\big(\xi_{ij}+v_{ij}-\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta}\big):\ k\in A^{c},\ \bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})\Big\}

is uniformly bounded by
2C_{U}
and is VC-type, because the only varying part is the sign of a linear threshold in the
|A|
-dimensional parameter
\bm{\delta}
. A standard maximal inequality for bounded VC-type U-processes therefore yields

\sup_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})}\max_{k\in A^{c}}\left\lvert G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta})-\mathbb{E}G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta})\right\rvert=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|A|+\log m}{n}}\right).

(37)

It remains to control the expectations. The density of
\xi_{ij}
is
g
. Since

g(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{\varepsilon}(u)f_{\varepsilon}(u+t)\,du,

Cauchy–Schwarz implies
g(t)\leq g(0)
for all
t
. Therefore, for any real
t
,

\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\mathrm{sgn}(\xi_{ij}+t)\right\rvert=2\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\xi_{ij}\leq-t)-\mathbb{P}(\xi_{ij}\leq 0)\right\rvert\leq 2g(0)\left\lvert t\right\rvert.

Using this and
\left\lvert x_{ij,k}\right\rvert\leq 2C_{U}
, we obtain for every
k\in A^{c}
and
\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\lvert v_{ij}-\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\lvert v_{ij}\right\rvert+\frac{C}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\lvert\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta}\right\rvert.

The first average is bounded by Cauchy–Schwarz:

\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\lvert v_{ij}\right\rvert\leq\left\{\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}(v_{A,i}-v_{A,j})^{2}\right\}^{1/2}\leq Cn^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}.

For the second average, Lemma
A.6
gives

\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\lvert\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta}\right\rvert\leq\left\{\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}(\bm{x}_{ij,A}^{\top}\bm{\delta})^{2}\right\}^{1/2}\leq C\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}.

Hence

\sup_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})}\max_{k\in A^{c}}\left\lvert\mathbb{E}G_{n,k}(\bm{\delta})\right\rvert\leq C\Big(n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}+r_{n}\Big).

(38)

Combining (
37
) and (
38
), and then evaluating at
\bm{\delta}=\bm{\delta}_{o}
on the event
\{\bm{\delta}_{o}\in\mathcal{B}(r_{n})\}
, yields

\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{A^{c}}^{(o)}\right\rVert_{\infty}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|A|+\log m}{n}}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\right),

which is (
36
).
∎

Lemma C.3

(High-probability verification of the oracle events)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
hold, the penalty derivative satisfies (
4
), and the signal-strength and tuning conditions (
11
) hold. Then

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)\big)\to 1.

Proof.

First verify
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)
. By Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
A.8
,

\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq C\left\{\sqrt{|A|}\lambda_{0}+R_{q}^{1/2}\tau_{\star}^{1-q/2}+\Delta_{n,\kappa}\frac{\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}

with probability tending to one. Moreover, (
21
) gives

\max_{j\in A}\left\lvert\bar{\theta}_{j}-\theta_{j}^{*}\right\rvert\leq\Delta_{n,\kappa}\frac{\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}

with probability tending to one. Therefore, on an event whose probability tends to one,

\displaystyle\min_{j\in A}\left\lvert\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{(0)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\min_{j\in A}\left\lvert\theta_{j}^{*}\right\rvert-\max_{j\in A}\left\lvert\bar{\theta}_{j}-\theta_{j}^{*}\right\rvert-\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle>a_{2}\lambda

by the first inequality in (
11
). Also, since
\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{A^{c}}=\bm{0}
,

\max_{k\in A^{c}}\left\lvert\widehat{\theta}_{k}^{(0)}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{\infty}<a_{2}\lambda

by the second inequality in (
11
) after enlarging
C_{\lambda}
if necessary. Hence
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)
occurs with probability tending to one.

Next verify
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)
. Lemma
C.2
yields a KKT subgradient
\bm{\Delta}^{(o)}
with
\bm{\Delta}_{A}^{(o)}=\bm{0}
and

\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{A^{c}}^{(o)}\right\rVert_{\infty}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|A|+\log m}{n}}+n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}\right).

By (
24
),

n^{-1/2}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}=O_{P}\left(R_{q}^{1/2}\tau_{\star}^{1-q/2}+\Delta_{n,\kappa}\frac{\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Since
\lambda_{0}\asymp\sqrt{\log m/n}
,

\sqrt{\frac{|A|+\log m}{n}}\leq C\left(\sqrt{|A|}\lambda_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{n}}\right)\leq C\eta_{n}.

Therefore the second inequality in (
11
) implies

\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{A^{c}}^{(o)}\right\rVert_{\infty}<a_{1}\lambda

with probability tending to one, after increasing
C_{\lambda}
if necessary. This is exactly
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)
.

Combining the two parts proves the lemma.
∎

Appendix D
Proofs of the second-stage results

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Fix any oracle minimizer
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}
. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)
, the adaptive weights satisfy

w_{j}(\lambda)=0,\quad j\in A,\qquad w_{k}(\lambda)\geq a_{1}\lambda,\quad k\in A^{c}.

On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)
, there exists a subgradient vector

\bm{\Delta}^{(o)}\in\partial\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})

such that

\bm{\Delta}_{A}^{(o)}=\bm{0},\qquad|\Delta_{k}^{(o)}|<a_{1}\lambda\leq w_{k}(\lambda)\quad\text{for every }k\in A^{c}.

Let

F_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}(\lambda)|\theta_{k}|

be the second-stage objective. For any
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, convexity of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\cdot;\widehat{\mathbf{U}})
gives

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})-\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\geq(\bm{\Delta}^{(o)})^{\top}(\bm{\theta}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}).

Because
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}_{A^{c}}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{A}^{(o)}=\bm{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})-F_{\lambda}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)})

\displaystyle\geq(\bm{\Delta}_{A^{c}}^{(o)})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{A^{c}}+\sum_{k\in A^{c}}w_{k}(\lambda)|\theta_{k}|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\in A^{c}}\Big\{w_{k}(\lambda)-|\Delta_{k}^{(o)}|\Big\}|\theta_{k}|\geq 0.

(39)

This proves that every oracle minimizer is also a minimizer of the weighted second-stage problem.

Conversely, if
\bm{\theta}
minimizes
F_{\lambda}
, then (
39
) must hold with equality. Because each coefficient in the last sum is strictly positive on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)
, equality forces
\bm{\theta}_{A^{c}}=\bm{0}
. Once the inactive coordinates vanish, the second-stage objective reduces to the unpenalized oracle objective on the active subspace, so
\bm{\theta}
must minimize
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathbf{U}})
subject to
\theta_{A^{c}}=0
. Hence
\bm{\theta}
is an oracle minimizer.

Therefore the minimizer sets of (
5
) and (
14
) coincide.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Lemma
C.3
shows that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda)\big)\to 1.

By Theorem
3.2
, every second-stage minimizer is therefore an oracle minimizer with probability tending to one. Hence it is enough to bound the prediction error of an arbitrary oracle minimizer on the event

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sep}}(\lambda)\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{score}}(\lambda).

On that event,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{y}}^{(1)}(\lambda)-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{3}{n}\left\lVert(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{3}{n}\left\lVert(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(40)

This decomposition is simply the squared norm of

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bm{y}^{*}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}})+(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}-(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}.

Now control the three pieces one by one. Lemma
C.1
yields

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(o)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=O_{P}\Big(\frac{|A|}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\bm{v}_{A}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Big).

(41)

Lemma
A.8
gives, with probability tending to one,

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C\,\left\lVert\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{p}+\frac{n^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{n\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right).

(42)

Finally, because
A=\{k:|\theta_{k}^{*}|\geq\tau_{\star}\}
, Lemma
A.1
implies

\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{A})\bm{y}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}_{A^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq R_{q}\tau_{\star}^{2-q}.

(43)

To remove
\bm{v}_{A}
from (

), use
