Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles using Musca domestica extract as a novel bioresource for therapeutic evaluation
Authors: Mariyam Shafiq, Saiqa Tabassum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGreen synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) using biological resources offers a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical methods. While plants and microbes are commonly employed, insect-derived biomaterials remain largely unexplored. This study investigates Musca domestica L. (houseflies) extract as a novel bioresource for rapid, chemical-free biosynthesis of Ag–NPs, transforming an underutilized organism into a value-added scientific resource.Methods and resultsAg-NP formation was initially indicated by a visible color change and confirmed via UV–Vis spectroscopy, revealing a surface plasmon resonance peak at 475 nm. SEM analysis showed predominantly spherical nanoparticles with an average size of 76 ± 6 nm, while XRD confirmed a face-centered cubic crystalline structure with a crystallite size of 9.72 nm. FTIR analysis identified functional groups associated with proteins, phenolics, and other biomolecules, supporting nanoparticle formation and stabilization. Dynamic light scattering indicated a hydrodynamic size of 258.5 nm (PDI = 0.371), and zeta potential (− 7.98 mV) suggested moderate colloidal stability. In vitro assays demonstrated that the Ag–NPs exhibited notable anti-inflammatory (21.73%), anti-diabetic (36.76%), and antioxidant activity (DPPH: 78.5%; H2O2: 74.94%), with slightly higher performance than AgNO3 and extract controls (p < 0.05, ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD test).ConclusionThis study establishes Musca domestica L. extract as an effective, eco-friendly bioresource for rapid green synthesis of Ag–NPs. The enhanced bioactivity of the biogenic nanoparticles underscores the importance of nanoparticle formulation in therapeutic applications. Future investigations focusing on dose–response, mechanistic insights, and cytotoxicity evaluations are warranted to fully realize their biomedical potential.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04628-0.

Introduction

Background

In recent decades, nanoparticles (NPs) have emerged as a subject of immense importance across multiple scientific disciplines. Their applications have proliferated in various industries, including pharmaceuticals, medicine, cosmetics, construction, and electronics, making them integral to materials science [1]. The growing interest in nanoparticles is driven by their unique properties and potential for innovative applications in these fields. Nanoparticles are distinguished by their nanoscale size, typically ranging from 1 to 100 nm, which results in a high surface area-to-volume ratio, enhanced surface reactivity, and unique physicochemical properties. They also exhibit diverse morphologies, including nanotubes, nanorods, nanoconjugates, and nanowires, which differ significantly from their bulk counterparts [1].

Furthermore, metal NPs have garnered widespread attention owing to their unique properties, including anticancer, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activities, as well as distinctive physicochemical characteristics such as controlled morphology, conductivity, particle size distribution, and optical and magnetic properties [2, 3]. Although various methods exist for synthesizing NPs, physical and chemical approaches are recognized for their energy-intensive nature, environmental toxicity, and high cost. In contrast, biosynthesis, often referred to as "green synthesis," offers an eco-friendly alternative that requires lower energy consumption and financial investment, resulting in biocompatible nanoparticles [4]. Biosynthesis employs a range of biological sources, such as plants, microbes, and algae, to produce metallic and metal oxide nanoparticles, offering a sustainable alternative to traditional synthesis methods [5].

Silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) are widely recognized for their antimicrobial properties and have applications in food preservation, water treatment, agriculture, aquaculture, and various biomedical fields [2, 6, 7]. In addition, Ag–NPs have demonstrated therapeutic potential, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antidiabetic activities [8–10]. These biological effects are generally attributed to their ability to modulate cellular pathways, inhibit key enzymes, and interact with reactive oxygen species, thereby contributing to reduced inflammation, improved metabolic regulation, and protection against oxidative stress [10–12].Studies have also substantiated the therapeutic potential of Ag–NPs, characterizing them as antinociceptive, antiproliferative and antiangiogenic agents [8, 13]. Biosynthesized nanoparticles, particularly silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs), have been shown to exhibit potent anti-inflammatory properties by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, effectively reducing inflammation [9].

Research gap

Traditionally, the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) has predominantly relied on plant and microbial bioresources. Plant-mediated approaches have utilized species such as Moringa oleifera, Azadirachta indica (Neem), Bryophyllum pinnatum, and Citrullus colocynthis, where metabolites including flavonoids, tannins, and proteins act as reducing and stabilizing agents [2, 14, 15]. Microbial systems, including fungi and bacteria, have also been explored for biogenic Ag-NP production, though these processes often require longer incubation periods, precise culture conditions, and post-culture processing [16].

In contrast, the use of animal-derived systems, particularly insects, remains underexplored despite their rich biochemical composition. Musca domestica L. (housefly) was selected in this study based on several practical and mechanistic considerations: its global abundance and ease of collection, minimal maintenance requirements, and the presence of diverse biomolecules, including proteins, enzymes, and metabolites, capable of reducing Ag+ ions and stabilizing nanoparticles [15, 17–19]. The use of whole insect extract provides a complex biochemical environment that supports rapid nanoparticle formation (within 15 min) without the need for additional chemicals, highlighting a chemical-free and efficient synthesis approach.

Compared to plant and microbial systems, insect-based synthesis offers distinct advantages: it avoids lengthy extraction or incubation procedures typical of fungal cultures, eliminates the need for controlled growth media, and leverages a naturally concentrated reservoir of bioactive molecules. Furthermore, insects present fewer ethical constraints and lower operational costs than vertebrate models [20]. By demonstrating that a commonly overlooked organism like Musca domestica L. can serve as a functional bioresource for nanotechnology, this study pioneers an alternative platform for sustainable and scalable nanoparticle synthesis, bridging a gap in unconventional biological sources such as algae and bacteria. However, it is important to note that all biological systems inherently exhibit variability in their biochemical composition, which can influence nanoparticle synthesis and properties. Despite this, plant- and microbe-mediated approaches have been extensively explored, whereas insect-based systems remain comparatively under-investigated. In this context, the present study also reflects a shift in perspective, where an organism commonly regarded as a nuisance and public health concern is explored as a potential source of functional biomaterials for nanotechnology applications.

Research aim

The primary aim of this research was to establish Musca domestica L. extract as a novel and sustainable bioresource for the rapid, chemical-free green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs). This study sought to comprehensively characterize the physicochemical properties of the resulting nanoparticles and systematically evaluate their in vitro biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic effects. Additionally, by directly comparing the biological performance of biosynthesized Ag–NPs with that of silver ions (AgNO3), the work aims to delineate the specific contributions of nanoparticle formulation and surface-associated biomolecules to therapeutic efficacy. Through this approach, the study not only explores an underutilized biological material but also demonstrates its potential as a scalable, value-added platform for innovative nanotechnological and biomedical applications.

Methods

Experimental design, and laboratory setting

Study design

The experimental workflow comprised several sequential stages: (i) collection and taxonomic verification of houseflies, (ii) preparation of aqueous extract, (iii) biosynthesis of Ag–NPs, (iv) detailed physicochemical characterization including UV–Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, SEM, EDX, XRD, DLS, and zeta potential analysis, and (v) in vitro assessment of therapeutic potential. Each step was designed to ensure reproducibility and reliability, with triplicate measurements, multiple runs, and standardized protocols applied throughout. Statistical analysis is conducted to determine significance, compare groups, and interpret data.

Laboratory setting

All experimental procedures were conducted at the SZABIST University, Karachi. Houseflies were collected from the meat, fruit, and vegetable markets in Karachi. The biosynthesis, characterization, and in-vitro biomedical applications of Ag–NPs were performed using advanced laboratory equipment to ensure precision in experimental results. These settings allowed accurate data collection for detailed nanoparticle analysis and facilitated reproducibility for future researchers.

Collection & extract preparation of houseflies

Following a modified protocol by Jakinala et al. [21], 300 adult houseflies (Musca domestica L.) were collected randomly in polythene bags from meat, fruit, and vegetable markets in Karachi. This exploratory approach was intended to assess the practical potential of M. domestica extract without introducing bias from laboratory-reared specimens or chemical additives. Flies were euthanized via asphyxiation, and a subset of 30 specimens was sent to the Department of Zoology, University of Karachi, for taxonomic verification. Macroscopic and microscopic examinations confirmed the species identity.

The collected flies were washed three times with ethanol and deionized water to remove surface contaminants. To preserve the natural biochemical composition, no organic solvent-based extraction (ethanol or methanol) was performed. The flies were then homogenized in deionized water, filtered through the exoskeleton using a sieve, and centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and stored at 4°C for subsequent silver nanoparticle synthesis and in vitro analyses [21, 22].

As this study was exploratory, flies were collected randomly without controlling for gender or developmental stage. While this introduces natural variability in metabolite composition, it reflects real-world conditions and allows a practical evaluation of the extract’s utility. Future studies may employ laboratory-reared flies of defined age and gender to standardize metabolite profiles, optimize nanoparticle synthesis, and enhance reproducibility and mechanistic understanding.

Comparison with ethanolic/methanolic extracts While prior studies have used ethanolic or methanolic extracts of bioresource for nanoparticle synthesis, the present study utilized an aqueous extract to reduce solvent toxicity and improve biocompatibility. Comparative data from previous studies were cited to contextualize our results [21–24]. This demonstrates that aqueous extract can provide comparable reduction and capping efficacy to ethanol/methanol extracts, while maintaining a more environmentally friendly approach.

Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles

The biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles was carried out by mixing the prepared housefly extract with a 1 mM aqueous solution of silver nitrate (AgNO3) at a ratio of 1:9 (extract: AgNO3). A control sample, containing distilled water instead of the extract, was prepared to account for any non-biological reduction of silver ions. The mixture was incubated in a shaking water bath at 70°C for 15 min [25]. The selected temperature and incubation time were based on preliminary optimization trials, which indicated that these conditions promoted efficient reduction of Ag+ ions, as evidenced by a rapid and stable color change from colorless to reddish-brown, without compromising the stability or uniformity of the nanoparticles. The formation of Ag–NPs under these conditions is consistent with previously reported green synthesis protocols, where moderate heat accelerates the bioreduction process without denaturing the bioactive compounds in the extract [21, 22, 26].

Following incubation, the colloidal suspension was visually monitored for the characteristic reddish-brown color, indicating successful nanoparticle formation. The resulting Ag–NPs were stored at 4°C until further physicochemical characterization and in vitro biochemical analyses. This approach ensures reproducibility, as the standardized temperature and time conditions consistently yielded nanoparticles of comparable size and stability across replicate preparations as demonstrated in Tables 1 and 2. Characterization of biogenic nanoparticles

Visual investigation of nanoparticles

Formation of Ag–NPs was first confirmed visually, followed by quantitative UV–Vis spectroscopy using a BIOBASE BK-S360 spectrophotometer over 300–600 nm. Measurements were performed in triplicate across multiple days to ensure reproducibility [21, 22, 25]. A summary of UV–Vis absorbance runs and replicates is provided in Table 1.

Morphological analysis (SEM)

A drop of the colloidal suspension was deposited on a glass coverslip, air-dried, and observed under SEM (JSM-6380) at 35,000 × magnification [3, 12]. Particle shapes and sizes were determined, and size distribution was calculated as σ/μ, providing insight into homogeneity [27]. To avoid confusion with DLS polydispersity index (PDI), this is reported as “size distribution from SEM.”

Structural analysis (XRD)

Dried Ag-NP powder was analyzed using a Bruker D2 Phaser XRD system with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) in Bragg–Brentano geometry (2θ = 10–80°, 1°/min). Crystal planes were assigned using JCPDS Card No. 04-0783. Crystallite sizes were calculated using the Scherrer equation [26].

EDX was performed on SEM-mounted samples over 0–20 keV to determine elemental composition and assess potential contaminants [28].

Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential

DLS and zeta potential were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP in 12 consecutive runs to ensure reproducibility. Samples were dispersed in distilled water and calibrated against standard polystyrene beads [32].

In Vitro biological evaluations of biosynthesized Ag–NPs

All assays were performed at a single concentration of 500 µg/mL. Anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, DPPH radical scavenging, and H2O2 scavenging activities were assessed according to previously published protocols [31, 33–35]. Results were expressed as percentage inhibition or scavenging relative to respective positive and negative controls. A fixed concentration was selected to enable comparative screening across treatments under uniform conditions.

As only one concentration was tested, direct IC50 calculation was not feasible. The single concentration was chosen to provide a preliminary assessment of therapeutic potential and to compare effects with controls (AgNO3 and standard drugs).

Justification for single concentration use In this study, a single concentration of 500 µg/mL was employed for all biomedical assays to provide an initial assessment of the biological potential of the biosynthesized silver nanoparticles. This approach allowed for efficient screening and proof-of-concept evaluation of their activity, conserving resources while confirming that the nanoparticles exhibit measurable effects. However, it is acknowledged as a limitation, as it does not allow determination of IC50 values or a full dose–response profile. Future studies should include multiple concentrations to establish potency and therapeutic windows more accurately.

Anti-inflammatory activity

The anti-inflammatory properties of Ag–NPs were assessed following the protocols of [36] using a protein denaturation assay. Briefly, a working albumin solution was prepared by diluting 2 mL of 100 mg/10 mL PBS stock into 28 mL PBS. For each test sample, 300 µL of working albumin solution was mixed with 400 µL of Ag–NPs solution or Diclofenac sodium as a positive control. Vehicle control tubes contained 400 µL of DMSO. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 10 min, followed by heating at 65°C for 15 min to induce protein denaturation, and then cooled to room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 660 nm using a spectrophotometer, with PBS as a blank. Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Homogeneity of variance was confirmed using Levene’s test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed using IBM SPSS v26. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [4, 31, 33]. To enhance reproducibility, replicate numbers for all assays, UV–Vis measurements, SEM and DLS runs are summarized in Table 2.

Results and discussion

Macroscopic and microscopic morphological characterization of Musca domestica L.

The external morphology of Musca domestica L. was examined both macroscopically and microscopically to confirm specimen identity. As shown in Fig. 1, “Panel a” (dorsal view) revealed large red compound eyes, short aristate antennae with prominent lateral bristles, and a gray thorax marked with four longitudinal black stripes. Wings were positioned dorsally, and the tarsus comprised five tarsomeres with a pretarsus terminating in two claws. “Panel b” (lateral view) showed a single pair of functional forewings that were transparent and membranous with distinct venation, characteristic of Diptera. Verification of desired colour change at 70 °C

The initial qualitative assessment of Ag-NP formation relied on observable color changes, an established preliminary indicator of nanoparticle synthesis [23]. Upon addition of Musca domestica L. extract to a colorless silver nitrate solution and incubation at 70°C in a shaking water bath, the solution gradually changed to a red-brown hue, forming a distinct brown colloid within 15 min (Fig. 3). The observed color change provides a rapid and straightforward verification method, suitable for guiding subsequent quantitative analyses, such as UV–Vis spectroscopy and electron microscopy. Notably, variations in incubation time and temperature may affect the rate and intensity of the color change, suggesting that environmental and procedural parameters play a role in particle formation efficiency.

UV–Vis spectroscopy analysis

The formation of silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) was further confirmed by UV–Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 4). The observed SPR peak at 475 nm confirms the formation of Ag–NPs and falls within the typical range of 400–500 nm reported for green-synthesized silver nanoparticles [25]. This range is attributed to surface plasmon resonance (SPR), a phenomenon arising from the collective oscillation of conduction electrons at the interface between negatively charged electrons and the positively charged metal lattice upon interaction with incident light [25].Fig. 4Absorbance spectrum obtained by UV–Vis spectrophotometer of biosynthesized Ag–NPs with λmax at 475 nm

Absorbance spectrum obtained by UV–Vis spectrophotometer of biosynthesized Ag–NPs with λmax at 475 nm

The well-defined peak confirms the successful reduction of Ag+ ions to metallic Ag0 nanoparticles [32]. The gradual decrease in absorbance on either side of the peak suggests a relatively narrow size distribution, corroborating the morphological data obtained from SEM. The UV–Vis results indicate that the synthesized nanoparticles are predominantly spherical and in the nanoscale range, consistent with the expected optical properties of biogenic Ag–NPs.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis

SEM was employed to directly visualize the morphology and size distribution of the synthesized Ag–NPs (Fig. 5). The images revealed predominantly spherical nanoparticles with diameters ranging approximately from 70 to 82 nm. The average particle size was determined to be 76 ± 6 nm. Similar findings have been reported in several studies, including a study conducted in 2024 [12]. This observed variation of 0.0058 is described here as “size distribution from SEM”, rather than polydispersity index (PDI), since PDI is conventionally derived from dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements [27].Fig. 5SEM image showing the spherical shape and uniform size distribution of Ag–NPs

SEM image showing the spherical shape and uniform size distribution of Ag–NPs

The DLS analysis (Sect. 3.8) reported a hydrodynamic diameter of 258.5 nm with a PDI of 0.371. The larger DLS value compared to SEM is expected, as DLS measures the particle plus its surrounding hydration layer and any potential aggregation in solution, whereas SEM measures dry, individual nanoparticles. These differences highlight that the Ag–NPs, while relatively uniform in their intrinsic size, may form loose aggregates in suspension, contributing to the larger hydrodynamic diameter. Similar results were observed in a 2025 study [3].

The SEM-based size distribution, combined with the UV–Vis SPR peak, suggests that the biosynthesized Ag–NPs are spherical and moderately monodisperse. The relatively narrow size range is likely facilitated by capping and upzing biomolecules present in the housefly extract, which limit uncontrolled particle growth. These findings support the effectiveness of the bio-mediated synthesis route and provide a foundation for correlating nanoparticle size with their biological activities.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD analysis was conducted to evaluate the crystalline nature and phase purity of the biosynthesized Ag–NPs (Fig. 6). The diffraction pattern exhibited prominent peaks at 38.1° (111), 44.3° (200), 64.4° (220), and 77.3° (311), consistent with the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure of metallic silver [12]. These reflections confirm the formation of crystalline Ag–NPs at the nanoscale. The calculated average crystallite size, using the Debye–Scherrer equation, was approximately 9.72 nm, indicating nanocrystalline properties.Fig. 6XRD spectrum of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) from housefly extract

XRD spectrum of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) from housefly extract

Additional minor peaks were observed in the diffraction pattern, which do not correspond to metallic silver. These may arise from residual biomolecules, salts, or other inorganic elements present in the Musca domestica L. extract. Such residues could crystallize during the drying process and contribute to the XRD signal. This phenomenon is commonly reported in biologically synthesized nanoparticles, where capping agents or medium-derived salts introduce extra peaks [32]. The presence of these additional peaks does not compromise the confirmation of Ag–NPs but indicates a complex matrix of biologically mediated nanoparticles.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

The EDX spectrum (Fig. 7) provided a detailed elemental composition of the biosynthesized Ag–NPs. A strong peak at 3 keV confirmed the presence of silver, which accounted for 20.01% of the total mass. The relatively low silver content, compared to theoretical 100% silver, reflects the presence of stabilizing biomolecules and other elements derived from the housefly extract [7]. These capping molecules, including proteins, sugars, and phenolics, coat the nanoparticle surface and are detected by EDX, reducing the apparent silver mass fraction.Fig. 7EDX spectrum showing the elemental composition of Ag–NPs with a dominant silver peak

EDX spectrum showing the elemental composition of Ag–NPs with a dominant silver peak

Other elements detected included C, O, Na, Mg, Al, Ca, and Au. Carbon and oxygen are expected, as they arise from organic biomolecules present in the housefly extract. Sodium and magnesium may be derived from salts or mineral components within the extract. Trace amounts of aluminum, calcium, or gold could result from environmental exposure of the flies, sample preparation, or the substrate used for SEM imaging. These findings align with previous reports of biologically synthesized nanoparticles where non-metallic residues and elemental traces are commonly observed [13].

The combined XRD and EDX analyses confirm the successful synthesis of crystalline Ag–NPs, stabilized by a complex biological matrix from Musca domestica L. extract. These analyses highlight that the nanoparticles are not purely metallic but are hybrid nanostructures containing both silver cores and capping biomolecules, which likely contribute to their biological functionality.

Biomolecule Identification via Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (Comparative study)

The FTIR spectrum of the Musca domestica L. extract revealed characteristic absorption bands corresponding to diverse biomolecular functional groups, which were also observed in the biosynthesized Ag–NPs spectrum with notable variations (Fig. 8 a and b), summarized in Table 3. In the extract, a broad band in the range of 3700–3200 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups present in phenolics, proteins, and carbohydrates. A less intense band in the 2800–2600 cm−1 region corresponds to C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic chains in sugars and other organic molecules [1]. The band around 1651–1631 cm−1 is assigned to C=O stretching of carbonyl groups and aromatic structures [3], while the region between 1365 and 1030 cm−1 represents C–N, C–O, and C–O–C stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of amines, alcohols, ethers, and reducing sugars [30].Fig. 8FTIR spectra of a biosynthesized Ag–NPs and b Musca domestica L. extract, showing functional group variations associated with nanoparticle formation

FTIR spectra of a biosynthesized Ag–NPs and b Musca domestica L. extract, showing functional group variations associated with nanoparticle formation

In comparison, the biosynthesized Ag–NPs spectrum retained these functional group signatures but exhibited shifts in peak positions and changes in intensity, indicating interactions between silver ions and biomolecules during nanoparticle formation. The broad O–H/N–H band around 3273 cm−1 appeared more intense and slightly shifted, suggesting enhanced hydrogen bonding and possible involvement of hydroxyl- and amine-containing compounds in nanoparticle synthesis [3]. Such changes may also be influenced by environmental factors such as pH and local ionic conditions during synthesis, which can alter hydrogen bonding and biomolecular conformation.

The bands at 1637 cm−1 and 1344 cm−1 showed slight shifts relative to the extract, indicating potential interaction of carbonyl and aromatic groups with Ag+ ions. These groups may contribute to electron transfer processes required for reduction, although this cannot be conclusively established from FTIR alone. Increased intensity in the 1023 cm−1 region further suggests coordination of C–O and C–N containing biomolecules with the nanoparticle surface, consistent with a stabilizing (capping) function [7, 18].

A distinct band at 430 cm−1 (Fig. 8a), absent in the pure extract (Fig. 8 b), is attributed to Ag lattice vibrations, confirming the formation of silver nanoparticles and their interaction with oxygen-containing functional groups. The appearance of this band, along with peak shifts and broadening, supports the occurrence of biomolecule-mediated reduction and stabilization processes [28].

Zeta size and potential

The hydrodynamic size of the biosynthesized Ag–NPs was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and found to be 258.5 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.371 (Fig. 9a). This hydrodynamic size is substantially larger than the size observed in SEM analysis (76 ± 6 nm), which represents the core metallic nanoparticles [43]. Similar findings have been reported in the literature, with [28] determining the average hydrodynamic size of Ag–NPs to be 231.8 nm. The discrepancy arises because DLS measures the effective diameter of particles in suspension, including the hydration layer and any adsorbed biomolecules from the housefly extract that act as capping agents. Furthermore, partial aggregation in aqueous media can contribute to a larger observed size in DLS measurements.Fig. 9a Zeta size distribution of Ag–NPs synthesized using housefly extract, showing an average hydrodynamic size of 258.5 nm with a PDI of 0.371. b Zeta potential distribution indicating a surface charge of − 7.98 ± 5.93 mV

a Zeta size distribution of Ag–NPs synthesized using housefly extract, showing an average hydrodynamic size of 258.5 nm with a PDI of 0.371. b Zeta potential distribution indicating a surface charge of − 7.98 ± 5.93 mV

The PDI of 0.371, while below 0.5, indicates moderate polydispersity, reflecting some size variation in the colloidal suspension [22, 44]. In comparison, the “size distribution from SEM” suggests relatively uniform individual nanoparticles but does not account for the surrounding hydration layer or aggregation in solution. This comparison emphasizes the importance of using both imaging and hydrodynamic measurements to fully characterize nanoparticle populations.

The zeta potential of the Ag–NPs was measured at − 7.98 mV (Fig. 9b). Although a negative value suggests some degree of electrostatic repulsion, it is relatively low compared to the ± 30 mV threshold typically considered necessary for long-term colloidal stability [44]. Therefore, the nanoparticles may possess moderate short-term stability, sufficient for the current in vitro assays, but might be prone to aggregation over extended storage or under varying physicochemical conditions. This moderate stability likely results from a combination of electrostatic repulsion and steric stabilization provided by biomolecules from the extract, such as proteins and polysaccharides [32].

Overall, the hydrodynamic size and zeta potential measurements provide complementary insights: while SEM confirms the small nanoscale dimensions and uniform shape of individual Ag–NPs, DLS reflects the behavior of biologically capped nanoparticles in solution, highlighting the influence of the biomolecular corona on particle size and stability. These observations underline the importance of the biosynthesis approach, where extract components contribute both to nanoparticle formation and colloidal stabilization [37].

For anti-diabetic activity, evaluated via α-amylase inhibition, Ag–NPs exhibited 36.76% ± 0.97%b inhibition, slightly higher than AgNO3 (34.99% ± 0.68%b) and significantly higher than the extract (2.57% ± 0.60%c) and DMSO (0.00%c). Glucophage, the standard drug, showed the highest inhibition (62.04% ± 1.06%^a^) (Table 1). One-way ANOVA demonstrated significant differences among treatments (F4,10 = 67.84, p < 0.001), and Tukey HSD confirmed that Ag–NPs were significantly more effective than the extract (p < 0.05), though less active than Glucophage. This indicates that the nanoparticulate form enhances α-amylase inhibition compared to the crude extract, suggesting potential applications in managing postprandial hyperglycemia [38, 44, 45].

Antioxidant potential was assessed using DPPH and H2O2 radical scavenging assays. In the DPPH assay, Ag–NPs exhibited 78.5% ± 3.2%a scavenging activity, significantly higher than AgNO3 (37.1% ± 4.3%b) and the extract (45.0% ± 0.1%b), but slightly lower than vitamin C (90.0% ± 4.2%a) (F4,10 = 109.42, p < 0.001). Tukey HSD analysis confirmed that Ag–NPs’ scavenging activity was significantly greater than both AgNO3 and the extract (p < 0.05), suggesting that surface-bound biomolecules and nanoscale structure synergistically enhance free radical neutralization [33].

Similarly, in the H2O2 scavenging assay, Ag–NPs demonstrated 74.94% ± 2.8%b inhibition, markedly higher than AgNO3 (36.71% ± 0.66%c) and the extract (24.27% ± 1.7%c), though slightly lower than ascorbic acid (85.76% ± 1.3%a) (F4,10 = 18.95, p < 0.001). Tukey HSD analysis confirmed that Ag–NPs were significantly more active than both AgNO3 and the extract (p < 0.05), reinforcing the superior radical scavenging capacity of the nanoparticulate form [31].

Overall, biosynthesized Ag–NPs exhibited moderate anti-inflammatory (21.73%), anti-diabetic (36.76%), and antioxidant activities (DPPH: 78.5%; H2O2: 74.94%), consistently surpassing AgNO3, housefly extract and other controls, while standard drugs and antioxidants remained the most active, validating the assay methodology. These findings underscore the therapeutic advantage of the nanoparticulate form and support the potential of Musca domestica-mediated Ag–NPs as multifunctional bio-nanomaterials for biomedical applications.

Discussion

The biosynthesis of Ag–NPs using Musca domestica L. extract represents a complex biologically mediated redox system in which diverse insect-derived metabolites collectively govern nanoparticle formation and stabilization.

The UV–Vis spectral analysis further confirms nanoparticle formation, with an SPR peak observed at 475 nm within the typical range of 400–500 nm for green-synthesized Ag–NPs [25]. Comparable studies have reported SPR peaks at 463 nm (Justicia adhatoda), 488 nm (Equisetum arvense), and 445 nm (Withania coagulans), indicating that the optical response is strongly influenced by the biological source [25]. The slight red-shift observed in the present study may be attributed to biomolecule-induced changes in the local dielectric environment and mild interparticle interactions, which increase the effective particle size [32]. These findings emphasize that nanoparticle optical properties are governed not only by size but also by interfacial chemistry and surface-bound organic layers [34, 46]. The physicochemical characteristics, including moderate dispersity (PDI = 0.385) and the discrepancy between primary particle size and hydrodynamic diameter, indicate the formation of a biomolecular corona and soft agglomeration in aqueous environments [3]. Such behavior is widely reported in green-synthesized nanoparticles and is attributed to the adsorption of organic constituents rather than actual particle growth [37]. This suggests that the nanoparticles exist as hybrid organic–inorganic systems, where surface-bound biomolecules significantly influence colloidal behavior and functionality.

The colloidal stability of the synthesized nanoparticles appears to be primarily governed by steric rather than electrostatic effects. The relatively low zeta potential, together with the increased hydrodynamic size, suggests that stabilization is achieved through a biomolecular coating that limits aggregation via physical hindrance [15, 37]. This interpretation is further supported by structural characterization, where additional diffraction features and non-silver elemental signals are more plausibly attributed to residual biomolecules or environmental constituents associated with the houseflies’ habitat rather than impurity-driven nanoparticle formation [19, 43]. While such hybrid structures introduce complexity compared to chemically synthesized nanoparticles, they also provide enhanced interfacial functionality.

The biological activities observed in this study reflect a combined contribution from both nanoparticle-specific properties and dissolved silver ions. Although Ag–NPs exhibited slightly higher activity than AgNO3, the difference was not significant, indicating that ionic silver plays a substantial role in the observed effects. This is particularly evident in the anti-diabetic assay, where comparable inhibition suggests that enzyme interaction is largely governed by Ag+ ions, consistent with previous findings [27, 37]. These results indicate that nanoparticle-mediated and ionic mechanisms operate concurrently, with nanoparticles potentially enhancing local interactions while ion release contributes to biochemical reactivity.

The anti-inflammatory activity observed is comparatively lower than that reported in some literature, highlighting the influence of synthesis route and capping composition on biological performance. Mechanistically, Ag–NPs are known to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and TNF-α, inhibit hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), and downregulate cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression [9]. Additionally, inhibition of enzymes such as cyclooxygenase and nitric oxide synthase reduces the production of inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins and nitric oxide [45]. The moderate activity observed in this study may therefore be attributed to physicochemical factors such as particle heterogeneity, surface coverage by biomolecules, and limited accessibility of active sites [33].

The antioxidant activity of Ag–NPs (78.5% DPPH scavenging) aligns with previously reported values, although variability across studies reflects differences in synthesis conditions and concentrations. Importantly, the FTIR analysis provides molecular-level evidence supporting the observed ROS scavenging behavior. The broad O–H/N–H band (3700–3200 cm−1) and intensified C–N/C–O functional groups (1365–1030 cm−1) indicate the presence of phenolic, hydroxyl, and proteinaceous biomolecules on the nanoparticle surface, which are well known for their electron- and hydrogen-donating capacity [24, 31]. The slight shift in the C=O/C=C region (~ 1651–1631 cm−1) further suggests their involvement in redox interactions and surface coordination [30].

These functional groups facilitate the neutralization of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide ions, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals, through electron transfer and hydrogen donation mechanisms [12, 33]. Thus, the antioxidant performance can be attributed to a coupled mechanism involving both the metallic core and the biomolecular corona, where the nanoparticle surface acts as a reactive interface while the associated biomolecules enhance radical scavenging efficiency. Such ROS-scavenging activity plays a critical role in reducing oxidative stress and preventing biomolecular damage [46].

The anti-diabetic activity (36.73%) further supports the biomedical potential of biosynthesized Ag–NPs and can be attributed to the inhibition of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes such as α-amylase and α-glucosidase [38]. By limiting the breakdown of complex carbohydrates into glucose, these nanoparticles contribute to glycemic control. In addition, nanoparticles have been reported to exhibit insulin-mimetic behavior, enhance glucose uptake, and regulate oxidative stress associated with diabetes progression [38]. However, the similar activity observed between Ag–NPs and AgNO3 suggests that ionic silver is a dominant contributor in this system, reinforcing the importance of considering dissolution dynamics in interpreting biological effects.

The synthesis conditions, particularly the use of housefly extract, controlled temperature, and short reaction duration, demonstrate a measurable relationship with the physicochemical properties of the Ag–NPs. SEM analysis indicated an average particle size of ~ 76 nm, while the PDI value of 0.371 suggests a moderately broad size distribution, consistent with typical DLS observations for biologically synthesized systems. XRD analysis revealed a much smaller crystallite size (~ 9.72 nm), indicating that each nanoparticle observed in SEM is likely composed of multiple crystalline domains; this discrepancy arises because XRD measures coherent diffraction domains, whereas SEM captures the overall particle size, including aggregated or polycrystalline structures. A similar difference between SEM and DLS measurements can be attributed to DLS reporting the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes the solvation layer and surface-bound biomolecules, often resulting in larger apparent sizes. FTIR analysis supports these findings by identifying functional groups (e.g., O–H, C=O, C–N) associated with biomolecules that act as reducing and capping agents, contributing to nanoparticle stabilization and influencing interfacial interactions. Collectively, the SEM–DLS–XRD–FTIR correlations suggest that the green synthesis approach produces moderately dispersed, polycrystalline nanoparticles with biologically functionalized surfaces, where size heterogeneity and surface chemistry may influence their overall behavior and activity in a realistic manner [3, 15, 37].

Overall, this study highlights the potential of insect-derived extracts as novel bioresources for nanoparticle synthesis. The use of Musca domestica L. introduces a unique biochemical environment that influences nanoparticle formation, interfacial chemistry, and biological performance. Unlike extensively studied plant-based systems, this approach contributes to expanding the scope of green nanotechnology. However, variability in biological composition remains a challenge, potentially affecting reproducibility and scalability [47]. Future studies should focus on standardization of extract composition, detailed investigation of silver ion release, and advanced biological evaluation to establish clearer structure–function relationships and enhance translational applicability.

Future directions and recommendations

Future investigations should aim for a more detailed mechanistic understanding of nanoparticle formation by isolating and characterizing the specific biomolecular fractions responsible for reduction and capping. While the present findings suggest a synergistic role of proteins, phenolics, and other metabolites, the relative contribution of each component remains unclear. Advanced analytical approaches, such as proteomic and metabolomic profiling, could identify key functional molecules and elucidate their binding interactions with metal ions, enabling more controlled and reproducible synthesis pathways.

To strengthen the biological interpretation of these findings, systematic dose–response evaluations are necessary to determine IC50 values and establish concentration-dependent effects. This would facilitate rigorous comparison with existing nanomaterials and conventional agents, while clarifying whether observed activities are primarily nanoparticle-driven or influenced by residual ionic silver or surface-associated biomolecules.

Standardization of the biological source represents another critical requirement. Variations in metabolite composition due to age, diet, or physiological state of Musca domestica L. may significantly influence nanoparticle characteristics. The use of controlled, lab-reared specimens under defined conditions could minimize batch-to-batch variability and enhance reproducibility, a recognized limitation in bio-mediated synthesis systems.

Further studies should include cytotoxicity and biocompatibility assessments using relevant mammalian cell models to define safety profiles, which is essential if the biomedical potential of these nanoparticles is to be claimed. In parallel, stability optimization through surface engineering, such as controlled functionalization or selective purification of capping layers, may improve colloidal stability and enable more predictable interactions in biological environments.

In agricultural contexts, biologically synthesized nanoparticles are increasingly explored as alternatives to conventional plant disease management strategies. Compared to traditional agrochemicals, nanoparticle-based systems offer enhanced surface reactivity, improved tissue penetration, and the potential for targeted or sustained delivery of active agents. These properties are particularly relevant for managing plant pathogens, as nanoscale interactions can disrupt microbial membranes, interfere with viral replication, or modulate plant defense responses [45]. Among the most studied nanomaterials in plant systems are silver, zinc oxide, silica, and chitosan-based nanoparticles, chosen for their antimicrobial efficacy and relative biocompatibility [4].

Moreover, large-scale production, cost-effectiveness, and consistency of biologically synthesized nanoparticles remain major barriers. Environmental considerations, such as nanoparticle accumulation, potential phytotoxicity, and impacts on soil microbiota, also require thorough evaluation. Regulatory frameworks for nanoparticle use in agriculture are underdeveloped in many regions, further complicating widespread adoption [46].

Scaling up bio-mediated nanoparticle synthesis continues to be a significant challenge. Unlike chemical synthesis routes, biological systems inherently introduce variability affecting particle size, morphology, and functional performance. The development of controlled bioprocessing systems, together with standardized extraction and synthesis protocols, will be essential for achieving reproducibility at an industrial scale. Life-cycle assessment and sustainability analysis will also be crucial to validate the environmental and economic feasibility of these systems compared to conventional technologies.

Conclusion

Silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) were successfully synthesized using houseflies extract as a novel bioresource via a rapid and green approach. The present study demonstrates that Musca domestica L. extract can serve as a novel and effective bioresource for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs). The nanoparticles exhibit well-defined crystalline structures, moderate colloidal stability, and surface functionalization by biomolecules, as confirmed through UV–Vis, SEM, FTIR, Zeta Potential. DLS, and XRD analyses. Mechanistic insights suggest that proteins, phenolics, and other metabolites act synergistically to reduce Ag+ ions and stabilize the resulting nanoparticles, while FTIR-identified functional groups provide molecular-level justification for their potent antioxidant activity.

Functionally, the biosynthesized Ag–NPs exhibit promising anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-diabetic activities. Although ionic silver contributes to some observed effects, the surface-bound biomolecules and nanoparticle interface likely enhance overall performance, highlighting the importance of hybrid organic–inorganic structures. These findings underscore the potential of insect-mediated nanoparticle synthesis as a sustainable and versatile platform for biomedical applications.

Future work focusing on cytotoxicity evaluation, controlled synthesis, and detailed mechanistic studies will be critical to fully harness the therapeutic potential of these biogenic nanoparticles. Beyond biomedical applications, the insights gained here may inform the design of bio-mediated nanomaterials for agricultural, environmental, and industrial applications, expanding the scope of green nanotechnology while maintaining safety and efficacy.
