Surface-related factors affecting bacterial adhesion on dental implants: a short literature review
Authors: Sunyoung Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental implants offer a predictable solution for tooth replacement, yet peri-implant diseases, including peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis, remain a significant clinical concern and are primarily driven by bacterial biofilm formation on implant surfaces. The unique anatomy of peri-implant tissues, characterized by the absence of a periodontal ligament and distinct soft tissue attachment, increases susceptibility to microbial colonization. Bacterial adhesion is a dynamic process initiated by reversible attachment to the acquired pellicle, followed by irreversible binding via bacterial adhesins and the production of extracellular polymeric substances, leading to biofilm maturation. Implant surface topography and physicochemical properties strongly influence these processes. While rough surfaces facilitate bacterial retention by providing protective niches, polished transmucosal surfaces reduce plaque accumulation and improve cleanability. Differences in surface properties between titanium and zirconia have been reported, with zirconia showing a tendency for lower bacterial adhesion in vitro, although clinical outcomes are shaped by multiple factors beyond surface characteristics. Integrating knowledge of implant topography, material-dependent surface properties, and microbial behavior is essential for guiding implant design, minimizing biofilm formation, and promoting long-term peri-implant tissue health.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants have become a routine and highly successful treatment modality for the replacement of missing teeth, with high long-term survival rates.123 Despite these favorable outcomes, biological complications, particularly peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis, remain a major clinical concern.456 Epidemiological studies indicate that peri-implant mucositis affects a large proportion of implant patients, and a considerable subset progresses to peri-implantitis, which is characterized by progressive bone loss and may ultimately compromise implant survival.789

Bacterial biofilm formation on implant surfaces is recognized as the primary etiological factor in peri-implant diseases.1011 Although the inflammatory process shares similarities with periodontitis, the peri-implant environment differs fundamentally from that of natural teeth. The absence of periodontal ligament fibers and the distinct nature of peri-implant soft tissue attachment may reduce intrinsic defense mechanisms against bacterial challenge, rendering implant surfaces particularly susceptible to microbial colonization.1213

In this context, implant surface characteristics have received increasing attention as critical determinants of bacterial adhesion. While a dental implant system consists of an implant embedded in bone and an abutment exposed to the oral environment, the abutment serves as the primary interface for bacterial colonization and peri-implant tissue interactions. Consequently, both the surface properties and the intrinsic material characteristics of abutments play an important role in bacterial adhesion. Titanium has long been considered the gold standard for dental implants due to its excellent biocompatibility and mechanical strength.1415 Recently, zirconia has emerged as a viable alternative, motivated by esthetic demands and favorable soft tissue responses.1617

This narrative review aims to provide an overview of evidence on surface-related factors affecting bacterial adhesion on dental implants. It integrates findings on surface topography and physicochemical characteristics, with particular attention to differences among implant and abutment materials, to develop a comprehensive framework for understanding bacterial-implant interactions, with particular emphasis on the relative clinical relevance of surface-related factors.

This narrative literature review was conducted to summarize current evidence on surface-related factors affecting bacterial adhesion on dental implant materials, with a focus on titanium and zirconia. Literature searches were performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. The search was carried out using combinations of keywords including “dental implants,” “titanium,” “zirconia,” “bacterial adhesion,” “biofilm formation,” “surface roughness,” “surface chemistry,” “surface charge,” “surface free energy,” and “wettability.” No publication date restrictions were applied for sections addressing general mechanisms of bacterial adhesion and surface-related factors in order to include both classical and foundational studies. For the comparative section focusing on titanium versus zirconia, only in vitro, in situ, and in vivo experimental studies published from January 2016 onwards were included to ensure the synthesis of recent and methodologically comparable evidence. Studies were included if they investigated bacterial adhesion, biofilm formation, or microbial colonization on dental implant surfaces, particularly titanium and zirconia. Both experimental and relevant clinical studies were considered. Articles not written in English, studies not related to implant materials, or studies not addressing microbial outcomes were excluded.

BACTERIAL ADHESION MECHANISMS ON DENTAL IMPLANTS

Bacterial adhesion to dental implant surfaces is a dynamic and multifactorial process that plays a central role in the initiation of peri-implant diseases.18192021 This process begins immediately after implant placement or exposure to the oral environment, where the material surface is rapidly coated with a conditioning film known as the acquired pellicle.222324 It is composed of salivary proteins, glycoproteins, lipids, and other macromolecules,2526 and mediates initial bacterial adhesion.2728

The initial stage of bacterial adhesion is typically described as reversible adhesion.2930 In this phase, bacterial cells approach the implant surface and are influenced by long-range physicochemical forces, commonly described within the framework of DLVO theory, including van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic effects.3132 The net outcome of these interactions determines whether bacteria remain transiently associated with the surface or are repelled. Because these forces are relatively weak, bacteria can still be detached by salivary flow, mastication, or oral hygiene measures at this stage.

If conditions are favorable, reversible adhesion becomes irreversible adhesion. This stage is characterized by the establishment of stronger, more specific interactions between bacterial cell surface structures and receptors within the acquired pellicle.33 Bacteria express a variety of adhesins, including fimbriae, pili, and non-fimbrial proteins, which facilitate binding to host-derived molecules such as mucins, proline-rich proteins, and statherin.34353637 These molecular interactions anchor the bacteria firmly to the surface, making detachment significantly more difficult. Concurrently, bacterial cells begin to produce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), a complex matrix composed of polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids.38 EPS not only stabilizes the adhesion of individual bacteria but also promotes cohesion between neighboring cells.3940

Following stable attachment, the process of biofilm formation and maturation begins. Early colonizers, predominantly Gram-positive facultative anaerobes such as streptococci, initially dominate the biofilm.41 These pioneer species modify the local microenvironment by consuming oxygen and producing metabolic byproducts, thereby creating favorable conditions for the colonization of secondary and late colonizers, including Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria.42 Interbacterial interactions, such as co-adhesion and co-aggregation, play a crucial role in this transition.4344 Additionally, bacteria communicate through quorum sensing, regulating gene expression in response to population density and facilitating coordinated biofilm development.45 As the biofilm matures, it becomes increasingly structured and heterogeneous, with the formation of microcolonies and water channels that allow for nutrient distribution and waste removal.46 The EPS matrix, which stabilizes these structures, acts as a protective barrier, enhancing bacterial resistance to antimicrobial agents and host immune responses.4748 This increased resilience is a key factor in the persistence of biofilms on dental implant surfaces and contributes to the pathogenesis of peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis.

From a structural standpoint, a dental implant system can be broadly divided into two components: an implant and an abutment. The implant is embedded within the alveolar bone, whereas the abutment connects the implant to the prosthetic restoration and extends through the soft tissue into the oral cavity.49 Among these components, the transmucosal abutment is exposed to the oral microbial environment under normal conditions. As a result, it serves as a major site for bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation. Continuous exposure to saliva, dietary substrates, and diverse oral microorganisms makes the abutment particularly susceptible to microbial colonization.

In contrast, the implant is typically isolated from the oral environment by the surrounding bone and soft tissue, thereby limiting bacterial access under healthy conditions. However, this protective barrier is often compromised under both pathological and non-pathological conditions. In pathological conditions such as peri-implantitis, inflammatory processes can lead to bone resorption and soft tissue breakdown, thereby exposing the implant surface to the oral environment.91050 Similarly, non-pathological or anatomical factors, such as peri-implant mucosal recession or improper implant positioning, may also result in partial exposure of the implant.5152 Upon exposure, the implant surface becomes susceptible to bacterial adhesion and subsequent biofilm formation, as observed on the abutment.

Given these considerations, it is essential to understand not only the biological mechanisms of bacterial adhesion and biofilm maturation but also the role of implant surface characteristics in these processes. Surface-related factors, including roughness, surface chemistry, and surface energy, can significantly influence the initial attachment of bacteria and the subsequent development of biofilms.24535455 A comprehensive understanding of these interactions is crucial for the design and optimization of dental implant surfaces aimed at minimizing bacterial colonization and improving long-term clinical outcomes.

SURFACE TOPOGRAPHY AND ROUGHNESS

Surface topography is one of the most extensively investigated factors influencing bacterial adhesion on dental implants, with roughness suggested to play an important role in the initial colonization and subsequent biofilm formation.56575859 A commonly cited concept in this field is Bollen’s threshold roughness, which proposes that a surface roughness of approximately 0.2 µm may represent a critical level for bacterial retention.60 Below this value, further reductions in roughness appear to have limited additional effects on bacterial adhesion, whereas above this level, microbial accumulation tends to increase. The underlying mechanism behind this phenomenon is closely related to the physical characteristics of the surface. Rough surfaces provide irregularities that serve as protective niches for bacterial cells.53 These features reduce the effect of shear forces generated by saliva flow, mastication, and oral hygiene procedures, thereby facilitating bacterial retention. In addition, surface irregularities increase the available surface area for bacterial attachment and enhance the likelihood of mechanical interlocking between bacterial cell walls and the implant surface.61 These conditions promote the transition from reversible to irreversible adhesion, ultimately accelerating biofilm formation. This relationship between increased surface roughness and enhanced microbial colonization has been consistently demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo studies.6263646566676869

However, the threshold roughness should be interpreted with caution, as surface roughness alone does not fully capture the complexity of surface topography relevant to bacterial adhesion. The most commonly used roughness parameter, Ra, defined as the arithmetic average of the absolute height deviations from the mean surface plane, provides information only on vertical height variations and therefore fails to adequately capture spatial and functional aspects of surface topography that are directly relevant to bacterial retention. Moreover, surfaces with similar Ra values may exhibit markedly different spatial distributions, peak-valley geometries, and three-dimensional architectures, all of which can influence bacterial adhesion behavior.56

Furthermore, in addition to microscale roughness, nanoscale surface topography can significantly influence bacterial adhesion through distinct mechanisms.70 Nanoscale features may alter physicochemical interactions and energy barriers between bacteria and the surface, and depending on their size and geometry, can either inhibit or promote attachment.59 Moreover, certain nanostructures have been reported to affect bacterial behavior or induce mechanical deformation of cell membranes, highlighting that adhesion is not determined solely by average roughness parameters.59 Accordingly, a more comprehensive consideration of surface topography, including spatial parameters and nanoscale features, is important for better reflecting the complexity of bacterial adhesion processes.

The clinical relevance of surface topography is particularly evident in the transmucosal abutment region of dental implants. This portion of the implant system penetrates the soft tissue and is directly exposed to the oral environment, making it highly susceptible to bacterial colonization. Given its continuous exposure to saliva, dietary components, and oral microorganisms, controlling plaque accumulation on the abutment surface is critical for maintaining peri-implant tissue health. For this reason, transmucosal abutments are typically designed with highly polished, mirror-like surfaces.607172 While such polishing often reduces roughness below the proposed 0.2 µm threshold, its clinical benefit should not be attributed solely to achieving a specific Ra value. Rather, the reduction of surface irregularities, minimization of plaque-retentive features, and improved cleanability collectively contribute to decreased bacterial adhesion.

In contrast, the implant component of a dental implant system is usually designed with a roughened surface, often achieved through sandblasting, acid etching, or anodization, to promote osseointegration.7374 While this roughness is beneficial for mechanical stability and long-term implant success within the bone, it becomes a disadvantage when the implant surface is exposed to the oral environment. As discussed earlier, the implant is normally covered by bone and soft tissue, but exposure can occur due to pathological or anatomical factors, such as peri-implantitis, mucosal recession, or implant malposition. When exposed, the roughened implant surface presents a favorable environment for bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation due to the increased surface area and protective microtopography. Compared to the relatively smooth abutment surfaces, these rough implant surfaces are more susceptible to microbial colonization and more difficult to clean through routine oral hygiene practices.7576

SURFACE CHEMISTRY, SURFACE CHARGE, SURFACE ENERGY, AND WETTABILITY

The physicochemical properties of the implant surface, including surface chemistry, surface charge, surface energy, and wettability, play an important role in bacterial adhesion, particularly in the transmucosal abutment region, which is directly exposed to the oral environment.535758

The initial attachment of oral microorganisms to the abutment is governed by a complex interplay of physicochemical interactions at the material-biofilm interface. Most oral bacteria carry a net negative charge at physiological pH due to anionic components in their cell walls, such as teichoic acids in Gram-positive species and lipopolysaccharides in Gram-negative species.7778 Accordingly, electrostatic interactions may contribute to early adhesion, with similarly charged surfaces potentially reducing bacterial attachment and oppositely charged surfaces favoring attraction under simplified conditions.

The surface charge of the abutment is influenced by its chemical composition and oxide layer characteristics and is commonly described in relation to the point of zero charge (PZC), defined as the pH at which the net surface charge is zero. In general terms, surfaces tend to exhibit a positive charge at pH values below their PZC and a negative charge above it.79 However, in the oral environment, these electrostatic interactions are substantially modified by the presence of ionic components in saliva. In particular, divalent cations such as calcium can promote ionic bridging between negatively charged bacterial cells and surfaces, thereby reducing the effect of electrostatic repulsion.80

Moreover, implant surfaces are rapidly coated with an acquired pellicle composed of salivary proteins and glycoproteins, which mediates subsequent bacterial adhesion.658182 As a result, the direct influence of intrinsic surface charge is likely to be largely attenuated, and bacterial attachment is more accurately determined by the combined effects of surface properties, pellicle composition, and microbial characteristics.

Surface free energy (SFE) is a fundamental physicochemical property of solid materials, defined as the excess energy per unit area present at the surface compared to the bulk.81 Atoms and molecules at a surface exist in a thermodynamically unfavorable state due to incomplete bonding, resulting in a tendency to interact with surrounding phases to minimize the system’s free energy. In the context of dental implants, SFE plays a critical role in mediating protein adsorption, conditioning film formation, and subsequent bacterial adhesion. Wettability, commonly assessed by contact angle measurements, is directly related to SFE and reflects the ability of a liquid to spread across a solid surface.83 Surfaces with high SFE are generally more reactive and tend to promote interactions with polar molecules, whereas low-SFE surfaces exhibit reduced wettability and are typically more hydrophobic. This relationship is described by Young’s equation, which correlates the interfacial tensions among solid, liquid, and vapor phases. A contact angle lower than 90° indicates a hydrophilic surface with high surface free energy, while a contact angle higher than 90° corresponds to a hydrophobic surface with low surface free energy. Because direct measurement of SFE in solids is challenging, it is typically estimated indirectly using contact angle data and theoretical models. Among these, the Owens-Wendt approach is widely used, decomposing SFE into dispersive and polar components. More advanced models, such as the van Oss-Chaudhury-Good approach, further partition surface energy into Lifshitz-van der Waals and acid-base components, providing a more comprehensive framework for understanding biological interactions.8485

Bacterial adhesion is often discussed in relation to the interplay between surface wettability and the intrinsic hydrophobicity of microbial cells.8687 Bacterial hydrophobicity is determined by the composition and structure of the cell envelope, including surface proteins, lipopolysaccharides, and extracellular polymeric substances.8889 In simplified physicochemical models, hydrophobic bacteria tend to exhibit greater affinity for hydrophobic surfaces, whereas more hydrophilic bacteria may preferentially interact with hydrophilic substrates. However, in the oral environment, these interactions are considerably more complex, and adhesion is more accurately determined by the combined effects of surface properties and bacterial characteristics within a dynamic biological environment.

From a clinical perspective, these physicochemical properties are important primarily in governing the initial stages of bacterial adhesion and early colonization on transmucosal surfaces, thereby influencing the onset of biofilm formation. They also contribute to protein adsorption and acquired pellicle formation, which define the biological interface actually encountered by microorganisms. Physicochemical properties should be understood as intrinsic material characteristics determined by composition and surface processing; however, they are not fixed attributes and may be significantly modified upon exposure to the oral environment. In the oral cavity, saliva, ionic species, and rapid surface conditioning rapidly alter these properties, often masking the originally engineered surface characteristics. Therefore, while these properties are essential for understanding bacterial adhesion mechanisms and guiding material design, their clinical interpretation should be made cautiously within the context of the dynamic oral environment.

MATERIAL-DEPENDENT DIFFERENCES IN BACTERIAL ADHESION: TITANIUM VERSUS ZIRCONIA

Titanium and zirconia are widely used for transmucosal abutments and present distinct physicochemical characteristics that have been hypothesized to influence bacterial adhesion. However, a critical appraisal of the literature indicates that material-dependent effects cannot be interpreted independently of the methodological context in which they are observed.

Evidence from in vitro studies remains inconsistent and does not demonstrate a uniform directional trend. While some investigations report lower bacterial adhesion on zirconia compared to titanium,6869909192 others show higher adhesion on zirconia or no significant differences between the materials.879394959697 Importantly, the internal validity of these findings is frequently limited by substantial heterogeneity in experimental design, including differences in surface roughness, bacterial species selection, incubation time, and the presence or absence of an acquired pellicle. Among these variables, surface topography and bacterial species characteristics appear to exert a dominant influence, often exceeding the contribution of material composition. Furthermore, results derived from simplified single-species or short-term models raise concerns regarding their translational relevance to complex, multispecies biofilm systems. From a mechanistic perspective, material-related differences in surface free energy, hydrophilicity, and surface charge may influence early-stage protein adsorption and initial bacterial attachment under simplified conditions.58 Titanium, characterized by a reactive oxide layer, may promote more extensive protein adsorption, whereas zirconia exhibits comparatively inert surface behavior.108 However, these intrinsic differences are rapidly attenuated following the formation of the acquired salivary pellicle, which effectively masks the underlying substrate and dictates subsequent microbial attachment.2326

Taken together, the current body of evidence is characterized by methodological heterogeneity, limited consistency, and constrained translational applicability. Both in vitro and in vivo data fail to support a reproducible or clinically decisive advantage of either titanium or zirconia with respect to bacterial adhesion. Material-dependent effects are therefore strongly context-dependent and frequently modulated by surface topography, biological conditioning, and environmental factors. Accordingly, they should be interpreted within a biologically conditioned framework rather than as intrinsic properties of the biomaterial alone.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Available evidence consistently supports the importance of surface topography, particularly surface roughness, in influencing bacterial adhesion and plaque accumulation on dental implant components.626590109 Both experimental and clinical studies indicate that smoother surfaces are associated with reduced bacterial retention and improved plaque control, especially in the transmucosal abutment region. Accordingly, the use of highly polished abutment surfaces represents a well-supported and clinically reliable strategy for minimizing biofilm formation.

There is also evidence suggesting that exposure of roughened implant surfaces to the oral environment is associated with increased bacterial colonization and reduced cleanability.67110 Once exposed, these surfaces present microstructured features that favor biofilm retention and complicate mechanical debridement. Clinical observations further indicate that such conditions are associated with increased difficulty in maintaining peri-implant health.111 These findings support the importance of early detection of soft tissue changes and prevention of implant surface exposure as part of routine clinical management.76 In this context, the use of instruments that minimize surface damage is considered important to prevent unintended increases in surface roughness during clinical procedures.112113 Preservation of surface integrity may therefore contribute to maintaining the anti-plaque benefits of originally polished or engineered surfaces.

In terms of material selection, the clinical impact of titanium versus zirconia is less clearly supported by direct evidence. While some studies suggest that zirconia may exhibit lower initial bacterial adhesion under certain conditions, the overall body of evidence is inconsistent and does not demonstrate a reproducible or clinically decisive advantage. Therefore, current data do not support prioritizing material selection over surface finishing in efforts to reduce bacterial colonization.

Based on the available evidence, surface finishing and maintenance-related factors appear to play a more critical role in clinical outcomes than intrinsic material properties.93104 Accordingly, meticulous polishing of transmucosal components and maintenance of a smooth surface profile should be regarded as primary strategies for minimizing biofilm-related complications.

Patient-related factors, including oral hygiene practices and history of periodontitis, are consistently associated with peri-implant disease risk and may represent important contributing factors to clinical outcomes.11114 While optimization of implant surface characteristics may contribute to reducing bacterial adhesion, these effects should be interpreted within the broader context of patient behavior and host-related factors.

Overall, clinical strategies aimed at reducing biofilm formation should prioritize interventions with consistent evidence, particularly surface smoothness and exposure control, while recognizing that several commonly applied measures are supported primarily by indirect or inferential evidence rather than definitive comparative data.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of the current evidence, including heterogeneity in experimental models, variability in surface characterization methods, and the difficulty of isolating material effects from surface topography and biological conditioning factors, bacterial adhesion on dental implant surfaces appears to be governed primarily by surface-related and biologically conditioned factors rather than by intrinsic material differences alone.

Although titanium and zirconia exhibit distinct physicochemical properties under idealized conditions, available in vitro, in situ, and in vivo studies do not demonstrate a consistent or clinically meaningful superiority of either material with respect to bacterial adhesion. In contrast, the influence of surface roughness is consistently demonstrated across experimental and clinical studies, highlighting its dominant role in early biofilm formation. Highly polished transmucosal surfaces are associated with reduced bacterial retention, whereas exposure of roughened implant surfaces leads to increased biofilm accumulation.

From a clinical perspective, three main implications emerge: (1) current evidence does not support a definitive advantage of titanium or zirconia in preventing bacterial colonization; (2) maintaining smooth, well-finished transmucosal surfaces and preventing exposure of roughened implant surfaces represent the most reliable strategies for minimizing biofilm-related complications; and (3) future research should prioritize clinically relevant, multispecies and long-term models that better reflect the dynamic oral environment, particularly with respect to pellicle-modified surfaces and real-world implant exposure scenarios.
