Demoralization in patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder: results from an exploratory cross-sectional report
Authors: Annalisa Maraone, Matteo Panfili, Valentina Roselli, Daniele D’Agostini, Irene Pinucci, Tommaso Accinni, Lorenzo Tarsitani, Francesco Saverio Bersani, Massimo Pasquini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic, early-onset condition often associated with high rates of treatment resistance, and affective disturbances are frequent comorbidities and linked to poorer therapeutic outcomes. Recently, the concept of “demoralization syndrome” has been proposed as not fully overlapping with major depressive episodes. Given the disabling nature of OCD, it is relevant to specifically evaluate the states of demoralization, considering that this condition may have treatment implications partially different from those related to other forms of affective disturbances. The aim of the present exploratory study was to evaluate demoralization in subjects with OCD and its relationship with the severity of OCD-related symptoms.MethodsAdults with a primary diagnosis of OCD were consecutively recruited from the OCD outpatient clinic of Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Policlinico Umberto I (Rome, Italy). Participants were assessed with clinical interviews as well as with the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) for OCD severity, the Demoralization Scale (DS) for demoralization, and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) for depressive symptoms. Descriptive and correlational analyses have been performed.ResultsA total of 43 adults with OCD were included. Both depressive and demoralization symptoms were highly prevalent, with clinically-significant demoralization observed in 88% of patients. A subset of individuals exhibited clinically-significant demoralization without clinically-relevant depression. Demoralization severity showed significant associations with the severity of obsessions.ConclusionDemoralization was highly prevalent in our sample, and it was significantly associated with the severity of obsessions. Recognizing demoralization states in OCD may improve diagnostic precision and guide tailored therapeutic interventions, complementing standard treatments for the diseases. Background

Obsessive-compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a severe and early onset neuropsychiatric disorder that affects from 1% to 3% of the general population [1, 2].

OCD often follows a chronic course, with high rates of treatment resistance and refractoriness. Recent studies have identified various factors that may negatively influence treatment outcomes, leading to reduced therapeutic response and increased chronicity, such as gender, age of onset, motor tics, levels of insight, and duration of untreated illness [3–5]. These findings indicate relevamt differences in both the clinical course and symptomatic manifestations of patients with OCD, underscoring the importance of early interventions and individualized approaches that take into account the heterogeneity of clinical presentation [6, 7].

Mood disorders are often comorbid with OCD, with data describing the presence of depressive symptoms in 56.6% of the OCD patients [8]. This association is present both in developmental age and in adulthood, and the presence of depressive symptoms seems to negatively influence the outcome of the therapeutic intervention [8–10]. Additionally, data also underline the need to reduce the latency time of intervention for patients who show depressive symptoms in order to improve the efficacy of treatments and prevent the worsening of the clinical picture [11, 12].

In the last years, the construct of “demoralization syndrome” has been introduced as related to reactive depression among patients with chronic medical, neurodegenerative and psychiatric pathologies (such as psychosis). This syndrome is marked by a constellation of symptoms, including a pervasive sense of hopelessness, a loss of meaning and purpose in life, feelings of helplessness and entrapment, a perceived personal failure in coping with stressful circumstances, as well as pronounced pessimism and diminished motivation or drive to act [13].

The concept of demoralization has been defined by Frank as a mental state characterized by the lack of spirit or courage that leads the subject to disorder and confusion [14]. Subsequent clinical studies have conceptualized demoralization as a defined clinical construct in the context of affective disorders that differs from major depressive episodes for the lack of anhedonia and of loss of pleasure and interest in life activities, which are essential criteria for the diagnosis of a major depressive episode [10, 15, 16]. A recent study in patients with non-psychotic affective disorders found that demoralization was diagnosable in more than 50% of patients [17]. Also, a recent study observed high levels of demoralization in individuals with comorbid substance use and psychiatric disorders, with only limited improvements in demoralization levels being recorded durinf the treatment course in absence of targeted demoralization-focused interventions [18].

It is possible that investigating the presence of demoralization in OCD could be clinically helpful as OCD can be chronically disabling and, due to the ego-dystonic and pervasive nature of the symptoms, it may produce experiences of hopelessness, loss of meaning, and perceived failure. Going beyond the severity of symptoms, in fact, OCD also affects identity, agency, and the person’s sense that life is manageable or purposeful. Assessing demoralization can thus be relevant, as it may potentially improve diagnostic precision by separating treatable depressive symptoms from illness-related distress and loss of agency, and it may help to explain the states of persistent suffering which can be present in patients with OCD even when depressive symptoms are not severe. Assessing demoralization may also support more tailored interventions focused on coping, meaning, hope, and treatment engagement [19, 20]; in fact, while major depressive episodes usually respond to pharmacological treatments with antidepressants, certain psychotherapeutic approaches (such as humanistic-existential and meaning-centered therapies) have been proposed as interventions in the clinical management of demoralization [17, 21, 22].

Considering the above, the aim of the present exploratory study was to evaluate the presence of demoralization in subjects with OCD and its relationship with the severity of OCD-related symptoms.

Materials and methods

Participants were consecutively recruited (between December 2021 and July 2024) among patients referring at the OCD outpatient clinic of Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Policlinico Umberto I (Rome, Italy). Inclusion criteria for the present study were diagnosis of OCD (based on the criteria of Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5th Edition – DSM5) [23] and adulthood (i.e. included individuals were at least 18 years old); exclusion criteria were: concomitant psychotic disorder, current substance use disorders, refusal to sign informed consent. All participants gave their written informed consent to be included in the study. The present research is part of a research protocol on psychopathological features of OCD approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (Rif. 6897 - Prot. 0784/2022).

In relation to the present research, patients were assessed with clinical interview and using the Italian versions of the following scales. The clinician-rated Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (YBOCS) [24, 25] was used to assess the severity of obsessions and compulsions; analyses were based on scores on obsessions and compulsions; analyses were also performed on the total score and on symptoms subdimesions (time occupied by obsessive/compulsive symptoms, interference due to obsessive/compulsive symptoms, distress associated with obsessive/compulsive symptoms, resistance against obsessive/compulsive symptoms, degree of control over obsessive/compulsive symptoms). The self-rated Demoralization Scale (DS) was used to assess demoralization symptoms [26, 27], which consists of 24 items covering 5 dimensions (loss of meaning and purpose, dysphoria, disheartenment, helplessness, sense of failure); DS scores ≥ 25 have been considered suggestive of clinically-significant demoralization symptoms, as observed in previous studies [28]. Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) is a self-rating scale consisting of 9 items investigating depressive symptoms as described in DSM5, covering a time frame of the past two weeks [29]; PHQ scores ≥ 8 have been considered suggestive of clinically-relevant depressive symptoms, as observed in previous studies [29]. The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) is a 17-item clinician-administered depression ssessment scale [30, 31]; HAM-D scores ≥ 8 were considered suggestive of clinically-relevant depressive symptoms, as observed in previous studies [30, 31].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive analyses are presented as mean±standard deviation or as percentages. Inferential analyses were based on Spearman correlations. To explore whether the relationship of demoralization levels with OCD severity was independent by depression levels, Spearman partial correlations were also performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05; p values between 0.05 and 0.08 are reported as near significant findings. A non-parametric test was chosen as certain explored variables were non-normally distributed.

Discussion

The present study, to the best of our knowledge, is one of the first to explore the presence of demoralization in patients with OCD.

In relation to the overall clinical features of the sample, the results of this study, consistently with the scientific literature, showed that comorbidity in OCD is frequent: nearly four out of ten patients (39.5%) had a comorbid psychiatric disorder. Based on PHQ and HAM-D self-report scales, the percentage of patients presenting clinically-relevant depressive symptoms ranged between 72.1 and 76.7%, indicating a high prevalence of depressive experiences and in line with the existing literature; further, 11.6% of participants had an ongoing comorbid major depressive episode at the time of evaluation based on clinical interview.

The present study aimed to investigate the presence of demoralization in subjects with OCD and its relationship with the severity of OCD-related symptoms. The results highlighted that 38 patients (88.4%) showed clinically-significant demoralization, suggesting the presence of feelings of hopelessness, helplessness, and loss of meaning. The results are in line with previous studies indicating higher prevalence of demoralization syndrome in individuals with mental disorders than in the general population: demoralization syndrome, in fact, has been described in 2–5% of individuals in the general population [32, 33], in approximately 50% of patients with cancer or with severe chronic illness [34–36]. and in more than 50% in patients with mental disorders different from OCD [17].

Previous data suggested that, within the area of affective disturbances, the clinical features of demoralization and major depressive episodes do not fully overlap. Indeed, our results showed that a portion of patients with clinically-significant demoralization did not exhibit clinically-relevant depressive symptoms (assessed with PHQ, HAM-D or clinical interview). Moreover, the severity of obsessions showed significant correlations with levels of demoralization also when clinically-relevant depressive symptoms (assessed with PHQ cutoff, with HAM-D cutoff, and with ongoing major depressive episode) or continuous depressive symptoms (assessed with HAM-D) were included as covariates (the association was near significant when PHQ continuous values were used as covariate). These results overall support the possibility that, within the area of affective disturbances, demoralization and major depression can have partially (although not fully) distinct features and emphasize the importance of further investigating this phenomenon to fully understand the emotional distress of patients [37].

Consistent with the theoretical conception that defines demoralization as involving existential and motivational areas, demoralization syndrome appears to be closely linked to the subjective experience of depressive suffering, which influences the perceived psychological well-being but does not necessarily align with the objectively observed symptoms severity.

OCD is characterized by a chronic and disabling course, substantial functional impairment, and limited response to conventional pharmacological treatments in a relevant proportion of patients [3–5, 38, 39]. Core features (such as intrusive and ego-dystonic obsessions, persistent doubt, and diminished sense of control) may progressively undermine agency, identity coherence, and perceived life meaning, thereby fostering experiences of helplessness, pessimism, hopelessness, and perceived failure.

The core dimensions of demoralization, as described in the literature, call for targeted and integrated therapeutic interventions [19, 20]. In this context, the partial differentiation between the psychopathological features of demoralization and major depressive episodes may underlie the need for precise diagnostic assessment and individualized clinical approaches. The potential persistence of demoralization-related experiences despite adequate pharmacological management suggests the potential value of integrating interventions targeting meaning, coping and hope [19]; it is thus possible that existentially oriented and meaning-centered psychotherapeutic approaches may complement standard treatments in addressing this dimension of distress [20–22].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. This is an exploratory study, with sample size lower than optimal, and the statistical analyses are limited to the data currently available. The fact than some participants were taking antidepressant medications at the moment of the evaluation is a potential confounder. The high prevalence of symptoms observed may have been influenced by the tertiary-care setting. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow to evaluate the inferences of demoralization on follow up, longitudinal studies are necessary to examine the potential impact of demoralization on illness-course variables of OCD.

In conclusion, based on the findings of this exploratory study on demoralization in patients with OCD, it appears important to further investigate and delineate this psychopathological construct, which may warrant distinct clinical assessment strategies and an integrated therapeutic approach.
