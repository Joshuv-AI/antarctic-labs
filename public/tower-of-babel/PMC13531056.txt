Postpartum Aortic Dissection Without Aortic Dilation in a Woman With an ACTA2 Gene Mutation

History of Presentation

A 36-year-old woman who had an uncomplicated vaginal delivery of her third child experienced sudden-onset back pain at approximately 1:00 AM on postpartum day 8 while breastfeeding. She presented to a local hospital, where blood pressure measurements in both arms were within the normal range and showed no interarm discrepancy. Her vital signs were stable, and she was alert and oriented, with no signs or symptoms of organ malperfusion; however, laboratory testing revealed an elevated D-dimer level. As the back pain persisted, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) was performed, revealing an acute Stanford type B aortic dissection without aortic dilatation (Figure 1). She was subsequently transferred to our hospital, which was equipped to provide emergency invasive management if required.

Past Medical History

She had no past medical history, including hypertension or connective tissue disease, and no history of alcohol consumption or smoking. With the assistance of a genetic counselor at our hospital, it was confirmed that there was no family history of aortic dissection or aneurysm among second-degree relatives. She had 2 prior pregnancies and deliveries without pregnancy-related complications, including gestational hypertension; preeclampsia; eclampsia; hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count syndrome; or gestational diabetes mellitus.

Investigations

On examination, the patient had a Japan Coma Scale score of I-1. Blood pressure was 112/52 mm Hg in the right arm and 109/60 mm Hg in the left arm, with a heart rate of 68 beats/min, an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and a body temperature of 36.0 °C. Electrocardiography showed no arrhythmias. Blood tests showed an elevated D-dimer level of 2.6 μg/mL (reference value: <0.5 μg/mL fibrinogen equivalent units).

Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a patent false lumen extending from the distal aortic arch to the level of the renal arteries, without evidence of aortic dilatation. All major abdominal branch vessels originated from the true lumen, and no signs of organ ischemia were observed.

Genetic testing using next-generation sequencing revealed a known pathogenic missense mutation in the actin alpha 2, smooth muscle (ACTA2) gene (p.Arg149Cys—a missense variant in the ACTA2 gene causing an arginine-to-cysteine substitution at codon 149).

Management

Intravenous nicardipine hydrochloride at 2 μg/kg/min achieved adequate blood pressure control. The patient was subsequently transitioned to oral therapy with sustained-release nifedipine at 40 mg/d and propranolol at 30 mg/d. Her blood pressure remained stable at approximately 110/60 mm Hg, with a heart rate of around 55 beats/min.

Follow-up CT during hospitalization showed no aortic dilatation, consistent with an uncomplicated acute Stanford type B aortic dissection. Therefore, preemptive thoracic endovascular aortic repair was not performed, and the patient was discharged home on hospital day 22.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Serial CT performed every 6 months has shown virtually no progression of aortic diameter (Figure 2), and the patient remains under medical management with close surveillance. According to current guidelines,1 if future aortic enlargement is observed and surgical intervention becomes necessary, open surgical repair will be preferred over endovascular treatment, unless emergent intervention is required.

Discussion

Pathogenic variants in ACTA2, which encodes smooth muscle alpha-actin, are a well-established cause of heritable thoracic aortic disease. A defining feature of ACTA2-associated aortopathy is the occurrence of acute aortic dissection at relatively small aortic diameters, often in the absence of preceding aneurysmal dilatation.2,3 This contrasts with sporadic cases, where progressive enlargement usually precedes dissection.4 Cohort studies have consistently shown that ACTA2 mutation carriers may dissect below conventional surgical thresholds.5,6 Regalado et al5 reported that a substantial proportion of ACTA2-related dissections occurred at ascending aortic diameters <50 mm, with some near normal diameters. The current case is concordant with these observations, as dissection developed without measurable aortic enlargement. This finding reinforces the concept that structural and functional abnormalities of vascular smooth muscle, rather than absolute aortic size alone, play a central role in ACTA2-related aortic fragility. Accordingly, reliance solely on aortic diameter for risk stratification may be insufficient in this population.

Pregnancy and postpartum period represent additional high-risk states in patients with genetic aortopathies. Hemodynamic stress, increased cardiac output, and hormonal effects on the vascular extracellular matrix are thought to contribute to aortic vulnerability.7 In ACTA2 mutation carriers, dissections have been reported both during pregnancy and postpartum, even in the absence of prior aortic dilatation. Importantly, the clinical presentation of peripartum type B aortic dissection is often nonspecific and may overlap with more common conditions such as musculoskeletal pain, pulmonary embolism, hypertension, or gastrointestinal disorders, contributing to delayed or missed diagnosis at initial evaluation.8 This diagnostic challenge is particularly relevant in ACTA2 mutation carriers, who frequently lack aortic enlargement or syndromic features that would otherwise raise suspicion. The present case, which developed during the postpartum period, further supports the concept that this interval represents a critical window of risk and highlights the importance of maintaining a low threshold for advanced imaging in postpartum patients with unexplained chest, back, or abdominal pain.9

Compared with syndromic aortopathies such as Marfan syndrome or Loeys-Dietz syndrome, ACTA2-associated aortopathy often lacks overt systemic features, making early recognition challenging. In addition, it may present with unique vascular phenotypes, including early-onset stroke, livedo reticularis, and premature coronary artery disease, reflecting diffuse smooth muscle dysfunction rather than isolated connective tissue weakness.10 The absence of phenotypic stigmata and aortic enlargement in this case underscores the need to consider ACTA2 mutations in young patients presenting with aortic dissection, particularly in the peripartum setting.

Finally, the mutation in this patient was identified only after the acute event. The absence of family history and lack of aortic dilatation limited opportunities for anticipatory management. Given the incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity of ACTA2-associated disease, improved strategies for identifying at-risk individuals remain an important unmet need.

Conclusions

This case highlights that ACTA2-associated aortopathy can present with acute aortic dissection without prior aortic dilatation, making diameter-based risk assessment insufficient. The peripartum period represents a particularly high-risk window, and the nonspecific presentation of type B dissection may delay diagnosis. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and consider early imaging in peripartum patients with unexplained chest, back, or abdominal pain, even in the absence of syndromic features or family history. Improved recognition of ACTA2-related disease is essential for earlier identification and preventive management.
