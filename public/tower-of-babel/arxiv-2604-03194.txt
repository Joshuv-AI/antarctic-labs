On Matrices Whose Distinct Eigenvalues Are Fully Captured by Quotient Matrices
Bilal Ahmad Rather, [2mm] School of Mathematics, Statistics, Shandong University of Technology, Zibo 255049, China

Abstract
Let M \displaystyle M be the n \displaystyle n -square matrix partitioned into ℓ 2 \displaystyle\ell^{2} blocks b i ​ j \displaystyle b_{ij} according to some partition P = { C 1 , … , C ℓ } \displaystyle P=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{\ell}\} of index set { 1 , … , n } \displaystyle\{1,\dots,n\} . The quotient matrix Q = ( q i ​ j ) \displaystyle Q=(q_{ij}) is a k \displaystyle k -square matrix, with ℓ ≤ k ≤ n − 1 \displaystyle\ell\leq k\leq n-1 , where ( i ​ j ) \displaystyle(ij) -th entry is the average row sum (or column sum) of the corresponding block b i ​ j \displaystyle b_{ij} in M \displaystyle M . The partition P \displaystyle P is said to be equitable if row sum of each block b i ​ j \displaystyle b_{ij} is constant. In this case, the matrix Q \displaystyle Q is referred to as the equitable quotient matrix of M \displaystyle M , and the spectrum of Q \displaystyle Q is the subset of the spectrum of parent matrix M \displaystyle M . We characterize some classes of matrices such that their equitable quotient matrix Q \displaystyle Q contains all the distinct eigenvalues of M \displaystyle M , thereby information can be obtained form the smallest matrix Q \displaystyle Q without actually analyzing the parent matrix M . \displaystyle M. We present necessary and the sufficient conditions for distinct eigenvalue of M \displaystyle M contained in the spectrum of of Q \displaystyle Q in terms of eigenspaces. We end up article with some applications, where distinct eigenvalues of a parent matrix can be completely encoded by quotient matrix.

3
Characterization of matrices with two distinct eigenvalues in quotient matrix

Generalizing the above idea for the square matrices of order
\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle\pi=\bigl\{\{1\},\{2,3,4\}\bigr\}

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle m_{42}+m_{43}+m_{44}=c_{22},m_{12}+m_{13}+m_{14}=c_{12}

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle Q
be a real
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrix
\displaystyle Q=\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{bmatrix},
with two distinct real eigenvalues
\displaystyle\sigma(Q)=\{\alpha,\beta\},
with
\displaystyle\alpha\neq\beta.

Define the
\displaystyle 4\times 4
matrix
\displaystyle M
(depending on
\displaystyle Q,\alpha,\beta

M=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{c_{11}+\alpha}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{11}-\alpha}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{12}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{12}}{2}\\
\displaystyle\frac{c_{11}-\alpha}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{11}+\alpha}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{12}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{12}}{2}\\
\displaystyle\frac{c_{21}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{21}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{22}+\beta}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{22}-\beta}{2}\\
\displaystyle\frac{c_{21}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{21}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{22}-\beta}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{c_{22}+\beta}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.12)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\pi=\bigl\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle M

\displaystyle 4,

1&0\\
0&1\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

W=\operatorname{im}P=\{(a,a,b,b)^{\mathsf{T}}:a,b\in\mathbb{C}\}.

The equitable quotient
\displaystyle Q
is defined by the equationM P equals P Q.The sum of the elements in row
\displaystyle 1
and row
\displaystyle 2
for cell
\displaystyle\{1,2\}
is expressed as
\displaystyle a_{11}+a_{12}=\frac{c_{11}+\alpha}{2}+\frac{c_{11}-\alpha}{2}=c_{11},
while the sum across columns
\displaystyle\{3,4\}
is given by
\displaystyle b_{11}+b_{12}=\frac{c_{12}}{2}+\frac{c_{12}}{2}=c_{12}.
For rows
\displaystyle 3
and
\displaystyle 4
(cell
\displaystyle\{3,4\}

\displaystyle\{1,2\}
is
\displaystyle c_{11}+c_{12}=\frac{c_{21}}{2}+\frac{c_{21}}{2}=c_{21}

\displaystyle\{3,4\}
is
\displaystyle d_{11}+d_{12}=\frac{c_{22}+\beta}{2}+\frac{c_{22}-\beta}{2}=c_{22}

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle j
are equivalent to
\displaystyle c_{ij}
, leading to the expression

MP=\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{bmatrix}=P\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{bmatrix}=PQ.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\pi
is equitable, and its equitable quotient is precisely
\displaystyle Q
.
Consider the following vectors,
\displaystyle v_{1}=(1,1,0,0)^{\mathsf{T}},
and
\displaystyle v_{2}=(0,0,1,1)^{\mathsf{T}},
which form a basis of
\displaystyle W=\operatorname{im}P

\displaystyle X_{1}=(1,-1,0,0)^{\mathsf{T}},
and
\displaystyle X_{2}=(0,0,1,-1)^{\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle W^{\perp}

\mathbb{R}^{4}=W\oplus W^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\oplus\operatorname{span}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}.

Since
\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle W
is
\displaystyle M
-invariant and the restriction

\displaystyle M|_{W}
has matrix representation
\displaystyle Q
in the basis
\displaystyle\{v_{1},v_{2}\}

Mv_{1}=c_{11}v_{1}+c_{21}v_{2},\qquad Mv_{2}=c_{12}v_{1}+c_{22}v_{2}.

Given
\displaystyle\sigma(Q)=\{\alpha,\beta\}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle W

-1\\[-1.0pt]
0\\[-1.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{c_{11}+\alpha}{2}-\frac{c_{11}-\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{c_{11}-\alpha}{2}-\frac{c_{11}+\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{c_{21}}{2}-\frac{c_{21}}{2}\\
\frac{c_{21}}{2}-\frac{c_{21}}{2}\end{bmatrix}

-\alpha\\
0\\

In a similar manner,
\displaystyle MX_{2}=\beta X_{2}.
Consequently,
\displaystyle X_{1}
serves as an eigenvector of
\displaystyle M
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle X_{2}
functions as an eigenvector of
\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle X_{1},X_{2}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle M
that admit an eigenvector in
\displaystyle W
. Let the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle Q
be
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, Thus,
\displaystyle M|_{W}
has eigenvalues
\displaystyle\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
. On the other hand, we have enforced
\displaystyle MX=\alpha X
with
\displaystyle X\notin W
, so
\displaystyle\alpha

is also an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle M
on the
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional subspace

\displaystyle\operatorname{span}\{X\}
. Thus the full spectrum of
\displaystyle M
is
\displaystyle\{\alpha^{[2]},\beta,\gamma\},
and all three distinct eigenvalues
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta,\gamma
appear in the quotient matrix
\displaystyle Q
. Finally, since
\displaystyle Q
is a
\displaystyle 3\times 3
matrix, it is the smallest possible equitable quotient that can contain three distinct eigenvalues.

Example 3.3
.

We illustrate Theorem

with and example.

0&2&5\\
0&0&7\end{bmatrix}
be a matrix with eigenvalues
\displaystyle\{5,2,7\}.

So,
\displaystyle\alpha=5,\beta=2,\gamma=7,
and we have to construct a matrix
\displaystyle M_{4}
whose spectrum is

\displaystyle\{5^{[2]},2,7\},
and whose smallest equitable quotient is exactly
\displaystyle Q
containing all the three distinct eigenvalues of
\displaystyle M.
Comparing
\displaystyle c_{ij}
with
\displaystyle Q
, we have
\displaystyle c_{11}=5,c_{12}=-3,c_{13}=5,c_{21}=0,c_{22}=2,c_{23}=5,c_{31}=0,c_{32}=0,

Thus, by Theorem

, the
\displaystyle 4\times 4

\displaystyle M
in with

Substituting the values of

\displaystyle Q
with spectrum
\displaystyle\sigma(Q)=\{5,2,7\}
.
Next, we generalize the above ideas for a general matrix of order
\displaystyle n
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 3
and let
\displaystyle Q=\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{bmatrix}

be a
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrix with two distinct eigenvalues
\displaystyle\alpha\neq\beta.
If
\displaystyle\alpha
is the eigenvalue of matrix
\displaystyle M_{n}
with multiplicity
\displaystyle n-1
. Then with equitable partition
\displaystyle\pi=\bigl\{\{1\},\{2,3,\dots,n\}\bigr\}
,
\displaystyle M
is given by

M=\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&\dfrac{c_{12}}{\,n-1\,}\,\mathbf{1}_{1\times(n-1)}\\
c_{21}\,\mathbf{1}_{(n-1)\times 1}&\alpha I_{n-1}+\dfrac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}J_{n-1}\end{bmatrix},

(3.17)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{r\times s}
is the
\displaystyle r\times s
all–ones matrix,
\displaystyle I_{n-1}
is the identity, and

\displaystyle J_{n-1}
is the
\displaystyle(n-1)\times(n-1)
all–ones matrix.

Proof.

Write
\displaystyle M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
in block form with respect to the partition
\displaystyle\pi=\{\{1\},\{2,\dots,n\}\}
as

M=\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}&r^{\mathsf{T}}\\
s&B\end{bmatrix},

where
\displaystyle r,s\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
and
\displaystyle B\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
. For
\displaystyle\pi
to be equitable with quotient
\displaystyle Q=\bigl(c_{ij}\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}
, the row-sums from each cell to each cell must be constant. From cell
\displaystyle C_{1}=\{1\}
to
\displaystyle C_{1}
, the sum is
\displaystyle m_{11}
, so we must have
\displaystyle m_{11}=c_{11}.
From
\displaystyle C_{1}
to
\displaystyle C_{2}
, the sum is
\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{n}m_{1j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}r_{j}=c_{12}.
From
\displaystyle C_{2}
to
\displaystyle C_{1}
, for every
\displaystyle i\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, the entry
\displaystyle m_{i1}
must be the same, say
\displaystyle c_{21}
, and we get
\displaystyle m_{i1}=c_{21}
for all
\displaystyle i\geq 2,
that is,
\displaystyle s=c_{21}\mathbf{1}
. From
\displaystyle C_{2}
to
\displaystyle C_{2}
, for each
\displaystyle i\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, the row sum across columns
\displaystyle 2,\dots,n
must be constant,

\sum_{j=2}^{n}B_{ij}=c_{22}\quad\text{for all }i\geq 2.

The choices in (
3.17
) now leave us with

m_{11}=c_{11},\qquad r=\frac{c_{12}}{\,n-1\,}\mathbf{1}_{n-1},\quad B=\alpha I_{n-1}+\frac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}J_{n-1}.

So, we have

\sum_{j=2}^{n}m_{1j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{c_{12}}{\,n-1\,}=c_{12}.

Further, for each
\displaystyle i\geq 2
,

m_{i1}=c_{21},\quad\sum_{j=2}^{n}B_{ij}=\alpha+\frac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}\cdot(n-1)=\alpha+(c_{22}-\alpha)=c_{22}.

As a result, the row sum from one cell to the next are
\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{bmatrix}=Q
. In this case,
\displaystyle\pi
is equitable, and its equitable quotient matrix is
\displaystyle Q
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1},
and
\displaystyle u=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.
Then the partition subspace spanned by
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle u
is

\mathcal{S}_{\pi}=\{(a,b,\dots,b)^{\mathsf{T}}:a,b\in\mathbb{C}\}

Its orthogonal complement of dimension
\displaystyle n-2
with respect to the standard inner product is

\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}=\{(0,x_{2},\dots,x_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}:x_{2}+\dots+x_{n}=0\}.

By standard theory of equitable partitions, the restriction of
\displaystyle M
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\pi}
in the basis
\displaystyle\{e_{1},u\}
is represented by the quotient matrix
\displaystyle Q
. Thus, the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle M|_{\mathcal{S}_{\pi}}
are exactly
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
, which are same as the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle Q
.

Let’s look at what happens on
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}
.Let
\displaystyle x=(0,y)^{\mathsf{T}}
where
\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}y=0
. After that, we have

Mx=\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&r^{\mathsf{T}}\\
s&B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r^{\mathsf{T}}y\\
By\end{bmatrix}.

There is no way for
\displaystyle r^{\mathsf{T}}y=0
since
\displaystyle r
is a scalar multiple of
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n-1}
and
\displaystyle y
has no sum. Now,

By=\left(\alpha I_{n-1}+\frac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}J_{n-1}\right)y=\alpha y+\frac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}J_{n-1}y.

However,
\displaystyle J_{n-1}y=(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}y)\mathbf{1}=0
, which means that
\displaystyle By=\alpha y
and we get

Mx=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\alpha y\end{bmatrix}=\alpha x.

So, each vector in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}
has an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle\alpha
and is an eigenvector of
\displaystyle M
. Since
\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}=n-2
, this contributes
\displaystyle\alpha
with geometric (and hence algebraic) multiplicity at least
\displaystyle n-2
. Together with the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\alpha
coming from the restriction
\displaystyle M|_{\mathcal{S}_{\pi}}
(because
\displaystyle Q
has eigenvalues
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta
), we see that
\displaystyle\alpha
has algebraic multiplicity
\displaystyle n-1,
and
\displaystyle\beta
appears once, coming from the other eigenvalue of
\displaystyle Q
. Hence, the spectrum of
\displaystyle M
is
\displaystyle\operatorname{spec}(M)=\{\beta,\alpha,\dots,\alpha\}.

Next, we shoe that
\displaystyle M
must be necessarily of the form (
3.17
). Suppose
\displaystyle M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
has exactly two distinct eigenvalues
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
with algebraic multiplicities
\displaystyle n-1
and
\displaystyle 1
, and that the partition
\displaystyle\pi=\{\{1\},\{2,\dots,n\}\}
is equitable with quotient
\displaystyle Q
. Let
\displaystyle S_{\pi}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}
be as above, and assume
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}\subseteq E_{\alpha}.
Recall,
\displaystyle M
as before:

M=\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}&r^{\mathsf{T}}\\
s&B\end{bmatrix}.

Equitability condition of
\displaystyle\pi
forces us

m_{11}=c_{11},\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}r_{j}=c_{12},s=c_{21}\mathbf{1},\quad\sum_{j=2}^{n}B_{ij}=c_{22}\ \text{for all }i.

Now, take
\displaystyle x=(0,y)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{\perp}
, so
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}y=0
and
\displaystyle Mx=\alpha x
.
As before

Mx=\begin{bmatrix}r^{\mathsf{T}}y\\
By\end{bmatrix}=\alpha\begin{bmatrix}0\\
y\end{bmatrix}.

So,
\displaystyle r^{\mathsf{T}}y=0
, for all
\displaystyle y
with
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}y=0
. This implies
\displaystyle r
lies in the span of
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
, that is,
\displaystyle r=\delta\mathbf{1}
for some scalar
\displaystyle\delta
, and from the row-sum condition we obtain

\delta=\frac{c_{12}}{\,n-1\,}.

Similarly,
\displaystyle By=\alpha y
, for all
\displaystyle y
with
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}y=0
. This means that

(B-\alpha I_{n-1})y=0\quad\text{for all }y\perp\mathbf{1}.

So, it follows that
\displaystyle B-\alpha I_{n-1}
has image contained in
\displaystyle\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
and kernel containing
\displaystyle\{\mathbf{1}\}^{\perp}
. Thus
\displaystyle B-\alpha I_{n-1}
is a rank–
\displaystyle 1
matrix of the form

\displaystyle B-\alpha I_{n-1}=u\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
, for some
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
. The equitability condition on row-sums
\displaystyle\sum_{j}B_{ij}=c_{22}
gives us

\alpha+(n-1)u_{i}=c_{22}\quad\text{for all }i.

So,
\displaystyle u_{i}
is constant, say
\displaystyle u_{i}=\gamma
for all
\displaystyle i
, and
\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}.
Thus,
\displaystyle B=\alpha I_{n-1}+\dfrac{c_{22}-\alpha}{\,n-1\,}J_{n-1}
, and we recover (
3.17
). This shows
\displaystyle M
must have the claimed form. That completes the proof.

Classification of matrices even with two distinct eigenvalues as in Theorem
3.4
remains open at large. Like, if the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle M
are
\displaystyle\{\alpha^{[i]},\beta^{[n-i]}\}
,
\displaystyle 2\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
with equitable partition
\displaystyle\pi=\{\{1,2,\dots,i\},\{i+1,\dots,n\}\}
. A similar type of classification of matrices
\displaystyle M
with spectrum
\displaystyle\{\alpha^{[i]},\beta^{[j]},\gamma^{[n-i-j]}\}
. It is more challenging for matrices having
\displaystyle t\geq 3
distinct eigenvalues.

Now, we illustrate Theorem
3.4
by the following example for matrix of order
\displaystyle 10.

Example 3.5
.

Choose a
\displaystyle 2\times 2
quotient as
\displaystyle Q=\begin{bmatrix}1&-8\\
4&13\end{bmatrix}.
The eigenvalues of
\displaystyle Q
are
\displaystyle\{5,9\}.
So, here
\displaystyle\beta=9
and
\displaystyle\alpha=5
, the eigenvalue we want to repeat in
\displaystyle M
. Consider a partition
\displaystyle\pi=\bigl\{\{1\},\{2,3,\dots,10\}\bigr\}

with

]4&&&&\\
4&&&&\\
\vdots&&B&&\\

\displaystyle B=5I_{9}+\frac{8}{9}J_{9}

\displaystyle 5+\frac{8}{9}=\frac{53}{9},
and each off-diagonal entry is
\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}
. Clearly, the partition
\displaystyle\pi=\{\{1\},\{2,\dots,10\}\}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle Q=\begin{bmatrix}1&-8\\
4&13\end{bmatrix}.
The partition subspace of

, which is of dimension

\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle 9

4
Distinct eigenvalues of Quotient matrices

In this section, we discuss the necessary and the sufficient condition for the matrix
\displaystyle M
with some equitable partition
\displaystyle\pi
such that its quotient matrix contains all the distinct eigenvalues. We stat with a
\displaystyle 4\times 4
matrix.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a matrix with spectrum
\displaystyle\{\leftthreetimes_{1},\leftthreetimes_{2},\leftthreetimes_{2},\leftthreetimes_{2}\},
such that
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1}\neq\leftthreetimes_{2},

and let
\displaystyle\pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
be an equitable partition of
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4\}
with
characteristic matrix
\displaystyle P
and equitable quotient matrix
\displaystyle B
, satisfying
\displaystyle MP=PB.

Let
\displaystyle W=\operatorname{im}P
be the subspace of vectors that are constant on each
cell of
\displaystyle\pi
, and let
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes}
denote the eigenspace of
\displaystyle M
corresponding to
an eigenvalue
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes
.

1.

For any
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes\in\mathbb{C}
,
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes\in\sigma(B)
if and only if
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes}\cap W\neq\{0\}.
Equivalently,
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle B
if and only if
\displaystyle M
has an
eigenvector for
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes
whose entries are constant on each cell of
\displaystyle\pi
.

2.

In particular, the equitable quotient
\displaystyle B
contains
both
distinct
eigenvalues
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1}
and
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{2}
of
\displaystyle M
if and only if
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}\cap W\neq\{0\}

and
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}\cap W\neq\{0\},
that is, if and only if
\displaystyle M
admits at least one eigenvector for each of

\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1},\leftthreetimes_{2}
that is constant on the cells of
\displaystyle\pi
.

3.

If, in addition, the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{2}
has geometric multiplicity

\displaystyle 3
(equivalently,
\displaystyle\dim E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}=3
), then for any equitable partition

\displaystyle\pi
with at least two cells we always have
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}\cap W\neq\{0\}
,
so
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{2}\in\sigma(B)
automatically. In this (diagonalizable) case,
\displaystyle B

contains both eigenvalues
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1},\leftthreetimes_{2}
if and only if
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}\cap W\neq\{0\},

that is, the unique (up to scaling) eigenvector of
\displaystyle M
for
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1}
is
constant on each cell of
\displaystyle\pi
.

Proof.
Let
\displaystyle M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a matrix with spectrum
\displaystyle\{\leftthreetimes_{1},\leftthreetimes_{2},\leftthreetimes_{2},\leftthreetimes_{2}\},
such that
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1}\neq\leftthreetimes_{2},

and let
\displaystyle\pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
be an equitable partition of
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4\}
with
characteristic matrix
\displaystyle P
and equitable quotient matrix
\displaystyle B
, satisfying
\displaystyle MP=PB.

We note that
\displaystyle P
is a matrix with its
\displaystyle j
-th column as the indicator vector of the cell

\displaystyle C_{j}
. Suppose
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle B
and let

\displaystyle y\neq 0
satisfy
\displaystyle By=\leftthreetimes y
. Then, we have

M(Py)=P(By)=\leftthreetimes(Py).

So
\displaystyle x=Py
is an eigenvector of
\displaystyle M
with eigenvalue
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes
. By construction,

\displaystyle x
is a linear combination of the characteristic vectors of the cells
\displaystyle C_{j}
,
hence
\displaystyle x
is constant on each cell and
\displaystyle x\in W
. Therefore, we obtain

x\in E_{\leftthreetimes}\cap W,\qquad x\neq 0,

and
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes}\cap W\neq\{0\}
,
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes}
denote the eigenspace of
\displaystyle M
corresponding to
an eigenvalue
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes
.

Conversely, suppose that
\displaystyle x\in E_{\leftthreetimes}\cap W
with
\displaystyle x\neq 0
.
There is a number
\displaystyle y
in the set
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{k}
such that
\displaystyle x=Py
, so we have

MPy=Mx=\leftthreetimes x=\leftthreetimes Py.

With
\displaystyle MP=PB
, we have
\displaystyle P(By-\leftthreetimes y)=0.
Since
\displaystyle P
has full column rank,
\displaystyle By=\leftthreetimes y
, which means that
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes\in\sigma(B)
.
Thus, for
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes=\leftthreetimes_{1}
and
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes=\leftthreetimes_{2}
, we see that

\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1},\leftthreetimes_{2}\in\sigma(B)
if and only if

E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}\cap W\neq\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}\cap W\neq\{0\}.

Equivalently, there exists for each
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2\}
an eigenvector of
\displaystyle M
for

\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{i}
that is constant on each cell of the partition
\displaystyle\pi
.

Assume in addition that the geometric multiplicity of
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{2}
is
\displaystyle 3
, so

\displaystyle\dim E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}=3
and
\displaystyle\dim E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}=1
, with

\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}\oplus E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}=\mathbb{C}^{4}
. Let
\displaystyle\pi
have
\displaystyle k
cells, so

\displaystyle\dim W=k
. Since
\displaystyle k\geq 2
is necessary for the quotient
\displaystyle B
to have at least
two distinct eigenvalues, we restrict to
\displaystyle k\geq 2
. By the dimension formula for subspaces of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{4}
,

\dim(E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}\cap W)\;\geq\;\dim E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}+\dim W-4=3+k-4=k-1.

For
\displaystyle k\geq 2
we have
\displaystyle k-1\geq 1
, so
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}\cap W\neq\{0\}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{2}\in\sigma(B)
for any equitable partition
\displaystyle\pi
with at least two
cells. Therefore, in this diagonalizable case, the only extra requirement for
\displaystyle B
to
contain
both
eigenvalues
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1},\leftthreetimes_{2}
is that
\displaystyle E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}

also intersect
\displaystyle W
nontrivially, that is, that the (unique up to scaling)
eigenvector for
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1}
is constant on each cell of
\displaystyle\pi
.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0&0\\

0&0&\tfrac{3}{2}&-\tfrac{1}{2}\\

The spectrum of
\displaystyle M
is
\displaystyle\{2,2,2,1\},
one of the eigenvectors for
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{1}=1
is
\displaystyle v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\\
1\end{bmatrix}.
The other eigenvectors for
\displaystyle\leftthreetimes_{2}=2
are

\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
-1\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

There is no doubt that
\displaystyle\dim E_{\leftthreetimes_{1}}=1
and
\displaystyle\dim E_{\leftthreetimes_{2}}=3
. We can now use Proposition
4.1
.
The partition
\displaystyle\pi=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\}
of the index set
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4\}
is equitable. The characteristic matrix of
\displaystyle\pi
is
\displaystyle P=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

0&1\\

and its column space, as a subspace of vectors constant on each cell
\displaystyle\{1,2\}
and

is

By definition of the equitable quotient
\displaystyle B
, the equation
\displaystyle MP=PB
gives
\displaystyle B=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\

can be faithfully recovered from an equitable quotient matrix
\displaystyle Q
arising from a partition of its index set. Starting from the notion of an equitable partition, we identified broad classes of matrices for which the equitable quotient matrix contains all distinct eigenvalues of
\displaystyle M
. In these cases, the (possibly much smaller) matrix
\displaystyle Q
completely encodes the distinct eigenvalues of the parent matrix
\displaystyle M
, and hence provides a substantial reduction in dimension without any loss of spectral information at the level of distinct eigenvalues (Theorem
3.4
).

A central contribution of our work is the derivation of necessary and sufficient conditions for a distinct eigenvalue of
\displaystyle M
to appear in the spectrum of
\displaystyle Q
in terms of the eigenspaces of
\displaystyle M
and the structure of the partition (Theorem
4.3
). These conditions clarify how the choice of partition interacts with the geometry of eigenspaces, and they characterize precisely when an equitable quotient captures a given eigenvalue.

We have also explored applications of these ideas in the context of graph matrices, where
\displaystyle M
arises from a natural matrix associated with a graph, and the partition is induced by a structural decomposition such as vertex classes or symmetry orbits. In this setting, we showed that a suitable (and sometimes slightly enlarged) equitable partition allows one to read off the distinct eigenvalues of the parent matrix from the corresponding quotient matrix (Proposition
5.1
and Theorem
6.4
). This leads to efficient ways of obtaining spectral information for large graphs by working with comparatively small quotient matrices. Possible directions for future work include extending these techniques to broader classes of matrices and partitions (for example, almost equitable partitions), studying stability under perturbations of
\displaystyle M
and of the partition, and further exploiting these ideas in algorithmic applications where one needs to approximate or track eigenvalues of large structured matrices.
