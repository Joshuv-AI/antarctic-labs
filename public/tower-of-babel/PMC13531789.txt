Minimal comfort feeding in palliative care in the light of the Anscombean-Thomistic virtue ethics theory of action
Authors: John Y Rhee, Michael Egan, Xavier Symons, Pablo Requena Meana, Mária Kolesárová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ethical terrain surrounding feeding at the end-of-life is emotionally charged and complex. In the literature, the recently introduced notion of “minimal comfort feeding” (MCF), defined as deliberate limitation of feeding or hydration, given only in response to visible signs of hunger or thirst, for a patient with a longer prognosis, has been proposed as a comfort measure at the end of life and as an alternative to voluntarily stopping eating and drinking (VSED) in advance care planning. However, the same term may be applied to two morally distinct acts: 1) providing comfort in the context of active dying, and 2) a hastening death. In this paper, we argue for conceptual clarity around these two practices and their ethical valence, drawing on an Anscombean-Thomistic virtue ethics framework that deals with moral evaluation of human action and the doctrine of double effect. We present two clinical cases highlighting each of these ethical scenarios. Through the cases, we show how intentionality and some circumstances such as timing critically shape the morality of the act. We propose a new nomenclature with the aim of fostering ethical clarity of the morally distinct acts: “proportionate comfort feeding” (PCF) for MCF where nutrition and hydration is provided for comfort with the understanding that it may hasten death but is its foreseen, unintended outcome, and “minimal feeding to hasten death” (MFHD) for cases where MCF is chosen with the specific intention to hasten death.Keywords: Minimal comfort feeding, Palliative care, Action theory, Doctrine of double effect, Intention

Introduction

Nutrition and hydration in palliative care is a deeply important issue to patients and families as well as an ethically charged and emotional one [1]. Palliative care physicians are frequently confronted with ethical issues around feeding, which range from risks of feeding due to dysphagia to withholding all nutrition and hydration [1].

Currently, there is a spectrum of approaches to feeding in palliative care, ranging from normal meals, for those who are able to independently feed themselves, to comfort feeding only (CFO), for those who are unable to do so, to the other end of voluntarily stopping eating and drinking (VSED) in order to hasten death [2–4]. CFO traditionally refers to providing food and/or fluids to a patient at the end-of-life with the primary aim of enhancing comfort, rather than sustaining life [3]. Comfort feeds are given at mealtimes, where a care-partner provides food or hydration to the patient at set times during the day, usually by hand feeding. On the other hand, VSED refers to a patient’s decision to intentionally refuse oral nutrition and hydration in order to hasten death [5, 6]. VSED may be undertaken by a capacitated patient, and in some discussions has also been proposed in advance directives, particularly in the context of progressive cognitive decline [2, 7].

Recently, there has been a new concept on feeding in palliative care introduced to the literature, described as minimal comfort feeding (MCF) [4]. MCF is described as the deliberate limitation of nutrition and hydration, not in timed intervals, but in response to signs of hunger or thirst [4]. This idea was introduced as a potentially ethically acceptable alternative to VSED by advance directive, where a patient may decide ahead of time that they would like MCF if they were to reach a stage of, for example, advanced dementia. MCF is also distinct from CFO, where in CFO, nutrition and hydration are still given at set times during the day to mimic meals, whereas MCF is only given to signs of hunger or thirst.

In this article, we explore the ethics of MCF through the lens of action theory within the Anscombean-Thomistic virtue ethics tradition. By proposing two clinical cases, we analyze situations where MCF may be ethically acceptable and where there may be ethical concerns. The theory of the evaluation of human action inherent to the Anscombean-Thomistic [8–11] virtue ethics framework evaluates the moral permissibility of human acts based on their object, intention, and circumstances, and has traditionally regarded the intentional hastening of death as morally impermissible. This is an ethical framework with a robust and rich Thomistic history and tradition [8, 10–13] that we refer to, but a full defense of it is outside the scope of this paper.

Two clinical cases

The following cases are intended to isolate and illustrate the morally salient features of MCF. The cases are designed not to exhaust the range of clinical presentations but to highlight the conceptual poles necessary for the ethical analysis of selected medical actions. Further, the cases are designed to be similar to cases clinicians come across in their routine clinical practice. In particular, Case 1 reflects what is routine in comfort-focused hospice care. Case 2 deliberately foregrounds a phenomenon of intention to hasten death in order to demonstrate the conditions under which the Anscombean-Thomistic action theory reaches its specific ethical conclusion. The cases, therefore, help to highlight to the clinician the distinct moral realities of the acts under consideration, despite their, physical similarity on the surface.

Case #1

Justine is a woman in her 80s with widely metastatic non-small cell lung cancer on hospice. She has slowly been deteriorating over the past few weeks, requiring more frequent and higher doses of morphine for shortness of breath. She is also becoming increasingly somnolent, sleeping for the majority of the day. Within this context, the family is asking the hospice medical director Justine’s prognosis, and he estimates that it may be in the range of one to two months. Justine is still able to stay awake for periods of time and engage with family, albeit a bit confused due to hypoxia, as well as to eat. However, she does not have much of an appetite, frequently coughs when she takes food or water, and given this, the family has opted to provide her with feeds only when she appears she is looking for food.

Case #2

Mark is a man in his 60s with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, with predominant limb weakness. Recently, it has been harder for him to even get into his wheelchair. His swallowing is mostly preserved. However, given his ongoing loss of autonomy, he asks his physician about physician-assisted suicide / medical aid in dying. His physician notes that given he is still early in his diagnosis, he does not yet qualify because he has a prognosis of greater than six months. Mark decides to undergo minimal comfort feeding, and over a period of a few weeks, he becomes increasingly somnolent, fatigued, and less interactive. At that point, he becomes eligible for physician-assisted suicide/medical aid-in-dying and requests and undergoes the procedure.1

Theory of action inherent to Anscombean-Thomistic virtue ethics framework

The analysis of human actions is one prism through which the morality of different medical procedures can be assessed. Some theorists, particularly virtue ethics and natural law bioethicists, focus on three different dimensions of an act: the object, intention, and circumstances. According to Aquinas [9], the morality of an act depends primarily on its object, that is, what the will directly chooses under a given description. Intention refers to the further purpose for which the act is performed, and circumstances refer to secondary factors that may increase or diminish moral responsibility. Contemporary interpreters such as Anscombe [10] and Boyle [14] have emphasized that properly specifying the object of an act is essential for moral evaluation, since the same physical behavior may constitute different moral acts depending on how it is described. To clarify the ethical differences between forms of feeding at the end-of-life, we draw on an Anscombean-Thomistic account of human action [9, 10]. This framework is widely used in virtue ethics traditions and underlies classical formulations of the doctrine of double effect (DDE).

The Anscombean part of this framework contributes, beyond DDE, in two ways relevant to the analysis of MCF. First, Anscombe offers a more rigorous method for specifying the object of an act through the notion of intentional action under a description, which allows one to distinguish acts that are materially similar but differ in their practical-rational structure. Second, the broader virtue ethics frame situates individual acts within a dispositional account of the agent, therefore, directing attention to the moral character of the person as expressed through her habitual choices and goals. These contributions allow the framework to address not just what MCF is in a particular clinical case, but also what kind of moral agent is constituted by these kinds of choices.

The moral permissibility of hastening death or suppressing consciousness in specific situations has traditionally been explained by the principle of double effect, also commonly known as the doctrine of double effect (DDE). The DDE, which is the framework classically introduced by Aquinas [9], later developed by moral theologians and philosophers such as Mangan [15] and Boyle [14], and further clarified by contemporary ethicists [16], provides criteria for evaluating moral actions that produce significant good effects and serious bad effects at once. Traditionally, the following conditions are required: an act, which must in itself be good or at least neutral, can have both good effects (alleviation of suffering) and bad effects (hastening death) but that the bad effect cannot be brought on by the good effect, that the bad effect cannot be intended, and that there must be proportionally grave reason for permitting the bad effect [15]. The term “proportionate” is used here in the classical sense in the DDE, where an act is proportionate when the good sought is commensurate with, and not outweighed by, the foreseen but unintended harms [17, 18]. In end-of-life care, proportionality refers to calibrating interventions to the patient’s clinical condition, prognosis, and risk of harm.

Application of DDE depends on correct specification of the object of the act. If an act is specified as “hastening death,” it cannot be justified by DDE, even if relief of suffering is also intended. By contrast, if the act is specified as “relieving suffering,” with death foreseen but not intended, DDE may be applicable. A commonly discussed application of the DDE in medicine concerns palliative sedation for refractory pain [19]. In cases where a patient experiences intractable pain that cannot be relieved by other means, clinicians may administer sedative medications to proportionately reduce consciousness in order to alleviate suffering. The good effect sought is relief of otherwise uncontrollable distress; the foreseen but unintended bad effect may include loss of interaction or, in rare cases, potential shortening of life. According to DDE, such sedation may be permissible if the object of the act is relief of pain rather than as causing death, if death is not intended either as an end or as a means, and if the intervention is proportionate to the severity of pain.

In Intention, Anscombe argues that actions are not merely physical movements but are specified by the reasons for which they are performed [10]. Answering the question, “What is the agent doing?” is essential for distinguishing acts that may appear outwardly similar yet differ in moral character. We adopt an Anscombean-Thomistic account of human action, according to which acts are evaluated based on three sources, as mentioned above: 1) the moral object, 2) the intention (or end), and 3) the circumstances [8]. We interpret this framework in light of Anscombe’s account of intentional action, which emphasizes that acts are specified under descriptions that reflect practical reasoning [10].

When we evaluate the morality of an action, we have to ask what the goal or purpose the act is directed towards. This underlying purpose is what we call the “object” of the act. The ‘object chosen’ in an act is often the most difficult part of the action to characterize. The “object” provides the act with its moral character, because it shows what the will has deliberately set itself toward, and reason judges whether that aim is in line with the good [15]. For example, if a physician administers pain medication, the moral object might be described as “relieving pain,” rather than “allowing the patient to rest” or “facilitating family interaction” (which instead would be considered the intention in terms of further motivation). The object answers the question, “What is being done?”

Intention refers to the further end sought beyond the immediate object of the act. It answers the question of why one chooses that particular act and evaluates whether the anticipated end is good [15, 20]. It is important here, to distinguish between “intention” (finis operantis) and “intentionality” (finis operis). Within an Anscombean-Thomistic framework, the object of act is both a physical event and an inherently intentional action specified under a description that reflects the agent’s practical reasoning, one at the level of what the will has set itself toward under a practical description, and what we may call the act’s basic “intentionality” (finis operis). For example, the question, “What is the agent doing?” admits of different answers corresponding to different levels of description, and identifying the morally relevant object requires discerning which description correctly characterizes what the will has deliberately set itself toward and is often considered the “intentionality”. Conceptually distinct is the “intention” (finis operantis), which answers a different question of “Why is the agent doing this?”, that is the further purpose for which the act is chosen. In other words, the intention of the agent “finis operantis” can also be referred to as the intended end or the motivation of the subject, the “why” of the action. This is different to the “intentionality” of an act, which is part of the object in that human acts are always about something, and always pointed toward some end as their immediate target. It is not a separate thing from the act, but rather, it is the inner structure of the act. Therefore, “intentionality” is inherent in the object of act, traditionally called “finis operis” or the goal of an action [20].

Anscombean-Thomistic virtue ethics theory of action applied to minimal comfort feeding

In Case 2, the inner “intentionality” of the act (finis operis), limiting feeding to bring about physiological decline and hasten death, differs structurally from Case 1, where limiting feeding may be eventually possible within the comfort care due to person´s physiological conditions in the process of dying. In the later case the feeding is proportioned to comfort the dying patient. There are some risks as aspiration or episodes of somnolence, that can be a cause a premature death. In this case, however, death is not intended. The further “intention”(finis operantis) in Case 2 may include eligibility for assisted death for pain liberation, whereas in Case 1 the intention is clearly aimed to symptom relief of the living person.

Finally, circumstances, which include consequences, are secondary elements of a moral action and contribute to increasing or decreasing the moral weight of human acts as well as the agent’s responsibility to the acts. They do not change the moral quality of actions [15, 20], but may increase or diminish its moral gravity without changing its fundamental object [8]. These can include factors such as imminence of death, proportionality of intervention, and foreseeable risk. For example, proportioning feeding in a patient who is actively aspirating and imminently dying (Case 1) differs morally from proportioning feeding in a patient who is medically stable and capable of prolonged survival (Case 2). In both cases, the physical act may appear as a limitation of feeding; however, the surrounding clinical context alters the moral weight of the decision. Circumstances can therefore intensify responsibility or mitigate culpability, even when the object and intention remain constant.

In Case 1, the object of the act may be described as “proportional feeding a dying patient for comfort,” which can include “the reduction of feeding for comfort” whereas in Case 2, the object may be more precisely described as “retaining feeding in order to hasten decline.” Although the action, reduced feeding, may appear similar, the moral object differs when specified according to what the agent is doing under a description. A pure material reading of the object can identify the underlying physical act as the same in both cases, i.e. “feeding in response to expressed hunger or thirst” or eventually “limitation of feeding”, and would locate the moral differentiation primarily in the further intention (motivation of the agent) and circumstances. However, we argue that such a superficial physical reading is inaccurate. In Case 1, the will chooses feeding a dying patient in response to signs of hunger or thirst as a comfort measure, whereas, in Case 2, the agent’s will chooses restricting nutrition in aiming to cause a physiological decline rather than comfort. Even if the bodily movements overlap, these are not the same basic “intentionality” in these two actions. The description of gradual nutrition withholding in order to hasten decline is therefore, the most appropriate specification of the moral object that correctly captures the basic “intentionality” of the act as it occurs in Case 2.

In recent literature, MCF has been proposed particularly in the context of advance directives for progressive dementia, where patients seek to avoid prolonged survival in advanced cognitive decline [4]. In these discussions, the limitation of feeding may function not only as a comfort measure but as a structured mechanism for shortening the course of illness.

We can say that MCF can correspond to two different objects of action, with very different inner “intentionalities”. When the patient has a terminal illness and is close to death, the object of the act is feeding or hydrating in response to physical manifestations of hunger or thirst, and this could be considered a morally good object. However, if the patient does not have a terminal illness or has a longer life expectancy, then the moral character of the object changes, and the underlying act is of different species. This is because the moral object of the act is no longer to actively feed or hydrate a person who appears hungry or thirsty, but rather gradually limit her access to nutrition in order to hasten her death.Similarly, in the case of MCF, if it is carried out with the intention of hastening the death of a patient, it would be considered impermissible using the DDE. The question, however, lies in how to potentially approximate intention in both the patient and the clinician to whom the patient is requesting MCF. Here, it would appear that timing of MCF is crucial to its moral valence because timing can give us an information about the act´s intentionality.

In Case 1, MCF may hasten the death of the patient, though her prognosis is already very short and it is more probable that her death would be caused by her terminal illness in the first place. In addition, risks as aspiration and somnolence can contribute to hastening death. In this case, although MCF in itself or the MCF-related complications may hasten the death, it is permissible according to DDE, as it is focused towards comfort in active dying and not to hastening death. In other words, the DDE applies here where, given the patient is eating much less, it is possible that MCF may contribute to hastening her death, but the death is not intentionallycaused by using MCF even if it can be foreseen as a possible event. However, in Case 2, the patient’s prognosis is much longer than even six months. In this case, the intention is not comfort, though that may be a secondary intention. The basic intentionality here is hastening of death due to various kinds of further intentions or motivations, as for example the perceived loss of autonomy that is causing the patient distress or importance of person´s control over the moment of death. Because the inner intentionality of the act is hastening of the patient’s death, this would not be permissible within the Anscombean-Thomistic action theory framework outlined above.

The two cases presented mark the conceptual poles of the ethical spectrum, but many clinical situations fall somewhere in between. In these intermediate cases, the circumstances may play an important role in clarifying the moral object of the act. For example, in Case 1, if the patient is experiencing frequent aspiration events, then it may be that set time points for feeding or hydrating may actually contribute to hastening of that patient’s death. When feeding is, instead, provided in response to visible signs of hunger or thirst, the object of the act is the relief of that discomfort, and aspiration, though foreseen as a possible consequence, is not intended, and the DDE permits the act on those grounds. The same additionally applies to other medical situations where a patient is clearly accumulating harm from nutrition or hydration rather than benefit, such as third-spacing fluid for patients with severe cirrhosis or heart failure, where even modest differences in fluid intake can have serious consequences for dyspnea, renal function, and pain or discomfort.

Proposed nomenclature for differentiation

Given the fact that there appears to be a different act where MCF is used as a planned hastening of death versus MCF is applied in end-of-life care, to not cause confusion, we argue that there should be a difference in wording used to distinguish between the two concepts. In other words, the moral significance of this distinction lies in preventing a linguistic slide whereby comfort-focused hospice feeding becomes confused, in clinical practice, with structured hastening of death [3], resulting in clinicians and families misunderstanding the moral nature of each act being proposed.

In the case of MCF as an advance care planning option to hasten death, we would recommend the term “minimal feeding to hasten death” (MFHD) as opposed to MCF at the end-of-life, as “proportionate comfort feeding” (PCF). Here, “to hasten death” flags the moral distinction of hastening death, which has a distinct moral object of act. We propose using “proportionate” in the latter, as, grounded in an action theory-based framework, this term conveys an ethical evaluation consistent with avoiding both undertreatment and overtreatment at a time in life where the body may no longer be able to absorb food and water in a similar way to someone who is not at the end-of-life. This approach integrates medical knowledge, the virtue of practical wisdom, and the patient’s particular circumstances while still taking into account object and intentionality of a particular moral action.

Conclusion

Minimal comfort feeding (MCF) has been recently introduced as a novel advanced care planning tool in palliative care, and as an alternative to voluntarily stopping eating and drinking (VSED). We argue that though MCF is presented as a uniform concept, no matter the intent, using the framework of action theory, MCF offered to imminently dying patients for the sake of comfort, proportionate comfort feeding (PCF), is inherently and ethically distinct from minimal feeding to hasten death (MFHD), in which MCF is used as a planned intentional reduction of feeding to hasten death. We posit that by adopting more precise terminology, we can foster better ethical discernment and clarity for clinicians and families in making decisions around end-of-life care.
