Optical conductivity of topological semimetal Nb 2n+1 Si n Te 4n+2
Seongjin Ahn

Abstract
We study the linear optical conductivity of the Nb 2n+1 Si n Te 4n+2 family of layered van der Waals materials, which has recently gained considerable attention owing to its dimensionality-tunable electronic structure with a quasi-one-dimensional nodal-line state. At zero temperature, we analytically show that the Drude weight exhibits strong anisotropy: along the nodal-line direction it is finite at charge neutrality, whereas in the transverse direction it vanishes quadratically with Fermi energy. On the other hand, the interband optical conductivity exhibits the same linear frequency dependence along both the longitudinal and transverse directions, with only a direction-dependent slope in the low-frequency regime. We further analyze the leading finite-temperature corrections to the intraband and interband optical conductivities, showing that the zero-temperature results remain valid up to experimentally relevant temperatures.

Introduction

The material dimensionality is of central importance in condensed matter physics as it governs the physical properties of materials. Reducing the dimensionality in particular can have strong consequences. For example, one-dimensional (1D) transport is governed by the universal quantum of conductance

Dirac SSH model

In this section, we briefly introduce the Dirac SSH model that captures the low-energy bands forming the nodal line
23
;
21
;
4
. The Nb
2n+1
Si
n
Te
4n+2
family consists of an array of quasi-1D
\mathrm{NbTe_{2}}
metallic chains embedded in a 2D semiconductor, with the integer
n
controlling the separation between neighboring chains. An isolated
\mathrm{NbTe_{2}}
chain can be described by an SSH-like model with two sites in each unit cell. Unlike the conventional SSH model, however, the nonsymmorphic glide-mirror symmetry enforces equal nearest-neighbor hoppings along the chain, thus forbidding dimerization and keeping the spectrum gapless. Including the interchain coupling between neighboring chains then yields the following Dirac SSH Hamiltonian for the low-energy bands:

\mathcal{H}(\bm{k})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\phi(\bm{k})\\
\phi^{*}(\bm{k})&0\end{pmatrix},

(1)

where
\phi(\bm{k})=\bigl(t+t^{\prime}e^{-ik_{y}l_{y}}\bigr)\bigl(1+e^{-ik_{x}l_{x}}\bigr)
,
l_{x}
and
l_{y}
are the lattice constants and
t
(
t^{\prime}
) denotes the intrachain (interchain) hopping in the effective coupled-chain picture. The energy dispersions are given by
\varepsilon_{\pm}(\bm{k})=\pm|\phi(\bm{k})|
with
|\phi(\bm{k})|^{2}=4\cos^{2}\!\frac{k_{x}l_{x}}{2}\,A(k_{y}l_{y})^{2}

where
A(k_{y}l_{y})\equiv\sqrt{t^{2}+t^{\prime 2}+2tt^{\prime}\cos k_{y}l_{y}}
.
Note that the nodal line is formed along the Brillouin-zone edge
k_{x}l_{x}=\pi
, as shown in Fig.
1
. By expanding the energy dispersion around a point on the nodal line [
\bm{k}_{0}=(\pi/l_{x},k_{y})
] with a small deviation
\delta k_{x}
along the
x
axis, we obtain
\varepsilon_{\pm,\bm{k}}\approx\pm\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})\delta k_{x}
, where
v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})=l_{x}A(k_{y}l_{y})/\hbar
is the
k_{y}
-dependent Fermi velocity that describes low-energy dynamics near the nodal line.
Note that this form of dispersion clearly shows the quasi-1D nature of the low-energy physics: for each fixed
k_{y}
the low-energy dispersion reduces to a 1D Dirac dispersion along the
x
direction, while
k_{y}
parametrizes the family of chains.

II.2
Kubo formula

In this work, we limit our scope to the linear optical response. The optical conductivity can be obtained from the Kubo formula
13
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}(\omega)=

\displaystyle-g\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\int_{BZ}\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{f_{s,\bm{k}}-f_{s^{\prime},\bm{k}}}{\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{s^{\prime},\bm{k}}}

(2)

\displaystyle\times\frac{M_{i}^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{k})M_{j}^{s^{\prime}s}(\bm{k})}{\hbar\omega+\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{s^{\prime},\bm{k}}+i\Gamma}

where
g=2
is the spin degeneracy,
i,j=x,y
and
s,s^{\prime}=\pm
represent band indices. Here,
f_{s,\bm{k}}\equiv f(\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}};\mu,T)
is the Fermi distribution function with
f(\varepsilon;\mu,T)=1/\left[1+e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right]
, where
\mu
is the chemical potential and
M_{i}^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{k})=\langle s,\bm{k}|\hbar\hat{v}_{i}|s^{\prime},\bm{k}\rangle
is the velocity matrix element with the velocity operator
\hat{v}_{i}
obtained from the relation
\hat{v}_{i}=\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}/\partial(\hbar k_{i})
. Here
\Gamma=\hbar/\tau
is a phenomenological parameter with
\tau
representing the quasi-particle lifetime limited by disorders or interactions, and the clean non-interacting limit corresponds to
\Gamma\rightarrow 0
.

III.1
Drude Weight

) and at finite doping (

) with
\Gamma=0.001t
.

In this subsection, we examine the intraband contribution to the optical conductivity at zero temperature. For intraband transitions, the term
(f_{s,\bm{k}}-f_{s^{\prime},\bm{k}})/(\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{s^{\prime},\bm{k}})
in Eq. (
2
) reduces to the derivative of the Fermi distribution and the velocity matrix element simplifies to
M_{i}^{ss}(\bm{k})=\partial\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}/\partial k_{i}
. Then, the optical conductivity takes the form
\sigma^{\mathrm{intra}}_{ii}(\omega)=iD_{ii}(\mu)/(\hbar\omega+i\Gamma)
with the Drude weight

D_{ii}(\mu)=e^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\varepsilon\;\Phi_{ii}(\varepsilon)\left[-\frac{\partial f_{s,\bm{k}}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right].

(3)

where

\Phi_{ii}(\varepsilon)=g\sum_{s}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,v_{s,i}(\bm{k})^{2}\,\delta\!\big(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}\big)

(4)

is the transport spectral function.
At zero temperature, the derivative of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function with respect to Fermi energy becomes a negative delta function, i.e.,
-\partial f_{s,\bm{k}}/\partial\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}\rightarrow\delta(\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})
. Thus the relation between the Drude weight
D_{ii}(\mu)
and the transport spectral function
\Phi_{ii}(\varepsilon)
reduces to

D_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})=e^{2}\Phi_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}),

(5)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Fermi energy. We restrict the following analysis to
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\geq 0
, where only the conduction band (
s=+
) crosses the Fermi level. This restriction follows from the electron-hole symmetry of the Dirac SSH model, which ensures that the optical conductivity depends only on
|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}|
.

We first consider the low-doping regime
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\ll\absolutevalue{t-t'}
, where we obtain the Drude weight up to the second order of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
with
D_{ii}=D^{0}_{ii}+\delta D_{ii}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. We recast
\Phi_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})
into the form

\Phi_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})=\int_{-\pi/l_{y}}^{\pi/l_{y}}\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}};k_{y})

(6)

where

\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}};k_{y})=g\sum_{s}\int\frac{dk_{x}}{2\pi}\,v_{s,i}(\bm{k})^{2}\,\delta\!\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}-\varepsilon_{s,\bm{k}}\right),

(7)

is the transport spectral function of an isolated 1D Dirac chain at transverse momentum
k_{y}
.
In the linear approximation, the energy dispersion of the conduction band near the nodal line is
\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}\approx\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})|k_{x}|
, where

v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})=\frac{l_{x}}{\hbar}\sqrt{t^{2}+t^{\prime 2}+2tt^{\prime}\cos(k_y l_y)},

(8)

and thus
v_{+,x}(\bm{k})^{2}=v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})^{2}
is independent of
k_{x}
. The
k_{x}
integral in Eq. (
7
) can then easily be performed analytically, giving
\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{xx}(k_{y})=g\,v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})/(\pi\hbar)
, and the 1D Drude weight

D^{\mathrm{1D}}_{xx}(k_{y})=g\frac{e^{2}v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})}{\pi\hbar}.

(9)

This expression is precisely the Drude weight of a 1D massless Dirac liquid
8
, where the appearance of
\hbar
reflects the quantum nature. Note that the 1D Drude weight is independent of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
because for 1D Dirac fermions the Fermi velocity is always
\pm v_{\mathrm{F}}
, regardless of the doping level relative to charge neutrality.
Using Eqs. (
5
) and (
7
) with the obtained
\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{xx}
immediately yields

D^{0}_{xx}=\frac{2ge^{2}}{\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}\,\frac{l_{x}}{l_{y}}\,(t+t^{\prime})\,E(m),

(10)

where we use the convention
E(m)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\sqrt{1-m\sin^{2}\theta}\,d\theta
. The elliptic parameter is related to
t
and
t^{\prime}
through

m=\frac{4tt^{\prime}}{(t+t^{\prime})^{2}}.

(11)

Note that Eq. (
10
), obtained with the linearized dispersion, gives the
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
-independent leading term
D^{0}_{xx}
in the expansion
D_{xx}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})=D^{0}_{xx}+\delta D_{xx}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
.
This is because
D_{xx}
is simply the
k_{y}
-average of
D^{\mathrm{1D}}_{xx}(k_{y})
, thus inheriting its
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
-independence.

The leading
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
correction to
D_{xx}
, i.e.,
\delta D_{xx}
, arises from the nonlinearity of the band dispersion away from the nodal line. Expanding up to the third order in
k_{x}
, we obtain the energy dispersion

\varepsilon_{\pm,\bm{k}}\approx\pm\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})|k_{x}|\mp\frac{\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})l_{x}^{2}}{24}\,|k_{x}|^{3},

(12)

which shifts the Fermi momentum and thereby modifies the Fermi velocity.
The leading-order solution of
\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
in Eq. (
12
) for
k_{x}
is
k_{x}^{*}\approx k_{0}+k_{0}^{3}l_{x}^{2}/24
where
k_{0}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}/\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})
. The Fermi velocity up to the second order of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
is written as

\begin{split}v_{x}(k_{x}^{*},k_{y})&=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left.\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{{+},\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{x}}\right|_{k_{x}^{*}}\\
&\approx v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})\!\left[1-\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{8\hbar^{2}v_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}(k_{y})}\right].\end{split}

(13)

Evaluating the 1D delta-function integral in
\Phi_{xx}^{\mathrm{1D}}
[Eq. (
7
)] with the two symmetric Fermi-surface roots
\pm k_{x}^{*}
, we obtain the quadratic correction in
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
to the transport spectral function

\delta\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{xx}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}};k_{y})=-\frac{gl_{x}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{8A(k_{y}l_{y})}.

(14)

Then, the Drude weight correction of the second order of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
is obtained through the
k_{y}
-average of
\delta\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{xx}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}};k_{y})
and Eq. (
3
):

\delta D_{xx}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})=-\frac{ge^{2}l_{x}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\int_{-\pi/l_{y}}^{\pi/l_{y}}\!\!\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{8A(k_{y}l_{y})}.

(15)

Using the complete elliptic integrals of the first kind
K(m)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!(1-m\sin^{2}\theta)^{-1/2}\,d\theta
,
D_{xx}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})
is expressed as

\begin{split}D_{xx}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})&=\frac{2ge^{2}}{\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}\frac{l_{x}}{l_{y}}\left[\left(t+t^{\prime}\right)\,E(m)-\frac{K(m)}{8(t+t^{\prime})}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right],\end{split}

(16)

The negative
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
correction arises because at finite doping the Fermi velocity is reduced below its linear-dispersion value
v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})
[Eq. (
13
)].

The Drude weight along the
y
-direction in the low-doping limit can also be obtained asymptotically using the same approach. Within the linear band approximation, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{\pm,\bm{k}}\approx\pm\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})|k_{x}|
, we obtain

\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{yy}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}};k_{y})=\frac{g(tt^{\prime})^{2}l_{y}^{2}\sin^{2}(k_{y}l_{y})\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{\pi\hbar^{2}l_{x}A(k_{y}l_{y})^{5}}.

(17)

In order to obtain the Drude weight through
D_{yy}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})=e^{2}\Phi_{yy}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})
, we need to take the
k_{y}
-average of
e^{2}\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{yy}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})
, which requires evaluating

\frac{1}{2\pi l_{y}}\!\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{2}\varphi}{A(\varphi)^{5}}\,d\varphi=\frac{8}{\pi l_{y}(t+t^{\prime})^{5}}I(m),

(18)

where

\begin{split}I(m)=&\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\frac{\sin^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\varphi}{(1-m\sin^{2}\varphi)^{5/2}}\,d\varphi\\
=&\frac{(2-m)E(m)-2(1-m)K(m)}{3m^{2}(1-m)}.\end{split}

(19)

Here,
K(m)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-m\sin^{2}\theta}}
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind.
Substituting Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
) into the
k_{y}
-average of
\Phi^{\mathrm{1D}}_{yy}(k_{y};\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})
, we obtain the Drude weight along the
y
direction

D_{yy}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})=\frac{ge^{2}}{3\hbar^{2}\pi^{2}}\frac{l_{y}}{l_{x}}\frac{(t^{2}+t^{\prime 2})E(m)-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}K(m)}{(t-t^{\prime})^{2}(t+t^{\prime})}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

(20)

It should be noted that
D_{yy}
vanishes at charge neutrality and grows as
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
in contrast to
D_{xx}
, which is finite at charge neutrality and decreases as
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. Moreover, in the small-
t^{\prime}
limit,
t^{\prime}\ll t
(corresponding to a large number of layers),
D_{xx}
approaches
gD_{0}l_{x}/\pi l_{y}
, whereas
D_{yy}\to 0
, where
D_{0}=te^{2}/\hbar^{2}
. This behavior of the Drude weight is a direct consequence of the quasi-1D nature of the band structure in the small-
t^{\prime}
limit: at leading order, the system behaves as a collection of 1D Dirac node along the
x
direction, thus suppressing all

-directed transport while leaving

-directed transport finite.

III.2
Interband optical conductivity

We now consider the interband contribution to the optical conductivity. As in the previous section, we first present asymptotic analytic results in the low-frequency regime (
\hbar\omega\ll|t-t^{\prime}|
), where interband transitions occur predominantly near the nodal line. For the analytical derivation we take the clean limit (
\Gamma\to 0
). Although this approximation would produce a very small quantitative deviation from the numerical results shown in Fig.
3
, where
\Gamma=0.001t
is used, it does not alter the qualitative behavior because the relevant photon energies for interband transitions are much larger than
\Gamma
.

Within the clean limit, the real part of the interband optical conductivity follows from the Kubo formula [Eq. (
2
)] as

\begin{split}\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{ii}(\omega)&=g\frac{\pi e^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{s\neq s^{\prime}}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{f_{s,\bm{k}}-f_{s^{\prime},\bm{k}}}{\Delta\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}s}(\bm{k})}\\
&\quad\times\bigl|M_{i}^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{k})\bigr|^{2}\,\delta\!\left(\hbar\omega-\Delta\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}s}(\bm{k})\right),\end{split}

(21)

\Delta\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}s}(\bm{k})=\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}}(\bm{k})-\varepsilon_{s}(\bm{k})
. Note that on-shell,

\Delta\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}s}=\hbar\omega>0
, and thus only the
(s,s^{\prime})=(-,+)

pair contributes. For simplicity, we work at charge neutrality (
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}=0
). Note that a finite doping suppresses interband absorption for
\hbar\omega<2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
through Pauli blocking, opening a sharp optical gap at
\hbar\omega=2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
, as discussed in our numerical results below.

For the
x
- and
y
-components of the matrix elements, with straightforward algebra one finds

\bigl|M_{x}^{-+}(\bm{k})\bigr|^{2}=\frac{l_{x}^{2}}{4}\,\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}^{2},

(22)

and

\bigl|M_{y}^{-+}(\bm{k})\bigr|^{2}=\frac{t^{\prime 2}l_{y}^{2}\,\bigl(t^{\prime}+t\cos(k_yl_y)\bigr)^{2}}{A(k_{y}l_{y})^{4}}\,\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}^{2}.

(23)

Both matrix elements are proportional to
\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}^{2}
, differing only in the direction-dependent prefactor. Since
\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}^{2}
becomes the constant
(\hbar\omega/2)^{2}
under the on-shell condition
\Delta\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}s}(\bm{k})=\hbar\omega
, the momentum dependence is entirely contained in the prefactor. Then, one encounters the following formula during the
k_{x}
integration of Eq. (
21
):

\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\!\!dk_{x}\,\delta\!\bigl(\hbar\omega-2|k_{x}|l_{x}A(k_{y}l_{y})\bigr)=\frac{1}{l_{x}A(k_{y}l_{y})}\,\Theta\!\bigl(\Lambda-k_{x}^{*}\bigr)

(24)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside function,
k_{x}^{*}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2l_{x}A(k_{y}l_{y})}
, and
\Lambda
is the momentum cutoff for the low-energy effective model. In the low-frequency regime we are considering here,
k_{x}^{*}\ll\Lambda
and thus we drop the step function in the derivations that follow. Substituting Eqs. (
22
) and (
24
) into Eq. (
21
) with
\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}}=\hbar\omega/2
and performing the
k_{y}
integral via the substitution
\varphi=k_{y}l_{y}
, together with the integral result

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!d\varphi\frac{1}{A(\varphi)}=\frac{4K(m)}{t+t^{\prime}},

(25)

we obtain the real part of the optical conductivity contributed solely by interband transitions along the
x
axis, given by

\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=g\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\,\frac{l_{x}}{l_{y}}\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{16\pi(t+t^{\prime})}\,K(m).

(26)

The optical conductivity along the
y
direction can be obtained similarly. Substituting Eq. (
23
) and the
k_{x}
integration result [Eq. (
24
)] into Eq. (
21
),
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{yy}(\omega)
is written as

\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{yy}(\omega)=\frac{ge^{2}t^{\prime 2}l_{y}\omega}{16\pi l_{x}}\,I_{y}(t,t^{\prime}),

(27)

where

I_{y}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!d\theta\,\frac{\bigl(t^{\prime}+t\cos\theta\bigr)^{2}}{A(\theta)^{5}}.

(28)

To evaluate
I_{y}
in closed form, we differentiate both sides of Eq. (
25
) twice with respect to
t^{\prime}
. Making use of the relation
\partial^{2}A^{-1}/\partial t^{\prime 2}=3(t^{\prime}+t\cos\theta)^{2}A^{-5}-A^{-3}
, we obtain the following equality

\begin{split}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{\prime 2}}\left[\frac{4K(m)}{t+t^{\prime}}\right]=&\frac{2}{(t+t^{\prime})t^{\prime 2}}\!\left[K(m)+\frac{3t^{\prime 2}-t^{2}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{2}}\,E(m)\right].\\
=&3I_{y}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{4}{(t+t^{\prime})(t-t^{\prime})^{2}}\,E(m).\end{split}

(29)

Solving Eq. (
29
) for
I_{y}
and substituting it into Eq. (
27
), we obtain

\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{yy}(\omega)=g\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\,\frac{l_{y}}{l_{x}}\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{24\pi(t+t^{\prime})}\!\left[K(m)+\frac{5t^{\prime 2}-t^{2}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{2}}\,E(m)\right].

(30)

It is worth comparing our results with those of other nodal-line semimetals. For a 2D nodal-line semimetal whose nodal ring is protected by
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry or mirror symmetry, no interband optical transitions can be induced and the optical conductivity is purely contributed from intraband transitions
3
. For a 3D nodal-ring semimetal, the interband conductivity saturates to a frequency-independent constant along the axial direction and grows as
\sigma\propto\omega
along the radial direction
3
. In contrast, both
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}
and
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{yy}
of the present 2D nonsymmorphic nodal-line semimetal grow linearly with
\omega
at low frequencies [Eqs. (
26
) and (
30
)], providing a distinctive optical signature of the nonsymmorphic nodal-line physics that is absent in both 2D symmorphic and 3D nodal-ring systems.

Figure
3
(a) shows our numerically calculated
results at charge neutrality (i.e.,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}=0
). Note that both conductivities grow linearly at low frequencies, consistent with the analytic formulas derived above (dashed lines). A sharp peak at higher frequencies arises from transitions near the van Hove singularity, at which the joint density of states diverges [Fig.
3
(b)].
At finite doping [Fig.
3
(c),
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}=0.1t
], interband transitions are suppressed for
\hbar\omega<2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
with a sharp onset at
\hbar\omega=2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
(d).

IV
Finite-temperature effects

In this section, we extend our previous analysis of the zero-temperature results to finite temperature
T>0
. At finite temperature, the Drude weight can be calculated using Eq. (
3
) with the chemical potential
\mu(T)
determined from the condition of fixed carrier density measured relative to charge neutrality:

n_{\mathrm{ex}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon\,\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon)\left[f(\varepsilon;\mu,T)-f(\varepsilon;-\mu,T)\right].

(31)

Here,
n_{\mathrm{ex}}
denotes the excess carrier density relative to charge neutrality.
Within the linear approximation
\varepsilon_{\pm,\bm{k}}\approx\pm\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{y})|k_{x}|
, the low-energy density of states is constant,

\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{D}_{0}=\frac{2gK(m)}{\pi^{2}l_{x}l_{y}(t+t^{\prime})}.

(32)

Applying the Sommerfeld expansion to Eq. (
31
), we obtain

n_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathcal{D}_{0}\mu(T)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}+\mathcal{O}(T^{4})

(33)

With
\partial\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon)/\partial\varepsilon=0
for the constant low-energy density of states,
n_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathcal{D}_{0}\mu(T)
.
Since
n_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathcal{D}_{0}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
at
T=0
, we obtain

\mu(T)=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}},

(34)

indicating that the chemical potential does not shift with temperature at any order in
T
within the linear approximation. This is qualitatively distinct from familiar cases such as graphene, where the density of states is linear in energy and
\mu
shifts at leading order with
\mu\approx\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}[1-\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}/6\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]
and the 2D electron gas, where
\mu\approx\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}[1-\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}/12\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]
.

Including the cubic term in the dispersion [Eq. (
12
)],
the density of states acquires an energy-dependent correction. Performing the
k_{x}
integration with the cubic dispersion, retaining terms up to the second order of
\varepsilon
, gives

\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon)\approx\mathcal{D}_{0}\left(1+\beta\varepsilon^{2}\right),

(35)

where

\beta=\frac{E(m)}{8K(m)(t-t^{\prime})^{2}}.

(36)

Substituting Eq. (
35
) into the Sommerfeld expansion of Eq. (
31
), and using the fixed-density condition, we obtain the chemical potential to order
T^{2}
after straightforward algebra:

\mu(T)\approx\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}-\frac{\pi^{2}\beta}{3}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}}{1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}.

(37)

Note that the correction is of relative order
\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\sim\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/(t-t^{\prime})^{2}
, which is small in the low-doping regime
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\ll|t-t^{\prime}|
.

We now calculate the finite-temperature Drude weight including the
shift of the chemical potential derived in Eq. (
37
).
Using the Sommerfeld expansion, we approximate the Drude weight at finite temperature as

D_{ii}(T)\approx D_{ii}(\mu)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}D^{\prime\prime}_{ii}(\mu)

(38)

where primes denote differentiation with respect to
\mu
. By expanding
D_{ii}(\mu)=D_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})+D^{\prime}_{ii}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})\delta\mu+\mathcal{O}(\delta\mu^{2})
with
\delta\mu=\mu(T)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
given by Eq. (
37
), we obtain
D_{xx}(\mu)=D_{xx}^{0}-C_{xx}\mu^{2}
, where

D_{xx}^{0}=\frac{2ge^{2}}{\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}\frac{l_{x}}{l_{y}}(t+t^{\prime})E(m),

(39)

and

C_{xx}=\frac{ge^{2}l_{x}}{\hbar^{2}l_{y}}\frac{K(m)}{4\pi^{2}(t+t^{\prime})}.

(40)

Substituting
D^{\prime\prime}_{xx}(\mu)=-2C_{xx}
into Eq. (
38
), we obtain the finite-temperature Drude weight along the
x
direction,

D_{xx}(T)=\frac{2ge^{2}}{\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}\frac{l_{x}}{l_{y}}(t+t^{\prime})E(m)-\frac{ge^{2}l_{x}K(m)}{4\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}l_{y}(t+t^{\prime})}\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}}{3}\,\frac{1-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\right].

(41)

Using a similar algebraic calculation, we obtain
D_{yy}(\mu)=C_{yy}\mu^{2}
, where

C_{yy}=\frac{ge^{2}l_{y}}{\hbar^{2}l_{x}}\frac{(t^{2}+t^{\prime 2})E(m)-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}K(m)}{3\pi^{2}(t-t^{\prime})^{2}(t+t^{\prime})},

(42)

With
D^{\prime\prime}_{yy}=2C_{yy}
substituted into Eq. (
38
), the finite-temperature Drude weight along the
y
direction is then

D_{yy}(T)=\frac{ge^{2}l_{y}}{\hbar^{2}l_{x}}\frac{(t^{2}+t^{\prime 2})E(m)-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}K(m)}{3\pi^{2}(t-t^{\prime})^{2}(t+t^{\prime})}\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}}{3}\,\frac{1-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\right].

(43)

It is worth noting that the finite-temperature effects are strongly anisotropic and opposite in sign:
D_{xx}
decreases with
T
, while
D_{yy}
increases with
T
.
To order
T^{2}
, both results for
D_{xx}
and
D_{yy}
can be obtained from the zero-temperature Drude weights by the substitution

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\to\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}}{3}\,\frac{1-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}.

(44)

In the low-doping limit where the linear band approximation is valid, the substitution reduces to the simple replacement
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\to\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}/3
, which is the standard Sommerfeld result for a system with an energy-independent density of states. The correction factor
(1-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})/(1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})
captures the suppression of the thermal correction due to the downward shift
\delta\mu<0
[Eq. (
37
)] arising from the nonlinear band curvature beyond the linear band regime.

For typical parameters of the Nb
2n+1
Si
n
Te
4n+2
family (
t\sim 1
eV,

t^{\prime}\sim 0.2t
), the thermal energy scale
\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}/3\sim 10^{-3}
eV
2
at room temperature is three orders of magnitude smaller than
t^{2}
. Likewise, the chemical potential shift
|\delta\mu|\sim\pi^{2}\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}/[3(1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})]\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
is also negligibly small at room temperature. This finding suggests that the zero-temperature results of Eqs. (
16
) and (
20
) provide an excellent approximation at all experimentally accessible temperatures.

In the following, we discuss the finite-temperature effects on the interband optical conductivity. At finite temperature, the Fermi factors in Eq. (
21
) become
f_{-,\bm{k}}-f_{+,\bm{k}}=1-2f_{+,\bm{k}}=1-2f(\varepsilon_{+,\bm{k}};\mu,T)
, with
\mu(T)
given by Eq. (
37
). This leads to two distinct finite-temperature effects. First, the sharp optical gap at
\hbar\omega=2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
is replaced by a thermally smeared edge at
\hbar\omega=2\mu(T)
:

1-2f(\hbar\omega/2;\mu,T)=\tanh\!\frac{\hbar\omega-2\mu(T)}{4k_{\mathrm{B}}T},

(45)

with the gap edge shifting slightly downward from
2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
to
2\mu(T)<2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
at finite
T
, by an amount
2|\delta\mu|
[Eq. (
37
)]. Second, the low-frequency analytic results [Eqs. (
26
) and (
30
)], which were derived at
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}=0
, remain unchanged for
\hbar\omega\gg k_{\mathrm{B}}T
since the thermal factor[Eq. (
45
)] saturates to unity.
For
\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
at charge neutrality, the thermal factor in Eq. (
45
) reduces to
\hbar\omega/4k_{\mathrm{B}}T
. Since the zero-temperature interband conductivity is linear in
\omega
, this implies a crossover from
\sigma\propto\omega
for
\hbar\omega\gg k_{\mathrm{B}}T
to
\sigma\propto\omega^{2}/T
for
\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
. Such a crossover may be observable in sufficiently clean samples with
\Gamma\ll\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, where the interband optical conductivity is not entirely obscured by the Drude peak. In practice, the crossover should be easier to observe in the
y
-direction response, where the Drude peak remains much weaker than in the
x
direction, as already seen in the zero-temperature results.

V
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we have studied the optical conductivity of the Nb
2n+1
Si
n
Te
4n+2
family materials.
We first investigated the intraband optical conductivity. At low doping
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\ll|t-t^{\prime}|
, we showed that the Drude weight along the direction of the nodal line is finite at charge neutrality with Planck’s constant appearing, implying that the longitudinal Drude weight inherits the quantum nature of 1D Dirac physics. In contrast, the transverse Drude weight vanishes quadratically with Fermi energy as in an ordinary metal. In the high-doping regime, we showed that a kink structure in the Drude weight arises from the Lifshitz transition between open and closed Fermi surfaces.
For the interband optical conductivity, we showed that both
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{xx}
and
\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{yy}
shows linear frequency dependence in the low-frequency regime with only a direction-dependent slope, despite the anisotropic quasi 1Delectronic structure. At higher frequencies, both conductivities develop a sharp peak arising from interband transitions near the van Hove singularity.

Regarding finite-temperature effects, we showed that, within the linear band approximation, the chemical potential does not deviate from the Fermi energy due to the energy-independent density of states. Accounting for the nonlinear band curvature (up to cubic order), however, we showed that the chemical potential shifts downward and the resulting finite-temperature corrections to the Drude weight can be captured by the simple substitution
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\to\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^{2}}{3}\frac{1-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{1+\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}
in the zero-temperature result. For typical material parameters of the Nb
2n+1
Si
n
Te
4n+2
family, the thermal corrections at room temperature are several orders of magnitude smaller than
t^{2}
, and thus the zero-temperature results should provide an excellent approximation at experimentally accessible temperatures.

In conclusion, we reveal optical fingerprints of the 1D nature of the Nb
2n+1
Si
n
Te
4n+2
family of nodal-line semimetals, providing both physical insight into their optical response and concrete guidance for their experimental identification.
