Facilitators and barriers to medication adherence in systemic lupus erythematosus: a qualitative study in China
Authors: Yulin Zou, Nur Zakiah Mohd Saat, Ching Sin Siau, Sheng Wang, Zhigao Yang, Yue Che, Youfang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedication adherence in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) remains suboptimal worldwide due to complex psychosocial and structural barriers. However, few studies have explored clinical experts’ perspectives on facilitators and barriers to adherence, particularly within the Chinese healthcare context. This study aimed to explore clinical experts’ perspectives on medication adherence among patients with SLE and to identify multidimensional factors influencing adherence. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with clinical experts and transcribed verbatim. Data were analysed using thematic analysis with NVivo 12. Themes were organized into five predefined dimensions: patient-related, disease-related, treatment-related, medical care-related, and socioeconomic-related factors. Each theme was classified as either a facilitator or a barrier. Visual analytic methods, including heatmaps, word clouds, frequency matrices, Sankey diagrams, and bipartite network graphs, were used to illustrate expert consensus and thematic relationships. A total of 38 themes were identified, including 18 facilitators and 20 barriers. All 16 experts identified symptom relief or mild disease, complex treatment regimens, distressing medication side effects, and high medication costs among the most frequently reported barriers. The most commonly reported facilitators included effectively managed side effects and personalized health education. Themes were unevenly distributed across dimensions, with a higher concentration of facilitators in medical care-related factors and a predominance of barriers within treatment-related factors. Variations in thematic emphasis may reflect differences in clinical roles and levels of patient engagement among experts. Medication adherence in patients with SLE is influenced by multiple interrelated factors. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease requiring long-term immunosuppressive therapy to prevent flares and preserve organ function1–3. However, medication non-adherence remains a major global challenge, with reported rates ranging from 30% to 80%4–6, and is associated with increased disease activity and poorer clinical outcomes.

Previous studies have identified multiple factors influencing adherence in SLE, including treatment complexity, psychological distress, and health literacy, primarily using quantitative or patient-reported approaches7. While informative, these approaches may not fully capture context-specific barriers observed in routine clinical practice. Healthcare professionals (HCPs), including rheumatologists, nurses, and pharmacists, can provide valuable insights into the clinical and system-level factors shaping adherence behaviours.

In China, medication adherence may be further influenced by variability in healthcare systems, differences in insurance coverage, and culturally shaped beliefs about medications. Features of outpatient care, such as limited consultation time and lack of continuity, may also affect long-term treatment management.

However, qualitative studies exploring clinical experts’ perspectives on medication adherence among patients with SLE in China remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to explore facilitators and barriers to medication adherence among patients with SLE from the perspectives of clinical experts in a tertiary hospital in China using thematic analysis.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed thematic analysis following the six-phase process described by Braun and Clarke8. The study was underpinned by an interpretivist paradigm, acknowledging that clinical experts’ perspectives on medication adherence are socially and contextually constructed.

A hybrid analytic approach, incorporating both inductive and deductive elements, was adopted, as detailed in the data analysis section. This approach enabled the identification of data-driven themes while also allowing interpretation within an established theoretical framework.

To ensure transparency and rigor in reporting, the study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ-32). Reporting was also informed by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR). The completed COREQ checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Third Clinical Medical College of China Three Gorges University, Gezhouba Central Hospital of Sinopharm (Approval No. 2024Y003), and the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM) Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. JEP-2024-676). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team (not the study participants) comprised members with backgrounds in rheumatology, pharmacy, and nursing, all of whom held postgraduate qualifications and had training and experience in qualitative research and clinical practice in SLE management. Interviews were conducted by members of the research team with prior training in qualitative interviewing.

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study by documenting analytic decisions, conducting regular team discussions, and reflecting on how the researchers’ clinical backgrounds and assumptions may have influenced data interpretation.

Selection and recruitment of participants

This study used purposive sampling to recruit a diverse group of clinical experts directly involved in the clinical management of patients with SLE from a tertiary hospital in China. Inclusion criteria were: (1) currently working in the hospital’s rheumatology department or related departments involved in SLE management (including nephrology, dermatology, and clinical pharmacy); (2) at least five years of clinical experience in SLE; and (3) willingness to participate in audio-recorded interviews.

A total of 16 experts were recruited, including 9 rheumatologists, 4 nurses, and 3 pharmacists. The sample size was guided by thematic saturation; no new major themes emerged after the 14th interview, and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation.

Data collection

The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on existing literature on medication adherence and informed by the World Health Organization’s multidimensional framework of adherence. The initial guide was reviewed by members of the research team with expertise in rheumatology, pharmacy, and qualitative research to ensure clarity and content relevance. The interview guide was pilot-tested with two clinical experts, and minor refinements were made prior to data collection.

Interviews were conducted in person between January and March 2025. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 min and was guided by the semi-structured interview guide focusing on facilitators and barriers to adherence among patients with SLE. The full interview guide is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

The guide included open-ended questions such as: “What personal factors help or hinder SLE patients in adhering to medication regimens?” and “How do disease-related factors influence medication adherence in patients with SLE?”. Probing questions were used throughout to encourage elaboration and clarify responses.

Data processing and management

All interviews were conducted in Chinese. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Chinese and anonymized by assigning each participant an interview number (e.g., I01 to I16). The transcripts were translated into English by bilingual members of the research team, including a formally trained bilingual researcher. To ensure accuracy and consistency, the translated transcripts were reviewed and discussed within the research team to maintain fidelity to the original meaning. All data were managed using NVivo 12 software.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted following the six-phase approach described by Braun and Clarke, supported by NVivo 12 software. A hybrid analytic approach combining both deductive and inductive strategies was employed.

Deductively, an initial coding framework was developed based on the World Health Organization’s five dimensions of medication adherence (patient-related, disease-related, treatment-related, medical care-related, and socioeconomic-related factors). This framework provided a theoretically informed structure to organize the data and ensure alignment with established adherence frameworks.

Inductively, open coding was performed to allow new themes to emerge directly from the data beyond the predefined framework. The coding framework was iteratively refined through constant comparison and team discussions to ensure that it accurately reflected participants’ perspectives.

Two researchers (Y.L.Z. and S.W.) independently coded all transcripts. Coding discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions, and where necessary, a third senior reviewer (Z.G.Y.) was consulted to achieve agreement. This iterative process enhanced the credibility and consistency of the analysis.

Following coding, themes were identified, reviewed, and defined according to Braun and Clarke’s approach. All themes were mapped onto the five adherence dimensions and classified as facilitators or barriers based on expert interpretations. The final coding framework is provided in Supplementary Material 3.

To support data interpretation, visual analytic tools, including heatmaps, word clouds, frequency matrices, Sankey diagrams, and bipartite network graphs, were generated using Python (version 3.10) based on the coded qualitative data derived from the thematic analysis.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 16 clinical experts were interviewed, including 9 rheumatologists, 4 nurses, and 3 pharmacists from a tertiary hospital in China. The sample comprised 10 females and 6 males, aged 30–58 years. Most participants held a bachelor’s degree (n = 12), while others had a master’s degree (n = 3) or a doctorate (n = 1). Clinical experience in SLE ranged from 5 to 28 years (median, 15 years). Detailed participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Theme frequency across dimensions and endorsement

To provide an overview of expert consensus on medication adherence in patients with SLE, 38 themes were identified and categorized into five dimensions: patient-related, disease-related, treatment-related, medical care-related, and socioeconomic-related factors. These themes comprised 18 facilitators and 20 barriers.

The frequency of expert endorsement was assessed across all 16 interviews. Among facilitators, effectively managed side effects (16/16), personalized health education (16/16), digital tool utilization (15/16), convenient medical services (15/16), high-quality provider-patient communication (15/16), continuous follow-up and support (15/16), acute disease flare or obvious symptoms (14/16), multidisciplinary collaboration (13/16), and family and social support (13/16) were most frequently reported.

In contrast, commonly reported barriers included symptom relief or mild disease (16/16), complex treatment regimens (16/16), distressing medication side effects (16/16), high medication costs (16/16), limitations of digital tools (15/16), financial constraints leading to selective medication use (14/16), poor health literacy (14/16), and psychological burden or emotional issues (12/16).

The distribution of themes across dimensions was uneven. Medical care-related factors showed a higher concentration of frequently endorsed facilitator themes, whereas treatment-related factors exhibited a greater number of highly endorsed barrier themes.

The heatmap visually represents these patterns, with colour intensity corresponding to the number of experts endorsing each theme (see Fig. 1 and Supplementary Material 4).

Lexical patterns in facilitator and barrier narratives

Experts demonstrated distinct lexical patterns when describing facilitators versus barriers to medication adherence. Word frequency visualisations derived from coded transcripts were used to identify prominent recurring concepts in expert narratives.

For facilitators, frequently cited terms included doctor-patient communication (33), continuous follow-up (30), family support (29), multidisciplinary collaboration (27), and personalized education (26). These reflect the importance of interpersonal relationships, healthcare system engagement, and tailored information delivery in supporting medication adherence. Additional facilitator-related keywords included adequate insurance coverage (20), smart reminder tools (18), pharmacist consultation (14), online consultation (13), and charity fund (11), highlighting the role of both formal healthcare support and digital innovations (see Fig. 2A)).

A: Word cloud of facilitator-related terminology. B: Word cloud of barrier-related terminology.

In contrast, barrier narratives were characterized by terms such as medication side effects (25), inadequate disease awareness (24), treatment complexity (23), psychological stress (21), and self-discontinuation (17). Other commonly reported barriers included insufficient reimbursement (16), treatment fatigue (15), discontinuation after symptom relief (12), and difficult-to-use digital tools (12), indicating a combination of structural, emotional, and behavioural challenges. Terms such as healthcare resource constraints (10), low education level (10), and polypharmacy (8) further underscored the multifactorial nature of non-adherence (see Fig. 2B)).

These lexical patterns reinforce the themes identified in the coding framework and provide insights that may inform the development of patient-centred communication and adherence interventions (see Supplementary Material 5).

Expert agreement on key adherence influencers

The level of expert agreement on each adherence-related theme revealed clear patterns in perceived priorities. All 38 themes were ranked according to the number of experts (out of 16) who independently identified them, allowing comparison of consensus across the five predefined dimensions: patient-related, disease-related, treatment-related, medical care-related, and socioeconomic-related factors.

Among facilitators, several themes demonstrated high levels of agreement. Effectively managed side effects (16/16), personalized health education (16/16), digital tool utilization (15/16), convenient medical services (15/16), high-quality provider–patient communication (15/16), continuous follow-up and support (15/16), acute disease flare or obvious symptoms (14/16), multidisciplinary collaboration (13/16), and family and social support (13/16) were consistently emphasized. These findings primarily reflect strengths within medical care-related factors, alongside contributions from treatment-, socioeconomic-, and patient-related dimensions.

In contrast, agreement on barriers was more concentrated. Experts unanimously identified symptom relief or mild disease (16/16), complex treatment regimens (16/16), distressing medication side effects (16/16), high cost of medications (16/16), limitations of digital tools (15/16), financial burden leading to selective medication use (14/16), poor health literacy (14/16), and psychological burden or emotional issues (12/16) as key barriers. These high-consensus barriers spanned treatment-, disease-, patient-, and socioeconomic-related dimensions.

Digital tool utilization (15/16) was frequently identified as a facilitator, particularly for supporting medication reminders. However, limitations of digital tools (15/16) were also commonly reported as a barrier, particularly among older patients, which may reflect variability in acceptance and usability.

Overall, this ranking highlights the most consistently endorsed facilitators and barriers and provides an evidence base to inform targeted interventions and policy strategies (see Fig. 3).

Expert emphasis across dimensions

A Sankey diagram was developed to illustrate how expert attention flowed from dimensions to themes and subsequently to individual experts. The width of each stream corresponds to the number of experts (out of 16) who mentioned each theme, reflecting the level of consensus.

Across dimensions, medical care-related factors received the most concentrated expert attention. Frequently cited facilitators included personalized health education (16/16), convenient medical services (15/16), high-quality provider–patient communication (15/16), continuous follow-up and support (15/16), and multidisciplinary collaboration (13/16), suggesting strong agreement on the importance of healthcare delivery and service organization in supporting medication adherence.

Treatment-related factors were predominantly characterized by high-frequency barriers. Nearly all experts identified complex treatment regimens (16/16) and distressing medication side effects (16/16) as major barriers, while limitations of digital tools (15/16) were also commonly reported. In contrast, facilitators such as effectively managed side effects (16/16), digital tool utilization (15/16), and simplified treatment regimens (8/16) were identified as important enablers when treatment complexity and tolerability were addressed.

Within patient-related factors, both facilitators and barriers were identified; however, barriers received greater emphasis. Commonly cited barriers included poor health literacy (14/16) and psychological burden or emotional issues (12/16). In contrast, facilitators such as high health literacy (4/16), accurate disease perception (4/16), active self-management (4/16), and high psychological resilience (4/16) were less frequently mentioned.

For disease-related factors, acute disease flare or obvious symptoms (14/16) was the most emphasized facilitator, suggesting that heightened disease activity was often associated with improved adherence. Conversely, symptom relief or mild disease (16/16) emerged as the most frequently reported barrier. Socioeconomic-related factors predominantly reflected systemic and financial challenges. Barriers such as high cost of medications (16/16) and financial burden leading to selective medication use (14/16) received strong consensus. Expert–theme interaction network

A bipartite expert–theme interaction network was constructed to visualize the relationships between the 16 experts (I01–I16) and the facilitator and barrier themes they discussed. Experts and themes were represented as two distinct node types, with themes color-coded as facilitators or barriers. The size of each theme node reflected the number of experts endorsing that theme, indicating the level of consensus.

Several themes demonstrated strong consensus across experts. Barrier themes such as symptom relief or mild disease, complex treatment regimens, distressing medication side effects, and high cost of medications were mentioned by all experts (16/16), indicating unanimous agreement. Similarly, facilitators including effectively managed side effects and personalized health education were also endorsed by all experts (16/16). Other highly connected facilitators included digital tool utilization, high-quality provider-patient communication, continuous follow-up and support, and convenient medical services, each linked to the majority of experts.

Variation in node connectivity reflected differences in thematic breadth rather than disagreement. Experts I02 and I03 were linked to the widest range of themes (37 connections each), followed by Expert I01 (36 connections), indicating extensive thematic coverage. In contrast, Experts I13, I11, and I12 showed fewer connections (16, 14, and 14 links, respectively), suggesting a more focused narrative scope.

Importantly, even experts with fewer connections remained linked to core high-consensus themes, indicating overall conceptual alignment. Patterns of thematic co-occurrence were also evident. For example, patient-related barriers such as poor health literacy were frequently linked with psychological burden, suggesting that cognitive and emotional challenges were often discussed together. Simplified treatment regimens were commonly associated with digital tool utilization, indicating that technological support was viewed as a complementary strategy to reduce treatment complexity.

Overall, the network illustrates both convergence and variation in expert perspectives and reveals clustered relationships between themes. These findings provide a structured understanding of how facilitators and barriers interact across dimensions (see Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study identified 38 themes influencing medication adherence among patients with SLE, as reported by multidisciplinary clinical experts, spanning five domains: patient-related, disease-related, treatment-related, medical care-related, and socioeconomic-related factors. These findings highlight the complex and multidimensional nature of adherence in real-world clinical settings.

Treatment-related factors emerged as the most consistently reported barriers, particularly complex regimens and medication side effects, which are well recognized in SLE management9–11. Misunderstanding of medications and unrealistic expectations further emphasize the need for clearer communication and patient education12. Conversely, simplified regimens and effective management of side effects were identified as key facilitators, supporting the importance of personalized treatment and shared decision making13–16. Digital tools showed mixed effects, suggesting the need for tailored and user-friendly implementation strategies17,18.

Patient-related factors were strongly influenced by health literacy, cognitive capacity, and psychological burden. Limited understanding of disease, emotional distress, and behavioural challenges such as forgetfulness contributed to non-adherence, consistent with previous findings19,20. In contrast, psychological resilience and active self-management were associated with better adherence, highlighting the importance of integrating educational and psychosocial support interventions21,22.

Medical care-related factors were predominantly facilitators, particularly high-quality provider-patient communication, continuous follow-up, and multidisciplinary collaboration. These findings underscore the importance of relationship continuity and team-based care in supporting long-term adherence.

Disease-related factors demonstrated a dual effect. Acute symptoms and disease severity appeared to be associated with improved adherence, whereas symptom relief and the chronic nature of SLE reduced patients’ perceived need for ongoing treatment. This pattern may reflect reactive adherence behaviour widely reported in chronic conditions23,24, and emphasizes the need for sustained patient education and monitoring even during periods of clinical stability25–27.

Socioeconomic-related factors, although less frequently discussed, were consistently identified as important barriers. Financial burden, medication costs, and limited insurance coverage remain key challenges despite ongoing healthcare reforms28–30. Strengthening financial protection and improving access to long-term therapies may play a critical role in supporting adherence31.

Overall, these findings suggest that interventions to improve medication adherence in SLE may benefit from a multidimensional approach, integrating patient education, treatment optimization, healthcare system support, and policy-level interventions.

Limitations

As this study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in Yichang, China, findings may not be transferable to other settings with different healthcare infrastructures or patient populations. Furthermore, the study focused solely on expert perspectives, without direct input from patients. Although some patient-related insights were inferred from clinical experiences shared by experts, the absence of firsthand patient narratives limits the ability to capture the full spectrum of adherence challenges.

Conclusion

As perceived by multidisciplinary clinical experts, this qualitative study highlights the complexity and multifaceted nature of medication adherence in patients with SLE. Experts identified facilitators and barriers influencing adherence behaviour across patient-, disease-, treatment-, medical care-, and socioeconomic-related factors.

Barriers with the strongest consensus included treatment complexity, medication side effects, and symptom relief or mild disease status. All experts identified these as key contributors to adherence issues. Other commonly reported barriers included low health literacy, psychological stress, and financial burden due to high medication costs and low reimbursement.

Facilitators with high expert consensus included effectively managed side effects, personalized health education, high-quality provider-patient communication, convenient medical services, and digital tools. Many of these factors were primarily classified under medical care-related factors, underscoring the importance of system-level interventions.

Overall, these findings highlight the need for multidimensional strategies to improve medication adherence in SLE. Interventions may benefit from integrating patient education, psychological support, simplified treatment regimens, and accessible, continuous care. Economic support measures, including improved insurance coverage and charitable programs, may further reduce financial barriers. Designing tailored, system-supported adherence interventions informed by real-world clinical insights may enhance treatment continuity and long-term outcomes for patients with SLE.
