Lumbar microspur-related ventral cerebrospinal fluid leak localized by digital subtraction myelography: A case report
Authors: Daito Nakamura, Jun Tsugawa, Ritsurou Inoue, Yuko Kimura, Sho Takeshita, Hironori Fukumoto, Hayatsura Hanada, Kimiya Sakamoto, Kouhei Nii, Toshio Higashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is commonly caused by spinal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. Microspur-related ventral dural tears are an increasingly recognized etiology of SIH; however, lumbar lesions remain extremely rare. A 41-year-old woman presented with severe orthostatic headache and nausea. Whole-spine magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated spinal longitudinal extradural CSF collection (SLEC), suggesting spinal CSF leakage. Computed tomography myelography identified a small calcified lesion at the L1/2 level consistent with a discogenic microspur compressing the ventral dural sac. Digital subtraction myelography (DSM) demonstrated active contrast extravasation into the epidural space at the corresponding level, consistent with a ventral dural tear caused by the microspur. Targeted epidural blood patch treatment at the L1/2 level resulted in rapid symptom resolution without recurrence during 18 months of follow-up. This case demonstrates that lumbar microspurs can cause SLEC-positive SIH through ventral dural injury. DSM was useful for dynamic localization of the active CSF leak and facilitated targeted treatment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of lumbar microspur-induced SIH confirmed by DSM

Introduction

Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is characterized by orthostatic headaches caused by spinal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak without a preceding dural puncture or trauma that may lead to a CSF fistula [1]. SIH has an estimated incidence of approximately 5 per 100,000 person-years, with a female predominance (female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1). However, the actual incidence may be higher due to increased awareness and improved diagnostic sensitivity [2]. The exact cause of spontaneous spinal CSF leak remains unknown; however, recent reports have suggested that microspurs and calcified discogenic microprojections may contribute to this condition [3].

Herein, we present a case of CSF leak attributable to a microspur. Computed tomography (CT) myelography and digital subtraction myelography (DSM) accurately localized the CSF leakage site; the patient's symptoms promptly improved following epidural blood patch (EBP) treatment.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old woman presented with a chief complaint of headache, which was orthostatic, worsening when upright and resolving in the supine position. She had no remarkable medical or family history. The patient also had no history of allergies. She had no clinical features suggestive of connective tissue disorders, including Marfan syndrome or Ehlers–Danlos syndrome. In addition, she had no history of recent trauma, chiropractic manipulation, vigorous exercise, heavy lifting, severe coughing, or Valsalva-triggering events before symptom onset. Two days prior to admission, she awoke with headache and nausea. The headache was diffuse and dull in character, with a severity of 9/10 on the numerical rating scale when upright. It worsened within 3 minutes after sitting or standing and improved within 5 minutes after lying down. The headache was accompanied by nausea, but not by vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, tinnitus, hearing disturbance, diplopia, vertigo, neck pain, or cranial autonomic symptoms such as lacrimation, rhinorrhea, or conjunctival injection. Despite rest, the symptoms did not improve. Subsequently, she visited a local neurosurgeon; However, conventional head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan revealed no abnormalities. Due to the orthostatic headache, cerebrospinal fluid hypovolemia was suspected; she was referred to our hospital and admitted for full examination.

On admission, her height and weight were 154.2 cm and 41.8 kg (body mass index, 17.5 kg/m2), respectively, and her vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 109/82 mmHg; pulse rate, 84 beats/min + normal sinus rhythm; respiratory rate, 12 breaths/min; and body temperature, 37.3°C. General physical examination revealed no abnormalities. Neurological examination revealed clear consciousness without cranial nerve abnormalities. No motor palsy, sensory disturbance, or ataxia was observed. Moreover, the patient exhibited normal limb tendon reflexes without pathological reflexes. No meningeal irritation signs, including the Kernig sign, Brudzinski sign, or nuchal rigidity, were observed. Importantly, her headache appeared upon head elevation >30° and quickly disappeared in the supine position. The patient was placed on bed rest throughout the day.

Blood tests revealed a mildly elevated white blood cell count of 10,300/µL (3000-8400/µL); however, no other biochemical or coagulation system abnormalities were observed. Chest radiography and electrocardiography were unremarkable. Head MRI without contrast enhancement showed no abnormalities in the brain parenchyma; the ventricular and subarachnoid spaces were considered age-appropriate. No obstructive lesions were observed in the main trunk vessels. Whole-spine MRI demonstrated findings consistent with spinal CSF leakage (Fig. 1). Based on the clinical presentation and imaging findings, the patient was diagnosed with spontaneous intracranial hypotension. According to the Schievink classification system, the present case was categorized as SLEC-positive SIH. Although contrast-enhanced brain MRI may demonstrate characteristic findings of SIH, including pachymeningeal enhancement and brain sagging, spinal MRI in our patient clearly demonstrated extensive SLEC. The patient initially underwent conservative treatment with bed rest and fluid replacement. As the symptoms persisted after conservative therapy, EBP was performed after obtaining informed consent. On 45 days after admission, an EBP procedure was performed at the L1/2 level. Autologous blood (15 mL) was injected into the L1/2 interlaminar space under fluoroscopic guidance. Following the procedure, the patient was able to walk without headache aggravation. Postoperative spinal MRI scan revealed decreased epidural spinal fluid retention. The patient was discharged 55 days after admission and remained symptom-free for 18 months after treatment.

Discussion

Herein, we report a case of spontaneous intracranial hypotension caused by a discogenic microspur at the L1/2 level. The CSF leak was precisely localized using DSM, and the patient was successfully treated with a targeted EBP. This case highlights the diagnostic value of DSM in identifying microspur-related CSF leaks, even at the lumbar level.

Although the exact cause of SIH remains unclear, several mechanisms have been implicated, including spinal meningeal diverticula or nerve root cysts, regional attenuation of the dura, weak nerve root sheaths, and minor trauma [4].

Recent reports have shown that nontraumatic SIH can occur following various factors: (1) dural injury due to microspur, a calcified discogenic microprojection, (2) dural dissection of the nerve root sleeve, or (3) CSF venous fistula [[5], [6], [7]]. A microspur is a condition in which a portion of the intervertebral disc calcifies and protrudes into the spine, perforating the dura mater similar to a knife cut along the long axis and causing spinal fluid leak [6]. Beck et al. [6] identified microspurs in all cases of ventral dural injury after identifying the site of CSF leak and performing hemoperfusion to repair the dura mater [6]. Approximately 60% of CSF hypovolemia cases caused by spinal fluid leak from the spine are due to damage to the ventral dura mater with microspurs [6]. Our patient had a relatively low BMI (17.5 kg/m²), which may have contributed to increased vulnerability of the ventral dura mater to penetration by the microspur. Previous studies have suggested an association between low BMI and high-flow ventral CSF leaks caused by osseous spicules or microspurs [8].

The epidemiology of microspurs is unknown; however, they are most common in women in their 30s–50s and thought to be congenital rather than age-related [6,8]. Similar cases indicating microspur-related SIH have been reported, with the following characteristics [3,6,7,[9], [10], [11]]: (1) lesions are typically located at the cervicothoracic level; (2) atypical clinical manifestations may occur, such as headache without orthostatic features; (3) lesions are typically small and located ventrally, making CT myelography more useful than conventional spinal MRI; and (4) duraplasty may be required in cases resistant to EBP treatment. Approximately 70% of untreatable SIH cases are related to microspurs [6].

In our case, a microspur was identified at the L1/2 lumbar vertebral level, which differs from the predominantly cervicothoracic distribution described in the literature. This finding suggests that the diagnostic search for microspurs in patients with SIH should be extended beyond the cervicothoracic region to include the lumbar spine. Precisely identifying the CSF leakage site in patients with SIH is often challenging; however, awareness that microspurs can occur at any spinal level may facilitate earlier diagnosis.

DSM involves enhancing the contrast agent by suppressing the density of the surrounding tissue through subtraction of pre and postcontrast images [12,13]. In our case, CT myelography identified a microspur at L1/2 with dural compression but could not directly confirm active leakage at this site. DSM enabled dynamic visualization of active CSF leakage at the L1/2 level, thereby confirming the suspected leak site. The imaging findings were most consistent with a ventral dural tear caused by the microspur rather than a nerve root sleeve leak. The presence of SLEC and active contrast extravasation on DSM suggested a relatively high-flow CSF leak pattern. This precise localization facilitated targeted EBP treatment at the L1/2 level, leading to prompt symptom resolution. In SLEC-positive SIH, precise localization of the spinal CSF leak is essential for targeted treatment, and dynamic spinal imaging techniques may provide greater clinical utility than contrast-enhanced brain MRI in selected cases. Our case demonstrates that combining CT myelography to identify potential microspur sites with DSM to confirm active leakage can optimize the diagnostic workup and enable targeted treatment.

SIH diagnosis is often straightforward and based on characteristic headache and imaging findings. Most cases improve with rest and EBP procedures; however, chronic subdural hematoma may still occur; therefore, careful attention is required. In our case, the patient had a favorable outcome following targeted EBP at the confirmed leak site. However, surgical treatment should be considered in cases of recurrent or refractory disease [6,8]. Because Vishteh et al. [7] first reported SIH associated with microspurs in 1998 [7], similar cases have been reported and surgical treatment of microspurs has been performed in refractory cases [3,6,7,[9], [10], [11]].

This study has limitations inherent to single-case reports. The findings cannot be generalized and should be interpreted cautiously, underscoring the need for larger case series or prospective multicenter studies to establish the optimal diagnostic and therapeutic approach for microspur-related SIH.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of lumbar microspur-related SIH in which the CSF leak was confirmed by DSM. This case demonstrates that microspurs can cause ventral CSF leaks not only at the cervicothoracic level but also in the lumbar spine. DSM provided precise localization of the active leak and facilitated successful targeted treatment. When SLEC is identified on spinal MRI in patients with suspected SIH, microspur-related CSF leaks should be considered, even at the lumbar level. DSM may be useful for confirming the leak site when CT myelography suggests, but does not definitively localize, the source of leakage.
