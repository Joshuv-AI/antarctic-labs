MLC for parabolically bounded primitive renormalization
Jeremy Kahn, Alex Kapiamba, and Mikhail Lyubich

Abstract
. We prove a priori bounds and MLC (local connectivity of the Mandelbrot set ℳ \mathcal{M} ) for a class of infinitely renormalizable parameters whose renormalization type is primitive but can approach the cusp of ℳ \mathcal{M} . To this end we develop and refine a variety of tools that allow us to control degeneration of renormalizations. They include the Thin-Thick Decomposition, the Value Calculus, the Wanderers Theorem, and the Wave Lemma.

,

[
Lyu97
]
.

Quadratic-like renormalization can be divided into two types,

and
satellite
.
The next move towards MLC was made by Kahn, establishing

bounds at all parameters of

[

.
This paper (along with

]
,

Quasi-Additivity Law

)

;

These results were then expanded in
[

]

.
Recently, Dudko and Lyubich have produced

.

1.1.
Statement of the main result

Let
M\subsetneq\mathcal{M}
be a small Mandelbrot set, we will call
M
a
(combinatorial) type
. There is a unique
p\geq 2
, called the
associated period
, such that every polynomial corresponding to a parameter in
M
is quadratic-like renormalizable with period
p
. The type is
prime
when there is no other type
M^{\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{M}
satisfying
M\subsetneq M^{\prime}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a family of types, and let
f_{0}:U_{0}\to V_{0}
be a quadratic-like map with quadratic-like restrictions
(f_{n+1}:=f_{n}^{p_{n}}:U_{n}\to V_{n})_{n=0}^{N}
. The sequence
(f_{n})
has
combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}
if each
f_{n}
is hybrid conjugate to a quadratic polynomial in some
M_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
with associated period
p_{n}
. When
N=\infty
, the sequence
(f_{n})
has
a priori bounds
if the restrictions can be selected so that

\mmod(V_{n}\setminus\overline{U_{n}})\geq{\epsilon}>0.

If the bounds depend only on
\mathcal{C}
when
n
is large enough (depending on
\mod(V_{0}\setminus\overline{U_{0}})
),
then we will say that
\mathcal{C}
has
beau bounds
.

The
main hyperbolic component

\Delta_{0}
of
\mathcal{M}

is the component of
\Int\mathcal{M}

corresponding to quadratic polynomials with an attracting fixed point.
For each
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
, there is a unique parameter
c_{p/q}\in\partial\Delta_{0}
corresponding to a polynomial with a parabolic fixed point with multiplier
e^{2\pi ip/q}
.
There is a unique prime type
M_{p/q}
attached to
\Delta_{0}
at
c_{p/q}
; these types are
satellite
, and all other prime types are
primitive
.
The closure
\hat{M}_{p/q}
of the component of
\mathcal{M}\setminus\{c_{p/q}\}
intersecting
M_{p/q}
is called the
p/q
-limb
of
\mathcal{M}
.
In
[
BD88
]
,
Branner and Douady used a quasiconformal surgery, inserting an extra limb into the filled Julia sets, to define a continuous dynamical embedding
\Phi_{2}:\hat{M}_{1/2}\to\hat{M}_{1/3}
, and their argument can be easily generalized to construct embeddings
\Phi_{q}:\hat{M}_{1/q}\to\hat{M}_{1/{(q+1)}}
for all
q\geq 2
.
Choosing the embeddings so that each new limb is inserted “far away” from the limb containing the critical point, we define a
parabolic tail
to be a family of types of the form

Figure 1.1.
The boundary of the
\mathcal{M}
zoomed in near the limbs
\hat{M}_{1/10}
(left) and
\hat{M}_{1/20}
(right). The small Mandelbrot sets
highlighted in red and blue belong to the same parabolic tails respectively.

\left\{\Phi_{n}\circ\Phi_{n-1}\circ\dots\circ\Phi_{q}(M)\mid n\geq q\right\}

for some
q\geq 2
and type
M\subset\hat{M}_{1/q}
(see Figure
1.1
).

We say that a family of types is
parabolically bounded
if
it is a finite union of types and parabolic tails. Our main result is:

Theorem A
.

Every parabolically bounded family of prime primitive types has beau bounds.

While the beau bounds in Theorem A provide some control for an infinitely renormalizable quadratic polynomials with parabolically bounded prime primitive combinatorics, for example it follows from
[
McM94
]
that such polynomials support no invariant line fields on their Julia sets, they are not enough on their own to establish MLC. Let us say that a family of types
\mathcal{C}
is
rigid
if MLC holds at any infinitely renormalizable quadratic polynomial in
\mathcal{M}
with combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}
. The rigidity theorem in
[
Lyu97
]
asserts that any family of types
\mathcal{C}
with beau bounds and satisfying a certain
secondary limbs condition
is rigid.
However, any family of types containing small Mandelbrot sets in infinitely many distinct limbs does not satisfy the secondary limbs condition.
We observe that the argument in
[
Lyu97
]
actually provides a weaker condition for families of types, which we call
bounded puzzle Teichmüller diameter
, that suffices for rigidity:

Theorem B
.

Every family of prime primitive types with beau bounds and bounded puzzle Teichmüller diameter is rigid.

Parabolic tails are shown to have bounded puzzle Teichmüller diameter in
[
DKLP
]
,
so Theorems A and B together yield:

Theorem C
.

Any parabolically bounded family of prime primitive types is rigid.

We remark that our results can also be combined with those in
[
DL23a
]
, providing beau bounds and rigidity for families of types containing finitely many primitive or satellite types and finitely many primitive parabolic tails.
However, such a combination requires transferring between quadratic-like maps and the
\psi^{\bullet}
-quadratic-like maps introduced in
[
DL23a
]
, which is beyond the scope of this article.

1.2.
Outline of the proof

For a quadratic-like map
f\colon U^{\prime}\to U
with connected filled Julia set
\mathfrak{K}
, the
width
of
f
is defined to be
\mathcal{W}(f)=\frac{1}{\mmod(U\setminus\mathfrak{K})}
.
If
f
is renormalizable with renormalization
\mathcal{R}f
,
we want to consider the width
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)
.
However,
\mathcal{R}f
is not uniquely defined as a quadratic-like map:
we can for instance restrict
\mathcal{R}f
to make
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)
arbitrarily large.
We want to choose the renormalization so that
the domain of
\mathcal{R}f
is “near-maximal”
(and therefore
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)
is near-minimal)
this is achieved by considering
pseudo-quadratic-like (
\psi
-ql) maps,

generalizations of quadratic-like maps introduced in
[
Kah06
]

that admit canonical renormalizations.

In
[
Kah06
]
, beau bounds for bounded primitive combinatorics follow from the main technical theorem there, known as “if it’s Bad Now, it was Worse Earlier” (BNWE):
if
f
is an infinitely renormalizable
\psi
-ql map,
with combinatorics in some bounded family of primitive types,
and the width is large at some renormalization level,
then the width was twice as large at some recent earlier level.
Indeed,
a priori
bounds follow immediately by considering the
record
renormalization levels
with width larger than all previous levels.

As in
[
KL08
]
and
[
KL09a
]
,
we will produce beau bounds here by showing “if it’s Bad Now, it was Much Worse Earlier”(BNMWE).
Despite its sardonic nature
we will call a family with BNWE
optimistic
2
2

2

BNWE can also be easily turned into “if it’s bad now, it will be better in the future”
,
and one with BNMWE,
super-optimistic
.
More precisely,
we say that a family of types
\mathcal{C}
is super-optimistic
if for all
M>0
,
there is some finite family
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
such that any renormalizable
\psi
-ql map
f
with combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)\ggg_{\mathcal{C},M}1
satisfies
\mathcal{W}(f)\geq M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)
.
Using BNWE from
[
Kah06
]
,
we have the following fact which is proven implicitly in
[
KL08
]
:

Lemma
.

Any super-optimistic family of primitive types has beau bounds.

Theorem A then follows immediately from:

Theorem D
.

Any parabolically bounded family of prime primitive types is super-optimistic.

To prove Theorem D, our analysis starts off similarly to that in
[
Kah06
]
. If
f:U^{\prime}\to U
is a renormalizable
\psi
-ql map with period
p
, then its renormalization
\mathcal{R}f
defines a finite collection
\mathcal{K}
of small filled Julia sets in the dynamical plane of
f
. The set
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
is a hyperbolic Riemann surface; its
thick-thin decomposition induces a lamination on
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
called the
canonical lamination

\mathcal{F}
.
For any proper arc
\gamma
in
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
, its
canonical width
is the extremal width of all the leaves of
\mathcal{F}
homotopic to
\gamma
; the function
X
taking proper arcs to their canonical width is the
canonical weighted arc diagram
of
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
. If
Y
is a restriction of
X
, then the
magnitude
of
Y
is defined by
\|Y\|=\sum_{\gamma}Y(\gamma)
.

A proper arc
\gamma
in
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
vertical
if it connects
\partial\mathcal{K}
to
\partial U
, and
horizontal
if it connects
\partial\mathcal{K}
to
itself; we denote
the restrictions of
X
to vertical and horizontal curves by
X^{\mathrm{ver}}
and
X^{\mathrm{hor}}
respectively.
We can directly relate
X
to the widths of
f
and
\mathcal{R}f
by

(1.1)

\mathcal{W}(f)\geq\|X^{\mathrm{ver}}\|

and

(1.2)

p\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)\asymp\|X^{\mathrm{ver}}\|+\|X^{\mathrm{hor}}\|+O(p).

Thus
,
if

(1.3)

\|X^{\mathrm{ver}}\|\geq 2{\epsilon}\|X^{\mathrm{hor}}\|

for some
0<{\epsilon}<1/2
, then (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) imply that

(1.4)

\mathcal{W}(f)\succeq p{\epsilon}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)\geq 2\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)

when
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)\ggg_{1}1
and
p\ggg_{\epsilon}1.

In
[
Kah06
]
, (
1.3
) is achieved for a universal
{\epsilon}
when
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)\ggg_{p}1
. The central idea is that we can pull back
X
by
f
and apply the Grötzsch inequality to see some loss of horizontal weight dictated by the
core entropy
of
f
; that is the entropy of
f
restricted to its Hubbard tree
\mathcal{T}
. For primitive combinatorics the core entropy is always positive, so after applying several pullbacks the loss of horizontal weight can be made definite. The resulting definite vertical weight is then pushed forward by the Covering Lemma of
[
KL09
]
.

With unbounded combinatorics, we immediately encounter a problem: if the geometry of
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
does not respect the combinatorial structure of
\mathcal{T}
, for example lots of weight in
X
is supported on arcs that intersect
\mathcal{T}
many times, then we cannot relate the loss of horizontal weight to the core entropy of
f
. This possibility is excluded in
[
Kah06
]
, where it is shown that after some restriction
X
is “aligned” with
\mathcal{T}
, but the argument relies on the bounded topology of
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
.
We split our analysis into two cases: the “tame” case where the geometry of
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
suitably respects
\mathcal{T}
, and the “wave” case where it does not.
In both cases, the key to our analysis is that the parabolically bounded condition forces the unbounded part of the topology to occur where the dynamics is incredibly simple: away from a finite collection of components of
\mathcal{K}
the map
f
looks like a translation.

In the tame case, our argument differs from that in
[
Kah06
]
.
We suppose that (
1.3
) does not hold for any definite
{\epsilon}
, so
\|X^{\mathrm{ver}}\|
is almost negligible.
Pulling back
X^{\mathrm{hor}}
by
g=f^{p}
, we therefore get a weighted arc diagram
g^{*}X^{\mathrm{hor}}
that, up to some error,
dominates

X^{\mathrm{hor}}
.
Using the intersection arcs with the Hubbard tree
\mathcal{T}
, we can assign
values

\val(X^{\mathrm{hor}})
and
\val(g^{*}X^{\mathrm{hor}})
to the weighted arc diagrams. The domination relation implies that

\val(g^{*}X^{\mathrm{hor}})\geq\val(X^{\mathrm{hor}})
, and the positive entropy of
g
acting on
\mathcal{T}
implies that
\val(g^{*}X^{\mathrm{hor}})\leq\val(X^{\mathrm{hor}})/2
; this is our contradiction. The technical difficulty is that we have error terms that depend on the topology of
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
. Our parabolically bounded condition allows us to replace
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
with a bounded topology subsurface, completing the argument in this case.

In the wave case, the wild geometry of
U\setminus\mathcal{K}
produces a particular type of lamination in the complement of
\mathcal{T}
called a
wave
, which was first introduced in
[
DL23a
]
.
For bounded combinatorics,
the
Wave Lemma
in
[
DL23a
]
asserts that when we push forward a wave by the dynamics of
f
, we produce either some definite vertical width or a wider wave; by considering the widest wave we must therefore have definite vertical width.
In our setting we show that the same holds; the main difference is that we want to push forward by an unbounded iterate of our map.
The parabolically bounded condition allows us to observe first, that most of a wave is localized to a single limb in
\mathcal{T}
, and second, that for waves localized to a single limb we can use the near-translational part of the dynamics to treat large iterates similarly to a single iterate; this completes the argument in this case.

2
, we recall the theory of quadratic-like and pseudo-quadratic-like maps; we also introduce the theory of
value
for weighted arc diagrams, which is a tool that can rule out dynamically invariant weighted arc diagrams for quadratic-like maps. In Section
3
, we outline of the proof of Theorem D.
In Section
4
, we show that the analysis can be divided into the “tame” and “wave” cases, which are studied in Sections
5
and
6
respectively.
In Section
7
, we prove Theorem B.
To streamline the organization of our argument, there are several self-contained discussions which we delay to the appendices.
In Appendix
A
we recall the theory of ideal markings, canonical laminations and weighted arc diagrams, and a new tool: a
Uniform Thick-Thin Decomposition
for bordered Riemann surfaces
(initially developed by Kahn and Lyubich in
[
KL26
]
).
In Appendix
B
we introduce
translation markings
, a marked disk with near-translational dynamics, and bound the width of combinatorially long arcs in these markings.
In Appendix
C
, we introduce the concept of
cloning
a
cross section
of a directed graph, and bound the leading eigenvalue and corresponding eigenvector of the result.
In Appendix
D
, we state and prove the
Wanderers Theorem
, a variant of the Covering Lemma.
The results from Appendices
B
,
C
, and
D
are all used in Section
5
. After the appendices, we include an index of the terms and notation used throughout this article for the readers convenience.

1.3.
Future perspective

To fully resolve MLC, rigidity must be shown for all families of combinatorial types;
as a general principle this should follow from proving (properly defined)
a priori
bounds.
Here we produce a brief ontology of renormalization types and then discuss methods of intermediate renormalization that we hope and expect to be sufficient for the general case.

For a prime satellite type
M_{p/q}
,
we let
[0;a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
be the continued fraction expansion of
p/q
;
we set

h(M)=h(p/q)=\sup_{n}a_{n}\text{ and }d(M)=d(p/q)=k.

Recalling that
\mathcal{M}
is the set of all
c
such that the orbit of
0
under
z\mapsto z^{2}+c
is bounded, the
root
and
principal tip
of
\mathcal{M}
are the parameters
c=1/4
and
c=-2
respectively. For any small Mandelbrot set
M
, its root and principal tip are the images of the root and principal tip of
\mathcal{M}
under the tuning homeomorphism
\mathcal{M}\to M
.
For
r
the root of
M
, the
limb

\hat{M}
of
M
is defined to be the closure of the component of
\mathcal{M}\setminus\{r\}
intersecting
M
. For
t
the principal tip of
M
, the
principal decorations
of
M
are the closures of the components of
\mathcal{M}\setminus\{t\}
avoiding
M
; we denote the union of the principal decorations by
\hat{M}^{1/2}
.

For any prime and primitive type
M
, there is a unique infinite sequence
(M_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that
M_{0}=\mathcal{M}
,
M_{n}\subset M_{n-1}
is a small Mandelbrot set mapped to some satellite
M_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
under the straightening homeomorphism
M_{n-1}\to\mathcal{M}
for all
n\geq 1
, and
M\subset\hat{M}_{n}
for all
n
.
When
n
is sufficiently large, we have
M\subset\hat{M}^{1/2}_{N}
for all
N\geq n
;
we let
\l(M)
be the minimal such value of
n
,
and then
p_{n}/q_{n}=1/2
for all
n>\l(M)
.
We let
h(M)
and
d(M)
be the maximum of
h(p_{n}/q_{n})
and
d(p_{n}/q_{n})
over all
n\leq\l(M)
.
To complete our definitions, we let
\l(M)=0
when
M
is prime and satellite.

For any family of prime types
\mathcal{C}
, we can define

\l(\mathcal{C})
,
h(\mathcal{C})
, and
d(\mathcal{C})
to be the supremum over all
M\in\mathcal{C}
of
\l(M)
,
h(M)
, and
d(M)
respectively.
We classify
\mathcal{C}
into four different cases:

(1)

Anti-molecule (and bounded satellite):

l(\mathcal{C}),h(\mathcal{C}),d(\mathcal{C})<\infty
.

(2)

Near-parabolic:

h(\mathcal{C})=\infty
and
l(\mathcal{C}),d(\mathcal{C})<\infty
.

(3)

Near-neutral:

d(\mathcal{C})=\infty
and
l(\mathcal{C})<\infty
.

(4)

Virtually-satellite:

l(\mathcal{C})=\infty
.

Corresponding to these four cases are four renormalization theories
(which we may think of as four fingers to be combined with the thumb of quadratic-like renormalization):

•

puzzle (or generalized) renormalization,

•

near-parabolic renormalization,

•

sectorial (neutral and satellite unified as “molecule”) renormalization,

•

and finally: virtual (near-molecule primitive) renormalization.

The bounds in
[
Kah06
]
were extended to the bounded satellite case in
[
DL23a
]

(preceded by
[
DL23
]
on some special satellite combinatorics of high type
3
)
yielding the general quadratic-like case for bounded combinatorics.

The puzzle renormalization
was introduced in
[
Lyu97
]
to prove bounds
3
3

3

This work gave an important insight into the problem.
However, it was performed in the
non-degenerate regime

that cannot be directly combined with results in the
near-degenerate regime.

On the other hand, all the pieces handled in the near degenerate regime
can naturally be unified (so far).

for special types of high primitive combinatorics,
using estimates of moduli
that build on the work of Yoccoz showing MLC in the non-infinitely renormalizable case.
It was then used in the near-degenerate regime in
[
KL08
,
KL09a
]
to show super-optimism for renormalization types bounded away from the main molecule of
\mathcal{M}
,
thereby completing case (1) above.
It will also appear as an endgame in the other three cases.

Near-parabolic renormalization began with the work of Lavaurs and reached maturity with the

bounds (once again assuming high type)
in the work of Inou and Shishikura
[

]
3

that found numerous application (see e.g.,
[

]
).
The general neutral case,
combining near-parabolic and sectorial renormalization,
was recently handled in
[

]

What remains are the primitive classes with

,

, or

The current paper dealing with the primitive near-parabolic combinatorics of

and

.

It is feasible that this strategy can bring the story to a completion.

1.4.
Terminology and notation

Let us collect here some of main terminology and notation used throughout this article.
We denote:

•

x\oplus y=(x^{-1}+y^{-1})^{-1}
the harmonic sum;

•

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})
the extremal width of a path family
\mathcal{F}
;

•

A\ggg_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}B
for
A\geq CB
for a large constant
C
depending only on the parameters
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
;

•

the “big-O” notation
A\ggg O(A)
;

•

\partial^{i}V
the ideal boundary of a Riemann surface
V
;

•

\partial_{C}X
the Caratheodory boundary of a set
X\subset\mathbb{C}

By default, paths are considered up to reparameterization, and in a Riemann surface
V
a path

is proper if it extends continuously to a path
\gamma:[0,1]\to V\cup\partial^{i}V
with
\gamma(0),\gamma(1)\in\partial^{i}V

1.5.
Long story of this paper

This project was initially undertaken by Kahn and Lyubich, around 2007–2011, with some additional work up to 2015.
These two authors were able to completely treat the case of one small Julia set per limb
[
KL26
]
,
and, in doing so, developed most of what is now in Appendices A and B, along with some of its application in Section
5
. More than one sketch was produced for how to handle the general case. The project was resumed and completed by Kahn and Kapiamba in 2024, based originally on one such sketch,
but then pivoting to use the theory of waves that had recently been developed by Dudko and Lyubich
[
DL23a
]
.

2.
Pseudo-quadratic-like maps

We recall some standard preliminaries for the “modern theory of
a priori
bounds”, such as pseudo-quadratic-like maps, renormalization, and Hubbard trees. For more details, we refer the reader to
[
Kah06
]
or
[
DL23a
]
.

2.1.
Pseudo-quadratic-like maps

A
quadratic-like map

f:U^{\prime}\to U
is a holomorphic double branched covering between two Jordan disks
U^{\prime}\Subset U\subset\mathbb{C}
.
The annulus
U\setminus\overline{U^{\prime}}
is called the
fundamental annulus
of
f
,
and the
modulus
of
f
is defined by

\mmod f:=\mmod(U\setminus\overline{U^{\prime}}).

The
filled Julia set
of
f
is the set
\mathfrak{K}
of nonescaping points; that is

\mathfrak{K}:=\left\{z\mid f^{n}(z)\in U^{\prime}\text{ for all }n\geq 0\right\}.

The filled Julia set
\mathfrak{K}
is connected if and only if the unique critical point of
f
belongs to
\mathfrak{K}
.

More generally, a
pseudo-quadratic-like (
\psi
-ql) map
is a pair of holomorphic maps

F=(f,\iota):(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})

between two conformal disks
\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{U}
such that:

(1)

f:\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{U}
is a double branched covering;

(2)

\iota:\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{U}
is an immersion;

(3)

\mathfrak{K}
and
\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}
are compact connected full sets satisfying

\iota^{-1}(\mathfrak{K})=f^{-1}(\mathfrak{K})=\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}.

We will call
\mathfrak{K}
the filled Julia set of
F
.
The
width
of
F
is defined by

\mathcal{W}(F):=\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathfrak{K})=\frac{1}{\mmod(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathfrak{K})}.

While
\psi
-ql maps are more abstract than quadratic-like maps, they are closely related. Indeed, note that any quadratic-like map
f:U^{\prime}\to U
with connected filled Julia set
\mathfrak{K}
is a
\psi
-ql map
(f,\iota):(U^{\prime},\mathfrak{K})\rightrightarrows(U,\mathfrak{K})
with
\iota
the identity. The following fact from
[
Kah06
, Theorem 7.3]
ensures that any
\psi
-ql map restricts to a quadratic-like one near its filled Julia set:

Theorem 2.1

(Kahn)
.

For any
M>0
there exists
\mu>0
such that: if

F=(f,\iota):(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})

is a
\psi
-ql map with
\mathcal{W}(F)\leq M
, then there exist neighborhoods
U^{\prime}\subset U
of
\mathfrak{K}
such that the restriction
f\circ\iota^{-1}:U^{\prime}\to U
is a quadratic-like map with
\mmod f\geq\mu
.

Theorem
2.1
allows us to iterate a
\psi
-ql map
F=(f,\iota):(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})
in a neighborhood of the filled Julia set
\mathfrak{K}
; by abuse of notation we will write
F=f\circ\iota^{-1}
there.
To iterate
F
globally, setting
\mathcal{U}^{0}=\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{U}^{1}=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, for all
m>1
we can inductively define the
restriction

F=(f,\iota):\mathcal{U}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{U}^{m-1}\text{ with }\mathcal{U}^{m}=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}^{m-1}\times\mathcal{U}^{m-1}:f(x)=\iota(y)\},

where
f
and
\iota
are the component-wise projections. These restrictions induce the
iterations

F^{m}=(f^{m},\iota_{m}):\mathcal{U}^{m}\to\mathcal{U}^{0}

given by
f^{m}=f^{m-1}\circ f
and
\iota_{m}=\iota_{m-1}\circ\iota
.

We will say that the
\psi
-ql -map
F
is
periodically repelling
if every periodic cycle in
\mathfrak{K}
is repelling.

2.2.
Localization

Let us recall the idea of “localization” from
[
Kah06
, Section 7.4.1]
.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be a hyperbolic Riemann surface, let
\gamma
be a homotopically non-trivial Jordan curve in
\mathcal{U}
, and let
U
be a component of
\mathcal{U}\setminus\gamma
. For
\pi:\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\mathcal{U}
the universal cover with deck transformation group
\Delta
, let

\tilde{\gamma}
be a lift of
\gamma
by
\pi
and let

\Delta_{\gamma}
be the stabilizer of
\tilde{\gamma}
.
The
subsurface cover
of
\mathcal{U}
corresponding to
U
is the Riemann surface
\tilde{U}=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}/\Delta_{\gamma}
.
The curve
\tilde{\gamma}
descends to a copy of
\gamma
inside the subsurface cover.
Cutting
\tilde{U}
along this copy of
\gamma
yields one component that is not mapped to
U
by the covering
\tilde{U}\to U
; gluing this component to
U
along
\gamma
yields the
localization
of
U
inside
\mathcal{U}
.

2.3.
Renormalization

A quadratic-like map
f:U^{\prime}\to U
is called
quadratic-like renormalizable
with period
\mathbf{n}>1
if some restriction
f^{\mathbf{n}}:V^{\prime}\to V
is a quadratic-like map with connected filled Julia set
K
; we additionally require that
K
contains the critical point of
f
.
The restriction is called a
quadratic-like renormalization
of
f
of period
\mathbf{n}
.
The renormalization is called
prime
if there
is no renormalization of period
1<m<\mathbf{n}
, and
primitive
if the sets
f^{j}(K)
with
1\leq j<\mathbf{n}
are pair-wise disjoint.
Note that for a given period
\mathbf{n}
, there is no canonically defined quadratic-like renormalization of
f
of period
\mathbf{n}
, indeed there are infinitely many different possible choices of
U^{\prime}
and
U
. However, when the renormalization is prime the small filled Julia set
K
is uniquely determined. One benefit of working with
\psi
-ql maps instead of quadratic-like maps is that
we can use the uniqueness of the small filled Julia set to construct a canonical renormalization.

Let
F=(f,\iota):(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})
be a
\psi
-ql map. We will say that
F
is
(
\psi
-ql) renormalizable
with period
\mathbf{n}
if it has a quadratic-like restriction (as in Theorem
2.1
) that is quadratic-like renormalizable with period
\mathbf{n}
, and the corresponding renormalization is prime. Let us additionally assume that the renormalization is primitive.
For
K
the corresponding small filled Julia set and
\mathcal{K}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{\mathbf{n}-1}F^{j}(K)
the cycle of small filled Julia sets,
let
\mathcal{V}
be the localization of a small neighborhood of
K
inside
\mathcal{U}\setminus(\mathcal{K}\setminus K)
.
For the iterate
F^{\mathbf{n}}=(f^{\mathbf{n}},\iota_{\mathbf{n}}):\mathcal{U}^{\mathbf{n}}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{U}^{0}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\mathbf{n}}=(f^{\mathbf{n}})^{-1}(\mathcal{K})
, let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
be the localization of
K
inside
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbf{n}}\setminus(\mathcal{K}^{\mathbf{n}}\setminus K)
.
The pair of maps
(f^{\mathbf{n}},\iota_{\mathbf{n}}):\mathcal{U}^{\mathbf{n}}\to\mathcal{U}
lift to a
\psi
-ql map

\mathcal{R}F=(g,\jmath):(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},K^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{V},K),

which we call the
(
\psi
-ql) renormalization
of
F
.
With this construction, the renormalization
\mathcal{R}F
is uniquely defined by
F
. We will say that
F
has
M
-amplification
for some
M\geq 1
if

\mathcal{W}(F)\geq M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F).

2.4.
Limbs and rotation number

Let
F:(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})
be a primitively renormalizable periodically repelling
\psi
-ql map with cycle of small filled Julia sets
\mathcal{K}
.

There is a unique fixed point of
F
, called
\beta_{F}
, such that
\mathfrak{K}\setminus\{\beta_{F}\}
is connected.
Removing the other fixed point, which is called
\alpha_{F}
, splits
\mathfrak{K}
into
q\geq 2
connected components.
These components are the
limbs
of
\mathfrak{K}
, and we will label them by
(\mathfrak{L}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}
according to their counter-clockwise circular ordering around
\alpha_{F}
, chosen so that
\mathfrak{L}_{0}
contains the critical point. There is some
0\leq p<q
coprime to
q
so that
F
maps
\mathfrak{L}_{j}
homeomorphically to
\mathfrak{L}_{j+p}
for all
j\neq 0
; the fraction
p/q
is called the
combinatorial rotation number of
F
at
\alpha_{F}
. As
F
maps
\mathfrak{K}
to itself as a double branched cover, it follows that the unique critical value of
F
is in
\mathfrak{L}_{p}
and
F(\mathfrak{L}_{0})=\mathfrak{K}
. Note that the limbs do not contain
\alpha_{F}
, and
\overline{\mathfrak{L}_{j}}=\mathfrak{L}_{j}\cup\{\alpha_{F}\}
for all
j
.

For any interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
we can associate a subset of
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
by first intersecting with
\mathbb{Z}
and then projecting; by slight abuse of notation we will also call this subset
I
. For any such interval, we denote
\mathfrak{L}_{I}:=\bigcup_{j\in I}\mathfrak{L}_{j}.

Supposing now that
F
is renormalizable with small filled Julia sets
\mathcal{K}
, for any interval
I
we set
\mathcal{K}_{I}=\mathcal{K}\cap\mathfrak{L}_{I}
. We let
U_{I}\subset\mathcal{U}
be an open Jordan neighborhood of
\mathfrak{L}_{I}
, chosen only up to isotopy rel
\mathcal{K}
.

2.5.
Hubbard trees

Let
F:(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})
be a primitively renormalizable and periodically repelling
\psi
-ql map with cycle of small filled Julia sets
\mathcal{K}
.

We suppose for now that
\mathfrak{K}
is locally connected.
In this case, we define the
Hubbard tree
of
F
to be the smallest connected set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathfrak{K}
containing
\mathcal{K}
; it is compact and forward invariant.
We set
\mathcal{L}_{j}=\mathfrak{L}_{j}\cap\mathcal{T}
for all
j
and
\mathcal{L}_{I}=\mathfrak{L}_{I}\cap\mathcal{T}
for any interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.
A point
x\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is called a
branch point
of
\mathcal{T}
if
\mathcal{T}\setminus\{x\}
has at least three components. The branch points of
\mathcal{T}
are all repelling periodic points of
F
; if
F
has combinatorial rotation number
p/q
with
q>2
then
\alpha_{F}
is also a branch point of
\mathcal{T}
.
We denote by
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
the union of
\mathcal{K}
with all the branch points of
\mathcal{T}
and
\alpha_{F}
. We will call the components of
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
the
vertices
of
\mathcal{T}
and denote the set of vertices by
V(\mathcal{T})
. The complement of the vertices in
\mathcal{T}
is a union of disjoint paths in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\hat{\mathcal{K}}
; we will call each such path an
edge
of
\mathcal{T}
and denote the set of edges by
E(\mathcal{T}).
These sets of vertices and edges form an abstract tree, which we will call the
combinatorial Hubbard tree
.
The
combinatorial distance
between two vertices of
\mathcal{T}
is length of the shortest path in the combinatorial Hubbard tree connecting them.

We can similarly view
f^{-1}(\mathcal{T})
as an abstract tree whose vertices are the components of
f^{-1}(\hat{\mathcal{K}})
and whose edges are the components of
f^{-1}(E(\mathcal{T}))
.
Thus we have a 2-to-1 map
f:f^{-1}(E(\mathcal{T}))\to E(\mathcal{T})
.
Moreover, the tree
f^{-1}(\mathcal{T})
contains a subtree
i^{-1}(\mathcal{T})

whose edges map to the edges of
\mathcal{T}
under
i
.
It induces a natural surjection
i:E(i^{-1}(\mathcal{T}))\rightarrow\mathcal{T}
.

The composition
f\circ\iota^{-1}
induces a linear transformation
T_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}\to\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}
. As the renormalization of
F
is prime,
T_{F}
is primitive and has a unique Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}\geq 1
; as the renormalization is primitive we have
\lambda_{F}>1
. We will call
\lambda_{F}
the
core eigenvalue
of
F
(the quantity
\log\lambda_{F}
is called the
core entropy
of
F
).
The inverse composition
\iota\circ f^{-1}
induces the transpose linear transformation
T_{F}^{\top}
, which has the same Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
.
For any
n>1
the compositions
f^{n}\circ\iota_{n}^{-1}
and
\iota_{n}\circ f^{-n}
similarly induces a linear transformation
\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}\to\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}
, these are exactly
T_{F}^{n}
and
(T_{F}^{\top})^{n}
.

Let us now suppose instead that
\mathfrak{K}
is not locally connected. In this case,
\mathfrak{K}
admits a locally connected
combinatorial model

\mathfrak{K}_{\mathrm{com}}
obtained as the quotient of the unit disk by a rational lamination. In this case, there is a natural projection
\pi:\mathfrak{K}\to\mathfrak{K}_{\mathrm{com}}
and
F
descends to a map
F_{\mathrm{com}}:\mathfrak{K}_{\mathrm{com}}\to\mathfrak{K}_{\mathrm{com}}
.
The combinatorial model has an analogously defined Hubbard tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{com}}
with vertices
V(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{com}})
, edges
E(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{com}})
, linear transformation
T_{F}
and eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
; we can lift by
\pi
to get the corresponding objects for
F
.

2.6.
Hubbard arc-diagrams

Let
F:(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\rightrightarrows(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})
be a primitively renormalizable and periodically repelling
\psi
-ql map with cycle of small filled Julia sets
\mathcal{K}
and Hubbard tree
\mathcal{T}
.
We equip
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
with the minimal ideal marking as in Appendix
A.1
and recall the definition weighted arc-diagrams from Appendix
A.2.2
.

We will say that a proper path or arc in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
horizontal
,
vertical
, or
bivertical

if both, one, or neither of its endpoints lie in
\partial_{C}\mathcal{K}
respectively.
We can define three arc-diagrams in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
associated to the Hubbard tree as follows (see Figure
2.1
). We define the
vertical Hubbard arc-diagram

\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}
to be the unique maximal AD in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
consisting of vertical arcs that can be realized in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
.
We define the
horizontal Hubbard arc-diagram

\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
to be the unique maximal AD in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
consisting of horizontal arcs that can be realized in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
without intersecting
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}
.
For any edge
\gamma\in E(\mathcal{T})
, there is a unique bivertical arc in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
that intersects
\mathcal{T}
only in
\gamma
; we define the
bivertical Hubbard arc-diagram

\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}\equiv\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(\mathcal{T})

to be the set of all such arcs.
These three arc-diagrams are closely related, for example we can relate the intersection numbers of any arc with
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}
:

Figure 2.1.
The disk
\mathcal{U}
with small filled Julia sets represented as disks. The horizontal, vertical, and bivertical Hubbard arc-diagrams are drawn in blue, purple, and red respectively.

Lemma 2.2
.

If each limb of
\mathcal{T}
contains at most
\mathbf{b}
many components of
\mathcal{K}
, then
we have

(2.3)

\left<\alpha,\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}\right>\leq 2\mathbf{b}(\left<\alpha,\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}\right>+1)

for any arc
\alpha
in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

We can use
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}
to cut
\alpha
into
\left<\alpha,\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}\right>+1
segments
\alpha_{i}
.
If an endpoint of some
\alpha_{i}
lies on a small Julia set
K
, then we will call
K
a
combinatorial endpoint
of
\alpha_{i}
. If an endpoint of some
\alpha_{i}
lies on an arc of
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ver}}
that connects a small Julia set
K
to
\partial_{C}\mathcal{U}
, then we will also call
K
a combinatorial endpoint.
Each
\alpha_{i}
has exactly two combinatorial endpoints, and by homotoping
\alpha_{i}
close to
\mathcal{T}
we observe that
\left<\alpha_{i},\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}\right>
is equal to the combinatorial distance between its combinatorial endpoints.

As each limb of
\mathcal{T}
contains at most
\mathbf{b}
components of
\mathcal{K}
, the combinatorial distance between any vertex of
\mathcal{T}
and
\alpha_{F}
is at most
\mathbf{b}
. Hence the combinatorial distance between any two vertices of
\mathcal{T}
is at most
2\mathbf{b}
; (
2.3
) immediately follows.
∎

Notice in conclusion that the bivertical Hubbard arc-diagram
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}\equiv\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(\mathcal{T})
lifts
under
f
to the bivertical Hubbard arc diagram
f^{*}(\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}})
rel the tree
f^{-1}(\mathcal{T})

and it lifts under
i
to the bivertical Hubbard arc diagram
i^{*}(\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}})
rel the tree
i^{-1}(\mathcal{T})
.
Moreover, there are well defined push-forwards

f_{*}:\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(f^{-1}(\mathcal{T}))\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(\mathcal{T}),\quad i_{*}:\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(i^{-1}(\mathcal{T}))\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(\mathcal{T}),

and we will use the following natural conventions:

f^{*}\beta=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(f^{-1}(\mathcal{T}))\ |\ f_{*}\alpha=\beta\},\quad i^{*}\beta=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}(i^{-1}(\mathcal{T}))\ |\ i_{*}\alpha=\beta\}.

2.7.
Values for weighted arc-diagrams

Let
F
be a
\psi
-ql map as in the previous subsection. The results in this section are purely combinatorial, so for simplicity may imagine that
F
is a quadratic polynomial, so in particular
\iota
is an inclusion (see
[
Kah06
, Section 4.1]
for details of transferring combinatorial data from
\psi
-ql maps to quadratic polynomials).

We recall the definition of
domination
from Appendix
A.3.3
. Let us say that a WAD diagram on
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
horizontal
if it is supported on horizontal arcs.
Let
\val
be a function that assigns a non-negative real number to every WAD on

\mathcal{U}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{n}
with
n\geq 0
. If

(1)

\val(X)>0
for any horizontal WAD
X
;

(2)

for any WADs
X
on
\mathcal{U}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
Y
on
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
, if
X\multimap Y
then
\val(X)\geq\val(Y)
;

(3)

there is some
\lambda>1
so that
\val((f^{*})^{n}X)\leq\lambda^{-1}\val(X)
for every WAD
X
on
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
and every
n\geq 0
;

then we will call
\val
an
\lambda
-expanding valuation
for
F
.
Recalling the core eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
from Section
2.5
, we observe that a
\lambda_{F}
-expanding valuation always exists:

Theorem 2.4
.

If
F
is a primitively renormalizable and periodically repelling
\psi
-ql -map with core eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
, then there is a
\lambda_{F}
-expanding valuation for
F
.

Before proving Theorem
2.4
, we need the following two arithmetic facts:

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1\ldots n
,
and,
for each
i
,

(2.6)

a_{i}^{2}+b_{i}^{2}\leq c_{i}^{2}.

Then

(2.7)

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwarz and (
2.6
),
for all
i
and
j
,

(2.8)

a_{i}a_{j}+b_{i}b_{j}\leq\sqrt{(a_{i}^{2}+b_{i}^{2})(a_{j}^{2}+b_{j}^{2})}\leq c_{i}c_{j}.

Adding this up over all
i
and
j
, we obtain (
2.7
).
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

For any sequences
(a_{j})
and
(w_{j})
of nonnegative real numbers,

(2.10)

\left(\bigoplus w_{j}\right)\left(\sum a_{j}\right)^{2}\leq\sum w_{j}a_{j}^{2}.

Proof.

See
[
Kah06
, Lemma 10.1]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We recall the linear transformations
T_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}\to\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
from Section
2.5
. We have a natural bijection between
E(\mathcal{T})
and
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}
, so we can view any non-negative element of
\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}
as a WAD with support in
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}
. From this viewpoint,
T_{F}
and its transpose
T_{F}^{\top}
are exactly the linear transformation generated by

\beta\mapsto\sum_{\alpha\in\iota^{*}\beta}f_{*}\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta\mapsto\sum_{\alpha\in f^{*}\beta}\iota_{*}\alpha

respectively. (Informally speaking, the former summation is taken over all
f
-push-forwards of
\beta
(rel
\mathcal{T}
),
while the latter is taken over all
f
-pullbacks (rel
\mathcal{T}
).
Additionally,
as
f^{n}\circ\iota_{n}^{-1}
and
\iota_{n}\circ f^{-n}
induce the linear transformations
T_{F}^{n}
and
(T_{F}^{\top})^{n}
respectively,
f^{n}_{*}\iota_{n}^{*}\beta=(f_{*}\iota^{*})^{n}\beta
for all
\beta\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}
and
n\geq 0
.

Let
v
and
w
be WADs corresponding to eigenvectors with eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
of
T_{F}
and
T_{F}^{\top}
respectively and fix some
n\geq 0
. Thus

\displaystyle\lambda_{F}^{n}v(\beta)=\sum_{\alpha\in\iota_{n}^{*}\beta}v(f^{n}_{*}\alpha)\text{ and }\lambda_{F}^{n}w(\beta)=\sum_{\alpha\in(f^{n})^{*}\beta}w((\iota_{n})_{*}\alpha)

for all
\beta\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}
.
For any
\beta\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}}
and
\alpha\in\iota_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{biv}})

we set

\l(\beta)=v(\beta)\sqrt{w(\beta)},\quad\l(\alpha)=\lambda_{F}^{-n}\,v(f_{*}^{n}\alpha)\sqrt{w((i_{n})_{*}\alpha)}.

Thus

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\iota_{n}^{*}\beta}l(\alpha)=l(\beta)

and

(2.12)

\sum_{{\alpha\in f_{*}^{n}\beta}}l(\alpha)^{2}=\lambda_{F}^{-n}l(\beta)^{2}.

For any
n\geq 0
and arc
\gamma
in
\mathcal{U}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{n}
, we set

3.
Super-optimism

Let us say that a family of combinatorial types
\mathcal{C}
is
{\epsilon}
-optimistic
if any
n
-times renormalizable
\psi
-ql map
F

with combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}^{n}F)\ggg_{\mathcal{C},n}1
satisfies
\mathcal{W}(F)\geq 2^{n}{\epsilon}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}^{n}F).

The optimism principle from
[
Kah06
]
can be precisely stated as:

Theorem 3.1

(Kahn)
.

There is some universal
{\epsilon}>0
such that any bounded family of primitive types is
{\epsilon}
-optimistic.

Let us recall from the introduction that a family of types
\mathcal{C}
is
super-optimistic
if for all
M>0
, there is a finite set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
such that any renormalizable
\psi
-ql map
F
with combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)\ggg_{\mathcal{C},M}1
satisfies
\mathcal{W}(F)\geq M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)
. Said more plainly: we can achieve arbitrary amplification by removing a finite set of combinatorial types.
Using Theorem
3.1
, we can prove the following lemma from the introduction:

Lemma 3.2
.

Every super-optimistic family of prime primitive types has beau bounds.

Proof.

Let
0<{\epsilon}<1
be the universal quantity in Theorem
3.1
, and set
M=2/{\epsilon}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a super-optimistic family of primitive types, so by definition there is some finite set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
such that any renormalizable
\psi
-ql map
F
with combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)\ggg_{\mathcal{C}}1
satisfies
\mathcal{W}(F)\geq M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)
.
Let
N\geq 1
be an integer large enough so that
2^{N}{\epsilon}\geq 2
.

Let
F
be an infinitely renormalizable
\psi
-ql map whose renormalizations
(F_{0}=F,\dots,F_{n}=\mathcal{R}^{n}F,\dots)
have combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}
.
For any
n\geq 0
, either
(F_{n},\dots,F_{n+N})
has combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, or there is some
n\leq m<n+N

so that
(F_{n},\dots,F_{m})
has combinatorics in

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
(F_{m},F_{m+1})
has combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
In the former case it follows from Theorem
3.1
that

\mathcal{W}(F_{n})\geq 2^{N}{\epsilon}\mathcal{W}(F_{n+N})\geq 2\mathcal{W}(F_{n+N})

when
\mathcal{W}(F_{n+N})\ggg_{\mathcal{C}}1
, and in the latter case it follows from Theorem
3.1
and our choice of
M
that

\mathcal{W}(F_{n})\geq 2^{m-n}{\epsilon}\mathcal{W}(F_{m})\geq{\epsilon}M\mathcal{W}(F_{m+1})=2\mathcal{W}(F_{m})

when
\mathcal{W}(F_{m})\ggg_{\mathcal{C}}1.

It follows from the above that there is some
C>0
depending only on
\mathcal{C}
such that if
\mathcal{W}(F_{n})>C
for some
n\geq N
, then
\mathcal{W}(F_{m})\geq 2\mathcal{W}(F_{n})
for some
n-N\leq m<n
. Hence if
\mathcal{W}(F_{n})>C
for some
n\geq kN
, then

\sup_{0\leq j<N}\mathcal{W}(F_{j})>2^{k}C.

As each
\mathcal{W}(F_{j})
is finite, it follows that
\mathcal{W}(F_{n})\leq C
for all
n\ggg_{\mathcal{C}}1
, so
\mathcal{C}
has beau bounds.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

While it is not explicitly stated there, a version of Lemma
3.2
first appeared in the proof of
[

in the context of combinatorial types satisfying the

3.1.
Parabolically bounded combinatorics

For some
q\geq 2
, let
f^{\prime}
and
f
be quadratic polynomials corresponding to parameters in
\hat{M}_{1/q}
and
\hat{M}_{1/(q+1)}
respectively. Let
\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{K}
be the filled Julia sets of
f
and
\tilde{f}
with limbs
\mathfrak{L}_{j}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{L}_{j}
respectively.
For
1\leq k<q
, a
k
-limb insertion
from
f^{\prime}
to
f
is a continuous embedding

\phi:\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}\to{\mathfrak{K}}
that is holomorphic on
\Int\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}
and satisfies:

(1)

For all
k-q<j<k
,
\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{j}^{\prime})\subset\mathfrak{L}_{j}
and
\phi\circ f^{\prime}=f\circ\phi
on
\mathfrak{L}_{j}^{\prime}
.

(2)

\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{k}^{\prime})\subset\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{k})
and
\phi\circ f^{\prime}=f^{2}\circ\phi
on
\mathfrak{L}_{k}^{\prime}
.

A
k
-limb insertion

\Phi:\hat{M}_{1/q}\to\hat{M}_{1/(q+1)}

is a continuous embedding
such that for any quadratic polynomial
f^{\prime}
corresponding to a parameter
c\in\hat{M}_{1/q}
and
f
the quadratic polynomial corresponding to
\Phi(c)
, there is a
k
-limb insertion from
f^{\prime}
to
f
.
A
1
-limb insertion from
\hat{M}_{1/2}
to
\hat{M}_{1/3}
was first constructed in
[
BD88
]
via quasiconformal surgery, and their argument can be easily generalized to produce
k
-limb insertions from
\hat{M}_{1/q}\to\hat{M}_{1/(q+1)}
for all
q
and
k
.

For all
q\geq 2
, let
\Phi_{q}:\hat{M}_{1/q}\to\hat{M}_{1/(q+1)}
be a
k_{q}
-limb insertion. Let us say that the sequence of limb insertions
(\Phi_{q})
is
balanced
if
k_{q}\to\infty
and
q-k_{q}\to\infty
when
q\to\infty
; for example we can take
k_{q}=\lfloor q/2\rfloor.
For any balanced sequence
(\Phi_{q})
of limb insertions, we define a
parabolic tail
to be a combinatorial type
\mathcal{C}
of the form

\mathcal{C}=\left\{\Phi_{n}\circ\dots\Phi_{q}(M)\mid n\geq q\right\}

for some small Mandelbrot set
M\subset\hat{M}_{1/q}
.

A family of combinatorial types
\mathcal{C}
is
parabolically bounded
if it is a union of a finite set of types and finitely many parabolic tails. This definition is a little hard to work with directly, so let us give a more combinatorial characterization.

Let
F:(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime})\to(\mathcal{U},\mathfrak{K})
be a periodically repelling renormalizable
\psi
-ql map and let
\mathcal{K}
be the corresponding cycle of small filled Julia sets.
For any
\mathbf{b}\geq 1
, we will say that the renormalization of
F
is
parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded
if:

(1)

F
has combinatorial rotation number
1/q
for some
q\geq 2
;

(2)

each limb
\mathfrak{L}_{j}
of
\mathfrak{K}
contains at most
\mathbf{b}
many components of
\mathcal{K}
; and

(3)

any component of
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathfrak{L}_{0}
is mapped into
\bigcup_{|j|\leq\mathbf{b}}\mathfrak{L}_{j}
by
F
.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a parabolically bounded family of types, then there is a finite set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
and
\mathbf{b}\geq 1
so that: if the renormalization of a periodically repelling
\psi
-ql map is in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, then the renormalization is parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded.

Proof.

Let
f^{\prime}
be renormalizable quadratic polynomial corresponding to a parameter in
\hat{M}_{1/q}

with filled Julia set
\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}
, limbs
\mathfrak{L}_{j}^{\prime}
, and cycle of small filled Julia sets
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
.
Let
\phi
be a
k
-limb insertion from
f^{\prime}
to a polynomial
f
corresponding to a parameter in
\hat{M}_{1/(q+1)}
with filled Julia set
\mathfrak{K}
and limbs
\mathfrak{L}_{j}
. Setting
\mathcal{K}=\phi(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})\cup f(\phi(\mathcal{K}))
, it follows that
\mathcal{K}
is a cycle of small filled Julia sets for
f
. If every limb of
\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}
contains at most
\mathbf{b}
many components of
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, then every limb of
\mathfrak{K}
also contains at most
\mathbf{b}
many components of
\mathcal{K}.
If
k,q-k>\mathbf{b}
and every component of
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
in
\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{\prime}
is mapped into
\bigcup_{|j|\leq\mathbf{b}}\mathfrak{L}_{j}^{\prime}
by
f
, then every component of
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathfrak{L}_{0}
is mapped into
\bigcup_{|j|\leq\mathbf{b}}\mathfrak{L}_{j}^{\prime}
by
f
.
Hence if
k,q-k>\mathbf{b}
, then

is renormalizable with parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded combinatorics.

It follows from the above that, up to removing a finite set, any parabolic tail corresponds to parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded renormalizations; the proposition immediately follows.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Conversely, any
\mathbf{b}
the maximal family of types containing parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded renormalizations is parabolically bounded.

4.
Dichotomy

In this section we prove the dichotomy in Proposition
3.7
.
We will use the definitions of ideal markings, canonical laminations and weighted arc diagrams, and waves detailed in Appendix

We equip the complement of the Hubbard tree

.

corresponding to either

or a component of
\mathcal{K}
,
let
V_{2}(\mathcal{G})
denote the remaining vertices, and let
E(\mathcal{G})
denote the edges of
\mathcal{G}
. As each vertex
v\in V_{2}(\mathcal{G})
is a branch point of
\mathcal{T}
, the valence in
\mathcal{G}
of any such
v
is at least 3.

Every interval of
\mathbf{L}_{j}
corresponds to a side in
\mathcal{T}
of some
v\in V_{1}(\mathcal{G})\setminus\{\alpha_{F}\}
or
e\in E(\mathcal{G})
. Each such vertex or edge has at most
2
sides, hence

(4.10)

|\mathbf{L}_{j}|\leq 2\left(|E(\mathcal{G})|+|V_{1}(\mathcal{G})|-1\right).

As the renormalization of
F
is parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded,
|V_{1}(\mathcal{G})|\leq\mathbf{b}+1
.
The desired bound then follows immediately from (
4.10
) and Lemma
4.9
.
∎

With the above facts, we are ready to prove Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of all horizontal and vertical leaves in
\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K})
, so
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})=\|X\|
. For all
n\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subset\mathcal{F}
denote the sublamination consisting of leaves
\beta
with
\langle[\beta],\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}\rangle\geq n.
Thus if
\mathcal{A}
is a set of arcs with
\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\alpha,\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}\rangle\geq n
, then

\|X|_{\mathcal{A}}\|\leq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F}_{n}).

We assume that

(4.11)

{n^{2}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F}_{n})}\geq{360\mathbf{b}M}{\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})}

for some
n\geq 0
; otherwise
X
is
M
-aligned with
\mathcal{T}
by definition. We also assume that
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T})\leq M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)
; otherwise
F
has
M
-amplification.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
by the inclusion
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
.
Lemma
4.7
implies that every leaf of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
contributes at least
n/2
leaves to
\mathcal{G}
, so

(4.12)

n^{2}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\leq 4\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{G})

by Lemma
2.9
.

For every interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}^{0}
, let
\mathcal{G}({\mathbf{I}})\subset\mathcal{G}
denote the sublamination consisting of leaves with an endpoint in
{\mathbf{I}}
.
As
\mathcal{I}
contains at most
6\mathbf{b}q\leq 6\mathbf{b}\mathbf{n}
intervals by Lemma
4.8
,
there is an interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
with

(4.13)

6\mathbf{b}\mathbf{n}\mathcal{W}({\mathcal{G}}({\mathbf{I}}))\geq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{G}).

As

\mathbf{n}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)\leq 4\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})+O(1)

by Lemma
4.3
,
(
4.11
), (
4.12
), and (
4.13
) imply that

\frac{\mathcal{W}({{\mathcal{G}}}({\mathbf{I}}))}{\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)}\geq\frac{n^{2}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F}_{n})}{120\mathbf{b}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})}\geq 3M

when
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)\gg 1
.
There is a wave
S\subset\mathcal{G}({\mathbf{I}})
with

2\mathcal{W}(S)+\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T})\geq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{G}({\mathbf{I}}));

hence
\mathcal{W}(S)\geq M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)
, so
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
has
M
-wide controllable waves.
∎

Combining Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
immediately yields Proposition
3.7
.

Remark 4.14
.

Note that the only part of the definition of parabolically bounded used in the proof of Proposition
3.7
is the bound
\mathbf{b}
on the number of small filled Julia sets in each limb of
\mathfrak{K}
. Hence Proposition
3.7
holds more generally; this observation may be useful in producing
a priori
bounds for other families of combinatorial types.

5.
The tame case

In this section, we will prove Proposition
3.8
.
Recall that we have fixed some parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded
\psi
-ql map in Section
3
, and that we implicitly assume all constants to depend on
\mathbf{b}
.

We define the
translation region

to be the subsurface
U_{(\mathbf{b},q-\mathbf{b})}.

Any arc
\alpha
in the translation region has a unique lift
F^{*}\alpha
that is also an arc, and usually in the translation region.
We can then form the equivalence relation on arcs in the translation region generated by
\alpha\sim F^{*}\alpha
; we call each resulting equivalence class a
translation class
.
We will say that a set of arcs
\mathcal{A}
in the translation region is
good
if it contains only horizontal arcs and for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}
, no arc in the translation class of
\alpha
intersects an arc in the translation class of
\beta
.
When a certain good set of arcs is clear from the context,
we’ll say that an arc is good if it belongs to this good set.

For an arc
\alpha
in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
,
we define its
combinatorial length
to be

\ell(\alpha)=1+\langle\alpha,\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}\rangle.

For any set of arcs
\mathcal{A}
, we denote
\ell(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\ell(\alpha).

We will arrange our whole discussion so as to consider only horizontal and vertical arcs (in particular, we ignore bivertical arcs);
we accordingly denote the horizontal and vertical part of the canonical WAD by
X=W_{\operatorname{can}}^{h+v}(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K})
.
Our first step towards proving Proposition
3.8
will be observing that most of the canonical weight is supported on a good set of arcs with bounded combinatorial length:

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
M
and
\epsilon
there exists
L
such that:
if
F
is
[M,\epsilon]
-HTND and
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame, then either
F
has
M
-amplification or there is a good set of arcs
\mathcal{A}
with
\ell(\mathcal{A})\leq L
and

\|X|_{\mathcal{A}}\|>(1-{\epsilon})\|X\|.

Conversely, we will also see that a good set of arcs with bounded combinatorial length cannot have most of the canonical weight:

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
L
there exists
\epsilon
such that: if
F
is
[L]
-HTND and
\mathcal{A}
is a good set of arcs with
\ell(\mathcal{A})\leq L
, then

\|X|_{\mathcal{A}}\|\leq(1-{\epsilon})\|X\|.

Together Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
are almost contradictory, however they have opposite dependence on quantifiers. To produce a contradiction, we first note that Lemma
5.1
implies that translation classes with a definite proportion of weight have bounded combinatorial length:

Corollary 5.3
.

For any
M
and
{\epsilon}
there exists
L
such that: if
F
is
[M,{\epsilon}]
-HTND,
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame, and
\mathcal{A}
is a translation class with
\|X|_{\mathcal{A}}\|\geq{\epsilon}\|X\|
, then either
F
has
M
-amplification or
\ell(\mathcal{A})\leq L
.

The last observation we need is that a good set of arcs with bounded combinatorial length is contained in a bounded number of translation classes:

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
L
, if
F
is
[L]
-HTND and
\mathcal{A}
is a good set of arcs with
\ell(\mathcal{A})\leq L
, then
\mathcal{A}
is contained in at most
3\mathbf{b}
translation classes.

By playing Lemma
5.1
and Corollary
5.3
back and forth against each other to control the heaviest
n
translation classes, and using Lemma
5.4
to bound
n
,
we can prove Proposition
3.8
:

Proof of Proposition
3.8
.

Setting
N=3\mathbf{b}
,
{\epsilon}_{0}=1
, and fixing some
M
, there exist sequences
(L_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
and
({\epsilon}_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
such that each
L_{n}
depends on

\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}:=\frac{{\epsilon}_{n-1}}{2(N-n+1)}

as in Corollary
5.3
, and each
{\epsilon}_{n}
depends on
\tilde{L}_{n}:=\sup_{i\leq n}L_{i}
as in Lemma
5.2
. We set
\delta=\min{\epsilon}_{n}/2

and let
L
depend on
\delta
as in Lemma
5.1
. Note that all the above constants depend only on
\mathbf{b}
and
M
, thus when
F
is
[M]
-HTND it follows that
F
is HTND relative to each
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
and
\tilde{L}_{n}
.
We assume for the sake of contradiction that
F
does not have
M
-amplification.

It follows from Lemma
5.1
that there is a good set of arcs
\mathcal{A}
with
\ell(\mathcal{A})\leq L
and

(5.5)

\|X|_{\mathcal{A}}\|>(1-\delta)\|X\|.

It then follows from Lemma
5.4
that we can write
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{A}_{N}
, where each
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is contained in a single translation class and
\|X|_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}\|\geq\|X|_{\mathcal{A}_{n+1}}\|.

Let us suppose that for some
0\leq n<N
we have

(5.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X|_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\|\leq(1-{\epsilon}_{n})\|X\|

and
\ell(\mathcal{A}_{i})\leq L_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
; both of these facts hold trivially for
n=0
.
As we have ordered the
\mathcal{A}_{i}
in decreasing order by weight, it then follows from (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) that

\frac{\|X|_{\mathcal{A}_{n+1}}\|}{\|X\|}\geq\frac{(1-\delta)-(1-{\epsilon}_{n})}{N-n}=\frac{{\epsilon}_{n}-\delta}{N-n}\geq\frac{{\epsilon}_{n}}{2(N-n)}=\tilde{\epsilon}_{n+1}.

Corollary
5.3
therefore implies that
\ell(\mathcal{A}_{n+1})\leq L_{n+1}
.
As
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
is a good set of arcs with combinatorial length at most
\tilde{L}_{n+1}
, it then follows from Lemma
5.2
that

\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\|X|_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}\|\leq(1-{\epsilon}_{n+1})\|X\|.

By induction on
n
, we can conclude that

\|X|_{\mathcal{A}}\|=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|X|_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\|\leq(1-{\epsilon}_{N})\|X\|\leq(1-2\delta)\|X\|,

which contradicts (
5.5
).
∎

We will prove Lemmas
5.1
,
5.4
and Corollary
5.3
in Section
5.2
after some preparation in Section
5.1
. We will outline the proof of Lemma
5.2
in Section
5.3
; the details of the argument will then occupy the rest of this section.

5.1.
Translation markings

In this section we apply the theory of translation markings introduced in Appendix
B
to our setting. For simplicity we will assume that
\mathfrak{K}
is locally connected; the general case can be handled similarly.

Let us think of
\mathbb{D}
as the universal cover of
\mathcal{U}^{0}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{0}
, with
0\in\mathbb{D}
mapping to the fixed point
\alpha_{F}
.
Simultaneously viewing
\mathbb{D}
as the universal cover of
\mathcal{U}^{1}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{1}
, with
0\in\mathbb{D}
mapping to the fixed point
\alpha_{f}
, the maps
f
and
\iota
lift to a holomorphic isomorphism
f_{\mathrm{in}}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
and a holomorphic immersion
\iota_{\mathrm{in}}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
respectively; we denote
F_{\mathrm{in}}=(f_{\mathrm{in}},\iota_{\mathrm{in}})
.

We consider the components of
\mathcal{K}
that are “closest” to the fixed point
\alpha_{F}
, that is those that are connected to
\alpha_{F}
by a path in
\mathcal{T}
that avoids
\mathcal{K}
.
Each such path
lifts to a path in
\mathbb{D}
connecting
0
to an interval in
\partial\mathbb{D}
that covers the ideal boundary of a component of
\mathcal{K}
.
We define the
inner translation marking

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}
to be the ideal marking of
\mathbb{D}
induced
by all intervals that can be obtained this way.

Proposition 5.7
.

The pair
(F_{\mathrm{in}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}})
is an
(n,p)
-translation marking with
n\leq q
and
p\leq\mathbf{b}
. If
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame, then

\mathcal{W}(F_{\mathrm{in}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}})\leq O(M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)).

Proof.

We can write
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}=({\mathbf{I}}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}}
for some
N\geq 1
, labeled so that
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
covers a component of
\mathcal{K}
for all odd
j
and so that the counter-clockwise circular ordering of the intervals is the ordering on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
.
We set
\mathcal{T}_{j}:=\mathcal{T}\cap\mathfrak{L}_{j}
and
\mathcal{T}_{j}^{\prime}:=F^{-1}(\mathcal{T})\cap\mathfrak{L}_{j}
.
As
\mathcal{T}_{j}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{j}
for all
\mathbf{b}\leq j<q-\mathbf{b}-1
, it follows that
\iota_{\mathrm{in}}
extends to a proper map fixing
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
for all odd
j
.
As
f
maps
\mathcal{T}_{j}^{\prime}
homeomorphically to
\mathcal{T}_{j+1}
for all
\mathbf{b}\leq j<q-\mathbf{b}-1
, we can choose the labeling so that
f_{\mathrm{in}}
extends to a homeomorphism from
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
to
{\mathbf{I}}_{j+2p}
for some integer
p
and all
0<j<N-2p
.
Thus
(F_{\mathrm{in}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}})
is a translation marking.
As each limb of
\mathfrak{K}
contains at most
\mathbf{b}
components of
\mathcal{K}
, it follows that
p\leq\mathbf{b}
and
2(n+1)p=N
for
n\leq q-2\mathbf{b}
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}
be the ideal marking of
\mathbb{D}
induced by all the intervals corresponding to the components of
\mathcal{K}
closest to
\alpha_{F}
, not just the ones in the translation region.
Thus
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}
: if
j\neq 0
then
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
is an interval in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}
, and
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}
is a multi-interval in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}
of length at most
2(2\mathbf{b}+1)+1
.
If
{\mathbf{I}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}
covers the Caratheodory boundary of a component of
\mathcal{K}
, then we have

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}}^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F).

Otherwise,
\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}}^{\prime})
is exactly the flux through some arc
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
; as
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame it then follows that

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}}^{\prime})\leq O(M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)).

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(F_{\mathrm{in}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|j|\leq 2p}\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}}_{j})

\displaystyle\leq(4p+2(2\mathbf{b}+1)+1)(\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)+O(M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)))

\displaystyle=O(M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f))

as desired.
∎

Let us now view
\mathbb{D}
as the universal cover of
\mathcal{U}^{0}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{0}
, with
0\in\mathbb{D}
mapping to a point
*\in\mathcal{U}^{0}\setminus\mathfrak{K}
in the translation region.
Simultaneously viewing
\mathbb{D}
as the universal cover of
\mathcal{U}^{1}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{1}
with
0\in\mathbb{D}
mapping to the same base point, the maps
f
and
\iota
lift to a holomorphic isomorphism
f_{\mathrm{out}}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
and a holomorphic immersion
\iota_{\mathrm{out}}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
respectively; we denote
F_{\mathrm{out}}=(f_{\mathrm{out}},\iota_{\mathrm{out}})
.

For every side of a component of
\mathcal{K}
, any path in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathfrak{K}
connecting
*
to that side lifts to a path in
\mathbb{D}
connecting
0
to an interval in
\partial\mathbb{D}
that covers the ideal boundary of a component of
\mathcal{K}
. We define the
outer translation marking

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{out}}
to be the ideal marking of
\mathbb{D}
induced by all the intervals that can be obtained in this way from paths inside the translation region.

Proposition 5.8
.

The pair
(F_{\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{out}})
is a cardinality
\leq 4\mathbf{b}q
translation marking. If
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame, then

(5.9)

\mathcal{W}(F_{\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{out}})\leq O(M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}f)).

Proof.

The argument that
(F_{\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{out}})
is a translation marking is identical to the proof in Proposition
5.7
. The computation of the standard width is similar; the only difference is now the standard width is bounded by the width to
O(\mathbf{b})
many intervals that cover a component of
\mathcal{K}
, the flux through
O(\mathbf{b})
many arcs in
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
, and the width of two waves over the
\partial_{C}\mathcal{L}_{0}
.
When
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame, so
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
does not have
M
-wide controllable waves, we similarly recover (
5.9
).
∎

5.2.
Good arcs

In this subsection we study good sets of arcs and prove Lemma
5.1
, Corollary
5.3
, and Lemma
5.4
. We recall the definition of a long arc for a translation marking from Appendix
B
.

Let us say that an arc in the translation region
has a long segment
if its lift into either
(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}})
or
(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{out}})
contains a long arc.
Note that any arc with a long segment must intersect an arc in its translation class, and hence cannot be good.
We observe that arcs with bounded combinatorial length and without long segments stay in a bounded part of the translation region:

Proposition 5.10
.

If
\alpha
is a horizontal arc in the translation region with
\ell(\alpha)\leq L
and
\alpha
has no long segments, then there is an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
with
|I|\leq 2L
such that
\alpha
is contained in
U_{I}
.

Proof.

For all intervals
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathcal{H}_{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
denote the subset consisting of arcs in
U_{I}
.
The arcs in
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
cut
\alpha
into segments
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{N}
, where

N=\langle\alpha,\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}\rangle=\ell(\alpha)-1.

For each
n
, let
I_{n}
be the minimal interval so that
\alpha_{n}
is contained in
U_{I_{n}}
.
Thus
\alpha
is contained in
U_{I}
for some interval
I
with

(5.11)

|I|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}|I_{n}|.

Let
D
be the component of
\mathcal{U}\setminus(\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}})
containing
\alpha_{F}
when
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
is chosen in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
. Any segment
\alpha_{n}
in
D
corresponds to a lift of
\alpha
to an arc in
(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{in}})
; if
|I_{n}|>3
then then the lifted arc must be long. Any segment
\alpha_{n}
not in
D
either satisfies
I_{n}=1
or corresponds to a lift of
\alpha
to an arc in
(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{out}})
; again if
|I_{n}|>3
then the lifted arc is long.
As
\alpha
has no long segments, it follows that
|I_{n}|\leq 2
for all
n
. Hence
|I|\leq 2(N+1)=2\ell(\alpha)
.
∎

With Proposition
5.10
, we can prove Lemma
5.4
:

Proof of Lemma
5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a good set of arcs with
\ell(\mathcal{A})\leq L
and let
k
be the number of distinct translation classes that intersect
\mathcal{A}
.
Proposition
5.10
implies that the union of all these translation classes form an arc diagram in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
that contains at least
(q-2\mathbf{b}-3L)k
arcs. The total number of arcs in an arc-diagram in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is at most
3\mathbf{b}q
, so

k\leq\frac{3\mathbf{b}q}{q-2\mathbf{b}-3L}\leq 3\mathbf{b}+1/2

when
q\ggg_{L}1
. As
k
is an integer, the proof is complete.
∎

We can now prove Lemma
5.1
and Corollary
5.3
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We assume that
F
is
M
-tame, and that
F
does not have
M
-amplification. Let
\Gamma
denote the set of all arcs in the support of
X
. For all
L\geq 1
, we denote
P(L)=U_{(\mathbf{b}+3L,q-\mathbf{b}-3L)}
. Let
\Gamma_{L}\subset\Gamma
be the subset consisting of horizontal arcs in
P(L)
, and let
G_{L}\subset\Gamma_{L}
be the subset consisting of arcs with combinatorial length at most
L

and without long segments. Theorem

implies that

\|X\|\geq\mathbf{n}(\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)/2-O(1))\geq(q/3)\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)

when
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)\gg 1
.

Any arc in
\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{L}
must contribute its weight in
X
to either
\mathcal{W}(F)
, the flux through some arc in
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
outside
P(L)
, the total width of some component of
\mathcal{K}
outside
P(L)
, or the width of a wave over
\partial_{C}\mathcal{L}_{0}
in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
. As we assume that
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame and
F
does not have
M
-amplification, it follows that

(5.12)

\|X|_{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{L}}\|=O(LM\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F))\leq({\epsilon}/2)\|X\|

when
F
is
[M,{\epsilon},L]
-HTND.

For any
L\geq 1
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{L}\setminus G_{L}
,
then either
\ell(\gamma)>L
or
\gamma
has a long segment.
As
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is
M
-tame, so
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{hor}}
is
M
-aligned with
\mathcal{T}
, it therefore follows from
Propositions
5.7
,
5.8
and Theorem
B.1

that

(5.13)

\|X|_{\Gamma_{L}\setminus G_{L}}\|\leq\frac{O(M\|X\|)}{(L-1)^{2}}+O(M\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)+q)\leq({\epsilon}/2)\|X\|

when
L\ggg_{{\epsilon}}1
and
F
is
[M,{\epsilon}]
-HTND.
Combining (
5.12
) and (
5.13
) yields

\|X|_{\Gamma\setminus G_{L}}\|=\|X|_{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{L}}\|+\|X|_{\Gamma_{L}\setminus G_{L}}\|\leq{\epsilon}\|X\|.

Now we observe that
X(F^{*}\alpha)\geq X(\alpha)
for any arc
\alpha
in the translation region with
F^{*}\alpha
defined.
Thus for any
\alpha,\beta\in G_{L}
, we have
\langle(F^{*})^{k}\alpha,\beta\rangle=0
for all
0\leq k<3L
.
As
\alpha,\beta
have combinatorial length
\leq L
and no long segments, it then follows from Proposition

5.4.

We now recall the the concepts of cross sections and cloning from Appendix

Lemma 5.18

For simplicity we assume that

For

.

is parabolically

-bounded,

Corollary 5.21
.

For suitable

,

Proposition 5.22
.

Suppose that

is parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded.
Let

and

be eigenweights for

and

respectively with eigenvalue

.
For any

that lie in the translation region,

and

. Moreover,

Proof.

There are finitely many parabolically
\mathbf{b}
-bounded renormalization types with

;
by Lemma

,

\mathcal{T}\cap L_{\mathbf{b}+1}
is a cross section for the push-forward map on

Therefore,

No near self-domination

We can now use the results of Section

Proof of Proposition

.

By Theorem

.

5.6.
The subsurface and WAD for an interval

We now turn to the proof of Proposition

Because the full return map

In this subsection,

We begin this subsection with a combinatorial discussion where we can think of

-quadratic-like map

For any interval

We begin with the following two combinatorial lemmas.

Lemma 5.26

There is a component

of

\mathcal{K}
).

Let

Then by that Corollary,

On the other hand, we observe that, for any

Lemma 5.27

∎

If

5.7.

We assume for the rest of this section that

is

-well controlled

We fix

that lie entirely in

,
and let

Lemma 5.30
.

For
L_{1}\ggg_{\epsilon,L}1
and
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F)\ggg_{\epsilon}1
, we can choose

in the translation region with

(5.31)

\left\|X^{b}_{I}\right\|<3\epsilon L_{1}\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}F).

For a given interval

, there are three kinds of contributions to

that have at least one endpoint in

to outside

This has an upper bound of

From

6.2.
Breakable waves

In this section we prove Proposition
6.2
. Recalling from Appendix
A.3
the definition of “breaking” a wave, we will first show that breakable waves are terminal, restricting our analysis to unbreakable waves. For long unbreakable waves we have three cases: waves over the critical limb, waves in the translation region, and waves connecting the critical limb to the translation region.
In these three cases, pushing forward yields respectively either self-intersection, which automatically implies that the width is small, an intersection with a shift of the wave, which implies the width is small by the Shift Lemma, or a lamination connecting components of
\mathcal{K}
, which implies that the width is bounded by the renormalization width;
in each case we can conclude that the wave cannot be very wide.

Let us now be more precise.
We recall from Appendix
A.3
that a path
\gamma
in
(\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T},\mathcal{I})

breaks
under
F
if
F_{*}\gamma
is not a single path.
For any wave
A
in
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{T}
and
n\geq 0
, we define the
n
-unbreakable part

A^{n\text{-}{\mathrm{ubr}}}\subset A
to be the subwave consisting of leaves

\alpha
such that
F_{*}^{m}\alpha
does not break under
F
for all
0\leq m<n
. We define the
n
-breakable part
of
A
to be

.
For any
0<\lambda<1
, we will say that

is
(n,\lambda)
-unbreakable
if
\mathcal{W}(A^{n\text{-}{\mathrm{ubr}}})\geq\lambda\mathcal{W}(A)
.

We split our proof of Proposition
6.2
into two parts. The first part is showing that controllable waves are either unbreakable or terminal, and the second is showing that unbreakable waves are short. More precisely, we have the following two propositions:

Proposition 6.10
.

For any
n
,
k
, and
\lambda
there exists
\eta
and
\delta
such that: if
A
is a
[n,\lambda]
-wide wave and

is
(n,\lambda)
-unbreakable or
(\delta,\eta)
-terminal. Moreover,

does not depend on
\lambda
, and

does not depend on

.

Proposition 6.11
.

There is a universal

such that for any
\lambda
there exists
\lambda^{\prime}
satisfying: for all

, any
[k,\lambda]
-wide
(N,\lambda^{\prime})
-unbreakable

-controllable wave is
\lambda
-short.

Together, Propositions

Proof of Proposition

.

Let

be the universal constant in Proposition

, and let

-wide
k
-controllable wave.

We set

. For any
\lambda_{1}

\mathcal{W}(B)\geq\mathcal{W}(A^{N\text{-}{\mathrm{br}}})\geq\lambda_{1}\mathcal{W}(A).

Choosing
\lambda_{1}\gg\lambda_{2}
for some
\lambda_{2}
, if
\mathcal{W}(A^{N\text{-}{\mathrm{ubr}}})\geq(1-\lambda_{1})\mathcal{W}(A)
, then

6.11
implies that

is
\lambda_{2}
-short, so

\mathcal{W}(B)\geq\mathcal{W}(A^{N\text{-}{\mathrm{br}}})+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{W}(A^{N\text{-}{\mathrm{ubr}}})\geq\lambda_{2}\mathcal{W}(A).

Combining (

.

As

is short, and hence
12\mathbf{b}
-controllable, for any
\lambda_{3}
it follows from Proposition

that

\delta_{3}
and universal

.
Choosing

By the above, either

\lambda

∎

Our first step in proving Propositions

For any

with

\mathcal{W}(D)\geq\lambda_{2}\mathcal{W}(C)\geq\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\mathcal{W}(A)

or
C
is
(\delta_{2},\eta_{2})
-terminal for some
\delta_{2}
and universal
\eta_{2}
.
Choosing
\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda
, as
F_{*}^{q-j}I
intersects
\mathbf{L}_{0}
it follows that
D
is a critical
\lambda
-visit for

Choosing
\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\gg_{\delta_{2}}0

7.
Rigidity

We now sketch the framework for upgrading
a priori
bounds to rigidity; for more details we refer the reader to
[
Lyu97
]
.

Let
M\subset\mathcal{M}
be a prime and primitive small Mandelbrot set, and let
f
be a quadratic polynomial corresponding to a parameter in
M
.
Let
I^{0}(f)
denote the closure of the union of the external rays of
f
landing at
\alpha_{f}
together with the equipotential of
f
at potential
1
. The set
I^{0}(f)
cuts
\mathbb{C}
into the
Yoccoz puzzle pieces
of level
0
.
For all
n>1
, denoting
I^{n}(f):=f^{-n}(I^{0}(f))
, the Yoccoz puzzle pieces of level
n
are defined to be the components of
\mathbb{C}\setminus I^{n}(f)
.
Let
Y^{n}(f)
denote the level
n
Yoccoz puzzle piece containing the critical point of
f
.
For
p
the associated period of
M
, there is some minimal
n\geq 0
depending only on
M
so that
f^{n}:Y^{n+p}(f)\to Y^{n}(f)
restricts to a quadratic-like map with connected filled Julia set;
n
is called the
renormalization level
of
M
.

For
n
the renormalization level of
M
and
f,g
two quadratic polynomials corresponding to parameters in
M
, there is a canonical homeomorphism
I^{n}(f)\to I^{n}(g)
induced by the canonical markings of external rays and equipotentials.
Let
d_{M}(f,g)
denote the
Teichmüller distance
between
I^{n}(f)
and
I^{n}(g)
, that is half of the
\log
of the smallest dilatation of a quasiconformal homeomorphism
h:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
that agrees with the canonical map
I^{n}(f)\to I^{n}(g)
. We define the
puzzle Teichmüller diameter

\text{diam}(M)
to
be the max of
d_{M}(f,g)
over all such
f
and
g
.
Let us say that set
\mathcal{C}
of prime and primitive types has
bounded puzzle Teichmüller diameter
when

\sup_{M\in\mathcal{C}}\text{diam}(M)<\infty.

A family of types
\mathcal{C}
is
combinatorially rigid
if any two infinitely renormalizable quadratic-like maps with identical combinatorics in
\mathcal{C}
are hybrid equivalent; if
\mathcal{C}
is combinatorially rigid then it is automatically rigid in the sense from the introduction.
The following theorem is proved in
[
Lyu97
]
, although it is not stated in this generality there:

Theorem B
.

Every set of prime primitive types with beau bounds and bounded puzzle Teichmüller diameter is rigid.

Proof.

The entire construction from
[
Lyu97
, Section 10]
can be applied in this setting without change. The entire purpose of
[
Lyu97
, Section 11]
, and more specifically
[
Lyu97
, Main Lemma]
, is to show that the secondary limbs condition implies bounded puzzle Teichmüller diameter.
∎

Appendix A
Ideal markings and laminations

Here we introduce the concept of an ideal marking and then define the canonical lamination and waves in terms of these markings.

A.1.
Ideal markings

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a hyperbolic Riemann surface without cusps.
Let
\pi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathcal{V}
be the universal covering, and let
\Lambda
be the limit set of the group
\Delta
of deck transformations. The
ideal boundary
of
\mathcal{V}
is

\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}:=(\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda)/\Delta.

The
ideal closure

\mathcal{V}
is
\overline{\mathcal{V}}:=\mathcal{V}\cup\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
.

We define an
ideal marking
of
\mathcal{V}
to be a countable set

\mathcal{I}=\{{\mathbf{I}}_{n}\}_{n}
of pair-wise disjoint
open connected subsets of the
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
such that
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}=\overline{\bigcup_{n}{\mathbf{I}}_{n}}.
We will call the elements of
\mathcal{I}

intervals
, and the elements of the complement
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}\setminus{\bigcup_{n}{{\mathbf{I}}_{n}}}

endpoints
. The
cardinality
of
\mathcal{I}
is the number of intervals it contains.
Note that we allow intervals to be entire components of the ideal boundary, in which case the interval is homeomorphic to
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Fixing an ideal marking
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{V}
,
we will call a connected open subset
I
of the ideal boundary of
\mathcal{V}
that is the union of intervals and endpoints a
multi-interval
in
\mathcal{I}
.
We define the
combinatorial length

I
to be the number
\ell(I)
of intervals in
\mathcal{I}
it contains.
We will say that a multi-interval
J
is
well-inside

I
, and write
J\Subset I
, if
\overline{J}\subset I
.
We will say that
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
is an
end-interval
of
I
if
{\mathbf{I}}\subset I
and
{\mathbf{I}}\not\Subset I
; if
I
is an entire component of
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
then
I
has no end-intervals.

We will say that another ideal marking
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{V}
is a
refinement
of
\mathcal{I}
if every interval in
\mathcal{I}
is a multi-interval in
\mathcal{J}
. We will say that the refinement is
minor
if the combinatorial length in
\mathcal{J}
of every interval in
\mathcal{I}
is one; that is every interval in
\mathcal{J}
can be obtained from an interval in
\mathcal{I}
by removing at most one point.
The ideal markings of
\mathcal{V}
are partially ordered by refinement, and there is a unique minimal ideal marking in this order given by the set of all components of
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
.

We will say that a
partial ideal marking
of
\mathcal{V}
is a nonempty countable set
\mathcal{J}
of pairwise disjoint connected open sets and points in
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
. From the partial marking we can form the
induced marking

by taking the smallest tiling that contains each interval in
\mathcal{J}
and has each point in
\mathcal{J}
as an endpoint.

For any subset
X
of
\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
,
we will say that a proper path
\gamma
in
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
is
subordinate to
X
if, up to homotopy rel its endpoints,
\gamma
belongs to every neighborhood of
X
in
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
.
We will say that a proper path
\gamma
in
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
is a
path in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
if both its endpoints belong to intervals in
\mathcal{I}
and it is not subordinate to a multi-interval with combinatorial length at most two.
An
arc
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
is a homotopy class of simple paths.
A
lamination
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
is a lamination in
\mathcal{V}
consisting of paths in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
. We will say that a path
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})

ends
at a multi-interval
I
if it has an endpoint in
I
. We will say that a lamination
\mathcal{F}
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
ends at
I
if every leaf ends at
I
; we will say that
\mathcal{F}
is
localized
if it ends at an interval in
\mathcal{I}
.

Let
S
be a lamination in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
subordinate to a multi-interval
B
in
\mathcal{I}
, that is every leaf of
S
is subordinate to
B
; we will call
S
a
wave subordinate to
B
. We will call the smallest such multi-interval
B
the
base
of
S
. We define the
ends

of
S
to be the smallest multi-intervals
I
and
J
such that each leaf of
S
has one endpoint in
I
and the other in
J
. We define the
crest
of
S
to be
C=B\setminus(I\cup J)
; we will say that
S

crests over

X\subset\partial^{i}\mathcal{V}
, or equivalently that
S
is a
wave over

X
, if
X\subset C
.

A.2.
The canonical weighted arc diagram

A.2.1.
Rectangles

Let
\overline{{\bm{\Pi}}}
be a standard rectangle with horizontal sides
{\mathbf{I}}
and

{\mathbf{J}}
, and let
\overline{\mathcal{F}}=\overline{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})
be the vertical foliation on
\overline{{\bm{\Pi}}}
. We let

\overline{\mathcal{W}}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})\equiv{\mathcal{W}}(\overline{\mathcal{F}})

be its conformal
width
, which is also equal to the width of the
full path family in
\overline{{\bm{\Pi}}}
connecting
{\mathbf{I}}
to
{\mathbf{J}}
.
When the width of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is greater than 2, we can remove square buffers from
\overline{{\bm{\Pi}}}
to obtain a quadrilateral
{\bm{\Pi}}
supporting a foliation
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})
with width

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})\equiv\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{W}(\overline{\mathcal{F}})-2.

If instead the width of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is at most two, then we take
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})
to be the empty foliation, so
\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})\equiv\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})=0
.
Denoting the hyperbolic distance and length in
\overline{{\bm{\Pi}}}
by
d_{\mathrm{hyp}}
and
\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}
respectively, the following lemma relates the conformal width to hyperbolic geometry:

Lemma A.1
.

Let
{\bm{\gamma}}
and
{\bm{\delta}}
be the hyperbolic geodesics in
\overline{{\bm{\Pi}}}
that share endpoints with
{\mathbf{I}}
and
{\mathbf{J}}
respectively. For any
{\epsilon}>0
and
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\epsilon}=\left\{z\in{\bm{\gamma}}\mid d_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z,{\bm{\delta}})<{\epsilon}\right\}
, we have:

\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{\epsilon})=\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{J}})+O_{\epsilon}(1).

A.2.2.
Weighted arc diagrams

Now let us fix a Riemann surface
\mathcal{V}
with ideal marking
\mathcal{I}

as in the previous subsection.
A
weighted arc diagram
(WAD) on
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
is a function
W:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that any two arcs in the support of
W
do not intersect.
The
norm
of a weighted arc diagram is defined as

\left\|W\right\|=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}W(\alpha).

Denoting the Euler characteristic of
\mathcal{V}
by
\chi(\mathcal{V})
,
the number of arcs in the support of any WAD on
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
is at most
3|\chi(\mathcal{V})|+|\mathcal{I}|
.

For any lamination
\mathcal{F}
and arc
\alpha
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
, let
\mathcal{F}(\alpha)\subset\mathcal{F}
denote the sublamination consisting of leaves represented by
\alpha
.
The lamination
\mathcal{F}
determines a WAD
W_{\mathcal{F}}
on
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
defined by

W_{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha)=\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F}(\alpha)).

We will call
W_{\mathcal{F}}
the WAD
induced by

\mathcal{F}
; any WAD induced by a lamination is called
valid
.

A.2.3.
The canonical lamination and arc diagram

Let
\pi:\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathcal{V}
be the universal cover of
\mathcal{V}
, and let
\Lambda
be the limit set of the group
\Delta
of deck transformations.
The ideal marking
\mathcal{I}
lifts to an ideal marking
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
of
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
. Every interval
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
is mapped to an interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
as either a homeomorphism or a universal cover; in either case we will call
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
a
lift
of
{\mathbf{I}}
.

If
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
is an annulus with the minimal ideal marking, then we can identify
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
with an infinite strip
S=\{z:|\text{Im}z|<R\}
for some
R>0
so that
\Delta
is the group generated by
z\mapsto z+1
and
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}=\{\mathbb{R}\pm iR\}.

In this case,
the foliation of
S
by vertical lines descends to a lamination on
\mathcal{V}
,
which we
denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)=\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})(\alpha)
for
\alpha
the unique arc in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
. We also define the
flux
through
\alpha
to be

\flux(\alpha)=\flux(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})(\alpha):=1/\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha))=\mmod(\mathcal{V}).

We suppose now that
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
is not an annulus with the minimal ideal marking.
Any arc
\alpha
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
connecting intervals
{\mathbf{I}}
and
{\mathbf{J}}
lifts to an arc
\tilde{\alpha}
in
(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathcal{I}})
connecting some intervals
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
that cover
{\mathbf{I}}
and
{\mathbf{J}}
respectively. These lifted intervals determine an ideal quadrilateral with “horizontal” sides
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
, and corresponding foliations
\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{I}},\tilde{\mathbf{J}})
as above.
This foliation descends to a lamination
\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)=\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})(\alpha)
on
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
whose width does not depend on the choice of lift
\tilde{\alpha}
.
For an arc
\alpha
as above, we also define the
flux
through
\alpha
to be

\flux(\alpha)=\flux(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})(\alpha):=1/{\overline{\mathcal{W}}(\tilde{\mathbf{I}},\tilde{\mathbf{J}})}.

Denoting the set of all arcs in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
by
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
, the union

\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}=\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I}):=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)

is a lamination, called the
canonical lamination
of
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I}).

The weighted arc diagram

W_{\operatorname{can}}=W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
induced by
\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}
is called the
canonical WAD
of
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
.
The canonical WAD is maximal in the sense that if
W
is another valid WAD on
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
then

W(\alpha)\leq W_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)+2

for all arcs
\alpha
. Note that the function
\alpha\mapsto\flux(\alpha)
is usually not a weighted arc diagram as every arc has positive flux.

For any interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{I}})=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})({\mathbf{I}})
the set of oriented arcs in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
that start at
{\mathbf{I}}
.
We will call

W_{\operatorname{can}}({\mathbf{I}})=W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})({\mathbf{I}}):=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{I}})}W_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)

the
local canonical weight
of
{\mathbf{I}}
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
.
We use oriented arcs so that the weight of any arc connecting
{\mathbf{I}}
to itself is counted twice. In particular, we have

2\|W_{\operatorname{can}}\|=\sum_{{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}}W_{\operatorname{can}}({\mathbf{I}}).

A.2.4.
Local widths

If
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
is simply connected and
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
is a lift of
{\mathbf{I}}
, then there exists some minimal multi-interval
\tilde{I}
in
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
that contains the closure of
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
; the
local width
of
{\mathbf{I}}
is defined to be

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}})=\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I},{\mathbf{I}}):=\overline{\mathcal{W}}(\tilde{\mathbf{I}},\partial^{i}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\tilde{I}).

This width does not depend on the choice of lift
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
.
If instead
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
is not simply connected and
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
is a lift of
{\mathbf{I}}
, then the stabilizer of
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
in
\Delta
is a cyclic group generated by a hyperbolic Möbius transformation
\tau
. The Riemann surface
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}/\langle\tau\rangle
is an annulus; the
local width
of
{\mathbf{I}}
in this case is defined as

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}})=\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I},{\mathbf{I}}):=\mathcal{W}(\tilde{\mathcal{V}}/\langle\tau\rangle).

We define the
total width
of
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
to be

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})=\sum_{{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}}).

A.2.5.
Thick-thin decomposition

It follows immediately from the definitions that we can relate the local weight and local width of an interval by

(A.2)

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}})-O(|\chi(\mathcal{V})|+|\mathcal{I}|)\leq W_{\operatorname{can}}({\mathbf{I}})\leq\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}}).

In fact, the following theorem, first proved in
[
KL26
]
, compares the local weight and width of
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
:

Theorem A.3

(Thick-thin decomposition)
.

If
\mathcal{V}
is a Riemann surface with ideal marking

\mathcal{I}
, then:

(A.4)

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})-O(|\chi(\mathcal{V})|+|\mathcal{I}|)\leq 2\|W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})\|\leq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I}).

Proof.

The second inequality follows immediately from (
A.2
), so we only need to prove the first inequality. We set
p=|\chi(\mathcal{V})|+|\mathcal{I}|.

We consider first the case where every interval in
\mathcal{I}
is simply connected.
For every
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
, let
\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}
be the hyperbolic geodesic in
\mathcal{V}
with the same endpoints as
{\mathbf{I}}
.
We set
\Gamma=\bigcup\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}
.

Let
\pi:\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathcal{V}
be a universal covering, and let
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
be the corresponding lift of
\mathcal{I}
. For any multi-interval
\tilde{I}
in
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
, we let
\gamma_{\tilde{I}}
be the hyperbolic geodesic in
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
with the same endpoints as
\tilde{I}
. Note that if
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
is a lift of
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
, then
\pi
maps
\gamma_{\tilde{\mathbf{I}}}
isometrically to
\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}
.

Let
d_{\mathrm{hyp}}
and
\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}
denote hyperbolic distance and length respectively in both
\mathcal{V}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
.
We now fix some
{\epsilon}>0
small enough so that for any disjoint hyperbolic geodesics
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
in
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\cong\mathbb{D}
with none of the three separating the other two, if
x\in\gamma_{0}
then the
{\epsilon}
-ball around
x
intersects at most one of
{\epsilon}_{1}
and
{\epsilon}_{2}
.

For any oriented arc
\alpha
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
connecting intervals
{\mathbf{I}}
and
{\mathbf{J}}
with corresponding lift
\tilde{\alpha}
in
(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathcal{I}})
connecting interval
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
, we set

\gamma_{\alpha}=\{\pi(z):z\in\gamma_{\tilde{\mathbf{I}}},d_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z,\gamma_{\tilde{{\mathbf{J}}}})<{\epsilon}\}.

For any interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
, we define the
thin part
of
\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}
to be
\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\mathrm{thin}}:=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{I}})}\gamma_{\alpha}
. We define the
thin part
of
\Gamma
to be
\Gamma^{\mathrm{thin}}=\bigcup\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\mathrm{thin}}.

It follows from Lemma
A.1
that

W_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)=\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\gamma_{\alpha})+O(1),

hence

(A.5)

2\|W_{\operatorname{can}}\|=\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thin}})+O(p).

For any interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
and lift
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
adjacent to some intervals
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{1},\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{2}
, we similarly define the
cuspidal part
of
\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}
to

\gamma_{{\mathbf{I}}}^{\mathrm{cusp}}=\{\pi(z):z\in\gamma_{\tilde{\mathbf{I}}},d_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z,\gamma_{\tilde{{\mathbf{I}}}_{1}}\cup\gamma_{\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{2}})<{\epsilon}\},

which does not depend on the choice of lift.
We define the
cuspidal part
of
\Gamma
to be
\Gamma^{\mathrm{cusp}}=\bigcup\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\mathrm{cusp}}.

For any interval
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
, we define the
thick part
of
\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}
to be
\gamma_{{\mathbf{I}}}\setminus(\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\mathrm{thin}}\cup\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\mathrm{cusp}});
we similarly define

\Gamma^{\mathrm{thick}}=\Gamma\setminus(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thin}}\cup\Gamma^{\mathrm{cusp}}).

Let
D\subset\mathcal{V}
be the region bounded by
\Gamma
, it follows from Gauss-Bonnet formula that the hyperbolic area of
D
is
O(p)
.
We consider a maximal net of
{\epsilon}
-separated points in
\Gamma^{\mathrm{thick}}
; it contains approximately
\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thick}})/{\epsilon}
many points.
Our choice of
{\epsilon}
ensures that the hyperbolic disks of radius
{\epsilon}/2
centered at these points do not overlap, so the total area of their intersection with
D
is bounded by
O(p)
. Hence

\frac{\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thick}})}{{\epsilon}}\cdot{\epsilon}^{2}=O(p),

so

(A.6)

\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thick}})=O(p/{\epsilon}).

For any lift
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
of some
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
adjacent to intervals
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{2}
, let
\tilde{I}
be the minimal multi-interval in
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
containing
\tilde{\mathbf{I}},\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{1}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{2}
. We set

\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\epsilon}=\left\{\pi(z)\mid z\in\gamma_{\tilde{\mathbf{I}}},d_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z,\gamma_{\tilde{I}})<{\epsilon}\right\},

which does not depend on the choice of lift.
It follows from Lemma
A.1
that
\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\epsilon})=\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}})+O(1)
.
Setting
\Gamma^{\epsilon}=\bigcup\gamma_{\mathbf{I}}^{\epsilon}
, our assumption on
{\epsilon}
ensures that
\Gamma^{\epsilon}\subset\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\mathrm{cusp}}
, so

(A.7)

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})\leq\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\epsilon})+O(|\mathcal{I}|)\leq\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\mathrm{cusp}})+O(|\mathcal{I}|).

Combining (
A.5
), (
A.6
), and (
A.7
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle\leq\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thin}})+\l_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{thick}})+O(|\mathcal{I}|)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|W_{\operatorname{can}}\|+O(p)

as desired.

Now let us return to the general case where some intervals in
\mathcal{I}
may not be simply connected. We consider a minor refinement
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{I}
.
Thus for any interval
{\mathbf{J}}\in\mathcal{J}
there is a unique
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathcal{I}
with
{\mathbf{J}}\subset{\mathbf{I}}
, moreover either
{\mathbf{I}}={\mathbf{J}}
or
{\mathbf{I}}=\overline{{\mathbf{J}}}
.
If
{\mathbf{I}}={\mathbf{J}}
, then it follows immediately from the definitions that
\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}})=\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{J}})
. If
{\mathbf{I}}=\overline{{\mathbf{J}}}
, then instead we have

\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{I}})\leq\mathcal{W}({\mathbf{J}})+O(1).

Hence

(A.8)

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})\leq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{J})+O(p).

Let
\alpha
be an arc in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
, and let
B(\alpha)
be the set of all arcs in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{J})
that are equivalent to
\alpha
in
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})
. As the union
\bigcup_{\beta\in B(\alpha)}\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{can}}(\beta)
is a lamination consisting of paths homotopic to
\alpha
, it follows from the maximality of the canonical weighted arc diagram that

\sum_{\beta\in B(\alpha)}W_{\operatorname{can}}(\beta)\leq W_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)+O(1).

Hence

(A.9)

\|W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{J})\|\leq\|W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})\|+O(p).

We can choose
\mathcal{J}
so that every interval in
\mathcal{J}
is simply connected, so (
A.4
) holds for
\mathcal{J}
. Together with (
A.8
) and (
A.9
), we can conclude

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})\leq\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{J})+O(p)\leq 2\|W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{J})\|+O(p)\leq 2\|W_{\operatorname{can}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{I})\|+O(p)

as desired.
∎

Appendix B
Translation markings

Fixing some positive integers

and setting

, let

be a cardinality

with the intervals labeled according to their counter-clockwise circular ordering.
Let

be a holomorphic isomorphism that extends continuously to a homeomorphism from

to
{\mathbf{I}}_{j+2p}
for all

. Let

be a holomorphic immersion that extends continuously to a homeomorphism from
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
to
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}

.

(f,\iota)
by

, we will call the pair

.

Let us say that an arc in

For any

-translation marking

\|W_{\operatorname{can}}^{\mathrm{lo}}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{I})\|=O(\mathcal{W}(F,\mathcal{I})+np).

Setting

automatically,
hence

(B.2)

\|W_{\operatorname{can}}^{\mathrm{lo}}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{I})\|\leq\mathcal{W}(X^{\mathrm{lo}})+\mathcal{W}(F,\mathcal{I}).

Let
A
,
B
and
C
be the sublaminations of

consisting of all the leaves connecting some
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
to
{\mathbf{I}}_{k}
with

both odd, one of
i,j
odd, and
i,j
both even respectively.
Fixing some
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
and
{\mathbf{I}}_{k}\subset\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus I
with
2p<j<k<N-2p
, let

be the sublamination consisting of leaves connecting
{\mathbf{I}}_{j}
to
{\mathbf{I}}_{k}
.
Note that

many such sublaminations

.

Let us recall the definition of the breakable and unbreakable parts under

of laminations from Appendix
A.3
.
If

, so

, then every leaf of

intersects every leaf of

; hence

.
If both

and

Thus

(B.3)

\mathcal{W}(X^{\mathrm{lo}}\cap X^{\mathrm{ubr}})+\mathcal{W}(A^{\mathrm{lo}})\leq 4N.

If both

Appendix C

In this appendix we introduce the concept of cloning a cross section of a directed graph, and estimate the leading eigenvalue and associated eigenvector of the result.

Let

be a finite directed graph with vertex set

.
We say that

(with the same source), each edge out of

, and let

Let

.
Let

be the matrix for

.
Suppose that

is primitive (i.e.

Let

,

.
We observe that, when

, and

(for

.
We also observe that, when

, every directed path of length

in

that

at a vertex in

must begin in some

, with

.

Let

be the labeled directed graph on

to

in

to

(2)

.

By (

is large,

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\lambda^{n}
is uniformly large (and positive) on
[1,2]
, while
h^{\prime}(\lambda)
is bounded on that interval.
We conclude that there is a unique solution to (
C.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and that it lies in
(1,2)
.

Moreover,
letting
\alpha=1/n
,
we can recast (
C.2
) as

(C.6)

\alpha\log h(\lambda)=\log\lambda.

Letting
g(\alpha,\lambda)=\alpha\log h(\lambda)-\log\lambda
, we see that
g(0,1)=0
, and
\partial_{\alpha}g(0,1)=\log h(1)
and
\partial_{\lambda}g(0,1)=-1
.
Therefore,
by the Implicit Function Theorem,
we can solve (
C.6
) for
\lambda\equiv\lambda(\alpha)
for all
\alpha
near 0,
with
\lambda(0)=1
and
\lambda^{\prime}(0)=\log h(1)
.
Hence

\lambda_{n}-1\sim\frac{\log h(1)}{n}

and the Theorem follows.
∎

Appendix D
The Wanderers Theorem

Here we state and prove the Wanderers Theorem,
which is similar in spirit to the Covering Lemma and the Quasi-Additivity Law from
[
KL09
]
.
We state the theorem in a somewhat simplified form that will be sufficient for its application in this paper.

Theorem D.1
.

Suppose
X
and
Y
are Riemann surfaces and
g\colon X\to Y
is proper and holomorphic, of degree
D
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is a finite set of disjoint compact connected subsets of
Y
whose union contains the critical values of
g
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of components of
g^{-1}(\bigcup\mathcal{B})
,
and suppose
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathcal{A}
.
Suppose that
g\colon\bigcup\hat{\mathcal{A}}\to\bigcup\mathcal{B}
is at most
d
to 1.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be an oriented partial foliation of
X
such that every leaf of
\mathcal{F}

(1)

begins at an element of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
,

(2)

ends at an element of
\mathcal{A}
,

(3)

visits at least
n-1
elements of
\mathcal{A}
in between, and

(4)

does not cross through the component of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
where it begins.

Then

(D.2)

\left\|\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{F})\right\|\leq\frac{2d}{n}\left\|W_{\operatorname{can}}^{h+v}(Y\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{B})\right\|+C(|\chi(Y)|,|\mathcal{B}|,D).

Before proving this theorem,
we should recall the definition of the
route
of a
good path
from
[
KL09
]
;
we then have the following lemma, which is left to the reader:

Lemma D.3
.

Suppose that

(1)

C
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{2}
are good paths in
X
rel
\mathcal{A}
,

(2)

\operatorname{rt}(C_{1})=\operatorname{rt}(C_{2})
,

(3)

|\operatorname{rt}(C)|=|\operatorname{rt}(C_{1})|
,
and

(4)

\delta_{k}(C)\subset Q_{k}(C_{1},C_{2})
.

Then
\operatorname{rt}(C)=\operatorname{rt}(C_{1})
.

Proof.

First suppose that
g
is a (branched) Galois cover.
We can trim the leaves of
\mathcal{F}
at their terminal ends so that every resulting leaf is good (in the sense of
[
KL09
]
) and has a route of length exactly
n
. We will still call the resulting foliation
\mathcal{F}
; its width is at least as great as the original
\mathcal{F}
.
We partition
\mathcal{F}
by route; the leaves with the same route form a parallel family, with a canonical linear order up to reversal. For each route
p
, we denote the corresponding subset of
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{F}_{p}
. Each
\mathcal{F}_{p}
(and indeed the whole of
\mathcal{F}
) has a measure given by extremal width. We can form buffers of width 4 at both ends of each route class; we let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
be
\mathcal{F}_{p}
with these buffers removed, and denote the corresponding little rectangles, as in
[
KL09
]
, by
\tilde{Q}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
.

Let
p
and
q
be two routes, and suppose that, for some
\sigma\in\Deck(X/Y)
,
\sigma Q_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{p})\cap Q_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{q})\neq\emptyset
as oriented homotopy classes in
X\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{A}
. As in
[
KL09
]
, we can choose leaves in each of the two buffers of
\sigma\mathcal{F}_{p}
and
\mathcal{F}_{q}
(for four leaves in all), such that the four leaves are disjoint; we can then label three of these leaves as
C
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{2}
such that they satisfy the hypotheses of Lemma
D.3
. By that Lemma, we can conclude that
\sigma p=q
.

Because we have already cut out buffers,
each
\tilde{Q}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
is mapped injectively by
g
into the corresponding canonical rectangle for
Y\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{B}
.
We pull back the canonical metric for the latter to each
Q_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
; then each leaf of each
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
has length at least
n
.
If two of these
\tilde{Q}_{k}
’s map by
g
to intersect
and have the same orientation
,
their associated routes are related by an element
\sigma
of the deck group.
This latter relation is an equivalence relation,
and each equivalence class has at most
d
elements,
because each leaf of
\mathcal{F}
begins at an element of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
.
Thus
g
is at most
2d
to 1 on
\bigcup_{p}\tilde{Q}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
, and the pullback metric has area at most
2d\left\|W_{\operatorname{can}}(Y\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{B}\right\|
.
The Theorem then follows.

Now suppose that
g
is not Galois. As in
[
KL09
]
, we can form a branched cover
h\colon\breve{X}\to X
such that
\breve{g}:=g\circ h
is a Galois branched cover of degree at most
D!
. We let
\breve{A}=h^{-1}(A)
,
\breve{\hat{A}}=h^{-1}\hat{A}
, and
\breve{\mathcal{F}}=h^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{F}}
. Then
W(\breve{\mathcal{F}})=(\deg h)W(\mathcal{F})
, and
\breve{g}\colon\breve{\hat{A}}\to\mathcal{B}
is at most
d(\deg h)
to 1. Applying (
D.2
) to the Galois cover
\breve{g}
, we obtain

\left\|\mathcal{W}(\breve{\mathcal{F}})\right\|\leq\frac{2d(\deg h)}{n}\left\|W_{\operatorname{can}}^{h+v}(Y\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{B})\right\|+C(|\chi(Y)|,|\mathcal{B}|,D!).\qed
