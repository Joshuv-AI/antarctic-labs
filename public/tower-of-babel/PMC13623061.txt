Cardiovascular Stress Physiology and Interoceptive Pathways to Brain Aging and Health
Authors: Peter J. Gianaros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Individuals with a phenotypic tendency to react to acute psychological stressors with large and prolonged rises in blood pressure (stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity) may be at risk for hypertension and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The latter outcomes themselves drive cerebrovascular disease and later-life dementias. At issue here is the plausibility of whether stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity could be considered as a form of cardiovascular stress physiology that relates to cerebrovascular disease and dementia vulnerability via “bottom-up” or interoceptive pathways of the brain-heart axis.Method:This Scientific Spotlight describes a heuristic Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis on cardiovascular physiology and interoceptive pathways to aging-related pathology within the brain.Results:Some evidence in need of replication suggests that stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity relates to white matter brain lesions; risk for ischemic strokes; and higher rates of vascular determinants of dementia.Conclusion:A new generation of research is needed to understand the interoceptive pathways by which cardiovascular stress physiology could predispose to cerebrovascular disease and dementia, as well as to identify biopsychosocial factors that could be modified to support healthy brain aging.

Do large and prolonged blood pressure reactions to acute psychological stressors increase vulnerability to cerebrovascular disease and dementia?

The empirical landscape bearing on this question is barren. This Scientific Spotlight first calls attention to the public health context of peripheral vascular determinants of cerebral small vessel disease (cerebrovascular disease) and dementia (for recent reviews, see1,2). It then describes new knowledge about the brain-heart axis, specifically as it informs the plausibility of the hypothesis that large and prolonged stressor-evoked blood pressure reactions could accumulate over time to promote small vessel changes within the brain that eventuate in cerebrovascular disease and dementia. It next summarizes illustrative evidence that stressor-evoked blood pressure reactions are associated with vascular determinants of cerebrovascular disease and dementia. It closes by proposing a Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis. This hypothesis is offered to stimulate new ideas about cardiovascular stress physiology as not only a brain→body pathway to chronic diseases of peripheral organs, but also a body→brain pathway to healthy and unhealthy cerebrovascular and neurocognitive aging.

PUBLIC HEALTH CONTEXT OF VASCULAR DETERMINANTS OF DEMENTIA

Dementia encompasses conditions defined by a loss of function across multiple cognitive domains. Dementia reduces quality of life and presages premature death.3 Dementia incidence is declining in the US,4 but it is still a growing public health problem—with cumulative prevalence and death rates for all dementias in the US nearly doubling since 2000.5 Globally, it is estimated that over 57 million people are living with dementia, and this population is expected to exceed 150 million by 2050.6 Risk for dementia increases after midlife, especially for the 2 most prevalent dementias for which there is considerable overlapping neuropathology: Alzheimer’s disease (AD)7 and vascular dementia (VaD), which is sometimes encompassed by the broader term vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID).2,8,9 Hence, midlife is a period when the burden of dementia risk factors—particularly the burden of vascular determinants of dementia described next2,10–14—influences trajectories of risk for later-life neurocognitive decline.15–18

Over the last several decades, there has been an increasing focus on 2 specific midlife vascular determinants of later-life neuropathology, accelerated cognitive decline, and multiple dementias: elevated blood pressure19–26 and arterial stiffening (arteriosclerosis).1,27–36 Efforts to understand how these and other midlife vascular risks influence later-life dementias are motivated by 2 main reasons. The first is that midlife vascular risk factors may be modified through behavioral change, and the second is that their pathological influences within the brain may be preventable.2,37–40 Elevated blood pressure and arterial stiffening are coupled to each other within the context of vascular aging,41 with some evidence suggesting that arterial stiffening may play a role in age-related rises in blood pressure.42 The neuropathological sequalae of blood pressure and arteriosclerosis manifest as signs of cerebrovascular disease seen on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and autopsy, including lesions to white matter tissue, brain atrophy, and an enlargement of perivascular (Virchow–Robin) spaces.43–45 These cerebrovascular changes themselves appear preclinically by midlife, emerging years before cognitive impairment and dementia.9,46 What is more, these cerebrovascular changes often co-occur across the spectrum of dementias diagnosed in later life.47 For example, those diagnosed with AD often exhibit cerebrovascular (small vessel) pathology,48–52 and the pathological hallmarks of AD and signs of cerebrovascular disease are observed simultaneously in ∼30% to 40% of dementia cases on autopsy.53 At present, cerebrovascular pathology is considered a major and common feature of multi-cause cognitive impairments and dementias that are characterized by comorbid or coexisting brain pathologies.2 For many, midlife vascular risk factors that include elevated blood pressure and arteriosclerosis thus (i) shape the course of preclinical cerebrovascular disease and (ii) appear to be a common, modifiable source of vulnerability to multiple dementias.2,37,54–59

New insights into the brain-heart axis are reshaping how we think about the two-way flow of information between the cardiovascular and central nervous systems,60,61 and these insights have expanded perspectives on hemodynamic and vascular contributions to brain aging and health.1 Moreover, these insights may support the plausibility of conceptualizing mechanistic connections between cardiovascular stress physiology, cerebrovascular disease, and vulnerability to dementia via “bottom-up” or interoceptive pathways of the brain-heart axis.

NEW INSIGHTS INTO THE BRAIN-HEART AXIS: IMPLICATIONS FOR BOTTOM-UP PATHWAYS TO BRAIN AGING AND HEALTH

It is well understood that blood pressure is a regulated parameter of physiology that drives blood flow, oxygen, nutrients, and energy substrates to all organs, including high-flow organs such as the brain. The brain itself receives ∼15% to 20% of cardiac output despite accounting for a negligible amount of body weight.1 The classical understanding of how blood pressure is regulated, as well as how it may regulate brain activity in health and disease, is being updated by novel discoveries about the brain-heart axis.60,61 Most notably, recent findings demonstrate that the brain directly encodes and integrates information about blood pressure and flow via mechanosensory mechanisms of cells that comprise the neurovascular unit.60,61 Key neurovascular unit cells are astrocytes, microglia, pericytes, endothelial cells, and neurons. Primary mechanosensory mechanisms enabling the brain—via elements of the neurovascular unit—to encode and integrate information from tonic and phasic changes in blood pressure and flow include the recently identified Piezo channels (ionic channel complexes distributed along cell membranes). These channels encode mechanical forces (eg, tensile stress due to pressure elevations and shear stress due to flow velocity) that cause deformation, tension, and other cellular changes that result from local pressure and flow dynamics.60,61 Among the cells expressing Piezo channels are astrocytes located throughout brain areas important for blood pressure control (eg, nucleus of the solitary tract, area postrema, olfactory bulb, hypothalamus, medial temporal lobe, cingulate cortex, and insula).62 These cells play a fundamental role in neurovascular coupling via “central baroreception”.60,61 Engagement of Piezo channels thus track the mechanical forces derived from pressure and flow, and they convey this information via brain-wide signaling patterns that influence neural excitability and network activity. As just one example, mitral cells within the olfactory bulb express Piezo channels that track blood pressure oscillations in nearby vessels.63 From the olfactory bulb, mechanosensory information about blood pressure is transmitted to the olfactory cortex, as well as to brain areas that comprise what have been called the “central autonomic network” and “allostatic-interoceptive network.”64 Hubs of these networks include neural ensembles that support a broad range of integrated cognitive, affective, and behavioral functions. They also register bodily states (via interoception) and regulate multiple parameters of peripheral physiology.65,66 Vascular dynamics are thus linked to higher-order neural circuitries via “bottom-up” mechanical blood pressure sensing, which may impact cerebral blood flow and the provision of metabolic substrates for coordinated neuronal activity patterns that are important for processes beyond just “top-down” or brain→body blood pressure regulation via the autonomic nervous system.60,61 Consistent with this interpretation, functional neuroimaging studies indicate that a portion of blood-oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal oscillations are attributable to corresponding oscillations in blood pressure and flow, and these oscillations may support long-range (brain-wide) neural coordination.60 With an eye toward brain aging, dynamic blood pressure and flow oscillations reflecting vasomotion and possibly supporting cerebral tissue oxygenation, glymphatic waste clearance, and periarterial drainage of amyloid-β, have been increasingly linked to neurocognitive functions that decline in later life and are affected by dementia pathology; namely, memory and executive function.67,68

CARDIOVASCULAR STRESS PHYSIOLOGY: A PATHWAY TO BRAIN AGING AND HEALTH?

In what follows, a provisional Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis is proposed to stimulate new lines of thinking about the possible connections between cardiovascular stress physiology, cerebrovascular disease, and dementia. The hypothesis is informed by what is known about “bottom-up” or interoceptive pathways of the brain-heart axis, as well as the interplay between peripheral and central vascular dynamics that change with age and exposure to risk factors for cardiovascular disease. A premise is that metabolically exaggerated and behaviorally mediated variability in blood pressure (eg, during experiences of psychological stress) may accumulate over time to influence or combine synergistically with established vascular determinants of dementia (eg, arteriosclerosis) to adversely affect the neurovascular unit and thus coordinated brain signaling, brain health and aging, and neurocognitive function. Acute psychological stressors—defined in a stress typology framework69 as short-term demands that are categorized as meaningful, threatening, and taxing to one’s coping capacity70—typically raise blood pressure via autonomic adjustments in stroke volume and peripheral vascular resistance in most people.71 There are wide and stable (phenotypic) differences across people, however, in the magnitude, patterning, duration, and direction of stressor-evoked cardiovascular reactions, particularly blood pressure reactions.72 Stressor-evoked blood pressure reactions may arise from predictive neural processes that are instantiated in brain systems for autonomic control, and these processes may contextually calibrate the oxygen, nutrient, energy delivery, and perfusion functions of blood pressure with the anticipated metabolic demands of stressors.73 More broadly, these predictive processes have been described alongside other stressor-evoked physiological (eg, neuroendocrine) adjustments as providing a “metabolic outlay” for a given context.64 Some people have a “trait-like” phenotype to exhibit relatively large or “exaggerated” rises in blood pressure that are disproportionate to the metabolic demands of a given stressor or context.73 From a life course vantage, this blood-pressure phenotype appears to be more common among older adults74,75, cf.,76. Also in a life course context of aging, other parameters of stressor-evoked cardiovascular physiology change in opposite directions. Meta-analytic75,76 and other evidence (eg,77) indicates, for example, that heart rate reactivity to stressors declines with age due to constraints on the dynamic range of heart rate and autonomic nervous system aging among other factors. Thus, dimensions of cardiovascular stress physiology are not interchangeable over the lifespan and what is referred to as stressor-evoked reactivity should not be treated as a unitary construct. Different dimensions of stressor-evoked cardiovascular reactivity may also have different mechanistic pathways to disease outcomes, especially in relation to neurocognitive endpoints and neurovascular outcomes at the level of the brain. Here, the focus is on blood pressure.

Stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity has been associated with vascular determinants of dementia, including hypertension78 and adverse changes to the functional and structural characteristics of the heart and blood vessels, such as endothelial dysfunction, ventricular hypertrophy, carotid artery thickening, and arterial stiffness (arteriosclerosis).71,78–82 It is unresolved as to whether a phenotype to exhibit large or prolonged blood pressure reactions to acute stressors is a marker or contributing cause of cardiovascular risk, and the few findings that exist are mixed with respect to associations with late-stage clinical outcomes and mortality.83,84 With only a few exceptions, most work on stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity has focused on peripheral preclinical and clinical outcomes of the heart and vasculature. By contrast, there has been scant work on blood pressure reactivity’s relationship to central or cerebrovascular antecedents of dementia. As noted, such a relationship is conceivable because the outcomes associated with stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity (eg, hypertension, arterial thickening, and arterial stiffening) are themselves peripheral vascular determinants of cerebrovascular disease and dementia.85,86 Moreover, the 3 lines of evidence reviewed next suggest that stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity appears to associate with (i) white matter pathology, (ii) stroke, and (iii) tissue reductions in the medial temporal lobe.

The first line of evidence indicates greater stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity associates with canonical signs of preclinical cerebrovascular disease; namely, white matter hyperintensities (WMH). As signs of cerebrovascular disease, WMH appear on MRI as signal changes that do not reflect cavitation (presence of cerebrovascular fluid; CSF).49 WMH increasingly appear in middle and later adulthood,87 especially in association with vascular risk factors, such as hypertension and arterial stiffness.88 A greater midlife WMH burden associates with cognitive decline and risk for stroke and dementia.89 Waldstein et al90 showed that in 67 adults (55 to 81 years, 75% men), those who exhibited greater blood pressure reactivity to a battery of 3 acute psychological stressors had more WMH.In that study, blood pressure reactivity accounted for 6% to 14% of the variance in WMH. Study limits, however, included testing a small and mostly later-life sample in a cross-sectional design that constrained generalizability and inferences about midlife and future neurovascular risk. Importantly, though, results persisted after accounting for known cardiovascular risks, including tonic blood pressure. The latter could suggest the unique significance of stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity in relation to cerebrovascular disease.

A second line of evidence derives from work showing that in ∼2300 midlife men (42 to 60 years at baseline), greater blood pressure reactivity to the psychological anticipation of exercise predicted a 72% increased risk of stroke over 11 years, after accounting for known cardiovascular risks, medication use, and aerobic fitness. Results also suggested a dose-response gradient: each 10 mm Hg rise in SBP to anticipating exercise predicted a 15% increase in risk of ischemic stroke.91 These results align with the possibility that greater stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity in midlife may confer risk for later cerebrovascular pathology.

A third line of correlational evidence suggests an association between stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity and reduced volume in the medial temporal lobe. In 2 small cross-sectional studies of predominantly young adults (Ns=32-40; mean ages <25 years; <12% men across both studies), larger blood pressure reactions to cognitive performance stressors were associated with a reduced volume of the medial temporal lobe, encompassing the hippocampus.92,93 Volumetric reductions in the medial temporal lobe, particularly in the hippocampus, associate in mid- to later-life with known vascular risk factors predicted by blood pressure reactivity, such as hypertension (eg,94,95). Owing to the small, cross-sectional, and younger samples, temporal ordering and generalizability here are uncertain. And especially because of the inclusion of young adults and a lack of testing mediation pathways, there are knowledge gaps concerning midlife and intermediate factors possibly linking stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity to later-life medial temporal lobe volume. For these reasons, findings from existing reactivity studies of brain tissue changes need to be interpreted with caution.

The few lines of evidence on the cerebrovascular correlates of stressor-evoked blood pressure could be provisionally interpreted by a Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis proposed here and schematized in Figure 1. By this heuristic hypothesis, an individual’s tendency to react to acute psychological stressors with large and sustained rises in blood pressure may accumulate over time to confer risk for preclinical cerebrovascular disease, possibly mediated or moderated (eg, amplified) by systemic vascular changes that increase arterial stiffness and alter body→brain interoceptive information flow via pressure waves propogated along the vasculature. In this way, repeated, large magnitude, and prolonged stressor-evoked blood pressure rises and associated increases in arterial stiffness may contribute to or exacerbate the pathological consequences of pulsatile blood flow in the brain (ie, cerebral pulsatility). The consequences of the latter include a greater delivery of pulsatile mechanical energy to small and vulnerable vessels in the brain (ie, the cerebral microvasculature), where pathological damage may accrue in neurovascular units to impair neurocognitive and physiological control functions that are integrated with interoception in the context of aging.64,96

BODY→BRAIN MECHANISMS OF NEUROVASCULAR DAMAGE: CEREBRAL PULSATILITY AND SMALL VESSEL VULNERABILITY

To expand, blood flow and pressure exhibit dynamic oscillations in the cerebral blood vessels. These oscillations encompass different functional processes and they are due to different underlying mechanisms that operate over different frequencies. Different oscillations in cerebral pressure and flow also have different influences on neurovascular health, pathology, and neurocognitive aging.97 A major oscillation is cardiac in origin, which is often referred to as cerebral pulsatility. The latter is also called high-frequency pulsatility, which refers to dynamic variations in cerebral blood flow and pressure due to the changes in systemic blood pressure arising from the ejection of blood out of the left ventricle with each heartbeat.98 Because the skull cannot expand and contract in response to each pressure wave from the heart (ie, because the brain is encased within a fixed bone compartment), the energy from pulsatile flow and pressure propagates into cerebral vessels, fluids, cells, and tissues.97 Multiple mechanisms serve to dampen against excess cerebral pulsatility and therefore protect cerebral small vessels from pulsatile pressure and flow.98,99 However, pulsatility may be undampened or otherwise amplified for several reasons in the context of aging, including impaired autoregulation (ie, the central stabilization of pressure and flow despite variation in peripheral pressure) and a loss of vascular compliance due to arterial stiffness.99 High-amplitude cardiac pulsatility beyond normative levels causes mechanical stress to the cerebral microvasculature, resulting in neurovascular damage.86 Collectively, such neurovascular effects may increase over time and contribute to neuropathology and dementia risk with aging.86,97,100,101 In addition to cerebral pulsatility operating at the speed of heart rate, there are fundamentally different and lower-frequency oscillations in pressure and flow that are caused by respiration (operating at the breathing frequency) and by rhythmic changes in vascular smooth muscle tone and diameter (vasomotion), the latter of which has been increasingly implicated in glymphatic waste clearance (see below). Several non-invasive methods to measure and quantify cerebral pulsatility across different vascular territories in the time and frequency domains are being increasingly used in clinical applications.97 In the current context and unless noted, cerebral pulsatility or simply pulsatility will be synonymous with high-frequency cardiac pulsatility. Here, pulsatility is conceptualized as a possible mediator or moderator of the influences of cardiovascular stress physiology and vascular aging on dementia vulnerability.

In the absence of disease, otherwise healthy compliant conduit arteries normally serve to decrease pulsatility distal to the heart.99 This is due to the buffering (Windkessel) effects of elastic arteries that expand and recoil in response to pressure changes with each heartbeat. Decreased pulsatility due to greater arterial compliance effectively dampens the transmission of mechanical stress from high-pressure pulsatile blood flow to small blood vessels within the brain and other organs (eg, heart),96,102,103 thus buffering against neurovascular damage and associated signs of preclinical cerebrovascular disease. Another buffering mechanism that curtails the delivery of pulsatile energy into the microcirculation entails the partial reflection of pressure waves in the vasculature as these waves travel from low-to-high resistance vessels.98 Arterial stiffness, which increases with age, elevated blood pressure, and exposure to cardiovascular risk factors, can impair pulsatile buffering mechanisms and increase the exposure of small blood vessels to large and damaging pressure and flow oscillations.99 Arterial stiffness is also now considered as a biomarker of vascular age and the health of the brain-heart axis itself.1

Greater pulsatility due to vascular aging and dysfunction along the brain-heart axis is related to cerebrovascular disease, impaired cognitive performance, AD, and VaD.86,88,104–109 Mechanistically, systemic arterial stiffness amplifies pulsatility throughout the peripheral and cerebral vasculature.98 Thus, stiffer arteries that result from aging, hypertension, or other factors (eg, the possible cumulative effects of stressor-evoked blood pressure changes) deliver more mechanical energy to tissues and cells, especially to cells of the neurovascular unit. Loss of arterial compliance (ie, increased arterial stiffness) may further exert adverse effects on baroreception by the brain mediated by Piezo channels, which may be critical not only for blood pressure control, but also neurocognitive function and healthy brain aging.60,61 Enhanced or undampened pulsatility may also disrupt the brain’s glymphatic waste clearance system that is increasingly implicated in dementia.67 In the context of arterial stiffening and vascular dysfunctions that normally buffer against pulsatility, the recurrent propagation of pulsatile energy from repeated and exaggerated stressor-evoked rises in blood pressure into the cerebral vessels among otherwise reactive individuals may be a substrate for damage to brain tissue (Figure 1).

Notwithstanding the plausible substrates for neuropathology, studies on the acute effects of blood pressure rises on simultaneous changes in cerebral pulsatility are limited. And those that exist generally lack an inclusion of the age groups, stressor paradigms, and biological measures needed to fully evaluate predictions about cardiovascular stress physiology and pathways to brain aging and dementia vulnerability. One study of 61 adults (aged 18 to 70 years; 49% men), for example, examined blood pressure and pulsatility changes in response to resistance exercise, which can increase systolic blood pressure by over 10 mm Hg.110 Here, larger rises in blood pressure were associated with a corresponding increase in cerebral blood velocity pulsatility, which appeared to be partly explained by concurrent exercise-evoked and transient rises in central arterial stiffness, as measured by pulse-wave velocity. Notably, older adults exhibited larger increases in central arterial stiffness, which may have amplified the transmission of pulsatile energy into cerebral blood vessels. Outside of such studies focusing on nonpsychological testing paradigms and as noted above, there has been little work on the simultaneous relationships between psychological stressor-evoked blood pressure changes of large magnitude, tonic and phasic measures of arterial stiffness, and measures of cerebral pulsatility, especially in the context of aging, neuropathology, and neurocognitive function. One prior study of 25 healthy adults (mean age 39; 48% men) found that a performance-titrated Stroop stressor that increased peripheral pressure pulsatility and mean arterial pressure did not significantly change average central pulsatility in the middle cerebral artery.111 The latter findings among healthy younger adults are consistent with the summary above that in the absence of disease and premature biological aging, compliant conduit arteries normally serve to dampen pulsatility distal to the heart.111 However, in that study, it is worth noting that even in this sample, greater task-evoked increases in pulsatility in the middle cerebral artery were associated across individuals with worse performance on measures of executive function. As acknowledged by the authors, this pilot study did not include older adults and it lacked measures of vascular aging, such as large artery stiffness measured by pulse-wave velocity. These design features precluded comprehensive testing of whether individual differences in stressor-evoked changes in peripheral pressure and cardiovascular dynamics influenced by arterial stiffness (arteriosclerosis) relate to hemodynamic pulsatility transmission into the brain. More broadly, the small sample sizes in existing studies such as those described above constrain mediation and moderation tests of the vascular factors possibly linking or influencing stressor-evoked cardiovascular reactivity effects on cerebral pulsatility and emergent neurocognitive outcomes in the context of aging that are depicted in Figure 1.

TOWARDS A NEW WAVE OF CARDIOVASCULAR STRESS SCIENCE ON INTEROCEPTIVE PATHWAYS TO BRAIN AGING AND HEALTH

Looking ahead, there are many open questions about cardiovascular stress physiology and risk for cerebrovascular disease and dementias. The provisional ideas embodied within the Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis are meant to be empirically tested, challenged, and updated. They are also meant to help evolve conventional ideas about cardiovascular stress physiology so that they incorporate new information about the interoceptive or body→brain pathways of psychological stress to healthy and unhealthy neurocognitive aging. To maintain focus and because of what has been recently discovered about central baroreception and the mechanical effects of arterial stiffness and pulsatility on the aging neurovascular unit, hypotheses and illustrations were constrained to blood pressure. But cardiovascular reactions to stressors encompass more than blood pressure. Cardiovascular stress physiology is multidimensional, and its features are modified by context and aging. In these regards, there is a need to focus on multivariate patterns of stress physiology that incorporate the kinetics of reactivity, recovery, and even habituation across organ systems and across multiple parameters of physiology. As outlined below, there are also domains of findings and brain-behavior associations that await integration into a broader conceptual framework, especially concerning (i) what has been termed “blunted” or small-magnitude stressor-evoked cardiovascular reactivity, (ii) discrepant findings on cardiovascular stress physiology and neuropsychological performance, (iii) other forms of blood pressure dynamics not considered in detail above, and (iv) biopsychosocial moderators amenable to intervention.

First, there is now unambiguous evidence that some forms of blunted stressor-evoked cardiovascular reactivity are not being benign (eg,84), indicating a need to examine nonlinear or biphasic associations and multiple brain-based pathways linking cardiovascular stress physiology to disease processes.112 For example, blunted stressor-evoked cardiovascular reactivity may not be neuroprotective but instead correspond to (or drive) brain hypoperfusion, insufficient metabolic support to the neurovascular unit during cognitive demand, and thus greater neural vulnerability—especially among those already at risk for cerebrovascular disease and dementia.86 Consistent with the latter possibility, one study of older adults showed that blunted heart rate and blood pressure reactions to an isometric handgrip exercise exhibited a greater volume of white matter hyperintensities.113 Rather than cascading neurovascular damage through mechanical stress and amplified pulsatile energy due to early vascular aging and exaggerated blood pressure reactivity, an extension or future biphasic modification of the Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis would be that hypoperfusion driven by inadequate (blunted) hemodynamic responding and cumulative cardiovascular risk burden may be another mechanistic pathway to neurocognitive risk via stressor-evoked reactivity.114 The latter interpretation may be compatible with some the following selected brain-behavior associations.

Second, brain-behavior associations in the small cardiovascular stress reactivity literature appear mixed, and these associations need to be clarified within a broader interpretive framework. Existing evidence, for example, appears varied on stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity and neuropsychological performance, with some findings of negative correlations between stressor-evoked blood pressure reactivity and performance in later-life samples (eg,115,116) but positive correlations in young adults (eg,117). These mixed findings may be attributable to the timing of stress and cognitive assessments relative to each other, age of the study samples, specific stressor and neuropsychological tasks used, and use of cross-sectional vs. longitudinal designs (for review, see114). At present, more work is needed to evaluate alternate neurovascular risk pathways for neurocognitive vulnerability beyond those illustrated in Figure 1. Again, such pathways could plausibly encompass blunted reactivity and inadequate or otherwise altered hemodynamic support for cerebral perfusion.

Third, there are major empirical gaps regarding midlife cardiovascular stress physiology, low-frequency oscillatory blood pressure dynamics due to vasomotion, and longitudinally determined neurocognitive aging outcomes. More precisely, there is a need to conceptually integrate what is known about cardiovascular stress physiology with what is becoming increasingly known about low-frequency oscillations in blood pressure and flow due to vasomotion (from ∼0.04 to 0.15 Hz) and neurocognitive health and aging.67,68 It may be that slow rhythmic blood pressure kinetics that function over different timescales (eg, over the course of seconds to minutes to hours) have divergent influences on the neurovascular unit. For example, enhanced cerebral pulsatility operating at a fast time scale and amplified by arterial stiffness may accumulate over time to eventuate in mechanical neurovascular burden (Figure 1), whereas normative low-frequency blood pressure oscillations (operating at a slower frequency and contributing to bulk vascular flow through vasomotion over longer timescales) are protective (eg, contributing to oxygenation and waste removal). Arteriosclerosis with aging or exposure to cumulative cardiovascular stress physiology would be expected to amplify small vessel damage due to cardiac pulsatility and attenuate low-frequency vasomotion protection. How the latter blood pressure and flow dynamics and different kinds of oscillatory changes in pressure and flow might interact (eg, with synergistic or diathesis-stress effects) or operate in parallel (eg, with additive and independent effects) in the context of acute or chronic psychological stress remains unknown at this time.

Lastly, there are still gaps concerning how known biological, psychological, social, and behavioral risks (eg, age, menopause, chronic stress, social disadvantage, sleep, physical inactivity, etc.) contribute to (or modify) associations of cardiovascular stress physiology and cerebrovascular disease. For example, a greater biopsychosocial cardiovascular risk burden might exacerbate (eg, amplify) the influence of cardiovascular stress physiology on cerebrovascular outcomes via the interoceptive vascular-mechanical pathways embodied within the Neurovascular Burden of Stress hypothesis. Equally, there are knowledge gaps concerning resilience factors that could plausibly alter interoceptive cardiovascular pathways for neuroprotection in aging.

The science of cardiovascular stress physiology and possible links to neurovascular pathology via dysfunction along the brain-heart axis is just beginning. Because of the public health burden of cerebrovascular disease and dementias, there is a need to make the empirical landscape bearing on these links less barren.
