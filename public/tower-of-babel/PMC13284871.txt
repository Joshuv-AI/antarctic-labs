Development and Application of a Desirability of Outcome Ranking (DOOR) Endpoint for Acute Bacterial Skin and Skin Structure Infections
Authors: Jessica Howard-Anderson, Toshimitsu Hamasaki, Lizhao Ge, Deborah Collyar, Sara E Cosgrove, Sarah B Doernberg, Holly Geres, Ramya Gopinath, Thomas L Holland, Heather A King, Sumathi Nambiar, Daniel Rubin, Henry F Chambers, Vance G Fowler, Scott R Evans, Helen W Boucher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDesirability of outcome ranking (DOOR), a paradigm for the design, analysis, and interpretation of clinical trials based on patient-centered benefit-risk evaluation, could help inform drug development for acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSIs).MethodA multidisciplinary committee created an infectious diseases DOOR endpoint, incorporating clinical response, infectious complications, serious adverse events, and mortality, and adapted this for ABSSSI. We retrospectively applied DOOR to 2 previous ABSSSI randomized controlled registrational trials (OASIS-1 and OASIS-2), both comparing omadacycline with linezolid. We evaluated the DOOR distribution and estimated the probability of having a more desirable outcome with omadacycline compared with linezolid. For OASIS-2, we used the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey Version 2 (SF-36v2) scores as a “tiebreaker” for participants who would otherwise have the same DOOR outcome. We performed DOOR component, partial credit, and subgroup analyses.ResultsThe ABSSSI DOOR demonstrated similar overall clinical outcomes between treatment arms for OASIS-1 and OASIS-2. The probability of a more desirable outcome for a participant treated with omadacycline, compared with linezolid, was 50.6% (95% confidence interval [CI], 47.4%–53.7%) and 52.1% (95% CI, 49.2%–55.0%) in OASIS-1 and OASIS-2, respectively. In OASIS-2, applying a health-related quality-of-life score as a tiebreaker did not significantly change the DOOR probability. Partial credit and subgroup analyses demonstrated similar overall results.ConclusionsDOOR may provide additional perspective on benefit versus risk in individual patients in ABSSSI trials. Future trials using DOOR prospectively will continue inform how to incorporate patient-centered outcomes in ABSSSI trials.

The optimal endpoint for evaluating new anti-infective agents in acute bacterial skin and skin structure infection (ABSSSI) trials is unclear. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends that registrational clinical trials use an early clinical response endpoint (eg, ≥ 20% reduction in lesion size at 48–72 hours) as the primary efficacy endpoint, and secondary endpoints should consider resolution of ABSSSI symptoms at 7–14 days after therapy [1]. These recommendations were supported by an external, multidisciplinary team from the Biomarkers Consortium of the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH). However, the inclusion of this early clinical response endpoint in 2013 has led to challenges for the pharmaceutical industry as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) recommends primarily using a later assessment of the clinical outcome at a “test of cure” visit [2–4].

Neither the FDA- nor EMA-recommended primary endpoint specifically addresses how patients feel or function, priorities that the FDA has encouraged for new drug development [3]. For infections similar to ABSSSI, where differences in mortality between treatment strategies are unlikely [5], other important outcomes that may differentiate between antibiotic agents should be evaluated, such as patient experience. A patient-reported outcome (PRO) was recently developed by FNIH for ABSSSI [6] and is currently undergoing FDA evaluation [7]. However, we are not aware of any clinical trials using this PRO; thus, there is an unmet need for an endpoint that comprehensively evaluates patient outcomes.

Developed to advance clinical trial science, desirability of outcome ranking (DOOR) is a paradigm for the design, monitoring, analysis, interpretation, and reporting of research studies based on patient-centered benefit-risk evaluation. DOOR provides a global assessment of patient outcome and incorporating DOOR into ABSSSI trials could allow for a more complete understanding of the patient experience. Additionally, DOOR enables unique component and partial credit evaluations, which can inform treatment selections based on patient preferences [8–10]. In “Benefit-risk balance for medicinal products,” the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences recommends: (1) prespecifying DOOR-like endpoints that represent a global patient outcome for trial protocols in parallel with efficacy and safety endpoints, (2) conducting DOOR analyses on these endpoints, (3) and reporting on these analyses when publishing results [11].

The Antibacterial Resistance Leadership Group (ARLG) has developed an infectious diseases DOOR and explored its use and feasibility in complicated urinary tract infection (cUTI), hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia (HABP/VABP), and complicated intra-abdominal infection (cIAI) [12–14]. Employing a multidisciplinary task force, we adapted the infectious diseases DOOR endpoint for ABSSSI and retrospectively applied this to 2 ABSSSI randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing omadacycline with linezolid [15, 16]. Distinct from prior DOOR analyses, we also modeled how health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) data could be incorporated into DOOR.

METHODS

DOOR Task Force and DOOR Analysis Strategy

ARLG established a multidisciplinary DOOR Task Force in the spring of 2020, which included physicians, statisticians, and scientists from academia, FDA, National Institutes of Health (NIH), and the pharmaceutical industry, with expertise in infectious diseases, trial design, statistical analysis, drug regulation, HRQoL, and patient experience. We also had a patient advocate as an integral task force member [12, 13]. Through consensus-building and iterative feedback, we created a general infectious diseases DOOR endpoint (Figure 1A) that could be customized for common infectious disease syndromes, including cUTI, HABP/VABP, cIAI, and now ABSSSI (Figure 1B).

Selection and Design of Primary ABSSSI Trials

The DOOR Task Force engaged with many pharmaceutical companies about using DOOR in infectious disease trials. Paratek Pharmaceuticals agreed to share deidentified data from the completed OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 trials [15, 16]. Both were phase 3, multicenter, double-blind, double-dummy, randomized noninferiority trials comparing omadacycline with linezolid. In OASIS-1, participants were initiated on intravenous omadacycline or linezolid with the option to transition to the oral formulations of these antibiotics after 3 days. In OASIS-2, oral omadacycline was compared with oral linezolid. In both studies, the duration of therapy was 7–14 days and the primary efficacy endpoint was early clinical response, defined as survival with a reduction of the lesion size by at least 20% at 48–72 hours after treatment initiation. Both studies also included an investigator assessment of clinical response at a post-treatment evaluation (7–14 days after end of treatment). In OASIS-2, investigators collected HRQoL data using a validated measure, the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey Version 2 (SF-36v2, Optum Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA), which participants completed at baseline and at a post-treatment evaluation (Table 1) [17, 18].

Application of ABSSSI DOOR and Variable Abstraction

Absence of Clinical Response

To define this event, we used the investigator assessment of clinical response at the post-treatment evaluation. In the primary DOOR analysis, any participant who did not meet the study definition of clinical success (ie, clinical failure or indeterminate) was classified as having an absence of clinical response. Clinical response was generally defined as improvement or resolution of infection such that further antibiotics were not needed. We did not include the early clinical response endpoint in the DOOR analysis as the task force did not believe this clearly reflected how patients feel or function.

Infectious Complications

Two board-certified infectious disease clinicians (J.H.A. and H.W.B.) reviewed the lowest-level terms and the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities dictionary derived terms for all adverse events (AEs) to determine if any met the criteria for the prespecified ABSSSI DOOR infectious complications (Figure 1B). AEs were reviewed according to a DOOR Task Force standard operating procedure. During initial review, and after discussion and agreement of the full DOOR Task Force, the reviewers added “progression of or new ABSSSI” as an infectious complication. This was a common AE, and the Task Force believed that including this was consistent with the original intent of the infectious complication variable, defined as “newly identified complications or progression of the original infection that was not present at enrollment, including the development of Clostridioides difficile.” All infectious complications were required to be identified after enrollment and to occur during the AE monitoring period (Table 1). Reviewers were blinded to the treatment and had to agree on the determination of infectious complications. Any events that were unable to be resolved were reviewed by the full DOOR Task Force.

Nonfatal Serious Adverse Events (SAEs)

Nonfatal SAEs were adapted from the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) E6 Good Clinical Practice guidelines (21 CFR 312.32) and included any event that: (1) was life-threatening, (2) required inpatient hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, (3) resulted in persistent or significant disability/incapacity, or (4) was a congenital anomaly/birth defect [19]. Death was excluded from the SAE definition, as all patients who died were automatically given the least desirable DOOR rank (rank 5). The DOOR analysis included all participants who had an SAE during the follow-up period used for AE monitoring in the original study (Table 1).

Health-related Quality-of-Life

For OASIS-2, we used the change (difference in score from baseline to post-treatment evaluation) in the norm-based SF-36v2 Mental Component Summary and Physical Component Summary scores as a DOOR “tiebreaker.” To calculate the norm-based SF-36v2 component scores, we used data from the general United States population from 2005 to 2006 as the reference population [20, 21].

Ranking

As previously described, a mutually exclusive DOOR rank is determined by the survival status and how many events (absence of clinical response, infectious complications, and nonfatal SAEs) occurred for each participant [13]. Rank 1 is the most desirable and includes participants who were alive and did not experience any of the undesirable events. Rank 5 is the least desirable and includes participants who died. Ranks 2 through 4 include participants who were alive with 1, 2, or 3 events, respectively (Figure 1). For the OASIS-2 tiebreaker analysis, if 2 participants had the same DOOR (eg, alive with 2 events), then the participant who had the largest increase in their HRQoL score was given the more desirable DOOR rank. This was performed separately for the SF-36v2 Physical and Mental Component Summary scores.

Statistical Analysis

Our analysis used the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) populations from the primary studies, defined as all randomized participants without a sole gram-negative pathogen at baseline [15, 16]. The primary analysis compared DOOR distributions between treatment arms and estimated the DOOR probability (ie, probability of having a more desirable outcome in one treatment arm compared with the other) using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney statistic with a 2-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) [22, 23]. A DOOR probability of 50% indicates no statistical difference in the DOOR outcome between groups. Additionally, we estimated DOOR probabilities specific to each DOOR component event (eg, clinical failure, infectious complications, nonfatal SAEs, and death). For OASIS-2, we estimated an additional DOOR probability, where the difference in SF-36v2 Physical and Mental Component Summary scores was used as a tiebreaker for participants who otherwise had the same rank [24].

For subgroup analyses, we estimated DOOR probabilities for participants with specific clinical or infection-related characteristics. The subgroups were chosen a priori by the DOOR Task Force, based on clinical relevance and availability of the variables. Similar to prior work, we performed a sensitivity analysis, where we changed the classification of participants with indeterminate or missing outcomes in 3 ways: (1) participants with indeterminate or missing outcomes were ranked above those with clinical failure if they otherwise had the same rank (“tiebreaker” analysis); (2) participants with indeterminate or missing outcomes were counted as “clinical cure”; and (3) participants with indeterminate or missing outcomes were excluded [12, 13].

Partial Credit Analysis

Using partial credit scoring can allow for personalized grading of DOOR. As previously described, DOOR categories can be given scores similar to an academic test. Rank 1 (most desirable) is scored at 100, and Rank 5 (least desirable) is scored at 0. Ranks 2–4 can be given a score from 0 to 100, as long as the original rank order is maintained. Ranks 2–4 can be adjusted based on individual preferences or values. Treatment arms are compared by estimating the difference between the mean partial credit scores in each group. A difference of 0 indicates no significant difference between groups [12, 13].

For OASIS-1, we used the same 3 hypothetical scoring keys (Scenarios A, B, and C) from our previous cUTI and HABP/VABP DOOR analyses [12, 13]. These scoring keys were chosen to demonstrate feasibility of a partial credit analysis but do not necessarily represent realistic patient values; both Scenario A and B represent the extremes patient preferences. For OASIS-2, we modified Scenario A since no participants died in the study and thus all participants would have had the same score. Given this, we gave the next least desirable outcome (alive with 3 events) a score of 0 as well.

Analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. All analyses were conducted using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) or R statistical software R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient Consent Statement

The analysis described in this manuscript was performed on deidentified data that does not contain human subjects data that would allow a participant to be identified and thus does not necessitate obtaining consent.

RESULTS

OASIS-1 had 627 participants included in the mITT population, and OASIS-2 had 720. Both studies included participants with wound infections, cellulitis/erysipelas, or major abscesses. OASIS-2 had a larger proportion of participants with wound infections than OASIS-1 (33% vs 59%) (Table 1). 80% of the participants in each study had the most desirable DOOR outcome (alive with no prespecified undesirable events) and very few participants died (Figures 2 and 3).

For both OASIS-1 and OASIS-2, the DOOR distribution between treatment arms was similar. In OASIS-1, the probability that a participant treated with omadacycline would have a more desirable outcome than a participant treated with linezolid was 50.6% (95% CI, 47.4%−53.7%) (Figure 2). In OASIS-2, the probability that a participant treated with omadacycline would have a more desirable outcome than a participant treated with linezolid was 52.1% (95% CI, 49.2%–55.0%) (Figure 3).

Overall, the DOOR components (clinical failure, infectious complications, nonfatal SAEs, and death) were rare. In both trials, the only identified infectious complication was progression of or new ABSSSI (Supplementary Table 1). Although no significant differences were observed between treatment arms for any of the DOOR component analyses, the component analyses provide a more in-depth understanding of the potential tradeoffs between efficacy and safety when comparing omadacycline with linezolid. For example, in both trials, the clinical efficacy outcome (absence of clinical response) favored omadacycline, although this was not statistically significant (Figures 2 and 3).

Incorporating the SF-36v2 Physical or Mental Component Summary score as a tiebreaker slightly increased the DOOR probability in favor of omadacycline, but this was not statistically significant (Figure 4). Additionally, a subgroup analysis did not demonstrate significant differences between omadacycline and linezolid in any of the groups studied (presence of diabetes, type of infection at enrollment, age, sex, and Staphylococcus aureus identified as a causative pathogen) in OASIS-1. In OASIS-2, participants with diabetes had a more desirable outcome in the omadacycline arm compared with the linezolid arm (DOOR probability 58.1%, 95% CI 50.0%–65.7%) (Figure 5).

When using hypothetical scenarios or “scoring keys” for a partial credit analysis, we did not observe any treatment differences between groups (Supplementary Figure 1). The results were also similar when we changed how participants with missing or indeterminate outcomes were counted in the DOOR analysis (Supplementary Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated how an infectious diseases DOOR framework can be explored in ABSSSI trials, similar to our prior work in cUTI, HABP/VABP, and cIAI [12–14]. DOOR allows clinicians and patients to have a more comprehensive understanding of the benefits and risks of choosing one treatment over another and provides a framework for sharing more information on the benefit-risk profile than is typically presented in clinical trials. This information can be valuable even if significant differences between treatment arms are not observed, providing assurance on the benefit-risk tradeoffs when considering individual patient-level data.

For both OASIS-1 and OASIS-2, the DOOR analyses demonstrated results concordant to the primary published studies [15, 16]. However, the primary studies were noninferiority studies and thus only able to conclude that omadacycline was noninferior to linezolid. The DOOR outcome and its analysis characterize the overall patient experience by integrating both benefits and harms of treatment, allowing clinicians and patients to understand how outcomes with omadacycline and linezolid compare across the full spectrum of clinical effects.

Because OASIS-2 included the SF-36v2, we were able to explore incorporating HRQoL into DOOR as a tiebreaker. This approach was recently used in an RCT comparing dalbavancin to standard therapy for complicated S. aureus bacteremia [25]. We did not observe a meaningful overall change in the DOOR probability when SF-36v2 Physical and Mental Component Summary scores were included. This may be due to limitations in the captured aspects of the patient experience with these summary scores or could represent a true equivalence between treatments. Although SF-36v2 is a validated HRQoL measure, it is not specifically designed for infectious diseases, including ABSSSI. Additionally, many of the questions in the SF-36v2 have a recall period of 4 weeks, which could have been confusing for participants at the post-treatment evaluation, occurring 14–28 days after the end of therapy, and may not align with the timing of their symptoms [20]. A recent systematic review of HRQoL measures used in ABSSSI studies demonstrated that further work is needed to identify an ideal tool that captures HRQoL in patients with ABSSSI [26].

Including DOOR in subgroup analyses can provide information on which patient populations may benefit from a new antibiotic. In the OASIS-2 subgroup analysis, we found that participants with diabetes had a more desirable outcome when treated with omadacycline than linezolid. However, because there were only 45 participants with diabetes, this finding should be cautiously interpreted and viewed as hypothesis-generating. An in-depth analysis of pooled data from OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 found similar clinical outcomes between linezolid and omadacycline, regardless of diabetes [27].

This study's strengths include using 2 large multicenter, double-blind RCTs (both with >600 participants). Additionally, we were able to apply a DOOR framework that was created by a multidisciplinary committee, including a patient advisor and experts in clinical trial and drug regulation. This work builds on our prior experience using DOOR in infectious diseases and now demonstrates that HRQoL data can also be integrated into DOOR.

However, this study has limitations. First, we only had data available from 2 registrational ABSSSI trials, using the same antibiotics and funded by the same pharmaceutical company. Because SAEs and death are rare in ABSSSI trials, we had few participants in Ranks 3, 4 and 5, which can make it difficult to differentiate between the 2 treatment arms using DOOR. Combining these least desirable ranks could allow for simplicity and reduced variation without sacrificing discrimination and could be considered in the future. The review of infectious complications was conducted post hoc and relied on coded data that may not capture the full details of the AE. However, the only infectious complications noted were progression of or new ABSSSI, and no participants experienced more severe complications or progression of their infection, such as bacteremia, septic shock, or osteomyelitis. The DOOR analysis may be more informative to use in investigator-initiated or pragmatic trials that often enroll a more acutely ill patient population. A separate ambulatory DOOR may also be considered for participants in ABSSSI trials who are not admitted to a hospital and unlikely to have any infectious complications. Lastly, as previously mentioned, there are limitations to the HRQoL tool used. Future work should study if prospectively collecting additional data on adverse events, duration of symptoms, and HRQoL would allow for improved discrimination using DOOR in ABSSSI trials.

In summary, we demonstrated that DOOR is feasible to use and can be explored in future ABSSSI trials. Using DOOR can provide clinicians and patients with a better understanding of the benefits and risks of new anti-infectives being studied to treat skin infections. Future ABSSSI trials should prospectively incorporate DOOR and more specific HRQoL instruments to further our understanding of how to use these tools and prioritize patient-centered clinical trials.
