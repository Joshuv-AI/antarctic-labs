Mixture-aware closure of the N-phase Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard mixture model
Marco F.P. ten Eikelder, Aaron Brunk

Abstract
Diffuse-interface (phase-field) models are widely used to describe multiphase mixtures and their interfacial dynamics. In multiphase settings, however, the constitutive closure should remain meaningful across different representations of the same mixture. Existing N-phase phase-field constructions commonly enforce reduction only when a phase is absent (restriction to a face of the Gibbs simplex), but do not address the natural requirement that physically identical phases can be merged without changing the governing equations. This requires characterizing thermodynamically admissible, mixture-aware constitutive closures that are consistent with merging identical phases at the PDE level. Here, we show that, under a small set of structural axioms, PDE-level reduction consistency uniquely fixes the admissible free-energy structure to an ideal-mixing contribution to an ideal-mixing contribution, a symmetric mean-field interaction term, and a constant-coefficient quadratic gradient penalty. The same requirement constrains the Onsager mobility matrix to a pairwise-exchange form with bilinear degeneracy in the volume fractions, yielding a thermodynamic closure that includes Maxwell–Stefan-type mobilities as a special case. These results provide a consistent closure for N-phase Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard mixture models and, in the bulk-only setting, for multiphase Maxwell–Stefan diffusion systems. Numerical experiments confirm the predicted mixture-aware reduction properties and illustrate the capabilities of the N-phase Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard framework in representative multiphase-flow computations.

The present work is concerned with a different, complementary notion of reduction that arises naturally in mixture models:
merging physically identical phases

In applications, several phases may represent the same physical phase, sharing identical constitutive parameters, or a single phase may be split into sublabels for modeling or numerical convenience. Such a requirement would, in general, conflate label bookkeeping with physical mixing and is conceptually delicate in view of the Gibbs paradox: the entropy of mixing depends on distinguishability and changes under purely formal relabelings. Instead, we impose reduction at the level that is physically and computationally decisive for NSCH models, namely the

2.1.  Governing equations

We consider the Navier-Stokes Cahn-Hilliard system with non-matching densities given by
(
38
)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}+\nabla\lambda-{\rm div}\bm{\tau}-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.1b)

in a spatial domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(of dimension
d=2,3
), boundary
\partial\Omega
and unit outward normal
\mathbf{n}
. The state variables are the phase velocity
\mathbf{v}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, volume fraction variables
\phi_{\alpha}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\alpha=1,...,N
and Lagrange multiplier pressure
\lambda:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
subject to the initial conditions
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{v}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})
. The constant specific phase densities are
\rho_{\alpha}
, the partial mass densities are
\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}=\rho_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}
, and the mixture density is given by
\rho=\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}
. The force vector is represented by
\mathbf{g}=-g\mathbf{j}
, with
g
the gravitational constant and
\mathbf{j}=\nabla y
the vertical unit vector and
y
the vertical coordinate. The viscous stress is
\bm{\tau}=\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})
, where the dynamic viscosity is
\nu
,
\bar{\lambda}=-2/d
, and the symmetric velocity gradient is denoted by
\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}=(\nabla\mathbf{v}+(\nabla\mathbf{v})^{T})/2
. The Helmholtz free energy
\Psi
associated with the system (
2.1
) belongs to the constitutive class:

\displaystyle\Psi=\Psi\left(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi}\right),

(2.2)

where the vector of order parameters is
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}
.
The associated chemical potentials are

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}.

(2.3)

The model consists of the momentum balance equation (
2.1a
), which governs the mixture momentum balance, along with
N
phase mass balance equations (
2.1b
).

Remark 2.1

(Mixture velocity)
.

The system is formulated in terms of the mass-averaged velocity
\mathbf{v}
. Alternatively, it can be expressed using other mixture velocities; for details on the volume-averaged velocity formulation, we refer to
38
.

Next, the absence of void spaces implies that the volume fractions are subject to the saturation constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1.

(2.4)

We incorporate the saturation constraint through the addition of the following equation:

\displaystyle{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=0,

(2.5)

which follows from the addition of the mass balance equations (
2.1b
) using (
2.4
). The diffusive fluxes are
\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}
, where
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
denotes the degenerate mobility matrix, and where
g_{\alpha}=\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}(\mu_{\alpha}+\lambda)
is an effective chemical potential.
Incorporating the chemical potential as a separate state variable, we now arrive at a system that contains
2N+2
equations for
2N+2
(independent) state variables (
\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N},\lambda,\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N}
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}+\nabla\lambda-{\rm div}\bm{\tau}-\rho\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}(\mathbf{J}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.6b)

\displaystyle{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.6c)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle~=0,

(2.6d)

In this formulation, the pressure
\lambda
acts as a Lagrange multiplier that enforces (
2.6c
), see also
38
.

Remark 2.2

(Pure phases)
.

In the regions of pure phases (
\phi_{\alpha}=1
for some
\alpha=1,...,N
), the peculiar velocities vanish (
\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=0
) since the mobility is assumed degenerate. As a consequence, the phase velocity is divergence-free (
{\rm div}\mathbf{v}=0
) in these regions.

Remark 2.3

(Saturation constraint)
.

As an alternative, one can directly substitute the saturation constraint (
2.4
) to reduce the set of volume fraction state variables to
\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}
for some fixed
{\beta}\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
. This leads to a reduced system in which (
2.6a
)-(
2.6d
) constitute
2N+1
equations for
2N+1
state variables (
\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},\lambda,\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}
). This system is not symmetric in terms of
\phi_{\alpha}
.

2.2.  Structural properties

The system possesses underlying structural properties governed by balance laws and dissipation identities. Specifically, under appropriate boundary conditions, it conserves the phase masses, the volume-fraction integrals, and the total mass; preserves the saturation constraint pointwise; and dissipates the total energy

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}~{\rm d}\Omega=

\displaystyle~0,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\alpha}~{\rm d}\Omega=0,\qquad\alpha=1,...,N,

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\rho~{\rm d}\Omega=

\displaystyle~0,

(2.7b)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=1\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t)=1,\quad t>0\right),

(2.7c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)=

\displaystyle~-\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{g_{\alpha}\right\}\right)\leq 0,

(2.7d)

Here the energy
\mathcal{E}
and dissipation rate
\mathcal{D}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right):=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Omega}K\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)+G\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)+\Psi\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)~{\rm d}\Omega,

(2.8a)

\displaystyle K\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}\rho\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)|\mathbf{v}|^{2},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle G\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)=

\displaystyle~\rho\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)gy,

(2.8c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{g_{\alpha}\right\}\right):=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}2\nu\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right):\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right)+\nu\left(\bar{\lambda}+\frac{2}{d}\right)\left({\rm div}\mathbf{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~\quad\quad+\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}\left(\nabla g_{\alpha}\right)^{T}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}~{\rm d}\Omega\geq 0,

(2.8d)

where we decomposed the energy density of
\mathcal{E}
into the kinetic energy density
K
, the energy density due to gravity
G
and the free energy density
\Psi
.

Remark 2.4

(Conservative form)
.

In the absence of gravity (
\mathbf{g}=0
), the model may be written in conservative form by inserting the Korteweg tensor identity:

\displaystyle{\rm div}\bm{\varsigma}=\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta},

(2.9)

where the Korteweg tensor is

\displaystyle\bm{\varsigma}=

\displaystyle~\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}

(2.10)

In order to be consistent with angular momentum conservation, the Cauchy stress is a symmetric second-order tensor. This is only guaranteed if the Korteweg stress
\bm{\varsigma}
yields a symmetric matrix, which restricts the choices that are consistent with thermodynamics.

2.3.  Equilibrium conditions

In this section we consider the stationary equilibrium conditions of system (
2.6
) in absence of gravity and no-slip boundary conditions for the velocity. To this end we consider the standard equilibrium assumptions
\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}=0
and
\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}=\bm{0}
. Under these assumptions, also
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathcal{E}=0
and hence
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{v},\{g_{\alpha}\})=0
which yields
\nabla\mathbf{v}=0
, i.e.
\mathbf{v}=0
and using the kernel of
M
we find
\nabla(g_{\alpha}-g_{{\beta}})=0
, i.e.
g_{\alpha}-g_{\beta}=c_{\alpha{\beta}}=\text{const}
. Substitution into the system yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}+\nabla\lambda

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0,

(2.11b)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle~=0.

(2.11c)

Rewriting (
2.11a
) using the saturation constraint and
\nabla(g_{\alpha}-g_{\beta})=0
yields

0=\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}+\nabla\lambda=\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}=\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\nabla g_{\alpha}=\rho\nabla g_{\alpha}.

(2.12)

Since the total density is nonzero everywhere, we conclude
\nabla g_{\alpha}=0
for all
\alpha
and hence
g_{\alpha}=c_{\alpha}=\textrm{const}
. In particular, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=0\qquad\text{for all }\alpha=1,...,N.

(2.13)

Hence, we find that the volume fractions
\phi_{\alpha}
are characterized by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}+\lambda=\hat{c}_{\alpha}.

(2.14)

with
\hat{c}_{\alpha}:=\rho_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}
. Equation (
2.14
) has the form of a constrained Euler–Lagrange equation. Indeed, the equilibrium conditions for
\phi_{\alpha}
can be interpreted as the following energy minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{c}_{\alpha}(\phi_{\alpha}-m_{\alpha})+\lambda\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N},\\
\langle\phi,e_{\alpha}\rangle=m_{\alpha}\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})+\lambda\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-1\right)

(2.15)

where
m_{\alpha}
are the mean values of
\phi_{\alpha}
. Hence, we can understand
\hat{c}_{\alpha}
as Lagrange multipliers for mean value of
\phi_{\alpha}
and
\lambda
as a Lagrange multiplier for the saturation constraint.

3.  Mixture-aware closure

Here we derive a
mixture-aware thermodynamic closure
, i.e. admissible constitutive choices for the free-energy density and mobility tensor such that the
N
-phase model reduces exactly to an
(N\!-\!1)
-phase model when two phases are physically identical and merged. To this end we specify the constitutive model class and the axioms imposed on the family of
N
-phase closures.

3.1.  Model class and axioms

Throughout,
\Psi
denotes the Helmholtz free-energy density and
\mathbf{M}
the mobility tensor of the
N
-phase model. Quantities associated with the reduced
(N\!-\!1)
-phase system are denoted by a hat, e.g.
\hat{\Psi}
and
\hat{\mathbf{M}}
. The phrase “same functional form” means that
(\Psi,\mathbf{M})
and
(\hat{\Psi},\hat{\mathbf{M}})
are obtained from a single constitutive form, differing only through the number of phases and the corresponding coefficient sets.

A1. Admissible compositions.

We define the Gibbs simplex for
N
phases by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\Big\{\bm{\phi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\ \Big|\ \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}=1,\ \phi_{\beta}\geq 0\ \ (\beta=1,\ldots,N)\Big\}.

(3.1)

The order parameters belong to the Gibbs simplex:
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}
. We visualize the Gibbs simplex for
N=3
in
Figure

1
, and provide details in
Appendix

A
. Constitutive relations are formulated on the interior of
\mathcal{G}
(all
\phi_{\alpha}>0
) and extended to the boundary when needed.

A2. Local first-gradient free energy and chemical potentials.

The Helmholtz free-energy density is local and of first-gradient type, i.e. of the form (
2.2

in
\bm{\phi}
and
\nabla\bm{\phi}
on

\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1

A3. Constant-capillarity.

We restrict attention to a constant-capillarity constitutive class in which the second derivatives of
\Psi
with respect to gradients are independent of the local composition. Equivalently, the tensor

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})\partial(\nabla\phi_{\beta})}

(3.2)

is assumed independent of
\bm{\phi}
(while it may depend on material coefficients).

A4. Mobility closure and admissibility.

We impose the following requirements on the mobility tensor:

(M1) Symmetry:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=\mathbf{M}_{\beta\alpha}(\bm{\phi}),

(3.3)

(M2) Positive semidefiniteness:

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}(\bm{\phi})\bm{\xi}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\bm{\xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

(3.4)

(M3) Constraint compatibility:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\bm{\phi})\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\mathsf{T}},

(3.5)

(M4) Spatial isotropy:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=M_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\mathbf{I},\qquad\alpha,\beta=1,\ldots,N,

(3.6)

(M5) Degeneracy:

\displaystyle\text{if }\phi_{\alpha}=0\text{ or }\phi_{\alpha}=1\text{ then }\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=\mathbf{0},

(3.7)

for scalar mobilities
M_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})
and the identity tensor
\mathbf{I}
.

We consider the case in which phases

\displaystyle\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1}=\nabla\phi_{1}+\nabla\phi_{N},\qquad\nabla\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}=\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\quad(\alpha=2,\ldots,N-1).

,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{1}=

\displaystyle~\rho_{1}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{1}+\rho_{N}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{N},

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}\ \ (\alpha=2,\ldots,N-1),

(3.13b)

where the
N
-phase fluxes
\{\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{N}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{1},

(3.14a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}\quad(\alpha=2,\ldots,N-1),

(3.14b)

with the reduced fluxes
\{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N-1}
of the same Onsager form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}.

(3.15)

A6. Reduction for absent phase.

We require absent phases not to influence the system. For any
K\geq 1
, define the embedded
(N+K)
-phase state

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{\alpha}=\phi_{\alpha}\quad(\alpha=1,\ldots,N),\qquad\tilde{\phi}_{N+1}=\cdots=\tilde{\phi}_{N+K}=0.

(3.16)

Then the
(N+K)
-phase constitutive closures must restrict to the original
N
-phase closures in the sense that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N+K}\tilde{\phi}_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\mu_{\alpha},

(3.17a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}\qquad(\alpha=1,\ldots,N),

(3.17b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{N+1}=

\displaystyle~\cdots=\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{N+K}=\mathbf{0}.

(3.17c)

A7. Absent phase remains absent.

We require that an absent phase remains absent. If
\phi_{\alpha}=0
initially, then
\phi_{\alpha}=0
for all later times.

A8. Invariant under relabelling.

We assume that the constitutive closures are
label-invariant
: for every permutation
\pi
of the phase labels, with associated permutation matrix
P_{\pi}
, the relabeled state

P_{\pi}\bm{\phi}
induces the relabeled closures

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}(P_{\pi}\bm{\phi})_{\alpha}\,\nabla\mu_{\alpha}[P_{\pi}\bm{\phi}]=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\,\nabla\mu_{\alpha}[\bm{\phi}],

(3.18)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}[P_{\pi}\bm{\phi},\,P_{\pi}\mathbf{g}])_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~(P_{\pi}\mathbf{J}[\bm{\phi},\,\mathbf{g}])_{\alpha},

(3.19)

where
\mathbf{J}=(\mathbf{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{J}_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}
and

\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

A9. Identical-phases symmetry.

When two phases are physically identical, the constitutive closures are invariant under exchanging their labels. In particular, if phases
p
and
q
are physically identical then we require:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}(\mathbf{P}_{pq}\bm{\phi})_{\alpha}\,\nabla\mu_{\alpha}[\mathbf{P}_{pq}\bm{\phi}]=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\,\nabla\mu_{\alpha}[\bm{\phi}],

(3.20)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}[\mathbf{P}_{pq}\bm{\phi},\,\mathbf{P}_{pq}\mathbf{g}])_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{P}_{pq}\mathbf{J}[\bm{\phi},\,\mathbf{g}])_{\alpha},

(3.21)

where
\mathbf{P}_{pq}
denotes the permutation matrix that swaps the label
p
and
q
, and where
\mathbf{J}=(\mathbf{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{J}_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}
and

\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

A10. Objectivity and isotropy.

The constitutive closures are objective and isotropic with respect to spatial rotations. For every rotation
\mathbf{Q}\in SO(d)
, define the rotated phase fields by

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{Q}}(x):=\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}x).

(3.22)

We require that the constitutive closures transform covariantly under this change of frame:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{Q}}(x)\,\nabla\mu_{\alpha}[\bm{\phi}^{\mathbf{Q}}](x)=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{Q}\Big(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\,\nabla\mu_{\alpha}[\bm{\phi}]\Big)(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}x),

(3.23)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}[\bm{\phi}^{\mathbf{Q}},\,\mathbf{g}^{\mathbf{Q}}])_{\alpha}(x)=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{Q}\,(\mathbf{J}[\bm{\phi},\,\mathbf{g}])_{\alpha}(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}x),\qquad\alpha=1,\ldots,N,

(3.24)

Equivalently, if
\bm{\phi}
is the phase-field solution of the
N
-phase system, then the rotated field

\bm{\phi}^{\mathbf{Q}}
solves the same constitutive system in the rotated frame.

3.2.  Derivation of the free energy

Here we derive the admissible structure of the Helmholtz free-energy density

\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})
from the axioms in
Section

3.1
. The derivation proceeds in the
following order. First, we derive the bulk part of the Helmholtz free-energy density with an ideal mixing term and an interaction part. After that we derive the capillarity gradient terms. Our guiding axiom in this derivation is A5 (Merging identical phases).

3.2.1.  Bulk free energy

Let
\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi}):=\Psi(\bm{\phi},\mathbf{0})
denote the bulk (homogeneous) energy.

Ideal mixing energy

We assume that phase
1
and
N
are identical and consider the merging of these via (
3.8
). This merge affects only how
\hat{\phi}_{1}
is split into
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{N}
. Therefore, when we compare the
N
-phase and
N-1
-phase capillary-forces via (
3.11
), the only bulk contributions that can be uniquely determined are those that depend on
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{N}
separately (i.e.
is sensitive to the partition
\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{N}
). Indeed, any term that depends on
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{N}
only through
\hat{\phi}_{1}
cannot be distinguished. As such, we first separate the part of the free energy that is sensitive to the partition. Motivated by A6 (Reduction for absent phase), A8 (Invariant under relabelling), and A9 (Identical-phases symmetry), we consider the ansatz:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\text{ideal}}(\bm{\phi}):=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}f_{\alpha}(\phi_{\alpha}),\qquad\hat{\Psi}_{0}^{\text{ideal}}(\bm{\phi}):=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N-1}f_{\alpha}(\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}),

(3.25)

for
\mathcal{C}^{2}
scalar functions
f_{\alpha}
that can depend on the phase number only via the material constants of that phase. Denoting the chemical potentials associated with these free energies by
\mu_{\alpha}^{\text{ideal}}
and
\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\text{ideal}}
, we find

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}^{\text{ideal}}=f_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\phi_{\alpha}),\quad\alpha=1,...,N,\qquad\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\text{ideal}}=f_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}),\quad\alpha=1,...,N-1.

(3.26)

Applying (
3.11
) to (
3.25
) provides the condition:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{1})\nabla\phi_{1}\;+\;\phi_{N}f_{N}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{N})\nabla\phi_{N}=\hat{\phi}_{1}f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\phi}_{1})\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1}.

(3.27)

Using independence of
\nabla\phi_{1}
and
\nabla\phi_{N}
, (
3.27
) implies equality of the coefficients of
\nabla\phi_{1}
and

\nabla\phi_{N}
separately:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{1})=

\displaystyle~\hat{\phi}_{1}f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\phi}_{1}),

(3.28a)

\displaystyle\phi_{N}f_{N}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{N})=

\displaystyle~\hat{\phi}_{1}f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\phi}_{1}).

(3.28b)

Fixing
\phi_{1}
and varying
\phi_{N}
subject to
\phi_{1}+\phi_{N}=\hat{\phi}_{1}
, and noting that the material constants of phase
1
and
N
coincide, yields that the scalar quantity

uf_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(u)
is constant for each
\alpha
on
(0,1)
:

\displaystyle uf_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(u)=W_{\alpha}\qquad\text{for all }u\in(0,1),

(3.29)

for constants
W_{\alpha}
. The solution of (
3.29
) is given by:

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(u)=W_{\alpha}u\log u+a_{\alpha}u+b_{\alpha},

(3.30)

with constants
a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}
.
Noting that affine terms do not affect

\nabla\mu_{\alpha}
, which is the only way the free energy appears in the NSCH model, allows us to discard these terms in (
3.11
). As such, the only relevant part is

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\text{ideal}}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}W_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}.

(3.31)

Quadratic interaction term

Define the remainder (enthalpic) bulk energy by

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\text{enth}}(\bm{\phi}):=\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\Psi_{0}^{\text{ideal}}(\bm{\phi}).

(3.32)

Restricted to the homogeneous bulk setting, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{N}\Big(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{0}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}\partial\phi_{\gamma}}\Big)\nabla\phi_{\gamma},

(3.33)

Thus reduction consistency (
3.11
) constrains the coefficient functions

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}(\bm{\phi}):=\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{0}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}\partial\phi_{\gamma}}(\bm{\phi}),\qquad\gamma=1,\dots,N.

(3.34)

Axiom A5 (Merging identical phases), through (
3.11
), restricts the dependence of
\Psi_{0}^{\text{enth}}
on redistributions of
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{N}
at fixed

\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{N}
. In particular, it forces all mixed second derivatives of
\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{enth}}
to be
constant (independent of
\bm{\phi}
), and hence
\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{enth}}
must be a quadratic form in

\bm{\phi}
up to affine gauge terms. We therefore write

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\text{enth}}(\bm{\phi})=-W\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta},\qquad\chi_{\alpha\beta}=\chi_{\beta\alpha},

(3.35)

where the constants
\chi_{\alpha\beta}
parametrize the interaction matrix, and where we take a common factor
W
for convenience. Combining (
3.31
) and
(
3.35
) yields the bulk energy (up to affine gauges)

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}W_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}-W\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}.

(3.36)

3.2.2.  Capillarity term

Linear gradient terms

We next consider possible terms linear in gradients:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{lin}}(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\mathbf{b}_{\alpha}(\bm{\phi})\cdot\nabla\phi_{\alpha},

(3.37)

where each
\mathbf{b}_{\alpha}(\bm{\phi})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
depends only on the local composition. By A2 (Local first-gradient free energy and chemical potentials), the chemical potentials induced by (
3.37
) are

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{lin}}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}.

(3.38)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi}):=\partial_{\phi_{\alpha}}\mathbf{b}_{\beta}(\bm{\phi})-\partial_{\phi_{\beta}}\mathbf{b}_{\alpha}(\bm{\phi}),\qquad\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}=-\mathbf{a}_{\beta\alpha},

(3.39)

and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\mu_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{lin}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\,\nabla\!\Big(\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}\Big).

(3.40)

We now invoke A10 (
Objectivity and isotropy
). For fixed composition
\bm{\phi}
, each coefficient

\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a spatial vector-valued constitutive quantity whose arguments are only the scalar variables
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}
. Under a spatial rotation, these scalar inputs are unchanged, while isotropy requires the constitutive response to rotate covariantly. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{Q}\in SO(d).

(3.41)

The only vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
invariant under all rotations is the zero vector. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\equiv\mathbf{0}\qquad\text{for all }\alpha,\beta.

(3.42)

Substituting into (
3.38
) and (
3.40
) yields

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{lin}}=0,\qquad\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\mu_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{lin}}\equiv\mathbf{0}.

(3.43)

Thus linear gradient terms do not contribute to the capillary-force closure and may be discarded (
\Psi_{\mathrm{lin}}=0
).

Quadratic gradient terms

The above reasoning shows that the leading admissible gradient dependence is quadratic. Axiom A3 (Constant-capillarity) specifies that
the Hessian of
\Psi
with respect to
\nabla\bm{\phi}
is independent of
\bm{\phi}
. This yields the constant-coefficient quadratic form

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm quad}(\nabla\bm{\phi})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta},\qquad\kappa_{\alpha\beta}=\kappa_{\beta\alpha}.

(3.44)

3.2.3.  Final form and structural summary

Combining (
3.36
) and (
3.44
) yields the derived free-energy structure:

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}.

(3.45a)

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}W_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}-W\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta},

(3.45b)

where the subscript
0
refers to the standard free energy that does not contain any gradient contributions, whereas the gradient term represents an excess free energy in the interfacial region. Here
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}=\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha}
defines the symmetric (volume-based) capillarity matrix that includes both self- and cross-interaction terms among phases. We note that the symmetry assumptions yield a symmetric Korteweg stress, cf. (
2.9
). The associated chemical potential takes the form:

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=\partial_{\phi_{\alpha}}\Psi_{0}-\sum_{{\beta}}\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\Delta\phi_{\beta},

(3.46)

where we used the independence of the state variables
\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}
.

In the incompressible literature it is common to work with capillarity tensors that exclude self-interactions, see e.g.
4
. With the aid of the saturation constraint (
2.4
) we reformulate the gradient contribution.

Lemma 3.1

(Gradient contribution to the free energy)
.

The gradient contribution of the free energy (
3.45
) may be written as

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}=

\displaystyle~-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta},

(3.47a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sigma_{{\beta}\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}+\kappa_{{\beta}{\beta}}-2\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\geq 0,

(3.47b)

where
\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}
only accounts for interfacial contributions between different phases.

Proof.

See
32
.
∎

The chemical potential may now be written as

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\partial_{\phi_{\alpha}}\Psi_{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\beta}\\
{\beta}>\alpha\end{subarray}}\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}\Delta\phi_{\beta}.

(3.48)

Note that at the continuous level all these formulations are equivalent by using the saturation condition. Under the assumption that the matrix
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}
is symmetric positive semi-definite, (
3.45
) yields a convex gradient energy, which is more amenable to discretization. We emphasize that the reduced form
\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}
without the saturation constraint does not imply a convex energy functional, as this matrix is not symmetric positive semi-definite.

Remark 3.2

(Capillarity coefficients are not surface tensions)
.

We note that neither
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}
nor
\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}
represents a surface tension quantity.

Remark 3.3

(Alternative ideal free-energy forms)
.

Again, noting that affine terms do not affect the model, the ideal contribution

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{ideal}}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}W_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}

(3.49)

is for
W_{\alpha}=\Lambda_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}
, equivalent to

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{ideal}}(\tilde{\bm{\rho}})\sim\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\Lambda_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\log\!\Big(\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}{\rho_{0}}\Big)

(3.50)

for any fixed reference density
\rho_{0}>0
, and for
W_{\alpha}=\Gamma_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}/M_{\alpha}
equivalent to

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{ideal}}(\mathbf{c})\sim\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\Gamma_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\log\!\Big(\frac{c_{\alpha}}{c_{0}}\Big),

(3.51)

where
c_{\alpha}=\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}/M_{\alpha}
is the molar concentration and
c_{0}>0
is a fixed reference concentration.

3.3.  Derivation of the mobility tensor

In this section we derive a reduction-consistent Onsager mobility tensor
\mathbf{M}(\bm{\phi})
for the flux law
in
Section

2
. We work within the mobility model class of
Section

3.1
, in particular symmetry, positive
semidefiniteness, and constraint compatibility (A4/M1–M3), and we impose the diffusive-flux reduction requirement
(A5).

From (
3.14
) we find:

\displaystyle-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}(\mathbf{M}_{1\beta}+\mathbf{M}_{N\beta})\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}-(\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{N1})\nabla g_{1}-(\mathbf{M}_{1N}+\mathbf{M}_{NN})\nabla g_{N}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{1\beta}\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{11}\nabla\hat{g}_{1},

(3.52a)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}-\mathbf{M}_{\alpha 1}\nabla g_{1}-\mathbf{M}_{\alpha N}\nabla g_{N}=-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha 1}\nabla\hat{g}_{1}

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad\alpha=2,...,N-1.

(3.52b)

The arbitrariness of
\nabla g_{\alpha}
provides the conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{1\beta}+\mathbf{M}_{N\beta}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{1\beta},\quad\text{for}\quad\beta=2,...,N-1.

(3.53a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha\beta}\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha=2,...,N-1.

(3.53b)

We have the identities:

\displaystyle\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}=

\displaystyle~\nabla g_{\beta}\quad(\beta=2,\dots,N-1),

(3.54a)

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla g_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla g_{N}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{g}_{1},

(3.54b)

and hence:

\displaystyle\nabla\hat{g}_{1}=\frac{\phi_{1}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}}\nabla g_{1}+\frac{\phi_{N}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}}\nabla g_{N}.

(3.55)

Using (
3.54
)–(
3.55
) in (
3.52b
)-(
3.52a
) gives

\displaystyle-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}(\mathbf{M}_{1\beta}+\mathbf{M}_{N\beta})\nabla g_{\beta}-(\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{N1})\nabla g_{1}-(\mathbf{M}_{1N}+\mathbf{M}_{NN})\nabla g_{N}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{1\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{11}\Big(\frac{\phi_{1}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}}\nabla g_{1}+\frac{\phi_{N}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}}\nabla g_{N}\Big),

(3.56a)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}-\mathbf{M}_{\alpha 1}\nabla g_{1}-\mathbf{M}_{\alpha N}\nabla g_{N}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha 1}\Big(\frac{\phi_{1}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}}\nabla g_{1}+\frac{\phi_{N}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}}\nabla g_{N}\Big),\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha=2,...,N-1.

(3.56b)

Since (
3.56
) holds for all admissible driving fields, the coefficients of the
independent gradients must vanish, i.e.:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{N1}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{11}\frac{\phi_{1}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}},

(3.57a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{1N}+\mathbf{M}_{NN}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{11}\frac{\phi_{N}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}},

(3.57b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha 1}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha 1}\frac{\phi_{1}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}},\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha=2,...,N-1,

(3.57c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha N}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha 1}\frac{\phi_{N}}{\hat{\phi}_{1}},\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha=2,...,N-1.

(3.57d)

We proceed by considering variations
(\phi_{1},\phi_{N})\rightarrow(\phi_{1}+\delta,\phi_{N}-\delta)
that keep
\hat{\phi}_{1}
fixed. The mobilities
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha 1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{11}
are unchanged by these variations. Along redistributions
(\phi_{1},\phi_{N})\mapsto(\phi_{1}+\delta,\phi_{N}-\delta)
that keep

\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{N}
(and thus
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
) fixed, relations (
3.57c
)-(
3.57d
) imply that

•

\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{N1}
depends linearly on
\phi_{1}
(equivalently,
(\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{N1})/\phi_{1}
is split-independent),

•

\mathbf{M}_{1N}+\mathbf{M}_{NN}
depends linearly on
\phi_{N}
(equivalently,
(\mathbf{M}_{1N}+\mathbf{M}_{NN})/\phi_{N}
is split-independent),

•

\mathbf{M}_{\alpha 1}
depends linearly on
\phi_{1}
for
\alpha=2,\ldots,N-1
(equivalently,
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha 1}/\phi_{1}
is split-independent),

•

\mathbf{M}_{\alpha N}
depends linearly on
\phi_{N}
for
\alpha=2,\ldots,N-1
(equivalently,
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha N}/\phi_{N}
is split-independent).

These split-linearity statements are consequences of merging the
specific
identical pair
(1,N)
and therefore constrain only how mobility entries respond to redistributions of
\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{N}
between
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{N}
.
In particular, they enforce linear dependence on
\phi_{1}
or
\phi_{N}

along such redistributions
, but they do not
(by themselves) determine how the same entries depend on other volume fractions, e.g. on
\phi_{\alpha}
for

\alpha\in\{2,\ldots,N-1\}
, since these are held fixed during the redistribution. Symmetry of
\mathbf{M}
only transfers
the same split-linearity between corresponding rows and columns (e.g.
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha 1}=\mathbf{M}_{1\alpha}
), but it
does not introduce new dependence on
\phi_{\alpha}
. To obtain a closed constitutive form for
all
pairs

(\alpha,\beta)
that is consistent with performing the same merge operation for arbitrary identical phases and for any
number of phases (A5), we now introduce an extensible, label-covariant mobility form compatible with M1-M4. We choose

\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\beta}=M_{\alpha\beta}\mathbf{I}
from pairwise exchange mobilities by prescribing, for
\alpha\neq\beta
,

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=-m_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\mathcal{M}(\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}),\qquad m_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=m_{\beta\alpha}(\bm{\phi})\geq 0,

(3.58)

where
m_{\alpha{\beta}}(\bm{\phi})=m_{\alpha{\beta}}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})
and where
\mathcal{M}:(0,1)^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a single symmetric scalar function,

\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathcal{M}(v,u)
, shared by all pairs (label covariance). The diagonal entries are then fixed by the
constraint compatibility
M(\bm{\phi})\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
:

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\alpha}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}m_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})\mathcal{M}(\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}).

(3.59)

We now impose reduction consistency. Let phases
1
and
N
be identical. By identical-phases symmetry,
m_{1\alpha}=m_{N\alpha}
for all
\alpha=2,\dots,N-1
. The off-diagonal relation yields, for each

\alpha=2,\dots,N-1
,

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{1\alpha}=M_{1\alpha}+M_{N\alpha}.

(3.60)

Substituting the form (
3.58
) and using
\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{N}
and
\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}=\phi_{\alpha}
gives

\displaystyle-m_{1\alpha}\mathcal{M}(\hat{\phi}_{1},\phi_{\alpha})=-m_{1\alpha}\mathcal{M}(\phi_{1},\phi_{\alpha})-m_{1\alpha}\mathcal{M}(\phi_{N},\phi_{\alpha}).

(3.61)

Cancelling
-m_{1\alpha}
(for
m_{1\alpha}>0
) yields the functional equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\phi_{1}+\phi_{N},\phi_{\alpha})=\mathcal{M}(\phi_{1},\phi_{\alpha})+\mathcal{M}(\phi_{N},\phi_{\alpha}),\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1.

(3.62)

Since
\alpha
is arbitrary and
\phi_{\alpha}
can take any value in
(0,1)
, (
3.62
) implies that for each fixed

v\in(0,1)
the map
u\mapsto\mathcal{M}(u,v)
is additive on
(0,1)
. By symmetry of
\mathcal{M}
, the same holds in
the second argument. Together with the natural boundary condition
\mathcal{M}(0,v)=0
(no mobility involving an absent
phase) and mild regularity (e.g. continuity), this forces
\mathcal{M}
to be bilinear:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=cuv.

(3.63)

The constant
c>0
may be absorbed into the coefficients
m_{\alpha\beta}
, and we therefore set, without loss of
generality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=uv.

(3.64)

With (
3.64
), the mobility tensor is

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~-m_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}\quad(\alpha\neq\beta),

(3.65a)

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\alpha}(\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}m_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}=\phi_{\alpha}\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}m_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\beta},

(3.65b)

with
m_{\alpha\beta}=m_{\beta\alpha}\geq 0
.

4.  Constitutive closure specification

Section
3
identified the admissible
structure
of the constitutive closure from the mixture-aware axioms. To obtain a concrete closed model, one still has to specify the free-energy and mobility coefficients within this admissible class. In this section, we introduce a convenient parameterization of the free energy and mobility tensor and discuss the resulting constitutive behavior, in particular for distinct and identical phases.

4.1.  Free energy

To expose explicitly the diffuse-interface thickness scale, we introduce a parameter
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, choose
W_{\alpha}=W=\bar{W}/\varepsilon_{0}
and
\kappa_{\alpha\beta}=\varepsilon_{0}\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
where
\bar{W}>0
and
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
are independent of
\varepsilon_{0}
. This yields the free energy

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})=\frac{\bar{W}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigl(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}\Bigr)+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}.

(4.1)

Here
\bar{W}
sets the bulk energy scale,
\chi_{\alpha\beta}
determines the location and depth of the minima of the homogeneous free energy on the Gibbs simplex,
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
determines the energetic cost of gradients, and
\varepsilon_{0}
sets the diffuse thickness scale.

The parameterization (
4.1
) separates the bulk and gradient contributions by the standard factors
1/\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
. In the NSCH model, as in diffuse-interface models more generally, the diffuse-interface thickness is an emergent property of the free energy rather than an independently prescribed quantity. The parameter
\varepsilon_{0}
controls its overall scale, but the actual interface width also depends on the coefficients
\bar{W}
and
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
, and therefore is not equal to
\varepsilon_{0}
in general. In fact, because an
N
-phase model generally contains multiple pairwise interfaces, the diffuse-interface width is interface-dependent. Given the pairwise interface widths
\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}
for interfaces
\alpha
–
{\beta}
, we define the representative interface width by
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}\min_{\alpha<\beta}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}
. A precise definition of the corresponding pairwise interface widths
\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}
is given in
32
.

In practical terms, one chooses
\chi_{\alpha\beta}
and
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
so that the desired bulk states and interfacial couplings are represented, and then chooses
\varepsilon_{0}
so that the resulting diffuse interfaces are resolvable on the mesh. The quantitative calibration of
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
to target surface tensions and numerical interface widths is treated in
32
. Below we discuss the homogeneous (bulk) free energy behavior.

We split the homogeneous part of the free energy into an entropic and an interaction enthalpic contribution,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\Psi_{0,\mathrm{ent}}(\bm{\phi})+\Psi_{\mathrm{int}}(\bm{\phi}),

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\mathrm{ent}}(\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\frac{\bar{W}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha},

(4.2b)

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\mathrm{int}}(\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~-\frac{\bar{W}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}.

(4.2c)

The first term represents the ideal-mixing entropy term that favors mixing, whereas the second term contains the coefficient-dependent interaction structure.

4.2.  Regularization of the free energy

The Flory–Huggins (FH) bulk free-energy density contains entropic contributions of the form

\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}
. For admissible compositions
\phi_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
this expression is smooth;
however, as
\phi_{\alpha}\to 0^{+}
the associated chemical potential diverges, which makes the practical computation of minima or coexistence points very challenging.
To obtain a globally smooth bulk potential with bounded derivatives on
[0,1]
, we replace
\phi\log\phi
by
a polynomial surrogate and calibrate the interaction parameters such that the pure phases become local
minima of the resulting bulk potential.

The symmetric interaction matrix

\chi_{\alpha\beta}
is, in general, fully populated. However, in the current calibration we retain only
its diagonal entries for convenience,

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha\beta}=\chi_{\alpha}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta},\qquad\alpha,\beta\in\{1,\ldots,N\},

(4.5)

although this restriction is not required by the formulation.

To remove the logarithmic singularity we approximate the function
x\log x
on
(0,1]
by a degree-six
polynomial

\displaystyle P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{6}a_{k}x^{k}\approx x\log x,\qquad x\in(0,1],

(4.6)

where the coefficients
\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{6}
are obtained by a least-squares fit over a dense sampling of

(0,1]
. The polynomial bulk energy used in the computations is then

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\frac{\bar{W}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}P(\phi_{\alpha})-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}^{2}\right).

(4.7)

Replacing
x\log x
by
P(x)
yields a smooth bulk potential on
[0,1]
, but does not by itself enforce that
the pure phases are minima. We therefore calibrate the interaction parameter(s) using the binary case so
that the pure states are stationary points and, in fact, local minima of the polynomial bulk potential.

On the binary manifold
(\phi,1-\phi)
, with the remaining phases set to zero, (
4.7
)
reduces to

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}_{0}(\phi,1-\phi)=\frac{\bar{W}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Big(P(\phi)+P(1-\phi)-\chi\big(\phi^{2}+(1-\phi)^{2}\big)\Big),

(4.8)

where
\chi
denotes the effective binary interaction parameter for this calibration. Imposing stationarity
at the pure states,

\mathrm{d}\widehat{\Psi}_{0}(\phi,1-\phi)/\mathrm{d}\phi=0
at
\phi=0
(and by symmetry at
\phi=1
), yields

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{1}{2}\Big(2a_{2}+3a_{3}+4a_{4}+5a_{5}+6a_{6}\Big)=2.0753225165.

(4.9)

With (
4.9
), the pure phases
\phi\in\{0,1\}
are stationary points of

\widehat{\Psi}_{0}(\phi,1-\phi)
. Moreover, for the calibrated value (
4.9
) one has

\mathrm{d}^{2}\widehat{\Psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}\big|_{\phi=0,1}>0
, so that
\phi=0
and
\phi=1
are local
minima of the polynomial bulk potential. In the diagonal choice (
4.5
), we take

\chi_{\alpha}=\chi
for all phases in the present implementation.

To match the barrier height of the standard Ginzburg–Landau (GL) double well, we choose the bulk prefactor

\bar{W}
such that the barrier of the polynomial surrogate equals
\max_{\varphi\in[-1,1]}W_{\mathrm{GL}}(\varphi)=1/4
,
where
\bar{W}_{\mathrm{GL}}(\varphi)=(1-\varphi^{2})^{2}/4
for
\varphi\in[-1,1]
. Using the affine mapping

\varphi=2\phi-1
on the binary manifold, the GL potential becomes
\bar{W}_{\mathrm{GL}}(\phi)=4\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}
,
which attains its maximum
1/4
at
\phi=1/2
.

Let
\widehat{\psi}(\phi)
denote the binary polynomial bulk energy with unit prefactor (
\bar{W}=1
),

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}(\phi)=P(\phi)+P(1-\phi)-\chi\big(\phi^{2}+(1-\phi)^{2}\big).

(4.10)

We then set

\displaystyle\bar{W}=\frac{1/4}{\widehat{\psi}(1/2)-\widehat{\psi}(1)}=0.6923807595.

(4.11)

With (
4.9
)–(
4.11
), the surrogate (
4.7
) is smooth on
[0,1]
,
renders the pure phases as local minima, and reproduces the GL barrier height by construction. The
resulting partial bulk-energy contributions are shown in Figure

between GL, classical FH, and the polynomial surrogate is provided in Figure

4.3.  Mobility tensor

In
Section

3.3
we derived a reduction-consistent mobility class and, for practical use, we restrict here to an isotropic mobility
tensor of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}(\bm{\phi})=M_{\alpha{\beta}}(\bm{\phi})\mathbf{I},\qquad\alpha,{\beta}=1,\ldots,N,

(4.12)

with a scalar mobility matrix
M_{\alpha{\beta}}
that is symmetric, positive semidefinite, and satisfies

M(\bm{\phi})\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
.

Using the constraint compatibility
M\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
, we have

M_{\alpha\alpha}=-\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}M_{\alpha{\beta}}
and therefore, for each
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}M_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla(g_{\beta}-g_{\alpha})

(4.13)

Thus diffusion is driven only by differences of generalized potentials.

We choose the mobility as:

\displaystyle M_{\alpha{\beta}}=-4m_{0}\varepsilon_{0}(\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}+\rho_{\beta}^{-1})^{-2}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta},

(4.14)

with
m_{0}
a constant for which we choose
m_{0}=10^{-3}
. This provides the standard mobility form in the two-phase case.
We provide the details in
Appendix

C
. In numerical computations we require the matrix
M_{\alpha{\beta}}
to remain negative semi-definite, and therefore we choose the regularization:

\displaystyle M_{\alpha{\beta}}=-4m_{0}\varepsilon_{0}(\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}+\rho_{\beta}^{-1})^{-2}(\phi_{\alpha})_{+}(\phi_{\beta})_{+},

(4.15)

and
M_{\alpha\alpha}=-\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}M_{\alpha{\beta}}
which complies with the compatibility constraint
M\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
.

Remark 4.1

(Maxwell–Stefan mobility)
.

Selecting
M_{\alpha{\beta}}=m(\rho)\rho_{\alpha}\rho_{\beta}/\rho
yields the Maxwell–Stefan matrix

\displaystyle M_{\alpha{\beta}}(\bm{\phi})=m(\rho)\Big(\delta_{\alpha{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}{\rho}\Big),

(4.16)

so that for
\alpha\neq{\beta}
,

\displaystyle M_{\alpha{\beta}}=-m(\rho)\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}{\rho}=-m(\rho)\frac{\rho_{\alpha}\rho_{\beta}}{\rho}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}.

(4.17)

Substituting (
4.17
) into the form (
4.13
) shows explicitly that each pairwise
exchange contribution is proportional to
\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}
, and hence vanishes when either phase is absent.

Remark 4.2

(Elliott–Garcke mobility)
.

The mobility structure used in
13
can be written in the form

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}=l_{\alpha}(\phi_{\alpha})\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{l_{\beta}(\phi_{\beta})}{\sum_{\gamma}l_{\gamma}(\phi_{\gamma})}\right).

(4.18)

Choosing
l_{\alpha}(\phi_{\alpha})=m(\rho)\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}=m(\rho)\rho_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}
,
provides

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}=m(\rho)\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}{\rho}\right)=m(\rho)\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}{\rho}\right).

(4.19)

This coincides exactly with the Maxwell–Stefan mobility matrix (
4.16
).

6.  Conclusions

We have studied constitutive closures for the
N
-phase incompressible Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard mixture model from the viewpoint of
mixture-awareness
, namely invariance under merging physically identical phases. The central point idea is that reduction should not only be required when a phase is absent, but also when two labels represent the same phase and are merged into a single phase.

Within the model class considered here, this principle has strong consequences. For the free energy, PDE-level reduction consistency under merging identical phases selects an ideal-mixing contribution together with a symmetric pairwise interaction term and a constant quadratic gradient penalty. For the mobility, the same principle yields a pairwise-exchange Onsager structure with bilinear degeneracy in the volume fractions. Thus, rather than proposing one possible closure, the paper identifies a distinguished multiphase closure within the chosen axiomatic setting. We note that the merging of identical phases naturally provides two complementary properties. Namely, the model reduces correctly on faces of the Gibbs simplex when a phase is absent. At the same time, it ensures that an absent phase remains absent.

We also introduced a concrete constitutive parameterization and discussed the resulting energy landscapes for distinct and identical phases. This provides a closed model form suitable for both analysis and computation. We numerically demonstrated the mixture-aware properties, and demonstrated the ability of the framework to perform ternary and quaternary simulations.

Several questions for future research remain open. First, the mixture-aware framework presented here has been formulated for an incompressible flow model with a single momentum equation. A natural next step is to apply it to other mixture-theoretic diffuse-interface formulations, including compressible mixtures
35
and mixture models with
N
-momentum equations
37
. Second, it is well known in the
N
-phase flow literature that quaternary coexistence points are typically unstable. In this respect, the present framework seems well suited for studying higher-order junctions and their stability in multiphase diffuse-interface flows. In particular, it would be interesting to clarify whether nominal quaternary coexistence states can persist within a mixture-aware closure, or whether they necessarily relax toward lower-order interfacial configurations. Closely related to this issue, the sharp
-
interface asymptotic analysis of the present mixture
-
aware closure, and the identification of the corresponding limiting free
-
boundary problem, remain open problems. Finally, since the underlying PDE framework was only recently introduced, a rigorous mathematical analysis of the system is still lacking, including results on the existence of weak solutions, well
-
posedness, and long
-
time behavior.

Appendix A
Gibbs simplex

To visualize states parameterized by non-negative phases subject to a single linear relation, it is convenient to use the
Gibbs simplex
. Consider a set of
N
non-negative fractions
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N}
satisfying

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=1.

(A.2)

The admissible set is the
(N-1)
-dimensional simplex. The Gibbs triangle corresponds to the case
N=3
, in which the simplex is represented as an equilateral triangle: each vertex is a pure state (
\phi_{\alpha}=1
for one phase), each edge corresponds to binary mixtures (one phase identically zero), and the interior represents fully ternary states. Lines of constant
\phi_{\alpha}
are straight and parallel to the edge opposite the
\alpha
th vertex. An example Gibbs triangle with the associated coordinate axes and simplex boundaries is shown in Figure
1
.

For
N=3
, we use barycentric coordinates on an equilateral triangle with vertices associated with
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
, and
\phi_{3}
. A convenient Cartesian embedding is obtained by placing the vertices at

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}=(0,0),\qquad\bm{v}_{2}=(1,0),\qquad\bm{v}_{3}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right),

(A.3)

so that any composition maps to

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\phi_{\alpha}\,\bm{v}_{\alpha}=\left(\phi_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\phi_{3},\ \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\phi_{3}\right),

(A.4)

with the constraint
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\phi_{\alpha}=1
enforced implicitly.

For
N=4
, each vertex of the tetrahedron corresponds to a pure state, each edge to a binary mixture, each face to a ternary subsystem (
\phi_{\alpha}=0
for one phase), and the interior to fully quaternary states. A convenient realization uses barycentric coordinates with respect to four vertices
\{\bm{v}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{4}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, so that

\displaystyle(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\phi_{\alpha}\,\bm{v}_{\alpha}.

(A.5)

One simple choice is the regular tetrahedron with vertices

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}=(0,0,0),\quad\bm{v}_{2}=(1,0,0),\quad\bm{v}_{3}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,0\right),\quad\bm{v}_{4}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}},\,\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\right),

(A.6)

which yields the explicit mapping

\displaystyle x=\phi_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\phi_{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}\phi_{4},\quad y=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\phi_{3}+\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}\phi_{4},\quad z=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\phi_{4}.

(A.7)

This embedding enforces
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\phi_{\alpha}=1
implicitly and makes the lower-dimensional subsimplices immediately visible: setting
\phi_{4}=0
recovers the Gibbs triangle on the face spanned by
(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3})
, while analogous triangular faces are obtained by setting any one phase to zero.

Appendix B
Proofs of reduction-consistency properties

Theorem B.1

(Reduction by merging identical phases)
.

Assume
N\geq 3
and consider two phases, without loss of generality phases
1
and
N
, which are identical in the sense that

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{N},

(B.1a)

\displaystyle\chi_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle=\chi_{N\alpha},\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N,

(B.1b)

\displaystyle\kappa_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{N\alpha},\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N,

(B.1c)

\displaystyle m_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle=m_{N\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1.

(B.1d)

Define the merged variables by

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\phi_{1}+\phi_{N},

(B.2a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\phi_{\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1.

(B.2b)

Then the
N
-phase NSCH system reduces to the
(N-1)
-phase NSCH system for

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\phi}}=(\widehat{\phi}_{1},\widehat{\phi}_{2},\dots,\widehat{\phi}_{N-1}),

(B.3)

with the reduced coefficients:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\rho_{1},\qquad\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}:=\rho_{\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1,

(B.4a)

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{11}

\displaystyle:=\chi_{11}=\chi_{1N}=\chi_{NN},

(B.4b)

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\chi_{1\alpha}=\chi_{N\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1,

(B.4c)

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{{\beta}\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\chi_{{\beta}\alpha},\qquad{\beta},\alpha=2,\dots,N-1,

(B.4d)

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}_{11}

\displaystyle:=\kappa_{11}=\kappa_{1N}=\kappa_{NN},

(B.4e)

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\kappa_{1\alpha}=\kappa_{N\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1,

(B.4f)

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}_{{\beta}\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha},\qquad{\beta},\alpha=2,\dots,N-1,

(B.4g)

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle:=m_{1\alpha}=m_{N\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1,

(B.4h)

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{{\beta}\alpha}

\displaystyle:=m_{{\beta}\alpha},\qquad{\beta},\alpha=2,\dots,N-1.

(B.4i)

Proof.

Free energy and chemical potentials

We define the reduced free energy by

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\nabla\hat{\bm{\phi}})

\displaystyle=\hat{\Psi}_{0}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\hat{\phi}_{\beta},

(B.5a)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{0}(\hat{\bm{\phi}})

\displaystyle=W\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N-1}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\log\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}-W\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\chi}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\hat{\phi}_{\beta}.

(B.5b)

We denote

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}=\partial_{\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}}\hat{\Psi}_{0}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\hat{\phi}_{\beta},\qquad\hat{g}_{\alpha}=\frac{\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}+\lambda}{\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}}.

(B.6)

For every
\alpha=2,\dots,N-1
, the chemical potential of the original
N
-phase system is

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=W(\log\phi_{\alpha}+1)-2W\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\beta}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\phi_{\beta}.

(B.7)

Using symmetry of
\chi
and
\kappa
together with the assumptions

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha 1}=\chi_{1\alpha}=\chi_{N\alpha}=\chi_{\alpha N},\qquad\kappa_{\alpha 1}=\kappa_{1\alpha}=\kappa_{N\alpha}=\kappa_{\alpha N},

(B.8)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=W(\log\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}+1)-2W\Big(\chi_{\alpha 1}(\phi_{1}+\phi_{N})+\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle~~-\Big(\kappa_{\alpha 1}(\Delta\phi_{1}+\Delta\phi_{N})+\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\phi_{\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle=W(\log\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}+1)-2W\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\chi}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{\phi}_{\beta}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\hat{\phi}_{\beta}=\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}.

(B.9)

Hence

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}=\hat{g}_{\alpha},\qquad\nabla g_{\alpha}=\nabla\hat{g}_{\alpha},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1.

(B.10)

Effective chemical potential of the merged phase

Since
\rho_{1}=\rho_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla g_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla g_{N}=\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\Big(\phi_{1}\nabla(\mu_{1}+\lambda)+\phi_{N}\nabla(\mu_{N}+\lambda)\Big).

(B.11)

Using the explicit form of the chemical potentials, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\mu_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla\mu_{N}

\displaystyle=W\phi_{1}\nabla(\log\phi_{1}+1)+W\phi_{N}\nabla(\log\phi_{N}+1)

\displaystyle\quad-2W\phi_{1}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{1\beta}\nabla\phi_{\beta}-2W\phi_{N}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{N\beta}\nabla\phi_{\beta}

\displaystyle\quad-\phi_{1}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{1\beta}\nabla\Delta\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{N}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{N\beta}\nabla\Delta\phi_{\beta}.

(B.12)

By the assumptions on the coefficients and the identities

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla(\log\phi_{1}+1)+\phi_{N}\nabla(\log\phi_{N}+1)=\nabla\phi_{1}+\nabla\phi_{N}=\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1},

(B.13a)

\displaystyle\chi_{11}\nabla\phi_{1}+\chi_{1N}\nabla\phi_{N}=\hat{\chi}_{11}\nabla(\phi_{1}+\phi_{N})=\hat{\chi}_{11}\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1},

(B.13b)

\displaystyle\kappa_{11}\nabla\Delta\phi_{1}+\kappa_{1N}\nabla\Delta\phi_{N}=\hat{\kappa}_{11}\nabla\Delta(\phi_{1}+\phi_{N})=\hat{\kappa}_{11}\nabla\Delta\hat{\phi}_{1},

(B.13c)

this becomes

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\mu_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla\mu_{N}=W\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1}-2W\hat{\phi}_{1}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\chi}_{1\beta}\nabla\hat{\phi}_{\beta}-\hat{\phi}_{1}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\kappa}_{1\beta}\nabla\Delta\hat{\phi}_{\beta}.

(B.14)

On the other hand, the reduced chemical potential of the merged phase is

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{1}=W(\log\hat{\phi}_{1}+1)-2W\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\chi}_{1\beta}\hat{\phi}_{\beta}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\kappa}_{1\beta}\Delta\hat{\phi}_{\beta},

(B.15)

and therefore

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{1}=W\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1}-2W\hat{\phi}_{1}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\chi}_{1\beta}\nabla\hat{\phi}_{\beta}-\hat{\phi}_{1}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\kappa}_{1\beta}\nabla\Delta\hat{\phi}_{\beta}.

(B.16)

Hence

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\mu_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla\mu_{N}=\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{1},

(B.17)

and consequently

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla g_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla g_{N}=\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{g}_{1}.

(B.18)

Diffusive flux of the merged phase

Adding the phase equations for
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{N}
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\phi}_{1}+\operatorname{div}(\hat{\phi}_{1}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{1}^{-1}\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{N})=0.

(B.19)

We now show that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{N}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{M}_{1\beta}\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta},

(B.20)

where the reduced mobility has the same form,

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{\alpha\beta}=\begin{cases}-\hat{m}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\hat{\phi}_{\beta},&\alpha\neq\beta,\\[3.99994pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\neq\alpha}\hat{m}_{\alpha\gamma}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\hat{\phi}_{\gamma},&\alpha=\beta.\end{cases}

(B.21)

For
\beta=2,\dots,N-1
, using
m_{1\beta}=m_{N\beta}
we obtain

\displaystyle M_{1\beta}+M_{N\beta}=-m_{1\beta}\phi_{1}\phi_{\beta}-m_{N\beta}\phi_{N}\phi_{\beta}=-\hat{m}_{1\beta}\hat{\phi}_{1}\hat{\phi}_{\beta}=\hat{M}_{1\beta}.

(B.22)

For the diagonal part,

\displaystyle M_{11}+M_{N1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\neq 1}m_{1\gamma}\phi_{1}\phi_{\gamma}-m_{N1}\phi_{N}\phi_{1}=\sum_{\gamma=2}^{N-1}m_{1\gamma}\phi_{1}\phi_{\gamma},

(B.23)

and similarly

\displaystyle M_{1N}+M_{NN}=\sum_{\gamma=2}^{N-1}m_{1\gamma}\phi_{N}\phi_{\gamma}.

(B.24)

Therefore

\displaystyle(M_{11}+M_{N1})\nabla g_{1}+(M_{1N}+M_{NN})\nabla g_{N}=

\displaystyle~\left(\sum_{\gamma=2}^{N-1}m_{1\gamma}\hat{\phi}_{\gamma}\right)\big(\phi_{1}\nabla g_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla g_{N}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left(\sum_{\gamma=2}^{N-1}\hat{m}_{1\gamma}\hat{\phi}_{\gamma}\right)\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{g}_{1}=\hat{M}_{11}\nabla\hat{g}_{1},

(B.25)

where we used (
B.18
). Altogether,

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{N}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{M}_{1\beta}\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}.

(B.26)

Thus the merged phase
\hat{\phi}_{1}
satisfies exactly the reduced phase equation.

Diffusive fluxes of the remaining phases

Let
\alpha\in\{2,\dots,N-1\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}M_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}=-\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}M_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}-M_{\alpha 1}\nabla g_{1}-M_{\alpha N}\nabla g_{N}.

(B.27)

Using
m_{\alpha 1}=m_{\alpha N}
and (
B.18
), we find

\displaystyle M_{\alpha 1}\nabla g_{1}+M_{\alpha N}\nabla g_{N}

\displaystyle=-m_{\alpha 1}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{1}\nabla g_{1}-m_{\alpha N}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{N}\nabla g_{N}

\displaystyle=-\hat{m}_{\alpha 1}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\big(\phi_{1}\nabla g_{1}+\phi_{N}\nabla g_{N}\big)

\displaystyle=-\hat{m}_{\alpha 1}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha}\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{g}_{1}=\hat{M}_{\alpha 1}\nabla\hat{g}_{1}.

(B.28)

Since
M_{\alpha\beta}=\hat{M}_{\alpha\beta}
and
\nabla g_{\beta}=\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta}
for
\beta=2,\dots,N-1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{M}_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\hat{g}_{\beta},\qquad\alpha=2,\dots,N-1.

(B.29)

Hence all remaining phase equations also reduce to the
(N-1)
-phase system.

Capillary force

Finally, the capillary force in the momentum equation satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\nabla(\mu_{\beta}+\lambda)

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}\nabla(\mu_{1}+\lambda)+\phi_{N}\nabla(\mu_{N}+\lambda)+\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\phi_{\beta}\nabla(\mu_{\beta}+\lambda)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla(\hat{\mu}_{1}+\lambda)+\sum_{\beta=2}^{N-1}\hat{\phi}_{\beta}\nabla(\hat{\mu}_{\beta}+\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta=1}^{N-1}\hat{\phi}_{\beta}\nabla(\hat{\mu}_{\beta}+\lambda).

(B.30)

This is precisely the capillary force of the reduced
(N-1)
-phase system.

The above identities show that the merged variables
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
satisfy exactly the
(N-1)
-phase NSCH system with the reduced constitutive coefficients. This proves the claim.
∎

Theorem B.2

(Reduction for absent phase)
.

If a solution of the
N
-phase NSCH system satisfies
\phi_{\gamma}\equiv 0
,
then the restriction of the
N
-phase NSCH system to
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
coincides with the
(N-1)
-phase NSCH system obtained by deleting phase
\gamma
and canonically relabeling the remaining indices.

Proof.

Free energies

We first identify the reduced free energy. Denote by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\phi}}:=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{\gamma-1},\phi_{\gamma+1},\dots,\phi_{N})

(B.31)

the vector of active phases. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(\widehat{\bm{\phi}},\nabla\widehat{\bm{\phi}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Psi}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{\phi}})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta=1\\
\alpha,\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta},

(B.32a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{\phi}})

\displaystyle=W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=1\\
\alpha\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}-W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta=1\\
\alpha,\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}.

(B.32b)

Because
\phi_{\gamma}=0
and
\nabla\phi_{\gamma}=0
, the restriction of (
3.45
) to
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
is exactly (
B.32
).

Chemical potentials

For every active index
\alpha\neq\gamma
, the chemical potential of the
N
-phase system is

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=W(\log\phi_{\alpha}+1)-2W\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\beta}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\phi_{\beta}.

(B.33)

Restricting to
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
gives

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=W(\log\phi_{\alpha}+1)-2W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\beta}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\phi_{\beta},\qquad\alpha\neq\gamma.

(B.34)

On the other hand, the reduced chemical potential associated with (
B.32
) is

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\alpha}=W(\log\phi_{\alpha}+1)-2W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\chi_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\beta}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\Delta\phi_{\beta},\qquad\alpha\neq\gamma,

(B.35)

As a consequence, the chemical potentials coincide:
\mu_{\alpha}=\hat{\mu}_{\alpha},\alpha\neq\gamma
, and through the identification
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}
we have
g_{\alpha}=\hat{g}_{\alpha},\alpha\neq\gamma
, so the effective chemical potentials of all active phases agree with those of the reduced system.

Diffusive fluxes of active phases

Next, we analyze the diffusive fluxes. For the active phases we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}M_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}M_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}-M_{\alpha\gamma}\nabla g_{\gamma},\qquad\alpha\neq\gamma.

(B.36)

Invoking
M_{\alpha\gamma}=-m_{\alpha\gamma}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\gamma}
, we find

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\gamma}\nabla g_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m_{\alpha\gamma}\phi_{\alpha}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\left(W\nabla\phi_{\gamma}-2W\phi_{\gamma}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\gamma\beta}\nabla\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{\gamma}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\gamma\beta}\nabla\Delta\phi_{\beta}+\phi_{\gamma}\nabla\lambda\right).

(B.37)

Since
\phi_{\gamma}=\penalty 0
and
\nabla\phi_{\gamma}=\penalty 0
, we obtain

M_{\alpha\gamma}\nabla g_{\gamma}\equiv 0
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}M_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta},\qquad\alpha\neq\gamma.

(B.38)

For
\alpha,\beta\neq\gamma
, the coefficients
M_{\alpha\beta}
are exactly the mobility coefficients of the reduced system, because the deleted phase contributes nothing when
\phi_{\gamma}=0
. Since also
\nabla g_{\beta}=\rho_{\beta}^{-1}\nabla(\widehat{\mu}_{\beta}+\lambda)
for
\beta\neq\gamma
, the phase equations for all active phases coincide with those of the reduced
(N-1)
-phase model.

Diffusive fluxes of the absent phase

We now consider the
\alpha
th phase equation. The diffusive flux is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}M_{\alpha\beta}\nabla g_{\beta},

(B.39)

where for
\beta\neq\alpha
we have
M_{\alpha\beta}=-m_{\gamma\beta}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}=0
and for the diagonal term, since

M_{\alpha\alpha}=\sum_{\delta\neq\alpha}m_{\alpha\delta}\phi_{\gamma}\phi_{\delta}
, we compute

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla g_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho_{\gamma}}\left(\sum_{\delta\neq\gamma}m_{\gamma\delta}\phi_{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle~~~\times\left(W\nabla\phi_{\gamma}-2W\phi_{\gamma}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\chi_{\gamma\beta}\nabla\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{\gamma}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\gamma\beta}\nabla\Delta\phi_{\beta}+\phi_{\gamma}\nabla\lambda\right).

(B.40)

Again
\phi_{\gamma}=\penalty 0
and
\nabla\phi_{\gamma}=\penalty 0
, hence

\displaystyle M_{\gamma\gamma}\nabla g_{\gamma}\equiv 0,\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\gamma}\equiv 0.

(B.41)

Thus the
\gamma
th phase equation decouples completely.

Capillary force

Finally, the capillary force in the momentum equation reduces as

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\nabla(\mu_{\beta}+\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\nabla(\mu_{\beta}+\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=1\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}^{N}\phi_{\beta}\nabla(\widehat{\mu}_{\beta}+\lambda),

(B.42)

because
\phi_{\gamma}=0
and
\mu_{\beta}=\widehat{\mu}_{\beta}
for all
\beta\neq\gamma
. This is precisely the capillary force of the reduced system.

The above conclusions show that after deleting the inactive phase
\gamma
, the restricted
N
-phase NSCH system coincides with the
(N-1)
-phase NSCH system.
∎

Corollary B.3

(Absent phase remains absent)
.

If a phase is absent, i.e.
\phi_{\gamma}(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
,
then it remains absent, i.e.
\phi_{\gamma}(\cdot,t)\equiv 0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Consider the
\gamma
th phase equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\gamma}+\operatorname{div}(\phi_{\gamma}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\gamma}^{-1}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{J}_{\gamma}=0,\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\gamma}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}M_{\gamma\beta}\nabla g_{\beta}.

(B.43)

By Theorem
B.2
, whenever
\phi_{\gamma}\equiv 0
the restriction of the system to the face
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
is well defined and the
\gamma
th diffusive flux vanishes identically:
\mathbf{J}_{\gamma}\equiv 0
. Hence, on
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
, the
\gamma
th phase equation reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\gamma}+\operatorname{div}(\phi_{\gamma}\mathbf{v})=0.

(B.44)

The identically zero function
\phi_{\gamma}\equiv 0
is a solution of this equation and attains the initial datum

\phi_{\gamma}(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
. Therefore, by uniqueness of classical solutions, it follows that

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}(\cdot,t)\equiv 0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(B.45)

∎

Proposition B.4

(Boyer–Lapuerta free energies are not mixture-aware)
.

For
N=3
, the Boyer–Lapuerta free energy is not mixture-aware.

Proof.

For
N=3
, the free energy of Boyer–Lapuerta is

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{B}}(\phi,\nabla\phi)=\frac{12}{\varepsilon}F_{\Lambda}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3})+\frac{3\varepsilon}{8}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\Sigma_{\alpha}|\nabla\phi_{\alpha}|^{2},

(B.46)

with

\displaystyle F_{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle~\sigma_{12}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{13}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2}+\sigma_{23}\phi_{2}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle~+\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\phi_{3}(\Sigma_{1}\phi_{1}+\Sigma_{2}\phi_{2}+\Sigma_{3}\phi_{3})+\Lambda\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2},

(B.47)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=\sigma_{12}+\sigma_{13}-\sigma_{23},\qquad\Sigma_{2}=\sigma_{12}+\sigma_{23}-\sigma_{13},\qquad\Sigma_{3}=\sigma_{13}+\sigma_{23}-\sigma_{12}.

(B.48)

If phases
1
and
2
are physically identical, then capillary-force reduction consistency would require that, after merging,

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\mu_{1}+\phi_{2}\nabla\mu_{2}=\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{1},\qquad\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}.

(B.49)

This does not hold. Indeed, on the face
\phi_{3}=0
the bulk term reduces to

\displaystyle F_{\Lambda}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},0)=\sigma_{12}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2},

(B.50)

so that already at the bulk level

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi_{1}}F_{\Lambda}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},0)=2\sigma_{12}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}^{2},\qquad\partial_{\phi_{2}}F_{\Lambda}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},0)=2\sigma_{12}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}.

(B.51)

Hence the capillary-force contribution

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\partial_{\phi_{1}}F_{\Lambda}+\phi_{2}\nabla\partial_{\phi_{2}}F_{\Lambda}=6\sigma_{12}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}\nabla(\phi_{1}\phi_{2})

(B.52)

still depends on the redistribution between
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, rather than only on the merged variable
\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
. The separate gradient terms
|\nabla\phi_{1}|^{2}
and
|\nabla\phi_{2}|^{2}
also depend on the redistribution between
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
.

∎

Proposition B.5

(Steinbach free energy is not mixture-aware)
.

The free energy structure

\displaystyle\Psi=

\displaystyle~\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})+\Psi_{\nabla}(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi}),

(B.53a)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\nabla}(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha<\beta}\omega_{\alpha\beta}\bigl|\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{\beta}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\bigr|^{2},

(B.53b)

for coefficients
\omega_{\alpha\beta}\geq 0
is not mixture-aware.

Proof.

Consider the ternary case
N=3
. We consider the phases
1
and
3
to represent the same phase and set

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{1}:=\phi_{1}+\phi_{3},\qquad\hat{\phi}_{2}:=\phi_{2}.

(B.54)

The capillary force should satisfy

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\mu_{1}^{\nabla}+\phi_{2}\nabla\mu_{2}^{\nabla}+\phi_{3}\nabla\mu_{3}^{\nabla}=\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{1}^{\nabla}+\hat{\phi}_{2}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{2}^{\nabla},

(B.55)

where
\mu_{\alpha}^{\nabla}
and
\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\nabla}
denote the chemical potentials induced by
\Psi_{\nabla}
alone. Restricting to the face
\phi_{2}=0
we have

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{3}=1,\qquad\hat{\phi}_{2}=\phi_{2}=0,\qquad\nabla\hat{\phi}_{1}=\nabla\hat{\phi}_{2}=0.

(B.56)

Hence the reduced gradient chemical potentials are evaluated at a constant state, so their gradients vanish
\nabla\hat{\mu}_{1}^{\nabla}=\nabla\hat{\mu}_{2}^{\nabla}=0
, i.e. the capillary force vanishes as well:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{1}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{1}^{\nabla}+\hat{\phi}_{2}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{2}^{\nabla}=0.

(B.57)

On the face
\phi_{2}=0
we have
\nabla\phi_{2}=0
, and therefore on this face we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{\nabla}=\omega_{13}\bigl|\phi_{1}\nabla\phi_{3}-\phi_{3}\nabla\phi_{1}\bigr|^{2}

(B.58)

The corresponding chemical potentials are

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{\nabla}=

\displaystyle~2\omega_{13}\bigl(\phi_{1}\nabla\phi_{3}-\phi_{3}\nabla\phi_{1}\bigr)\cdot\nabla\phi_{3}+2\omega_{13}\operatorname{div}\!\Big(\phi_{3}(\phi_{1}\nabla\phi_{3}-\phi_{3}\nabla\phi_{1})\Big),

(B.59a)

\displaystyle\mu_{3}^{\nabla}=

\displaystyle~-2\omega_{13}\bigl(\phi_{1}\nabla\phi_{3}-\phi_{3}\nabla\phi_{1}\bigr)\cdot\nabla\phi_{1}-2\omega_{13}\operatorname{div}\!\Big(\phi_{1}(\phi_{1}\nabla\phi_{3}-\phi_{3}\nabla\phi_{1})\Big).

(B.59b)

On the face
\phi_{2}=0
, one has
\phi_{3}=1-\phi_{1}
and hence

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{3}=-\nabla\phi_{1},\qquad\phi_{1}\nabla\phi_{3}-\phi_{3}\nabla\phi_{1}=-(\phi_{1}+\phi_{3})\nabla\phi_{1}=-\nabla\phi_{1}.

(B.60)

Substituting gives

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\nabla\mu_{1}^{\nabla}+\phi_{3}\nabla\mu_{3}^{\nabla}

\displaystyle=4\omega_{13}\nabla|\nabla\phi_{1}|^{2}+2\omega_{13}(\Delta\phi_{1})\nabla\phi_{1},

(B.61)

which does in general not vanish nor can it be absorbed into the pressure.
∎

Appendix C
Connection to the standard two-phase mobility tensor

In the binary case (
N=2
),
M\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
implies
M_{11}=-M_{12}
and
M_{22}=-M_{21}
, so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{1}=M_{12}\nabla(g_{1}-g_{2}),\qquad\mathbf{J}_{2}=-\mathbf{J}_{1}.

(C.1)

Introducing the order parameter
\varphi:=\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
(hence
\phi_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\varphi)
and
\phi_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\varphi)
),
subtracting the phase equations yields a single evolution equation
for
\varphi
of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\operatorname{div}(\varphi\mathbf{v})+\operatorname{div}\!\Big((\rho_{1}^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1})\mathbf{J}_{1}\Big)=0.

(C.2)

Substituting (
C.1
) gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\operatorname{div}(\varphi\mathbf{v})+\operatorname{div}\!\Big((\rho_{1}^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1})M\nabla(g_{1}-g_{2})\Big)=0,

(C.3)

where
M=M_{12}=M_{21}
. In order to compare with the
standard two-phase order-parameter formulation
36
;
9
, we introduce a rescaled mobility:

\displaystyle\breve{M}:=-(\rho_{1}^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1})^{2}M,

(C.4)

and rewrite the exchange driving force in terms of
\breve{\mu}+\omega\lambda
. A direct calculation yields

\displaystyle g_{1}-g_{2}=(\rho_{1}^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1})(\breve{\mu}+\omega\lambda),\qquad\breve{\mu}:=(\rho_{1}^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1})^{-1}\big(\rho_{1}^{-1}\mu_{1}-\rho_{2}^{-1}\mu_{2}\big),

(C.5)

where
\omega:=(\rho_{1}^{-1}-\rho_{2}^{-1})/(\rho_{1}^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1})
. Substituting (
C.5
) into (
C.3
) gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\operatorname{div}(\varphi\mathbf{v})-\operatorname{div}\!\Big(\breve{M}\nabla(\breve{\mu}+\omega\lambda)\Big)=0,

(C.6)

which matches the form of the phase-field equation used in
36
.

For the mobility choice (
4.14
) we find:

\displaystyle\breve{M}=\epsilon m_{0}(1-\varphi^{2}).

(C.7)

The constant
\epsilon m_{0}
therefore sets the diffusion time scale in the binary reference case. Furthermore, this form reproduces the standard degenerate factor
(1-\varphi^{2})
in the two-phase
order-parameter formulation.

For the Maxwell–Stefan choice (
4.16
), we obtain

\displaystyle M=M_{12}=-\epsilon m_{0}\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}=-\frac{\epsilon m_{0}}{4}\rho_{1}\rho_{2}(1-\varphi^{2}),

(C.8)

and hence

\displaystyle\breve{M}=\frac{\epsilon m_{0}}{4}\frac{(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})^{2}}{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}(1-\varphi^{2}).

(C.9)

Up to the overall constant prefactor, this also reproduces the standard degenerate factor
(1-\varphi^{2})
. In the case of identical densities we retrieve
\breve{M}=\epsilon m_{0}(1-\varphi^{2})
.
