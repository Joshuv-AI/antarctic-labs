Digital engagement and psychosocial health experiences among late adolescents and young adults in Arada sub-city, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: An exploratory mixed-methods study
Authors: Sabontu Akalu Wakie, Kebron W Aweke, Mikiyas G Teferi, Zayed H Tesfaye, Nebil Abdella Shukralla, Muluken Bekele Wondimagegnehu, Aman Worku Gizaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media has become an important environment for communication, identity formation, and social interaction among young people. However, its role in psychosocial well-being and digital safety remains under-researched in low-resource settings, including Ethiopia.ObjectiveThis study explored perceptions of social media use and described self-reported psychosocial and digital safety experiences among late adolescents and young adults in Arada Sub-City, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional exploratory mixed-methods study with a strong qualitative emphasis was conducted among 30 purposively selected late adolescents and young adults aged 17–21 years. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires and in-depth interviews. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, while qualitative data were analyzed thematically.ResultsProlonged social media engagement was common, with 26.7% of participants reporting sessions lasting more than 3 hours and 90.0% reporting use without adult supervision. Online harassment was reported by 36.7% of participants. More than half, 53.3%, reported perceived behavioral or social changes related to social media use, including online-initiated romantic relationships. Qualitative narratives described both perceived benefits, such as communication and learning, and risks, including excessive use, exposure to inappropriate content, harassment, and distressing online interactions.ConclusionAmong late adolescents and young adults in this exploratory urban Ethiopian sample, social media was perceived as both beneficial and potentially harmful. Findings highlight the need for context-sensitive digital literacy, online safety education, and youth-centered psychosocial support strategies.Keywords: late adolescents, young adults, social media, psychosocial health, online harassment, digital safety, Ethiopia

Introduction

Adolescence and young adulthood are critical developmental periods marked by rapid physical, psychological, cognitive, and social changes. During these stages, individuals experience important shifts in identity formation, emotional regulation, peer relationships, autonomy, and decision-making. 1 These transitions create both opportunities for growth and vulnerabilities to psychosocial stressors and risk-related behaviors. Experiences during adolescence and early adulthood have a lasting influence on adult health and behavior, making this life stage particularly important for public health and social research. 2

In recent years, the rapid expansion of digital technology has become increasingly important within the social environments in which young people communicate, learn, and form relationships. Social media platforms, characterized by their accessibility, interactivity, and user-generated content, have become central to adolescents’ daily lives.3,4 Globally, billions of individuals engage with social media, and young people constitute one of the most active user groups.5,6 In Ethiopia, increasing internet penetration and mobile phone use have contributed to a growing presence of adolescents on social networking sites, particularly platforms such as Facebook. 7 This expanding digital engagement is associated with new patterns of communication, information exchange, and social interaction among young people. 8

Alongside its benefits, growing evidence suggests that extensive exposure to social media may be associated with adverse behavioral and psychological outcomes among adolescents. 9 Researchers have documented associations between high levels of media use and increased risk of cyberbullying, online harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, risky sexual behaviors, substance use, and reduced academic engagement. 4 In this study, we used a psychosocial and digital safety framing rather than a clinical behavioral health framework. This framing recognizes that social media use may relate to young people’s emotional experiences, interpersonal relationships, exposure to online risks, and perceived behavioral or social changes. Recent literature has also expanded concerns beyond screen time alone, emphasizing the role of algorithmically mediated content exposure, personalized feeds, social comparison, misinformation, cyberbullying, and repeated exposure to harmful or distressing material.10,11 Young people’s heightened sensitivity to peer influence, coupled with developing self-regulatory capacity, may shape how they experience social media in relation to behavior and emotional well-being. 12 These concerns have contributed to increasing recognition of adolescent social media use as a potential public health issue. 9

Despite increasing global attention to social media use and youth well-being, empirical evidence from low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia, remains limited. Much of the available literature comes from high-income settings, where patterns of digital access, parental supervision, online safety systems, digital literacy, gender norms, and sociocultural context may differ substantially from those in Ethiopia.2,13 In particular, there is limited localized research examining how late adolescents and young adults in Ethiopia perceive social media use and how they describe experiences related to psychosocial well-being, online harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, excessive or difficult-to-control use, and online-initiated interpersonal or romantic relationships.

To address this gap, this study used an exploratory mixed-methods design combining descriptive quantitative findings with qualitative narratives from young people in Arada Sub-City, Addis Ababa. Therefore, this study aimed to explore perceptions of social media use and describe self-reported psychosocial and digital safety experiences among late adolescents and young adults aged 17–21 years. In this study, psychosocial and digital safety experiences were defined as self-reported experiences related to online harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, perceived excessive social media use, online-initiated interpersonal or romantic relationships, and perceived behavioral or social changes.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional exploratory study employing a mixed-methods design with a strong qualitative component was conducted to examine social media use, perceptions, and self-reported psychosocial and digital safety experiences among late adolescents and young adults. The study was carried out in Arada Sub-City, one of the ten administrative sub-cities of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Arada is an urban setting characterized by high population density and increasing access to mobile phones and internet services among young people. Data collection took place between May and August 2024. The reporting of this study conforms to the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guideline. 14 A completed GRAMMS checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of late adolescents and young adults aged 17 to 21 years who were residents of Arada Sub-City at the time of data collection. Eligibility criteria included current use of at least one social media platform and willingness to participate in both questionnaire based and interview based data collection. Individuals who were not active social media users or who were unable to provide informed consent or assent were excluded. The selected age group reflected high levels of digital engagement and developmental relevance to psychosocial and digital safety experiences.

Sample size and sampling strategy

A total of 30 adolescents and young adults participated in the study. Participants were selected using a purposive sampling strategy to ensure inclusion of individuals with direct and sustained experience of social media use. Recruitment was conducted within the community and educational settings in Arada Sub-City. The sample size was determined based on feasibility and the aim of achieving thematic saturation for the qualitative component rather than statistical representativeness. The sample was not intended to be statistically representative, and quantitative findings were used only to descriptively summarize patterns within this exploratory sample. All 30 participants completed the quantitative questionnaire and participated in the qualitative in-depth interviews.

Data collection instruments and procedures

Data were collected using two complementary instruments. Quantitative data were obtained through a self-administered semi-structured questionnaire designed to capture socio demographic characteristics, duration and intensity of social media use, adult supervision, exposure to online harassment, and perceived behavioral changes associated with social media. The questionnaire was developed in English and translated into the local language to enhance comprehension.

Qualitative data were collected through face-to-face in depth interviews using a semi-structured interview guide. The guide explored perceptions of social media, perceived benefits and disadvantages, experiences of online interactions, exposure to harassment, and perceived links with interpersonal and romantic relationships. Interviews were conducted in the participants’ preferred local language, audio recorded with consent, and lasted approximately 15 to 30 minutes. Data collection tools were pilot tested prior to use to ensure clarity and appropriateness.

Variables and operational definitions

Key variables included age, sex, educational status, duration of social media use measured in years, average session duration, presence of adult monitoring, experiences of online harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, and self-reported behavioral or social changes related to social media use.

In this study, late adolescents and young adults referred to participants aged 17–21 years. This wording was used because the study included participants aged 20 and 21 years, who fall outside the commonly used 10–19-year age range for adolescence.

Social media use was defined as participants’ self-reported use of online social networking platforms for communication, information sharing, entertainment, relationship formation, or social interaction.

Behavioral, psychosocial, and digital safety experiences were operationally defined as participants’ self-reported experiences related to social media use. These included behavioral experiences, such as perceived excessive or difficult-to-control use and changes in daily routines; psychological or psychosocial experiences, such as distress, anxiety, discomfort, or emotional responses to online interactions; and social experiences, such as online-initiated friendships, romantic relationships, face-to-face meetings with online contacts, online harassment, and exposure to inappropriate or sexual content. These outcomes were exploratory and self-reported, and the study did not assess clinical psychiatric diagnoses.

Data management and statistical analysis

Quantitative data from completed questionnaires were checked for completeness and entered into computer software for analysis. Data cleaning was performed prior to analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and patterns of social media use. Results were presented as frequencies and percentages. No inferential statistical analyses were conducted because of the small purposive sample and exploratory study design. Percentages were used only to describe the study sample and were not interpreted as representative prevalence estimates.

Qualitative data analysis

Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English for analysis. Thematic analysis was conducted manually using an inductive approach. Transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarization with the data. Initial codes were generated from recurring ideas, experiences, and meanings expressed by participants. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into broader categories, from which overarching themes were developed. Themes reflected perceptions of social media benefits, perceived risks, patterns of use, behavioral and social experiences, and online harassment.

The coding framework and emerging themes were reviewed and discussed by the research team to improve consistency and credibility. Formal inter-coder reliability statistics were not calculated because the qualitative component used exploratory thematic analysis rather than quantitative content analysis. However, analytic rigor was strengthened through repeated transcript review, comparison of codes and themes, consensus discussion, and the use of representative participant quotations to support the themes. Reflexivity was addressed through research team discussions about how investigators’ professional backgrounds, assumptions, and prior expectations could influence interpretation. Qualitative findings were used to contextualize and elaborate the descriptive quantitative findings at the interpretation stage.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was conducted at the interpretation stage using a joint display approach. Descriptive quantitative findings were compared with qualitative themes to identify areas of convergence, expansion, and contextual explanation. Qualitative narratives were used to elaborate how participants experienced the patterns observed in the descriptive quantitative data.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Arada Sub-City Health Bureau Institutional Review Board. Participation was voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants aged 18 years and above after explanation of the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and confidentiality measures. For participants under 18 years of age, written consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and assent was obtained from the adolescent prior to participation.

Given the sensitive nature of some interview topics, including online harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, image-based abuse, and online-initiated sexual or romantic encounters, additional participant protection procedures were applied. Participants were informed that they could decline to answer any question, pause the interview, or withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Interviews were conducted in private settings to protect confidentiality and reduce discomfort. Interviewers were instructed to monitor for signs of distress during data collection. If a participant became uncomfortable or distressed, the interview could be paused or discontinued. Participants who experienced distress or requested support were referred to available psychosocial support services through local health facilities or relevant community-based services in Arada Sub-City.

Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained by excluding personal identifiers from transcripts and datasets. Participant quotations were labeled using anonymized identifiers rather than names. Data were stored securely and used solely for research purposes.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 30 late adolescents and young adults aged 17 to 21 years participated in the study. All percentages are presented descriptively to summarize the study sample and should not be interpreted as population-level prevalence estimates. Males accounted for 66.7% of participants and females for 33.3%. The age distribution was relatively even across the range, with 16.7% aged 17 years, 16.7% aged 18 years, 20.0% aged 19 years, and 23.3% each aged 20 and 21 years. Regarding educational status, 43.3% were enrolled in high school, 33.3% were attending college, 16.7% were enrolled in technical and vocational education and training institutions, and 6.7% were in elementary school (Table 1). All participants reported regular access to the internet and ownership or consistent use of a mobile phone.

Patterns and intensity of social media use

All participants reported current use of at least one social media platform, with Facebook identified as the most commonly reported platform. A total of 60.0% reported using social media for more than three years, 30.0% for one to three years, and 10.0% for less than one year. Regarding session duration, 43.3% reported spending less than one hour per session, 30.0% reported one to three hours, and 26.7% reported more than three hours per session. The majority of participants, 90.0%, reported that their social media use occurred without adult supervision, while 10.0% reported some form of monitoring (Table 2).

Reported behavioral or social change was described by 1 of 3 participants who had used social media for less than one year, 4 of 9 participants who had used it for one to three years, and 11 of 18 participants who had used it for more than three years. Qualitative perceptions of social media use

Qualitative findings were organized into five themes: social connection and emotional expression; learning and information access; excessive use and difficulty regulating time online; exposure to inappropriate content and online harassment; and online-initiated relationships and offline encounters. These themes helped contextualize the descriptive quantitative findings on prolonged use, limited adult monitoring, harassment, and perceived behavioral or social changes.

Theme 1: Social connection, communication, and emotional expression

Participants described social media as a convenient way to maintain relationships, particularly with friends and relatives living far away. One participant stated:

“It helps me restore my contact with my lost friends, especially those who live abroad, because it is cheaper and easier than calling” (P01, male, 21 years).

Some participants also described improved confidence and easier emotional expression through text-based communication:

“I have developed self confidence because I am able to say what I feel through text messages” (P02, male, 21 years).

Theme 2: Learning, information access, and perceived benefits

Participants also reported that social media provided access to information, educational content, and opportunities for learning. One participant noted:

“Using Facebook helped me promote my reading skill, and I can say that is an advantage” (P03, male, 17 years).

Theme 3: Excessive use and difficulty regulating time online

Several participants described frequent or prolonged social media use, including difficulty controlling time spent online. One participant stated:

“I can say I am internet addicted. I use Facebook daily, and sometimes I chat the whole night” (P04, male, 20 years).

Another participant described emotional discomfort when not using social media:

“Social media is addictive. If I don’t use it, I feel anxious, and it takes my time and money” (P05, male, 21 years).

However, not all participants described social media use as disruptive. One participant reported limited perceived impact because of lower time investment:

“It doesn’t have any impact on me because I don’t invest much of my time” (P06, female, 20 years).

Theme 4: Exposure to inappropriate content and online harassment

Participants described exposure to offensive or inappropriate content, including sexual material:

“There are a lot of pornographic pictures and posts that are offending” (P07, male, 17 years).

Online harassment was also reported, including sexual harassment and unwanted contact. One female participant stated:

“Men approach you for sex in different ways, pretending to be friends” (P08, female, 17 years).

Other forms of harassment included identity-based insults:

“I have been harassed because I am black. She insulted me” (P09, male, 21 years).

Participants also described image-related abuse and its emotional consequences:

“He edited her photo to make it look naked and posted it. She was traumatized and couldn’t leave her house” (P10, female, 20 years).

Theme 5: Online-initiated relationships and offline encounters

Participants described social media as a space where friendships and romantic relationships were initiated. One participant stated:

“I met my girlfriend on Facebook, and she taught me what love is” (P11, male, 20 years).

Some online relationships progressed to offline encounters:

“I had sex once with a girl I met on Facebook after chatting for a year” (P12, male, 21 years).

Participants described these encounters as mixed, with some reporting positive relationships and others reporting discomfort, pressure, or uncertainty.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The integrated findings suggest convergence between the descriptive quantitative patterns and the qualitative narratives (Table 5). For example, the high proportion of participants reporting social media use without adult monitoring was reflected in narratives describing prolonged, private, and sometimes difficult-to-control use. Similarly, quantitative reports of online harassment were expanded by qualitative accounts describing sexual harassment, unwanted contact, identity-based insults, and image-related abuse. Reports of behavioral or social change were also contextualized by narratives describing online-initiated friendships, romantic relationships, and offline encounters. These integrated findings should be interpreted cautiously because the quantitative data are descriptive and the study was exploratory.

Discussion

This exploratory mixed-methods study provides preliminary insight into how late adolescents and young adults in one urban Ethiopian setting perceive and experience social media use in relation to psychosocial well-being and digital safety. Participants’ accounts suggest that social media may function as both a source of connection and learning and a potential context for psychosocial and digital safety risks. This duality is consistent with literature describing the complex role of social media during adolescence and young adulthood.15,16 By combining descriptive quantitative findings with in-depth qualitative narratives, the study captures not only patterns of use but also how adolescents interpret and experience their online engagement.

Our findings are best interpreted within a psychosocial and digital safety framework. Rather than measuring clinical behavioral health outcomes, this study explored how participants perceived social media in relation to emotional experiences, interpersonal relationships, online harassment, inappropriate content exposure, and perceived behavioral or social changes. This framing is also consistent with a digital determinants of health perspective, in which digital access, platform design, online safety, digital literacy, gender norms, and social context may shape young people’s exposure to both opportunities and risks in online environments.10,11

The descriptive findings showed frequent and prolonged social media engagement within this small purposive sample 17 Participants frequently emphasized the affordability and convenience of social media, particularly for maintaining connections with friends and relatives living abroad. This finding aligns with evidence from low-resource settings suggesting that digital platforms serve as important substitutes for more costly or inaccessible forms of communication. 18 Beyond communication, adolescents described perceived educational and psychosocial benefits, including access to information, improved confidence, and greater ease in expressing emotions. These experiences support previous research indicating that online environments have been described as spaces for identity exploration, social support, and emotional expression during adolescence. 8

At the same time, participants described challenges associated with largely unsupervised and intensive social media use. Nearly all adolescents reported the absence of adult monitoring, a pattern echoed in qualitative accounts of frequent exposure to inappropriate content and online harassment. Prior studies have demonstrated that limited parental mediation is associated with increased exposure to online risks, including cyberbullying and sexual solicitation.13,19 In this study, adolescents’ narratives illustrated how such exposure was experienced emotionally, with reports of distress, anxiety, and social withdrawal. These findings are consistent with concerns that unsafe digital environments may be associated with adverse psychosocial experiences among young people.

Placing these findings within a broader evidence landscape highlights persistent gaps in research on adolescent social media use in low- and middle-income countries. Much of the existing literature on social media and adolescent health originates from high-income settings, with comparatively little attention to low-resource contexts.8,18 Reviews of digital health interventions in LMICs have identified only a limited number of studies addressing adolescent health promotion through social media, particularly in relation to sexual and reproductive health and mental well-being.2,20 While studies from Asia and Latin America suggest associations between heavy social media use and adverse mental health or risk behaviors, these findings are rarely framed within a health equity or social determinants perspective. By situating adolescents’ experiences within an urban Ethiopian setting, this study contributes context-specific evidence on how digital access, gender dynamics, and economic factors intersect to shape online behavior and perceived health outcomes.4,18

Problematic patterns of social media use also emerged in adolescents’ accounts. Several participants described difficulty regulating time spent online, sometimes characterizing their use as excessive or addictive. Over one-quarter of participants reported spending more than three hours per session, and qualitative narratives described perceived difficulties with sleep, concentration, and daily functioning in the context of prolonged use. These experiences are consistent with prior research linking intensive social media engagement to impaired self-regulation, emotional dependence, and reduced academic focus during adolescence.9,21 Although clinical outcomes were not assessed in this study, adolescents’ self-reported perceptions point to the relevance of Problematic Interactive Media Use as a behavioral health concern within this population.

Exposure to online harassment emerged as a particularly salient adverse experience, affecting more than one-third of participants. Sexual harassment was reported more frequently by female participants in this sample; however, this descriptive pattern should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size and absence of inferential testing. 22 Experiences of identity-based harassment and image manipulation described by participants highlight the potential for social media to reproduce and amplify offline power imbalances. Such experiences have been associated with anxiety, diminished self-esteem, and social isolation in adolescents, underscoring the need to consider online safety as an integral component of adolescent psychosocial health. 19

Participants described social media as a context in which some interpersonal and romantic relationships were initiated and navigated. More than half of participants reported behavioral or social changes they perceived as related to online interactions, including online-initiated romantic relationships. While some adolescents described positive outcomes, others reported discomfort, deception, or pressure during offline encounters, echoing concerns raised in earlier research about emotional and sexual risks associated with digitally mediated relationships.23,24 These findings suggest that, for some participants, relationship formation occurred within hybrid online–offline spaces, reinforcing the importance of relationship and sexual education that explicitly addresses digital contexts.

From a public health perspective, these exploratory findings can be interpreted within a digital determinants of health framework, while recognizing that the study was not designed to establish causal pathways or population-level estimates. The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health emphasizes that access to digital technologies, digital literacy, and patterns of use influence health outcomes and equity. 21 In low-resource settings, unequal access to devices and internet connectivity may exclude some adolescents, particularly girls, from online health information and services, while those who are connected may face heightened exposure to online risks.2,20 Framing social media use within this broader digital determinants perspective underscores the importance of integrating digital literacy, online safety, and gender-sensitive approaches into adolescent health strategies.

Overall, the findings suggest that responses to adolescent social media use should focus on promoting informed, safe, and balanced engagement rather than restrictive or punitive approaches. Digital literacy interventions involving adolescents, parents, and educators have shown promise in reducing online risk exposure and improving coping strategies. 25 Schools and community-based programs may provide feasible entry points for such interventions in urban Ethiopian settings. By grounding recommendations in adolescents’ lived experiences, this study highlights the need for context-sensitive strategies that acknowledge both the benefits and risks of social media use while supporting adolescents’ psychosocial well-being.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its strong qualitative component, which provided contextual insight into how late adolescents and young adults perceived and experienced social media use beyond descriptive counts alone. The inclusion of participant narratives enhanced the depth and credibility of the findings by illustrating how online engagement was experienced in participants’ own words.

This study also has several limitations. First, the small purposive sample limits generalizability, and the quantitative findings should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory rather than representative prevalence estimates. Second, data were self-reported, which may have introduced recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive experiences such as harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, and online-initiated relationships. Third, the cross-sectional exploratory design precludes causal inference. Therefore, reported relationships between social media use and psychosocial or behavioral experiences should be interpreted as perceived associations rather than causal effects. Larger studies using representative sampling and longitudinal designs are needed to further examine these relationships.

Conclusion

This exploratory mixed-methods study suggests that social media is embedded in the daily lives of late adolescents and young adults in Arada Sub-City, Addis Ababa, and may offer both perceived benefits and potential risks. Participants described social media as useful for communication, social connection, learning, and self-expression, while also reporting concerns related to prolonged use, limited adult monitoring, online harassment, inappropriate content exposure, and online-initiated romantic interactions. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the study used a small purposive sample and was not designed to produce representative prevalence estimates or establish causal relationships. Nevertheless, the findings highlight the need for context-sensitive digital literacy education, online safety promotion, and youth-centered psychosocial support strategies. Future studies using larger and more representative samples are needed to examine associations between social media use, digital safety, and psychosocial well-being among young people in Ethiopia and similar low-resource settings.
