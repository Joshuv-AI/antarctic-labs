Sample Complexity of Stochastic Optimization with Integer Variables
Hongyu Cheng, Yinghao Zheng, Marco Molinaro, Amitabh Basu

Abstract
We establish sample complexity results for stochastic optimization over the integers, especially with a view to understand the complexity with respect to the corresponding continuous optimization problem. We show that integer optimization can sometimes require strictly more samples and sometimes strictly smaller number of samples, depending on the structure of the objective and constraints. 1. For Lipschitz objectives over subsets of the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} ball, the statistical complexity of general stochastic mixed-integer, nonlinear, nonconvex optimization is exactly the same as stochastic linear optimization with just bound constraints. 2. For Lipschitz objectives over subsets of the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} ball, we show that integer optimization can require strictly smaller sample size compared to the continuous setting in a certain regime. To get to this result, we also establish tight sample complexity results for nonconvex continuous stochastic optimization which, to the best of our knowledge, do not appear in prior work. 3. For strongly convex, smooth objectives, integer optimization has high statistical complexity compared to the continuous setting. In particular, we show that integer optimization requires Ω ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ 2 ) \Omega(1/\epsilon^{2}) samples to report an ϵ \epsilon -approximate solution, compared to the well-known O ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) O(1/\epsilon) sample complexity from the continuous optimization literature.

Introduction

The fundamental question in statistical machine learning is a stochastic optimization problem:

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in X}F_{D}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim D}[f(\mathbf{x};z)]

(1)

where
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the constraints the decision variable
\mathbf{x}
must satisfy,
f:X\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is a fixed function,
\mathcal{Z}
is a measurable space and
D
is a probability distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
. The goal is to solve this problem (to within some desired error tolerance) without having explicit knowledge of the distribution
D
and only having access to i.i.d samples from
D
. We must “learn” enough about the objective function
F_{D}
, which depends on the distribution
D
, from the sample, to be able to report a good solution with high confidence, i.e., high probability over the random sample
13
. In this paper, we investigate the central notion of sample complexity, i.e., the smallest number of i.i.d samples from
D
that are needed to solve the problem to a given level of accuracy with high confidence. This obviously depends on the nature of the function
f
(e.g., assuming
f(\cdot\,;z)
is convex for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
), the constraint set
X
, as well as the family of distributions
D
one wants to target. The focus is typically on the structure of
f
and
X
, with minimal assumptions on
D
.

While the sample complexity question has been studied extensively in the continuous convex optimization setting (we summarize the state-of-the-art results below), the setting with integer decision variables seems to have received much less attention, to the best of our knowledge. This paper is an attempt to close this gap and in the process understand the relative difficulty of continuous versus discrete stochastic optimization in terms of quantitative sample complexity bounds. It has been known for a while that the algorithmic and information complexity of convex optimization with integer variables is much higher (exponential in the dimension) than continuous convex optimization
12
;
5
;
2
. To the best of our knowledge, the statistical aspect has not been rigorously studied: Does stochastic optimization with integer variables need a much larger number of samples compared to its continuous counterpart? This paper makes progress on this statistical question.

1.1
Our contributions and relation to prior work

We investigate three standard notions of sample complexity within stochastic optimization: for uniform convergence, for empirical risk minimization, and sample complexity for any decision rule/algorithm.
Uniform convergence (UC)
analysis aims to provide bounds on the minimum number of samples needed so that
|F_{S}(\mathbf{x})-F_{D}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\epsilon
for all
\mathbf{x}\in X
, for a given tolerance
\epsilon>0
with probability
1-\delta
for some confidence parameter
\delta>0
, where

F_{S}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\mathbf{x};z_{i}),

(2)

is the so-called
empirical risk minimization (ERM)
or
sample average approximation (SAA)
objective based on the i.i.d. samples
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\sim D
. If uniform convergence holds, then minimizing the ERM objective (
2
) to within
\epsilon/2
error will guarantee a solution to (
1
) to within
\epsilon
error with probability
1-\delta
. While uniform convergence is thus sufficient for ERM to work, it is not necessary in general; for example, one could use a smaller number of samples to approximate the objective
F_{D}
in a small neighborhood around the minimizer (so that ERM can succeed), as opposed to over all of the domain
X
. This was first rigorously established in
14
for stochastic convex optimization and sharpened in
7
and
3
(see the discussion in Appendix
A
). Thus, an important question within stochastic optimization is to decide whether ERM has strictly smaller sample complexity compared to UC. Finally, minimizing the ERM objective (
2
) is just one way to use the data
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
. In general, one could consider any function
A_{m}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})
that returns a solution
\hat{x}\in X
for the problem (
1
). Potentially, one could get away with an even smaller number of samples compared to ERM because one is allowed to do other kinds of computations on the data. In fact, this can happen for stochastic convex optimization as a result of lower bounds on ERM in
7
and results on stochastic gradient descent and online optimization as discussed in
14
(see Appendix
A
). In Table
1
, we put the sample complexity for a general algorithm/solution estimator under the column “Any data-driven algorithm”. As the above discussion shows, the sample complexity of UC is always at least as large as the sample complexity of ERM which is at least as large as the sample complexity when allowing for any solution estimator.

Characterization of Lipschitz stochastic optimization over
\ell_{\infty}
.

We first consider stochastic optimization where each loss function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}\,;z)
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
over the box of radius
R
, namely
B^{(\infty)}_{R}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq R\}
. Here we give a
complete characterization of the sample complexity
across the different modes of convergence: uniform convergence, ERM, and any data-driven algorithm.We show that the sample complexity is the same in all these settings, and equal to
\Theta\big(\tfrac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}(d+\log(\tfrac{1}{\delta}))\big)
. This is summarized in the first row of Table
1
, and formally stated in Section
3
. An important message of this set of results is that the most complicated mixed-integer nonlinear, nonconvex problems have the same sample complexity as simple stochastic linear programming problems with just bound constraints.

To establish this, we prove an upper bound on uniform convergence for Lipschitz functions (possibly nonconvex) over the whole of
B^{(\infty)}_{R}
, which implies the same upper bound for all the other settings; note it includes the particularly interesting case of
mixed-integer convex
optimization, where
X
is the set of mixed-integer points in some convex subset of
B^{(\infty)}_{R}
, i.e.,
X=C\cap(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-n})
for some convex set
C
and
n\leq d
. The characterization is then obtained by a matching lower bound for any learning algorithm for linear loss functions over the whole box
X=B^{(\infty)}_{R}
, i.e., it is achieved by stochastic linear programming with simple variable bound constraints; adding integrality does not change anything, since the optimal solutions are attained at the vertices of the box, and so the lower bound holds even for
X=B^{(\infty)}_{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Equivalences and separations under
\ell_{2}
-norm.

Next, we show how the geometry of the bounding region (scaled box, in the previous part) can affect the sample complexity of the different problems. More concretely, we now consider loss functions
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}\,;z)
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{2}
over the
Euclidean ball
of radius
R
, namely
B^{(2)}_{R}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq R\}
. As discussed above, when the loss functions are
convex
and the feasible region is the continuous set
X=B^{(2)}_{R}
, existing results imply a separation between the sample complexity of uniform convergence, ERM, and a general algorithm
14
;
7
;
3
; see Table
2
in Appendix
A
for the precise bounds.

This leads the question of what happens when one allows nonconvexity in the objective and integrality constraints: we saw above that they do not make any difference over the
\ell_{\infty}
ball. First, we show that if we do not have integrality constraints, but allow nonconvex Lipschitz objectives over the
\ell_{2}
ball, the sample complexity is the same as the
\ell_{\infty}
case, namely
\Theta\!\big(\tfrac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}(d+\log(\tfrac{1}{\delta}))\big)
for UC, ERM, and general algorithms. This is summarized in the second row of Table
1
and stated in
Theorem

4.1
. In particular, the separation between anchored UC, ERM and general proper learning algorithms for the convex case does not translate to the nonconvex setting, and there is no gain to be had using anything other than ERM. This is significant because many important problems in modern machine learning and data science are nonconvex stochastic optimization problems, e.g., neural network based approaches.

Allowing integrality constraints on top of nonconvexity in the
\ell_{2}
ball makes the story even more intriguing. Consider minimizing over the integer points in the
\ell_{2}
ball, i.e.,
X=B_{R}^{(2)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We show that the sample complexity is now characterized by a term
H_{2}(d,R)
arising out of the geometry of the integer points in the
\ell_{2}
ball, where
H_{2}(d,R)=0
for
0<R<1
and
H_{2}(d,R)=\min\{d,\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor\}\log(ed/\min\{d,\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor\})
for
R\geq 1
. More precisely, the sample complexity is now
\Theta\big(\tfrac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}(H_{2}(d,R)+\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))\big)
, for all modes of convergence: UC, ERM, any algorithm. This is summarized in the third row of Table
1
. Comparing with the bound for the continuous feasible set
X=B^{(2)}_{R}
we see that this is actually
strictly smaller than the continuous bound
when
R^{2}<d
. That is, perhaps surprisingly, this stochastic integer-constrained problem can be statistically easier than the corresponding continuous version.
Intuitively, this happens because the convex hull of the integer points in the
\ell_{2}
ball has a very different geometry compared to the ball when the radius is small (less than
\sqrt{d}
); which does not happen in the
\ell_{\infty}
case.

Strongly convex, smooth objectives.

We also investigate stochastic optimization of smooth and strongly convex functions. This is an important setting where, in continuous convex optimization, one can obtain sample-complexity bounds with faster rates as a function of the error
\epsilon

(
15
, Chapter 8)
. As in prior works (see
8
;
9
and the references therein), we consider a compact feasible set
X
and further assume the loss functions to
1
-Lipschitz over
X
.

Our first main result in this setting is that stochastic integer optimization has a
much higher sample complexity
compared to continuous optimization. More specifically, we show that any proper learning algorithm requires at least
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
samples for optimizing over the integer points in a box, as a function of
\epsilon
. In comparison, it is known that the sample-complexity of optimizing over a continuous box is
O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)

11
;
8
(see also
Theorem

5.6
in this paper). Thus, unlike in the previous
\ell_{2}
case, imposing integrality constraints makes the problem harder.

Moreover, we also consider stochastic smooth/strongly convex optimization over all integer points
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For that, we impose an assumption that the distribution over the loss functions satisfies a sub-Gaussian increment condition. This natural condition generalizes the previous 1-Lipschitz assumption to unbounded domains (
Section

5
), and we show that some distributional assumption is required for finite sample-complexity in this setting (
Section

5
). Practically relevant problems like regularized logistic regression satisfy this condition (
Section

5
) and cannot be directly handled with the older assumptions from the literature. Here we prove another separation phenomenon: while uniform convergence is not possible even with this sub-Gaussian assumption
Theorem

5.4
,
ERM solves the unbounded integer problem with the optimal number of samples (
Theorem

5.5
). Even over bounded domains, ERM has strictly better sample complexity than uniform convergence in the regime where the condition number of the loss function is smaller than
\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}
. This is the only situation out of all those we consider in which we see a separation between uniform convergence and ERM. This is summarized in the last row of Table
1
.

Related work.

Most work on sample complexity of stochastic optimization has focused on the convex setting, i.e.,
f(\cdot\,;z)
is convex for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
X
is a closed, convex set. As discussed above, the results in
14
;
7
;
3
clear up the picture for Lipschitz convex functions; see Appendix
A
for a detailed discussion.
(
1
)
provides information-theoretic lower bounds for stochastic convex optimization and identifies the roles of dimension, norm geometry, strong convexity, and sparsity. Work in the strongly convex setting, with and without smoothness assumptions, is summarized in
8
;
9
. See also
(
10
, Chapter 4)
for a textbook exposition of stochastic convex optimization.

In the nonconvex setting, research effort has concentrated on methods to find stationary points or local minima. State-of-the-art results are summarized in
(
10
, Chapter 6)
. Our focus in this paper is different from most work in the nonconvex stochastic optimization literature, and closer to work cited above in the convex setting, since we focus on global minimizers. Moreover, we consider the setting where the samples reveal complete information about
f(\cdot\,;z_{i})
; in contrast, most work in the nonconvex regime assumes only pointwise (subgradient and function value) access to the stochastic loss function.

There is also an enormous literature on empirical optimization in stochastic programming and stochastic discrete optimization, often through asymptotic, gap-dependent, or model-specific guarantees. The textbook
15
is a good summary of these developments. We do not discuss these lines of work in any further detail as they are not directly relevant to our emphasis on finite-sample bounds.

Preliminaries

In this section we define some basic notions from stochastic convex optimization and sample complexity. We assume that
f:X\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
from (
1
) is such that

\mathbf{x}\in X
. For any probability distribution
D

\mathcal{Z}

\mathbb{E}_{z\sim D}[f(\mathbf{x};z)]\in\mathbb{R}

F_{D}

F_{S}

2

We write

for the selected empirical and population minimizers. We assume throughout that the minimizers of both
F_{D}
and
F_{S}
exist (for all samples
S
). In this paper, this is without loss of generality since
f(\cdot\,;z)
is continuous on its domain for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
; in
Sections

3
and
4

Section

5

Lipschitz Stochastic Optimization over a Subset of a Box

In this section we show that when the domain is the
\ell_{\infty}
-ball, there is no separation between convex vs nonconvex and continuous vs discrete Lipschitz stochastic optimization.

More precisely, we focus on the domain being the
\ell_{\infty}
-ball
B_{R}^{(\infty)}
of radius
R
.
Throughout this section, we equip
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\ell_{\infty}
norm, and consider a loss map
f:B_{R}^{(\infty)}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
such that each function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x};z)
is
1
-Lipschitz (not necessarily convex) on
B_{R}^{(\infty)}
with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
, for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
f:B_{R}^{(\infty)}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
f(\cdot\,;z)
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for every family
\mathfrak{D}
of distributions, every
\epsilon>0
, and every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

m_{B_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\mathrm{a-UC}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D})\leq\left\lceil C\frac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil.

Moreover, there exist absolute constants
c,c_{0}>0
such that, for every
d,R\geq 1
, there is a family
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R}
of distributions over
1
-Lipschitz linear functions such that, for every
0<\epsilon\leq c_{0}R
and every
0<\delta\leq 1/4
,

m_{B_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R})\geq c\frac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

The formal proof of Theorem
3.1
is given in
Appendix

C
, but we sketch the main ideas in the proof here.

The upper bound follows from a bracketing and chaining argument for the anchored loss class. After anchoring at the origin, Lipschitzness gives
|f(\mathbf{x};z)-f(\mathbf{0};z)|\leq R
on
B_{R}^{(\infty)}
, and the
\ell_{\infty}
covering entropy of the box is of order
d
. This gives anchored UC with the stated sample size, and hence the same upper bound for every feasible subset of the box.

The lower bound already holds for stochastic linear optimization with only bound constraints. The hard instances are indexed by a hidden sign vector
\mathbf{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, with population objective
F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})=-(\rho/d)\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\rangle
. Thus the unknown distribution determines which vertex
R\mathbf{b}
of the box is exposed by the linear objective. An
\epsilon
-optimal output must have enough average alignment with this exposed vertex. Each sample is a coordinate-wise biased coin experiment: it first chooses a coordinate
j\in[d]
uniformly at random, and then returns a sign with mean
\rho b_{j}
. Therefore, unless
m
is of order
d/\rho^{2}
, no proper algorithm can learn the objective direction well enough to output a near-optimal point. Taking
\rho
of order
\epsilon/R
gives the dimension term, and the one-coordinate coin flip gives the
\log(1/\delta)
confidence term. Unlike the ERM lower bounds in
7
, this construction does not rely on a bad empirical minimizer. The ambiguity is in the population linear objective itself, and hence the lower bound applies to every proper algorithm.

We remark that since the lower bound uses only linear functions, it actually also holds when
X=B_{R}^{(\infty)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{-\lfloor R\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor R\rfloor\}^{d}
, with
R
replaced by
\lfloor R\rfloor
. Together with the upper bound from
Theorem

3.1
, this shows that continuous and integral feasible regions, as well as convex and nonconvex losses, have the same sample complexity in this
\ell_{\infty}
setting.

Lipschitz Stochastic Optimization over a Subset of a Euclidean Ball

In the previous section we observe that when the domain is the
\ell_{\infty}
-ball, different variants of Lipschitz stochastic optimization collapsed to the easiest setting: linear optimization with variable constraints. We now show that when the domain is the
\ell_{2}
-ball, new phenomena occur and there is a strict separation between convex vs nonconvex functions and continuous vs integral feasible sets.

More precisely, we now consider the domain being the Euclidean ball
B_{R}^{(2)}
of radius
R
,
equip
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Euclidean norm, and consider a loss map
f:B_{R}^{(2)}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
such that each function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x};z)
is
1
-Lipschitz (not necessarily convex) on
B_{R}^{(2)}
with respect to
\ell_{2}
, for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

We start by obtaining tight sample complexity bounds for the continuous feasible set, nonconvex function setting. The proofs for this section are given in
Appendix

D
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
f:B_{R}^{(2)}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
f(\cdot\,;z)
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{2}
for every

. Then there exists an absolute constant

such that for every family
\mathfrak{D}
of distributions, every
\epsilon>0
, and every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

m_{B_{R}^{(2)}}^{\mathrm{a-UC}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D})\leq\left\lceil C\frac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil.

Moreover, there exist absolute constants
c,c_{0}>0
such that, for every
d\geq 1
and
R>0
, there is a family
\mathfrak{D}
of probability distributions over functions that are
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{2}
such that for every
0<\epsilon\leq c_{0}R
and every
0<\delta\leq 1/4
,

m_{B_{R}^{(2)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D})\geq c\frac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

This is in contrast to the convex Lipschitz setting discussed in the Introduction, where the dimension-dependent term in the ERM sample complexity is only of order
Rd/\epsilon
(see Table
2
in Appendix
A
). The upper bound follows from the same bracketing and chaining argument used in
Theorem

3.1
. The lower bound uses the additional freedom provided by nonconvexity. We take an exponential-size packing
\mathcal{W}\subseteq B_{R}^{(2)}
with pairwise distances of order
R
, and place a small
1
-Lipschitz tent around each point of
\mathcal{W}
, with disjoint supports. A distribution then hides one center
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}
by biasing the activation probability of the tent at
\mathbf{u}
. The population objective has its unique minimizer at this hidden center, and every
\epsilon
-optimal proper output must fall in the corresponding tent. Thus optimization reduces to identifying the hidden element of
\mathcal{W}
from biased Bernoulli observations. Since
\log|\mathcal{W}|=\Theta(d)
and the bias is of order
\epsilon/R
, Fano’s inequality and a two-point testing argument give the two terms in the lower bound.

We now consider integral feasible sets, that is, subsets of

X_{R}^{(2)}:=B_{R}^{(2)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

We show that stochastic optimization over these smaller feasible sets can have a provably smaller sample complexity. The fact that this phenomenon happens in the
\ell_{2}
domain, and not on the
\ell_{\infty}
domain from the previous section is due to the mismatch between the geometry of the integer lattice and that of the standard Euclidean ball. More concretely, integer points in a Euclidean ball have sparse support: at most
\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor
coordinates can be nonzero. When
R^{2}\ll d
, this sparsity makes the combinatorial scale much smaller than that of the full integer cube.

To capture this, define
H_{2}(d,R)
by
H_{2}(d,R)=0
for
0<R<1
, and

H_{2}(d,R):=\min\{d,\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor\}\log\left(ed/{\min\{d,\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor\}}\right)

for
R\geq 1
. When
1\leq\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor<d
, this becomes
H_{2}(d,R)=\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor\log(ed/\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor)
and corresponds to (as shown in
Appendix

D
) the log number of integer points in the ball
B^{(2)}_{R}
, while when
\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor\geq d
, we have
H_{2}(d,R)=d
, which corresponds to the usual
d
-dimensional Euclidean covering scale. We show that this term
H_{2}(d,R)
precisely characterizes the sample complexity of optimization over subsets of the integer set
X^{(2)}_{R}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose the functions
f(\cdot\,;z)
are 1-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{2}
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for every family
\mathfrak{D}
of distributions, every
\epsilon>0
, and every
0<\delta<1/2
,

m_{X_{R}^{(2)}}^{\mathrm{a-UC}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D})\leq\left\lceil C\frac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(H_{2}(d,R)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil.

Moreover, there exist absolute constants
c,c_{0}>0
such that, for every
d\geq 1
and
R\geq 1
, there is a family of distributions
\mathfrak{D}
on 1-Lipschitz with respect to
\ell_{2}
such that for every
0<\epsilon\leq c_{0}R
and every
0<\delta\leq 1/4
,

m_{X_{R}^{(2)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D})\geq c\frac{R^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(H_{2}(d,R)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Thus, over the Euclidean integer ball, anchored UC, ERM, and the best algorithm all have the same sample complexity, up to constants. The rate is governed by
H_{2}(d,R)
that encodes the difference between geometry of the
\ell_{2}
ball and the integer points in it, and is strictly smaller than the continuous nonconvex rate when
\lfloor R^{2}\rfloor<d
. We next show that this statistical advantage of integrality disappears, and in fact reverses, under smoothness and strong convexity.

5
Stochastic Optimization of Strongly Smooth/Convex Functions

We next consider stochastic optimization of smooth, strongly convex losses, both in unbounded domains and also on a box domain. The main result is that in this setting there is a significant qualitative separation in the sample complexity in the scenarios considered: First, for unbounded integer constrained problems, anchored uniform convergence has infinite sample complexity (
Theorem

5.4
) while ERM has finite sample complexity (
Theorem

5.5
); this happens because ERM witnesses a
localization
phenomenon, where it suffices to control the stochastic process around the optimum. Moreover, unlike in the previous section, we prove that integer constrained optimization is provably harder than continuous optimization. More precisely, while in this integer constrained setting ERM has sample complexity of order
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}
, we show that in the continuous case it has “accelerated” sample complexity of order
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
(
Theorem

5.6
).
Actually, as we show in
Section

5
, some assumption on the function distribution is required for any learning with finitely many samples in the unbound domain case, and thus we work throughout this section under a natural sub-gaussian increment assumption discussed in the sequel.

More precisely, in this section the loss map is defined on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while the parameter
R\in[1,\infty]
specifies the feasible box
B_{R}^{(\infty)}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq R\}
, with the convention that
B_{\infty}^{(\infty)}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Fix
0<\mu\leq L
and set
\kappa:=L/\mu
. Recall that a function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
-strongly convex
and
L
-smooth
if
h-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
and
\frac{L}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}-h
are convex. Equivalently, whenever
h
is differentiable,

\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\leq h(\mathbf{y})-h(\mathbf{x})-\langle\nabla h(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3)

We consider loss maps
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\cdot\,;z)
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Since the defining convexity conditions are preserved under averaging, the population and empirical objectives
F_{D}
and
F_{S}
satisfy the same strong-convexity and smoothness bounds whenever they are finite.

Sub-Gaussian Increment Condition.

We say that a distribution over
\mathcal{Z}
satisfies
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian increment condition over a domain
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
if for every
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim D}\exp\left(\lambda\left[f(\mathbf{x};z)-f(\mathbf{y};z)-F_{D}(\mathbf{x})+F_{D}(\mathbf{y})\right]\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right).

(4)

Related assumptions on centered differences of sample losses appear, for example, in
(
4
, Theorem 5)
.
We use
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma}
to denote the class of all distributions satisfying this sub-Gaussian increment condition.
In sample complexity statements over a feasible set
X
, we interpret this class relative to the same set: (
4
) is imposed only for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X
, and no increment control outside
X
is required. Note that the increment condition controls differences of losses rather than absolute offsets, and so remains meaningful on an unbounded domain.

Remark 5.1

(Necessity of increment condition)
.

Some centered tail control is necessary in the unbounded setting. Without it, smoothness and strong convexity alone do not allow proper learning with finite sample complexity, already for one-dimensional quadratic losses
f(x;z)=\frac{\mu}{2}(x-z)^{2}
. To see this, fix
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, number of samples
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and an arbitrary proper algorithm
A_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
; we show that with probability greater than
\delta
this algorithm has excess loss more than
\epsilon
for some distribution. Choose
p\in(\delta^{1/m},1)
, and construct the distribution
D
satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim D}[Z=0]=p
and
\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim D}[Z=(A_{m}(\mathbf{0})+\lceil 2\sqrt{\epsilon/\mu}\rceil)/(1-p)]=1-p
. The population loss
F_{D}
is minimized at
\mathbb{E}Z=A_{m}(\mathbf{0})+\left\lceil 2\sqrt{\epsilon/\mu}\right\rceil
, but with probability
p^{m}>\delta
all samples are 0, so the algorithm outputs
A_{m}(\mathbf{0})
and incurs an excess loss of at least
\frac{\mu}{2}(2\sqrt{\epsilon/\mu})^{2}=2\epsilon>\epsilon
.

Remark 5.2

(Lipschitzness implies the increment condition)
.

Suppose that
f(\cdot\,;z)
is
L
-Lipschitz on
X
for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, and let
D
be any distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
. Fix
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X
and put
Y:=f(\mathbf{x};Z)-f(\mathbf{y};Z)
for
Z\sim D
. Then
|Y|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
, and Hoeffding’s lemma
(
13
, Lemma B.7)
gives

\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda(Y-\mathbb{E}Y))\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}L^{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus
D
satisfies the sub-Gaussian increment condition over
X
with
\sigma=L
.

Remark 5.3

(Regularized Logistic Loss)
.

Section

5
gives one sufficient condition for the increment assumption, but the condition can hold even when the sample losses are not Lipschitz. We note that this happens for regularized logistic loss when the feature vectors are bounded. This setting corresponds to the loss map

f(\mathbf{x};(\mathbf{a},b))=\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\log(1+\exp(-b\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle)),

where
b\in\{\pm 1\}
and
\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\leq M
for some
M
. Since
u\mapsto\log(1+\exp(-u))
is
1
-Lipschitz and
1/4
-smooth,
f(\cdot\,;(\mathbf{a},b))
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
(\mu+M^{2}/4)
-smooth. Also, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|\log(1+\exp(-b\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle))-\log(1+\exp(-b\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}\rangle))|\leq M\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

The quadratic term is deterministic and cancels after centering. Hoeffding’s Lemma therefore gives the increment condition with
\sigma=M
. The same argument applies if the logistic term is replaced by
\ell(-b\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
, where
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex margin loss that is
\rho
-Lipschitz and
\beta
-smooth. In that case one may take
L=\mu+\beta M^{2}
and
\sigma=\rho M
.

Sample complexity of a-UC vs ERM and integer vs continuous.

We now turn to our results about sample complexity in this setting. Let
X_{R}^{(\infty)}:=B_{R}^{(\infty)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
denote the set of integer points in the box of radius
R
. The proofs for this section are given in
Appendix

E
.

Our first theorem proves a tight bound on the anchored uniform convergence for the integer domain
X^{(\infty)}_{R}
. Crucially, the lower bound shows that this complexity goes to infinity as the box size increases.

Theorem 5.4
.

Suppose the functions
f(\cdot\,;z)
are
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that, for every
1\leq R<\infty
, every
\epsilon>0
, and every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

m_{X_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\mathrm{a-UC}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\leq\left\lceil C\frac{\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil.

Moreover, there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every
d\geq 1
and every
1\leq R<\infty
, there is such a loss map
f(\cdot\,;\,\cdot)
for which, for every
0<\delta\leq 1/4
and every
\epsilon>0
,

m_{X_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\mathrm{a-UC}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\geq c\frac{\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

In contrast, we now show that ERM is qualitatively stronger in this setting: the sample complexity remains finite even when considering optimization over all the integers
X^{(\infty)}_{\infty}:=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, i.e., when the box radius
R=\infty
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Suppose the functions
f(\cdot\,;z)
are
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that, for every
R\in[1,\infty]
, every
0<\epsilon\leq\mu d
, and every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

m_{X_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\mathrm{ERM}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\leq\left\lceil C\frac{\sigma^{2}d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil.

Moreover, there exist absolute constants
c,c_{0}>0
such that, for every
d\geq 2
and every
R\in[1,\infty]
, there is such a loss map
f(\cdot\,;\,\cdot)
for which, for every
0<\delta\leq 1/4
and every
0<\epsilon\leq c_{0}\mu d
,

m_{X_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\geq c\frac{\sigma^{2}d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

As mentioned before, this difference comes from the fact that ERM in this setting exhibits a localization phenomenon, requiring empirical-process control only on a (random) region near the optimal solution; in fact, strong convexity localizes this region to scale
\sqrt{\kappa d}
, truncated by the box diameter. Note also that the lower bound in Theorem
5.5
is for any algorithm; thus, no algorithm can improve upon ERM (up to constants).

Finally, we consider the continuous version of the problem, namely when the domain is the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case, we show that ERM actually has “accelerated” rates of order
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
, instead of order
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}
that is best possible for the integer case, as shown by the previous theorem. This happens because in the continuous case we can avoid an additional rounding loss in the localization phenomenon, which is unavoidable in the integer case (due to our matching lower bound).

Theorem 5.6
.

Suppose the functions
f(\cdot\,;z)
are
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that, for every nonempty closed convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, every
\epsilon>0
, and every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

m_{K}^{\mathrm{ERM}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\leq\left\lceil C\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu\epsilon}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil.

We note that this statement does not follow directly from existing SGD bounds of order
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
(e.g.,
11
and
8
: their results assume an objective that is both
1
-strongly convex and
1
-Lipschitz on the feasible set. When the feasible set is unbounded, however, this class is empty, since no finite-valued function on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be both strongly convex and globally Lipschitz. While the Lipschitz condition is generalized by our sub-Gaussian increment condition (Remark
5
), there is no existing result in the continuous setting under the weaker sub-Gaussian increment condition. Thus, we prove Theorem
5.6
to compare to the integer case with the same assumptions, even on unbounded domains.

Appendix B
Learning-theoretic tools

We first state the testing and KL facts used in the lower bounds.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite set with
|\mathcal{V}|\geq 2
, let
\{P_{v}:v\in\mathcal{V}\}
be probability distributions on a common measurable space, and let
Q
be another probability distribution on the same space. Then every hypothesis test or decorder
\widehat{v}
with values in
\mathcal{V}
satisfies

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P_{v}[\widehat{v}\neq v]\geq 1-\frac{|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|Q)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}.

Moreover, if
P
and
Q
are probability distributions on a common measurable space,
E
is an event, and

P(E)\geq 1-\delta,\qquad Q(E)\leq\delta

for some
0<\delta\leq 1/4
, then

\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{2\delta}.

Proof of
Appendix

B
.

For the first claim, let
V
be uniform on
\mathcal{V}
and, conditionally on
V=v
, let
Y
have distribution
P_{v}
. Let
\bar{P}:=|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P_{v}
. By Fano’s inequality
[
6
, Proposition 2.3.3 and Corollary 2.3.4]
(see also
16
, Section 2.7.1
),

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P_{v}[\widehat{v}\neq v]\geq 1-\frac{I(V;Y)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}.

If
|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\sum_{v}\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|Q)=+\infty
, the desired bound is trivial. Otherwise all
P_{v}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
, and the standard identity

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|Q)=I(V;Y)+\mathrm{KL}(\bar{P}\|Q)

implies
I(V;Y)\leq|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\sum_{v}\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|Q)
.

For the second claim, apply the KL data processing inequality
[
6
, Proposition 2.2.13 and Corollary 2.2.14]
to the indicator
y\mapsto\mathbf{1}_{E}(y)
. The random variable
\mathbf{1}_{E}(Y)
has distribution
\mathrm{Bern}(P(E))
when
Y\sim P
and distribution
\mathrm{Bern}(Q(E))
when
Y\sim Q
. Hence

\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)\geq\mathrm{kl}(P(E)\|Q(E)),

where
\mathrm{kl}
denotes the binary relative entropy. Since
P(E)\geq 1-\delta
and
Q(E)\leq\delta
,

\mathrm{kl}(P(E)\|Q(E))\geq\mathrm{kl}(1-\delta\|\delta)=(1-2\delta)\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{2\delta},

where the last inequality uses
\delta\leq 1/4
.
∎

.

The following elementary KL bounds hold.

For every

Definition B.3
.

Let
(T,\rho)
be a metric space, let
A\subseteq T
, and let

. A finite set

is an

such that
\rho(u,v)\leq r
. The covering number of

If no finite

N(r,A,\rho)=+\infty
. When

is a normed space and

.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbb{R}^{d}

.

Let

\mathbf{x}\in K
, for some

be such that

for every

. Suppose that, for some

Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that, for every family
\mathfrak{D}
of distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
, every
\epsilon>0
, and every

Proof of
Appendix

.

Fix

\mathbf{x}\in K

Then

\mathcal{G}_{K}:=\{g_{\mathbf{x}}:\mathbf{x}\in K\}

is an

cover
\mathcal{G}_{K}
and have

-size at most
\alpha
. Hence, for every
0<\alpha<1

[

N_{[]}(\alpha,\mathcal{G}_{K},L_{2}(D))\leq N(\alpha R,K,\|\cdot\|)\leq\left(\frac{3}{\alpha}\right)^{h}.

S

C.1
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

By

B

0<\eta<1
,

N(\eta R,B_{R}^{(\infty)},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{\eta}\right)^{d}\leq\left(\frac{3}{\eta}\right)^{d}.

Appendix

B
, applied with
K=B_{R}^{(\infty)}
,
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, and

, gives the stated upper bound.

It remains to prove the lower bound. We prove it with
c_{0}=1/8
and
c=1/1024
. Let
\mathcal{Z}:=[d]\times\{\pm 1\}
and define

f(\mathbf{x};(j,k)):=-kx_{j},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)},\ (j,k)\in\mathcal{Z}.

For
\mathbf{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
and
0<\rho\leq 1/2
, let
D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho}
be the distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
defined by

D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho}(j,k):=\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{1+\rho kb_{j}}{2}\qquad\forall j\in[d],\ k\in\{\pm 1\}.

For
b\in\{\pm 1\}
and
0<\rho\leq 1/2
, let
D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{b,\rho}
be the distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
defined by

D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{b,\rho}(1,k):=\frac{1+\rho kb}{2}\qquad\forall k\in\{\pm 1\}.

All other pairs have probability zero. Set

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{dim}}_{R}:=\left\{D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho}:\mathbf{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d},\ 0<\rho\leq 1/2\right\},

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{R}:=\left\{D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{b,\rho}:b\in\{\pm 1\},\ 0<\rho\leq 1/2\right\},

and
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R}:=\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{dim}}_{R}\cup\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{R}
.
For every
(j,k)\in\mathcal{Z}
, the map
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x};(j,k))
is linear and
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
on
B_{R}^{(\infty)}
.

We prove the dimension and confidence terms separately. Both parts reduce optimization to biased-coin hypothesis testing.

Dimension term.

It suffices to prove the claim for
\delta=1/4
, since the confidence requirement becomes stronger as
\delta
decreases. We average over a uniformly random sign vector and show that success forces large average correlation with this vector, while the sparse coordinate observations bound this correlation by a testing argument. We show that no learning rule with

m\leq\frac{R^{2}d}{512\epsilon^{2}}

succeeds with probability at least
3/4
under every distribution in
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{dim}}_{R}
. If no positive integer satisfies this bound, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, fix such an integer
m
, set
\rho:=4\epsilon/R\leq 1/2
, and let
A_{m}:\mathcal{Z}^{m}\to B_{R}^{(\infty)}
be any learning rule. For
\mathbf{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, let
F_{\mathbf{b}}
denote the population objective corresponding to
D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho}
. Then

\displaystyle F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k\in\{\pm 1\}}D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho}(j,k)kx_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{d}x_{j}\left(1\cdot\frac{1+\rho b_{j}}{2}+(-1)\cdot\frac{1-\rho b_{j}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\rho b_{j}x_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho}{d}\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\rangle\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}.

Thus,
F_{\mathbf{b}}
is minimized over
B_{R}^{(\infty)}
at
\mathbf{x}_{D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho}}^{\star}=R\mathbf{b}
, and

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})=-\rho R.

Therefore,

F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})=\rho R-\frac{\rho}{d}\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\rangle\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}.

Let
B
be uniform on
\{\pm 1\}^{d}
. Conditional on
B=\mathbf{b}
, let

S=((J_{1},K_{1}),\dots,(J_{m},K_{m}))\sim(D^{\mathrm{dim}}_{\mathbf{b},\rho})^{m},

and define the normalized output vector

Y:=\frac{A_{m}(S)}{R}\in[-1,1]^{d}.

Write
Y_{j}
for the
j
th coordinate of
Y
. Then

F_{B}(A_{m}(S))-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{B}(\mathbf{x})=\rho R\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\langle B,Y\rangle\right).

Fix
j\in[d]
, and define

I_{j}:=\left\{i\in[m]:\ J_{i}=j\right\},\qquad N_{j}:=|I_{j}|.

Let

T_{j}:=\left((B_{\ell})_{\ell\neq j},\ (J_{i})_{i\in[m]},\ (K_{i})_{i\notin I_{j}}\right).

Condition on
T_{j}
. Then
I_{j}
and
N_{j}
are fixed, the only remaining randomness lies in
B_{j}
and
(K_{i})_{i\in I_{j}}
, and
B_{j}
remains uniform on
\{\pm 1\}
.
Moreover,

(K_{i})_{i\in I_{j}}\mid(T_{j},B_{j}=1)\sim P_{+}^{\otimes N_{j}},\qquad(K_{i})_{i\in I_{j}}\mid(T_{j},B_{j}=-1)\sim P_{-}^{\otimes N_{j}},

where

P_{+}(s)=\frac{1+\rho s}{2},\qquad P_{-}(s)=\frac{1-\rho s}{2}\qquad\forall s\in\{\pm 1\}.

Using total variation and Pinsker’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{j}Y_{j}\mid T_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}\mid T_{j},B_{j}=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}\mid T_{j},B_{j}=-1]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{+}^{\otimes N_{j}}}[Y_{j}]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{-}^{\otimes N_{j}}}[Y_{j}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\mathbb{E}_{P_{+}^{\otimes N_{j}}}[Y_{j}]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{-}^{\otimes N_{j}}}[Y_{j}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}\left(P_{+}^{\otimes N_{j}},P_{-}^{\otimes N_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}\left(P_{+}^{\otimes N_{j}}\|P_{-}^{\otimes N_{j}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\sqrt{2N_{j}},

where the last inequality uses product additivity of KL divergence and
Appendix

B
. Taking total expectations and using Jensen’s inequality gives

\mathbb{E}[B_{j}Y_{j}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[B_{j}Y_{j}\mid T_{j}]\right]\leq\rho\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{2N_{j}}]\leq\rho\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}[N_{j}]}=\rho\sqrt{\frac{2m}{d}},

where the last equality uses
\mathbb{E}[N_{j}]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(J_{i}=j)=m/d
. Summing over
j
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\langle B,Y\rangle\right]=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[B_{j}Y_{j}]\leq\rho\sqrt{\frac{2m}{d}}\leq\frac{1}{4},

where the last inequality follows from
m\leq R^{2}d/(512\epsilon^{2})
and
\rho=4\epsilon/R
. On the other hand, if
A_{m}
succeeded with probability at least
3/4
under every distribution in
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{dim}}_{R}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{B}(A_{m}(S))-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{B}(\mathbf{x})\leq\epsilon\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}.

On this event,

\frac{1}{d}\langle B,Y\rangle\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{\rho R}=\frac{3}{4},

since
\rho=4\epsilon/R
. Since
\frac{1}{d}\langle B,Y\rangle\in[-1,1]
, this gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\langle B,Y\rangle\right]\geq\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot(-1)=\frac{5}{16},

contradicting the bound
\mathbb{E}[d^{-1}\langle B,Y\rangle]\leq 1/4
established above. Thus

m_{B_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,1/4;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{dim}}_{R})\geq\frac{R^{2}d}{512\epsilon^{2}}.

By monotonicity in the confidence parameter, the same dimension lower bound holds for every
0<\delta\leq 1/4
.

Confidence term.

We next isolate the one-coordinate coin test that yields the confidence term.
Set
\rho:=2\epsilon/R\leq 1/4
. Let
A_{m}:\mathcal{Z}^{m}\to B_{R}^{(\infty)}
be any learning rule. If
F_{b}
denotes the population objective corresponding to
D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{b,\rho}
, then

F_{b}(\mathbf{x})=-\rho bx_{1}\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)},

so

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{b}(\mathbf{x})=-\rho R.

Define

\widehat{b}(S):=\begin{cases}+1,&[A_{m}(S)]_{1}>0,\\
-1,&[A_{m}(S)]_{1}\leq 0.\end{cases}

If

F_{b}(A_{m}(S))-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{b}(\mathbf{x})\leq\epsilon,

then

\rho\left(R-b[A_{m}(S)]_{1}\right)\leq\epsilon,

which implies
b[A_{m}(S)]_{1}\geq R/2>0
, and hence
\widehat{b}(S)=b
. Therefore, if
A_{m}
succeeded with probability at least
1-\delta
under both
D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{+1,\rho}
and
D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{-1,\rho}
, then for

E:=\{\widehat{b}(S)=+1\},

we would have

P_{+}^{\otimes m}(E)\geq 1-\delta,\qquad P_{-}^{\otimes m}(E)\leq\delta,

where
P_{+}
and
P_{-}
denote the single-sample distributions
D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{+1,\rho}
and
D^{\mathrm{conf}}_{-1,\rho}
. Since
Appendix

B
gives

\mathrm{KL}(P_{+}\|P_{-})\leq 4\rho^{2},

additivity of KL divergence under products and
Appendix

B
imply

4m\rho^{2}\geq m\,\mathrm{KL}(P_{+}\|P_{-})=\mathrm{KL}(P_{+}^{\otimes m}\|P_{-}^{\otimes m})\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{2\delta}.

Therefore,

m\geq\frac{1}{8\rho^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)=\frac{R^{2}}{32\epsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right),

where the equality uses
\rho=2\epsilon/R
. Hence

m_{B_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{R})\geq\frac{R^{2}}{32\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{2\delta}.

By the inclusions
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{dim}}_{R}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{R}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R}
,

m_{B_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R})\geq\max\left\{\frac{R^{2}d}{512\epsilon^{2}},\frac{R^{2}}{32\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{2\delta}\right\}.

Since
\delta\leq 1/4
, we have
\log(1/(2\delta))\geq\frac{1}{2}\log(1/\delta)
. Therefore,

m_{B_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{R})\geq\max\left\{\frac{R^{2}d}{512\epsilon^{2}},\frac{R^{2}}{64\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right\}\geq\frac{R^{2}}{1024\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right),

where the last inequality uses
\max\{u,v\}\geq(u+v)/2
.

D.1
Packing and tent lower bounds

Lemma D.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every
d\geq 1
and every integer
1\leq s\leq d
, one can find a set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
with
|\mathcal{U}|\geq 2
such that

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}=s\quad\forall\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U},\quad\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\rangle\leq s/2\quad\forall\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U},\ \mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\quad\text{and }\log|\mathcal{U}|\geq cs\log\frac{ed}{s}.

Proof of
Section

D.1
.

Sample a random vector
\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
by choosing
S=\{i\in[d]:u_{i}\neq 0\}
uniformly among all subsets of
[d]
of size
s
, and then choosing independent signs on
S
. Let
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
be an independent copy, set
S^{\prime}=\{i\in[d]:u^{\prime}_{i}\neq 0\}
, and let
L:=|S\cap S^{\prime}|
. We bound

\mathbb{P}\left[\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\rangle>\frac{s}{2}\right].

First suppose that
d\leq 4es
. Conditional on
S,S^{\prime}
, the inner product is a sum of
L\leq s
independent signs. If
L=0
, the probability above is zero. Otherwise Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left[\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\rangle>\frac{s}{2}\right]\leq\exp(-c_{1}s)\leq\exp\left(-c_{2}s\log\frac{ed}{s}\right),

for absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
.

It remains to consider the case
d>4es
. The event
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\rangle>s/2
implies
L>s/2
. Let
r_{0}:=\lceil s/2\rceil
. Conditioning on
S
and union bounding over all
r_{0}
-subsets of
S
,

\mathbb{P}[L\geq r_{0}]\leq\binom{s}{r_{0}}\frac{\binom{d-r_{0}}{s-r_{0}}}{\binom{d}{s}}\leq\binom{s}{r_{0}}\left(\frac{s}{d}\right)^{r_{0}}\leq\left(\frac{es}{r_{0}}\cdot\frac{s}{d}\right)^{r_{0}}.

Since
d>4es
, we have
2es/d<1/2
, and hence

\mathbb{P}\left[L>\frac{s}{2}\right]\leq\left(\frac{2es}{d}\right)^{s/2}\leq\exp\left(-c_{3}s\log\frac{ed}{s}\right)

for an absolute constant
c_{3}>0
. Set
c:=\frac{1}{8}\min\{c_{2},c_{3}\}
. The two cases give

\mathbb{P}\left[\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\rangle>\frac{s}{2}\right]\leq\exp\left(-8cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right).

Let

M:=\max\left\{2,\left\lfloor\exp\left(2cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right)\right\rfloor\right\}.

If
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{M}
are sampled independently as above, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{1\leq i<j\leq M}\left\{\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle>\frac{s}{2}\right\}\right)\leq\binom{M}{2}\exp\left(-8cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right).

If
M=2
, the right-hand side is less than one. If
M\geq 3
, then
M\leq\exp(2cs\log(ed/s))
, and therefore

\binom{M}{2}\exp\left(-8cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-4cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right)<1.

Thus there is a realization with all pairwise inner products at most
s/2
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be the resulting set of
M
vectors. It remains to check its size. If
\exp(cs\log(ed/s))\leq 2
, then
M\geq\exp(cs\log(ed/s))
. Otherwise,

\left\lfloor\exp\left(2cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right)\right\rfloor\geq\exp\left(2cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right)-1\geq\exp\left(cs\log\frac{ed}{s}\right).

This proves the lemma.
∎

\mathcal{W}\subseteq X

|\mathcal{W}|\geq 2

D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}
. Since at most one tent is positive at any point,

F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1+\rho}{2}\psi_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1-\rho}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}\setminus\{\mathbf{u}\}}\psi_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}).

Hence
F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{u})=-(1+\rho)r/8
. This is the unique population minimum over
X
. Indeed, inside the positive support of
\psi_{\mathbf{u}}
,

F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1+\rho}{2}\psi_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})\geq-\frac{1+\rho}{8}r,

with equality only at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}
. Outside this support, either
\mathbf{x}
lies in another tent and
F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{x})\geq-(1-\rho)r/8
, or no tent is active and
F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{x})=0
. Therefore

F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{x})-F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{u})\geq\frac{\rho r}{4}

whenever
\psi_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Assume
0<\epsilon\leq r/16
and set
\rho:=8\epsilon/r
. Then
\rho\leq 1/2
, and hence
D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}\in\mathfrak{D}
. Also
\rho r/4=2\epsilon
. Since the positive supports are disjoint, any
\epsilon
-optimal output identifies the hidden point:

\mathbf{x}\in X,\quad F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{x})-\min_{\mathbf{z}\in X}F_{\mathbf{u},\rho}(\mathbf{z})\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{x}\text{ lies in the positive support of }\psi_{\mathbf{u}}.

We now prove the testing claim. Suppose that a decoder
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:\mathcal{Z}^{m}\to\mathcal{W}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}}[\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)=\mathbf{u}]\geq 1-\delta\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}

for some
0<\delta\leq 1/4
. Then

m\geq\frac{1}{96}\rho^{-2}\left(\log|\mathcal{W}|+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

We first prove the confidence term. Fix distinct
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{W}
and let

E:=\{S\in\mathcal{Z}^{m}:\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)=\mathbf{u}\}.

The success guarantee gives
D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}(E)\geq 1-\delta
. Since
E\subseteq\{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)\neq\mathbf{v}\}
, it also gives
D_{\mathbf{v},\rho}^{\otimes m}(E)\leq\delta
. Thus

D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}(E)\geq 1-\delta,\qquad D_{\mathbf{v},\rho}^{\otimes m}(E)\leq\delta.

By
Appendix

B
,

m\,\mathrm{KL}(D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}\|D_{\mathbf{v},\rho})=\mathrm{KL}(D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}\|D_{\mathbf{v},\rho}^{\otimes m})\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{2\delta},

where the equality is additivity under products. The distributions
D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}
and
D_{\mathbf{v},\rho}
differ only in coordinates
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
, so
Appendix

B
gives
\mathrm{KL}(D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}\|D_{\mathbf{v},\rho})\leq 8\rho^{2}
. Since
\delta\leq 1/4
,

m\geq\frac{1}{32}\rho^{-2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

If
\log|\mathcal{W}|<4\log 2
, then, since
\delta\leq 1/4
,

\log|\mathcal{W}|+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\leq 3\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Hence the confidence bound gives

m\geq\frac{1}{96}\rho^{-2}\left(\log|\mathcal{W}|+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

It remains to prove the testing claim when
\log|\mathcal{W}|\geq 4\log 2
. Let
P_{0}
be the reference distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
under which the membership indicators are independent and

\mathbb{P}_{V\sim P_{0}}[\mathbf{w}\in V]=\frac{1-\rho}{2}\qquad\forall\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}.

By
Appendix

B
, followed by additivity of KL divergence under products and
Appendix

B
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{P}_{S\sim D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}}[\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)\neq\mathbf{u}]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{|\mathcal{W}|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{KL}(D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}\|P_{0}^{\otimes m})+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{W}|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{m|\mathcal{W}|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{KL}(D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}\|P_{0})+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{W}|}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{4m\rho^{2}+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{W}|}.

Here the last inequality uses the fact that
D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}
and
P_{0}
differ only in coordinate
\mathbf{u}
.
Since the decoder has error probability at most
\delta
for every
\mathbf{u}
,

\delta\geq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{P}_{S\sim D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}}[\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)\neq\mathbf{u}]\geq 1-\frac{4m\rho^{2}+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{W}|}.

As
\delta\leq 1/4
, this gives

4m\rho^{2}+\log 2\geq\frac{3}{4}\log|\mathcal{W}|.

Since
\log|\mathcal{W}|\geq 4\log 2
, we have

m\geq\frac{1}{8}\rho^{-2}\log|\mathcal{W}|.

Together with the small-entropy case above, this proves the testing claim.

Now let
A_{m}:\mathcal{Z}^{m}\to X
be any proper algorithm that is
\epsilon
-optimal with probability at least
1-\delta
for every distribution in
\mathfrak{D}
. Define
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)
to be the unique
\mathbf{w}
whose positive support contains
A_{m}(S)
, and define it arbitrarily if no such
\mathbf{w}
exists. The optimization-to-identification implication above gives

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim D_{\mathbf{u},\rho}^{\otimes m}}[\widehat{\mathbf{u}}(S)=\mathbf{u}]\geq 1-\delta\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{W}.

The testing claim therefore gives

m\geq\frac{1}{96\rho^{2}}\left(\log|\mathcal{W}|+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Since
\rho=8\epsilon/r
,
1/\rho^{2}={r^{2}}/(64\epsilon^{2})
, this proves the lemma.
∎

E.1
Auxiliary localization and empirical process bounds

Lemma E.1
.

Let
R\in[1,\infty]
, let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, and let
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}}F(\mathbf{x})
. Then, for every
\mathbf{x}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}
and
s\geq 0
,

F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq s\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+\frac{s}{\mu}}.

Proof of
Section

E.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in B_{\lfloor R\rfloor}^{(\infty)}}F(\mathbf{y})
. When
R<\infty
, the feasible integer set is exactly
B_{\lfloor R\rfloor}^{(\infty)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. When
R=\infty
,
\mathbf{u}
is the unconstrained minimizer. We first construct
\mathbf{q}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}
such that
\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/2
and
\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{u}\rangle=0
. If
R=\infty
, let
\mathbf{q}
be a coordinatewise nearest-integer rounding of
\mathbf{u}
; then
\nabla F(\mathbf{u})=0
.

If
R<\infty
, define
\mathbf{q}
coordinatewise as follows. For every coordinate with
u_{j}\in(-\lfloor R\rfloor,\lfloor R\rfloor)
, let
q_{j}
be a nearest integer to
u_{j}
. For every coordinate with
u_{j}=\pm\lfloor R\rfloor
, set
q_{j}=u_{j}
. Since
\lfloor R\rfloor
is an integer, this gives
\mathbf{q}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}
and
\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/2
. First-order optimality over the box gives
\nabla_{j}F(\mathbf{u})=0
on every interior coordinate, while
q_{j}-u_{j}=0
on every boundary coordinate. Thus
\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{u}\rangle=0
.

The upper inequality in (
3
) gives

F(\mathbf{q})-F(\mathbf{u})\leq\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{u}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{Ld}{8}.

By optimality of
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
over
X_{R}^{(\infty)}
,

F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-F(\mathbf{u})\leq\frac{Ld}{8}.

First-order optimality of
\mathbf{u}
over
B_{\lfloor R\rfloor}^{(\infty)}
gives
\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{u}\rangle\geq 0
for every
\mathbf{y}\in B_{\lfloor R\rfloor}^{(\infty)}
. Hence the lower inequality in (
3
) gives

F(\mathbf{y})-F(\mathbf{u})\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\forall\mathbf{y}\in B_{\lfloor R\rfloor}^{(\infty)}.

Applying this bound to
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
gives

\|\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa d}.

If
F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq s
, then

F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{u})\leq F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-F(\mathbf{u})+s\leq\frac{Ld}{8}+s,

and another application of strong convexity gives

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\kappa d}{4}+\frac{2s}{\mu}}.

The triangle inequality gives

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa d}+\sqrt{\frac{\kappa d}{4}+\frac{2s}{\mu}}\leq 2\sqrt{\kappa d+\frac{s}{\mu}},

and, when
R<\infty
, the diameter bound gives
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq 2\lfloor R\rfloor\sqrt{d}
. Combining these two bounds yields

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+\frac{s}{\mu}}.

For
R=\infty
, this is the preceding bound. This proves the lemma.
∎

We next isolate the empirical-process input used in the ERM proof: a high-probability chaining bound for sub-Gaussian increments, followed by its Euclidean-ball specialization.

Lemma E.2
.

Let
(T,\rho)
be a nonempty compact metric space. Let
(G_{t})_{t\in T}
be a stochastic process such that, for every
s,t\in T
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\lambda(G_{s}-G_{t})\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\rho(s,t)^{2}}{2}\right).

Set
\Delta:=\sup_{s,t\in T}\rho(s,t)
. Then there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that, for every
u\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-u^{2}}
,

\sup_{s,t\in T}|G_{s}-G_{t}|\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{\Delta}\sqrt{\log N(\varepsilon,T,\rho)}\,d\varepsilon+u\Delta\right).

Proof of
Section

E.1
.

For a real random variable
X
, write

\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\left\{K>0:\ \mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{X^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)\leq 2\right\}.

Fix
s,t\in T
. If
s=t
, then
G_{s}-G_{t}=0
. If
s\neq t
, put
X=(G_{s}-G_{t})/\rho(s,t)
. For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, applying the assumed exponential bound with
\lambda=\alpha/\rho(s,t)
gives

\mathbb{E}\exp(\alpha X)=\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\rho(s,t)}(G_{s}-G_{t})\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right).

By Jensen’s inequality,
\alpha\mathbb{E}X\leq\alpha^{2}/2
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and hence
\mathbb{E}X=0
. Thus
18
, applied to
X
, gives an absolute constant
C_{1}>0
such that
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}
. Therefore,

\|G_{s}-G_{t}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\rho(s,t)\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}\rho(s,t).

Thus the process
(G_{t})_{t\in T}
satisfies
18
. By
18
, there is an absolute constant
C_{2}>0
such that, with probability at least
1-2e^{-u^{2}}
,

\sup_{s,t\in T}|G_{s}-G_{t}|\leq C_{1}C_{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N(\varepsilon,T,\rho)}\,d\varepsilon+u\Delta\right).

Since
N(\varepsilon,T,\rho)=1
for every
\varepsilon\geq\Delta
, the integral over
[\Delta,\infty)
is zero. Taking
C:=C_{1}C_{2}
proves the claim.
∎

Lemma E.3
.

Let
A\subseteq B_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{0},R)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbf{x}_{0}\in A

Then there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that, for every

1-e^{-t}

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A}G_{\mathbf{x}}\leq CaR(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{t}).

Proof of
Section

E.1
.

The case
R=0
is immediate because
A=\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\}
. Now assume
R>0
.

Apply
Section

E.1
to the compact metric space
A
with metric
d_{A}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=a\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
. Since

-smooth. For this distribution,

f(\mathbf{x};Z)-f(\mathbf{y};Z)-F_{D_{0}}(\mathbf{x})+F_{D_{0}}(\mathbf{y})=-\langle Z,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle,

which is
\sigma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
-sub-Gaussian. Hence
D_{0}
belongs to the admissible distribution class associated with this loss map.

Fix
1\leq R<\infty
. Since the loss is already anchored,
\bar{F}_{D_{0}}=F_{D_{0}}
and
\bar{F}_{S}=F_{S}
. If
S=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m})\sim D_{0}^{m}
and
\bar{Z}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}
, then

F_{D_{0}}(\mathbf{x})-F_{S}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\bar{Z},\mathbf{x}\rangle.

Since
X_{R}^{(\infty)}=\{-\lfloor R\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor R\rfloor\}^{d}
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}}|F_{D_{0}}(\mathbf{x})-F_{S}(\mathbf{x})|=\lfloor R\rfloor\|\bar{Z}\|_{1}.

Writing
\bar{Z}=(\sigma/\sqrt{m})\xi
with
\xi\sim N(\mathbf{0},I_{d})
, the anchored uniform deviation is
(\sigma\lfloor R\rfloor/\sqrt{m})\|\xi\|_{1}
.

Dimension term.

Since
\xi_{1}
is standard normal, choose an absolute constant
\eta_{0}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}[|\xi_{1}|<\eta_{0}]\leq 1/8
. Then
\mathbb{E}|\{j:|\xi_{j}|<\eta_{0}\}|\leq d/8
, so Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left[|\{j:|\xi_{j}|<\eta_{0}\}|\geq\frac{d}{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Hence, with probability at least
3/4
,
\|\xi\|_{1}\geq\eta_{0}d/2
.
Consequently, if
m<\eta_{0}^{2}\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d^{2}/(4\epsilon^{2})
, then the anchored uniform deviation is larger than
\epsilon
with probability at least
3/4
. Since
\delta\leq 1/4
, any sample size satisfying anchored UC must also satisfy

m\geq c_{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}

for an absolute constant
c_{1}>0
.

Confidence term.

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}\sim N(0,d)
, the Gaussian tail lower bound gives an absolute constant
\eta_{1}>0
such that, for every
0<\delta\leq 1/4

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}\right|\geq\eta_{1}\sqrt{d\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right]\geq 2\delta.

Since
\|\xi\|_{1}\geq|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}|
, the same lower bound holds with
\|\xi\|_{1}
in place of
|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}|
.
Therefore, if
m<\eta_{1}^{2}\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2}
, then the anchored uniform deviation is larger than
\epsilon
with probability greater than
\delta
. Thus every valid anchored UC sample size also satisfies

m\geq c_{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}

for an absolute constant
c_{2}>0
. Combining the two lower bounds and using
\max\{u,v\}\geq(u+v)/2
proves

m_{X_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\mathrm{a-UC}}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\geq c\frac{\sigma^{2}\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}d}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

for an absolute constant
c>0
.

Proof of the upper bound in

Fix
R\in[1,\infty]
,
D\in\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma}
, and a sample

. Let

be the selected population and empirical minimizers. Define

and set
G(\mathbf{x}):=\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})-\Delta_{S}(\mathbf{x})
. Since
F_{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq F_{S}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
, we have
\Delta_{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq 0
, and hence
\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq G(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})
.

For any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the increment assumption and independence give

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\lambda(G(\mathbf{x})-G(\mathbf{y}))\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}}{2m}\right).

For
s\geq 0
, set
A_{s}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}:\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})\leq s\}
. By
Section

E.1
,

A_{s}\subseteq B_{2}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\star},2\sqrt{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+\frac{s}{\mu}}\right).

The set
A_{s}
is finite, hence compact. Indeed, this is immediate when
R<\infty
, while for
R=\infty
the localization bound makes
A_{s}
a bounded subset of the lattice. Since
G(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=0
, applying
Section

E.1
with
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}^{\star}
, radius
2\sqrt{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+s/\mu}
, and
a=\sigma/\sqrt{m}
gives, with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{s}}G(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{(d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+s/\mu)(d+t)}{m}},

where
C_{1}>0
is an absolute constant.

We prove the relative bound
G(\mathbf{x})\leq\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})/2+r
uniformly over
X_{R}^{(\infty)}
by peeling the level sets of
\Delta_{D}
. Let
T:=d+\log(1/\delta)
and

r:=C_{2}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}T}{m}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}T}{\mu m}\right),

where
C_{2}>0
is fixed below. Apply this bound with
s_{k}=2^{k}r
and
t_{k}=\log(1/\delta)+(k+1)^{2}
, so that
e^{-t_{k}}=\delta e^{-(k+1)^{2}}
. For
k\geq 0
, let
E_{k}
be the event that

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})>C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{(d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+2^{k}r/\mu)(T+(k+1)^{2})}{m}}.

The estimate gives
\mathbb{P}(E_{k})\leq e^{-t_{k}}
, and hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{k\geq 0}E_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{-t_{k}}=\delta\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{-(k+1)^{2}}<\delta.

Let
\mathcal{E}:=\bigcap_{k\geq 0}E_{k}^{c}
. Then
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta
, and on
\mathcal{E}
, for every
k\geq 0
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{(d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+2^{k}r/\mu)(T+(k+1)^{2})}{m}}.

Since
T\geq 1
and
(k+1)^{2}\leq 4\cdot 2^{k}T
, the right-hand side is bounded as

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq 3C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{(d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}+2^{k}r/\mu)2^{k}T}{m}}

\displaystyle\leq 3C_{1}\,2^{k/2}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}T}{m}}+3C_{1}\,2^{k}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{rT}{\mu m}}

\displaystyle\leq 3C_{1}\,2^{k/2}\frac{r}{C_{2}}+3C_{1}\,2^{k}\frac{r}{\sqrt{C_{2}}},

where the last line uses the definition of
r
. Choose
C_{2}\geq 1
so that
3C_{1}/C_{2}+3C_{1}/\sqrt{C_{2}}\leq 1/4
. Then

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})\leq\begin{cases}r,&k=0,\\
2^{k-2}r,&k\geq 1.\end{cases}

Therefore, on
\mathcal{E}
,

G(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})+r\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}.

Indeed, if
\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})\leq r
, then
G(\mathbf{x})\leq r
. If
2^{k-1}r<\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2^{k}r
for some
k\geq 1
, then
\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}
and
G(\mathbf{x})\leq 2^{k-2}r<\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})/2
.

Applying the relative bound to
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S}
gives

\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq G(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})+r,

and hence
\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq 2r
. Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta

F_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})-F_{D}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq 2C_{2}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{m}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu}\frac{d+\log(1/\delta)}{m}\right).

Consequently, every integer

m\geq\left\lceil 16C_{2}^{2}\sigma^{2}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\max\left\{\frac{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}}{\epsilon^{2}},\frac{1}{\mu\epsilon}\right\}\right\rceil

makes the excess bound at most
\epsilon
. Since
\kappa\geq 1
and
\lfloor R\rfloor\geq 1
, the assumption
\epsilon\leq\mu d
gives

\frac{1}{\mu\epsilon}\leq\frac{d\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

This proves the desired upper bound for ERM sample complexity.

The Hessian of the quadratic part is

\begin{pmatrix}2\mu&-2\mu\tau\\
-2\mu\tau&2\mu\tau^{2}+L/2\end{pmatrix}.

Its determinant is
\mu L
. Also, using
\tau^{2}\leq\kappa/16
, its trace is at most

2\mu+2\mu\tau^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\leq 2\mu+\frac{L}{8}+\frac{L}{2}\leq L,

(5)

where the last inequality uses
\kappa\geq 64
. Therefore the largest eigenvalue is at most
L
, and the smallest eigenvalue is at least
\mu L/L=\mu
. Thus every block is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth.

The quadratic part ties exactly the two integer points
(0,0)
and
(\tau,1)
. Indeed, for an integer point
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, write

. Then

(0,0)
and
(\tau,1)

\mu p^{2}+\frac{L}{4}y(y-1)\geq\frac{\mu}{4}\left(p^{2}+\kappa y(y-1)\right)\geq\frac{\mu}{4}.

The linear perturbation is controlled by the same integer gap. We claim that

\left|\frac{x}{\tau}\right|+\left|\frac{x}{\tau}-1\right|\leq 3\left(p^{2}+\kappa y(y-1)\right)\qquad\forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0),(\tau,1)\}.

Indeed, since

, if

, then the excluded endpoints force

and
\tau\geq 1

\left|\frac{x}{\tau}\right|+\left|\frac{x}{\tau}-1\right|\leq 1+2\frac{|p|}{\tau}\leq 3p^{2}.

If

y(y-1)\geq 1
and
|y|+|y-1|\leq 3y(y-1)
for integer

. Thus

\left|\frac{x}{\tau}\right|+\left|\frac{x}{\tau}-1\right|\leq|y|+|y-1|+2\frac{|p|}{\tau}\leq 3y(y-1)+2p^{2}\leq 3\left(p^{2}+\kappa y(y-1)\right),

where the last inequality uses
\kappa\geq 1
.

Assume

. Then, at every integer point outside

\gamma\left|\frac{x}{\tau}-1\right|+\gamma\left|\frac{x}{\tau}\right|\leq\frac{\mu}{8}\left(p^{2}+\kappa y(y-1)\right).

For

This equals
\gamma
at
(0,0)

\frac{\mu}{8}\left(p^{2}+\kappa y(y-1)\right)\geq 3\gamma.

Thus
(\tau,1)
is the unique integer minimizer of
\phi_{+1}
. Similarly,

\phi_{-1}(x,y)-\phi_{-1}(0,0)=\mu p^{2}+\frac{L}{4}y(y-1)+\gamma\frac{x}{\tau}.

This equals
\gamma
at
(\tau,1)
, and the same bound shows that it is at least
3\gamma
at every other integer point outside

. Thus
(0,0)
is the unique integer minimizer of
\phi_{-1}
.
The right-hand side in the block error bound is zero when the decoder is correct and at most
\gamma/12
when it is wrong. Since the estimates above show that every wrongly decoded point has excess at least
\gamma

1\leq\kappa<64
. Assume again that
0<\epsilon\leq c_{1}\mu d/72
, and set
\gamma:=\epsilon/(c_{1}d)
. On
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, take the sample loss

f(\mathbf{x};Z):=\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\langle Z,\mathbf{x}\rangle,

and, for each
\mathbf{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, let
D_{\mathbf{b}}:=N((\mu/2)\mathbf{1}+\gamma\mathbf{b},\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. The Hessian is
\mu I_{d}
, so the sample losses are
\mu
-strongly convex and
\mu
-smooth. Since
L\geq\mu
, they are admissible for the given
(\mu,L)
class. The corresponding population objective is

F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim D_{\mathbf{b}}}f(\mathbf{x};Z)=\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\left\langle\frac{\mu}{2}\mathbf{1}+\gamma\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle.

The same Gaussian calculation verifies the sub-Gaussian increment condition.
Since
\epsilon\leq c_{1}\mu d/72
, we have
\gamma\leq\mu/72
. Therefore, the integer minimizer in coordinate
j
is
1
if
b_{j}=1
and
0
if
b_{j}=-1
. These minimizers belong to
X_{R}^{(\infty)}
because
R\geq 1
. Define
\widehat{b}_{j}(\mathbf{x})=1
when
x_{j}\geq 1
and
\widehat{b}_{j}(\mathbf{x})=-1
when
x_{j}\leq 0
, and write
\widehat{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x}):=(\widehat{b}_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\widehat{b}_{d}(\mathbf{x}))
. Then every wrongly decoded coordinate costs at least
\gamma
, so

F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})-\min_{\mathbf{z}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}}F_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{z})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(d-\langle\widehat{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{b}\rangle\right)\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in X_{R}^{(\infty)}.

The preceding dimension and confidence testing arguments apply with
\bar{d}
replaced by
d
. The only change from the two-dimensional block construction is that the one-coordinate Gaussian mean separation is
2\gamma
rather than
2\gamma/\tau
, so the testing estimates have no
\kappa
loss. Therefore, for an absolute constant
c_{4}>0
,

m_{X_{R}^{(\infty)}}^{\star}(\epsilon,\delta;\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma})\geq c_{4}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(d^{2}+d\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Since
R\geq 1
gives
\lfloor R\rfloor\geq 1
, we have
\min\{\kappa,\lfloor R\rfloor^{2}\}\leq\kappa<64
. Hence the last inequality implies the claimed bound with constant
c_{4}/64
. Taking
c_{0}:=c_{1}/72
and
c:=\min\{c_{3},c_{4}/64\}
completes the proof.
∎

E.5
Proof of
Theorem

5.6

The proof follows the proof of the upper bound in
Theorem

5.5
, with the lattice localization step replaced by its continuous analogue. Fix a nonempty closed convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a distribution
D\in\mathfrak{D}^{\mathrm{sc}}_{\mu,L,\sigma}
, and a sample
S\sim D^{m}
. Let

\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in K}F_{D}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in K}F_{S}(\mathbf{x})

be the population and empirical minimizers. They exist because
K
is closed and the strongly convex objectives are coercive, and they are unique by strong convexity. Define
\Delta_{D},\Delta_{S}
, and
G
as in that proof, with
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
replaced by
\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}
. Then
\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq G(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})
, and the same sub-Gaussian increment bound holds for
G
with parameter
\sigma/\sqrt{m}
.

The localization step is sharper in the continuous problem and uses only strong convexity. Fix
\mathbf{x}\in K
and
0<t<1
, and set
\mathbf{x}_{t}:=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}+t\mathbf{x}
. Since
K
is convex,
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in K
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}
minimizes
F_{D}
over
K
, strong convexity gives

F_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star})\leq F_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\leq(1-t)F_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star})+tF_{D}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\mu}{2}t(1-t)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

Dividing by
t
and letting
t\downarrow 0
gives

\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})=F_{D}(\mathbf{x})-F_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star})\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in K.

Thus, for
s\geq 0
, the level set

A_{s}=\{\mathbf{x}\in K:\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})\leq s\}\subseteq B_{2}\left(\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star},\sqrt{\frac{2s}{\mu}}\right).

Unlike the lattice localization in
Section

E.1
, this bound has no
\kappa d
term. The set
A_{s}
is compact because it is closed and bounded by the inclusion above. Applying
Section

E.1
with
A=A_{s}
,
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star}
, radius
\sqrt{2s/\mu}
, and
a=\sigma/\sqrt{m}
gives an absolute constant
C_{1}>0
such that, with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{s}}G(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{s(d+t)}{\mu m}}.

Let
T:=d+\log(1/\delta)
and set

r:=C_{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}T}{\mu m},

where
C_{2}>0
is fixed below. Apply the preceding bound with
s_{k}=2^{k}r
and
t_{k}=\log(1/\delta)+(k+1)^{2}
. For
k\geq 0
, let
E_{k}
be the event that

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})>C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2^{k}r(T+(k+1)^{2})}{\mu m}}.

The estimate gives
\mathbb{P}(E_{k})\leq e^{-t_{k}}
, and hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{k\geq 0}E_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{-t_{k}}=\delta\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{-(k+1)^{2}}<\delta.

Let
\mathcal{E}:=\bigcap_{k\geq 0}E_{k}^{c}
. Then
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta
, and on
\mathcal{E}
, for every
k\geq 0
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2^{k}r(T+(k+1)^{2})}{\mu m}}.

Since
T\geq 1
and
(k+1)^{2}\leq 4\cdot 2^{k}T
, the right-hand side is at most

C_{3}\,2^{k}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{rT}{\mu m}}=C_{3}\,2^{k}\frac{r}{\sqrt{C_{2}}}

for an absolute constant
C_{3}>0
. Choose
C_{2}
so that
C_{3}/\sqrt{C_{2}}\leq 1/4
. Then

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}}G(\mathbf{x})\leq 2^{k-2}r\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

Therefore, on
\mathcal{E}
,

G(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})+r\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in K.

Indeed, if
\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})\leq r
, then
\mathbf{x}\in A_{r}
and
G(\mathbf{x})\leq r
. If
2^{k-1}r<\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2^{k}r
for some
k\geq 1
, then
\mathbf{x}\in A_{2^{k}r}
and
G(\mathbf{x})\leq 2^{k-2}r<\Delta_{D}(\mathbf{x})/2
.
Applying the relative bound to
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S}
gives

\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq G(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})+r,

and hence
\Delta_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})\leq 2r
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

F_{D}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{S})-F_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{D}^{\star})\leq 2C_{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu m}\left(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

The stated sample complexity bound follows by requiring the right-hand side to be at most
\epsilon
.
