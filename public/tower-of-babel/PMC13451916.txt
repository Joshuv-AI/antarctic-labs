Predictive value of peripheral blood hsa-miR-122-5p and hsa-miR-486-5p for response to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in lung squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Gege Li, Xu Cheng, Chongyu Su, Zhidong Liu, Tao Wen, Tianyu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Blood markers may help predict response before lung cancer surgery.•Two blood molecules were linked to tumor burden in lung squamous cancer.•Baseline blood signals differed between good and poor treatment responders.•Changes in blood signals during treatment tracked treatment effectiveness.•Blood-based markers may support less invasive treatment decision-making.

Introduction

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has profoundly transformed the therapeutic landscape of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1]. Given their remarkable clinical efficacy, increasing attention has been directed toward evaluating the safety and therapeutic potential of ICIs in the perioperative setting, including both neoadjuvant [2] and adjuvant treatment for resectable NSCLC [3]. More recently, phase III trials of perioperative or neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy have demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in pathological response and event-free survival in patients with resectable NSCLC, further supporting the incorporation of immunotherapy-based strategies into early-stage disease management [[4], [5], [6]]. Compared with adjuvant immunotherapy, neoadjuvant immunotherapy is theoretically more advantageous because the presence of an intact primary tumor provides a sustained source of tumor antigens, while preserved lymphatic architecture facilitates more robust systemic immune activation. In addition, neoadjuvant treatment allows direct assessment of pathological response in resected specimens [7].

To date, ICIs have been increasingly incorporated into neoadjuvant treatment strategies for NSCLC [[8], [9], [10]], and have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for neoadjuvant treatment in patients with locally advanced NSCLC [11]. Particularly in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), neoadjuvant immunotherapy-based regimens have demonstrated encouraging therapeutic activity [12,13]. Despite these advances, however, only a subset of patients derives substantial benefit from ICI-based neoadjuvant therapy. Nearly half of patients fail to respond, and some may even experience hyperprogressive disease [14]. Therefore, the identification of precise and reliable biomarkers capable of predicting treatment efficacy is of considerable clinical importance, as it would facilitate optimal patient selection for neoadjuvant immunotherapy-based strategies.

Currently investigated biomarkers for ICI-based treatment include programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), and immune-related features of the tumor microenvironment. However, these biomarkers have several limitations in the neoadjuvant setting, including tissue dependence, spatial and temporal heterogeneity, limited feasibility of repeated sampling, relatively high cost, and inconsistent predictive performance across different patient populations [[15], [16], [17]]. Therefore, minimally invasive blood-based biomarkers with good reproducibility and suitability for longitudinal monitoring may provide additional value for perioperative treatment decision-making.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. Dysregulated miRNA networks are involved in multiple cancer-related biological processes, including tumor initiation, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, apoptosis resistance, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and treatment resistance [18]. In cancer management, miRNAs have attracted increasing attention not only as functional regulators of tumor biology but also as potential diagnostic, prognostic, predictive, and therapeutic biomarkers [19]. Circulating miRNAs can be detected in plasma, serum, and other body fluids, where they may be protected from degradation through association with extracellular vesicles, proteins, or lipoprotein complexes. These characteristics make circulating miRNAs attractive candidates for liquid biopsy-based cancer detection and treatment monitoring [20].

Recent studies have further supported the clinical relevance of circulating miRNA profiling in NSCLC. Circulating miRNA panels have shown potential value in distinguishing patients with NSCLC from healthy individuals, stratifying lung adenocarcinoma and LUSC, predicting prognosis, and reflecting treatment response [21]. In the context of immunotherapy, miRNAs may also participate in the regulation of antitumor immunity by modulating immune-cell function, tumor–immune interactions, and the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis [22]. These findings suggest that circulating miRNAs may serve not only as passive biomarkers of tumor burden but also as biologically meaningful indicators of immune responsiveness.

In this prospective study, we enrolled patients with LUSC who underwent neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy followed by surgery and performed comprehensive profiling of peripheral blood microRNAs during treatment (Fig. 1A). Method and material

Patients and sample collection

Between October 2020 and June 2021, this prospective study enrolled patients with resectable LUSC who received neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in the Second Department of Thoracic Surgery, Beijing Chest Hospital, Capital Medical University. Eligible patients had clinical TNM stage T1–4N1–3M0 or T2–4N0M0 disease, and a total of 24 patients were initially included. Among them, paired plasma samples derived from peripheral blood collected both before and after treatment were successfully obtained from 15 patients. Clinical and pathological characteristics, as well as treatment-related data, were extracted from the medical records.

Pathological response to neoadjuvant therapy was independently assessed by experienced pathologists at Beijing Chest Hospital, Capital Medical University. Patients were classified into a pCR group and a non-pCR group. pCR was defined as the absence of viable tumor cells in both the resected primary tumor specimen and tumor-draining lymph nodes.

All enrolled patients were followed longitudinally. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the initiation of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy to death from any cause or the date of last clinical follow-up. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the time from the initiation of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy to the occurrence of any adverse event, including disease progression, postoperative recurrence, or death.

Peripheral blood samples were collected from enrolled patients before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Plasma was separated within 2 h after blood collection, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chest Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval No YJS-Revision-2020–014–01), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and healthy volunteers before enrollment and sample collection.

Treatment response evaluation

The diagnosis of lung squamous cell carcinoma was established primarily on the basis of chest computed tomography (CT), fiberoptic bronchoscopy, and histopathological findings from biopsy specimens. Tumor staging was determined according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system for lung cancer (2017). Operative time was recorded by operating room nurses and defined as the interval from skin incision to wound closure. Radiographic response was assessed by the investigators according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1) and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD).

Plasma miRNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

Peripheral blood samples were collected from the 15 patients with LUSC within 1 week before the first cycle of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and within 1 week before surgical resection after completion of neoadjuvant treatment. Blood samples were processed within 2 h after collection. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by a second centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove residual cells and cellular debris. The plasma supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

Total RNA enriched for small RNAs was extracted from plasma samples using the miRcute Serum/Plasma miRNA Isolation Kit (catalog No DP503; TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity and small RNA distribution were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only RNA samples that passed quality control were used for library preparation.

Small RNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Small RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 3′ and 5′ adapters were sequentially ligated to small RNAs, followed by reverse transcription and PCR amplification with indexed primers. The amplified libraries were size-selected to enrich small RNA-derived inserts, and library quality and concentration were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Qualified libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts.

Single-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform with a read length of 1 × 50 bp by Beijing Enze Kangtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control, adapter trimming, and removal of low-quality reads. Clean reads with lengths corresponding to mature miRNAs were retained for downstream analysis. The filtered reads were mapped to the human reference genome and annotated against known miRNA sequences in miRBase release 22.1. miRNA expression levels were quantified as raw read counts and normalized for subsequent differential expression analysis (Fig. 1B).

Statistical analysis

Differential expression analysis between groups was performed using the edgeR package (version 3.12.1) in R. This package applies a Poisson-based model together with an empirical Bayes approach for the statistical analysis of digital miRNA expression data. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. miRNAs with |log2(fold change)| ≥ 0.585 and adjusted P ≤ 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed miRNAs.

All other statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.4. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (frequencies), whereas continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range) or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate according to data distribution. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the associations between clinical characteristics and pathological response. OS and EFS were compared between the pCR and non-pCR groups using the log-rank test. Differences in miRNA expression between independent pathological response groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas paired Student’s t-test was used to compare variables before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy within the same patients. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes

A total of 15 patients with resectable LUSC who underwent surgical resection after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, together with 4 healthy volunteers, were included in this study. The patients had stage IIA–IIIB disease, with a mean age of 61.27 ± 5.78 years, and 13 patients were male (86.67%). All patients completed 2–3 cycles of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, and all immunotherapy regimens were based on PD-1 inhibitors.

The timing of surgery was determined through multidisciplinary discussion at our center. All patients underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, and one patient did not achieve R0 resection. The median operative time was 180 (120–191) min, the median intraoperative blood loss was 200 (100–300) mL, the median postoperative pleural drainage volume was 1000 (710–1260) mL, and the median chest tube duration was 6 (4–7) days. Intensive care unit stay ranged from 0 to 2 days. Pathological assessment showed that 8 patients (53.33%) achieved pCR, whereas 7 patients (46.67%) were classified into the non-pCR group (Table S1).

During follow-up, no tumor-related adverse events were observed in patients in the pCR group, and none received additional postoperative antitumor treatment. In the non-pCR group, 1 patient developed disease progression 8 months after surgery, 2 patients received adjuvant traditional Chinese medicine at 25 and 30 months after surgery, respectively, and 3 patients had died by the time of analysis (Fig. 1C). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients who achieved pCR had significantly better OS and EFS than those without pCR. The log-rank P values were 0.046 for OS and 0.016 for EFS, respectively (Fig. 1D).

Baseline levels of hsa-miR-122–5p and hsa-miR-486–5p were associated with tumor burden

To identify miRNAs closely associated with tumor development, we first performed differential expression analysis between baseline peripheral blood samples from patients with LUSC and peripheral blood samples from 4 healthy volunteers (Fig. 2A). A total of 796 miRNAs were detected. We then evaluated whether these dysregulated miRNAs were associated with tumor burden. Patients were stratified according to primary tumor stage into the T1+T2 and T3+T4 groups. The baseline level of hsa-miR-122–5p was significantly higher in the T1+T2 group than in the T3+T4 group, with a 4.49-fold difference between the two groups (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2C, D). In contrast, hsa-miR-486–5p showed the opposite pattern: its baseline level was significantly higher in the T3+T4 group than in the T1+T2 group, with a 1.40-fold increase in patients with higher primary tumor burden (P < 0.05; Fig. 2C, E).

We further assessed the association between baseline plasma miRNA levels and nodal stage. Patients were divided into the N0+N1 and N2 groups according to radiographic nodal status. The baseline level of hsa-miR-122–5p was significantly higher in the N0+N1 group than in the N2 group, with a 2.25-fold difference (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F, G). By contrast, hsa-miR-486–5p was not significantly different between the N0+N1 and N2 groups (Fig. 2F, H). These results suggest that baseline hsa-miR-122–5p is inversely associated with both primary tumor burden and nodal burden, whereas hsa-miR-486–5p is positively associated with primary tumor burden but not nodal stage.

Baseline hsa-miR-122–5p and hsa-miR-486–5p levels were associated with pathological response and survival-related outcomes

To investigate the relationship between baseline plasma miRNA levels and treatment response, patients were stratified according to postoperative pathological response. The baseline level of hsa-miR-122–5p was significantly higher in the pCR group than in the non-pCR group, with a 2.39-fold difference between the two groups (P = 0.0032; Fig. S1A, B). In contrast, the baseline level of hsa-miR-486–5p was significantly higher in the non-pCR group than in the pCR group, with a 1.76-fold difference (P = 0.0007; Fig. S1A, D).

We next explored the association between baseline levels of these two candidate miRNAs and survival time. Baseline hsa-miR-122–5p level was not significantly correlated with survival time (P = 0.511; Fig. S1C). In contrast, baseline hsa-miR-486–5p level was significantly associated with survival time (P = 0.0004; Fig. S1E), and patients with higher hsa-miR-486–5p levels tended to have shorter survival. Together, these findings indicate that a higher baseline level of hsa-miR-122–5p was associated with favorable pathological response, whereas a higher baseline level of hsa-miR-486–5p was associated with poor pathological response and unfavorable survival-related outcomes.

Dynamic changes in hsa-miR-122–5p and hsa-miR-486–5p reflected treatment response to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy

Because baseline hsa-miR-122–5p and hsa-miR-486–5p were associated with tumor burden and pathological response, we further examined whether their treatment-induced dynamic changes were related to therapeutic efficacy. Paired pre- and post-treatment plasma miRNA levels were compared within each response group.

When patients were stratified by pathological response, both hsa-miR-122–5p and hsa-miR-486–5p increased significantly after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in the pCR group. Specifically, hsa-miR-122–5p increased by 1.58-fold after treatment (P = 0.0369), and hsa-miR-486–5p increased by 1.27-fold after treatment (P = 0.0349) (Fig. 3A–C). Discussion

Mechanistically, an intact host immune system may facilitate the release or exposure of tumor neoantigens, thereby promoting the activation of tumor-specific T cells. In this context, preoperative immunotherapy may offer distinct advantages in eradicating both primary tumor cells and micrometastatic disease [23]. Given the substantial antitumor activity and durable clinical benefit achieved with ICI-based neoadjuvant strategies [24], this approach has increasingly been recommended as a preferred treatment option for patients with locally advanced NSCLC. For patients with resectable NSCLC, the identification of reliable biomarkers capable of predicting neoadjuvant treatment response and prognosis would be highly valuable for treatment optimization and improvement of long-term outcomes [25]. At present, tissue-based biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression [26], tumor mutational burden (TMB) [27], and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [28], have been introduced into clinical practice. However, these markers are limited by factors such as high cost, invasiveness, variable patient compliance, and substantial heterogeneity in clinical performance, partly owing to interindividual differences in immune status [25].

Accumulating evidence indicates that miRNAs play pivotal roles in the initiation and progression of human lung cancer. miRNAs regulate multiple malignant phenotypes, including proliferation, growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis, and have also been implicated in replicative immortality, apoptosis resistance, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming. Depending on the specific molecule and biological context, miRNAs may either promote tumor progression or facilitate immune-mediated tumor elimination [29]. Moreover, certain miRNAs can directly target PD-L1 and modulate PD-L1/cyclin E-related signaling, thereby constraining NSCLC cell proliferation [30]. These findings support an important role for miRNAs in lung cancer immunotherapy. Nevertheless, the predictive significance of miRNAs in patients with resectable NSCLC receiving neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy remains insufficiently defined.

In the present study, we analyzed plasma miRNA profiles before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in patients with resectable NSCLC and further incorporated other clinicopathological variables that might influence treatment efficacy. Rather than relying solely on baseline measurements, we focused on dynamic changes during treatment, reasoning that such longitudinal alterations might more accurately reflect therapeutic response. In principle, both ICIs and chemotherapy can influence the proliferation, migration, chemotaxis, and activation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), thereby contributing to tumor cell killing. We therefore hypothesized that tumor cells may counteract these therapeutic pressures through a series of biological responses, including the secretion of exosomes carrying characteristic molecular cargo, and that these processes may be reflected by dynamic changes in peripheral blood biomarkers [[31], [32], [33]]. On this basis, we performed plasma miRNA sequencing to delineate molecular features associated with differential treatment response. Comparative analyses of baseline and post-treatment samples further indicated that several miRNAs were significantly associated with therapeutic efficacy.

Among the 16 significantly dysregulated miRNAs identified in patients with cancer, miR-122–5p and miR-486–5p were particularly notable because of their significant associations with tumor size and nodal stage. Lower tumor burden was accompanied by higher miR-122–5p levels. In contrast, patients with T1–2 disease exhibited lower miR-486–5p expression than those with T3–4 disease. Importantly, with the progression of chemoimmunotherapy, patients who achieved pCR showed significant increases in peripheral blood miR-122–5p and miR-486–5p, whereas this pattern was not observed in patients with poor pathological response. Similarly, patients who experienced nodal downstaging during treatment also exhibited significant post-treatment increases in both miR-122–5p and miR-486–5p, while no such changes were detected in patients without evident nodal regression. Collectively, these findings suggest that both baseline expression patterns and on-treatment dynamics of specific miRNAs may provide clinically relevant information regarding tumor burden and sensitivity to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.

Because miRNAs exert their biological functions primarily through the regulation of downstream target genes, the biological roles of miR-122–5p and miR-486–5p may in part be inferred from the functions of their targets. Previous studies have identified cyclin G1 (CCNG1) and Bcl-W as direct targets of miR-122, both of which are downregulated through binding of miR-122 to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of their respective mRNAs [34,35]. Bcl-W, a member of the Bcl-2 family, plays a key role in protecting cancer cells from intrinsic apoptosis [36]. miR-122-mediated repression of Bcl-W may therefore promote apoptotic susceptibility. In addition, miR-122 has been shown to suppress CCNG1 expression [34]. CCNG1 is closely linked to tumor development and cell-cycle control, and has been reported to interact with p53-related regulatory pathways [37,38]. Taken together, these data support the notion that miR-122 functions predominantly as a tumor-suppressive miRNA and may represent a candidate molecule for antitumor intervention.

The biological significance of miR-486–5p appears to be more complex. Previous studies have systematically analyzed the target gene network of miR-486–5p in tumor cells and shown, through KEGG pathway enrichment, that multiple miR-486-associated pathways are centrally involved in NSCLC pathogenesis. By targeting CDKN1A, miR-486 may relieve cell-cycle restraint and drive uncontrolled proliferation, while regulation of RAF1 may activate the MAPK/ERK pathway and further amplify proliferative signaling. Notably, in our study, patients with favorable treatment responses exhibited significant increases in miR-486–5p during therapy, despite its association with higher baseline primary tumor burden. One possible explanation is that immune activation induced by therapy may increase miR-486–5p expression in immune cells, thereby attenuating SNAI1 signaling, promoting CD8+ T-cell migration into the tumor microenvironment, and enhancing antitumor immunity; additionally, tumor cell killing by infiltrating immune cells may lead to the release of intracellular miR-486–5p into the circulation [39,40]. These observations suggest that miR-486–5p may have context-dependent biological effects and may function not only as a regulator of oncogenic pathways but also as a potential modulator of immune responsiveness.

In our cohort, miR-122 and miR-486 were differentially expressed between patients with LUSC and healthy controls, supporting their potential diagnostic relevance. Their associations with overall survival further suggest prognostic significance. Although the causal mechanisms underlying these observations remain to be established, it is plausible that miR-122 and miR-486 influence the tumor immune microenvironment through regulation of key downstream genes. Some of these genes have been reported to correlate strongly with immune infiltration, tumor microenvironment scores, and expression of immune-related proteins in LUSC [41,42], implying that these miRNAs may participate in shaping the immune landscape and modulating antitumor immune responses. Based on our current findings, hsa-miR-122–5p appears more likely to function as a tumor-suppressive miRNA, potentially inhibiting primary tumor progression and lymph node metastasis, and may serve as a positive predictor of response to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. By contrast, hsa-miR-486–5p may be associated with increased primary tumor burden at baseline and may represent a negative predictor of treatment response before therapy. Nevertheless, both markers demonstrated dual translational potential as indicators of tumor burden and as dynamic biomarkers for treatment monitoring. Combined assessment and therapeutic modulation of these miRNAs may therefore enhance their clinical utility in precision diagnosis, response prediction, and targeted intervention for NSCLC.

Further studies are warranted to elucidate the immunoregulatory mechanisms mediated by miR-122–5p- and miR-486–5p-associated gene networks and to determine their value as biomarkers for immunotherapy. In particular, our finding that baseline miR-122–5p levels were significantly associated with pCR suggests that this miRNA may serve as a promising predictor of immunotherapy responsiveness. Overall, our results provide new insight into the immunoregulatory roles of miR-122 and miR-486 in NSCLC and may offer a rationale for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies targeting miR-122- and miR-486-mediated pathways.

Several limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. First, although patients were prospectively enrolled, the study was conducted in a single center with a limited sample size, which may have introduced selection bias and restricted the generalizability of the findings. Larger prospective studies with independent external validation are therefore required. Second, although we identified significant associations between plasma miRNA profiles and clinical response to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, we did not investigate treatment-related dynamic changes in the downstream target genes of these miRNAs. This issue merits further mechanistic and translational investigation.
