StateScribe: Towards Accessible Change Awareness Across Real-World Revisits Conference: ; April; 2026
Ruei-Che Chang, Xirui Jiang, Rosiana Natalie, Hao Chen, Vlad Roznyatovskiy, Jianzhong Zhang, Kang G. Shin, Ke Sun, Anhong Guo

Abstract
. Real-world environments evolve continuously, yet blind and low-vision (BLV) individuals often have limited access to understanding how they change over time. Unexpected or relocated objects, layout modifications, and content updates (e.g., price changes) can introduce safety risks and cognitive burden. While existing visual assistive technologies can describe immediate surroundings, they operate as one-off interactions and lack mechanisms to surface meaningful changes across revisits. Informed by a survey of 33 BLV individuals, we develop StateScribe , a system that supports accessible awareness of real-world changes across revisits. StateScribe employs a dual-layer memory architecture that integrates episodic scene memory and object-centric temporal memory to enable scalable and structured change tracking. It provides both live descriptions of the current scene, and descriptions of what has changed, when and where it occurred across revisits, such as “The shop on your right has a “CLOSED” sign; it was open at this time last week.” Our evaluation shows that StateScribe maintains high accuracy ( F 1 F_{1} -score = 83.1% ) across 11 revisits, while remaining low-latency (mean <1.42s ) and memory-efficient ( <55MB ) across 110 revisits. A user study with nine BLV participants demonstrates that StateScribe improves change awareness across revisits in three real-world locations. Finally, we discuss implications for long-term AI-assisted companions that support broader change observation using multimodal sensing, extend beyond changes to other memory capabilities, and adapt to individual users, intents, and contexts.

1.
Introduction

Real-world environments evolve continuously with many unanticipated changes,
yet blind and low-vision (BLV) individuals often have limited access to information that helps them understand how familiar spaces transform over time.
For example, newly introduced obstacles, relocated objects in shared spaces, content changes (
e.g.,
price updates), or layout modifications can pose safety risks and increase cognitive burden.
Despite these challenges, there remains a limited understanding of what constitutes meaningful real-world changes for BLV individuals, and how to convey them effectively.

While existing human-powered services (
e.g.,
Be My Eyes
(
3
)
, Aira
(
1
)
), AI-powered visual assistive technologies (
e.g.,
Seeing AI
(
52
)
Be My AI
(
30
)
, WorldScribe
(
7
)
), or live video AI (
e.g.,
ChatGPT Live Video
(
59
)
, Gemini Live
(
18
)
) can describe immediate surroundings, they typically operate as one-off sessions that retain only within-session memory.
As a result, they lack persistent 3D spatial memory of a location
(
9
)
and mechanisms for surfacing meaningful changes across revisits.
Enabling such longitudinal assistance poses several challenges:

(i)

Maintaining longitudinal memory in 3D space is difficult
, as systems must identify and store the most relevant observations from the 3D real world without overloading with redundant information.

(ii)

Scaling memory storage and retrieval over extended use is difficult
, as spatial and temporal data grow continuously, increasing retrieval latency and confusion.

(iii)

Incorporating change-awareness into real-time interactions can easily overwhelm users
, especially if updates are frequent and noisy.

Based on a survey of 33 BLV individuals, we identified categories of changes that are difficult to perceive yet significantly affect daily life, such as newly introduced obstacles (
e.g.,
scattered packages in shared apartments), changes to signage along routine commuting routes (
e.g.,
removed or added signage), and updated pricing or layout during grocery shopping.
To address these challenges, we present StateScribe, a system designed to support accessible awareness of real-world changes by using a smartphone interface and sensor data.
StateScribe employs a dual-layer memory architecture that integrates Episodic Scene Memory (ESM) and Object-centric Temporal Memory (OTM).
Specifically, ESM continuously updates information from recent visits within a bounded context window, archiving older data to maintain an up-to-date representation of the scene over time.
In contrast, OTM selectively maintains key objects, tracking their states and spatial locations across visits while recording meaningful changes.
This design enables scalable, structured, and efficient storage and retrieval.

Using these memory structures and smartphone 3D data (
e.g.,
RGB-D images and camera
intrinsics), StateScribe retrieves ESM and OTM by querying the current camera pose to
identify the most recent frames from prior visits and detect significant changes.
Based on 3D data and spatial correspondences, it generates descriptions that clearly
communicate
what has changed, and when and where the change occurred.

For example, a user revisiting the market (Figure
1
b) a week later might hear:
“The fruit stand at your 10 o’clock is selling watermelon and guava; it sold apples and oranges last week,”

“The bakery on your right now shows a ‘CLOSED’ sign; it was open at this time last week,”
or
“A construction site is at your 1 o’clock, 10 feet away.”

Beyond change descriptions, StateScribe also provides live descriptions (Figure
1
a) to support real-time scene understanding.
StateScribe also enables interactive exploration of how their environment has evolved, where users can query via speech,
e.g.,

“Has it blossomed now?”
, with responses:
“Yes, the trees have blossomed; they had not last week”
(Figure
1
b).

In a technical evaluation against two baselines, a
Live model
that actively prompts for change detection and a
Offline video model
that processes full-visit videos, StateScribe achieved an
F_{1}
-score of
83.1%
across
11
visits (291 changes), outperforming the
Live model
(40.1%) and
Offline video model
(27.4%). It also showed higher spatial accuracy, with lower clock-direction errors (in hour units;
M=0.24
vs. 1.98 and 1.86) and smaller distance estimation errors (in feet;
M=0.68
vs. 5.29 and 6.49). Additionally, StateScribe remained low-latency (
mean<1.42s
) and memory-efficient (
<55MB
) across
110
revisits to simulate long-term use.
A user study with nine BLV participants demonstrated that StateScribe enhanced change awareness across revisits in three real-world locations (
e.g.,
office, grocery store, outdoor courtyard).
Finally, we discuss implications for long-term AI-assisted companions that support broader change observation using multimodal sensing, extend beyond changes to other memory capabilities, and adapt to individual users, intents, and contexts.

3.
Formative Study: Understanding Real-World Changes Meaningful to BLV People

To address a key question,

What constitutes meaningful real-world changes for BLV people?
,
we conducted a survey (Appendix
Formative Study Questionnaire
) with 33 BLV people
(10 male, 21 female, 2 non-binary; mean age=45.1, SD=15.9), including 16 blind and 17 low-vision individuals (Table
1
).

3.1.
Results

Reported below are our findings on common real-world changes and their
impact on the everyday life of these BLV individuals.

3.1.1.
Changes during daily commutes

Participants frequently reported
temporary barriers
near construction
sites (N=28), such as fences, traffic cones, scaffolding, tape, and detours,
which obstructed their usual routes. The construction sites also sometimes

removed or obscured key landmarks
(N=7), including signage, pedestrian
signals, and tactile paving. In addition, natural changes, such as snowbanks,
flooding (N=23), and fallen or overhanging tree branches, further disrupted
familiar routes.
Furthermore, the
replacement of familiar landmarks
caused confusion,
as F15 noted:
“A bus stop that used to be just a bench was replaced with
a metal shelter. I didn’t expect it and bumped into it.”

While some high-level changes can be anticipated (
e.g.,
via weather or transit
updates), pedestrian-level changes remained difficult to detect, with little
warning (F20:
“no commotion or construction noise”
).
Participants often noticed them only after collisions, cane detection,
or others’ alerts.

3.1.2.
Changes during grocery shopping

Beyond
temporary barriers
(
e.g.,
carts or displays blocking aisles),
participants reported
spatial changes
during grocery shopping,
including product relocations (N=24) and aisle rearrangements (N=21).
These unexpected changes caused frustration (F18:
“The chip aisle
became canned food that I had to find someone to locate it.”
).
Also,
price changes and sales signage
(N=19) often went unnoticed,
leading to surprise at checkout (F6:
“I defaulted to the old price.”
).
To cope with these changes, some participants used human assistance (
e.g.,

Aira
(
1
)
) or AI-powered apps (
e.g.,
Seeing AI
(
52
)
),
occasionally scanning the environment to locate items (F28).
However, most relied on nearby shoppers or store staff and expressed
a desire for advance awareness of such changes.

3.1.3.
Changes within the home

Participants frequently encountered
temporary obstacles or clutter

(N=26), including blocked pathways (N=16), furniture rearrangements (N=11),
and seasonal decorations (N=9).
Such changes stemmed from both participants (N=9) and other household members,
with most living with family (N=14) or roommates (N=7).
For example, F20 noted that everyday household behaviors (
e.g.,
leaving boxes
on the floor) introduced disruptive clutter.
These disruptions were particularly challenging as many participants did
not use a cane in familiar home environments, leading to collisions
(F28:
“A chair left in my walking path caused me to fall.”
).
While a few low-vision participants could detect changes under good lighting,
most became aware only through physical contact, often resulting in
frustration or minor injuries.

3.2.
Summary of Meaningful Visual Changes

In sum, participants mainly relied on memory or direct encounters to navigate
familiar environments, occasionally using assistive tools (
e.g.,
white canes,
mobile AT) to manage unexpected changes. However, awareness remained largely reactive,
highlighting the need for proactive information and advance alerts to reduce
navigation difficulties, hazards, and frustration. Based on these insights, we
categorize meaningful changes as follows:

(i)

Object-Level Changes:
Changes involving the presence
or the identity of individual objects at a location.

–

Appeared:
Newly introduced objects not previously present
(
e.g.,
construction barriers, clutter).

–

Removed:
Previously existing objects that are no longer
present (
e.g.,
missing landmarks or signage).

–

Replaced:
Objects substituted by different ones at the
same location (
e.g.,
a bench replaced by a bus shelter).

(ii)

Spatial Changes:
Changes that alter the arrangement
or navigability of the environment.

–

Relocated:
Objects moved to new locations (
e.g.,
product
relocations in stores).

–

Layout Changes:
Modifications to spatial layout that
affect navigation (
e.g.,
aisle rearrangements, blocked pathways).

(iii)

Attribute Changes:
Changes in the properties or state
of existing objects without altering their identity or position (
e.g.,
price changes,
signage updates, on/off status).

4.
StateScribe

StateScribe is a system with spatial and temporal memory that observes and
describes real-world changes across visits, going beyond live descriptions
of the current scene.

For example, when a BLV user explores a new market (Figure
1
),
StateScribe provides live descriptions for scene understanding, such as

“I see a fruit stand with apples and oranges”
or
“I see
a bakery with an ‘OPEN’ sign on its door.”

Meanwhile, it continuously builds a memory from captured frames.
Upon revisiting the market a week later, StateScribe compares the current view
with stored memory and announces changes during navigation,
e.g.,

“The fruit stand at your 10 o’clock is selling watermelon and guava;
it sold apples and oranges last week,”

“The bakery on your right
now shows a ‘CLOSED’ sign; it was open at this time last week,”

or
“A construction site is at your 1 o’clock, 10 feet away.”

Users can also query temporal changes via speech,
e.g.,

“Has it
blossomed now?”
, receiving responses such as
“Yes, the trees have
blossomed; they had not last week.”

Below, we describe how StateScribe achieves this experience with different
system modules.

4.1.
Memory Construction Module

In this module, StateScribe streams essential sensor data from the smartphone
to the backend for processing and storage within a novel dual-layer memory
architecture:
(i)
an Episodic Scene Memory and
(ii)
an
Object-Centric Temporal Memory.
These memory structures are informed by our formative study, which shows that
observed changes primarily fall into three categories: single-object changes,
spatial changes (
e.g.,
multi-object rearrangement), and attribute changes within
an object.
However, this design may be limited in capturing fast motion or activity-based
changes, which may require additional sensors or computational resources.
We leave these extensions to future work and discuss them in Section
7
.

Episodic Scene Memory (ESM).

To capture the user’s visual context in real time,
we design ESM to retain full observations within a configurable temporal
context window (
e.g.,
the last 10 days, 5 hours, or single visit),
while archiving older data (Figure
2
a).
ESM serves as the primary memory retrieval source,
storing data streamed from the mobile app to the backend at one data
frame per second (FPS), including RGB-D frames, confidence maps, camera poses,
and associated metadata. Upon receipt, the backend resizes images,
refines depth using confidence maps, generates visual embeddings,
and asynchronously compresses and stores each instance as a

scene
object on local disk.

Object-centric Temporal Memory (OTM).

To enable persistent tracking of key objects, we introduce OTM,
an object-centric memory layer that maintains long-term object
dynamics beyond ESM’s temporal window.
Specifically, OTM maintains a collection of tracked real-world objects,
each represented as a chronological sequence of discrete snapshots.
Each snapshot captures the object’s state at a specific moment,
including its status relative to the previous scene (
e.g.,
appeared,
disappeared, replaced), description, visual embedding, and a world-anchored
3D bounding box (Figure
3
f,g).
A new OTM entry is created when a change is detected for an object
(
e.g.,
it appears, disappears, or is replaced).
A new snapshot is appended to an existing entry when a changed object
shares a similar 3D location with a prior OTM object, which we detail next.

4.2.
Change Detection and Memory Update Module

In this module, StateScribe identifies changes when a user revisits a location
by comparing the current frame with its ESM and OTM constructed from prior visits.
The process involves:

(i)
selecting a reference frame from ESM,

(ii)
comparing reference and current frames to detect changes,
and
(iii)
updating OTM.
We develop algorithms and integrate lightweight VLMs to balance
accuracy and latency for live interactions (Section
4.7
).

Step 1: Selecting a reference frame from ESM.

The key challenge is to develop a scalable pipeline to efficiently identify
the appropriate reference frame from the ESM as it grows over time.
To address this, StateScribe utilizes a hierarchical frame retrieval technique.
First, the current camera pose is used as a query to filter and retrieve
scene objects in the ESM with similar poses, where the differences lie
within translation (
d_{thres}
) and rotation (
\theta_{thres}
) thresholds.
Each candidate ESM object is then evaluated against the current camera
pose using a bidirectional visibility score
S_{\textit{overlap}}
.
Specifically, let
P_{r}
denote the set of valid 3D points back-projected from
a reference ESM object
r
using its RGB-D data and camera intrinsics, where each

\mathbf{p}\in P_{r}
represents a 3D point in the reference camera coordinate frame.
Let
\pi_{c}(\cdot)
denote the projection function that maps a 3D point from the
reference camera coordinate to the current camera frame
c
.
The resulting projection onto the current frame is referred to as
the
visibility map
(Figure
3
f, right).
We define the coverage ratio of
the visibility map
over
the current frame as:

(1)

o_{r\rightarrow c}=\frac{1}{|P_{r}|}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in P_{r}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\pi_{c}(\mathbf{p})\in\Omega_{c}\right]

where
\Omega_{c}
denotes the valid image domain of the current frame.
Similarly,
o_{c\rightarrow r}
is computed in the reverse direction.
The final bidirectional visibility score is defined as the harmonic mean
of the two directional coverage ratios:

(2)

S_{\text{overlap}}=\frac{2\,o_{r\rightarrow c}\,o_{c\rightarrow r}}{o_{r\rightarrow c}+o_{c\rightarrow r}}

Second, candidates that satisfy the overlap thresholds are grouped into
temporal clusters via DBSCAN
(
15
)
based on their timestamps
(Figure
3
c).
Specifically, clustering is performed with a temporal radius of
\epsilon

seconds and a minimum cluster size of
N_{c}
frames.
StateScribe then scans the clusters in chronological order, discards those whose
changes have already been announced, and selects the reference frame with
the highest visibility score
S_{\textit{overlap}}
from the first valid cluster.
In our setup, we set
d_{thres}=1.5
meters,
\theta_{thres}=40{\degree}
,

\epsilon=10
, and
N_{c}=2
.

Step 2: Comparing reference and current frames to detect changes.
Once a
reference frame is selected, StateScribe identifies scene changes by prompting a VLM
with both the reference and current frames to highlight changed objects using
bounding boxes (Prompt#
StateScribe: Towards Accessible Change Awareness Across Real-World Revisits
).
It returns the type of change for each detected object, including appeared,
removed, or content changed, along with a textual description and a confidence score.
StateScribe then rejects bounding boxes whose overlap with their corresponding visibility
masks (described in step 1) falls below a threshold
X
(Figure
3
f).
The remaining 2D bounding boxes are further filtered by confidence and size, then
passed to a segmentation model to extract object masks and generate visual embeddings.
These masks are subsequently projected into 3D space to estimate the objects’
3D locations.
Our current setup processes at 1 FPS, and we empirically set the temporal clustering parameters to
X=0.45
.

Step 3: Updating OTM.

Each detected change, along with its 2D mask, 3D bounding box, and the associated
change type and description, is then updated in OTM. We determine if a detected
object is new or corresponds to an existing OTM entry by computing the 3D Intersection
over Union (IoU) between the detected 3D bounding box
B_{new}
and a prior 3D
bounding box
B_{prior}
:

(3)

\text{IoU}(B_{new},B_{prior})=\frac{V_{int}}{V_{new}+V_{prior}-V_{int}}

where
V_{int}
denotes the intersection volume of the two boxes. StateScribe iterates
over existing objects in OTM and retains candidate prior boxes
B_{prior}

whose IoU with
B_{new}
exceeds a minimum threshold
\gamma
. For the remaining
candidates, StateScribe computes the cosine similarity between the visual embeddings of
the new and prior objects. If the similarity exceeds a threshold
Y
, the object is
treated as the same instance and recorded as a new snapshot in the corresponding OTM
entry; otherwise, it is stored as a new OTM entry. Identified change snapshots,
including change type, descriptions, reference, and current images, are then queued in the scheduling module to present (Section
4.5
).
We set
\gamma=0.08
and
Y=0.7
.

4.3.
Live Description Generation Module

In this module, StateScribe generates live visual descriptions to support the scene
understanding. Unlike prior systems that use multiple VLMs and adaptive prompting
for context-aware descriptions
(
7
;
10
)
, StateScribe adopts a simplified approach using frame filtering and a single VLM with a fixed prompt
(Prompt#
StateScribe: Towards Accessible Change Awareness Across Real-World Revisits
).
To detect new scenes, it computes visual embeddings for incoming frames and
compares them with the most recently described frame using cosine similarity;
frames exceeding a threshold (
\tau_{\text{visual}}
) are treated as redundant
and skipped.
To further reduce repetition, generated descriptions are also compared via text
embeddings with prior outputs, and those exceeding a threshold
(
\tau_{\text{text}}
) are discarded. The remaining descriptions are queued
for presentation (Section
4.5
).
We empirically set
\tau_{\text{visual}}=0.85
and
\tau_{\text{text}}=0.80
by default.

4.4.
Interactive Q&A Module

In this module, StateScribe allows users to interrupt ongoing descriptions and issue
queries. It supports change-related queries over single or multiple objects
across time, within specified intervals, and their spatial variations.
This is achieved by a Q&A agent that interprets each query and invokes tools over
ESM, OTM, and spatial data (Prompt#
StateScribe: Towards Accessible Change Awareness Across Real-World Revisits
):
(i)

ESM retrieval
for current scene queries, returning the

N
most recent frames;

4.6.
Mobile User Interface

StateScribe provides a mobile app interface (Figure
2
a).
The home screen displays visits as cards, each with a thumbnail and an AI-generated title.
When returning to a location, users can select the corresponding card to open

4.7.
Implementation Details

To enable StateScribe’s spatial and temporal memory capabilities, we use a smartphone
as the primary device to access depth information, which is not available on the current
smartglasses that typically rely on monocular cameras. We also adopt a low frame rate
(i.e., 1 FPS) to support long-term use and scalability for memory storage, retrieval,
and live interaction, in contrast to the higher frame rates used in prior
systems
(
7
;
10
)
.
Our StateScribe mobile app is deployed on an iPhone 17 Pro and relies on camera poses
and RGB-D images are continuously captured via Apple’s ARKit framework.
These data streams are transmitted to the backend at one FPS over a TCP connection.
StateScribe leverages ARKit’s built-in geometric relocalization to align the current
camera coordinates with those from prior visits to the same location.
Each location is assigned a unique identifier by the mobile application.
Upon successful relocalization, the backend uses this identifier to load the
corresponding ESM and OTM.
The backend is hosted on a MacBook M4 Max for our user study, while the technical
evaluation is conducted on a desktop with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 7965WX CPU
and an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU.
We set the ESM context window to a single visit, meaning it retains and retrieves
data only from the last visit for both the user and technical evaluation.

We adopt lightweight VLMs for live interactions to balance latency and accuracy.
Specifically, live descriptions are generated using
Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite
Preview
, while change detection, Q&A, and buffered
description summarization are handled by
Gemini 3 Flash Preview
.
FastSAM
(
67
)
is used for 2D mask segmentation. Visual embeddings
are extracted using a pre-trained DINOv3 model
(
53
)
, and
text embeddings are generated with
all-MiniLM-L12-v2
.

Datasets

To simulate extended use, we constructed a dataset of repeated visits to the same environments over time. The dataset includes three environments (Figure

5.3.3.
StateScribe’s latency and memory footprint over extended use

To model long-term usage and characterize latency and memory growth, we repeated 11 visits
10 times, yielding 110 visits per scenario. This yields 17,350 frames for Office, 17,460 for
Grocery, and 18,360 for Outdoor; each corresponds to approximately 5 hours of data at 1 FPS.
In terms of
latency
, it remains low across all scenarios, including Grocery
(Median=0.37s, Mean=1.42s), Office (Median=0.21s, Mean=1.32s), and Outdoor (Median=0.14s,
Mean=1.11s), which reflected long-tailed distributions with occasional outliers.
This indicated that most interactions were processed quickly, with only rare spikes.
To further examine latency contributions, we decomposed processing time into system components.
Across all scenarios,
VLM inference
dominated latency (Grocery: M=1.02s; Office:
M=1.08s; Outdoor: M=0.91s). In contrast,
frame queuing
for processing (M=0.11s,
0.10s, 0.09s),
reference matching
for retrieving past frame data (M=0.27s, 0.12s,
0.07s), and
post-processing
for updating OTM (M=0.01s across all scenarios)
contributed relatively minor overhead.
Notably, reference matching was higher in Grocery (M=0.27s) than in Office (M=0.12s) and
Outdoor (M=0.07s), likely due to the denser scenes increasing matching complexity.
Overall, these results suggest that StateScribe maintains stable performance over extended use,
with latency primarily bounded by VLM inference rather than system-level overhead.

Over extended use for 110 visits, StateScribe maintained a bounded memory footprint that scales
proportionally with the number of visits. The final OTM size across 110 visits was 49.4 MB
(Grocery), 54.6 MB (Office), and 39.4 MB (Outdoor). The Office scenario exhibited the largest
memory due to more change snapshots (N=1,517) of tracked objects (N=204), while Grocery showed
moderate growth with moderate snapshots (N=1,107) of tracked objects (N=192), and Outdoor
remained smaller due to fewer snapshots (N=999) of tracked objects (N=174).

Overall, latency did not increase with OTM size, as our memory architecture reads only prior-
visit data from ESM, discards outdated information, and stores only essential object changes
in OTM. The current scale of OTM (110 visits) also remains computationally manageable
during retrieval.

6.3.
Results

6.3.1.
How effectively does StateScribe support users in perceiving
and understanding changes in different environments?

Overall, participants successfully completed most tasks within around 10 minutes, achieving
completion rates of 82.9% (shared office), 82.7% (grocery), and 89.6% (outdoor).
In the shared office scenario, issues were mainly due to camera aiming: some regions
(e.g., parts of desks) were not captured, leading to incomplete memory, and subtle
view changes, such as slightly pulled-out chairs, were hard to detect (4/9 misses).
In the grocery scenario, removing the cart from the aisle had a lower completion rate (50%)
than in other tasks, as participants focused the camera on nearby shelf items,
and often missed objects at the periphery of the scene.
Consequently, the shopping cart, positioned near the edge of the scene, was
often partially or not captured at all.
Additionally, sparkling water boxes on the upper shelves introduced visual ambiguity,
leading to missed detections of Lay’s chips (3/8 misses).
In contrast, the outdoor scenario yielded higher performance, as the larger space
and sparser object layout made changes easier to capture and detect.

6.3.2.
How do participants perceive the user experience
of StateScribe?

Participants rated StateScribe as easy to learn and use (M=5.8, SD=0.8), noting that automatic
descriptions and the hold-to-ask interaction were intuitive. However, some found manually
retrieving prior visits cumbersome, suggesting solutions by
“using GPS and
auto-relocalization when the camera is always on, like smartglasses”
(P1).
Participants perceived the descriptions as accurate (M=6.0, SD=1.0) and expressed high trust
in StateScribe (M=5.7, SD=1.4), supported by temporal and directional details (also evidenced
by our technical evaluation at Section
5.3.2
).
For instance, P8 noted
“it was cool how it could say 7 minutes ago was this,
5 minutes ago was that,”
while P3 emphasized that
“exact directionality makes
me confident to trust the system.”

These perceptions were corroborated by low-vision users (P1, P6, P8), who could verify
details at close range, and blind users, who found descriptions consistent over time
(e.g., P4:
“when it matches what it said before, that gives me confidence that
it’s accurate.”
).
Although participants reported reasonable coverage of the objects of interest
(M=5.2, SD=1.6), they desired richer contextual interactions from StateScribe. For example,
P4 wanted descriptions tailored to intent (
“narrowly focused if I am only
interested in chips”
), while P1 preferred a more human-like tone, such as reminders
that
“You were here two days ago and did [X], and [Y] was present at that time.”

Participants were impressed by StateScribe’s memory capabilities, which are not supported by
current off-the-shelf visual assistive technologies. For example, P5 described it as a

“promising integration with Seeing AI scan function,”
while P3 noted,

“I’m astounded at how well it remembers and describes differences as you move.
That’s not something Be My AI can do.”

Participants also found the descriptions helpful (M=5.9, SD=1.2) and expressed willingness
to use StateScribe in real-world settings (M=5.6, SD=1.4).
For example, P8 described a bus navigation experience, noting that StateScribe could be helpful:

“Maps told me to stand in one place to catch the bus today, but it was relocated by
a couple of hundred feet, so I had to walk around to find it. This [StateScribe] could help me
find the relocation out more quickly.”

P2, who was a mental health counselor, suggested
“it could be helpful to know
the person’s attire, like in military manner or slovenly from time to time, and if their
facial expression changes during conversation.”

Despite positive feedback, some participants reported information overload and wanted
more control over the information delivered, especially when using other aids.
For example, P6 suggested omitting changes, like
“A chair appeared in front of you.
It was not there before,”
when already perceived with a cane.
We discuss potential improvements further in later sections.

7.
Discussion and Future Work

We discuss our lessons learned and design implications for extending StateScribe to
broader use cases in the long term.

Device choices and interface constraints.

Camera-based ATs face persistent challenges, including object aiming

(
21
;
57
;
37
;
43
;
50
;
36
)
,
content coverage
(
25
)
, and social acceptability
(
34
;
48
;
2
)
.
StateScribe currently relies on sensory inputs from a smartphone (RGB-D images and camera poses)
to construct spatial memory. However, a depth camera requires an RGB camera with a standard
FoV for alignment, limiting coverage and demanding more precise aiming.
This may explain higher performance outdoors (89.6%) than in denser indoor settings
(82.7% grocery, 82.9% office).
Some participants also raised usability concerns about continuous handheld use.
We envision that StateScribe can generalize to such wearables with RGB-D input and camera pose
tracking to offer hands-free interaction
(
10
)
, though privacy and social
concerns could remain
(
2
)
.
Finally, StateScribe required manual selection of prior visits.
Future work could enable automatic relocalization (e.g., via GPS) to streamline revisits.

Detecting more types of changes with additional sensors.

As noted in Section
4.1
, StateScribe focuses on object-level changes,
enabling effective real-time detection and across revisits. However, at 1 FPS,
it may miss fast or transient changes (e.g., moving cars, flashing lights, or human
activities). Although increasing frame rate could help, it may introduce trade-offs
in computation, storage, and retrieval.
An alternative is to augment StateScribe with additional sensors. For instance, event cameras
can capture high-frequency motion and light changes asynchronously, while infrared cameras
can detect otherwise invisible changes such as temperature variations (e.g., heating or
cooling objects).
Integrating these modalities could expand the range of detectable changes and
improve robustness by referencing different sensor outputs.

Integrating different information cursors to deliver the right information timely.

StateScribe uses the user’s 3D location (from camera pose) as an information cursor, a mechanism
to indicate information of interest, to retrieve memory, and detect changes across revisits.
However, our study showed that user needs extended beyond change detection and vary by
context, and StateScribe does not always surface the most relevant information in time.
For example, participants often held items and sought visual details (
e.g.,
color
or flavor) in grocery settings, or needed detailed information (
e.g.,
whether
stairs go up or down) and signage texts in outdoor settings.
These needs could be better supported by prior systems that use different user activities
as information cursors, such as leveraging hand-object interactions as intent
cues
(
42
;
37
;
36
)
, or using camera motion to obtain adaptive visual details
(
7
)
.
Together, these works shift visual assistance from reactive question answering to
proactive interaction.
However, effectively integrating multiple information cursors and delivering the right
information at the right time remains an open challenge.
Future work should explore how to model user context, select appropriate information cursors,
and map user intent to timely descriptions.

Towards a long-term assistive AI companion.

To our best knowledge, StateScribe is the first AI-driven live description system with memory
for change detection, outperforming both a VLM-only
Live model
and an

Offline video model
(Section
5.3.1
). It also scales to extended use
across three scenarios with low latency and memory overhead (Section
5.3.3
).
However, challenges remain in making it a reliable long-term assistive companion.
Participants expected StateScribe to account for their assistive context (e.g., using a white cane)
or information preference to reduce redundant information, and to provide richer contextual
reminders when revisiting locations, similar to a human companion (Section
6.3
).
Furthermore, memory should extend beyond object-level changes to capture semantic
relationships and structured knowledge within environments.
For example, participants wondered if StateScribe’s memory could extend beyond individual
object changes to a more holistic, global understanding (e.g., store layout,
aisle-level organization).
This could be enabled by integrating structured scene memory as an additional
layer
(
68
;
28
;
16
)
, potentially augmented
by community-contributed data collected over time.
Future work could explore designing AI companions that better model human-like guidance,
adapt to users’ evolving contexts, and integrate more global, structured representations
of space for meaningful interactions.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced StateScribe, a system that enables accessible awareness of
real-world changes across revisits. Informed by a formative study, we identified meaningful
change categories for BLV people and developed a dual-layer memory architecture comprising
Episodic Scene Memory and Object-centric Temporal Memory.
This architecture supports real-time change detection, persistent tracking across revisits,
and enhanced scene understanding through accurate spatial descriptions, live descriptions,
and queries over current and past changes.
Our technical evaluation shows that StateScribe detected changes more accurately than the
VLM-only live model or offline video model while remaining low-latency and memory-efficient
for extended use. A user study further showed that StateScribe helped BLV people effectively
identify changes across revisits in different locations.
Through this work, we also recognized that enabling an AI-assisted companion requires
broader observation of changes, reasoning over those changes, and adapting information
to individual users, intents, and contexts.

Prompt 1: Visual Change Detection System Prompt

System Prompt

Goal: report only real, substantive scene-content changes, with confidence for each reported change.

Safety priority:

•

A false positive is 100x worse than a miss.

Never invent a change or a bbox just to satisfy the JSON format.

Never report these:

•

Viewpoint, perspective, or parallax differences from camera motion.

•

Lighting, shadow, reflection, glare, exposure, or background ambiance changes.

•

Apparent text loss caused by glare/exposure.

Objects that only shifted position or orientation without a real state/content change, except for clear location changes of large furniture or fixtures.

Slight object movement or small repositioning.

Humans. Ignore people entirely and never place a bbox on them.
