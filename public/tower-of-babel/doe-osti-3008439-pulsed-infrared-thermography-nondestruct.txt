Abstract
SiC-SiCf composites, consisting of silicon carbide fibers embedded in a silicon carbide matrix, are
advanced materials with high thermal conductivity, temperature stability, and resistance to
radiation damage. Traditional methods for quality control of fabricated SiC-SiCf composites
involve nondestructive evaluation (NDE) with X-ray computed tomography (XCT). However,

1. Introduction
SiC-SiCf composites, consisting of silicon carbide fibers embedded in a silicon carbide matrix, are
advanced materials with high thermal conductivity, temperature stability, and resistance to
radiation damage. Because of these material properties, SiC-SiCf composites are promising fuel
cladding materials candidates for light water reactors (LWR) and high temperature gas cooled
reactors (HTGR). Traditional methods for quality control of fabricated SiC-SiCf composites
involve nondestructive evaluation (NDE) with X-ray computed tomography (XCT). However,
XCT imaging of typical SiC-SiCf structures for cladding applications can involve several hours.
In this project, we investigate an alternative approach to NDE of SiC-SiC composites that involves
rapid (on the order of seconds) imaging with Pulsed infrared thermography (PIT). Imaging of
internal material defects with PIT involves deposit of a thermal pulse on the specimen surface and
recording the surface temperature with fast frame infrared (IR) camera while heat is diffusing into
the specimen bulk [1-6]. Material defects, such as cracks, voids, and delamination have lower
thermal diffusivity than the material matrix. Because of thermal resistance of defects results in
locally slower heat decay from the material surface. Therefore, observation of the transient surface
temperature allows to image internal material defects. The goal of this project is to investigate PIT
imaging of planar and tubular SiC-SiCf specimens.

2. Pulsed Infrared Thermography Imaging of Planar SiC-SiCf
 Specimens
Preliminary development investigated PIT imaging of engineered SiC-SiCf composite architecture
consisting of outer monolithic SiC layers for a planar design. The specimens manufactured by
General Atomics (GA) are 12 x 12 x 1.3 mm SiC composite with a 0.05mm thick monolith layer
on both sides. Photographs of the front side with unpolished outer SiC monolith and back side with
polished SiC monolith are shown in Figures 1(a) and 1(b), respectively.

 (a) (b)
Figure 1 – Photographs of General Atomics engineered SiC-SiCf composite planar specimen with
12 x 12 x 1.3mm dimensions and 0.05 mm thick monolith layer on both sides. (a) Front side with
unpolished outer SiC monolith. (b) Back side with polished outer SiC monolith.

 PIT imaging was performed using a megapixel fast-frame infrared camera (FLIR X8501SC,
3-5 μm spectral range), a 50 mm lens with 1.8 cm extender rings, and a 6.4 kJ flash lamp delivering
a 2 ms thermal pulse. Images were recorded at 473.4 Hz frequency for a total imaging time of 2.54
seconds. yielding a spatial resolution of approximately 31 μm per pixel. Thermograms were pre-
processed by subtracting pre-flash frames to enhance contrast and reveal transient thermal
signatures indicative of subsurface anomalies.
 Figure 2(a) shows a thermography frame of the SiC-SiCf composite specimen front side
immediately after flash, visualizing subsurface porosity defects in monolith. Figure 2(b) shows a
thermography frame of the SiC-SiCf composite specimen front side 2ms after flash, visualizing
subsurface SiCf fibers. Figure 2(c) shows a thermography frame of the SiC-SiCf composite
specimen back side immediately after flash, visualizing subsurface porosity defects in monolith.
Figure 2(d) shows a thermography frame of the back side 2ms after flash, visualizing subsurface
SiCf fibers. Qualitatively, thermography images made from the side with unpolished monolith
layer have better visibility of fiber fine features (Figure 2(b)) than the thermography images made
from the polished side of the specimens (Figure 2(d)).

 Imaging results in Figure 2 indicate that PIT can visualize subsurface SiCf fibers and to
distinguish between the SiCf fibers and the SiC matrix within the composite without extensive
image processing. SiCf fibers and the SiC matrix exhibit differences in thermal properties due to
their microstructural variations. SiCf fibers, often polycrystalline with a preferred orientation, can
have thermal conductivities in the range 10-20 W/mK, depending on their diameter and
concentration of impurities. The SiC matrix, which may be more amorphous or have a different
grain structure, can have higher thermal conductivity, typically in the range 50-100 W/mK.

 To confirm the ability of PIT to image the structure of SiCf, we compare qualitatively the PIT
and X-ray transmission images of the same specimens in Figure 3. Figure 3(a) displays the PIT
image of the front side of the specimen with unpolished monolith 2ms after flash (same as Figure
2(b)), and Figure 3(b) displays X-ray transmission image of the same specimen. Because X-ray
transmission is sensitive to density variations, the X-ray transmission image visualizes subsurface
SiCf fibers.

3. Computer Vision Analysis of Defects in PIT Images of SiC
 Monolith
 A computer vision analysis was performed to isolate and quantify material defects in the
monolith in the thermogram of specimen's front side (unpolished monolithic layer) immediately
after the flash (Figure 4(a)). The region of the thermogram inside the dashed red line was
thresholded to reveal porosity defects in the monolith (Figure 4(b)).

 (a) (b)
Figure 4 – (a) Thermogram of front side with unpolished monolith immediately after flash (b)
Multi-level thresholding based on maximum correlation for better visualization of defects.

 In the computer vision analysis, multi-level thresholding based on maximum correlation
criteria segmented potential defect in the monolith. This was followed by topological structural
analysis that enumerated defects and computed geometric descriptors, including major/minor axes
of fitted ellipses, area, perimeter, Feret diameter, circularity, roundness, and solidity. Feret
diameter is defined as the longest distance between boundary points (F = P/π, where P is the
perimeter). Circularity indicates how closely a shape resembles a perfect circle. Particle roundness
is a measure of how sharp or smooth the edges and corners of a particle are. Solidity quantifies the
compactness of a particle or object in an image. It is calculated as the ratio of the object's area to
the area of its convex hull. The convex hull of a particle in an image is the smallest convex polygon
that encloses all the particle's pixels.
 The parameters for the 23 potential defects in the monolith in Figure 4(b) are listed in Table 1.
The Feret diameter values range from 0.047 mm (minimum) to 0.408 mm (maximum), with an
average of 0.128 mm. Circularity values averaged 0.93, indicating predominantly near-circular
shapes consistent with porosity voids, while solidity metrics (average 0.91) suggested compact,
non-fragmented structures.

4. Conclusions
 We investigate an alternative approach to NDE of SiC-SiCf composites using rapid imaging
with Pulsed infrared thermography (PIT). PIT images of a planar SiC-SiCf specimen show the
structure of the surface monolith layer and internal SiCf structures. The capability of PIT imaging
in visualizing SiCf structures is qualitatively confirmed by observing similarity in the PIT and X-
ray transmission images of the same specimen. Computer vision analysis of defects in the PIT
image of the monolith was performed with thresholding followed by topological structural analysis
that computed geometric descriptors, including major/minor axes of fitted ellipses, area, perimeter,
Feret diameter, circularity, roundness, and solidity. These results demonstrate PIT's efficacy in
resolving micro-defects in SiC-SiCf cladding, where voids or delamination could compromise
integrity under high temperature and irradiation environment. By automating enumeration and
sizing, the approach reduces subjectivity and supports high-throughput quality control during
manufacturing. Future efforts will focus on refining computer vision-based algorithms for defects
detection. This will include development of defect depth estimation using thermal diffusivity
models, incorporating layer-specific properties (e.g., thermal conductivity, density, and specific
heat for Cr-coating, monolithic SiC, and SiC-SiC composite layers). Additionally, PIT system
upgrades—including dual-pulse excitation—will boost heat delivery and improve PIT imaging
resolution.
