Prey-predator dynamics with additional food and Holling type-III response: application to Bemisia tabaci
Authors: D. Bhanu Prakash, Ch Chaitanya, D. K. K. Vamsi, Ahmad Aloqaily, Nabil Mlaiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interactions between pests and their natural enemies play a central role in ecological balance and sustainable pest management. In the present work, we examine a prey–predator system in which the predator population experiences intra-specific competition and receives an external food supplement. The feeding mechanism of the predator follows a Holling type-III functional response. We demonstrate that the proposed formulation admits a unique, globally positive solution and investigate the stability of its equilibria. Analytical exploration reveals the occurrence of distinct bifurcation phenomena, including transcritical and saddle-node transitions. Numerical investigations further illustrate the emergence of hysteresis loops, indicating bistability within specific parameter ranges. Experimental observations concerning the predation of Bemisia tabaci by Orius albidipennis validate the Holling type-III assumption and serve to parameterize the theoretical model. The findings underscore the dual impact of additional food and predator competition in shaping biological control outcomes and improving pest suppression efficiency.

Introduction

Biological control, which employs living organisms to regulate pest populations, is a vital component of environmentally responsible pest management. Traditional chemical pesticides often yield quick results but introduce ecological and health-related side effects. In contrast, biocontrol methods aim to maintain agricultural productivity while preserving ecological integrity1. However, the success of such methods depends on the complex dynamics between pests, their predators, and the surrounding environment. Without careful design, even a beneficial intervention can destabilize an ecosystem and cause the outbreak or extinction of species.

Mathematical modelling provides a quantitative approach to understanding these interactions. Prey–predator systems capture how populations evolve through mutual influences, enabling researchers to identify stable coexistence regimes, thresholds for extinction, and possible oscillatory behaviours. Among various forms of predator feeding responses, the Holling type-III (sigmoidal) form has received particular attention. It characterizes situations where predation is weak at low prey densities due to predator learning or prey refuge effects and accelerates as prey become more abundant. Such behaviour is commonly observed in insect predators, sometimes referred to as “lazy predators”, which respond more actively only when prey are sufficiently available.

A growing body of research has refined these models by incorporating ecological mechanisms such as prey grouping, predator interference, harvesting pressure, and the influence of additional food resources2–5. The inclusion of non-reproductive supplemental food alters predator survival and functional response characteristics, which can either stabilize or destabilize the ecosystem depending on food quality and abundance6,7. Analytical and numerical studies in this area have revealed that these factors often lead to bifurcations, multi-stability, or even chaotic transitions, emphasizing the need for rigorous mathematical treatment8–10.

Beyond qualitative dynamics, several works have explored control-oriented formulations of predator–prey interactions. Optimal and time-optimal control approaches aim to determine effective intervention strategies, such as adjusting food supplementation or predator release rates, to reach desired population levels while minimizing time or cost11,12. These frameworks bridge theoretical ecology and practical pest management by converting ecological processes into systems amenable to optimization.

To enhance sustainability in pest suppression, research increasingly focuses on ecological modeling and predator-based control mechanisms. The management of key pests such as Tetranychus urticae using biological agents under controlled conditions demonstrates the potential for integrating multiple natural enemies within polyhouse systems13. Mathematical approaches have further advanced understanding by incorporating factors such as additional food resources and interspecific competition into prey-predator models, yielding refined predictions of system stability and control efficacy14. Studies on eco-epidemiological stability15, predator harvesting effects in stochastic environments16, and delay-induced dynamics in Leslie-Gower models17 contribute to the theoretical foundation of these systems. Furthermore, bifurcation analyses of additional food-provided systems with anti-predator behavior extend this understanding, offering valuable insights into the nonlinear mechanisms underlying biological control efficiency18.

From an applied perspective, Bemisia tabaci (whitefly) is a significant agricultural pest responsible for severe yield losses in multiple crops. Its biological suppression through predatory bugs such as Orius albidipennis has been studied experimentally, with the predator exhibiting a type-III functional response to prey density19. This feature makes the system ideal for examining the combined roles of predator competition and supplemental feeding in maintaining long-term stability.

The combination of these three mechanisms—intra-specific competition among predators, provision of supplementary food, and a Holling type-III (sigmoidal) functional response—is ecologically well-motivated. First, predatory arthropods such as Orius spp. are known to exhibit interference and contest competition at high densities, which limits their per-capita consumption rate10. Second, supplementary food (e.g., pollen, factitious prey, or sugar solutions) is a standard augmentative strategy that sustains predator populations during periods of low pest density7. Third, the sigmoidal (type-III) response captures prey-switching and learning behavior frequently observed in generalist predators: at low pest densities predation is negligible, but it accelerates sharply once a density threshold is crossed. Individually, each mechanism has been studied; however, their combined effect—particularly how supplementary food interacts with intra-specific competition under a sigmoidal response—remains unexplored. This gap motivates the present formulation.

Recent studies further support the relevance of these combined mechanisms. Mishra and Parshad20 demonstrated that cannibalistic interactions among natural enemies, when coupled with additional food, can produce qualitatively different dynamics than either mechanism alone. Similarly, Mishra et al.21 showed that harvesting interventions in additional-food systems lead to complex bifurcation structures including imperfect bifurcations22. These findings underscore the importance of studying multiple ecological mechanisms simultaneously.

The present study formulates and analyses a prey–predator system that includes intra-specific competition among predators and external food support. The main objectives are to identify equilibrium states, assess their stability, and detect possible bifurcation scenarios. The investigation further incorporates a time-optimal control problem where the quality and quantity of the additional food act as controllable variables. Such a framework provides insights into how auxiliary feeding strategies can enhance biological control efficiency without disrupting ecosystem balance.

Provision of additional food - consequences

In this section, we present the consequences of providing additional food by studying the possibility of existence in three different scenarios: Pest eradication, pest dominance and the coexistence of pest and natural enemies.

The ecological implications of supplementary feeding are multifaceted. Although intended to support predator populations, indiscriminate provisioning can undermine pest suppression. When excessive, it may reduce predation pressure on pests, allowing their resurgence; when insufficient, predators fail to survive.

Three qualitative outcomes can be recognized. First, complete pest eradication is theoretically impossible under the given functional response, as the pest-free equilibrium is never globally stable. Second, dominance of the pest species arises only under specific bistable conditions where predators fail to establish. Third, stable coexistence occurs when parameters favour balanced growth and competition.

As pest-free equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_2$$\end{document} is saddle whenever it exists, the arbitrary provision of additional food can never lead to the complete pest eradication for this system irrespective of the initial behaviour of the system in the absence of additional food.

Now consider the case of pest dominance. In this system, there is no clear case of pest dominance possible as the natural enemy free equilibrium is stable only in the regions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{13}, \ A_{14},\ A_{25}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{35}$$\end{document}. However in these regions, the system exhibits bistability where both the equilibria \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E^*$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_1$$\end{document} are stable. The dynamics goes to one of these equilibria depending on the initial condition. Therefore, altering the natural enemy population can change the stability of the system and avoid pest dominance stage.

In the rest of the regions, only interior equilibria \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E^*$$\end{document} is stable. As the system can exhibit upto 5 interior equilibria and the real positive equilibria are either saddle or stable, the system can exhibit bistability among the interior equilibria which highlights the importance of initial prey and predator populations. Therefore, choosing the initial populations so that the system lead to the interior equilibria with least pest population will be the best strategy to reduce pest dominance. Also it is important to note that the minimum pest population that can be reached is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\epsilon }{1+\frac{\epsilon }{\gamma }}$$\end{document}. This highlights the importance of intra-specific competition for the pest management.

Practical biocontrol guidelines

The above mentioned theoretical findings translate into the following actionable recommendations for biological control practitioners: Gradual predator augmentation over mass release. The hysteresis analysis (Section 5.4) shows that the system’s long-term state depends on the path taken through parameter space. The sensitivity of model outcomes to these assumed parameters is explored through the heatmaps in Figures 12, 13 and the stability region plots in Figures 14, 15, which sweep broad parameter ranges rather than relying on single point estimates.

The experimental validation presented in this section is structured in three tiers. First, the Holling type-III functional response assumption - the core biological hypothesis of the model - is directly validated by fitting the consumption data of Shahpouri et al.19 to the sigmoidal curve, yielding parameters c = 0.967 and a = 11.83 (Fig. 11). Second, the persistence threshold predicted by Lemma 4.2 is shown to be consistent with the sustained predation observed across all tested prey densities in19. Third, the bistability prediction under supplementary feeding constitutes a falsifiable experimental hypothesis, stated explicitly in Table 3.

Discussions and conclusions

The study provides a comprehensive assessment of a prey–predator system incorporating intra-specific competition and an external food source under a Holling type-III response. The analysis confirms that the system admits bounded solutions and diverse equilibria whose stability depends sensitively on ecological parameters. Bifurcation and hysteresis analyses reveal that multiple equilibria may coexist, and transitions between them can be abrupt. Such behaviour implies that ecological control strategies must avoid parameter regions that induce bistability, as small perturbations could shift the system toward pest dominance.

This study comprehensively studied the importance of additional food and intra-specific competition in the dynamics of the system (2). The global dynamics of the system is studied in section 6. In this section, we observed that the provision of additional food can lead to the bistability condition. The system can converge to the pest dominant equilibrium or any of the 5 possible interior equilibria based on the initial conditions. Taking this analysis further, it is observed from the theorem 4.4 that the minimum equilibria pest population is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\epsilon }{1+\frac{\epsilon }{\gamma }}$$\end{document}. Therefore, in the case where the stable equilibrium is only any of the interior equilibria, this work gives the least amount of stable pest population, which is directly proportional to the intra-specific competition (ε). As intra-specific competition is due to prey scarcity, higher competition leads to the difficulty in reaching the equilibrium state.

Figure 5 depicted the nature of various equilibria with respect to intra-specific competition. This plot depicts the bistability region where the system exhibits two stable interior equilibria. As ε changes with time over the region [0.002, 0.02], the system stabilises either in the top branch or bottom branch of the bistability region depending on the path taken by the trajectory. The dependence on the history of the trajectory plays a crucial role in the biological control of pests. As pest eradication is never stable in this system, keeping the pest at low levels so that they won’t harm the crop, is the next best available strategy. Therefore, the bottom branch in the bistability region is more desirable to the top branch for pest management. Therefore, to remain in the bottom branch, it is advisable to increase natural enemies population over time rather than mass release of natural enemies for this case. Also, the sudden jump after the second saddle-node bifurcation can lead to immediate increase of pests. This sudden surge is drastic not only because of the damage the increased pest can cause to the crop but also the reversibility of damage takes time as the system should reach the first saddle-node bifurcation point. These conclusions provide valuable insights to the biocontrol strategies which is a double-edged sword.

The model suggests that optimal management requires careful calibration of both the amount and nutritional quality of supplemental food. Maintaining predators near the coexistence equilibrium ensures minimal pest density while avoiding destabilization. Intra-specific competition plays a protective role by preventing predator overpopulation and the resulting oscillatory outbreaks.

Empirical observations confirm that Orius albidipennis follows a Holling type-III functional response in its predation on Bemisia tabaci, consistent with the findings of Shahpouri et al. (2019)19. The estimated model parameters (c = 0.967, a = 11.83) effectively capture the nonlinear feeding dynamics and provide a realistic foundation for theoretical analysis. The validated model successfully reproduces the experimental trends and delineates stability thresholds that align with established ecological predictions. These outcomes highlight the robustness of the Holling type-III framework in describing predator efficiency at varying prey densities and underscore its relevance in designing biocontrol strategies for sustainable pest management.

The model adopts a deterministic framework; incorporating environmental and demographic variability in future work could better capture dynamics near critical thresholds. It assumes a well-mixed, spatially uniform system, and can be extended to include spatial heterogeneity, dispersal, and patch dynamics for greater ecological realism. Parameterisation currently relies on limited empirical estimation; further experimental studies could enhance accuracy and predictive strength. The framework focuses on a single prey–predator interaction, with scope to expand toward multi-species systems and more complex ecological relationships.

From a practical perspective, the findings highlight that biocontrol is not a linear process. The quality of additional food, competition among predators, and historical population trajectories collectively shape long-term outcomes. Sustainable pest management therefore requires adaptive strategies informed by both theoretical and empirical evidence. Future extensions of this work could incorporate stochastic environmental effects, spatial dispersal, or evolutionary adaptation in predator behaviour. Such additions would enhance the realism of the model and provide deeper insight into how ecological and environmental variability influence biological control success.

From a management perspective, the following implications deserve emphasis:

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) design. The model’s prediction that pest eradication is never globally stable under a type-III response (Section 6) has a direct IPM implication: practitioners should aim for suppression below economic injury levels rather than eradication. The minimum achievable pest density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\epsilon }{1+\epsilon /\gamma }$$\end{document} provides a theoretical lower bound that can guide the setting of action thresholds.

Timing of interventions. The hysteresis analysis implies that the order of management actions matters. For instance, first reducing pest density through a targeted intervention (e.g., a compatible reduced-risk pesticide) and then releasing predators positions the system on the low-pest branch, from which it is unlikely to jump to the high-pest state unless competition increases dramatically.

Monitoring for early warning. The proximity to saddle-node bifurcation points can be detected through increased variance and autocorrelation in pest population time series—classical early warning signals of critical transitions26. Incorporating such monitoring into biocontrol programmes would allow pre-emptive adjustments to supplementary feeding or predator release rates.
