Role of trust and trustworthiness in implementation science studies: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Whitney C Irie, Patricia Maritim, Mungabo Chongo, Cory D. Bradley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImplementation science seeks to promote the adoption and sustainment of evidence-based interventions across diverse settings. While technical and organisational determinants of implementation have been extensively studied, relational factors—particularly trust and trustworthiness—remain under-theorised despite being frequently invoked in implementation research. Trust is often embedded implicitly within broader constructs such as stakeholder engagement, partnership quality or acceptability, yet is rarely explicitly defined, systematically measured or positioned clearly within implementation frameworks. No systematic review has comprehensively synthesised how trust functions within implementation science studies.Methods and analysisThis systematic review protocol follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines and is registered with PROSPERO. We will conduct a targeted search on Scopus for 14 journals that are recognised by experts as being the most relevant in publishing implementation science literature. Supplemented by hand searching through Global Implementation Research and Applications. Studies examining trust or trustworthiness as a variable (explicitly or implicitly through related constructs) in relation to implementation processes or outcomes will be included. Two reviewers will independently screen titles/abstracts and full texts using Rayyan. Data extraction will capture trust conceptualisation, measurement approaches, levels of operation, constituent perspectives and inferred relationships with implementation outcomes. Quality assessment will use design-appropriate tools (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme for qualitative studies, Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool for mixed-methods). Given anticipated heterogeneity, we will conduct narrative and thematic synthesis rather than meta-analysis, exploring patterns by study design, setting, theoretical underpinnings, implementation phase and trust level.Ethics and disseminationThis systematic review of published literature does not require ethical approval. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations at implementation science networks and non-technical summaries for community partners and policymakers.PROSPERO registration numberPROSPERO 2025 CRD420251270920.

Background

Implementation science and the under-theorisation of trust

Implementation science seeks to understand and improve processes through which evidence-based interventions (EBIs), programmes, policies and practices are adopted, implemented with fidelity and sustained within real-world settings.1 Over the past two decades, the field has made substantial progress in developing implementation frameworks, identifying determinants of implementation success, cataloguing implementation strategies and specifying implementation outcomes distinct from clinical or service outcomes.1–4

Despite these advances, relational and social dimensions of implementation, particularly trust and trustworthiness, remain under-theorised and inconsistently examined.5 Trust is frequently invoked across implementation studies through constructs such as patient/constituent engagement,6 7 partnership quality,8 credibility of evidence or implementers,9 institutional legitimacy10 and transparency.5 These constructs often function as mechanisms shaping implementation trajectories, yet trust itself is rarely explicitly defined, systematically measured or positioned clearly within implementation frameworks. Instead, trust frequently appears embedded within broader implementation constructs such as acceptability or feasibility, limiting our ability to build cumulative knowledge about trust’s distinct role in implementation processes and outcomes.

Defining trust in implementation contexts

Trust has been defined across disciplines as ‘a psychological state that determines a person’s willingness to be vulnerable under conditions of risk or uncertainty and interdependence’.11 This formulation foregrounds three conditions: the trustor’s vulnerability, the presence of risk or uncertainty about outcomes and interdependence between trustor (the one extending trust) and trustee (recipient or holder of that trust). Within healthcare contexts, trust has been further specified as the willingness to act on the words, actions and decisions of another.12 Within those operating at multiple levels—interpersonal trust between providers and patients or among team members, organisational trust in leadership and institutions, institutional trust in health systems and policies and community trust in interventions and implementers, including implementation and care delivery practices.10 13

Three conditions make trust consequential: vulnerability, risk or uncertainty and interdependence. Analogously, these are also constitutive features of implementation. Normalisation Process Theory’s framing of implementation as the process by which ‘the strategic intentions of one group of actors are translated into the everyday practices of others’14 makes this relational structure explicit. Implementation processes require clinicians, teams and organisational leaders to disengage from familiar practices and commit to less certain alternatives, a transition that produces cognitive vulnerability to the credibility of the EBI, professional vulnerability to the competence and integrity of those designing and supporting implementation and structural vulnerability to organisational and policy actors whose decisions govern the conditions of practice change. What May et al14 make clear in their definition is that translation among actors with differential power, knowledge and stakes is not possible without a basis for cooperative action under uncertainty. Thus, Anderson and Griffith’s15 formulation of trust-as-behaviour, the degree to which actors choose to cooperate under conditions of risk and interdependence, applies directly to implementing teams and extends to configurations that include patients as participants in implementation work. Treating

Trustworthiness, closely related but conceptually distinct, refers to characteristics that make an individual, organisation or system worthy of trust, such as demonstrated competence, benevolence, integrity and transparency.16 Whereas trust characterises the psychological state of the trustor, trustworthiness focuses on the attributes of the trustee as the antecedent that gives trustors reason to trust, meaning that the work of building trust depends substantively on the actions and characteristics of the trustee rather than the trustor.15 Drawing on Hardin’s encapsulation model,17 an actor, institution and in the case of implementation, the innovation and implementation process is perceived as trustworthy when it demonstrates a material interest in the welfare of those who are asked to trust it. The review anticipates that encapsulation will be implicit in strategies that seek to build buy-in, in methods using co-design or other participatory approaches and in the process of scientific inquiry engaged through research partnerships. Through the spectre of structural commitments, operational processes and observable outcomes,15 the architecture of trustworthiness translates directly to implementation contexts requiring the cooperation of those asked to adopt new practices. Ultimately, both trust and trustworthiness are essential for understanding relational dynamics in implementation.

Evidence of trust’s importance across disciplines

Research across multiple disciplines demonstrates trust’s importance for processes central to implementation.5 18–20 Within implementation science specifically, emerging evidence suggests trust influences key implementation outcomes. Maiorana et al found that trust among patients, providers and institutions was essential for acceptability of a health information exchange implementation.20 Metz et al demonstrated that trust-building among implementation team members increased commitment, motivation and inclusive decision-making.21 Recent theoretical work by Metz et al proposes that trust-building should be recognised as an implementation strategy in its own right, capable of directly contributing to implementation outcomes and moderating effects of other implementation strategies.5

Critical gaps in current knowledge

Despite growing recognition of trust’s importance, significant gaps limit our understanding of how trust functions within implementation science. First, conceptualisation varies dramatically. Taylor et al’s22 synthetic review of 50 years of trust research in healthcare found tremendous variability in how trust is conceptualised and defined, hindering cumulative knowledge building. Within implementation science specifically, it remains unclear whether trust should be understood primarily as a determinant of implementation success, a mechanism through which implementation strategies exert effects, a contextual factor moderating implementation processes or an implementation outcome itself. Conceptual variability also extends to the level of analysis at which trust is examined. Most trust scholarship in healthcare has centred on the patient-provider relationship, with comparatively less systematic attention to trust among teams implementing and delivering the intervention or between implementing teams and organisational leaders, despite team-level and inter-organisational relational dynamics being more proximal drivers of practice change. This review will examine the extent to which implementation studies attend to trust and trustworthiness at team, organizational, and inter-organizational levels as well as patient-provider trust in the uptake and adoption of health interventions.

Second, measurement approaches lack consistency. Hamilton et al’s scoping review found that trust within healthcare teams is often not explicitly defined and is measured using bespoke, non-validated instruments created for individual studies.18 Similar challenges exist in measuring trust in vaccination contexts,23 public health systems10 and community partnerships.19 This measurement inconsistency prevents comparing findings across studies, testing theoretical propositions and building cumulative evidence about trust’s role in implementation.

Third, integration with implementation frameworks remains incomplete. While widely used frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) include constructs tangentially related to trust—networks and communications, leadership engagement, formally appointed implementation leaders and cosmopolitanism—trust itself is not explicitly theorised within CFIR or its recent updates.2 24 Similarly, Proctor et al’s1 4 influential taxonomy of implementation outcomes—acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, penetration, sustainability and cost—does not directly address trust, though trust likely influences multiple outcomes. Recent theoretical work has begun addressing this gap,5 but empirical testing remains limited.

Fourth, constituent perspectives and power dynamics warrant deeper examination. Trust is not monolithic; it operates differently across diverse contexts and communities and is shaped by historical, cultural, institutional and structural factors including racism, marginalisation and power differentials.10 Understanding whose trust matters, in whom or what and how trust dynamics intersect with equity considerations is essential for developing implementation approaches that centre marginalised communities and address health inequities.

Rationale for this systematic review

Anderson and Griffith15 argue that advancing trust research requires identifying the factors that predict trusting behaviours, namely cooperation under conditions of risk or uncertainty and interdependence, and related factors explaining why interventions succeed or fail which mirrors the remit of implementation science. Uniquely, the alignment between trust scholarship and implementation inquiry positions implementation research as a productive site for clarifying trust’s role and developing the framework specifications, measurement approaches and strategies that are largely implied in the literature.

To our knowledge, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesised how trust and trustworthiness are conceptualised, operationalised and function within implementation science studies. Existing reviews have examined trust in specific contexts—healthcare teams,18 participatory health research networks,19 vaccination23—but none have systematically examined trust across the breadth of implementation science research. This represents a critical gap given trust’s apparent importance for implementation success as identified by both researchers and practitioners.

This systematic review addresses this gap by examining trust as it operates within implementation science studies across diverse settings, populations, innovations and implementation phases. Our objectives are to: (1) synthesise how trust and trustworthiness have been conceptualised and defined; (2) document measurement and operationalisation approaches; (3) examine levels at which trust operates (interpersonal, team, organisational, institutional, community); (4) assess trust’s relationship with implementation processes and outcomes; and (5) determine how trust is integrated and can be mobilised within implementation frameworks. By providing comprehensive synthesis, we aim to inform development of trust-focused implementation strategies, measurement tools and theoretical models that can advance both implementation research and practice.

The contemporary moment sharpens this need. Concerns about mis- and disinformation, contested scientific authority and varying institutional responses to recent public health crises have compounded these dynamics.25 The conditions under which evidence-based practices are taken up into routine practice are organisational and clinical as well as societal, because clinicians, organisations and the publics they serve shape their willingness to commit to practice change. The source of credibility failures in structural and institutional features of healthcare delivery should be understood at the level of the care delivery ecosystem.15 A systematic synthesis of how trust has been conceptualised, measured and analysed within implementation science studies is therefore both timely and necessary, providing the empirical foundation for the conceptual, methodological and strategic refinements the field requires.

Methods

Protocol design and registration

This systematic review protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement.26 27 The completed PRISMA-P checklist is provided as online supplemental material. This protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420251270920). The study is anticipated to be conducted from 2026 to 2027. Any substantive amendments to this protocol will be documented in the PROSPERO record with dates and rationale. We intend to use a metanarrative approach to our systematic review which will facilitate a cohesive synthesis of the literature that accounts for the heterogeneity in the conceptualisation of trust, differing methodologies used to explore the concept as well as variability in analysis approaches and outcome measures associated with trust.28 Furthermore, this approach will allow us to systematically surface congruencies and tensions that exist in how trust is conceived in the field of implementation science with the goal of generating theory-building knowledge.

Planning phase

In the first phase, the team met to deliberate on the most suitable approach for conducting the review. Weekly meetings were centred on exploring the suitability of different review approaches including the metanarrative approach, the scope of the review, the activities under each phase of the review such as the search strategy as well as the roles and responsibilities of the team members. The team discussed the different ways that trust has manifested in the implementation science literature and used their experience in both research and practice to identify additional terms and keywords to the initial search strategy that had been developed by WI. Moreover, the team also identified potential journals and databases where the search would be done, eligibility criteria and anticipated findings that would be synthesised.

Search phase: information sources and search strategy

The search strategy is designed to capture literature where trust or trustworthiness is discussed in relation to implementation science processes or outcomes. This approach combines two concept groups: (1) trust and trust-related constructs and (2) implementation science concepts and outcomes. The trust construct group includes both explicit trust terminology (eg, trust, mistrust, distrust, trustworthiness) and implicit trustworthiness constructs frequently discussed in implementation contexts but not always labelled as trust. These include credibility, alignment, authenticity, transparency, accountability, reliability, integrity, psychological safety, collaboration, partnership/relationships, stakeholder buy-in and risk perception.

The implementation science concept group includes core terminology related to implementation processes as well as widely used implementation outcomes and system-level concepts (eg, implementation, knowledge translation, adoption, fidelity, sustainability, acceptability, appropriateness, penetration and spread). Our search strategy was developed iteratively. The finalised search strings are presented in online supplemental file 1, Search Strategy.

Our search will be targeted towards (i) journals that have been identified as being relevant in publishing implementation science articles based on Mielke et al’s survey on expert opinion29 and (ii) journals which were launched after 2020 and have grown significantly, publishing implementation science articles from geographically diverse settings across the globe since Mielke et al’s survey was based primarily on the views of US experts. Using Scopus, we will deploy the search strategy that will cover 14 journals: Implementation Science, Implementation Science Communications, Implementation Research and Practice, Translation and Behavioral Medicine, Journal of Clinical and Translational Science, Clinical and Translational Science, JGR Clinical and Translational Research, Health Research Policy and Systems, BMC Health Services Research, BMJ Quality and Safety, BMJ Open, International Journal of Health Policy and Management, International Journal for Equity in Health and PLOS Global Public Health and Frontiers of Health Services Research. Whereas we will hand search through one journal, Global Implementation Research and Applications. We will also conduct backward citation searching (reviewing reference lists of included studies) and forward citation searching of seminal papers on trust in implementation.5 20 There are no time restrictions on when included articles ought to have been published.

All searches will be documented comprehensively including database platform, search date, years covered, number of records retrieved and complete search strings with syntax. Search results will be exported and managed using Rayyan,30 a web-based systematic review management tool.

Population

Studies involving stakeholders engaged in implementation processes will be included. Eligible stakeholders include healthcare providers (physicians, nurses, allied health professionals), organisational leaders and staff (administrators, managers, supervisors, quality improvement teams), community partners (community health workers, lay health advisors, community-based organisation staff), patients or service users where relevant to implementation (eg, as active implementation partners in patient-centred implementation initiatives), implementation practitioners or facilitators, and policymakers or system-level actors (public health officials, policy implementation specialists). Studies focusing exclusively on clinical populations or individual patients without examination of implementation processes or stakeholder involvement will be excluded.

Exposure of interest

We will include studies examining trust and trustworthiness defined broadly. This includes studies that explicitly examine trust using terms such as trust, mistrust, distrust, trustworthiness, institutional trust, community trust, stakeholder trust, interpersonal trust, organisational trust or relational trust. It also includes studies examining implicit relational or credibility-related constructs that function as trust mechanisms within implementation processes, such as participatory engagement, partnership quality, collaboration, credibility or legitimacy of evidence or implementers, transparency, buy-in or relational dynamics between researchers, implementers, organisations and communities.

Given our aim to explore how trust is conceptualised within implementation science, we adopt this intentionally inclusive definition. During screening, we will apply structured decision rules: if trust or related terms appear in title, abstract, or keywords, or if relational constructs clearly function as trust mechanisms based on authors’ descriptions, studies will proceed to full-text review for detailed assessment. This approach balances breadth (capturing varied conceptualisations) with manageability (maintaining focus on implementation-relevant trust).

Trust may be examined as a determinant or predictor of implementation success, a mechanism or mediator through which implementation strategies exert effects, a contextual factor or moderator of implementation processes, an implementation outcome to be achieved, or in other roles within implementation research.

Comparator

This review does not require formal intervention-control comparisons. Where studies describe variations in trust levels (high vs low trust conditions, before-after trust-building interventions, trust across different stakeholder groups), these will be documented descriptively to illuminate trust’s potential influence on implementation.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes are implementation determinants, processes and outcomes as influenced by or related to trust, mistrust, distrust or trustworthiness. For this review, implementation determinants refer to barriers, facilitators, contextual conditions, needs, assets, constraints or explanatory factors that influence implementation. Determinants may occur across patient, provider, organisational, community, system, policy, sociocultural or relational levels and may include factors such as leadership support, organisational culture and climate, readiness, resources, workflow fit, stakeholder engagement, intervention characteristics, communication, prior experiences with institutions, perceived credibility, legitimacy, transparency, accountability and relational histories.

Implementation processes refer to the activities, phases, strategies, mechanisms, relational dynamics, and contextual work through which implementation unfolds, including exploration, planning or preparation, stakeholder engagement, communication, decision-making, adaptation, strategy selection or tailoring, training, facilitation, implementation support, monitoring, reflection, evaluation, sustainment planning, spread and scale-up.

Implementation outcomes refer to acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration and sustainability, as well as reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation and maintenance. We will also extract related implementation indicators as defined by study authors, including reach, routinisation, institutionalisation, maintenance, spread, scale, implementation progress, implementation success and implementation failure. Because trust-related dynamics may not always be labelled using formal implementation science terminology, we will code studies conceptually when authors describe relational, organisational, community or stakeholder trust dynamics that are linked to implementation determinants, processes or outcomes.

Secondary outcomes include trust conceptualisation and definition approaches; measurement methods and instruments; levels at which trust operates (interpersonal dyads, teams, organisations, institutions, communities); constituent perspectives on trust (whose trust, directed toward whom or what); and positioning of trust within implementation frameworks (as determinant, mechanism, moderator, contextual factor or outcome).

Outcomes may be assessed qualitatively (interview themes, ethnographic observations, case study findings), quantitatively (validated scales, study-specific measures, administrative data) or through mixed methods at any time point reported in studies.

Study designs

We will include diverse empirical study designs to capture the breadth of how trust has been examined in implementation research. Eligible designs include qualitative studies employing phenomenology, ethnography, case studies or other qualitative approaches; quantitative studies including randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, observational designs (cohort, cross-sectional, case-control) and survey research; mixed-methods studies combining qualitative and quantitative approaches; and hybrid effectiveness-implementation designs (Types I, II or III). We will exclude study protocols lacking empirical findings, editorials and commentaries (though these will be reviewed for additional references), conference abstracts without full empirical data, dissertations and theses, book chapters and purely theoretical papers without original empirical findings.

Context

Studies conducted in any setting where evidence-based practices, programmes, policies or innovations are implemented will be eligible. This includes healthcare settings broadly defined (hospitals, clinics, primary care, specialty care, long-term care facilities); public health systems (health departments, community health programmes, disease prevention initiatives); community-based organisations; educational settings (schools, universities) where health-related interventions are implemented; social service systems (child welfare, mental health services, substance use treatment, supportive housing); and other organisational contexts involving health-related implementation. Studies will be eligible regardless of geographic location or country income level (high-, middle- or low-income countries) with additional attention to how socio-cultural environments shape local conceptualisations of trust.

Other restrictions

Studies published in English will be included due to resource limitations for translation. No date restrictions will be applied; we will search from database inception to the final search date to capture the full evolution of trust-related implementation research.

Study selection process

Retrieved records will be imported into Rayyan, where automated deduplication will be supplemented by manual review. Screening will occur in two stages: title/abstract screening followed by full-text review.

For title and abstract screening, we will focus on ensuring the study type and manuscript type meet criteria. Full-text screening will follow similar procedures. Two reviewers will independently assess full texts against all eligibility criteria, with disagreements resolved through discussion or third-reviewer adjudication. Reasons for exclusion at full-text stage will be systematically documented. Studies excluded for multiple reasons will have all reasons recorded. Inter-rater reliability for full-text screening will be calculated and reported.

Before full screening, two reviewers will pilot the rubric on 20 articles, meet to discuss discrepancies and clarify criteria, refine the rubric as needed (online supplemental file 2, Study Screening Rubric), and repeat pilot testing until achieving acceptable agreement (Cohen’s kappa≥0.70). Subsequently, two reviewers will independently screen all full texts, with disagreements resolved through discussion or, if needed, adjudication by a third reviewer. Inter-rater reliability will be calculated and reported.

Mapping and data extraction phase

We will develop a comprehensive, structured data extraction form in Rayyan or REDCap or (Research Electronic Data Capture) capturing bibliographic information; study characteristics (design, purpose, theoretical frameworks used, implementation frameworks applied); setting and context (setting type, geographic location, country income level, population characteristics, health condition addressed); implementation phase (pre-implementation, preparation, active implementation, sustainment); trust/trustworthiness variables including conceptualisation and definition, measurement approaches and instruments, psychometric properties if reported, stakeholder perspectives (whose trust, directed toward whom/what), level of trust examined (interpersonal, team, organisational, institutional, community), and role of trust in the study (determinant, mechanism, moderator, contextual factor or outcome); implementation strategies employed and whether explicitly aimed at building trust/trustworthiness; implementation outcomes measured and their relationship to trust; and key findings regarding trust’s influence on implementation with statistical results for quantitative studies and themes for qualitative studies (online supplemental file 3, Data Extraction Form).

The extraction form will be piloted on 5–10 included studies by all team members, refined based on pilot results to ensure clarity and comprehensiveness, and then applied systematically. Two reviewers will independently extract data for each study. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, with fourth-reviewer consultation if needed. If data are missing or unclear, we have elected not to contact study authors to maintain feasibility given anticipated review scope and timeline; missing data will be documented as ‘not reported’.

Appraisal phase: risk of bias and study quality assessment

Given heterogeneity in study designs and this review’s conceptual focus, quality assessment will be used to contextualise findings rather than exclude studies. We will use design-appropriate tools: the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Qualitative Checklist32 for qualitative studies, assessing clear aims, appropriate methodology, appropriate recruitment, reflexivity consideration, ethical rigour, data analysis quality and findings clarity; the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT)33 for mixed-methods studies, assessing qualitative, quantitative, and integration components; and MMAT criteria adapted for quantitative non-randomised studies for observational and quasi-experimental designs.

Two reviewers will independently conduct quality assessments for each study, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Quality ratings will be reported descriptively and, where feasible, findings will be examined stratified by quality level to assess whether study quality influences conclusions.

Data synthesis phase

Given anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, trust conceptualisations, measurement approaches and implementation contexts, we will conduct meta-narrative and thematic synthesis following guidance by the Realist And Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses (RAMESES reporting guidelines, Popay et al34 guidance on narrative synthesis and the Cochrane Handbook.35 Quantitative meta-analysis will not be conducted. Our data synthesis will be conducted in two phases. In the first phase, we will construct overarching narratives about trust from the selected literature and thereafter build a conceptual framework of trust in implementation science through an approach similar to Mengistu and Manolova.36

Our approach to building the overarching narratives will include four main steps. First, we will develop the preliminary synthesis through textual description and tabulation. Studies will be grouped thematically by review question. Summary tables will present study characteristics, trust conceptualisations, measurement approaches, levels of trust operation and key findings. We will use descriptive statistics (frequencies, proportions, ranges) to characterise the evidence base—for example, calculating how often each implementation outcome is examined in relation to trust, how trust is positioned within studies (determinant, mechanism, outcome) and the level at which trust is examined.

Second, we will explore relationships within and between studies through deductive content analysis aligned with review questions and inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s37 guidance. For conceptualisation (Research Question 1), we will synthesise definitions and theoretical groundings used across studies, developing a taxonomy of trust types identified. For levels of operation (Research Question 2), we will describe patterns in how interpersonal, team, organisational, institutional and community trust are examined across contexts. For implementation outcomes (Research Question 3), we will synthesise findings by each outcome (implementation determinants, processes and outcomes), documenting direction and consistency of trust-outcome relationships and mechanisms through which trust appears to influence outcomes. For framework integration (Research Question 4), we will examine how trust is positioned within established implementation conceptual frameworks and identify frameworks addressing trust/trustworthiness most explicitly. For knowledge gaps (Research Question 5), we will identify conceptual gaps (under-theorised aspects), measurement gaps (lack of validated instruments, inconsistent operationalisation) and methodological gaps (underutilised designs, unexplored relationships).

We will explore heterogeneity descriptively by examining patterns across study design (qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods; observational vs experimental), implementation setting (healthcare, community, school, other), level of trust (interpersonal, organisational, institutional, community), implementation phase (pre-implementation, active implementation, sustainment), use of implementation frameworks and geographic region and country income level. Where both qualitative and quantitative findings address similar questions, we will integrate findings using a convergent approach,33 allowing qualitative findings to provide depth and context for quantitative patterns.

Third, we will assess synthesis robustness by examining whether findings differ by study quality, assessing consistency of findings across studies examining similar questions and identifying gaps in evidence including underexamined trust levels, settings, outcomes or stakeholder perspectives.

Finally, meta-narratives will be identified through inductive coding and iterative review among the team members. The team will review the narratives conceptually using the following techniques.31

Results of the meta-narratives analysis will be organised by review question and presented through comprehensive summary tables, meta-narrative synthesis organised thematically and visual depictions where appropriate (eg, geographic distribution of studies, temporal trends in trust conceptualisation, mapping of trust across implementation frameworks). We will conclude by discussing broader implications for implementation science theory, research, measurement and practice.

The second phase of our synthesis is aimed at theory building around trust in implementation science. Meta-narratives are a useful approach to present a summary of the findings; however, in order to generate robust theoretical concepts, framework development is warranted.36 38 To do so, the research team will conduct Conceptual Framework Analysis, which offers an approach to generating concepts from data drawn from heterogeneous sources to construct new theoretical insights.38 This analysis begins with a reexamination of the metanarratives mapped in the first phase of the synthesis through a deconstruction of the ontological, epistemological and methodological contributions to the literature.

Recommendation phase

The research team will deliberate on the most appropriate avenues to disseminate the recommendations from the review process for researchers, practitioners and policy makers. Findings from the metanarratives will be presented to different audiences including: convenings with community partners, presentations during implementation science network meetings in the US and Africa attended by review team members. Moreover, theoretical contributions from the review will be submitted to peer-reviewed journals for scholarly feedback with the goal of refining our conceptual framework for trust and expanding the reach of the findings to others interested in the area of focus. Finally, we will gather the feedback given into briefs and non-technical summaries suitable for different audiences.

Evaluation and reporting criteria

Assessment of bias due to missing results

We will not formally assess risk of bias due to missing results (eg, publication bias, selective outcome reporting) for several reasons. This review does not involve quantitative meta-analysis or pooled effect estimation that would enable statistical tests for publication bias such as funnel plots or Egger’s test. The conceptual and heterogeneous nature of included studies precludes meaningful quantitative assessment of publication bias. Additionally, tools for assessing publication bias in narrative syntheses remain underdeveloped.

However, we will acknowledge and discuss narratively in our Discussion section potential impacts of missing studies, including likelihood that studies with null or negative findings regarding trust may be less frequently published, possibility that trust is measured but not reported in implementation studies when not a primary focus, and limitations of English-language-only searching which may miss relevant non-English publications.

Assessment of certainty of evidence

We will not conduct formal certainty-of-evidence assessment using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) or similar approaches. GRADE is designed for assessing confidence in effect estimates from intervention effectiveness reviews, whereas this review addresses conceptual and descriptive questions rather than intervention effectiveness. The heterogeneous, primarily observational nature of anticipated studies and use of narrative rather than quantitative synthesis make GRADE assessment inappropriate.

Instead, robustness of synthesis will be evaluated through quality assessment using design-appropriate tools, examination of consistency of findings across studies, consideration of methodological limitations when interpreting findings, and transparency about strength and limitations of evidence supporting each conclusion. The reporting of the study will be done in line with the RAMESES reporting guidelines.28 We will use the six guiding principles that guide a metanarrative approach to evaluate the review processes and the robustness of our findings: pragmatism, pluralism, historicity, contestation, reflexivity and peer review.1 9

Discussion

Expected contributions

This systematic review will provide the first comprehensive synthesis of how trust and trustworthiness are conceptualised, measured, and function within implementation science studies. We anticipate several important contributions. First, by synthesising definitions and conceptualisations across diverse studies, we will clarify conceptual boundaries of trust within implementation science, distinguishing trust from related constructs and identifying areas of conceptual consensus and divergence. This conceptual clarity is foundational for advancing both research and practice.

Second, by systematically documenting measurement approaches, we will identify validated instruments suitable for implementation research contexts, common approaches and their limitations, and critical measurement gaps warranting future development. These findings will inform creation of implementation-specific trust measures and promote measurement consistency enabling comparison across studies.

Third, by examining trust’s relationships with implementation outcomes, we will elucidate mechanisms through which trust influences implementation success. Understanding how trust affects outcomes—whether through enhancing stakeholder engagement, facilitating information sharing, promoting innovation adoption or other pathways—will advance mechanism-focused implementation science and theory development.

Fourth, by assessing how trust is integrated within implementation frameworks, we will highlight frameworks that explicitly address trust and identify opportunities for trust integration in frameworks where it remains implicit. This will inform framework refinement and selection for implementation projects where trust dynamics are salient.

Fifth, findings will directly inform practice by identifying trust-building strategies employed in implementation contexts, documenting facilitators and barriers to trust development, and highlighting contexts where trust appears particularly influential. These practice-relevant findings will support implementation practitioners in designing trust-focused implementation approaches.

Implications for implementation science

This review addresses calls for greater attention to relational aspects of implementation. By systematically examining trust, we respond to implementation practitioners’ consistent identification of trusting relationships as foundational to implementation success—a perspective not yet matched by systematic research synthesis. Bridging this research-practice gap will ensure research agendas align with practitioner-identified priorities.

The review also contributes to equity-oriented implementation science. Trust dynamics are inextricably linked to power, historical context, and structural factors including racism and marginalisation.10 Understanding how trust operates across diverse communities, how trust has been eroded through historical injustices, and how trustworthiness can be demonstrated and rebuilt is essential for implementation approaches, particularly those centring historically marginalised communities. Our synthesis will document whose perspectives on trust have been included in research and whose have been absent, identifying equity-focused research gaps.

Additionally, this review will inform development of implementation strategies explicitly focused on trust. Current implementation strategy taxonomies (eg, Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change; Powell et al3) include strategies that may build trust (eg, stakeholder engagement) but do not explicitly name trust-building as a strategy. Recent theoretical work proposes trust-building should be recognised as an implementation strategy5; our synthesis will provide empirical grounding for this proposition by documenting trust-building approaches used across implementation studies.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include comprehensive, systematic searching across major databases plus targeted journal searching; rigorous, transparent methods following PRISMA-P guidelines with protocol pre-registration; inclusive conceptualisation capturing both explicit and implicit trust; and synthesis approach suited to anticipated heterogeneity, emphasising narrative and thematic synthesis over inappropriate quantitative pooling.

Limitations include restriction to English-language publications due to resource constraints, potentially missing relevant non-English studies; heterogeneity in how trust is conceptualised and measured across studies, which may complicate synthesis; and inability to conduct meta-analysis, meaning we cannot quantify trust’s effects on implementation outcomes. However, narrative synthesis is more appropriate given heterogeneity and will provide rich, contextualised understanding.
