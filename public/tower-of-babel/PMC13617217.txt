Association Between Delivery Room Continuous Positive Airway Pressure and Neonatal Outcomes in Late Preterm and Term Infants: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Yoxin Chin, Maclean Hill, Arun Sett, Niranjan Thomas, Abdul Razak
Published: 2026

Introduction

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) in the delivery room is a cornerstone of respiratory support for preterm neonates, particularly those born extremely preterm (before 28 weeks' gestation), as endorsed by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) [1]. In this population, applying a constant pressure at the end of expiration effectively stabilises functional residual capacity and supports work of breathing, reduces the need for mechanical ventilation [2, 3], and reduces complications like bronchopulmonary dysplasia [4, 5, 6]. However, the application of CPAP has increasingly extended to late preterm (34–36 weeks) and term (37 weeks and beyond) infants with respiratory distress, despite ILCOR guidelines noting insufficient evidence to support its routine use in this population [1]. This shift in practice prompts a closer examination of whether a uniform approach to using delivery room CPAP, originally tailored to the needs of extremely preterm infants, can be safely and effectively applied to infants born closer to or at term.

The physiology of late preterm and term infants differs significantly from that of their extremely preterm counterparts [5]. Lungs in late preterm and term infants are comparatively more developed and compliant, with greater alveolar stability, airway stability, and surfactant quantity and quality [7, 8]. While CPAP in extremely preterm infants compensates for developmentally immature physiology, its role in more mature lungs may not yield the same benefits. Due to the physiological maturity of the late preterm respiratory system, the application of CPAP based on evidence extrapolated from more immature infants may have unintended consequences [9, 10, 11]. Given these distinctions, the routine use of delivery room CPAP (DR‐CPAP) in late preterm and term infants warrants scrutiny to determine its appropriateness and safety [1]. Some reports have suggested an association with pneumothorax [9, 10, 11], a complication potentially linked to the application of positive pressure in more compliant lungs unaccustomed to such support. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of DR‐CPAP in late preterm and term infants, exploring whether its routine use aligns with favourable outcomes or if it may, in fact, confer harm, such as an increased incidence of pneumothorax, thereby guiding evidence‐based refinements to neonatal resuscitation practices.

Methods

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was prospectively registered with Open Science Framework (OSF; registration DOI: https://osf.io/fj8pe/). It is conducted and reported as per the guidelines from preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses (PRISMA) [12].

Search Strategy

A systematic search of the literature was conducted using appropriate pre‐specified search terms within the following databases: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Excerpta Medica Database (Embase), Emcare, and Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE) from inception through March 26, 2025. The detailed search strategy for each database is outlined in the Supporting Information. Additionally, the reference lists and citations of studies included in the systematic review were searched to identify further relevant studies.

Eligibility Criteria

Type of studies: Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies evaluating one or more predefined outcomes of systematic review were eligible for inclusion in the study. Systematic reviews, descriptive studies, case series and case reports were excluded.

Population: The population of interest comprises late preterm (34–36 weeks' gestation) and term (≥ 37 weeks' gestation) infants. Studies with mixed gestational ages, including those < 34 weeks, were included if separate data for late preterm and term infants were provided.

Exposure and Comparison: The exposure of interest is the administration of CPAP in the delivery room to late preterm and term infants (≥ 34 weeks' completed gestation). The comparator group includes infants who did not receive CPAP but may have received other forms of support, such as supplemental oxygen or low‐flow oxygen therapy. Infants requiring higher levels of respiratory support than CPAP, such as non‐invasive ventilation or mechanical ventilation in the delivery room, were not considered part of the comparison group.

Outcomes: The following outcomes were evaluated in this systematic review:

Primary outcome: Pneumothorax, determined by any lung imaging technique, including chest x‐ray or lung ultrasound, at any time before discharge.

Primary outcome: Pneumothorax, determined by any lung imaging technique, including chest x‐ray or lung ultrasound, at any time before discharge.

Secondary outcomes: Pneumothorax requiring intervention (needle thoracocentesis or intercostal drain), the need for intubation and ventilation (as defined by authors for any indication), surfactant administration (as defined by authors for any indication), duration of respiratory support (as defined by authors), pulmonary haemorrhage (as defined by authors), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission (as defined by authors for any indication) and mortality. All outcomes assessed before discharge.

Secondary outcomes: Pneumothorax requiring intervention (needle thoracocentesis or intercostal drain), the need for intubation and ventilation (as defined by authors for any indication), surfactant administration (as defined by authors for any indication), duration of respiratory support (as defined by authors), pulmonary haemorrhage (as defined by authors), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission (as defined by authors for any indication) and mortality. All outcomes assessed before discharge.

Data Extraction

Y.C. searched the databases as listed above with the help of an information specialist. Y.C. and M.H. screened the titles and abstracts using Covidence and the shortlisted studies were reviewed by Y.C. and M.H. to determine eligibility for inclusion based on screening form (see Supporting Information) [13]. Each of the study deemed eligible for inclusion was examined for information (study design, inclusion, key characteristics and outcomes) using a standardised data collection form. No blinding strategies were used. Any missing or unclear information was pursued by attempts at contacting the corresponding authors via email at least twice. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third author (N.T.). A final list of literature after the search was assimilated using an online reference management software EndNote 21 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA).

Assessment of Risk of Bias

Risk of Bias in Non‐randomised studies of Exposures tool was used to assess the risk of bias for the observational studies [14]. This tool evaluates seven domains of bias: confounding, selection of participants, classification of exposures, deviations from intended exposures, missing data, measurement of outcomes and selection of reported results. Studies were classified as having low risk of bias, some concerns, high risk and very high risk. For RCTs, Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool was applied, covering five domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes and selection of reported results [15]. The RCTs were classified as low, some or high concerns for risk of bias. The use of both these tools were performed by Y.C. and M.H. and any conflicts were resolved by N.T.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each study that was included. Data synthesis involved pooling ORs with 95% CIs to estimate the effect of DR‐CPAP on outcomes, using a random‐effects model to account for anticipated heterogeneity across studies. Results given in risk ratios (RRs) were converted to ORs using the appropriate formula [16]. Analyses were performed separately for RCTs and observational studies, including unadjusted and adjusted data in observational studies, to explore the impact of confounding factors, with statistical analysis conducted using Review Manager version 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Nordic Cochrane Center, Copenhagen, Denmark). For outcomes with significant differences, the absolute risk difference (ARD) and its 95% CIs were reported, along with the number needed to treat or harm and its 95% CI, to aid clinical interpretation. Cochrane Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach was used to assess the level of certainty of evidence [17].

Assessment of Heterogeneity

Pooled forest plots were qualitatively evaluated to assess heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using I 2 values (derived from the chi‐squared Q‐statistic) in the meta‐analysis, with significant heterogeneity defined a p‐value for chi‐squared < 0.10. Upon detecting significant statistical heterogeneity, data were reviewed for errors or inconsistencies during extraction and entry. This was assessed independently by two authors (Y.C. and M.H.) and any discrepancies were resolved with a third author (A.R.). Subgroup analyses, if data were available, were conducted separately for late preterm infants (34–36 weeks' gestation) and term infants (≥ 37 weeks' gestation) to explore sources of heterogeneity. No additional strategies, such as meta‐regression, were planned a priori if subgroup did not resolve heterogeneity.

Results

Literature Search and Study Selection

A total of 1851 records were identified through the literature search. After removing 730 duplicates, 1121 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 1099 were excluded. Twenty‐two full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility, with 18 excluded (reasons provided in the Supporting Information). An additional four reports were identified through other sources, of which two were excluded. In total, six reports were included in the review and meta‐analysis; one report contained two distinct studies, resulting in seven included studies (two randomised controlled trials and five observational studies). This process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Study Characteristics

A total of 135 472 infants were included across seven studies: 135 149 from five observational studies [10, 18, 19, 20] and 323 from two RCTs [21, 22]. Most studies included all babies, with a mix of late preterm and term infants, although some focused on specific groups, such as elective caesarean deliveries or infants with transient tachypnea of the newborn. DR‐CPAP pressures ranged from 5 to 6 cm H2O, with some studies using pressures up to 8 cm H2O. Outcome Analysis

Primary Outcome (Pneumothorax)

Meta‐analysis of unadjusted data from four studies (127 959 participants) showed a significantly increased risk of pneumothorax in the DR‐CPAP group compared to the no DR‐CPAP group (unadjusted OR: 45.79; 95% CI: 31.47–66.63; ARD: 67 more per 1000 infants; 95% CI: 47 more to 96 more), corresponding to a NNH of 15 (95% CI: 10–21) (Table 2; Figure 2) [10, 19, 20]. Similarly, analysis of adjusted data from three studies (113 435 participants) also demonstrated increased risk with DR‐CPAP (adjusted OR: 24.18; 95% CI: 2.32–251.63; ARD: 24 more per 1000 infants; 95% CI: 2–252 more; NNH = 41, 95% CI: 4–500) [10, 19]. The certainty of evidence was rated moderate for both unadjusted and adjusted data, downgraded due to risk of bias, inconsistency and potential residual confounding, despite the large effect size (Table 2; Figure 2). Meta‐analysis of the two included RCTs did not yield a pooled estimate, as there were no pneumothorax events in either of the groups [21, 22].

Secondary Outcomes

In observational studies, there was no significant difference between groups for pneumothorax requiring intervention (unadjusted OR: 1.41; 95% CI: 0.67–2.93) (Table 2; Figure S1). DR‐CPAP was associated with an increased risk of surfactant administration (unadjusted OR: 5.68; 95% CI: 2.97–10.86; 1 study, 6913 participants ARD: 15 more per 1000 infants; 95% CI: 6–31 more; NNH = 67; 95% CI: 32–167; very low certainty) (Table 2; Figure S2) [18]. Observational data also showed a significant increase in NICU admission in the DR‐CPAP group compared to those who did not receive DR‐CPAP (unadjusted OR: 14.56; 95% CI: 13.27–15.98; 1 study, 94 469 participants; ARD: 469 more per 1000 infants; 95% CI: 446–492 more; NNH = 2; 95% CI: 2–2; moderate certainty) (Table 2; Figure 3) [19].

Forest plots showing the effect of delivery room CPAP on NICU admission: (top) unadjusted data from observational studies; (bottom) data from randomised controlled trials.

In contrast, the meta‐analysis of the two included RCTs showed the infants randomised to receive DR‐CPAP had a significant reduction in NICU admission (OR: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.09–0.63; 2 studies, 323 participants; ARD: 95 fewer per 1000 infants; 95% CI: 116–44 fewer; NNT = 11; 95% CI: 9–23; very low certainty) (Table 2; Figure 3) [21, 22]. RCTs also reported no significant differences between groups for intubation and ventilation (Table 2; Figure S3) or mortality (Table 2; Figure S4) [21, 22].

No data were available from either RCTs or observational studies on duration of respiratory support or pulmonary haemorrhage, and there were no separate data available to be able to perform the pre‐specified subgroup analysis.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis included seven studies encompassing over 135 000 late preterm and term infants. Both unadjusted and adjusted analyses from observational studies showed an increased risk of pneumothorax with DR‐CPAP (moderate certainty). Observational data also demonstrated higher rates of NICU admission (moderate certainty) and surfactant administration (very low certainty) in infants receiving DR‐CPAP. In contrast, the two included small‐sized RCTs suggested a possible reduction in NICU admissions with DR‐CPAP (very low certainty), with no significant differences observed in other outcomes such as pneumothorax, intubation, or mortality (all very low certainty).

While the evidence suggests a possible increased risk of pneumothorax with DR‐CPAP, the certainty of these findings is moderate and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In addition, no pneumothorax events were reported in RCTs, though these RCTs were not powered to detect such differences. However, the demonstrated association warrants consideration of how DR‐CPAP is applied in more mature infants. Unlike extremely preterm infants, who have underdeveloped muscles and limited capacity to generate negative intrapleural pressure, late preterm and term infants can produce substantial inspiratory effort to establish functional residual capacity and clear lung fluid. When this intrinsic effort is combined with external positive pressure, particularly when DR‐CPAP is initiated at 5 cm H2O and escalated to 8 cm H2O as per preterm protocols, the resulting transpulmonary pressures may become excessive, increasing the risk of air leak. This physiological interaction may help explain the observed association with pneumothorax [23]. A more selective approach, such as using DR‐CPAP only for infants with respiratory distress who require supplemental oxygen to maintain saturations, and withholding it in those with normal saturations, may help reduce the risk of pneumothorax, as suggested by a few quality improvement initiatives that reported reduced rates with such strategies [11, 18, 24].

The evidence from observational studies, which included large cohorts and were adequately powered to detect differences in outcomes like pneumothorax, contrasts with the limited data from the small, underpowered RCTs. However, interpreting observational findings requires caution due to potential confounding by indication. Infants receiving DR‐CPAP or escalating pressures on DR‐CPAP often have more severe respiratory distress and are inherently at higher risk of pneumothorax regardless of the intervention. Some studies attempted to mitigate this by adjusting for clinical markers such as delivery room oxygen use and respiratory severity, while others adjusted only for basic demographics like gestational age and sex, leaving residual confounding likely. Although randomised trials minimise such biases, detecting rare but serious events like pneumothorax [25] demands very large sample sizes and careful stratification by severity of respiratory distress or oxygen needs. Despite these challenges, the observational data offers important and compelling signals that merit further rigorous evaluation.

In contrast to the potential harms suggested by observational data, data from RCTs shows a reduction in NICU admissions with the use of DR‐CPAP. This raises the possibility that, when applied appropriately, CPAP may help facilitate lung fluid clearance and reduce the need for further respiratory support. However, these findings are based on small studies involving highly selected populations, such as infants born by elective caesarean section or those with transient tachypnea of the newborn, and may not be generalisable to the broader population of late preterm and term infants. Furthermore, the two RCTs enrolled different study populations, with one trial including only symptomatic infants requiring respiratory support and the other including all infants born by elective caesarean section, introducing clinical heterogeneity that should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimate. Nevertheless, both trials met the predefined eligibility criteria for this review and were included in the pooled analysis. It should be also noted that these trials limited CPAP to 5 cm H2O, a more conservative approach compared to the higher or escalating pressures used in many observational studies. This distinction suggests that low‐pressure, targeted use of DR‐CPAP in carefully chosen infants may offer benefit while avoiding the risks observed with more liberal or aggressive strategies.

Strengths and Limitations

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on the use of delivery room CPAP in late preterm and term infants, drawing from both randomised controlled trials and large observational studies. It was prospectively registered and conducted using rigorous methods, including a comprehensive literature search, independent data screening and standardised risk of bias assessments. The inclusion of both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, where available, allowed for a more nuanced interpretation of observational data, while the application of GRADE methodology provided transparent and systematic evaluation of the certainty of evidence. By incorporating physiological reasoning and contrasting findings from RCTs and real‐world data, the review offers valuable insights into both potential harms and selective benefits of DR‐CPAP.

However, the findings are constrained by several important limitations. Much of the evidence base comes from observational studies that are inherently susceptible to bias and residual confounding, even when statistical adjustment was performed. The small number and limited size of available RCTs, mostly involving highly selected populations and conservative CPAP strategies, restricts generalisability and limits confidence in pooled estimates. In addition, there was a general lack of data on secondary outcomes, with limited event rates reported, and key outcomes such as duration of respiratory support and pulmonary haemorrhage were not addressed in the included studies. The absence of granular data also precluded planned subgroup analyses, preventing exploration of heterogeneity in effect across gestational age, delivery mode, respiratory distress severity or CPAP pressure levels. Overall, the evidence was rated as low to moderate certainty, making it difficult to draw firm clinical conclusions and highlighting the need for large, well‐designed studies to guide safe and effective use of DR‐CPAP in this population.

Conclusion

In summary, this systematic review highlights the complexity of applying DR‐CPAP in late preterm and term infants, with observational data suggesting a potential increase in adverse outcomes, while small RCTs indicate a possible benefit when CPAP is used conservatively in selected populations. Nonetheless, the findings underscore the need for a more cautious and individualised approach to DR‐CPAP in this group, avoiding routine or aggressive application of high pressures. Well‐designed, adequately powered trials are needed to determine how best to use DR‐CPAP in late preterm and term infants; however, there are currently no ongoing clinical trials addressing these important uncertainties. Future studies should focus on defining optimal pressure levels, escalation strategies and comparison with other non‐invasive supports such as supplemental oxygen or high‐flow therapy.
