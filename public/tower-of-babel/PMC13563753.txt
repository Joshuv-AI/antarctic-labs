Reconstructing the post-pandemic landscape: a retrospective, hospital-based surveillance of respiratory pathogens in hospitalized children in Chenzhou, China, 2021–2025
Authors: Xiaomei Lei, Xuemei Yan, Aoying Li, Ziting Tang, Tan Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & objectivesThe COVID-19 pandemic significantly altered the global ecology of respiratory pathogens. This study aimed to characterize epidemiological shifts and explore determinants of infection risk among hospitalized children at a single center in Chenzhou, China, during the post-pandemic period.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using hospital-based surveillance data of acute respiratory infections in children from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2025. Nasopharyngeal swabs from 42,505 cases were tested via multiplex PCR for 13 common respiratory pathogens. Age-stratified positivity rates were calculated, and multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify independent risk factors, adjusting for year, season, and sex.ResultsThe data revealed an altered pathogen landscape. Rhinovirus (RV) was consistently detected at a high frequency, becoming a persistently prevalent agent (annual average positivity: 33.65%). The seasonal peak of influenza A virus (FluA) approached pre-pandemic levels, co-circulating with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and parainfluenza virus (PIV) in autumn/winter. Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) exhibited a notable epidemic surge in 2023 (16.03%), followed by a rapid decline to 0.56% by 2025. Regression analysis, adjusted for multiple covariates, identified age as a significant independent risk factor and revealed three consistent age-related pathogen patterns: (1) RSV/PIV predominance in infants and toddlers; (2) peak incidence of RV, adenovirus (ADV), and human metapneumovirus (HMPV) in preschool children; and (3) increasing risk of MP and Chlamydia pneumoniae with age. Notably, RSV resurged earlier (2021) than reported in Western countries, and the elevated endemicity of RV was more evident..ConclusionThis retrospective analysis of large-scale surveillance data describes changes in respiratory pathogen ecology among hospitalized children in Chenzhou during the post-pandemic period, characterized by age-dependent patterns and region-specific rebound dynamics. These observed season–age risk patterns may help generate hypotheses for informing clinical stratification and could inform local surveillance efforts, though prospective validation is required before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13865-8.

Introduction

Respiratory infections in children constitute a major global public health challenge, affecting not only acute morbidity and mortality but also long-term health outcomes and socioeconomic burdens. These infections are among the leading causes of outpatient visits and hospitalizations in children [1–3]. Infants and young children, with their immature immune systems and distinct respiratory anatomy, are particularly vulnerable; infections in this population often progress to bronchiolitis [4], pneumonia, and other severe lower respiratory tract diseases [5, 6], significantly increasing the healthcare burden. Furthermore, recurrent respiratory infections are closely associated with the risk of developing chronic respiratory diseases such as asthma [7, 8]. Although existing studies have described the seasonal characteristics [9], age distribution patterns [10, 11], and interactions among common pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus, rhinovirus, and influenza virus [12, 13], most studies are based on cross-sectional or short-term data, lacking long-term continuous surveillance. The implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs; e.g., mask-wearing, social distancing, hand hygiene, school closures, and travel restrictions) during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022) likely disrupted these patterns; yet, data on the post-pandemic reconstruction of the respiratory pathogen spectrum, especially among hospitalized children, remain limited.

Despite extensive research, critical knowledge gaps persist. First, the local impact of NPIs and the subsequent rebound effects on respiratory pathogen transmission in hospitalized children have not been fully characterized. Second, most studies have failed to adequately reveal age-specific differences in pathogen spectrum composition and infection trends among children of different age groups (e.g., infants [< 1 year], toddlers [1–2 years], preschoolers [3–6 years], and school-aged children [7–14 years]). Third, data tracking the long-term evolution of the pathogen spectrum—including shifts in dominant pathogens and emergence of novel variants—remain scarce in pediatric populations. These gaps hinder our ability to predict future outbreaks, optimize vaccine strategies, and formulate precise prevention and control measures.

We conducted a retrospective observational study to investigate changes in the epidemiological characteristics of acute respiratory infections among hospitalized children during the pandemic (2021–2022) and post-pandemic (2023–2025) periods. The study was based on clinical and laboratory data from all pediatric patients presenting with acute respiratory infection symptoms who underwent multiplex respiratory pathogen nucleic acid testing at the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou, Hunan Province, during this period. This design provided an extended surveillance duration (5 years), a large sample size (over 40,000 cases), and allowed for detailed age stratification. Our analysis employed descriptive epidemiology to compare annual and seasonal positivity rates, pathogen composition, and age-group distributions across years, with a focus on the transitions before and after the adjustment of COVID-19 prevention policies. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent associations between age and infection risk for each pathogen. This study aims to generate hypothesis-generating evidence to inform clinical management, vaccine planning, and public health preparedness for future respiratory outbreaks in children.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective study of pediatric patients (age 0–14 years) hospitalized with acute respiratory tract infections (ARTIs) at the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2025. Data extraction was performed on January 15, 2026, encompassing all eligible cases during this period. The dataset for 2025 was complete; however, data from March to June 2023 were unavailable due to policy restrictions. Nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected on the day of hospital admission and tested within 24 h. Nucleic acid extraction was performed using the Tiangen (TIANLONG) nucleic acid extraction reagent and the GeneRotex automated extraction instrument. The 13-Respiratory Pathogen Multiplex Detection Kit (Fluorescence PCR–Capillary Electrophoresis Method, Ningbo Haier Shi Gene Technology Co., Ltd., Registration No. 20183400518) was used for pathogen detection. Amplified products were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis using the CE2400 fully automated instrument. Each run included a negative control (nuclease-free water), a positive control provided by the kit, and an internal extraction control to ensure assay validity and monitor inhibition. The laboratory participates in annual national external quality assessment (EQA) programs, with consistently excellent results (the 2025 EQA results are provided as Related files 1 ). Throughout the entire study period (January 2021–December 2025), the testing platform, kit version, personnel, and procedures remained consistent. The following respiratory pathogens were tested: influenza A (including H1N1 and H3N2), influenza B, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenovirus, parainfluenza virus, rhinovirus, human metapneumovirus (MPV), coronavirus, human bocavirus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Chlamydia pneumoniae.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) hospitalization with a diagnosis of ARTI, including acute upper respiratory infection, acute bronchitis, acute laryngitis, bronchiolitis, or pneumonia; and (2) complete clinical and laboratory records. Cases with missing key data (e.g., age, sex, diagnosis, or pathogen results) were excluded. From an initial pool of 42,510 cases, five were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in a final cohort of 42,505 children (26,559 males, 15,946 females). Participants were stratified into four age groups for analysis: infants (< 1 year), toddlers (1–2 years), preschoolers (3–6 years), and school-aged children (7–14 years).

Statistical analysis

Categorical and continuous data were compared using standard non-parametric tests (chi-square, Fisher’s exact, Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the independent effect of age on infection risk for each of the 13 pathogens, we performed multivariable logistic regression. The primary predictor was age group, with infancy (< 1 year) as the reference category, compared with toddler (1–2 years), preschool (3–6 years), and school-age (7–14 years). Models were adjusted for sex, year (2021–2025), and season. All models met key assumptions (independence, linearity via dummy coding, absence of severe multicollinearity) and converged successfully. Model fit was assessed using Akaike information criterion (AIC; range: 4,848.85–52,988.23) and McFadden’s pseudo-R² (range: 0.0056–0.0822). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou (Approval No. 2025006; date: January 12, 2025).

Results

Overview

Our study included 42,505 pediatric cases of acute respiratory tract infection (ARTI) (Table 1). The overall pathogen detection rate was 73.0% (31,045/42,505). A statistically significant difference was observed between males (73.8%) and females (71.8%) (χ² = 13.8, P < 0.001). Positive rates varied markedly across age groups (χ² = 1179.5, P < 0.001), following an inverted V-shaped pattern: 69.5% in infants (< 1 year), rising to 77.8% in toddlers (1–2 years), peaking at 80.0% in preschoolers (3–6 years), and then declining significantly to 58.7% in school-aged children (7–14 years).

Single-pathogen infection was the most prevalent pattern (50.2%), followed by no pathogen detected (27.0%) and co-infection with two or more pathogens (22.9%). Rhinovirus (RV) was the most frequently detected pathogen (33.7%), substantially outpacing all others. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV, 12.0%), parainfluenza viruses (PIV, 9.2%), adenovirus (AdV, 8.7%), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP, 8.2%) formed a second tier of common pathogens (Fig. 1). Together, these top five pathogens accounted for over 70% of all detections.

Temporal dynamics

Annual trends in overall pathogen detection

The overall pathogen detection rate fluctuated significantly across the complete years of 2021, 2022, 2024, and 2025 (P < 0.001)(Fig. 2). After a rate of 76.92% in 2021, detection declined markedly to a nadir of 64.90% in 2022, a ~ 15.6% relative decrease, likely reflecting the impact of ongoing pandemic-era restrictions. This was followed by a substantial resurgence to a peak of 78.88% in 2024 (a 21.6% relative increase from the 2022 low), before moderating to 71.88% in 2025 (Fig. 2). The positivity rate for 2023, based on available months (January–February, July–December), was 75.20%. This value should be interpreted with caution, as it is not directly comparable to the complete-year rates due to the missing four months (March–June) of data. This overall V-shaped pattern—a sharp drop in 2022 followed by a strong rebound in 2024—suggests a notable post-pandemic re-establishment of respiratory pathogen circulation intensity, potentially driven by the relaxation of non-pharmacological interventions.

Annual trends of major pathogens

We demarcated the study period into “during the pandemic” (2021–2022) and “post-pandemic” (2023–2025), using Spring 2023 as the dividing point. The overall pathogen detection rate was significantly higher in the post-pandemic period (75.5%) compared to the pandemic period (66.4%) (P < 0.001), representing a relative increase of 13.7%. For 2023, all annual positivity rates reported below are based on the months with available data (January–February and July–December). The annual trends for key pathogens are detailed in Fig. 3. Notably:

Rhinovirus (RV), which is frequently detected even in asymptomatic children, maintained a high average detection rate of 33.65% over the study period. RV detection dropped to a low of 23.09% in 2022, then surged by 74.2% to a peak of 40.23% in 2024, before slightly declining to 38.32% in 2025. Based on available months, RV positivity in 2023 was 32.10%. This 74% relative increase from 2022 to 2024 suggests a post-pandemic resurgence in RV detection, although this may partly reflect detection of viral RNA rather than active infection.

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) exhibited a volatile, single-wave epidemic pattern. Following a pronounced outbreak in 2023 (peak positivity among available months: 16.03%), it underwent a near-complete collapse, with detection rates plummeting by 96.5% to 0.56% in 2025. This dramatic outbreak-collapse pattern is characteristic of large epidemic waves followed by population immunity.

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) showed a contrasting trajectory: a high of 23.08% in 2021 dropped sharply by 72.3% to 6.39% in 2022, then rebounded progressively, reaching 18.33% in 2025 (a 187% increase from 2022). In the available months of 2023, RSV positivity was 14.70%. The 187% relative increase from 2022 suggests a recovery of typical RSV seasonal patterns after pandemic disruption.

Influenza A virus (FluA) was not detected in 2021 but re-emerged rapidly, rising to 8.94% in 2022 (a steep increase from 0%) and then fluctuating between 3.8% and 7.2% in subsequent years. In 2023 (available months), FluA positivity was 5.45%. Its reappearance coincided temporally with the relaxation of non-pharmaceutical interventions.

Other pathogens, including adenovirus (AdV) and parainfluenza viruses (PIV), were consistently detected. AdV increased by 200% from 3.74% in 2021 to 11.19% in 2024; PIV peaked in 2023 at 11.69% (available months), representing a 271% increase from 2021.

These quantitative trends illustrate how the relaxation of pandemic-era measures differentially affected the detection frequency of respiratory pathogens, with some (e.g., RV, RSV) exhibiting marked increases while others (e.g., MP) showed an outbreak-collapse pattern. As the 2023 data are based on a partial year, direct percentage changes between 2022 and 2023 are not reported.

Shifts in seasonal patterns following the COVID-19 pandemic

Based on aggregated seasonal data from complete years (2021, 2022, 2024, 2025) and the available months of 2023 (January–February and July–December), the epidemiological patterns of major respiratory pathogens are summarized below. For 2023, seasonal analyses are restricted to winter (January–February) and summer (July–August) due to missing spring data (March–June)(Fig. 4).

Rhinovirus (RV), which is frequently detected in both symptomatic and asymptomatic children, showed the highest annual detection rate (average 33.65%) with a distinct autumn peak (42.84% in complete years), followed by spring (37.88%), and a winter nadir (25.62%). The autumn peak was 69% higher than the winter nadir, highlighting marked seasonal variation. In 2023, RV showed high summer activity (available July–August: 38.10%). It is important to note that PCR detection reflects viral RNA presence and does not necessarily indicate active infection or disease causation, particularly for RV.

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) showed a pronounced summer predominance (13.01% in complete years), approximately double the rates in other seasons. In 2023, MP peaked sharply in the available summer months (July–August: 19.74%), further supporting its summer-favoring pattern.

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) displayed a bimodal pattern with higher activity in spring (13.64%) and winter (12.37%). The spring peak was 10% higher than the winter peak in complete years. Because spring 2023 data are missing, RSV’s spring peak cannot be assessed for that year; RSV was notably elevated in the available winter (January–February: 15.20%) and summer (July–August: 16.50%) months of 2023.

Influenza A virus (FluA) presented dual peaks in summer (9.25%) and winter (7.91%) across complete years. The summer peak was 17% higher than the winter peak in complete years, possibly reflecting post-pandemic ecological shifts.

Influenza B virus (FluB) demonstrated a clear winter preference (5.55%), with winter rates 3.2 times higher than summer rates.

Parainfluenza virus (PIV) peaked in autumn (11.69%), with autumn rates 1.8 times higher than spring rates.

Human metapneumovirus (MPV) was most prominent in spring (12.69%), over 3.5 times its summer rate. Spring 2023 data are unavailable, so MPV spring activity was assessed from 2021, 2022, 2024, and 2025 only.

Across the entire study period (based on complete years and available 2023 data), Rhinovirus (RV) had the highest average detection rate (33.65%), followed by RSV (12.04%) and AdV (8.69%).

Age-stratified patterns

Annual trends in pathogen detection by age group

Based on complete annual data from 2021 to 2025 (noting that 2023 data are only available for January–February and July–December, with March–June missing), the overall positivity rates (proportion of children testing positive for at least one pathogen) across pediatric age groups exhibited distinct temporal patterns (Fig. 6).

Infant group (< 1 year): The positivity rate followed a V-shaped trajectory, declining from 78.00% in 2021 to a nadir of 57.70% in 2022, then rebounding to 69.23% in 2023, 76.27% in 2024, and stabilizing at 72.03% in 2025. This represents a 26% relative decline from 2021 to 2022, followed by a 33% relative increase from 2022 to 2024, suggesting a recovery of pathogen circulation among the youngest children in the post-pandemic period.

Toddler group (1–2 years): Positivity rates remained persistently elevated throughout the study (range: 69.55% in 2022 to 83.38% in 2024), with a steady upward trend: 81.45% (2021) → 69.55% (2022) → 78.81% (2023) → 83.38% (2024) → 78.65% (2025). The 2024 peak represented a 20% relative increase from the 2022 trough, indicating sustained high exposure in this age group.

Preschool group (3–5 years): This group consistently exhibited the highest positivity rates among all age groups (range: 75.14% in 2022 to 85.83% in 2024), with a relatively stable pattern: 76.98% (2021) → 75.14% (2022) → 80.39% (2023) → 85.83% (2024) → 77.62% (2025). The 2024 peak of 85.83% was the highest across all age–year combinations, suggesting a pronounced “catch-up” infection wave in preschool children.

School-age group (6–14 years): This group showed the most dynamic pattern, with positivity rates fluctuating substantially: 57.04% (2021) → 48.95% (2022) → 69.26% (2023) → 65.23% (2024) → 47.77% (2025). A single-peak surge was observed in 2023 (69.26%), representing a 41% relative increase from 2022 and a 45% relative increase compared with 2025, suggesting a transient epidemic wave temporally associated with the resumption of school and social activities after the relaxation of pandemic restrictions.

Overall trends: The five-year average positivity rates were 69.46% (Infant), 77.82% (Toddler), 80.01% (Preschool), and 58.71% (School-age). Preschool children consistently bore the highest infection burden, whereas school-age children experienced the most pronounced fluctuations. Compared with 2022, positivity rates increased in all age groups during the post-pandemic period (2023–2024), followed by a moderate decline in 2025, particularly among school-age children. It is important to note that the 2023 data cover only a portion of the year; therefore, the positivity rates and year-to-year comparisons involving 2023 should be regarded as preliminary and require confirmation from complete annual data.

Age-stratified infection risk

Based on age-stratified positivity rates (Fig. 7), distinct and quantifiable age-distribution patterns were observed for key respiratory pathogens. Rhinovirus (RV) was the most prevalent agent across all ages, peaking in preschool children (40.14%). Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) exhibited a sharp decline with age, from 14.53% in infants to only 1.87% in school-aged children. Conversely, Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) showed a marked increase, rising from 2.55% in toddlers to 14.61% in school-aged children. Influenza A (FluA) activity was higher in older children (preschool: 7.18%; school-aged: 7.15%) compared to younger groups. Viruses such as parainfluenza (PIV) and human metapneumovirus (MPV) were predominantly detected in infants and toddlers (PIV: ~11.5%; MPV: 6.38–8.51%) and decreased substantially in school-aged children. These patterns highlight a clear shift in etiology: RSV, PIV, and MPV predominantly affect the youngest children, while MP and FluA become more prominent with increasing age.

Based on the multivariable analysis adjusting for sex, year, and season (full regression results for all covariates are presented in Supplementary Tables 1–13), distinct age-related risk patterns for respiratory pathogens were quantitatively identified (Fig. 8). Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) showed the most dramatic increase with age, with odds ratios rising from 2.09 in toddlers to 6.61 in school-aged children (95% CI: 5.80–7.54), representing a more than six-fold increase from infancy, making it the dominant pathogen in older children. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) exhibited the steepest decline, with OR falling to 0.07 in school-aged children (95% CI: 0.06–0.09), indicating a 93% reduction in risk compared to infants. Adenovirus (AdV) showed an inverted U-shaped pattern, peaking in preschool children (OR = 5.01, 95% CI: 4.48–5.59) and then declining but remaining elevated in school-aged children (OR = 2.35). Other viruses with increasing risk included influenza B (OR up to 2.26), seasonal H3N2 (OR up to 2.07), and influenza A (OR ~ 1.66 in preschool and school-aged groups). In contrast, parainfluenza virus, bocavirus, human metapneumovirus, and coronavirus all showed significantly lower risks in school-aged children compared to infants. These findings highlight a clear age-dependent etiological shift: infants and toddlers are predominantly affected by RSV, PIV, and bocavirus, whereas MP, influenza viruses, and adenovirus become the primary pathogens in preschool and school-aged children, with MP and RSV representing the two extremes of age-related susceptibility.

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of hospital-based surveillance data from 42,505 pediatric acute respiratory infection cases (2021–2025) delineates the reconfigured epidemiological landscape of respiratory pathogens among hospitalized children in Chenzhou following the COVID-19 pandemic. Our data suggest a distinct pattern: rhinovirus (RV) was consistently detected at high frequency, establishing itself as a perennial presence in the clinical landscape, while the seasonal peak of influenza A virus (FluA) has largely re-established. Together with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and parainfluenza virus (PIV), these pathogens form a complex co-circulation pattern during autumn and winter. We documented the epidemic trajectory of Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) from its notable outbreak (positivity: 16.03% in available months of 2023) to its near disappearance (0.56% in 2025). Crucially, multivariable regression modeling confirmed age as an independent determinant of infection risk and identified three robust age-pathogen association patterns: the predilection of RSV and PIV for younger children; the peak incidence of RV, adenovirus (AdV), and human metapneumovirus (MPV) in preschoolers; and the age-progressive risk for MP and Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP), with MP risk increasing over six-fold from infancy to school age (aOR = 6.61, 95% CI: 5.80–7.54). These findings provide evidence for understanding post-pandemic epidemiological shifts and offer a potential framework for season- and age-informed public health surveillance.

Our findings both align with and extend the current understanding of pediatric respiratory pathogen epidemiology. First, the observed age-distribution patterns—where RSV and PIV predominantly infect infants [14, 15], while MP risk escalates with age [16]—corroborate well-established pre-pandemic observations, thereby reinforcing age as a fundamental biological determinant of susceptibility [17]. Second, the definitive restoration of FluA’s winter epidemic peak post-2023 echoes the global trend of rapid seasonal rebound following the relaxation of non-pharmaceutical interventions [18–20].

However, our study also highlights several deviations that may be specific to the Chenzhou region:

1、MP Dynamics: While MP’s predilection for older children is consistent with prior knowledge, our continuous large-scale surveillance captured its trajectory—from a sharp outbreak in the available months of 2023 to a dramatic decline to near-baseline levels by 2025. This pattern contrasts with reports of sustained activity or periodic outbreaks elsewhere [21–24], possibly reflecting the combined influence of accumulated local susceptibility, regional control measures, and antibiotic prescribing practices in Chenzhou. It is important to note that the 2023 MP positivity rate is based on partial-year data; therefore, the full annual peak may differ from the observed value.

2、RSV Intensity and Timing: We observed an anomalously high RSV peak as early as 2021 (positivity: 23.08%), preceding the large-scale “immunity debt”-associated surges reported in Western countries [25]. This suggests that local transmission dynamics, climatic factors [26], and viral subtype turnover, in addition to population-level susceptibility gaps, may have driven an earlier and pronounced RSV rebound in our setting.

3、Sustained RV Hyperendemicity: RV maintained a persistently high level of circulation throughout the study period, with an annual average detection rate of 33.65% and a peak of 51.66% in autumn 2024. Its dominant position as a background pathogen was not only more pronounced than that reported in many international studies [27, 28], but also consistent with surveillance findings from other regions within China [29, 30]. This further indicates that RV is a critically important and persistently prevalent pathogen contributing to the post-pandemic respiratory disease burden among hospitalized children.

In summary, our work validates stable, age-dependent epidemiological patterns while highlighting that the rebound magnitude, timing, and decline of specific pathogens (e.g., MP, RSV) post-pandemic can exhibit significant local heterogeneity. This complexity in the observed post-pandemic evolution of respiratory pathogen ecology is likely influenced by three mechanistic axes: (i) host factors (immune maturation and behavior explaining age preferences), (ii) altered population susceptibility dynamics following the “immunity debt” period, and (iii) intrinsic pathogen traits interacting with social behaviors (sustaining RV endemicity and FluA seasonality).

Our findings have potential translational implications that require further validation. The age–pathogen associations we identified may generate hypotheses for rapid clinical risk stratification and could inform empirical management frameworks. When integrated with seasonal patterns, they suggest the possibility of constructing a dual-dimensional “season–age” framework to support local surveillance efforts. However, this framework should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than immediately actionable; its potential to reduce antibiotic misuse or prevent severe outcomes requires prospective validation before clinical implementation.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings:

First, as a single-center study, the results primarily reflect the characteristics of pathogens circulating among the pediatric population in the Chenzhou region. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable to the national level or to settings with different geographical, demographic, or healthcare environments. Second, our study cohort consisted exclusively of hospitalized children who likely represent more severe cases. Therefore, the detected pathogen spectrum may not represent the prevalence of pathogens causing mild or asymptomatic infections in the community. Third, the retrospective design introduces limitations inherent to such designs. The analysis relied on the quality and completeness of existing medical records. Key potential confounding factors—such as detailed vaccination history, antibiotic use, socioeconomic status, or specific exposure risks—were not available to comprehensively adjust for them in our models. Fourth, the use of multiplex PCR detects pathogen nucleic acids, which does not directly distinguish between active infection, asymptomatic carriage, or prolonged shedding [31]. The high detection rates for some viruses (e.g., rhinovirus) should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. Fifth, as described in the Methods, data from March to June 2023 were unavailable. All comparisons involving 2023 in this Discussion are based on available months (January–February and July–December); direct year-to-year comparisons with those for complete years (2021, 2022, 2024, 2025) should be interpreted with caution.
