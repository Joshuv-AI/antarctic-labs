What are the views and experiences of human papillomavirus (HPV) self-sampling for cervical screening in high-income countries? A qualitative systematic review using the Theoretical Domains Framework
Authors: Alice Le Bonniec, Sinéad Woods, Therese McGlacken, Mairead O’Connor, Estelle McLaughlin, Therese Mooney, Patricia Fitzpatrick, Noirin E Russell, Caroline Mason Mohan, Laura Heavey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorldwide, cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women. Screening reduces cervical cancer mortality through detection of early-stage cancers and identification and treatment of precancers. Human papillomavirus (HPV) self-sampling is a novel addition to clinician-collected sampling that may help increase screening uptake. This systematic review aimed to assess the views and experiences of HPV self-sampling in high-income countries populations at varying stages of self-sampling implementation.MethodsSystematic searches were conducted in PubMed, PsycINFO, EMBASE, and CINAHL in April 2024. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies were included. The findings were mapped to the specific domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). Study quality was assessed using the qualitative CASP checklist.FindingsThirty-six studies were included. The review identified 19 sub-categories within the TDF (13 initial domains and 6 subcategories in the “environmental context and resources domain”). The most commonly identified themes, reflecting both positive and negative views and experiences of self-sampling, related to the following TDF categories: “knowledge”; “skills”; “beliefs about capabilities” (concerns about performing the self-test correctly); “beliefs about consequences” (concerns about test reliability); “intentions”; “social influences” (religion, cultural beliefs); “emotions” (fear, embarrassment) and “environmental context and resources” (autonomy, privacy, convenience). In the latter, previous negative experiences with procedures performed by providers were often reported, enhancing the interest for self-sampling.DiscussionThe findings can inform and help to adapt or modify the implementation of HPV self-sampling by anticipating the needs of and challenges faced by the eligible populations. Clear instructions on how to perform the test should be provided. While the findings suggest that providing multiple methods for accessing the kit (e.g., by mail or in clinics) may be optimal, further research is needed to assess feasibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27668-8.Keywords: Systematic review, Cervical screening, HPV self-sampling, Qualitative, Theoretical Domains Framework, High-income countries

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide [47], and was responsible for approximately 350,000 deaths globally in 2022 (WHO [58]). It is largely preventable through Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and cervical screening. Cervical screening has been shown to effectively reduce the incidence of, and mortality from, cervical cancer [30]. However, 2022 data from 20 EU countries indicate wide variation in screening uptake, ranging from 78.8% in Sweden to just 4.5% in Romania (Eurostat [21]).

International literature has identified various barriers to cervical screening uptake. Barriers to screening may relate to emotional factors (e.g., embarrassment, fear of pain, fear of a serious diagnosis), limited knowledge, restricted access, or time constraints [12, 53]. In addition, the involvement of healthcare professionals in the collection of the sample can be a barrier. More precisely, having a male smear taker or a previous negative experience of cervical screening performed by a clinician can prevent women from attending cervical screening [12, 53].

Behavioural science is increasingly applied in the public health sector (West & Gould [56]). Behavioural concepts and theories can help to identify the factors influencing health behaviours at the individual, community and societal level and can enhance the design of health interventions to improve health outcomes (WHO [59]). The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) is a synthesis of 33 theories of behaviour and behaviour change clustered into 14 domains [4]. The TDF can offer a comprehensive overview of factors influencing screening participation. For example, it has been used to identify strategies to maximise cervical screening uptake in Ireland [43]. It was also used in a systematic review to synthesise the barriers and facilitators to attendance for cervical cancer screening in EU member states [53].

In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) called for global action to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem and launched their global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer (WHO [57]). Elimination of cervical cancer is achieved by a country when fewer than four new cases of cervical cancer are diagnosed for every 100,000 women in its population [25]. HPV vaccination, cervical screening and treatment are the key three pillars to achieve cervical cancer elimination (WHO [57]).

Self-sampling for HPV is an alternative method to clinician-collected HPV testing for cervical cancer screening. Recommended by the WHO as an additional screening method, HPV self-sampling has been introduced in numerous countries worldwide such as The Netherlands, Australia and Sweden [32, 52]. Several strategies have been implemented internationally to offer HPV self-sampling to eligible participants, including opt in or opt-out approaches with direct mail outs, collection at GP practices, and distribution within specific communities [14]. In addition, strategies such as distribution in pharmacies or in mobile units could be considered. HPV self-sampling may help to increase cervical screening participation [18, 62]. It may also help to reduce health disparities in screening by improving access for under-screened and vulnerable populations [23].

The international literature has shown a high level of acceptability for HPV self-sampling [40]. The main reasons cited by women for preferring self-sampling over clinician sampling are less pain or physical discomfort, ease of use, convenience, ability to perform the test in private, and less embarrassment or anxiety [41]. At the opposite, lack of self- confidence with collecting a reliable sample is the most frequently cited reason for choosing clinician- collected samples [41]. Healthcare professionals have raised concerns about missed opportunities for clinicians to discuss screening and other health issues with women, the risk of non-attendance for follow-up after a positive HPV self-sample, and the potentially lower sensitivity of self-collected tests [51, 63]. Several studies have concluded that the clinical accuracy (performance in terms of sensitivity and specificity) of self-collected HPV tests is high [2, 22, 33], but other studies have indicated a decreased sensitivity for CIN2 + with self-sampling [48].

This systematic review aimed to assess the views and experiences of populations in high-income countries regarding HPV self-sampling. This study builds on a previous systematic review exploring values and preferences of HPV self-sampling published in 2021 [41], which included 72 studies published between 2002 and 2018. As HPV self-sampling for cervical screening is progressively implemented in the screening programmes of high-income countries, new data on its acceptability are emerging. Consequently, it is necessary to provide an update on how this new device is perceived by populations invited to participate in cervical screening.

Methods

Methods were applied according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The systematic review was registered with PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42024527231).

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were based on the SPICE (Setting, Perspective, Intervention, Comparison and Evaluation) framework [8]:

Setting

The systematic review focused on HPV self-sampling in high-income countries (according to the World Bank Group classification). There were no limits on study size, or data collection methods. Self-sampling methods using swab test, brush test, at home or at health centre, with or without clinician assistance were included.

Perspective

Populations of interest for this review were (1) women and people with a cervix (> 18 years old) who were invited to use an HPV self-sampling test (whether they did it or not); and (2) women and people with a cervix (> 18 years old) who were questioned about the use of HPV self-sampling.

Phenomenon of interest

Studies exploring experiences, views, values, preferences and acceptability of HPV self-sampling were included.

Comparison

Articles comparing HPV self-sampling with clinician provided HPV sampling were included.

Evaluation

Qualitative studies related to the experiences, views, values, preferences and acceptability of HPV self-sampling were considered. Mixed-methods studies were included only if qualitative data were presented separately. Only articles that were peer-reviewed and published in English were included.

The search strategy was made of three main concepts including several keywords: “cervical cancer/HPV”; “self-sampling”; and “views, experiences, values and preferences”. A similar search approach was repeated in all databases but keywords were associated with relevant MESH/thesaurus terms depending on the database. The search strategies used for each database are available in Appendix I.

The search included articles from October 2018 to April 2024, as articles up to October 2018 were already included in Nishimura’s review about values and preferences of HPV self-sampling, published in 2021 [41].

Covidence software was used to screen the references. Two independent reviewers completed title and abstract screening and full text screening (ALB as first reviewer, SW or TMG as second reviewer). Disagreements were resolved through discussion with the three reviewers. The PRISMA flow diagram of the literature selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Framework analysis

Both deductive and inductive approaches were followed to analyse the data. The TDF framework [4] was used to categorise the results of the included studies deductively. The first author (ALB) manually coded the results of each study to the domains of the TDF. This process was reviewed by SW, TMG and MOC and adjustments were made accordingly. A consensus was made on splitting the “environmental context and resources” domain into several categories (based on the description of this domain provided by the authors of the TDF). The subcategories were the following: “barriers and facilitators”, “salient events”, “resources/material resources”, “organisational culture/climate”, “environmental stressors”, “person × environment interaction”. An inductive thematic analysis [9] was then conducted by ALB on the data mapped with each individual TDF domain. Themes were developed within each TDF domain. For example, in the “Knowledge” TDF domain, several themes were identified, such as “Lack of knowledge about HPV and cervical cancer”, “Lack of knowledge about HPV self-sampling” and “Lack of education about the female anatomy”. The themes developed were checked by SW, TMG and MOC and adjustments were made accordingly.

Quality assessment

The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist for qualitative studies was used to evaluate the quality of the studies. Two independent reviewers (ALB and SW/TMG) completed the evaluation and any conflicts were resolved by discussions with a third reviewer (SW or TMG). No studies were excluded based on this evaluation. Regarding the mixed-methods studies, the CASP checklist for qualitative studies was used to evaluate the qualitative parts of the mixed methods studies as only qualitative components were extracted. Consequently, lower scores may reflect less information reported on qualitative components in mixed methods studies.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Thirty-six studies were included. Studies were conducted in various countries: USA (10); Australia (8); Canada (5); New Zealand (4); The Netherlands (3); Sweden (2); Denmark (1); Ireland (2); France (1). The number of participants in these studies varied from 7 to 199. Among the included studies, 12 articles focused on minority groups (ethnic or gender and sexual minorities), 4 focused on under screened/never screened groups, and 6 focused on both. One article focused on women living with HIV and 3 focused on specific age groups (younger or older women). Participants had experience of using an HPV self-sampling test in 14/36 studies. The authors used a theoretical framework in 22 articles. Data were collected using focus groups (10), interviews (18), a mix of both (4), yarning (form of conversation) (1), or open-ended questionnaires (3). Data were analysed using several methods as described by the authors: thematic/content analysis (29); interpretive description (1); structural analysis (1); systematic text condensation (1); rapid qualitative assessment (1); phenomenological/phenomenographic analysis (2); not specified (1).

Methodological quality

The quality assessment conducted with the CASP checklist revealed that most of the studies met the criteria regarding the clarity of the aims of the research, appropriateness of the qualitative methodology, recruitment strategy, data collection, ethical issues, data analysis, clarity of research findings and value of the research. However, the assessment revealed a frequent lack of information about the appropriateness of the research design to address the aims of the research and consideration of the relationship between researcher and participants. Following the CASP guidance, a scoring system was not applied. Details of the CASP assessment for each individual study are available in Appendix III.

Deductive analysis: domain frequency

The 19 theoretical domains (13 initial domains and the 6 subcategories in the “environmental context and resources” domain) are presented in Fig. 2, ordered from the least frequently found in the studies to the most frequently found. This order reflects the number of studies that included data relevant to a domain that were coded to that domain. The most frequently identified domain was “environmental context and resources: barriers and facilitators” (n = 34), followed by “beliefs about capabilities” (n = 31); “beliefs about consequences” (n = 28); “skills” (n = 25); “emotion” (n = 23); “knowledge” (n = 23); “social influences” (n = 22); “environmental context and resources: salient events” (n = 21); “intentions” (n = 21); “environmental context and resources: resources/material resources” (n = 18); “optimism” (n = 14); “environmental context and resources: organisational culture/climate” (n = 9); “environmental context and resources: environmental stressors” (n = 8); “goals” (n = 7); “memory, attention and decision processes” (n = 5); “environmental context and resources: person × environment interaction” (n = 5); “social/professional role and identity” (n = 1).

Frequency of each TDF category

The domains “reinforcement” and “behavioural regulation” were not identified in any of the included studies.

Knowledge

Lack of knowledge about HPV self-sampling was outlined in 23 articles. In countries where HPV self-sampling for cervical screening is available, many participants did not know about the existence of this option, and a general lack of knowledge about this new procedure was observed (n = 15): “had I known that [self-collection] was an option, I would’ve done it when it came out, basically.” [15]. Lack of knowledge about HPV and cervical cancer in general was also expressed in 6 studies: “[I knew] absolutely nothing.” [1].

Finally, additional issues highlighted included a lack of information provided after testing to interpret results (n = 6), limited education about female anatomy (n = 3), and confusion between the HPV test and the Pap smear (n = 3).

Skills

The domain of “skills” was identified in 25 articles. A frequent finding was the need for accessible information and clear instructions on how to perform the self-sampling test (n = 14), such as: “a small piece of paper that you can bring to the bathroom with you, with step-by-step instructions” [50]. Among participants who tried self-sampling, the procedure was reported as easy to do in 10 articles: “It’s just so easy and just so simple. I wouldn’t have that stress of going in and just laying up on the bed with my legs open. It’s just so easy, so easy to do” [16].

A sense of empowerment and increased control over their own health was also identified as a positive aspect of the self-sampling procedure in 8 articles: “I guess it was that sense of having control over my own health and it being a choice I can make, it being something I can do myself, rather than it being done to me” [15].

While some difficulties were reported with test completion (n = 3), language barriers (n = 2), or administrative processes (n = 2), HPV self-sampling was generally perceived as acceptable and approachable (n = 9). Comfort with one’s own anatomy was identified as a facilitator in two articles.

Beliefs about capabilities

The "beliefs about capabilities" domain was identified in 31 articles. Participants frequently expressed concerns about their ability to collect the sample correctly (n = 27). This is illustrated in the following quote: “I feel like for some reason, I may not do it right, and I could get the wrong diagnosis. I’d wait for a trained professional to do it so I can definitely be able to rely on what they find. No, I just don’t trust doing it myself, although, I probably could, but I’d wait for a trained professional because they would know what to do in there and get what they’re looking for.” [37].

Some concerns related to a fear of hurting oneself (n = 6): “I don’t feel safe as I might not do it right and might hurt myself.” [17]. Some participants also noted that discomfort with their own bodies could be a barrier (n = 3). As a result, the need for education prior to the procedure or support through resources or healthcare professionals was highlighted in 12 articles: “It is written down with pictures, but it would be easy as well if you could watch a video. For example, you could go to this website and look how it should be done. […] I find that important” [29]. Nonetheless, the self-sampling procedure was perceived as easy to perform in 9 articles: “you can’t really go too wrong if there’s only one way up there” [46].

Beliefs about consequences

The “beliefs about consequences” domain was found in 28 articles. The most common theme in this domain was related to concerns about the accuracy of HPV self-sampling, compared to a sample taken by a healthcare professional (n = 20). This concern is illustrated by the following quote: I think if I get something like this self-sampling kit in the mailbox without information I would think “Is this trustworthy?” Come on, I don't believe in it, this looks like some kind of toy, something like that.” [31]. Similarly, some participants believed that healthcare professionals could perform a more thorough examination through traditional screening (n = 8): “…they (the midwife or gynaecologist) will notice if there are any deviations, I think, I don’t know. And that there is a human being you can talk to if you have any queries, that’s it.” [42].

The need for a follow-up traditional test after an abnormal HPV self-sampling result was a source of confusion, with participants in four articles expressing a preference for undergoing only one test: “But it also said, if it’s not good, you have to go to the GP again. I thought, then I’ll just go to the GP right away. I did not ask for it.” [29]. HPV self-sampling was perceived as having the potential to save lives in 3 articles.

Finally, participants raised logistical concerns, including the reliability of postal return (n = 3), potential delays in processing home-based tests (n = 2), and the hygiene or preservation of the sample (n = 2).

Intentions

Intentions to use self-sampling and preferences for screening modality were explored in 21 articles. Participants stated that they would be more likely to use HPV self-sampling or to use it again over a traditional screening method in 9 articles. A willingness to try HPV self-sampling was expressed in 4 other articles. In contrast, a preference for traditional screening by a healthcare professional was expressed in 10 articles: “I’d rather go to a healthcare provider. Now me personally. Now if I didn’t have access to one, then I think that I would.” [13]. A reluctance to replace routine healthcare visits with self-sampling was noted in two articles.

Environmental context and resources – Salient events/critical incidents

References to “salient events or critical incidents” (a subcategory of the "environmental context and resources domain”) were identified in 21 articles. Negative past experiences with traditional screening methods were commonly reported as factors increasing interest in self-sampling (n = 14). For example, participants described uncomfortable or painful examinations: “The less visits in the horse straddle position the better. It is an uncomfortable visit (physically and mentally)” [11]. As a result, the availability of HPV self-sampling appeared to motivate screening participation among people who were apprehensive about clinician-collected samples (n = 5): “For me, [self-collection] made the difference between having one and not having [a cervical screening test]… I’m not having that invasive procedure [the clinician-collected cervical screening test] ever again.” [15]. This preference was also observed among people who had never previously participated in screening: “Yeah, I like that! That’s what I’ve been waiting for… even for someone who’s never done it, I would do that” [38]. In contrast, some participants indicated that previous negative experiences could deter them from undergoing any invasive procedures, including self-sampling (n = 2).

The experience of other self-sampling procedures such as bowel cancer screening kits or the use of tampons can make participants feel at ease with an HPV self-sampling test (n = 3): “I don’t see why not; I use tampons” [46].

While people with self-sampling experience generally gave positive feedback, those who required follow-up clinician-collected screening sometimes reported negative experiences (n = 2).

Environmental context and resources – Barriers and facilitators

The "barriers and facilitators” (subcategory of the "environmental context and resources” domain) was the most frequently identified, appearing in 34 articles. Within this category, numerous facilitators were associated with the use of self-sampling. Convenience and flexibility were particularly valued (n = 22). Participants liked that they could do the test in their own time, without leaving their home: “I think a lot of people are busy and this sample at-home kit makes it easier for people to do it at home when their lives are fast and chaotic” [3]. The comfort of the self-sampling procedure and the fact that it is painless were mentioned in 21 articles: “That I didn’t have to go through any pain of having a GP doing it with the gadget that causes me pain and there was no pain involved in having it [self-collection] done that way which I was really thankful for.” [16].

The privacy offered by self-sampling was often mentioned as a facilitator (n = 19): “I really actually enjoyed the process… less intrusive, that’s what I liked, not having someone else to look at your bits to do it, and that whole whakamā [reticence] around it, and just something I could do with no concerns or no difficulties.” [1]. Similarly, the absence of a healthcare professional during the procedure was considered an important benefit by many participants (n = 15). Some participants preferred to avoid male providers or felt pressure to make themselves ‘presentable’ for appointments (barriers that self-sampling helped eliminate): “[With HPV self-sampling], we would not have to open our legs in front of professionals.” [50].

Regarding practical barriers to traditional screening, participants appreciated not having to make an appointment or travel to a clinic, and felt that self-sampling saved them time (n = 18): “I didn’t have to go into an office and make an appointment and all that nonsense… if it was less expensive and less like taking time out of my life to do it, I would do it at home, for sure. Yeah. It’s just more comfortable.” [34].

HPV self-sampling was also perceived as less invasive and less intrusive in 11 articles, with the absence of speculum insertion identified as a key facilitator for participants: “I think more women would come for self-sampling. I know some of my friends feel intimidated by the Pap test. They think it is invasive personally and physically. (...) So yeah, I think having the choice to go by themselves, they would definitely go.” [50]. In addition, participants appreciated the bodily autonomy associated with self-sampling and the ability to control the procedure themselves (n = 12). For example, if they experienced discomfort, they could stop or adjust their position; something not always possible during clinician-collected sampling: “There is more control—I can stop if I feel any discomfort.” [17].

Social influences

Social influences were discussed in 22 articles. The most common theme was the importance of healthcare professionals’ recommendations to use HPV self-sampling, along with the need for their support and clear explanations (n = 10). Although self-sampling does not necessarily involve healthcare professionals, their influence was still emphasised by many participants: “My general health and what my doctor knows about my general health or whatever, can influence me either way, can sway me either way. Like I said, I feel like that’s the role we need to take, then I will go that way.” [37]. Adopting a collective or community-based approach to implementing HPV self-sampling was often suggested by participants (n = 5), particularly to target specific minority groups: “…make it more catchy and you know, like a group of some people coming together and then bang, you give them all those information and stuff…if that could be done as a collective thing with other Pacific Island people…I’m sure that would work” [10].

The influence of significant others (e.g., friends and family) was noted in two articles, and in four articles, participants reported that they would recommend self-sampling to others: “I found the self-test so easy and good; once it was done, I came back to my workplace and encouraged ladies I work with to go and get it done.” [1]. Regarding cultural and religious beliefs, the need to remove stigma surrounding cervical screening was mentioned in 4 articles. Taboos surrounding sexual health were noted in three articles, but self-sampling was perceived as compatible with cultural or religious beliefs in another three. For example, a participant said that “If you get such a self-sampling kit at home, you don’t even have to go to a doctor, so it is completely halal.” [31].

Emotions

References to emotions were found in 23 articles. Self-sampling was often described as less embarrassing than having the sample taken by a healthcare professional (n = 10): “It meant that I didn’t have to be humiliated, embarrassed on top of that table” [16]. A fear of finding something wrong was also mentioned as a barrier to screening participation, regardless of the type of screening test (n = 4): “I feel I have enough things to deal with. And taking that test will make me start wandering/worrying? Do I have this or don’t I have it [cancer]?” [37].

Fear and shock were reported by participants who received an abnormal result (n = 2), and confusion arose when results from self-sampling differed from those of clinician-collected samples (n = 3). As a result, participants emphasised the need for support following an abnormal result (n = 4), as well as the importance of receiving such news in an empathetic manner, ideally in person (n = 2). This is illustrated by a participant: “They rang me and said that the doctor wanted to talk to me about it. So, she contacted me and said to me that I’ve got HPV and that’s cancer. So I hung up the phone and cried because I thought I was going to die.” [1].

Stigma surrounding cervical screening and sexual health in general was mentioned in 4 articles: “Women don’t want to be associated with STIs” [50]. Similarly, some participants mentioned a fear and a discomfort related to touching their genital areas (n = 2). In addition, concerns about the impact of a positive HPV result on a woman's relationship and her partner were reported in three articles. Finally, participants expressed concerns about using an unfamiliar procedure (n = 2), missing other conditions that might be detected by a healthcare professional (n = 2), and relying solely on HPV self-sampling (n = 2): “I would prefer it [self-swab]. But would be scared to do one and not the other [Pap test].” [11].

Discussion

Summary of results

In the 36 articles included, the most common TDF categories extracted from the results were: “knowledge”; “skills”; “beliefs about capabilities”; “beliefs about consequences”; “intentions”; “environmental context and resources”; “social influences” and “emotions”. A lack of knowledge about HPV self-sampling was frequently reported, even in countries where it is available. Overall, self-sampling was perceived as acceptable and may encourage participation among under-screened populations. Participants in included studies frequently expressed concerns about their ability to perform the self-test correctly and about the reliability of the results. Consequently, a need for accessible information and clear instructions was expressed. Social influences appear to play an important role, particularly through the community and healthcare professionals. HPV self-sampling was considered a good alternative to reduce embarrassment associated with clinician-collected samples. Autonomy, privacy, convenience, and bodily control were major facilitators associated with HPV self-sampling. Negative past experiences with traditional screening further underscored the value of offering this option.

Comparison with other work

Our systematic review updated the previous review conducted by Nishimura and colleagues 41 about values and preferences of HPV self-sampling and including articles published up to 2018. The previous review reported similar findings, including high acceptability of HPV self-sampling and low self-confidence in collecting a reliable sample. These similarities suggest a consistency in views on HPV self-sampling over time.

The systematic review by [53], which examined barriers and facilitators to cervical cancer screening attendance in EU member states, also used the TDF to analyse its findings. Although Stuart and D’Lima’s review did not focus on HPV self-sampling, it reported findings similar to those of the current review. In both reviews, negative past experiences, such as painful or humiliating cervical screening procedures performed by healthcare professionals, were common. Our findings highlight HPV self-sampling as a valuable alternative, offering people greater bodily autonomy and comfort. In the “social influences” domain, both reviews noted the significant role of healthcare professionals. Similarly, in the “knowledge” domain, a lack of awareness about cervical cancer and screening was a shared theme. Within the “environmental context and resources” domain, Stuart and D’Lima identified barriers such as poor accessibility and time constraints, whereas our review identified facilitators specific to HPV self-sampling, including convenience, privacy, and time-saving.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative systematic review identifies a range of factors to consider when implementing HPV self-sampling for cervical screening in high-income countries. The TDF provided a useful framework for categorising findings from the included articles and offered a comprehensive overview of factors influencing views and experiences of HPV self-sampling from a behavioural science perspective. However, this review has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Its focus on qualitative studies may have overlooked factors better captured through quantitative designs. In addition, the review couldn’t distinguish between views and experiences of under-screened people and regularly screened people. Moreover, all included studies were weighted equally, regardless of sample size. Finally, the review is subject to the limitations of the included studies, particularly in terms of representativeness, which limits the generalisability of the findings.

Recommendations for policy and practice and future research

The findings of this systematic review can inform the implementation of HPV self-sampling for cervical screening in high-income countries by anticipating the needs and challenges of eligible populations. The main findings indicate that while HPV self-sampling is generally acceptable, participants expressed significant concerns about their ability to collect the sample correctly and the reliability of the results. Therefore, clear, step-by-step instructions should accompany the self-sampling kit. Ideally, these resources should be co-developed with eligible populations to ensure they are appropriate, accessible, and easy to understand. In addition, informing people about the necessity to perform a clinician – taken test if the self-sampling test detects HPV is essential. While HPV self-sampling could increase screening participation, it might not decrease cancer mortality if people do not complete follow-up. A concern for increasing existing health inequalities was raised [55] as underserved populations are less likely to complete follow-up after an abnormal screening result [6].

As highlighted in our findings, HPV self-sampling may be particularly relevant for specific populations, such as under-screened or underserved groups. Indeed, 26 out of the 36 included studies focused on specific groups that are known to be under-screened (e.g. ethnic minorities, gender minorities) or directly targeted under-screened or never-screened people. Negative past experiences, such as painful, uncomfortable, or embarrassing examinations conducted by healthcare professionals, can be significant barriers to staying up to date with cervical screening. For example, two articles focusing on older women [7]; [42] showed that women over 60 years old might feel vulnerable and exposed during a traditional screening procedure. HPV self-sampling was reported by older women as less painful and more comfortable than a traditional screening test. Our findings suggest that the availability of self-sampling can be a decisive factor in whether individuals participate in cervical screening. This highlights the importance of offering self-sampling as an option within screening programmes whenever feasible. However, for people who already attend traditional screening regularly, clinician-collected samples should remain the preferred option. Although HPV self-sampling does not always involve healthcare professionals, our findings indicate that their recommendations and explanations remain important to participants. Therefore, healthcare professionals should remain involved in the self-sampling process, for example, by recommending self-sampling to their patients and/or distributing the kits. Involvement of healthcare professionals is also crucial at a later stage if abnormal results are found, to perform further tests (sample taken by a clinician or colposcopy).

Additionally, participants valued the ability to perform the test at home. Offering multiple access options for the kits (e.g., by mail or in clinics) may be optimal, though further research is needed to assess the feasibility of such approaches.

Conclusion

This qualitative systematic review, guided by the TDF, provides a comprehensive overview of key factors influencing views and experiences of HPV self-sampling for cervical screening in high-income countries. The findings indicate that self-sampling is generally perceived as acceptable and may encourage participation among people who do not attend clinician-based screening. However, concerns were raised about the ability to perform the test correctly and the reliability of self-collected samples. Future research should focus on developing clear, user-friendly instructions and actively involve key stakeholders to ensure effective implementation.
