Comparative efficacy and safety of extended versus continuous infusion of beta-lactam antibiotics for severe infection: a network meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: Liujun Zhou, Qing Tang, Jianping Zhu, Gang Liang, Yi Yang, Yuhua Zhao, Zhenwei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged infusion (extended [EI, 2–4 h] or continuous [CI, 24 h] extended) of beta-lactam antibiotics is considered to have advantages for patients with severe infection compared with intermittent bolus (IB). However, the choice of EI and CI is unclear due to the lack of direct comparison. We aimed to compare the EI and CI of beta-lactams in patients with severe infections using a network meta-analysis method.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang Database, and Weipu Database for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing EI, CI, or IB with beta-lactams in adults with severe infections. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. A frequentist network meta-analysis with a random-effects model was performed. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.FindingsThirty-five RCTs (10,627 patients) were included. Risk of bias was moderate to high in most studies. For mortality, EI ranked highest (SUCRA 74.20%) with numerically lower rates versus IB (EI: OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.55–1.17; CI: OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.62–1.02). Both EI and CI significantly improved clinical cure rates versus IB (EI: OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.13–2.23; CI: OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.05–1.85), and EI ranked first (SUCRA 87.72%). For microbiological success, CI ranked highest (SUCRA 83.03%), followed by EI (SUCRA 42.98%) and IB (SUCRA 23.99%), but no significant difference was found. For hospital stay, EI was associated with a reduction of borderline statistical significance (MD -3.49 days, 95% CI -6.79 – -0.08), whereas CI did not show a significant reduction (MD 1.11 days, 95% CI -1.24 – 3.63), and EI ranked best (SUCRA 98.09%). No significant adverse event differences were observed. Subgroup analyses showed variable treatment rankings across categories, with no statistically significant subgroup effects.ConclusionIn patients with severe infections, both EI and CI improved clinical cure versus IB, whereas mortality did not differ significantly. Indirect evidence suggests EI may be more effective than CI in most outcomes except microbiological response. EI trended to shorten hospital stay but the difference was of borderline significance. Considering its practical feasibility, EI appears to be a favorable option based on current evidence. However, this finding is based on indirect evidence and requires confirmation in head-to-head trials.Registration PROSPERO CRD420251242437Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06142-2.Keywords: Prolonged infusion, Extended infusion, Continuous infusion, Beta-lactam antibiotics, Network meta-analysis

Introduction

Severe infections are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients worldwide [1]. Timely administration of appropriate antibiotics is crucial to improving clinical outcomes in these patients [2]. Beta-lactam antibiotics are effective against a broad spectrum of bacteria and are commonly used to treat life-threatening infections. The efficacy of beta-lactams is primarily driven by the time that the free drug concentration remains above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the pathogen (%T > MIC), classifying them as time-dependent antibiotics. Maximal bacterial killing is achieved when concentrations are maintained substantially above the MIC for the entire dosing interval [3, 4]. However, the pharmacokinetics of antibiotics are often significantly altered in critically ill patients with sepsis or septic shock due to increased volume of distribution and augmented renal clearance [5, 6]. This can lead to subtherapeutic exposures and an increased risk of treatment failure with conventional intermittent bolus (IB) dosing [7, 8].

Extended infusion (EI, 2–4 h) and continuous infusion (CI, 24 h) of beta-lactams can increase the probability of target attainment according to pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) simulation, which is also validated by in vivo studies [9–11]. A large amount of evidence, including numerous randomized controlled trials and subsequent meta-analyses, has demonstrated that, compared to IB, both EI and CI of beta-lactams are associated with improved clinical outcomes, including lower mortality, in patients with severe infection [12–23].

However, there are many practical difficulties hindering the implementation of prolonged infusion, especially CI. Critically ill patients usually receive multiple drug infusions, such as vasopressors, sedatives, analgesics, and parenteral nutrition [24, 25]. A 24-h CI can be logistically challenging in this setting, often requiring dedicated lines and additional nursing resources, and raises concerns about drug stability over such a prolonged period at room temperature [26, 27]. Adverse events may occur because of the decomposition of antibiotics during CI.

Compared to 24-h CI, EI requires less than half the time of administration and is easier to implement. Thus, it is important to know the adequate duration for the infusion of beta-lactams in severely ill patients. However, head-to-head studies that determine the superiority of EI and CI are lacking. To address this knowledge gap, we conduct a network meta-analysis to indirectly compare the efficacy and safety of EI versus CI of beta-lactam antibiotics in patients with severe infections. The results of this study aim to provide clinicians with a clearer evidence base for selecting the optimal and most practical beta-lactam infusion strategy for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Registration

The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration number CRD420251242437.

Search study and selection criteria

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, and Weipu Database from inception to September 5, 2025 without language restrictions. The search strategy combined terms for beta-lactam antibiotics with infusion modalities. Detailed search strategies for each database, including the original Chinese terms for CNKI, Wanfang, and Weipu, are presented in the Table S1. In addition, we manually checked the reference lists of the included studies to obtain additional relevant articles. Two investigators independently performed a systematic search of the above database to obtain potentially eligible studies. Any divergence was resolved by the third one.

Severe infection was defined as infections requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission or associated with high illness severity scores (e.g., APACHE II > 15 or SOFA > 5), including but not limited to sepsis, septic shock, hospital-acquired pneumonia, ventilator-associated pneumonia, complicated intra-abdominal infections, and bloodstream infections. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were eligible if they enrolled adult patients (≥ 18 years) with severe infections and compared EI or CI of any beta-lactam antibiotic with each other or with intermittent bolus (IB, ≤ 1 h) using the same antibiotic in all arms. The beta-lactam antibiotics analyzed in this study were restricted to those listed in Table S2. Studies were excluded if they were non-RCTs, included patients with viral or fungal infections, urinary tract infections, central nervous system infections, or those receiving renal replacement therapy at baseline, or if they reported only PK/PD outcomes without clinical data.

Data analysis

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form. Extracted information included study characteristics, patient demographics, infection type, severity scores (APACHE II, SOFA), antibiotic regimen, coadministered antibiotics, and outcomes. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at the longest available follow-up. Secondary outcomes included clinical cure rate, microbiological success, adverse events, and hospital length of stay. Corresponding authors were contacted for missing data when necessary.

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, with judgments of "low risk", "some concerns", or "high risk" across five domains [28].

For the network meta-analysis, we used a frequentist approach with a common heterogeneity parameter across all comparisons. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0 R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the netmeta package (version 3.3.1) and validated using the MetaInsight web application (https://crsu.shinyapps.io/metainsight/). We estimated relative treatment effects with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for all pairwise comparisons among EI, CI, and IB. For categorical outcomes (mortality, clinical cure rate, microbiological success, adverse events), we calculated odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI. For continuous outcomes (hospital length of stay, duration of antibiotic therapy), we calculated mean differences (MD) with 95% CI. A random-effects model was applied to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the estimated between-study standard deviation (τ). The between-study variance τ2 was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method.

We assessed the transitivity assumption by comparing the distribution of potential effect modifiers (patient severity, infection type, antibiotic class) across studies grouped by comparison type. Statistical inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence was evaluated globally using the design-by-treatment interaction model, with p < 0.05 indicating significant inconsistency. Additionally, we planned to use the node-splitting method to assess local inconsistency when feasible.

Subgroup analyses were prespecified according to antibiotic subclass (meropenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefepime, and other beta-lactams, exclude febrile neutropenia), infection type (sepsis, pneumonia, and other infections), sample size (large vs small studies), and risk of bias (low /some concerns, and high risk). For each subgroup, we performed network meta-analysis to estimate treatment effects (odds ratios [ORs] with 95% confidence intervals [95% CI]) for all comparisons and derived surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values. Between-study heterogeneity within each subgroup was assessed using τ. To test for differences between subgroups, we calculated the p value for subgroup interaction using a Q-test based on the ANOVA framework, with p < 0.05 indicating statistically significant effect modification.

Publication bias was assessed for outcomes with at least ten studies by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Asymmetry suggested by funnel plots or a p value < 0.05 on Egger’s test was considered indicative of potential publication bias.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant except where otherwise specified for heterogeneity or inconsistency tests.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A systematic search of seven electronic databases yielded 9,968 initial records. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, RCTs involving 10,627 patients with severe infections were included in the quantitative synthesis for the mortality outcome. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The full‑text articles excluded after assessment, along with the reasons, are listed in Table S3. Patients with variable age, disease severity (APACHE II score), and infection types were enrolled. The baseline characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The distribution of potential effect modifiers (infection type, baseline severity, antibiotic class) was similar between the studies comparing CI vs IB and those comparing EI vs IB, supporting the transitivity assumption. The study-level outcome data for each included trial are provided in Table S4. 19 studies compared CI with IB [10, 29–46], and 16 studies compared EI with IB [23, 47–61]. The duration of antibiotic therapy ranged from 4 to 14 days across studies.

Primary outcome: all-cause mortality

Data on all-cause mortality were available from 28 RCTs (10,339 patients). The network meta-analysis results are shown in Fig. 3 and summarized in Table 2. Both EI and CI were associated with numerically lower mortality compared with IB, although the confidence intervals included the null value. EI had the highest probability of being the best treatment for reducing mortality (SUCRA 74.20%), followed by CI (67.66%) and IB (8.14%). Heterogeneity was low (τ = 0.18). The funnel plot appeared symmetrical, and Egger’s tests suggested no significant publication bias (p = 0.13 for CI vs IB; p = 0.81 for EI vs IB; Fig. 3D).

(29 RCTs, 5,267 patients; Fig. 4, Table 2): Both EI and CI significantly improved clinical cure compared with IB (EI: OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.13–2.23; CI: OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.05–1.85). Microbiological success

(18 RCTs, 2,067 patients; Figure S1, Table 2): Point estimates favored both EI and CI over IB, but confidence intervals were wide and included the null value. CI ranked highest (SUCRA 83.03%), although estimates were imprecise. Considerable heterogeneity was present (τ = 0.54). No publication bias was detected (p > 0.05).

Adverse events

(11 RCTs, 8,199 patients; Figure S2, Table 2): No significant differences were observed among the three infusion strategies. IB had the most favorable safety profile (SUCRA 64.93%). Heterogeneity was moderate (τ = 0.77). No publication bias was detected (p > 0.05).

Hospital length of stay

(22 RCTs, 9,733patients; Figure S3, Table 2): EI was associated with a reduction in hospital stay compared with IB that was of borderline statistical significance (MD -3.49 days, 95% CI -6.79 to -0.08). EI ranked best with high probability (SUCRA 98.09%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (τ = 3.83 days). The 95% prediction interval for the effect estimate ranged from -11.71 to 4.73 days.

Evidence certainty assessment (CINeMA)

We assessed the certainty of evidence for each outcome using the CINeMA framework (Table S5). For all-cause mortality, the direct evidence for CI vs IB was rated as moderate certainty (some concerns of bias, no serious imprecision or heterogeneity), while the direct evidence for EI vs IB was rated as very low certainty due to major concerns of bias and indirectness. The comparison between EI and CI was entirely based on indirect evidence and was rated as very low certainty for all outcomes. For clinical cure rate, direct evidence for CI vs IB was rated as low certainty and for EI vs IB as moderate certainty; the indirect EI vs CI comparison was again very low certainty. For microbiological success, adverse events, and hospital length of stay, most direct and all indirect comparisons were rated as very low certainty, primarily due to risk of bias, imprecision, and/or substantial heterogeneity. Due to the open-chain structure of our network (no direct EI vs CI trials), the incoherence domain was not applicable for any comparison.

Assessment of global inconsistency

In our network, direct head-to-head comparisons between EI and CI were absent, and all evidence for the CI vs EI comparison was derived via the common reference of IB. As a result, the network is open and contains no closed loops that would allow estimation of agreement between direct and indirect evidence. Consequently, both the node-splitting method and the design-by-treatment interaction model, which require a full network with at least one closed loop, could not be implemented. We explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation of the current evidence base.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses for all-cause mortality and clinical cure are summarized in Tables 3 and 4. For all-cause mortality, the ranking of treatments varied across subgroups: EI was most favorable in some subcategories (e.g., meropenem, exclude febrile neutropenia, large sample size), whereas CI ranked highest in others (e.g., piperacillin/tazobactam, pneumonia). No statistically significant subgroup effects were found (p for interaction > 0.05). For clinical cure, EI ranked first in most subgroups (meropenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, pneumonia, exclude febrile neutropenia, etc.), although in the cefepime subgroup, IB ranked highest (SUCRA 62.11%), and in the ‘others’ antibiotic subgroup, CI ranked first (SUCRA 81.17%). Tests for subgroup differences were not significant.

Notably, the subgroup of studies with low risk or some concerns (i.e., excluding high risk-of-bias studies) yielded the following effect estimates: for all-cause mortality, EI vs IB: OR 0.84 (95% CI 0.46–1.56), CI vs IB: OR 0.86 (95% CI 0.58–1.06); for clinical cure, EI vs IB: OR 1.51 (95% CI 0.94–2.43), CI vs IB: OR 1.43 (95% CI 1.02–2.18).

To assess whether the inclusion of FN patients (a clinically distinct population) influenced our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the four FN trials [23, 50, 51, 58]. For all‑cause mortality (24 RCTs, 9,915patients), the effect estimates remained consistent with the main analysis (EI vs IB: OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.50–1.12; CI vs IB: OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.62–1.03), with EI still ranking highest (SUCRA 80.94%). For clinical cure (25 RCTs, 9,176patients), EI vs IB showed an OR of 2.03 (95% CI 1.36–3.04) and CI vs IB an OR of 1.30 (95% CI 1.10–1.68), again with EI ranking first (SUCRA 98.26%). These results confirm that the inclusion of FN studies did not alter the results.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this network meta-analysis provides the first comprehensive comparison of EI versus CI of beta-lactam antibiotics in patients with severe infections, using IB as the common reference. The synthesis of 35 RCTs involving over 10,000 patients shows that EI significantly improves clinical cure rates compared with IB, with favorable trends for mortality and no increase in adverse events. For hospital length of stay, the point estimate favored EI and the confidence interval excluded zero, but the upper bound was very close to zero (MD -3.49 days, 95% CI -6.79 – -0.08) and substantial heterogeneity (τ = 3.83 days) existed. CI improves clinical cure but does not shorten hospital stay. Indirect evidence suggests that EI may be favorable over CI across most outcomes, but the ranking should be interpreted cautiously given the absence of direct head-to-head trials.

The improved clinical cure with both EI and CI strategies validates the PK/PD rationale for prolonged administration [6, 11, 62–64]. Beta-lactams are time-dependent antibiotics, and maintaining free drug concentrations above the MIC is essential for efficacy, particularly in critically ill patients with altered pharmacokinetics [65–67]. Our findings confirm that both strategies achieve this more effectively than IB.

The dissociation between improved clinical cure and nonsignificant mortality reduction with EI is not unexpected. Mortality in severe infections depends on many factors beyond antibiotic efficacy, including comorbidities, source control, and organ support [68–70]. Clinical cure, as a more direct measure of treatment response, may be more sensitive to differences in antibiotic exposure. Similar patterns have been reported in previous meta-analyses, including the work by Vardakas and colleagues, who found a 30% mortality reduction with prolonged infusion but noted inconsistencies with clinical cure outcomes [16].

For hospital length of stay, the point estimate suggested a 3-day shorter stay with EI, and the confidence interval excluded zero, but the upper bound was very close to zero (MD -3.49 days, 95% CI -6.79 – -0.08), substantial heterogeneity was present (τ = 3.83 days). The 95% prediction interval for the effect estimate ranged from -11.71 to 4.73 days, crossing zero, indicating that future studies could find a reduction, no effect, or even a modest prolongation. Thus, while a numerical reduction was observed, the evidence is not robust. Shorter hospitalizations (if confirmed) could reduce costs, decrease hospital-acquired complications, and improve patient throughput [71–73]. Importantly, EI offers practical advantages regardless of statistical significance: easier workflow integration, fewer line conflicts, and no need for 24-h dedicated access [52, 71]. These advantages facilitate the translation of EI into real‑world practice.

EI may offer practical advantages over CI, but comparative effectiveness remains uncertain and requires confirmation in direct head-to-head trials. It offers a highly effective strategy that is feasible across diverse healthcare settings, from resource-limited wards to advanced ICUs. The ease of administration-requiring only half the infusion time-makes EI particularly attractive where intravenous access is limited, nursing resources are stretched, or drug stability is a concern [74–76].

Subgroup analyses generally support the robustness of the main findings. For mortality, the direction of effect consistently favored EI strategies across most subgroups, although statistical significance was not always reached due to small sample sizes. In the meropenem subgroup, EI showed a strong trend toward mortality reduction (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.19–1.24) and ranked highest (SUCRA 89.1%), while in the piperacillin/tazobactam subgroup, CI was associated with the greatest mortality reduction (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.03–1.18; SUCRA 95.1%) [12, 77]. For FN patients, a distinct subgroup analysis was performed because no CI studies were available. These differences may reflect class-specific pharmacokinetic properties or variations in study populations and should be interpreted cautiously. Tests for subgroup differences were not significant (p > 0.05 for all), suggesting that treatment effects are broadly consistent across antibiotic classes, infection types, and study characteristics.

Previous meta-analyses have shown the superiority of prolonged intravenous over IB for beta-lactams in severe infections [78, 79] . Vardakas and colleagues reported a 30% mortality reduction with prolonged infusion compared with short-term infusion (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.56–0.87) in patients with sepsis [16]. Our study extends this work by providing the first indirect comparison between EI and CI, showing that both are effective, but EI offers additional benefits in reduced hospital stay. The mortality reduction we observed with EI (OR 0.72) is consistent with previous estimates, supporting the validity of our analysis.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the mortality reduction with EI did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.55–1.03), and the confidence intervals included the null value; larger trials powered for mortality are needed. Second, we observed substantial heterogeneity for some outcomes, particularly hospital length of stay (τ = 3.83 days). The 95% prediction interval for the effect estimate of EI vs IB on hospital stay ranged from -11.71 to 4.73 days, indicating that the true effect in a future study could range from a large reduction to a modest increase. This heterogeneity reflects clinical and methodological diversity across trials. Third, the comparison between EI and CI is entirely based on indirect evidence via the common comparator of IB, as no head-to-head trial directly comparing EI and CI has been published. Our CINeMA assessment further underscores this concern: the certainty of evidence for all indirect comparisons between EI and CI was rated as very low across all outcomes. Therefore, our findings regarding EI versus CI should be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating rather than definitive. Fourth, 43.2% of the included studies had a high risk of bias, primarily due to lack of blinding, which may influence subjective outcomes such as clinical cure. However, a sensitivity analysis excluding these high-risk studies yielded similar results for mortality and clinical cure, suggesting robustness [30, 47, 49]. Fifth, our results apply mainly to the nine beta‑lactam antibiotics prespecified in the protocol and may not extend to other agents. One study (Nicolau_2001) was retained despite permitting other beta‑lactams because its primary intervention was ceftazidime (a pre-specified agent) and data could not be separated; Class pharmacodynamics support this decision, and its exclusion did not change the conclusions. Finally, we cannot completely rule out publication bias for some secondary outcomes, although the primary findings for mortality and clinical cure were robust to this bias.

Conclusions

In patients with severe infections, both EI and CI significantly improved clinical cure compared with IB, whereas no significant differences in mortality were observed among the three strategies. EI trended to shorten hospital stay but the difference was of borderline significance. Indirect evidence suggests that EI may be favorable over CI for most outcomes except microbiological response, but this finding is solely based on indirect comparisons and should be interpreted with caution. Given its potential favorable efficacy and greater practical feasibility, EI appears to be a favorable option based on current indirect evidence. Direct head-to-head trials are needed to confirm its superiority over CI.
