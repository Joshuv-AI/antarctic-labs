Quantification of Credal Uncertainty: A Distance-Based Approach
Xabier Gonzalez-Garcia, Siu Lun Chau, Julian Rodemann, Michele Caprio, Krikamol Muandet, Humberto Bustince, Sébastien Destercke, Eyke Hüllermeier, Yusuf Sale

Abstract
Credal sets, i.e., closed convex sets of probability measures, provide a natural framework to represent aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in machine learning. Yet how to quantify these two types of uncertainty for a given credal set, particularly in multiclass classification, remains underexplored. In this paper, we propose a distance-based approach to quantify total, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty for credal sets. Concretely, we introduce a family of such measures within the framework of Integral Probability Metrics (IPMs). The resulting quantities admit clear semantic interpretations, satisfy natural theoretical desiderata, and remain computationally tractable for common choices of IPMs. We instantiate the framework with the total variation distance and obtain simple, efficient uncertainty measures for multiclass classification. In the binary case, this choice recovers established uncertainty measures, for which a principled multiclass generalization has so far been missing. Empirical results confirm practical usefulness, with favorable performance at low computational cost.

The growing impact of machine learning in societal and scientific applications has intensified the need for predictive models that are not only accurate but also uncertainty-aware. Central to this discussion is the distinction between
aleatoric
and
epistemic
uncertainty
(
31
)
. The former refers to variability that is inherent in the data-generating process and hence irreducible, whereas the latter reflects limited knowledge about the predictive mechanism.
This distinction is also operationally important, as only epistemic uncertainty is reducible — for instance, by collecting more data or improving the model.

Accordingly, a broad range of methods has been developed to learn predictive models that can represent
both
types of uncertainty, often by means of higher-order formalisms such as second-order distributions
(
26
;
34
;
38
;
52
)
, or through the framework of
imprecise probability

(
58
;
8
)
, which extends classical probability theory beyond the precise, axiomatic setting of Kolmogorov.
Within the latter,
credal sets
, i.e., (convex) sets of probability measures
(
36
;
58
)
, offer a natural and intuitive alternative for machine learning
(
13
;
59
;
37
, cf.)
: randomness within each probability measure captures aleatoric uncertainty, while the extent of the set itself reflects epistemic uncertainty.

In this paper, we address the complementary task of
(credal) uncertainty quantification
: given a prediction in the form of a credal set, we seek summaries (
viz.
uncertainty measures) that capture the extent of predictive uncertainty.
A central and nontrivial challenge is
disaggregation
, i.e., how to decompose (total) uncertainty into its aleatoric and epistemic components.
While the literature offers numerous measures of total, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty for credal sets
(
4
;
6
;
2
;
18
, e.g.)
, comparatively little work addresses the requirements of modern machine learning.
In particular, many existing proposals either lack a clear semantic interpretation of the decomposed components, often relying on an assumed additive decomposition, or they are computationally too costly for use in (large-scale) machine learning pipelines.
In this spirit,
30
provide a critical discussion of uncertainty measures and highlight several pitfalls that arise when such notions are used for credal uncertainty quantification, especially in machine learning settings.
Moreover, they propose measures that satisfy natural axiomatic desiderata and whose theoretical expectations align well with empirical behavior, for instance in selective prediction experiments.
A key limitation, however, is that their treatment is restricted to the binary case, and a principled extension to multiclass classification has so far been missing.
More generally, credal uncertainty quantification in the multiclass setting has received comparatively little attention in machine learning, arguably due to the additional conceptual and computational challenges
(
50
)
. This gap motivates the present work.

Contributions.

We present a principled framework for multiclass credal uncertainty quantification that yields a family of measures of total, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty with clear semantics and strong computational properties.
Our approach is distance-based, partly inspired by
49
, but formulated for credal sets rather than second-order distributions, and grounded in the framework of Integral Probability Metrics (IPMs)
(
41
)
.
We instantiate the framework with the total variation distance, which yields particularly simple and efficient measures for multiclass classification. In the binary case, this choice recovers the uncertainty measures of
30
.
Empirical results on standard benchmarks show that the resulting measures are competitive with existing baselines, thereby making them well tailored to machine learning needs.

2
Preliminaries

We consider supervised classification with instance space
\mathcal{X}
and finite label set
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,K\}
.
Further, we assume that the learner has access to (i.i.d.) training data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(x^{(n)},y^{(n)})\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
Hypotheses are probabilistic predictors
h:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{K-1}
, mapping each
x\in\mathcal{X}
to a distribution
p(\cdot\mid x,h)
over
\mathcal{Y}
, where
\Delta_{K-1}
denotes the probability simplex on
\mathcal{Y}
.
Our focus is predictive uncertainty at a query
x_{q}
, i.e., uncertainty about the predicted label
\hat{y}_{q}
.
While
h(x_{q})=p(\cdot\mid x_{q},h)
accounts for aleatoric uncertainty, it leaves uncertainty about the ground truth distribution itself implicit.
To make this explicit, we move from point predictions in
\Delta_{K-1}
to set-valued predictions
H:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{K-1})
, where
\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{K-1})
is a suitable family of subsets of the simplex (e.g., nonempty closed sets).
The prediction at
x_{q}
is the set
H(x_{q})=\mathcal{Q}_{x_{q}}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
, a
credal prediction
.
When the context is clear, we simply write
\mathcal{Q}
.
Often, we consider
finitely generated
credal predictions, i.e., sets of the form

\mathcal{Q}=\mathrm{conv}\{p^{(1)},\dots,p^{(M)}\}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1},

where
p^{(m)}\in\Delta_{K-1}
are a finite collection of predictive distributions (e.g., induced by an ensemble of probabilistic classifiers), and
\mathrm{conv}(\cdot)
denotes the convex hull.
Given
\mathcal{Q}
and
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, the lower and upper probabilities are

\displaystyle\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(A)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(A),\qquad\overline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(A)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(A),

which summarize the range of plausible probabilities assigned to
A

(
58
)
.
For finitely generated credal sets, these quantities can be computed from the generators: since
p\mapsto p(A)
is linear, the extrema over
\mathcal{Q}
are attained at extreme points, and hence

\displaystyle\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(A)=\min_{m\in[M]}p^{(m)}(A),\qquad\overline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(A)=\max_{m\in[M]}p^{(m)}(A).

3
Uncertainty Quantification

Empirical evaluation remains indispensable, but an axiomatic perspective is equally valuable, as it clarifies what qualitative behavior one should expect from a measure and whether such expectations align with its formal properties.
This is particularly important in light of recent critiques of uncertainty quantification methods, which have highlighted that some widely used approaches fail to satisfy basic properties one would reasonably expect
(
63
)
.

Following
3
;
32
, we consider a generic measure
f:\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{K-1})\to\mathbb{R}
that maps a credal set
\mathcal{Q}
to a scalar, and we recall a collection of common axioms for uncertainty measures:

A1

Boundedness: There exists
C>0
such that we have
f(\mathcal{Q})\in[0,C]
for all credal sets
\mathcal{Q}
.

A2

Continuity: The functional
f
is continuous (with respect to an appropriate topology on
\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{K-1})
).

A3

Monotonicity: If
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, then
f(\mathcal{Q})\leq f(\mathcal{M})
.

A4

Probability consistency: If
\mathcal{Q}=\{p\}
is precise, then
f(\mathcal{Q})
reduces to a functional of the single distribution
p
, i.e., the measure carries no residual set-level structure when the credal set is a singleton.

A5

Subadditivity: For a joint credal set
\mathcal{Q}
on
\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\mathcal{Y}_{2}
with marginals
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
,

f(\mathcal{Q})\leq f(\mathcal{Q}_{1})+f(\mathcal{Q}_{2}).

A6

Additivity: Under (imprecise) probabilistic independence, equality holds in (A5)
(
22
, see)
.

We emphasize that A5–A6 are
conditional
desiderata tied to notions of (imprecise) independence, whereas A1–A4 are regularity and consistency requirements.
In particular, monotonicity and probability consistency formalize that enlarging the set of plausible distributions should not reduce uncertainty and that the framework agrees with the precise case.
Moreover, machine learning applications often impose computational constraints, especially in multiclass settings with many labels.
This motivates complementing the above desiderata with an additional requirement.

A7

Extreme-point characterization: Let
\mathrm{ext}(\mathcal{Q})
denote the set of extreme points of
\mathcal{Q}
. Then
f(\mathcal{Q})=f(\mathrm{ext}(\mathcal{Q}))
.

Axiom A7 formalizes an invariance principle with respect to convexification.
When a credal set is specified via a set of generators and then closed under convex combinations, A7 ensures that the uncertainty measure depends only on the extreme points and is unaffected by the particular interior representation of
\mathcal{Q}
.
This property can substantially simplify computation by reducing evaluation of
f(\mathcal{Q})
to
\mathrm{ext}(\mathcal{Q})
, for example when
\mathcal{Q}
is induced by a finite collection of predictive models such as an ensemble of probabilistic classifiers.

3.1
Existing Measures

In the following, we briefly review prominent existing approaches to credal uncertainty quantification. We focus on measures most commonly used in machine learning practice. For each family, we highlight key limitations that motivate our distance-based framework. For additional critical discussion, see e.g.
30
;
50
.

3.1.1
Entropy Measures

Arguably the most prominent measure of uncertainty for a (precise) probability measure
p\in\Delta_{K-1}
is the Shannon entropy

(
53
)
,

S(p)=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y)\log_{2}p(y).

Entropy is minimal when
p
assigns all probability mass to a single label and maximal when
p
is the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
, while also enjoying well-known axiomatic and operational justifications
(
23
)
.
Shannon entropy can be lifted from precise distributions to credal sets by taking its upper and lower envelopes over
\mathcal{Q}

(
6
;
2
)
.
Concretely, define

\displaystyle S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})\coloneqq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}S(p),\qquad S_{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})\coloneqq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}S(p).

The upper envelope
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})
is commonly interpreted as a measure of total uncertainty, while the lower envelope
S_{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})
captures a notion of aleatoric uncertainty. Assuming an additive decomposition, epistemic uncertainty is then quantified by the residual gap
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})-S_{\ast}(\mathcal{Q}).

Criticism.
The upper envelope
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})
is known to satisfy the classical axioms (A1)–(A6) under standard assumptions.
From a machine learning perspective, however, its practical use can be limited, since computing
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}S(p)
is a nontrivial optimization problem that has no closed-form solution and may require iterative solvers at prediction time.
Moreover, apart from falling off as a “residual” from the additive decomposition of total uncertainty,
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})-S_{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})
is lacking a theoretical (axiomatic) grounding and clear semantic interpretation
(
30
)
.

3.1.2
Hartley Measures

In contrast, set-theoretic approaches model uncertainty by defining a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
of possible outcomes, distinguishing between what is plausible and what is ruled out.
A standard way to quantify the uncertainty associated to the set
A
is Hartley’s non-specificity
(
27
)
,
H(A)=\log|A|
, which depends only on how many (equally likely) outcomes remain possible.
To lift Hartley-type non-specificity to credal sets, one can combine
\log|A|
with a set function derived from the lower probability
\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}
via a Möbius transform
(
4
)
. Concretely, define

\displaystyle\operatorname{GH}(\mathcal{Q})\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}}\log|A|\;m_{\mathcal{Q}}(A),

where
m_{\mathcal{Q}}(A)\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{B\subseteq A}(-1)^{|A\setminus B|}\,\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(B)
is the Möbius inverse of
\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}
.
In the literature,
\operatorname{GH}(\mathcal{Q})
is commonly interpreted as a measure of epistemic uncertainty.
Total uncertainty can again be quantified by
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})
. Assuming an additive decomposition, one then defines the corresponding aleatoric component as the residual
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})-\operatorname{GH}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Criticism.
The quantity
\operatorname{GH}(\mathcal{Q})
captures a notion of
imprecision
by quantifying, in a set-theoretic manner, how many alternatives remain plausible, largely independent of the shape of the distributions in
\mathcal{Q}
.
It vanishes for precise predictions (
\mathcal{Q}=\{p\}
) and satisfies several desirable axiomatic properties in the imprecise probability literature
(
4
)
.
From a machine learning perspective, however, its practical use is limited by unfavorable scaling. Evaluation requires summation over the power set
2^{\mathcal{Y}}
and thus becomes prohibitive even for moderate
K
.
Moreover, the associated aleatoric component defined via the residual
S^{\ast}(\mathcal{Q})-\operatorname{GH}(\mathcal{Q})
inherits the general drawback of additive disaggregation schemes, namely that it is specified by subtraction rather than by an independent semantic principle.

3.1.3
Other Approaches

Recently,
distance-based
notions of uncertainty
(
49
;
7
;
18
, e.g.)
have attracted attention in the literature.
For instance,
49
develop uncertainty measures for second-order distributions by comparing a distribution-over-distributions to a reference notion of certainty via probability metrics. Our work is aligned with this perspective, but is formulated for credal predictions and thus conceptually extends the distance-based idea to credal sets.

In a related line of work,
18
study extensions of IPMs
(
41
)
to imprecise models.
An IPM is a discrepancy between probability measures defined through a class
\mathcal{F}
of measurable test functions (typically bounded, and often chosen to enforce additional regularity such as Lipschitz continuity).
For two probability measures
p
and
q
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{A})
, the IPM is

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q)\;\coloneqq\;\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\Bigl|\;\int f\,\mathrm{d}p\;-\;\int f\,\mathrm{d}q\;\Bigr|.

Intuitively,
d_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q)
measures how well
p
and
q
can be distinguished by tests in
\mathcal{F}
, namely by the largest discrepancy in expected function values.
Many standard distances are special cases of IPMs, obtained by choices of the test-function class
\mathcal{F}
.

18

4
A Family of Novel Measures

As we highlighted in the preceding section, current approaches to credal uncertainty quantification have limitations.
They often rely on additive decompositions, so the resulting components lack direct semantic meaning, or scale poorly.
In this section, we propose a distance-based framework for credal uncertainty quantification.

Total uncertainty.

From a distance-based perspective, total uncertainty quantifies how far a credal set
\mathcal{Q}
is from
full certainty
.
We identify fully certain predictions with the vertices of the
K
-simplex, i.e., with the Dirac measures

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\Delta_{K-1})=\{\,\delta_{y}:y\in\mathcal{Y},\ \delta_{y}(A)=\mathbf{1}_{\{y\}}(A)\,\},

each representing complete certainty about a single class.
Since closeness to one vertex necessarily implies greater distance from others, it is natural to take the
nearest
Dirac measure as the reference point of zero uncertainty.
To measure the distance of the credal set (as a set-valued object) full certainty, we compute the Hausdorff-type distance from
\mathcal{Q}
to each Dirac measure and select the closest one:

\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\;:=\;\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right)

(1)

For fixed
y
, the inner supremum measures the worst-case distance to
\delta_{y}
over all elements of
\mathcal{Q}
.
The outer infimum then selects the deterministic state that is closest to
\mathcal{Q}
in this worst-case sense.
Thus, (
1
) captures how far
every
probabilistic model in
\mathcal{Q}
is from some state of full certainty.

Aleatoric uncertainty.

For credal predictions
\mathcal{Q}
, aleatoric uncertainty is generally not uniquely determined, since different
p\in\mathcal{Q}
may exhibit different levels of randomness.
Consequently, any scalar quantification of aleatoric uncertainty for
\mathcal{Q}
reflects an additional modeling (or summarization) choice and necessarily loses information.
Yet, scalar summaries are often needed in downstream applications that require a total ordering or thresholding of predictions.

We first consider the precise setting and then lift this notion to credal predictions.
For a single predictive distribution
p
, aleatoric uncertainty quantifies its intrinsic randomness and is obtained as the distance to full certainty, i.e., to the nearest Dirac measure. Equivalently, this is the special case of (
1
) in which the credal set reduces to
\{p\}
:

\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p):=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right).

(2)

Rather than forcing a scalar summary, we therefore define aleatoric uncertainty on the quantification level as the
set
of all plausible randomness values induced by elements of
\mathcal{Q}
:

\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\;:=\;\bigl\{\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p)\;:\;p\in\mathcal{Q}\bigr\}.

(3)

This set-valued quantification is not merely a formal convenience, but a principled consequence of the credal representation.
Indeed, any direct aggregation of (
3
) into a single scalar implicitly selects or combines elements of
\mathcal{Q}
and thereby resolves part of the uncertainty that the credal set is meant to represent.

To make the set-valued quantification operational in downstream tasks, we explicitly separate the quantification level from the summary level.
Let
\mathfrak{A}
denote the class of all aleatoric uncertainty sets attainable from credal predictions, i.e.,

\mathfrak{A}\;\coloneqq\;\bigl\{\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\;:\;\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}\bigr\}.

A (task-dependent) summary operator is a mapping

\Phi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{Z},

where
\mathcal{Z}
is an application-specific output space (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or another ordered space).
Given such a
\Phi
, we define the corresponding actionable aleatoric summary by

\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\;\coloneqq\;\Phi\bigl(\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\bigr).

This formalizes that any scalar (or vector-valued) aleatoric score used in practice is not part of quantification itself, but results from an additional summarization choice.

A summary operator
\Phi
is called
lossless
(on
\mathfrak{A}
) if it is injective, i.e., if

\Phi(A)=\Phi(B)\;\Longrightarrow\;A=B\qquad\text{for all }A,B\in\mathfrak{A}.

In that case,
\Phi(\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}))
retains the full information contained in the set-valued aleatoric uncertainty
\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
.

A canonical choice in our setting is the endpoint summary

\Phi_{\mathrm{int}}(A)\;\coloneqq\;\bigl(\inf A,\sup A\bigr),

which yields
\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi_{\mathrm{int}}}(\mathcal{Q})\;=\;\bigl(\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}),\,\overline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\bigr)
, where

\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\coloneqq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p),\quad\overline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\coloneqq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p).

Under mild conditions (e.g., if
\mathcal{Q}
is convex and compact and
p\mapsto\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p)
is continuous), the image
\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a compact interval, so that

\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})=\bigl[\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}),\,\overline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\bigr].

Hence, in this case, the endpoint summary is lossless and provides an exact finite-dimensional representation of the set-valued aleatoric uncertainty.

When a downstream task requires a total ordering (as in selective prediction), an ordering rule can be imposed on top of the endpoint summary (e.g., lexicographically), without changing the underlying set-valued aleatoric quantification.

Epistemic uncertainty.

Our aim is to quantify the imprecision represented by a credal set. Intuitively, a larger credal set corresponds to greater ignorance about the data-generating mechanism. Although quantities like the volume of a credal set have been considered, they show limited effectiveness in multiclass classification tasks
(
50
)
.
We instead quantify epistemic uncertainty via the (half) maximal pairwise distance within the credal set, i.e., via half its maximal diameter
(
17
;
55
;
15
)
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}\,d_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q)

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\Bigl(\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]-\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]\Bigr).

In contrast to volume-based notions, the diameter depends only on the maximal discrepancy among plausible predictive distributions, not on the overall size of the set.
A large diameter means that
\mathcal{Q}
contains at least two distributions with strongly conflicting predictions.
This disagreement captures ignorance about the ground truth and is independent of the intrinsic randomness of the individual elements of
\mathcal{Q}
(cf. Proposition
4.4
).
The factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
provides a convenient normalization and, in particular, ensures that epistemic uncertainty is bounded by total uncertainty (Proposition
4.3
).

4.1
Theoretical Properties

Since the axioms introduced earlier are formulated for scalar-valued functionals on credal sets, they apply directly to total and epistemic uncertainty.
By contrast, aleatoric uncertainty is quantified by the set-valued object (
3
).
To connect it to the axiomatic framework, we therefore consider scalar summaries of (
3
).
In the following, we focus on the canonical endpoint summaries

\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
and
\overline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Remark 4.1
.

The properties established below rely on two standing assumptions on the function class
\mathcal{F}
:
First, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
is rich enough to separate probability measures, so that the IPM
d_{\mathcal{F}}
defines a metric on
\Delta_{K-1}
(rather than only a pseudo-metric).
For example, if
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the unit ball of an RKHS with characteristic kernel
k
, then the corresponding MMD is a metric on a suitable class of probability distributions; see, e.g.,
40
.
Second, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a uniform class with respect to weak convergence
(
41
;
47
)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}
is equicontinuous and has uniformly bounded span,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\sup f-\inf f\bigr)<\infty.

By
41
, this ensures good continuity properties of the induced IPM.
On a finite label space
\mathcal{Y}
, equicontinuity is automatic.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a uniform class with respect to weak convergence such that
d_{\mathcal{F}}
is a metric on
\Delta_{K-1}
. Then
\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
and
\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
satisfy A1–A4 and A7;
\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
satisfies A1, A2, and A4; and
\overline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
satisfies A1–A4.

While we focus here on endpoint summaries, the same perspective extends to any scalar summary operator
\Phi
.
Which axioms hold for the resulting aleatoric summary
\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})=\Phi(\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}))
depends on the choice of
\Phi
.

We next show several structural properties that further strengthen the interpretation of the proposed framework.
First, total uncertainty is no smaller than epistemic uncertainty and any element of
\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
be a credal set. Then, for every
p\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\leq\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p)\leq\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}).

Second, epistemic uncertainty does not depend on the location of the credal set in the simplex (i.e., it is translation-invariant within
\Delta_{K-1}
).

Proposition 4.4
.

For any two credal sets
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
that are translates of each other within the simplex,

\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}).

Finally, we relate our epistemic quantification to MMI based on lower and upper envelopes.
Unlike MMI, our measure operates directly on the credal set, while remaining related.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
be a credal set. Then the maximal diameter is upper bounded by the MMI of its lower probability:

\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\bigl(\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}},\,\overline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}\bigr).

Equality holds whenever the lower expectation over
\mathcal{Q}
coincides with the Choquet integral with respect to
\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, e.g., when
\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is convex
(
54
)
.

Closed-form expressions and computational properties depend on the specific choice of
\mathcal{F}
.
In the next section, we specialize to the total variation test class, which satisfies the assumptions of Remark
4.1
on finite label spaces and yields explicit formulas for all proposed quantities.

4.2
Total Variation Instantiation

We instantiate the proposed framework with total variation (TV), using the test class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}\;\coloneqq\;\{\mathbf{1}_{A}:A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\}

Then, for all
p,q\in\Delta_{K-1}

d_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p,q)=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}}\bigl(p(A)-q(A)\bigr).

This choice is natural in multiclass classification because it requires no additional geometric structure on the label space and yields following simple closed-form expressions.

Proposition 4.6

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}

5
Experiments

Empirically assessing uncertainty quantification is difficult, especially because there is no ground truth. We therefore conduct selective prediction experiments, where the model may abstain on inputs it deems uncertain. Accuracy–rejection curves (ARCs)
29
visualize this: sort test points in decreasing order by decreasing uncertainty score, reject the top
r\%
, and compute accuracy on the remaining. For an uncertainty-aware learner that can distinguish certain from less certain cases, accuracy should be an increasing function of the rejection rate
r
.

We benchmark TV-instantiated measures against entropy and Hartley baselines. The endpoint summary is chosen for aleatoric uncertainty owing to its lossless guarantees, and instances are ranked lexicographically. The rationale is grounded in selective prediction: an instance whose
minimum
attainable uncertainty across the credal set is already high should be rejected before one that admits at least some low-uncertainty explanation.
Implementation details and experimental setup are described in Appendices
C
and
D
.

Figure

18
by reporting ARCs for total, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty on
cifar-10
and
cifar-100
, computed from ensemble-induced credal sets. Across all three components, our measures consistently match or outperform the baselines on both datasets.
Table

presents overall performance on a broader collection of KEEL datasets (detailed in Appendix
D
). Alongside the Area Under the Curve (AUC), we report the Monotonicity Ratio (MR), defined as the percentage of bins in which the AR curve does not decrease, capturing shape differences AUC may overlook. Competitive performance together with low computation times highlights a key benefit of our approach. Additional experiments are provided in Appendix
E
.

Limitations.

We identified a pathological case, though it stems from a representational artifact rather than a deficiency of the measures themselves. An adversarial or miscalibrated model in the credal set can shift the zero uncertainty reference point, and the resulting quantities inherit this effect. An illustrative example is given in Appendix
E
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a distance-based framework for credal uncertainty quantification in multiclass classification. It yields principled measures of total, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty. We showed that the measures admit clear semantics, satisfy natural desiderata under mild assumptions, and recover established binary measures. For the total variation instantiation, we derived closed-form expressions with favorable complexity. Empirically, our measures are competitive with entropy and Hartley baselines while offering substantial runtime gains. A natural direction for future work is to further study alternative IPM classes (e.g., Wasserstein or kernel-based variants) as well as extensions beyond classification to structured prediction and decision-making under imprecision.

Appendix A
Related work

The idea behind
credal sets
(and the terminology) originates from
35
and was later popularized and given a solid theoretical foundation by
58
. As introduced in Section
4
, credal sets usually—not always—refer to closed and convex sets of probability measures. This is due to the fact that under “coherence” axioms, the representing set of dominating probabilities is automatically closed and convex, see §3.3 in
58
.
Notably, credal sets are only one of several imprecise probabilistic models
[
8
]
, alongside capacities
[
21
]
,
interval probabilities
[
62
,
61
]
, and lower previsions
[
58
,
56
]
, to name only a few. A credal set makes the distinction between sources of uncertainty explicit: randomness within each probability measure captures aleatoric uncertainty, while the extent of the set itself reflects epistemic uncertainty. Due to their intuitive appeal and their generality, credal sets have received increasing attention in the machine learning and statistics community recently. Methodological applications range from Bayesian neural networks
[
12
]
and optimization
[
48
]
to hypothesis testing
[
19
]
and conformal prediction
[
14
,
16
]
.
How to learn credal sets algorithmically has also attracted growing attention in recent years, with several complementary lines of work.
Prominent directions include relative-likelihood approaches (e.g., CreRL), which define plausibility via a likelihood-ratio budget and search for a diverse set of high-likelihood hypotheses
[
37
]
; wrapper-style methods (Credal Wrapper) that aggregate independently trained models to obtain classwise lower/upper probability bounds and hence box credal sets
[
59
]
; distance-filtered ensembles (Credal Ensembling) that retain only the closest predictions under a chosen metric when forming the set
[
42
]
; credal deep ensembles that learn per-class lower/upper bounds with specialized heads and losses
[
60
]
; and credal Bayesian deep learning, which aggregates BNNs trained under diverse priors and takes the convex hull of sampled posteriors
[
13
]
.

A central question for machine learning practitioners is how to extract from it meaningful summaries of total, aleatoric, and epistemic uncertainty that can drive downstream tasks such as selective prediction, out-of-distribution detection, or active learning. Several recent works have addressed this challenge.
30
provided a comparison of existing credal uncertainty measures, identifying issues such as ad hoc additive decompositions and violations of desirable axiomatic properties; notably, the measures they found to be best justified are limited to the binary classification setting, leaving the multiclass case underexplored.
50
investigated whether the geometric volume of the credal set is a meaningful measure of epistemic uncertainty, finding it to be of limited effectiveness in multiclass classification.
51
proposed a label-wise decomposition of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, moving from global scalar summaries toward per-class quantification better suited to the structure of multiclass problems.
28
proposed uncertainty measures for credal sets grounded in proper scoring rules. These newer contributions build on a rich foundation in imprecise probability theory and uncertainty quantification
45
,
46
,
39
,
57
,
5
,
3
,
2
,
1
,
11
.

In contrast to the existing approaches discussed above, our work departs from the current literature in three key respects.
First, we abandon the additive decomposition convention and instead provide independent, semantically grounded definitions for each uncertainty component—total, aleatoric, and epistemic—based on distance-based principles within the IPM framework.
Second, rather than reducing credal aleatoric uncertainty to a single scalar, we characterize it as the
set
of aleatoric uncertainty values attainable across the credal set, thereby preserving the ambiguity encoded by the credal prediction; when a summary is needed for downstream tasks, we provide aggregation strategies that are lossless in a well-defined sense, e.g., the endpoint summary.
Third, we place particular emphasis on computational tractability: the resulting family of measures is designed to scale to modern multiclass classification settings, yielding closed-form expressions with favorable complexity for common IPM choices such as total variation.

p\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\leq\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p)\leq\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}).

Proof.

Epistemic Uncertainty.
For any
p,q\in\mathcal{Q}
and any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, the triangle inequality gives
d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,q\right)\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right)+d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(\delta_{y},q\right).
Taking the supremum over
p,q\in\mathcal{Q}
and using that the two terms depend on different variables,

\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,q\right)\leq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right)+\sup_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(\delta_{y},q\right)=2\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right).

Then, taking
\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}
on the right-hand side yields

\frac{1}{2}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,q\right)\leq\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right),

which is precisely
\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})\leq\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Aleatoric Uncertainty.
Since
p\in\mathcal{Q}
, we have
d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(p,\delta_{y}\right)\leq\sup_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}}\!\left(q,\delta_{y}\right)
for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Taking
\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}
on both sides preserves the inequality, yielding
\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}}(p)\leq\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
.
∎

Proposition
4.4
.

For any two credal sets
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
that are translates of each other within the simplex, it holds that

\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}).

Proof.

For a fixed
t\in[-1,1]^{K}
with
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}t_{y}=0
, define
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\{p^{\prime}\in\Delta_{K-1}:p^{\prime}(\{y\})=p(\{y\})+t_{y},\;\forall y\in\mathcal{Y},\;\text{for some }p\in\mathcal{Q}\}
. Expanding the expectation of
f\in\mathcal{F}
under
p^{\prime}
gives
\mathbb{E}_{p^{\prime}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]+\sum_{y}f(y)\,t_{y}
. Hence,

\sup_{p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{p^{\prime}}[f]-\inf_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[f]=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]-\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[f].

Halving both sides and taking
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
yields
\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})=\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{Q})
.

∎

Proposition
4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
be a credal set. Then, instantiating (
1
), (
2
), and (
4
) with the test class
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}
yields the following closed-form expressions:

\displaystyle\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=1-\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\{y\}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{AU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p)

\displaystyle=1-\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(\{y\}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\bigl|p(\{y\})-q(\{y\})\bigr|.

Proof.

We derive each expression separately for the test class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}=\{\mathbbm{1}_{A}:A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\}
.

Total Uncertainty.
The total variation distance between a probability measure
p
and a Dirac measure
\delta_{y}
reduces to

d_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p,\delta_{y})=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}}|p(A)-\delta_{y}(A)|=1-p(\{y\}).

Indeed, since
\delta_{y}(A)\in\{0,1\}
for every
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, the supremum is attained at both
A=\{y\}
and
A=\{y\}^{c}
, giving
1-p(\{y\})
. Substituting into the definition of total uncertainty,

TU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\bigl(1-p(\{y\})\bigr)=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\bigl(1-\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{y\})\bigr)=1-\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\{y\}).

Aleatoric Uncertainty.
The same identity
d_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p,\delta_{y})=1-p(\{y\})
applied to a single probability measure gives

AU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p)=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\bigl(1-p(\{y\})\bigr)=1-\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(\{y\}).

Epistemic Uncertainty.
The total variation distance between two probability measures on
\mathcal{Y}
satisfies

d_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p,q)=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}}|p(A)-q(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}|p(\{y\})-q(\{y\})|,

Substituting into (
4
),

EU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}|p(\{y\})-q(\{y\})|=\frac{1}{4}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}|p(\{y\})-q(\{y\})|.

∎

Proposition
4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{K-1}
be a credal set and let

For
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}

for every

,

Proof.

Lower endpoint.

, the map

is concave, as the pointwise maximum of affine functions is convex
[

]
. By the Bauer minimum principle

]

\mathcal{Q}

Proposition
4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
and let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\Delta_{1}
be a credal set with

a:=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{0\}),\qquad b:=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{0\}),\quad 0\leq a\leq b\leq 1.

Then, for
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\min\{1-a,\,b\},

\displaystyle\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\min\{a,\,1-b\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-a),

and in particular

\operatorname{TU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=\underline{\operatorname{AU}}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})+2\,\operatorname{EU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q}).

Proof.

The result follows by restricting Proposition
4.6
to
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
.

Total Uncertainty.
From (
4.6
),
TU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=1-\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{y\})
. Evaluating for each class:

TU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=1-\max\{\inf_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{0\}),1-\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{0\})\}=\min\{1-a,b\}.

Aleatoric Uncertainty.
In the binary case,

\max\{p(\{0\}),p(\{1\})\}=\max\{p(\{0\}),1-p(\{0\})\}
,
so from (
4.6
),

\underline{AU}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=1-\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\max\{p(\{0\}),1-p(\{0\})\}=1-\max\{b,1-a\}=\min\{a,1-b\}.

Epistemic Uncertainty.
In the binary case,
|p(\{0\})-q(\{0\})|=|p(\{1\})-q(\{1\})|
, so the
\ell_{1}
sum in TV reduces to
2|p(\{0\})-q(\{0\})|
. By (
4.6
),

EU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=\tfrac{1}{4}\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{Q}}2|p(\{0\})-q(\{0\})|=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}p(\{0\})-\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}q(\{0\})\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-a).

Additive decomposition.
Direct verification:
\min\{a,1-b\}+2\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}(b-a)=\min\{a,1-b\}+b-a=\min\{1-a,b\}
.
∎

Appendix D
Experimental Setup

The experimental setup follows that of
18
. We construct the credal set from an ensemble of 10 classifiers, and predictions are obtained by voting among the models and breaking ties with the average probabilities. In Figure
2
, each classifier generates predictions over 10 test data batches, which are assigned uncertainty values by each measure, and Accuracy Rejection (AR) curves illustrate the measures’ performance. In AR curves, predictions with the highest uncertainty are progressively rejected, and the accuracy on the remaining samples is recorded. By plotting the accuracy against the fraction of rejected examples, we visualize how well the uncertainty measure separates easy (high-confidence) from hard (low-confidence) predictions. A steeper curve indicates a more informative uncertainty measure, as rejecting uncertain examples leads to a faster increase in accuracy. Results are averaged over batches to provide a robust evaluation. The experimental setup runs with AR curves discretized into 30 equal-width bins.

Beyond visual comparison, AR performance is commonly summarized through the Area Under the Curve (AUC), which provides a compact measure of overall ranking quality. However, its expressiveness is inherently limited, as it fails to account for the shape of the curve: two AR curves may yield identical areas while exhibiting different behaviors. Since, by construction, accuracy should not decrease as increasingly certain samples are retained, we complement the AUC with a shape sensitive indicator of monotonicity. Specifically, we define the Monotonicity Ratio (MR) as the proportion of bins in which the AR curve does not decrease. Intuitively, high MR indicates that rejecting uncertain samples never harms accuracy.

Monotonicity Ratio (MR).

Let
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{B})
denote an AR curve evaluated over
B
bins.
We define the successive differences as

\Delta_{i}=s_{i+1}-s_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,B-1.

The
Monotonicity Ratio
(MR) is defined as

\text{MR}=\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{i=1}^{B-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Delta_{i}\geq 0\}},

(7)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
denotes the indicator function.

This appendix extends the empirical validation of Section

37

E.1
Credal Set Construction via Relative Likelihood

The central question behind credal set construction is to keep every model that fits the data sufficiently well, measured relative to the best available fit. Given a hypothesis
h
and a dataset
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the likelihood is defined as

L(h)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(y_{i}\mid x_{i},h).

(8)

Following
37
, the model that maximizes
L
over the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
serves as the empirical MLE and acts as the reference point of best fit. The credal set at a query
x
is then constructed by retaining all models whose likelihood remains within a prescribed ratio of this reference:

\mathcal{Q}_{x,\alpha}=\left\{p(\cdot\mid x,h):\frac{L(h)}{\sup_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}L(h^{\prime})}\geq\alpha\right\},

(9)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
controls the size of the credal set: values close to
1
yield low imprecision (only models whose likelihood is close to the MLE are retained), while values approaching
0
admit nearly all probabilistic models.

For the experiment, we consider an ensemble of nine models with likelihood thresholds uniformly spanning
\alpha\in[0,1]
, namely
\{h_{0.1},\ldots,h_{1.0}\}
, where
h_{\alpha}
denotes a model whose relative likelihood is at least
\alpha
. In this way, even when using the same transformer architecture, we force a credal predictor generated by models with different states of knowledge. To ensure sufficient variability across the likelihood spectrum, the neural networks are initialized with TOBIAS
[
37
]
.

Figure
3
shows that the proposed measures are again competitive with the baselines on both
fashion-mnist
and
svhn
. In the entropy and Hartley frameworks, the component derived from the additive decomposition tends to underperform, whereas all three components of our framework yield monotonic AR curves with slightly higher AUC values overall.

E.2
Pathological Case

We now augment the ensemble with an additional member
h_{0.0}
. Since this threshold imposes no constraint on the likelihood,
h_{0.0}
is free to be any model regardless of its fit to the data. We set
h_{0.0}
to predict a fixed class
y^{*}
for every input
x
, i.e.,
p(\cdot\mid x,h_{0.0})=\delta_{y^{*}}
. This model produces predictions entirely disconnected from the training data, corrupting the credal set and, consequently, any uncertainty quantification derived from it. As observed in Figure
4
, the proposed measures appear more sensitive to this pathological case than the baselines. We next analyze the reason behind.

When a Dirac measure
\delta_{y^{*}}
belongs to
\mathcal{Q}
, we have
\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\{y\})=0
for all
y\neq y^{*}
, since
\delta_{y^{*}}(\{y\})=0
for
y\neq y^{*}
. The closed-form expression (
4.6
) then reduces to
TU_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{Q})=1-\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\{y^{*}\})
.

The total uncertainty measure becomes anchored entirely to the class
y^{*}
, regardless of whether
y^{*}
is the correct label. Even when the majority of plausible models in
\mathcal{Q}
agree on a different class with high confidence, the single Dirac measure suffices to redirect the reference vertex from the consensus class to
y^{*}
, distorting the semantic interpretation of the measure. A similar effect propagates to the epistemic uncertainty. The maximal diameter of
\mathcal{Q}
becomes dominated by the pair
(\delta_{y^{*}},p)
for whichever
p\in\mathcal{Q}
is furthest from
\delta_{y^{*}}
.
This explains the inverted AR curves in Figure
4
: since the models with higher
\alpha
assign near zero probability to
y^{*}
for most instances,
\underline{p}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\{y^{*}\})\approx 0
and
TU,EU\approx 1
almost everywhere, as the cumulative distributions confirm. The few instances with slightly lower uncertainty (e.g.,
TU,EU\approx 0.99
) are those where high-likelihood models place some residual mass on
y^{*}
—paradoxically indicating lower model confidence, yet ranked as less uncertain.
