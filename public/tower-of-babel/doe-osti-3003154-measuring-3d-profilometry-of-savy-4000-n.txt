 Introduction
 The SAVY-4000 container series is a general-purpose interim storage container for nuclear
materials, developed and maintained by Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) [1]. It is the first
vented, general-use nuclear material container to be demonstrated as meeting the requirements outlined in
DOE M 441.1-1, the Nuclear Material Packaging Manual [2]. Due to the challenging radiation, thermal,
and corrosive storage conditions that the SAVY containers must endure, continuous surveillance
techniques are employed to ensure the containers meet all safety standards and specifications. These
inspections are typically performed by human operators, who check for issues such as corrosion, O-ring
deterioration, corrosion, filter integrity, and potential manufacturing defects. However, human inspections
alone are not sufficient, and automated inspection technologies, such as the ATIS system, as well as other
automated systems are also utilized [3].

 The MicroCam TubeInspect, developed by Novacam Technologies Inc., is designed to address
the challenges of understanding how manufacturing variations in the SAVY-4000 container series may
affect performance. It is a 3D profilometry measurement system that enables detailed analysis of surface
features, including defects, surface roughness, and manufacturing variations. This advanced tool
significantly enhances rapid surveillance techniques for both pristine and used containers. In this study,
container properties such as surface roughness, thickness, and geometric attributes like circularity are
measured for SAVY-4000 containers. Artificially corroded or dented containers are examined to
demonstrate the MicroCam's ability to quantify defects. A sensitivity analysis is also conducted,
comparing the MicroCam results to those obtained using more precise instruments such as confocal
microscopy. This comparison aims to provide valuable insights into container quality, durability, and
potential improvements in manufacturing processes.

 Methodology
 The MicroCam TubeInspect system is a high-precision optical metrology tool designed for
comprehensive inspection and analysis of cylindrical surfaces, such as tubes, pipes, and storage
containers. Using 3D laser scanning and interferometry technologies, TubeInspect captures detailed
surface profiles of the ID (inner diameter) and OD (outer diameter) measurements of hollow cylindrical
devices, for which nuclear material storage containers are ideal candidates. For reference, the nominal
design dimensions for the SAVY-4000 5 qt container, the primary focus of this analysis, are provided in
Table 1. The system’s core components include a laser scanning unit that emits a beam to scan the
surface, measuring the distance via time-of-flight or phase-shift methods, and an interferometric
measurement technology that analyzes the interference patterns for enhanced surface profiling with
micrometer resolution. A CNC control system positions the object under the laser scanning unit, ensuring
consistent, repeatable measurements. The data acquisition process begins with placing the object under
the scanning system, where the CNC system moves the container along a predefined path for full surface
coverage. The laser scanning unit captures the surface, generating a 3D point cloud that records the
container’s geometry with high precision. This point cloud is processed to create surface roughness maps,
revealing microvariations across the container's surface and identifying manufacturing inconsistencies. In
addition, thickness measurements and container geometric properties, such as circularity and eccentricity,
are calculated to assess the container’s dimensional accuracy and structural integrity. The typical
resolution of the TubeInspect system is within 2-4 microns, ensuring reliable measurements for
manufacturing quality control. The system’s high sampling rate of 100 kHz and the rotational speed of 60
RPM ensure comprehensive scanning coverage while maintaining precise alignment.

thickness, surface roughness, and geometric measurements across the container height. Post-processing
analysis is performed using MATLAB to further evaluate the data and extract meaningful insights [4].

 Dents
 Dents are a common feature among deployed nuclear material storage containers. Particularly of
interest is the fact that accelerated corrosion has been observed on dents. In addition to corrosive wall
loss, wall compression due to dents also play a role in deterioration of containers. Concurrent work on
estimating residual stress from dented container morphology is also on-going [5]. For this work,
understanding how container morphology before and after denting allows for generating high-resolution
meshes from which volume displacement, surface area change, and displacement fields can be resolved.

 To ensure that the exterior data and the interior container data align with one another, the data is
binned into groups based on their Θ𝑖𝑖 and 𝑧𝑧𝑖𝑖 values. A small enough bin size used such that analysis can be
performed at a high resolution, but large enough that every bin contains at least several data point. Over
binning and under binning the dataset results in several problems, which are discussed more in section 5.
Within a given bin, mean values are assigned to the local bin chosen to represent that bin’s data points.

 Surface Roughness
 Surface roughness mapping is a critical step in understanding the overall quality and performance
of containers. Techniques such as profilometry and laser scanning allow researchers and manufacturers to
generate detailed surface texture measurements, enabling the identification of subtle variations across
different parts of the container, including the collar, body, and lid. By comparing roughness profiles at
these key locations, areas susceptible to wear, contamination, or weak sealing can be promptly identified,
facilitating targeted improvements to the manufacturing process. See Figure 4 for a representative
container surface roughness map.

performance and user experience. A smoother sealing surface, for instance, helps achieve a tighter seal
and prevents leaks or contamination, which is particularly important in nuclear applications. Considering
this, it is not recommended that the TubeInspect be used for determining the surface roughness of the O-
Ring surface. Excessively rough surfaces, on the other hand, can contribute to material wear over time,
potentially compromising structural integrity and impacting the ease of use.

 Dimensional Tolerancing
 The TubeInspect allows various forms of dimensional inspection for cylindrical objects. After
generating a point cloud of data, a dimensional analysis of these points can be performed relative to a
computer aided design (CAD) model. This analysis is done through the PolyWorks InspectorTM software,
a widely used dimensional analysis tool. Complex geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T)
analysis can be done through this platform as well. GD&T is a symbolic language used to communicate
information about the geometric requirements and allowable variation of manufactured parts and
assemblies.

 To conduct this dimensional analysis, a CAD model, often built to nominal dimensions, is input
into Polyworks. Then a user can select the features and associated datums they would like to study on the
CAD model itself. After this, the user will upload their point cloud dataset to the workspace. At this point,
the point cloud needs to be aligned with CAD model. This can be done in many ways including an
automatic fit, selecting point pairs on each model, or by various other methods. Once aligned, the point
cloud will be superimposed over the CAD model and can be defined as the “measured data”.

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 6

 At this point, a user can perform many forms of dimensional inspection. A heat map can be generated
to show a gradient of deviations from nominal dimensions of the CAD model. Measurements such as
length and diameter can be easily made by selecting areas of the point cloud and fitting the associated
feature to it. If the user has defined datums from the CAD model, the same datums can be fit to the point
cloud. With this information, GD&T analysis can be done for the point cloud data.

 Notably, the TubeInspect is well suited for GD&T requirements for cylindrical objects. This is due to
the probe being always aligned parallel to the Z axis (vertical axis of cylinder). Examples of these GD&T
requirements are concentricity, total runout, and cylindricity, in addition to other features that can be
applied to the face of a curved surface such as a profile callout, shown in Figure 5. These results can be
easily inputted into a report within Polyworks that shows visual as well as numerical results of the
analysis.

 Figure 5. GD&T metrics of a SAVY-4000 container

 It should be noted that the TubeInspect is limited in terms of features that it can inspect. For example,
through wall holes, features hidden behind others in the radial direction (a feature behind a lip, for
example), or features near the bottom of the system or in the chuck, cannot be accessed by the probe, and
thus measurements cannot be made. In addition, features that are perpendicular to the Z axis are difficult
to get accurate measurements for as they are out of plane relative to the probe. In the case of a container
lid, the top face may act as a datum to control the location of features in the Z axis, such as a drainage
hole. The top face could not be reliably measured to establish a datum, and the drainage hole would not
be able to be measured either due to it being a through-wall hole. Thus, it can be concluded that while the
TubeInspect can excel at measurements specific to cylindrical objects, it should not be viewed as a
replacement to other dimensional inspection tools such as a coordinate measuring machine (CMM) if full
dimensional inspection is desired. The TubeInspect does offer great promise in regard to dimensional
inspection to study features such as diameter changes before and after drop scenarios for nuclear material
storage containers and other cylinder specific measurements.

 Corrosion
 Much of the measurement techniques used to capture container corrosion have already been
discussed. Instead, the focus of this section is on identifying container corrosion through numerical
methods and algorithms, and to quantify its sensitivity. This section describes the detection, analysis, and
classification of corrosion on container surfaces using a combination of imaging data and robust statistical
methods. The analysis begins by binning the measured radius data into a two-dimensional grid defined
over the angular and vertical dimensions. Once the data is organized into discrete bins, a median filter is
applied to each bin to compute a local baseline, or 𝐵𝐵(𝑖𝑖, 𝑗𝑗) for the radius measurements.

 This is essential because the median is less sensitive to outliers than the mean. It therefore
provides a stable reference value for each local region on the container's surface. The radius heat map
(Figure 6) displays the spatial distribution of the inner radius after the data is binned, revealing the
underlying geometric structure. To quantify deviations from the expected geometry, the local difference is
calculated by subtracting the median baseline from the measured radius in each bin. This difference is
then normalized by the local standard deviation, computed over the same neighborhood, to yield a z
score:

Here, 𝜎𝜎(𝑖𝑖, 𝑗𝑗) represents the local standard deviation and 𝜖𝜖 is a small constant introduced to avoid division
by zero. The resulting z-Score heatmap (see Figure 6) highlights regions where the deviation is
statistically significant. Higher absolute z-Score values indicate areas where the radius is considerably
different from the local norm, which may be symptomatic of corrosion. Using these computed z-Scores,
thresholds are applied to classify the container surface into distinct categories. The corrosion
classification map shown in Figure 6 differentiates between regions of uncorroded material, areas
exhibiting general corrosion, and zones showing pitting corrosion. Notably, in the container analyzed in
Figure 6, the entire surface was affected by corrosion due to FeCl₃ exposure; the region labeled as
“uncorroded” did not signify an absence of corrosion but rather corresponded to a dent in the container
due to the resulting morphological changes to the inner wall of the container. This refined classification is
critical for prioritizing maintenance efforts and ensuring the long-term- durability and safety of the
container.

 Environmental factors, such as humidity, temperature, and exposure to corrosive agents, along with
material characteristics like changes in protective coatings, can influence the progression of corrosion. By
using a median filter to establish a local baseline, the analysis adapts to natural variations in the
container's geometry and isolates genuine anomalies. The combination of the radius heatmap, the local
z-score heatmap, and the corrosion classification map provides a comprehensive framework for detecting
and categorizing corrosion, thereby informing maintenance strategies and efforts to mitigate further
deterioration.

 Resolution Analysis
 Including all data from high-resolution scans in plots and analyses can be computationally
demanding. One option to address this challenge is to decimate the data during measurement; however,
even keeping to every 20th measurement value still yields a sampling rate of 5 kHz, leading to 5,000
measurements per rotation at the fastest speed. While techniques such as interpolation, filtering, and
analytical functions can also reduce data volume, binning often proves the most efficient, offering
simplified data management with minimal computational overhead.

 A key consideration in binning is ensuring that each bin contains at least one data point. For a
full-body container scan, for instance, adjusting the discretization of the inner and outer surfaces allows
the surface area to be approximated using Delany triangles. As empty bins increase, these triangles
underrepresent the surface, causing the estimated area to deviate from its true value, as shown in Figure 8.
The relationship between the ideal container surface area, considering the 𝑟𝑟 and 𝑧𝑧 bounds scanned, and
the Delany triangle approximation can be visualized as a function of the discretization grid size. Beyond
600 discretization’s, the number of empty bins rises dramatically, attributed in part to the 664 rotations
performed during the spiral scan, which breaks the number of 𝑧𝑧 increments in 664 parts. It should be

noted that due the high radial sampling rate, the radial measurements can be sampled with much more
than 600 bins.

 The final analysis is to determine what effect rotation velocity has on resolving the minimum depth of
a single corrosion pit. Several scans of varying velocity’s were performed, and plotting the depth against
rotational speed (RPM) revealed a trend: faster scans often registered shallower pits and smoother surface
features (Figure 9), implying that higher measurement speed can blur fine details. Because this effect is
partly stochastic, the loss of resolution is not uniform, but the data still highlights the need to control both
rotational and lateral speeds when precise pit characterization matters.

 One way to increase the resolution is by using a smaller laser spot probe. A smaller laser spot
probes a smaller spot size, so each measurement reflects a more local height instead of an average over
neighboring peaks and valleys. Because lateral resolution is limited to roughly half the spot diameter,
shrinking the spot from 50 µm to 10 µm reduces the minimum resolvable feature size from roughly 25
µm to roughly 5 µm. As it currently stands, the TubeInspect is unlikely to detect pitting corrosion in
features less than 10 µm

 Conclusion
 The Novacam TubeInspect offers promising results for being incorporated into the container
surveillance programs at LANL. While it does possess limitations regarding minimum corrosion pit
detection depths, low surface roughness resolution, and an inability to measure container corners, it excels
at resolving detailed profilometry measurements of the inner and outer wall of SAVY-4000 containers.
Although smaller spot size interferometer probes are available, the associated increase in measurement
time can significantly limit their practicality. Ultimately, the Novacam TubeInspect is most effective
when applied for targeted, high-resolution profilometry on select containers of interest, rather than
serving as a universal tool for comprehensive dimensional tolerancing.
