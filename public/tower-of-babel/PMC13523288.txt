Efficacy of belimumab in children with systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Ying Yang, Tianji Gao, Shaonan Xu, Bei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of belimumab combined with standard therapy in children with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in a single-center retrospective observational cohort.MethodsA total of 60 children with active SLE were screened, and 40 eligible patients were included. Patients were classified into a belimumab plus standard therapy group (n = 20) and a standard therapy alone group (n = 20). Belimumab was administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg every 2 weeks for the first three doses and every 4 weeks thereafter. Clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, SLEDAI-2000 scores, glucocorticoid doses, and adverse events were compared between groups. The 6-month assessment was considered the primary observation time point, while 12-month outcomes were exploratory.ResultsAt 6 months, renal and mucocutaneous remission rates were 83% (15/18) and 94% (15/16) in the belimumab group, compared with 78% (7/9) and 85% (11/13) in the control group. At 12 months, the corresponding rates were 94% (15/16) and 94% (15/16) in the belimumab group, and 89% (8/9) and 85% (11/13) in the control group. C3, C4, and SLEDAI-2000 scores improved significantly in both groups. Most clinical and serological outcomes did not differ significantly between groups. Oral glucocorticoid doses decreased more markedly in the belimumab group than in the control group (53.55 ± 8.43 to 6.72 ± 3.50 mg/day vs. 55.50 ± 6.05 to 14.81 ± 5.54 mg/day; P < 0.05). One patient experienced reversible reductions in IgG levels and B-cell counts, with no severe infections or infusion reactions.ConclusionsBelimumab plus standard therapy showed a potential glucocorticoid-sparing effect in pediatric SLE, but larger prospective studies are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety.Keywords: Belimumab, Glucocorticoids, Immunosuppressants, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Clinical efficacy

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune-mediated, diffuse connective tissue disorder characterized by multisystem involvement, heterogeneous clinical manifestations, and a relapsing-remitting course [1]. In pediatric populations, SLE typically presents with a subacute onset and exhibits distinct clinical features compared with adult disease [2–4]. Approximately 70% of affected children develop cutaneous involvement, with malar rash observed in about half of cases, although a subset of patients may remain free of skin lesions throughout the disease course. In addition to dermatological manifestations, major organ systems are frequently affected, including the kidneys, hematological system, and central nervous system [5–8]. In China, the prevalence of SLE is estimated at 30–70 per 100,000 individuals [9], with childhood SLE (cSLE) accounting for 10%–20% of all cases [10]. Compared with adult cases, cSLE is generally more aggressive, with higher rates of disability and mortality, thereby imposing substantial burdens on both families and healthcare systems [11–14]. According to the 2019 recommendations issued by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), standard-of-care therapy consists of glucocorticoids (GCs), hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), and immunosuppressive agents (IAs). However, prolonged use of these regimens, beyond well-recognized adverse effects, may also result in growth retardation and gonadal function suppression in children [15].

Belimumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that modulates immune activity and mitigates inflammation and tissue injury [1]. Evidence from international studies indicates that belimumab can effectively alleviate clinical symptoms, reduce disease activity, and facilitate GC sparing without causing significant side effects [16, 17]. This antibody was approved in China in 2019 for use as an adjunct to standard therapy in patients with SLE [18], and became available in our institution thereafter; in the present cohort, all patients who received belimumab were treated after approval and institutional availability. Nevertheless, its application in cSLE remains limited. Evidence for its efficacy and safety in this population is still lacking. Against this backdrop, the present study aims to evaluate the safety and clinical utility of belimumab by analyzing its therapeutic efficacy in cSLE in a single-center retrospective observational cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective observational cohort study included pediatric patients diagnosed with SLE who were treated in both outpatient and inpatient settings at the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology of Baoding Children’s Hospital between January 2018 and December 2024. Patients were consecutively screened through the hospital electronic medical record system. This study was not designed as a randomized controlled trial at the beginning of the study period; rather, it was designed retrospectively after clinical experience with belimumab had accumulated. Patients were classified according to actual treatment exposure into a belimumab group, receiving belimumab plus standard therapy, and a standard-therapy control group, receiving standard therapy alone. Treatment allocation reflected real-world clinical decision-making rather than randomization. Clinical characteristics and laboratory parameters were collected and analyzed retrospectively, and treatment outcomes were compared between the two groups at 6 and 12 months post-treatment. The primary endpoint was assessed at 6 months because all included patients completed the 6-month assessment. Outcomes at 12 months were considered secondary and exploratory because 4 patients in the belimumab group were lost to follow-up. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Baoding Hospital, Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. 2024-40). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. No a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective exploratory study. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated at our center during the study period who fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria and had available follow-up data.

Treatment regimens

The control group received standard-of-care therapy in accordance with the Expert Consultation on Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment of Childhood Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (2022 Version) [16]. Although the study period started before publication of the 2022 Chinese expert consensus, patients received standard-of-care therapy according to contemporaneous clinical practice. The 2022 expert consensus was used retrospectively as a reference framework to describe the components of standard therapy. Treatment was tailored to each patient, primarily consisting of GCs, HCQ, and IAs. Specifically, GCs were tapered once clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters remained stable for 2–4 weeks. During the early phase of treatment, the dose was typically reduced by 5–10 mg per adjustment, followed by more gradual reductions of 2.5–5 mg. Upon achieving low disease activity, a low maintenance dose was continued long term. The same GC tapering principles were applied to both groups. GC dose reduction was based on improvement in clinical manifestations and stability or improvement of laboratory parameters during follow-up, rather than treatment group assignment alone. IAs included cyclophosphamide (Cyp), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and cyclosporine (CYSP). HCQ was administered as part of the baseline regimen.

The belimumab group received belimumab in addition to the above standard treatment. Belimumab was administered via intravenous infusion at a dose of 10 mg/kg. The induction phase consisted of three doses given at 2-week intervals, followed by maintenance dosing every 4 weeks, with a total treatment duration ranging from 6 months to 1 year. Belimumab was initiated after the diagnosis of SLE had been established and after standard therapy, including GCs and IAs, had been administered according to disease activity. Belimumab was considered in patients with persistently active disease, serological activity, active organ involvement, or difficulty reducing GCs. The decision to initiate belimumab was made by the treating physicians after discussion with the patients’ guardians. Belimumab was approved for pediatric use in April 2019 and became available in our institution thereafter. No patient in the belimumab group received belimumab before approval or institutional availability.

Data collection

Clinical and laboratory data were collected for both the belimumab and control groups, including demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex), treatment details, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings (complement component 3 [C3], complement component 4 [C4], protein in urine, and anti-double-stranded DNA [dsDNA] titers), and adverse events. Disease activity was evaluated using the SLEDAI-2000 with a focus on multisystem involvement. Data were primarily extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The assessed outcomes included clinical manifestations, C3, C4, proteinuria conversion, anti-dsDNA antibody conversion, SLEDAI-2000 scores, oral GC dose reduction, and adverse events. Organ-specific remission rates were calculated only among patients with the corresponding organ involvement at baseline. Therefore, renal remission was evaluated among patients with baseline renal involvement, and mucocutaneous remission was evaluated among patients with baseline mucocutaneous involvement.

Disease flares or relapses were not included as formal outcomes because the retrospective records did not consistently document all variables required for standardized flare classification, such as visit-to-visit SLEDAI changes, reasons for treatment escalation, and patient-level flare adjudication. Analyses at 12 months were performed using available cases and were considered secondary and exploratory. Because detailed baseline variables for patients with and without 12-month follow-up data could not be completely retrieved, a formal comparison between patients with complete and incomplete 12-month follow-up was not performed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples t-test, and within-group paired comparisons using the paired t-test. For non-normally distributed data, results were presented as median (interquartile range) (M [Q1–Q3]). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while paired comparisons were examined by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or the Friedman test for repeated measures across multiple time points. Categorical variables were denoted as frequencies and percentages [n(%)] and were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test. Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were less than 5. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical data

Baseline characteristics

A total of 60 pediatric patients with cSLE were screened between January 2018 and December 2024. Twenty patients were excluded according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria: 8 patients did not complete the prescribed belimumab course, 4 had poor adherence to standard therapy, 5 had incomplete follow-up data, and 3 had severe complications. Finally, 40 pediatric patients with SLE were included in this study, including 20 patients in the belimumab group and 20 patients in the control group (see Fig. 1 for the STROBE-style participant flow diagram).

Flowchart of participant screening in this study

The belimumab group comprised 20 patients, including 9 males and 11 females, with a mean age of 10.5 ± 2.67 years. All patients (100%) in this group completed the 6-month assessment, and 16 (80%) completed the 12-month follow-up. The remaining 4 patients in the belimumab group were lost to follow-up at 12 months. Therefore, the 12-month analyses in the belimumab group were based on available cases and should be interpreted as secondary and exploratory. The control group also included 20 patients, consisting of 8 males and 12 females, with a mean age of 10.6 ± 2.25 years. All patients in the control group completed the 12-month follow-up. No statistically significant differences in age and sex composition were observed between the two groups (both P > 0.05).

Baseline treatment regimens

The belimumab group received a combination of GCs, IAs, HCQ, and belimumab, whereas the control group was treated with GCs, IAs, and HCQ. The choice of immunosuppressive therapy varied according to the pattern of organ involvement. In the belimumab group, 9 patients (45%) received Cyp and 11 (55%) were administered MMF. Neither methotrexate (MTX) nor CYSP was used. In the control group, 6 patients (30%) were treated with Cyp, 8 (40%) with MMF, 2 (10%) with MTX, and 4 (20%) with CYSP. The same GC tapering principles were applied to both groups, and dose reduction was determined according to clinical manifestations and follow-up laboratory parameters rather than treatment group assignment alone.

Clinical manifestations

At baseline, all patients in both groups exhibited multisystem involvement. In the belimumab group, involvement of the following organs or systems was observed: the kidneys (n = 18; 90%), the nervous system (n = 6; 30%), skin and mucous membranes (n = 16; 80%), and the hematological system (n = 14; 70%). In addition, fever was detected in 14 patients (70%). In the control group, renal involvement was observed in 9 patients (45%), neuropsychiatric involvement in 5 (25%), mucocutaneous involvement in 13 (65%), hematological involvement in 15 (75%), and fever in 13 (65%). The proportion of renal involvement at baseline was numerically higher in the belimumab group than in the control group, reflecting the real-world nature of treatment allocation.

The denominators for organ-specific remission differed from the total number of patients because renal remission was evaluated only among patients with renal involvement at baseline, and mucocutaneous remission was evaluated only among patients with mucocutaneous involvement at baseline. Therefore, renal outcomes were calculated among 18 patients in the belimumab group and 9 patients in the control group, whereas mucocutaneous outcomes were calculated among 16 and 13 patients, respectively.

At 6 months post-treatment, the frequency of organ/system involvement decreased markedly in the belimumab group, with renal damage reduced to 3 cases (17%) and mucocutaneous manifestations to 1 case (6%), whereas the control group showed persistent mucocutaneous involvement in 2 cases (15%). At 12 months, only 1 patient (6%) in the belimumab group continued to exhibit mucocutaneous symptoms. By contrast, in the control group, renal involvement persisted in 1 patient (11%) and mucocutaneous damage in 2 patients (15%). These findings indicate clinical improvement in both groups.

Laboratory parameters

C3

In the belimumab group, C3 levels rose from 0.534 ± 0.290 g/L at enrollment to 0.807 ± 0.161 g/L at 6 months post-treatment and further to 0.861 ± 0.133 g/L at 12 months. The 12-month C3 analysis in the belimumab group was based on the 16 patients with available follow-up data. Likewise, the control group exhibited an increase in C3 levels from 0.474 ± 0.224 g/L at baseline to 0.813 ± 0.160 g/L at 6 months and 0.864 ± 0.133 g/L at 12 months. The within-group increases at both 6 and 12 months were statistically significant (P < 0.05, respectively). No statistically significant between-group difference in C3 levels was observed at the assessed time points. See Fig. 2.

Comparison of C3 levels before and after treatment in the two groups

C4

A similar pattern was observed for C4. In the belimumab group, C4 levels increased from 0.093 ± 0.066 g/L at baseline to 0.179 ± 0.069 g/L at 6 months and 0.200 ± 0.068 g/L at 12 months. The 12-month C4 analysis in the belimumab group was based on the 16 patients with available follow-up data. In the control group, C4 levels were elevated from 0.080 ± 0.050 g/L to 0.163 ± 0.060 g/L at 6 months and 0.181 ± 0.071 g/L at 12 months. Both groups demonstrated statistically significant increases at 6 and 12 months compared with baseline (all P < 0.05). No statistically significant between-group difference in C4 levels was observed at the assessed time points. See Fig. 3.

Comparison of C4 levels before and after treatment in the two groups

Protein in urine

In the belimumab group, the rate of negative conversion of proteinuria increased from 0% at baseline to 83.3% at 6 months and further to 93.8% at 12 months. Proteinuria negative conversion was calculated among patients with renal involvement at baseline and available follow-up data. In the control group, the corresponding rates rose from 0% at baseline to 100% at 6 months, followed by a slight decline to 88.9% at 12 months. This decrease in the control group was attributable to recurrent proteinuria in one patient. Review of the medical record showed good treatment adherence and relatively stable GC and immunosuppressive tapering in this patient; therefore, the recurrence was not considered to be clearly related to poor adherence, abrupt GC reduction, or major immunosuppressive adjustment. Although the belimumab group showed a continued increase at 12 months, whereas the control group exhibited a modest decrease after 6 months, the difference in these changes lacked statistical significance (P > 0.05). See Fig. 4.

Comparison of proteinuria negative conversion rates in cSLE cases with renal damage between the two groups

Anti-dsDNA antibody titers

In the belimumab group, the rate of negative conversion of anti-dsDNA antibodies rose from 15.0% at baseline to 80.0% at 6 months and further to 87.5% at 12 months. The 12-month anti-dsDNA antibody analysis in the belimumab group was based on the 16 patients with available follow-up data. In the control group, the conversion rate increased from 20.0% at baseline to 75.0% at 6 months but declined slightly to 70.0% at 12 months. Despite the continued upward trend during the 12-month follow-up in the belimumab group and the modest decrease after 6 months in the control group, the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). See Fig. 5.

Comparison of anti-dsDNA antibody conversion rates at baseline and after treatment between the two groups

SLEDAI-2000 scores

In the belimumab group, SLEDAI-2000 scores declined from 13 ± 4 at baseline to 2 ± 2 at 6 months and further to 1 ± 1 at 12 months. The 12-month SLEDAI-2000 analysis in the belimumab group was based on the 16 patients with available follow-up data. In the control group, SLEDAI-2000 scores decreased from 12 ± 6 at baseline to 2 ± 3 at 6 months and 1 ± 2 at 12 months. The reductions in SLEDAI-2000 scores observed at both 6 and 12 months were statistically significant compared with baseline (P < 0.05, respectively). No statistically significant between-group difference in SLEDAI-2000 scores was observed at the assessed time points. See Fig. 6.

Comparison of SLEDAI-2000 scores before and after treatment between the two groups

Oral GC dosage

Compared with baseline, oral GC doses were substantially reduced in both groups at 6 and 12 months. GC tapering followed the same clinical principles in both groups and was based on improvement in clinical manifestations and stability or improvement of laboratory parameters during follow-up. In the belimumab group, the mean dose decreased from 53.55 ± 8.43 mg to 6.72 ± 3.50 mg at 12 months, whereas in the control group, it declined from 55.50 ± 6.05 mg to 14.81 ± 5.54 mg at 12 months. The degree of reduction in GC dosage was significantly greater in the belimumab group than in the control group (P < 0.05). This finding suggests a potential GC-sparing effect of belimumab; however, because GC tapering was individualized rather than protocol-mandated, residual physician discretion and confounding cannot be fully excluded. See Fig. 7.

Comparison of oral GC dosage at baseline and after treatment between the two groups

Adverse events

In the belimumab group (n = 20), all patients completed the 6-month follow-up, and 16 completed the 12-month follow-up. Severe systemic infection, hypersensitivity reactions, and infusion-related adverse events were absent during these periods. However, one patient (5%) experienced recurrent reductions in immunoglobulin G (IgG < 6.0 g/L) and B-cell counts (< 177/µL) following the use of belimumab, accompanied by an increased frequency of non-severe infections. To address these symptoms, intermittent intravenous infusion of human immunoglobulins was administered. Consequently, IgG levels and B-cell counts were elevated, and the infection risk was mitigated. In the control group (n = 20), all patients completed follow-up, and no severe systemic infections, hypersensitivity reactions, or infusion-related adverse events were reported. No deaths occurred in either group during the observation period.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective observational cohort study, both belimumab plus standard therapy and standard therapy alone were associated with improvements in disease activity, complement levels, and organ manifestations in children with active SLE. The main between-group signal observed in this cohort was a greater reduction in GC dosage in the belimumab group. However, renal remission, mucocutaneous remission, complement improvement, proteinuria conversion, anti-dsDNA antibody conversion, and SLEDAI-2000 scores did not show statistically significant between-group differences. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory real-world evidence rather than definitive evidence of superiority of belimumab over standard therapy alone.

Juvenile-onset SLE is generally associated with a more aggressive disease course, greater organ involvement, and a higher need for individualized treatment strategies than adult-onset SLE [19]. Belimumab represents the first biological agent approved for SLE treatment in nearly six decades. It received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2011 for adult patients and was subsequently extended to children aged ≥ 5 years in 2019 based on emerging clinical evidence [20]. The updated EULAR recommendations support the use of biologic agents, including belimumab, in patients with persistently active or relapsing SLE, particularly when disease control remains inadequate or GC tapering is difficult despite standard therapy [21]. Mechanistically, belimumab modulates immune responses and mitigates inflammation and tissue injury by inhibiting B-cell activating factor [1]. Despite these advances, evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of belimumab in pediatric populations remains relatively limited. Conventional therapy for pediatric SLE continues to rely on GCs in combination with antimalarial agents and immunosuppressants [15]. However, prolonged GC exposure is associated with significant adverse effects, including growth retardation and metabolic disturbances. This highlights the importance of minimizing steroid burden, ideally reducing prednisone to ≤ 5 mg/day, as a key therapeutic goal [22].

Our findings suggest that adding belimumab to standard therapy may be associated with clinical and serological improvement in children with active SLE, but the magnitude of benefit should be interpreted carefully. In this study, renal and mucocutaneous remission rates were numerically high in both groups at 6 and 12 months. In the belimumab group, renal remission was observed in 83.3% of patients at 6 months and 93.8% at 12 months, while mucocutaneous remission was observed in 93.8% at both time points. However, these organ-specific analyses were based on small denominators, and the between-group differences were not statistically significant. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that belimumab produced superior organ-specific remission compared with standard therapy alone.

Laboratory parameters also improved in both groups. C3 and C4 levels increased significantly from baseline in both the belimumab and control groups, while proteinuria and anti-dsDNA antibody conversion showed favorable trends over time. In the control group, the proteinuria negative conversion rate decreased from 100% at 6 months to 88.9% at 12 months. This change was driven by one patient with recurrent proteinuria. Review of the medical record showed good treatment adherence and relatively stable GC and immunosuppressive tapering in this patient; therefore, the recurrence was not considered to be clearly related to poor adherence, abrupt GC reduction, or major immunosuppressive adjustment. Given the small denominator of control patients with renal involvement at baseline, this change should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect individual disease fluctuation rather than a robust group-level difference.

The most consistent between-group difference in the present study was the greater reduction in oral GC dosage in the belimumab group. The mean GC dose decreased from 53.55 ± 8.43 mg/day at baseline to 6.72 ± 3.50 mg/day at 12 months in the belimumab group, compared with a reduction from 55.50 ± 6.05 mg/day to 14.81 ± 5.54 mg/day in the control group. This result suggests a potential GC-sparing effect of belimumab in children with active SLE. However, although the same GC tapering principles were applied to both groups, tapering was not protocol-mandated. In clinical practice, GC dose reduction was determined according to clinical manifestations and follow-up laboratory parameters rather than treatment group assignment alone. Therefore, residual physician discretion and confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded.

These observations are broadly consistent with previous studies. In the PLUTO trial, a randomized, placebo-controlled study of intravenous belimumab in pediatric SLE, belimumab showed a numerically favorable treatment response and an acceptable safety profile when added to standard therapy [23]. Real-world pediatric studies have also reported improvement in disease activity, complement levels, and GC reduction after belimumab treatment, although most of these studies were limited by retrospective design, small sample size, and heterogeneity in background therapy [17, 24, 25]. The GC-sparing effect of belimumab has also been corroborated in adult populations. Data from international multicenter cohorts indicate that, after 6 months of combination therapy, the mean daily prednisone dose in adult patients is reduced by approximately 6–12 mg [26]. Furthermore, a phase III trial conducted in East Asia (n = 677) demonstrated that, among patients with a baseline prednisone dose > 7.5 mg/day, belimumab significantly facilitated GC tapering [27]. Across pediatric and adult studies, belimumab has shown generally consistent efficacy and safety trends, although the strength of evidence remains greater in adult SLE than in cSLE [28, 29].

Only one patient in the belimumab group experienced recurrent reductions in IgG and B-cell counts, accompanied by an increased frequency of non-severe infections. The patient improved after intermittent intravenous immunoglobulin supplementation, and no severe systemic infection, hypersensitivity reaction, infusion-related adverse event, or death occurred during follow-up. These findings suggest that belimumab was generally well tolerated in this cohort. Nevertheless, the sample size was small, and rare adverse events could not be reliably assessed. Longer follow-up and larger multicenter studies are required to further clarify the safety profile of belimumab in children with SLE.

Disease flares or relapses were not analyzed as formal outcomes in the present study. Although previous adult and pediatric studies have suggested that belimumab may reduce the risk of disease flare [30, 31], the retrospective records in our cohort did not consistently document all variables required for standardized flare classification, such as visit-to-visit SLEDAI changes, reasons for treatment escalation, and patient-level flare adjudication. To avoid misclassification and unreliable post hoc interpretation, flare outcomes were not included in the present analysis. This issue has been acknowledged as an important limitation.

The current study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective observational cohort study with a modest sample size, and no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated at our center during the study period. Therefore, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Second, treatment allocation was not randomized and may have been influenced by disease phenotype, physician judgment, drug availability, and family preference, leading to potential confounding by indication. Third, baseline organ involvement was not fully balanced between groups, especially renal involvement, which was more frequent in the belimumab group. Fourth, 4 patients in the belimumab group were lost to follow-up at 12 months; therefore, 12-month analyses were based on available cases and were considered secondary and exploratory. Because detailed baseline variables for patients with and without 12-month follow-up could not be completely retrieved, we were unable to perform a formal comparison between patients with complete and incomplete 12-month data, which may have introduced attrition bias. Fifth, disease flares or relapses could not be reliably assessed because the retrospective records did not consistently document all variables required for standardized flare classification. Sixth, cumulative GC exposure and the proportions of patients reaching specific prednisone thresholds, such as ≤ 10 mg/day, ≤ 7.5 mg/day, or ≤ 5 mg/day, could not be reliably extracted. Finally, GC tapering was individualized rather than protocol-mandated. Further basic and clinical research is warranted to more comprehensively define the efficacy and safety profile of belimumab in patients with cSLE.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective observational cohort study, belimumab plus standard therapy was associated with improvement in disease activity and laboratory parameters in children with active SLE and showed a potential GC-sparing effect. However, most clinical and serological outcomes were not significantly different from standard therapy alone in this small cohort, and 12-month analyses were limited by incomplete follow-up in the belimumab group. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Larger prospective controlled studies with standardized follow-up, predefined GC tapering protocols, and systematic flare assessment are needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of belimumab in children with SLE.
