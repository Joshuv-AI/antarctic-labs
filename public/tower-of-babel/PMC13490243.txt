Zinc supplementation ameliorates colitis in mice by sensitizing pathogenic CD4+ T cells to ferroptosis
Authors: Zongkuo Li, Jianting Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is driven by the hyperactivation of pathogenic Th1 and Th17 subsets. Although clinical zinc deficiency strongly correlates with severe IBD outcomes, the precise immunomodulatory mechanisms underlying this association remain unknown. To systematically investigate the role of zinc in CD4+ T cell fate determination and intestinal homeostasis, we evaluated its immunomodulatory dynamics using a murine colitis model alongside in vitro differentiation assays employing human primary naive CD4+ T cells. We found that zinc supplementation remarkably ameliorated experimental mucosal damage by abruptly halting the local infiltration of pathogenic CD4+ T cells. Both in vivo and in vitro analyses revealed that zinc does not merely stall T cell polarization, but actively sensitizes these effector subsets to cell demise. Comprehensive transcriptomic sequencing pinpointed the ferroptosis cascade as the critical mediator of this clearance mechanism. Specifically, zinc systematically suppressed T cell anti-ferroptotic defenses by downregulating GPX4 and SLC7A11, while concurrently amplifying pro-ferroptotic drivers such as ACSL4. This targeted metabolic stress profoundly sensitized hyperactive T cells to ferroptosis, effectively purging the inflammatory mucosal niche. Ultimately, these findings reframe the fundamental role of zinc in intestinal immune homeostasis, exposing ferroptosis as a druggable vulnerability in IBD and providing a strong mechanistic rationale for zinc-based clinical interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02192-6.

Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by segmental intestinal inflammation and irreversible bowel damage [1, 2]. While therapeutic regimens incorporating immunosuppressants and targeted biologics achieve clinical responses, a substantial proportion of patients fail to maintain sustained remission [3, 4]. This high rate of therapeutic failure necessitates exploring alternative strategies, such as modulating essential trace elements. Zinc regulates intestinal mucosal immunity and metabolic homeostasis. Clinically, zinc deficiency is highly prevalent in IBD patients, correlating directly with increased risks of hospitalizations, surgeries, and disease-related complications [5, 6].

Notably, IBD frequently presents with the pathological expansion of visceral adipose tissue, an active immunological niche heavily infiltrated by pro-inflammatory CD4+ T cells [7]. Upon activation, these T cells (predominantly Th1 and Th17 subsets) secrete potent cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-17 A, driving a vicious cycle of local inflammation and mucosal barrier disruption [7, 8].

The clonal expansion of these pathogenic subsets imposes stringent metabolic demands, rendering them susceptible to lipid reactive oxygen species accumulation. Failing to clear these lipid peroxides triggers ferroptosis, an iron-dependent regulated cell death [9, 10]. The glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) antioxidant axis prevents such lipid peroxidation and subsequent cell death [11, 12]. However, whether zinc modulates this ferroptotic network to dictate CD4+ T cell fate during IBD pathogenesis remains unknown.

Methods

Patient enrollment

Peripheral blood samples (approximately 15–20 mL) were collected from 12 newly diagnosed patients with IBD. The available demographic and clinical characteristics of the IBD cohort, including the distribution of Crohn’s Disease and Ulcerative Colitis, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Diagnosis of IBD was rigorously confirmed based on standard clinical, endoscopic, and histological criteria. To eliminate confounding treatment effects, patients receiving prior immunomodulators or biologic therapies were excluded from the study. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion, and the study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval No.2021 − 0333).

Human CD4+ T cell isolation

For in vitro assays, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were initially isolated from the freshly drawn whole blood using standard Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. Subsequently, human naive CD4+ T cells were purified from the PBMC fraction utilizing a magnetic bead-based negative selection approach (Miltenyi Biotec) ensuring the isolated precursors remained “untouched” and functionally viable. The purity of the isolated naive CD4+ T cells was routinely verified to be ≥ 95% via flow cytometry.

Following isolation, these naive T cell precursors were seeded in complete culture medium and activated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies to mimic T cell receptor (TCR) stimulation. The cells were then cultured in vitro for 5 days under standard Th1 or Th17 polarizing conditions. To evaluate the specific immunomodulatory and metabolic effects of zinc, these polarization cultures were concurrently supplemented with either a vehicle control or a pre-determined concentration of zinc (5µM), and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Chronic DSS-induced colitis model and zinc intervention

Eight-week-old male mice underwent three cycles of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, MP Biomedicals) administration to induce chronic colitis. Each cycle comprised 7 days of 2% DSS via drinking water followed by a 7-day recovery period. Concurrent with DSS cycling, mice received daily oral gavages of either 20 mM ZnCl₂ (200 µL per mouse) or an equivalent volume of 20 mM NaCl. Disease progression was monitored daily through body weight tracking and Disease Activity Index (DAI) scoring. All animal experiments and care procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2024 − 126). Furthermore, all in vivo studies were strictly conducted and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the data. Male mice were exclusively utilized in this study to eliminate potential confounding effects of estrous cycle-driven hormonal fluctuations on mucosal immune responses and colitis severity.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Following euthanasia, the entire colons were excised, and their lengths were measured to assess macroscopic disease severity. Tissues were then fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. For histopathological evaluation, 4–5 μm sections were stained with standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate mucosal architecture and inflammatory infiltration.

To map the mucosal accumulation of target immune populations, parallel sections were subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Following standard antigen retrieval and blocking procedures, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD4, F4/80, MPO, and IL-17 A (Abcam). Immunoreactivity was visualized using a standard HRP-DAB detection system, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

For quantitative IHC analysis, five randomly selected high-power fields (400×magnification) were evaluated per colonic section. The percentage of positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software. All histological scoring and quantification were performed in a double-blinded manner by two independent pathologists to ensure objectivity.

Tissue dissociation and flow cytometric analysis

Single-cell suspensions were generated from excised spleens and colonic lamina propria tissues utilizing standard mechanical dissociation and enzymatic digestion (Collagenase and DNase 4). To ensure data accuracy, cells were first stained with a fixable viability dye to exclude dead cells. Subsequently, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS containing 1% BSA), blocked with anti-CD16/32 (Fc-specific) antibodies, and stained for defining surface markers. For the evaluation of pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 subsets, cells were then fixed, permeabilized, and stained for key intracellular cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-17 A). Data acquisition was performed on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer, with subsequent quantitative analysis executed via FlowJo software.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mechanically homogenized colonic tissues and in vitro cultured CD4 + T cells utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) or an RNA-Quick Purification Kit (esunbio), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were subsequently verified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Equivalent amounts of high-quality total RNA (1 µg) were then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) utilizing a standard first-strand cDNA synthesis kit. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green detection chemistry on a standard thermocycler system to evaluate the mRNA expression levels of signature cytokines and T cell transcription factors. The complete sequences of all forward and reverse primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. Relative gene expression was calculated utilizing the standard 2−ΔΔCt method, with all target genes normalized to the endogenous expression of a designated housekeeping gene (β-actin).

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from colonic tissues and cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined utilizing a standard BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein lysates were denatured in loading buffer, resolved via SDS-PAGE, and electrophoretically transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h prior to overnight primary antibody incubation at 4 °C. The primary antibodies targeted core ferroptotic regulators (GPX4, GCH1, SLC7A11, ACSL4, TFRC; all from Huabio) and β-ACTIN (Proteintech) as an internal loading control. Following standard washing steps and incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, target protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

RNA sequencing and pathway enrichment

RNA integrity from isolated CD4+ T cells was verified via Bioanalyzer (Thermo Fisher). Novogene Bioinformatics Technology executed library construction and Illumina NovaSeq paired-end sequencing. Differential expression analysis utilized the DESeq2 R package, with significant genes defined by an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05. Differentially expressed genes subsequently underwent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to evaluate ferroptosis pathway modulation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical representations were generated utilizing GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Quantitative data are strictly presented as the mean ± standard error (SD). Comparisons involving three or more experimental groups were subjected to a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Result

Zinc supplementation ameliorates clinical Symptoms and mucosal damage in DSS-induced murine colitis

To evaluate the in vivo immunomodulatory efficacy of zinc, we established a well-characterized DSS-induced murine colitis model (Fig. 1A). As expected, DSS administration in vehicle-treated control mice drove severe, progressive weight loss and dramatically elevated disease activity index (DAI) scores, reflecting overt rectal bleeding, diarrhea, and systemic morbidity. In contrast, zinc supplementation effectively ameliorated these symptoms (Fig. 1D, E). Consistent with these symptomatic improvements, macroscopic assessments revealed that zinc administration significantly prevented DSS-induced colon shortening, a hallmark indicator of severe intestinal inflammation (Fig. 1B, C). Furthermore, histological evaluation of colonic tissues corroborated these protective effects. While vehicle-treated colitis mice exhibited extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, crypt destruction, and severe epithelial ulceration, zinc-supplemented mice maintained relatively intact mucosal architecture, yielding markedly lower histopathological scores (Fig. 1A).

Zinc supplementation restricts mucosal inflammatory infiltration

Given these structural improvements, we next examined the composition of local inflammatory infiltrates. Immunohistochemical mapping and subsequent quantification revealed extensive mucosal and submucosal accumulation of CD4+ T cells, F4/80+ macrophages, and MPO+ neutrophils, alongside robust IL-17 A protein expression, in the colons of DSS-challenged mice (Fig. 2A, B). Strikingly, zinc administration systematically restricted the recruitment of these innate and adaptive immune populations, driving a particularly profound reduction in pathogenic CD4+ T cell density and IL-17 A accumulation within the lamina propria.

Reflecting this robust cellular clearance, we assessed the local inflammatory milieu at the transcriptional level. Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated that the colonic mRNA expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (including Ifng, Tnfa, Il17a, Il6, and Il1β) was substantially suppressed in zinc-treated mice (Fig. 2C). Notably, the expression of the Th2-associated cytokine Il4 remained unaltered, underscoring that zinc specifically dismantles the Th1/Th17 inflammatory axis rather than inducing a state of global immunosuppression.

Zinc curtails systemic and local pro-inflammatory CD4+ T cell polarization

To define the functional phenotypes underlying this restricted infiltration, we evaluated CD4+ T cell populations across systemic lymphoid tissues and the local intestinal microenvironment. Flow cytometric profiling of splenic lymphocytes demonstrated that DSS induction drove a systemic expansion of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD4+ T cells (Fig. 3A, B). Zinc supplementation effectively blunted this systemic activation, while the inherently sparse population of splenic IL-17 A-producing cells remained unaffected across all groups (Fig. 3C). The observed discrepancy, where zinc primarily reduced IL-17 A+ cells in the colon rather than the spleen, likely stems from the fact that the inflamed colonic microenvironment provides the localized cytokine milieu (e.g., elevated IL-6) necessary to drive robust Th17 expansion, which is less pronounced in the systemic reservoir of the spleen. Locally, within the colonic lamina propria, DSS provoked a dense accumulation of pathogenic CD4+ T cell subsets, sharply elevating the frequencies of IFN-γ+, TNF-α+, and IL-17 A+ populations. Aligning with our earlier transcriptional data, zinc administration strictly restrained this local polarization, mitigating the expansion of all three pro-inflammatory CD4+ T cell populations in the inflamed colon (Fig. 3D-F).

Zinc restricts the in vitro pro-inflammatory differentiation of human naive CD4+ T cells

To rigorously translate our in vivo murine findings to human clinical pathology, we isolated primary naive CD4+ T cells from the peripheral blood of IBD patients and matched healthy controls (HC). Following in vitro activation under lineage-specific Th1 and Th17 polarizing conditions, we observed that zinc supplementation profoundly restrained these precursors from differentiating into pathogenic phenotypes. Intracellular flow cytometric analysis across both the HC and IBD cohorts consistently demonstrated significantly diminished frequencies of IFN-γ+, TNF-α+, and IL-17 A+ effector CD4+ T cells relative to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4A-D). Transcriptional profiling further corroborated this phenotypic shift; quantitative RT-PCR confirmed that zinc exposure markedly downregulated the mRNA expression of signature pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNG, TNF, and IL17A) alongside their respective lineage-specifying master transcription factors (TBX21 and RORC) (Fig. 4E). Together, these data indicate that zinc directly blunts the pro-inflammatory programming of human CD4+ T cells at the transcriptional level.

Zinc dampens CD4+ T cell inflammatory responses by driving the ferroptosis signaling pathway

Seeking the molecular drivers of this phenotypic suppression, we subjected these CD4+ T cells to RNA sequencing. Differential gene expression analysis captured distinct transcriptional shifts separating the zinc-treated and control groups (Fig. 5A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis mapped these differentially expressed genes predominantly to the ferroptosis signaling cascade, a finding also confirmed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 5B, C). Reflecting these global transcriptomic signatures, hierarchical clustering isolated a definitive segregation of ferroptosis-related expression profiles. Crucially, zinc administration systematically downregulated the transcription of core anti-ferroptotic factors (GPX4 and SLC7A11) while concurrently upregulating pro-ferroptotic drivers (ACSL4 and TFRC) (Fig. 5D). Western blot analysis validated this pro-ferroptotic shift at the translational level; zinc treatment actively depleted GPX4, GCH1, and SLC7A11 protein reserves, coupling this clearance with a distinct accumulation of ACSL4 and TFRC in the activated CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5E). Subsequent densitometric quantification confirmed significant alterations in the expression of these ferroptotic proteins relative to β-ACTIN (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

The refractory nature of IBD alongside highly variable patient responses to conventional immunosuppressants necessitates exploring multidimensional therapeutic strategies [13]. Emerging evidence increasingly points toward dietary micronutrients regulating mucosal immunity. Addressing this intersection, our study defines a distinct immunometabolic role for zinc during intestinal inflammation. Zinc supplementation effectively ameliorates DSS-induced murine colitis by systematically restricting the infiltration and polarization of pathogenic IBD CD4+ T cells. Mechanistically, our data reveal that zinc actively drives these hyperactive T cells into ferroptosis, establishing a clear metabolic-immune axis in IBD pathogenesis.

Clinical observations consistently link trace element deficiencies to gastrointestinal inflammatory disorders. Zinc deficiency is highly prevalent among IBD patients, correlating directly with exacerbated disease severity, disrupted lipid metabolism, and pathological visceral fat accumulation [5, 14]. Translating these epidemiological associations into functional models, our in vivo data demonstrate that zinc administration actively limits mucosal erosion, crypt destruction, and clinical weight loss during active colitis. These protective phenotypes confirm that local zinc homeostasis dictates intestinal epithelial integrity and restrains localized hyperinflammation. Based on FDA guidelines, the 20 mM (approx. 13 mg/kg) zinc dose used in our mice translates to a Human Equivalent Dose of ~ 1.05 mg/kg, or ~ 63 mg/day for a 60 kg adult. This dosage falls within the safe clinical range (50–150 mg/day) typically used for therapeutic supplementation without inducing systemic toxicity [15].

IBD pathogenesis is primarily orchestrated by an aberrant adaptive immune response where the hyperactivation of specific CD4+ T cell subsets (predominantly Th1 and Th17 cells) fuels a destructive cytokine cascade [16, 17]. While biologics neutralizing IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-17 A improve clinical outcomes, frequent non-responsiveness highlights the need to target upstream modulators of this pathogenic network. Cytometric profiling of systemic lymphoid tissues and the colonic lamina propria confirms that zinc intervention strictly limits the expansion of IFN-γ+ and IL-17 A+ CD4+ T cells. Assays utilizing human primary naive CD4+ T cells corroborated these murine observations; zinc directly restricts the differentiation of naive precursors into effector subsets, coupling this suppression with the transcriptional downregulation of lineage-specifying factors (TBX21 and RORC). This cross-species consistency demonstrates the fundamental capacity of zinc to dismantle the pro-inflammatory T cell axis.

At the molecular level, zinc executes this immunomodulation by exploiting CD4+ T cell ferroptosis. As an iron-dependent regulated cell death driven by excessive lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis acts as a critical checkpoint for immune cell fate. The massive metabolic demands of T cell activation render hyperactive subsets highly susceptible to lipid oxidative stress [18, 19]. Our transcriptomic profiling pinpointed the ferroptosis cascade as the primary target of zinc intervention. Rather than protecting these cells, zinc systematically dismantles their antioxidant defenses by downregulating core anti-ferroptotic factors (GPX4 and SLC7A11) while concurrently amplifying pro-ferroptotic drivers (ACSL4 and TFRC). By forcing the lethal accumulation of lipid reactive oxygen species during clonal expansion, zinc triggers the targeted elimination of these pathogenic T cells. This deliberate metabolic sabotage effectively purges the mucosal niche of the secondary messengers required for Th1 and Th17 commitment. Mechanistically, our findings align with the canonical ferroptosis pathway where the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis serves as the primary defense against lipid peroxidation, while ACSL4 acts as a key driver by enriching membranes with polyunsaturated fatty acids [20]. By simultaneously dismantling this defense and activating pro-ferroptotic drivers, zinc forces pathogenic T cells into an unresolvable state of oxidative stress [21].

Acknowledging these findings, specific aspects of this signaling axis require further resolution. While we verified the efficacy of zinc in sensitizing pathogenic CD4 + T cells to ferroptosis, the precise intracellular zinc sensors and specific transporter families (ZIP/ZnT) mediating this metabolic shift remain undefined. Future investigations utilizing targeted genetic knockout models will clarify whether this ferroptotic modulation relies on direct transcriptional interference or broader epigenetic reprogramming. From a clinical perspective, recent studies have reinforced the role of zinc in promoting mucosal healing. However, practical issues such as the choice of formulation (e.g., zinc carnosine for better adherence) and the risk of copper deficiency during prolonged high-dose therapy must be considered [22].

A limitation of the current in vivo study is the exclusive use of male mice, which was intended to limit confounding hormonal fluctuations during disease induction. However, biological sex is a critical variable in IBD pathogenesis. Existing literature indicates that estrogen exerts potent immunomodulatory effects and can inherently upregulate antioxidant defense systems, potentially increasing baseline resistance to ferroptosis. Consequently, the threshold for zinc-induced ferroptotic sensitization may differ in female models. Future studies employing female mice and ovariectomized models are necessary to definitively map the interplay between zinc homeostasis, sex hormones, and T cell ferroptosis.

Ultimately, this study defines zinc as an active immunometabolic regulator capable of alleviating intestinal inflammation. By dismantling the Th1 and Th17 pro-inflammatory axis through targeted ferroptosis sensitization, zinc acts as a potent and translationally viable dietary intervention. These findings establish a rigorous mechanistic foundation for integrating precision zinc modulation into the clinical management of IBD.

Our study elucidates a novel immunometabolic paradigm wherein zinc supplementation effectively abrogates intestinal inflammation. Rather than merely suppressing immune activation, zinc orchestrates the targeted ferroptotic demise of hyperactive Th1 and Th17 subsets. This targeted clearance is driven by a systematic recalibration of the cellular redox defense network—specifically, the dampening of the GPX4/SLC7A11 anti-ferroptotic axis and the concurrent amplification of pro-ferroptotic drivers such as ACSL4. Ultimately, these mechanistic insights fundamentally reframe zinc from a passive nutritional trace element to a critical gatekeeper of CD4+ T cell fate, providing a compelling theoretical and therapeutic rationale for integrating precision zinc-targeted interventions in the clinical management of IBD.
