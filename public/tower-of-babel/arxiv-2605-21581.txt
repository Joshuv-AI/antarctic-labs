Abstract

We revisit the computation of four-point wavefunction coefficients and correlators for external conformally coupled scalars exchanging a particle of generic mass and spin. Much of the phenomenology of cosmological collider physics in the near-de Sitter limit follows from these functions. Computing them in detail is a central challenge in the cosmological bootstrap. Using the cosmological Grassmannian, we write these objects in closed form using hypergeometric functions and Legendre polynomials. We achieve this by writing the standard bootstrap differential equation using the Plücker coordinates of the Grassmannian, and using the basis of Mandelstam invariants. The exchange in the
s
-channel can be written in terms of a hypergeometric function of the
S
Mandelstam, while the spin information appears as an overall Legendre polynomial factor that also depends on the other Mandelstams. We fix the boundary conditions by first demanding the absence of unphysical singularities, and, for correlators, by further matching to a kinematic limit in momentum space. Our formulae in Grassmannian space are much simpler than their counterparts in momentum space, demonstrating another useful application of the Grassmannian as a kinematic space for cosmology.

Introduction

A central challenge that triggered the cosmological bootstrap was the computation of four-point correlators in de Sitter space, particularly those due to the exchange of massive spinning particles. This was a technical hurdle for standard perturbative techniques, but also an important phenomenological benchmark for cosmological collider physics. The objective is to show that both the derivation and the final form of these correlators simplify drastically, when expressed in terms of different kinematic variables. This novel take on the cosmological collider serves two purposes. Moreover, it broadens the applicability of this new formalism, showing that it can describe correlators with massive internal particles, rather than being restricted to theories with only massless fields.

Setup and Review

In this section, we will give a quick overview of the setup and review the necessary background. We will focus on the de Sitter case, but the extension to Anti-de Sitter is straightforward.

2.1
Particles in de Sitter Space

The flat slicing of four-dimensional de Sitter is described by the following line element

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\frac{L^{2}}{\eta^{2}}(-\mathrm{d}\eta^{2}+\mathrm{d}x^{2})\,,

(2.1)

where
L
is the de Sitter radius,
-\infty<\eta<0
is conformal time, and
x^{\mu},\>\mu=1,2,3,
are the spatial coordinates. We want to describe equal-time correlation functions of fields on the three-dimensional boundary at
\eta\approx 0
. A scalar particle, in particular, is described by a field
\Phi(\eta,x^{\mu})
, which behaves close to the boundary as

\Phi(\eta,x^{\mu})\xrightarrow{\ \eta\to 0\ }\bar{\phi}(x^{\mu})\,\eta^{\bar{\Delta}}+\phi(x^{\mu})\,\eta^{\Delta}\,,

(2.2)

where
\bar{\Delta}
is the scaling dimension of the field and
\Delta\equiv 3-\bar{\Delta}
. The scaling dimension is related to the mass
M
of the particle via
M^{2}L^{2}=\bar{\Delta}(3-\bar{\Delta})
, which implies

\bar{\Delta}=\frac{3}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{9}{4}-M^{2}L^{2}}\,,

(2.3)

i.e.
\bar{\Delta}
is always taken to be the smallest root (when real). By this choice, the “growing” mode on the boundary is always the field
\bar{\phi}(x^{\mu})
for
M^{2}L^{2}<9/4
.

We can construct a wavefunctional
\Psi[\bar{\phi}]
, which describes the amplitude of the boundary profile in the vacuum state. It admits the following expansion

\Psi[\bar{\phi}]\approx\exp\left(-\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}^{3}x_{n}\>\psi_{n}(x_{1}^{\mu},\dots,x_{n}^{\mu})\,\bar{\phi}(x_{1}^{\mu})\cdots\bar{\phi}(x_{n}^{\mu})\right),

(2.4)

where
\psi_{n}
are the “wavefunction coefficients.” The isometry group of dS
4

\mathrm{SO}(4,1)
acts on them as conformal transformations, so they can be interpreted as correlation functions of dual primary operators
\mathcal{O}(x^{\mu})
in a three-dimensional conformal field theory with scaling dimension
\Delta
:

\psi_{n}(x_{1}^{\mu},\dots,x_{n}^{\mu})\equiv\langle\mathcal{O}(x^{\mu}_{1})\cdots\mathcal{O}(x^{\mu}_{n})\rangle\,.

(2.5)

For bulk massless particles of spin
\ell
, the dual operators are currents
J^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{\ell}}
with scaling dimension
\Delta=\ell+1
that obey the conservation equation
\partial_{\mu_{1}}J^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{\ell}}=0

\langle\Phi(0,x_{1}^{\mu})\cdots\Phi(0,x_{n}^{\mu})\rangle
, which also transform like correlators of primary operators with scaling dimension
\bar{\Delta}
in a three-dimensional conformal field theory.

Both the wavefunction coefficients and the correlators admit a perturbative expansion in terms of Feynman diagrams that depend on the details of the bulk theory. We will focus on the leading contribution to the

Cosmological Grassmannian

In momentum space, correlators depend on the spatial momenta
k^{\mu}_{i}
and polarization vectors
\epsilon^{\mu}_{i}

\lambda_{i},\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\epsilon_{\beta\gamma}

\lambda_{i}\mapsto\rho\lambda_{i},\>\bar{\lambda}_{i}\mapsto\rho^{-1}\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\Lambda\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{1}&\lambda_{1}^{2}\\

\displaystyle E_{L}

A Lesson from Flat Space

As a warm-up, we consider

3.2
Casimir from (Dual) Twistors

We first find the quadratic Casimir in (dual) twistor space, restricting ourselves to external conserved currents or conformally coupled scalars. One can think of (dual) twistors as the four-component (anti-)spinorial representations
Z^{A}\hskip 1.0pt(W_{A})
of the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathrm{SO}(3,2)
, whose index can be lowered and raised via the symplectic form

\Omega_{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1_{2\times 2}\\
-1_{2\times 2}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\Omega^{AB}=-\Omega_{AB}\,.

(3.5)

For example,
W^{A}\equiv\Omega^{AB}W_{B}
and
W_{A}\equiv\Omega_{AB}W^{B}
, with contractions defined as

\displaystyle W_{1}\cdot W_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv W_{1,A}\Omega^{AB}W_{2,B}\,,

(3.6)

\displaystyle W_{1}\cdot\partial_{W_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv W_{1,A}\partial_{W_{2,A}}\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{W_{1}}\cdot\partial_{W_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv-\partial_{W_{1,A}}\Omega_{AB}\partial_{W_{2,B}}\,.

In the following, we will focus our attention on dual twistors for simplicity. A dual twistor can be assigned to each conserved current, and this choice furnishes another remarkable kinematic space for wavefunction coefficients of conserved currents
[
11
,
15
]
. This is conceptually similar to the use of spinor helicity variables in flat space, where the spinors of the Lorentz group
\mathrm{SO}(3,1)
can be used to describe scattering amplitudes of massless particles.

A generator of the isometry group
\mathrm{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})
acts on dual twistors via a simple “rotation”

F(W_{A})\to F({M_{A}}^{B}W_{B}),\quad M\in\mathrm{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})\,.

(3.7)

An infinitesimal transformation
{M_{A}}^{B}={\delta_{A}}^{B}+{X_{A}}^{B}
, with
X^{AB}=X^{BA}
, therefore yields

\displaystyle F(W_{A}+{X_{A}}^{B}W_{B})

\displaystyle\simeq F(W_{A})+{X_{C}}^{B}W_{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{C}}F(W_{A})

\displaystyle\simeq F(W_{A})-X^{DB}W_{B}\Omega_{DC}\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{C}}F(W_{A})

\displaystyle\simeq F(W_{A})+\frac{1}{2}X^{BD}J_{BD}[F(W_{A})]\,,

(3.8)

from which we can read the following ten differential operators:

J_{AB}=J_{BA}=-W_{A}\Omega_{BC}\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{C}}-W_{B}\Omega_{AC}\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{C}}\equiv-W_{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{B}}-W_{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{A}}\,.

(3.9)

It is straightforward to determine their commutation relations:

[J_{AB},J_{CD}]=\Omega_{CB}J_{AD}+\Omega_{CA}J_{BD}+\Omega_{DB}J_{AC}+\Omega_{DA}J_{BC}\,.

(3.10)

These commutators are consistent with the fact that the operators
J_{AB}
realize a representation of the algebra of
sp(4,\mathbb{R})\simeq so(3,2)
on functions of dual twistors (i.e., for our purposes, correlators). These generators can then be used to compute the quadratic Casimir:

\mathcal{C}_{12}=\frac{1}{4}(J_{1,AB}+J_{2,AB})(J_{1}^{AB}+J_{2}^{AB})\,.

(3.11)

A careful computation
6
6

6

In our conventions,
\partial_{W_{A}}W_{B}=\delta^{A}_{B}
, but
\partial_{W^{B}}W^{A}=-\delta^{A}_{B}
.
shows that the Casimir takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{12}=

\displaystyle-2(\ell_{1}^{2}+\ell_{2}^{2}-2)+(W_{1}\cdot W_{2})(\partial_{W_{1}}\cdot\partial_{W_{2}})+h_{1}+h_{2}-2

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(W_{1}\cdot\partial_{W_{2}})(W_{2}\cdot\partial_{W_{1}})-\frac{1}{2}(W_{2}\cdot\partial_{W_{1}})(W_{1}\cdot\partial_{W_{2}})\,,

(3.12)

where
\ell_{i}
are the spins of the external particles, and
h_{i}
are their helicities. We have used that
F(\rho W_{i})=\rho^{2h_{i}-2}F(W_{i})
, which is demanded by little group-covariance of the correlators in twistor space.

We have based our presentation on the dual twistors
W_{a,A}
. In terms of the twistors
Z^{A}
, the generators simply turn out to be

J_{AB}=Z_{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{B}}+Z_{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{A}}\,,

(3.13)

for which the Casimir can be determined in a similar fashion.

3.3
Acting in the Grassmannian

The orthogonal Grassmannian arises from (dual) twistor space via a Schwinger parametrization of the
\mathrm{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})
-invariant correlators, which must be constructed from inner products of (dual) twistors

F(W_{i}\cdot W_{j})=\int\mathrm{d}^{6}c_{ij}\>A_{4}(c_{ij})\,\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}c_{ij}W_{i}\cdot W_{j}\right).

(3.14)

The action of the Casimir in the Grassmannian can then be found through differentiation and integration by parts, starting from (
3.12

2.11

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{12}[A_{4}]=

\displaystyle-5\partial_{c_{12}}(c_{12}A_{4})-c_{14}c_{23}\partial_{c_{24}}\partial_{c_{13}}A_{4}-c_{13}c_{24}\partial_{c_{14}}\partial_{c_{23}}A_{4}

(3.15)

\displaystyle-\partial_{c_{13}}\partial_{c_{23}}(c_{13}c_{23}A_{4})-\partial_{c_{14}}\partial_{c_{24}}(c_{14}c_{24}A_{4})+\sum_{(ij)\neq(34)}\partial_{c_{12}}\partial_{c_{ij}}(c_{12}c_{ij}A_{4})

\displaystyle+(c_{13}c_{24}-c_{23}c_{14})\partial_{c_{12}}\partial_{c_{34}}A_{4}-2(\ell_{1}^{2}+\ell_{2}^{2}-2)A_{4}\,.

Although this expression is not particularly insightful, we will soon see how it greatly simplifies for physical correlators of the form (
2.25

As a first application, we check how the Casimir acts on the contact
\Phi^{4}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{12}\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(S+1)}{\mathcal{E}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{12}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4(1+S+4S^{2})}{\mathcal{E}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{12}^{3}\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{8(1+S-4S^{2}+36S^{3})}{\mathcal{E}}\,,\quad\dots

The most interesting feature of the Casimir operator is its action on single-channel contributions to the correlators. Indeed, the general structure of the Casimir equation (

\Big[-\mathcal{C}_{12}+(\Delta(3-\Delta)-J(J+1))\Big]\,A_{4,s}^{\Delta,J}(C)=A^{J}_{4,\rm c}(C)\,.

(3.17)

Such a structure parallels the flat-space result

\Big[-s+M^{2}\Big]\,\mathcal{A}_{s}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm c}\,,

(3.18)

where

is the Casimir of the exchanged particle,

is the exchange diagram in the
s

is a contact amplitude. In the Grassmannian, we naturally describe

Recall that, for external scalars, the most general

\displaystyle F(S)=\>\frac{1}{(\Delta-1)(\bar{\Delta}-1)}\Bigg(

4.1
EFT Expansion

As a first example, we want to specialize the general answer to the exchange of a
principal series scalar

\Delta=\frac{3}{2}+i\lambda,\>\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. In the EFT limit,

4

Regardless of the values of

c_{2}

{}_{3}F_{2}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}1,1,1\\[2.0pt]
\Delta,\bar{\Delta}\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S\Bigg]=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(n!)^{2}}{(\Delta)_{n}(\bar{\Delta})_{n}}(2S)^{n}\,,

(4.11)

(a)_{n}\equiv a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)=\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}\,.

(4.12)

This result features a

5
Exchanging Spinning Particles

In this section, we show how to describe conformally coupled scalars exchanging particles with spin-
J

A_{4,s}^{\Delta,J}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}F(S)P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\,,

5.1
Differential Equation

We must now require that the action of the Casimir collapses the exchange to a different, spin-dependent contact interaction. The scattering amplitudes of massless scalars exchanging a massive spin-
J

\Big[-s+M^{2}\Big]\,\mathcal{A}_{\rm s}=s^{J}P_{J}\bigg(\frac{u-t}{s}\bigg)\,.

(5.2)

For example, one can easily verify that, in the case of a spin-
1

The natural uplift to a differential equation in the Grassmannian then is:

\Big[-\mathcal{C}_{12}+(\Delta(3-\Delta)-J(J+1))\Big]\Bigg[\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}F(S)P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\Bigg]=\frac{\Gamma(J+1)^{2}}{\mathcal{E}}(2S)^{J}P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\,.

(5.3)

The right-hand side of the above differential equation can be fixed using the known form of
A_{4,s}^{\Delta,J}
for
\Delta=J+1
, as it does not depend on the mass of the exchanged particle. In particular, we have inferred it from the exchange of photons and gravitons in
[
4
]
. We have also confirmed that the correlator it generates in momentum space by encircling the pole in
\tau=0
matches the expected result, i.e. the output of the spin-raising operators in
[
2

7
]
on the scalar seed

The action of

\mathcal{C}_{12}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}F(S)P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\Bigg]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\,\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{J}\Big[F(S)\Big]\,,

(5.4)

where we have defined the differential operator

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{J}\Big[F(S)\Big]=\Big[S^{2}(2S-1)F^{\prime\prime}(S)+2S(3S-1)F^{\prime}(S)+(2(S+1)-J(J+1))F(S)\Big]\,.

(5.5)

We have simplified the result via the recursion relation satisfied by Legendre polynomials

(J+2)P_{J+2}(z)=(2J+3)zP_{J+1}(z)-(J+1)P_{J}(z)\,.

(5.6)

Remarkably, the Legendre polynomial factors out of the equation and cancels with the right-hand side of (

3.15

5.2
Full Solution

We are left with the following equation to solve:

S^{2}(2S-1)F^{\prime\prime}(S)+2S(3S-1)F^{\prime}(S)+(2(S+1)-\Delta(3-\Delta))F(S)=-\Gamma(J+1)^{2}(2S)^{J}\,.

(5.7)

Setting

and solve for

. Since only the source depends on

G(S)=\frac{1}{(\Delta+J-1)(\bar{\Delta}+J-1)}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}1,J+1,J+1\\[2.0pt]
\Delta+J,\bar{\Delta}+J\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S\Bigg]\,.

(5.8)

Putting everything together, the correlator of four conformally coupled scalars exchanging a massive spin-
J

A_{4,s}^{\Delta,J}\supset\frac{\Gamma(J+1)^{2}}{(\Delta+J-1)(\bar{\Delta}+J-1)}\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}1,J+1,J+1\\[2.0pt]
\Delta+J,\bar{\Delta}+J\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S\Bigg](2S)^{J}P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\,.

(5.9)

Note that these functions have a regular

The most general solution to (

\displaystyle\boxed{\begin{aligned} A_{4,s}^{\Delta,J}&=\frac{\Gamma(J+1)^{2}}{(\Delta+J-1)(\bar{\Delta}+J-1)}\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}}\,(2S)^{J}P_{J}\bigg(\frac{U-T}{S}\bigg)\Bigg({}_{3}F_{2}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}1,J+1,J+1\\[2.0pt]

Again, imposing the absence of a branch cut for

\displaystyle c_{1}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+J)\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+J)\Gamma(\Delta-1)^{2}}{2\Gamma(J+1)^{2}\Gamma(2\Delta-2)}\Big[1+\tilde{f}(\Delta)\Big]\,,

\displaystyle c_{2}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+J)\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+J)\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}-1)^{2}}{2\Gamma(J+1)^{2}\Gamma(2\bar{\Delta}-2)}\Big[1-\tilde{f}(\Delta)\Big]\,.

The AdS

The above results can be converted to momentum space via the integral (

As a first example, we study the Grassmannian formulae (

\displaystyle\mathrm{Disc}_{0<\tau<\bar{\tau}_{s}}[\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1-2S(\tau))]

we obtain for conformally coupled scalars

which matches Equation (4.10) in

\psi_{4,s}^{\Delta=3}=-\frac{1}{E}\int_{0}^{\bar{\tau}_{s}}\mathrm{d}\tau\>\frac{\log(1+2|S(\tau)|)}{2\tau|S(\tau)|}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{|S(\tau)|}\bigg)-\frac{1}{\tau|S(\tau)|}\,,

6.2
Generic Massive Scalars

As a second example, we consider the Grassmannian formula (

of a generic massive scalar and show that it matches the known momentum-space answer for the given choice of coefficients

6.3
Generic Spinning Particles

As a final example, we study the Grassmannian formula (

6.4
Full Solution from Homogeneous Seeds

Throughout the paper, we have treated
A_{4}
as a single object with a given analytic structure. However, it is also worthwhile to consider the integral of (
4.15
) (and similarly for spinning exchanges) over the
\tau
plane by separating the contributions from the homogeneous and inhomogeneous terms.

•

The homogeneous term has a branch cut for both
S(\tau)>1/2
and
S(\tau)<0
, which translates to a branch cut for
\tau_{*}<\tau<\bar{\tau}_{s}
above the real line.

•

The inhomogeneous term has a branch cut for
S(\tau)>1/2
. After assigning an
i\epsilon
prescription to the Mandelstams, this function has a branch cut for
\tau_{*}<\tau<0
above the real line, where
\tau_{*}
is one of the two negative solutions of
S(\tau_{*})=1/2
.

The general contour of integration in Figure
2
can be deformed to compute the discontinuity of each term along the respective branch cut above the real line, up to semi-arc contributions around the branch points (which are vanishing for internal scalars).
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{4,\rm h}

\displaystyle\propto-\int_{\tau_{*}}^{\bar{\tau}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{2\pi i}\,\frac{c_{1}}{\tau E}\,\mathrm{Disc}\Bigg[(2S(\tau))^{\Delta-2}{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta-1,\Delta-1\\[2.0pt]
2\Delta-2\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S(\tau)\Bigg]\Bigg]+\cdots\,,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\psi_{4,\rm inh}

\displaystyle\propto-\int_{\tau_{*}}^{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{2\pi i}\,\frac{1}{\tau E}\,\mathrm{Disc}_{\tau}\Bigg[{}_{3}F_{2}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}1,1,1\\[2.0pt]
\Delta,\bar{\Delta}\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S(\tau)\Bigg]\Bigg]\,.

Since we have tuned the free coefficients to exactly cancel the discontinuity associated with
S>1/2
, their sum recovers

\psi_{4,\rm tot}\propto-\int_{0}^{\bar{\tau}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{2\pi i}\,\frac{c_{1}}{\tau E}\,\mathrm{Disc}\Bigg[(2S(\tau))^{\Delta-2}{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta-1,\Delta-1\\[2.0pt]
2\Delta-2\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S(\tau)\Bigg]\Bigg]+\cdots\,.

(6.11)

In this range, the discontinuity only affects the power law, so that the inhomogeneous solution is irrelevant. However, this is a representation of the
full
solution, which can equivalently be interpreted as a
homogeneous
solution of the Casimir equation, integrated along the open contour in the
\tau
plane shown in Figure
3
. In practice, the net effect of the inhomogeneous term, in the conversion to momentum space, is simply to reduce the length of the branch cut, therefore modifying the effective integration contour.

Figure 3
:
Effective integration contour for (
6.11
), when interpreted in terms of the homogeneous solutions only. Their analytic structure differs from that of the full
A_{4,s}
, so the only way to mimic the full result is to have a contour with two endpoints. We have omitted the branch cut below the real line.

We expect the following mechanism to explain how the homogeneous solution to the Casimir equation can generate the full solution in momentum space. Given the action of the Casimir on
A_{4}(C)
, it can be made to act on
\Lambda
(appearing as
\delta(C\cdot\Lambda)
in (
2.9
)) by integrating by parts. These steps generate boundary terms if the integral is performed over a region of the orthogonal Grassmannian with boundaries, so a contact interaction emerges as the mismatch. Schematically, we get

\int\mathrm{d}C\>\delta(C\cdot\Lambda)\,\mathcal{C}_{12}[A_{4}(C)]=\Box_{12}[\psi_{4}(\Lambda)]+(\textrm{boundary term})\,.

(6.12)

This implies that, for a function
A_{4}(C)
that is annihilated by the operator
[\mathcal{C}_{12}-\Delta(3-\Delta)]

\Big[-\Box_{12}+\Delta(3-\Delta)\Big]\psi_{4}(\Lambda)=(\textrm{boundary term})=\frac{1}{E}\,.

(6.13)

Note that the boundary of the region of integration is located at
\tau=0
, i.e. the locus in the Grassmannian where
\mathcal{E}
vanishes and where contact interactions are entirely localized.

7
Conclusions

The cosmological Grassmannian provides a kinematic arena in which de Sitter isometries are manifest and many properties of cosmological correlators resemble those of scattering amplitudes. In this paper, we studied the case of generic massive exchange, where the correlator in momentum space is a rather complicated function of momentum ratios. We managed to find a much simpler function in the Grassmannian, involving hypergeometric (for the massive exchange piece) and Legendre functions (encoding spin information). The result showcases the power of finding a convenient set of kinematic variables for cosmology. Other interesting examples were discussed in
[
17
,
24
,
6
,
5
]
. Promising directions for future work include:

•

It would be interesting to understand how weight-shifting operators act in the Grassmannian, and use them to establish the connection between exchanges of particles with integer-spaced scaling dimensions.

•

We have solved the case of four external particles and a single exchange. In principle, the Grassmannian can be used to represent correlators of any number of external particles. In those cases, where several massive exchanges take place, multiple Casimir equations might be necessary to find the solution. Formulating the problem in this space could yield interesting results and potentially make contact with the rules of the
kinematic flow

[
1
,
9
,
8
,
10
]
.

•

Finally, it would be intriguing to determine expressions for loop diagrams in the Grassmannian, as they will likely be much simpler than their counterparts in momentum space.

With the cleaner description of the simplest tree-level exchange in the Grassmannian at hand, more complicated dynamical questions in cosmology are now within reach. As we better understand the cosmological Grassmannian, the bootstrap rules needed to answer these questions should become clearer. This opens a promising direction for the study of cosmological correlators.

guarantees the entire removal of the branch cut is the following. The discontinuity function—or, more specifically, its analytic continuation away from the
S>1/2
half-line—is always a homogeneous solution of the same Casimir equation as the exchange diagram (
5.7
). By imposing that the discontinuity vanishes at
S=1/2
, we simultaneously enforce the solution that does not have a local logarithmic behavior, and impose its overall coefficient to vanish, therefore removing the branch cut entirely. For example, (
A.1
) is indeed the homogeneous solution of the Casimir equation without a logarithmic behavior at
S=1/2
, which is itself non-vanishing at that point.

Appendix B
Computation of the Scalar Exchange

In this appendix, we study the integral (
6.7
), first in the
k_{s}\to 0
limit, then in full generality.

B.1
Collapsed Limit

Before we begin, we recall the following discontinuity:

\mathrm{Disc}_{x<0}[x^{\alpha}]\equiv(-|x|+i\epsilon)^{\alpha}-(-|x|-i\epsilon)^{\alpha}=2i\sin(\pi\alpha)|x|^{\alpha}\,.

(B.1)

By defining
x\equiv\langle\bar{1}\bar{2}\rangle\langle\bar{3}\bar{4}\rangle\,\tau/E
, equation (
6.7
) reduces (for
\Delta=3/2+i\lambda
) to

\psi_{4,s}^{\Delta}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\bar{x}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{xE}\,\frac{c_{1}}{\lambda^{2}+1/4}{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda,\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
1+2i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\,2S(x)\Bigg]\,\mathrm{Disc}_{x}[(2S(x))^{-1/2+i\lambda}]+\cdots\,,

(B.2)

where we do not report the analogous
c_{2}
term and the contribution from the semi-arcs. In terms of
u,v
defined in (
6.6
), we have

2S(x)=-\frac{(x-\bar{x}_{s})(x-x_{s})}{x},\quad\bar{x}_{s}=\frac{uv(1-u)(1-v)}{(u+v)^{2}},\quad x_{s}=\frac{uv(1+u)(1+v)}{(u+v)^{2}}\,.

(B.3)

We recall the surprising feature that, for this range of
\tau
, the
{}_{3}F_{2}
has no discontinuity and does not contribute to the integral, although we are describing the
full
solution in momentum space. We observe that the discontinuity does not affect the hypergeometric functions, since
2S(x)
ranges from
-\infty
to
0
. In particular, using (
B.1
) with respect to the variable
S
yields

\mathrm{Disc}_{x}[(2S(x))^{-1/2+i\lambda}]=-2i\cosh(\pi\lambda)(2|S(x)|)^{-1/2+i\lambda}\,,

(B.4)

where the sign is determined by
S^{\prime}(x)>0
in the relevant region of integration.

We first argue that the contribution from the semi-arcs vanishes. Near
x=\bar{x}_{s}
, the integrand behaves as
(x-\bar{x}_{s})^{-1/2\pm i\lambda}
, i.e. its integral along the semi-arc tends to
0
. Similarly, near
x=0
, one can use hypergeometric identities to show that the integrand behaves logarithmically. From now on, we will focus on the
c_{1}
term only for clarity:

\psi_{4,s}^{\Delta}\supset\frac{c_{1}\cosh(\pi\lambda)}{\pi E(\lambda^{2}+1/4)}\int_{0}^{\bar{x}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}\,(2|S(x)|)^{-1/2+i\lambda}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda,\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
1+2i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\,-2|S(x)|\Bigg]\,.

(B.5)

Owing to the monotonicity of
S(x)
in
x\in(0,\bar{x}_{s})
, it is convenient to integrate with respect to
2|S(x)|\equiv p(x)
instead, which returns

\frac{c_{1}\cosh(\pi\lambda)}{\pi E(\lambda^{2}+1/4)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\sqrt{(p+x_{s}+\bar{x}_{s})^{2}-4x_{s}\bar{x}_{s}}}\,p^{-1/2+i\lambda}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda,\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
1+2i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\,-p\Bigg]\,.

(B.6)

We now want to study the
k_{s}\to 0
limit of the above integral. To this end, we first observe that

x_{s}-\bar{x}_{s}=\frac{2uv}{u+v}\equiv\epsilon\ll 1\,,

(B.7)

which allows us to rewrite the square root in (
B.6
) as

\sqrt{(p+x_{s}+\bar{x}_{s})^{2}-4x_{s}\bar{x}_{s}}=\sqrt{(p+\epsilon)^{2}+4\bar{x}_{s}p}\,.

(B.8)

The
dominant non-analytic term
in
\epsilon
can be easily extracted via the
method of regions
.

Method of Regions
: We briefly outline the “method of regions” for evaluating small parameter expansions of integrals. Consider the following function of
\epsilon
:

I(\epsilon)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,F(\epsilon;x)\,.

(B.9)

We want to study the
\epsilon\to 0
expansion of
I(\epsilon)
. First, there exists a characteristic scale
\bar{x}(\epsilon)
that naturally splits the domain of integration into a “hard” region
x\gg\bar{x}(\epsilon)
and a “soft” region
x\ll\bar{x}(\epsilon)
. We then split the integral accordingly:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,F(\epsilon;x)=\int_{0}^{\bar{x}(\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}x\,F(\epsilon;x)+\int_{\bar{x}(\epsilon)}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,F(\epsilon;x)\,.

(B.10)

In the hard region, one performs a series expansion of the integrand
F(\epsilon;x)
and extends the domain of integration back to
(0,+\infty)
. Similarly, in the soft region, one first changes the variable of integration to
x=\bar{x}(\epsilon)\,y
, and then performs the same operations:

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{x}(\epsilon)}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,F(\epsilon;x)

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,\partial_{\epsilon}^{n}\big[F(\epsilon;x)\big]\big|_{\epsilon=0}\,\epsilon^{n}\equiv I_{\rm hard}(\epsilon)\,,

(B.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{x}(\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}x\,F(\epsilon;x)

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}y\>\bar{x}(\epsilon)\,\partial_{\epsilon}^{n}\big[F(\epsilon;\bar{x}(\epsilon)\,y)\big]\big|_{\epsilon=0}\,\epsilon^{n}\equiv I_{\rm soft}(\epsilon)\,.

Unsurprisingly, the hard region has IR divergences and the soft region has UV divergences, but the sum of the two terms above is the finite expansion of
I(\epsilon)=I_{\rm soft}(\epsilon)+I_{\rm hard}(\epsilon)
.

Throughout this example, we have assumed that
I(\epsilon)
is analytic at
\epsilon=0
, which is not the case for the computation at hand. However, the strategy we have described is still valid in less regular scenarios.

The natural scale that identifies the two regions is the value
\bar{p}
found by studying the saddles of the integrand, i.e.

\bar{p}\sim\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\bar{x}_{s}}\,.

(B.12)

It is straightforward to verify that the hard region only contributes to the analytic part of
\psi_{4,s}^{\Delta}
. We can study the soft region by setting
p=\bar{p}\,y
:

\frac{c_{1}\cosh(\pi\lambda)}{\pi E(\lambda^{2}+1/4)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\bar{x}_{s}}}\bigg(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\bar{x}_{s}}\bigg)^{i\lambda}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{y+\big(1+\frac{\epsilon y}{4\bar{x}_{s}}\big)^{2}}}\,y^{-1/2+i\lambda}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\bigg[\cdots\bigg|-\frac{\epsilon^{2}y}{4\bar{x}_{s}}\bigg]\,.

(B.13)

We find the leading contribution by performing the integral after setting
\epsilon=0
:

\psi_{4,s}^{\Delta}\supset\frac{c_{1}\cosh(\pi\lambda)}{\pi E(\lambda^{2}+1/4)}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda)\Gamma(-i\lambda)}{\sqrt{4\pi\bar{x}_{s}}}\bigg(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\bar{x}_{s}}\bigg)^{i\lambda}\,.

(B.14)

Substituting
\bar{x}_{s}\approx uv/(u+v)^{2}
and the general value of
c_{1,2}(\Delta)
allowed by the absence of folded singularities (
4.13
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{F}(u,v)\equiv k_{s}\psi_{4,s}^{\Delta}\supset

\displaystyle\>-\frac{(uv)^{1/2+i\lambda}}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\frac{\cosh(\pi\lambda)}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{3}{2}+i\lambda\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{3}{2}-i\lambda\big)}{\lambda^{2}+1/4}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\big)}{\Gamma(1+2i\lambda)\Gamma(-i\lambda)}\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\Big)^{2}\Gamma(-i\lambda)^{2}(1+\tilde{f}(i\lambda))\,.

(B.15)

Using Gamma function identities simplifies the above result to

\lim_{u,v\to 0}\hat{F}(u,v)=-\Big(\frac{uv}{4}\Big)^{1/2+i\lambda}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\big)^{2}\Gamma(-i\lambda)^{2}}{2\pi}(-i\sinh(\pi\lambda))(1+\tilde{f}(i\lambda))+\mathrm{c.c}\,,

(B.16)

which matches the desired result (
6.5
), provided that we set

\tilde{f}(\Delta)=\frac{1}{\sin(\pi(\Delta-3/2))}\,,

(B.17)

as claimed in Section
4.2
. In the computations above, we have implicitly assumed a finite
\bar{x}_{s}
as
u,v\to 0
, i.e.
u/v=(k_{3}+k_{4})/(k_{1}+k_{2})=\mathcal{O}(1)
, consistently with just demanding
k_{s}\to 0
.

In AdS
4
, on the other hand, the bulk-to-bulk propagator in momentum space takes the form
[
31
,
26
]

G_{F}(z,z^{\prime})=(zz^{\prime})^{3/2}I_{\nu}(k_{s}z_{<})K_{\nu}(k_{s}z_{>})\,,

(B.18)

with
\nu=\Delta-3/2
,
z_{<}=\mathrm{min}(z,z^{\prime})
and
z_{>}=\mathrm{max}(z,z^{\prime})
. In the collapsed limit, it is easy to show from the Witten diagram that one has

\lim_{u,v\to 0}\hat{F}^{(\rm AdS)}(u,v)=\Big(\frac{uv}{4}\Big)^{1/2+\nu}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\big)^{2}\Gamma(-\nu)}{\Gamma(1+\nu)}\,,

(B.19)

which confirms the boundary condition
c_{2}^{(\rm AdS)}(\Delta)=0
and is correctly predicted by the value of
c_{1}^{(\rm AdS)}(\Delta)
in (
4.16
) imposed by regularity in the Grassmannian. Performing a Wick rotation on the Witten diagram
z\mapsto-i\eta(1-i\epsilon)
recovers the time integral representation of the wavefunction coefficient, thus also corroborating the validity of
c_{1,2}^{(\rm WF)}(\Delta)
.

B.2
Full Result

We now go back to the study of the integral (
B.6
)

I_{\Delta}(u,v)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\sqrt{(p+x_{s}+\bar{x}_{s})^{2}-4x_{s}\bar{x}_{s}}}\,p^{-1/2+i\lambda}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda,\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
1+2i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\,-p\Bigg]\,,

(B.20)

which we will soon show to be equal to the following combination of
Kampé de Fériet
(KdF) functions:

\displaystyle I_{\Delta}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi p_{-}^{i\lambda}}{p_{+}^{1/2}\cosh(\pi\lambda)}F^{1|1|1}_{\,0|1|1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
-\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
1+2i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}\\[2.0pt]
1\end{array}\Bigg|\,p_{-},1-\frac{p_{-}}{p_{+}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+1/4}\frac{\Gamma(1+2i\lambda)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\big)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{p_{-}}{p_{+}}\bigg)^{1/2}F^{1|2|1}_{\,0|2|1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\[2.0pt]
-\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}1,1\\[2.0pt]
\frac{3}{2}+i\lambda,\frac{3}{2}-i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}\\[2.0pt]
1\end{array}\Bigg|\,p_{-},1-\frac{p_{-}}{p_{+}}\Bigg]\,,

where
p_{\pm}\equiv(\sqrt{x_{s}(u,v)}\pm\sqrt{\bar{x}_{s}(u,v)})^{2}
, and
\bar{x}_{s},x_{s}
were defined in (
B.3
). The KdF functions admit the following power series representations

\displaystyle F^{1|1|1}_{\,0|1|1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{1}\\[2.0pt]
-\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}a_{2}\\[2.0pt]
b_{2}\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}a_{3}\\[2.0pt]
b_{3}\end{array}\Bigg|\,x,y\Bigg]

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m,n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{m+n}(a_{2})_{m}(a_{3})_{n}}{(b_{2})_{m}(b_{3})_{n}}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\frac{y^{n}}{n!}\,,

(B.33)

\displaystyle F^{1|2|1}_{\,0|2|1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{1}\\[2.0pt]
-\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}a_{2},\tilde{a}_{2}\\[2.0pt]
b_{2},\tilde{b}_{2}\end{array}\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}a_{3}\\[2.0pt]
b_{3}\end{array}\Bigg|\,x,y\Bigg]

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m,n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{m+n}(a_{2})_{m}(\tilde{a}_{2})_{m}(a_{3})_{n}}{(b_{2})_{m}(\tilde{b}_{2})_{m}(b_{3})_{n}}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\frac{y^{n}}{n!}\,.

The first KdF function is, in particular, an Appell
F_{2}
. The sum of this integral with its shadow (
6.8
) is shown to match the result of
[
2
]
in Figure
4
.

) (in blue) and (3.36) in

To prove this result, we first substitute the Mellin-Barnes representation of the hypergeometric function

{}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda,\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\\[2.0pt]
1+2i\lambda\end{array}\Bigg|\,-p\Bigg]=\frac{\Gamma(1+2i\lambda)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\big)^{2}}\int_{-i\infty}^{+i\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{2\pi i}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda+s\big)^{2}\Gamma(-s)}{\Gamma(1+2i\lambda+s)}\,p^{s}\,.

(B.34)

This allows us to easily evaluate the integral with respect to
p

[
21
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{p_{-}^{i\lambda+s}}{p_{+}^{1/2}}\,\mathcal{I}_{\Delta}(u,v;s)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}p\>\frac{p^{-1/2+i\lambda+s}}{\sqrt{(p+p_{-})(p+p_{+})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{-}^{i\lambda+s}}{p_{+}^{1/2}}\Gamma\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda+s\bigg)\Gamma\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-i\lambda-s\bigg){}_{2}F_{1}\Bigg[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda+s\\[2.0pt]
1\end{array}\Bigg|\,1-\frac{p_{-}}{p_{+}}\Bigg]\,.

We must now perform the integral with respect to
s
:

I_{\Delta}(u,v)=\frac{p_{-}^{i\lambda}}{p_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{\Gamma(1+2i\lambda)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\big)^{2}}\int_{-i\infty}^{+i\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{2\pi i}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda+s\big)^{2}\Gamma(-s)}{\Gamma(1+2i\lambda+s)}\,(p_{-})^{s}\,\mathcal{I}_{\Delta}(u,v;s)\,.

(B.37)

This is achieved by summing the residues of the integrand in
s=m
and
s=m+1/2-i\lambda
, with
m=0,1,2,\dots
, arising from the Gamma functions. It is straightforward to show that these two sets of poles, combined with the power series representation of the hypergeometric
{}_{2}F_{1}
, reproduce exactly the power series of the two KdF functions with the appropriate prefactors (
B.2
). Given this result, the full correlator of the scalar exchange is given by

\displaystyle\hat{F}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{uv}{u+v}\frac{\cos(\pi(\Delta-3/2))}{\pi(\Delta-1)(\bar{\Delta}-1)}\,[c_{1}(\Delta)I_{\Delta}(u,v)+c_{2}(\Delta)I_{\bar{\Delta}}(u,v)]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{uv}{u+v}\bigg[\frac{\Gamma(\Delta-1)^{2}}{\Gamma(2\Delta-2)}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{\sin(\pi(\Delta-3/2))}\bigg)I_{\Delta}(u,v)+(\Delta\leftrightarrow\bar{\Delta})\bigg]\,.

(B.38)

The correlator in AdS
4
is obtained by only keeping the first term in the above formula, with
\sin(\pi(\Delta-3/2))\mapsto 1
, and flipping the overall sign.
