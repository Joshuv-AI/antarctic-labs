Summary
 Density functional theory (DFT) simulations have been carried out to evaluate the potential
for tritium trapping by metal vacancies in four phases (i.e., (Fe, Cr, Ni), AlFe3, NiAl, and AlFe)
identified near the interface between the Al coating and 316 stainless steel (316 SS) cladding. In
addition, an ab initio thermodynamics approach has been employed to predict the temperature
and T2-partial pressure dependence on the thermodynamics of tritiated defects. Key results in
this work suggest that metal vacancies in the four phases have the potential to favorably trap
tritium species. This thermodynamic trend can be correlated to the high energy cost of having an
interstitial tritium in the lattice, which has been calculated to be at least 0.48 eV. By combining
the results of this study with previous theoretical works investigating tritium behavior in other
Fe-Al phases identified in the aluminide coating, suggest that metal vacancies are generally able
to trap tritium in various Fe-Al aluminide phases. Especially, it was found that Al vacancies are
the most efficient to trap tritium, followed by Fe vacancies, then Ni and Cr vacancies. Strong
interactions between tritium and the metal vacancies are occurring by the formation of Fe—T
bonds. The formation of Cr—T, Ni—T, or Al—T bonds are found less energetically favorable than
Fe—T bonds.

1.0 Introduction
 In the design of the TPBAR the inner surface of the 316 SS structural pressure boundary
cladding is coated with an iron aluminide (Fe-Al) matrix to reduce tritium permeation into the
surrounding coolant. Although most of the tritium is absorbed by the getter, post-irradiation
evaluation (PIE) indicates that a small fraction of tritium is trapped in the aluminide coating.1
While it has been observed that tritium permeation is delayed due to the aluminide coating, the
actual mechanisms for tritium trapping are still elusive. So far, observations show that tritium is
trapped “somewhere” in the aluminide coating and not the 316 SS. Because experimental
detection of tritium is challenging, we rely on DFT simulations to provide insights into the
mechanisms and energetics of tritium trapping in the various Fe-Al phases of the aluminide
coating and compare the energetics of interstitial and substitutional tritium in metal vacancies
(e.g., TFe or TAl sites) to assess the potential for trapping by metal vacancies.
 Based on the STEM imaging conducted by M. Olszta,2 four regions can be distinguished in
the aluminide coating: (i) surface, (ii) outer-iron aluminide, (iii) inner-iron aluminide, and (iv)
interface with 316 SS, with each region having a different set of Fe-Al phases. In previous projects,
numerical simulations have investigated both the kinetics of interstitial tritium 3,4 and its
thermodynamic behavior5,6 in Fe-Al phases found in the outer- and inner-iron aluminide coating.
Key results are that interstitial tritium would diffuse similarly in all the Fe-Al phases identified and
that some phases can more effectively trap tritium while others have less potential for trapping.
It was also found that there could be a correlation between interstitial tritium solubility and the
potential for trapping by metal vacancies (Figure 1c and 1d), that is, if the insertion of an
interstitial tritium in a Fe-Al phase costs more than 0.3 eV (empirically determined), then trapping
by metal vacancies should be preferred. The current trend suggests that Fe-Al phases found in
the inner-region of the Al coating have more potential for trapping than the Fe-Al phase of the
outer-iron aluminide region (see Figure 1d under histogram). While the Fe-Al phases found in the
inner- and outer-iron aluminide coating are Al-rich (> 70 at.% Al), the phases found near the
interfacial region with 316 SS generally have ≤ 50 at.% Al, as shown in Figure 1b. Such composition
gradients could affect tritium behavior in the aluminide coating. So far, four phases have been
identified at the interface, namely, Fe (with 5% Ni and 5% Cr), AlFe3, NiAl, and AlFe.

2.0 Computational Details
 DFT calculations have been performed with the VASP code.7 All the simulations used the
generalized gradient approximation (GGA) exchange-correlation as parametrized in the Perdew,
Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) functional.8 A cutoff energy of 350 eV for the plane-wave basis set
has been used and spin-polarization has been taken into account. For each simulation, the
Brillouin zone was sampled with a Γ-centered k-point mesh of 3×3×3 and the total energy was
converged with a criterion of 10-5 eV/cell. Due to their similar electronic structure, the
pseudopotential of standard hydrogen (1H) has been used to describe tritium (3H), however, to
account for the isotopic effect, the mass in the pseudopotential has been modified to match that
of the isotope atom.
 For each of the four phases investigated in this work (i.e., Fe (with 5% Ni and 5% Cr), AlFe3,
NiAl, and AlFe), we first fully relaxed the supercell structure made of a 4×4×4 translation of the
primitive cell, then we introduced interstitial tritium and metal vacancy defects for which only
the atomic coordinates were allowed to relax. The lattice parameters were kept fixed to their
relaxed defect-free bulk structures values. Subsequently, tritium loading of the metal vacancy
has been investigated by filling it with several tritium atoms. The energy of multiple
configurations was evaluated and only the most energetically favorable one at each loading is
used for thermodynamic calculations.

3.0 Results and Discussion
3.1 Atomic model structure of (Fe, Cr, Ni)
 Based on electron diffraction spectroscopy (EDS) analysis, the Fe phase detected at the
interface contains about 5% Ni and 5% Cr (below simply labelled as (Fe, Cr, Ni)). In order to find
an atomic model that reproduces the stoichiometry experimentally determined, we generated
58 special quasi-random structures (SQS) and evaluated their energies. Figure 2 shows the
relative energies of all the structures generated. The horizontal dashed line used as reference is
the average of all the 58 energies. We selected the lowest energy structure, as shown in Figure
2, to investigate the thermodynamic behavior of tritium in the (Fe, Cr, Ni) phase.

Figure 2: Relative energy of the 58 SQS structures generated. The horizontal dashed line
represents the average energy of the 58 structures and has been used as reference.

3.2 Formation energy of metal vacancies in the four interfacial phases
 Starting from the experimental structure of (Fe, Cr, Ni), AlFe3, NiAl, and AlFe, we created
supercells made of a 4×4×4 translation of the primitive cell and found lattice parameters that are
in good agreements with the experimental values of 2.870 Å for (Fe, Cr, Ni),10 2.895 Å for AlFe3,11
2.880 Å for NiAl,12 and 2.910 Å for AlFe,13 as shown in Figure 3a. As a first step, we have explored
the energy landscape of metal vacancies in each structure and localized the atomic site leading
to the lowest vacancy formation energy. For each species, the atom site with the lowest vacancy
formation energy is represented by a polyhedron in Figure 3b.

3.3 Temperature and T2-partial pressure dependence of tritiated vacancies
 The tritiation of metal vacancies has been performed for up to six tritium atoms. The tritium
loading dependent defect energy has been calculated and is plotted in Figure 5 for each material
phase. Unfortunately, no general trends can be drawn from the results, indicating that each
material phase interacts differently with tritium. Adding tritium into metal vacancies of the (Fe,
Cr, Ni) phase can help lower the energy cost of forming the vacancy. The energetically most
favorable tritium loading for the Fe vacancy is two, while it is four for both the Cr and Ni
vacancies, indicating that the latter vacancies can accommodate more tritium than Fe vacancy.
In the case of AlFe3, adding tritium into the Fe vacancy is not energetically favorable as the 1 st
tritiation already increases the defect formation energy compared to the bare Fe vacancy.
However, for the Al vacancy, a tritium load of up to five tritium helps to reduce the initial energy
cost of the vacancy, suggesting that Al vacancies are more capable of accommodating tritium
species than Fe vacancies in AlFe3 material. In the case of the NiAl phase, neither Al nor Ni
vacancies favorably accommodate tritium since the 1st tritiation already increase the formation
energy of the defect. Finally, in the case of the AlFe phase, it seems that adding tritium into both
Al and Fe vacancies doesn’t increase or decrease much the defect formation energy, indicating
both defects are relatively unaffected by tritium loading for up-to six tritium in the vacancy space.
 While exploring different tritium configurations in the vacancy space, we noticed that the
energetically most favorable and common configurations obtained are those when tritium is
bound to a Fe species. As shown by the image inserts in Figure 5, the formation of Fe—T bonds
are very often preferred over the formation of Al—T, Cr—T, or Ni—T bonds.

Figure 5: Calculated defect formation energy as a function of tritium loading in metal vacancies.

 In order to investigate the effect of temperature and tritium partial pressure (𝑝(T2 )) on the
relative thermodynamic stability of tritiated and non-tritiated metal vacancies, ab initio
thermodynamic calculations have been performed. This allows calculation of the Gibbs free
energy of defects at conditions relevant to in-reactor operation, which are a temperature of ~700
K and tritium partial pressures ranging from 10-6 to 102 mbar.14 Figure 6 summarizes the
thermodynamics for the 1st tritiation of metal vacancies in the four interfacial phases and the
purple area highlights the tritium partial pressure of potential interest.
 Focusing on the range of 𝑝(T2 ) relevant to in-reactor conditions, Figure 6 shows that none
of the tritiated vacancies become more energetically favorable than the non-tritiated vacancies
of the chemical species they have been added to. This indicates that regardless of the T2-partial
pressure, the addition of tritium in a vacancy will always be thermodynamically unfavorable.
However, these results should also be put in correlation with the energy cost of having an
interstitial tritium species in the lattice.

3.4 Trapping potential of metal vacancies
 We estimate the trapping potential of metal vacancies by comparing the energy of a tritiated
vacancy with the energy of an interstitial tritium and a non-tritiated vacancy, non-interacting with
each other. In this comparison, a positive energy value indicates that tritium is more favorable as
an interstitial species, while a negative energy value suggests that tritium prefers bind with a
metal vacancy. As shown in Figure 7a, for all the vacancies and all four material phases, the
energy of tritium trapping is negative, indicating that in all cases tritium prefers bind to a metal
vacancy rather than remain as an interstitial tritium in the lattice. While it is not
thermodynamically favorable to tritiate a vacancy, relative to non-tritiated vacancy, it is still
thermodynamically more favorable than having an interstitial tritium.
 In addition, it is interesting to correlate the trapping trends of Figure 7a with the formation
energy of interstitial tritium, shown in Figure 7b. As the energy of having an interstitial tritium is
relatively high, trapping by a metal vacancy is preferred.

3.5 Comparison of tritium trapping potential for various phases in Al coating
 Based on STEM imaging, four regions can be distinguished in the aluminide coating: (i)
surface, (ii) outer-iron aluminide, (iii) inner-iron aluminide, and (iv) interface with 316 SS, with
each region having a different set of Fe-Al phases. In Figure 8, we color coded the different phase
names to help in their localization in the aluminide coating. A red phase name indicates a phase
found in the outer-iron aluminide region. A green phase name indicates a phase found in the
inner-iron aluminide region. A black phase name indicates a phase found at the interface of the
Al aluminide region.
 By combining the theoretical results currently available informing about the thermodynamic
of tritium in various Al coating phases, the following trend about tritium trapping can be drawn.
Figure 8a shows that in most cases, tritium prefers to be bound to a metal vacancy rather than
being an interstitial species. Figure 8a also shows that Al vacancies are the main driver for tritium
trapping in the Al coating since trapping is favorable for most phases except in the outer-iron
aluminide phase Al12(TM)2.35. Tritium trapping by Fe vacancies is also important, since only one
phase, namely FeNiAl5, shows a positive tritium trapping energy (i.e., interstitial tritium
preferred). Ni vacancies could also be a good trapping metal vacancy to some extent; however,
tritium trapping depends on the material phase. Lastly, Cr vacancies present mixed results,
however, it should be noted that the series of phases investigated do not have a lot of Cr species
in their composition, therefore, the trend regarding its trapping potential is still elusive.

Figure 8: Overall comparison of tritium trapping potential for various phases observed in the Al
coating. (a) Energy of trapping tritium in a metal vacancy. (b) Formation energy of interstitial

 It is interesting to correlate the trapping trends of Figure 8a with the formation energy of
interstitial tritium, shown in Figure 8b. For that, we empirically determined a threshold of 0.3 eV
for the formation energy of interstitial tritium (grey dashed line in Figure 8b). If the formation
energy of interstitial tritium is larger than 0.3 eV, then tritium trapping by metal vacancies is
generally preferred, however, if the formation energy of interstitial tritium is lower than 0.3 eV,
then tritium remaining as interstitial species is preferred. This empirically determined threshold
of 0.3 eV seems to be a good value based on the theoretical data currently available.

 13

Conclusion
 Density functional theory simulations have been carried out to investigate the potential for
tritium trapping by metal vacancies in several phases found in the Al coating. It was found that
Al vacancies can trap tritium the most efficiently, followed by Fe vacancies, then Ni vacancies,
and finally Cr vacancies. A good mechanism for tritium trapping is by formation of strong Fe—T
bonds, which are stronger than Cr—T, Ni—T, or Al—T bonds. This trend has been theoretically
obtained across the range of phases investigated. Altogether, while tritium interacts differently
with each phase, the study shows that tritium trapping and retention could be more efficient if
metal defects, in particular Al vacancies, are present and if the solubility of interstitial tritium in
the different phases is low, thereby, providing a thermodynamic driving force for tritium being
bound to metal vacancies.
