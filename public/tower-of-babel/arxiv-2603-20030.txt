Abstract
A triangulation of a surface is k \displaystyle k -irreducible if every non-contractible curve has length at least k \displaystyle k and any edge contraction breaks this property. Equivalently, every edge belongs to a non-contractible curve of length k \displaystyle k and there are no shorter non-contractible curves. We prove that a k \displaystyle k -irreducible triangulation of a surface of genus g \displaystyle g has O ⁡ ( k 2 ​ g ) \displaystyle O(k^{2}g) triangles, which is optimal. This is an improvement over the previous best bound k O ⁡ ( k ) ​ g 2 \displaystyle k^{O(k)}g^{2} of Gao, Richter and Seymour [ Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series B, 1996 ].



1
Introduction

The main objects of study in this article are
(surface) triangulations
. There are many inequivalent definitions of those, and for us they will be simplicial complexes homeomorphic to surfaces. Rephrased in a graph-theoretical language, this means that a triangulation is a graph embedded in a surface where all the faces are topological disks and have degree three, and the graph is
simple
: multiple edges and loops are forbidden.

In 1922, Steinitz
[
42
]
proved that all triangulations of the
\displaystyle 2
-dimensional sphere could be obtained from the tetrahedron by
splitting vertices
. Nowadays, it is more common to consider the inverse operation of
contracting an edge
, which consists in identifying the endpoints of an edge and collapsing the two adjacent triangles. Such a contraction must preserve the properties of triangulations, and in particular must not induce multiple edges and loops. Thus, Steinitz proved that the tetrahedron is the unique
irreducible triangulation
of the sphere: every triangulation of the sphere can be reduced to the tetrahedron by iteratively contracting edges, and the tetrahedron cannot be contracted further.

Barnette initiated the study of the analogous problem on the projective plane
[
3
]
, which led to the classification of irreducible triangulations of surfaces up to Euler genus
\displaystyle 4
by Sulanke
[
43
]
by means of the computer program
surftri
. It is now known that for every surface of Euler genus
\displaystyle g
, the number of irreducible triangulations is finite and that they have at most
\displaystyle 13g-4
vertices, see Joret and Wood
[
30
]
. The latter improves previous bounds by Barnette and Edelson
[
2
]
and by Nakamoto and Ota
[
39
]
. This bound has been extended to surfaces with boundaries by Boulch, Colin de Verdière and Nakamoto
[
6
]
.

A closed curve on a surface is
non-contractible
if it is not homotopic (intuitively, if it cannot be deformed continuously) to a point. Any closed walk on the edges of a triangulation determines a closed curve on the underlying surface. The
edge-width
of a triangulation is the length of the shortest (by the number of edges) non-contractible closed walk. A triangulation is
\displaystyle k
-irreducible
if it has edge-width at least
\displaystyle k
and it is irreducible for this property: any edge contraction reduces this edge-width. Equivalently, every edge belongs to a non-contractible curve of length
\displaystyle k
and there are no shorter non-contractible curves. We refer to the PhD thesis of Melzer for a proof of this equivalence
[
37
, Lemma 3.1]
and for a thorough introduction to
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulations, in particular the different motivations for which they have been introduced.

Since loops and multiple edges are forbidden, triangulations of surfaces of positive Euler genus have edge-width at least
\displaystyle 3
. From the definitions, it is easy to see that irreducible triangulations and
\displaystyle 3
-irreducible triangulations match. Therefore,
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulations generalize irreducible triangulations, and one is naturally led to wonder about their combinatorial properties.

This problem was first studied by Malnič and Nedela
[
35
]
who proved that the number of
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulations of a surface is finite (they also observed that this follows from a variant of Robertson-Seymour theory), see also Juvan, Malnič and Mohar
[
31
]
. The current best known bound, due to Gao, Richter and Seymour
[
24
]
is that a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulation of a surface of Euler genus
\displaystyle g
has
\displaystyle k^{O(k)}g^{2}
edges. In that paper, the authors say that they expect the correct bound to depend linearly on
\displaystyle g
instead of quadratically. In his aforementioned PhD thesis, Melzer
[
37
, p. 18]
states “Our expectation would be something like
\displaystyle O(k^{2}g)
, but we lack any evidence at all to conjecture”. Our main result confirms the expectations of Gao, Richter, Seymour and Melzer (see Corollary
1.3
for the case of non-orientable surfaces).

Theorem 1.1
.

Any
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulation of an orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of genus
\displaystyle g
has at most
\displaystyle 966k^{2}g=O(k^{2}g)
edges.

The bound
\displaystyle O(k^{2}g)
in Theorem
1.1
is tight up to the value of the constant which we have not tried to optimize: intuitively it can be achieved by taking
\displaystyle g

\displaystyle k
by
\displaystyle k

[
37

for precise descriptions of families of
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulations of size
\displaystyle\Theta(k^{2}g)
. One interesting follow-up question would be to determine the correct constant in some asymptotic regimes in
\displaystyle g
and
\displaystyle k

Note that an immediate application of the Euler formula yields similar bounds for the number of vertices and faces.

Systems of curves.

Our proof of Theorem
1.1

\displaystyle k

41

For the case of
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle k

Connections to systolic geometry.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
provide discrete counterparts to results in systolic geometry. This subfield of differential geometry investigates topological spaces endowed with continuous metrics (typically Riemannian) and aims at understanding the structure and properties of their shortest non-contractible curves, called
systoles
. We refer to the book of Katz
[
33
]
for an introduction.

The famed systolic inequality of Gromov
[
25
]
states that for any surface of genus
\displaystyle g
and area
\displaystyle A
, the systole has length
\displaystyle O(\sqrt{A/g}\log g)
. This is known to be tight: Brooks
[
8
]
and Buser and Sarnak
[
9
]
provided families of hyperbolic surfaces achieving this bound. There are discrete analogues of these results for embedded graphs
[
29
,
15
,
34
]
, showing that the edge-width of a triangulation with
\displaystyle n
triangles is similarly bounded by
\displaystyle O(\sqrt{n/g}\log g)
and that this is asymptotically tight. Equivalently, this shows that any triangulation of edge-width
\displaystyle k
has
\displaystyle\Omega(k^{2}g/\log^{2}g)
triangles
2
2

2

Note that the growth is sensibly different if one considers the number of vertices instead of the number of triangles, see Melzer
[
37
, Section 2.2]
. Bounding the size of
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulations then amounts to looking for a
reverse systolic inequality
, bounding how far from this lower bound a locally extremal object for the systolic inequality can be. From this perspective, it is noteworthy that the logarithmic factor, which is known to be required for the systolic inequality, disappears in our bounds in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, even though our bounds are also tight.

We are not aware of any works investigating continuous versions of such a reverse systolic inequality. One difficulty is that the space of Riemannian metrics on a surface is not compact, and extremal surfaces for the systolic inequality in negative Euler characteristic are expected to exhibit singularities, see for example Gromov
[
25
, pp.62-65]
. This can be addressed by adding additional assumptions on the curvature, restricting to hyperbolic surfaces as in Fortier Bourque and Rafi
[
7
]
, or nonpositively curved surfaces as in Katz and Sabourau
[
32
]
. Since the discrete setting of triangulations does not suffer from these analytic issues, it provides a convenient testbed for the study of locally extremal surfaces. The framework of geodesic currents of Bonahon
[
5
]
could provide an interesting middle-ground between these two worlds, keeping a continuous flavour but with strong compactness properties.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we provide the main definitions used throughout the paper. The central objects of study are topological surfaces and curves on them whose definitions are recalled in Section
2.1
. A (discrete) geometry on a surface allowing to measure lengths can then be defined in two related ways: either by the mean of an embedded graph also called combinatorial surface (see Section
2.2
), or using a system of curves as in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Surfaces and curves

In this paper, we use the term
surface
for a closed orientable topological surface, except in Section
6
where we deal with non-orientable surfaces. It is always denoted by
\displaystyle S
and is sometimes implicit. We refer to Massey
[
36
]
for topological background.

Curves.

A
curve
on a surface
\displaystyle S
is a continous map
\displaystyle c:[0,1]\rightarrow S
and a
closed curve
on
\displaystyle S
is a continuous map
\displaystyle c:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to S
, where
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
is the one-dimensional circle
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Since all the curves in this article are closed, we will often just write curve instead of closed curve. Two closed curves
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle c^{\prime}
on
\displaystyle S
are
(freely) homotopic
if there is a continuous map
\displaystyle h:[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow S
such that
\displaystyle h(0,t)=c(t)
and
\displaystyle h(1,t)=c^{\prime}(t)
. Being freely homotopic is an equivalence relation on closed curves on
\displaystyle S
and we refer to
(free) homotopy classes
for an equivalence class of closed curves with respect to free homotopy. We always denote curves with latin letters and homotopy classes with greek letters. A closed curve is
contractible
if it is homotopic to a constant curve. A curve is
simple
if it does not self-intersect. A closed curve is
primitive
if it is not homotopic to the
\displaystyle k
-fold concatenation of a curve
\displaystyle c
for some
\displaystyle k\geqslant 2
. In particular, a primitive curve is not contractible.

2.2
Combinatorial surfaces

Combinatorial surfaces.

A
combinatorial surface
is an embedding
\displaystyle\phi:G\to S
of a graph
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
, possibly with multiple edges and loops, in a surface
\displaystyle S
, such that
\displaystyle S\setminus\phi(G)
is a disjoint union of open disks. These disks are called the
faces
of
\displaystyle G
. The counterclockwise sequence of arcs (i.e., oriented edges) in the boundary of a face is called a
face boundary
or
facial walk
. The
degree of a face
is the length of its facial walk.

Two combinatorial surfaces
\displaystyle\phi:G\to S
and
\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
are
isomorphic
if there exists a graph isomorphism
\displaystyle\theta:G\to G^{\prime}
and a homeomorphism
\displaystyle\psi:S\to S^{\prime}
such that
\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}\circ\theta=\psi\circ\phi
. The isomorphism class of a combinatorial surface can be conveniently encoded by a
rotation system
describing the circular orderings of the (oriented) edges around each vertex. See Mohar and Thomassen
[
38
]
for more details on this structure. Most of the time, the embedding
\displaystyle\phi
is implicit and we identify
\displaystyle G
and the corresponding combinatorial surface.

Let
\displaystyle\phi:G\to S
be a combinatorial surface. The
length (with respect to
\displaystyle G
)
of a curve
\displaystyle c
in
\displaystyle S
that crosses only the vertices of
\displaystyle G
and not its edges is the number of times this curve passes through a vertex of
\displaystyle G
. It is denoted
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(c,G)
. The
systole
of
\displaystyle G
is the minimum length over all non-contractible closed curves
\displaystyle c
on
\displaystyle S
passing only through vertices of
\displaystyle G
of
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(c,G)
. We will use the common abuse of language to also call systole such a non-contractible closed curve of minimal length when it leads to no confusion.

Triangulation.

A combinatorial surface
\displaystyle(S,G)
is a
triangulation
if
\displaystyle G
is simple (no loops nor multiple edges) and every face has degree
\displaystyle 3
.
A triangulation
\displaystyle T
on a surface
\displaystyle S
is
\displaystyle k
-irreducible
if

1.

the systole of
\displaystyle T
is equal to
\displaystyle k

2.

for every edge
\displaystyle e=(uv)
of
\displaystyle T
, there is a non-contractible curve
\displaystyle c
of length
\displaystyle k
on
\displaystyle T
crossing consecutively
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
.

Remark 2.1
.

In line with the introduction, we define our triangulations to be simplicial, but note that this is implied by
\displaystyle k
-irreducibility for
\displaystyle k\geqslant 3
. Indeed, if there is a loop or a bigon formed of two parallel edges, it has to be contractible and thus bounds a disk, but then the edges inside that disk cannot belong to a systole as there would be a shortcut, violating
\displaystyle k
-irreducibility.

2.3
Systems of curves

Systems of curves.

A
system of curves
on a surface
\displaystyle S
is a family
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})
of non-contractible closed curves on
\displaystyle S
in
general position
, i.e., they only cross transversely and at most two curves cross at a point.

An
intersection
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is a pair
\displaystyle(i,s)\not=(j,t)
such that
\displaystyle c_{i}(s)=c_{j}(t)
. Because a system of curves is in general position, the set of intersections is an isolated subset of points in
\displaystyle S
. The
crossing number of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is its number of intersections. Note that
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
defines a
\displaystyle 4
-regular embedded graph on
\displaystyle S
whose vertex set is the intersections of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and its edges are segments of the curves
\displaystyle c_{i}
. Conversely, a
\displaystyle 4
-regular embedded graph
\displaystyle\phi:G\to S
determines a system of curves by ‘going straight’ at each crossing. We will freely use this identification and use the vocabulary of embedded graphs, i.e., vertices, edges and faces for a system of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. Note however that in this context the embedded graph might not be a combinatorial surface because its complement could have topology. A system of curve is
connected
if the corresponding graph is connected.

Two systems of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{p}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\{c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{p}\}
are
homotopic
if for all
\displaystyle i
in
\displaystyle[1,p]
,
\displaystyle c_{i}
is freely homotopic to
\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{i}
. We say that a system of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is in
minimal position
if it contains no contractible curves and has the minimal crossing number over all systems of curves homotopic to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. We say that two curves
intersect essentially
if they intersect at least once in minimal position.

Length of curves.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a system of curves on
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle c
be a curve transverse to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. The
length
of
\displaystyle c
with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is the number of crossings of
\displaystyle c
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. We recall that this quantity is denoted
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(c,\mathcal{C})
. The
length
of a free homotopy class
\displaystyle\gamma
is the minimal number of crossings of a representant
\displaystyle c
of
\displaystyle\gamma
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. We use the notation
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(\gamma,C)
as a shortcut for
\displaystyle\min_{c\in\gamma}\operatorname{cr}(c,C)
. If the length of
\displaystyle c
equals the length of its free homotopy class, we say that
\displaystyle c
is a
geodesic
(equivalently
\displaystyle c
is in minimal position with
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
). For
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in minimal position, we define the
systole
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
to be
\displaystyle\min_{\gamma}\operatorname{cr}(\gamma,\mathcal{C})
where
\displaystyle\gamma
runs over all non-trivial free homotopy classes of closed curves in
\displaystyle S
. Here again, we sometimes also call a closed curve achieving this minimum length a systole.
We say that a system of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in minimal position on a surface
\displaystyle S
is
filling
if for any non-trivial homotopy class
\displaystyle\gamma
we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(\gamma,\mathcal{C})>0
. Equivalently, a system of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in minimal position on a surface
\displaystyle S
is filling if and only if it corresponds to a combinatorial surface (i.e. the complement of the curves is a disjoint union of topological disks).

Geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered and
\displaystyle k
-irreducible systems of curves.

Recall from the introduction that a system of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
on a surface
\displaystyle S
is
geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered
if all its smoothings are geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered and it does not have a connected component consisting of exactly one contractible curve. We say that a system of curves is
\displaystyle k
-irreducible
for some
\displaystyle k\geqslant 1
if it is geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered and every non-contractible closed curve
\displaystyle c
on
\displaystyle S
has length at least
\displaystyle k
. This mirrors the definition for triangulations.

A system of curves
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is
tight
if it does not contain a contractible curve disjoint from the other curves and if for any smoothing
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
there is a curve in minimal position with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
which is shorter with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
than with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. The following is an (equivalent reformulation of) a notion introduced by Schrijver, who proved the following theorem:

Theorem 2.2

(
[
40
, Theorem 5]
)
.

A system of curves on an orientable surface is tight if and only if it is made of primitive curves in minimal position.

It is immediate that a geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered system of curves is tight, and therefore it is in minimal position. Conversely, a tight system of curves is geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered for some
\displaystyle k
. Note also that a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible system of curves is necessarily filling. While a geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered system of curves may not be filling, by cutting the surface along simple non-contractible closed curves that it misses, we can obtain a new surface of smaller genus in which it is filling, and the smaller genus will only improve our bounds. So without loss of generality we assume henceforth that geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered system of curves are filling.

Remark 2.3
.

In this article, we often alternate between different metric models. In order to minimize confusion, we use the following guidelines:

•

in a triangulation
\displaystyle T
, we consider curves which are walks on the edges of the triangulation, and their lengths are the number of edges that they use.

•

in a combinatorial surface
\displaystyle G
, we consider curves which cross
\displaystyle G
only at vertices, and their lengths are the number of vertices that they cross.

•

in a system of curves

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

4
Short filling systems for geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered systems of curves

The main result of this section is the following proposition, which extracts from the property of being geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered a more topological property: the existence of a filling family of short curves.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered system of curves. Then there is set of curves of lengths at most
\displaystyle 2k
with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
which is in minimal position and filling.

The proof of this proposition relies partially on the notion of universal cover. We refer to Massey
[
36
, Chapter 5]
for an introduction to this classical object in algebraic topology and to Farb and Margalit
[
23
, Section 1.2]
for the specifics in the case of curves on surfaces. Given a surface
\displaystyle S
different from the sphere, its
universal cover

\displaystyle\tilde{S}
is homeomorphic to the plane
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Given a curve
\displaystyle c:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to S
in a surface
\displaystyle S
, a
lift

\displaystyle\tilde{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\tilde{S}
of
\displaystyle c
is a map to the universal cover
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
that commutes with the projections
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{S}\to S
. When the lift
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
is simple we call it a
line
.

Proof.

We will build a set of curves of lengths at most
\displaystyle 2k
made of geodesics of length at most
\displaystyle k
and some curves that are obtained by concatenation of two of these, and then show that these curves are filling.

The following claim is standard in geometry of curves on surfaces. As it is used many times throughout our proof, we provide a standalone proof.

Claim 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle c^{\prime}
be non-contractible curves on
\displaystyle S
in minimal position. Then any two lifts
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{c^{\prime}}
in the universal cover
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
are lines that intersect at most once.

Proof.

Assume that the lift
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
has a self-intersection. Then, by Jordan curve theorem in
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
, it forms a monogon: there is a disk in
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
bounded by a subline of
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
. By homotopy, one can remove this monogon contradicting the fact that
\displaystyle c
was in minimal position on
\displaystyle S
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{c^{\prime}}
are lines in
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
.

Let us assume for contradiction that two lifts
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{c^{\prime}}
intersect twice. Then, again by Jordan curve theorem, they form a bigon: there is a a disk in
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
bounded by one subline
\displaystyle\alpha
of
\displaystyle\tilde{s}
and one subline
\displaystyle\beta
of
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
. Now, the homotopy moving
\displaystyle\alpha
to
\displaystyle\beta
projects to a homotopy on the surface, and leads to a new pair of curves intersecting less. This contradicts the hypothesis that
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle c^{\prime}
were in minimal position.
∎

The next claim is the first step in defining the curves we will work with.

Claim 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle v
be a crossing of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and
\displaystyle e
,
\displaystyle e^{\prime}
two edges adjacent to
\displaystyle v
that are consecutive for the counter-clockwise ordering of edges around
\displaystyle v
. Then there is a geodesic of length at most
\displaystyle k
that passes consecutively through
\displaystyle e
and
\displaystyle e^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the system of curves obtained by smoothing
\displaystyle v
and connecting
\displaystyle e
with
\displaystyle e^{\prime}
. As
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered, there exist homotopic geodesics
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle c^{\prime}
respectively for
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(c,\mathcal{C}^{\prime})<\operatorname{cr}(c,\mathcal{C})\leqslant k
. Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
differ from each other only in the neighborhood of
\displaystyle v
in
\displaystyle S
where the smoothing has been performed, we may assume that
\displaystyle c=c^{\prime}
and the curve
\displaystyle c
must use at least once the gap between
\displaystyle e
and
\displaystyle e^{\prime}
formed by the smoothing. Therefore,
\displaystyle c
crosses consecutively
\displaystyle e
and
\displaystyle e^{\prime}
in a neighborhood of
\displaystyle v

∎

Let
\displaystyle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}

\displaystyle e_{4}

of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

We first handle the case where the surface is a torus.

Claim 4.4

If the surface
\displaystyle S

Because

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle e_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
lift to lines crossing

and
\displaystyle c_{2}

and
\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle 2k
-saturating. The following lemma shows that one can extract a filling family of size
\displaystyle O(g)

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a filling system of curves in minimal position on a closed orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of genus
\displaystyle g\geqslant 1
. Then there is a sub-system
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
of at most
\displaystyle 3g-1
curves which is also filling.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

For
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
a system of curves in minimal position, the
subsurface spanned by
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is a closed subsurface
\displaystyle S(\mathcal{C})\subseteq S
defined by first taking a regular neighborhood
\displaystyle N(\mathcal{C})
of the curves in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
, and then filling the components of
\displaystyle S\setminus N(\mathcal{C})
which are topological disks.

We proceed greedily. We pick a curve
\displaystyle c_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}:=\{c_{1}\}
. Then we define a finite sequence of curves and curve systems
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}
for
\displaystyle i\geqslant 1
as follows

•

if
\displaystyle S(\mathcal{C}_{i})=S
, we stop

•

if not, we pick
\displaystyle c_{i+1}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{C}_{i},c_{i+1})>0
and there exists a closed curve
\displaystyle c
in minimal position with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{C}_{i},c)=0
but
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(c_{i+1},c)>0
, and we set
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i+1}=\mathcal{C}_{i}\cup\{c_{i+1}\}
.

Note that the sequence is well defined because
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is filling. For each
\displaystyle i
, let
\displaystyle S_{i}
be the subsurface spanned by
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}
. By definition
\displaystyle S_{i-1}\subset S_{i}
. By the first property in the construction of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i+1}
from
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}
it follows by induction that each
\displaystyle S_{i}
is connected. By the second property, each inclusion
\displaystyle S_{i-1}\subset S_{i}
is topologically strict in the sense that each
\displaystyle S_{i}
contains at least one essential (non-contractible and not homotopic to a boundary) simple closed curve disjoint from
\displaystyle S_{i-1}
. Since the surface
\displaystyle S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
has at least one boundary and is connected and orientable, this implies that it either has negative Euler characteristic, or is an annulus whose boundaries are not homotopic to a boundary of
\displaystyle S_{i}
. In the second case, by connectedness the genus of
\displaystyle S_{i-1}
is smaller than the one of
\displaystyle S_{i}
. As the genus of
\displaystyle S_{i}
is smaller than
\displaystyle g
, this case happens at most
\displaystyle g
times. Finally, we recall that the Euler characteristic is additive:
\displaystyle\chi(S_{i-1})+\chi(S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1})=\chi(S_{i})
. So since
\displaystyle\chi(S_{1})\leqslant 0
, the first case happens at most
\displaystyle 2g-2
times.

In total, there are at most
\displaystyle g+2g-2
inclusions of subsurfaces, and thus at most
\displaystyle 3g-1
curves in the constructed system of curves when the algorithm stops.
∎

We can now combine our tools to obtain a first polynomial upper bound.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a
\displaystyle k
-saturating system of curves. Then the crossing number of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is at most
\displaystyle 2((3g-1)\cdot k)^{2}
.

Proof.

By the definition of a
\displaystyle k
-saturating system of curves and thanks to Lemma
4.6
, there exists a filling family
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=(s_{i})_{i\in I}
of closed curves of length at most
\displaystyle k
with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and of size at most
\displaystyle 3g-1
. In particular, for every
\displaystyle i\in I
,
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(s_{i},\mathcal{C})=k
.

Recall that by the definition of
\displaystyle k
-saturating, the system
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{S}
is in minimal position. We think of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
as a
\displaystyle 4
-valent graph that is a combinatorial surface. In other words:

•

Every curve of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
intersect transversely the curves
\displaystyle s_{i}
, and

•

Every intersection vertex of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is inside a face of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
.

We denote by
\displaystyle F
the faces of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
. For such a face
\displaystyle f
, let
\displaystyle p(f)
denote the perimeter of
\displaystyle f
i.e. the number of crossings of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
with the boundary of
\displaystyle f
. Then, by double counting,
\displaystyle\sum_{f\in F}p(f)=2\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C})\leqslant 2\cdot k\cdot\#\mathcal{S}\leqslant 2(3g-1)k
.

Similarly let
\displaystyle n(f)
denote the number of vertices of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in the face
\displaystyle f
. Then
\displaystyle\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{f\in F}n(f)
.
As
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is in minimal position, the restriction of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
to any face
\displaystyle f
is also in minimal position. This implies that any two curves in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
cross at most once in
\displaystyle f
, since otherwise they would form a bigon and thus would not be in minimal position. Thus
\displaystyle n(f)\leqslant\frac{p(f)\cdot(p(f)-1)}{2}
.
So

\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{f\in F}n(f)\leqslant\sum_{f\in F}\frac{p(f)^{2}}{2}-\frac{p(f)}{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{f\in F}p(f)\right)^{2}\leqslant\frac{(2(3g-1)k)^{2}}{2}.\qed

Combining Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.7
provides us with a first bound of
\displaystyle O(k^{2}g^{2})
on the number of vertices in a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible system of curves, and thus on the number of edges in a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulation via the reduction in Section
3
. In the next section we add another ingredient to strengthen this bound to
\displaystyle O(k^{2}g)
.

Remark 4.8
.

Note that the family
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in Theorem
4.7
is not assumed to be geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered, and thus this theorem could be of independent interest. It is easy to see that Theorem
4.7
is tight up to a constant factor, by considering a genus
\displaystyle g
orientable surface obtained by identifying opposite edges in a
\displaystyle 2g
-gon, and a system of curves made of
\displaystyle k
parallel curves between each pair of opposite edges.

Upper bounds for geodesically
\displaystyle k
-covered systems of curves

In this section, we show:

To prove this result, we first establish a preliminary lemma on configuration of arcs in a disk. An
arc
in a disk is a curve with endpoints on the boundary of the disk. For a system of arcs
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
in minimal position in a disk, we denote by
\displaystyle d
the largest distance between a face of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
and the boundary of the disk (where we measure lengths in terms of crossings with
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
) and by
\displaystyle n
the number of intersections between pairs of arcs of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
. The following inequality allows us to bound
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle d
and
\displaystyle p
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
,
\displaystyle p
,
\displaystyle d
,
\displaystyle n
be as above. Then
\displaystyle(6d+7)p\geqslant n
.

Our proof relies on a recent structural result of Hickingbotham, Illingworth, Mohar and Wood
[
28

and the fact that a graph of treewidth
\displaystyle k
with
\displaystyle n
vertices has less than
\displaystyle kn
edges (see, e.g., Baste, Noy and Sau

4

).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle G
be the intersection graph of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
: it has one vertex per arc of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
and an edge between vertices corresponding to intersecting pair of arcs. By definition,
\displaystyle G
has
\displaystyle p
vertices and
\displaystyle n
edges.

Let
\displaystyle M_{G}
be the map graph of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
which has one vertex for each face of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
, including the outer face, and edges between vertices corresponding to faces that share at least one vertex. Let
\displaystyle\operatorname{rad}(M_{G})
its radius, that is, the minimum radius of a ball that covers
\displaystyle M_{G}
. Then, by
[
28
, Theorem 5]
, we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{tw}(G)\leqslant 6\cdot\operatorname{rad}(M_{G})+7
, where
\displaystyle\operatorname{tw}(G)
denotes the tree-width of
\displaystyle G
.

Let
\displaystyle v_{\infty}
be the vertex of
\displaystyle M_{G}
corresponding to the external face. Then
\displaystyle v_{\infty}
is at distance at most
\displaystyle d
from every face of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
in the dual graph of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
. It follows from the definitions that the distances in the map graph are smaller than the one in the dual graph, so
\displaystyle v_{\infty}
is at distance at most
\displaystyle d
in
\displaystyle M_{G}
from every point of
\displaystyle M_{G}
. Thus
\displaystyle\operatorname{rad}(M_{G})\leqslant d
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{tw}(G)\leqslant 6d+7
.

Since a graph of tree-width
\displaystyle t
and
\displaystyle v
vertices has strictly less than
\displaystyle tv
edges,
\displaystyle(6d+7)p\geqslant n
.
∎

We can now conclude the proof of Theorem

.

The proof starts like the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle 2k
with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
as a
\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle F
and denote by
\displaystyle n(f)
the number of vertices of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in a face
\displaystyle f
, and by
\displaystyle p(f)
the perimeter of
\displaystyle f

Claim 5.2
.

For any face
\displaystyle f

\displaystyle n(f)\leqslant(3k+7)\cdot p(f)

Proof.

In order to prove this claim we show that
\displaystyle d\leqslant\frac{k}{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle k

We conclude using

6
Non-orientable surfaces

In this section, we explain how to extend our results to the setting of non-orientable surfaces. The main tool that we rely on is the standard concept of
orientable double cover
, of which we summarize the main properties of interest for us in the following proposition:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a graph embedded on a non-orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of (non-orientable) genus
\displaystyle g\neq 1
. Then there exists a graph
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
embedded on an orientable surface
\displaystyle S^{\prime}
of (orientable) genus
\displaystyle g
and a continuous map
\displaystyle\pi:S^{\prime}\rightarrow S
such that
\displaystyle G^{\prime}=\pi^{-1}(G)
and we have the following properties:

1.

The map
\displaystyle\pi
is a connected covering map with two sheets, i.e., it is a local homeomorphism,
\displaystyle S^{\prime}
is connected and every point in
\displaystyle S
has two preimages via
\displaystyle\pi
. In particular, the graph
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
has twice as many vertices, edges and faces as
\displaystyle G
, and

2.

The preimage of a geodesic of length at most
\displaystyle k
on
\displaystyle G
is either a geodesic of length
\displaystyle 2k
or the union of two geodesics of length
\displaystyle k
in
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
.

The first property is a standard fact valid for any non-orientable manifold, we refer for example to Hatcher
[
27
, Chapter 3.3]
for a reference. The second property follows from the fact that for any non orientable surface of genus
\displaystyle g\neq 1
, the preimage of a non-contractible curve by the projection map
\displaystyle\pi
is never contractible. This in turn follows from the fact that fundamental groups of non-orientable surfaces of genus at least
\displaystyle 2
have no torsion. One way to see this is that such a fundamental group acts freely as an isometry on the universal cover of the surface, which is
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
or the hyperbolic plane
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{2}
(this is where we use that
\displaystyle S
is not a projective plane), and there are no free isometries of finite order on either of these spaces.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

We first consider the case
\displaystyle g\neq 1
. In this case, we can apply Proposition
6.1
to lift a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulation
\displaystyle T
of a non-orientable surface of genus
\displaystyle g
into a graph
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
embedded on an orientable surface of genus
\displaystyle g
. By Property
1
of that proposition,
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
has at most twice as many edges as
\displaystyle T
, and by Property
2
, while it may not be
\displaystyle k
-irreducible, it has the property that through every edge of
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
there is a geodesic of length at most
\displaystyle 2k
.

We now apply to
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
the same proof techniques as in Section
3
: we consider an edge inclusion-wise minimal subgraph
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
with the property that the length of geodesic nooses in
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
is the same as in
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
, and we denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
the system of curves associated to
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
. We claim that
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is geodesically
\displaystyle 4k
-covered, and the proof is identical to the proof in Proposition
3.1
:

•

If a smoothing of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
corresponds to the deletion of an edge
\displaystyle e
in
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
, then we consider a noose of length at most
\displaystyle 2k
in
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
, whose length decreases after deletion of
\displaystyle e
: such a noose exists by definition of
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
and yields a geodesic of length at most
\displaystyle 4k
for
\displaystyle\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
whose length decreases after smoothing.

•

If a smoothing of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
corresponds to the contraction of an edge
\displaystyle e
in
\displaystyle G^{\prime}
, then we consider the geodesic of length at most
\displaystyle 2k
going through
\displaystyle e
in
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
. This corresponds to a geodesic of length at most
\displaystyle 4k
for
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, whose length decreases after smoothing.

We can now apply Theorem
1.2
to obtain that the crossing number of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is at most
\displaystyle 120(4k)^{2}g=1920k^{2}g
, and thus the number of edges in
\displaystyle T
satisfies
\displaystyle|E(T)|=1/2|E(T^{\prime})|=3|V(T^{\prime})|+6g-6\leqslant 2\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})+6g-6\leqslant 3840^{2}g+6g-6\leqslant 3846k^{2}g
.

We now handle the case where
\displaystyle T
is a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulation of the projective plane, i.e.,
\displaystyle S
has non-orientable genus
\displaystyle 1
. As before, we consider an edge inclusion-wise minimal subgraph
\displaystyle G
with the property that shortest non-contractible nooses on
\displaystyle G
still have length
\displaystyle k
, and we denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
the system of curves associated to
\displaystyle T
. Note that
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is
\displaystyle 2k
-irreducible, but Theorem
2.2
does not apply on non-orientable surfaces, and thus we do not know
a priori
whether
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is in minimal position.

By assumption, there exists a geodesic
\displaystyle c
of length
\displaystyle k
going through every edge of
\displaystyle T
, we choose one arbitrarily. Note that
\displaystyle c
must be a simple curve, otherwise there would be a shorter geodesic. On a projective plane, any non-contractible closed curve cuts the surface into a disk, so we can use
\displaystyle c
as the analogue of the filling family we used for
\displaystyle k
-saturating systems of curves. We consider the system of arcs induced by
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
on the disk
\displaystyle D:=S\setminus c
, and this system of arcs must be in minimal position. Indeed, the techniques of Schrijver
[
40
, Proof of Main Lemma]
do apply in the setting of a surface with boundary: if the system
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
was not in minimal position, there would be a monogon or a bigon in the disk
\displaystyle D
(see for example Hass and Scott [Proof of Lemma 1.6]
[
26
]
), and this would allow for a smoothing that does not change the length of boundary to boundary paths in
\displaystyle D
. Since in the projective plane, any pair of non-contractible curves intersect, this smoothing would not change the length of geodesics, contradicting the
\displaystyle 2k
-irreducibility of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
.

There remains to bound the size of a system of arcs in minimal position in a disk. We could use Lemma
5.1
as before, but in this case there is a stronger and more direct bound. Here, the perimeter of the disk is
\displaystyle 4k
since the length of
\displaystyle c
is
\displaystyle 2k
and gets doubled when cutting because
\displaystyle c
is one-sided, and thus there are
\displaystyle 2k
simple arcs, crossing pairwise at most once. Actually, each pair of arcs has to cross exactly once, otherwise there would be a path of length less than
\displaystyle 2k
joining two antipodal points on the boundary of
\displaystyle D
and thus the systole would be less than
\displaystyle 2k
, contradicting
\displaystyle 2k
-irreducibility. Such an arrangement of arcs is called an arrangement of pseudolines in the disk. This directly implies that there are exactly
\displaystyle\binom{2k}{2}=k(2k-1)
crossings of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
in
\displaystyle D
and thus on
\displaystyle S
.

In order to conclude, we proceed as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section
3
. In order to do that, we claim that that there are no monogons or bigons in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
: if there were such a monogon or bigon, after cutting it by
\displaystyle c
, we would observe in
\displaystyle D
either a monogon, a bigon, two non-crossing parallel arcs, a bigon between an arc and
\displaystyle c
or a pair of arcs crossing twice in
\displaystyle D
(because
\displaystyle k>1
). All of these cases are incompatible with the fact that the arcs form an arrangement of pseudolines. We obtain that
\displaystyle|V(T)|\leqslant\frac{2}{3}\operatorname{cr}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{2k(2k-1)}{3}\leqslant\frac{4}{3}k^{2}
, and thus
\displaystyle|E(T)|\leqslant 4k^{2}
.
∎

In the case of the projective plane, this proof technique is very efficient and yields a tight bound on the number of crossings in a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible system of curves. However, there is an inherent loss when converting this bound in the setting of triangulations, and we obtain an upper bound of
\displaystyle 2k(2k-1)/3
on the number of vertices of a
\displaystyle k
-irreducible triangulation. This should be compared to a construction of Melzer
[
37
, Section 6.2.1]
with
\displaystyle k(k-1)+1
vertices, which he conjectured to be optimal. Closing this gap remains an interesting open problem.
