Distributed Approximate Maximum Matching and Minimum Vertex Cover via Generalized Graph Decomposition
Peter Davies-Peck

Abstract
The classic lower bound of Kuhn, Moscibroda and Wattenhofer [JACM 2016] states that approximate maximum matching and approximate vertex cover (among other problems) in the LOCAL model require Ω ⁡ ( min ⁡ { log ⁡ n log ⁡ log ⁡ n , log ⁡ Δ log ⁡ log ⁡ Δ } ) \Omega(\min\{\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}},\frac{\log\Delta}{\log\log\Delta}\}) rounds, for any polylogarithmic or smaller approximation ratio. As a function of Δ \Delta , this complexity was subsequently matched for constant-approximate weighted vertex cover [Bar-Yehuda, Censor-Hillel and Schwartzman, JACM 2017] and constant-approximate maximum matching [Bar-Yehuda, Censor-Hillel, Ghaffari and Schwartzman, PODC 2017]. One might expect, therefore, that the true complexity should be Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ Δ log ⁡ log ⁡ Δ ) \Theta(\frac{\log\Delta}{\log\log\Delta}) , and the n n -dependent term in the lower bound is just an artefact of the proof method. We show that this is not the case, and a term dependent on n n is in fact required. Specifically, we show randomized algorithms for 2 + ε 2+\varepsilon -approximate maximum matching and approximate (weighted) minimum vertex cover taking O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n log 2 ⁡ log ⁡ n ) O(\frac{\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}) rounds. Our algorithms are based on a novel graph decomposition result generalizing the method of Miller, Peng and Xu [SPAA 2013], which we use to reduce the ‘effective’ degree of high-degree graphs. We expect that this decomposition may be of further use for other problems.



1
Introduction

We study the central problems of approximate maximum matching and approximate vertex cover in the classic
LOCAL
model of distributed computing. The
LOCAL
model is a graph-based, message passing model, which proceeds in synchronous rounds. The
n
nodes of the graph are the processors, and to begin with they do not have any knowledge about the input graph (other than, generally, the value of
n
, or at least a polynomial upper bound). If the problem involves input (for example node or edge weights), then nodes only know their own local part of the input (i.e. their own weight and/or the weights of their incident edges). In each round, nodes can perform any arbitrary computation, and send messages of arbitrary size, but only to their neighbors in the input graph - that is, the communication graph is the same as the input graph. The goal is to allow, in as few rounds as possible, all nodes to output their own local part of the required output, so that overall the output is consistent and correct.

The problem of approximate maximum matching is to output a matching (a set of non-adjacent edges) of size within a certain approximation factor of the maximum possible. The problem of approximate minimum vertex cover is to output a set of nodes that are adjacent to all edges, where the set is within the approximation factor of the minimum possible size. In the weighted version, nodes are given non-negative weights as input, and it is the sum of weights of included nodes that should be minimized rather than the number of included nodes.

Maximum matching and minimum vertex cover are highly related problems, with a linear programming formulation in which they are dual programs. The exact problems are NP-complete (minimum vertex cover was one of Karp’s 21 NP-complete problems
[
15
]
), and can easily be shown to require
\Omega(n)
rounds in
LOCAL
. Distributed algorithms therefore focus on approximations. Of particular interest is the problem of maximal matching, often considered one of the most central problems in the model. A maximal matching is a
2
-approximate maximum matching, and the vertex set given by taking all endpoints of matched edges is a
2
-approximate minimum vertex cover.

On the lower bound side, the classic lower bound of Kuhn, Moscibroda, and Wattenhofer

The reason for the

for all

\Theta(\frac{\log\Delta}{\log\log\Delta})

These algorithms are based on a novel method of graph decomposition that generalizes the well-known procedure of Miller, Peng and Xu

1

1

.

Here, we show a new analysis of the procedure that allows the use of any distribution for the shift variables, rather than requiring the memorylessness property of the exponential distribution. We then use a particular distribution (with a
polynomially

Lemma 3
.

For any constant
\ell\geq 4
, and for
\alpha=\frac{\ell\log n}{\log\log n}
, there is a randomized

(and
CONGEST
) algorithm, running in
\log^{3/\ell}n

•

All clusters

, and so diameter at most
2\log^{3/\ell}n
.

•

For any

.

Most prior applications of the clustering of
[
21
]
use logarithmic or higher diameter; here we focus on a clustering of sublogarithmic diameter and show that this can still provide useful properties for distributed graph algorithms. The intuition behind the use of this clustering is that some algorithms can be adapted to have some decisions made by clusters rather than nodes. Since clusters need to aggregate information to their centers in order to perform the computation necessary to make these decisions, the algorithm is slowed down by a factor of
O(q)
, where
q
is the radius of the cluster. However, the number of clusters of radius
q
adjacent to any node (which one can think of as the effective degree) is only
2^{O(\frac{\alpha}{q})}
. So, if an algorithm with a round complexity that is a function of maximum degree
T(\Delta)
can be adapted in this way, it would require
O(qT(2^{O(\frac{\alpha}{q})}))
rounds. We adapt algorithms with
T(\Delta)=O(\frac{\log\Delta}{\log\log\Delta})
complexities, and so we end up with complexities of

O(\frac{q\log 2^{O(\frac{\alpha}{q})}}{\log\log 2^{O(\frac{\alpha}{q})}})=O(\frac{\alpha}{\log\frac{\alpha}{q}})=O(\frac{\log n}{\log^{2}\log n})\kern 5.0pt.

However, the adaptations required to the algorithms are not straightforward - significant changes are required to the previous algorithms for both problems in order to preserve critical functionality of the algorithms while passing some of the decisions to clusters. One major issue is that the process described above happens in parallel for all values of
q
, from
0
to
\log^{3/\ell}n
, which adds a level of ‘asynchrony’ that must be accounted for in the algorithm.

Distributed Approximate Maximum Matching

In this section we give our approximate maximum matching algorithm. This algorithm is derived by adapting the following simple framework (Algorithm
1
): a fractional matching is maintained by setting all edge weights initially to
\frac{1}{2\Delta}
, and iteratively raising them by a multiplicative factor of
K

\frac{1}{2K}

\frac{1}{2}

Also, in every two rounds, edges attempt to join the matching with probability equal to their weight, and are successful if none of their neighbors also attempt to join in the same round (this takes two rounds per iteration because it takes a communication round for nodes to determine if they successfully joined, and a second round to inform their neighbors).

e=\{u,v\}
:

Set edge weight

.
Repeat every two rounds:

•

If
\sum_{e^{\prime}\ni u}w(e^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2K}
and
\sum_{e^{\prime}\ni v}w(e^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2K}

w(e)\leftarrow Kw(e)
.

•

Self-nominate with probability
w(e)
.

•

If
e
self-nominates and no neighbouring edges do,

e
joins
M
, edge
e
and all neighboring edges become

inactive (set
w(e)=0

\sum_{\{v,x\}\in C^{\prime}}w^{i}(\{v,x\})=\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}
;

•

\sum_{\{v,x\}\in C^{\prime}}w^{j}(\{v,x\})=\kappa^{j}_{v,C^{\prime}}
; and

•

for all

\sum_{\{v,x\}\in C^{\prime}}w^{i^{\prime}}(\{v,x\})<\kappa^{i^{\prime}}_{v,C^{\prime}}
.

Then, observe how

changes between rounds

and

\kappa^{i^{\prime}}_{v,C^{\prime}}\leq\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}K^{2}
for all

may increase by a factor of

in round

but will henceforth not increase again until round

. We also have that every
2r_{C^{\prime}}\leq 2\log^{0.3}n

will decrease by a

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[i,j-1]\cap S}\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}K^{2-t}\leq 2K^{2}\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}=2K^{2}\sum_{\{v,x\}\in C^{\prime}}w^{i}(\{v,x\})\kern 5.0pt.

Summing over all such intervals

, we get:

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in S}\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}\leq 2K^{2}\sum_{\{v,x\}\in C^{\prime}}\sum_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}w^{i}(\{v,x\})\kern 5.0pt.

So,

\displaystyle\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}\sum_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}w^{i}(\{v,x\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{clusters $C^{\prime}$}}\sum_{\{v,x\}\in C^{\prime}}\sum_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}w^{i}(\{v,x\})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}K^{-2}\sum_{\text{clusters $C^{\prime}$}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in S}\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}\kern 5.0pt.

Summing over both endpoints of edge
\{u,v\}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}\left(\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w^{i}(\{v,x\})+\sum_{\{u,x\}\in E(u)}w^{i}(\{u,x\})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}K^{-2}\sum_{\text{clusters $C^{\prime}$}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in S}\kappa^{i}_{v,C^{\prime}}+\kappa^{i}_{u,C^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\ell^{2}\log^{1-6/\ell}n}{144K^{5}\log^{2}\log n}\kern 5.0pt.

Without loss of generality, assume that

is the endpoint of edge
\{u,v\}
with higher total weight in round

, i.e.

\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w^{i}(\{v,x\})\geq\sum_{\{u,x\}\in E(u)}w^{i}(\{u,x\})\kern 5.0pt.

Then, the probability that edge

is at least

\displaystyle\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\text{$\{v,x\}$ self-nominates and no neighbbouring edge does}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\geq\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w(\{v,x\})\cdot\prod_{\{v,y\}\in E(v)}\left(1-w(\{v,y\})\right)\cdot\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E(x)}\left(1-w(\{x,y\})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\geq\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w(\{v,x\})\cdot 4^{-\prod_{\{v,y\}\in E(v)}w(\{v,y\})}\cdot 4^{-\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E(x)}w(\{x,y\})}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\geq\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w(\{v,x\})\cdot 4^{-\frac{1}{4}}\cdot 4^{-\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w(\{v,x\})

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w^{i}(\{v,x\})+\sum_{\{u,x\}\in E(u)}w^{i}(\{u,x\})\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Here we used the inequality

for

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\left(\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w^{i}(\{v,x\})+\sum_{\{u,x\}\in E(u)}w^{i}(\{u,x\})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq\prod_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\left(\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w^{i}(\{v,x\})+\sum_{\{u,x\}\in E(u)}w^{i}(\{u,x\})\right)}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\in[\frac{\ell^{2}\log n}{\log^{2}\log n}]}\left(\sum_{\{v,x\}\in E(v)}w^{i}(\{v,x\})+\sum_{\{u,x\}\in E(u)}w^{i}(\{u,x\})\right)}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq e^{-\frac{\ell^{2}\log^{1-6/\ell}n}{576K^{5}\log^{2}\log n}}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq e^{-\frac{\ell^{2}\log^{1-501/\ell}n}{576\log^{2}\log n}}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq e^{-\Omega(\log^{1-\delta}n)}\kern 5.0pt.

letting

.

2.2
Discussion of success probability

We note that, while this probability of success in Theorem

3.2
Algorithm Overview

Our algorithm proceeds in
1+O(\frac{\log 1/\varepsilon}{\log\log n})

stages
, comparable to the levels in
[
6
]
(though in the algorithm of
[
6
]
the levels are handled concurrently, whereas here we handle them sequentially). The goal in each stage is to reduce all non-terminated nodes’ weight by a factor of
\log^{\Theta(1)}n
.

Throughout the algorithm, nodes can be in one of four states: active, inactive,
InCover
, and
NotInCover
.
InCover
, and
NotInCover
are terminal states, and indicate that the vertex is in and out of the final vertex cover respectively; once nodes reach those states, they will inform their neighbors and cease participation.

Each stage consists of two phases. In phase
1
, all non-terminated nodes start as active, and some will become inactive during the phase. No nodes terminate during phase
1
. Active nodes
u
send requests asking to increase weight on their adjacent edges (and therefore correspondingly reduce their own weight
w(u)
). Requests are sent by individual nodes, but are answered by the recipient’s cluster. Because requests are answered with a delay (based on the diameter of the recipient’s cluster), weight sent in requests is “locked up” until the request is answered, and therefore nodes do not know exactly how much weight they have available. Because of this, when clusters fulfil requests, they must leave sufficient weight aside to cover all requests that
v
might send before the cluster next receives updated weights. Nodes become inactive once their weight reduces by a
\log^{0.1}n
factor, compared to their weight at the start of the phase. The goal of phase
1
is to ensure that there are no edges between active nodes, i.e. at least one endpoint of every edge becomes inactive.

In phase
2
, active nodes again send requests. Since there are no edges between active nodes, requests are only received by inactive nodes. Again, requests are answered by clusters, but since they are answered only on behalf of inactive nodes, no weight needs to be held back to account for weight locked up in sent requests. So, the cluster can use the entire remaining weight of nodes to answer requests, and knows the true current value of
w(v)
for all inactive nodes
v
it contains (since such weight values are only updated by the cluster itself).

During phase
2
, no nodes become inactive, but nodes
v
can terminate in one of two ways: if
v
’s weight reaches the target value of
\varepsilon^{\prime}w_{0}(v)
, then
v
can join the vertex cover. If
v
has no non-terminated neighbors, it outputs
NotInCover
. Note that in this case, all of
v
’s neighbors must have already output
InCover
, since they could not have output
NotInCover
while
v
was still non-terminated.

The goal of phase
2
is to ensure than no active nodes remain, i.e. all active nodes will terminate. This means that at the end of a stage, the only non-terminated nodes are inactive, and therefore their weights have decreased by at least a
\log^{0.1}n
factor since the start of the stage. Inactive nodes are set to be active again for the start of the next stage.

Since each stage reduces weights by a
\log^{0.1}n
factor, after
1+\log_{\log^{0.1}n}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=1+O(\frac{\log 1/\varepsilon}{\log\log n})
stages all remaining nodes
v
must have weight below
\varepsilon^{\prime}w_{0}(v)
, and so must terminate.

Graph Decomposition

In this section we show the graph decomposition result that forms the backbone of our algorithms. Our graph decomposition is a generalization of a well-known randomized procedure of Miller, Peng, and Xu
[
21
]
(see Algorithm
6
): nodes each independently generate ‘shift’ variables, and then join the cluster of the node with the lowest ‘shifted’ distance value. This procedure can be show to produce low-diameter clusters with few inter-cluster edges, and it is straightforward to implement in

and
CONGEST
(with round complexity equal to the largest possible cluster radius). The clustering produced by the procedure is highly related to other forms of network decomposition, such as the
(C,D)
-network decompositions that were introduced by Awerbuch, Luby, Goldberg and Plotkin
[
1
]
and are now one of the most fundamental tools in distibuted graph algorithms (see e.g.
[
23
]
). Indeed, Algorithm

can be adapted to give an
(O(\log n),O(\log n))
-network decomposition in
O(\log n)
rounds of
LOCAL
with high probability

v

\delta_{u}-dist(u,v)
value and the next highest. One can show that with high probability these gaps will all be at at least
2

(O(\log n),O(\log n))

Here, we show how to analyze Algorithm

under an arbitrary distribution
F
, i.e. without requiring the memorylessness property. We focus on the case where clusters have low (sublogarithmic) diameter, and the number of clusters adjacent to each node is relatively high, but less than polynomial in
n
.

In this work, our shift variable distributions will be of the following form:

Definition 1
.

[Shift variable distributions]
Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a cumulative distribution function, with the following properties:

•

F
is non-decreasing and absolutely continuous.

•

F(0)=0
.

•

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}F(x)=1
.

We define also
G:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow[0,1]
as
G(x)=1-F(x)
; when defining specific functions to use, it is often more convenient to do so by defining
G
. Let the probability density function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the derivative of
F
.

When applying Algorithm
6
with some shift variable distribution
F
, for some fixed node
v
, let
d_{w}
denote
dist(w,v)
. Then, define

C_{q}^{\bm{d}}:=|\{w\neq v:\delta_{w}\geq\max\{\delta_{CC(v)}-d_{CC(v)}-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}\}|\kern 5.0pt.

That is,
C_{q}^{\bm{d}}
is the number of nodes
w
for which
\delta_{w}-d_{w}
is within
\bm{d}
of the ‘winning’ value of
v
’s cluster center, and
\delta_{w}
is at least
q
. While we allow any
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{N}
for generality, in our applications we are particularly concerned with
C_{q}^{2}
. Notice that
C_{q}^{2}
is an upper bound on the number of clusters of radius at least
q
adjacent to
v
(other than
v
’s own cluster). (Here ‘radius’ means the maximum distance from that cluster’s center to any of its members.) This is because, since
F
has domain
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the radius of any cluster
C
is at most the shift variable generated by
C
’s cluster center (since otherwise the furthest member from the center would have been its own cluster in preference to joining
C
). Also, for a cluster centered at
w
to be adjacent to
v
(but not contain it), we must have
\delta_{CC(v)}-d_{CC(v)}-2\leq\delta_{w}-d_{w}<\delta_{CC(v)}-d_{CC(v)}
(where
CC(v)
denotes the cluster center of
v
’s cluster).

The remainder of this section will be dedicated to proving bounds on
C_{q,\bm{d}}
based on
F
. Since we will want a high-probability bound on this quantity, rather than bounding
\mathbf{E}\left[C_{q,\bm{d}}\right]
, we will instead bound
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]
for some
\gamma(q)
.

Theorem 15
.

Consider running Algorithm
6
with some shift variable distribution
F
satisfying Definition
1
. Let
\pi^{\bm{d}}(q):=\sup_{x\geq q}\left(\frac{G(x)}{G(x+2)}-1\right)
, and let
\gamma(q)
be such that
e^{\gamma}(q)-1\leq\frac{F(1)}{2\pi^{\bm{d}}(q)}
. Then, for any node
v
,
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]\leq 2e
.

Proof.

We begin with the following expression for
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]
, using the law of total probabilities over the possible cluster centers for
v
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V}\mathbf{Pr}\left[u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{u\in V}\mathbf{Pr}\left[u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}|\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]f(p+d_{u})dp\kern 5.0pt.

We bound the two main terms separately. First:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}|\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{w\in V\setminus\{u\}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\delta_{w}-d_{w}<p\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{F(p+d_{u})}\prod_{w\in V}F(p+d_{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{F(p+d_{u})}\prod_{w\in V}\left(1-G(p+d_{w})\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Using the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for all
x\geq 0
, and separating out the term incurred by
v
itself, with
d_{v}=0
,

\displaystyle\prod_{w\in V}\left(1-G(p+d_{w})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-G(p)}{e^{-G(p)}}\cdot\prod_{w\in V}e^{-G(p+d_{w})}

\displaystyle=F(p){e^{G(p)}}\cdot e^{-\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}

\displaystyle\leq eF(p)\cdot e^{-\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}\kern 5.0pt.

Using this bound,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}|\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{2}F(p)}{F(p+d_{u})}\cdot e^{-\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{1-\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}\kern 5.0pt.

Bounding the
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]
term, we note that
v
cannot be adjacent to a cluster centered at itself, since if there is such a cluster,
v
will be in it (and
C_{q,d}
does not count
v
’s own cluster). That is why, in the following bound, the sum is over
V\setminus\{u,v\}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,d}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt=\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{w}\geq\max\{\delta_{u}-d_{u}-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt=\prod_{w\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}vert\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\}}}\right],

where the last equality holds since the indicator variables for each
w
are independent. For a Bernoulli variable
X
that occurs with probability
\mathbf{p}
, we use the following standard bound (often used in proof of the Chernoff bound):

\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)X}\right]=(1-\mathbf{p})e^{0}+\mathbf{p}e^{\gamma(q)}=1+\mathbf{p}(e^{\gamma(q)}-1)\leq e^{\mathbf{p}(e^{\gamma(q)}-1)}\kern 5.0pt.

Plugging this in for
X=\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt=\prod_{w\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}e^{\mathbf{Pr}\left[\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\right](e^{\gamma(q)}-1)}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq e^{(e^{\gamma(q)}-1)\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{v\}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\right]}\kern 5.0pt.

For any
w\neq v
, we can bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F(p+d_{w})-F(\max\{q,p-\bm{d}+d_{w}\})}{F(p+d_{w})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{F(1)}\cdot\left(G(\max\{q,p-\bm{d}+d_{w}\})-G(p+d_{w})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{G(p+d_{w})}{F(1)}\cdot\left(\frac{G(\max\{q,p-\bm{d}+d_{w}\})}{G(p+d_{w})}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{G(p+d_{w})}{F(1)}\cdot\sup_{x\geq q}\left(\frac{G(x)}{G(x+2)}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi^{\bm{d}}(q)G(p+d_{w})}{F(1)}\kern 5.0pt.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{(e^{\gamma(q)}-1)\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{v\}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\delta_{w}\geq\max\{p-\bm{d}+d_{w},q\}|\delta_{w}\leq p+d_{w}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\pi^{\bm{d}}(q)(e^{\gamma(q)}-1)}{F(1)}\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{v\}}G(p+d_{w})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}\kern 5.0pt.

Putting the bounds together,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{u\in V}\mathbf{Pr}\left[u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}|\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}|u\text{ is $v$'s cluster center}\land\delta_{u}-d_{u}=p\right]f(p+d_{u})dp

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{u\in V}e^{1-\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}f(p+d_{u})dp

\displaystyle=e\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})}\cdot\sum_{u\in V}f(p+d_{u})dp\kern 5.0pt.

We now evaluate the integral by substitution, setting our new function
H(p):=-\sum_{w\in V}G(p+d_{w})
. Notice that the derivative
h
of
H
is
\sum_{w\in V}f(p+d_{w})
. So, we can rephrase the above inequality as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{1}{2}H(p)}\cdot h(p)dp\kern 5.0pt.

Integration by substitution then gives:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,\bm{d}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e\int_{H(0)}^{H(\infty)}e^{\frac{z}{2}}dz

\displaystyle=e\left[2e^{\frac{z}{2}}\right]_{H(0)}^{0}

\displaystyle\leq 2e\kern 5.0pt.

∎

For our applications, we will use
\textsc{Partition}(F)

Proof of Lemma
3
.

The radius of any cluster is at most the shift variable of its cluster center. The probability that any particular shift variable
\delta_{v}
exceeds
\log^{3/\ell}n
is at most
G(\log^{3/\ell}n)\leq\log^{\frac{-3\alpha}{\ell}}n=n^{-3}
. Therefore, by a union bound over all nodes

\log^{3/\ell}n
and all clusters are of radius at most
\log^{3/\ell}n
. The algorithm therefore only needs to run for
\log^{3/\ell}n
rounds before all nodes know the identity of their cluster center with high probability. This proves the first part of the lemma statement.

For the second part, we fix any node
v
and bound
C_{q,2}
which as discussed is an upper bound on the number of clusters of radius at least
q
adjacent to
v
, excluding
v
’s own cluster.
C_{q,2}+1
is therefore an upper bound on the quantity including
v
’s cluster. Note that we now need only consider
q\leq\log^{3/\ell}n
(since for higher

, with high probability
C_{q,2}
= 0).

We will apply Theorem
15
, but we must first bound
\pi^{2}(q)
and choose a suitable
\gamma(q)
. We have

\displaystyle\pi^{2}(q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\geq q}\frac{G(x)}{G(x+2)}-1

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\geq q}\left(\frac{1+x}{3+x}\right)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\geq q}\left(1+\frac{2}{1+x}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\geq q}e^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+x}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+q}}.

Since
q\leq\log^{3/\ell}n<\frac{\alpha}{9}
,
e^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+q}}\geq e^{17}
.

To apply Theorem
15
, we require
\gamma(q)
such that
e^{\gamma(q)}-1\leq\frac{F(1)}{2\pi^{2}(q)}
. We choose
\gamma(q)=\frac{1}{6}e^{\frac{-2\alpha}{1+q}}
. Then, since
\gamma(q)<1
,

\displaystyle e^{\gamma(q)}-1\leq 2\gamma(q)=\frac{1}{3}e^{\frac{-2\alpha}{1+q}}\leq\frac{1-2^{-\alpha}}{2e^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+q}}-1}\leq\frac{F(1)}{2\pi^{2}(q)}\kern 5.0pt.

So, applying Theorem
15
,
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,2}}\right]\leq 2e
.

By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,2}}\geq 5n^{3}\right]\leq\frac{2e}{5n^{3}}\leq n^{-3}\kern 5.0pt.

So, with probability at least
1-n^{-3}
, we have
e^{\gamma(q)C_{q,2}}<5n^{3}
, and therefore

\displaystyle C_{q,2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ln(5n^{3})}{\gamma(q)}

\displaystyle<6\ln(5n^{3})e^{\frac{2\alpha}{q+1}}

\displaystyle<e^{\frac{3\alpha}{q+1}}-1

(since
q\leq\log^{3/\ell}n
).

By a union bound, this occurs for all nodes

with probability at least

. Then, all nodes have at most

adjacent clusters of radius at least

∎

5
Conclusions and Open Problems

In this work, we showed a new graph decomposition result that allowed us to improve the complexities of approximate maximum matching and minimum weighted vertex cover as a function of
n
. This opens up new possibilities for improved complexities on high degree graphs. Our
\log\log n
-factor improvement demonstrates that the
O(\frac{\log\Delta}{\log\log\Delta})
upper bounds for these problems are not tight for high
\Delta
, but there is still a large gap between upper and lower bounds; one open question is how close one can get to the
\Omega(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}})
lower bound of Kuhn, Moscibroda and Wattenhofer
[
17
]
.

Another major question is whether our techniques here can be extended to more difficult problems. For example, can one similarly use a decomposition to make
\log\log n
-factor improvements for:

•

Approximate maximum independent set,
1+\varepsilon
-approximate maximum matching, and
2+\varepsilon
-approximate
weighted
maximum matching, following
[
3
]
?

•

Maximal matching and maximal independent set?

Any
o(\log n)
-round algorithm for the latter problems would be a very interesting result, since they are often considered the most central problems in the
LOCAL
