IV-ICL: Bounding Causal Effects with Instrumental Variables via In-Context Learning
Vahid Balazadeh Hamidreza Kamkari Medha Barath, Ricardo Silva Rahul G. Krishnan

Abstract
The instrumental-variables (IV) setting is standard for partial identification of causal effects when unobserved confounding makes point identification impossible. Existing approaches face methodological bottlenecks: closed-form bound estimands are required—e.g., Balke-Pearl equations in binary IV—and even when available, designing accurate estimators requires manual effort tailored to each estimand. While direct Bayesian inference of the causal effects, instead of the bounds, circumvents these challenges, it is often computationally intensive and suffers from high prior sensitivity or under-dispersed posteriors. As a remedy, we introduce IV-ICL, an amortized Bayesian in-context learning method that learns the marginal posterior distribution of the causal effects directly and derives bounds as its quantiles. Unlike standard variational inference that optimizes exclusive KL divergence, amortized Bayesian inference minimizes the expected inclusive KL, a mass-covering objective. We empirically observe that optimizing inclusive KL can recover the entire identified set across diverse data-generating processes, while exclusive-KL (e.g. with variational inference) of the same Bayesian formulation collapses onto a single mode and fails to cover the identified set. We evaluate IV-ICL on synthetic and semi-synthetic IV benchmarks and show it produces intervals that are more reliably valid and more informative compared to efficient semi-parametric, Bayesian, and plug-in baselines, at 20 20 – 500 × 500\times lower inference time. Beyond methodology, we propose a procedure to convert randomized controlled trials into IV benchmarks with provably preserved ground-truth causal effects that enables a more realistic evaluation of partial-identification methods.

Amortized Bayesian inference.
Recent work suggests that one way to cut the cost of Bayesian inference is amortized learning with in-context models: pre-train a single transformer on a wide library of synthetic DGPs, and perform inference on new datasets in a single forward pass. Prior-data Fitted Networks (PFNs) are a successful instantiation of this idea

PFNs meet partial identification.

On such a prior, the trained posterior avoids the under-dispersion that breaks exclusive KL variational inference, so its quantiles are usable as bounds. Building on these observations, we introduce IV-ICL, a Bayesian method for partial identification that resolves

Bayesian inference has been used as a framework for partial identification

Covariate-Adjusted Bounds.

Incorporating covariates can sharpen IV bounds by exploiting conditional independence structures

General partial identification problems can be formulated as constrained optimization over response functions. For instance,

The computational complexity of such methods, however, grows rapidly with the number of variable categories. A parallel line of work optimizes over distributions of response functions directly using deep generative or neural-network parameterizations

Notation and Assumptions.
We adopt the potential outcome framework
[
54
]
. Assume we observe a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of
N
i.i.d. samples from random variables
\mathbf{O}=\left(\mathbf{X},Z,T,Y\right)
, where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the vector of observed covariates,
Z\in\{0,1\}
is the binary instrument,
T\in\{0,1\}
denotes the binary treatment, and
Y\in[0,1]
is the bounded outcome (binary or continuous). For
z,t\in\{0,1\}
, we let
T(z)
be the potential treatment under intervention
Z\coloneqq z
. We further denote the potential outcome under intervention
T\coloneqq t
by
Y(t)
. Finally, we define
Y(z,t)
as the potential outcome under intervention
Z\coloneqq z,T\coloneqq t
.

We focus on the binary setting as most mature methods and evaluation benchmarks exist only for this setting.
Our goal is to estimate the sample average treatment effect (SATE):

\lambda(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\E\left[Y(t=1)-Y(t=0)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_{i}\right].

(1)

We generally cannot rule out the existence of unobserved variables that can confound the causal effect of the treatment on the outcome. In such settings, the SATE is not point-identified, i.e., it cannot be uniquely determined from the data alone, as multiple causal effects are compatible with the same observed distribution
[
52
,
47
]
. We thus turn to partial identification: estimating an informative set interval
[\ell,u]
that contains the true SATE. We specifically focus on the standard IV setting, which can provably lead to nontrivial values of
\ell
and
u

[
8
,

,

]

Assumption 1

(Standard Instrumental Variable)
.

(i)

Consistency

Y=Y(z,t)
for all

, and

.

(iv)

Bounding SATE via Bayesian Inference

Consider a parameterized family of joint distributions
\{P_{\psi}\}_{\psi\in\Psi}
over observed variables and all the potential treatments and outcomes. We specifically consider the following factorization:

\displaystyle P_{\psi}\big(\mathbf{X},\ Z,\ \{T(z)\}_{z\in\{0,1\}},\ \{Y(t)\}_{t\in\{0,1\}},\ T,\ Y\big)

(2)

\displaystyle=P_{\psi}\big(\mathbf{X}\big)\cdot P_{\psi}\left(Z\mid\mathbf{X}\right)\cdot P_{\psi}\big(\{T(z)\}_{z\in\{0,1\}},\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\{0,1\}}\mid\mathbf{X}\big)\cdot\mathbb{I}\big\{T=T(Z),Y=Y(T)\big\}.

This factorization is specifically designed to satisfy
1
. The term
\mathbb{I}\big\{T\!=\!T(Z),Y\!=\!Y(T)\big\}
enforces consistency.
Z
is conditionally independent of potential outcomes given
\mathbf{X}
, and the potential outcome only depends on the intervention
T\coloneq t
.

Now, consider a prior
\pi
on
\Psi
. Given an observational dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we can compute a posterior belief on the parameters using Bayes’ rule,
\pi\left(\psi\mid\mathcal{D}\right)\propto\pi(\psi)\times P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
,
where
P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
is the marginal distribution of
P_{\psi}
on the observed random variables.

Marginal Posterior of the SATE.
For each parameter
\psi
and dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the joint distribution
P_{\psi}
in
Equation

2
pins down a SATE, i.e.,
\lambda(\psi;\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}\E_{P_{\psi}}[Y(t=1)-Y(t=0)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_{i}]
. We then define the marginal posterior of SATE as

\pi_{\text{sate}}\left(B\mid\mathcal{D}\right)\coloneqq\int\mathbb{I}\left\{\lambda(\psi;\mathcal{D})\in B\right\}\ \pi\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\psi\mid\mathcal{D}\right),

(3)

where
B
is an arbitrary Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Under a sufficiently broad prior,
\pi_{\text{sate}}
assigns non-negligible mass across the entire identified set, so its quantiles serve as natural estimators of the lower and upper bounds. Specifically, for a chosen
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define

\underline{\lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq Q_{\alpha/2}\!\big(\pi_{\text{sate}}\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}\right)\big),\;\overline{\lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq Q_{1-\alpha/2}\!\big(\pi_{\text{sate}}\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}\right)\big),

(4)

where
Q_{\beta}(\mu)
denotes the
\beta
-quantile of a distribution
\mu
. We return the interval
\left[\underline{\lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{D}),\;\overline{\lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{D})\right]
as our final estimator of the SATE bound. This formulation does
not
require a closed-form estimand for the lower or upper bound, resolving
L1
.

Why Standard Bayesian Inference Fails.
Classical Bayesian inference of
\pi_{\text{sate}}
via MCMC or variational inference can suffer from three issues along the unidentifiable parts of
\Psi
, where the likelihood
P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
is flat
[
57
]
. First, MCMC mixes poorly along these directions, and variational inference—which minimizes the mode-seeking exclusive KL—under-disperses, collapsing onto a single mode of the identified set (
L3
). Second, the posterior shape along these directions is dictated entirely by the prior even with infinite data, producing prior sensitivity (
L4
)
[
57
,
27
]
. Third, inference must be re-run per dataset, which is computationally prohibitive at scale (
L5
).
Section

5
addresses
L3
–
L5
via amortized in-context learning with an inclusive KL objective.

5
Implementing the Bayesian Approach via Amortized In-Context Learning

To implement the Bayesian framework described above, we must specify:
(i)
a computational method for approximating the marginal posterior
\pi_{\text{sate}}
, and
(ii)
the parameter space
\Psi
and prior
\pi
. We address the former first via amortized inference, then describe our prior construction in
Section

5.1
.

Approximating the Posterior via Amortized In-Context Learning.

Rather than computing the posterior for each dataset, we employ amortized inference and train a single model that directly approximates the marginal posterior of the SATE for any input dataset. Specifically, we seek a model
q_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}\right)\approx\pi_{\text{sate}}\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}\right)
, where
\theta
are the model parameters. We learn
\theta
such that this approximation holds across diverse datasets. Specifically, we optimize the following loss

\mathcal{L}(\theta)\coloneqq\E_{\psi\sim\pi,\,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}}\left[-\log q_{\theta}\left(\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right].

(5)

At each evaluation, we first sample a parameter
\psi\sim\pi
, then sample an observational dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
of i.i.d. samples from
P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
. Since
\psi
is known, the ground-truth SATE
\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
is computable directly from the data-generating process without any closed-form bound estimand. The loss is the negative log-likelihood under
q_{\theta}
of the ground-truth SATE values for datasets sampled from the prior.

Equivalence to Inclusive KL.

A key property of the loss in
Equation

5
is that its optimization is equivalent to minimizing the expected inclusive KL between the true marginal posterior and our model:

\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;\arg\min_{\theta}\,\E\left[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\text{sate}}\left(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\ \middle\|\ q_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right)\right],

(6)

where the outer expectation is over datasets drawn from the prior. Similar derivations have been shown in prior work
[
44
,
45
]
; for completeness, we provide ours in
Appendix

B
.

This equivalence is the key technical reason IV-ICL resolves
L3
. The inclusive KL is
mass-covering
: minimizing it forces
q_{\theta}
to assign positive density wherever
\pi_{\text{sate}}
has mass, which prevents the under-dispersed posteriors that arise when minimizing the mode-seeking exclusive KL via standard variational inference. In partial identification, where
\pi_{\text{sate}}
is intentionally diffuse over the identified set, this property is essential for valid bound estimation.

Architecture and Inference.

We follow a similar architecture to standard PFNs
[
24
,
7
]
and model
q_{\theta}
as a transformer encoder taking the entire observational dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
as its context. We embed each row
\mathbf{o}^{(i)}\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
into a single token with a fixed dimension via a learnable embedding layer. The encoder processes the resulting sequence of
|\tilde{\mathcal{D}}|
tokens through a stack of self-attention and MLP layers. To produce a dataset-level representation, we average the output tokens of the encoder. We omit positional encoding to enforce permutation-invariance over the rows. Full training and architectural details, including hyperparameters, appear in
Appendix

C
.

5.1
Prior Data Generation

Optimizing the training loss in
Equation

5
does
not
require an explicit parametrization for
P_{\psi}
. Instead, it only requires a prior that can:
(i)
generate observational datasets
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
consisting of tuples
\left(\mathbf{x},z,t,y\right)
from
P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
while adhering to the IV structure in
Equation

2
, and
(ii)
compute the ground-truth SATE
\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
. Still, not every choice of prior leads to useful posteriors. If the prior is not sufficiently rich and fails to capture a diverse range of DGPs, then even the optimal approximation
q_{\theta^{\star}}\approx\pi_{\text{sate}}
will not generalize to new unseen datasets. Moreover, the prior must distribute mass broadly over the range of possible SATE values: if it concentrates near zero, the trained model will inherit that bias and produce overly narrow posteriors at inference time (
L4
).

To address diversity, we draw inspiration from the success of tabular foundation models
[
25
,
36
,
28
]
and construct a prior that contains a rich set of datasets with sufficient coverage to approximate real-world scenarios. Our setting, however, introduces a constraint absent in standard supervised tabular models: the joint distribution
P_{\psi}
must satisfy the IV constraints stated in
1

P_{\psi}
must admit the factorization in
Equation

2

Z
being unconfounded given covariates and having no direct effect on the outcome beyond its influence through the treatment. Hence, we propose the following prior data generation process, mirroring the factorization in
Equation

2
:

1.
Base table and covariate generation

P_{\psi}\big(\mathbf{X}\big)
: We begin by generating a “base” table with varying numbers of rows and columns, drawn from a large collection of synthetic datasets. We follow the prior data generation scheme used by tabular foundation models in
Hollmann et al. 2025
,
Balazadeh et al. 2025
: features are produced by passing samples through randomly initialized neural networks, ensuring the prior covers a rich set of tabular feature distributions. From the base table, we randomly select columns and denote them as our covariates
\mathbf{X}
.

2.
Instrument propensity

P_{\psi}\left(Z\mid\mathbf{X}\right)
: To guarantee the unconfoundedness of the instrument (
1
), we generate the instrument
Z
as
only
a function of the covariates. We create a random function
f_{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
, using synthetic function generators similar to
Balazadeh et al. 2025
. The instrument propensity is then defined as
P_{\psi}\left(Z=1\mid\mathbf{X}\right)=\mathrm{sigmoid}\left(f_{\psi}(\mathbf{X})\right)
.

3.
Potential outcome and treatment generation

P_{\psi}\big(\{T(z)\}_{z\in\{0,1\}},\ \{Y(t)\}_{t\in\{0,1\}}\mid\mathbf{X}\big)
: The conditional distribution of the potential treatments and outcomes can be characterized by a
16
-dimensional probability vector, where each of the
16
entries corresponds to one of the
|\{0,1\}^{4}|=16
configurations of the binary
T(z=0),T(z=1),Y(t=0)
, and
Y(t=1)
. We denote this probability vector by
\mathbf{p}_{\psi}(\mathbf{X})\in\Delta^{15}
in the probability simplex.

To sample these conditional probability vectors, we select
16
additional columns from the base table to get a raw
{\mathbf{g}^{0}_{\psi}}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{16}
and apply a softmax function to obtain an initial
\mathbf{p}^{0}_{\psi}(\mathbf{X})=\mathrm{softmax}({\mathbf{g}^{0}_{\psi}}(\mathbf{X}))
.
To ensure that the resulting samples cover the entire probability simplex (
L4
), we apply a post-processing step. We first sample a target average vector
\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{\psi}\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\gamma,\ldots,\gamma)
in the simplex. We choose a small concentration parameter (e.g.,
\gamma=0.1
) to get draws that are sparse and spread broadly over the simplex. We then add a single constant vector
\mathbf{c}_{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{16}
to the pre-softmax logits of every individual:
{\mathbf{g}_{\psi}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\leftarrow{\mathbf{g}^{0}_{\psi}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\mathbf{c}_{\psi}
. We specifically choose
\mathbf{c}_{\psi}=\log\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{\psi}-\log(\sum_{i}\mathbf{p}_{\psi}(\mathbf{x}_{i})/N)
,
where the logarithms are componentwise. We then calculate the final probability vector by applying
\mathbf{p}_{\psi}(\mathbf{X})=\mathrm{softmax}({\mathbf{g}_{\psi}}(\mathbf{X}))
again. This log-domain shift translates each per-individual logit toward the target by an amount equal to the log-discrepancy between target and current population mean. The shift is approximate (because
\mathrm{softmax}
does not commute with averaging) but is sufficient to push the population mean substantially toward
\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{\psi}
, and consequently to reshape the prior distribution of
\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
to span a wide range of values across
[-1,1]
.

6
A More Realistic Evaluation: Converting RCTs to IV Benchmarks

Evaluating IV methods is challenging because ground-truth causal effects are rarely known for real datasets. Fully synthetic benchmarks allow exact evaluation but may not reflect realistic data distributions. To bridge this gap, we propose a procedure to convert RCTs into observational IV datasets while preserving the ground-truth causal effects from the experiment.

RCTs provide gold-standard estimates of causal effects through randomization. However, RCT data cannot directly serve as IV benchmarks because there is no instrument-treatment-confounder structure. Our key insight is that we can
synthetically
introduce such structure while using accept-reject sampling to ensure the resulting observational outcomes match the original RCT outcomes. This preserves the ground-truth causal effect while creating a realistic IV estimation problem.

Given an RCT dataset of i.i.d. samples from
(\mathbf{X},T^{\text{rct}},Y)
with
balanced
treatment assignment, we construct an IV dataset as follows.
(i)
Split
\mathbf{X}
into observed covariates
\mathbf{O}
(available to estimators) and hidden confounders
\mathbf{U}
.
(ii)
Sample the instrument
Z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{z}(\mathbf{O}))
, where the propensity
p_{z}
depends only on observed covariates. This ensures the instrument satisfies conditional unconfoundedness.
(iii)
Sample the treatment
T^{\text{synth}}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z))
, where
p_{t}
depends on observed covariates, hidden confounders, and the instrument. Note that
p_{t}
is synthetically designed to introduce confounding through dependence on
\mathbf{U}
; the dependence on
Z
provides instrument relevance.
(iv)
Retain samples if and only if
T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}
. For accepted units, use the original
Y
. The following establishes that this procedure produces valid IV data, proved in
Appendix

D
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose the original RCT has a treatment assignment with
\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{rct}}=1)=0.5
,
p_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
, and
p_{z}(\mathbf{O})\in[\epsilon^{\prime},1-\epsilon^{\prime}]
for some
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. Then the dataset resulting from the above procedure satisfies the following.

(IV Validity).
The data-generating process satisfies
1
.

(Causal Effect Preservation).
The population average treatment effect (PATE) in the accepted population equals the RCT PATE:

\displaystyle\E[Y(1)\!-\!Y(0)\!\mid\!T^{\text{rct}}=T^{\text{synth}}]=\E[Y\!\!\mid\!T^{\text{rct}}\!=\!1]-\E[Y\!\!\mid\!T^{\text{rct}}\!=\!0].

Note that while our target estimand is the SATE,
Proposition

1
establishes preservation of the PATE. However, the two are connected: the SATE is itself a Monte Carlo estimator of the PATE over the covariate distribution, and the RCT PATE (our ground-truth label) is estimated via its own Monte Carlo average over the available RCT samples. Both sources of approximation vanish as sample size grows and remain practically reliable for the RCTs we use.

Why this is a useful benchmark.

Our procedure decouples the components of an IV benchmark by their realism. In particular, the entire outcome distribution is preserved exactly: accepted units retain their original RCT outcomes, so we evaluate against the real, complex outcome-generating mechanism (e.g., job-training effects on earnings) rather than a simulated one. What we synthesize is only the treatment/instrument assignment structures, which are typically simpler than the outcome process. The procedure should be viewed not as a replacement for real IV data, but as a controlled bridge between toy simulators and real IV settings without ground-truth.

Experiments

Baselines.
We choose the following standard baselines for IV.

(i) Plug-in Estimator
: The plug-in estimator from
Section

3
, with TabPFN-v2 as the base classifier; requires the Balke-Pearl equations.

(ii) Nonparametric Efficient Estimators
: The efficient frequentist methods of
Levis et al. 2025
,
Whitehouse et al. 2025
, which requires the Balke-Pearl bounds; we use TabPFN-v2 as the base model.

(iii) Bayes-BP (Bayesian Inference over the Bounds)
: Bayesian inference on the conditional probabilities
p_{yt.z}(\mathbf{x})
under a GP prior; we sample posteriors, evaluate the Balke-Pearl equations on each sample, and average. This baseline isolates epistemic uncertainty in the identifiable
p(Y,T\mid Z,\mathbf{X})

[
50
,
57
]
, but still requires the closed-form bounds and per-dataset optimization.

(iv) Bayes (Pure Bayesian Inference)
: The fully Bayesian approach discussed in
Section

4
that directly infers the posterior on the SATE without using the Balke-Pearl equations. We follow the factorization in
Equation

2
and use a GP prior. This baseline mirrors the formulation IV-ICL amortizes with a different prior, allowing us to isolate the effect of replacing MCMC and variational inference with amortized in-context learning, and the GP prior with the one discussed in
Section

5.1
. For both Bayesian baselines we use sparse variational inference for scalability
[
60
,
18
]
.

Quantile levels.
The Bayes baseline and IV-ICL both return a marginal posterior over the SATE; we convert this posterior into a bound interval by taking the
\alpha/2
and
1-\alpha/2
quantiles as in
Equation

4
. We use
\alpha=0.01
for both. For Bayes-BP, which targets the lower/upper bounds directly, we use
\alpha=0.1
.

Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate methods along three axes:
validity
,
informativeness
, and
efficiency
. All the metrics are averaged over 10 random seeds, and we report mean
\pm
standard error.

(i) Validity
: When the true bounds
[\ell^{\star},u^{\star}]
are known—only in the fully synthetic benchmark where the complete DGP is available—we report a binary indicator that equals 1 if the estimated interval contains the true interval, i.e.,
\hat{\ell}\leq\ell^{\star}
and
\hat{u}\geq u^{\star}
. For RCT-derived benchmarks, the sharp IV bounds are not known; the RCT provides an estimated causal-effect label, but not the induced IV identified set. We therefore report a binary indicator that equals 1 if
\lambda^{\star}\in[\hat{\ell},\hat{u}]
. The distinction will be clear based on the benchmark.
(ii) Informativeness (Width)
: We report the normalized width of the estimated interval divided by the width of the Manski natural bounds
[
39
]
as the measure of informativeness/tightness. Note that the width of the Manski bounds is always
w=(\max(Y)-\min(Y))
. Hence, our informative measure is defined as
(\hat{u}-\hat{\ell})/w
. A tighter estimated interval (
\ll 1
) indicates the method extracts more information from the IV structure.
(iii) Efficiency
: We record the inference time in seconds per
1{,}000
samples of the inference dataset to measure efficiency.

Note that an interval estimator is only useful if it is valid, i.e., it contains the true causal quantity. Informativeness and efficiency metrics are mostly meaningful only after validity is achieved; a narrow interval that excludes the truth can lead to systematically incorrect decisions.

8
Conclusion and Limitations

We introduced IV-ICL, a Bayesian in-context learning approach for partial identification under instrumental variables. By learning the marginal posterior of the sample average treatment effect (SATE) directly and minimizing an inclusive KL training objective, IV-ICL addresses five key limitations of partial identification under IV (
L1
–
L5
). Our experiments demonstrate that IV-ICL achieves superior validity–informativeness trade-offs compared to existing baselines with orders-of-magnitude faster inference. Beyond the methodological contribution, we introduced a prior data generation procedure that produces a diverse family of IV-consistent synthetic datasets with full support over the SATE that enabled large-scale pre-training. We also proposed a novel procedure to convert randomized controlled trial data into realistic IV benchmarks with preserved RCT causal-effect labels. This is useful for evaluating partial identification methods on realistic data.

Several limitations remain. First, while IV-ICL achieves perfect validity across our benchmarks, valid intervals are not formally guaranteed under arbitrary prior–data mismatch.
The inclusive KL objective enforces mass-covering only with respect to the prior used at training time; downstream coverage on real data depends on the prior assigning sufficient mass near the true DGP. We empirically probed this dependence through calibration curves and analyzing IV-ICL’s sensitivity to sample-size and covariate size, but achieving exact frequentist calibration in amortized methods remains an open problem
[
43
,
41
]
. Second, although IV-ICL is architecturally agnostic to outcome type, the current implementation samples binary outcomes during pretraining and discretizes continuous outcomes at inference time. We leave the implementation of the fully continuous setting to future work.

Balke-Pearl Equations

The closed-form sharp bounds of
Balke and Pearl 1994
,
Balke and Pearl 1997
for the standard binary IV model arise from a constrained linear program whose decision variables are the probabilities of the sixteen response-function strata for a binary
(T,Y)
pair under a binary instrument
Z
. The exclusion restriction and consistency (
1
) impose linear equality constraints between these strata probabilities and the observational distribution
p_{yt.z}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(Y=y,T=t\mid Z=z,\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})
for
y,t,z\in\{0,1\}
. Maximizing or minimizing the linear functional
\E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}]
subject to these constraints yields, by LP duality, sharp bounds expressible as the maximum of eight linear lower-bound expressions
\phi_{l,j}
and the minimum of eight linear upper-bound expressions
\phi_{u,j}
, each a different linear combination of the eight conditional probabilities
\{p_{yt.z}(\mathbf{x})\}
. Each
\phi_{l,j}
corresponds to a worst-case allocation of mass across the unidentifiable response-function strata under one extremum of the LP, with an analogous reading for
\phi_{u,j}
. The bounds are pointwise sharp
[
9
]
: there exist data-generating processes consistent with
\{p_{yt.z}(\mathbf{x})\}
at which the conditional SATE attains each endpoint.
Levis et al. 2025
extend sharpness from the conditional SATE to the population SATE. The eight expressions for the lower and upper conditional bounds are:

\displaystyle=p_{11.1}(\mathbf{x})+p_{00.0}(\mathbf{x})-1

\displaystyle\phi_{l,2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=p_{11.0}(\mathbf{x})+p_{00.1}(\mathbf{x})-1

\displaystyle\phi_{l,3}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-p_{01.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\phi_{l,4}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-p_{01.0}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.0}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\phi_{l,5}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=p_{11.0}(\mathbf{x})-p_{11.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{01.0}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.0}(\mathbf{x})

(7)

\displaystyle\phi_{l,6}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=p_{11.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{11.0}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.0}(\mathbf{x})-p_{01.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\phi_{l,7}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=p_{00.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{01.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{10.1}(\mathbf{x})-p_{01.0}(\mathbf{x})-p_{00.0}(\mathbf{x})

Appendix B
Inclusive KL Equivalence

We derive the equivalence stated in
Equation

6
. Recall the loss

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\E_{\psi\sim\pi,\,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}}\left[-\log q_{\theta}\left(\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right].

By the law of total expectation,

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\E_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim P^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}}\!\E_{\psi\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})}\left[-\log q_{\theta}\left(\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right],

where we have used Bayes’ rule to swap the joint sampling
(\psi,\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\sim\pi\cdot P_{\psi}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
for the marginal-conditional decomposition
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim P^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
and
\psi\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
. The inner expectation is

\E_{\psi\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})}\left[-\log q_{\theta}\left(\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right]=\E_{b\sim\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})}\left[-\log q_{\theta}\left(b\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right],

where the second equality follows from the definition of
\pi_{\text{sate}}
as the pushforward of
\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
through
\psi\mapsto\lambda(\psi;\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
. Using the cross-entropy decomposition,

\E_{b\sim\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})}\left[-\log q_{\theta}\left(b\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\right)\right]=H\left(\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\right)+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\ \middle\|\ q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\right),

where
H(\cdot)
is the entropy. The entropy term does not depend on
\theta
, so

\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\arg\min_{\theta}\,\E_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim P^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}}\left[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\ \middle\|\ q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\right)\right],

which is precisely the expected inclusive KL between the true marginal posterior and our approximation. The inclusive KL is mass-covering in the standard sense: any region with
\pi_{\text{sate}}
-mass for which
q_{\theta}
assigns zero density contributes infinite penalty, so the optimum
q_{\theta^{\star}}
must support the entire support of
\pi_{\text{sate}}
. ∎

Appendix C
Training Details and Inference

Output level.
The averaged representation is projected through a linear head producing a
K
-dimensional vector (
K=1{,}024
) of logits over a discretized output space. Since
Y\in[0,1]
, the SATE lies in
[-1,1]
; we partition
[-1,1]
into
K
uniform bins and apply softmax to obtain a probability distribution
q_{\theta}([k]\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
over bin indices
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

Inferring Bounds.
At inference time, given a new observational dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the trained model
q_{\theta^{\star}}
produces an approximation to
\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})
in a single forward pass. We read off the bound as
\left[Q_{\alpha/2}\left(q_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})\right),\;Q_{1-\alpha/2}\left(q_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})\right)\right]
. Inference is one forward pass per dataset, resolving
L5
.

Training Process.
We train
\theta
by performing gradient descent updates with the AdamW optimizer
[
35
]
on a Monte-Carlo estimate of the loss in
Equation

5
. At each iteration, we sample a batch of parameters
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{B}\sim\pi
. For each
\psi_{b}
, we generate an observational dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{b}\sim P_{\psi_{b}}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{O}}}
and compute the ground-truth SATE
\lambda(\psi_{b};\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{b})
analytically. We discretize the SATE values into uniformly spaced bins on
[-1,1]
via the mapping
w\mapsto\lfloor K\times(w+1)/2\rfloor
to obtain
[\hat{\lambda}(\psi_{b};\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{b})]
(clipped to be at most
K-1
). The estimated loss is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log q_{\theta}\left([\hat{\lambda}(\psi_{b};\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{b})]\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{b}\right).

(9)

We minimize
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
for a sufficient number of steps until the loss converges. We denote the trained parameters by
\theta^{\star}
.

Inferring SATE Bounds.
Once trained, the transformer model can take new unseen datasets
\mathcal{D}
and produce
q_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})\approx\pi_{\text{sate}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})
in a single forward pass without further training. We then read off the bound interval
\left[Q_{\alpha/2}\left(q_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})\right),\;Q_{1-\alpha/2}\left(q_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})\right)\right]
via
Equation

4
, computing the quantiles directly on the discretized marginal posterior.

Hyperparameters.
We list the training hyperparameters in
Table

3
. The transformer architecture is a standard encoder with self-attention layers and MLPs, omitting positional encoding to enforce permutation-invariance over dataset rows. The total pre-training cost is
69.5

Appendix D
Proof of Proposition
1

Proof.

We prove each claim in turn.

Part 1: IV Validity.

Instrument unconfoundedness:
By construction,
Z
is generated as
Z_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p_{z}(\mathbf{O}_{i}))
, depending only on observed covariates
\mathbf{O}
. Since
Z
does not depend on
\mathbf{U}
, we have

\displaystyle Z\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{U}\mid\mathbf{O}.

(10)

Moreover, the RCT outcomes
Y(t)
are pre-determined and do not depend on the synthetically generated
Z
. Since the original RCT satisfies
T^{\text{rct}}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y(t)\mid\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U})
by randomization, and
Z
is generated independently of
Y(t)
given
\mathbf{O}
, we have
Z\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y(t)\mid\mathbf{O}
.

Exclusion restriction:
The outcome
Y_{i}
for each accepted unit is the original RCT outcome, which was generated before and independently of the synthetic instrument
Z
. The instrument
Z
was only used to generate the synthetic treatment
T^{\text{synth}}
, not the outcome. Therefore,
Z
has no direct effect on
Y
other than through its effect on
T
.

Part 2: SATE Preservation.

Let
\gamma=\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{rct}}=1)=0.5
by the balancing assumption. For any unit with covariates
(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)
, define
p:=p_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)
. The acceptance probability is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}\mid\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{synth}}=1,T^{\text{rct}}=1\mid\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)+\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{synth}}=0,T^{\text{rct}}=0\mid\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)

\displaystyle=p\cdot\gamma+(1-p)\cdot(1-\gamma)=p\cdot 0.5+(1-p)\cdot 0.5=0.5.

(11)

Note that this acceptance probability does not depend on
(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)
. Therefore, the accepted subsample is a uniform random subsample of the original balanced RCT. Moreover, for the accepted units, the treatment assignment follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z,T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{synth}}=1,T^{\text{rct}}=1\mid\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)}{\mathbb{P}(T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}\mid\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)}=\frac{p\cdot 0.5}{0.5}=p=p_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z).

(12)

This confirms that the treatment mechanism in the accepted sample is exactly the intended confounded propensity.

Since acceptance is independent of all covariates and potential outcomes, conditioning on acceptance does not change the distribution of potential outcomes:

\displaystyle\E[Y(t)\mid T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}]=\E[Y(t)].

(13)

Therefore, the SATE in the accepted sample equals the SATE in the original RCT:

\displaystyle\E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}]

\displaystyle=\E[Y(1)\mid T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}]-\E[Y(0)\mid T^{\text{synth}}=T^{\text{rct}}]

(14)

\displaystyle=\E[Y(1)]-\E[Y(0)]

(15)

\displaystyle=\E[Y\mid T^{\text{rct}}=1]-\E[Y\mid T^{\text{rct}}=0],

(16)

where the last equality uses the unconfoundedness of the original RCT.
∎

Appendix E
Details of the Benchmark

E.1
Details of the Synthetic Binary Outcome Benchmark

We generate 10 synthetic IV datasets with binary instrument, treatment, and outcome, containing
n=2048
samples:

1.

Covariates:
Sample
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
d\sim\text{Unif}\{5,6,7,8,9,10\}
, where each entry is drawn from either
\mathcal{N}(5,1)
or
\text{Unif}(-10,5)
, chosen randomly.

2.

Instrument generation:
Generate weights
\mathbf{w}_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from either
\mathcal{N}(1,2)
or
\text{Unif}(-2,2)
. Compute logits as
\ell_{Z}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_{Z}+\varepsilon_{Z}
, where
\varepsilon_{Z}
is noise from either
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
or
\text{Laplace}(0,1)
. Standardize:
\tilde{\ell}_{Z}=(\ell_{Z}-\bar{\ell}_{Z})/\text{std}(\ell_{Z})
. Sample
Z\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\sigma(\tilde{\ell}_{Z}))
.

3.

Potential treatment/outcome:
Generate weights
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 16}
and compute logits
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{E}
where
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 16}
is noise. Apply row-wise softmax to obtain strata probabilities
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 16}
, where each row sums to 1.

4.

Treatment and outcome strata:
The 16 columns correspond to combinations of:

•

Treatment strata: Always-Takers (AT), Never-Takers (NT), Defiers (DE), Compliers (CO)

•

Outcome strata: Always-Good (AG), Always-Bad (AB), Effective (EF), Harmful (HF)

5.

Observable generation:
For each unit
i
, sample the stratum from the categorical distribution defined by
\mathbf{P}_{i}
, then determine
(T_{i},Y_{i})
based on
Z_{i}
and the sampled stratum.

6.

Ground-truth bounds:
Compute the observational probabilities
p_{yt.z}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
analytically from the strata probabilities, then apply the Balke-Pearl equations to obtain
\ell(\mathbf{x}_{i})
and
u(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.

E.2
Details of the Jobs Benchmark

The original National Supported Work (NSW) Demonstration is an RCT evaluating job training effects on earnings. It includes the following covariates:
age
,
education
,
black
,
hispanic
,
married
,
nodegree
,
re74
,
re75
, where the last two features correspond to the earning in 1974 and 1975. The treatment is a binary indicator of assignment to job training program. Finally, the outcome variable is the amount of earnings in 1978 (re78). We apply log transforms to the outputs to get less skewed outcome distribution:
\texttt{re74}\leftarrow\log(\texttt{re74}+1)
,
\texttt{re75}\leftarrow\log(\texttt{re75}+1)
,
Y\leftarrow\log(\texttt{re78}+1)
.

Covariate Split.
We split the features into observed covariates (
\mathbf{O}
), which consist of
age
,
education
,
black
,
hispanic
,
married
,
nodegree
, and unobserved covariates (
\mathbf{U}
), which are
re74
,
re75
(pre-program earnings).

Synthetic Instrument propensity.
We use
p_{z}(\mathbf{O})=\sigma(s_{z}(\mathbf{O})+b_{z})
, where
s_{z}(\mathbf{O})
includes nonlinear effects of the observed demographics and
b_{z}
is calibrated such that
\E[Z]\approx 0.5
. In particular, we make sure positivity by clipping
p_{z}\in[0.05,0.95]
.

Synthetic Treatment propensity.
We use
p_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U},Z)=\sigma(s_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U})+\beta\cdot Z+b_{t})
, where
s_{t}(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{U})
includes confounding effects through pre-program earnings, and
\beta
controls instrument strength. We vary
\beta
from 0.25 to 8 to produce different instrument strengths.

Appendix F
Additional Experiments - Airplane Demand Benchmark

We adapt the airplane ticket demand benchmark from
Hartford et al. 2017
to our binary IV setting. The original benchmark simulates airline pricing with unobserved confounding between price and demand. We modify this to have:
(i)
a binary instrument
Z\sim\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)
, representing high vs. low fuel cost regimes,
(ii)
a binary treatment
T\in\{0,1\}
, representing regular vs. premium pricing, obtained via soft thresholding of the latent continuous price, and
(iii)
a continuous outcome
Y
representing ticket sales. The ground-truth SATE is computed analytically from the structural model.

The original benchmark models airline ticket pricing and sales as follows. The covariates are
\mathbf{X}=(s,\mathbf{e})
where
s\sim\text{Unif}(0,10)
is time-of-year and
\mathbf{e}\in\{0,1\}^{7}
is a one-hot customer type. The instrument is chosen as
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
represents fuel cost variation. The treatment value corresponds to the price
P=\frac{\text{sensf}(s)(Z+3)+25+V-\mu_{p}}{\sigma_{p}}
where
V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{p}^{2})
and
\text{sensf}(\cdot)
is a seasonal function. Finally, the outcome is defined as
Y=g(s,\mathbf{e},P)+\varepsilon
where
\varepsilon=\rho\frac{\sigma_{y}}{\sigma_{p}}V+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\eta
with
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2})
. The correlation between
V
and
\varepsilon
induces confounding.

We modify the benchmark to have binary instrument and treatment. Specifically, we choose the instrument as
Z\sim\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)
, and map it to standardized proxy using
\tilde{Z}=-(Z-0.5)/\sqrt{0.25}
. Moreover, we compute
P
using the same formula as above, but with
\tilde{Z}
in place of the original Gaussian instrument. We then standardize the price and then apply sigmoid to get our binary treatment. We generate datasets with
n=2048
samples.

Appendix H
Additional Experiments - Calibration and Sensitivity Analysis

The main experiments report
validity
as the binary indicator that an estimated interval contains the ground-truth SATE, averaged over
10
seeds at a single nominal credibility level. Here we further evaluate IV-ICL’s interval estimator along two complementary axes:
(i)

calibration
(
Section

H.2
), the empirical coverage of the credible interval as a function of the nominal level
1-\alpha
, and
(ii)

sensitivity
(
Section

H.3
), the behavior of coverage and interval width as we vary the sample size
n
and the covariate dimensionality
d
. Both studies share the same trained IV-ICL checkpoint used for the main results, so any variation we report is attributable to the data-generating process rather than to retraining.

H.1
Synthetic IV Data-Generating Processes

We design three families of IV-consistent DGPs that differ in the complexity of the structural link functions. In every family,
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sampled i.i.d. from
\mathrm{Unif}(-2,2)^{d}
,
Z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
is the binary instrument, and
U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a latent confounder of
T
and
Y
(
Z
is exogenous in
U
). The treatment is generated as

T\mid\mathbf{X},Z,U\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{T}),\quad p_{T}\;=\;\mathrm{sigmoid}\!\left(a\cdot\tilde{Z}+h_{T}(\mathbf{X})+\gamma_{T}U\right),

(17)

where
\tilde{Z}=(Z-0.5)/\sqrt{0.25}
is the standardized instrument,
a=2
controls instrument strength,
\gamma_{T}=1
controls confounding, and
p_{T}
is clipped to
[0.05,0.95]
to enforce positivity. The continuous outcome is

Y\;=\;\mu_{T}(\mathbf{X})+\gamma_{Y}U+\sigma_{Y}\eta,\quad\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad\gamma_{Y}=1,\;\;\sigma_{Y}=0.5,

(18)

so
Z
does not appear in the outcome equation, satisfying the exclusion restriction in
1
.

The three families differ only in the link functions
(h_{T},\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
:

•

Linear
:
h_{T}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}_{T}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
and
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}_{Y0}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+t\beta
, with
\mathbf{w}_{T},\mathbf{w}_{Y0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{d}/d)
and
\beta\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0.5,2.0)
(random sign). The treatment effect is homogeneous.

•

Polynomial
: same as Linear but with an added centered quadratic term in
h_{T}
, a sinusoidal
\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\sin(\mathbf{w}_{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
, and
\mu_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\beta+\kappa\cdot(x_{1}^{2}-4/3)
for
\beta\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0.5,1.5)
and
\kappa\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0.3,0.8)
(random signs). The quadratic terms are mean-centered (
\E[X^{2}]=4/3
for
X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-2,2)
) so they introduce no marginal shift.

•

DeepNonlinear
:
h_{T}
,
\mu_{0}
, and
\mu_{1}
are independent two-layer
\tanh
-MLPs with hidden width
16
and i.i.d. Gaussian weights, producing fully nonlinear treatment-assignment, baseline, and causal effect surfaces.

For each generated dataset, we treat the SATE
\lambda(\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\mu_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i})]
as the ground truth, matching the SATE convention in
Equation

1
. We use the same trained IV-ICL checkpoint as in
Section

7.1
; continuous outcomes are handled at inference time via the discretization of
Levis et al. 2025
, as in our other continuous-outcome benchmarks.

Setup.
For each DGP, we generate
K=100
datasets at
n=1024
and
d=5
from independent seeds. For each dataset we run a single forward pass of the trained model to obtain the marginal posterior approximation
q_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})
, and read off the credible interval at
18
nominal levels
1-\alpha\in\{0.01,\ldots,0.995\}
as
[Q_{\alpha/2},Q_{1-\alpha/2}]
. Empirical coverage at level
1-\alpha
is the fraction of the
K
datasets whose sample SATE lies inside the predicted interval, and is the multi-seed analogue of the validity metric used in
Section

7.1
.

Results.

Figure

4
reports empirical coverage versus nominal credibility, with
\pm 1
standard error bands. Across all three DGPs, empirical coverage
exceeds
the nominal level for every credibility level. The model is also more conservative on the more nonlinear DGPs (
\text{DeepNonlinear}\succeq\text{Polynomial}\succeq\text{Linear}
at every credibility between
0.4
and
0.9
). This observation is consistent with the inclusive KL loss that induces conservative over-coverage.

Setup.

n\in\{256,512,1024,2048,4096\}
at

, and a dimensionality sweep with
d\in\{2,4,8,16,32\}
at

. Each cell uses
