 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This project will develop standardized analytical procedures to support understanding of the
 chemical composition of wet manure feedstocks, and the digestate formed following anaerobic
 digestion of organic waste to produce renewable natural gas (RNG). The developed methods will
 be validated to improve component mass closure, eliminate ambiguities around key constituents,
 and document the precision and accuracy of the methods. Following validation of the analytical
 laboratory methods for characterization of wet organic waste feedstocks and digestate, NLR will
 use the same samples to build and validate near-infrared (NIR) models for high throughput
 analysis, potentially deployable at commercial facilities, enabling increased production of low
 carbon intensity RNG from wet organic wastes.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 Enhances the laboratory’s core competencies

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Construct and operate dry anaerobic digesters

 Pilot-scale (500m3) dry (≥20% TS) anaerobic digesters will be constructed and run as batch
 processes on a mix of dairy manure and corn stover. Feedstock and digestate samples will be
 collected, along with measurement of biomethane production rates. Small samples (~30g) of
 crop residues (wheat straw, corn stover) and dry energy crops (Miscanthus, switchgrass, reed
 canary grass) will be digested in nylon bags within the larger bulk manure and stover dry
 digestion pile, for additional diversity of solid digestate samples under dry digestion conditions.

 Task 1 Results:

 Shell completed construction and operation of the two pilot batch dry anaerobic digestors.

 Task 2: Construct and operate anaerobic Continuous Stirred Tank Reactors (CSTRs)

 Replicated Pilot-scale (5,000 liters working volume) continuously stirred tank reactors (5-13%
 TS) anaerobic digesters will be constructed and fed daily on a mix of dairy manure and crop
 residues (straw/stover). Feedstock and digestate samples will be collected, along with
 measurement of biomethane production rates.

 Task 2 Results:

 Shell completed construction and operation of replicate anaerobic digestor Continuous Stirred
 Tank Reactors (CSTRs) using dairy manure and crop residue (straw/stover) as feedstocks.

 Task 3: Deliver commercial and Pilot digestate and manure samples

 Manure feedstock and digestate samples representative of a range of total solid (TS)
 concentrations from anaerobic co-digestion will be key to developing a robust analytical
 methodology for wet biomass samples.

 Task 3 Results:

 Special Considerations: NLR received samples from Shell at a time when NLR was changing its
 policy regarding Risk Group 2 biological organism. At the time the CRADA was signed no
 restrictions were placed on manure and digestate materials. In 2023, samples such as manure and
 anaerobic digestate could only be handled inside a limited-access Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2)
 laboratory which did not have sufficient equipment to meet the requirements for analysis.

 NLR safety officers worked with the analytical team to develop acceptable sterilization or
 inactivation techniques that would minimally alter sample chemistry. The preferred method
 would have been gamma irradiation, but NLR was unable to find any commercial facilities that
 were willing to handle non-medical samples in a timely fashion without compromising the
 samples. Initial work at “Pasteurization” of the materials showed that, for the volumes present,
 heat around 100oC would need to be applied for at least 24 hours. Our facility autoclaves were
 unable to provide that length of treatment and tests were very messy. Based on Espinosa et al, a
 protocol was adopted that required all samples to be inactivated at 90-95oC for 72 hours prior to
 handling outside a controlled biological safety laboratory.

 Delays in analysis likely caused significant changes in the composition of wet materials. Samples
 arrived at NLR in May 2023 and were not analyzed by Near-Infrared Reflectance (NIR) until
 January 2024. Continuation of digestion is almost certain even at refrigerated temperatures.

 All samples were scanned by NIR while wet prior to drying (as discussed in the wet NIR section
 below). Drying the samples very likely changed the chemical composition of the materials. Free
 organic acids would have volatilized prior to analysis. Lignin can change structure at
 temperatures above 40oC.

 Task 4: Develop improved wet chemistry methods

 The current NLR laboratory analytical procedures (LAPs) are a good starting procedure for
 development of new compositional analysis methods and initial tests will be based on those
 procedures. Additional components that are likely to be present in manures and digestates
 include fat or oils, a high protein content, and residual starch from grain-based feeds.

 3

 Task 4 Results:

 Special Considerations: NLR’s requirements for sample handling and sterilization of work areas
 after handling materials eliminated the possibility of performing Nuclear Magnetic Resonance
 (NMR) as that facility is heavily used and could be effectively sterilized. Liquid process samples
 were run on High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) without inactivation of
 pathogens but confined to specific instruments, which similarly limited the ability to perform
 analyses. Extremely low concentrations in the liquid samples could be an artifact of the long
 durations before analysis could be performed.

 Straw material required no modification to the standard herbaceous analysis procedure already
 performed at NLR.

 NLR performed compositional analysis on dried manures and pressed solids according to the
 NLR procedure for herbaceous materials (Figure 1). The behavior of the materials during
 hydrolysis and filtration steps was indicative of materials remaining in the non-structural fraction
 that interfere with lignin isolation. A more aggressive extraction for removal of fatty materials is
 necessary.

 The solid materials were dried prior to analysis and were not “washed” free of soluble materials
 as might have been performed if the materials were not potentially biohazardous. This
 necessitated a water wash/extraction of the solid material prior to compositional analysis. The
 water washes for the samples are typically analyzed for soluble carbohydrates, and analysis of
 wash extracts indicated there were no soluble materials within our calibration ranges.

 Starch is a potential component in herbaceous material that contains grains. Some manure
 samples have been known to contain trace starches if the cattle were fed grain. Tests of manure
 samples showed no indication of starch presence, and this analysis was not performed for
 subsequent materials.

 A portion of the liquid process samples were analyzed for monomeric carbohydrates, oligomeric
 carbohydrates, soluble lignin, and select organic acids according to the NLR Laboratory
 Analytical Procedure (LAP). Results of these tests showed very low concentrations (Figure 2)
 that were all at or below our calibration range. It was determined that there was not sufficient
 concentration to pursue optimization of the analysis for these untreated materials as they
 presented a more significant biosafety risk.

 Task 5: Demonstrate mass closure on diverse digestate and manure samples

 Method development in Task 4 will be optimized on a subset of the total samples to reduce effort
 and conserve limited sample mass. Once the methods are optimized, the entire sample set
 provided by Shell (~200 samples) will be analyzed according to the developed analytical
 protocols. These samples will represent the expected sample population diversity that will be
 modeled by the NIR method later in the project.

 Task 5 Results:

 NLR performed compositional analysis of 60 manure samples, 50 pressed solids, and 50 straw
 samples using methods described in Task 4. Liquid process samples were not analyzed. Special
 considerations were described in Tasks 3 and 4.

 Results for the compositional analysis for each material indicated very little diversity in samples
 for each type of material. Extractives represented the most variable component, likely because of
 varying amounts of non-structural materials that were not wash free of the solids prior or
 analysis. Glucan, xylan, and lignin component measurements were very similar for each material
 type but varied significantly between types of material.

 Figure 3. Box-and-whisker plot of compositional results for major constituent components for
 manure, pressed solids, and straw samples.

 Task 7: Develop NIR composition model for dried and ground digestate and manure

 It is anticipated that dried and ground material will be better suited to NIR predictive modeling
 than the wet material that is present in the digestion facility. A preliminary model that correlates
 the NIR spectra to the measured compositional data will be evaluated for efficacy in providing
 comparable compositional data compared to the wet chemical methods.

 Task 7 Results:

 NLR scanned the dried and ground digestate, manure and straw materials on a Metrohm XDS
 laboratory-grade dispersive near-infrared (NIR) spectrometer (400–2500 nm) using an optical
 grade quartz ring cup. Each sample was scanned twice and the average of the spectra was used to
 build the predictive model. Images of this spectrometer can be found in Figure 4 and
 representative spectra of each material are presented in Figure 5.

 A principal component analysis (PCA) plot of the materials shows that each material is spectrally
 distinct from the other materials. The first two principal components in the PCA (85.5%, 9.0%
 variance explained) clearly distinguish the type of material and do not contain information
 regarding chemistry. Principal component three is related to sample manure sample 46 which
 had unusually high ash and low organic material. The third and fourth principal components
 (4.4% and 0.7%, respectively) show distinguishing features possibly related to chemistry. The
 amount of spectral variance remaining after grouping (100 – 94.5% = 5.5%) is very small and
 does not show great promise for a robust spectral model.

 Multiple predictive models for glucan, xylan, and lignin were built to assess the feasibility of
 measuring those components with the spectral library and composition provided. Figures 7, 8,
 and 9 present the results of those models for glucan. The top row plots the root mean squared
 error of cross validation (RMSECV) which should be comparable to the wet chemical method
 errors of ~1.5%. The bottom row plots the coefficient of determination (R²) value of the
 predicted and measured values which should be above 0.7 for a model to have sufficient
 predictive ability.

 8

 The only models for which the R2 is greater than 0.7 are mixed models with manure, pressed
 solids, and straw or manure and pressed solids. No model built to predict manure, pressed solids,
 or straw alone was sufficiently robust to accurately predict composition from the spectra. This is
 a very important finding as it indicates that there is not sufficient spectral variability for the NIR
 model to distinguish the samples. It was observed that the wet chemical data for each type of
 material have very little variability as well. A robust model requires spectral and chemical
 covariance and in the absence of variance the models will not be robust.

 For the models with multiple materials used to develop a prediction, which had R2 values over
 0.7, a closer examination of the predicted versus measured values (Figure 10) shows that there is
 little actual correlation to glucan values. The model appears to classify the three materials and
 then predict the average value for that group. As there is little chemical variance between
 samples, this prediction provides a close estimate of the true measured value which is sufficient
 to exceed R2 of 0.7 with minimal ability to determine chemical structures. Similar trends were
 seen in all constituents modeled.

 Unfortunately, the NIR failed to predict chemical composition for the materials provided by
 Shell and showed only an ability to classify materials.

 NLR researchers felt that the lack of variability in the sample set was the primary reason that the
 predictive models were not suitable for prediction of composition. With the addition of similar
 samples from the NLR spectral library, sufficient chemical and spectral variability could be
 introduced to enable the prediction of the Shell material to improve.

 Results of the NLR/Shell combined dataset are presented in Figures 12 and 13. The statistics
 associated with the samples remains largely the same. This was surprising as the NLR/Shell
 model has considerably more diversity and should provide a more capable predictive ability. To
 test this predictive ability, NLR predicted the composition of a set of manure samples that had
 not been provided by Shell and had demonstrated wider compositional variability than the Shell
 set. Predictions with the Combined Model generated with only Shell samples showed poor
 prediction with a root mean squared error of prediction (RMSEP) of 7.7 and R2 0.61. Prediction
 of the samples with the NLR/Shell model provide improved statistics of RMSEP 1.77 and R2 0.9.
 This improvement in predictive ability demonstrates the capability of NIR to predict composition
 with a calibration set that is sufficiently diverse.

 Task 8: Develop NIR composition model for wet (as is) digestate and manure

 Samples on-site at the conversion facility are often very wet and drying of the material represents
 an added complexity that may not be easily transferred to a conversion facility. However, a NIR
 predictive model based on scans of the wet samples is expected to be.

 Task 8 Results:

 Special Considerations: The wet material was scanned on untreated materials and presented the
 highest potential of exposure to Risk Group 2 biological organisms. These scans were performed
 within a laboratory hood using limited amounts of material and minimal stirring. NIR
 spectrometer selection was limited to instruments that would physically fit within the hood or
 could use fiber optic cables for spectral collection. Ideally, the benchtop research-grade
 spectrometer would have been used to provide optimal spectral quality.

 20

 Given the poor predictive ability of the dry model using ideal laboratory grade spectrometers, it
 is not expected that a model built from wet material using spectrometers with reduced spectral
 quality would yield a suitable predictive model.

 Wet samples were scanned on two instruments with different presentation and spectral
 capabilities. Samples were scanned with a Thermo Antaris Fourier-transform near-infrared (FT-
 NIR) coupled with an Avantes fiber optic reflectance probe while the samples were mixed by a
 magnetic stir bar. The Neospec scanner was used with a spinning plate to present the sample
 uniformly to the spectrometer. Figure 16 shows these spectrometers and the scanning process.

 Statistical quality of models for all components did not to meet the requirements for R2 greater
 than 0.7. Figure 18 plots the statistical quality of all models.

 Unfortunately, the NIR failed to predict chemical composition for the wet materials provided by
 Shell.

 Task 9: Publish NIR models

 Publication of NIR predictive model precision and accuracy will document the ability of
 predictive models to determine the composition of manures and digestates in AD production
 plants for both wet (as is) and dried material. The ability to easily measure compositions without
 the need for an advanced compositional laboratory would greatly benefit AD facilities without
 trained analytical personnel.

 Task 9 Results:

 No work was completed under this Task because the inability of the model to predict the wet
 material rendered the results unsuitable for publication.
