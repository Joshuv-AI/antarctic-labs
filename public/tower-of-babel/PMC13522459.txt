Perceived and received support among patients with cancer receiving palliative care in Malta: a qualitative study
Authors: Weronika Kamińska-Skrzyńska, Raymond Zammit, Krzysztof Sobczak, Joëlle Azzopardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the study was to examine the experiences of patients with cancer receiving palliative care regarding support, broadly defined. The research employed a qualitative method using semi-structured in-depth interviews with 30 adults receiving palliative care in Malta in a three-month period in 2024 (June-August). The data were analysed using Thematic Analysis, which allowed an inductive approach to identify emerging issues through open coding. The study identified 3 main themes of support: Support provided by the medical profession; Support and feeling supported in daily activities; Support received and support given. Patients receiving palliative care perceived medical support as being recognised rather than being treated impersonally. Feeling supported was associated with the ability to continue daily activities or maintain social connections, with relatives’ encouragement playing an important role. Study participants also provided support; they were assisting relatives in everyday tasks, sharing information about their illness, and preparing tokens - a memento to be left behind after their passing. Findings emphasise the crucial role of a sense of support rooted in relationships, as well as the possibility of continuing to participate in everyday routines and social life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58069-x.Keywords: Palliative care, Cancer, Support, Community, End-of-life care, Quality-of-lifeSubject terms: Cancer, Health care, Oncology, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The World Health Organisation (WHO) defines palliative care by emphasising its role in addressing significant “psychosocial or spiritual” needs1. Though the terms ‘palliative’ and ‘hospice’ care are often used interchangeably in common parlance, hospice care typically begins when patients have a life expectancy of 6 months or less, while palliative care can extend for much longer, especially when initiated early2. The Global Atlas of Palliative Care, published jointly by the Worldwide Hospice Palliative Care Alliance and the WHO, reports that “worldwide, over 56.8 million people are estimated to require palliative care every year, including 31.1 million prior to and 25.7 million near the end of life”2. As societies continue to age, and the prevalence of cancer and chronic diseases increases, experts predict that by 2040, “the proportion of people worldwide in need of palliative care will increase from 25% to 47%”3.

On a global scale measuring palliative care development from 1 (unknown) to 4 (high), the WHO ranks Malta on Level 3b, that is, “generalized palliative care provision”1. According to WHO indicators, this means that palliative care services exist within various forms of support, including pain management and training programs for interdisciplinary teams1. In Malta, palliative care is primarily provided at the Sir Anthony Mamo Oncology Centre (SAMOC) and Mater Dei Hospital (the main general hospital), as well as by Hospice Malta, a non-governmental organisation. While many patients treated at SAMOC require palliative support, the scope of palliative care has expanded to include those with non-cancer diagnoses managed by other medical facilities4. This broader approach is reflected in a 2016 study which reported that, in the preceding year, doctors from various specialities in Malta provided palliative care to an average of 10.5 individuals, with 35.4% of those surveyed reporting such involvement4. Malta’s small geographic size, close-knit families, and short travel distances may also facilitate access to medical care and continuity of support. The characteristic close-knit families in Maltese society create, as Vella and Cassar mentioned, “social contexts that give space for everyone to know everybody”5. This generates a unique situation of support during illness, not only from family but also from other people in the community, as well as a sense that our illness is not just ours and we are not alone in it.

In Europe, according to WHO data, most people require palliative care due to cancer (40.6%)2. It is not surprising, therefore, that palliative care is associated primarily with cancer disease. As Abel and Kellehear6 note, palliative care is focused mainly on people with cancer. It is also linked with the social framework for illness by assigning meanings, names or metaphors to specific conditions, helping individuals understand their new reality7. Vrinten et al.8 report that their data show that, among all diseases, people are most afraid of cancer (59%). Taber et al.9 found that public perceptions of palliative care are shaped by specific social assumptions. In their study, 42,5% of respondents associated palliative care directly with death, and nearly a third believed that palliative care was synonymous with hospice care9. A similar proportion disagreed that “the goal of palliative care is to give people under care more time at the end of life”9. These social understandings of illness and care influence how individuals respond to a cancer diagnosis.

Upon receiving a diagnosis, individuals have to take on a new social identity – associated with being a patient – characterised by specific social expectations related to their behaviour, interactions, and expressing emotions10,. Goldstein and Dommermuth11 described the “sick role cycle”, outlining various stages of illness, while Strauss12 developed the concept of the “illness trajectory” in which patients’ actions are shaped by the nature of their illness. Lakey and Orehek13 further propose the Relationship Regulation Theory, which highlights the impact of social support on health and well-being during illness.

Abela14 emphasised that the “psychosocial problems that arise in palliative care [.] are one of the pillars of palliative care.” As McLoughlin et al.15 pointed out, “dying people and their families/caregivers may experience isolation, feel excluded from social circles and distanced from their communities.” These challenges require support, especially if a patient lives alone and wants palliative (or hospice) care at home. When transitioning to home care, for example, the lack of support experienced by individuals may be manifested in their eating-related behaviour. Hestevik et al.16 noted that those “living alone experienced loneliness, which affected their appetite and mealtime experiences.” While these authors noted that some individuals in care facilities try to join similar groups to eat meals together, this posed its own challenges, such as difficulties in integrating into established social circles17.

Support can be understood from the perspective of its recipients, highlighting the distinction between the support that is provided and the support that is perceived as meaningful18. Heins et al.19 found that patients with cancer receiving palliative care did not view the mere provision of care as sufficient to meet their needs, while Yang et al.20 reported that when support was perceived as individualised, patients functioned better during their illness. Similarly, Elvira, Ibáñez del Prado, and Cruzado21 emphasise the importance of different forms of support in enhancing well-being during palliative care.

Support is typically offered by healthcare facilities or affiliated institutions as a complement to medical treatment. Effective support requires that caregivers understand the specific needs of each patient, acknowledging that patients and their carers may experience illness in different ways22. Moreover, through the social construction of illness, particularly cancer, patients may perceive certain behaviours in their environment as unsupportive22. Laryionava et al.23 note that relatives of patients with advanced cancer may unintentionally exert pressure by expecting improvement or insisting on alternative treatments, which can provoke negative emotions such as anger, sadness, and feelings of being misunderstood23.

Methods

Design

The aim of this qualitative study was to explore the experiences of patients with cancer receiving palliative care in Malta in relation to all aspects of support. Given the limited qualitative research on this topic within the Maltese context, a broad exploratory approach was adopted to capture the holistic experience of support and to identify its key dimensions for this population. The study examined the following research questions: (1) What forms of support are provided to people with cancer in palliative care? (2) What specific types of support do they need? (3) What are the main challenges they face while coping with advanced cancer in palliative care? To minimise bias, these research questions were operationalised through the thematic structure of the interviews rather than being asked directly. The Interview Guide (Supplementary Materials) was structured around an introductory question (“What does your usual day look like?”) and four thematic categories: (1) everyday life with illness and hobbies; (2) relationships with medical services; (3) social relations and support; and (4) reflections on life achievements, including their sense of satisfaction and self-fulfilment. Although one of the thematic areas explicitly addressed support, the questions were intentionally framed in an open-ended, relational manner. For example, instead of directly inquiring about specific forms of support, patients were invited to speak about the people they rely on or enjoy spending time with. This approach ensured that support remained an underlying reference point while allowing participants to highlight what was meaningful to them, thus enabling multiple dimensions of support to emerge from their personal narratives. Consistent with the semi-structured nature of the interviews, participants were also encouraged to raise and discuss any other topic they considered important24. The research was designed and conducted in accordance with the Standards for the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ)25 checklist guidelines (Supplementary Materials – COREQ table and Interview Guide).

Setting

The study was conducted in two care contexts within the Maltese palliative care system: the Sir Anthony Mamo Oncology Centre (SAMOC) and through home-based services provided by Hospice Malta. At SAMOC, interviews were conducted in private rooms whenever possible. In the few cases where interviews were conducted in shared rooms, bedside curtains were used to provide visual privacy and maintain confidentiality. Participants receiving hospice home care were interviewed at home. Participant distribution across these settings depended on patients’ availability and willingness to participate during the three-month study period. Recruitment was mediated by institutional intermediaries, one at SAMOC and another at Hospice Malta, who approached eligible patients in relevant departments to ask whether they would be willing to participate. Interested patients were then provided with an informed consent form. Consenting patients then discussed with the intermediary when and where the interview could be held, and the interviewer was informed accordingly. Participants and sampling

The study sample consisted of 30 participants who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed with cancer, and (2) receiving palliative care in Malta (either at SAMOC or through Hospice Malta), between June and August 2024; (3) aged 18 years or older; (4) willing to be interviewed (informed consent); and (5) in a physical and cognitive condition to engage in conversation with the researcher (Author 1). Participants were recruited through intermediaries (one at SAMOC and another at Hospice Malta). Exclusion criteria included: (1) lack of awareness about one’s palliative care status; (2) significant cognitive impairment (e.g., dementia or delirium) that prevented informed consent; (3) severe physical frailty (e.g., extreme exhaustion or intense pain) that would make the interview too burdensome.

A purposive sampling approach was used to recruit participants who met the inclusion criteria and who were available during the three-month study period. The target sample size was set at 30 participants to ensure a broad qualitative exploration within the available timeframe. This number was found to be sufficient, as the final interviews confirmed the recurring themes, suggesting that a degree of data saturation had been achieved.

Data collection

The semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted by Author 1, a sociologist specialised in medical sociology. These were held primarily in English, since most people in Malta understand English, which is one of Malta’s official languages (the other being Maltese). Three participants, however, asked for a family member to be present to translate so they could express themselves better. This request was accepted to ensure their voices were included despite the language barrier. These participants could still understand the interview questions in English and could also follow how their responses were being translated.

All interviews were conducted face-to-face within the medical facility, except for home-based hospice care, where interviews were held in the participants’ homes. All participants provided informed consent. Given Malta’s small population and the limited number of patients receiving specialist palliative care, demographic characteristics such as age and type of cancer were not collected in accordance with data protection requirements. Collecting and sharing such information with local co-researchers could increase the risk of deductive participant identification when combined with interview data and clinical context. However, most participants were elderly (with only one young adult of around 40 years of age), and female (21 out of 30). Due to institutional privacy protocols, intermediaries did not disclose the number of individuals who declined to participate. In five instances, participants chose to have a loved one present during the conversation. Three to provide translation and two for other personal reasons. While the presence of a third party can introduce bias, respecting the participants’ preference for a shared conversation was prioritised.

The significant variation in interview length (5 to 61 min, with a mean of 19 min) reflected the participants’ physical and emotional condition at the time of the interview. Only two interviews lasted approximately 5 min. Despite their limited capacity, these individuals expressed a strong wish to take part in the study and shared their experiences to the extent that their strength allowed. In keeping with ethical principles, their voices were valued equally, and the interviews were kept brief to respect their comfort and energy limits.

Analysis

Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim by Author 1 to ensure data immersion, and then deleted after transcription, with Author 1 and Author 2 having access to the transcripts. These were then analysed using Thematic Analysis26,27 incorporating an inductive approach and open coding. Coding was performed manually by Author 1. Data analysis began with Author 1 and Author 2 reviewing each transcript, identifying recurring patterns, and assigning codes to emerging themes. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached.

Although participants were recruited from inpatient, outpatient, and home-based contexts (see Table 1), the data were analysed as a single dataset since the aim of the study was to identify shared and overarching experiences of support among individuals with advanced cancer receiving palliative care within the Maltese healthcare system. Separating the data by care setting was not considered analytically meaningful as the settings did not constitute stable or defining features of participants’ everyday lives. Movement between care contexts was often temporary and functional: for example, short-term hospital admissions for symptom management or routine medical consultations, followed by a return to home-based care. Consequently, the care setting did not appear to fundamentally shape participants’ social experiences or perceptions of support. Rather, support was experienced across multiple contexts and relationships that extended beyond any single institutional environment.

A consolidated dataset linking the assigned codes to their corresponding transcripts was then created as part of the audit trail. Then, to ensure analytical consistency, all authors reviewed the coding framework, which was subsequently grouped into 3 main themes: (1) Support provided by the medical profession; (2) Support and feeling supported in daily activities; (3) Support received and support given. The analytical process remained strictly inductive: these three final themes were not pre-defined but emerged directly from the participants’ narratives through open coding.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The research team was international and interdisciplinary, comprising two sociologists, a bioethicist, and a medical doctor, none of whom were involved in the patients’ clinical care. This allowed the study to integrate sociological, bioethical, and clinical perspectives on support for patients with cancer receiving palliative care, thereby enabling a multidimensional interpretation of the qualitative data.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Faculty Research Ethics Committee and permission from the relevant medical institutions. Due to strict data protection requirements arising from Malta’s specific socio-demographic context, a small country where the risk of participant identification is particularly high, no data on gender identity, specific age, or cancer diagnosis could be collected. Combining basic demographic details in such a small population could easily allow individuals to be identified. While this limits demographic-based comparisons, confidentiality and participant protection remain paramount. The study therefore prioritised participants’ narratives over demographic differentiation, focusing on the shared cultural experience of receiving palliative care in Malta and aiming to provide a foundational understanding of how patients with cancer experience this support in this specific context.

When interviews were conducted in participants’ homes (as part of hospice home care), particular attention was given to ensuring privacy. Participants were asked whether they preferred to be interviewed alone or in the presence of a family member, and the interview only proceeded after each participant confirmed their preferred level of privacy.

Results

A total of 30 interviews were conducted with adults receiving palliative care for cancer, as described in the Methods section. The excerpts presented below illustrate the three main themes that emerged from the analysis.

Support provided by the medical profession

Medical support was primarily associated with the healthcare team and the facility, often being discussed in relation to procedures, cancer type, and its treatment. Participants highlighted the specific nature of palliative care, emphasising gestures and attentiveness that made them feel respected as individuals, rather than being treated merely as a medical case:

We also get a choice of menu. They bring us a menu for lunch the day before so they can prepare. In other wards, no: ‘This is what you got’. Here it is different; here you have a choice. It sort of makes you feel like you are someone, not just a number, which is nice. (P5)

Beyond access to medical services such as tests or physiotherapy, participants particularly valued the way care was delivered. Individuals valued a personalised approach to medical care. One participant, for example, highlighted the convenience of communicating with her doctor via check-up calls rather than having to attend physically for every consultation:

I usually have about one week without appointments, and then I have three times, and the doctor is very good. She phones me, so I don’t need to come. But with [other procedures] I have to come, but then the doctor coordinates all tests and results together. (P1)

These accounts suggest that personalised care was closely linked to the flexibility of service provision. Participants valued opportunities to make choices within the care setting, such as selecting meals, as well as adaptations to the mode of communication with healthcare professionals, including telephone consultations in place of some in-person appointments. Such flexibility appeared to support a sense of being recognised as an individual with specific needs and preferences, rather than as a recipient of standardised care.

Interviewees also emphasised the interpersonal quality of their interactions with the interdisciplinary team:

They talk to me as if I were their father, not just a patient. […] I come here every six weeks, and even when I see a different doctor, there is always progress. They give me plenty of time, they are patient with me, they ask the necessary questions, and they always provide something that I find truly helpful. […] There is constant improvement, and I look forward to each visit because I know something positive awaits me. Every time I leave, I feel happier. They are amazing. (P27)

The family-like atmosphere of the care setting was another aspect highlighted by participants. As one person noted, this fostered a sense of never being alone:

‘They [the hospital department] are very nice. The department is always clean, and I feel that they are very helpful. The reception is very helpful and I think that there is a family environment. I found support from [the Chaplain]. […] The thing is, I’m never alone. (P2)

Across interviews, participants consistently emphasised that palliative care differed markedly from earlier stages of cancer care. Many recalled specific experiences that elicited positive emotions. This suggests that for these individuals, support is experienced most strongly when they are treated as unique persons rather than solely as recipients of medical services. As reflected in the participants’ accounts, this relational approach extends beyond interactions with medical professionals and encompasses the wider hospital environment, creating a multidimensional structure of institutional support. Participants’ narratives also indicated that support from healthcare professionals was not perceived primarily through the delivery of clinical treatment itself. The accounts collectively suggest that support was experienced when clinical care was accompanied by the recognition of the individual such that participants perceived support most strongly when they felt treated as a person, not just as another patient.

Support and feeling supported in daily activities

Most participants emphasised the importance of support from family, friends, neighbours, or social workers during palliative care. Such support often included assistance with tasks such as attending medical appointments, preparing meals, or maintaining their home:

I have five close friends as my backup. One of them used to call me every day at 7:30 in the morning when I was at home. She used to call me in the morning and ask: ‘[Name], what can I cook for you today? What do you need me to buy for you?’ Another would say: ‘I’m coming over in half an hour. Do you need anything?’ They all call early in the morning to see what I need. (P5)

For some participants, friends provided closer support than family, who were only relied upon in emergencies. One interviewee, for example, described relying on relatives only when she faints and needs help getting to the hospital. Her travelling and lifestyle led family members to assume that she is fine and requires minimal support:

My brother took me. He came to the airplane […] he had special permission, with police, with security. He gets the ambulance. My sister follows the ambulance […] They don’t ask: ‘How are you?’ They say: ‘You ok, yes? So, I have to say ‘Yes’, even if I’m not ok. If I’m in the hospital, they come. The moment I’m discharged from the hospital, they disappear. (P1)

Despite having supportive friends who are very willing to help, asking for help is actually challenging as it requires acknowledging one’s own vulnerability:

We have a group [on a chat platform] and I say: ‘Guys, I’m vomiting, can somebody make me rice? Just rice.’ But when I’m ok, I’m managing everything on my own: cleaning, washing. I wish I could learn how to ask for help. People tell me: ‘When we help you, we feel happy’. So, they tell me: ‘Give us that joy’. Our friends become more like family, especially if you are single, and older. (P1)

Support could also arise spontaneously. One participant described how neighbours rallied when an emergency occurred:

If there is an emergency, the neighbours will help. When my wife had to call an ambulance […] she left the doors open and all the neighbours came here. She was surprised [laughing]. If anything happens, they will help. Everyone knows my condition, so they say: ‘If you need any help, ask.’ (P8).

These narratives reveal an ongoing tension between maintaining daily independence and personal autonomy, and acknowledging the need for support as their condition progresses. They further indicate that the patients’ own understanding of their needs may differ considerably from the assumptions or interpretations of those close to them, possibly also creating challenges in the provision and acceptance of support.

However, some individuals lived alone and, despite extensive support from the medical facility, had to manage daily tasks independently. This also necessitates adapting their home environment to maintain safety:

I don’t do a lot really, because I live alone. My husband died 2 years ago, so I’m sort of stuck in the house, because I can’t get out, unless somebody comes and helps me to go downstairs. Because when I got cancer, the treatment destroyed all the nerves and most muscles, so I can’t use them properly. I try every day. I try to push myself … never far away …, I try to do better. I managed to go to the kitchen but there are many things that can trick me. I’ve got all things organized so I’m quite safe now. (P12)

Participants also mentioned how support enabled them to continue activities they enjoyed and valued before their illness, such as travelling or spending quality time with loved ones:

It was a mad thing to do. We went on a cruise for a week. We wanted to be 100% sure that I could do this, if anything happens, because of medical expenses, but thank God nothing happened […] I went on a cruise for six days. If the oxygen concentrator stopped working, there was a clinic on the boat, since if they were unable to help with the oxygen, I would be dead. I enjoyed this trip, I loved it […] This cruise was the cherry on the cake [laughing]. (P8)

The thing I enjoyed the most was travelling. Ever since I’ve been in this state [of the disease], I try to get the best of life and travel. The last place I’ve went to was [anonymized]. It was always like a bucket list for me. I went with [names of the family members] as well. And I enjoyed it very much. (P17)

Despite the advanced stage of their illness and the associated medical risks, participants viewed travel, often made possible through the support of loved ones, as a meaningful way of pursuing cherished life goals and aspirations. The accounts suggest that such experiences extended beyond recreation, serving as opportunities to reaffirm personhood and agency in the face of progressive illness. Travel may therefore be interpreted as a temporary disruption of the illness-centered narrative, allowing participants to engage with aspects of identity and life that transcend their condition.

Due to their health condition, some participants had to shift to indoor activities instead of outdoor ones, thereby reducing their social interactions. One participant described how relocation had limited his social contact, noting that while he maintains contact with friends by phone, this was not enough for him:

I used to go to the pub every day, drink tea with friends, neighbours. It was my usual everyday thing. And we talked, shared information about everyone. We knew everything. We were like family. But that was before I moved into my daughter’s house […] This is my problem right now. Now I don’t know her neighbours, I can’t go out. (P26)

Overall, support from others was a significant element of participants’ daily lives. None expressed a desire to cope entirely on their own. The experiences shared by patients showed that their daily lives are shaped not only by the disease itself but also by the ongoing effort to navigate it. Interestingly, perceptions of disease stage can influence the type and extent of support offered by loved ones, and these do not always align with the patient’s expectations. Participants’ narratives suggest that support took multiple forms, spanning both routine assistance embedded in everyday life and more intensive support during periods of crisis. The findings further reveal an inherent tension within supportive arrangements: measures designed to enhance care and security could inadvertently restrict social connectedness. For example, relocating closer to healthcare services or becoming increasingly reliant on family caregiving often reduced opportunities for interaction with established social networks. Nevertheless, support was not experienced solely as a source of dependency. Rather, participants described it as a resource that enabled them to preserve autonomy, exercise agency, and continue engaging in activities that remained meaningful despite the progression of illness.

Support received and support given

Support within palliative care is not unidirectional, and many participants actively sought to support their loved ones even while receiving care themselves. One participant, for example, used WhatsApp to maintain regular contact with his loved ones, sharing updates on how he was feeling or the results of medical tests. He described tailoring the frequency and content of these messages according to the closeness of each relationship, emphasising that he did not only expect support because of the advanced stage of his cancer, but that he also wanted to support his loved ones since they were also affected by his illness:

I communicate with them a lot […] I send WhatsApp messages. For example, today: ‘Guys, today I’m ok’; and not just wait for them to ask: ‘How are you?’ and if they don’t text you, you don’t text them. No, sometimes I know people are thinking about me, but maybe they don’t have time. So, keeping close family and friends informed at different intensity, depending on how close they are, is how I keep everyone informed. I don’t expect that because I have terminal cancer, and everyone has come to me. No, no, it’s two ways. I keep them informed. (P9)

Participants also described support from relatives as frustrating and challenging at times. Patients with cancer in palliative care sometimes have no desire to take pills or participate in daily activities. Nevertheless, they try to comply out of consideration and love for their loved ones:

My husband helps me a lot, because he looks after me, because he gives me the pills and I always forget about them and about everything. If it was up to me, I wouldn’t take the pills. I wouldn’t do anything. I can tell you, I do it for him. (P6)

Some participants expressed their care by preparing tokens of appreciation for friends and relatives. One person, who had resumed diamond painting during her illness, adapted her craft to create smaller pieces, as larger ones were not feasible in the hospital setting. She produced these artworks continuously to ensure she gave one to each person in her social circle:

At the moment I’m doing diamond paintings. Now, this is the only thing that I can do […] The other diamond paintings are pretty small, something, just a little gift, to some friends, just to say thank you. They will enjoy it because they appreciate these things, so they’ll keep it as a token, from me. (P5)

Another participant handcrafted jewellery, thoughtfully incorporating her relatives’ favourite colours into each piece:

My hobby is doing jewellery […] for my daughter, sister. Yes, as a gift. I try to match colours, their favourite colours. (P22)

Even within the constraints of their illness and while receiving palliative care, some participants continued to support their families by engaging in activities they valued. For one participant, this meant preparing meals and managing household tasks:

Everything in the kitchen, all sweets. I … do this service for my family, especially to cook and to prepare family dinner. I love cooking. I love to prepare pastries. I enjoy doing housework for my family, and to prepare everything that my family needs. (P13)

Care can also be expressed in other creative ways. One participant continued performing live music, deriving personal satisfaction while bringing joy to others:

I play in a restaurant, twice a week, in [city], so basically, I’m very active. It is very good for me to play. I love music and I try to make people happy. For example, yesterday night we were full, about 90–95 people, eating and listening. (P21)

A consistent theme across participants’ accounts was the to resist a solely recipient-oriented role within networks of care. Through activities such as digital communication, crafting gifts, and engaging in household or creative pursuits, individuals drew upon their remaining abilities and resources to sustain meaningful reciprocity. The findings highlight the importance of reciprocity in sustaining relational identity, suggesting that for individuals living with advanced illness opportunities to provide support may be as meaningful as receiving it.

Professional experience also shaped the ways participants engaged with others. One participant, a former nurse, described how her medical background helped her navigate her new social role within the healthcare system. She remained actively engaged in managing her own illness, while also becoming a source of information and support for others receiving care in the same ward, who often turned to her with questions about their own experience:

I was a nurse. At the moment, I come at hospital and keep reminding myself that I’m a patient. But I communicate very well with the staff. And I find myself really at ease. I know what is going to happen. I know what has to be done. I ask questions, and you feel more secure […] I speak to other patients in the corridor because everybody is concerned about what’s going on. (P4)

Not all participants engaged extensively with fellow patients, however. Some tended to stay isolated in their rooms. A participant from the palliative care unit put it this way:

Other patients, we do not really go to each other’s room. They are hiding there, staying there. (P5)

Comparing these two accounts illustrates the social dynamics of the hospital ward. While uncertainty about illness and treatment sometimes prompted patients to seek information and support from one another (P4), opportunities for such exchanges were often constrained by the isolation and withdrawal of fellow patients (P5). As a result, the provision and receipt of peer support remained limited in this setting.

The categories of support received and support given highlight the reciprocal nature of supportive relationships. Participants described not only receiving support from others but also providing support to their loved ones. These experiences suggest that reciprocity could take different forms depending on an individual’s circumstances. In some cases, supporting others involved actions intended to promote the well-being of family members, such as adhering to recommended pharmacotherapy despite having little personal motivation to take medication.

Discussion

This study utilised a qualitative methodology using semi-structured, in-depth interviews. We analysed the narratives of 30 patients with cancer receiving palliative care in Malta, focusing on their experiences of support from medical professionals, relatives, neighbours and local communities. Consistent with the study objectives, we explored participants’ perceptions of the availability and quality of support. While the study initially explored support through broad categories, the inductive thematic analysis allowed for more nuanced themes to emerge, reflecting the complex reality of care in Malta. By examining these experiences within the Maltese context, this study provides a unique perspective that challenges universal models of palliative care, which are often derived from large-scale populations28. The findings underscore the importance of socio-cultural context. In the Maltese environment, formal healthcare systems and informal community and family networks are closely intertwined. Examining how patients navigate support within these dense social networks offers a richer understanding of the support they seek within a distinct cultural environment.

This discrepancy between receiving support and feeling supported often arises when the provided support does not align with the individual’s needs. This is explained by Relational Regulation Theory, which distinguishes between the mere receipt of assistance and its perceived quality and value by the individual, directly linking this perception to the social relationships in which the person is situated14.

Participants’ needs included continuous contact with relatives (P1, P6, P9) or friends (P1, P5), spiritual support from a Chaplain (P2), or reassurance that support would be available if needed (P8). Support was also experienced through fulfilling personal goals, such as travel (P8, P17), creating mementoes for relatives or friends (P5, P22), maintaining familiar routines (P13), or sharing experiences with other patients in care (P4). Feeling supported was further enhanced by feeling understood by medical staff (P5, P27) or by staff who actively facilitated daily life (P1). Conversely, a lack of support manifested in various ways, including isolation and unfamiliarity with new surroundings (P26) or limited social interaction within the care environment (P5). Some researchers suggest that existential concerns and a perceived loss of autonomy are more prevalent than pain and physical symptoms29,30. Consistent with this, participants in our study emphasised that while access to medical care was important, the manner in which it was provided, including interpersonal relationships with medical staff, was equally critical (P1, P2, P5, P27). Participants highlighted the support they received in daily life and the importance of continuing to participate in their familiar routines, which contributed to a sense of meaning (P13, P21). They also emphasised the role of social relationships in preserving a sense of normalcy despite illness, whether through maintaining connections with loved ones (P9), supporting other individuals in palliative care (P4), or creating tokens for family and friends (P5, P22). These findings highlight that, despite the specific challenges associated with their condition, such as illness, side effects, pain, and fatigue, patients expressed a strong desire to provide support to others. This corresponds with the work of McCauley et al.31, who demonstrated that mutual support often manifests as emotional care, particularly when other forms of assistance are no longer feasible. Our study indicates that the forms of support provided by patients receiving palliative care depend on their individual life situations and social roles. Continuing to provide support offers them a sense of agency and allows them to remain meaningful to their loved ones, a finding also observed by Black et al.32 and Viftrup et al.33 Although the category of reciprocal support was not the central focus of this research and warrants further exploration, our results suggest that recognizing this as a key aspect of the palliative care experience could inform future interventions. Such initiatives could focus on fostering reciprocity to enhance social dignity and preserve the individual identity of patients receiving palliative care. This may also strengthen coping strategies within the palliative care setting. Viitala et al.34 demonstrated the profound significance of maintaining social relationships for patients’ sense of self-worth and coping, thereby preserving the continuity of their identity. Similarly, research involving patients with advanced cancer has shown that continuing previously performed activities was a crucial aspect of their lived experience, providing them with a vital sense of purpose and importance35.

Clinical significance

The findings of this study offer several practical implications for palliative care. Care in this context involves not only the patients and the interdisciplinary team but also a wide range of stakeholders, including families, communities, and policymakers36,37. This study demonstrates that palliative care extends beyond symptom management to encompass a multidimensional support system that shapes the person’s quality of life. The research shows that these dimensions are deeply interconnected; when providing medical support, healthcare providers must recognise that deficiencies in social, familial, or relational domains, such as a lack of understanding within the family or a loss of social identity, may decrease the effectiveness of clinical interventions. Clinical success in palliative care should therefore be assessed not only by symptom control but also by the integration of relational stability and the preservation of social identity into the person’s care plan.

Conclusions

This study aimed to examine the support experienced by patients with cancer receiving palliative care in Malta. Although the literature on medical and social support is extensive, our findings highlight an important contextual dimension. Participants emphasised the primary importance of emotional support. The specific source of this support, whether from the medical team, neighbours, friends, or family, was less important than the experience of being cared for, the openness to receiving help, and the opportunity to maintain everyday routines. Although participants described receiving various forms of support, their accounts indicated that receiving assistance was not necessarily equivalent to feeling supported. In line with Relational Regulation Theory, the subjective experience of support appeared to depend on the personal significance of supportive interactions and the extent to which they met individual needs. The findings also highlighted the role of reciprocity. Participants frequently described a desire to continue supporting others, even while receiving care themselves. Such experiences were linked to feelings of being important to loved ones, retaining a sense of agency, and maintaining continuity in their identity. Together, these findings point to the relational nature of palliative care, which extends beyond the provision of services to encompass patients’ interactions within both their close relationships and the broader care setting.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the inclusion criteria required participants to be willing and able to engage in a semi-structured interview and to provide written consent. As a result, individuals experiencing more severe illness, fatigue, or cognitive impairment were not represented. While this approach ensured the collection of rich, reflective narratives, it excluded the experiences of those facing the greatest clinical or social challenges, and it is quite possible that our findings cannot be generalised to such situations. Future research could consider alternative data collection methods, such as shorter or multiple brief interviews or proxy reports from caregivers to capture the perspectives of patients with more advanced illness.

Second, although English is an official language in Malta, it is not the native language for most participants. Some nuances of participants’ experiences may therefore have been lost or expressed less precisely. Family members also acted as translators in three interviews. While this may have influenced participants’ responses, it was necessary to include individuals who were not comfortable communicating in English but who wished to share their experiences of palliative care. At the same time, some participants asked for a family member to be present, suggesting that family members might be supportive rather than disruptive in such situations.

Finally, recruitment relied on clinical staff (intermediaries) at the institutions where the research was carried out to facilitate contact with potential participants. While this was necessary to respect privacy and institutional protocols, some eligible individuals may not have been approached or may have been unavailable during the study period. However, data saturation was still reached in our sample, indicating that the collected narratives were sufficient to capture the range of experiences relevant to the research question. Despite these considerations, the study provides in-depth insight into the experiences of support among patients receiving palliative care in Malta and highlights the interplay between the medical, social, and emotional dimensions of care.
