Feasibility Evaluation of a Standalone Virtual Reality System for Neonatal Resuscitation Training
Authors: Shoichiro Amari, Masato Eto, Hiroshi Oyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaintaining competency in neonatal resuscitation requires repeated practice. However, equipment costs, space, and instructor availability constrain mannequin-based simulation. Standalone virtual reality (VR) training may improve access. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and safety of a standalone VR-based neonatal resuscitation training application (NCPR-VR) focused on positive-pressure ventilation, among already-certified perinatal healthcare professionals. This single-arm exploratory feasibility study involved 32 already-certified perinatal healthcare professionals (11 physicians, 14 nurses, and 7 midwives). Feasibility and acceptability were assessed by session completion, self-reported usefulness/recommendation, System Usability Scale (SUS), User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ), and Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ). Safety was monitored by Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) and adverse event reporting. Analyses were limited to descriptive statistics. All 32 participants completed the VR session without adverse event–induced discontinuation (completion rate, 100%). Median perceived usefulness and willingness to recommend the application were both 5 (IQR, 4–5) on a 5-point Likert scale. The median SUS score was 65.0 (IQR, 55.0–73.1), indicating moderate usability. UEQ subscales showed favorable ratings for attractiveness, stimulation, and novelty. IPQ involvement was unsatisfactory and experienced realism only satisfactory; presence and spatial presence were acceptable. Simulator sickness was low (median SSQ, 1.9); three participants reported mild symptoms resolving within 10 min. No serious adverse events occurred. NCPR-VR was feasible and acceptable among already-certified providers, with full session completion and no serious adverse events. Involvement and experienced realism require improvement. Trial registration: UMIN000058975 (University hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry; registered September 6, 2025).Keywords: Neonatal resuscitation, Virtual reality, Simulation-based training, Self-directed learning, Skill maintenance, Feasibility study

Background

Neonatal resuscitation is a core competency for perinatal healthcare professionals [1]. Approximately 5% of newborns require positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) at birth [2], and infants in the neonatal intensive care unit may also experience sudden respiratory or circulatory deterioration. Therefore, all perinatal healthcare professionals must acquire and maintain adequate neonatal resuscitation skills.

In Japan, the Japan Society of Perinatal and Neonatal Medicine has implemented the Neonatal Cardio–Pulmonary Resuscitation (NCPR) Project, which provides a skill certification system and simulation-based training (SBT) courses [3]. The abbreviation "NCPR" (Neonatal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation) is registered as an international trademark (Registration No. 5463550) by the Japan Society of Perinatal and Neonatal Medicine.Although SBT is widely recognized as effective for acquiring both technical and non-technical resuscitation skills [4, 5], it requires dedicated mannequins, equipment, and trained instructors, which limits training access in resource-constrained settings.

Given that a formal training course for recertification is typically attended only once every 3 years [6], many certified NCPR providers in Japan face substantial challenges in maintaining proficiency over time, particularly those with limited cumulative bedside resuscitation experience or infrequent access to SBT opportunities. Delays in initiating ventilation within the first 60 s after birth—the so-called “Golden Minute”—still occur in rare but severe cases of neonatal asphyxia [7] and may be associated with adverse outcomes. Therefore, scalable solutions to support repeated, self-directed practice for skill maintenance between conventional SBT opportunities are needed.

Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising training modality in healthcare education [8, 9], allowing learners to experience realistic clinical scenarios in a safe and repeatable environment. Several VR-based neonatal resuscitation applications have been developed, addressing procedural rehearsal, team-based interaction, or scenario-based learning [10–12]. However, no existing system simultaneously combines (1) a fully standalone configuration relying only on a commercially available head-mounted display, (2) a self-directed workflow in which scenario presentation and performance feedback are delivered by the application without any instructor involvement, and (3) a primary instructional focus on initiating effective PPV within the first 60 s after birth and sustaining it under guideline-based decision-making.

To address this gap, the authors developed NCPR-VR, a standalone VR application for neonatal resuscitation training designed specifically to support skill maintenance for already-certified providers engaged in perinatal care who face limited opportunities for on-the-job resuscitation or conventional off-the-job SBT. The present study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and safety of NCPR-VR among this intended target population.

Design

Design Rationale

NCPR-VR was designed specifically to support skill maintenance for already-certified neonatal resuscitation providers who work in perinatal hospitals or clinics—where resuscitation readiness is clinically required—but face limited opportunities for on-the-job resuscitation or conventional off-the-job SBT between certification cycles. Within the Japanese NCPR framework, this population includes the majority of A-level certified providers—a heterogeneous group comprising physicians, nurses, midwives, and other clinicians whose cumulative bedside experience and access to simulation vary widely. The application is not intended as a standalone procedural training tool for pre-certification novices, nor as a replacement for the initial certification course or mannequin-based SBT, which cover procedural skill acquisition (e.g., mask seal, bag handling) that cannot be adequately reproduced in a controller-based VR environment.

Based on this target-population focus, the application was developed on the following core concepts. (A) To enable immersive, scenario-based training focused on PPV—a clinically critical component of neonatal resuscitation that requires accurate execution and sustained performance, yet provides limited opportunities for on-the-job training. (B) To address instructor shortages and the limited availability of conventional SBT by enabling certified providers to independently complete scenario-based training and receive structured feedback directly from the application, without involvement of NCPR instructors. (C) To improve learner accessibility and future scalability by exclusively relying on commercially available standalone VR hardware during training, without requiring additional equipment, personal computers, or internet connectivity.

Development and Runtime Environment

Unity was used to develop the application, which was deployed on a standalone head-mounted display (Meta Quest 3, Meta Platforms, Inc.), with dedicated handheld controllers (Meta Quest Touch Plus controllers). An author (S.A.), an experienced neonatologist and a certified NCPR instructor with expertise in healthcare provider education, led the development to align the VR system design with real-world clinical workflows and educational requirements. The application was designed to operate entirely offline. No internet connection is required during the VR experience.

Methods for Evaluation

Study Objective

This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and safety of NCPR-VR among already-certified practicing perinatal healthcare professionals.

Study Design

This study employed a single-arm exploratory feasibility design.

Participants and Recruitment

Perinatal healthcare professionals—including physicians, nurses, and midwives—holding valid NCPR certification were eligible for participation.

NCPR certification comprises three levels: B, A, and I. The B certification is obtained by completing a standardized 3-hour course primarily intended for physicians, nurses, medical students, and nursing students working outside perinatal medical institutions; the course does not cover resuscitation interventions that require tracheal intubation or drug administration. The A certification is obtained by completing a standardized 5-hour course primarily intended for physicians, nurses, and midwives working in perinatal medical institutions. Although prior B certification is not required, the A course covers all aspects of neonatal resuscitation, including tracheal intubation and drug administration, and is therefore regarded as a more advanced certification than B. However, since no clinical experience is required at any level, A-certified providers form a heterogeneous group, ranging from clinicians with extensive bedside resuscitation experience to those with little or no clinical experience and infrequent access to off-the-job SBT between certification cycles. The I certification is an instructor qualification for A-certified providers who wish to train future providers; it is obtained by meeting additional prerequisites, completing an instructor course, and demonstrating sufficient understanding of the NCPR guideline and instructional competence during the course. While those in perinatal practice who have little or no clinical experience and infrequent access to off-the-job SBT were the primary skill-maintenance target for whom NCPR-VR was developed, the eligibility criteria for this study were intentionally broadened to include all NCPR-certified providers (B, A, and I levels). This decision reflected the exploratory nature of the study, which sought to capture a wide range of user responses—including those from more experienced practitioners—to obtain a comprehensive picture of acceptability and usability across the certified provider spectrum. In contrast, pre-certification individuals were not included because familiarity with the NCPR algorithm and basic resuscitation concepts is considered a prerequisite for meaningful engagement with the scenario; without this foundational knowledge, completing the scenario-based training would be expected to be highly challenging.

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from September to November 2025. Invitation emails were sent to perinatal medical institutions located near the authors’ affiliated institution, and individuals who voluntarily responded to the invitation were enrolled. The total number of invitees and the reasons for non-response were not systematically tracked, and a precise response rate could therefore not be calculated.

Exclusion criteria were a self-reported history of severe motion sickness or vertigo, or any acute condition that the investigators judged likely to preclude safe participation, including but not limited to acute illness with malaise, respiratory distress, impaired consciousness, or balance disturbance. No participants were excluded after screening. Because this was an exploratory feasibility study focusing on feasibility, acceptability, and safety, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Procedure

Participants provided informed consent based on their voluntary decision to participate after receiving a written explanation of the study. Participants then wore the head-mounted display and completed the NCPR-VR experience sequentially, progressing through the pre-training, training, and post-training scenes. The VR session was terminated immediately if severe VR-related adverse symptoms were experienced during the process.

Participants were expected to be native Japanese speakers; thus, Japanese versions of these questionnaires were utilized. The authors translated the questionnaires following established cross-cultural translation guidelines [21], including forward and back translation and expert review. Participants completed the SUS, UEQ, and IPQ after the VR experience. The SSQ was administered both before and after the VR session.

Participants also completed a custom questionnaire that was designed to collect qualitative feedback and specific comments regarding NCPR-VR, including a 5-point Likert-scale assessment of perceived usefulness (1 = not useful at all; 5 = very useful) and willingness to recommend the application to junior healthcare professionals (1 = not willing at all; 5 = very willing).

Feasibility was operationalized as the completion rate of the entire VR workflow, comprising pre-training, training, and post-training scenes. Acceptability was operationalized as self-reported perceived usefulness and willingness to recommend, along with scores on the SUS, UEQ, and IPQ. Safety was assessed by the occurrence and severity of adverse events during or after the VR session, as reflected in the SSQ scores and in self-reporting of any symptoms or discontinuation.

Data Analysis

SUS, UEQ, IPQ, and SSQ responses were scored following each instrument’s established scoring algorithms [22–25]. The difference between post-experience and pre-experience scores was regarded as VR-induced simulator sickness for the SSQ [26]. The simulator sickness score was set to zero when this difference yielded a negative value (i.e., pre-experience SSQ score greater than post-experience score). Given the exploratory nature of this feasibility study and the limited sample size, all analyses were limited to descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. No inferential testing or multivariable modeling was performed, in line with the exploratory and feasibility-oriented scope of the study. R (version 4.5.2) was used for descriptive computations [27].

Outcomes

Participant Characteristics

This study included 32 perinatal healthcare professionals, comprising physicians (n = 11), nurses (n = 14), and midwives (n = 7). All participants held valid NCPR certification, comprising I (n = 15), A (n = 15), and B (n = 2). The median duration of experience in perinatal care was 11.0 years (IQR, 3.6–16.5). Feasibility and Acceptability

All 32 enrolled participants completed the full self-directed VR workflow (pre-training, training, and post-training scenes) (completion rate, 100%). On the 5-point Likert scale, the median perceived usefulness of the VR application for NCPR training was 5 (IQR, 4–5), and the median willingness to recommend the application to junior healthcare professionals was also 5 (IQR, 4–5).

The detailed values of the standardized instruments are presented in Table 2. The median SUS score across all participants was 65.0 (IQR, 55.0–73.1), corresponding to a “Marginal High” level on the Bangor et al. SUS acceptability ranges [22]. Subgroup values by profession are presented in Table 2: median SUS scores were 67.5 (IQR, 62.5–71.3) for physicians, 68.8 (IQR, 63.1–76.9) for nurses, and 55.0 (IQR, 48.8–56.3) for midwives. Median UEQ subscale scores corresponded to “Excellent” for Attractiveness, Stimulation, and Novelty; “Above average” for Perspicuity and Efficiency; and “Good” for Dependability, according to UEQ benchmark categories [23]. Median IPQ subscale scores were classified as “Excellent” for Presence, “Marginal” for Spatial Presence, “Unsatisfactory” for Involvement, and “Satisfactory” for Experienced Realism according to established interpretive thresholds [24]. The relatively low Involvement and only Satisfactory Experienced Realism scores indicate that user engagement and perceived realism remain important areas for improvement.

Taken together, these findings indicate that the standalone, self-directed NCPR-VR workflow was feasible to deliver and acceptable to this sample of certified perinatal healthcare professionals.

Safety

The median SSQ score was 1.9 (IQR, 0.0–11.2), indicating overall low VR-induced discomfort within the acceptable range [25]. Three participants showed SSQ scores greater than 20 (22.44, 29.92, and 41.14) and reported mild symptoms, including headache and nausea, after the VR session. All symptoms resolved spontaneously within approximately 10 min. No participants experienced severe symptoms or required discontinuation of the VR experience.

Qualitative Feedback

In free-text responses, 13 participants (4 physicians, 5 nurses, and 4 midwives) reported challenges with controller operation. Common comments included initial confusion regarding which buttons to press, the time required to master the controls, and concerns about forgetting control sequences during the session. Conversely, several participants observed that immersion improved after repeated exposure, stating that subsequent sessions enabled greater engagement with the scenario itself while they focused primarily on operation during the first session. Nine participants (4 physicians, 3 nurses, and 2 midwives) provided positive evaluations of the scenario experience, describing it as a “realistic simulation” that enabled them to experience the overall NCPR workflow. Eleven participants (5 physicians, 3 nurses, and 3 midwives) expressed favorable views regarding the core concept of NCPR-VR, emphasizing its suitability for repeated practice, its value for self-directed training, and its effectiveness in rehearsing actions under time pressure.

Lessons Learned

This feasibility study provides evidence that a standalone, self-directed VR application can support skill-maintenance training for already-certified perinatal healthcare professionals without instructor involvement, as demonstrated by the 100% session completion rate, high perceived usefulness and willingness to recommend, and absence of serious adverse events. The real-time simulation model, in which the virtual neonate’s physiological parameters continued to change even while awaiting user input, appeared to convey the time-critical nature of neonatal resuscitation—consistent with positive free-text comments referring to “a sense of tension” and “the time pressure of resuscitation.”

However, IPQ Involvement was rated as “Unsatisfactory” and Experienced Realism only as “Satisfactory,” indicating that the current implementation does not yet sufficiently engage learners or convey realism. Qualitative feedback provides some insight into these findings: multiple participants reported difficulty mastering the controller operations, particularly during initial exposure, which may have impeded immersion and engagement. Conversely, several participants noted that immersion improved with repeated exposure, suggesting that the operational burden of controllers may diminish over time. These observations highlight controller interaction as a priority area for iterative improvement. Descriptively, midwives showed lower SUS, UEQ Perspicuity, and IPQ Involvement scores than other professions; however, given the very small subgroup size (n = 7), this observation should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only.

Generalizability

Because NCPR-VR runs on a commercially available standalone head-mounted display without internet connectivity or specialized infrastructure, the findings are not limited to a specific institutional setting and may be applicable across a range of clinical environments. However, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. Only 32 participants were recruited through convenience sampling near the authors’ affiliated institution; moreover, the total number of invitees was not systematically tracked, precluding calculation of a precise response rate. Those who volunteered to participate may therefore differ systematically from those who did not, introducing potential selection bias and limiting generalizability. Furthermore, although the sample likely includes providers from the primary intended target population—those practicing in perinatal settings who have completed formal certification but have limited access to clinical resuscitation or SBT opportunities—it also includes highly experienced practitioners; the degree to which the findings reflect the feasibility and acceptability of the intended target subpopulation therefore remains uncertain. In addition, no comparator was included in this study; the relative value of NCPR-VR compared with conventional mannequin-based simulation therefore cannot be determined from the present data. Beyond these methodological constraints, the application was developed based on the Japanese NCPR guideline, and adaptation would be required for use in regions with different resuscitation algorithms or educational frameworks.

Future studies should examine the application’s educational impact (e.g., skill retention and transfer) when integrated with conventional SBT, and include direct comparisons with mannequin-based simulation to clarify its proper role within neonatal resuscitation education. In the longer term, development of adaptive VR training systems that tailor scenario difficulty and feedback to the learner’s objective skill level across a spectrum of experience levels may further enhance the utility of VR-based neonatal resuscitation training for a broader range of providers.
