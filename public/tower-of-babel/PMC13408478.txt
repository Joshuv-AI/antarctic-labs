Spatial and network principles behind neural generation of locomotion
Authors: Salif Komi, August Winther, Grace A Houser, Thomas Topilko, RJF Sørensen, Silas Dalum Larsen, Madelaine C Adamsson Bonfils, Guanghui Li, Rune W Berg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWalking is fundamental to humans and animals, yet the neural principles underlying movement generation remain unclear. In particular, the relationship between neuronal cell types, networks, and functions has been difficult to establish. Here, we propose that the spatial organization of the spinal cord governs network-driven locomotor rhythms and patterns. An asymmetric “Mexican hat" connectivity - local excitation with longer-range inhibition and a longitudinal skew - accounts for proper motor dynamics, while segregation of cell types in the transversal plane allows descending fibers to find appropriate targets and control network dynamics. We extract these principles via a model of the mouse spinal cord, where synaptic connections are sampled probabilistically from cell-specific projection patterns derived from single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics. Essential aspects of locomotion are induced and controlled without extensive parameter optimization. We additionally predict propagating activity bumps during rhythmic movement. This work reveals universal spatial principles linking cell types, connectivity, and behavior across species.Subject terms: Spinal cord, Network models

Introduction

Rhythmic motor behaviors such as walking and swimming are driven by specialized neural circuits within the spinal cord, known as central pattern generators (CPGs). These networks consist of diverse neuronal populations characterized by distinct genetic identities. During recent decades, genetic tools have been instrumental in classifying neuronal subtypes1–4, motivated by the assumption that genetic identity correlates with specific motor functions5. Some genetically defined neurons have been convincingly linked to core functions such as rhythm generation6–9 or coordination of left-right and flexor–extensor alternation10–13, but most of these functions appear to be distributed across multiple neuronal classes4,5,14–16. These findings suggest that function is not strictly reducible to genetic identity and could instead be supported by redundant pathways. Consequently, other factors, such as the network’s architecture, may play a decisive role. In accordance, differentially expressed genes that segregate adult cell types are often associated with axon guidance, cell adhesion, or general spatial organization17. However, the architecture of spinal CPGs remains elusive. Although motoneuron connectivity and reflex pathways have been partially mapped18, these provide limited insights into the CPG network itself, whose intrinsic connectivity remains largely unresolved. In response to these gaps, modular models have been proposed18,19 in which neuronal connectivity is constrained by predefined functional rules. Such models have been successful in capturing various aspects of motor control, notably the contribution of certain cell types to quadrupedal coordination20–22. Yet, these models face some limitations. First, they often assume that rhythm generation depends on pacemaker neurons, an assumption that has been difficult to demonstrate conclusively in mammals. Second, they impose constraints that tend to link frequency to amplitude, making it challenging to control these features independently. Moreover, modular models are at odds with the observed cellular organization, which seems to adhere more to a diverse biological continuum than to discrete structural logic23,24.

Here, we propose an alternative framework that bridges cellular identity, spatial organization, and network architecture to emergent dynamics. By reframing the logic that governs CPG connectivity, this approach offers insights into the fundamental mechanisms underlying rhythmic motor control.

Results

Space as a principle for motor control

A key feature of spinal circuits is the generation of rhythmic activity. Although intrinsic cellular dynamics have been proposed as a potential mechanism14–16,20, we focus on network-generated rhythms. Spinal population recordings demonstrate that all phases of the cycle are represented nearly equally, a phenomenon known as rotational dynamics25–28. To date, only one model, the BSG model25, has explained these dynamics using a randomly connected network. Nonetheless, it is known that spinal networks are not random. This raises intriguing questions: How do rhythms and rotational dynamics emerge from non-random networks without the need for specialized pacemaker cells? What properties of the modeled random networks enriched them with rhythmogenic and rotational properties and could these properties also be found in real spinal networks? We propose that the solution lies in theoretical neuroscience, which has developed an understanding of how networks can generate rhythms through their spatial organization29–31. We will explain this further below, including why spatial organization is likely a clue.

A common feature of the spinal cord across species is the spatial organization of genetically identified cell types1,4,5,32. They show transverse segregation and various projection patterns in the rostrocaudal (RC), mediolateral (ML), and dorsoventral (DV) directions. For example, in mice, a subset of excitatory neurons (V2a/Vsx2) is located in intermediate laminae along the DV axis from where they exhibit strong projection biases in the ventral and caudal directions (Fig. 1A–C, data have been generously provided by Kathe et al.33 and reanalyzed with permission). Another coarse group of cells, the V1 inhibitory interneuron, is known to localize ventrolaterally and project primarily rostrally34,35 during the embryonic and early postnatal stages. Similar observations extend to most spinal populations, with various degrees of directional specificity during development. However, in adults, the diversity of cell types clearly forms a projection pattern with neurons that span both longitudinal and transverse directions and exhibit dissimilar projection extensions36,37 (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Movie 1; this experiment was done as a part of this study38). Considering these observations, we propose that spatial organization plays a crucial role in motor function.

We investigate spatial organization in model networks that have previously been shown to generate rhythms with rotational dynamics25. We assign locations to neurons by ordering them according to their sequential activity along the longitudinal axis of the spinal cord of arbitrary length (Fig. 1D). The assumption is that neurons with similar phases are close in space, reflecting the spatial coherence observed in motoneurons39 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The main components of the network are excitatory (E) and inhibitory (I) neurons, which make synapses with various targets along the line. When inspecting the net output from an arbitrary location S0, that is, the sum of all synaptic weights in small spatial bins, a striking picture emerges: excitation dominates locally, while inhibition dominates at longer ranges, forming an asymmetric profile resembling a “Mexican Hat” (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 2). This is also apparent in the mean network connectivity matrix, where local excitation and longer-range inhibition are visible as diagonal bands (inset, Fig. 1E). We define the net synaptic output along the longitudinal axis arising from S0 as the “projectome". A “Mexican hat"-like projectome arises when excitatory and inhibitory projection lengths differ29,30. This configuration can easily emerge since multiple neuronal subpopulations have different projection patterns, as observed in spinal cord cell types (Fig. 1A). At this point, it is important to note that the local excitation and longer-range inhibition do not imply that there is no local inhibition or no longer-range excitation—only that they dominate, respectively.

To investigate the functional advantages of this projectome, we construct networks of five subpopulations (sp1–5) (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 2). Each cell type has a Gaussian probability to form synapses with a mean projection length (μp) and width (σp). Together, the populations form the Mexican hat projectome (sp1–5, Fig. 1F). This is the spatial principle of our theory. To cultivate this principle, we probe the network dynamics using a rate-based formalism (see the “Methods” section). All networks produce rhythmic activity in response to constant input, regardless of their size (Supplementary Fig. 2). Next, we explore the potential roles of specific cell types by changing their gain. In particular, we find that individual populations modulate distinct rhythmic properties. For example, amplitude can be selectively increased with minimal effect on frequency by enhancing local excitation in the central lobe (sp3, Fig. 1G). In contrast, modulating the gain of ascending inhibitory projections (sp2) allows control of the frequency without altering amplitude (Fig. 1H). Interestingly, rhythmic activity can be slowed or stopped while preserving static, localized neural activity. Similar effects are observed with other modulations of the projectome (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Movie 2). We also test the effect of changing the gain of the long-range projecting populations Sp1 and Sp5, and find that they have very little influence on the frequency and amplitude.

These phenomena can be explained by an asymmetry of the projectome. Symmetric network architectures, characterized by localized excitation and global inhibition, are known to support stable “bumps" of activity31, analogous to ring attractor dynamics in Drosophila40. However, when asymmetry is introduced into the projection profile, these activity bumps can transform into traveling waves30,31,41,42. Thus, key functional dynamics, such as rhythms, rotations, traveling waves, and slowdowns/halts, emerge from and are modulated by a set of cell types with asymmetric projections. We propose that modulation of this asymmetry is the primary purpose of descending control during locomotion.

Constructing the mouse spinal cord

As demonstrated above, placing cell types with various projection lengths along a linear axis can produce wave dynamics similar to those observed in undulatory species43. While tetrapod walking is often assumed distinct from undulatory locomotion—due to the complexity of flexor–extensor limb muscles and the variety of gaits—this assumption lacks rigorous experimental validation. Given the evolutionary conservation of these cell types across vertebrates5,18, we propose that similar organizational principles may also govern limbed locomotion. To explore this hypothesis, we focus on the mouse spinal cord, where data on cell types and their spatial organization are available.

First, we identify the cell types in the mouse spinal cord and determine their spatial organization. While traditional models emphasize transverse connectivity as a key determinant of spinal cord functions, our principles highlight the importance of longitudinal projections. To provide a comprehensive analysis, we incorporate the transverse positions of the identified cell types as a feature for subsequent investigation and comparison. To achieve this, we employ a combination of single-nucleus RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics on a transverse section of the mouse lumbar spinal cord, which was previously obtained33 (Fig. 2A). Using a harmonized cell atlas17, we identify the locations of 69 neuronal populations (28 inhibitory, 38 excitatory, and 3 motoneurons) that span the entire dorsoventral (DV) axis. We aggregate populations with the same developmental origins17 and refer to these groups as our spinal cell types (Supplementary Fig. 4A). This provides a quantitative distribution of cell types in a cross-section of the lumbar cord, which aligns with distributions established by other methods, e.g., Allen Mouse Spinal Cord Atlas (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Although there are differences in cellular composition in the longitudinal direction5,32,44, the assumption that these are preserved within the lumbar region is a valid starting point. Hence, we extend the map accordingly (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 5A). This enables projections of a given presynaptic neuron to target regions in both longitudinal and transverse directions. Since CPGs can function even after deafferentation18, we focus on cells in the ventral territory of the spinal cord, excluding dorsal horn interneurons and sensory feedback. Along with motoneurons (Mn), we include eight cell types: excitatory V0v, V2aNfib, V2aZfhx, and V3, as well as inhibitory dI6, V0d, V1, and V2b (color-coded in Fig. 2C). We model the extent of the T13–S4 segments and distribute hindlimb motoneuron pools based on their identity and known segmental locations45,46 (Fig. 2D).

Next, we establish network connectivity employing similar projection rules as previously introduced (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). We assign Gaussian probability distributions of synaptic terminals on the three axes of space (i.e., RC, ML, and DV), which are adjusted for each cell type based on empirical observations of projection trends throughout development1,47 (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 1). For example, V2a tend to have local and long-range ipsilateral projections, V0, dI6, and V3 are primarily commissural, and V1 and V2b have mainly ascending and descending ipsilateral axons, respectively. Although part of this data was acquired from newborn mice, which are not fully developed, we argue that this information is useful, since the cellular diversity of adult phenotypes and projection migrations are largely established during neurogenesis, even before circuit assembly48. Furthermore, the relative expression levels of marker genes for long-range and local projections2 performed on the snRNA dataset support most of our choices, i.e., long-range projections are associated with expression of specific genes (Foxp2 and Zfhx3) and local projections are associated with others (Neurod2 and Nfib, Supplementary Fig. 4C).

Synapses between pairs of neurons are sampled from the projection distributions of the presynaptic partner (Supplementary Fig. 5B and C). These assumptions allow inferring the connectivity matrix (Fig. 2E), the connection probability between cell types (Fig. 2F), as well as the contra- and ipsilateral contributions to the projectome, which strikingly resembles a Mexican-hat (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Fig. 5C and D, and Supplementary Movie 3).

Finally, we ask whether our spatial model constitutes a valid representation49 of the mouse lumbar cord. The relative fraction of cell types mirrors the cell counts reported for the mouse17,33, and the L2–L5 region exhibits a higher overall fraction of cells, reflecting lumbar enlargement (Supplementary Fig. 6A–E). Simulated axonal projections resemble pan-neuronal axonal tracings (Supplementary Fig. 6F), and the distributions of the premotor interneurons of a sample pair of flexor/extensor muscles (Tibialis anterior and Gastrocnemius medialis) are spatially intermingled in the transverse plane but differ longitudinally, aligning with established experimental retrograde labeling50 (Supplementary Fig. 7A and B). Hence, the model captures essential structural features of the mouse spinal cord. We can now test whether functional neuronal activity emerges as a consequence of this network architecture.

Emergent locomotor activity

Our objective is to predict the activity of the network from its synaptic structure. For that, we again employ the rate-based formalism to investigate the dynamics of our model25. This formalism results in a set of equations, where the connectivity matrix (W, Fig. 3A) provides a link between structure and function through its eigenvalues (Supplementary Fig. 8A). Specifically, if the largest eigenvalue (dominant mode) exceeds the stability threshold and possesses an imaginary component, the network exhibits oscillatory behavior. Our model has two prominent eigenvalues associated with rhythmic activities (Fig. 3B), termed modes 1 and 2. Mode 1 is associated with four “bumps" of activity, alternating between the right and left hemicords, which we interpret as walking (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Movie 4). In contrast, mode 2 corresponds to synchrony between these “bumps" and represents a bounding mode. Interestingly, the structure of the dominant modes appears to be insensitive to the specific transverse location of the cells, and to cell-to-cell type connectivity (Supplementary Fig. 8B and C), suggesting that they emerge as a consequence of the longitudinal projectome alone.

For comparison with the experimental data, we focus on two pairs of antagonistic motoneuron pools45,46. These motoneurons are activated by premotor interneurons that transmit the spatial “bumps" of activity. The emergent structure of the bumps approximates the extent and spatial alternation of the groups of antagonist muscles (right, Fig. 3B), and captures the synergistic activation of functionally related pools Supplementary Fig. 8D. When constant input activates all neurons in the network (Fig. 3C), mode 1 naturally becomes dominant and manifests as periodic rostro-caudal waves (Fig. 3C). This propagation leads to an alternating muscular activity between pairs of antagonistic muscles, between sides of the cord, as well as between segments traditionally recognized as extensor and flexor centers (i.e. L2 and L5) (Fig. 3E and F). Although changing the position of motoneuron pools along the rostro-caudal axis preserves the structure of the dominant mode, it significantly alters motor output (Supplementary Fig. 8D and E), underscoring the critical role of their spatial organization in achieving functional outcomes. The population exhibits all phases and shows rotational dynamics in PCA space (Fig. 3D–F).

In summary, a projectome that captures the main tendencies in projection patterns and soma locations found in the mouse spinal cord can not only generate rhythms, rotation, and patterns, but also the two main types of gait: walking and bounding. Furthermore, an unbiased exploration of the parameter space of the projectome indicates that while the parameterization of this projectome is not unique, it is necessary to produce rhythmic activity (Supplementary Figs. S9–S11). Together, these elements point to spatial projection patterns as a key mechanism that simultaneously drives rhythmogenesis and pattern formation within the spinal cord.

Descending control of locomotion

We next ask whether targeted descending excitation and modulation can reproduce the control of locomotion, as observed experimentally. In the rate-based framework, the firing rate of each neuron is a smooth function where the slope is the gain (Fig. 4A). The gain and input can be modulated by, e.g., descending fibers. We introduce two descending inputs: one glutamatergic from the caudal medulla (LPGi51) that excites cells in intermediate laminae, and fibers from raphe nucleii that release serotonin, which causes gain modulation in target cells in the ventrolateral spinal cord52 (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 11, and Supplementary Movie 4).

We simulate network behavior under gradually increasing glutamatergic activation (from LPGi) and serotonin levels (top, Fig. 4C). Serotonin selectively modulates the gain of populations near the raphe terminals52, altering the projectome in a distinct manner (bottom, Fig. 4C). These adjustments lead to changes in motor behavior. At low levels of serotonin, the ipsilateral projectome shows minimal asymmetry. In contrast, the contralateral contribution seems to favor excitation at long ranges, producing a slow left–right alternating pattern that mimics walking. As the 5-HT input increases, the ipsilateral projectome becomes increasingly asymmetric. At the same time, contralateral contributions shift in favor of local excitation and longer-range inhibition, giving rise to faster locomotor patterns such as trotting and bounding (Fig. 4D–G). This progression is also reflected in the shifting positions of the eigenvalues and relative changes in firing rates (Supplementary Fig. 11). The specific changes in dominance of the contralateral projectome are a fundamental mechanism for gait changes as revealed by the unbiased parameter search previously performed (see the “Methods” section, Supplementary Fig. 9) and corroborated by experimental observations12.

A global excitatory input can activate the network as effectively as specific input from LPGi. However, rearranging the cell positions in the transverse plane makes only the global input effective, while the LPGi input fails to produce rhythms (Supplementary Fig. 10). Similar results are observed with raphe gain modulation, indicating that the transversal position is essential for descending tracts to target a reduced group of cells required for proper functions. This aligns with observations that both pharmacological activation and brainstem stimulation can induce rhythmic activity in the spinal cord.

In summary, our model of the mouse spinal cord, grounded in the principle of linking structure with function and cell types, successfully generates activity that aligns with realistic motor behaviors and recapitulates important features of descending control. The explicit incorporation of genetic cell types now allows for a comparison between computational manipulation experiments and reported experimental findings.

Manipulation of cell types

A common experimental strategy has been to perturb cell types and characterize the effects on activity. An example is a type of inhibitory interneuron (V1), which can be manipulated tractably using various genetic approaches11,53. In general, silencing of V1 neurons causes both hyperflexion11,53 and a slowdown of locomotor rhythmicity54–56. This slowdown is counterintuitive and has been difficult to explain. Furthermore, perturbations of V1 neurons appear to have other effects than frequency alone, such as an increase in the spiking and flexor–extensor duty cycle56,57. Could these and other previous experiments, such as silencing V0, be explained in our framework?

Here, we first perform a “silencing" of the V1 interneurons in the model. This is done selectively first by hypopolarization and then ablation (Fig. 5A–C). The result is a slowdown, in agreement with the experiments. In addition, the burst amplitude increases, whereas the left/right and flexor/extensor phase relations are unaffected (purple Fig. 5B and C). Since the L2 root is primarily controlling hindlimb flexor muscles, the observation of increased amplitude aligns with the experimentally observed phenotype of hyperflexion53,56. Although this agreement is reassuring, it does not, in itself, explain the mechanism. We suggest the following explanation, which is rooted in the spatial principle: In the intact situation, the ascending inhibition (sp2, Fig. 1F) prevents the spread of local excitation in the rostral direction. This effect, combined with an excitatory projection bias towards the tail, causes the bump of activity to propagate caudally. When decreasing or eliminating the ascending inhibition (sp2), the local excitation bump will remain for a longer time, which effectively increases the descending inhibition and therefore causes a slowdown in caudal propagation. The reverse is also true: When the ascending inhibition is strengthened, the bump will move faster in the caudal direction, as seen when increasing the gain of sp2 (Fig. 1H). A similar argument also applies to the descending inhibition (sp4), which is provided by V2b cells58 in the mammalian spinal cord. In reality, this is more complicated since these fibers are less numerous than V1 cells11, and their respective ratio ensures an asymmetry in longitudinal inhibition. In addition, the amplitude of the motor output is generally increased due to the imbalance of the E/I ratio when silencing V1 or V2b. Silencing both V1 and V2b will result in an uncontrolled fast spread of excitation, resulting in a collapse of the flexor/extension alternation11. A similar effect is observed in the model upon blocking of inhibition (data not shown). It should be noted that another recent model also successfully explained elements of this type of manipulation in mice performing rhythmic scratching59.

Another experimental manipulation that has been performed is targeting the V0 interneurons using genetic ablation. This caused quadrupedal hopping at all locomotion frequencies12. We test the effect in our model by first hyperpolarizing V0, which alters the commissural projectome37 (cyan Fig. 5D). Hyperpolarization causes both an increase in locomotor frequency and a right–left L2 synchronization, that is, a hopping (cyan area, top Fig. 5E). Complete ablation of V0 cells has similar effects (bottom). The ipsilateral phase of L2–L5 and muscle amplitudes are largely unaffected, while the right–left coordination changes (Fig. 5F).

In conclusion, we demonstrate that previous experimental observations can be reproduced and explained by mechanistic principles. The function arises out of dynamics, dynamics arises out of the network, and the network is built out of cell types with spatial segregation and projection biases. The framework also proves to be remarkably robust to perturbation and does not require parameter optimization to produce sensible motor activity (Supplementary Figs. 8–12).

Discussion

Although individual cell types are often assumed to be tailored for specific motor functions1,5,19, such a direct link remains a hypothesis that is rarely explained in the literature. It boils down to a simple question: Does gene expression directly map to function and, if so, how? An important clue is that several molecular markers that segregate cell types are associated with axon guidance, cell adhesion, or general spatial organization17. Here, we propose that elements of motor function emerge from the spatial structure of the spinal network, where the location of cell types, together with their longitudinal projections, constitutes the principle of organization. In doing so, we focus on the emergence and control of rhythms and patterns, and do not address the crucial contributions of sensory feedback or non-rhythmic movement.

We test our theory in models, where the spatial distribution of cell types is extracted empirically using single-cell sequencing combined with spatial transcriptomics and where the cell type projection trends are gleaned from the literature (Fig. 2). However, this information is sparse and obtained in partially developed animals, and the cell types we used here are recognized as heterogeneous classes4,60, characterized by various morphologies and projection patterns at later time points. As a consequence, we used spatially biased synaptic probabilities with large variances to capture this variability. This allows for the coexistence of ascending, descending, or bifurcating morphologies in most classes and appears to be critical for the proper functioning of the network (Supplementary Fig. 12). Furthermore, we argue that a fine-grained refinement of the cell types and their morphologies would not disrupt the overarching tendencies of their class of origin. Instead, we anticipate that this refinement will lead to more nuanced control of the dynamics, since subclasses are often segregated in space35,61. This segregation improves the precision and diversity of functions without altering the fundamental mechanisms.

Our primary objective was to test whether the projections, if preserved in adulthood, are sufficient to support functional dynamics. An asymmetric projectome organization as predicted in this study can readily give rise to emergent bumps of activity that propagate in the caudal direction (Fig. 3). These will be observed as traveling waves globally and as rhythms locally, that is, in segments and motor pools. Hence, we stress that these two are the same phenomenon, and our prediction is that local rhythmic activity is a part of a traveling wave.

The idea of traveling waves is not new and has been proposed and demonstrated in various species34,44,62–64. The propagation of waves in limbed vertebrates has also been demonstrated, for example, in frogs65, salamanders66, and newborn rodents67–69. There are contradictory results70 and the issue remains to be investigated further.

A beneficial feature of the projectome organization is that its symmetry can be adjusted through gain modulation. This enables fine control over rhythmic activities, including the ability to slow down, stop at any point in the cycle, or maintain a focal bump of activity for voluntary control (see Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). Since the shape and size of the projectome determine properties of the activity, e.g., frequency, amplitude, and spatial correlation (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 9), modulation of individual cell types that make up the projectome constitutes a convenient means of descending control. Hence, modulation of spatial areas, e.g., terminal regions of descending tracts, or cell types with well-characterized effects, e.g., V0 and V1, resulted in behaviors in agreement with experimental reports (Fig. 5). However, our model is rate-based and intentionally omits detailed biophysics. Therefore, it cannot address how specific channels, receptors, or conductances contribute to dynamics, beyond their broad effects on gain or synaptic strength. Consequently, we describe changes in gait and network state as abstract gain modulations of the projectome, a compact way to account for diverse modulatory influences rather than a single, identified mechanism. By representing activity as a continuous variable, the model sacrifices the richness of spiking patterns observed in spinal neurons, and its conclusions therefore apply to population-level activity rather than precise spike timing or patterns. Finally, the model assumes narrow normal distributions of intrinsic properties and synaptic weights, not because such uniformity is established experimentally, but because current data do not yet capture their full variability. We probed the impact of these simplifications by introducing intermediate-complexity spiking neuron models (adaptive exponential integrate and fire), and preliminary simulations indicate that they preserve the dynamical predictions of the rate-based formulation (data not shown).

Previous models have produced convincing motor dynamics from putative cell-type modules18–20. Some focus on particular aspects of motor control, such as the role of cell types in flexor–extensor coordination during rhythmic behaviors11,59, while others address interlimb coupling and quadrupedal coordination8,20–22, which we do not explore in the current study. Our approach contrasts with these traditional models on key aspects. Existing models assume a fixed connectivity between modules, without spatial dependence. Our network model is assembled from the bottom up, following probabilistic spatial rules without a priori constraints on connectivity. Next, the rhythm generation in traditional models is contingent on cellular pacemaker properties18,19,22, whereas here it is an emergent mode of the network. In addition, the two theories have different dynamics: traditional modular networks consist of unbalanced excitatory modules without negative feedback71,72 and are connected through reciprocal inhibition, resulting in alternating activity. Our model has a sparse, recurrent, and balanced E/I that results in rotational dynamics with a smooth, continuous cycle of activity seen in experiments25–28,73. Last, in modular models, the amplitude and frequency of the rhythm are tied to each other20. In our model, controlling these aspects independently is a straightforward feature of the system. Nevertheless, several properties of our model rely on the balance between excitation and inhibition to individual neurons (zero-mean synaptic weights). Real circuits are likely to operate under a softer and dynamically maintained balance. This helps stabilize the network and produces organized patterns, but is an idealization that, while supported by experiments74,75, needs further substantiation.

In conclusion, our model supports the theory that appropriate limbed motor behaviors can be generated primarily through the statistical properties of the geometry of spinal cord cell types. This finding highlights the critical role of spatial organization within the spinal network. Here, we describe the circuitry as a finite set of discrete cell classes rather than a continuous landscape of neurons. However, we postulate that the nearly continuous diversity of neural identities and morphologies naturally aligns with the spatial continuum, offering a compelling framework for realizing a diverse range of projectomes in response to spatially structured inputs, including sensory signals. This framework could therefore naturally extend to a continuous formulation and accommodate sensory neurons within the same organizational scheme, without altering the core mechanisms.

Our approach, with its clearly defined physical structure, lays the foundation for investigating motor diseases that affect spinal cord integrity, such as spinal cord injuries. In that specific case, the disruption of descending input will have multiple effects in the model: First, it will reduce the overall drive to the spinal network and hence prevent the formation of a traveling bump of activity. This could be mitigated by the application of neurochemical compounds that increase the drive or by increasing sensory input, for instance, by epidural electrical stimulation. It is interesting to note that stimulation is more effective when targeted spatiotemporally, underscoring the importance of timely sensory feedback, which is missing in the present study. Second, the injury will disrupt the fine-grained descending interaction with local spinal circuitry, preventing adjustments in stepping. Third, if the injury is within the lumbar segment, it will damage the local projectome and hence affect the formation of rhythms and appropriate patterns. By incorporating the transcriptome of each cell, our approach opens exciting possibilities to study minimal procedures to restore motor functions through genetic targeting and regrowth of specific cell population projections within the cord.

AAV Injections

Tissue clearing and immunolabeling

To enable imaging of the entire central nervous system (CNS), tissue clearing and antibody-labeling protocols were optimized to enhance the visualization of fluorescent signals throughout the CNS. Whole-spinal-cord segmentation of neuronal projections

Axonal projection segmentation was performed using a workflow inspired by the DeepTRACE pipeline. The 3D images were processed using open-access software (TRAILMAP)77 with default parameters, and another open-access analysis tool for light sheet microscopy files, which is optimized for the pretrained model 3 from DeepTrace, DeepTrace). This processing generated a 3D probability map of axons, which was projected onto the longitudinal axis to quantify local, rostral, and caudal projections. The 640-nm spinal cord scans, acquired at 2 μm isotropic resolution, were divided into overlapping chunks (2 mm per chunk, with 10% overlap) along the rostro-caudal axis. Each chunk was segmented using the three models from the DeepTRACE pipeline. The output of the respective models was combined by selecting the highest probability value for each voxel across all models. The combined probability maps were then subjected to iterative binarization at eight thresholds ranging from 20% to 90% of the maximum intensity value. The binarized segmentations were skeletonized, and the resulting skeletons were weighted by their respective probability thresholds before being summed together. Small disconnected components (<100 voxels) were subsequently removed from the skeletonized data. Finally, the cleaned skeletons of all data chunks were merged to reconstruct the entire spinal cord skeleton, and projection density was estimated throughout the rostro-caudal extent of the specimen.

Random networks

Fifty random networks of size N = 500 were generated. In all networks, excitatory (inhibitory) neurons constituted 1/2 of the N units. A synapse from neuron j to neuron i was placed in an N × N matrix W (Supplementary Fig. 2), where columns denote source neurons and rows denote target neurons, using a binomial draw over the synapse probability, psynapse = 0.1. Synaptic weights wij were sampled from a Gaussian distribution wij~N(μ=1,σ=0.1). According to Dale’s principle, inhibitory synapses were multiplied by −1 so that the inhibitory and excitatory columns in W were strictly nonpositive and nonnegative, respectively. A detailed balance between excitation and inhibition74,75,79 was imposed on W such that ∑j=1Nwij=0 for each row i. Finally, the diagonal of W was set to zero, i.e., removing self-synapses. Random oscillatory networks were assigned a 1D spatial coordinate based on a phase-space coherence rule (Supplementary Fig. 2F). From the connectivity W and the coordinates, the synapse-weighted relative distances at which excitatory and inhibitory units tended to project were averaged over all (n = 50) networks (Supplementary Fig. 2G).

Spatial unfolding

The balanced sequence generator (BSG) model of rotational dynamics consists of a sparse network with randomly coupled excitatory and inhibitory neurons subject to local balance between excitatory and inhibitory synapses25. The dynamics of such networks may be predicted by linear stability analysis, where one examines the spectrum of eigenvalues belonging to the network connectivity matrix W (see “Mathematical note: Spectral analysis”). The BSG model was proposed as a motor generating circuit in the spinal cord25, as it is so far the only model capable of generating rhythms without pacemaker cells and generating rotational dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D), i.e. exhibit sequential activity with a continuous distribution of phases (Supplementary Fig. 2D). It also allows independent control of amplitude and frequency and explains other essential features of movement. However, the BSG model also has limitations. One limitation is that only some network instantiations have the rhythmogenic property. A second limitation is that the frequency is related to the size of the network (Supplementary Fig. 2E). The dominant frequency decreases as the size of the network increases. Both effects are small-size effects due to some random networks having a dominant eigenvalue (i.e., the rightmost eigenvalue) above the stability line with a nonzero imaginary part (red arrows Supplementary Fig. 2A). For larger networks, the eigenvalue distribution approaches the unitary circle80. Hence, the dominant mode moves close to the x-axis, and the frequency decreases. In addition, large random networks exhibit a sharp transition from quiescence to chaos, effectively prohibiting the rhythmic state from emerging81,82. To circumvent these problems, we aim to pinpoint the features of small networks that give rise to rhythms and use them to build a more detailed model involving space and cell types, which can be directly tested in experiments. We start by unfolding the BSG model, i.e., assigning spatial location to neurons in the network, to identify features that enriched the network with rhythmogenic properties.

The oscillatory networks were unfolded on a line according to their phase, i.e., assuming that correlation in phase reflects spatial proximity (Supplementary Fig. 2F and G). Examining the relative distances at which excitatory and inhibitory units tended to project revealed an asymmetric “Mexican Hat" over the relative distances, that is, where excitation dominates locally with inhibition at longer ranges (Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Fig. 2G, H). We then adapted such an asymmetric “Mexican Hat" structure and imposed it on networks through subpopulations with distinct distance-based projection biases. This gave rise to consistent sequential activity at stable frequencies across network sizes (Supplementary Fig. 2H–J). Although asymmetry is not sufficient for rhythm generation, it is necessary. It is possible, as we can see in a simulation (Supplementary Movie 2), to stop the propagation of a wave by adjusting the projectome where it is not symmetric in this case.

Spatially linear Mexican Hat networks can be realized from five underlying subpopulations, sp1, sp2, sp3, sp4 and sp5, each contributing to the five distinct lobes on the projectome via their projection biases (Supplementary Fig. 3). We conjecture that spinal locomotor networks function with this mechanism. In our theory, adjusting the effective asymmetry of Mexican Hat connectivity along the rostro-caudal spinal axis provides a parsimonious mechanism for rhythm- and pattern generation in animals. It also provides a straightforward means for the descending input to control the rhythm and motor activity. The next step is to include the spatial distribution of cell types in the transverse plane to match these to the sub-populations in the model, i.e., which cell types play which role in the projectome. The purpose of this is to investigate a potential link between cell type and function and how that can be explained in our framework.

To determine the spatial distributions of cell types unbiasedly, we used a combination of spatial omics data. Curated and annotated single-cell/nucleus RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics data sets of the lumbar spinal cord were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE184370 (single nucleus33), GSE184369 (spatial transcriptomics33), and the SeqSeek repository17. Our primary single-cell data set was the SeqSeek-labeled data, which classifies neurons into 69 genetically distinct populations: 28 inhibitory, 38 excitatory, and 3 motoneuron-related (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Neuronal position and relation to dynamics

One of the proposed principles of rhythm and pattern generation and descending control is the importance of the spatial location of cell types in the transverse plane, as well as the shape of the projectome in the longitudinal direction. Hence, an obvious computational experiment to do is to probe the effect of the scrambling position of various neuronal positions.

First, we take the connectivity matrix from above and perform the linear systems analysis procedure to estimate the spatial structure of the emergent dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 8A). Every network instantiation has a connectivity matrix (left). We compute the eigenspectrum of the gain-scaled connectivity matrix (effective connectivity matrix) and identify the dominant eigenvalues (i.e the eigenvalue with the largest real component) which represent dynamical modes of the network (middle panel, Supplementary Fig. 8A). Each eigenvalue has an associated eigenmode (mode) and eigenvector that carries information about the phase and amplitude of every neuron (conceptual sketch of modes 1 and 2 in the third panel). We represent the mode by plotting the eigenvectors in space (right panel). Mode 1 corresponds to the most dominant eigenvalue (i.e., the most amplified dynamical mode of the network). Mode 2 corresponds to the second dominant mode (i.e., the second most likely to be expressed by the network). The first mode expresses alternation between the right and left sides. The second mode has right–left synchrony. The distinction can be interpreted as walking (mode 1) and bound locomotion, e.g., gallop (mode 2). The precise right–left phase lag can be adjusted by gain modulation of the contralateral projectome, e.g., by adjusting the gain of the commissural projecting cell type V0 (Supplementary Movie 4 and Supplementary Fig. 9C).

The location of neurons in the transverse plane can now be scrambled (cf. Supplementary Fig. 8B and C). Cell type connectivity (middle, Supplementary Fig. 8B), the two dominant eigenmodes (right), and the output of the motoneuron pool (bottom, lateral tibialis and soleus) are shown in the control situation, where they have clear rhythmic alternating activity. When scrambling the position of interneurons in the transverse plane, the cell type connectivity matrix is radically affected (left and middle, Supplementary Fig. 8C). Nevertheless, the first and second eigenmodes still contained alternating rhythms and synchrony (right panel), and the motoneuron pool output looked similar, albeit with reduced amplitude (bottom). Hence, the motor rhythm and pattern generation of the model are remarkably resilient to structural changes in the interneuron population. Similar resilience has been observed using genetic strategies to perturb the orderly position of motoneurons92.

However, when the motoneuron pools were scrambled in the rostrocaudal direction, there was a dramatic effect (cf. Supplementary Fig. 8D and E). The location of the motoneuron pools in the lumbo-sacral region of the cord (segments T13–S4) were extracted from anatomical studies45,46 and placed in the model (Supplementary Fig. 8D). The spatial pattern of activity in the first mode has a convenient almost one-to-one correspondence with the distribution of motoneuron pools (compare broken and colored lines, right, Supplementary Fig. 8D). The motoneurons receive input like any other spinal neuron, hence from near and far. Since the Mexican hat topology provides mainly excitatory input from local interneurons, the main activation of motoneurons occurs when the bump of activity overlaps with the motoneuron pool. Hence, the large overlap of the bump and the pool results in a clear rhythmic motor nerve activity (bottom). This also means that when the rostrocaudal position of motoneurons is scrambled, the overlap with the propagating bump is poor, and the motor nerve output is largely non-rhythmic (Supplementary Fig. 8E). In conclusion, the position of interneurons is not crucial for the generation of rhythms and patterns, whereas the position of motoneuron pools is. Next, the question is what features of the rostrocaudal projections, i.e., the projectome, are important for rhythm and pattern generation.

Various forms of projectome and relation to dynamics

The projectome is made up of synaptic projections from various cell types at a given point to various other locations in the spinal cord. We have some general experimental information on the projection directions of various neurons (Table S1), but details and statistics are still unknown. Therefore, it is interesting to investigate how sensitive the activity of the model is to various forms of projection patterns—whether there is a unique projection solution to generate the appropriate outcome or whether many different solutions exist. Specifically, we asked whether different projectome features give rise to similar underlying emergent activity? We did this by varying the structure of the projectome in a systematic and objective manner. First, we arbitrarily assign projection bias, that is, having an asymmetric distribution of synaptic targets in the rostrocaudal and mediolateral directions, of a given cell type, and then calculate the dominant activity mode (first and second panel, Supplementary Fig. 9A). Then we randomly changed the projection biases, again calculated the dominant mode and extracted the RC activity profile for left and right hemicord, hence called “hemimodes". This was repeated 500 times, each with a new random projection bias. We preserved the ipsi-contra biases established from the literature. The right side hemimodes were concatenated, plotted, and hierarchically clustered, using UMAP93 and the Louvain method for community detection94 (third and fourth panel). The hemimodes were then divided into eighteen clusters, with two highly different clusters selected for illustration (cyan and magenta, Supplementary Fig. 9B). Once the right hemimodes were identified, we performed a subgrouping employing the left hemimodes (Supplementary Fig. 9C). This leads to subclusters with highly similar hemimodes (compare transparent and solid colors, C).

As can be seen (Supplementary Fig. 9C right) projectomes can be radically different, yet generate very similar motor output patterns (cf. Phase plots right). The ipsilateral projectomes are shaped as a Mexican hat (top two projectomes) or have inverted Mexican hat topology (bottom two). The effective dynamical mode in space is visualized with the phase of rhythmic activity for the right and left hemicord (right, shown in gray scale). The difference between the top and the second top is the right–left synchrony (top) and the alternation (second top). As can be seen in the contralateral projectome, the only qualitative difference is that there is a local minimum in the contralateral projectome. For the magenta case, the shape is an inverted Mexican hat in the ipsilateral projectome, and the shape of the contralateral projectome has different options: If the contralateral projectome has a local maximum, there is right/left alternation, whereas if there is a local minimum, there is right/left synchrony. Hence, we conclude that rhythm and patterning can be achieved in two ways: First, rhythms can be generated by a Mexican hat or an inverted Mexican hat projectome. The right/left synchrony can be achieved if the local extremum at the origin of the contralateral projectome is pointed in the same direction as the ipsilateral projectome, regardless of whether it is a Mexican hat or an inverted Mexican hat. Similarly, a right/left alternation arises if the local extremum of the contralateral projectome points in the opposite direction to the ipsilateral projectome.

Next, we investigate how the topology of the Mexican hat can be realized. We tried different combinations of cell types in different network models. It turned out that there are multiple solutions to form an ipsilateral projectome shaped like a Mexican hat, with a local maximum in the contralateral projectome (Supplementary Fig. 9D). Both combinations are a distinctly different combination of cell types, yet the generated rhythm and pattern are qualitatively similar (cf. right-phase hemimodes). This illustrates an important point: The rhythm and pattern generation is robust to various configurations and not dependent on cell types per se, but crucially dependent on the spatial projection profile of the whole population. What is the role of cell types then? As we will see below, it is about descending control (see the section “Roles of descending control of motor activity").

Finally, we try a non-Mexican hat shape, the symmetric purely excitatory ipsilateral projectome with the symmetric reciprocal inhibitory projectome (Supplementary Fig. 9E right). Such a projectome was incapable of generating rhythms or patterns in the rostro-caudal direction. This suggests that two features are important for rhythm- and pattern generation: First, there must be an asymmetry in the rostrocaudal direction. Second, there must be either a Mexican hat topology (although with asymmetry) or an inverted Mexican hat topology. Looking closer at the latter case, while an inverted Mexican hat is able to generate rhythms and patterns, it is difficult to slow down and stop the bump of activity, because sustained local activity is unstable. Therefore, we suggest that vertebrate species that have to be able to arrest the progression of movement, for instance, a cat stopping the locomotion when seeing a mouse, is only possible if the projectome has a regular Mexican hat topology. In other invertebrate species where motor arrest is not required for any posture, for instance, a swimming movement where the stop has a straight spinal cord, an inverted Mexican hat topology could also generate appropriate motor behaviors. However, since the regular Mexican hat topology can serve movements in any species, we propose this as the most universally conserved.

In conclusion, this analysis first shows that the asymmetrical Mexican Hat topology is the most common profile to produce rhythms and patterns among randomly generated networks. Second, while the longitudinal position of motoneurons is crucial for proper motor output, the position of the interneurons is not crucial. This raises an intriguing question: is the position of interneurons important for something else, and if so, what? As we shall see, the position of the internurons is important for the descending control of all aspects of motor generation.

Role of interneuron position and descending control of motor generation

To investigate the role of the position of interneurons in the transverse plane we consider two situations: first, the situation where the position is based on empirical data, the single cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics (control condition, (Supplementary Fig. 10A)); second, a condition in which the soma positions are randomly scrambled (Supplementary Fig. 10B). The cell type correlation matrix in the two situations has the same configuration as earlier (middle, Supplementary Fig. 8B). The change in cell type connectivity is visible by having more even distribution, except for the connection to motoneurons, which is kept (vertical black row). Next, we provide two types of descending input: A well-known descending excitatory input (associated with vGlut2), which comes from the caudal brain stem51 (the lateral paragigantocellular nucleus, LPGI, middle panel). When the positions of cell types were scrambled, rhythm and pattern generation were disrupted and replaced by a tonic activity (cf. A and B, middle Supplementary Fig. 10). The cell type target specificity is indicated by a half-circle polar distribution. When the descending input was aiming globally, the scrambling had no effect (cf. bottom, Supplementary Fig. 10A and B). This observation delineates a possible role for spinal cell types. The descending control of motor activity is only possible due to the spatial segregation that cell types represent, combined with their specific and dissimilar rostrocaudal projection patterns.

Modifying projectome by descending input and hence gait

The projectome is defined as the sum of the excitatory and synpatic input as a function of the rostrocaudal distance from a given point. If the gain of a certain cell type is changed by, e.g., descending input, the firing rate of this cell type may change and hence the synaptic input to the target (Supplementary Fig. 11A). This can be viewed as a change in the connectivity, also called “effective connectivity". Once the connectivity is changed to a new effective connectivity by gain modulation of individual cell types by descending input, the projectome is also changed and therefore also the eigenvalue spectrum and hemimodes (right, Supplementary Fig. 11A). To investigate this further, we considered three different values of gain (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Gain modulation in this model is accomplished by descending input from glutamatergic neuron in the caudal brain stem (LPGi) or serotonergic cells located in the caudal Raphe nucleus (bottom). The population dynamics is simulated with a ramping up of these descending fibers (Supplementary Fig. 11C). The size of the effective projectomes increases as the gain increases (second-bottom panels), which also results in a faster frequency and wave propagation due to stronger asymmetry in the projectomes. The eigenvalue spectra for the three situations also demonstrate that there are two dominant modes, which swap places as the descending drive increases (arrows). The consequence is that the right–left activity switches from alternation to synchrony (cf. hemimodes, bottom-right).

Stability of the rhythmic activity

An important issue with modeling is how much the parameters of the model need to be optimized to induce a certain behavior. In our case, behaviors are the generation of rhythms and patterns. The issue to investigate is the sensitivity to the length of axonal projections and the spread of the projections (left and middle panels, Supplementary Fig. 12A). Next, we define two metrics to characterize the effect of the parameters and hence the robustness of the model (Supplementary Fig. 12A). First, we define the distance of the eigenvalue from the linear stability line as the “Margin of stability", M. Second, we define the variability of the position of the dominant mode normalized by the position, ΔR/R. Using these two metrics, we first inspect the effects of neuronal density, i.e., how densely populated the cord is, on the robustness of the modes. As the density increases, the variability decreases and the margin of stability increases (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Three sample densities are shown (right). Hence, having more cells in the cord generally stabilizes dynamical modes, which perhaps could explain the large number of spinal interneurons that participate in the relatively simple low-dimensional rhythmic locomotor activity. Finally, we inspect the impact of the width of projection distributions on the robustness. There is an optimal in both the variability and the margin of stability. The variability has a minimum around 700 μm (red bar), which is the same place where the margin of stability has a maximum (Supplementary Fig. 12C). This optimum depends on the density. Spinal network instantiations shown for three projection widths (100, 900, and 1700 μm, right).
