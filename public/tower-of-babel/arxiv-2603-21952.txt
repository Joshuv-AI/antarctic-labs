Parsimonious Subset Selection for Generalized Linear Models with Biomedical Applications
Anant Mathur, Benoit Liquet, Samuel Muller, Sarat Moka

Abstract
High-dimensional biomedical studies require models that are simultaneously accurate, sparse, and interpretable, yet exact best subset selection for generalized linear models is computationally intractable. We develop a scalable method that combines a continuous Boolean relaxation of the subset problem with a Frank–Wolfe algorithm driven by envelope gradients. The resulting method, which we refer to as COMBSS-GLM, is simple to implement, requires one penalized generalized linear model fit per iteration, and produces sparse models along a model-size path. Theoretically, we identify a curvature-based parameter regime in which the relaxed objective is concave in the selection weights, implying that global minimizers occur at binary corners. Empirically, in logistic and multinomial simulations across low- and high-dimensional correlated settings, the proposed method consistently improves variable-selection quality relative to established penalised likelihood competitors while maintaining strong predictive performance. In biomedical applications, it recovers established loci in a binary-outcome rice genome-wide association study and achieves perfect multiclass test accuracy on the Khan SRBCT cancer dataset using a small subset of genes. Open-source implementations are available in R at https://github.com/benoit-liquet/COMBSS-GLM-R and in Python at https://github.com/saratmoka/COMBSS-GLM-Python .



1
Introduction

Generalized linear models (GLMs), introduced by Nelder and Wedderburn

(
32
)
, extend classical linear regression to
handle non-Gaussian outcomes, such as binary indicators, counts
(e.g., Poisson), and positive-valued responses (e.g., exponential),
by modeling the response through an exponential-family distribution
and linking its conditional mean to a linear predictor via an
appropriate link function
(
33
;
14
)
. This framework unifies many widely used
models, including linear, logistic, and Poisson regression. Common
link functions include the identity link (Gaussian), logit link
(Binomial), log link (Poisson), and reciprocal link (exponential).

Recent advances in data acquisition and storage have enabled the
collection of complex, high-dimensional datasets across many domains,
including biology, engineering, economics, finance, and the health
sciences
(
9
;
7
)
.
In biomedical and clinical research in particular, modern studies
routinely record thousands to millions of candidate predictors, for example, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotypes in genome-wide association studies
(
26
)
, gene expression measurements in cancer profiling
(
21
)
, imaging-derived variables, and electronic
health records. In such settings,
variable selection
is essential for both predictive
performance and scientific interpretability
(
13
)
.
By identifying a parsimonious set of relevant predictors, feature
selection mitigates overfitting and improves generalization, sharpens
estimation and inference by removing irrelevant or redundant
variables, and yields models that are easier to interpret and deploy
in practice
(
17
)
.

A classical approach to variable selection is
best subset
selection
: for a prescribed model size
k
, one seeks the subset of

k
predictors that yields the best fit under a chosen criterion
(typically the negative log-likelihood, possibly with a stabilizing
quadratic shrinkage term)
(
1
)
.
Unlike unconstrained GLM estimation, this
requires searching over an exponentially large collection of candidate
subsets, which makes the problem computationally challenging; in fact,
it is NP-hard even in the special case of linear regression

(
31
)
. In practice, the subset size
k
(and any
accompanying shrinkage level) is treated as a tuning choice and is
selected to optimize predictive performance on unseen data
(
15
)
.

Addressing this computational challenge for generalized linear models is the focus of the present paper. Building on the continuous Boolean relaxation approach of
29
, which was developed for linear regression, and subsequently adapted to best
subset selection in linear dimension reduction models
(
23
)
, we establish a general method for best subset selection in the GLM setting.

The key idea is to relax the intractable combinatorial search over binary subsets to a smooth optimization over the polytope
\{\bm{t}\in[0,1]^{p}:\sum_{j=1}^{p}t_{j}=k\}
for a prescribed model size
k
, where
t_{j}\in\{0,1\}
encodes variable exclusion or inclusion. We solve this constrained problem using a variant of the Frank–Wolfe algorithm
(
10
;
19
)
. In contrast to the original COMBSS method for linear regression, which replaces the constraint with a penalty and yields an unconstrained formulation, our approach enforces exact cardinality, thereby enabling systematic exploration of models at each prescribed
k
. We further equip the Frank–Wolfe algorithm with a homotopy scheme that progressively deforms the relaxed landscape until its concave, ensuring that the final solution converges to a binary corner of the polytope.
The resulting algorithm, which we refer to as COMBSS-GLM, is simple to implement, leverages standard
GLM solvers, such as
glmnet

(
11
)
, as black-box inner routines, and scales to
high-dimensional problems with thousands of predictors.

Although COMBSS-GLM applies to any GLM with a convex negative log-likelihood, and the theoretical results are established at this level of generality, we conduct experiments primarily for logistic and multinomial regression, which are the most prevalent GLMs in high-dimensional biomedical classification. Our key contributions are as follows.

•

We formulate best subset selection in GLMs through a
continuous Boolean relaxation that preserves the fixed-model-size
structure and supports direct optimization on a simplex slice.

•

We develop a homotopy Frank–Wolfe algorithm with envelope
gradients; each iteration requires one penalized GLM fit and a

k
-smallest-component vertex update, with no projection step.

•

We establish a curvature-based large-parameter regime under
which the relaxed objective is concave in the selection weights,
implying that global minimizers lie at binary corners; for
logistic and baseline multinomial models, we provide explicit
sufficient thresholds.

•

Through simulations and two biomedical case studies ,a high-dimensional rice GWAS (p = 158,210 SNPs;
(
26
)
) and a multiclass cancer gene-expression problem (p = 2,308, C=4 tumour types;
(
21
)
), we show that COMBSS-GLM improves sparse recovery while maintaining strong predictive performance, and yields highly parsimonious models in practice.

The remaining paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce relevant notation, formulate best subset selection for GLMs, and develop the continuous Boolean relaxation underlying COMBSS-GLM.
In Section
3
, we present the COMBSS-GLM algorithm, establish the concavity threshold and monotonicity properties of the
relaxed objective, and discuss hyperparameter tuning including the default choice of the homotopy grid size,
N
.
In Section
4
, we report simulation studies comparing COMBSS-GLM against penalized regression competitors in the logistic
regression setting.
In Section
5
, we apply the COMBSS-GLM algorithm to two biomedical
datasets: a rice genome-wide association study (GWAS) study and the small round blue cell tumor (SRBCT) Khan cancer gene-expression
dataset. Concluding remarks are discussed in Section
6
.
Proofs and additional numerical results are collated in the
supplementary material.

Methodology

Throughout the paper, all vectors are understood to be column vectors.
For a scalar
a_{0}
and a vector
\bm{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{p})^{\top}
, we
write
(a_{0},\bm{a})
to denote the concatenated vector

(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{p})^{\top}
.
For a binary vector
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{p})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
, we
denote by
|\bm{s}|
the number of nonzero (equal to one) entries in

\bm{s}
.
For vectors
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the Hadamard (elementwise)
product is denoted by
\bm{a}\odot\bm{b}:=(a_{1}b_{1},\dots,a_{p}b_{p})^{\top}
.
For a vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\diag(\bm{v})
denotes the

p\times p
diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by
\bm{v}
.
We denote by
\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the vector of all ones.
For a function
f
,
\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{x}f(x)
denotes the set of minimizers of

f
over its domain.
All vector square roots are understood elementwise. For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\operatorname{conv}\{A\}
denotes the convex hull of

A
, namely the set of all convex combinations of points in
A
.

2.1
Preliminaries

We now provide a mathematical formulation of GLMs and best subset selection for these models.
Let
\mathcal{D}=(X,\bm{y})
, where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}
is a design matrix
whose rows have the form
(1,x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
(the leading
1

corresponding to the intercept), and
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}
is
the response vector with entries drawn from an exponential-family
distribution.
In a GLM, an invertible link function
g
relates the conditional mean
to a linear predictor via

g\!\left(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\bm{x}]\right)=\beta_{0}+\bm{x}^{\top}\beta,

where
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is an intercept and

\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a coefficient vector

(
27
)
.

The parameters
(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
are estimated by maximum likelihood.
Writing
\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D}):=\log L(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})
for the
log-likelihood, the MLE solves

\displaystyle\min_{\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\;\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;-\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D}).

(1)

For standard GLMs (Gaussian, Binomial, Poisson),

-\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})
is convex in
(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})

(
27
)
. However, except in the case of linear regression the
minimizer has no closed form and is computed numerically

(
3
)
.

In this GLM setting, a common formulation of best subset selection is the cardinality-constrained regularized MLE

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \minimize_{\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\;\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\quad&-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\\
\subjectto\quad&\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}=k,\end{aligned}

(2)

for a prescribed model size
k
, where

\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}:=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbb{I}(\beta_{j}\neq 0)
counts the
number of nonzero coefficients subjected to selection (with
\mathcal{J}

typically excluding the intercept and any mandatory variables), and

\lambda\geq 0
is an optional ridge penalty.
In practice,
k
and
\lambda
are chosen to minimise an out-of-sample
criterion, such as validation or cross-validation error.

2.2
Boolean Relaxation

In this section, we provide a continuous relaxation of the best subset
selection problem stated in (
2
).
Towards this, we first reformulate (
2
) as an equivalent
binary constrained optimization.
Since the design matrix
X
has
p+1
columns (including the
intercept), we number them using the index
j=0,1,2,\dots,p
.
To keep it general, we assume that
m
of the variables, in addition to
the intercept, are mandatory and are always included in the model.
Without loss of generality, we assume they are the first
m
variables
indexed from
1
to
m
, while the index
0
corresponds to the
intercept.

For any binary vector
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{p-m})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{p-m}
, let
X_{[\bm{s}]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+1+|\bm{s}|)}
denote the submatrix of
X
obtained by retaining the intercept
column, mandatory columns and all other columns with indices
m+j

where
s_{j}=1
.
With this notation, define
\mathcal{D}_{[\bm{s}]}=(X_{[\bm{s}]},\bm{y})
, and
for every
\bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{p-m}
, let

(\widehat{\beta}_{\bm{s},0},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{s}})
be the low-dimensional MLE
solution of the problem

\displaystyle\min_{\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\,\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\bm{s}|}}-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D}_{[\bm{s}]})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}_{2},

(3)

where
\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D}_{[\bm{s}]})
is the log-likelihood
function of the GLM of interest with the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{[\bm{s}]}
.
Furthermore, define

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{\bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{p-m}:|\bm{s}|=k\},

which is the set of all binary vectors of length
p-m
with exactly

k
ones.
Then, solving (
2
) is equivalent to solving the binary
constrained problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \minimize_{{\bm{s}}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\,\,-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\widehat{\beta}_{\bm{s},0},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{s}};\mathcal{D}_{[\bm{s}]})+\lambda\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{s}}\|^{2}_{2}.\end{aligned}

(4)

For any fixed
\bm{s}
, finding a good approximation to the MLE

(\widehat{\beta}_{\bm{s},0},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{s}})
is relatively easy using a
standard software package such as
glmnet
in
R
.
In linear regression, we even have a closed-form expression, namely
the ridge estimator.
However, as mentioned earlier, solving the combinatorial problem
(
4
) exactly is known to be NP-hard, making best subset
selection a difficult task for larger values of
p
. For instance, the R package
bestglm

(
28
)
based on the leaps-and-bounds method
(
12
)
can handle only data with dimension
p\leq 30
.

To overcome this challenge, we provide a Boolean relaxation
of (
4
) by moving the constraints from the binary space

\{0,1\}^{p-m}
to the hypercube
[0,1]^{p-m}
.
For
\bm{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{p-m})^{\top}\in[0,1]^{p-m}
, let

T_{\bm{t}}=\diag(\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{m\;\text{times}},t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{p-m})\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\bm{t}}=\sqrt{I-T_{\bm{t}}^{2}},

where
I
denotes an identity matrix of appropriate dimension.
Note that
\Gamma_{\bm{t}}
is a square diagonal matrix of size

p\times p
with zeros in the first
m
locations and

\sqrt{1-t_{j}^{2}}
for the next
m+j
locations.
Now take
\delta>0
and define

\displaystyle h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}):=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\beta_{0},T_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

where
\ell
is the log-likelihood function of the GLM under
consideration.
Note that
\ell(\beta_{0},T_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})
is obtained by replacing
the coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}
with

T_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m},t_{1}\beta_{m+1},\dots,t_{p-m}\beta_{p})^{\top}

in the log-likelihood function
\ell
.
For each
\bm{t}\in[0,1]^{p-m}
, let

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}}\;h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}).

(6)

Since
\delta>0
, the additional ridge penalty

\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}=\delta\sum_{j=1}^{p-m}(1-t_{j}^{2})\beta_{m+j}^{2}

in
h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
penalizes each coefficient

\beta_{m+j}
separately so that we enforce the support implied by

\bm{t}
: if
t_{j}=0
then
(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})_{m+j}=0
, whereas when

t_{j}=1
the coefficient
(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})_{m+j}
incurs no penalization.
By defining

\mathcal{T}_{k}:=\{\bm{t}\in[0,1]^{p-m}:\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{t}=k\},

which is a relaxation of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, and by using a solution

(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})
of (
6
),
we define

\displaystyle f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}}),

(7)

and write a Boolean relaxation of (
4
) as

\displaystyle\minimize_{{\bm{t}}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\,f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t}).

(8)

Here,
f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
depends on
\delta
through

(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})
.

In the sequel, we develop a gradient-based algorithm for
(
8
) that iteratively updates
\bm{t}
using the gradient

\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
where
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
.
In the special case of linear regression, the inner minimizer

(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})
admits a closed-form expression
for interior points
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
, and one may compute

\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
by differentiating this closed form
with respect to
\bm{t}
; see
(
29
)
.
For general GLMs, however,
(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})

typically has no closed-form expression, and differentiating the
optimizer is undesirable.
Fortunately, Danskin’s envelope theorem yields an exact expression for

\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
on the interior that does not
require differentiating
(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})
; see
Section
3.2
for details.

:
Boolean relaxation surface
f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
for
logistic regression with
\lambda=0
and varying curvature
parameter
\delta
. The surfaces are plotted over the domain

\bm{t}=(t_{1},t_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
with five geometrically
increasing values of
\delta
shown in different colours according
to the colour bar. As
\delta
increases, the relaxed objective
becomes more peaked, driving the solution toward binary corners.

3
Algorithm

We now present a simple homotopy algorithm, adapted from

19
and
30
, for
minimizing the relaxed objective in (
8
).
The method increases the curvature parameter
\delta
geometrically and
performs a Frank–Wolfe (FW) update on
\bm{t}
at each stage using an

envelope (sub)gradient
of the value function

f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t}):=\min_{\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}}\,h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}).

Let

\mathcal{M}_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t}):=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}}\,h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
,
and define the envelope-gradient set

\partial^{\mathrm{env}}f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t}):=\operatorname{conv}\Bigl\{\nabla_{\bm{t}}h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}):(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})\in\mathcal{M}_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})\Bigr\}.

Given any
(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})\in\mathcal{M}_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})

(see (
6
)), we take

\bm{g}_{\delta}(\bm{t}):=\nabla_{\bm{t}}h_{\delta,\lambda}\bigl(\bm{t},\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}}\bigr)\in\partial^{\mathrm{env}}f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t}),

which coincides with the gradient
\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})

whenever the minimum is attained and the minimizer is unique (see
Section
3.3
).
Each iteration requires only (i) solving the inner penalized GLM once
to obtain
\bm{g}(\bm{t})
, and (ii) selecting the
k
smallest
components of
\bm{g}(\bm{t})
to form a vertex
\bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{p-m}
.
No projections are needed and the update is an explicit convex
combination, making the method straightforward to implement.
Details on how to employ existing GLM solvers to compute

\bm{g}_{\delta}(\bm{t})
at a given
\bm{t}
are provided in
Section
3.2
.

Input:

Uniform penalty
\lambda\geq 0
,
sparsity level
k
,
initial curvature parameter
\delta_{\min}>0
, max curvature parameter
\delta_{\max}>\delta_{\min}
,
grid size
N
, and learning rate
\alpha

Initialization:

i\leftarrow 0
,

\bm{t}\leftarrow\left(\frac{k}{p-m}\right)\bm{1}
, Set
r=(\delta_{\max}/\delta_{\min})^{1/N}

for

i=\{1,2,\dots,2N\}

do

1

\delta\leftarrow\min\{\delta_{\min}r^{i},\delta_{\max}\}
;

2

Compute
\bm{g}_{\delta}(\bm{t})\in\partial^{\mathrm{env}}f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
;

3

Let
\bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{p-m}
select the
k
smallest components of

\bm{g}_{\delta}(\bm{t})
;

4

\bm{t}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\bm{t}+\alpha\,\bm{s}
;

5

Fit
(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
on the last vertex
\bm{s}
;

6

Output:

\bm{s}
and
\bm{\beta}
.

Algorithm 1

COMBSS-GLM

The rationale for increasing
\delta
gradually, rather than using a
fixed value, rests on three properties established in this paper.
First, by Proposition
1
,

f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
is continuous and non-decreasing in
\delta

for each fixed
\bm{t}
; in particular, the relaxed objective and its
minimisers change
continuously
as
\delta
varies.
Second, at
\delta=0
with
\lambda=0
, the value function is constant
in
\bm{t}
(since the inner minimisation over
\bm{\beta}
is equivalent, via
the substitution
\bm{\beta}_{u}\mapsto\bm{t}\odot\bm{\beta}_{u}
, to an unconstrained
minimisation that is independent of
\bm{t}
), hence trivially convex,
and the landscape admits effective gradient-based exploration.
Third, by Theorem
1
, once
\delta

reaches
\delta_{\mathrm{conc}}
,
f_{\delta,\lambda}
becomes concave
over
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, guaranteeing that every minimiser is a binary corner

\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
The geometric homotopy schedule therefore continuously deforms the
relaxed objective from this tractable small-
\delta
regime to a
concave regime whose global minimisers are exactly the discrete subsets
sought (at
\delta_{\max}=\delta_{\mathrm{conc}}
), steering the
Frank–Wolfe iterates along this continuous path toward an optimal or
near-optimal binary corner.

Algorithm
1
initializes at the interior
point
\bm{t}_{0}:=\frac{k}{p-m}\bm{1}
, the centroid of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, and
from a small curvature level
\delta_{\min}>0
, which is then increased
geometrically as
\delta_{i}=\delta_{\min}r^{i}
with
r>1
.
At each iteration, we compute an envelope (sub)gradient

\bm{g}_{\delta}
of the value function
f_{\delta,\lambda}
at the
current
\bm{t}
and
\delta
.
The Frank–Wolfe update requires solving the
linear minimization
oracle
(LMO) over the feasible set
\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
Given the current envelope (sub)gradient
\bm{g}_{\delta_{i}}(\bm{t})
, the
LMO is the linear program

\bm{s}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\ \langle\bm{g}_{\delta_{i}}(\bm{t}),\,\bm{u}\rangle.

(9)

Because the objective in (
9
) is linear and
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is a
polytope, an optimal solution is attained at an extreme point of

\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
As noted earlier, these extreme points are precisely the binary
vertices in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
; hence the LMO admits a binary solution

\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
Moreover, for any
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
we have

\langle\bm{g},\bm{s}\rangle=\sum_{j:s_{j}=1}g_{j}
, so minimizing

\langle\bm{g},\bm{s}\rangle
reduces to selecting the
k
smallest
entries of
\bm{g}_{\delta_{i},\lambda}(\bm{t})
(breaking ties
arbitrarily) and setting the corresponding components of
\bm{s}
to

1
, with the rest set to
0
.
We then update
\bm{t}
by the explicit convex combination

\bm{t}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\bm{t}+\alpha\bm{s}
, which moves
\bm{t}

toward this sparse vertex without requiring any projection.
The procedure terminates either after
N
iterations or once
\bm{t}

is within
\varepsilon
(in max-norm) of the current vertex,
indicating that
\bm{t}
is close to a binary corner.
Finally, we refit the GLM on the selected support
\bm{s}
using

X_{[\bm{s}]}
to obtain the
reported coefficients.

3.1
Column Normalization and Invariance of the Selection Problem

For numerical stability, we apply a column-wise normalization to the
non-intercept columns of the design matrix.
This preprocessing does not change the subset-selection problem, since
it corresponds to a simple reparameterization.

Let
X_{:j}
denote the
j
th column of
X
for
j=0,1,\dots,p
,
where
X_{:0}=\bm{1}
is the intercept column.
For
j=1,\dots,p
, define the column lengths
v_{j}:=\|X_{:j}\|_{2}
,
and assume
v_{j}>0
(columns with
v_{j}=0
can be removed).
Set
v_{0}:=1
and let
\bm{v}:=(v_{0},\dots,v_{p})^{\top}
.
Define the normalized design

\widehat{X}:=X\,\diag(1/\bm{v}),

(10)

so that
\widehat{X}_{:0}=X_{:0}
and
\widehat{X}_{:j}=X_{:j}/v_{j}
for
j\geq 1
.

To see the invariance, recall that the log-likelihood depends on
predictors only through the linear predictor

\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{j}\beta_{j}
.
Since
v_{j}>0
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{j}\beta_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Bigl(\frac{x_{j}}{v_{j}}\Bigr)\,(v_{j}\beta_{j}).

Thus, defining
\widehat{\beta}_{j}:=v_{j}\beta_{j}
(for
j\geq 1
) and

\widehat{x}_{j}:=x_{j}/v_{j}
leaves the linear predictor unchanged:

\beta_{0}+\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}=\beta_{0}+\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\,\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
.
Consequently, any likelihood-based subset-selection formulation
(including (
4
) and its relaxation) can be equivalently
posed using
\widehat{X}
in place of
X
, with a one-to-one correspondence
between coefficient vectors.
After the algorithm outputs a support
\bm{s}
, we refit the
corresponding low-dimensional GLM on the
original
design
matrix
X
to report coefficients on the original scale.

3.2
Fast Inner Fits and Gradients via Ridge Solvers

Due to the Frank–Wolfe step updates, we compute
\bm{g}(\bm{t})
only at
interior points
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
.
At such points, it is convenient to rewrite the inner objective so
that
\bm{t}
appears only through an
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-penalty, enabling the use
of standard ridge-penalized GLM solvers.

Recall that the inner objective is

h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\beta_{0},T_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}.

For an interior point
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
, the change of variables

\xi_{0}:=\beta_{0}
and
\bm{\xi}:=T_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}
gives

\bm{\beta}=T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\bm{\xi}
and

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\xi_{0},\bm{\xi};\mathcal{D})+\lambda\|T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\xi_{0},\bm{\xi};\mathcal{D})+\Bigl\|\bigl(\lambda I+\delta\,\Gamma_{\bm{t}}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\bm{\xi}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2},

(11)

so that
f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\min_{\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\;\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})
.
Using the envelope theorem from
35
,

\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\nabla_{\bm{t}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\widetilde{\xi}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}),

where
(\widetilde{\xi}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}})
is any minimizer.
Since the negative log-likelihood term does not depend on
\bm{t}
, and
the penalty is coordinate-separable,

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\bigl(\lambda I+\delta\,\Gamma_{\bm{t}}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\bm{\xi}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-m}\left(\frac{\lambda+\delta}{t_{j}^{2}}-\delta\right)\xi_{m+j}^{2},

(12)

it follows that for each
j=1,\dots,p-m
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{j}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})=-2(\lambda+\delta)\,\frac{\xi_{m+j}^{2}}{t_{j}^{3}},

and hence

\nabla_{\bm{t}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})=-2(\lambda+\delta)\,\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{m+1}^{2}/t_{1}^{3}\\
\vdots\\
\xi_{p}^{2}/t_{p-m}^{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Using solvers without coefficient-wise penalties.

While packages such as
glmnet
in
R
allow
coefficient-wise ridge weights, many solvers expose only a uniform

\mathcal{L}_{2}
-penalty.
To take advantage of such solvers, fix an interior
\bm{t}
and define

\omega_{j}(\bm{t}):=\begin{cases}\lambda,&j=1,\dots,m,\\[2.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda+\delta}{t_{j-m}^{2}}-\delta,&j=m+1,\dots,p.\end{cases}

Introduce the reparameterization

\bm{\theta}=\sqrt{\bm{\omega}(\bm{t})}\odot\bm{\xi}
,
i.e.,
\theta_{j}=\sqrt{\omega_{j}(\bm{t})}\,\xi_{j}
, and scale the design
columns accordingly using

\widetilde{X}_{\bm{t}}:=X\,\diag\!\bigl(\bm{\omega}(\bm{t})^{-1/2}\bigr)
.
Then
X\bm{\xi}=\widetilde{X}_{\bm{t}}\bm{\theta}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{j}(\bm{t})\,\xi_{j}^{2}=\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}
, so the
inner problem can be solved with any ridge GLM solver on

(\widetilde{X}_{\bm{t}},\bm{y})
using a uniform
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-penalty.
After solving for
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
, recover

\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}=\bm{\omega}(\bm{t})^{-1/2}\odot\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
.

3.3
Uniqueness of the Inner Minimizer on the Interior

Fix an interior point
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
.

No mandatory (unpenalized) variables

If
m=0
, then for any interior point
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p}
we have

\Gamma_{\bm{t}}^{2}=I-T_{\bm{t}}^{2}
with strictly positive diagonal, and

\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bigl(\lambda+\delta(1-t_{j}^{2})\bigr)\beta_{j}^{2},

so
h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
is
strongly convex

in
\bm{\beta}
(even when
\lambda=0
, since
\delta>0
and

t_{j}\in(0,1)
).
Consequently, whenever
-\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})
is convex in

(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
and the minimum is attained, the coefficient
minimizer is unique.
Under standard additional conditions ensuring strict convexity in the
intercept direction, the pair
(\widetilde{\beta}_{\bm{t},0},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\bm{t}})
is
unique.
The same conclusions hold for the reparameterized inner objective

\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})
, since for interior
\bm{t}

the change of variables
(\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})=(\beta_{0},T_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta})
is
one-to-one.

Intercept and
m
mandatory variables

When an intercept and
m
mandatory coefficients are left unscaled by

\bm{t}
, the quadratic term splits as

\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-m}\bigl(\lambda+\delta(1-t_{j}^{2})\bigr)\beta_{m+j}^{2},

so the non-mandatory coefficients always carry a strictly positive
quadratic weight on the interior.
Existence of a minimizer follows, for example, if

-\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})
is convex and coercive in the unpenalized
directions (intercept and any mandatory coefficients); see, e.g.,

(
27
, Chapters 2–3)
.
Uniqueness holds whenever the overall inner objective is strictly
convex in
(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
; a sufficient condition is strict
convexity of
-\ell(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta};\mathcal{D})
, while taking
\lambda>0

ensures strong convexity in all mandatory coefficients (and in all
coefficients if
m=0
).

Non-unique inner minimizer

Even if the inner minimizer is not unique, the algorithm remains
well-defined.
Since for every fixed
(\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})
the map

\bm{t}\mapsto\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})
is continuously
differentiable on
(0,1)^{p-m}
, Danskin–Clarke envelope results
apply to the value function

f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\min_{\xi_{0},\bm{\xi}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})
,
and the subdifferential satisfies

\partial f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\operatorname{conv}\Bigl\{\nabla_{\bm{t}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\xi_{0},\bm{\xi}):(\xi_{0},\bm{\xi})\in\mathcal{M}(\bm{t})\Bigr\}.

Hence, for any
(\widetilde{\xi}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}})\in\mathcal{M}(\bm{t})
, the vector

\bm{g}(\bm{t})=\nabla_{\bm{t}}\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\widetilde{\xi}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}})

is a valid generalized subgradient for use in Step
1
.
When
\mathcal{M}(\bm{t})
is a singleton, this reduces to the ordinary gradient

\bm{g}(\bm{t})=\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
.

3.4
Properties of the Objective Function

We now establish continuity and large-
\delta
concavity properties of the relaxed objective
f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
. Proofs of all the results reported here are in the supplemetary material.

Proposition 1

(Monotonicity and continuity in
\delta
)
.

For every fixed
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
, the map

\delta\mapsto f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
is
monotone non-decreasing
and continuous in
\delta>0
.

Let
X_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p-m)}
denote the submatrix of
non-mandatory columns.
For fixed
(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
and interior
\bm{t}
, write

\bm{\beta}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-m}
for the non-mandatory coefficients and

D_{\bm{\beta}_{u}}:=\diag(\bm{\beta}_{u})
.
Define
H_{\eta}(\eta):=\nabla_{\eta\eta}^{2}\!\left(-\frac{1}{n}\ell\right)(\eta)
,
the Hessian of
-\frac{1}{n}\ell
with respect to the linear
predictor
\eta
.
Let

\eta(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}):=\eta_{0}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})+X_{u}(\bm{t}\odot\bm{\beta}_{u})
,
where
\eta_{0}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
collects the intercept and
mandatory-feature contribution.
Then

\nabla_{\bm{t}\bm{t}}^{2}h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})=D_{\bm{\beta}_{u}}\,X_{u}^{\top}H_{\eta}\!\big(\eta(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})\big)X_{u}\,D_{\bm{\beta}_{u}}-2\delta D_{\bm{\beta}_{u}}^{2},

where the ridge term
\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
does not contribute as it
is constant in
\bm{t}
.

Theorem 1

(Concavity threshold in
\bm{t}
)
.

Assume
X_{u}^{\top}H_{\eta}(\eta)X_{u}\preceq 2\delta I
for all
\eta
.
Then, for every fixed
(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
, the map

\bm{t}\mapsto h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})
is concave on

(0,1)^{p-m}
.
Consequently,
f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\inf_{\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}}h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\beta_{0},\bm{\beta})

is concave on
(0,1)^{p-m}
(and extends concavely to
\mathcal{T}_{k}
), so
every minimizer of
\min_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})

can be chosen at a corner
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

For logistic and baseline multinomial regression, the standard
curvature bounds

H_{\eta}(\eta)\preceq\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{4n}I,&\text{logistic},\\[3.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{2n}I,&\text{multinomial},\end{cases}

yield the explicit sufficient thresholds. Writing

\nu_{\max}
for the largest eigenvalue of
X_{u}^{\top}X_{u}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{conc}}=\begin{cases}\nu_{\max}/(8n),&\text{logistic},\\
\nu_{\max}/(4n),&\text{multinomial}.\end{cases}

(13)

Remark 1
.

The Hessian formula above is stated for the binary case in which

\bm{\beta}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-m}
is a vector.
In the multinomial setting,
B_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-m)\times(C-1)}
is a
matrix and the Hessian of
h
with respect to
\bm{t}
sums contributions
from each of the
C-1
class-specific coefficient vectors.
The bound
H_{\eta}(\eta)\preceq\frac{1}{2n}I
follows from the fact that
the per-observation multinomial curvature matrix

\Sigma_{i}=\diag(\pi_{i})-\pi_{i}\pi_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(C-1)\times(C-1)}

has largest eigenvalue at most
\tfrac{1}{2}
, as can be shown
via the Gershgorin circle theorem (replacing the logistic bound

\pi_{i}(1-\pi_{i})\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
).
Theorem
1
and the threshold

\nu_{\max}/(4n)
hold for the multinomial case by the same proof.

Remark 2

(Computational complexity)
.

Algorithm
1
executes
2N
iterations, each
requiring one call to a penalised GLM solver plus
O(p)
overhead
(gradient extraction via Danskin’s theorem and a partial sort to identify
the
k
smallest components). The total cost is thus

2N\times T_{\mathrm{solver}}(n,p,C)
, where

T_{\mathrm{solver}}(n,p,C)
is the cost of a single penalised GLM fit.
With a modest budget such as
N=25
, this is comparable to fitting a
regularisation path with
50
penalty values.

4
Simulation Studies

We evaluate the performance of the proposed best subset selection
method for logistic regression through a series of simulation
experiments in both low-dimensional and high-dimensional settings.
The simulation design closely follows the linear model framework
established in
16
and
29
, adapted here to the
binary response setting.

4.1
Simulation Design

Data are generated from a logistic regression model.
Specifically, the binary response
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,

i=1,\ldots,n
, is drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with success
probability

P(y_{i}=1\mid\bm{x}_{i})=\frac{\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}{1+\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})},

(14)

where
\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the true coefficient
vector.
The intercept is fixed at
\beta_{0}=0.2
to ensure an approximately
balanced proportion of zeros and ones in the simulated responses.
Each row
\bm{x}_{i}
of the predictor matrix
X
is generated
independently from a multivariate normal distribution with zero mean
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
with diagonal elements

\Sigma_{j,j}=1
and off-diagonal elements

\Sigma_{i,j}=\rho^{|i-j|}
,
i\neq j
.
We vary the correlation parameter over

\rho\in\{0,\,0.2,\,0.4,\,0.6\}
to examine the effect of
predictor correlation on the performance of the methods. In these simulations, no mandatory variables are kept
(m=0)
.

We consider two configurations for the true coefficient vector

\bm{\beta}^{*}
:

•

Case 1: The first
k_{0}=10
components of

\bm{\beta}^{*}
are equal to
1
and all other components
are equal to
0
.

•

Case 2: The first
k_{0}=10
components of

\bm{\beta}^{*}
decay as
\beta_{i}^{*}=0.5^{\,i-1}
for

i=1,\ldots,k_{0}
, while all other components are equal to
0
.

For the low-dimensional setting we take
n=200
and
p=30
, and
for the high-dimensional setting
n=200
and
p=1000
.
For each combination of case, dimension, and correlation level, we
perform 50 independent replications.

4.2
Competing Methods and Tuning

We compare the proposed COMBSS-GLM method
(Algorithm
1
) against three penalized
likelihood methods: the Lasso
(
38
)
, the smoothly
clipped absolute deviation penalty (SCAD)
(
8
)
, and
the minimax concave penalty (MCP)
(
40
)
, all available
through the
ncvreg
package in
R

(
4
)
.

Algorithm
1
is executed for each candidate
model size
k=1,\ldots,20
using
N=25
iterations and a learning rate
\alpha=0.01
.
The optimal model size
k_{\mathrm{opt}}
is then selected using an
independent test set of
10{,}000
observations generated from the
same data-generation mechanism, by choosing the model size that yields
the lowest misclassification error, where predicted class labels are
obtained by thresholding the estimated probabilities at
0.5
.

For the Lasso, SCAD, and MCP, we fit each method over a grid of 50
regularization parameters
\lambda
, log-spaced from
\lambda_{\max}

down to a small fraction of
\lambda_{\max}

as implemented in
glmnet
package.
For each method, we choose
\lambda
to maximize classification
accuracy on the same independent test set of
10{,}000
observations,
and report the corresponding selected variable subset as the final
model.

4.3
Performance Metrics

We assess the methods using several performance metrics.
To evaluate
variable selection quality
, we compare the
selected set
\hat{S}
to the true active set

S_{0}=\{j:\beta_{j}^{*}\neq 0\}
and report:

•

Sensitivity (true positive rate):

|\hat{S}\cap S_{0}|/|S_{0}|
.

•

Specificity (true negative rate):

|\hat{S}^{c}\cap S_{0}^{c}|/|S_{0}^{c}|
, where

S^{c}=\{1,\ldots,p\}\setminus S
.

•

Selection accuracy: proportion of predictors whose
inclusion status matches the truth,

(|\hat{S}\cap S_{0}|+|\hat{S}^{c}\cap S_{0}^{c}|)/p
.

•

F1 score: harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity.

•

Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC): a balanced
measure of binary classification quality for the selected variable
set, which accounts for all four entries of the confusion matrix
and remains informative even when class sizes are unequal

(
5
)
.

In addition, we report
prediction accuracy
on the test set,
defined as the proportion of test observations for which the predicted
class label
\hat{y}_{i}=\mathbb{I}(\hat{p}_{i}>0.5)
agrees with
the true response
y_{i}
.
All metrics are averaged over the 50 replications, and standard errors
are reported.

nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) selected from the original High-Density Rice Array
(HDRA, 700k SNPs)
(
26
)
.

We applied our proposed method after applying standard preprocessing procedures, detailed in
39
. In summary, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) loci were excluded if
their call rates were below
95\%
, their minor allele frequency (MAF) was less than

5\%
, or if they deviated significantly from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Missing
genotype values for each SNP, which accounted for no more than
5\%
of the values
for any single marker, were imputed using the mean of the available non-missing values
for that SNP. After preprocessing, the resultant genotype data were scaled so that
each SNP had zero mean and unit variance, producing a design matrix of size
n=1155

and
p=158{,}210
. The phenotypic data consisted of continuous measurements of the average grain length of rice accessions, which exhibited a bimodal distribution with a
separation around
6
mm. The response variable was then dichotomised into two categories according to whether the grain length exceeded
6
mm, allowing the resulting binary outcome to
serve as the dependent variable in a logistic regression setting. Additionally, to account for
possible population stratification, the genotype data were preprocessed using the singular
value decomposition (SVD). For further details on the data preparation
process, readers are referred to
39
. We provide the final design
matrix alongside the corresponding binary outcome in the GitHub repository accompanying the paper.

The COMBSS-GLM methodology allowed for the identification of the best subset of predictors for model sizes, ranging from
k=1,\ldots,20
. The best-subset inclusion
path for models of size
k=1
to
k=10

SNP-3.16732086

within the

(

24
)
, was
consistently selected across all model sizes. This SNP has been previously reported
to be strongly associated with rice grain length and is known to play a crucial role in
regulating the trait
(
26
;
39
)
. Additionally, a cluster of
highly correlated SNPs located on chromosome 3 was repeatedly selected across models
of varying sizes, a result that is consistent with existing findings.

amplify associations with markers that would otherwise exhibit weaker signals under a
continuous outcome. Nonetheless, our method’s robust performance in recovering
previously recognised loci, as well as identifying potential novel regions of interest
in a computationally efficient fashion, demonstrates its utility for high-dimensional
studies such as GWAS.

Cancer Classification Using Gene Expression Data

To further demonstrate the generality of the proposed methods, we appy the COMBSS-GLM using the multinomial likelihood(Section S1 in the supplementary material) to a well-known cancer classification problem involving high-dimensional gene expression data. 6
Conclusion

We have presented a continuous optimisation method for best subset
selection in generalised linear models, extending the COMBSS approach
of
29
from linear regression to the broader GLM
family. The method replaces the combinatorial search over
\binom{p}{k}

candidate subsets with a smooth optimisation on a simplex slice, solved
by a Frank–Wolfe homotopy that requires only one penalised GLM fit per
iteration. We established sufficient conditions under which the relaxed
objective becomes concave in the selection weights, guaranteeing that
global minimisers correspond to valid subsets, and derived explicit
thresholds for logistic and multinomial models.

Simulations showed that the method consistently matches or outperforms
the Lasso, SCAD, and MCP in sparse recovery and
predictive accuracy, particularly when
p\gg n
. In a rice GWAS with

p=158{,}210
SNPs, the method recovered known trait-associated loci
within minutes on standard hardware. In the Khan SRBCT problem
(
C=4
,
p=2{,}308
,
n=63
), COMBSS-GLM achieved perfect test
classification with only
12
genes, more than
65\%
fewer than the
multinomial group Lasso.

Natural extensions include other exponential-family members (e.g. Poisson, negative binomial), systematic comparison with mixed-integer
optimisation solvers
(
2
)
, and incorporation of
group-structured sparsity (in the same spirit as
25
) for applications such as pathway-based
genomic analyses.

We briefly specialize the Boolean-relaxation framework to multinomial
regression with
C
classes, using class
C
as the baseline.
Let
\mathcal{D}=(X,\bm{y})
, where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the design matrix
(excluding the intercept) and
y_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,C\}
.
We parameterise the model by an intercept vector

\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C-1}
and a coefficient matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(C-1)}

(column
c
corresponds to class
c
).
For
i\in[n]
and
c\in[C-1]
, define the scores

\eta_{ic}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B):=\beta_{0,c}+x_{i}^{\top}B_{:c},

and probabilities

\pi_{ic}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B):=\frac{\exp(\eta_{ic}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B))}{1+\sum_{d=1}^{C-1}\exp(\eta_{id}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B))}\quad(c\in[C-1]),

and

\pi_{iC}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B):=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{d=1}^{C-1}\exp(\eta_{id}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B))}.

Let
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(C-1)}
be the one-hot encoding,

Y_{ic}=\mathbb{I}(y_{i}=c)
for
c\in[C-1]
.
The log-likelihood
\ell(\bm{\beta}_{0},B;\,\mathcal{D})
is
equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C-1}Y_{ic}\,\eta_{ic}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B)-\log\Bigl(1+\sum_{d=1}^{C-1}\exp(\eta_{id}(\bm{\beta}_{0},B))\Bigr)\right).

Boolean relaxation with mandatory features.

Assume
m
mandatory features (with indices
1,\ldots,m
without loss
generality). As in the binary case, for
\bm{t}\in[0,1]^{p-m}
define the
diagonal matrices

(T_{\bm{t}})_{jj}=\begin{cases}1,&j\leq m,\\
t_{j-m},&j>m,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Gamma_{\bm{t}}=\sqrt{I-T_{\bm{t}}^{2}}.

Given
\delta>0
and
\lambda\geq 0
, define the penalised objective

h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\bm{\beta}_{0},B)=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\bm{\beta}_{0},T_{\bm{t}}B;\,\mathcal{D})+\lambda\|B\|_{F}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}B\|_{F}^{2},

(S1)

and the value function

f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t}):=\min_{\bm{\beta}_{0},B}\,h_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\bm{\beta}_{0},B).

The relaxed subset-selection problem is

\min_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})
with

\mathcal{T}_{k}=\{\bm{t}\in[0,1]^{p-m}:\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{t}=k\}
.

Inner solves and gradients on the interior.

For interior
\bm{t}\in(0,1)^{p-m}
, it is convenient to move
\bm{t}
entirely into a quadratic penalty.
Let
\bm{\xi}_{0}:=\bm{\beta}_{0}
and
\Xi:=T_{\bm{t}}B
(so
B=T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\Xi
)
and define

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi;\,\mathcal{D})+\lambda\|T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\Xi\|_{F}^{2}+\delta\|\Gamma_{\bm{t}}T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\Xi\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\ell(\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi;\,\mathcal{D})+\Bigl\|(\lambda I+\delta\,\Gamma_{\bm{t}}^{2})^{1/2}T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\Xi\Bigr\|_{F}^{2}.

Then
f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\min_{\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi}\,\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi)
,
and by Danskin’s theorem, for any minimizer
(\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{0},\widetilde{\Xi})
,

\nabla f_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t})=\nabla_{\bm{t}}\,\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{0},\widetilde{\Xi}),

or a valid subgradient if the minimizer is not unique.
Since the likelihood term does not depend on
\bm{t}
, we differentiate only
the penalty. Using
T_{\bm{t}}
and
\Gamma_{\bm{t}}
,

\displaystyle\Bigl\|(\lambda I+\delta\,\Gamma_{\bm{t}}^{2})^{1/2}T_{\bm{t}}^{-1}\Xi\Bigr\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\Xi_{j:}\|_{2}^{2}

(S2)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum_{j=1}^{p-m}\left(\frac{\lambda+\delta}{t_{j}^{2}}-\delta\right)\|\Xi_{m+j,:}\|_{2}^{2},

(S3)

and hence for
j=1,\ldots,p-m
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{j}}\,\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi)=-2(\lambda+\delta)\,\frac{\|\Xi_{m+j,:}\|_{2}^{2}}{t_{j}^{3}},

and

\nabla_{\bm{t}}\,\phi_{\delta,\lambda}(\bm{t},\bm{\xi}_{0},\Xi)=-2(\lambda+\delta)\begin{pmatrix}\|\Xi_{m+1,:}\|_{2}^{2}/t_{1}^{3}\\
\vdots\\
\|\Xi_{p,:}\|_{2}^{2}/t_{p-m}^{3}\end{pmatrix}.

\omega_{j}(\bm{t}):=\begin{cases}\lambda,&j=1,\ldots,m,\\[2.0pt]

Appendix S6
Hyperparameter Tuning

The concavity threshold
\delta_{\mathrm{conc}}
from
Theorem
1
provides a natural anchor
for the geometric penalty schedule. For logistic and multinomial regression, we can compute it using
\nu_{\max}
as shown in (
13
).
The eigenvalue
\nu_{\max}
is not available in closed form for general
X_{u}
, but
can be computed efficiently by power iteration: starting from a random
unit vector
v
, one repeatedly forms
v\leftarrow X_{u}^{\top}X_{u}v/\|X_{u}^{\top}X_{u}v\|_{2}
and estimates
\nu_{\max}\approx\|X_{u}v\|_{2}^{2}
.
Convergence is geometric and requires only matrix–vector products with

X_{u}
and
X_{u}^{\top}
, making the cost negligible relative to the inner
solves.

Given
\delta_{\mathrm{conc}}
, in our simulations, we set

\delta_{\max}=\delta_{\mathrm{conc}},\qquad\delta_{\min}=10^{-3}\,\delta_{\mathrm{conc}},

so that the schedule begins well below the concavity threshold.

Controlling complexity via
N

The iteration budget
N
is the primary user-facing parameter governing
computational cost: by Remark
2
, the total cost of
Algorithm
1
is exactly
N
calls to the inner
GLM solver, so the user can trade accuracy against runtime by adjusting

N
alone, without changing any other hyperparameter.
Importantly, the geometric penalty schedule traverses the full range

[\delta_{\min},\delta_{\max}]
for any
N\geq 2
, with growth rate

r=(\delta_{\max}/\delta_{\min})^{1/N}
; a larger
N
takes finer steps
along this path but reaches the same endpoint
\delta_{\max}=\delta_{\mathrm{conc}}
.
Consequently, the subset selected by the algorithm is not sensitive to

N
once
N
is moderately large: the concave landscape at

\delta_{\max}
is identical regardless of
N
, and the Frank–Wolfe
iterates are guided toward the same binary corner.
