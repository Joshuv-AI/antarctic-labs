Ion-Specific Modulation of Ferri/Ferrocyanide-Driven Gold Dissolution Uncovered by Quantitative Quartz Crystal Microbalance Analysis
Authors: Zee Albeshir, Patrick F. Strobel, Aylin Aykanat, Jeffrey Mark Halpern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gold electrodes in ferri/ferro cyanide (FFCN) are widely treated as chemically stable, outer sphere benchmark systems, despite the growing reports of drift in electrochemical measurements. Here, we use quartz crystal microbalance to quantify FFCN-induced gold mass loss and isolate the roles of anion, cation, pH, buffer chemistry, and self-assembled monolayer. In the absence of FFCN, all solutions show positive mass shifts, indicating that the supporting electrolytes alone do not produce measurable gold loss over the experimental time scale. With FFCN present, gold etching becomes highly tunable: changing the supporting electrolyte, adjusting the pH, and changing the buffers significantly shifts the magnitude of gold dissolution. Additional oxygen- and solution-handling experiments show that oxygen depletion strongly reduces gold loss, while solution aging and ambient light exposure increases FFCN-drive gold dissolution. Neutral, long, well packed self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) attenuate etching, while charged or short-chain SAMs significantly accelerate gold loss. Overall, we map a systematic landscape where FFCN on gold behaves as a chemically active etchant, not an inert probe. Thus, electrochemical measurements must explicitly account for FFCN etching, which is influenced by salt, pH, buffer, and surface chemistry, and solution handling conditions.

Gold remains the benchmark substrate for interfacial electrochemistry because it supports a wide aqueous potential window, enables fast, outer-sphere electron transfer, and can support highly ordered self-assembled monolayers (SAMs), especially with alkylthiols through robust Au–S interactions. , Consequently, gold is routinely paired with a ferri/ferro cyanide (FFCN) redox couple, which is the canonical “inert” outer sphere benchmark for interrogating charge transfer phenomena and heterogeneous rate constants by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and cyclic voltammetry. , In this conventional picture, FFCN is treated as a passive reporter of electron transfer at a chemically stable gold interface, rather than a species that can measurably alter the electrode itself.

In practice, the use of FFCN as a redox couple has been used in a large range of applications. FFCN is used to probe the modification and stability of SAMs and other molecules and biomolecules on surfaces, − to evaluate antifouling coatings, validate sensors, − and to understand the electronic properties of materials. , Across these applications, the stability and reproducibility of the electrochemical system is crucial for plausible interpretation of electrochemical findings; the electrode surface and the SAM must remain chemically and structurally stable throughout the measurement. However, several electrochemical impedance studies have noted a persistent nonlinear drift in charge transfer resistance during impedimetric measurements. − Some of this drift has been attributed to structural relaxation or gradual desorption of the SAM, but this cannot explain instabilities observed on bare gold or in systems where SAM desorption is unlikely on the experimental time scale. ,, Some studies suggest that a Prussian Blue-like film could cause the nonlinear, Langmuir isotherm drift seen in EIS; however, the identity of the film is up to debate and clear Prussian Blue growth on gold is mainly seen at high anodic potentials (∼0.8 V vs Ag/AgCl). − More fundamentally, electrochemical and photochemical studies have shown that in cyanide-containing media, gold does not remain inert: it can dissolve via Elsner-type cyanidation, where cyanide ligands stabilize Au­(I) as [Au­(CN)2]−. , Recent reports have just begun to investigate FFCN-mediated etching of gold, linking dissolution to factors such as applied potential, local pH, and light exposure. ,−

Despite growing evidence of FFCN’s role as a potential etchant, there is a lack of systematic, quantitative understanding of how solution environment and interfacial chemistry control FFCN-driven gold dissolution. Prior studies typically focus on a narrow subset of solution conditions, but FFCN-mediated etching is a complex, multiparametric, chemical process governed by solution chemistry and surface interactions, which have yet to be systematically studied. From a broader electrochemistry standpoint, there is a fundamental gap in understanding how ion adsorption, interfacial pH, and SAM chemistry modulate FFCN-driven gold dissolution.

In this work, we use Quartz Crystal Microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D) to quantify FFCN-induced gold mass loss without an applied voltage to deconvolute the influence of solution and interfacial parameters. We first show that in the absence of FFCN, a broad set of electrolytes produces only a small positive mass shift on gold, indicating ion adsorption on gold rather than gold removal. In contrast, once FFCN is present, gold etching becomes highly tunable: changing anion or cation identity, electrolyte concentration, pH, buffer identity, or SAM chemistry modulates gold loss by more than an order of magnitude. Results and Discussion

Gold Dissolution by FFCN Can Be Measured Using QCM

Gold dissolution in cyanide containing media is commonly described by Elsner-type chemistry, in which CN– ligands stabilize Au­(I) as soluble dicyanoaurate ([Au­(CN)2]−), and thereby enable continued oxidative dissolution ,− 1 4Au+8CN−+O2+2H2O→4[Au(CN)2]−+4OH−

Considering that [Fe­(CN)6]3– /[Fe­(CN)6]4– contains six cyanide ligands bound to iron (Figure a), we first asked whether the standard FFCN redox couple produces measurable gold loss under ambient conditions. To evaluate this, we exposed gold-coated QCM sensors to phosphate buffer (PB) with and without 5 mM FFCN. Representative QCM traces collected over four sequential exposure/rinse cycles showed that PB without FFCN produced a small decrease in frequency on each exposure (net mass gain), whereas PB with FFCN produced an immediate increase in frequency (net mass loss) with stable postrinse plateaus (Figure b). We quantified the change in the mass from the postrinse plateaus of the final three cycles using the Sauerbrey relation 2 Δm=−C×Δfn Where Δm is the change in mass, C is the mass sensitivity constant, Δf is the change in the resonance frequency, and n is the harmonic number. PB without FFCN yielded a small positive mass change, likely due to the adsorption of phosphate on the surface, while PB with FFCN produced a net negative mass change, indicative of gold loss under these conditions (Figure c). This was further confirmed using SEM, where surfaces exposed to PB remained relatively smooth. Whereas FFCN-exposed surfaces showed roughening and discrete etch pits, thus indicating that the mass loss observed in the presence of FFCN corresponds to physical loss of gold from the surface (Figure d).

To test which component of the FFCN is responsible for the loss, and whether dissolved oxygen was required for dissolution, we compared [Fe­(CN)6]3– (ferricyanide), [Fe­(CN)6]4– (ferrocyanide), and the FFCN couple under ambient and oxygen-depleted conditions (Figure e). Under ambient conditions, ferricyanide produced a small mass gain, whereas ferrocyanide caused substantial mass loss. The mixed FFCN solution gave an intermediate but still strongly negative response. The small mass gain observed for ferricyanide under ambient conditions is attributed to the formation of a Prussian blue-like film, as supported by the visible blue film and confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Figure S1). In contrast, under oxygen-depleted conditions, ferrocyanide and FFCN showed less mass loss compared to the ambient conditions (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 respectively); ferricyanide showed no significant difference between oxygen-depleted and ambient conditions. A possible thermodynamic basis for the difference between ferricyanide and ferrocyanide is the relative stability of the cyanide complexes. Reported cumulative formation constants place the cyanide complexes in the following order: ferricyanide > Au­(CN)2 – > ferrocyanide (log β = 42.0, 38.3, and 35.0, respectively). In that context, ferricyanide may retain cyanide more strongly than ferrocyanide, which may contribute to the different dissolution responses observed here.

To determine whether the dissolution response observed with ferrocyanide could be reproduced by a simple Fe­(II) salt, we examined Fe­(II) sulfate (5 mM) under both ambient and oxygen-depleted conditions (Figure S2). In both cases, Fe­(II) produced a positive mass shift rather than gold loss demonstrating that Fe­(II) alone is insufficient to reproduce the dissolution observed for ferrocyanide-containing solutions. Prior studies have shown that cyanide adsorption on gold is part of the dissolution chemistry itself. − Upon cyanide adsorption, the oxidation potential of gold has been reported to shift sufficiently for oxygen to act as the oxidant in cyanide mediated gold dissolution. , In that context, our results support a baseline picture in PB in which cyanide containing hexacyanoferrate species and dissolved oxygen act together to promote chemically driven gold loss at the interface.

To further evaluate whether gold dissolution produced Au-cyanide species, recovered solids from concentrated, oxygenated hexacyanoferrate solutions after Au exposure were analyzed by XPS (Figures S3,S4). Survey spectra showed little to no detectable Au in the recovered solids, indicating that any Au-containing product was present at low atomic concentration (Figure S3). Despite the low abundance, high-resolution Au 4f spectra revealed weak Au signals in the recovered ferrocyanide, ferricyanide, and FFCN samples, with the Au 4f7/2 peak shifted from 84.5 eV for metallic Au to ∼85.3–85.5 eV after exposure to gold (Figure S4a). The higher binding energy indicates formation of Au­(I), which is the expected oxidation state for AuCN/Au-cyanide species. While the C 1s and N 1s regions could not uniquely confirm AuCN formation likely due to the low concentrations, the detection of Au­(I) in the recovered solids supports formation of Au-cyanide species during hexacyanoferrate-driven gold dissolution.

Additionally, prior studies have shown that light exposure of ferrocyanide solutions can promote gold dissolution whereas ferricyanide remains comparatively unreactive toward gold under similar conditions. , We, therefore, examined whether solution light history affected the dissolution response in our system once dissolved oxygen was substantially reduced. Under oxygen-depleted experimental conditions, light does not measurably alter the dissolution response for freshly prepared solutions (Figure S5). In contrast, longer-term solution history did affect the response. One-week-old solutions stored either in the dark or under ambient light caused substantially greater gold dissolution than freshly prepared solutions, with the largest mass loss observed for the solution stored under ambient light (Figure S6).

Together these results define the baseline behavior of the hexacyanoferrate system in PB. PB without FFCN does not induce measurable gold dissolution over the experimental time scale, whereas ferrocyanide and the conventional FFCN mixture both produce substantial gold loss under ambient conditions. Ferricyanide does not cause significant etching, and oxygen depletion strongly reduces the dissolution response of all three hexacyanoferrate solutions in PB. Because the 1:1 ferri/ferrocyanide mixture is standard in electrochemical studies, we continue to use this mixture in the following sections to determine how changes in the electrolyte composition and interfacial chemistry shift this baseline reactivity.

Influence of Ion Identity

Because FFCN is commonly used in electrolytes chosen for electrochemical conductivity and compatibility, we next examined how ion identity influences FFCN-mediated gold dissolution. To test this, we varied both the anion identity (NaX) and cation identity (MCl) at fixed FFCN concentration and compared each condition to the original baseline experiments in PB with and without FFCN.

We first examined the response of gold to the supporting electrolytes in PB without FFCN (Figure a). Across the anion series (NaX, X = CO3 2–, F–, H2PO4 –, SO4 2–, ClO4 –, NO3 –, Cl–, Br–, I–) most supporting electrolytes produced only small positive mass changes, indicating that the salts themselves do not dissolve gold under our experimental conditions (Figure a). Relative to PB alone, F, – ClO4 –, NO3 –, Cl–, and Br– gave significantly smaller positive mass changes (p < 0.05), while CO3 2–, H2PO4 –, and SO4 2– were not statistically different from PB alone. I– however, showed a significant increase in adsorption compared to the other anions (p < 0.01). Thus, in the absence of FFCN, ion identity primarily alters the extent of interfacial mass accumulation on gold rather than driving measurable gold loss.

Adding FFCN reveals that the interfacial differences strongly reshape the dissolution response (Figure b). Relative to the baseline PB with FFCN, ClO4 –, NO3 –, H2PO4 –, and SO4 2– additions yielded statistically indistinguishable losses compared to the baseline PB with FFCN. In contrast, the halides and carbonates drove significantly greater mass loss (Cl–, p < 0.05; CO3 2–, F–, Br– p < 0.01), with I– addition caused more than a 100-fold increase in gold dissolution (p < 0.001). Importantly, chloride also caused significantly greater mass loss than PB with FFCN, which is especially relevant considering that chloride-containing electrolytes are commonly used in electrochemical studies. The larger response observed in the carbonate containing solution may additionally reflect the ability of carbonate media to increase the local pH and stabilize oxidized gold species, thereby increasing gold solubility. , Prior studies have shown that under oxidizing conditions, halides can facilitate oxidative dissolution by stabilizing Au-anion interfacial species. − Additionally, recent work on Au–Cl adlayers further suggests that chloride can actively modify interfacial charge distribution and local potential of gold. In this context, the larger gold losses observed for the halides relative to the PB with FFCN suggest anion-dependent changes at the gold interface that increase susceptibility to chemically driven dissolution.

Because iodide produced by far the largest amount of dissolution, this raised the question whether this behavior reflected an amplification of the baseline PB with FFCN mechanism or a distinct iodide-enabled pathway. We therefore examined ferrocyanide, ferricyanide, and the FFCN mixture under oxygen-depleted conditions for both NaI and NaCl, with Cl– included as a comparison because it is the most commonly used anion in electrochemical analysis (Figure S7). Under oxygen-depleted conditions, chlorine containing ferricyanide, ferrocyanide, and FFCN solutions all show only small mass changes, closely resembling the suppressed bassline behavior that was observed in PB with FFCN. In contrast, iodide containing solutions did not follow this pattern: ferricyanide with iodide caused significant mass loss, upward of 12 μg, FFCN with iodide showed a ∼5% decrease in mass loss compared to that under ambient conditions, and ferrocyanide showed very minimal mass loss. The larger mass loss observed for ferricyanide in the presence of NaI is consistent with, but does not by itself prove, an iodide-assisted dissolution pathway, in which ferricyanide may act as an oxidizing component that facilitates interfacial Au–I chemistry. ,,, Thus, these results suggest that iodide does not simply strengthen the baseline oxygen dependent FFCN response observed in PB, but instead shifts the system into a distinct dissolution regime.

We next examined the cation series (MCl, M = Li+, Na+, NH4 +, K+, Rb+, Cs+) to determine whether cation hydration influences the mass response (Figure c). In PB without FFCN, Na+ was the only cation that produced a statistically significant decrease in interfacial mass accumulation. All other cations produced modest positive mass changes that were statistically indistinguishable from PB alone under our conditions. Thus, in the absence of FFCN, changing the cation does not measurably alter the baseline interfacial mass response.

In the presence of FFCN, however, the interfacial cation identity influenced gold dissolution (Figure d). Li+, which is the smallest and most strongly hydrated cation, suppressed gold etching, and instead Li+ showed a modest degree of adsorption compared to PB with FFCN (p < 0.01). NH4 + showed a response similar to that of the PB with FFCN baseline, while Na+, K+, Rb+, and Cs+ all produced significantly greater mass loss (Na+, Cs+, p < 0.05; Rb+, p < 0.01; K+, p < 0.001). K+ gave the strongest etching response (−162.8 ± 6.8 ng cm–2), followed by Rb+ (−100.4 ± 2.8 ng cm–2), while Na+ (−82.1 ± 0.3 ng cm–2) and Cs+ (−79.2 ± 3.8 ng cm–2) showed more moderate increases in dissolution. These results suggest that cation identity influences FFCN-driven gold loss primarily through changes in interfacial solvation rather than a simple monatomic size trend. −

Collectively, these findings show that supporting electrolyte composition strongly modulates the baseline FFCN response, with both anions and cations changing the degree of gold dissolution at the interface.

Influence of Ion Concentration

We varied the concentration of NaCl and KCl to isolate electrolyte concentration effects on FFCN-driven dissolution (Figure a,b). In the PB without FFCN, increasing the salt concentration produced minor changes (Figure c). However, in PB with FFCN, raising the concentration of both NaCl and KCl significantly increased gold dissolution (Figure d). We hypothesize that as the salt concentrations increase, chloride concentrations are likely to generate more chemisorbed Cl– adlayers that polarize the gold surface and promote gold dissolution. Additionally, the added salt should allow for more ions to pack on the surface, reducing the length of the diffuse layer length, and thereby raising the interfacial activity of the redox couple at the gold surface (Figure b). , Increasing the concentration of KCl significantly increased etching compared to NaCl, further suggesting that the less hydrated cations, contribute more to gold etching.

Influence of PB pH on Gold Etching

Similarly to ion concentration and ion identity, the pH of the electrolyte can be tuned to understand system behavior. To examine how the pH of the solution affects FFCN dissolution in PB, the pH of 4 and 10 were compared to the pH 7 (Figure c, reference point). We selected three distinct pH values within the range of 4–10 where the redox couple is chemically stable. In PB without FFCN, we observe the largest mass adsorption at pH 4, significantly different from pH 7; no significant change in mass adsorption was found at pH 10 (Figure a). In bulk solution, phosphate shifts from being dominated by H2PO4 – at pH 4 to a roughly H2PO4 –/ HPO4 2– mixture near neutral pH and almost entirely HPO4 2– by pH 10. Native charge argument would, therefore, predict more adsorption at higher pH; however, our results show the opposite trend. This trend is reflective of interfacial phosphate behavior on gold: at the gold interface, the pK a of the phosphate species is substantially lower than in bulk, and H2PO4 – and HPO4 2– can further deprotonate upon adsorption to give predominately HPO4(ads) or PO4(ads) on gold. Therefore, pH 4 is likely contributing to greater formation of HPO4(ads) or PO4(ads) on the gold surface.

pH dependence of gold mass change (at pH 4, 7, and 10; listed left to right as shown in the panel). (a) PB without FFCN, showing positive mass changes across all pH indicating net adsorption. Significantly more adsorption is observed at pH 4. (b) PB with FFCN, showing strong pH-dependent gold loss. All raw QCM frequency time traces are provided in Figure S11. Statistical significance is denoted as- ns: p > 0.05, *: p < 0.05, ***: p < 0.001.

At mildly acidic pH, the relatively positive gold surface and lower OH– concentration likely allow phosphate to pack into a dense, moderately charged inner sphere layer with limited competition from OH–. As the pH increases to 7 and 10, the interfacial phosphate species become more deprotonated and OH– becomes more abundant, we therefore expect stronger electrostatic repulsion and ligand-exchange competition between the phosphate and the OH–, which limits further phosphate coverage.

The pH series defines a PB pH regime in which FFCN switches between protected and strongly etching behavior. At pH 4, the net mass change is only slightly negative, and the mass loss is significantly less mass loss than at pH 7. At pH 10, however, we observe much larger gold loss, with more than a 5-fold increase compared to pH 7 (Figure b). We attribute the protection at pH 4 to the same compact phosphate adlayer that maximizes adsorption in a phosphate-only electrolyte. Under alkaline conditions, abundant OH– favors the formation and stability of soluble gold-hydroxide/oxide species and the deprotonation of CN–, which supports sustained oxidative dissolution. This is supported by the Elsner reaction, where gold oxidation and complexation by cyanide are thermodynamically favored in basic media.

Influence of Buffer Identity on Gold Etching

Electrochemical measurements can be carried out in many different buffers depending on the application and compatibility. To test the influence of different buffers on gold etching, we evaluated multiple buffer identities at a fixed pH of 7 and a concentration of 10 mM, except for PBS, which was used at 11.8 mM (Figure a). In the buffer-only controls, phosphate buffered saline (PBS) showed a significant decrease while carbonate-bicarbonate (CBC) showed a significant increase in the mass adsorbed. All other buffers, independent of their charging at pH 7 and osmolarity, produced a small net-positive change in the mass adsorbed that was not significant compared to PB.

With the addition of FFCN, 4-(2-Hydroxyethyl)­piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), which is a zwitterionic buffer, yields the strongest suppression in gold dissolution relative to PB (Figure b). 2-(N-morpholino)­ethanesulfonic acid (MES) and PBS also significantly reduced gold dissolution, where CBC produced the largest mass dissolution of the tested buffers. Tris­(hydroxymethyl)­aminomethane (Tris) and acetate buffer behaved similarly to PB.

The CBC result is consistent with the results observed in Figure b, which shows that carbonate anions promote gold dissolution, likely by modifying the local pH and stabilizing oxidized gold species. Among the buffers tested, PBS was the only buffer that was not compositionally matched to the 10 mM buffers but was tested due to its widespread use in electrochemical systems. Interestingly, PBS produced less mass loss than PB or PB with added salt in the presence of FFCN. The mixed ion composition appears to alter the interfacial response and decreases gold dissolution. MES has been shown to have an adsorption layer on the surface, which may reduce the FFCN-induced gold dissolution.

Several of these buffers tested have buffering ranges outside of neutrality; thus, we examined their native pH windows. In MES with FFCN, moving from pH 7 to pH 6 increased the magnitude of gold dissolution (Figure S13a,b). In CBC at pH 10, the FFCN-induced mass loss is comparable to that observed in PB with FFCN at pH 10, which could indicate that under strong alkaline conditions, the pH effect dominates, and buffer identity becomes less important (Figure S13c,d). In acetate without FFCN, as we lower the pH, we observe additional mass loss (Figure S13e,f). While at first glance this appears to contradict the PB with FFCN pH series, where lowering the pH from 7 to 4 suppresses etching (Figure b), MES, CBC, and acetate buffers at their pK a show increased etching which likely reflects the different interfacial chemistries of these buffers (Figure S13).

Influence of SAM Identity on FFCN Etching

Different SAMs are functionalized on gold surfaces for various reasons, including for conjugation, passivation, and understanding of their underlying properties. To probe how the surface chemistry modulates FFCN-induced etching, we formed thiol-based SAMs of 6-mercaptohexanol (6-MCH), 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (6-MCA), 6-aminohexanethiol (6-AHT), 3-mercaptopropanol (3-MCP), and 11-mercaptoundecanol (11-MUD) on gold (Figure a). First, we evaluated whether the monolayers are stable in PB without FFCN. We show that all monolayers have a small positive change in the mass adsorbed (Figure S14); therefore, within this experimental time frame, the SAMs are stable and any decrease in the mass adsorbed during FFCN exposure can be attributed to FFCN.

(b) Net mass change on thiol-modified Au surfaces with different terminal groups after FFCN exposure. (c) Net mass change of thiol-modified Au surfaces with different chain lengths. (d, e) Representative SEM images of Au after exposure to FFCN for (d) 6-MCH and (e) 3-MCP. All raw QCM frequency time traces are provided in Figure S15.

When exposing the SAMs to PB with FFCN, the net mass losses follow the order 6-MCH < 6-MCA < 6-AHT, showing that the neutral SAM provided protection against FFCN etching, whereas SAMs bearing ionizable terminal groups accelerate dissolution (Figure b). At pH 7, 6-MCH presents a neutral OH terminus and forms a relatively dense SAM, thus mainly acting as a steric barrier that slightly reduces FFCN etching compared to the gold baseline (Figure b). In contrast, 6-MCA contains a carboxylic acid terminus and 6-AHT contains an amine terminus; however, the apparent pK a of surface confined carboxylic acid and amine groups can differ substantially from their bulk values, with prior studies showing higher apparent pK a values for both carboxylic acids and amine-terminated monolayers on gold surfaces , Recent work further shows that pH-dependent electrochemical responses of amine terminated SAMs can reflect coupled ionization and conformational change rather than protonation alone. Accordingly, 6-MCA and 6-AHT are best viewed as interfacial groups that can introduce charge and electrostatic effects within the SAM, and for the amine-terminated surface, likely favor the attraction of the anionic FFCN redox couple to the surface, leading to the greater mass loss observed for 6-AHT.

We then compared 6-MCH to 3-MCP and 11-MUD to test the influence of chain length (Figure c). While the neutral OH– remains unchanged, the shorter backbone chain significantly enhanced FFCN etching, likely by reducing lateral chain stabilization and increasing permeability. In contrast, 11-MUD, did not show measurable mass loss. Surface morphology further supports the mass change: Figure d shows a relatively smooth surface for 6-MCH, while Figure e shows pitting on the surface for 3-MCP. It is a well-established that when thiols desorb, they can lift small gold clusters and leave behind nanoscale pinholes, accordingly the higher pit density and large mass losses are consistent with more extensive thiol loss and gold dissolution. ,

Conclusions

We demonstrated that even under conditions that are widely assumed to be benign, FFCN in phosphate buffer continuously etches the gold electrode, and that the extent of etching is tunable by the surrounding chemical environment. Together, these results support an Elsner-type cyanide-mediated framework, while also showing that ion specific interfacial chemistry can potentially introduce additional pathways for gold dissolution. Using QCM, we define four practical regimes for FFCN driven gold dissolution:(A)Weakly interacting anions (e.g., NO3 –, ClO4 –) and strongly hydrated cations (Li+, NH4 +) behave as spectators and keep gold loss near the PB baseline, whereas specifically adsorbing anions (especially halides and carbonate) and weakly hydrated cations (K+, Rb+, Cs+) shift the system into greater dissolution.(B)In PB, varying pH from mildly acidic to neutral/basic alters the charge-state of phosphate on the interface. Therefore, acidic PB forms a more protective phosphate later with minimal gold dissolution, whereas neutral and more alkaline pH accelerates gold dissolution.(C)Zwitterionic buffers such as HEPES and MES shift the system to a more protected state, whereas carbonate-bicarbonate media drives gold dissolution.(D)Neutral SAMs modestly protect the gold from FFCN driven dissolution, whereas charged or short-chain SAMs amplify FFCN-driven etching.

As a result, this has direct consequences on the gold surface that can convolve electron transfer kinetics with condition-dependent gold dissolution. Our findings, therefore, argue that routine variations in salt or pH may further accelerate gold-etching, and surface instability, creating additional confounding factors and misinterpretation of the system.

From a practical standpoint, our results show the FFCN should not be treated as a universally inert redox probe on gold. Instead, its reactivity depends on the full experimental context, including supporting electrolyte, pH, buffer identity, storage age, light exposure history, and whether the solution is handled under ambient air. Accordingly, to minimize FFCN driven gold dissolution, solutions should be prepared before use and degassed prior to gold exposure. Solution composition and storage history should also be explicitly reported to avoid misinterpreting condition-dependent gold dissolution as changes in electron transfer behavior.
