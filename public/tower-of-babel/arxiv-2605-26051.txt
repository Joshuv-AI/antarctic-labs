1.
Introduction

One of the classical results in extremal set theory
is the Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem
[
EKR61
]
.
It states that for all positive integers
n

and
r
such that
n\geq 2r
and for any family

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r}
such that any
two sets in
\mathcal{F}
have a nonempty intersection,
we have
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{r-1}
.
In particular, the equality is achieved when
\mathcal{F}
consists
of all sets containing
1
.
Since then, this result has been generalized
in many different directions, leading to numerous
variants of problems about intersecting families.

One way to generalize the Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem
is to consider
t
-intersecting families.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r}
is said to be

t
-intersecting
if any two elements
A,B\in\mathcal{F}

have
|A\cap B|\geq t
.
After a series of partial results
by Frankl, Wilson, and Frankl and Füredi

[
Fra78
,
Wil84
,
FF91
]
,
Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
AK97
]
completely determined
the maximum size of a
t
-intersecting family of
\binom{[n]}{r}
.
In particular, they proved that any
t
-intersecting family

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r}
with maximum size must be
of the form

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\left\{A\in\binom{[n]}{r}:|A\cap[t+2k]|\geq t+k\right\}

for some
k
, up to permutation of elements.

Another direction of study is to replace
\binom{[n]}{r}

with different families.
One of the most well-studied families is the family of
permutations of
[n]
.
Let
\Sigma_{n}
be the collection of permutations of
[n]
.
Two permutations
\pi,\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}

intersect in at least
t
elements

if
\pi(i)=\sigma(i)
for at least
t
values of
i
.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
is
t
-intersecting

if any two permutations in
\mathcal{F}
intersect
in at least
t
elements.
We are interested in determining the maximum
size of a
t
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
.

One class of
t
-intersecting families is the family

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathcal{A}_{k}}=\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}:\sigma(i)=i\text{ for at least }t+k\text{ indices }i\in[t+2k]\}.

Note that
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is the trivial family
consisting of all permutations that fix
1,2,\dots,t

(so it has size
(n-t)!
).
Analogous to Ahlswede and Khachatrian’s theorem,
we have the following conjecture.

1
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
is a
t
-intersecting family,
then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\max_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}|
.
Moreover, if
|\mathcal{F}|=\max_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}|
, then

\mathcal{F}=\sigma\mathcal{A}_{k}\tau
for some
k

and permutations
\sigma
and
\tau
.

In particular, by comparing
|\mathcal{A}_{k}|

across different
k
, the conjecture implies that

\mathcal{A}_{0}
is the maximum family for all
t\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

The most recent progress towards resolving this
conjecture is due to Kupavskii, who showed

[
Kup26+
, Thm. 1]
that the conjecture holds for

t\leq(1-o(1))n
, together with a sharp stability result.
Upon closer inspection (cf.
Remark

1
),
his result holds for all

t\leq n-O\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
.
By enhancing this result with several new ideas,
namely the iterative spread approximation
and a more delicate counting argument,
we improve the range of
t
in which this conjecture
holds while maintaining the same stability result.

1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon)

such that for any
n>n_{0}
and
t<n-n^{5/7+\varepsilon}
,
both of the following hold.

(a)

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
is a
t
-intersecting family,
then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\max_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}|
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
is a
t
-intersecting family
not contained in
\sigma\mathcal{A}_{k}\tau

for any
\sigma,\tau\in\Sigma_{n}
,
then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\left(1-\tfrac{1}{e}+o(1)\right)\max_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}|
.

1.1.
Previous Work and Context

1
has seen significant progress over
the past 50 years.

The first progress towards proving
1
was
due to Frankl and Deza
[
FD77
]
, who
proved
1
when

n
is sufficiently large in terms of
n-t
.
Cameron and Ku, and Larose and Malvenuto

[
CK03
,
LM04
]

proved
1
for
t=1
.

The next significant breakthrough for higher
t
came when
Ellis, Friedgut, and Pilpel
[
EFP11
]
showed that

1
holds for
t\leq c\log\log n
for
some constant
c>0
.
Their proof used Hoffman’s bound
(upper bound on the size of an independent set of a graph)
with weights chosen using representation theory
of the symmetric group
S_{n}
.
For about ten years, there was no further improvement
until Ellis and Lifshitz
[
EL22
]

enhanced Ellis, Friedgut, and Pilpel’s result
using Fourier-analytic techniques and
showed that
1
holds for all

t\leq c\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
for some constant
c>0
.

Kupavskii and Zakharov
[
KZ24
]

made a breakthrough by showing that
1

holds for all
t\leq c\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}
for some constant
c>0
,
using a novel technique called the

spread approximation method
.
This method is purely combinatorial and is based on
a previous breakthrough in the sunflower problem
by Alweiss, Lovett, Wu, and Zhang
[
ALWZ21
]
.
It has been used to give results on variants of Erdős–Ko–Rado
problems in various settings, such as
intersecting families of partitions

[
Kup26
]
, intersecting families of spanning trees

[
FHIKLMP26+
,
IK26+
]
, and the Hajnal–Rothschild problem

[
FK26+
]
.

Independently of Kupavskii and Zakharov’s proof

[
KZ24
]
,
Keller, Lifshitz, Minzer, and Sheinfeld
[
KLMS24
]

proved that
1
holds for all
t\leq cn

for some constant
c>0
by using Fourier-analytic techniques,
more specifically hypercontractivity of global functions.
Their proof share some high-level ideals to
[
KZ24
]
,
namely the use of some notion of pseudorandomness.
However, the technical tools used are completely different.

Finally, Kupavskii
[
Kup26+
]

refined the spread approximation technique to show that

1
holds for all
t\leq(1-\varepsilon)n

and
n>n_{0}(\varepsilon)
.
This the first result in which the maximal family
is not necessarily
\mathcal{A}_{0}
or
\mathcal{A}_{\lfloor(n-t)/2\rfloor}
,
but it can be
\mathcal{A}_{k}
for any
k
.
The numerous candidates for maximal families substantially
complicate the analysis.

As we shall explain in the next subsection
(and with more detail in
Remark

1
),
Kupavskii’s proof requires
t
to be at least

n-O\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
.
We overcome several obstacles in Kupavskii’s proof
and show that
1
holds
for all
t\geq n-n^{5/7+\varepsilon}
.

1.2.
Proof Outline

We now describe the high-level overview of
the spread approximation method and explain
our new contributions.
Broadly speaking, the two new ideas that allow us to get
past the previous result are (i) an iterative version of
spread approximation and (ii) a new analysis using

r
sets instead of just two as in
[
Kup26+
]
.
We now explain these two ideas in more detail.

In its most basic form (as in
[
KZ24
]
), the method of spread
approximation works by approximating a family
of permutations
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}

by a family of partial permutations
\mathcal{S}
.
Informally, a partial permutation is a permutation
where some entries are allowed to be undefined.
See
Section

2.1
for a precise definition.
Roughly speaking, family
\mathcal{S}
satisfies three properties:

•

each element of
\mathcal{S}
has size
(i.e., number of defined entries)
not much larger than
t
;

•

at least
(1-o(1))
-fraction of elements in
\mathcal{F}

extend an element in
\mathcal{S}
; and

•

\mathcal{S}
is
t
-intersecting.

The step of obtaining
\mathcal{S}
is called
the
spread approximation step
,
which relies on the “spread lemma”
(
Lemma

7
) introduced by
Alweiss, Lovett, Wu, and Zhang
[
ALWZ21
]

in the study of sunflower-free families, and later sharpened by
Tao and Hu
[
Tao20
,
Hu21
]
.

Next, we analyze
\mathcal{S}
further
by the process of peeling (described precisely in

Section

4.1
).
Informally, we alternate between

•

taking out partial permutations of maximum size; and

•

simplifying the family (by removing elements
from partial permutations in the family) so that
it remains
t
-intersecting.

The process partitions
\mathcal{S}
into layers, where the
k
-th
layer consists of partial permutations with
t+k
elements.
Each layer has some nice properties (
Proposition

1
)
that allow us to count and upper-bound the number of elements.
Ultimately, one can show that most elements in
\mathcal{F}

contain the same fixed
t
-element partial permutation
(i.e.,
\mathcal{F}
is very close to
\mathcal{A}_{0}
).
This yields the so-called stability result.
Finally, a simple counting argument shows that

\mathcal{F}
must actually be contained in
\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

When our family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
is too sparse
(which occurs when
t
is closer to
n
),
the spread approximation step no longer works.
To overcome this, Kupavskii
[
Kup26+
]

introduced an extra step, in which one
proves a density boost result (i.e., there exists a partial permutation
that contains higher-than-average number of elements in
\mathcal{F}
),
and used that to construct a better spread approximation.
Kupavskii’s density boost result relied on the ability
to get the initial spread approximation and perform the
peeling step, which,
as we shall explain in
Remark

1
,
inherently requires
t\geq n-\frac{C\log\log n}{\log n}
.

To overcome this issue, we introduce our idea (i)
of doing the spread approximation iteratively.
In particular, we do not start by
trying to get a spread approximation.
Instead, we begin by proving a density boost result.
This density boost result is very weak,
but it is enough to initiate a spread approximation,
which in turn gives a better density boost result.
Consequently, we alternate between finding a spread approximation
and proving a density boost result.
Each round of alternation results in an incrementally better
spread approximation and a better density boost,
and eventually, the density boost result is good enough
to construct our final spread approximation.
Our new iterative spread approximation step
results in
Theorem

2
,
which works whenever
t\leq n-n^{1/2+\varepsilon}
.

In the subsequent peeling step, Kupavskii
[
Kup26+
]

had to work harder in order to get a maximum family
that is not
\mathcal{A}_{0}
.
After constructing the peeling, he chose
k
such that
the
k
-th layer is large.
In this layer, one can show that there exist
partial permutations
A
and
B
such that
|A\cap B|=t

(in particular, is not strictly greater than
t
).
Thus,
|A\cup B|=t+2k
, giving our
(t+2k)
-element
partial permutation.
Then by a counting argument, one can show that most
partial permutations in the
k
-th layer
are contained in
A\cup B
, recovering the stability result.
This argument works whenever
t\leq n-n^{3/4+\varepsilon}

(see
Remark

2
for an explanation of why it
only works up to the exponent
\frac{3}{4}
).
Thus, combining Kupavskii’s argument
with our new spread approximation
step would give a proof of
1

for
t\leq n-n^{3/4+\varepsilon}
.

Improving the exponent past
\frac{3}{4}

requires a more elaborate counting argument,
which motivates the idea (ii) of
using
r
partial permutations instead of only two
partial permutations
A
and
B
.
More specifically, we show that there exist partial permutations

A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
with certain nice properties
(
Lemma

9
).
Then by counting partial permutations that have
intersection of size
t
with each of
A_{1}
,
…,
A_{r}
,
we show that most partial permutations are contained in

A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{r}
(
Corollary

5
).
This recovers the stability result.
We believe that our argument could be further optimized
to reduce the exponent (see
Remark

3

for further discussion).
Also, see
Remark

4
for a discussion
of why the exponent
\frac{5}{7}
was chosen.

.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and Convention

We ignore all floors and ceilings when they are not
important to the proof.

We use the standard asymptotic notations
(most prominently
O
,
o
,
\sim
,
\ll
, and
\lesssim
)
throughout the paper. We note the following:

•

f(n)\ll g(n)
,
g(n)\gg f(n)
, and
f(n)=o(g(n))

are equivalent.

•

f(n)\lesssim g(n)
,
g(n)\gtrsim f(n)
, and
f(n)=O(g(n))

are equivalent.

We let
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[n]}:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be the standard
n
-element set.
For any set
X
, we let

•

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}2^{X}}
be the set of all subsets of
X
,

•

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\binom{X}{k}}

be the set of all subsets of
X
of size
k
, and

•

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\binom{X}{\leq k}}

be the set of all subsets of
X
of size at most
k
.

Calligraphic letters (
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
, …)
will generally be used to denote families of sets.
As introduced in
[
KZ24
]
, we use the following notation in dealing with
families of sets:
for a set
X
and families
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{S}
,
we let

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathcal{F}[X]}

\displaystyle:=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:X\subseteq F\}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathcal{F}(X)}

\displaystyle:=\{F\setminus X:F\in\mathcal{F},X\subseteq F\}=\{F\setminus X:F\in\mathcal{F}[X]\}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{S}]}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{F}[A].

Note that
|\mathcal{F}[X]|=|\mathcal{F}(X)|
.

Rather than viewing
\Sigma_{n}
as a collection of permutations,
we view
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\Sigma_{n}}
as a collection of sets by having

\sigma:[n]\to[n]
correspond to the
set
\{(1,\sigma(1)),(2,\sigma(2)),\dots,(n,\sigma(n))\}
.
Thus,
\Sigma_{n}\subseteq\binom{[n]^{2}}{n}

(here,
[n]^{2}=[n]\times[n]
)
and a
t
-intersecting family of permutations of
[n]

corresponds to a
t
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
,
where any two sets in
\mathcal{F}
have intersection size
at least
t
.
A
partial permutation

is a subset of some element of
\Sigma_{n}
.
We note that for any
X\subseteq[n]^{2}
, we have

\Sigma_{n}(X)\leq(n-|X|)!
.

Throughout this paper, we make the following
blanket assumption on
n
and
t
.

(1)

\boxed{\text{Let }n\text{ and }t\text{ be
sufficiently large positive integers
such that }n^{0.99}<t<n.}

If
t<n^{0.99}
, then
Theorem

1
holds
due to a previous result such as
[
KZ24
]
.
We include this assumption to simplify some
technicalities involved in the proof.

For the entire paper, we let
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}u}:=n-t
.

2.2.
Elementary Estimates

In this section, we collect some elementary estimates
that will be used later.

1
.

For any integers
n\geq k\geq 0
,
we have
\binom{n}{k}\leq(en/k)^{k}
.

Proof.

Note that
\binom{n}{k}\leq\frac{n^{k}}{k!}

and
k!\geq\exp\left(\int_{1}^{k}\log x\,dx\right)=\left(\tfrac{k}{e}\right)^{k}

Combining these two estimates gives the lemma.
∎

2
.

For any positive integers
n\geq k
, we have

\frac{n!}{k!}\geq\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n-k}
.

Proof.

We first claim that
(k+1)(k+2)\cdots n\geq n!^{\frac{n-k}{n}}
. To prove this, note that

\displaystyle\big((k+1)(k+2)\cdots n\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{n(n-k)-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i}{n}\right\rfloor+k+1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=0}^{n(n-k)-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i}{n-k}\right\rfloor+1\right)=(1\cdot 2\cdots n)^{n-k},

and the middle inequality

\big\lfloor\frac{i}{n}\big\rfloor+k\geq\big\lfloor\frac{i}{n-k}\big\rfloor

follows from taking floor on both sides
of the inequality
\frac{i}{n}+k\geq\frac{i}{n-k}
,
which holds for all
i\leq n(n-k)
.

Finally, we have that

\frac{n!}{k!}\ =\ (k+1)(k+2)\cdots n\ \geq\ n!^{\frac{n-k}{n}}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n-k},

where the last inequality comes from the well-known estimate

n!\geq\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}
.
∎

3
.

If
a
,
b
and
x
are positive integers such that

a>b
and
x\geq 2b
, then

\left(\frac{x}{b}-1\right)^{b-a}\binom{x}{a}\ <\ \binom{x}{b}\ <\ \left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^{b-a}\binom{x}{a}

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{x}{b}}{\binom{x}{a}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{x-a}{a+1}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{x-(b-2)}{b-1}\right)\left(\frac{x-(b-1)}{b}\right),

which is clearly between

\left(\frac{x}{b}-1\right)^{b-a}
and
\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^{b-a}
.
∎

4
.

If
b=o(\sqrt{a})
, then

\binom{a+b}{b}=(1+o(1))\binom{a}{b}.

In particular, repeatedly applying this lemma gives

\binom{a+kb}{b}=(1+o(1))\binom{a}{b}
for all
nonnegative integer
k
(note that we treat
k

is a constant here).

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{a+b}{b}}{\binom{a}{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a+b)(a+b-1)\cdots(a+1)}{a(a-1)(a-2)\dots(a-b+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{b}{a-b+1}\right)^{b}<\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}}{a-b+1}\right)=1+o(1).\qed

2.3.
Bounds on
|\mathcal{A}_{k}|

In our language of viewing permutations as elements in

\Sigma_{n}
, the optimal family
\mathcal{A}_{k}

for each
0\leq k\leq\frac{n-t}{2}
can be written as

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\left\{F\in\Sigma_{n}:\begin{array}[]{c}F\text{ contains a }(t+k)\text{-element subset of }\\
\{(1,1),(2,2),\dots,(t+2k,t+2k)\}\end{array}\right\}.

Throughout this paper, we write

\max_{j}|\mathcal{A}_{j}|:=\max_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-t}{2}\right\rfloor}|\mathcal{A}_{j}|.

In this subsection, we provide elementary bounds on
the size of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
that we will use in our proof.
The following
Lemma

5

appeared in
[
Kup26+
]
;
we provide the proof for completeness.

5
[
Kup26+
, Lem. 5]
.

For any positive integers
n,t,k
,
we have
|\mathcal{A}_{k}|\leq\binom{t+2k}{k}(n-t-k)!
.
Furthermore, if
k=o(n-t-k)
, then

|\mathcal{A}_{k}|=(1+o(1))\binom{t+2k}{k}(n-t-k)!

Proof.

Let
T=\{(1,1),(2,2),\dots,(t+2k,t+2k)\}
.
To prove the upper bound, we note that
each element of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is counted at least once by
picking a
(t+k)
-element subset of
T
(there are

\binom{t+2k}{t+k}=\binom{t+2k}{k}
such subsets)
and permuting the remaining
n-t-k
indices.
Thus, we have
|\mathcal{A}_{k}|\leq\binom{t+2k}{k}(n-t-k)!
.

To prove the lower bound, we have to account
for the fact that an element might intersect
T

in more than
t+k
elements.
We claim that

\displaystyle\geq\binom{t+2k}{t+k}(n-t-k)!-(t+k+1)\cdot\binom{t+2k}{t+k+1}(n-t-k-1)!

\displaystyle\geq\binom{t+2k}{k}(n-t-k)!\left(1-(t+k+1)\cdot\frac{k}{t+k+1}\cdot\frac{1}{n-t-k}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-o(1))\binom{t+2k}{k}(n-t-k)!

speculate that the argument in this section has

3
.

At a very high level, the argument in this section
has two steps.

(1)

Find sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
with a “nice” structure.

(2)

Upper bound the number of sets

such that

|B\cap A_{i}|\geq t
for all

.

The key difficulty of optimizing this argument
is to find the right definition of what constitutes
“nice” in (1).
On one hand, we want it to be weak enough
so that (1) works at a wide range of
k
.
On the other hand, we want it to be strong enough
so that (2) can be carried out.
In our argument, “nice” means
|A_{1}\cap\dots\cap A_{r}|=t-(r-2)k
.

We believe that the condition

is quite strong,
to the point that it forces every element to appear in

A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
either
0
,
r-1
or
r
times.
In particular, we find it interesting that as

r
grows larger, the argument works on a larger range
of
k
, but the existence of such
r
sets

also guarantees the existence of

sets (one can check that

In short, as

goes larger, the argument works
for larger range of

but also produces a tuple of
sets with a stronger condition.
We believe that the right condition should not
become strictly stronger as
r
increase.
If we can find the right weaker condition,
then we may be able to replace the exponent
\frac{5}{7}

with a smaller one.
