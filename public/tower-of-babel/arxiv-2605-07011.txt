Dual-Agent Co-Training for Health Coaching via Implicit Adversarial Preference Optimization
Da Long, Lingyi Fu, Diya Michelle Rao, Jasmine Ruales Carrera, Yang Bai, Shandian Zhe

Abstract
Motivational-interviewing-based health coaching is an effective approach for improving mental health and promoting healthy behavior change. However, the scarcity of trained human coaches and the high cost of coaching services make such support inaccessible to many people who could benefit from it. This motivates the development of AI health coaches that can provide scalable and affordable support. Existing methods typically optimize only one side of the interaction: they either train a dialogue agent against a fixed client environment or train a client simulator against a fixed assistant. This one-sided setup can limit exploration of the interaction space and may be inefficient at developing the capabilities required by the target agent and pushing its performance boundaries. In this paper, we propose a dual-agent framework that interactively co-trains both the health coach agent and the client simulator. The coach is optimized with DPO using Pareto-dominant response pairs identified by a multi-dimensional LLM judge. In turn, the client is trained adversarially by reversing these preferences, inducing an implicit adversarial training dynamic. We further show that this co-training process admits a natural stochastic-game interpretation. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method effectively improves coaching quality across several important dimensions.



1
Introduction

Motivational interviewing is a widely used health-coaching approach for improving mental health and promoting healthy behavior change
(
11
;
6
)
. Its effectiveness depends not only on whether a conversation reaches a desirable final outcome, but also on whether the coach behaves appropriately throughout the interaction. At each turn, the coach must make context-sensitive decisions, such as eliciting and strengthening change talk, responding skillfully to sustain talk, expressing empathy, helping the client clarify needs and ambivalence, and, when appropriate, guiding the client toward concrete goals and action plans. In practice, however, the scarcity of trained human coaches and the high cost of coaching services make such support inaccessible to many people who could benefit from it. This creates an urgent need for AI health coaches that can provide scalable and affordable support.

Although many AI assistants have been developed for dialogue with human users
(
8
;
1
;
10
)
, most existing methods focus primarily on optimizing conversation-level outcomes. For health coaching, however, success depends on the quality of the coach’s behavior throughout the entire interviewing session, not merely on the final result. Moreover, existing training methods typically optimize only one side of the interaction: they either train a dialogue agent against a fixed client environment
(
2
;
16
;
15
)
, or train a client simulator against a fixed assistant
(
18
)
. This asymmetric setup can limit exploration of the interaction space during training and may be inefficient in developing the capabilities required by the target agents and driving them toward their full potential.

In this paper, we propose DACT, a dual-agent co-training framework for motivational-interviewing-based health coaching. Our method jointly trains a client simulator and a coach agent through interactive dialogue, enabling richer exploration of interviewing dynamics and, in particular, of the challenging interaction patterns that are most informative for improving coaching quality. Our main contributions are as follows.

•

Dual-Agent Preference Tree Construction.
Instead of relying on expensive expert demonstrations, we introduce an efficient and low-cost tree-based rollout procedure for collecting preference pairs during training. Each dialogue tree is initialized by prompting the client simulator with a short random persona generated by a low-cost third-party LLM, after which the coach and client interact to simulate a health-coaching interview. At selected dialogue states, the tree branches by re-sampling multiple coach utterances under the same interaction history and recursively expanding the resulting continuations. Preference pairs are then constructed from utterances at the same tree level according to their estimated Q-values.

•

Multi-Dimensional LLM Judge for Q-Value Estimation.
We design a multi-dimensional LLM judge to evaluate the coach’s behavior throughout the dialogue. To ensure that each coaching dimension is evaluated in an appropriate context, the judging process is decomposed into three stages: client-state identification, state-conditioned sentence-level functional labeling, and final reasoning-based aggregation and scoring. The resulting node-level scores are propagated upward with a discount factor and aggregated into Q-values that represent the overall quality of candidate coach utterances.

•

Implicit Adversarial Preference Training.
For the coach, we construct preference pairs by requiring the preferred response to outperform the rejected response on every judged dimension, yielding Pareto-dominant preference pairs for DPO training. This encourages the coach to improve across all targeted dimensions simultaneously. For the client simulator, we reverse the coach-side preferences and train it adversarially: responses that make the coach perform worse are treated as preferred. As a result, the client learns to produce utterances that are increasingly challenging for the current coach to handle appropriately, which in turn drives the coach toward stronger performance through co-training. We further show that this simple implicit adversarial dynamic admits a natural stochastic-game interpretation.

•

Experiments.
We evaluate DACT on three core coaching dimensions: cultivating change talk, softening or skillfully responding to sustain talk, and empathy. Blind ranking studies show strong agreement between the preferences induced by our LLM judge and those of human judges. Across several challenging client conditions, DACT consistently improves coaching quality throughout the interview compared with carefully prompted state-of-the-art LLM baselines and a purely supervised fine-tuning (SFT) baseline. Extensive ablations further demonstrate that our co-training framework substantially outperforms both one-sided training and purely supervised fine-tuning.

Problem formulation.

We formulate the co-training of the client simulator and the coach agent as a two-agent reinforcement learning problem,
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}^{C},\mathcal{A}^{U},P,\mathbf{R},\gamma,T)
,
where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the state space,
\mathcal{A}^{C}
and
\mathcal{A}^{U}
denote the action spaces of the coach and the client, respectively,
P
denotes the transition dynamics,
\mathbf{R}
denotes the reward signal,
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the discount factor, and
T
is the maximum horizon, i.e., the maximum number of coach turns in an interview session.
Each step corresponds to one coach turn in the dialogue. At step
t
, the state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
is the dialogue history immediately before the coach acts. Given
s_{t}
, the coach generates an utterance
c_{t}\in\mathcal{A}^{C}
according to its policy
\pi_{C}(\cdot\mid s_{t})
. The client then responds with an utterance
u_{t+1}\in\mathcal{A}^{U}
according to its policy
\pi_{U}(\cdot\mid s_{t},c_{t})
. The dialogue history is updated as
s_{t+1}=(s_{t},c_{t},u_{t+1})
.
The interaction terminates when the coach emits a termination token or when the maximum number of turns
T
is reached.

Unlike prior approaches that optimize only one side of the interaction while fixing the other, our goal is to jointly learn both the coach policy
\pi_{C}
and the client policy
\pi_{U}
. Consequently, from the perspective of the coach, the effective environment is non-stationary, since the transition dynamics are induced by the evolving client policy:
P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},c_{t})=\pi_{U}(u_{t+1}\mid s_{t},c_{t})
.
This co-evolving interaction structure enriches exploration of the dialogue space and exposes the coach to increasingly challenging conversational situations. As we show later, our joint training process admits a natural stochastic-game interpretation.

A well-executed motivational interview requires the coach to perform well across multiple dimensions throughout the entire dialogue, rather than merely achieving a desirable final outcome. We therefore introduce a vector-valued reward signal to evaluate the quality of each coach action:
\mathbf{R}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
.
In this work, we focus on three key dimensions (
d=3
), selected based on the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) 4.2.1 coding manual
(
7
)
:
Cultivating Change Talk
(CCT),
Softening Sustain Talk
(SST), and
Empathy
. Our framework can be naturally extended to incorporate additional dimensions. Each reward component is scored on a five-point scale,
r_{i}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
,
and the semantic meaning of each score is provided in Appendix
B
.

2.1
Preference Dialogue Tree Construction

After initialization—typically through lightweight supervised fine-tuning of a small LLM—we iteratively refine both policies using direct preference optimization (DPO)
(
10
)
. At each iteration, preference pairs are collected from a batch of dialogue trees generated by the previous client and coach policies.

Specifically, at iteration
k
, we construct each dialogue tree by letting the coach and client agents initiate an interview under their previous policies
(\pi_{C}^{k-1},\pi_{U}^{k-1})
. The dialogue first unfolds linearly until it reaches a
branching point
, namely, a selected coach turn at which branching begins. At that point, the coach samples multiple candidate utterances conditioned on the same dialogue history, each of which defines a separate branch. For each such coach branch, the client then independently samples multiple responses, yielding further branches in the rollout tree. This branching-and-rollout process continues recursively until each dialogue path terminates or a predefined maximum tree depth is reached. To ensure broad coverage of conversational dynamics, the branching point is selected from a range of dialogue steps, so that both early-stage and late-stage coaching decisions can be explored. The resulting balanced dialogue tree ensures that sibling nodes—utterances generated at the same depth under the same dialogue prefix—are compared under equal rollout budgets. This allows us to construct preference pairs in a controlled and less biased manner.

2.2
Multi-Dimensional LLM Judge

While human experts can provide high-quality and clinically informed evaluations of coaching performance, such evaluation is expensive and time-consuming. To enable scalable learning, we develop a multi-dimensional LLM judge that evaluates coach behavior throughout the dialogue tree. These turn-level scores are then propagated through the tree and aggregated into Q-value estimates. To support clinically meaningful evaluation, we encode extensive motivational interviewing (MI) knowledge
(
7
)
—including concepts such as change talk
vs.
sustain talk, the righting reflex, and OARS micro-skills—into the judge prompt, and perform a three-step evaluation procedure.

Step 1: Client-state identification.

The judge first classifies the client’s most recent utterance into one of five states:
engaged
(clear change talk),
ambivalent
(both change talk and sustain talk are present),
sustain-resistant
(reasons against change or explicit pushback),
emotional
(difficult emotions are in the foreground), or
informational
(neutral background sharing). This step is important because an MI-appropriate coach response is highly state-dependent: what is skillful for an engaged client (
e.g.,
reinforcing change talk) may be premature for an emotional client, where the coach should first acknowledge and sit with the feeling rather than immediately evoke change.

Step 2: State-conditioned sentence-level function labeling.

Conditioned on the identified client state, the judge then decomposes the coach utterance into individual sentences and assigns each sentence a function label from a 17-category taxonomy organized into three groups:

•

Group A
(MI-adherent, 6 categories):
open_question_evoking
,
simple_reflection
,
complex_reflection
,
double_sided_reflection
,
affirmation
,
summary
.

•

Group B
(neutral, 3 categories):
neutral_question
,
rapport_or_info
,
other
.

•

Group C
(MI-non-adherent, 8 categories):
leading_question
,
premature_planning
,
arguing_for_change
,
arguing_against_sustain
,
reassurance
,
unsolicited_advice
,
parroting
,
distorted_reflection
.

Step 3: Reasoning-based aggregation and scoring.

Finally, for each target dimension—CCT, SST, and Empathy—the judge produces detailed reasoning that references the relevant sentences and their function labels, identifies the applicable scoring rules, and aggregates them into a reward score in
\{1,2,3,4,5\}
. The output schema enforces that the reasoning fields appear before the score fields, which discourages post-hoc rationalization and makes the scoring process more transparent.

Q-value estimation.

Let
\mathbf{r}(n)=(r_{1}(n),r_{2}(n),r_{3}(n))
denote the immediate reward vector at node
n
. A node may correspond to either a coach utterance or a client utterance. Since our objective is to evaluate coaching quality, only coach nodes receive nonzero immediate rewards from the LLM judge; for client nodes, we set
\mathbf{r}(n)=\mathbf{0}
.

The immediate judge scores capture turn-level coaching quality, but they do not reflect the downstream effect of a coach utterance on future interaction trajectories. For example, an open question may receive only a moderate immediate score, yet elicit change talk from the client and thereby enable stronger coach responses in later turns. To account for such delayed effects, we propagate turn-level scores upward through the dialogue tree using a discounted recursive backup. Specifically, for each node
n
and each dimension
d
, we compute

Q_{d}(n)=\begin{cases}r_{d}(n),&\text{if $n$ is a leaf},\\[4.0pt]
r_{d}(n)+\gamma\cdot\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}(n)|}\sum_{n^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(n)}Q_{d}(n^{\prime}),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\mathcal{C}(n)
denotes the set of children of node
n
, and
\gamma
is the discount factor. This yields a dimension-wise Q-value that reflects both the immediate quality of the current response and its expected downstream effect on future dialogue development.

2.3
Implicit Adversarial Preference Training

Coach preference pairs.

To train the coach with DPO, we compare coach nodes at the same tree level within each dialogue tree and construct
Pareto-dominant preference pairs
using strict Pareto dominance over their Q-value vectors. Specifically, for two coach nodes
A
and
B
, we say that
A
dominates
B
(denoted by
A\succ B
) if

\forall d\in D:\ Q_{d}(A)\geq Q_{d}(B)\quad\wedge\quad\exists d\in D:\ Q_{d}(A)>Q_{d}(B).

(2)

When
A\succ B
, we add the pair
(A,B)
to the coach preference dataset, with
A
as the chosen response and
B
as the rejected response. This Pareto-based construction encourages the coach to improve coaching quality across
all
target dimensions, rather than over-optimizing a single dimension or improving one dimension at the expense of another.

Client preference pairs.

We define a challenge vector for each client node
u
in the dialogue tree:

S(u)=\bigl(-Q_{1}(u),-Q_{2}(u),-Q_{3}(u)\bigr).

Since
\{Q_{d}(u)\}
reflects the downstream coaching quality following the client utterance at node
u
, a smaller value of
Q_{d}(u)
—equivalently, a larger value of the corresponding component in
S(u)
—indicates that the subsequent coach behavior is worse on dimension
d
. Therefore, a larger challenge vector corresponds to a client utterance that is more challenging for the current coach.

We compare client nodes among comparable nodes at the same tree depth and construct
Pareto-dominant preference pairs
using the challenge vectors. Specifically, for two client nodes
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, we prefer
u_{1}
over
u_{2}
if

\forall d\in D:\ S_{d}(u_{1})\geq S_{d}(u_{2})\quad\wedge\quad\exists d\in D:\ S_{d}(u_{1})>S_{d}(u_{2}).

(3)

Such pairs are added to the client preference dataset with
u_{1}
as the chosen response and
u_{2}
as the rejected response. In other words, client-side preferences are constructed by reversing the coach-side notion of quality: utterances are preferred when they make the downstream coaching outcome worse across all target dimensions.

Both the coach and the client are then optimized using the standard DPO objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\right)\right)\right],

(4)

where
\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi_{\theta}^{k-1}
(or
\pi^{\text{SFT}}_{U}
for the client; see Appendix
G
) is the policy from the previous training round,
y_{w}
and
y_{l}
denote the chosen and rejected utterances, respectively, and
x
is the dialogue history. Our overall method is summarized in Appendix Algorithm
1
.

This construction induces an implicit adversarial dynamic between the two agents. The coach is trained to prefer responses that improve multi-dimensional coaching quality, while the client is trained to prefer utterances that make the subsequent coaching interaction more difficult. In effect, the client learns to produce utterances that challenge the coach, such as sustain talk that resists reframing, ambivalence that resists easy resolution, or emotionally complex statements that require more sophisticated empathy. The adversarial pressure is
implicit
because it arises through the Q-value-based preference construction rather than through an explicit adversarial objective. As the coach improves and resolves its current weaknesses, those failure modes no longer induce low Q-values; consequently, the client’s preference landscape automatically shifts toward exposing the next set of weaknesses. This produces a self-paced adversarial curriculum without requiring manual difficulty scheduling.

2.4
Stochastic-Game Interpretation

Although our method is implemented through preference optimization rather than direct game-theoretic optimization, the resulting co-training dynamics admit a natural stochastic-game interpretation. The key observation is that the coach-side and client-side preference pairs are consistent with two coupled latent utilities: the coach prefers utterances that improve downstream multi-dimensional coaching quality, whereas the client prefers utterances that reduce it.

To formalize this idea, let
\mathcal{D}=\{1,\ldots,d\}
denote the set of reward dimensions, and let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be any
strictly Pareto-monotone scalarization
, meaning that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bigl(\forall i,\ x_{i}\geq y_{i}\bigr)\ \wedge\ \bigl(\exists j,\ x_{j}>y_{j}\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Phi(x)>\Phi(y).

We emphasize that
\Phi
is introduced only for interpretation and is
not
used in training. Using
\Phi
, we define the latent coach and client utilities as

U_{C}(n)=\Phi(Q(n)),\qquad U_{U}(n)=-\,\Phi(Q(n)),

(5)

where
Q(n)=(Q_{1}(n),\ldots,Q_{d}(n))
is the dimension-wise backed-up return at node
n
.

Lemma 2.1

(Coach-side utility consistency)
.

If two comparable coach nodes
A
and
B
satisfy
A\succ B
in the strict Pareto sense of Eq.
2
, then
U_{C}(A)>U_{C}(B)
.

Lemma
2.1
shows that every Pareto-dominant coach preference pair is consistent with maximizing a scalar utility that is monotone in all coaching dimensions. Thus, the coach-side DPO update can be interpreted as increasing the probability of actions with higher latent coach utility.

Lemma 2.2

(Client-side adversarial consistency)
.

Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be two comparable client nodes. If the corresponding challenge vectors satisfy
S(u_{1})\succ S(u_{2})
,
then
U_{U}(u_{1})>U_{U}(u_{2})
, or equivalently,
U_{C}(u_{1})<U_{C}(u_{2})
.

Lemma
2.2
formalizes the adversarial role of the client simulator: client utterances are preferred precisely when they reduce the downstream latent utility of the coach.

Together, these two lemmas imply that our coach-side and client-side preference datasets are utility-consistent with a two-player stochastic game defined on the same dialogue state-transition structure as
\mathcal{G}
, but with opposing latent utilities
(U_{C},U_{U})
. Consequently, alternating DPO updates for the coach and the client can be interpreted as alternating KL-regularized policy-improvement steps for two adversarially coupled players. Detailed proofs and discussion are provided in Appendix
A
.

3
Related Work

Our work is most closely related to recent efforts on tree-based and multi-turn preference learning for LLMs. MCTS-DPO
(
13
)
uses Monte Carlo tree search to construct step-level preference pairs for single-agent reasoning, showing that tree rollouts can provide finer-grained supervision than outcome-only rewards. REFUEL
(
2
)
and SWEET-RL
(
16
)
extend preference- or value-based optimization to multi-turn interaction, addressing long-horizon credit assignment in dialogue and collaborative agent tasks. In therapeutic dialogue, CSO
(
15
)
applies MCTS and DPO to emotional support conversations by learning turn-level strategy preferences. Our method is related to these lines of work in using rollout-based credit assignment and preference optimization, but differs in three key respects: we study
dual-agent
health-coaching dialogue rather than single-agent reasoning or fixed-user interaction; we construct
strict Pareto-dominant
pairs from multi-dimensional MI rewards rather than scalar values; and we optimize utterance-level coaching behavior instead of strategy selection alone.

A second related thread concerns user simulation and interactive social training. PlatoLM
(
4
)
trains a dedicated user simulator to generate more human-like multi-round dialogue data, while SOTOPIA-
\pi

(
12
)
improves social agents through behavior cloning and self-reinforcement on LLM-rated interaction trajectories. DIAL
(
18
)
iteratively trains a realistic dialogue simulator through adversarial preference learning, and Roleplay-doh
(
5
)
uses expert-authored prompting principles to build clinically plausible simulated patients. These works demonstrate the value of learned or expert-guided simulators, but they either train only the simulator, keep the assistant fixed, or rely on filtered positive trajectories rather than pairwise preference optimization. In contrast, our framework jointly updates both the coach and the client simulator, and the client is trained not only for realism but also to expose the current weaknesses of the coach, yielding an implicit adversarial curriculum during co-training.

Finally, our work connects to preference optimization and multi-objective alignment. DPO
(
10
)
provides the basic optimization algorithm we use for both agents. Prior multi-objective alignment methods such as MODPO
(
17
)
typically rely on scalarization or weighted trade-offs across objectives. By contrast, our coach-side supervision is based on strict Pareto dominance across clinically grounded MI dimensions, avoiding the need to specify task-dependent weights between cultivating change talk, softening sustain talk, and empathy. More broadly, our use of an LLM judge also relates to the RLAIF and self-rewarding paradigm
(
1
;
14
)
, but we apply it in a structured, sentence-aware, multi-dimensional way tailored to motivational interviewing rather than generic helpfulness or single-turn alignment.

4.1
Setup

Personas and models.

We construct a pool of structured client personas as role-play conditioning for the client side. These personas are partitioned into disjoint sets to generate the SFT corpus, to condition the client agent during preference tree generation, and to drive client simulators at evaluation.
Both the coach and client agents share
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int4

(

)

(

)
adapters
\pi_{C},\pi_{U}

Co-evolution.
At each iteration, we generate
N{=}3
preference trees. Each tree is constructed through four alternating branching steps between the coach and client agents. At every step, each agent expands
M{=}3
child nodes, yielding
M^{4}{=}81
leaf trajectories per tree. Q-values are then backed up using the discounted recursion in Eq. (
1
), and both adapters are optimized with DPO, using GPT-5.4-mini as the judge. We run 13 co-training iterations in total. Full hyperparameters, prompt templates, data-generation details, and the scoring protocol are provided in Appendix
F

Evaluation Protocol

Competing methods.
We compare

against three baselines. (1)

5
Conclusion

We presented
DACT
, a dual-agent co-evolution framework in which a coach and a client adapter are jointly trained by DPO over preference trees scored against an absolute MI rubric. Across a matrix of coach methods and client conditions including both an in-distribution stress test and out-of-distribution client styles, the trained
DACT
coach achieves higher overall coaching quality than GPT-5.5 baselines given the same inference prompt and the LLM-judge rubric inlined into its prompt, and reduces sentence-level MI anti-pattern rate by more than an order of magnitude. A controlled ablation against a frozen-client variant shows that adaptive client co-evolution, rather than coach-only DPO, is the principal source of late-round training signal: with the client held fixed, coach quality plateaus well before the trained coach reaches its eventual ceiling.

Our results are obtained on a single base model, and the magnitude of
DACT
’s advantage on the in-distribution stress test may shift under expert MITI evaluation. Replication on additional base models and a full MITI expert evaluation are the most direct next steps.

Appendix A
Proofs for the Stochastic-Game Interpretation

In this appendix, we provide formal details for the stochastic-game interpretation stated in Section
2.4
.

We first introduce the scalarization used only for interpretation.

Definition A.1

(Strictly Pareto-monotone scalarization)
.

A function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
strictly Pareto-monotone
if for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bigl(\forall i,\ x_{i}\geq y_{i}\bigr)\ \wedge\ \bigl(\exists j,\ x_{j}>y_{j}\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Phi(x)>\Phi(y).

This condition is mild: it only requires that improving any reward dimension without degrading the others must increase the scalarized utility. For example, any positive weighted sum
\Phi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}x_{i}
with
w_{i}>0
satisfies this property. Importantly, such a scalarization is introduced solely to characterize the latent game structure; our training algorithm does not optimize any fixed scalarized reward.

Given a dialogue node
n
, let

Q(n)=\bigl(Q_{1}(n),\ldots,Q_{d}(n)\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

denote its multi-dimensional backed-up return. We define the latent coach and client utilities as

U_{C}(n)=\Phi(Q(n)),\qquad U_{U}(n)=-\,\Phi(Q(n)).

(6)

Lemma A.2

(Coach-side utility consistency)
.

If two comparable coach nodes
A
and
B
satisfy
A\succ B
in the strict Pareto sense, namely

\forall d\in\mathcal{D}:\ Q_{d}(A)\geq Q_{d}(B)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\exists d\in\mathcal{D}:\ Q_{d}(A)>Q_{d}(B),

then

U_{C}(A)>U_{C}(B).

Proof.

By assumption,
Q(A)
weakly dominates
Q(B)
in every dimension and strictly dominates it in at least one dimension. By strict Pareto-monotonicity of
\Phi
,

\Phi(Q(A))>\Phi(Q(B)).

Using the definition of the coach utility in Eq.
6
, we obtain

U_{C}(A)=\Phi(Q(A))>\Phi(Q(B))=U_{C}(B).

∎

Lemma
A.2
shows that every Pareto-dominant coach pair is consistent with a latent utility-maximizing player. Thus, the coach-side preference construction is not merely heuristic: it always agrees with an underlying scalar utility that is monotone in all coaching dimensions.

Lemma A.3

(Client-side adversarial consistency)
.

Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be two comparable client nodes, and recall that the challenge vector is defined by

S(u)=-Q(u).

If

S(u_{1})\succ S(u_{2})

in the strict Pareto sense, then

U_{U}(u_{1})>U_{U}(u_{2}),

or equivalently,

U_{C}(u_{1})<U_{C}(u_{2}).

Proof.

The condition
S(u_{1})\succ S(u_{2})
means

\forall d\in\mathcal{D}:\ -Q_{d}(u_{1})\geq-Q_{d}(u_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\exists d\in\mathcal{D}:\ -Q_{d}(u_{1})>-Q_{d}(u_{2}).

Equivalently,

\forall d\in\mathcal{D}:\ Q_{d}(u_{1})\leq Q_{d}(u_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\exists d\in\mathcal{D}:\ Q_{d}(u_{1})<Q_{d}(u_{2}),

so
Q(u_{2})
strictly Pareto-dominates
Q(u_{1})
. By strict Pareto-monotonicity of
\Phi
,

\Phi(Q(u_{2}))>\Phi(Q(u_{1})),

which implies

-\Phi(Q(u_{1}))>-\Phi(Q(u_{2})).

Using Eq.
6
, we obtain

U_{U}(u_{1})>U_{U}(u_{2}).

Equivalently, since
U_{U}=-U_{C}
under the same scalarization,

U_{C}(u_{1})<U_{C}(u_{2}).

∎

Lemma
A.3
formally captures the implicit adversarial effect of our client-side preference construction: the client is trained to prefer utterances that reduce the coach’s downstream utility across all target dimensions.

We can now state the overall interpretation.

Proposition A.4

(Natural stochastic-game interpretation)
.

Consider the two-player stochastic game induced by the dialogue state space
\mathcal{S}
, the coach and client action spaces
(\mathcal{A}^{C},\mathcal{A}^{U})
, the transition dynamics
P
, and the latent utilities
(U_{C},U_{U})
defined in Eq.
6
. Then the coach-side and client-side preference datasets constructed by our algorithm are utility-consistent with
U_{C}
and
U_{U}
, respectively. Consequently, alternating DPO updates for the coach and the client can be interpreted as alternating KL-regularized policy-improvement steps for two adversarially coupled players in this stochastic game.

Proof.

Utility consistency of the coach-side preference pairs follows from Lemma
A.2
, while utility consistency of the client-side preference pairs follows from Lemma
A.3
. Under the standard latent-utility view of pairwise preference optimization, DPO increases the relative likelihood of chosen actions over rejected ones with respect to a reference policy, and can therefore be interpreted as a KL-regularized policy-improvement step toward higher latent utility. Applying DPO to the coach-side dataset improves the coach policy with respect to
U_{C}
, while applying DPO to the client-side dataset improves the client policy with respect to
U_{U}
. Because
U_{U}
is defined as the negative scalarized downstream coaching quality, the two policy updates are adversarially coupled. Therefore, the alternating co-training procedure admits a natural stochastic-game interpretation.
∎

Discussion.

We emphasize that Proposition
A.4
is an interpretation result rather than a claim that our algorithm exactly solves the stochastic game or converges to a Nash equilibrium. Instead, it shows that the preference pairs used by our method are consistent with a pair of opposing latent utilities, and that the resulting alternating DPO updates can therefore be understood as a game-theoretic learning dynamic.

Appendix B
Details of Reward Scales

We use a three-dimensional reward signal,

\mathbf{R}=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}),

to co-train the health coach and client agents. These three dimensions are selected based on the MITI 4.2.1 coding manual
(
7
)
:
Cultivating Change Talk
(CCT),
Softening Sustain Talk
(SST), and
Empathy
.

Each reward component is scored on a five-point scale,

r_{i}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}.

The scale is ordinal and should not be interpreted as symmetric around an “average” level. In particular, a score of 3 corresponds to
neutral
motivational interviewing (MI) behavior, meaning that the coach’s response is neither clearly helpful nor clearly harmful from an MI perspective. Scores of 4–5 indicate MI-adherent behavior, where the coach actively cultivates change talk, softens sustain talk, or demonstrates empathic attunement. By contrast, scores of 1–2 indicate
MI-non-adherent
behavior, meaning that the coach exhibits clinically undesirable behaviors such as arguing for change, giving unsolicited advice, or dismissing the client’s emotions.

Importantly, a score of 2 should not be interpreted as merely “below-average MI”. Rather, it reflects the presence of an anti-pattern that a trained MI practitioner would regard as a meaningful clinical error. We therefore treat the lower end of the scale as qualitatively different from the neutral point, rather than as a symmetric numerical deviation from it.

Finally, we do not aggregate the reward vector into a single scalar reward. The three dimensions capture distinct and clinically meaningful aspects of MI competence, and, to our knowledge, the clinical literature does
not
provide a principled basis for assigning fixed trade-off weights among them. The detailed dimension-specific scoring rubric is provided in Appendix
F.4

The DPO signal in our pipeline is built entirely from LLM-judge scores. To check whether this preference signal aligns with how a trained MI expert would rank the same pairs, we ran a blinded pair-ranking study on

Appendix F
Pipeline Details

This section provides the implementation details for each stage of the
DACT
pipeline, including the persona pool used throughout, the synthetic SFT corpus that initializes the two adapters, the preference tree generation procedure, the LLM-judge protocol, the evaluation protocol, and the full hyperparameter summary.

F.1
Persona Pool

We generate a pool of
5{,}000
structured client personas using GPT-5.4-nano via the OpenAI Batch API, including
100
batched calls of
50
personas each. The generation is controlled by three components: a system prompt that establishes the persona-designer role, a user prompt that enforces three batch-level constraints described below, and a JSON schema passed to the API as a structured-output specification, which constrains every generated persona to a fixed field structure with strict enumerations and minimum-cardinality requirements.

The system prompt requires that “each persona must feel like a real person—with a coherent life story where their health conditions, physical limitations, occupation, goals, and challenges all logically connect.” The user prompt enforces three properties at the batch level.
Balance
requires that enumerable attributes (age, gender, ethnicity, activity level, geographic setting) be distributed uniformly across the batch, with no single value dominating.
Uniqueness
requires that free-text fields (occupation, body composition, health profile, physical limitations) differ across personas within a batch; no two personas in a batch share the same job title or the same health profile.
Coherence
requires an internal logic chain linking each persona’s attributes through the schema
health condition
\to
physical limitation
\to
challenge
\to
goal
, with at least three goals and three challenges per persona, each describing a concrete barrier or aim grounded in the persona’s specific life circumstances rather than a generic phrase.

Each persona is a JSON object whose fields and value spaces are defined by the schema. The schema fixes (i) enum-valued attributes including
gender
,
ethnicity
,
activity_level
, and the
geographic
setting; (ii) free-text attributes including a numeric
age
(constrained to
\geq 18
),
occupations
as a list of objects with job title, employment type, and a short description of work style, a free-text
body_composition
description, and lists of
health
conditions and
physical_limitations
; and (iii) the
goals
and
challenges
arrays, each with a minimum cardinality of three. An example is shown below.

F.2
SFT Data Generation

The synthetic SFT corpus is produced by prompting two GPT-4o-mini agents to role-play a coach and a client throughout a complete MI conversation, using role-specific system prompts that encode the supervisory target for each side. Each persona at indices
0
–
2{,}999
generates exactly one dialogue, yielding a corpus of
3{,}000
dialogues that is reused for both adapters:
\pi_{C}
is fine-tuned with the coach turns as the assistant role, while
\pi_{U}
is fine-tuned with the client turns as the assistant role, so the two adapters share dialogue content but receive distinct role-specific supervision.

Coach prompt for SFT data generation.

The coach side is steered by a comprehensive MI protocol that encodes what skilled coaching should look like. The prompt covers the four core MI stages (Engaging, Focusing, Evoking, Planning), the five MI principles (express empathy, develop discrepancy, avoid argumentation, roll with resistance, support self-efficacy), the four MI spirits (partnership, acceptance, compassion, empowerment), the OARS micro-skills (open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries), and goal-setting anchors aligned with the 2018 U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines. Pacing constraints discourage the coach from front-loading assessment questions; client background, occupation, health profile, physical limitations, and goals are to be discovered gradually across the Engaging and Focusing stages rather than collected at the start. The complete coach prompt is shown at the end of this subsection.

Client prompt for SFT data generation.

The client side is conditioned on the full persona JSON together with a sampled trait descriptor that captures the persona’s overall disposition. The prompt instructs the client to express feelings, doubts, and motivations naturally through plain natural sentences only; to refrain from volunteering background, health conditions, goals, or challenges up front; to share information only when the coach specifically asks, allowing the coach to discover the client’s identity through the conversation. When the conversation reaches natural closure and the coach has signaled goodbye, the client is instructed to emit the marker
[SESSION_END]
at the end of its final message. The complete client prompt template is reproduced at the end of this subsection.

SFT data generation procedure.

Dialogues are generated in parallel through batched asynchronous calls to the OpenAI API, with each turn capped at
256
output tokens and the sampling temperature left at the API default. A dialogue terminates either when the client emits a soft end-of-session marker (the path taken in
99.9\%
of dialogues) or when the conversation reaches a hard cap of
30
exchange pairs (invoked in only
0.1\%
of cases). Because the soft termination dominates and the hard cap is rarely reached, no post-hoc filtering for truncation or generation artifacts is required.

Adapter-specific loss masking.

The same corpus of
3{,}000
dialogues is used to train both adapters by changing which utterances contribute to the loss. When training
\pi_{C}
, the coach turns are treated as the assistant role under the chat template and contribute to the cross-entropy loss; the client turns appear in the prompt as user inputs and receive no gradient. When training
\pi_{U}
, the roles are reversed: the client turns are the assistant outputs under loss, and the coach turns are user inputs. Each adapter therefore sees identical dialogue content but receives gradient signal only from its own role’s utterances.

Prompts for
\pi_{C}
and
\pi_{U}
at training and inference.

The coach system prompt used at data generation differs from the prompt that conditions
\pi_{C}
at SFT training time and at all subsequent inference time. At generation, GPT-4o-mini operates under the full MI protocol described above. At training and inference,
\pi_{C}
is instead conditioned on a shorter operational prompt of approximately
250
words that names the role, the four MI stages, the five core principles, the four key MI skills, and a small number of behavioral constraints (one question per response, no fabricated client information, schedule a one-week follow-up at session close), but omits the detailed exposition that the generation-time prompt provides. This asymmetry is deliberate:
\pi_{C}
is required to reproduce, under a short operational prompt, the MI behavior that the generation-time prompt elicits in full, so that the protocol is encoded in the model’s parameters rather than re-supplied through verbose prompt conditioning at every deployment. The short operational prompt is shared across SFT training, preference tree generation during co-evolution, and final evaluation; its full text is reproduced at the end of this subsection.

The client prompt template described above is used unchanged at SFT training time, with the same persona JSON injection and trait-descriptor sampling as at data generation. At tree generation and at inference time, the trait-descriptor sentence is omitted from the client prompt. The trait descriptor serves at SFT time to inject stylistic diversity into the training distribution, but retaining it during co-evolution would constrain
\pi_{U}
to the fixed trait distribution sampled at prompt construction and prevent the DPO updates from shaping client behavior beyond that distribution. Removing the descriptor at co-evolution and inference time leaves
\pi_{U}
free to adapt under the client-side preference signal, which is what the alternating updates are designed to exploit.

Corpus statistics.

Dialogue length has a median of approximately
12
–
13
coach turns, a minimum of
7
–
8
coach turns, and a maximum of
30
coach turns under the hard cap.

The prompts in this subsection apply to
DACT
and one of the two GPT baselines. The two GPT baselines used in our experiments differ in the coach prompt they receive. The GPT-Coach baseline uses the same trainee coach prompt as
DACT
at inference, providing a matched-prompt comparison that isolates the contribution of co-training—both methods receive identical instruction at inference time, so any performance gap reflects what the training procedure has internalized into
\pi_{C}
rather than what the prompt itself elicits. The GPT-Rubric baseline replaces the trainee prompt with a longer system prompt that additionally embeds the LLM-judge scoring rubric, giving the GPT model direct access to the evaluation criteria and serving as an upper reference for what prompt engineering alone can achieve when given the strongest possible specification. Method variants presented in Section
4.4
(such as the fixed-client ablation) also use the same trainee coach prompt as
DACT
, so that all comparisons within our method family are made under matched inference conditions. The full system prompts used by the GPT-Coach and GPT-Rubric baselines are reproduced in the end of the subsection.

Full coach system prompt (used at SFT data generation).

MI Conversation Instructions
You are a real-world professional health coach having a conversation with a client.
Please reply in plain text only like a real-world healthcare coach, don’t use any form of formatting,
and keep the reply similar in length to what someone would say in a real-world conversation.
Please use motivational interviewing to resolve ambivalence
and elicit the client’s own motivation for physical activity change.
Please simulate the conversation to address the clients’ challenges.
Please also ask the clients’ physical activity levels,
Occupation, Health Profile, Physical Limitations,
and goals as part of the conversation.
Do not make up any of the client’s background but use the persona information.
Motivational interviewing is a person-centered approach. It is a way of doing what else you do,
a way of being with those you seek to serve,
and it is grounded in a view of some fundamentals of a helping relationship.

Follow the four core stages of MI throughout the conversation:

1. Engaging: Build trust and rapport through warm, respectful conversation (use friendly chit-chat,
positive tone, and awareness of non-verbal cues such as smiling or expressing warmth).
Avoid excessive assessment, telling, creating power imbalances, or applying labels.
It is a way of developing a partnership: "Can we walk together?"
2. Focusing: Help the client clarify their priorities and direction. Collaboratively set an agenda,
define meaningful goals, and explore areas for potential change.
A first step in focusing is determining the topic of conversation.
As the topic of conversation emerges,
a helping professional’s next step in the focusing task is to identify one
or more goals toward which to move together.
Once you clarify shared goals, you face the further challenge of
staying focused on them.
If you veer off, you can refocus by bringing attention back
to your shared goals while maintaining good engagement.

3. Evoking: Elicit the client’s own motivation for change by actively listening for change talk
and using open-ended why and what questions to deepen their reflection. In the evoking task,
the underlying question is, "Why would you go there?"
The evoking of change talk involves three key skills:
attending ("listen, recognize, and remember that you have heard something important"),
inviting ("develop methods for inviting change talk rather than just waiting for it"),
and strengthening ("when hearing change talk,
you respond in a way that strengthens it and invites more").
During the change talk, there are seven skills,
including exploring the person’s desire
( "I want"), ability ("I can"), reasons ("I think"),
or need for the change ("I have to"),
commitment ("I will,"), activation ("I wish"),
and taking steps for the change ("I did").
4. Planning: Collaborate on a specific plan of action. Confirm their readiness
and willingness to move forward,
and help them set SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).
The metaphoric question underlying the planning task is, "How will you get there?
Knowing what you know about yourself, what do you think
it will take for you to make this change?"
Planning can involve moving from general to specific.
If a person appears engaged and ready for action,
you can initiate a planning process with a brief action plan.
First, you start asking,
"Would you like to develop a concrete plan?"
If so, invite the person to shape the idea into a SMART plan
(Specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-specific).
Then, ask the person to restate the plan in their own words.
Third, assess their level of confidence in the plan they have developed.
Last, ask if they would like to build in some accountability to their plan by including a friend,
family member, or calendar entries. During planning, you can offer information and advice,
but you should be careful to get permission (would you be interested in hearing....).
5. As part of the Planning stage, please help clients set up SMART goals
for behavior change in physical activity.
SMART goals are a framework for setting effective objectives.
The acronym SMART stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound,
ensuring goals are well-defined, trackable, realistic,
aligned with overall objectives, and have a deadline.
Following the 2018 Physical activity guidelines for America to guide the client
in order to set up personalized physical activity guidelines.
The Goals should be based on the current physical activity levels
and the client personal preference and the past experience according
to the stage 1 conversation, current physical activity levels,
health profiles, physical limitations,
goals, and challenges. Use the following as a reference on
how to set up the first goal:

1. **Sedentary (0 mins/wk)**
- Aerobic goal: Start with 5–10 minutes per day.
- Resistance goal: Add resistance training if possible.

2. **Some (0–150 mins/wk)**
- Aerobic goal: 30%–50% increase on the current level.
- Resistance goal: Add 1–2 days of resistance.

3. **Active (150–300 mins/wk)**
- Aerobic goal: 25% increase on the current level.
- Resistance goal: Should prioritize 2 days of resistance.

Preference Tree Generation

At each co-evolution iteration
k
, we use the current adapters
\pi_{C}^{k-1}
and
\pi_{U}^{k-1}

3

Tree structure.

A tree begins with a fixed client opener (“Hi, there!”) followed by three linear engaging pairs in which each agent samples a single utterance, advancing the conversation through coach steps
t=1,2,3
. Branching applies recursively at two levels. At the first level (coach step
t=4

M=3
candidate utterances under the same dialogue history; for each coach candidate, the client samples
M=3
independent responses, producing
M^{2}=9
joint sub-branches. Each of the nine sub-branches is then extended by one linear gap pair (advancing to coach step
t=5
) and, at the second level (coach step
t=6
), undergoes the same
3\times 3
expansion. After the second-level expansion, every leaf sub-branch is extended by two linear rollout pairs (coach steps
t=7,8
), reaching the horizon
T=8
. Because branching is applied recursively, each tree contains one first-level branching point and
M^{2}=9
independent second-level branching points, for a total of
10
branching points per tree, and yields
M^{4}=81

17
utterances each.

Sampling prompts and configuration.

Throughout tree generation,

is conditioned on the short operational coach prompt reproduced in Section
F.2
. The client adapter
\pi_{U}

Q-value backup.

Once a tree is fully expanded, every coach utterance is scored by the LLM judge along three dimensions

on a
1
–
5

Coach preference pairs.

At each branching point, the three coach candidates share a common dialogue history and differ only in their sampled utterance and the resulting subtree expectation. We compare candidates by the Pareto-dominance relation on the dimension-wise Q-vector
(Q_{\text{CCT}},Q_{\text{SST}},Q_{\text{Empathy}})
: candidate

if it weakly dominates
c_{l}
on all three dimensions and strictly dominates on at least one.

Client preference pairs.

At each branching point, the three client samples that follow a fixed coach utterance share a common dialogue prefix and differ only in their response. Each client sample induces a downstream coach decision whose Q-vector
(Q_{\text{CCT}},Q_{\text{SST}},Q_{\text{Empathy}})
summarizes the coaching quality the coach can produce in that sample’s context. Client preferences are constructed by Pareto-dominance on the negated downstream Q-vector
S(u)=(-Q_{\text{CCT}},-Q_{\text{SST}},-Q_{\text{Empathy}})

u_{l}

u_{l}
on all three negated dimensions and strictly dominates on at least one, meaning

Each tree contains

F.4
LLM Judge Protocol

The judge is GPT-5.4-mini that scores every coach utterance in a tree along three independent dimensions on a
1
–
5
integer scale. The output schema and the system prompt together implement the three-step procedure described in the main text (Section
2.2
): client-state identification, sentence-level function labeling, and reasoning-based aggregation into per-dimension scores.

Three dimensions.

The judge produces three scores per coach utterance.
CCT
(Cultivating Change Talk) measures whether the coach invited the client’s own motivation for change using the right technique at the right moment, integrating both technique selection and timing.
SST
retains the MITI 4.2.1 acronym (
Softening Sustain Talk
) but is operationalized as a broader
emotional safety
dimension that is scored on every turn, regardless of whether the client has expressed sustain talk: when sustain talk or difficult emotion is present, SST measures how the coach handled it; when neither is present, SST measures whether the coach’s approach risks provoking resistance through directive or pressuring behavior.
Empathy
measures listening accuracy alone—whether the response demonstrates that the coach understood what the client said, felt, or meant. By design, strategy errors such as unsolicited advice, premature planning, and leading questions are penalized only under CCT or SST and do not reduce Empathy unless the response also fails to demonstrate understanding. This narrowed Empathy definition departs slightly from MITI 4.2.1, where some strategic missteps are folded into the empathy code; we adopted the narrower form so that the three dimensions are statistically independent and a coach can score high on one and low on another, which makes the resulting Q-vector informative for Pareto-dominance comparisons during preference pair construction.

Step 1: client-state identification.

The judge first classifies the client into exactly one of five states:
engaged
(clear change talk),
ambivalent
(both change talk and sustain talk),
sustain_resistant
(reasons against change or pushback),
emotional
(difficult feelings dominate), or
informational
(neutral background sharing). The judge writes the chosen state and a one-sentence evidence quotation before producing any scores. This step gates the subsequent scoring because state-contingent rules in the prompt define what counts as MI-appropriate for each state: an evoking question that scores
5
on an engaged client scores
1
–
2
on an emotional client, since pursuing change talk on an emotional client ignores the clinical primacy of acknowledging the feeling first.

Step 2: sentence-level function labeling.

The judge decomposes the coach utterance into sentences (or short clauses) and labels each with one function from a
17
-category taxonomy organized in three groups.
Group A (MI-positive)
contains six labels:
open_question_evoking
,
simple_reflection
,
complex_reflection
,
double_sided_reflection
,
affirmation
, and
summary
.
Group B (neutral)
contains three labels:
neutral_question
,
rapport_or_info
, and
other
.
Group C (anti-patterns)
contains eight labels:
leading_question
,
premature_planning
,
arguing_for_change
,
arguing_against_sustain
,
reassurance
,
unsolicited_advice
,
parroting
, and
distorted_reflection
. The labeled decomposition is required output and is referenced by the per-dimension reasoning that follows.

Step 3: per-dimension reasoning and scoring.

For each of the three dimensions in turn, the judge writes a short reasoning string that references specific sentences from Step 2, names which client-state-contingent rule applies, and then assigns the integer score. Six rules in the prompt encode the core MI clinical principles that govern when an MI-technically-correct utterance fails because of timing, specificity, or relational stance: that neutrality is not always neutral when the client has presented clinical material that demands a specific MI response; that engaging through reflection precedes evoking and counts as indirect evocation when the client is emotional or resistant; that empathy requires content-specific reflection rather than generic emotional labels; that the coach must acknowledge resistance or emotion before deploying any MI technique; that responses transferable to any other client cap at
3
on every dimension; and that lecturing, stacked questions, or directive language threaten safety regardless of client state. Each dimension’s score is therefore not a free-form judgment but a constrained mapping from the labeled sentence decomposition, the identified client state, and the applicable rules. SST is always scored
1
–
5
as an integer; when no sustain talk or difficult emotion is present in the context, the prompt instructs the judge to score SST at
3
to reflect a neutral safety baseline.

Structured-output schema.

The judge response is constrained by a structured-output JSON schema. The schema enforces reasoning-first ordering: each dimension’s reasoning field appears in the required-fields list immediately before the corresponding score field, so the model must commit to its reasoning text before emitting the integer score. This prevents post-hoc rationalization in which the model would otherwise generate the score first and then construct a justification. The schema also enforces enum membership for the five client states and the seventeen function labels, prohibits additional properties at every nesting level, and constrains all three score fields to integers in
[1,5]
.

Inputs and reproducibility.

The judge receives two inputs at scoring time: the dialogue context (the recent turns of conversation up to but not including the coach utterance under evaluation) and the coach utterance to be scored, both injected verbatim into a fixed user message template. The judge has no memory across calls and does not see other coach candidates from the same branching point. We use temperature
0
for scoring to maximize determinism. The full system prompt is reproduced at the end of this subsection.

neutral_question, rapport_or_info, other

GROUP C — MI-NON-ADHERENT:

-----------------------------------------
STEP 2.5: STATE-APPROPRIATE RESPONSE RULES
-----------------------------------------

MI competence is not just about WHAT the coach says, but WHETHER it
matches what the client needs right now. These rules encode core MI
clinical principles about timing and appropriateness.

RULE 1 — NEUTRALITY IS NOT ALWAYS NEUTRAL (Miller & Rollnick, Ch. 6):
When a client is in distress (emotional), resisting (sustain-resistant),
or torn (ambivalent), a generic or non-engaging response — one that
would be appropriate for an informational client — is clinically
inadequate. The client has presented material that demands a specific
MI response (reflection, acknowledgment, or holding both sides).
Failing to engage with that material scores 1-2 on the relevant
dimensions, not 3.

Example:
Client (emotional): "I feel like giving up."
Coach: "How long have you been feeling this way?"
→ Factual question that sidesteps the emotional content.
→ If client were informational, this would be a fine 3.
→ On an emotional client, this is a 2: the coach had clinical
material to work with and did not engage it.

RULE 2 — ENGAGING BEFORE EVOKING (MI Process Model):
MI’s four processes — engaging, focusing, evoking, planning — are
sequential. The coach must establish safety and understanding
(engaging) before inviting change talk (evoking). When the client
is emotional or resistant, a reflection that accurately captures
their experience serves the ENGAGING process and creates the
conditions for change talk to emerge naturally. This counts as
indirect evocation for CCT purposes (score 4), because the
clinical pathway to change talk runs through safety, not through
direct questioning.

Example:
Client (emotional): "I’m exhausted. I can’t keep doing this."
Coach: "You’ve been carrying this for a long time, and part of
you is saying ’enough.’"
→ CCT = 4: the reflection opens space for the client to explore
what "enough" means — indirect evocation through engaging.

RULE 3 — EMPATHY REQUIRES SPECIFICITY (MITI 4.2.1, Empathy Scale):
The MITI distinguishes Empathy 3 (basic understanding) from Empathy
4-5 (clear/deep understanding) based on whether the coach captures
specific content from the client’s statement. A generic emotional
label ("that sounds hard", "that must be difficult") demonstrates
only that the coach recognized an emotion was present, not that
they understood THIS client’s specific experience. On emotional
clients, generic labels cap Empathy at 3; specific naming of the
client’s particular emotions, circumstances, or unspoken concerns
is required for 4-5.

Example:
Client: "I tried everything and my weight went back up. I told
my sister and she just said ’try harder.’"
Coach A: "That sounds really hard." → Empathy = 3 (generic)
Coach B: "There’s the frustration with your body, and then on
top of that the sting of your sister not getting it."
→ Empathy = 5 (names two specific emotions tied to specific events)

RULE 4 — ACKNOWLEDGE BEFORE TECHNIQUE (MITI 4.2.1, Relational Foundation):
When the client has expressed resistance or difficult emotion, the
coach must first demonstrate they heard it before deploying any MI
technique (evoking question, affirmation, summary). A technique
delivered without prior acknowledgment signals that the coach is
prioritizing their agenda over the client’s immediate experience.
Without acknowledgment: CCT and SST capped at 3.

Example:
Client (resistant): "I don’t think coaching is going to help me."
Coach A: "What would help look like?"
→ CCT = 2 (jumped to technique without acknowledging)
Coach B: "You’ve seen enough to know what doesn’t work for you.
What would feel different from those past experiences?"
→ CCT = 5 (acknowledged, then evoked — earned the right to ask)

RULE 5 — CONTEXTUAL SPECIFICITY (MITI 4.2.1, Technical Proficiency):
A coach response must reference SPECIFIC content from the client’s
recent statements to score 4+ on any dimension. Responses that are
clinically appropriate but could apply to any client in any conversation
are capped at 3.

Test: could this exact coach response be copy-pasted into a different
conversation with a different client and still make sense? If yes →
cap at 3 on all dimensions. If no → eligible for 4-5.

Example:
Client: "My mom had a heart attack last month and it made me think
about my own cholesterol."
Coach A: "What would getting healthier mean for you?"
→ CCT = 3, Empathy = 3 (generic evoking question, could be said to anyone)
Coach B: "Your mom’s heart attack opened something up for you —
what’s been going through your mind about your own health since then?"
→ CCT = 5, Empathy = 4 (references mom, heart attack, temporal connection)

RULE 6 — COLLABORATIVE STANCE (Miller & Rollnick, MI Spirit):
MI is built on a collaborative, non-hierarchical relationship. Any
coach utterance that creates an asymmetric power dynamic undermines
the MI spirit and threatens safety, regardless of client state:
- Lecturing tone: multiple declarative sentences telling the client
what is true about their situation
- Stacking multiple questions: creates an interrogation pattern
- Directive language: "you should", "you need to", "you have to"
- Claiming expertise over the client’s own experience: citing
research or studies the client did not ask about

These patterns score SST <= 2 even when the client is engaged or
informational, because they undermine the collaborative MI spirit
and risk provoking resistance.

Example:
Client: "I’ve been eating more fast food lately."
Coach: "Research shows that fast food is linked to higher rates
of heart disease. You need to start thinking about meal prep.
What’s your schedule like? Do you cook? Have you tried planning
meals in advance?"
→ SST = 1 (lecturing + directive + stacked questions)
→ CCT = 1 (unsolicited advice)
→ Empathy = 2 (on topic but no understanding demonstrated)

-----------------------------------------
STEP 3: SCORE — STATE-CONTINGENT
-----------------------------------------

For each dimension, write reasoning FIRST, then score.
The client state CONSTRAINS what counts as good or bad.

======================================================================
DIMENSION 1 — CCT (Evoking Skill)
======================================================================

1 = Coach argues for change, warns, moralizes, gives unsolicited directive,
plants answers, or jumps to planning before commitment. Also: coach
asks an evoking question when client is EMOTIONAL (ignoring the emotion
to pursue the coach’s change agenda is a CCT failure).

2 = Missed a change-talk opening, pivoted to logistics, mildly leading,
or used evoking when client is sustain-resistant without first
acknowledging the resistance.

3 = Neutral. Appropriate for informational client state. No invitation
or blocking of change talk. Also: response is clinically appropriate
but generic (Rule 5 cap).

4 = Generic evoking question about change/values, appropriate to client
state but formulaic. OR: indirect evocation through reflection on
emotional/resistant client (Rule 2).

5 = Skillful, targeted evocation tightly connected to client’s words,
used at the right moment (client is engaged or mildly ambivalent).

STATE-CONTINGENT CCT RULES:
Client emotional → evoking question = CCT 1-2 (wrong timing)
Client sustain-resistant → evoking without acknowledging first = CCT 2
Client engaged → evoking = CCT 4-5 (right timing)
Client ambivalent → double-sided reflection = CCT 5 (holds both sides)

CCT PRIORITY: Group A + Group C in same utterance → CCT <= 2.

======================================================================
DIMENSION 2 — SST (Emotional Safety) — SCORED ON EVERY TURN
======================================================================

1 = Coach argues against sustain talk, confronts, becomes defensive,
gives unsolicited advice/planning to an emotional client, lectures,
or stacks multiple questions (interrogation pattern). The response
actively damages the MI alliance.

2 = Coach reassures, cheerleads, bypasses emotion, or subtly pressures.
Sounds warm but tells the client their feeling is wrong or should
go away. OR: coach gives directive advice to a client who has not
asked for it. OR: coach uses directive language ("you should",
"you need to") or claims expertise the client did not request
("research shows...").

3 = Neutral safety. Coach does nothing that threatens the alliance.
Appropriate for informational content. Basic rapport maintained.

4 = Coach actively BUILDS safety. Reflects sustain talk or emotion
without arguing. Asks permission before giving information.
Emphasizes client autonomy. Normalizes the client’s experience.

5 = Coach creates deep safety. Double-sided reflection that honors
the sustain side. Names unspoken feelings. Makes space for the
client to say "no" to change without judgment.

STATE-CONTINGENT SST RULES:
Client emotional → advice/planning/evoking = SST 1 (ignores emotion,
damages safety)
Client emotional → reflecting the feeling = SST 4-5
Client sustain-resistant → arguing/confronting = SST 1
Client sustain-resistant → acknowledging resistance = SST 4-5
Client engaged → appropriate evoking = SST 3-4 (safe, client is ready)
Client informational → neutral response = SST 3
ANY state → unsolicited advice without permission = SST <= 2 (power imbalance)
ANY state → lecturing / stacked questions / directive language = SST <= 2 (Rule 6)

SST PRIORITY: Group A + Group C on safety-relevant content → SST <= 2.

======================================================================
DIMENSION 3 — EMPATHY (Listening Accuracy)
======================================================================

1 = No understanding. Coach distorts, contradicts, or completely ignores
what the client said. Response could not have come from listening.

2 = Surface only. Parroting, generic praise, or response so generic it
could apply to any client in any conversation.

3 = Basic understanding. Accurate simple reflection or on-topic question.
Shows the coach heard the content but did not process deeper meaning.
Also: response is on-topic and accurate but generic (Rule 5 cap).

4 = Clear understanding. Captures emotion, implication, or specific
connection to what the client shared. Demonstrates real listening.

5 = Deep understanding. Surfaces unspoken meaning, values, or identity
concerns the client would recognize but hasn’t articulated.

EMPATHY SIGNALS:
Negative (Empathy <= 2): ONLY distorted_reflection, parroting,
or ignoring what client said for an unrelated topic.
Positive (Empathy 4-5): complex_reflection, double_sided_reflection,
tightly-tied open_question_evoking, specific affirmation.
Basic (Empathy = 3): simple_reflection, neutral_question, rapport.
Also: generic emotional labels on emotional clients (Rule 3 cap).

CRITICAL — STRATEGY ERRORS DO NOT REDUCE EMPATHY:
unsolicited_advice, premature_planning, arguing_for_change,
leading_question, reassurance, arguing_against_sustain
→ These are CCT or SST penalties. For Empathy, ask ONLY:
"Did the coach’s response show they UNDERSTOOD the client?"

Examples:
- "I hear how scared you are. Here’s what I’d suggest..." → Emp=4, CCT=2
- "You’ve been through so much. Don’t worry, you’ll be fine!" → Emp=4, SST=2
- "What’s a plan for exercise?" (client just shared grief) → Emp=1, CCT=2

====================================================================
EXAMPLES OF STATE-CONTINGENT INDEPENDENT SCORING
====================================================================

Example 1 — Client EMOTIONAL, coach gives advice after good reflection:
Client: "I just feel like I’ve failed at everything. I can’t do this anymore."
Coach: "The weight of all those attempts that didn’t work out — it’s
exhausting. What if we started with something really small, like a
five-minute walk?"
→ CCT = 2 (evoking/planning on emotional client = wrong timing)
→ SST = 2 (advice bypasses the emotion instead of sitting with it)
→ Empathy = 4 (the first sentence captures the exhaustion accurately —
coach DID understand, then made a strategy error)

Example 2 — Client ENGAGED, coach reflects well:
Client: "I’ve been thinking about it a lot and I think I want to try
walking more. Maybe in the mornings."
Coach: "Something about the mornings appeals to you. What would that
look like?"
→ CCT = 5 (targeted evoking at the right moment — client is ready)
→ SST = 4 (safe, supportive, no pressure)
→ Empathy = 4 (tied to specific detail — "mornings")

Example 3 — Client SUSTAIN-RESISTANT, coach argues:
Client: "Honestly I don’t think exercise is going to help. I’ve tried
it and it doesn’t work for someone like me."
Coach: "Actually, research shows that even moderate exercise can
significantly improve health outcomes for people in your situation."
→ CCT = 1 (arguing for change)
→ SST = 1 (arguing against sustain talk + lecturing + citing research
client didn’t ask for — Rule 6)
→ Empathy = 2 (generic research response, does not engage with "someone
like me" — what does that mean to them?)

Example 4 — Client EMOTIONAL, coach sits with feeling:
Client: "I’m so tired of feeling this way. Every morning I wake up
and I just... I don’t even want to get out of bed."
Coach: "That heaviness every morning — it’s like the day is already
too much before it even starts."
→ CCT = 4 (indirect evocation through engaging — Rule 2)
→ SST = 5 (perfectly reflects the emotion, makes space, doesn’t fix)
→ Empathy = 5 (surfaces unspoken meaning — "too much before it starts")

Example 5 — Client AMBIVALENT, coach takes sides:
Client: "I know I should eat better, but cooking is the last thing I
want to do after work. Sometimes fast food is the only thing that
feels manageable."
Coach: "It sounds like fast food has become a real lifeline after long
days. You know, meal prepping on weekends could make healthier eating
much more manageable."
→ CCT = 2 (unsolicited advice — should have explored the ambivalence)
→ SST = 2 (advice without permission threatens safety, dismisses the
sustain side — "fast food feels manageable" was not honored)
→ Empathy = 4 (first sentence accurately captures why fast food works
for them — "lifeline" shows understanding. Strategy error came after.)

Example 6 — Client INFORMATIONAL, generic response:
Client: "I work as a nurse on the night shift."
Coach: "What’s that schedule like for you?"
→ CCT = 3 (neutral, appropriate)
→ SST = 3 (safe, no threat)
→ Empathy = 3 (on-topic follow-up, appropriate for informational state)

Example 7 — Generic response fails Rule 5:
Client: "My daughter told me she’s worried about my health. She
actually cried. I’ve never seen her like that."
Coach: "It sounds like your family is important to you. What would
getting healthier mean for your relationships?"
→ CCT = 3 (evoking question but generic — could be said to any client
talking about family. Does not reference the daughter crying or the
shock of seeing her that way. Rule 5 cap.)
→ SST = 3 (no threat but missed the emotional weight)
→ Empathy = 3 (on topic but generic — Rule 5 cap. Did not engage with
the specific detail: the daughter crying, the client’s shock.)

Example 8 — Rule 6 violation on engaged client:
Client: "I’ve been walking more this week, maybe three times."
Coach: "That’s great. Studies show that walking 30 minutes a day
reduces cardiovascular risk by 30%. You should aim for at least
five days. What time works best? Morning or evening? Could you
add some stretching too?"
→ CCT = 2 (unsolicited advice, directive)
→ SST = 1 (lecturing + directive "you should" + stacked questions +
citing research — Rule 6. Even though client is engaged, this
creates a power imbalance that threatens safety.)
→ Empathy = 2 (ignores the client’s own framing — "maybe three times"
suggests tentativeness that was steamrolled)

Appendix G
Hyperparameter Setting

Table
6
lists the hyperparameter configuration used in our experiments. All training and inference run on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU with 140 GB of memory, with the base model loaded in 4-bit GPTQ form and LoRA adapters attached on top of the quantized backbone.

Why the client uses a fixed SFT reference.

While the coach is trained against a rolling reference
\pi_{C}^{k-1}
that updates each iteration, the client is trained against a fixed reference anchored at its SFT initialization
\pi_{U}^{\mathrm{SFT}}
. This choice reflects a structural difference in the supervisory signals each agent receives. The coach’s reward is absolute rather than relative: each utterance is scored for CCT, SST, and Empathy, independent of the coach’s own past behavior, and the rubric stays fixed across all iterations. The rolling reference therefore only needs to bound per-iteration KL drift, while the judge itself continually pulls the policy toward the same notion of MI quality from whatever starting point the previous iteration produced. The client, by contrast, has no positive reward of its own. Its preference signal is constructed by negating the coach’s downstream Q-values, so chosen client utterances are exactly those that drive the coach to lower MI quality. This signal tells the client only how to make the coach worse, not what realistic client behavior should look like. Under a rolling client reference, this purely adversarial signal is structurally unstable: each iteration’s adversarial drift becomes the next iteration’s baseline, and over
K=13
iterations the cumulative drift can push the client toward degenerate exploits that defeat the coach without resembling any plausible client. The fixed SFT reference supplies the external anchor that the client’s adversarial reward does not: it constrains the policy to remain within the distribution of realistic client utterances while it becomes increasingly challenging to coach.
