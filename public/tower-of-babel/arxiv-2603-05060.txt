Asymptotic Behavior of Multi–Task Learning: Implicit Regularization and Double Descent Effects
Ayed M. Alrashdi, Oussama Dhifallah, Houssem Sifaou

Abstract
Multi–task learning seeks to improve the generalization error by leveraging the common information shared by multiple related tasks. One challenge in multi–task learning is identifying formulations capable of uncovering the common information shared between different but related tasks. This paper provides a precise asymptotic analysis of a popular multi–task formulation associated with misspecified perceptron learning models. The main contribution of this paper is to precisely determine the reasons behind the benefits gained from combining multiple related tasks. Specifically, we show that combining multiple tasks is asymptotically equivalent to a traditional formulation with additional regularization terms that help improve the generalization performance. Another contribution is to empirically study the impact of combining tasks on the generalization error. In particular, we empirically show that the combination of multiple tasks postpones the double descent phenomenon and can mitigate it asymptotically.



I
Introduction

I-A

Motivation

Multi–task learning
[
11
,
14
,
29
]
is a promising technique for improving generalization performance. It consists of leveraging common information shared among several related tasks to enhance the generalization performance associated with each individual task.
One of the main challenges in multi–task learning is to identify learning formulations that can benefit each separate task.
This paper considers a popular multi–task formulation
[
21
]
associated with misspecified perception learning models (see equation (
II-B
)).
Recent literature
[
23
]
shows that this formulation can identify the common information that may benefit individual tasks.
Specifically, it shows that this multi–task formulation leads to superior generalization performance than traditional formulations.
This work provides a sharp asymptotic analysis of the multi–task setup described in
[
21
]
.
In particular, our analysis reveals an asymptotic equivalent formulation of the multi–task problem.
The asymptotic predictions are then used to identify an equivalent formulation. Moreover, our analysis illustrates that the considered multi–task formulation is asymptotically equivalent to a traditional formulation with additional regularization terms that are the main cause of the generalization improvements.

Classical learning theory
[
7
]
suggests that the generalization error exhibits a U-shaped curve pattern. That is, the generalization first decreases until it reaches a minimum. Classical thinking identifies this region as the under-fitting regime. After the minimum, the learning model may over-fit which causes a poor performance on new data samples.

In this regime, the generalization error is monotonically increasing as a function of the problem parameters. The objective is then to identify the location of the minimum known as the
sweet spot
. Modern machine learning methods
[
7
]
violate this property. Instead, many machine learning methods follow what is known as the
double descent curve
(see the references
[
7
,
10
,
8
]
).
The generalization error of these models initially decreases, then increases until it hits a peak referred to as the
interpolation threshold
. Beyond this peak, the generalization error declines monotonically with respect to the model parameters.
The study of such learning models has recently attracted significant attention since they violate classical results. Moreover, recent efforts
[
9
,
16
,
26
]
towards providing precise analysis of the double descent phenomenon focus on the single task problem. In particular, they present a theoretical understanding of the double descent phenomenon in regression and classification models as a function of
\kappa=k/n
. Here,
k
is the number of parameters, and
n
denotes the size of the training set. In this paper, we empirically study the impact of combining multiple related tasks on the behavior of the generalization error.
Our investigations indicate that the position of the interpolation threshold is influenced by the number of combined tasks. Moreover, the double descent effect can be reduced by aggregating a large number of tasks. Figure
1
illustrates these observations. It examines a regression model using the squared loss and a binary classification model using the logistic loss. In both cases,
T
tasks are combined following the formulation in
[
21
]
. The generalization error in both models exhibits a double descent pattern, with the interpolation threshold shifting to higher values as the number of tasks increases. Additionally, the results in Figure
1
indicate that combining a sufficiently large number of tasks can help mitigating the interpolation threshold.

I-B

Summary of Contributions

The main goal of this work is to provide a precise investigation of the effects of learning different yet related tasks, following the formulation presented in
[
21
]
. Our analysis shows that the combination of multiple related tasks is asymptotically equivalent to a traditional formulation with an additional regularization term. The regularization is given in an explicit form. Moreover, we show that the additional regularization depends on the similarity between the tasks and helps improve the generalization performance. Our analysis starts by providing a sharp asymptotic analysis of the popular multi–task formulation introduced in
[
21
]
for a fixed number of tasks
T
. Specifically, we show that the generalization error associated with the multi–task learning formulation concentrates in the large system limit for fixed
T
.
By solving a low–dimensional deterministic optimization problem, the asymptotic limit can be explicitly determined.
The resulting asymptotic predictions are subsequently employed to analyze the performance of the multi–task formulation in the regime where the number of combined tasks grows to infinity at a slower rate than the problem dimensions.
The given analysis employs a Gaussian equivalence theorem known as the convex Gaussian min–max theorem (CGMT)
[
36
]
.

Our precise characterization is valid for general convex loss functions, particularly a squared loss is used for regression tasks, and a logistic loss is employed for binary classification tasks.
Furthermore, it is valid for a broad class of generative models.
We specialize our general theoretical results to widely used regression and classification models. Empirical investigations show that the studied model exhibits a double descent phenomenon.
In particular, they demonstrate that the generalization error associated with the standard formulation is strictly decreasing after reaching the interpolation threshold at a value
\kappa^{\star}
. Additionally, they show that the combination of
T
related tasks shifts the interpolation threshold by a factor that depends on
T
.

I-C

Related Work

The concept of multi–task learning
[
11
,
14
]
is associated with various machine learning approaches, including transfer learning
[
31
,
29
]
. These methods are similar in that they leverage information from different yet related tasks to enhance generalization error. The key difference lies in their objective: multi–task learning aims to improve the generalization performance across all learning models, whereas transfer learning focuses on using information from previously solved tasks to enhance the generalization error of a specific target task.
Recent efforts
[
6
,
29
,
38
]
consider modeling the relatedness between the tasks. For instance, the work in
[
29
]
models the relatedness between the tasks in terms of the correlation between the shared parameters. Another approach
[
38
]
models the relatedness in terms of the prior distribution of the shared parameters. A different line of work
[
29
]
focuses on providing formulations that can uncover the shared information between the tasks. This work precisely analyzes a generalized version of the popular multi–task formulation introduced in
[
29
]
. The approach in
[
29
]
provides a natural extension of the support vector machine to a multiple task setting. We extend this formulation to solve general linear regression and binary classification learning problems.

While most research works focus on the practical aspects of the multi–task setting
[
39
,
40
,
13
]
, there have been several studies
[
24
,
12
,
25
,
37
]
that focus on providing precise performance analysis. Our work is particularly related to the analysis in
[
37
]
, which considers the least square support vector machine formulation. Compared to
[
37
]
, our contribution differs as follows. The analysis presented in this work is more general as it is valid for general convex formulations. In addition, this paper provides a precise characterization of the regularization effects of the multi–task formulation in
[
29
]
and examines the impact of task combination on the double descent phenomenon.

The analysis presented in this paper is aligned with recent literature on the precise high–dimensional analysis of convex regression formulations
[
36
,
20
,
18
,
4
,
34
,
3
]
and convex classification formulations
[
33
,
17
,
16
]
. A common tool used in this research direction is the convex Gaussian min–max theorem (CGMT)
[
22
,
35
,
36
,
2
]
. In this paper, we analyze the multi–task formulation in
[
29
]
associated with misspecified perceptron learning models using an extended version of the CGMT. A closely related work is the analysis presented in
[
36
]
, which uses the CGMT framework to precisely analyze a general convex regression formulation with possible inseparable loss function and regularization. Compared to
[

]
, our contribution differs as follows. The analysis presented in
[

assumes that the input feature vectors form a Gaussian–distributed matrix with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) components. In this paper, the input vectors from different tasks comprise a block diagonal matrix where the diagonal blocks are Gaussian with i.i.d elements, and the off-diagonal blocks are zero. In this case, the analysis presented in

II
Learning Models

II-A

Training Model

We consider a scenario in which the learner has access to
T
distinct learning tasks. For the
t^{th}
task, the training dataset is given by
\{(\boldsymbol{a}_{t,i},y_{t,i})\}_{1\leq i\leq n_{t}}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
represents the feature vector and
y_{t,i}
is the corresponding label (
\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n_{t}\},~t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
).
In this work, we assume the labels are generated based on the following model:

\displaystyle y_{t,i}=\varphi(\boldsymbol{a}_{t,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a hidden vector associated with the
t^{th}
task, and
\varphi(\cdot)
is a function that may be deterministic or probabilistic. Furthermore, we assume that the learning tasks are related in the following manner:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\sigma\boldsymbol{v}_{t}+\boldsymbol{v}_{0},~\forall t\in\{1,\dots,T\},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a task specific vector and
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a shared vector between all the tasks.
Observe that the parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
governs the degree of similarity among the tasks. Based on this, we define the similarity between tasks using the quantity
\rho
, given by

\rho=\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}}.

Note that
\rho\in[0,1]
, where values of
\rho
approaching
1
indicate that the tasks are highly similar, while lower values suggest that the tasks are dissimilar. Hereafter, we refer to
\rho
as the
similarity measure
.

In this work, we consider a misspecified learning scenario in which the learner has access only to partial observations of the input vectors during training process. Specifically, for each input vector
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,i}
, only a subset of its components, denoted by
(a_{t,ij},~j\in\mathcal{S})
, is available to the learner, where
\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
.
To simplify the analysis, we assume that the subset
\mathcal{S}
is fixed and does not depend on the sample index
i\in\{1,\dots,n_{t}\}
or the task index
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. Furthermore, we assume that the cardinality of
\mathcal{S}
is fixed at
k
, with
1\leq k\leq p
.

The analysis presented in this work is valid for input vectors and hidden vectors generated randomly as summarized in the subsequent assumption.

Assumption 1

(Input/Hidden Vectors)
.

For any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the input vectors
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{t,i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n_{t}}
are assumed to be known and drawn independently from a standard Gaussian distribution.
The vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are assumed to be independent of the input vectors and are generated independently from a uniform distribution on the unit sphere.
Without loss of generality, we assume that both
\boldsymbol{v}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
are unit-norm vectors.
Furthermore, the set
\mathcal{S}
is assumed to be selected uniformly at random.

In addition, the results hold in the high–dimensional asymptotic regime, where the problem dimensions
p
,
k
, and
n_{t}

grow large and satisfy the next assumption.

Assumption 2

(High–dimensional Asymptotics)
.

For any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, we assume that the number of samples and the number of known components of the input vector satisfy
n_{t}=n_{t}(p)
and
k=k(p)
with
\alpha_{t,p}=p/n_{t}(p)\to\alpha_{t}>0
and
\kappa_{t,p}=k(p)/n_{t}(p)\to\kappa_{t}>0
as
p\to\infty
, where
\kappa_{t}\leq\alpha_{t}
. Furthermore, the number of tasks
T\geq 1
is independent of the dimension
p
.

This paper relies on specific assumptions regarding the distribution of the input vectors, the generative model in (
2
), and the distribution of the hidden vectors. We emphasize that these assumptions are crucial for the validity of our asymptotic analysis. An interesting direction for future research is to relax the Gaussianity assumption by demonstrating universality results (see, e.g.,
[
28
,
30
]
).

II-B

A Multi–Task Learning Algorithm

Given the similarity among the learning tasks, a widely used training strategy
[
21
]
involves jointly learning the collection of hidden vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
. This approach incorporates a regularization term that reflects the task similarity structure given in (
3
). In particular, a commonly adopted formulation in multi–task learning takes the following general form:

\displaystyle\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\ell\Big(y_{t,i};\boldsymbol{b}_{t,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\rVert^{2}.

(4)

In the formulation above, the vector
\boldsymbol{b}_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is obtained by concatenating the components of the input vector
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,i}
corresponding to the index set
\mathcal{S}
.
The term
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
represents the mean of the optimization vectors
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
, defined as
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}/T
.
The parameter
\gamma_{1}\geq 0
regulates the strength of the regularization applied to each task, while
\gamma_{2}\geq 0
governs the regularization imposed on the average model. The formulation in (
II-B
) is applicable to general loss functions used in both regression and classification tasks.
The loss function
\ell(y;x)
can be expressed in one of the following two general forms:

\displaystyle\ell(y;x)=f(y-x),\quad\text{or}\quad\ell(y;x)=f(yx),

(5)

where the first expression corresponds to regression tasks, and the second is applicable to classification tasks. The function
f(\cdot)
denotes a general scalar function. Note that the optimization problem in (
II-B
) reduces to a standard (per-task) learning problem when
\gamma_{2}=0
. Furthermore, the formulation is symmetric across tasks when all tasks have an equal number of training samples. Throughout this work, we refer to the case
\gamma_{2}=0
as the
traditional formulation
, and to the case
\gamma_{2}>0
as the
multi–task formulation
.

II-C

Performance Measure

The main goal of this work is to precisely analyze the performance of the multi–task learning approach on unobserved test data. We use the
generalization error
to measure the performance of the considered multi–task learning formulation.
To reach a formal definition of the generalization error, we start by defining the vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t},~\text{and}~\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}(\mathcal{S}^{c})=\boldsymbol{0}_{p-k},

(6)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
denotes the components of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}
with index in the set
\mathcal{S}
, whereas
\boldsymbol{0}_{p-k}
corresponds to the all-zero vector of size
p-k
.
In this paper, it is assumed that the
t^{th}
task predicts the label of any new test sample
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{y}_{t,\text{new}}=\widehat{\varphi}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}].

(7)

In the above equation,
\widehat{\varphi}(\cdot)
denotes a pre-defined scalar function. Now, we are ready to define the generalization error. In particular, the
generalization error
associated with the
t^{th}
task can be defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p,t,\text{test}}=\frac{1}{4^{\vartheta}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}})-\widehat{\varphi}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}})\Big)^{2}\Big].

(8)

Here, the parameter
\vartheta
is set to
\vartheta=0
for regression tasks and
\vartheta=1
for binary classification tasks.
Note that the expectation in (
8
) is taken with respect to the distribution of
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}
and
\varphi(\cdot)
.

Validation Models
: In this paper, we present a precise asymptotic analysis of the formulation in (
II-B
). Our theoretical derivations provided in the appendix are applicable to a broad class of convex loss functions, and to general models satisfying (
2
).
However, for clarity and to facilitate interpretation of the results, we focus on two widely used loss functions in this paper: the
squared loss
and the
logistic loss
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\ell(y;x)=\frac{1}{2}(x-y)^{2},~\text{and}~\ell(y;x)=\log(1+e^{-xy}),

(9)

respectively.
These loss functions are employed to learn regression and classification models, respectively. In the case of the
regression
model, we assume that both
\varphi(\cdot)
and
\widehat{\varphi}(\cdot)
correspond to the
identity
function. For the
classification
model, both functions are taken to be the
sign
function. Throughout the paper, we refer to these setups as the
linear regression model
and the
binary classification model
, respectively.

Symmetric Multi–Task Formulation

In this section, we present a precise high–dimensional analysis of the multi–task learning approach. For the simplicity of analysis, we consider the case when all the tasks have the same training set size, that is,
n_{t}=n,\forall t\in\{1,\dots,T\}

III-A

Precise Asymptotic Predictions

The asymptotic predictions of the symmetric multi–task learning require a few definitions. We start by defining the following low–dimensional deterministic formulation

\displaystyle\min_{q,r\geq 0}\max_{\eta>0}~\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}-\eta)(q^{2}+r^{2})+\frac{q^{2}}{2}\ \frac{\gamma_{2}+\eta}{1+(1-\rho)\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\eta T}}\ \mathcal{G}(T,\eta)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{\ell(Y;.)}\left(rH+qS;\frac{\kappa}{(\gamma_{2}+\eta)}\Big(1+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\eta T}\Big)\right)\right],

where the function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(T,\eta)=1-\frac{\gamma_{2}\rho T}{\eta T+\gamma_{2}(1-\rho+\rho T)},

\displaystyle Y=\varphi\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\left[S\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\alpha}}+Z\sqrt{1-\frac{\kappa}{\alpha}}\ \right]\bigg),

(11)

and

and

The expectation in the objective function of the problem in (

III-B

Combining Large Number of Tasks

Here, we study the properties of the multi–task formulation when the number of tasks
T
grows to infinity slower than the dimensions
p
,
n
and
k
. We start our analysis by defining the following scalar formulation

\displaystyle\min_{q,r\geq 0}\max_{\eta>0}

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}-\eta)(q^{2}+r^{2})+\frac{q^{2}}{2}(\gamma_{2}+\eta)\Big(1-\frac{\gamma_{2}\rho}{\eta+\gamma_{2}\rho}\Big)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{\ell(Y;.)}\Big(rH+qS;\frac{\kappa}{\gamma_{2}+\eta}\Big)\Big],

(14)

where
H
and
S
are independent standard Gaussian random variables.
The theoretical result is stated in the next Lemma.

Lemma 1

(Large Number of Tasks)
.

Suppose that the assumptions
1
-
2
are satisfied. Moreover, assume that the tasks have the same training set size, i.e.,
\alpha_{t}=\alpha,\forall t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. Then, for any
\zeta>0
, the generalization error corresponding to the
t^{th}
task converges in probability as follows

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to+\infty}\lim_{p\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(\lvert{\mathcal{E}}_{p,t,\text{test}}-{\mathcal{E}}_{t,\text{test}}\rvert<\zeta\big)=1.

The scalar
{\mathcal{E}}_{t,\text{test}}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{t,\text{test}}=\frac{1}{4^{\vartheta}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi(c_{0}G_{1})-\widehat{\varphi}(c_{1}G_{1}+c_{2}G_{2})\right)^{2}\right],

(15)

where
c_{1}
and

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we precisely analyzed a popular multi–task formulation. Specifically, we provided an exact characterization of the generalization error corresponding to the considered multi–task formulation. The predictions are based on a multivariate version of the CGMT framework. Our precise results are then used to study the regularization effects of the considered multi–task formulation. Particularly, we showed that the multi–task formulation is asymptotically equivalent to a traditional formulation with an additional regularization that favors solutions aligned with the generative model. Moreover, we empirically studied the impact of combining tasks on the generalization error. In particular, it has been empirically shown that the combination of multiple tasks postpones the double descent phenomenon and can mitigate it asymptotically.

A-A

Multivariate Convex Gaussian Min-max Theorem

To rigorously prove the technical results stated in Theorem
2
, we use an extended version of the CGMT framework, that is called the multivariate convex Gaussian min-max theorem (MCGMT)
[
19
]
. The MCGMT replaces the high–dimensional analysis of a generally hard primary problem with a simpler formulation. In this paper, we consider primary problems of the form

\Phi_{p}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}}\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{u}),

(28)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
are optimization variables, and
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times k}
has independent standard Gaussian random components, for any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. Additionally, the vectors
\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{u}
are formed by the concatenation of the vectors
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, respectively. We refer to the formulation in (
28
) as the multivariate primary optimization (MPO).
Then, the corresponding multivariate auxiliary optimization (MAO) is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{p}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}}\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\rVert\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}+\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{u}),

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
are independent standard Gaussian random vectors, for any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. Here, we assume that
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times k}
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
are all independent of each other, the feasibility sets
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{Tk}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are convex and compact, and the function
\Upsilon:\mathbb{R}^{Tk}\times\mathbb{R}^{{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous
convex-concave
on
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\times\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
, where
n=\sum_{t=1}^{T}n_{t}
.
The following theorem shows that the optimization problems in (
28
) and (
29
) are equivalent in the large system limit.

Theorem 3

(MCGMT
[
19
]
)
.

For any fixed
T\geq 1
, define the open set
\mathcal{S}_{p}
.
Moreover, define the set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{p}={\mathcal{S}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{p}
. Let
\phi_{p}
and
\phi^{c}_{p}
be the optimal cost values of the MAO formulation in (
29

{\mathcal{S}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
and
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{p}
, respectively. Assume that the following properties are all satisfied

1.

There exists a constant
\phi
such that the optimal cost
\phi_{p}
converges in probability to
\phi
as
p
goes to
+\infty
.

2.

There exists a constant
\phi^{c}
such that the optimal cost
\phi^{c}_{p}
converges in probability to
\phi^{c}
as
p
goes to
+\infty
.

3.

There exists a positive constant
\zeta>0
such that
\phi^{c}\geq\phi+\zeta
.

Then, the following convergence in probability holds

\lvert\Phi_{p}-\phi_{p}\rvert\overset{p\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathbb{P}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p})\overset{p\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1,

where
\Phi_{p},
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{p}
are the optimal cost and the optimal solution of the MPO formulation in (

The above theorem allows us to analyze the generally easy MAO formulation given in (

\{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, i.e.,
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}
.
Our first objective is to express the optimization problem in (
A-B
) in the form of a MPO formulation in (
28
). Then, apply the MCGMT framework to formulate the corresponding MAO formulation. The final step is to study the asymptotic properties of the obtained MAO.

A-B
1
Formulating the MAO Problem

The first step to obtain a multivariate auxiliary formulation is to formulate our optimization problem in the form of the MPO in (
28
). To this end, we start by introducing additional optimization variables as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\max_{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}}

\displaystyle~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}u_{t,i}\boldsymbol{b}_{t,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\ell^{\star}\left(y_{t,i};u_{t,i}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\rVert^{2}.

(31)

Here, the function
\ell^{\star}(y;.)
denotes the convex conjugate of the loss function
\ell(y;.)
. Define the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times k}
as the concatenation of the vectors
\{\boldsymbol{b}_{t,i}^{\top}\}_{1\leq i\leq n_{t}}
. The multivariate version of the CGMT assumes that the feasibility sets of the MPO are convex and compact. Although these properties are not trivial in our case, one can follow the approaches in
[
36
,
20
,
17
]
to prove that the optimal solutions of the formulation in (
A-B1
) belong to convex and compact sets, asymptotically. This implies that one can equivalently formulate the problem in (
A-B1
) with convex and compact feasibility sets. In the rest of this paper, we only consider convex feasibility sets where the compactness is assumed implicitly.
Note that the labels
\{y_{t,i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n_{t}}
depend on the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}_{t}
, therefore, we cannot directly apply the MCGMT. To overcome this issue, we decompose the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}_{t}
without changing its statistics as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{B}_{t}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{B}_{t}\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t}=\boldsymbol{s}_{t}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t},

(32)

where the elements of
\boldsymbol{s}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times k}
are drawn independently from a standard Gaussian distribution, while
\boldsymbol{s}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}
are independent of each other.
Furthermore,
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}
denotes the entries of the vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
with index in the set
\mathcal{S}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}
is the normalized version of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}
.
Also,
\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
represents the projection matrix onto the orthogonal complement of the space spanned by the vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}
.
Note that the result in (
32
) is equality in distribution. Then, one can formulate the optimization problem (
A-B1
) as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\max_{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}}

\displaystyle~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{t}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{ts}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert^{2}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\ell^{\star}\left(y_{t,i};u_{t,i}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\rVert^{2}.

(33)

Note that the formulation in (
A-B1
) is in the form of the MPO problem introduced in (
28
). Moreover, one can see that the convexity assumption in the MCGMT framework is satisfied. Then, the corresponding MAO formulation can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\max_{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}}~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\rVert}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\lVert\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\rVert^{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{t}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}{n_{t}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\ell^{\star}\left(y_{t,i};u_{t,i}\right)+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert^{2}.

(34)

Here, the vectors
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
have components independently drawn from a standard Gaussian distribution. Next, we focus our attention on expressing the MAO formulation in (
A-B1
) in terms of scalar variables, then, studying its asymptotic properties.

A-B
2
Simplifying the MAO Formulation

We start the analysis of the auxiliary formulation by decomposing the optimization variable
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{t}=(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t})\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}+\boldsymbol{P}_{ts}\boldsymbol{r}_{t},

(35)

where
\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
is a free vector, and
\boldsymbol{P}_{ts}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times(k-1)}
is formed by an orthonormal subspace orthogonal to the vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ts}
. In addition, define the scalar
q_{t}
as follows

\displaystyle q_{t}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}.

(36)

Now, we fix
q_{t}
and the norm of
r_{t}=\lVert\boldsymbol{r}_{t}\rVert
in the formulation in (
A-B1
). Moreover, we solve over the direction of the optimization vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
. This optimization problem can be formulated as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert^{2}=q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2}\\
q_{t}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\rVert}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\rVert^{2},

(37)

where we drop the terms that are independent of the direction of the vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
.
In addition, we use the fact that
\frac{1}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{K}^{\perp}_{t}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\frac{1}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
. Note that the optimization in (
37
) is not convex due to the norm equality constraint. However, one can use an extended version of the approach proposed in
[
1
]
to solve (
37
). Specifically, the formulation in (
37
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda_{t},\eta_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\min_{{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}}~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\rVert}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}\big(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-q_{t}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\big(\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\rVert^{2}-q_{t}^{2}-r_{t}^{2}\big),

(38)

where the optimization variables
\eta_{t}
satisfy the regularity conditions in
\mathcal{F}_{t}
that will be defined later.
Here, the variables
\lambda_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
are the Lagrange multipliers. Next, define the vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{kT}
to be the concatenation of the optimization vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{w}=[\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\top},\cdots,\boldsymbol{w}_{T}^{\top}]^{\top}
. Then, the optimization problem expressed in (
38
) can be compactly formulated as follows

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{t},\eta_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0\end{subarray}}\min_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}~\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}q_{t}.

(39)

Here, the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{kT\times kT}
is defined as follows
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}=\gamma_{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+{\mathbf{\Delta}}
, where the matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}
is a weighted block diagonal identity matrix, i.e.,
{\mathbf{\Delta}}=\text{diag}(\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{k},\cdots,\eta_{T}\boldsymbol{I}_{k})
. Additionally, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is defined as follows
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}
. Furthermore, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times kT}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}=\left[-\frac{1}{T}\boldsymbol{I}_{k},\cdots\frac{T-1}{T}\boldsymbol{I}_{k},\cdots,-\frac{1}{T}\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\right],

(40)

where the matrix
\frac{T-1}{T}\boldsymbol{I}_{k}
is in the
t^{th}
block.
Here, the positive–definiteness constraint in the above formulation represents the regularity conditions introduced in (
38
), i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{\eta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}:\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0\}
. In the above formulation, the vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{kT\times 1}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}=\left[\frac{\|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\|}{n_{1}}\boldsymbol{g}_{1}^{\top}+\lambda_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1s}^{\top},\cdots,\frac{\|\boldsymbol{u}_{T}\|}{n_{T}}\boldsymbol{g}_{T}^{\top}+\lambda_{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{Ts}^{\top}\right].

(41)

Now, we are in a position to simplify the formulation in (
39
) over the optimization vector
\boldsymbol{w}
. Note that the formulation is now convex in
\boldsymbol{w}
. Moreover, it can be simplified as follows

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{t},\eta_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}q_{t}.

(42)

Note that the above steps simplify the optimization problem in (
39
) to a scalar formulation as given in (
42
). This implies that the MAO formulation obtained in (
A-B1
) can be equivalently reformulated as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}\geq 0}

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0,\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}\end{subarray}}~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}r_{t}\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{q_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{t}}{n_{t}}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\ell^{\star}\left(y_{t,i};u_{t,i}\right)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\|^{2}}{2n_{t}}V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\gamma_{1}-\eta_{t})(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2}).

(43)

Here, the vector
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
are formed by the concatenation of
\{q_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
and
\{r_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
, respectively. Moreover, the vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
are formed by the concatenation of
\{\eta_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
and
\{\lambda_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
, respectively. Also, the function
V_{p,t}(\cdot)
can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{p,tt}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}.

(44)

Additionally, the vector
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{kT}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{z}=[\lambda_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1s}^{\top},...,\lambda_{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{Ts}^{\top}]^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{C}_{p,tt}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
denotes the
t^{th}
diagonal block of the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}^{-1}
. Here, the formulation in (
A-B2
) is obtained after dropping terms that converge in probability to zero. This result can be justified by proving that these functions also converge uniformly in probability to the same limit
[
17
]
. It remains to simplify the formulation in (
A-B2
) over the optimization vector
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
. To this end, using the property in
[
32
, Example 11.26]
, we have the following equivalent representation

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n_{t}}(r_{t}\boldsymbol{h}_{t}+q_{t}\boldsymbol{s}_{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\|^{2}}{2n_{t}}V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\ell^{\star}\left(y_{t,i};u_{t,i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\mathcal{M}_{\ell(y_{t,i};.)}\big(r_{t}h_{t,i}+q_{t}s_{t,i};V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big).

(45)

Note that the above equality transforms a vector optimization to a sum of separable scalar formulations. This implies that the MAO formulation expressed in (
A-B2
) can be equivalently formulated as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}\geq 0}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0\end{subarray}}~\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\gamma_{1}-\eta_{t})(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2})-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{q}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\mathcal{M}_{\ell(y_{t,i};.)}\big(r_{t}h_{t,i}+q_{t}s_{t,i};V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big).

(46)

Note that our MAO problem is now expressed in terms of scalar optimization variables. Here, our final step is to solve over the variable
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.
Then, the MAO formulation obtained in (
A-B2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}\geq 0}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0\end{subarray}}~\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\gamma_{1}-\eta_{t})(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2})-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{q}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{p}\boldsymbol{\lambda}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\mathcal{M}_{\ell(y_{t,i};.)}\big(r_{t}h_{t,i}+q_{t}s_{t,i};V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big).

(47)

In the above, the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
has
(i,j)^{th}
component defined as

\displaystyle B_{ij}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{is}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}_{p,ij}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{js},~\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,T\},

(48)

where
\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}_{p,ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
represents the
(i,j)^{th}
block of the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}^{-1}
. Using the compact formulation in (
A-B2
), one can easily solve the optimization over the vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.
Particularly, the multivariate auxiliary formulation expressed in (
A-B2
) can be equivalently formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}\geq 0}

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\succ 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\gamma_{1}-\eta_{t})(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\mathcal{M}_{\ell(y_{t,i};.)}\big(r_{t}h_{t,i}+q_{t}s_{t,i};V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big).

(49)

Note that the above analysis expresses the multivariate auxiliary formulation in (
A-B1
) in terms of scalar variables as given in (
A-B2
). Then, it remains to study the asymptotic properties of the formulation in (
A-B2
). We refer to this problem as the scalar formulation.

A-B
3
Asymptotic Analysis of the Scalar Formulation

In this part, we study the asymptotic properties of the scalar formulation expressed in (
A-B2
). Note that the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}
is formed by weighted block identity matrices. This means that the spectrum of the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}
can be fully characterized by analyzing the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
defined as follows

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\boldsymbol{C}_{ii}=\frac{(T-1)\gamma_{2}}{T}+\eta_{i},~\forall i\in\{1,\dots,T\},\\
&\boldsymbol{C}_{ij}=-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{T},~\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,T\},i\neq j.\end{cases}

(50)

Given the form of the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{p}
, the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}_{p}
is formed by weighted block identity matrices. The weights can be obtained by computing the inverse of the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
defined above. Then, the components of the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}_{p}
defined in (
48
) converges in probability to the components of the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
defined as
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{L}
, where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product, and the matrix
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\boldsymbol{L}_{ii}=1,~\forall i\in\{1,\dots,T\},\\
&\boldsymbol{L}_{ij}=\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}},~\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,T\},~i\neq j.\end{cases}

(51)

Based on the asymptotic results proven in
[
15
]
, the sequence of random function
V_{p,t}(\cdot)
converges in probability as follows

\displaystyle V_{p,t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\xrightarrow{p\to\infty}V_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\kappa_{t}C_{tt}^{-1}.

(52)

Here, the scalar
C_{tt}^{-1}
denotes the
t^{th}
diagonal element of the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}
defined in (
50
).
Additionally, using the weak law of large numbers, one can show that the empirical average of the Moreau envelope function in (
A-B2
) converges to the following deterministic function

\displaystyle{F}_{t}(q_{t},r_{t},\boldsymbol{\eta})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{\ell(Y_{t};.)}\left(r_{t}H_{t}+q_{t}S_{t};V_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)\right],

(53)

where the expectation is over the standard Gaussian random variables
H_{t}
,
S_{t}
, and the random variable
Y_{t}
.
Also, the function
V_{t}(\cdot)
is the asymptotic function defined in (
52
). Additionally, the random variable
Y_{t}
is expressed as

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\varphi\bigg(\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\left[S_{t}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}}+Z_{t}\sqrt{1-\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}}\ \right]\bigg),

(54)

where
S_{t}
and
Z_{t}
are two independent standard Gaussian random variables.
The above analysis shows that the cost function of the scalar version of the auxiliary problem defined in (
A-B2
) converges in probability to the cost function of the following deterministic formulation

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}\geq 0}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{C}\succ 0\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\gamma_{1}-\eta_{t})(q_{t}^{2}+r_{t}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{\ell(Y_{t};.)}(r_{t}H_{t}+q_{t}S_{t};V_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta}))\right].

(55)

A first observation is that the deterministic problem in (
A-B3
) is not separable over the
T
tasks given that the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
is not a diagonal matrix. Now that we have obtained the asymptotic scalar formulation, it remains to study the asymptotic behavior of the generalization error corresponding to each task.

A-B
4
Asymptotic Characterization of the Generalization Error

In this part, we study the asymptotic properties of the generalization error corresponding to the multi–task approach employed in (
II-B
). The generalization error corresponding to the
t^{th}
task can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p,t,\text{test}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{\vartheta}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi(\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})-\widehat{\varphi}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}})\right)^{2}\right],

(56)

where
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}
is a new test input vector corresponding to the
t^{th}
task, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}
is as defined in (
6
).
Now, define the following two random variables

\displaystyle\nu_{1}=\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},~\text{and}~\nu_{2}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{t,\text{new}}.

For a given vectors
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
, note that the random variables
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
have a bivaraite Gaussian distribution with a zero mean vector and a covariance matrix given as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\rVert^{2}&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}&\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\rVert^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(57)

To precisely analyze the asymptotic behavior of the generalization error, it suffices to analyze the properties of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
. Define the random variables
\widehat{q}_{p,t}^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}_{p,t}^{\star}
for the
t^{th}
task as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{q}_{p,t}^{\star}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{ts}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t},~\text{and}~\widehat{r}_{p,t}^{\star}=\lVert\boldsymbol{P}_{ts}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t}\rVert,

(58)

where the matrix
\boldsymbol{P}_{ts}
is defined in (
35
). The decomposition in (
58
) shows that the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}
given in (
57
) can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{p}=\begin{bmatrix}1+\sigma^{2}&\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\kappa_{t}/\alpha_{t}}\widehat{q}^{\star}_{p,t}\\
\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\kappa_{t}/\alpha_{t}}\widehat{q}^{\star}_{p,t}&(\widehat{q}^{\star}_{p,t})^{2}+(\widehat{r}^{\star}_{p,t})^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, following the same lines as in
[
19
, Appendix B]
, the generalization error corresponding to the
t^{th}
task can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{p,t,\text{test}}=\frac{1}{4^{\vartheta}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\varphi(c_{0}G_{1})-\widehat{\varphi}(\widetilde{c}_{1,t}G_{1}+\widetilde{c}_{2,t}G_{2})\bigg)^{2}\right].

(59)

Here,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are two independent standard Gaussian random variables. Additionally,
c_{0}
,
\widetilde{c}_{1,t}
and
\widetilde{c}_{2,t}
are constants defined as follows

\displaystyle c_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}},~\widetilde{c}_{1,t}=\widehat{q}^{\star}_{p,t}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}},~\text{and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{2,t}=\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\right)(\widehat{q}^{\star}_{p,t})^{2}+(\widehat{r}^{\star}_{p,t})^{2}}.

(60)

Hence, to study the asymptotic properties of the generalization error, it suffices to study the asymptotic properties of the random quantities
\widehat{q}_{p,t}^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}_{p,t}^{\star}
. The following Lemma shows that the sequence of random variables
\widehat{q}_{p,t}^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}_{p,t}^{\star}
concentrates in the large system limit.

Lemma 3

(Consistency of the Multi–Task Formulation)
.

The random quantities
\widehat{q}_{p,t}^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}_{p,t}^{\star}
satisfies the following asymptotic properties
