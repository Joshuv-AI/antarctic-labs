Decomposition-Enhanced Network for financial time series forecasting
Authors: Jinyuan Huang, Qianqian Sun, Xinghua Zhang, Hong Du, Zheng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The extreme non-stationarity, high noise levels, and multi-timescale coupling in financial futures markets pose major challenges for time series forecasting. Existing models often struggle to disentangle localized shocks from global trends due to incompatible inductive biases. To address this issue, we propose a Decomposition-Enhanced Network (DENet). Following a divide-and-conquer paradigm, DENet adopts a multi-stream architecture: the main path extracts stable trends via moving averages and dual-path linear projections, while Auxiliary Stream I captures seasonal and local cyclical patterns using depthwise separable convolutions, and Auxiliary Stream II models high-frequency dynamics through a nonlinear autoregressive-style mapping. These components are integrated via an adaptive fusion mechanism, balancing global robustness and local structural sensitivity. Experiments on real-world futures data demonstrate that DENet outperforms a wide range of state-of-the-art benchmarks. Compared with seven state-of-the-art baselines, DENet achieves an average reduction of 10.15% in RMSE for daily forecasting on iron ore futures, and an average reduction of 22.57% in MAE under the 5-min, 12-step forecasting horizon. Furthermore, we integrate DENet’s dual-scale predictions into the R-Breaker strategy with parameter switching and dynamic position sizing. Backtesting results reveal that the annualized return of iron ore futures outperforms the baseline strategy by an average of 7.6 percentage points across five backtesting windows. Ultimately, DENet effectively bridges advanced structural modeling and actionable algorithmic trading.

Introduction

Financial futures price series exhibit pronounced non-stationarity, high noise, and multi-scale time variation1,2. They contain long-term trends driven by macroeconomic conditions and policy shifts, and they also contain local fluctuations shaped by market microstructure noise, liquidity shocks, and trading behavior. The coupling of trends, cyclical movements, and abrupt shocks within a single observed sequence makes it difficult to separate informative signals from noise and to achieve reliable time-series forecasting. This difficulty further prevents existing arbitrage strategies from moving beyond passive threshold-triggered reactions, which limits the ability to improve returns. Therefore, identifying a forecasting model that matches the properties of financial sequences is crucial for accurate price prediction.

In financial forecasting, model design often amounts to balancing inductive bias against fitting capacity. Very flexible models with weak bias can represent complex dependencies, but in the presence of heavy noise and regime changes, they are prone to learning spurious patterns and drifting over time. By contrast, linear and statistical models impose stronger structure and are usually stable and interpretable, yet they struggle with nonlinear momentum, shock propagation, and short cycles that tend to reside in the residual. Early work therefore focused on models such as ARIMA3 and GARCH4. Although well motivated mathematically, they often degrade under nonlinear volatility and structural breaks, limiting predictive accuracy. Classical machine learning methods such as gradient boosting decision trees5 introduce nonlinear feature interactions, but they still lack an explicit representation of multi-component, multi-scale, and regime-switching behavior, and can therefore fail when trends and shocks occur together. Deep learning offers more expressive sequence models. Recurrent networks such as LSTM6 and GRU7 use gating to capture long-range dependencies, but they are susceptible to vanishing and exploding gradients when dealing with long sequences. Transformers8 further expand capacity through self-attention, yet they may overfit in noisy settings, behave inconsistently when data are limited, and provide only weak financial priors or component-level structure for interpretation. Hybrid designs, including AT-LSTM9 and LSTM-Transformer10, seek to combine these strengths, but many are built by stacking modules in series or in parallel. Without a clear division of labor aligned with the component structure of financial time series, the modules remain coupled to the same objective, which limits specialization.

Decomposition-based forecasting provides an alternative. Models such as DLinear11 explicitly decompose the series and then forecast in a regression-like manner, introducing structural priors and improving interpretability; in practice, they can outperform more elaborate deep models. This line of work suggests that robustness often comes less from increasing capacity than from separating components across scales. However, many decomposition approaches still use linear mappings for residual and seasonal terms. This choice makes it harder to capture the short-term nonlinear effects that matter for futures trading and can ultimately constrain performance.

Deployable quantitative trading strategies also require high-quality predictive information. Many standard strategies have been studied. Trend breakout rules, as examined by Brock et al.12, aim to follow trends through breakout entries and risk control, but they are volatility-sensitive and can overtrade in range-bound markets. Bollinger-band strategies analyzed by Lento et al.13 use price-range statistics to capture mean reversion, but they are less effective when trends persist. The R-Breaker strategy balances trend following and reversal, fits short-cycle trading, and uses both offensive and defensive stops, which makes it a common benchmark. Its standard form, however, has practical limitations: it responds to intraday changes with delay, fixed parameters adapt poorly to changing volatility and trend conditions, and the absence of forward-looking information means decisions are triggered only after prices cross predefined thresholds. This leaves the strategy exposed to noise-driven false signals. These issues point to a missing ability to anticipate near-future states, which a forecasting model can help provide.

Overall, existing forecasting models for financial series exhibit several common shortcomings. First, many models do not incorporate inductive biases that are consistent with the statistical properties of financial time series. Trend, periodic, and shock components are commonly modeled in a joint manner, which often introduces mutual interference during the prediction process. The absence of explicit role separation and structural constraints across different temporal scales frequently results in feature aliasing and prediction drift, particularly in high-noise environments. Second, many existing approaches encounter difficulty in effectively utilizing both high-frequency and low-frequency data. Consequently, information across multiple temporal scales is not fully exploited, which reduces the stability of forecasting results. Third, most forecasting models primarily focus on minimizing predictive error and do not establish a close connection with concrete trading strategies. As a result, improvements in predictive accuracy do not necessarily translate into practical financial gains when such models are applied to real-world financial time series.

We aim to develop a forecasting approach that is accurate, interpretable, and consistent with the multi-scale structure of financial time series. To this end, we propose a Decomposition-Enhanced Network (DENet), and assess its practical value by integrating it into an R-Breaker strategy so that forecast gains can translate into trading performance.

DENet uses a structured design with one main path and two auxiliary streams, combining explicit component separation with the representational strength of deep learning. The main path performs trend–residual decomposition and uses a two-branch linear projection to model low-frequency trends while reducing temporal information loss. In parallel, one auxiliary stream applies depthwise separable convolutions to extract seasonal and local cyclical patterns efficiently, and the other uses a nonlinear autoregressive-style mapping to capture short-term momentum. An adaptive fusion module then combines the three outputs to support cooperative modeling across scales. This design reflects the coexistence of trend, cycle, and abrupt nonlinear behavior in futures series and targets the mismatch between generic predictors and financial data. For deployment, we feed DENet forecasts at two horizons, daily forecasts for macro trends and minute-level forecasts for micro fluctuations, into the full R-Breaker decision pipeline through parameter adaptation and dynamic position sizing, enabling the strategy to act on predictive information in realistic trading settings.

We make the following central contributions: First, we propose DENet, an architecture that explicitly decomposes financial sequences instead of attempting to learn all scales simultaneously, addressing the inductive bias mismatch common in generic deep learning models. Second, we implement DENet at both daily and minute-level resolutions. This dual-scale approach balances the data constraints of low-frequency history against the high microstructure noise inherent in high-frequency trading. Third, we evaluate DENet not just on error metrics, but through actual deployment. We integrate its dual-scale forecasts into an R-Breaker trading strategy to demonstrate how predictive information can dynamically adjust trading parameters and position sizing. Finally, we replace standard error minimization with a direction-aware hybrid loss accompanied by a feature-adaptive weighting mechanism, focusing the model on directional trend consistency over pure numerical precision.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section “Related work” reviews related work and explains how it informs our design. Section “Methods” presents DENet. Section “Experiment” reports experiments and analysis. Section “Conclusions” concludes the paper.

Related work

Financial time series forecasting models

A wide range of forecasting models have been studied in both academia and industry. The ARIMA model of Box and Jenkins3 combines autoregressive, integrated, and moving-average terms, offering a standard framework for (approximately) stationary series and serving as a canonical linear baseline. To capture volatility clustering, Engle proposed ARCH14, which models conditional variance as a function of past volatility, and Bollerslev extended it to GARCH4 to allow more flexible dynamics. These approaches are effective in many settings, but they remain largely linear and often struggle with nonlinear volatility and structural breaks, which limits forecasting accuracy in practice. Deep learning has expanded the set of available sequence models. LSTM6 and GRU7 rely on gating to model longer-range dependencies, but their performance is limited by vanishing and exploding gradients. Chen et al.15 introduced a gated-attention LSTM that reweights features within the recurrent network; it improves performance, but still inherits the limitations of LSTM-style recurrence. Transformers8 use self-attention to model global dependencies in parallel and have performed well in long-horizon forecasting. Li16 applied Transformer to financial prediction and verified their ability to capture global structure, while also pointing out their higher cost on long sequences and the limited inductive bias available for structured financial series. Liu et al.17 proposed iTransformer, which shifts attention from time tokens to variable tokens and studies why standard Transformers can be mismatched to multivariate time series. Taken together, these results indicate that forecasting performance depends not only on model capacity, but also on inductive biases and representations that align with data structure. Mao et al.18 proposed SF-Transformer, which incorporates spot–futures parity theory as domain knowledge, but its internal representation still does not naturally support a component-level, traceable forecasting process. Hybrid designs such as AT-LSTM9 and Transformer-LSTM10 aim to combine complementary architectures, yet many are built by stacking modules without a clear division of responsibilities, which can reduce the benefit in practice.

Decomposition-based forecasting has become an active area in recent years. The central idea is to split a complex series into components with different statistical properties, which simplifies modeling and matches the multi-scale superposition and heterogeneity often seen in financial data. DLinear11 uses a moving average to decompose a sequence into trend and residual components and models them separately, achieving low computational cost and, in some cases, stronger robustness than Transformers for long-term trends. NLinear11 further refines the projection step to better fit the data distribution. These models are closely related to our approach, but they typically adopt a single decomposition form and apply similar linear mappings across components, which makes it hard to capture nonlinear momentum. Their fusion is also often fixed (e.g., constant-weight aggregation), so component contributions cannot adjust to changing market conditions. Decomposition-based approaches have been widely explored19,20. FEDformer21 combines seasonal–trend decomposition with sparse frequency-domain representations and introduces a frequency-enhanced block to model dominant frequencies efficiently in long-horizon forecasting, but it is less effective when shocks are irregular or non-periodic.

Overall, the literature has moved from linear statistical models to deep learning and decomposition-based approaches, reflecting continued efforts to model the multi-scale, nonlinear, and time-varying behavior of financial series. Several limitations nevertheless recur. Linear models are constrained by linear assumptions and cannot capture richer cross-scale interactions. Single-path deep models often struggle to separate patterns by scale or frequency, and can miss both periodic structure and fast local effects; some also incur high computational cost and offer limited interpretability. Hybrids may amount to loosely motivated module stacking and still provide limited transparency. Decomposition methods offer a natural divide-and-conquer framework, but existing designs are often limited by the choice of decomposition, homogeneous component modeling, and inflexible fusion. These gaps motivate our decomposition-enhanced architecture.

Synergy between forecasting models and quantitative strategies

In quantitative trading, performance ultimately depends on anticipating near-future market states. Integrating a forecasting model into a trading strategy is therefore a key step from prediction to deployment. Prior work largely follows two directions: some studies use forecasts as direct trading signals to replace technical indicators, while others treat forecasts as auxiliary inputs for adjusting strategy parameters or decision rules. In both cases, the goal is to reduce the limitations of rule-based strategies by adding forward-looking information.

For R-Breaker, which we use as our deployment benchmark, existing integration work mainly focuses on parameter search or indicator-based augmentation. Gorgulho et al.22 used genetic algorithms to tune parameters, and Liu et al.23 proposed a quantum genetic algorithm to update key parameters dynamically. These methods can work well in-sample, but they depend heavily on historical optimization and may not adapt reliably as market conditions evolve. Cohen et al.24 used MACD and RSI to filter false signals, but these indicators are still derived from past prices and do not add genuine predictive information. Barroso et al.25 introduced volatility scaling for dynamic position adjustment; while this reduces exposure during high-volatility periods, it remains a rule based on historical volatility rather than forecasts. Qin et al.26 used R-Breaker as an intraday benchmark and compared it with a dynamic-threshold variant that incorporates forecasts on futures data. They reported improved risk-adjusted returns and reduced lag, but the interaction between forecasting and decision making remains limited, which caps the benefit of prediction.

In sum, existing studies often treat predictors as indicator replacements rather than integrating them into the strategy’s core mechanics. They also rarely tailor this integration to specific failure modes along the trading pipeline and sometimes understate that forecast quality underlies any downstream gain. In our setting, DENet provides dual-scale forecasts of macro trends and micro fluctuations. We then adapt R-Breaker through parameter adjustment and dynamic position control to incorporate these forecasts in a targeted manner, strengthening the link between prediction and execution and improving trading performance.

Decomposition-Enhanced Network (DENet)

Financial time series are typically noisy, non-stationary, and governed by complex time-varying dynamics. Under these conditions, a single forecasting paradigm often fails to balance long-term trend modeling with the capture of local variations. This study proposes a Decomposition-Enhanced Network (DENet). Rather than stacking modules in a heuristic manner, the framework follows a systems-oriented design that decomposes the task into multiple views and then fuses the extracted information, while incorporating adaptive feature calibration and multi-stream parallel feature extraction.

The central idea of DENet is to decompose financial forecasting into three subproblems: trend extrapolation, cyclical pattern recognition, and a nonlinear autoregressive mapping. These subproblems are addressed by three parallel pathways, and an adaptive fusion mechanism reconstructs a unified forecast of future closing-price sequence. From a domain perspective, this depthwise convolution mechanism allows each financial feature channel to learn local fluctuation and cyclical patterns independently. Pointwise convolution

The objective of pointwise convolution is to fuse complementary information across feature channels efficiently, thereby restructuring the feature space to strengthen the representation of seasonal patterns without introducing additional temporal computation. This operation uses a 1 × 1 kernel to perform a linear transformation only along the feature dimension, as formulated in Eq. (12):12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_{\textrm{pw}}[t,f'] = \sum _{f=0}^{F-1} W_{\textrm{pw}}[f,f'] \cdot S_{\textrm{dw}}[t,f] + b_{\textrm{pw}}[f'] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{\textrm{pw}} \in \mathbb {R}^{F \times F'}$$\end{document} denotes the pointwise convolution kernel, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{\textrm{pw}} \in \mathbb {R}^{F'}$$\end{document} denotes the channelwise bias term for each output feature dimension.

Unlike conventional convolutions that mix information across temporal and feature dimensions simultaneously, pointwise convolution performs feature mixing at the same time step via linear combinations across channels. For example, when modeling weekly effects, this operation can integrate features such as intraweek closing-price trends and RSI overbought and oversold signals to form more discriminative periodic representations. Moreover, the 1 × 1 kernel constrains the parameter count to O(F × F'), which reduces computational cost.

While reducing the number of parameters substantially, this design extracts characteristic features associated with local periodic fluctuations. 2.Auxiliary stream II: nonlinear autoregressive-style mapping

Auxiliary stream II: nonlinear autoregressive-style mapping

Price fluctuations in financial time series reflect not only global trends that can be approximated by linear fitting and local cyclical patterns that are amenable to convolutional modeling, but also pronounced nonlinear momentum effects associated with abrupt market reactions, asymmetric volatility propagation, and nonlinear dependence on recent price dynamics. These effects are often characterized by the absence of fixed cycles, asymmetric price movements, and a direct dependence on historical prices. Such dynamics are difficult to capture by linear trend extrapolation and are not reliably isolated by periodic decomposition. To address this challenge, Auxiliary Stream II is introduced as a shortcut pathway that learns an end-to-end nonlinear mapping from historical sequences to future values. The design of layered dimension reduction with nonlinear activation enables efficient modeling of nonlinear dependencies in time-series data. Taking the residual component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{\textrm{close}}^{\textrm{res}}$$\end{document} of the closing price sequence extracted from the main path trend-residual decomposition as input, this stream leverages the approximation capacity of multilayer perceptrons to model the nonlinear relationship between historical and future prices, as formulated in Eq. (14):14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_\mathrm{direct} = L_{\textrm{2}}\bigl ( ReLU\bigl ( L_{\textrm{1}}\bigl ( X_\mathrm{close}^\mathrm{res} \bigr ) + b_{\textrm{1}} \bigr ) \bigr ) + b_{\textrm{2}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{\text {close}}^{\text {res}} \in \mathbb {R}^{L}$$\end{document}, and L denotes the input sequence length.

The first linear transformation layer, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_1$$\end{document}, uses a weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_1 \in \mathbb {R}^{L \times \frac{L}{r}}$$\end{document} and a bias term to perform lightweight compression along the temporal dimension, where r is a predefined compression-ratio hyperparameter. A nonlinear activation function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ReLU}(\cdot ) = \max (0, \cdot )$$\end{document} is employed instead of Sigmoid or Tanh, since it better matches the asymmetric fluctuation patterns commonly observed in financial time series. Subsequently, a second linear transformation layer, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_2$$\end{document}, uses a weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_2 \in \mathbb {R}^{\frac{L}{r} \times H}$$\end{document} and a bias term to map the compressed representation to the prediction horizon. This layer refines the output through bias calibration and partially mitigates systematic errors that can arise from purely linear mappings. The final output is produced by a nonlinear autoregressive-style projection that directly models the influence of historical residual dynamics in closing prices on future price movements.

This pathway effectively emulates a nonlinear autoregressive-style process and emphasizes complex momentum-driven patterns in price fluctuations, thereby providing an additional historical reference perspective.

Global information fusion

The model produces three prediction components with distinct interpretations: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_\mathrm{main}$$\end{document} (trend-dominant), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_\mathrm{season}$$\end{document} (seasonality-related), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_\mathrm{direct}$$\end{document} (momentum-dominant). DENet integrates these complementary components into a unified output via a learnable weighted ensemble, the main path prediction is treated as the backbone signal and assigned a unit weight, while auxiliary streams are adaptively calibrated through learnable coefficients, as formulated in Eq. (15):15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y_\mathrm{raw}=P_\mathrm{main}+\beta \cdot P_\mathrm{season}+\gamma \cdot P_\mathrm{direct} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where β and γ are learnable coefficients that balance the contributions of the auxiliary streams and are optimized jointly with the model parameters. When periodic patterns become more salient, the value of β is expected to increase, whereas stronger nonlinear momentum typically leads to a larger value of γ. This mechanism enables adaptive alignment between stream contributions and prevailing market conditions.

In summary, DENet offers a unified framework covering feature calibration, multi-scale decomposition, and multi-view modeling. The proposed framework demonstrates favorable robustness and interpretability for financial time series forecasting.

Despite robust backtesting results, the R-Breaker strategy still has several inherent limitations. First, signal generation is delayed because decisions depend exclusively on static information from the previous trading day, which reduces responsiveness to intraday news and regime shifts. Second, the strategy does not explicitly characterize market states, since threshold triggering does not distinguish between trending and oscillatory regimes, which can impair robustness. Third, key execution parameters are fixed, which limits intraday adaptability to changes in market strength and volatility. Fourth, risk exposure is typically mechanical due to fixed position sizing, with limited ability to adjust exposure based on uncertainty or signal reliability.

Directional-aware hybrid loss

Deep learning is widely applied to financial time series forecasting tasks, demonstrating capability in capturing complex non-stationary market dynamics. As summarized in the systematic review by Sezer et al.27, a broad consensus exists in this field that the practical predictive performance of a model depends not only on numerical estimation accuracy but also on the correct identification of trend direction, rather than solely minimizing point errors.

Incorporating direction-aware constraints into regression losses represents an established paradigm to align model training objectives with practical forecasting and trading requirements. Existing frameworks constructed around the mean squared error (MSE) are susceptible to distortion from extreme price outliers and market gaps. Conversely, methods utilizing discrete sign functions present gradient discontinuity issues, which potentially impair convergence stability during the initial stages of training28.22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\begin{matrix} & {L} = {L}_{\text {SmoothL}}(\hat{y}, y) + \alpha \cdot \mathbb {E}\left[ \text {ReLU}\left( -\left( \Delta y_{\text {true}} \cdot \Delta y_{\text {pred}} \right) \right) \right] \\ & {L}_{\text {SmoothL}}(\hat{y}, y) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \frac{1}{2}\left( \hat{y} - y \right) ^2, & \left| \hat{y} - y \right| < 1, \\ \left| \hat{y} - y \right| - \frac{1}{2}, & \left| \hat{y} - y \right| \ge 1. \end{array}\right. } \end{matrix}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta y_{\text {true}} = y - y_{\text {last}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta y_{\text {pred}} = \hat{y} - y_{\text {last}}$$\end{document} represent the price changes of the ground truth and the predicted value, respectively, relative to the final closing price \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{\text {last}}$$\end{document} within the input window. The ReLU-based direction penalty term activates when the predicted direction deviates from the actual trend (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta y_{\text {true}} \cdot \Delta y_{\text {pred}} < 0$$\end{document}), whereas the penalty drops to zero when the directions align. Compared with discrete sign operations, the ReLU formulation ensures the differentiability of the loss function throughout the training process.The hyperparameter α controls the weight of the directional penalty and is configured according to the data frequency to accommodate the noise levels and trend cycles at different sampling intervals. This adapted loss function simultaneously constrains numerical fitting accuracy and directional consistency during training, thereby aligning the optimization objective closely with the practical requirements of financial forecasting.

Experiment

Experimental setup

Experimental environment and data

The proposed DENet framework was implemented using Python 3.12.3. Data preprocessing and manipulation were conducted utilizing NumPy 1.26.4 and Pandas 2.2.2, while the deep learning architecture was constructed within the PyTorch 2.5.1 framework (integrated with CUDA 12.1). Scikit-learn 1.5.1 was employed for data standardization and model evaluation to facilitate multi-step time series forecasting. All experiments were executed on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 Laptop GPU.

To evaluate the efficacy of the DENet model in financial time series forecasting, this study selected a diverse set of ferrous metal futures from major Chinese exchanges. The dataset includes Coking Coal(JM), Coke(J), and Iron Ore(I) from the Dalian Commodity Exchange (DCE); Wire Rod(WR) from the Shanghai Futures Exchange (SHFE); and Silicomanganese(SM) from the Zhengzhou Commodity Exchange (ZCE). Two distinct experimental configurations were designed to assess performance across different temporal granularities: Daily-Level Forecasting: This task aims to predict the closing price of the subsequent trading day. Given the coarser granularity of daily data, an extended temporal span ranging from January 5, 2015, to December 31, 2024, was utilized to ensure a sufficient volume of training samples. High-Frequency (5-minute) Forecasting: This task involved rolling predictions of the closing price sequence for the next 12 steps (equivalent to a one-hour horizon) based on 5-minute intervals. Due to the finer granularity, the dataset covered the period from September 1, 2016, 9:00 to December 31, 2024, 14:50:00.

While we acknowledge pronounced price co-movements across ferrous metal futures, and pairwise statistical dependence metrics29 or information-theoretic quantities30 can deliver substantial predictive gains, this work adopts a per-contract modeling paradigm that confines the feature space to individual contracts and excludes cross-asset information. Two rationales underpin this design choice. First, such a setup cleanly disentangles performance improvements stemming exclusively from the sequence modeling capacity of the DENet architecture, eliminating confounding biases induced by cross-sectional features. Second, computing pairwise dependence metrics on 5-minute high-frequency datasets incurs severe temporal alignment artifacts arising from asynchronous order matching and market microstructure noise. Accordingly, the present study solely concentrates on extracting deep temporal representations from individual assets, and we leave the integration of cross-asset topological structures for future investigations.

Dataset partitioning:

This experiment accounted for the sequential nature of time series data and enforced temporal unidirectionality when partitioning the training and test sets, thereby preventing information leakage during sample construction. The full dataset was split into training and test subsets with an 8:2 ratio. This split ensures that the training subset contains sufficiently diverse fluctuation patterns, trend dynamics, and technical-indicator regularities to support parameter estimation and representation learning in DENet. At the same time, a substantial held-out test subset was retained to enable an objective evaluation of generalization on previously unseen time series data.

To further reduce the risk of implicit leakage arising when test input windows overlap with the tail of the training period, this study introduced a gap parameter. The value of this parameter was set equal to the model input length (look-back length). For daily-level data, the input length and the gap parameter are both set to 22, and for minute-level high-frequency data, they are both set to 20. This design ensures that the input windows of test samples are strictly outside the temporal span used for training, so that the contextual information used to form test inputs does not appear in the training process. As a result, the evaluation more reliably reflects the generalization performance of the model. The results indicate quantitative differences in performance across the evaluated methods, with DENet achieving the highest accuracy on all five futures datasets under all evaluation metrics. Taking Iron Ore futures as an example, DENet attains a mean absolute error (MAE) of 12.428, a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 16.605, and a coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}) of 0.958. Compared to DENet, the baseline models exhibit varying degrees of performance degradation. The Transformer-based models, PatchTST and iTransformer, report relatively higher prediction errors. In contrast, the decomposition-oriented architectures and multiscale-mixing models demonstrate stronger competitiveness. For instance, TimesNet and N-BEATS achieve MAEs of 13.226 and 13.402 on Iron Ore, respectively. Nevertheless, DENet further reduces the MAE and RMSE by 6.03% and 4.25% compared to TimesNet on this dataset. Consistent trends are observed across the other futures datasets.

The performance advantage of decomposition-based and multiscale-mixing architectures over pure attention-based models suggests that explicitly extracting trend and periodic components is beneficial for forecasting financial time series. Financial data is typically characterized by high volatility and complex noise. Highly complex models that rely entirely on data-driven attention mechanisms can be sensitive to such noise, potentially leading to overfitting. Methods that separate the series into predictable sub-components, such as N-BEATS and TimesNet, help mitigate this issue and achieve better generalization performance.

Furthermore, while models like N-BEATS and TimesNet provide effective mechanisms for decomposition and periodicity extraction, DENet exhibits further improvements in forecasting accuracy. This improvement can be attributed to the targeted decomposition strategy of DENet, which appropriately balances representation capacity and model complexity. By adaptively separating the dynamic components of the sequence, DENet reduces the risk of overfitting observed in highly complex models while avoiding the limitations of simplistic linear approximations like DLinear. Consequently, it generates more stable representations that align with the variations in financial data.

Significance analysis of daily data

To further assess whether the daily-level performance differences between the proposed DENet model and competing forecasting methods are statistically significant, this study applied the Giacomini-White (GW) test36 and complemented it with the Diebold-Mariano (DM) test37. Both tests evaluate whether two models exhibit a systematic difference in expected out-of-sample predictive loss. Furthermore, given the large number of pairwise comparisons across five assets, multiple metrics, and various horizons, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction to the resulting p values. This correction mitigates the risk of Type I errors (false positives) associated with multiple testing, ensuring that non-significant results are interpreted with appropriate statistical caution.

To implement the significance tests, the time-indexed series of loss differentials must first be explicitly defined. Depending on the evaluation metric, We adopted the mean absolute error (MAE) as the loss function, which is defined as the absolute error loss: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L = |y_\mathrm{true} - y_\mathrm{pred}|$$\end{document}. The loss differential series at each time step t was then constructed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_t = L_\text{comp, t} - L_\text{base, t}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_\text{comp, t}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_\text{base, t}$$\end{document} denote the predictive losses of the comparison model and the baseline DENet model, respectively.

Under a baseline significance level of α = 0.05, the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy is evaluated using the FDR-adjusted one-sided p values. A corrected p value below 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference in forecasting performance. Moreover, based on the construction of the loss differential, a positive test statistic implies that the expected loss of the comparison model is systematically higher than that of DENet. Therefore, a positive and significant statistic confirms that DENet achieves superior predictive accuracy. The results demonstrate that for the majority of futures examined in this study, the differences in forecasting performance between the baseline model (DENet) and competing models robustly satisfy both the GW and DM significance tests. This consensus indicates that DENet possesses a statistically significant advantage in out-of-sample prediction over existing architectures.

Specifically for SM futures, while most models remain significantly outperformed by DENet, the adjusted p-values for TSMixer exceed 0.05. Consequently, the null hypothesis cannot be rejected at the designated significance level for this specific pair. This lack of significance likely stems from the unique characteristics of SM futures’ daily data during the sample period. Compared to other assets, SM futures exhibited more pronounced trading discontinuities, heavier-tailed return distributions, and more severe volatility in position structures. These data idiosyncrasies inevitably inflate the variance of the loss differential series. When combined with the rigorous FDR correction designed to penalize multiple comparisons, the statistical power of the tests is reduced, leading to cautious non-significance. Nevertheless, despite lacking strong statistical confirmation for this specific case, both the GW and DM statistics for TSMixer on SM futures remain positive. This indicates that, in point estimation terms, DENet still yields a lower overall forecasting loss, providing directional evidence of the model’s accuracy.

Overall, the dual validation from the GW and DM significance analyses, properly adjusted for multiple comparisons, strongly supports DENet’s effectiveness at the aggregate level. DENet is consistently more robust than advanced architectures such as PatchTST, DLinear, iTransformer, and TSMixer on daily forecasting tasks, further confirming its superior generalization capabilities in complex market environments.

The results demonstrate that DENet achieves the highest overall accuracy in 5-min multi-step forecasting across the five futures contracts. Taking Wire Rod (WR) futures as an example, the average MAE of DENet is reduced by 3.56%, 3.87%, 7.66%, 9.68%, 14.40%, 18.14%, and 24.55% compared to PatchTST, TimesNet, TimeMixer, iTransformer, N-BEATS, DLinear, and TSMixer, respectively; similar patterns are consistently observed across the other futures contracts.

Minute-level financial series exhibit pronounced intraday seasonality and complex non-linear fluctuations driven by transient market shocks. Based on the experimental results, models capable of preserving local semantic information (e.g., PatchTST) or extracting complex multi-periodic variations (e.g., TimesNet) demonstrate relatively strong competitiveness on such highly dynamic data. However, while these advanced baselines capture temporal dependencies effectively, they lack a dedicated mechanism to explicitly decouple underlying trends from abrupt market shocks. DENet addresses this issue through its decomposition mechanism, which separates smoother trend structure from shock-driven fluctuations. This separation allows the model to handle different dynamics in dedicated components and reduces the impact of noise, helping maintain stable predictive performance.

Furthermore, DENet shows strong scalability to longer horizons. As the prediction horizon increases from 1 to 12 steps, although errors inevitably rise, DENet generally maintains a performance advantage, achieving the lowest or near-lowest errors in most long-horizon cases. This pattern suggests that DENet captures persistent market dynamics rather than superficial correlations, leading to more reliable long-horizon forecasts.

Significance analysis of minute-level data

To further investigate the performance of DENet models in minute-level multi-step forecasting, we evaluated the statistical significance of the average forecasting results across the entire prediction horizon. Following the framework established in the daily-level analysis, we simultaneously applied the Giacomini-White (GW) and Diebold-Mariano (DM) tests, with all resulting p-values adjusted via the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) correction.

Since this scenario involves multi-step predictions, the loss sequence at each time step t is defined as the average Mean Absolute Error (MAE) over the forecasting horizon H. Specifically, the loss function is formulated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_t = \frac{1}{H} \sum _{k=1}^H |y_\text{true, t+k} - y_\text{pred, t+k}|$$\end{document}. The corresponding loss differential series is then computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_t = L_\text{comp, t} - L_\text{base, t}$$\end{document}, where a systematically positive loss differential series reflects the superior accuracy of the baseline DENet model. Significance analysis of daily-level ablation experiments

Furthermore, we perform a significance analysis on the daily ablation results for the two aforementioned futures using the Giacomini-White significance test. For single-step daily predictions, there are no overlapping forecast windows; temporal dependence only arises from the conditional heteroskedasticity and weak serial correlation inherent in financial time series. To rigorously address these properties, we utilize the Newey-West Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) covariance estimator for the loss differentials. In this analysis, the model prediction error is explicitly measured by the Mean Absolute Error (MAE). Following standard practice, the HAC lag truncation parameter is automatically selected using the rule \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L = \lfloor 4(T/100)^{2/9} \rfloor,$$\end{document} yielding a lag order of 5 for our effective sample size (T=443). Furthermore, to control the global false positive rate across multiple pairwise comparisons against the DENet model, we apply the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR-BH) correction to the p-values.

To ensure that the observed statistical significance reflects practically meaningful superiority rather than merely a large-sample artifact, we supplement the test statistics with explicit effect sizes. Experimental results show that under the trend–residual decomposition framework, introducing only Auxiliary Stream II does not consistently improve performance and may even lead to degradation in certain cases, whereas jointly incorporating auxiliary streams I and II yields a significant improvement in prediction accuracy. This phenomenon arises because auxiliary stream II performs a global nonlinear mapping on the residual sequence, which, when used in isolation, tends to misinterpret high-frequency noise as short-term momentum signals and thus introduces a risk of overfitting. In contrast, auxiliary stream I conducts local modeling on detrended multivariate sequences through moving average and convolution operations, enabling the extraction of stable temporal variation patterns. When combined, auxiliary stream I provides relatively stable structural representations, while auxiliary stream II complements the expression of nonlinear fluctuations. The adaptive weighting mechanism with learnable parameters suppresses noise interference to a certain extent and preserves effective momentum information, thereby substantially enhancing the overall predictive performance.

Significance analysis of minute-level ablation experiments

We still adopt the Giacomini–White (GW) test to conduct significance verification at the minute frequency, and verify the predictive superiority of the DENet architecture using minute-level data.

Unlike the single-step daily predictions, the minute-level experiments involve a 12-step rolling forecast. This overlapping prediction window naturally induces a moving-average structure (i.e., an MA(11) process) in the loss differential series, resulting in more complex serial correlation. As established in the forecast evaluation literature, the loss differential of an h-step overlapping forecast follows an MA(h-1) process; the HAC covariance estimator therefore requires a minimum lag length of h-1 to ensure consistent standard error estimation. To address this specific temporal dependence, we adjust the Newey-West HAC covariance estimator by employing a dual-rule mechanism for lag selection: we enforce a minimum lag constraint of h-1 = 11 to fully cover the overlapping horizon, combined with the standard automatic selection rule. Given the large effective sample size (T ≈ 27,198), this yields a final robust lag order of 13 for the 12-step average loss evaluation.

Empirical study on the integration of forecasting models and quantitative strategies

Comparative backtesting

To fulfill the requirements of predictive modeling and strategy evaluation, this study conducts a backtest utilizing 5-minute high-frequency intraday data from five futures commodities, including iron ore and coking coal, over the 2023–2024 period. We employ a rolling re-optimization walk-forward approach. Specifically, an approximate 15-month rolling window is initialized on May 16, 2023, advancing in one-month increments until the trailing edge of the window reaches December 31, 2024. Within each rolling window, the dataset is partitioned into training and testing sets at an approximate ratio of 6:4.

The backtesting framework incorporates realistic constraints for transaction costs. For each futures contract, the initial capital is set at 1.5 times the minimum margin required to open a single-lot position. Based on the high, low, and closing prices of the preceding trading day, six critical intraday thresholds for the R-Breaker strategy are calculated for the subsequent day: the long setup, short setup, long reversal, short reversal, long breakout, and short breakout levels. These thresholds are integrated with both daily and minute-level predictive outputs to construct a dual-scale prediction signal, which is subsequently embedded into an R-Breaker strategy framework equipped with a dynamic position sizing mechanism.

To ensure practical applicability, the backtesting procedure replicates trading frictions observed in real markets. Each transaction incurs a commission of 0.01% of the contract value, and a fixed slippage model is adopted with slippage set to one minimum price movement unit to capture execution price deviations. Consequently, while the proposed framework raises the return upper bound to some extent in favorable market conditions, the extreme drawdown risk of high-volatility assets remains an unavoidable structural shortcoming. This conclusion further verifies that a comprehensive and accurate predictive model provides only incremental optimization for quantitative trading strategies. Further subsequent work remains necessary.

Conclusions

This study proposes a Decomposition-Enhanced Network (DENet) for accurate modeling and forecasting of financial time series. The model explicitly decomposes the input sequence into trend, periodicity, and nonlinear shock components to enable targeted modeling. Specifically, the main path performs trend–residual decoupling to extract trend information in a stable manner. In parallel, two auxiliary streams model periodic and nonlinear dynamics via separable convolutions and a nonlinear autoregressive-style mapping, respectively. This architecture generalizes across both high- and low-frequency data and addresses the frequent mismatch between generic forecasting models and the statistical characteristics of financial sequences.

For empirical evaluation, this study considers ferrous metals and building materials futures, including Iron Ore and Coke. A multi-dimensional feature system is constructed, and closing prices are used as prediction targets. Performance was evaluated using root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}). Statistical significance is further assessed using the Giacomini–White (GW) test. Experiments on both daily and minute-resolution data show that DENet consistently outperforms representative forecasting baselines. In addition, backtesting is conducted to evaluate the practical applicability of the strategy. By integrating daily and 12-step-ahead predictions into the R-Breaker strategy, the proposed system enables forecast-driven parameter switching and dynamic position sizing. The backtesting results demonstrate the profitability and practical value of the proposed forecasting-and-strategy pipeline.

The model still has several limitations. Currently, the framework primarily relies on price and volume data, whereas characteristic indicators that often drive changes in real market conditions, including targeted macroeconomic indicators and news sentiment metrics, have not been fully differentiated, refined, and integrated into the model. Furthermore, the network has not been systematically validated on datasets that cover a wider range of temporal resolutions. Consequently, the model is currently unsuitable for application scenarios that impose stricter operational constraints, such as ultra-high-frequency trading, which is highly sensitive to latency.

In summary, DENet offers a practical way to predict financial time series by dividing the problem into distinct trend, cycle, and shock components. By connecting these forecasts directly to an R-Breaker strategy, we demonstrate that better forecasting architectures can translate directly to improved trading performance, moving beyond pure error metrics.
