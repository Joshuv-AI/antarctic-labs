Driving dynamics of a dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine with a quadruple crawler tracked system
Authors: Miao Xie, Bo Tian, Yuqi Li, Zhixiang Liu, Jun Mao, He Wang, Shuai Wang, Suning Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a novel dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine equipped with a quadruple tracked system, designed to enhance excavation efficiency and operational safety in confined underground environments. Conventional parallel anchoring machines often fail to accommodate the spatial constraints of narrow mine tunnels; our serial configuration overcomes this dimensional limitation. A critical challenge, mismatched motion between front and rear track units, was systematically addressed through a physics-based vehicle–terrain contact dynamics model. The model was implemented in RecurDyn multibody simulation software and evaluated under three representative operating conditions: synchronized motion, asynchronous compression, and active traction modes. Simulation results show strong agreement with experimental data from a 1:1 physical scale prototype, with an average relative error in dynamic response below 5%. This study not only demonstrates a viable new architecture for underground anchoring but also establishes a validated framework for coordinated motion control in multi-unit tracked vehicles, offering a scalable solution for real-world deployment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58493-z.Keywords: Anchoring machine, Quadruple crawler tracked system, Track–vehicle dynamicsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

China accounts for over 12,000 km of coal mine tunnel excavation annually. However, only approximately 20% of these tunnels are suitable for highly automated equipment such as shield machines or integrated bolter miners. The use of such advanced systems requires favorable geological conditions, including larger cross-sectional dimensions and stable ground. In contrast, the remaining 80% must rely on conventional excavation methods based on cantilever-type road headers, followed by manual installation of roof support anchors after the cutting cycle is completed. This sequential approach suffers from low operational efficiency, prolonged exposure of unsupported roof spans, and increased safety risks. These limitations highlight the urgent need for advanced, adaptable excavation technologies specifically designed for constrained and variable underground environments1–3.

To address these limitations, we propose a dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine equipped with a quadruple crawler tracked drive system. As shown in Fig. 1, the machine consists of three main components: a front vehicle operating the cutting function, an anchoring group on the articulated mechanism, and a rear auxiliary vehicle. The front vehicle handles excavation, while the rear vehicle provides permanent anchoring capability and additional driving force. The two vehicles are connected by a multi-degree-of-freedom articulated joint that accommodates uneven terrain and relative motion between units, with the anchoring drilling group mounted within this joint. This layout eliminates the need for a separate auxiliary system behind the cantilever cutter, shortens the temporary support zone, and enables early installation of permanent anchors. Consequently, excavation, temporary support, permanent anchoring, and material transport can operate in parallel, significantly improving overall cycle efficiency.

As the core component of the anchoring machine, the crawler system plays a critical role in ensuring operational robustness across the diverse and extreme conditions encountered in coal mines. This system has been extensively studied by numerous researchers4,5. Significant research has been devoted to modeling and analyzing the dynamics of tracked and articulated tracked vehicles, particularly in challenging terrains. Early efforts focused on fundamental dynamic modeling under specific motion conditions. For example, Sasaki et al. derived dynamic equations for articulated tracked vehicles during uphill climbing controlled by hydraulic actuators6, while Watanabe et al. developed a theoretical steering model validated via small-scale experiments7,8. Furthermore, Dai et al. expanded the dynamic models to multi-body vehicles9. Similarly, Kim, Miao et al., and Tota et al. separately examined vehicle response on undulating, inclined soft soil10, studied numerical method for multi-body system11, and proposed a linear dynamic model of the articulated joint to study transient behavior during turning12.

With advances in computational methods, recent studies have increasingly adopted multi-body dynamics (MBD) simulation and coupled numerical approaches13–15. Wu et al. used MBD software to correlate speed, drive torque, and center-of-gravity motion to assess stability under vertical acceleration. Yao et al. and Edlund et al. applied similar tools to analyze steering performance and design novel articulated forest transporters, respectively17,18. Meanwhile, the integration of discrete element method (DEM) with MBD has enabled more realistic terrain–track interaction modeling: Oida et al. pioneered DEM-based terrain mechanics for tracked vehicles19, and Chen et al. extended this coupling to study horizontal driving dynamics20.

Despite these advances, nearly all existing studies examine articulated tracked systems under a single operational mode—typically steady-state steering, climbing, or straight-line motion—and often assume homogeneous or idealized terrain conditions. Critically, few investigate coordinated multi-vehicle dynamics across multiple interacting states (e.g., synchronized motion, asynchronous compression, and active traction modes.) in spatially constrained, variable underground environments like coal mine tunnels. This gap limits the applicability of current models to real-world mining scenarios where excavation, support, and locomotion must be tightly integrated—a challenge directly addressed in this work.

In this work, we develop a novel dynamic model for the proposed dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine equipped with a quadruple crawler tracked drive system. Grounded in classical vehicle–terrain interaction theory, the model captures the coupled dynamics of the front and rear vehicles under three representative operating conditions: synchronized motion, asynchronous compression, and active traction modes. Detailed description of the operational modes is provided in Chap. 2.3. We validate the model through both multibody dynamic simulations in RecurDyn and experimental data from a 1:1 physical-scale prototype. The results show good agreement in dynamic response across simulation and experiment, confirming the robustness and fidelity of the proposed framework. This model provides a theoretical foundation for autonomous motion correction, coordinated control, and structural optimization of dual-vehicle anchoring systems in confined underground environments. While developed for coal mine tunneling, the methodology may also be adaptable to other extreme terrains, such as deep-sea or planetary exploration scenarios, where articulated tracked locomotion is required.

Construction of dynamic model of system

The dynamic behavior of the dual-vehicle serial anchoring system is governed by Newton’s second law of motion, applied separately to each vehicle unit and the articulation joint. For both the front and rear vehicles, translational dynamics are modeled through force equilibrium in the longitudinal and lateral directions, accounting for external resistive forces such as the soil-tractive force FH, the compaction resistance Rc, the positive (longitudinal) bulldozing resistance Rs, and the lateral bulldozing resistance . For rotational dynamics, moment equilibrium about the vertical axis is enforced for each vehicle unit, where the net yaw moment arises from the lateral offset of track forces relative to the vehicle centerline, the interaction torques at the articulation joint, and inertial resistance due to angular acceleration . Additionally, pitch dynamics of the front vehicle are captured through moment balance about the articulation axis, incorporating the vertical components of the interaction force FE and the effective ground contact length L. Construction of operational mode

This work focuses on three representative operational modes that capture essential dynamic couplings between the two tracked units:

Synchronized Motion

Both vehicles move forward at identical speeds with negligible relative motion at the joint. This mode reflects steady-state excavation on stable ground, ensuring smooth tunnel profiling and minimizing internal mechanical loads.

Asynchronous Compression

The front vehicle decelerates or halts (e.g., during cutting or temporary support installation), while the rear vehicle continues advancing, thereby compressing the articulated joint. This induces longitudinal force transmission from the rear to the front unit and emulates high-resistance scenarios such as encountering hard rock strata.

Active Traction

During high-load tasks—such as excavation in fractured rock or climbing inclined sections—the rear vehicle actively delivers supplementary driving torque to assist the front vehicle. In this mode, extra drive power is delivered from rear tracks to improve overall traction and prevent slippage of the front tracks. The torque distribution strategy is set such that the front vehicle receives 1.2 times the torque of the rear vehicle.

Simulation results

To validate the proposed analytical dynamic model of the dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine, a systematic comparison was conducted between its behavior predictions via the analytic dynamics and the high-fidelity responses generated by the RecurDyn virtual prototype. Both models were subjected to the identical operating condition: the as-same initial drive sprocket angular velocity profiles and consistent driving torque with as-same environment conditions such as soil cohesive modulus and frictional soil deformation modulus. Specific parameters utilized in simulation was listed in the Table 1 and tables in SI.

In accordance with Chinese coal industry standards, which restrict the maximum travel speed of underground tunneling equipment to 0.25 m/s, three representative operational modes were defined using combinations of this reference speed and a reduced speed of 0.15 m/s:

(i) Synchronized Motion: Both the front and rear vehicles travel forward at 0.25 m/s, resulting in negligible relative motion at the articulated joint. This mode represents steady-state excavation on stable ground, ensuring smooth tunnel profiling and minimizing internal mechanical loads.

(ii) Asynchronous Compression: The front vehicle advances at 0.15 m/s while the rear vehicle moves at 0.25 m/s, causing the articulated joint to compress. This configuration emulates high-resistance situations—such as encountering hard rock strata—where longitudinal force is transmitted from the rear unit to support the front during cutting or temporary support installation.

(iii) Active Traction: The front vehicle operates at 0.25 m/s while the rear vehicle reduces its speed to 0.15 m/s, reflecting a strategy in which supplementary driving torque is actively delivered by the rear tracks to assist the front unit. This mode is typical during high-load tasks, such as excavation in fractured rock or climbing inclined sections, and is employed to enhance overall traction and prevent slippage of the front tracks.

For each scenario, the RecurDyn virtual prototype was executed under these prescribed speed profiles. The resulting time histories of driving torque applied to all four tracks and the corresponding soil reaction forces were recorded and subsequently imported into the MATLAB-based analytical dynamic model as external inputs. This ensures that both models experience the exact same excitation, enabling a direct and fair comparison.

To investigate the influence of speed differences between the front and rear vehicles on the overall travel performance, physical experiments were conducted using the 1:1 prototype (in Fig. 10a) and a dedicated test ramp (in Fig. 10b). The primary objective of these physical tests is to verify the stability and dynamic response of the system under real-world operating conditions. While inevitably differing in detail from idealized simulations, the physical experiments provide critical validation of model robustness.

Experimental setup for the full-scale 1:1 prototype validation. (a) The the dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine with a quadruple crawler tracked system prototype used in field tests. (b) The dedicated test ramp constructed to simulate inclined underground roadway conditions, featuring a controlled slope angle and uniform surface texture to ensure repeatable experimental trials.

During testing, the machine was subjected to three representative terrain configurations: (i) both front and rear vehicles on level ground; (ii) the front vehicle ascending a slope while the rear remains on level ground; and (iii) the rear vehicle ascending a slope while the front remains on level ground.

These setups approximate the three operational modes defined in the virtual environment (synchronized motion, asynchronous compression, and traction mode) as closely as feasible in a physical setting. Although perfect equivalence between simulation and experiment cannot be achieved due to environmental variability, sensor limitations, and unmodeled real-world effects (e.g., soil heterogeneity, track wear, and hydraulic response delays), these three test conditions effectively capture the essential mechanical interactions of interest.

The physical prototype is powered by hydraulic motors, for which the output torque is directly proportional to the supply pressure. During testing, motor pressure and drive sprocket rotational speed were continuously recorded for both the front and rear vehicles across all three operational states: balanced, traction, and asynchronous compression modes. The resulting time-series data—particularly the transient trends and dynamic fluctuations—were analyzed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of load redistribution, shifts in torque demand, and inter-vehicle coupling behavior.

Moreover, a close correspondence was observed between the physical prototype and the virtual (simulation) model in terms of torque response, acceleration profiles, and velocity trajectories, despite inevitable real-world disturbances such as terrain heterogeneity and hydraulic hysteresis. This agreement demonstrates that the analytical model captures the essential dynamics of the dual-vehicle system under representative operating conditions. Consequently, the experimental findings not only validate the core assumptions of the proposed dynamic model but also establish a robust theoretical and empirical foundation for future underground industrial trials and the refinement of coordinated control strategies.

Simultaneously, the sudden reduction in the front vehicle’s longitudinal velocity disrupts the kinematic coordination between the two units. This mismatch induces strong inter-vehicle interaction forces, resulting in increased track slip on both front and rear drive sprockets and consequently causing the observed fluctuations in rotational speed.

Regarding rotational speed, the front drive sprocket maintains an average speed of 36.27 rpm, yet its standard deviation surges from 0.52 to 17.18, indicating severe speed oscillations. The rear sprocket, with an average speed of 30.01 rpm, also shows a notable increase in fluctuation, with its standard deviation rising from 0.83 to 3.45.

Compared to the synchronized state, the key distinction in asynchronous compression mode is that both vehicles experience elevated motor pressures, and the fluctuations in drive sprocket speeds are significantly amplified during these transient intervals.

This phenomenon is attributed to the mechanical interaction between the front and rear units. As the faster-moving rear vehicle pushes the slower front vehicle, the front track experiences increased slip due to excessive thrust. This higher slip results in a greater soil reaction force acting on the front vehicle, thereby demanding higher driving torque and leading to elevated front motor oil pressure. Simultaneously, the rear vehicle must overcome not only its own rolling resistance but also the additional load transferred through the articulated joint, which increases its working resistance and consequently raises its motor pressure as well. The intensified mutual interaction between the two vehicles further exacerbates track slip on both ends, causing the observed large fluctuations in drive sprocket rotational speeds during the transient intervals.

This behavior contrasts notably with that observed in asynchronous compression mode, where both vehicles experience elevated pressure levels and substantially larger speed oscillations. In traction mode, the reduction in overall pressure magnitude—particularly in the rear unit—and the marked suppression of rotational speed fluctuations stem from the distinct load-sharing mechanism inherent to this operational configuration. Specifically, the front vehicle, which possesses significantly greater mass than the rear unit, acts as the primary traction source, towing the lighter rear vehicle forward. As the front vehicle ascends a slope and decelerates, the inertial mismatch between the two units results in a relatively gentle coupling interaction; the lower-momentum rear vehicle exerts minimal impulsive force on the front, thereby mitigating transient disturbances. Consequently, the front motor must supply slightly increased torque—evidenced by a modest rise in mean oil pressure relative to the balanced state—to overcome not only its own resistance but also the drag imposed by the trailing unit. Conversely, the rear vehicle, operating in a passive assistive role, experiences reduced hydraulic demand, leading to a slight decrease in its motor pressure compared to synchronized motion. The asymmetry in mass distribution further enhances system stability: because the dominant inertia resides in the leading vehicle, the reactive forces transmitted through the articulated joint during traction are inherently smaller than those generated when the rear unit actively pushes the front. This mechanical advantage manifests in the consistently low speed variability across both tracks, underscoring the superior dynamic smoothness of traction-mode operation under uneven terrain conditions.

These experimental findings confirm that traction mode offers a more stable and controllable response compared to both synchronized and asynchronous compression configurations, particularly in scenarios involving slope transitions. The measured pressure and speed characteristics provide critical validation for the dynamic model and inform the development of adaptive control strategies aimed at optimizing inter-vehicle coordination during underground mining operations.

Conclusion

This study presents a dual-vehicle serial anchoring machine with a quadruple crawler tracked system and develops its corresponding comprehensive track–terrain contact dynamic model, comprising both a systemic dynamics model and individual track model. The dynamic behavior of the front and rear vehicles under three representative operational states was simulated using RecurDyn, and the accuracy of the proposed dynamic model was validated through comparison with analytical calculations. Furthermore, physical experiments were conducted on a full-scale prototype to evaluate travel performance across these operational states, providing critical empirical insights into real-world system behavior.

Theoretical modeling yielded closed-form equations for key kinematic and dynamic quantities, including vehicle longitudinal acceleration, inter-vehicle interaction forces, turning radii of the front and rear units, and angular accelerations of drive and idler sprockets in individual tracks. Comparative analysis between simulation and analytical results demonstrated excellent agreement: across all three operational modes, the average relative error between simulated and calculated responses for both whole-machine acceleration and single-track angular acceleration remained below 5%, thereby confirming the fidelity and predictive capability of the proposed track–terrain contact dynamic model.

Experimental investigations further revealed distinct performance characteristics associated with each operational mode. In balanced mode, the machine exhibited the lowest driving torque demand and the highest motion stability, as reflected by minimal fluctuations in motor pressure and drive sprocket speed. In asynchronous compression mode, significantly higher torque was required due to load transfer from the rear to the front vehicle during slope negotiation, resulting in pronounced pressure oscillations and reduced overall stability. In contrast, traction mode—where the heavier front vehicle tows the lighter rear unit—also demanded elevated torque but maintained superior stability compared to asynchronous compression mode, owing to smoother inter-vehicle force transmission and reduced dynamic coupling transients.

Collectively, these findings validate the theoretical framework, demonstrate the effectiveness of the physical prototype in capturing real-world dynamics, and provide a solid foundation for future underground industrial deployment, adaptive control design, and optimization of multi-unit articulated mining machinery operating in complex terrain environments.
