Neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit following pediatric emergency department visits in Türkiye: a retrospective single-center study
Authors: Samet Benli, Sibel Laçinel Gürlevik, Hasan Avşar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe neonatal period is characterized by increased vulnerability to disease and often non-specific clinical presentations, leading to frequent visits to pediatric emergency departments (EDs). This study aimed to evaluate the characteristics of neonates presenting to the ED, determine the main causes of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions, and identify factors associated with prolonged hospitalization.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included neonates aged 0–28 days who presented to the pediatric ED and were subsequently admitted to the NICU over a one-year period. Demographic characteristics, clinical diagnoses, laboratory findings, microbiological results, and length of hospital stay were analyzed. Prolonged hospitalization was defined as a length of stay > 7 days. Factors associated with prolonged stay were evaluated using both univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsAmong 4,573 neonatal ED visits, 378 (8.2%) required NICU admission. The most common causes of admission were jaundice (36.5%) and respiratory distress (27.5%). More than half of admissions occurred within the first postnatal week. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, refugee status remained independently associated with prolonged hospitalization (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.99, 95% CI 1.16–3.43; p = 0.013) after adjustment for sex, low birth weight, postnatal age at admission, and major clinical diagnoses. Low birth weight (aOR 2.56, 95% CI 1.10–5.94; p = 0.028), respiratory distress (aOR 2.90, 95% CI 1.26–6.69; p = 0.012), and jaundice with urinary tract infection (aOR 4.69, 95% CI 1.65–13.37; p = 0.004) were also independently associated with prolonged stay, whereas isolated jaundice was inversely associated with prolonged hospitalization (aOR 0.16, 95% CI 0.07–0.35; p < 0.001).ConclusionsNeonates presenting to the pediatric ED constitute a vulnerable population with a considerable need for NICU admission. Respiratory distress and infection-related conditions are major determinants of prolonged hospitalization, whereas jaundice is generally associated with shorter stays. Early identification of high-risk patients may improve clinical management and resource utilization.Keywords: Neonates, Emergency Department, Neonatal Intensive Care Unit, NICU admission, Jaundice, Respiratory distress, Length of stay, Risk factors, Refugee

Introduction

The neonatal period, defined as the first 28 days of life, represents a critical phase characterized by rapid physiological adaptation and increased susceptibility to disease. During this period, the frequent presence of non-specific clinical findings makes the evaluation of neonates particularly challenging, especially in pediatric emergency department (ED) settings [1].

In recent years, there has been a notable increase worldwide in the number of neonates presenting to pediatric EDs. This trend has been attributed to factors such as early postnatal discharge practices, insufficient parental education, and limited access to primary healthcare services [2, 3]. It has been reported that a substantial proportion of neonatal ED visits are due to non-urgent conditions, including jaundice, feeding difficulties, and upper respiratory tract infections [4, 5]. This situation contributes to overcrowding in emergency departments, increases healthcare costs, and exposes neonates to additional risks such as nosocomial infections [2].

The evaluation of neonatal patients is inherently complex due to the broad differential diagnosis associated with non-specific symptoms and the potential for rapid clinical deterioration. Consequently, clinicians often adopt a more cautious approach, leading to increased diagnostic testing and higher hospitalization rates [6]. Data from Türkiye comprehensively evaluating this patient population remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically assess the demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics, causes of admission, and short-term outcomes of neonates admitted to the neonatal unit following pediatric ED presentation, and to provide comprehensive single-center data from Türkiye regarding NICU admissions following pediatric emergency department presentation.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective single-center study was conducted between May 2024 and May 2025 at a tertiary care center, Cengiz Gökçek Women’s and Children’s Hospital. The study included neonates aged 0–28 days who presented to the pediatric emergency department and were subsequently admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Only patients admitted to the NICU following evaluation in the emergency department were included. During the study period, a total of 1,220 neonates were admitted to the NICU, including both emergency department and non-emergency admissions. The proportion of NICU admissions originating from the emergency department was calculated by dividing the number of ED-related NICU admissions by the total number of NICU admissions during the study period.

Participants

Neonates with missing essential clinical or laboratory data, those without a first hospitalization among repeated visits, patients directly admitted to the NICU from external centers without emergency department evaluation, and those older than 28 days at admission were excluded.

Data collection and definitions

Demographic characteristics (sex, gestational age, birth weight, prematurity status, postnatal age), sociodemographic variables (nationality/refugee status), time of presentation, and clinical admission diagnoses were recorded. Clinical diagnoses were classified according to predefined admission categories.

Laboratory parameters included hematological variables (hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count), biochemical parameters (urea, creatinine, AST, ALT, total and direct bilirubin), infection markers (C-reactive protein and procalcitonin), and, when available, hormonal parameters (TSH and T4).

Microbiological data included blood and urine culture results, culture positivity rates, and isolated microorganisms. Infection-related cases were evaluated according to the European Medicines Agency (EMA) neonatal sepsis criteria. For coagulase-negative staphylococci and other potential skin contaminants, culture positivity was considered clinically significant only when at least two clinical findings and two laboratory abnormalities consistent with neonatal sepsis were present. Isolates not fulfilling these criteria were considered probable contaminants and excluded from the analysis.

Length of hospital stay (LOS) was defined as a primary outcome variable, and patients were categorized into two groups: ≤ 7 days (short stay) and > 7 days (prolonged stay).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for skewed variables, while categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage. Normality was assessed using histograms, probability plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For group comparisons, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To identify factors associated with prolonged hospitalization (> 7 days), both univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included refugee status, sex, low birth weight (< 2500 g), postnatal age at admission, and major clinical admission diagnoses. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The number of variables included in the multivariable model was restricted to avoid model overfitting.

Continuous predictors were analyzed as continuous variables. Because procalcitonin values showed marked right-skewed distribution, natural logarithmic transformation was applied before regression analysis. However, procalcitonin was not included in the primary multivariable model because it was available in a limited subset of patients.

As a sensitivity analysis, a secondary multivariable logistic regression model was performed in the subgroup of patients with available procalcitonin measurements (n = 92). In this model, ln-transformed procalcitonin was adjusted for refugee status, sex, postnatal age at admission, and respiratory distress. Low birth weight was not included in this subgroup model because all low-birth-weight infants with available procalcitonin values experienced prolonged hospitalization, resulting in complete separation. Therefore, this analysis was considered exploratory.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 258,101 patients presented to the pediatric emergency department during the study period, of whom 4,573 (1.7%) were neonates. Among these, 378 neonates (8.2%) required admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), accounting for 31% of all NICU admissions during the study period. The patient selection process is summarized in the STROBE-compliant flowchart (Fig. 1). Of the admitted neonates, 224 (59.3%) were male. The median gestational age was 38 weeks (IQR: 37–39), and the median birth weight was 3000 g (IQR: 2700–3300). Presentations occurred most frequently between 16:00–24:00, and refugees constituted 31% of the cohort (Table 1).

The most common reasons for NICU admission were jaundice (36.5%), respiratory distress (27.5%), jaundice with urinary tract infection (11.4%), fever (8.2%), and feeding difficulties (5.6%) (Table 2). Admissions were most frequent in winter, followed by summer (Fig. 2). Respiratory distress predominated in winter, whereas jaundice was the leading cause in summer. When classified according to postnatal age at presentation, NICU admissions were most frequent during the first week of life (0–7 days), accounting for 53.2% of cases. A trend toward increasing length of hospital stay with advancing postnatal age was observed. The median length of stay was lowest in the first week (6 [4–9] days) and highest in the 22–28 day group (9 [7–12] days) (Fig. 3). Monthly analysis showed that NICU admissions were highest in February, July, and August. When standardized according to the number of emergency department visits, the highest admission rate was observed in April, while the lowest rates were seen in June and March (Fig. 4).

In univariate logistic regression analysis, refugee status, male sex, low birth weight, postnatal age at admission, respiratory distress, and jaundice with urinary tract infection were associated with prolonged hospitalization, whereas isolated jaundice was inversely associated with prolonged stay. In the multivariable logistic regression model, refugee status remained independently associated with prolonged hospitalization (aOR 1.99, 95% CI 1.16–3.43; p = 0.013). Low birth weight (aOR 2.56, 95% CI 1.10–5.94; p = 0.028), respiratory distress (aOR 2.90, 95% CI 1.26–6.69; p = 0.012), and jaundice with urinary tract infection (aOR 4.69, 95% CI 1.65–13.37; p = 0.004) were also independently associated with prolonged stay. In contrast, isolated jaundice was associated with a lower likelihood of prolonged hospitalization (aOR 0.16, 95% CI 0.07–0.35; p < 0.001). Procalcitonin was associated with prolonged stay in univariate analysis after natural logarithmic transformation (OR 2.71, 95% CI 1.24–5.92; p = 0.012). Because procalcitonin was available in only 92 patients, it was not included in the primary multivariable model. However, in a secondary sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with available procalcitonin values, ln-procalcitonin remained independently associated with prolonged hospitalization after adjustment for refugee status, sex, postnatal age at admission, and respiratory distress (aOR 2.46, 95% CI 1.12–5.41; p = 0.025) (Table 3).

Among neonates admitted with respiratory distress, 7 (6.7%) required invasive mechanical ventilation and 14 (13.5%) received non-invasive ventilation support. In this subgroup, prolonged hospitalization (> 7 days) was associated with lower birth weight (2948.7 ± 531.6 g vs. 3301.5 ± 507.5 g, p = 0.009), higher serum creatinine levels (0.46 [0.39–0.55] vs. 0.39 [0.30–0.44] mg/dL, p = 0.002), and higher procalcitonin levels (0.22 [0.11–0.31] vs. 0.11 [0.08–0.16] ng/mL, p = 0.010). Univariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that admission diagnosis was significantly associated with prolonged hospitalization. Jaundice was inversely associated with prolonged stay (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.06–0.16; p < 0.001), whereas respiratory distress (OR 5.56, 95% CI 3.35–9.25; p < 0.001) and jaundice with urinary tract infection (OR 7.34, 95% CI 3.17–16.98; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with an increased risk of prolonged stay.

No significant differences were found in C-reactive protein or procalcitonin levels between patients with and without positive blood or urine cultures (p > 0.05). However, platelet counts were significantly higher in patients with positive blood cultures (417.2 ± 136.5 vs. 362.8 ± 127.3, p = 0.009). In patients with positive urine cultures, lymphocyte and total bilirubin levels were higher, whereas AST levels were lower (p = 0.014, p = 0.036, and p = 0.047, respectively).

In length-of-stay comparisons, the prolonged stay group had higher median procalcitonin levels (0.235 [0.13–0.41] vs. 0.125 [0.09–0.19] ng/mL, p = 0.003) and lower hemoglobin levels (15.63 ± 2.97 vs. 16.26 ± 2.58 g/dL, p = 0.025). T4 levels also differed significantly between groups and were better described using median (IQR) due to skewed distribution (1.28 [1.10–1.50] vs. 1.38 [1.20–1.54], p = 0.005). In contrast, the short stay group had higher total and direct bilirubin levels (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002, respectively). No other laboratory parameters showed significant differences.

Compared with local patients, refugee neonates had a longer median hospital stay (8 [6–11] vs. 7 [5–9] days, p = 0.001) and a higher rate of prolonged hospitalization (56.4% vs. 41.4%; OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.18–2.85; p = 0.009). AST levels, neutrophil counts, and the rate of blood culture sampling were also higher in the refugee group. Admission day and time distributions differed significantly between groups (Table 4).

Blood cultures were obtained from 269 patients, of which 61 (22.7%) yielded clinically significant growth according to EMA criteria. The most common isolates were coagulase-negative staphylococci, including Staphylococcus epidermidis (39.3%) and Staphylococcus hominis (36.1%). Among the 61 neonates with clinically significant positive blood cultures, sepsis was evaluated according to the European Medicines Agency (EMA) neonatal sepsis criteria. The most common clinical findings were respiratory manifestations, which were present in 41 patients, followed by cardiovascular abnormalities in 32 patients, gastrointestinal symptoms in 27 patients, non-specific findings in 26 patients, temperature instability in 24 patients, and skin/subcutaneous lesions in 14 patients. Regarding laboratory criteria, elevated CRP and/or procalcitonin levels were the most frequent abnormalities, detected in 43 patients, followed by leukocyte count abnormalities in 42 patients, thrombocytopenia in 23 patients, metabolic acidosis in 21 patients, blood glucose abnormalities in 20 patients, and an elevated immature-to-total neutrophil ratio in 11 patients. According to EMA definitions, the presence of at least two clinical and two laboratory criteria supported the classification of these cases as clinically significant neonatal sepsis rather than culture contamination. Urine cultures were obtained from 194 patients, with growth detected in 64 (33%). The most common pathogens were Escherichia coli (53.1%) and Klebsiella spp. (26.6%). Four patients (1.2%) were diagnosed with congenital hypothyroidism. Exchange transfusion was performed in 6.5% of patients admitted for jaundice. The median total bilirubin level in these patients was 34 (30.2–38.6) mg/dL (range: 27–43.7 mg/dL).

Discussion

In this study, although neonates accounted for only 1.7% of all pediatric emergency department (ED) visits, they represented a distinctly high-risk subgroup with a substantial need for hospitalization. Notably, 8.2% of neonatal ED presentations resulted in admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and nearly one-third of all NICU admissions during the study period originated from the ED. This disproportionate contribution to inpatient neonatal care highlights the clinical burden posed by neonates despite their relatively small share of overall ED volume. These findings underscore the pivotal role of the ED in the timely recognition, triage, and management of neonatal illness. Moreover, by providing an integrated assessment of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and microbiological features, this study adds to the limited literature on NICU admissions after ED presentation and provides comprehensive clinical and microbiological data from a tertiary neonatal center in Türkiye.

There are limited studies evaluating neonatal admissions following pediatric ED visits. In the literature, NICU admission rates after ED presentation vary widely, ranging from 13% to 67% across different countries [2–4]. Studies from Türkiye have reported admission rates between 3.8% and 11.6%, although some reports have shown considerably higher rates [5, 7, 8]. In our study, the admission rate was relatively low compared to the literature. This may be explained by the high patient volume in our tertiary women’s and children’s hospital, where a substantial proportion of neonates can be managed without hospitalization. These findings suggest that many neonatal ED presentations may be clinically mild and that admission rates are influenced not only by disease severity but also by healthcare utilization patterns and referral characteristics.

Jaundice is reported as the most common reason for neonatal ED visits in the literature [4, 5]. Consistent with previous studies, jaundice was the leading cause of admission in our cohort. However, despite being the most frequent diagnosis, the distribution of admission causes was relatively balanced. Respiratory distress was the second most common reason for hospitalization, which is also consistent with findings from previous studies conducted in Türkiye [5, 7]. In contrast, some international studies have reported upper respiratory tract infections or bronchiolitis as the leading causes of ED presentations [2, 3]. This heterogeneity suggests that neonatal ED presentation patterns are influenced by region-specific factors, including epidemiology, healthcare system organization, and referral practices.

Seasonal analysis revealed that neonatal admissions were most frequent in February, July, and August. Jaundice predominated during summer months, whereas respiratory distress was more common in winter. While ED visits were highest in summer, NICU admissions peaked during winter. In contrast, studies from Türkiye have reported autumn as the most frequent season for ED visits [7, 8], and an Italian study reported August as the peak month for ED visits and February for admissions [2]. These differences suggest that neonatal ED utilization and hospitalization patterns may vary depending on geographic, climatic, and healthcare access factors.

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a well-known etiological factor in neonatal jaundice [9–11]. In our study, UTI was identified in a notable proportion of patients with hyperbilirubinemia requiring treatment. Escherichia coli was the most commonly isolated pathogen in urine cultures, consistent with previous studies [9, 11]. These findings support the importance of evaluating UTI as an underlying cause in neonates presenting with jaundice, particularly in cases exceeding treatment thresholds.

Among culture-positive patients, coagulase-negative staphylococci, particularly Staphylococcus epidermidis and Staphylococcus hominis were the most frequently isolated organisms in blood cultures. This is consistent with the literature, where these organisms are commonly reported in neonatal sepsis [12, 13]. However, as these bacteria are part of the skin flora, the possibility of contamination must always be considered. In our study, the use of European Medicines Agency (EMA) sepsis criteria to define clinically significant infections likely improved the accuracy of true infection identification by reducing the likelihood of misclassifying contaminant coagulase-negative staphylococcal growth as true bacteremia [12].

No significant differences were observed in C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels between patients with and without culture positivity. While some studies report higher diagnostic performance of these biomarkers in neonatal sepsis [14], others have shown findings similar to ours, particularly in heterogeneous neonatal populations [15]. This discrepancy may be related to the timing of measurement, stage of sepsis, and clinical variability. These findings suggest that single-time-point measurements of CRP and PCT may be insufficient for accurate diagnosis and should be interpreted alongside clinical findings and additional laboratory parameters, preferably using serial measurements to improve diagnostic accuracy [14, 15]. Because procalcitonin measurements were available only in a limited subgroup, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger prospective cohorts.

Exchange transfusion was performed in 6.5% of neonates admitted for jaundice, and all cases had underlying hematological conditions, predominantly ABO incompatibility. This finding is consistent with national and international studies reporting hematological causes as the leading indication for exchange transfusion [16–18]. Additionally, congenital hypothyroidism was diagnosed in 1.2% of patients, a rate higher than that reported in the general population. This difference may be attributed to the single-center design, selected patient population, and increasing detection rates associated with improved screening programs [19, 20].

Prolonged hospitalization (> 7 days) was strongly associated with respiratory distress and infection-related diagnoses. Similarly, higher procalcitonin levels and lower birth weight were observed in the prolonged stay group, indicating that disease severity plays a key role in determining length of stay. Previous systematic reviews have also identified respiratory support requirements and infection burden as major determinants of prolonged hospitalization in term neonates [21, 22]. In contrast, studies including broader NICU populations have emphasized prematurity and low birth weight as primary factors [22]. These findings suggest that determinants of length of stay may vary depending on patient population, with acute disease severity being more relevant in term neonates.

When comparing local and refugee neonates, refugee patients were found to have significantly longer durations of hospital stay and higher rates of prolonged hospitalization. Notably, this association persisted even after adjusting for variables such as sex, low birth weight, postnatal age at admission, and major clinical diagnoses. In the literature, a recent study by Solmaz et al. supports our findings by emphasizing that refugee neonates face distinct disparities in pediatric emergency department admissions compared to the local population, which is subsequently reflected in clinical outcomes [1]. Similarly, Baris et al., in their analysis of data from a tertiary hospital in Turkey, reported that refugee children exhibit differences in both emergency department visits and hospitalization rates compared to the resident population [23]. These findings suggest that the longer hospitalization observed among refugee neonates cannot be explained solely by differences in birth weight or admission diagnosis. Potential contributors may include delayed healthcare seeking, socioeconomic barriers, language-related challenges, and differences in pre-hospital care access. Nevertheless, because of the retrospective design, these mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously and require prospective confirmation.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective and single-center design may limit generalizability. Additionally, reliance on hospital records may have resulted in incomplete data for some variables, and causal relationships cannot be established. Furthermore, the lack of standardized protocols for ED admission decisions may have influenced hospitalization rates. However, the relatively large sample size and the comprehensive evaluation of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and microbiological data represent important strengths of this study.

Conclusion

Neonates presenting to the pediatric emergency department represent a high-risk population that contributes substantially to the burden of NICU admissions. In this study, jaundice was the most common cause of admission and was generally associated with shorter hospital stay, whereas respiratory distress and infection-related conditions emerged as the strongest determinants of prolonged hospitalization. Lower birth weight and elevated inflammatory markers were also associated with extended length of stay. In addition, refugee status remained independently associated with prolonged hospitalization after adjustment for key clinical factors, suggesting that both clinical severity and healthcare utilization patterns may contribute to hospitalization dynamics in this population. This study provides comprehensive single-center data from Türkiye regarding NICU admissions following pediatric emergency department presentation and highlights the pivotal role of emergency department–NICU integration in shaping hospitalization dynamics and clinical outcomes.
