Streaming Complexity Separations for Dense and Sparse Graphs
Yang P. Liu, Hoai-An Nguyen, Noah G. Singer, David P. Woodruff

Abstract
We identify a sharp separation in the streaming space complexity of Maximum Cut when the algorithm must output an approximate cut (rather than only the approximate value). For dense graphs, we show that O ⁡ ( n / ε 2 ) O(n/\varepsilon^{2}) space is sufficient and that Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) space is necessary. In contrast, for graphs with Θ ⁡ ( n / ε 2 ) \Theta(n/\varepsilon^{2}) edges, the situation is markedly different: we show that the problem requires Ω ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ ( ε 2 ​ n ) / ε 2 ) \Omega(n\log(\varepsilon^{2}n)/\varepsilon^{2}) space for any ε = ω ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \varepsilon=\omega(1/\sqrt{n}) , which is tight for the full range of ε \varepsilon . We also give an Ω ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n / ε 2 ) \Omega(n\log n/\varepsilon^{2}) -space lower bound against deterministic algorithms for outputting a ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) approximation to the value of the maximum cut. Using similar techniques we prove an analogous sharp separation in the streaming space complexity of Densest Subgraph and show that for every constant-arity CSP over a constant-size alphabet and the Similarity problem the space complexity in dense streams can be improved by shaving a logarithmic factor.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the streaming complexity of graph optimization problems.
Our focus is on understanding the space complexity required to output approximate solutions in a single pass over insertion-only streams.
In particular, we identify sharp separations for sparse and dense graphs for the streaming complexity of the fundamental
Max-Cut
problem. In
Max-Cut
, the task is to output a cut, i.e., a partition of the vertices of the input graph, such that the value (the number of edges crossing the partition) is at least a
(1-\varepsilon)
fraction of the maximum cut value. Our results also extend to other (hyper)graph optimization problems: constant-arity constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) and
Densest-Subgraph
.

Prior works
[
50
,
46
,
48
,
8
,
9
,
22
,
35
]
have studied streaming algorithms for
(1/2+\varepsilon)
-approximate
Max-Cut
(albeit the value version) in both the single-pass and multi-pass settings. Motivated by this and connections to sparsification/space-compression of graph cuts (and more generally CSPs), we study the
\varepsilon
-dependence of the space required for streaming
Max-Cut
for
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximations (values of
\varepsilon
bounded away from
1/2
). The simplest algorithm for streaming
Max-Cut
simply samples a uniform subset of
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
edges in the stream. Standard concentration bounds prove that this provides an additive approximation to
all cuts
in the graph, which implies a multiplicative approximation to
Max-Cut
(which cuts at least half the edges). The space required by this algorithm is
O(\varepsilon^{-2}n\log(n\varepsilon^{2}))
, because there are
\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{n/\varepsilon^{2}}\approx\exp(\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}n\log(n\varepsilon^{2})))
graphs with
n/\varepsilon^{2}
edges.

Our first result (see
Theorem

1.1
) is that this bound is tight in general, and we construct a family of graphs with
n/\varepsilon^{2}
edges such that any streaming algorithm outputting a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate
Max-Cut
requires at least
\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}n\log(n\varepsilon^{2}))
bits of space. However, the case of dense graphs is different, and applying techniques from
F_{0}
estimation gives an approximate
Max-Cut
streaming algorithm in dense graphs that only uses
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
bits of space.
In other words, we exhibit a dichotomy for streaming
Max-Cut
in sparse versus dense graphs.

Preliminaries

Terminology.

We consider undirected graphs
G=(V,E)
where
V
denotes the set of
n
vertices and
E
denotes the set of
m
edges. We say
G
is “dense" if
m=\Omega(n^{2})
whereas
G
is “sparse" if
m=O(n)
. We may refer to graphs with
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
edges as “sparse" (note that this is the same as
O(n)
for constant
\varepsilon
).
We denote
N(u)
as the set of adjacent vertices to vertex
u
in a graph. In general, we boldface random variables.

Sums of binomial coefficients.

Let
\mathsf{H}(p)\coloneqq-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
denote the binary entropy function.
We recall the following standard lemma from combinatorics:

2.1
Well-known, e.g.,
[
52
, Ch. 10, Cor. 9]
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq k\leq n/2
. Then

\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{i}\leq 2^{n\cdot\mathsf{H}(k/n)}.

The
Max-Cut
problem.

For an undirected
G=(V,E)
and a function
x:V\to\{0,1\}
representing a “cut”, we define the
cut value
of
x
as the fraction of edges whose endpoints are assigned differently by
x
:

\mathsf{val}_{G}\left\lparen x\right\rparen\coloneqq\Pr_{\{{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(E)}[x({\boldsymbol{u}})\neq x({\boldsymbol{v}})].

We define the
value
of
G
as

\mathsf{opt}_{G}\coloneqq\max_{x:V\to\{0,1\}}\mathsf{val}_{G}\left\lparen x\right\rparen,

the highest value of any cut.

G
is
bipartite
if
\mathsf{opt}_{G}=1
.
For every
\gamma\in[0,1]
, we define the set

\mathsf{Good}^{\gamma}_{G}\coloneqq\{x:\mathsf{val}_{G}\left\lparen x\right\rparen\geq(1-\gamma)\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{G}\}.

The
\varepsilon
-approximate
Max-Cut
problem
is to, given
G
, output a cut
x\in\mathsf{Good}^{\varepsilon}_{G}
.

One can also consider the related “value approximation” problem where the goal is to output a real number
v\in[0,1]
such that
v\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{G}
.
Note that the value of a uniformly random cut
{\boldsymbol{x}}:V\to\{0,1\}
is
\frac{1}{2}
in expectation and therefore
\mathsf{opt}_{G}\geq\frac{1}{2}
for every graph
G
.

One-way communication problems.

We prove most of our lower bounds by considering the following simple communication problem, called
Identity
, in the one-way model.

2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite set.
In the
\textsc{Identity}_{\mathcal{F}}
problem, Alice gets an input
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
sends a single message
\mathcal{M}
to Bob.
Bob, after arbitrary local computation, then needs to output
A
.

The following information-theoretic bound is folklore:

2.3
.

In any one-way protocol for
Identity
with constant success probability,
Alice must send Bob
\Omega(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
bits of information.

Technical overview

Here we give an overview of our results.

Randomized
\Omega(n\log(n\varepsilon^{2})/\varepsilon^{2})
lower bound for maximum cut (
Theorem

1.1
).

The goal of
Theorem

1.1
is to prove a lower bound against randomized streaming algorithms outputting an approximate maximum cut in a graph.

Given a fixed streaming algorithm and an input graph
G
, let
\mathtt{Compress}(G)
denote the state of the streaming algorithm after processing the edges of
G
.
If the streaming algorithm uses space
s
, we can think of this as a (possibly randomized) mapping
\mathtt{Compress}:\{\text{graphs}\}\to\{0,1\}^{s}
.
Our fundamental idea is then to construct a large family
\mathcal{F}
of graphs on
\Theta(n)
vertices (
\log|\mathcal{F}|=\Omega(n\log(n\varepsilon^{2})/\varepsilon^{2})
)
and show that
\mathtt{Compress}(G)
(typically) contains enough information about
G
to uniquely identify it among all graphs in
\mathcal{F}
.
Since
\mathtt{Compress}(G)
has only
s
bits, this is information-theoretically impossible unless
s\approx|\log(\mathcal{F})|
, allowing us to conclude the space lower bound.

Recall that
\mathtt{Compress}(G)
encapsulates the state of the streaming algorithm after
G
’s edges are streamed.
Thus, knowledge of
\mathtt{Compress}(G)
can help us to identify
G
from among a large family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
.
In particular, we can “pick up where we left off” in the stream;
that is, we can pick a graph
H
, add
H
’s edges to the stream, and then ask for an approximate maximum cut in
G\cup H
(with good probability).
Modulo failure probability, we can now forget about the streaming model, and think instead about the following query problem:
when can
G\in\mathcal{F}
be identified given the ability to query an approximate maximum cut in
G\cup H
for
H
of our choosing?

Note that in the simplest case, we might set
H
to the empty graph, and query an approximate maximum cut in
G
itself.
However, this gives us just
n
bits of information about
G
,
1
1

1

After all, a cut is just a string in
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

which is not enough to identify
G
in general (since
\log|\mathcal{F}|\gg n
).
Instead, we will need to query with many different
H
’s.
But which
H
’s should we use to “learn” about
G
?

Our main conceptual insight is that, by carefully constructing the graph family
\mathcal{F}
and the query graphs
H
,
we can also compute certain approximate
conditional
maximum cuts in
G
itself.
By this, we mean the following.
If
G
is a graph with vertex-set
V
, then a cut in
G
is just a string in
\{\pm 1\}^{V}
, i.e., a labeling of vertices by
\{\pm 1\}
values.
The standard maximum cut problem is to find the cut in
\{\pm 1\}^{V}
cutting the most edges in
G
.
In the “conditional” maximum cut problem, we are given, in addition to
G
, a subset
S\subseteq V
of the vertices and a cut
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{S}
on those vertices,
and we want to find the cut
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{V\setminus S}
on the remaining vertices
such that the full cut
xy\in\{\pm 1\}^{V}
cuts as many edges as possible.
In other words, we want to maximize the number of cut edges not over the full cube
\{\pm 1\}^{V}
, but over the subcube where the coordinates in
S
are fixed to
x
.

Specifically, in our setup, the graphs
G\in\mathcal{F}
are certain bipartite graphs on a vertex set
L\sqcup R
, with
|L|=|R|=n
,
and for every partial cut
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
on the left vertices, we hope to find an approximate conditional maximum cut
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}
on the right vertices.
We achieve this by defining a graph
H_{x}
on vertex set
V^{\prime}\supseteq V
in such a way that when we query an approximate maximum cut
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{V^{\prime}}
for
G\cup H_{x}
,

z_{L}
is forced to (mostly) equal
x
,
and
z_{R}
correspondingly (mostly) recovers an approximate conditional maximum cut.
(Here
z_{L}
and
z_{R}
denote, respectively,
z
’s restriction onto the left and right vertices of
G
.)

Our technical work now splits into two parts:

1.

We construct a very large family
\mathcal{F}
of bipartite graphs on vertex-set
L\sqcup R
such that for every
G_{1}\neq G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
,
except with probability 1% over the choice of the left-cut
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
, the set of approximate maximum right-cuts for
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
conditioned on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
are disjoint.
Specifically, to construct
\mathcal{F}
, we sample a set of random bipartite graphs with right-regularity
k=\Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2})
,
and then filter out graphs which fail certain desired criteria.

2.

We show how approximate maximum cut queries can be used to identify members of this family.
This step combines the standard Yao’s minimax lemma / averaging ideas and a careful construction of graphs
H_{x}
.

To conclude this subsection, we shed some additional light on Step (1), the construction of the large family of
k
-right-regular graphs
\mathcal{F}
.

\mathcal{F}
is required to satisfy two important properties:
firstly, the distribution of left-degrees of every graph in
\mathcal{F}
should be pseudorandom, in the sense that the total of squared left-degrees is
O(nk^{2})
and the total left-degree of vertices with left-degree
\Omega(k)
is
O(n\sqrt{k})
.
Secondly, the pairwise overlap in the edge-sets of graphs in
\mathcal{F}
is small.

We then show that we can separate each pair of distinct graphs
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
. Fix such a pair and sample left assignment
{\boldsymbol{x}}
uniformly at random.
We show that with high probability over
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, there does not exist any right assignment that is simultaneously near-optimal for both conditional instances
(G_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}})
and
(G_{2},{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
This is because fixing
x
induces at each right vertex
v
a signed imbalance which is the difference between the number of neighbors labeled
+1
and
-1
by
{\boldsymbol{x}}
; any near-optimal right assignment must agree with these imbalances on most vertices.
Thus, if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
shared a near-optimal right assignment under the same
x
, the difference in imbalance between the two has to be small.
On the other hand, the low-overlap property ensures that for most right vertices these imbalances are driven by essentially disjoint neighborhoods in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, leading to a large expected inconsistency under random
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
The bounded degree conditions allow us to amplify this to high probability via concentration, ruling out shared near-optimal right assignments.
This separation implies that any algorithm which succeeds on a constant fraction of the instances must distinguish between a constant fraction of the pairs of graphs in
\mathcal{F}
yielding the desired lower bound.

Randomized
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
upper bound for maximum cut.

Our algorithm is very simple and is an application of the
F_{0}
estimation algorithm from
[
16
]
. The algorithm of
[
16
]
estimates the number of distinct elements (so distinct edges here) in a stream up to a multiplicative
\varepsilon
approximation using
O(\log(\delta^{-1})/\varepsilon^{2}+\log n)
space with probability
1-\delta
. For any cut
x
, take
\mathrm{cut}(x)
to denote the set of edges that crosses
x
in the complete graph. Inclusion-exclusion therefore gives us

|G\cap\mathrm{cut}(x)|=|G|+|\mathrm{cut}(x)|-|G\cup\mathrm{cut}(x)|.

We can maintain
|G|
exactly in
\log n
bits and
|\mathrm{cut}(x)|
is known offline. We can use the
F_{0}
estimation algorithm to estimate
|G\cup\mathrm{cut}(x)|
in the following way: run the algorithm on the input stream and then add the edges of
\mathrm{cut}(x)
to the stream. Although the
F_{0}
algorithm gives a multiplicative approximation to
|G\cup\mathrm{cut}(x)|
, this translates to
\varepsilon n^{2}
additive error on every cut value. Therefore, the density of the graph gives the desired approximation.
Setting
\delta=2^{-\Theta(n)}
so that we can union bound over all cuts gives space
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
. We also note that since we are using an
F_{0}
sketch, we can handle the case where the same constraint is present in the stream multiple times. In this case, it is treated as if the constraint was only presented once.

Given that we are working in the setting where
\delta
is very small, the update time of the
F_{0}
sketch of
[
16
]
is
\operatorname{poly}n
. It is unclear how to speed up the update time while preserving the exact space complexity (note that in contrast uniform sampling has fast update time but suboptimal space). So, we also present an alternate algorithm which has a slightly worse space dependence (by a
\log(1/\varepsilon)
factor) but has only amortized
\operatorname{polylog}n
update time in
Appendix

B
.

Our alternate algorithm goes as follows.
The first key idea is to sample
n
random edges before the stream, as opposed to the naive approach of sampling
n
edges during the stream.
Then during the stream, we simply record which of the pre-sampled edges were actually present in the input graph with a simple bit array of size
n
and use these edges as our sample. But how do we sample
n
random edges before the stream in only
O(n)
bits of space? Our second key idea is that we do not need truly i.i.d. uniform samples.
So we construct a
d
-regular expander graph on vertex-set
\binom{n}{2}
(
d
depends only on
\varepsilon
) and take a random walk of length
n
in this graph starting from a random vertex. We can then use well-known concentration inequalities for random walks on expanders (see
[
64
]
) to conclude this gives a good estimator. Crucially, there is an
O(n+\log n)
-bit
implicit representation
of the list of vertices on the walk (which correspond to possible edges in the graph), since all we need to store is the starting vertex and a “next-step pointer” saying “go to my
i
-th neighbor” for
i\in[d]
for each vertex along the walk. We note that our techniques differ from
[
16
]
since we define the expander on all the possible edges of the input graph versus to generate random seeds.

Randomized
\Omega(n)
lower bound for maximum cut in dense graphs (
Theorem

1.2
).

We note that since
|V|=n
,
n
bits of space are required to
write down
the output. However, our lower bound shows that
\Omega(n)
bits of
working
space (i.e., not counting the output space) is also required for this task.

To establish our lower bound, we design an exponentially large family of inputs
\mathcal{F}
where the sets of approximately optimal solutions are pairwise disjoint. We then show that one can leverage an approximate
Max-Cut
streaming algorithm to correctly identify an input graph
G\in\mathcal{F}
.
The set of inputs are complete bipartite graphs.

Deterministic lower bound for maximum cut value (
Theorem

1.3
).

Our framework is similar to that of our randomized
\Omega(n\log(n\varepsilon^{2})/\varepsilon^{2})
lower bound. We design a large family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
and show that
G\in\mathcal{F}
can be identified given the ability to query an approximate maximum cut value in
G\cup H
for
H
of our choosing. In the deterministic setting, the query access we assume is stronger than in the randomized setting: we may augment
G
with any
H
and obtain exact deterministic approximations to the maximum cut value of
G\cup H
with no failure probability and therefore with an unbounded number of queries.

We instantiate
\mathcal{F}
as the family of graphs formed by the union of
\Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2})
perfect bipartite matchings on a vertex set of size
2n
. For every pair of distinct graphs
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, we construct a deterministic distinguishing test
\mathcal{T}(G_{1},G_{2})
. Specifically, we show that for each such pair there exists a cut
x
satisfying
|\mathsf{val}_{G_{1}}\left\lparen x\right\rparen-\mathsf{val}_{G_{2}}\left\lparen x\right\rparen|=\Omega(n/\varepsilon).
Using this cut, we define augmentation
H
such that the maximum cut value of
G\cup H
encodes
\mathsf{val}_{G}\left\lparen x\right\rparen
. By thresholding the resulting approximate maximum cut value, we can deterministically distinguish whether the unknown graph
G
is equal to
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
(with arbitrary behavior permitted otherwise). Running
\mathcal{T}(G_{1},G_{2})
over all possible pairs of graphs in
\mathcal{F}
and selecting the graph consistent with the largest number of tests uniquely identifies
G
, yielding the desired space lower bound.

4
Randomized lower bound for maximum cut (
Theorem

1.1
)

We start by defining the notion of
conditional
Max Cut,
where the input is a bipartite graph in which the left vertices have a fixed assignment
and the goal is to find the optimum assignment on the right vertices. We then give some lemmas which outline what conditions we want for our hard family of graphs.

4.1
“Conditional” Max Cut.

Let
G=(L\sqcup R,E)
be a bipartite simple graph and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
a fixed assignment to the left vertices.
The
value
of an assignment
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}
to the right vertices is

\mathrm{val}_{G\mid x}(y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}(1-x_{u}y_{v})=\frac{|E|}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}x_{u}y_{v}.

The optimum value over all assignments is

\mathrm{opt}_{G\mid x}\coloneqq\max_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}}\mathrm{val}_{G\mid x}(y).

Given
G
and
x
, we define the
discrepancy
of a vertex
v\in R
as

\delta_{G\mid x}(v)\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)}x_{u}.

(4.2)

This immediately gives, for a fixed right-assignment
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}
, the equation

\mathrm{val}_{G\mid x}(y)=\frac{|E|}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in R}y_{v}\cdot\delta_{G\mid x}(v).

(4.3)

Maximizing this over all right-assignments gives the equation

\mathrm{opt}_{G\mid x}=\frac{|E|}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in R}|\delta_{G\mid x}(v)|.

(4.4)

We can then define the
loss
of a particular right-assignment
y
as:

\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(y)\coloneqq\mathrm{opt}_{G\mid x}-\mathrm{val}_{G\mid x}(y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in R}\left\lparen|\delta_{G\mid x}(v)|+y_{v}\cdot\delta_{G\mid x}(v)\right\rparen.

(4.5)

Finally, for a goodness threshold
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we define the set of good right-assignments for
x
as:

\mathrm{Good}_{G\mid x}^{\tau}\coloneqq\left\{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}:\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(y)\leq\tau\right\}.

(4.6)

For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\mathrm{slack}(a,b)\coloneqq|a|+|b|-|a+b|
.
This quantity is the slack in the triangle inequality applied to
a
and
b
.
In particular, it is always nonnegative.
It vanishes iff
a
and
b
have the same sign (or either is zero),
and otherwise equals
\min(|a|,|b|)
.

Let
\underline{G}=(G_{1},G_{2})
denote an ordered pair of bipartite graphs on the same vertex-set
L\sqcup R
.
For a fixed left-assignment
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
and right-vertex
v\in R
, we define the
advantage

\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\coloneqq\mathrm{slack}(\delta_{G_{1}\mid x}(v),\delta_{G_{2}\mid x}(v)).

(4.7)

Our first lemma states that if two graphs share a good right-assignment, then the total advantage must be small.

4.8
.

For every pair of bipartite graphs
\underline{G}=(G_{1},G_{2})
and left-assignment
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
,
if
\mathrm{Good}_{G_{1}\mid x}^{\tau}\cap\mathrm{Good}_{G_{2}\mid x}^{\tau}\neq\emptyset
,
then
\sum_{v\in R}\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\leq 4\tau
.

Proof.

If
y\in\mathrm{Good}_{G_{1}\mid x}^{\tau}\cap\mathrm{Good}_{G_{2}\mid x}^{\tau}
,
then by definition, we have
\mathrm{loss}_{G_{1}\mid x}(y)+\mathrm{loss}_{G_{2}\mid x}(y)\leq 2\tau
.
But all
y
’s must incur total loss at least:

\displaystyle\min_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}}\left\lparen\mathrm{loss}_{G_{1}\mid x}(y)+\mathrm{loss}_{G_{2}\mid x}(y)\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}}\sum_{v\in R}\left\lparen|\delta_{G_{1}\mid x}(v)|+|\delta_{G_{2}\mid x}(v)|+y_{v}(\delta_{G_{1}\mid x}(v)+\delta_{G_{2}\mid x}(v))\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in R}\left\lparen|\delta_{G_{1}\mid x}(v)|+|\delta_{G_{2}\mid x}(v)|-|\delta_{G_{1}\mid x}(v)+\delta_{G_{2}\mid x}(v)|\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in R}\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v).\qed

Now, we establish conditions under which the advantage is large.
For
v\in R
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
, we decompose

\displaystyle\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\setminus\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)}x_{u},

\displaystyle\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)\setminus\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)}x_{u},

\displaystyle\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)}x_{u},

We then give the following lower bound on
\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)
:

4.9
.

For every pair of bipartite graphs
\underline{G}=(G_{1},G_{2})
, left-assignment
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
, and right-vertex
v\in R
:

\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\geq\mathrm{slack}\left\lparen\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\right\rparen-4|\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)|.

To prove this, we use the following fact:

4.10
Lipschitzness of the slack function.

The function
\mathrm{slack}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is 2-Lipschitz with respect to the
1
-norm:

|\mathrm{slack}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})-\mathrm{slack}(a,b)|\leq 2(|a^{\prime}-a|+|b^{\prime}-b|)
.

Proof.

Firstly, the absolute value function itself is
1
-Lipschitz:
We have
a^{\prime}=a+(a^{\prime}-a)\Longrightarrow|a^{\prime}|\leq|a|+|a^{\prime}-a|\Longrightarrow|a^{\prime}|-|a|\leq|a^{\prime}-a|
.
The same holds swapping
a
and
a^{\prime}
, hence
||a^{\prime}|-|a||\leq|a^{\prime}-a|
.
Now, we check:

\displaystyle|\mathrm{slack}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})-\mathrm{slack}(a,b)|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert|a^{\prime}|+|b^{\prime}|-|a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}|-|a|-|b|+|a+b|\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert|a^{\prime}|-|a|\right\rvert+\left\lvert|b^{\prime}|-|b|\right\rvert+\left\lvert|a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}|-|a+b|\right\rvert

(tri. ineq.)

\displaystyle\leq|a^{\prime}-a|+|b^{\prime}-b|+|a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}-(a+b)|

(
1
-Lipschitzness of
|\cdot|
)

\displaystyle\leq|a^{\prime}-a|+|b^{\prime}-b|+|a^{\prime}-a|+|b^{\prime}-b|

(tri. ineq.)

\displaystyle=2(|a^{\prime}-a|+|b^{\prime}-b|).\qed

Proof of
Lemma

4.9
.

Since
\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)=(\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\setminus\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v))\sqcup(\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v))
, we have
\delta_{G_{1}\mid x}(v)=\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)+\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)

and similarly for
\delta_{G_{2}\mid x}(v)
.
Hence, using the triangle inequality, we have:

\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)=\mathrm{slack}\left\lparen\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)+\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)+\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\right\rparen\\
\geq\mathrm{slack}\left\lparen\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\right\rparen-\left\lvert\mathrm{slack}\left\lparen\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)+\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)+\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\right\rparen-\mathrm{slack}\left\lparen\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)\right\rparen\right\rvert

and we can then apply
Lemma

4.10
.
∎

4.1
Probabilistic construction of a hard family of graphs

We construct the hard family
\mathcal{F}
of graphs using the so-called “deletion method”:
we sample a large family of random graphs, delete all graphs which violate desired criteria,
and show that the resulting family is still large because violations are improbable.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\text{right-regular}}(n,k)
be the space of
k
-right-regular bipartite simple graphs, generated by having each
v\in R
independently choose a uniform random
k
-subset of
L
as its neighbors.

For a bipartite graph
G=(L\sqcup R,E)
and a threshold
d
, we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tail}_{G}(d)\coloneqq\{u\in L:\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)>d\}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{TailDeg}_{G}(d)\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathrm{Tail}_{G}(d)}\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u).

4.11
Existence of hard family.

For every
\eta_{\mathrm{near}},\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}>0
and
C_{\mathrm{deg}}>1
, there exist
C_{\mathrm{size}},C_{\mathrm{stretch}}>0
such that the following holds:
for every sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq C_{\mathrm{stretch}}k\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{G}_{\text{right-regular}}(n,k)
of simple bipartite graphs satisfying:

1.

Right-regularity
:
for every
G\in\mathcal{F}
and
v\in R
, it holds that
\mathrm{deg}_{G}(v)=k
.

2.

Bounded sum of squared left-degrees:

for every
G\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that
\sum_{u\in L}\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{deg}}nk^{2}
.

3.

Light left-degree tail:

for every
G\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that
\mathrm{TailDeg}_{G}(2k)\leq\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}n\sqrt{k}
.

4.

Low overlap:
for every
G_{1}\neq G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that
|E(G_{1})\cap E(G_{2})|\leq\eta_{\mathrm{near}}nk
.

5.

Large size:

\log|\mathcal{F}|\geq C_{\mathrm{size}}nk\log(n/k)
.

Note that the first three properties impose requirements on each graph in the family
\mathcal{F}
individually;
the fourth property involves pairs of graphs,
and the fifth property involves the size of the entire family.

Proof.

By definition, any
{\boldsymbol{G}}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\text{right-regular}}(n,k)
deterministically satisfies Property 1.

Pick some fixed
p_{2}\in(1/C_{\mathrm{deg}},1)
.
For Property 2, sampling
{\boldsymbol{G}}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\text{right-regular}}(n,k)
, the degree
\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)
of a left vertex
u
is the sum of
n
independent indicator variables (one for each
v\in R
choosing
u
) with expectation
k/n
.
Thus
\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)\sim\text{Binomial}(n,k/n)
.
We have
\Exp[\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)]=k
and
\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u))\leq k
.
Therefore,
\Exp[\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)^{2}]=\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u))+\Exp[\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)]^{2}\leq k+k^{2}
.
By linearity of expectation,
\Exp\left[\sum_{u\in L}\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)^{2}\right]\leq n(k+k^{2})\leq p_{2}C_{\mathrm{deg}}nk^{2}
(taking
k
large enough).
By Markov’s inequality, the probability that this sum exceeds
C_{\mathrm{deg}}nk^{2}
is at most
(p_{2}C_{\mathrm{deg}})/C_{\mathrm{deg}}=p_{2}<1
.

For Property 3, sampling
{\boldsymbol{G}}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\text{right-regular}}(n,k)
, the expected total degree of the tail vertices is:

\displaystyle\Exp[\mathrm{TailDeg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(2k)]=\sum_{u\in L}\Exp\left[\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{deg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(u)>2k}\right]

\displaystyle=n\sum_{j=2k+1}^{n}j\binom{n}{j}(k/n)^{j}(1-k/n)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=nk\sum_{i=2k}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i}(k/n)^{i}(1-k/n)^{n-1-i}

\displaystyle=nk\cdot\Pr(\text{Binomial}(n-1,k/n)\geq 2k).

By standard multiplicative Chernoff bounds (since the mean is strictly
<k
),
this upper tail probability is bounded by
\leq e^{-k/3}
, hence
\Exp[\mathrm{TailDeg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(2k)]\leq nk\cdot e^{-k/3}
.
Hence by Markov’s inequality,
\Pr[\mathrm{TailDeg}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}(2k)>\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}n\sqrt{k}]\leq(nk\cdot e^{-k/3})/(\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}n\sqrt{k})=\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}^{-1}\sqrt{k}e^{-k/3}
.
Taking
k
sufficiently large, this is smaller than
0.49(1-p_{2})
.

For Property 4, consider sampling
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{G}}_{2}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\text{right-regular}}(n,k)
independently, and define the overlap
{\boldsymbol{X}}:=|E({\boldsymbol{G}}_{1})\cap E({\boldsymbol{G}}_{2})|=\sum_{v\in R}|\mathrm{N}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}_{2}}(v)|
.
Because each
v\in R
chooses its
k
neighbors independently,
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is the sum of
n
independent
hypergeometric
random variables.
Let
p_{\mathrm{near}}
denote the probability that
{\boldsymbol{X}}
exceeds
\eta_{\mathrm{near}}nk
.
Since
\Exp[{\boldsymbol{X}}]=n\cdot(k^{2}/n)=k^{2}
,
using Hoeffding’s extension of Chernoff bounds for sums of hypergeometrics,
we get

p_{\mathrm{near}}\leq(ek^{2}/(\eta_{\mathrm{near}}nk))^{(\eta_{\mathrm{near}}nk)}=\exp(-\eta_{\mathrm{near}}nk\ln(\eta_{\mathrm{near}}n/(ek)))\leq\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\eta_{\mathrm{near}}nk\ln(n/k)),

where the final inequality uses the assumption on
n
and takes
C_{\mathrm{stretch}}\gg e/\eta_{\mathrm{near}}
.

We now apply the deletion method: sample
N\coloneqq\lfloor 0.98(1-p_{2})/(p_{\mathrm{near}})\rfloor
graphs independently.
The expected number of graphs violating Property 2 or 3 is
\leq(p_{2}+0.49(1-p_{2}))N<N
.
The expected number of pairs violating Property 4 is
\binom{N}{2}p_{\mathrm{near}}<\frac{1}{2}(p_{\mathrm{near}}N)N<0.49(1-p_{2})N
.
Thus, the expected total number of violations is at most
(p_{2}+0.98(1-p_{2}))N
.
Hence, there exists a realization with at most
(p_{2}+0.98(1-p_{2}))N
violations.
Removing one graph from each violation leaves a simple family
\mathcal{F}
satisfying all properties with size
|\mathcal{F}|\geq 0.02(1-p_{2})N
.
Taking the logarithm yields Property 5. ∎

4.2
Separating conditional Max Cut instances

Now we show that distinct graphs in the hard family admit
incompatible sets of near-optimal right-assignments once the left assignment

x
is fixed at random. In particular, for two different graphs
G_{1},G_{2}
,
with high probability over the choice of
x
, there does not exist any
right-assignment that is simultaneously near-optimal for both conditional
Max Cut instances
(G_{1},x)
and
(G_{2},x)
. We will later show how to lift this to a lower bound for the standard (unconditional) Max Cut
problem.

Define the density parameter
C_{\mathrm{den}}\coloneqq 10^{6}
, and a corresponding regularity parameter
k\coloneqq\lfloor 1/(10^{5}C_{\mathrm{den}}\varepsilon)^{2}\rfloor
.
Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be a sufficiently small absolute constant such that
k
is large enough to invoke the prior lemma for all
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
.
The slack threshold is defined as
\tau\coloneqq\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{2000}
.
We will invoke
Lemma

4.11
with
\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{8000}
,
\eta_{\mathrm{near}}\coloneqq 10^{-8}
, and
C_{\mathrm{deg}}\coloneqq 10
.

4.12
Slack vs. minimum magnitude.

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}}
be independent, symmetrically distributed random variables.
Then
\Exp[\mathrm{slack}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}})]=\Exp[\min(|{\boldsymbol{X}}|,|{\boldsymbol{Y}}|)]
.

Proof.

Observe that for any real numbers
a,b
, we have
|a+b|+|a-b|=2\max(|a|,|b|)
.
Thus,

\mathrm{slack}(a,b)+\mathrm{slack}(a,-b)=2|a|+2|b|-(|a+b|+|a-b|)=2|a|+2|b|-2\max(|a|,|b|)=2\min(|a|,|b|).

Since
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is symmetric,
-{\boldsymbol{Y}}
has the same distribution as
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
.
Because
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
are independent, the joint distribution of
({\boldsymbol{X}},-{\boldsymbol{Y}})
is identical to
({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}})
. Therefore,
\Exp[\mathrm{slack}({\boldsymbol{X}},-{\boldsymbol{Y}})]=\Exp[\mathrm{slack}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}})]
.
Taking the expectation of the sum gives
2\Exp[\mathrm{slack}({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}})]=2\Exp[\min(|{\boldsymbol{X}}|,|{\boldsymbol{Y}}|)]
, which completes the proof.
∎

4.13
Minimum magnitude of Rademacher sums.

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}}
be independent Rademacher sums of lengths
m_{X},m_{Y}\geq m\geq 1
. Let
{\boldsymbol{W}}\coloneqq\min(|{\boldsymbol{X}}|,|{\boldsymbol{Y}}|)
.
Then
\Exp[{\boldsymbol{W}}]\geq C_{\mathrm{sep}}\sqrt{m}
, where
C_{\mathrm{sep}}\coloneqq 9/512\approx 0.0175
.

Proof.

We use the Paley–Zygmund inequality on
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is a sum of
m_{X}
independent Rademacher variables,
\Exp[{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}]=m_{X}
.
The fourth moment expands to
\Exp[{\boldsymbol{X}}^{4}]=m_{X}+3m_{X}(m_{X}-1)=3m_{X}^{2}-2m_{X}<3m_{X}^{2}
.
By Paley–Zygmund,
\Pr({\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}>m_{X}/4)\geq(1-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\frac{\Exp[{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}]^{2}}{\Exp[{\boldsymbol{X}}^{4}]}>\frac{9}{16}\frac{m_{X}^{2}}{3m_{X}^{2}}=\frac{3}{16}
.
Since
m_{X}\geq m
,
\Pr(|{\boldsymbol{X}}|>\sqrt{m}/2)\geq\Pr(|{\boldsymbol{X}}|>\sqrt{m_{X}}/2)\geq 3/16
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{Y}}
are independent,
\Pr({\boldsymbol{W}}>\sqrt{m}/2)=\Pr(|{\boldsymbol{X}}|>\sqrt{m}/2)\Pr(|{\boldsymbol{Y}}|>\sqrt{m}/2)\geq(3/16)^{2}=9/256
.
Because
{\boldsymbol{W}}\geq 0
, we have
\Exp[{\boldsymbol{W}}]\geq(\sqrt{m}/2)\cdot(9/256)=\frac{9}{512}\sqrt{m}=C_{\mathrm{sep}}\sqrt{m}
.
∎

4.14
Typically no shared good assignments.

With the parameters we defined, let
\mathcal{F}
be as in
Lemma

4.11
and let
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
be distinct.
Then

\Pr_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}}\left[\mathrm{Good}_{G_{1}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\tau}\cap\mathrm{Good}_{G_{2}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\tau}\neq\emptyset\right]\leq 0.01.

Proof.

Let
F(x)\coloneqq\sum_{v\in R}\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid x}(v)
.
By
Lemma

4.9
, we have
\mathrm{Good}_{G_{1}\mid x}^{\tau}\cap\mathrm{Good}_{G_{2}\mid x}^{\tau}\neq\emptyset\Longrightarrow F(x)\leq 4\tau\eqqcolon T^{*}
.
We now upper bound the probability over
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
that
F({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq T^{*}
.
Note
T^{*}=\frac{4n\sqrt{k}}{2000}=0.002n\sqrt{k}
.

First, we lower bound the expectation of
F({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
Let
\rho\coloneqq 10^{-6}

and define the set of typical vertices
V_{\mathrm{typ}}\coloneqq\{v\in R:|\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)|\leq\rho k\}
.
By the low overlap property,
\Exp_{\boldsymbol{v}}[|\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}({\boldsymbol{v}})|]\leq\eta_{\mathrm{near}}k
.
By the setting of
\eta_{\mathrm{near}}
and Markov’s inequality, we conclude
|V_{\mathrm{typ}}|\geq 0.99n
.

Fix
v\in V_{\mathrm{typ}}
.
Because the graphs are simple (no parallel edges),
when
{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{L}
,
\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)
is a sum of
|\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)|\leq\rho k
independent Rademacher random variables.
Thus
\Exp[|\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)|]\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v))}=\sqrt{|\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)|}\leq\sqrt{\rho k}
.
Further, because
G_{1},G_{2}
are
k
-right regular,
\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)
,
\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)
are independent sums of
m\coloneqq k-|\mathrm{N}_{G_{1}}(v)\cap\mathrm{N}_{G_{2}}(v)|\geq(1-\rho)k
independent Rademacher random variables.
Since
\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)
are independent symmetric variables, by
Lemmas

4.13
and
4.12
,

\Exp[\mathrm{slack}(\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v))]=\Exp[\min(|\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)|,|\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)|)]\geq C_{\mathrm{sep}}\sqrt{m}.

We finally have, sampling
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
:

\Exp[\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)]\geq\Exp[\mathrm{slack}(\mu^{1}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v),\mu^{2}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v))]-4\Exp[|\mu^{\cap}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)|]\\
\geq C_{\mathrm{sep}}\sqrt{m}-4\sqrt{\rho k}\geq\sqrt{k}(C_{\mathrm{sep}}\sqrt{1-\rho}-4\sqrt{\rho}).

Substituting our constants (
\rho=10^{-6},C_{\mathrm{sep}}\approx 0.0175
), we deduce
\Exp[\mathrm{adv}_{\underline{G}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)]\geq 0.0135\sqrt{k}
.
The total expectation therefore satisfies
\Exp[F({\boldsymbol{x}})]\geq 0.99n\cdot 0.0135\sqrt{k}\geq 0.0133n\sqrt{k}
.
The required deviation for a failure is
t\coloneqq\Exp[F({\boldsymbol{x}})]-T^{*}\geq 0.011n\sqrt{k}
.

Now, we apply McDiarmid’s bounded differences inequality to the function
F:\{\pm 1\}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}
applied to the independent Rademacher variables
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{u}\in\{\pm 1\})_{u\in L}
.
Given a fixed
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
,
flipping
x_{u}
changes
\delta_{G_{i}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}}(v)
by
2
if
v\in\mathrm{N}_{G_{i}}(u)
and
0
otherwise.
Hence, by
Lemma

4.10
, the maximum change in
F(x)
from flipping
x_{u}
is strictly bounded by
c_{u}\coloneqq 4(\mathrm{deg}_{G_{1}}(u)+\mathrm{deg}_{G_{2}}(u))
.

The variance proxy
\Sigma
is elegantly bounded via the squared
2
-norm of degrees property (
Lemma

4.11
, Item 2), applying the algebraic inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
:

\Sigma=\sum_{u\in L}c_{u}^{2}\leq 16\sum_{u}(\mathrm{deg}_{G_{1}}(u)+\mathrm{deg}_{G_{2}}(u))^{2}\leq 32\sum_{u}\left\lparen\mathrm{deg}_{G_{1}}(u)^{2}+\mathrm{deg}_{G_{2}}(u)^{2}\right\rparen\leq 640nk^{2}.

Applying McDiarmid’s inequality:

\Pr[F({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq T^{*}]\leq\exp\left\lparen-\frac{2t^{2}}{\Sigma}\right\rparen\leq\exp\left\lparen-\frac{2(0.011n\sqrt{k})^{2}}{640nk^{2}}\right\rparen=\exp\left\lparen-\Omega\left\lparen\frac{n}{k}\right\rparen\right\rparen.

Since
n\geq C_{\mathrm{stretch}}k
, taking
C_{\mathrm{stretch}}
sufficiently large this failure probability is at most
0.01
.
∎

4.3
Reducing conditional Max Cut to Max Cut

4.15
Gadget graph.

Let
L,R
be disjoint vertex sets.
For
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
, we define the following “gadget” graph
H_{x}
:
The vertex set is
L\sqcup R\sqcup\{s_{+1},s_{-1}\}
, where
s_{+1},s_{-1}
are two new “sink” vertices;
there is an edge of weight
W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{sink}}\coloneqq 10^{5}nk
between
s_{+1}
and
s_{-1}
, and, for each
u\in L
, an edge between
u
and
s_{-x_{u}}
with weight
W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}\coloneqq 2k
.

Note that the graphs
G
and
H_{x}
are individually bipartite, but their union
G\cup H_{x}
need not be.

4.16
.

Let the parameters be as in
Section

4.2
.
Suppose
G\in\mathcal{F}
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
.
Let
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{L\sqcup R\sqcup\{s_{\pm 1}\}}
be an assignment to the graph
G\cup H_{x}
.
If
\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{G\cup H_{x}}
,
then
z_{R}\in\mathrm{Good}_{G\mid x}^{\tau}
,
where
z_{R}
denotes the induced assignment on the vertices
R
.

Proof.

Let

\ell\coloneqq\mathsf{opt}_{G\cup H_{x}}-\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen,

denote the loss of the assignment
z
, so that we have assumed
\ell\leq\varepsilon\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{G\cup H_{x}}
.
We have
\mathsf{opt}_{G\cup H_{x}}\leq C_{\mathrm{den}}nk
(since the latter upper-bounds the total weight in
G\cup H_{x}
), hence
\ell\leq\varepsilon\cdot C_{\mathrm{den}}nk
.
By the setting of
C_{\mathrm{den}}
, we have
k=\lfloor 1/(10^{10}\varepsilon^{2}C_{\mathrm{den}}^{2})\rfloor\leq 1/(10^{10}\varepsilon^{2}C_{\mathrm{den}}^{2})
,
and hence
\varepsilon\sqrt{k}\leq 1/(10^{5}C_{\mathrm{den}})
.
This guarantees

\ell\leq\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{10000}.

(4.17)

Consider the assignment
z^{*}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L\sqcup R\sqcup\{s_{\pm 1}\}}

defined by
z^{*}(s_{+1})\coloneqq+1
,
z^{*}(s_{-1})\coloneqq-1
,

z^{*}(u)\coloneqq x_{u}
for
u\in L
, and
z^{*}(v)\coloneqq-\operatorname{sign}(\delta_{G\mid x}(v))
for
v\in R
.
This assignment cuts the sink-sink edge and all sink-left vertex edges.
Hence

\mathsf{opt}_{G\cup H_{x}}\geq\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{*}\right\rparen=W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{sink}}+nW_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}+\mathrm{opt}_{G\mid x}.

(4.18)

If
z(s_{+1})=z(s_{-1})
, then the sink-sink edge is not cut, so
\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen\leq nW_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}+|E(G)|
.

Equation

4.18
then gives
\ell\geq W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{sink}}-(|E(G)|-\mathrm{opt}_{G\mid x})\geq 10^{5}nk-nk
, contradicting
Equation

4.17
.
Hence,
z(s_{+1})\neq z(s_{-1})
.
By symmetry, we assume that
z(s_{+1})=+1
and
z(s_{-1})=-1
.

Let
\Delta\coloneqq\{u\in L:z_{L}(u)\neq x_{u}\}
.
We claim that

\ell\geq\sum_{u\in\Delta}\left\lparen 2k-\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)\right\rparen+\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(z_{R}).

(4.19)

Indeed, consider the “intermediate” assignment
z^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L\sqcup R\sqcup\{s_{\pm 1}\}}

which matches
z
on the right vertices and
z^{*}
(and therefore
x
) on the left vertices.
(That is,
z^{\prime}(s_{\pm 1})=\pm 1
,
z^{\prime}(u)=x_{u}
for
u\in L
, and
z^{\prime}(v)=z_{v}
for
v\in R
.)
We have

\ell=\mathsf{opt}_{G\cup H_{x}}-\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen\geq\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{*}\right\rparen-\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen\\
=\underbrace{(\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{*}\right\rparen-\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{\prime}\right\rparen)}_{\eqqcolon\ell_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}}+\underbrace{(\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{\prime}\right\rparen-\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen)}_{\eqqcolon\ell_{\text{left}\leftrightarrow\text{right}}}.

For the first term,
\ell_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}
, we have
\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{*}\right\rparen=W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{sink}}+nW_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}+\mathrm{opt}_{G\mid x}
,
while
\mathsf{val}_{G\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen z^{\prime}\right\rparen=W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{sink}}+nW_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}+\mathrm{val}_{G\mid x}(z_{R})
.
(In other words, both assignments satisfy all edges incident to the sinks.
Here,
z_{R}
denotes the assignment
z
restricted to the right vertices.)
Hence, the difference is
\ell_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}=\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(z_{R})
.
Meanwhile, we can write the second term as

\ell_{\text{left}\leftrightarrow\text{right}}=\sum_{u\in\Delta}\Big((\text{weight of edges incident to }u\text{ satisfied by }z^{\prime}\text{ but not }z)\\
-(\text{weight of edges incident to }u\text{ satisfied by }z\text{ but not }z^{\prime})\Big).

For every
u\in\Delta
, the first term is at least
W_{\text{sink}\leftrightarrow\text{left}}=2k
, while the second is pessimistically at most
\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)
.
Hence,

\ell\geq\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(z_{R})+\sum_{u\in\Delta}(2k-\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)).

(4.20)

Finally, recall the definition of the “tail vertices”
\mathrm{Tail}_{G}(2k)=\{u:\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)>2k\}
.
We split the sum in
Equation

4.20
over
u\in\Delta
into
u\in\mathrm{Tail}_{G}(2k)
and
u\not\in\mathrm{Tail}_{G}(2k)
;
in the latter case, we have immediately that
(2k-\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u))\geq 0
,
while in the former, we use the pessimistic lower bound
2k-\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)\geq-\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)
.
Therefore:

\ell\geq\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(z_{R})-\sum_{u\in\mathrm{Tail}_{G}(2k)}\mathrm{deg}_{G}(u)=\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(z_{R})-\mathrm{TailDeg}_{G}(2k).

By the light tail property and our setting of
\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}
,
\mathrm{TailDeg}_{G}(2k)\leq\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{8000}
.
Rearranging yields:

\mathrm{loss}_{G\mid x}(z_{R})\leq\ell+\mathrm{TailDeg}_{G}(2k)\leq\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{10000}+\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{8000}<\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{2000}=\tau.\qed

.

5
Upper bound for maximum cut in dense graphs

5.1
.

There exists an insertion-only randomized streaming algorithm which, given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices and
m\geq\alpha n^{2}
edges and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, outputs
x:V\to\{0,1\}
which has
\mathsf{val}_{G}\left\lparen x\right\rparen\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{G}
in
O(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}})
bits of space with probability at least
2/3
.

Proof.

We use the
F_{0}
estimation sketch of
[
16
]
which estimates
F_{0}
up to a multiplicative
1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime}
error with failure probability
\delta
, using
O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{\prime 2}}+\log N\right)
bits of space. Here, the universe is the set of all
N=\binom{n}{2}
possible edges of the input graph
G
.

During the stream, we maintain an
F_{0}
sketch with
\varepsilon_{m}=\varepsilon/10
and
\delta_{m}=1/9
to estimate
m
which is the number of distinct edges (recall that edges can appear multiple times - we aim to allow repeated edges but ignore their multiplicities). Let
\widehat{m}
denote its estimate for
m
. We feed the stream of inserted edges into a second
F_{0}
sketch, instantiating it with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{10}
and failure probability
\delta=\frac{1}{9\cdot 2^{n}}
. The space used is therefore
O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}+\log n\right)=O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right)
bits.

For any cut
x:V\to\{0,1\}
, let
\mathcal{C}(x)\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
denote the set of edges crossing the cut in the complete graph on
V
. The number of edges of
G
crossing the cut is exactly
|E\cap\mathcal{C}(x)|
. By the inclusion-exclusion principle:

|E\cap\mathcal{C}(x)|=m+|\mathcal{C}(x)|-|E\cup\mathcal{C}(x)|.

After the stream, we iterate over all
2^{n-1}
possible cuts
x
. For each cut, we instantiate a copy of the
F_{0}
sketch’s memory state and feed the offline edges of
\mathcal{C}(x)
into it. The sketch then outputs an estimate
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{x}
for
|E\cup\mathcal{C}(x)|
. We estimate the cut value as
\widehat{V}_{x}=\widehat{m}+|\mathcal{C}(x)|-\widehat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{x}
.

By a union bound, with probability at least
2/3
, for all
2^{n-1}
estimates (one per cut
x
) we have
that the estimate

\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{x}\coloneqq\widehat{m}+|\mathcal{C}(x)|-\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{x}

approximates the true cut value with additive error

\lvert\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{x}-|E\cap\mathcal{C}(x)|\rvert=|\widehat{m}-m|+\lvert\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{x}-|E\cup\mathcal{C}(x)|\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{m}m+\varepsilon^{\prime}|E\cup\mathcal{C}(x)|.

Since
|E\cup\mathcal{C}(x)|\leq\binom{n}{2}\leq n^{2}/2
for every cut
x
, this yields a uniform additive error bound of at most
\varepsilon_{m}m+\varepsilon^{\prime}n^{2}/2
per cut.

With
\varepsilon_{m}=\varepsilon/10
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon\alpha/10
, using assumption
m\geq\alpha n^{2}
, we have

\varepsilon_{m}m+\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}n^{2}}{2}=\frac{\varepsilon m}{10}+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha n^{2}}{20}\leq\frac{\varepsilon m}{10}+\frac{\varepsilon m}{20}\leq\varepsilon m.

So, every normalized cut value is approximated within additive error at most
\varepsilon
, and selecting the cut
x
that maximizes
\widehat{V}_{x}
yields a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation.
∎

Randomized lower bound for maximum cut in dense graphs (

1.2
)

In this section, we prove

-approximate cut given a dense bipartite graph.

6.1

Let

.

.
The protocol is simple:

•

[n]=\left\lparen S\cap T\right\rparen\sqcup\left\lparen S\cap\overline{T}\right\rparen\sqcup\left\lparen\overline{S}\cap T\right\rparen\sqcup\left\lparen\overline{S}\cap\overline{T}\right\rparen.

We similarly partition the edges,

S\boxtimes\overline{S}=\left\lparen(S\cap T)\boxtimes(\overline{S}\cap T)\right\rparen\sqcup\left\lparen(S\cap T)\boxtimes(\overline{S}\cap\overline{T})\right\rparen\sqcup\left\lparen(S\cap\overline{T})\boxtimes(\overline{S}\cap T)\right\rparen\sqcup\left\lparen(S\cap\overline{T})\boxtimes(\overline{S}\cap\overline{T})\right\rparen.

Only the second and third types of edges are cut by the cut
T
; thus, the value of the cut
T
is

\mathsf{val}_{\mathsf{Kb}(S)}\left\lparen 1_{T}\right\rparen=\frac{|S||\overline{S}|-|S\cap T||\overline{S}\cap T|-|S\cap\overline{T}||\overline{S}\cap\overline{T}|}{|S||\overline{S}|}=1-((1-p(S,T))q(S,T)+p(S,T)(1-q(S,T))),

(6.4)

where we define

\displaystyle p(S,T)\coloneqq\frac{|S\cap\overline{T}|}{|S|}

and

\displaystyle q(S,T)\coloneqq\frac{|\overline{S}\cap T|}{|\overline{S}|}.

(6.5)

Qualitatively, the cut value can only be close to
1
iff either (i)
p
and
q
are both very close to
0
, i.e.,
S
and
T
are almost the same, or (ii)
p
and
q
are both very close to
1
, i.e.,
S
and
\overline{T}
are almost the same.
(See
Figure

1
for a visual depiction.)
We now make this qualitative statement quantitative.

6.6
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/4
.
Then
\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Cross-multiplies to
\varepsilon\leq 2\varepsilon(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\Longleftrightarrow 2\varepsilon\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon\Longleftrightarrow 2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq 1\Longleftrightarrow\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
.
∎

6.7
.

Suppose
S,T\subseteq[n]
,
p=p(S,T)
and
q=q(S,T)
are as in
Equation

6.5
, and
0<\varepsilon<1/4
.
If
\mathsf{val}_{\mathsf{Kb}(S)}\left\lparen 1_{T}\right\rparen\geq 1-\varepsilon
, then either
\max\{p,q\}\leq 2\varepsilon
or
\min\{p,q\}\geq 1-2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By
Equation

6.4
, we have
(1-p)q+p(1-q)\leq\varepsilon
.
Thus, in particular,
(1-p)q\leq\varepsilon
and
p(1-q)\leq\varepsilon
.
Taking the second inequality, we deduce that either
p\leq 2\varepsilon
or
1-q\leq 2\varepsilon
.
Suppose first that
p\leq 2\varepsilon
.
Then the first inequality gives

q\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}},

which combined with
6.6
gives
q\leq 2\varepsilon
.
Otherwise, we have symmetrically that
1-p\leq 2\varepsilon
.
∎

Note that the relative Hamming distance between the binary strings
1_{S}
and
1_{T}
is:

d(1_{S},1_{T})\coloneqq\frac{|S\cap\overline{T}|+|\overline{S}\cap T|}{n}=\frac{|S|\cdot p(S,T)+|\overline{S}|\cdot q(S,T)}{n}.

(6.8)

We now get:

6.9
.

Suppose
S,T\subseteq[n]
and
0<\varepsilon<1/4
.
If
\mathsf{val}_{\mathsf{Kb}(S)}\left\lparen 1_{T}\right\rparen\geq 1-\varepsilon
, then either
d(1_{S},1_{T})\leq 2\varepsilon
or
d(1_{S},1_{T})\geq 1-2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from
Equations

6.8
and
6.7
(and
|S|+|\overline{S}|=n
).
∎

Finally, we use
Lemma

6.9
to prove
Theorem

6.2
assuming
Lemma

6.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

6.2
assuming
Lemma

6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be the set of strings promised by
Lemma

6.3
for
\delta=2\varepsilon
.
For each string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we let
S=\operatorname{supp}(x)
denote the support of
x
, and we create a corresponding graph
G=\mathsf{Kb}(S)
.
We let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of graphs so created, and we claim that
\mathcal{G}
fulfills the desiderata of
Theorem

6.2
.
Indeed:

1.

For each
G\in\mathcal{G}
, the number of edges in
G=\mathsf{Kb}(S)
for
S=\operatorname{supp}(x)
is
|S|(n-|S|)
, and by assumption on
w(x)
,
\frac{1}{3}n\leq|S|\leq\frac{2}{3}n
.

2.

|\mathcal{G}|=|\mathcal{S}|\geq 2^{Cn}
.

3.

Each
G\in\mathcal{G}
is bipartite by definition.

4.

We show that for every
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, there do not exist
G\neq G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
such
\mathsf{val}_{G}\left\lparen y\right\rparen\geq 1-\varepsilon
and
\mathsf{val}_{G^{\prime}}\left\lparen y\right\rparen\geq 1-\varepsilon
.
Let
S\coloneqq\operatorname{supp}(x)
,
S^{\prime}\coloneqq\operatorname{supp}(x^{\prime})
, and
T\coloneqq\operatorname{supp}(y)
.
Let
G\coloneqq\mathsf{Kb}(S)
and
G^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathsf{Kb}(S^{\prime})
.
Then
1_{T}=y
and
\mathsf{val}_{\mathsf{Kb}(S)}\left\lparen 1_{T}\right\rparen\geq 1-\varepsilon
and
\mathsf{val}_{\mathsf{Kb}(S^{\prime})}\left\lparen 1_{T}\right\rparen\geq 1-\varepsilon
.
We apply
Lemma

6.9
to deduce that (i) either
d(x,y)\leq 2\varepsilon
or
d(x,y)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
and (ii) either
d(x^{\prime},y)\leq 2\varepsilon
or
d(x^{\prime},y)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
.
Thus,
y\in\mathsf{Ball}^{2\varepsilon}_{\pm}(x)
and
y\in\mathsf{Ball}^{2\varepsilon}_{\pm}(x^{\prime})
, contradicting the third guarantee of
Lemma

6.3
.

∎

6.2
Solution to the combinatorial problem

We recall two useful simple properties of the binary entropy function: (i)
\mathsf{H}(\delta)
is strictly increasing over the interval
[0,1/2]
and (ii)
\mathsf{H}(1/2)=1
(so
\mathsf{H}(\delta)<1
for
\delta\in[0,1/2)
).

Proof of
Lemma

6.3
.

Let
T
be a size parameter to be chosen later. We claim that a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
of
T
independent and uniformly random strings fulfills the desiderata, even if
T
is exponentially large.

Firstly, we observe that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(x)|\leq K\coloneqq 2^{n\mathsf{H}(\delta)}
, where
\mathsf{H}(\delta)=-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
is the binary entropy function.
This follows from a simple counting argument:
|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(x)|=|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(0)|
by translation symmetry;
|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(0)|
is the number of
n
-binary strings of fractional Hamming weight
\leq\delta
.
Thus, we have by
Lemma

2.1
(and increasingness of
\mathsf{H}(\cdot)
) that

|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(0)|=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\delta n\rfloor}\binom{n}{i}\leq 2^{n\mathsf{H}(\lfloor\delta n\rfloor/n)}\leq 2^{n\mathsf{H}(\delta)}=K

since
\delta<1/2
.

Now we have immediately that
|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}(x)|\leq|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(x)|+|\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}(1-x)|=2K
(indeed, this will be an equality since
\delta<1/2
).

Next, we consider a fixed
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and uniformly random
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
: By the previous paragraph and symmetry,
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}}[y\in\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}})]=\Pr[{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}(y)]=\frac{2K}{2^{n}}
.

Now consider fixed
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
T
i.i.d. uniform strings
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
By independence and a union bound,

\Pr_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}}[\exists i\neq j\in[T]:y\in\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cap\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})]\leq\left\lparen\frac{2TK}{2^{n}}\right\rparen^{2}.

Finally, consider taking a union bound over all
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
:
We deduce

\Pr_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}}[\exists y\in\{0,1\}^{n},i\neq j\in[T]:y\in\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cap\mathsf{Ball}^{\delta}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})]\leq\frac{(2TK)^{2}}{2^{n}}.

This probability is strictly less than
1
if
T^{2}<\frac{2^{n}}{(2K)^{2}}=2^{n-2(1+n\mathsf{H}(\delta))}=2^{-2+(1-2\mathsf{H}(\delta))n}
,
i.e., if
T<2^{-1+(1-2\mathsf{H}(\delta))n/2}
.
As long as
\delta
is sufficiently small,
(1-2\mathsf{H}(\delta))/2>0
(i.e.,
\mathsf{H}(\delta)<1/2
) and so
T
can therefore be exponentially large.

(We can also take a union bound over
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}
to guarantee that
w({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\in[1/3,2/3]
for each
i\in[T]
.
This happens except w.p.
2^{\Omega(-n)}
where the constant does not depend on
\delta
, and so for sufficiently small
\delta
we can do a union bound.)
∎

7
Deterministic lower bound for maximum cut value (
Theorem

1.3
)

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.3
, establishing an

\Omega(n\log n/\varepsilon^{2})
space lower bound for deterministic streaming
algorithms that output a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximation to the
value
of
the maximum cut.

Our proof follows the same high-level strategy as the randomized lower bound
from
Section

4
, but differs in two important ways:
(i) we work with deterministic algorithms, and
(ii) we assume access to
value
queries instead of cut queries.

7.1
Constructing the hard family

7.1
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta>0
, there exists
C_{\mathrm{size}}>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
,
there is a family
\mathcal{F}
of bipartite
k
-regular graphs on the same vertex-set
L\sqcup R
with
|L|=|R|=k
such that

1.

Bipartite regularity:
Every
G\in\mathcal{F}
is a bipartite
k
-regular graph
L\sqcup R
.

2.

Mild differences between graphs:

For every
G_{1}\neq G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
,
|E(G_{1})\triangle E(G_{2})|\geq\eta nk
.

3.

Size:
\log_{2}|\mathcal{F}|\geq C_{\mathrm{size}}nk\log n
.

This can be proved similarly to
Lemma

4.11
, by considering the distribution

\mathcal{G}(n,k)
corresponding to the union
of
k
independent uniformly random perfect bipartite matchings on
L\sqcup R
,
where
L
and
R
are two fixed (disjoint) vertex sets of size
n
.

7.2
The augmentation gadget for maximum cuts

Let
A
be a graph on vertex set
V
with
|V|=N
, and let
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{V}
.
We define an augmentation gadget that encodes the cut value
\mathsf{val}_{A}\left\lparen x\right\rparen
into the
maximum cut value of a larger graph.
Specifically, for any
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{V}
, we create a new graph on vertex-set
V\sqcup\{s_{+1},s_{-1}\}
,
where
s_{+1},s_{-1}
are two new “sink” vertices.
Specifically,
H_{x}
has an edge between
s_{+1}
and
s_{-1}
with weight
5000Nk
,
and then, for every
w\in L\sqcup R
, an edge between
s_{-x_{w}}
and
w
with weight
100k
.

7.2
.

For every
k
-regular graph
A
on vertex-set
V
with
|V|=N
and cut assignment
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{V}
, we have:

\mathsf{opt}_{A\cup H_{x}}=5000Nk+100Nk+\mathsf{val}_{A}\left\lparen x\right\rparen.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathsf{opt}_{A\cup H_{x}}\geq 5000Nk+100k\cdot N+\mathsf{val}_{A}\left\lparen x\right\rparen
.
Specifically, consider the cut
x^{*}\in\{\pm 1\}^{V\sqcup\{s_{\pm 1}\}}
which extends
x
via
x^{\prime}(s_{+1})=+1,x^{\prime}(s_{-1})=-1
.
By construction,
x^{*}
cuts the sink-sink edge (with weight
5000Nk
).
Furthermore, it cuts the edges between
s_{-x_{w}}
and
w
for every
w\in V
,
since
x^{*}_{s_{-x_{w}}}=-x_{w}
and
x^{*}_{w}=x_{w}
.
This adds to the value
100k\cdot N
.
Finally, the only other edges cut are those which
x
also cuts in
A
, which is exactly
\mathsf{val}_{A}\left\lparen x\right\rparen
.

We now show that
x^{*}
is actually an optimum cut in
A\cup H_{x}
.
Suppose this is not true, and that
\mathsf{val}_{A\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen x^{\prime}\right\rparen>\mathsf{val}_{A\cup H_{x}}\left\lparen x^{*}\right\rparen
for some cut
x^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{V\sqcup\{s_{\pm 1}\}}
.
We proceed in cases to get a contradiction.

•

First suppose that
x^{\prime}_{s_{+1}}=x^{\prime}_{s_{-1}}
,
so that the sink-sink edge is not cut.
Consider flipping the assignment of
s_{+1}
.
By construction, the sink-sink edge is now cut, adding
5000Nk
to the cut value.
At the same time, it can decrease the cut value by at most
100k\cdot N
,
since this is the maximum total weight of edges connected to
s_{+1}
.
Since we have
5000Nk-100Nk>0
, it must be that
x^{\prime}_{s_{+1}}\neq x^{\prime}_{s_{-1}}
.
WLOG, we have
x^{\prime}_{s_{+1}}=+1
and
x^{\prime}_{s_{-1}}=-1
.

•

Now suppose that for some
w\in V
,
we have
x^{\prime}_{w}\neq x_{w}
(so that the edge between
s_{-x_{w}}
and
w
is not cut).
If we flip the assignment of
w
,
we increase the cut value by
100k
(the weight of the edge between
s_{-x_{w}}
and
w
),
and can decrease it by at most
k
(the weight of all other edges incident to
w
, since
A
is a
k
-regular graph).
Hence, we always have
x^{\prime}_{w}=x_{w}
, as desired.
∎

7.3
Reducing graph distinguishing to value separation

7.3
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be two
k
-regular bipartite matchings on
L\sqcup R
.
If
A_{1},A_{2}
differ in
\eta nk
edges, then there exists a cut
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{L\sqcup R}
such that
|\mathsf{val}_{A_{1}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen-\mathsf{val}_{A_{2}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen|>\Omega(\eta n\sqrt{k})
.

Proof.

Let
D\coloneqq E(A_{1})\triangle E(A_{2})
and
m\coloneqq|D|
.
Define an
n\times n
signed matrix
B
(rows indexed by
L
, columns by
R
) by

B_{uv}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }(u,v)\in E(A_{1})\setminus E(A_{2}),\\
-1&\text{if }(u,v)\in E(A_{2})\setminus E(A_{1}),\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

View a cut
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{L\sqcup R}
as a pair

(x\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
,
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{R})
.
Using the identity
\mathbf{1}[s_{u}t_{v}=-1]=(1-s_{u}t_{v})/2
and the fact that

|E(A_{1})|=|E(A_{2})|=nk
, we have

\mathsf{val}_{A_{1}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen-\mathsf{val}_{A_{2}}\left\lparen z\right\rparen=-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}By.

Hence it suffices to find
x,y
such that
|x^{\top}By|=\Omega(n\sqrt{k})
.

Pick
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}
uniformly at random.
For each
u\in L
, let

{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{u}\coloneqq(B{\boldsymbol{y}})_{u}=\sum_{v\in R}B_{uv}\,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{v}.

Let
d_{u}\coloneqq\sum_{v}B_{uv}^{2}
, the number of nonzeros in row
u
.
Then
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{u}
is a Rademacher sum with coefficient
\ell_{2}
-norm
\sqrt{d_{u}}
.
By the Khintchine inequality for
p=1
,

\mathbb{E}_{t}[|{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{u}|]\geq c\,\sqrt{d_{u}}

for an absolute constant
c>0
.
Summing over all
u\in L
and using linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\|B{\boldsymbol{t}}\|_{1}=\sum_{u}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{t}}|{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{u}|\geq c\sum_{u}\sqrt{d_{u}}.

Therefore there exists
y^{\star}\in\{\pm 1\}^{R}
such that

\|Bt^{\star}\|_{1}\geq c\sum_{u}\sqrt{d_{u}}.

Since each left vertex has degree
k
in each of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, we have

d_{u}\leq 2k
for all
u
.
Using
d_{u}\leq\sqrt{2k}\sqrt{d_{u}}
and summing over
u
gives
m=\sum_{u}d_{u}\leq\sqrt{2k}\sum_{u}\sqrt{d_{u}}
,
and hence

\sum_{u}\sqrt{d_{u}}\geq\frac{m}{\sqrt{2k}}
.
Combining with the previous bound,

\|By^{\star}\|_{1}\geq\frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}\,\frac{m}{\sqrt{k}}.

Now define
x^{\star}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
coordinatewise by

x_{u}^{\star}\coloneqq\mathrm{sign}((By^{\star})_{u})
.
Then

x^{\star\top}By^{\star}=\sum_{u}x_{u}^{\star}(By^{\star})_{u}=\sum_{u}|(By^{\star})_{u}|=\|By^{\star}\|_{1}.

Thus

|x^{\star\top}By^{\star}|\geq\frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}\,\frac{m}{\sqrt{k}}.\qed

7.4
Completing the lower bound

The lower bound follows from
Lemmas

7.3
,
7.1
and
7.2
and setting
k\coloneqq 1/\varepsilon^{2}
.
Specifically, every candidate pair of graphs
G_{1}\neq G_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
differs in
\Omega(n/\varepsilon^{2})
edges,
and therefore by
Lemma

7.3
, there is some cut
z
on which
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have maximum cuts differing in
\Omega(n/\varepsilon)
by weight.
This discrepancy can be detected via a single application of an
\varepsilon
-approximate maximum cut oracle.

A.1
Expander graphs

An
(N,d,\lambda)
-graph
is a
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices in which the absolute value of every nontrivial eigenvalue in the normalized Laplacian is at most
\lambda
.

A.1
Strongly explicit expander construction,
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
.

For any prime
p\equiv 1\mod 4
and every sufficiently large
N
there is a strongly explicit construction of an
(N,d,\lambda)
-graph where
d=p+2
and
\lambda\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{d-1}/d+o(1)
, and the
o(1)
term tends to zero as

tends to infinity.
(Strongly explicit means that the adjacency list of any vertex can be produced in time
\operatorname{poly}\log N
.)

A.2
Bertrand’s postulate for primes equivalent to
1
mod
4
,
[
17
]
.

For every
n\geq 7
, there exists a prime
p\in[n,2n]
such that
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

A.3
Chernoff bound for expander walks,
[
64
, Theorem 4.22]
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph and let
f:V\to[0,1]
be any function.
Consider a random walk
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t}
in
G
from a random start vertex
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(V)
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\Pr\left[\left\lvert\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}f({\boldsymbol{w}}_{i})-\mu(f)\right\rvert\geq\lambda(G)+\varepsilon\right]\leq 2\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}t)),

where
\mu(f)\coloneqq\Exp_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(V)}[f({\boldsymbol{v}})]

on a uniformly random vertex in

.

A.2
Permutation-Index
communication problem

A.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In the

\pi:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
and Bob receives a pair
(x,i)
for

[

Appendix B
Alternate sampling algorithm for dense streams

We present our algorithm
subsampling-dense-streams
(

) which we will use to provide alternate algorithms for all CSPs (including

),
Densest-Subgraph
,

, and

\operatorname{polylog}n

B.1
Sampling algorithm for dense streams.

For every
t,N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda>0
, there exists a randomized insertion-only streaming algorithm which, given a stream of elements
\mathcal{M}\subseteq[N]
with
|\mathcal{M}|\geq\alpha N
, outputs a sketch
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
in
O\left(t\log(1/\lambda)+\log N\right)
bits of space and

f:[N]\to[0,1]
and
\varepsilon>0
,
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
can be used to produce an estimate
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(f)\in[0,1]
for

\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(f)\coloneqq\Exp_{w\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{M})}f(w)
s.t.

\Pr_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\left[|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(f)-\mu_{\mathcal{M}}(f)|\geq\varepsilon+\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}\right]\leq 2\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}t)).

(
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(f)
is a
deterministic
function of
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
.)

Algorithm 1

subsampling-dense-streams (
N,t,\lambda
)

1:

Before the stream:

2:

Take
d
to be the smallest integer that equals
p+2
for a prime
p\equiv 1\mod 4
and meets condition
(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{d-1}/d\geq\lambda
.

3:

Fix a regular expander
\mathcal{E}

C.4
.

There exists an insertion-only streaming algorithm which, given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
on
n

edges and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, outputs

which has
\mathsf{den}_{G}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathsf{maxden}_{G}
in
O(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{4}})
bits of space with probability at least
2/3
.

Proof.

Similar to the algorithm for
Max-Cut
, we apply the
F_{0}
estimation sketch of
[
16
]
to get the result. Again the universe is the set of all possible edges of the input graph
G
.

During the stream, we maintain the exact edge count
m=|E|
using an
O(\log n)
bit counter. We also feed the stream of inserted edges into the
F_{0}
sketch, instantiating it with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}{4}
and failure probability
\delta=\frac{1}{2\cdot 2^{n}}
. The total space used is therefore
O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{4}}\right)
bits.

For any subset
S\subseteq V
, let
\mathcal{D}(x)
denote the set of
\binom{|S|}{2}
edges in the completed induced subgraph on
S
. Then, the number of edges in
G[S]
is
|E\cap\mathcal{D}(x)|
. By inclusion-exclusion, we have

|E\cap\mathcal{D}(x)|=m+|\mathcal{D}(x)|-|E\cup\mathcal{D}(x)|.

After the stream, we look at all
2^{n}
possible subsets
S
of
V
. For each subset, we instantiate a copy of the
F_{0}
’s memory state and feed the offline edges of
\mathcal{D}(x)
into it. The sketch then outputs estimate
\widehat{U}_{S}
. So, we estimate the number of induced edges as
\widehat{E}_{S}=m+|\mathcal{D}(S)|-\widehat{U}_{S}
, and the density as
\widehat{D}_{S}=\widehat{E}_{S}/|S|
.

With probability at least
2/3
, for all
2^{n}
subsets simultaneously, the additive error in estimating
|E\cap\mathcal{D}(S)|
is at most
\varepsilon^{\prime}\binom{n}{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}n^{2}}{8}
.
We restrict our search to subsets
S
where
|S|\geq m/n\geq\alpha n
, as any optimal subgraph must have at least this many vertices (since
\mathsf{maxden}_{G}\geq\mathsf{den}_{G}\left\lparen V\right\rparen=m/n\geq\alpha n
). For these valid subsets, the additive error in the estimated density is at most

D.1
All CSPs

We first extend the upper bound for
Max-Cut
to all other

\textsc{Max-CSP}_{k,q}
is an abstraction of the widely-studied
constraint satisfaction problem
.
Let
k,q\in\mathbb{N}
and let
V
be a finite set of
variables
.
A
constraint
is a pair
(v,\Pi)
, where
v:[k]\to V
is an injective function (i.e.,
v(i)\neq v(j)
for
i\neq j\in[k]
) and
\Pi:[q]^{k}\to\{0,1\}
is a function called the
predicate
.
An
assignment
is a function
x:V\to[q]
, and
x

satisfies
the constraint
(v,\Pi)
if
\Pi(x(v(1)),\ldots,x(v(k)))=1
.
An
instance

\Phi
of
\textsc{Max-CSP}_{k,q}
is a set of constraints, and the
value
of an assignment on an instance is the probability a random constraint is satisfied:

\mathsf{val}_{\Phi}\left\lparen x\right\rparen\coloneqq\Exp_{({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{\Pi}})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Phi)}[{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}(x({\boldsymbol{v}}(1)),\ldots,x({\boldsymbol{v}}(k)))]

and the value of an instance is the maximum value of any assignment:

\mathsf{opt}_{\Phi}\coloneqq\max_{x:V\to[q]}\mathsf{val}_{\Phi}\left\lparen x\right\rparen.

Max-Cut
is a special case of
\textsc{Max-CSP}_{k,q}
where
k=q=2
and every predicate is
\Pi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
with
\Pi(y_{1},y_{2})=1
iff
y_{1}\neq y_{2}
.
Other problems captured by
\textsc{Max-CSP}_{k,q}
include maximum directed cut, maximum unique games, and many others.

D.1
.

Let
k,q\in\mathbb{N}
.

\Phi

n

. The first sketch processes the insertions to
\mathcal{A}
, the second processes
\mathcal{B}
, and the third processes both (representing
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
). The total space required is
O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\log N\right)
.

With probability at least
2/3
, all three sketches succeed, providing estimates
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}}
,
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{B}}
, and
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}
with relative error at most
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. We estimate the similarity as

\frac{\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}}+\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{B}}-\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}}{\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}}.

The absolute additive error in the numerator is bounded by
\varepsilon^{\prime}(|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|+|\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}|)\leq 3\varepsilon^{\prime}|\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}|
. Since the denominator satisfies
\widehat{F}_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}\geq(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})|\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}|
, the additive error of the ratio is at most
\frac{3\varepsilon^{\prime}}{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\leq 4\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon
.
∎

D.4

For

Now, we prove the following useful lemma.

D.5
.

Let
N
be prime and let
h_{c,d}:[N]\to[N]
be the random hash function in
Lemma

D.4
.
For every set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[N]
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
, for the random variable
X_{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\left\lvert\left\{w\in\mathcal{S}:h_{c,d}(w)\leq t\right\}\right\rvert
, we have:

\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{|X|t}{N}\text{ and }\operatorname{Var}X_{\mathcal{S}}\leq\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}.

Proof.

We have:

X_{\mathcal{S}}=\left\lvert\left\{w\in\mathcal{S}:h_{c,d}(w)\leq t\right\}\right\rvert=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{S}}1[h_{c,d}(w)\leq t].

Therefore by linearity of expectation and one-wise independence,

\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr[h_{c,d}(w)\leq t]=\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\cdot t}{N}.

Further,

\displaystyle\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr[h_{c,d}(w_{1})\leq t\wedge h_{c,d}(w_{2})\leq t]

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr[h_{c,d}(w_{1})\leq t\wedge h_{c,d}(w_{2})\leq t]+\sum_{w\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr[h_{c,d}(w)\leq t]

\displaystyle=\binom{|\mathcal{S}|}{2}\cdot\frac{t(t-1)}{N(N-1)}+\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\cdot t}{N}

by two-wise independence, and so

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}X_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}-\left\lparen\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{S}|(|\mathcal{S}|-1)\cdot t(t-1)}{N(N-1)}+\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\cdot t}{N}-\left\lparen\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\cdot t}{N}\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\cdot t}{N}

\displaystyle=\Exp X_{\mathcal{S}}.

(Note in particular that
\frac{|\mathcal{S}|-1}{N}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{N}
since
|\mathcal{S}|\leq N
.)
∎
