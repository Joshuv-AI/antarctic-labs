Epidemiological shifts and clinical characteristics of childhood Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia in Beijing before, during, and after public health interventions (2017–2025)
Authors: Jing Qian, Mengyun Liu, Chunmei Zhu, Yajuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically explore the impact of public health intervention measures (including strict non-pharmaceutical interventions and subsequent relaxation) on the epidemiological trends and clinical characteristics of childhood Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP) pneumonia in Beijing, and provide evidence for precise prevention, control, diagnosis, and treatment.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 331 hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed CPP pneumonia (January 2017–September 2025) from the Capital Center for Children’s Health. Patients were stratified into three groups based on the intensity of population-level public health non-pharmaceutical interventions(NPIs) implemented in Beijing. Demographic data and clinical characteristics were collected.ResultsAmong the 331 patients (male: female ratio = 1.75:1), a significant surge was observed from January to September 2025 in the post-intervention group, which accounted for 7.91% (133 patients) of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) cases. The primary affected age group shifted from 0 to 3 years (60.4% in the pre-intervention group; 75.6% in the during-intervention group) to 6–18 years (90.1% in the post-intervention group; H = 172.24, P < 0.001). Compared to the pre-intervention group, the post-intervention group had significantly higher rates of co-infection (45.4% vs. 6.7%; χ²=12.8, P < 0.001), consolidation detected by computed tomography (56.6% vs. 26.1%; χ²=56.2, P < 0.001), subpleural masses (34.9% vs. 13.4%; χ²=89.4, P < 0.001), and bronchoscopy utilization (34.9% vs. 3.7%; χ²=85.3, P < 0.001). Markers of inflammation, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), and myocardial injury markers, such as creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), were also significantly elevated (P < 0.05). The poor response rate to macrolides increased from 3.7% in the pre-intervention group to 34.9% in the post-intervention group (χ²=19.8, P < 0.001).ConclusionsPublic health interventions caused a “suppression-rebound” effect in childhood Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP) pneumonia. This effect led to a shift in incidence toward school-aged children in Beijing. Following this rebound, post-intervention cases have shown more severe imaging findings and higher rates of macrolide unresponsiveness among CP pneumonia cases, which indicates the need for targeted school-based prevention and optimized antibiotic treatment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13779-5.

Introduction

Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia (CPP) is an important cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). With a global distribution, it poses a health threat to all age groups. The pathogen has unique biological characteristics; it is primarily transmitted through respiratory droplets, facilitating outbreaks in crowded places such as schools and families [1, 2]. Non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs), such as mask-wearing and social distancing, implemented during the global COVID-19 pandemic, significantly suppressed the transmission of respiratory pathogens. As a result, studies from Europe, South Korea, and other regions reported a near-disappearance of CPP infections during this period [2–4]. Subsequently, the relaxation of these measures and the resumption of social gatherings have led to a rebound in CPP infections in many countries, with observed shifts in age distribution and clinical severity [5–8]. As a core Chinese city with a dense and mobile population, Beijing represents a typical epidemiological pattern of childhood CPP infections. Previous studies, predominantly focused on pre-pandemic or short-term pandemic data, lack a comprehensive comparative analysis spanning the entire pandemic timeline [9–11]. This study analyzed childhood CPP cases in Beijing from January 2017 to September 2025, systematically exploring the impact of intervention measures on epidemiological trends and clinical characteristics. The findings aim to inform precise strategies for the prevention, control, diagnosis, and treatment of CPP in the post-pandemic era.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University, Capital Institute of Pediatrics—a tertiary pediatric referral center in Beijing, China. A total of 331 hospitalized children diagnosed with CAP and laboratory-confirmed CP infection were enrolled between January 2017 and September 2025. CP pneumonia (CPP) patients were included if meeting the following criteria: (1) aged < 18 years and presenting acute respiratory infection symptoms (cough, fever); (2) computed tomography (CT) confirmed CAP; (3) etiological criteria: serum CP-IgM antibody titer ≥ 1:16 (determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, ELISA), or positive CP nucleic acid in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), throat swabs, or sputum [12–14]; (4) complete clinical data (symptoms, laboratory indicators, imaging, and treatment follow-up).Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) Children with severe underlying diseases (e.g., hematological malignancies, severe immunodeficiency) that affect clinical diagnosis; (2) Children with incomplete key clinical data that prevents accurate determination of diagnosis and clinical status, including missing chest CT images and incomplete laboratory markers. (Fig. 1).

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Capital Institute of Pediatrics (No. SHERLL2025045). All participants were under 18 years of age, and written informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. The children’s parents have also informed and consented to the pubication of the study results. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Grouping classification

Participants were stratified into three groups according to the intensity of public health intervention measures implemented in Beijing. The stratification of non-pharmaceutical interventions(NPIs) intensity was based on the official public health announcements of the Beijing Municipal Health Commission, with key judgment indicators including mask-wearing requirements (mandatory vs. recommended vs. no requirement), social distancing policies (1-meter distance vs. no restriction), school operation status (closure vs. online teaching vs. in-person teaching), and gathering restrictions (limited number vs. no restriction).This stratification aimed to explore the impact of intervention changes on Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP) pneumonia. The groups were defined as follows: (1) Pre-intervention Group (2017–2019): no targeted respiratory pathogen interventions were implemented; (2) During-intervention Group (2020–2022): strict NPIs were applied in accordance with the guidelines of the National Health Commission of China, including mask-wearing, social distancing, and school closures; (3) Post-intervention Group (2023–September 2025): all NPIs have been discontinued per the notice of the Beijing Municipal Health Commission.

Bronchoscopy indications

The procedure was performed according to the Guideline of Pediatric Flexible Bronchoscopy in China (2018) [15]. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collection was performed by experienced physicians under general anesthesia using a flexible bronchoscope, which was inserted through the nose or mouth to reach the affected lung lobe. Sterile normal saline at 37 °C was instilled at a dose of 3–5 ml/kg body weight, followed by suction at a negative pressure of 10 to 15 kPa. The BALF recovery rate is ≥ 50%; all BALF samples are centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute for 10 min at 4 °C within 30 min after collection. The precipitate is used for nucleic acid extraction and PCR testing; since there is no obvious mucus aggregation in the collected samples, no liquefaction treatment is performed; the bronchoscope is advanced under direct vision to avoid contact with the upper respiratory tract mucosa. The sampling catheter is placed inside the affected lung lobe for lavage and suction according to the lung imaging.

Laboratory test

The core Pathogen detection for this study was performed by multiplex respiratory pathogens polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the “13 Respiratory Pathogens Multiplex Detection Kit” (Wuhan Zhongzhi Gene Technology Co., Ltd.). The detectable pathogens included Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, influenza A virus, influenza B virus, parainfluenza virus, human metapneumovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human rhinovirus, human bocavirus, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, adenovirus. Following the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, in accordance with the national public health testing requirements for hospitalized patients, all children admitted to our hospital from January 2020 onwards were additionally tested for SARS-CoV-2 using a dedicated detection kit:2019-nCoV Nucleic Acid Detection Kit(Fluorescent PCR Method, Shanghai BioGerm Medical Technology Co.Ltd.). This SARS-CoV-2 test was an independent detection procedure separate from the 13-pathogen multiplex kit, rather than an integrated component of the multiplex kit, rather than an integrated component of the multiplex assay. For samples collected from January 2017 to December 2019(pre-pandemic), no SARS-CoV-2 testing was performed as the pathogen had not been identified at that time. The 13-pathogen multiplex kit remained consistent across the entire study period(2017–2025) for detecting non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory pathogens, ensuring methodological uniformity for the core etiological detection.Negative and positive control samples were included during detection, and all procedures were performed strictly in accordance with the kit instructions to ensure result accuracy. Additionally, serum Chlamydia pneumoniae-specific IgM antibodies of all children were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Observation indicators

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system by trained researchers following a rigorous protocol. Double-checking procedures were implemented for all entries to ensure data accuracy and completeness. The clinical data included demographic information, clinical symptoms, laboratory results, chest imaging data, bronchoscopy findings, and treatment procedures. Two radiologists assessed pulmonary infiltrations based on chest CT. They classified lesions as consolidation, subpleural mass-like shadows, patchy shadows, spherical pneumonia, or ground-glass opacities.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Count data (n,%) were analyzed using the Chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test (for small sample sizes). Normally distributed measurement data (mean ± SD) were analyzed using the t-test (two groups) or analysis of variance (ANOVA, three groups). Non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as the median (P25, P75), and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while comparisons among three groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 331 patients were enrolled in this study, comprising 211 males and 120 females, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1.75:1. Gender distribution was balanced across groups, with no significant differences (χ²=1.59, P = 0.451). There was no significant difference in the detection methods of chlamydia pneumonia among the three groups (χ²=1.21, P = 0.546) (Table 1). From 2017 to September 2025 in Beijing, the number of CPP cases and their proportion among hospitalized CAP cases showed fluctuating trends. The number of cases was 69 in 2017; declined to 4 in 2022, and then rose to 133 cases in the first nine months of 2025. Regarding the proportion, the ratio of CPP cases to hospitalized CAP cases(CP/CAP) was 3.07% in 2017, decreased annually to 0.25% in 2022, rose slightly to 0.50% in 2024, and sharply increased to 7.91% in the first nine months of 2025 (Fig. 1). The age distribution of patients with CPP differed significantly among the three intervention groups(H = 172.24, P < 0.001). In the pre-intervention and the during-intervention groups, the majority of participants were infants (aged 0–3 years), accounting for 60.4% and 75.6% respectively. In contrast, school-age children (aged 6–12 years) and adolescents (aged 12–18 years) became the main groups during the post-intervention period, accounting for as much as 90.1% (Fig. 1).

Significant differences in seasonal distribution were observed among the three groups (χ²=59.324, P < 0.001). Spring (March–May) accounted for 35.0% (116/331) of total cases. Of these, 82 cases (53.9%) were from the post-intervention group, which was much higher than the pre-intervention group 24 cases (17.9%), and the during-intervention group 10 cases (22.2%). Analysis showed that post-intervention spring cases were mainly children and adolescents aged 6–18 years (77/82; 93.9%), while pre- and during-intervention spring cases were predominantly 0–3 year olds (75.0% and 90.0%, respectively) (Fig. 2). No similar age concentration was observed in summer, autumn, and winter.

There were no significant differences in clinical symptoms among the three groups of patients (all P > 0.05), including fever, cough, chest pain, and wheeze. Bacterial co-infections were predominantly due to Streptococcus pneumoniae (32/59, 54.2%) and Haemophilus influenzae (18/59, 30.5%). Viral co-infections were chiefly caused by respiratory syncytial virus (10/27, 37.0%) and influenza virus (8/27, 29.6%). Additionally, there was a significant difference in the co-infection rate among the three groups(χ²=12.8, P < 0.001), while no significant differences were observed in the infection spectrum(P > 0.05) (Table 1). Laboratory indicators differed significantly between the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods. PLT and CRP were significantly higher in the post-intervention group. CKMB was elevated in the post-intervention group (Z=-2.878, P = 0.004), indicating increased myocardial injury risk. There were no significant differences observed in WBC, NC, Hb, or liver enzymes (ALT and AST) and kidney function (Cr) (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

CT consolidation (χ²=56.2, P < 0.001) and subpleural mass (χ²=89.4, P < 0.001) increased sharply in the post-intervention group, indicating more severe lung involvement. These results were consistent with the presence of subpleural mass-like shadows, which are characteristic of CP pneumonia, as reported by Beijing Children’s Hospital [10]. Moreover, the proportions of imaging lesions in the Post-intervention group were as follows: 56.6% (86/152) consolidations, 34.9% (53/152) subpleural masses, 3.9% (6/152) diffuse tree-in-bud signs, 2.6% (4/152) interstitial changes, and 2.0% (3/152) spherical lesions (Fig. 3). The rates of bronchoscopy rose from 3.7% (5/134) in the pre-intervention group to 34.9% (53/152) in the post-intervention group (χ²=85.3, P < 0.001), indicating an increase in more severe cases that required advanced intervention.

All patients received antibiotic treatment, with macrolides (mainly azithromycin) used as the initial therapy in all three groups. Macrolide-unresponsiveness was defined as no improvement in clinical symptoms or imaging after 72 h of treatment. The treatment referred to standard anti-infection therapy with macrolide drugs recommended by Children’s CAP management guidelines (2024 revision) [13]. The rate of macrolide unresponsiveness increased significantly from 3.7% (5/134) in the pre-intervention group to 34.9% (53/152) in the post-intervention group (χ²=19.8, P < 0.001), a trend potentially linked to accumulated CP resistance during intervention. In the post-intervention group, a total of 34.9% (53/152) of patients showed poor clinical and radiological response after macrolide treatment. Among these unresponsive patients, 64.2% (34/53) were switched to alternative antibiotics, including levofloxacin (50.0%, 17/34), moxifloxacin (2.9%, 1/34), doxycycline (35.3%, 12/34), and minocycline (11.8%, 4/34). The remaining 35.8% (19/53) continued macrolide treatment with dosage adjustment due to mild symptoms.

Discussion

This study analyzed cases of childhood Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP) pneumonia at a tertiary pediatric referral center in Beijing from January 2017 to September 2025. It systematically explored the impact of public health intervention measures before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic on the disease’s epidemiological characteristics, clinical manifestations, and treatment responses of the disease. It provided critical evidence-based support for the prevention, control, diagnosis, and treatment of childhood CP pneumonia in the post-intervention era.

This study found that changes in public health intervention measures caused a decrease in case during the strict intervention period and a sharp rebound during the relaxed intervention period in childhood CP pneumonia in Beijing. This trend is consistent with the epidemiological pattern of Mycoplasma pneumoniae across different intervention stages [4, 16]. Notably, this study reveals the age distribution of CP pneumonia patients. Before and during the intervention periods, infants were the main affected group. However, after the intervention, school-age children and adolescents became the dominant group. The spring surge of CP pneumonia after the intervention was driven by school-age children and adolescents (6–18 years) returning to school. Further analysis revealed key factors that influenced the epidemiological trends. First, during the intervention period, infants had limited activity ranges (e.g., reduced outings and avoidance of gatherings), indirectly lowering their risk of CP exposure. Second, after the lifting of interventions, school-age children and adolescents quickly resumed in-person schooling. This group also engaged in more frequent social activities, resulting in significantly higher CP transmission efficiency compared to younger children. Additionally, long-term NPIs may havereduced immunity toCP in school-age populations, which could partly explain the observed increases in inflammatory marker and more severe imaging findings, though further immunologic studies are needed for confirmation [17]. These results are consistent with the trend of “atypical pathogens shifting to adolescents post-COVID-19” reported in Switzerland, Hangzhou, and other regions [3, 18]. This formed a sharp contrast with the pre-intervention pattern, where young children were predominantly affected due to kindergarten crowding [19]. This finding suggests that school-age groups should be prioritized for CP pneumonia prevention and control in the post-pandemic era.

In terms of clinical symptoms, there were no significant differences in core clinical symptoms (e.g., fever, cough, chest pain) among patients during the three intervention phases. This indicates that the basic pathogenic characteristics of CP infection have not changed due to the public health intervention measures. However, the proportion of co-infections increased from 6.7% before the intervention to 45.4% after the intervention, while the spectrum of infections (types of bacteria and viruses) remained unchanged. This suggests that children with CP pneumonia in the post-intervention period are more prone to mixed infections. This may be due to the increased overall transmission activity of respiratory pathogens following the removal of interventions. Therefore, in clinical practice, enhanced etiological screening for children with CP pneumonia in the post-intervention period is necessary to avoid missed diagnoses of co-infections and subsequent delays in treatment.

This study found that the rate of macrolide resistance in children with CP pneumonia in the post-intervention period was significantly higher than that in the pre-intervention period. A resistance rate of 34.9% is significantly higher than that reported in most regions worldwide [5–7]; this trend requires high vigilance. Although CP genotypes and susceptibility tests were not performed in this study. International studies have shown that long-term transmission interruption may lead to pathogen evolution, such as 23 S rRNA gene mutations that cause macrolide resistance [3, 20], which may be one of the mechanisms underlying the increased macrolide unresponsiveness rate in this study.

Laboratory indicators and imaging findings showed a “tendency toward severe disease” in CP pneumonia during the post-intervention period. The poor response rate to macrolides post-intervention in this study may be related to this. Long-term NPIs may have reduced the immunity of school-age populations to CP, potentially weakening the immune system’s ability to recognize and clear the pathogen, making them more prone to excessive inflammatory responses after infection [21]. This is supported by elevated CRP (inflammatory marker) and CK-MB(myocardial injury marker) in post-intervention group [22]. CP pneumonia shows atypical symptoms, and delayed diagnosis caused by limited detection in some primary hospitals and low clinician awareness may worsen the disease [17, 23, 24]. In addition, the extensive lesions (mainly consolidation and subpleural high-density mass-like shadows) and increased bronchoscopy rate after NPIs implementation may be related to the shift in age distribution toward school-age children and adolescents. Adolescents have more mature alveolar structures, stronger local cellular immune responses, and closer apposition of pleura and lung tissue. Thus, subpleural inflammation caused by CP infection tends to aggregate into mass-like high-density shadows (unlike young children, in whom inflammation is more likely to diffuse into patchy shadows). Additionally, the increased co-infection rate after NPIs implementation is another important factor contributing to more severe pulmonary imaging findings.

Study limitations

This was a single-center retrospective study. The samples mainly included hospitalized severe cases, which may overestimate the rates of severe disease and poor macrolide response. This potential selection bias limits the generalizability of the result to outpatients with mild symptoms and the broader pediatric population in Beijing. Further multi-center and prospective studies should be conducted, including outpatients in community health service centers, to improve the extrapolation of the results.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively analyzed 331 cases of childhood CP pneumonia in Beijing from January 2017 to September 2025. It clarified the impact of public health intervention measures on the disease’s epidemiology, clinical features, and treatment response, providing critical evidence for post-intervention management. A trend of suppression during the intervention period, followed by a sharp rebound afterward, was confirmed in childhood CP infection in Beijing. The age distribution shifted to school-age children and adolescents, and clinical features became more severe, including lung consolidation requiring bronchoscopy and an increased risk of macrolide resistance. Clinicians should adjust diagnostic strategies in a targeted manner, and genotyping monitoring should be conducted to support long-term prevention in the post-pandemic era.
