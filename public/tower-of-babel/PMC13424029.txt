GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and the Ocular Surface: A Narrative Review of Restoration, Remodeling, and Clinical Implications
Authors: Henry Bair, Molly Orlick, Zeba A Syed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have become a recurring topic in ophthalmology, yet most reviews emphasize posterior segment and neuro-ophthalmic disease while giving comparatively little attention to the ocular surface. In this review, we frame the relevant target not as isolated anterior structures but as a proposed metabolic surface unit composed of the lacrimal gland, tear film, conjunctiva, meibomian glands, corneal epithelium, corneal nerves, and the periocular tissues that support lid–globe function, and we argue that current evidence is best understood as restoration versus remodeling. Preclinical work provides the clearest biologic rationale: liraglutide studies have reported reduced lacrimal gland inflammation and fibrosis, and improved tear secretion, corneal epithelial migration, and nerve regeneration, while a semaglutide study in aged mice suggests lacrimal structural rescue through attenuation of senescence-associated inflammatory, oxidative, and fibrotic programs. Human evidence remains limited and largely observational, with retrospective diabetic cohorts showing lower rates of dry eye disease and superficial keratitis and a small clinical study showing better Schirmer testing and tear breakup time in GLP-1RA users. At the same time, emerging oculoplastic and imaging studies, particularly from obesity and weight loss populations, suggest periocular volume loss, brow descent, and dermatochalasis which may alter lid support, blink mechanics, and tear distribution. For anterior segment clinicians, the practical implication is that GLP-1RA exposure should prompt phenotype-based assessment of both ocular surface status and periocular geometry, although these recommendations remain expert extrapolations rather than guideline-level evidence. Current data are therefore consistent with a dual thesis: GLP-1RAs may be associated with biologic restoration at the lacrimal–corneal–neural axis and with structural remodeling of the periocular scaffold, although direct mechanistic support is presently stronger for the former than for the latter.Keywords: Corneal nerve, Diabetic keratopathy, Dry eye, GLP-1 receptor agonist, Ocular surface disease, Periocular remodeling

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have evolved rapidly from endocrine therapeutics to a recurring subject in ophthalmology. Most recent reviews, however, focus on posterior segment and neuro-ophthalmic disease; diabetic retinopathy, nonarteritic ischemic optic neuropathy, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, and idiopathic intracranial hypertension remain the dominant anchors, with dry eye usually treated as a brief subtopic rather than as a central clinical question [1–3]. A clear exception is the recent review by Kapantais and Tsoutsanis [4], written from an oculoplastics perspective and focused on periorbital, ocular adnexal, and ocular surface changes. This review focuses instead on the ocular surface, addressed to cornea and anterior segment clinicians.

The scope of such a review should be defined functionally rather than anatomically. The relevant unit is what we propose here as a metabolic surface unit: the linked system composed of the lacrimal gland, tear film, conjunctiva and goblet cells, meibomian glands, corneal epithelium, corneal nerves, and the lid and periocular structures that preserve surface integrity. It is used here as a clinical framing construct rather than a fully demonstrated biologic unit, because several named components, especially meibomian gland behavior, tear film lipid dynamics, and human conjunctival endpoints, remain largely unstudied in patients exposed to GLP-1RA. This functional framing also explains why glaucoma is excluded even though the angle and trabecular meshwork are anatomically anterior. The GLP-1RA–glaucoma literature has matured as a separate mechanistic lane centered on aqueous dynamics, intraocular pressure, and retinal ganglion cell or optic-nerve protection, with a dedicated meta-analysis and focused review already available [5, 6]. By contrast, the lens appears briefly, not because it belongs to the same mechanistic story as the ocular surface but because cataract studies involve the same drug class, the same metabolic-aging biology, and the same surgical populations served by anterior segment clinicians.

Diabetes is a particularly relevant setting for this question because it perturbs the ocular surface as a linked system rather than injuring one structure at a time. Tear instability, meibomian gland dysfunction, epithelial fragility, reduced corneal sensitivity, and delayed healing converge into a single clinical phenotype [7–11]. The cornea is central within that phenotype because its sensory nerves regulate tearing, blinking, epithelial maintenance, and wound healing through feedback loops connecting the ocular surface, lacrimal gland, and central nervous system [10].

The central argument of this review is that the ocular surface effect of GLP-1RAs is unlikely to be uniformly beneficial or uniformly adverse. The most clinically useful working model is dual: restoration at the lacrimal–corneal–neural axis [12–14] and remodeling at the level of periocular support tissues [4, 15, 16]. Methods

This narrative review was informed by structured searches of PubMed and Embase from database inception through 8 May 2026, with an updated search performed during revision to capture newly published semaglutide, tirzepatide, anti-obesity medication, and ocular surface literature. We included preclinical studies, observational cohorts, case–control studies, imaging studies, and narrative reviews when they directly informed ocular surface biology, periocular remodeling, or clinically relevant comparator literature. Because GLP-1RAs and related incretin-based agents are now used for both glycemic control and weight management, studies involving patients with type 2 diabetes and studies involving patients with obesity without type 1 or type 2 diabetes were considered separately when possible. Additional articles were identified by screening reference lists of retrieved papers. Because the goal was interpretive synthesis rather than formal evidence grading, selection was purposive rather than systematic, and no risk-of-bias scoring or meta-analysis was performed. Priority was given to studies with direct lacrimal, corneal, tear film, periocular, or lens outcomes and to papers that clarified why glaucoma was mechanistically outside the present scope.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

The Diabetic Ocular Surface as the Restoration Substrate

Diabetic Keratopathy and Corneal Nerves

The diabetic ocular surface is the most relevant substrate in which to evaluate a GLP-1RA effect; diabetic keratopathy is common, clinically important, and still underrecognized. Beyond irritation and fluctuating vision, it encompasses punctate epitheliopathy, recurrent erosions, delayed epithelial healing, reduced corneal sensitivity, neurotrophic change, ulceration, and in advanced cases, scarring or visually significant opacity [7, 10]. These abnormalities also matter perioperatively. A metabolically compromised surface is less forgiving after cataract, refractive, corneal, or adnexal intervention, which makes any drug class that plausibly alters surface homeostasis worth examining in anterior segment practice [17–19].

The cornea is especially informative as both a disease site and a readable output of broader diabetic neurotoxicity. Corneal sensory denervation destabilizes reflex tearing and blink behavior, impairs epithelial renewal, and can push the surface toward neurotrophic disease [10]. At the same time, corneal confocal microscopy has emerged as a biomarker of systemic diabetic peripheral neuropathy, and longitudinal nerve loss may precede or predict broader neuropathic progression [11, 20–22]. That dual role makes the corneal surface unusually useful for asking whether GLP-1RAs have clinically meaningful effects beyond glucose lowering alone.

Meibomian Gland Dysfunction and the Mixed Surface Phenotype

A second reason the diabetic ocular surface is a relevant substrate is that evaporative disease and meibomian gland dysfunction are already part of the baseline phenotype. In type 2 diabetes, tear breakup time, lipid layer integrity, and meibomian gland morphology are often abnormal, and patients with diabetic dry eye can show more severe gland dysfunction than nondiabetic controls [8, 9]. Molecular and clinical studies also point to broader conjunctival-corneal involvement, including altered mucin expression, goblet cell change, epithelial barrier disturbance, and immune activation [23–27]. The practical point is that diabetic surface disease is a mixed phenotype, not a purely aqueous problem, which makes both lacrimal and periocular effects relevant.

This substrate logic is population-dependent. Diabetes provides the clearest biologic setting in which to understand the restoration effect, because corneal nerve loss, epithelial fragility, lacrimal injury, and meibomian gland dysfunction converge into a measurable surface phenotype that GLP-1RAs might plausibly improve. The remodeling effect, by contrast, may depend more on the magnitude and velocity of weight loss than on glycemic status itself, which likely explains why periocular change is more prominent in obesity cohorts treated for weight management than in diabetic cohorts treated for glycemic control [15, 16]. Different halves of the dual model may therefore arise from different clinical substrates.

Biologic Rationale for a GLP-1RA Ocular Surface Effect

Lacrimal Gland Biology

The strongest direct mechanistic effect presently comes from the lacrimal gland. In diabetic mice, Sun et al. [12] identified GLP-1 receptor expression in lacrimal tissue, documented inflammatory infiltration, progressive fibrosis, glandular atrophy, and reduced tear secretion, and then showed that topical liraglutide attenuated inflammation and fibrosis while increasing tear production. This is significant because it provides a plausible route for a drug class effect on aqueous tear supply instead of requiring all ocular surface benefit to be interpreted as a downstream consequence of better systemic metabolism alone. Notably, however, this was a murine type 1 diabetic model, whereas the human surface studies are largely type 2 diabetes cohorts, so whether the same lacrimal inflammatory profile and treatment response translate directly across diabetic phenotypes remains uncertain.

Corneal Neuroepithelial Axis

The corneal neuroepithelial axis provides a second, more surface-specific rationale. Han et al. [13] showed that liraglutide enhanced migration of human corneal epithelial cells under high-glucose conditions, reduced oxidative stress and apoptosis, and promoted trigeminal axonal outgrowth through PI3K/AKT signaling. In diabetic mice, liraglutide also improved corneal fluorescein staining, tear production, corneal sensitivity, and conjunctival goblet cell density. The goblet cell finding is particularly relevant, as it extends the GLP-1RA effect beyond epithelium and nerves into the mucin-producing compartment of the proposed metabolic surface unit. These endpoints correspond directly to the phenotype that anterior segment clinicians recognize as diabetic surface disease: epithelial fragility, neurosensory dysfunction, tear-film instability, and impaired maintenance of the surface.

Semaglutide and Age-Related Surface Restoration

A newer semaglutide study extends the preclinical restoration signal beyond liraglutide and diabetes-specific models. In naturally aged mice, Zuo et al. [14] reported that semaglutide reduced dry eye-related phenotypes, improved corneal fluorescein staining, and preserved lacrimal gland structural integrity. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses suggested attenuation of acinar cell stress, fibroblast profibrotic programs, immune activation, and senescence-associated transcriptional signatures. This study is important because semaglutide is central to contemporary diabetes and obesity practice, but its ocular surface evidence remains preclinical and lacrimal-focused rather than human and corneal-phenotyped.

Limits to Mechanistic Inference

Taken together, the lacrimal and corneal studies suggest a biologic model of restoration, not merely an anti-inflammatory effect. Better tear secretion, less lacrimal fibrosis, improved epithelial migration, and enhanced nerve recovery are consistent with coordinated improvement across the surface axis, not isolated change in one compartment [12, 13]. That said, mechanistic inference still requires restraint. In validated human tissue work, GLP-1 receptor expression in the normal eye was low and limited to a small fraction of neurons in the ganglion cell layer [28]. That study did not survey every anterior ocular tissue, but it is a useful corrective against overconfident claims of abundant direct ocular receptor expression. Possible explanations for the discrepancy include diabetic-state upregulation of the receptor, functionally meaningful low-level expression, or benefits mediated partly through indirect systemic pathways, but current human data do not distinguish among these possibilities.

A second limit is intraclass heterogeneity. Liraglutide still supplies most of the diabetic corneal and lacrimal mechanistic evidence, whereas semaglutide is now central to contemporary obesity and diabetes practice and increasingly drives the weight loss-related questions seen in clinic. The semaglutide aged-mouse lacrimal study broadens preclinical support beyond liraglutide, but it does not solve the human translational problem: most clinical cohorts define exposure at the GLP-1RA class level and do not distinguish semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, or related incretin therapies with sufficient granularity for agent-specific ocular surface conclusions.

Human Evidence for Ocular Surface Effects

Database Studies, Active Comparators, and Tear Physiology

Population-level studies provide the main human evidence for ocular surface effects. Fan et al. [29] found that GLP-1RA use in patients with type 2 diabetes was associated with lower subsequent incidence of dry eye disease and superficial keratitis, while Pan et al. [30] found that GLP-1 agonists were among several glucose-lowering therapies associated with lower dry eye risk compared with metformin monotherapy. However, Su et al. [31] provide an important active comparator caveat: among adults with type 2 diabetes newly receiving sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors or GLP-1RAs, SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with lower dry eye incidence than GLP-1RAs. This does not refute a GLP-1RA surface effect, but it shows that the association is class-relative rather than absolute.

In a later cohort, Sun et al. [32] found that broader ocular surface disease in type 2 diabetes tracked with higher mean HbA1c, diabetic neuropathy, preexisting dry eye, and prior ocular procedures, whereas antihyperglycemic agents, particularly SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1RAs, appeared protective. Together, these studies suggest that the observed ocular surface effect probably reflects both drug-class biology and improvement of the underlying metabolic milieu.

The only direct clinical study of tear physiology to date is smaller but particularly valuable because it moves beyond diagnosis codes. In a single-center retrospective case–control study, Ottonelli et al. [33] compared 21 patients using GLP-1RAs with 14 nonusers with type 2 diabetes and found significantly higher Schirmer I values and longer tear breakup time in the GLP-1RA group, whereas symptom scores were numerically lower but not significantly different. Because it measured tear production and tear-film stability directly, the study provides physiologic support for the database literature. However, it remains small and retrospective, with differences in concomitant therapies, and cannot distinguish direct ocular effects from systemic metabolic improvement, comparator-drug effects, or selection bias. Taken together, the available human data are associative and do not establish mechanism or causality.

Anti-obesity Pharmacotherapy and Ocular Surface Outcomes in Patients Without Diabetes

The evidence for GLP-1RA among patients with obesity without diabetes is thinner but no longer absent, although it includes related incretin-based anti-obesity therapy rather than GLP-1RA exposure alone. Huang et al. [34] evaluated ocular outcomes among patients with obesity initiating tirzepatide, semaglutide, phentermine/topiramate, naltrexone/bupropion, or phentermine, excluding patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes and recent ocular diagnoses. In the 25,060 matched-pair comparison of tirzepatide versus semaglutide, no significant differences emerged across ocular outcomes. Compared with traditional anti-obesity medications, however, semaglutide and tirzepatide were associated with lower dry eye disease incidence in several comparisons. This study partially addresses the obesity-without-diabetes gap but remains claims-based; it does not provide Schirmer testing, tear breakup time, staining, meibography, esthesiometry, or confocal microscopy.

What the Human Literature Still Lacks

Human phenotyping remains narrow, and this is the central limitation of the field. No prospective human study has yet shown that GLP-1RAs improve corneal nerve density on in vivo confocal microscopy, accelerate epithelial wound healing, reduce recurrent epithelial breakdown, or improve endothelial health as measured by specular microscopy, endothelial cell density, pachymetry, or related anterior segment metrics. Equally important, meibomian gland morphology and function, tear film lipid behavior, and conjunctival goblet cell or mucin endpoints remain essentially unstudied in patients exposed to GLP-1RA, even though they are part of the proposed metabolic surface unit.

The available human literature is also population-skewed. The strongest surface restoration data still come from patients with type 2 diabetes, whereas obesity-without-diabetes evidence is represented mainly by claims-based ocular outcome studies rather than detailed tear film or corneal phenotyping. Huang et al. [34] partially closes this gap by including patients with obesity without type 1 or type 2 diabetes, but it should not be interpreted as proof of ocular surface restoration because the outcomes were diagnosis-code based.

These limitations should temper interpretation. GLP-1RAs may benefit the diabetic ocular surface, and semaglutide may have supportive preclinical lacrimal evidence, but current human data are not granular enough to determine whether the dominant mechanism is direct, systemic, or both, nor to establish whether individual molecules within the class behave similarly. That restraint also motivates the next question: whether the structures supporting the surface may be changing on a different timetable and in a different population.

Periocular Structural Remodeling and Surface Function

That question becomes more complex at the level of the periocular scaffold. In a large TriNetX cohort, Saffari et al. [15] found that GLP-1RA use was associated with brow ptosis in both type 2 diabetes and obesity, while dermatochalasis and higher frequencies of brow ptosis repair, blepharoptosis repair, blepharoplasty, and rhytidectomy were most evident in obesity cohorts. Radiographic data now provide an anatomic correlate. In patients imaged before and after GLP-1RA therapy, Sharma et al. [16] found that superficial midfacial volume fell more than deep volume and that total midfacial volume decreased by roughly 7% for every 10 kg of weight loss. These studies do not prove a class-specific toxic effect on periocular tissue; rapid weight loss is likely part of the mechanism. They do, however, show that GLP-1RA-associated facial change is measurable, patterned, and relevant to the periocular region rather than anecdotal observation alone.

The eyelids and periocular tissues are mechanically necessary for ocular surface function, not incidental to it. Normal lid position, globe support, and blink dynamics contribute to tear distribution, meibomian delivery, tear meniscus stability, and surface protection [4, 35, 36]. In patients with substantial weight reduction, deeper periocular and midfacial volume depletion could plausibly alter globe support and the lid–globe relationship; in more pronounced cases, mild posterior globe displacement or relative enophthalmos is conceivable, although direct evidence for this sequence in GLP-1RA users is still lacking [4, 16].

Direct evidence linking GLP-1RA-associated periocular change to ocular surface dysfunction is not yet available, so the functional argument here is inferential and grounded in adjacent blink and lid malposition literature. Incomplete blinking has been associated with worse dry eye symptoms, poorer tear film stability, more corneal and lid-margin staining, thinner lipid layer, worse meibography, and poorer meibum quality [37–40]. More broadly, eyelid malpositions are well-recognized drivers of ocular surface disease, altering lid apposition, exposure, tear clearance, and the distribution of the tear film across the cornea [35, 36, 41, 42]. It is therefore reasonable to suspect that changes in brow position, upper lid contour, superior sulcus hollowing, or palpebral aperture could change the mechanical environment in which the surface is maintained.

The relationship, however, is not perfectly linear. Studies of ptosis or upper lid surgery suggest that changing lid geometry does not map neatly onto standard dry eye metrics: meibomian gland morphology or topography may shift while short-term symptoms, tear breakup time, or gland-loss measurements remain modestly changed or unchanged [43–46]. The same caution should apply here. Current GLP-1RA studies do not prove that periocular change causes ocular surface symptoms, nor do they quantify blink mechanics, exposure, or tear meniscus change in patients exposed to the drug. Still, the periocular literature provides the main counterweight in this review to the restoration effect seen at the lacrimal–corneal–neural axis. The clinically important point is not that GLP-1RAs are uniformly beneficial or harmful for the anterior segment but that different tissues within the same surface unit may be moving in different directions.

Lens Findings: Adjacent But Secondary

The lens is included briefly, not because it belongs to the same mechanistic story as the ocular surface but because the cataract studies share the drug class, the metabolic aging biology of oxidative stress and glycation, and the surgical populations served by anterior segment clinicians. In adults without diabetes (patients with diabetes were excluded by design) aged 55 years or older who were overweight or obese, Ahuja et al. [47] found lower cataract risk with GLP-1RA use than with other weight loss drugs or no pharmacologic treatment. In adults with type 2 diabetes, Lin et al. [48] likewise found lower age-related cataract risk with GLP-1RA use than with metformin monotherapy, although the protective effect was weaker than that seen with SGLT2 inhibitors and did not translate into lower cataract surgery risk over 3 years. These findings are biologically plausible because diabetic lens injury is tied to oxidative stress, advanced glycation, and lens epithelial dysfunction [49]. Even so, the lens effect remains associative and secondary to the stronger ocular surface argument of this review.

Clinical Implications for Anterior Segment Practitioners

For clinicians, the most useful interpretation is phenotype-first rather than drug-first. It is plausible, though not yet documented in the same prospectively studied patient, that a person taking semaglutide, liraglutide, or another GLP-1RA might show better tear production or tear film stability while simultaneously developing superior sulcus hollowing, brow descent, or altered blink mechanics. That inferential possibility is especially relevant in refractive surgery evaluation after recent substantial weight loss, where small shifts in surface stability or lid geometry can disproportionately affect measurements and symptoms. The history should therefore capture the indication for therapy, duration of use, magnitude and tempo of weight loss, and concurrent antihyperglycemic agents, because obesity cohorts appear to show more overt periocular change and comparator drug classes differ in ocular outcomes [15, 16, 30, 32, 48].

The literature reviewed here also implies a temporal logic, although it has not yet tested it prospectively. During active rapid weight loss, especially in patients using a GLP-1RA for obesity, periocular change may outpace any gradual restoration benefit that depends on cumulative exposure and broader metabolic improvement. The restoration effect inferred from diabetic cohorts and tear physiology studies appears more gradual, whereas the remodeling effect aligns more closely with weight loss magnitude and likely with its velocity [15, 16]. For elective surface-dependent procedures, this suggests caution during the phase of steep ongoing weight loss: lid geometry, blink mechanics, and tear distribution may still be in flux even if tear metrics appear favorable. Once weight stabilizes, periocular anatomy is more likely to reflect a new baseline against which surface measurements can be interpreted. In practical terms, it is not only whether the patient is using a GLP-1RA but where they are in the treatment arc that matters. From a surgical standpoint, the most immediate concern is unstable eye measurements. Patients using GLP-1RAs during active weight loss may have changing eyelid shape, blink behavior, and tear film dynamics, which can affect measurements used for surgical planning. Consequently, elective procedures that depend on stable ocular surface and periocular anatomy, including laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) and other corneal refractive procedures, cataract surgery, eyelid cosmetic correction, blepharoplasty, and ptosis repair, should be approached with caution, and measurements should be treated as provisional during steep ongoing weight loss. When feasible, repeating keratometry, topography or tomography, biometry, tear film assessment, and lid position evaluation after weight and ocular surface findings have stabilized may be prudent.

On examination, findings should be interpreted in the context of treatment phase rather than as static measurements, so it is not enough to document Schirmer testing or tear breakup time alone. Lid position, blink completeness, corneal staining, and meibography should also be considered, and corneal sensitivity should be measured when diabetic keratopathy or neurotrophic disease is suspected, while in vivo confocal microscopy provides a richer assessment of subbasal nerve morphology when available [11]. Unexplained variability in keratometry or symptoms out of proportion to visible staining should prompt closer evaluation of blink quality and lid mechanics. This is particularly relevant before cataract, refractive, ptosis, or blepharoplasty surgery, where untreated surface disease can degrade biometry, keratometry, topography, and postoperative comfort [17–19, 50–52]. A refractive surgery candidate who has recently started a GLP-1RA and lost substantial weight is a concrete example: tear metrics may look improved while lid geometry and blink behavior are becoming less stable.

These recommendations, including any decision to delay elective surgery during active weight loss, should be read as pragmatic extrapolations from limited data rather than as guideline-level directives. The point is narrower and more defensible: the available literature gives anterior segment clinicians a reason to look for both restoration and remodeling instead of assuming a single direction of effect.

Future Directions

The next phase of the literature should move beyond diagnosis code associations and small retrospective case–control studies. Prospective cohorts should pair drug exposure with genuine surface and anterior segment phenotyping: symptom instruments, Schirmer testing, tear breakup time, noninvasive breakup time, osmolarity, tear meniscus metrics, staining, meibography, corneal esthesiometry, epithelial wound healing endpoints, specular microscopy, pachymetry, and in vivo confocal microscopy of the subbasal plexus. Because corneal confocal microscopy can detect early nerve regeneration after systemic or surgical interventions, it may be a useful longitudinal endpoint even before a GLP-1RA-specific nerve effect has been proven [53]. These endpoints should be interpreted within contemporary dry eye diagnostic frameworks, including TFOS DEWS III, which emphasizes tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities [54].

The same cohorts should collect standardized periocular measurements instead of leaving the two halves of the hypothesis in separate literatures. Margin reflex distance 1, brow position, levator function, palpebral fissure height, blink completeness and, where possible, imaging of superficial and deep facial compartments should be measured alongside the surface endpoints. Patients with type 2 diabetes and patients with obesity without type 1 or type 2 diabetes should be enrolled in parallel, and follow-up should extend for at least 12 months to capture both drug exposure and weight loss kinetics. Stratification by individual agent, dose, HbA1c change, weight loss velocity, and comparator therapy, especially SGLT2 inhibitors, is essential [15, 16, 30, 32, 48]. SGLT2 inhibitors should be treated as an important comparator for both ocular surface and periocular remodeling studies because they offer a distinct mechanism and weight loss profile against which to test whether periocular change is receptor-specific or primarily driven by weight loss.

Future work should also reflect what is already known about diabetic surface biology. The relevant disease dimensions are not only symptoms and tear volume but epithelial barrier integrity, goblet cell and mucin biology, immune activation, and nerve morphology across the conjunctival–corneal interface [23–27]. No study has yet examined whether GLP-1RA exposure changes perioperative surface behavior or biometric stability. Surgical cohorts in cataract, refractive, and oculoplastic practices would therefore be particularly informative, as they are the settings in which subtle surface instability becomes clinically consequential fast.

Conclusions

At present, the literature is best read through a working model of an emerging ocular surface phenotype rather than as proof of a fully defined one. Within that model, GLP-1RAs may influence an ocular surface system. Preclinical and early clinical studies point toward restoration at the lacrimal–corneal–neural axis, with evidence of improved tear production, better tear film metrics, epithelial repair, and nerve recovery, whereas emerging oculoplastic and imaging data point toward structural remodeling of the periocular scaffold that supports that same surface. The evidentiary balance is not symmetrical: restoration currently has stronger mechanistic support, whereas remodeling is chiefly observational and may reflect weight loss physiology rather than receptor-specific biology. That duality explains why the same drug class may improve some ocular surface endpoints while raising new questions about lid mechanics, surgical planning, and external appearance. For cornea and anterior segment clinicians, the clinically relevant unit is therefore not the cornea alone but the proposed metabolic surface unit in which the cornea resides.
