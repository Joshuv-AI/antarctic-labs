1.0 Introduction
 Condensation particle counters (CPCs), also known as condensation nucleus counters (CNCs) are
 sophisticated instruments designed to measure the concentration of aerosol particles in the atmosphere.
 These devices are pivotal in environmental monitoring, industrial applications, and scientific research,
 particularly in atmospheric studies. CPCs are vital for understanding the role of aerosols in climate
 systems. Aerosols influence cloud formation, radiative forcing, and atmospheric chemistry. By providing
 accurate measurements of particle concentrations and distributions, CPCs contribute to models that
 predict atmospheric impact and weather patterns (Mei et al. 2021). CPCs operate by enlarging submicron
 particles, including those as small as a few nanometers, to sizes detectable by optical methods. This report
 delves into the mechanisms, importance, and contributions of CPCs to atmospheric research at the
 U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) Aerial Facility (AAF),
 highlighting their role in advancing air quality assessment, pollution control, and atmospheric studies.

2.0 Instrument Description
 CPCs work by exposing aerosol particles to a supersaturated vapor, typically water or alcohol-based. The
 particles act as nucleation centers, leading to the condensation of the vapor into droplets. These droplets
 grow to approximately 10 µm in diameter, making them large enough to be detected and counted using
 optical scattering techniques (Chen and Pui 1995, Stolzenburg and McMurry 1991). The supersaturation
 process is diffusion-limited, ensuring uniform droplet sizes regardless of the original particle composition
 or dispersity. This characteristic allows CPCs to measure total particle concentrations without classifying
 them into size bins, making them ideal for applications requiring high sensitivity and broad detection
 ranges (Mei et al. 2021). Modern CPCs can detect particles as small as sub-3 nm and as large as 3 µm,
 with concentration measurements reaching up to 100,000 particles per cubic centimeter. They provide
 real-time readings with temporal resolutions as fine as in sub-second, enabling precise monitoring of
 dynamic aerosol environments (Kerminen et al. 2018).

 The instruments included in this comparison study represent a range of designs, working fluids, and
 detection capabilities from three manufacturers. A total of nine CPCs were evaluated: three TSI CPC
 3772, two TSI CPC 3789, one TSI CPC 3025, two Magic CPCs from Aerosol Dynamics Inc., and one
 Brechtel advanced Mixing CPC (aMCPC).(Bian et al. 2020, Hering et al. 2019, Kangasluoma and Attoui
 2019, Kangasluoma et al. 2017, Mei et al. 2021)

 The TSI CPC 3772 is a general-purpose, butanol-based CPC with a 50% counting efficiency (D50) cut
 size of approximately 10 nm. It is a robust and widely deployed instrument suitable for routine ambient
 aerosol monitoring. The TSI CPC 3789, also known as the versatile water-based CPC, replaces butanol
 with water as its working fluid and features a tunable D50 cut size ranging from approximately 2 nm to
 7 nm, offering flexibility across a range of measurement applications. The TSI CPC 3025 is a
 well-established ultrafine CPC (UCPC) with a D50 of approximately 3 nm, making it one of the most
 sensitive instruments for detecting freshly nucleated particles; it has long served as a reference standard in
 intercomparison studies.

 The Magic CPC, developed by Aerosol Dynamics Inc., employs a water-based, three-stage growth tube
 design (moderator, initiator, and growth region) that activates particles under gentle supersaturation

 conditions, avoiding the need for butanol. The Brechtel MCPC uses a mixing-type condensation
 approach, combining warm saturated and cool unsaturated airflows to generate supersaturation. Its
 compact, lightweight design and tolerance to varying pressures make it particularly well-suited for aircraft
 deployments and operations at different altitudes.

3.0 Experimental Setup
 The experimental setup used in this intercomparison study is illustrated in Figures 1 and 2. The system
 was designed to deliver a well-characterized, monodisperse aerosol simultaneously to all instruments
 under test, ensuring a fair and consistent comparison of number concentration measurements.

Figure 2. Illustration of the CPC comparison setup.

 3.1 Aerosol Generation and Conditioning
 Polydisperse aerosol particles were generated using an atomizer. The air was “pushed” through the
 atomizer to ensure delivery of polydisperse aerosol to a differential mobility analyzer (DMA) at specific
 flow rate. The output of the atomizer was dried and passed through a diluter before it was directed into the
 DMA, which selected particles of a specific electrical mobility diameter, producing a monodisperse
 aerosol stream. The size-selected aerosol was then passed through a residence chamber to allow the flow
 to stabilize and the particle concentration to become spatially uniform before reaching the instruments.

 The conditioned aerosol exiting the residence chamber was introduced into a common sampling manifold
 (a ¾- inch tubing with multiple pickup points along the sampling line). This manifold was designed to
 evenly distribute the aerosol to all instruments simultaneously, minimizing concentration gradients and
 particle losses across the sampling ports. An additional blower was installed to circulate air through the
 manifold and back to the residence chamber, thereby improving mixing and minimizing time delays in the
 manifold.

 3.2 Reference Measurements
 The aerosol electrometer (TSI 3068B) provided a primary, charge-based measurement of particle number
 concentration, independent of optical detection. This served as an absolute reference against which all
 CPCs were compared. Additionally, the scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS) was used to confirm the
 size and monodispersity of the DMA-selected aerosol throughout the experiment. Note that all the DMAs
 in this study were equipped with the TSI x-ray chargers.

4.2 Leave-One-Out Averaged CPC Comparison for the Same Size
 Particles
In the previous analysis (Section 4.1), all eight CPCs consistently undercounted relative to the aerosol
electrometer (AE), with slopes ranging from 0.736 to 0.860. This systematic undercounting suggests that
the AE-inferred concentrations may be biased high due to the presence of multiply charged particles in
the DMA-selected aerosol (100 nm). Since the AE measures total electrical current and assumes all
particles carry a single charge, the presence of doubly or triply charged larger particles would cause the
AE to overestimate the true number concentration.

 7

To remove this bias and provide a more representative reference, we adopted a leave-one-out (LOO)
averaging approach: for each CPC, the reference concentration was calculated as the mean of the
remaining seven CPCs. This method ensures that no instrument is compared with itself and provides a
CPC-consensus reference independent of the AE charge assumption. The results are presented in
Figure 6.

 Figure 6. Leave-one-out averaged CPC comparison for the aerosol particles with the same size.

All eight instruments now show R² values of 0.993 or higher, with six achieving R² = 0.999. This
represents a substantial improvement over the AE-referenced analysis (where R² ranged from 0.977 to
0.983) and confirms that the inter-CPC agreement is excellent when the AE charge-assumption bias is
removed. The slopes now range from 0.936 to 1.122, compared to the previous range of 0.736 to 0.860
when referenced to the AE. This confirms that the systematic undercounting observed in the AE
comparison was largely an artifact of the multiply charged particle bias in the AE reference, rather than a
true deficiency in the CPCs. The intercepts are now dramatically reduced, ranging from −105.8 to 51.5
cm⁻³, compared to the previous range of 333.8 to 546.2 cm⁻³ in the AE comparison. This indicates that the
systematic offsets observed earlier were primarily driven by the AE reference bias.

Note that TSI CPC 3772 units (a, b, c) show slopes of 0.975, 0.982, and 0.957, respectively, with
intercepts very close to zero (5.5 to 46.1 cm⁻³) and R² values of 0.998–0.999. The inter-unit consistency
remains excellent, with a slope spread of only 0.025, confirming their reliability for deployment.
Additionally, TSI CPC 3789 units (a, b) show slopes of 1.031 and 1.019, both slightly above unity, with
near-zero intercepts and R² = 0.999 for both. This indicates they count slightly higher than the group

 8

average, consistent with their lower tunable D50 relative to the CPC 3772 and Magic CPCs. Their
inter-unit agreement is outstanding (slope difference of only 0.012).

Magic CPCs (wcpc uas and wcpc uas backup) show slopes of 0.972 and 0.936, both below unity, with the
backup unit showing the lowest slope and a notable negative intercept of −105.8 cm⁻³. While the primary
Magic CPC unit performs well (R² = 0.999), the backup unit shows the weakest agreement in the group
(R² = 0.993, slope = 0.936), suggesting it may require recalibration or maintenance before future
deployments.

experiment was extended to include DMA-selected aerosol particles of different sizes, ranging from
30 nm to 180 nm. This "diffSize" test complements the previous "sameSize" analysis by assessing
whether the inter-CPC agreement holds across varying particle diameters, which may differentially affect
the activation and counting efficiencies of instruments with different D50 cut sizes and condensation
mechanisms. The same LOO averaging approach was applied, and the results are presented in Figure 7.

5.0 Summary
 This report presents a comprehensive intercomparison of nine condensation particle counters (CPCs)—
 three TSI CPC 3772, two TSI CPC 3789, one TSI CPC 3025, two Magic CPCs (Aerosol Dynamics Inc.),
 and one Brechtel advanced Mixing CPC (aMCPC)—evaluated against an aerosol electrometer (AE) and
 against each other using a leave-one-out (LOO) averaging method. The experimental setup employed an
 atomizer, a DMA, and a residence chamber to deliver well-characterized aerosol through a common
 sampling manifold to all instruments simultaneously.

 Key findings:
 1. The Brechtel aMCPC was identified as an outlier and excluded from further analysis due to the
 flooding problem. It exhibited the lowest R² (0.889) and the largest intercept (961.0 cm⁻³) when
 compared to the AE. Its removal improved the combined R² from 0.9533 to 0.9669, confirming it was
 a significant source of variance. Further characterization of this instrument is recommended before
 future deployments.

 10

 2. All remaining CPCs systematically undercounted relative to the AE (slopes of 0.736–0.860), likely
 due to multiply charged particles in the DMA-selected aerosol biasing the AE reference high. This
 finding has important implications: CPC-consensus references may be more reliable than AE
 references when the charge state of the aerosol is not well characterized.
 3. The LOO analysis revealed excellent inter-CPC agreement. When referenced against the CPC
 consensus rather than the AE:
      Same-size tests: R² ≥ 0.993, slopes of 0.936–1.122
      Different-size tests (30–180 nm): R² ≥ 0.990, slopes of 0.911–1.136
 4. The LOO intercomparison method proved to be a robust and reliable approach, yielding stable results
    across both same-size and different-size conditions (|Δ slope| ≤ 0.025 for all instruments). This
 method is recommended for future CPC intercomparison studies, particularly when an absolute
 reference may carry its own biases.

6.0 CPC Operation under Lower Pressures
 CPCs are often used in environments with significantly lower pressures, such as high-altitude locations or
 aboard aircraft. Under these conditions, their performance can deteriorate due to changes in saturation
 profiles and internal particle losses. Research has shown that below 500 hPa, the counting efficiency of
 CPCs decreases, and the cutoff diameter increases. This is due to the reduced vapor pressure over convex
 surfaces, which affects the supersaturation criteria necessary for condensation.(Bauer et al. 2023).

 6.1 Butanol-Based CPC Performance
 A study by Bauer et al. (2023) characterized the pressure-dependent performance of two CPC models: the
 Grimm 5410 CEN and the TSI 3772-CEN. Their performance was found to be in accordance with the
 CEN technical specifications at pressures between 1000 hPa and 750 hPa. However, below 500 hPa, both
 models exhibited a decline in performance, with differences between them. The study included
 simulations of saturation profiles and calculations of internal particle losses, which reproduced the
 observed performance decrease and highlighted the impact of the CPC's internal design.

 Key behavior trends:
  • Above about 750 hPa, the 3772-CEN behaves close to its standard characterization: 𝑑𝑑𝑝𝑝,50 near 7–8
 nm and plateau efficiency ~100 %.
  • Between 750 and 500 hPa, performance is still acceptable for many applications, but you already see
 a shift in cut-off by a few nanometers and a modest reduction in plateau efficiency.
  • Below 500 hPa (375 and 250 hPa), the study reports that both CEN CPCs (TSI 3772-CEN and
 Grimm 5410-CEN) show clearly degraded performance, with significantly higher 𝑑𝑑𝑝𝑝,50 and reduced
 plateau efficiencies, even though the internal flows and temperatures were kept within reasonable
 ranges.

6.2 Water-Based CPC Performance
There are no published data or validated simulations for CPC 3789 performance below 200 hPa; The
Mei et al. (2021) “simulation‑aided characterization” paper explicitly states that measurements and
modeling for the vWCPC 3789 were performed only between 500 and 1000 hPa (Hao et al. 2023,
Mei et al.,2021).

Given how the 3789 behaves between 500 and 1000 hPa, one can infer the likely direction of change if
the CPC is pushed below 200 hPa, but this would be extrapolation, not validated data:
 • At 500–1000 hPa, lowering pressure at fixed temperature settings reduces peak supersaturation,
 narrows the supersaturated region, lowers droplet growth, and decreases counting efficiency; these
 effects become “significant and larger than 10 %” once pressure drops below about 600 hPa at
 standard settings.
 • Optimizing the conditioner temperature at 500 hPa can restore near‑unity plateau efficiency for
 particles larger than about 15 nm, by increasing the temperature difference and thus the available
 water vapor.
 • Below ~200 hPa, the combination of very low gas density, reduced residence time, and more
 challenging heat/mass‑transfer conditions would almost certainly further reduce achievable
 supersaturation and droplet growth in the existing 3789 design, unless the hardware (flow rate,
 temperatures, tube geometry) were fundamentally re‑engineered. This is qualitatively consistent with
 why airborne CPCs for <200 hPa often use different working fluids such as FC‑43 instead of water or
 butanol.

Practical “best-guess” settings by pressure range:

Combining the 2021 and 2023 studies’ results yields this practical guidance for the vWCPC 3789
(7 nm mode):(Hao et al. 2023, Mei et al. 2021):
 • 900–1010 hPa (near sea level, ground operation):
 – Use factory settings: 30 °C, 59°C, 10 °C. (conditioner, initiator, moderator)
 – This gives high plateau efficiency and design cut-off sizes.
 • 700–900 hPa (moderate altitude/slightly reduced pressure):
 – Still acceptable with factory settings, but the onset of degradation appears as pressure approaches
 ~700 hPa.
 – If you can adjust, a slight reduction of conditioner temperature (e.g., 28–29 °C) while keeping
 saturator near 59–60 °C increases performance and improves activation of the smallest detectable
 particles.
 • 500–700 hPa (lower troposphere/pressurized cabin):
 – Mei et al. (2021) show that at 500 hPa, 24-27 °C, with a saturator at 60 °C maintains counting
 efficiency close to 1 for particles >15 nm.
 – The simulations in 2023 indicate that this setting ensures a peak saturation ratio ≥1.3, which they
 identify as a threshold for good performance under low pressure.

 13

  • 300–500 hPa (upper troposphere/aircraft, based on 2023 simulation studies):
 – The 2023 simulation suggests that further lowering conditioner temperature while keeping or
 slightly increasing saturator/initiator temperature (e.g., 27 °C, 62 °C) can maintain adequate
 supersaturation even down to tens of kPa (300–400 hPa), but this is based on simulations plus
 limited experiments and is not a factory-endorsed setting.
  • The guiding principle is to choose a temperature pair that yields a saturation peak just above ~1.3; in
 practice, that means larger temperature differences at lower pressure until you see stable, high plateau
 efficiency in test measurements.

 Below about 300 hPa, the authors still work mostly in simulation space and note that practical airborne
 use would require careful validation; TSI’s own datasheet only guarantees operation down to 75 kPa
 (~750 hPa), so anything lower is off-specification and must be justified by your own tests.

7.0 Recommendations for Future Deployments
  • After this testing, we have implemented the following actions:
 – Recalibrate the Magic CPC backup unit and retest before any field deployment.
 – Investigate the Brechtel aMCPC for potential flow, valve, optical, or firmware issues that may
 cause the flooding issue.
  • Our recommendations for future deployments:
 – Repeat the experimental comparison periodically before the future filed campaign.
 – Extend the intercomparison to lower pressures to evaluate instrument performance at different
 altitudes, which is particularly relevant for the AAF intended aircraft deployment applications.
 – Characterize the AE multiply charged particle bias quantitatively by comparing AE-inferred
 concentrations with charge-corrected SMPS data.

 14

 – Adopt the LOO method as a standard practice for future multi-CPC intercomparisons to provide a
 self-consistent, bias-free reference.
 – Calibrate the CPCs under the lower pressure to confirm the performance.
