Geometric numerical discretization of electromagnetic quasineutral models
Nishant Narechania \aff 1 \corresp, Emil Poulsen \aff 1, Eric Sonnendrücker \aff 1,2

Abstract
In this work, the geometric electromagnetic Particle-in-Cell (PIC) framework, GEMPICX , is extended to solve the quasineutral, fully kinetic Vlasov-Maxwell equations on dual grids using mimetic finite differences. The discrete action principle is derived, taking into account the duality between the grids. The temporal derivative of the electric field does not directly appear in the dynamical system for the quasineutral model. Hence, a discretized curl-curl equation is used to implicitly obtain the electric field at every time-step. This also circumvents the need to obtain electric potentials. A Lagrange multiplier is used to maintain the discretized divergence of the current density at machine zero.



1
Introduction

The study of magnetized plasmas is of crucial importance in applications such as fusion energy, space weather and astrophysics. Numerical simulations provide an effective way of studying plasma behavior, by avoiding the prohibitive costs of conducting in situ and remote measurements. The large variation in length and time scales in the dynamics of magnetized plasmas poses a challenge for numerical methods and computer simulations (
9
;
26
;
47
). To capture the diverse range of phenomena observed in magnetized plasmas, researchers have proposed numerous physical models over the years, with various numerical techniques based on these models. These models are broadly divided into three categories: kinetic models that describe the full velocity-space distribution function of each species; fluid models assuming a near-Maxwellian distribution, and therefore allowing the plasma to be characterized by moments of the distribution function such as density, temperature and bulk velocity; and hybrid models that are a combination of the other two. Kinetic models are used when kinetic effects such as non-Maxwellian distributions, wave-particle interactions and kinetic instabilities become important. They employ the Vlasov equation in combination with Maxwell’s laws of electromagnetism to describe the dynamics of the plasma. When the physical dimensions of the problem studied are much larger (>
\sim
20 times) than the Debye length, the plasma can be considered as being quasineutral across these length scales i.e. having an equal number of positive and negative charge carriers. Quasineutral models filter out the lower length scales over which charge separation phenomena occur. They also remove the description of high-frequency electromagnetic wave propagation from the Vlasov-Maxwell system. Quasineutral models are therefore reduced models that eliminate the need for resolving these small time and length scales, resulting in faster numerical schemes and more efficient use of computational resources.

Numerical algorithms for quasineutral systems have been developed over the years and still remain an active area of research in computational plasma physics.

There has also been a considerable amount of work done in the plasma physics community on methods that preserve certain geometric structures of the physical system of equations being solved. These structures could be divergences, gauge invariances or conservation laws for instance. For instance, energy-conserving PIC discretizations of the electrostatic Vlasov-Poisson

However, these energy-conserving implicit schemes are not derived from a variational or Hamiltonian principle, and therefore do not preserve the de Rham or Poisson structure of the Vlasov-Maxwell system. Methods that rely on the de Rham structure of the Maxwell equations were also being developed for PIC methods that used finite-element meshes

(
50

(
34

11

(

Besides finite elements, this approach has now also been developed with a discretization based on structure-preserving mimetic finite differences
(
33
)
. These mimetic discretizations employ operators that exactly imitate vector calculus identities on discrete spaces
(
7
)
. To achieve this, unknowns are represented as point values, edge integrals, face integrals, and volume integrals, constituting a discrete de Rham complex. Recently, this approach has also been extended to the drift-kinetic (DK) model and the hybrid model with drift electrons and kinetic ions, by
43
. In this work, we aim to extend this method to the quasineutral limit of the Vlasov-Maxwell PIC system with both kinetic electrons and ions. We start with the Lagrangian from
49
and then propose a discretized Lagrangian based on the geometric structure-preserving discretization related to mimetic finite differences. Importantly, our approach is derived directly in the quasineutral limit, rather than relying on asymptotic consistency of a full Vlasov-Maxwell solver upon the Debye length approaching zero, as in the work by
18
. Because the formulation is derived directly in the quasineutral limit, the scheme ensures the absence of electromagnetic and Langmuir waves in the numerical solution. On the contrary, asymptotic-preserving approaches rely on numerical damping to get rid of these waves in the quasineutral limit as the Debye length tends to zero. Another important feature of our model is that the discretized field equations and equations of motion for particles are both derived from the discretized Lagrangian, ensuring structure preservation in the semi-discretized space. At the semi-discrete level, we show that this system preserves the sum of the magnetic and kinetic energies, and maintains the zero-divergence requirement of the current density for the quasineutral model. We then check the wave spectra of single- and double-species quasineutral plasmas to verify our numerical model, using quasi-1D test cases with periodic boundary conditions. A wave-damping simulation with periodic boundary conditions is also conducted to verify the theoretically predicted damping rate obtained from the dispersion relation.

2
The quasineutral Vlasov-Maxwell model

2.1
Governing equations

In a fully kinetic model of a plasma, a species
s
is described using a phase-space distribution function
f_{s}
that is a function of space (
\bm{x}
), velocity (
\bm{v}
) and time (
t
). This function
f_{s}
evolves according to the Vlasov equation given by

\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{s}+\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}{(\bm{E}+\bm{v}\times\bm{B})}\cdot\nabla_{v}f_{s}=0,

(1)

where
q_{s}
and
m_{s}
are the particle charge and mass for the species
s
, and
\bm{E}
and
\bm{B}
are the electric and magnetic fields. (
\bm{X}
) and (
\bm{V}
) are characteristics of the Vlasov equation. In the quasineutral model, the electric and magnetic fields satisfy the following Maxwell’s equations:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{B}=\mu_{0}\bm{J},

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{B}}{\partial t}+\nabla\times\bm{E}={\bf 0},

(3)

\displaystyle\rho=0,

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{B}=0.

(5)

Here,
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of vacuum. Equations (
1
)–(
5
) together comprise the Vlasov-Maxwell system in the quasineutral limit. The charge density,
\rho
, and the current density,
\bm{J}
, are obtained by integrating the zeroth and first moments of the distribution function
f_{s}
over the velocity space:

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int f_{s}d\bm{v},

(6)

\displaystyle\bm{J}=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int f_{s}\bm{v}d\bm{v}.

(7)

In the full Vlasov-Maxwell system, without taking the quasineutral limit, the Ampère equation i.e. equation (
2
), also contains the displacement current term given by
\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial t}
, which can also be written as
\mu_{0}\epsilon_{0}\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial t}
. Here
c
is the speed of light and
\epsilon_{0}
is the permittivity of vacuum. In the full system, equation (
4
) becomes
\rho=\epsilon_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{E}
. The quasineutral model is obtained by taking the formal limit
\epsilon_{0}\to 0
, thus leading to the above equations. Integrating the Vlasov equation (
1
) over the velocity space and using the
\rho=0
condition from equation (
4
), gives the divergence-free condition for the current density:

\nabla\cdot\bm{J}=0.

(8)

The magnetic field can be written in terms of the magnetic vector potential
\bm{A}
as

\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}.

(9)

The Ampère’s equation (
2
) then can be written as

\nabla\times\nabla\times\bm{A}=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{A})-\nabla^{2}\bm{A}=\mu_{0}\bm{J}.

(10)

The quasineutrality condition,
\nabla\cdot\bm{J}=0
, makes it convenient to use the Coulomb gauge condition

\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0.

(11)

This further simplifies equation (
10
) to the Laplace equation

-\nabla^{2}\bm{A}=\mu_{0}\bm{J}.

(12)

We note that the use of periodic boundary conditions in the test cases considered here allows a straightforward imposition of the Coulomb gauge condition in equation (
11
). This in turn allows the curl-curl operator to be reduced to a Laplace operator that is invertible up to an additional constant. Non-periodic boundary conditions would introduce the need for additional constraints on
\bm{A}
at domain boundaries that are consistent with the Coulomb gauge.

2.2
Action principle

In order to obtain the above governing equations in the continuous form, we start from the action principle developed by
49
, which reads

L(\bm{A}(t),\phi(t),\bm{X}_{s}(t),\dot{\bm{X}_{s}}(t),\bm{V}_{s}(t))=\sum_{s}\int\left((m_{s}\bm{V}_{s}(t)+q_{s}\bm{A}(t,\bm{X}_{s}(t)))\cdot\dot{\bm{X}_{s}}(t)\right.\\
\left.-\frac{1}{2}m_{s}V_{s}^{2}-q_{s}\phi(t,\bm{X}_{s}(t))\right)f_{s,0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{0}-\int\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\left|\nabla\times\bm{A}(t,\bm{x})\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(13)

where
\bm{E}=-\frac{\partial\bm{A}}{\partial t}-\nabla\phi
and
\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
. The sum is over all particle species.

Remark 1

In principle the constraint
\nabla\cdot\bm{J}=0
follows from
\nabla\times\bm{B}=\bm{J}
, but we will not be able to enforce this exactly numerically. For this reason, we need to enforce it with a Lagrange multiplier which will yield a slight modification of the electric field used for pushing the particles, so that the divergence of the current generated by the particles stays 0.

The physical system can be derived from this action. The variations with respect to
\bm{X}_{s}
and
\bm{V}_{s}
yield the Euler-Lagrange equations for the particles,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\bm{X}}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{X}}
and
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{V}}=\bf 0
. In this case, they become

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{s}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{V}_{s},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{V}_{s}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\left(\bm{E}+\bm{V}_{s}\times\bm{B}\right).

(15)

The solutions of equations (
14
) and (
15
) are the characteristic equations of the Vlasov equation. Taking the total derivative of
f_{s}
, and using equations (
14
)–(
15
), gives the Vlasov equation for
f_{s}
.

On the other hand the variations in
\bm{A}
yield

\int\bm{B}\cdot\nabla\times\delta{\bm{A}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int\delta{\bm{A}}\cdot\bm{v}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\quad\forall\delta{\bm{A}},

(16)

which becomes in strong form

\nabla\times\bm{B}=\mu_{0}\bm{J}=\mu_{0}\sum_{s}\bm{J}_{s}=\mu_{0}\sum_{s}q_{s}\int f_{s}\bm{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}.

(17)

The variations in
\phi
yield

\sum_{s}q_{s}\int\delta{\phi}f_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}=0,\quad\forall\delta{\phi}

(18)

which is the quasineutrality condition

\rho=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int f_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}=0.

(19)

Equation (
19
) above also implies
\nabla\cdot\bm{J}=0
because of the continuity equation (
8
).
Moreover, the Faraday’s equation (
3
) follows directly from the definition of the fields from the potentials. Similarly, the Gauss law for magnetism (
5
) follows from the definition of the magnetic field.

2.3
The electric field equation

In the quasineutral model, without the displacement current
\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial t}
in the Ampère’s equation, the electric field cannot be evolved using its time derivative. An equation is therefore required to obtain the electric field at any time instant. Taking the curl of (
3
) and the time derivative of (
2
) and combining the two equations, we obtain

\nabla\times\nabla\times\bm{E}=-\mu_{0}\frac{\partial\bm{J}}{\partial t}.

(20)

Next, multiplying the Vlasov equation by
q_{s}\bm{v}
and integrating over the velocity space, we obtain

\frac{\partial\bm{J}}{\partial t}=\sum_{s}\frac{\partial\bm{J}_{s}}{\partial t}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}(\rho_{s}\bm{E}+\bm{J}_{s}\times\bm{B}-\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}_{s}).

(21)

where
\rho_{s}
,
\bm{J}_{s}
and
\mathbb{S}_{s}
are the contributions of species
s
to the charge density, current density and stress tensor, given by

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=q_{s}\int f_{s}d\bm{v},

(22)

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{s}=q_{s}\int f_{s}\bm{v}d\bm{v},

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{s}=m_{s}\int f_{s}\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}d\bm{v}.

(24)

Substituting equation (
21
) in equation (
20
) gives the following equation for the electric field

\nabla\times\nabla\times\bm{E}+\mu_{0}\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\rho_{s}\bm{E}=\mu_{0}\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}(-\bm{J}_{s}\times\bm{B}+\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}_{s}).

(25)

This equation can be solved at every time-step to obtain the electric field. It must be noted that we also have the option to solve equation (
20
) to obtain
\bm{E}
. However, in the discrete setting, this would require the use of a time discretization scheme to obtain the discrete
\frac{\partial\bm{J}}{\partial t}
. The electric field solution would depend on the discrete derivative used, which is not the case for equation (
25
). More importantly, the system in equation (
20
) is singular due to the curl-curl term. On the other hand, equation (
25
) is non-singular and determinate on account of the
\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\rho_{s}\bm{E}
term on the left hand side. The left hand side of equation (
25
) results in a symmetric, positive definite matrix after discretization, making it straightforward to solve.

3
Spatial discretization of fields

We use a dual-grid approach for the spatial discretization of our model, wherein the dual or adjoint grid vertices are the barycenters of the primal grid cells. The numerical values for the field variables are located on spaces based on points, edges, faces and volumes of hexahedral (3D) or quadrilateral (2D) cells. This discretization, based on mimetic finite differences, has already been used in
GEMPICX
for discretizing the fully kinetic
(
33
)
and the drift-kinetic
(
43
)
Vlasov-Maxwell models. The nodal, edge, face and volume spaces on the primal grids are denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{0}
,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\mathcal{C}_{3}
, respectively. Similarly, those on the dual grids are denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
, respectively. A duality exists between the primal and dual spaces. The nodes, edges, faces and cell volumes on the primal grid are each uniquely associated with the cell volumes, faces, edges and nodes on the dual grid, respectively. On the primal grid, the electric field
\bm{E}
and the vector potential
\bm{A}
are defined as edge-integrals and the magnetic field
\bm{B}
is defined as face-integrals. On the dual grid, the magnetic field intensity
\bm{H}
is defined as edge-integrals, the current density
\bm{J}
and electric field intensity
\bm{D}
are defined as face-integrals, and the charge density
\rho
is defined as volume-integrals. Such an approach allows for exact preservation of certain discretized quantities. For example, the discrete divergence of
\bm{J}
becomes a volume-integral on the dual grid, which is the same space where the charge density
\rho
is defined. These spaces are shown in Figure
1
for the primal grid.

3.1
Reduction operators

The reduction operators, also called restriction operators, relate a continuous field to its discretized counterparts defined on nodes, edges, faces and volumes. On the primal grid, these are:

•

\mathcal{R}_{0}
relates a scalar field to its values at primal grid nodes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}(\psi)_{i,j,k}=\psi(x_{i},y_{j},z_{k})={\bm{\uppsi}}_{i,j,k}.

(26)

Here,
\bm{\uppsi}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{1}
relates a vector field to its integrals on primal grid edges:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}^{x}(E_{x})_{i,j,k}=\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}E_{x}(x,y_{j},z_{k})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x={{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}={{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{x}_{i,j,k},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}^{y}(E_{y})_{i,j,k}=\int_{y_{j}}^{y_{j+1}}E_{y}(x_{i},y,z_{k})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y={{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k}={{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{y}_{i,j,k},

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}^{z}(E_{z})_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k}}^{z_{k+1}}E_{z}(x_{i},y_{j},z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z={{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j,k+1/2}={{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{z}_{i,j,k}.

(29)

Here,
\mathcal{R}_{1}(\bm{E})=(\mathcal{R}_{1}^{x}(E_{x}),\mathcal{R}_{1}^{y}(E_{y}),\mathcal{R}_{1}^{z}(E_{z}))=({{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{x},{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{y},{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{z})
and
{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}=({\bm{\mathsf{E}}}^{x},{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}^{y},{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}^{z})\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{2}
relates a vector field to its integrals on primal grid faces:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}^{x}(B_{x})_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k}}^{z_{k+1}}\int_{y_{j}}^{y_{j+1}}B_{x}(x_{i},y,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z={{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k+1/2}={{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}^{x}_{i,j,k},

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}^{y}(B_{y})_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k}}^{z_{k+1}}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}B_{y}(x,y_{j},z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z={{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k+1/2}={{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}^{y}_{i,j,k},

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}^{z}(B_{z})_{i,j,k}=\int_{y_{j}}^{y_{j+1}}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}B_{z}(x,y,z_{k})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y={{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k}={{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}^{z}_{i,j,k}.

(32)

Here,
\mathcal{R}_{2}(\bm{B})=(\mathcal{R}_{2}^{x}(B_{x}),\mathcal{R}_{2}^{y}(B_{y}),\mathcal{R}_{2}^{z}(B_{z}))=({{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}^{x},{{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}^{y},{{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}^{z})
and
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=({\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{x},{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{y},{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{z})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{3}
relates a scalar field to its volume integrals over primal grid cells:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{3}(\rho)_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k}}^{z_{k+1}}\int_{y_{j}}^{y_{j+1}}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}\rho(x,y,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z={\bm{\uprho}}_{i,j,k}.

(33)

Here,
\bm{\uprho}\in\mathcal{C}_{3}
.

The reduction/restriction operators on the dual mesh are similarly defined as follows:

•

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
relates a scalar field to its values at dual grid nodes:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\phi)_{i,j,k}=\phi(x_{i+1/2},y_{j+1/2},z_{k+1/2})=\tilde{\bm{\upphi}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k+1/2}.

(34)

Here,
\tilde{\bm{\upphi}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
.

•

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
relates a vector field to its integrals on dual grid edges:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{x}(H_{x})_{i,j,k}=\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}H_{x}(x,y_{j+1/2},z_{k+1/2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k+1/2}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{x}_{i,j,k},

(35)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{y}(H_{y})_{i,j,k}=\int_{y_{j-1/2}}^{y_{j+1/2}}H_{y}(x_{i+1/2},y,z_{k+1/2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k+1/2}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{y}_{i,j,k},

(36)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{z}(H_{z})_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k-1/2}}^{z_{k+1/2}}H_{z}(x_{i+1/2},y_{j+1/2},z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{z}_{i,j,k}.

(37)

Here,
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}(\bm{H})=(\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{x}(H_{x}),\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{y}(H_{y}),\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{z}(H_{z}))=(\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{x},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{y},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{z})
and
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}=(\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{x},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{y},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{z})\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
.

•

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
relates a vector field to its integrals on dual grid faces:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{x}(D_{x})_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k-1/2}}^{z_{k+1/2}}\int_{y_{j-1/2}}^{y_{j+1/2}}D_{x}(x_{i+1/2},y,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{x}_{i,j,k},

(38)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{y}(D_{y})_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k-1/2}}^{z_{k+1/2}}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}D_{y}(x,y_{j+1/2},z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{y}_{i,j,k},

(39)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{z}(D_{z})_{i,j,k}=\int_{y_{j-1/2}}^{y_{j+1/2}}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}D_{z}(x,y,z_{k+1/2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}_{i,j,k+1/2}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{z}_{i,j,k}.

(40)

Here,
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(\bm{D})=(\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{x}(D_{x}),\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{y}(D_{y}),\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{z}(D_{z}))=(\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{x},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{y},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{z})
and
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}=(\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{x},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{y},\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}^{z})\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
.

•

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
relates a scalar field to its volume integrals over dual grid cells:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}(\rho)_{i,j,k}=\int_{z_{k-1/2}}^{z_{k+1/2}}\int_{y_{j-1/2}}^{y_{j+1/2}}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\rho(x,y,z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{i,j,k}.

(41)

Here,
\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
.

3.2
Hodge operators

Field variables can be projected from primal grid spaces to their dual counterparts or vice versa using Hodge operators. Hodge operators of an arbitrary order of accuracy can be obtained using the method prescribed by
33
. In the current work, only second-order Hodge projections have been used and this simply constitutes the use of scaling factors to relate variables between primal and dual spaces. Consider arbitrary variables
\bm{K}
,
\bm{L}
,
\bm{M}
and
\bm{N}
, defined on primal nodes, edges, faces and cell volumes, respectively. Their corresponding dual mappings, defined on dual cell volumes, faces, edges and nodes are denoted as
\tilde{\bm{K}}
,
\tilde{\bm{L}}
,
\tilde{\bm{M}}
and
\tilde{\bm{N}}
, respectively. These would be given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}}=\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z{\bm{\mathsf{K}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}^{x}=\frac{\Delta y\,\Delta z}{\Delta x}{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}^{x},\quad\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}^{y}=\frac{\Delta x\,\Delta z}{\Delta y}{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}^{y},\quad\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}^{z}=\frac{\Delta x\,\Delta y}{\Delta z}{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}^{z},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}^{x}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta y\,\Delta z}{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}^{x},\quad\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}^{y}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x\,\Delta z}{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}^{y},\quad\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}^{z}=\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta x\,\Delta y}{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}^{z},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}=\frac{1}{\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z}{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}.

(42)

We can therefore define Hodge operators
\mathbb{H}_{0}
,
\mathbb{H}_{1}
,
\mathbb{H}_{2}
, and
\mathbb{H}_{3}
, and their corresponding inverses
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{3}
,
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
,
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{1}
, and
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{0}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}}=\mathbb{H}_{0}{\bm{\mathsf{K}}},\;\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{3}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}=\mathbb{H}_{1}{\bm{\mathsf{L}}},\;\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}=\mathbb{H}_{2}{\bm{\mathsf{M}}},\;\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{1}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}=\mathbb{H}_{3}{\bm{\mathsf{N}}},\;\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{0}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}.

(43)

Accordingly, the electric and magnetic fields can be related to their intensities as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}}=\mathbb{H}_{1}{\bm{\mathsf{E}}},\;\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}=\mathbb{H}_{2}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}},\;\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{1}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}.

(44)

The second order expressions of the various Hodge operators are simply the appropriate scaling factors multiplied by the identity matrix. This is equivalent to the classical Yee scheme. While the current work only uses second-order Hodge operators, the mimetic framework admits Hodge constructions of arbitrary order
(
33
)
. In the context of the full Vlasov-Maxwell system, higher-order Hodges have been shown to improve the accuracy of discrete differential operators and numerical dispersion properties, particularly at higher wavenumbers. To the best of our knowledge, however, such higher-order Hodge operators have not yet been investigated specifically within a geometric quasineutral PIC framework. Since the quasineutral approximation removes high-frequency electromagnetic modes while retaining low-frequency plasma dynamics, a dedicated assessment of the benefits of higher-order Hodges for quasineutral simulations remains to be seen.

3.3
Discrete scalar products

We can now also define discrete scalar products between primal scalar/vector fields and their dual counterparts to estimate
L^{2}
inner products. For the variables
\bm{K}
,
\bm{L}
,
\bm{M}
and
\bm{N}
defined above, these discrete scalar products are as follows:

{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}}=\sum_{i,j,k}{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}_{i,j,k}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{K}}}}_{i,j,k},

(45)

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}={\bm{\mathsf{L}}}^{x}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}^{x}+{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}^{y}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}^{y}+{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}^{z}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}}^{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k}\big({\bm{\mathsf{M}}}_{i,j+1/2,k+1/2}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k+1/2}+{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}_{i+1/2,j,k+1/2}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k+1/2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=\sum_{i,j,k} \big(}+{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{M}}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k}\big),

(47)

{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}=\sum_{i,j,k}{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k+1/2}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k+1/2}.

(48)

3.4
Discrete gradient, curl and divergence

In order to define the discrete gradient, curl and divergence operators, we first define a one-dimensional discrete derivative or difference operator

\mathbb{d}_{M_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&-1&1&0&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
0&&&-1&1\\
1&0&\ldots&0&-1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}\times M_{1}}.

We can represent a
\mathbb{d}
matrix and an identity matrix of size
N
as
\mathbb{d}_{N}
and and
\mathbb{I}_{N}
, respectively. Consider a grid with
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
,
N_{3}
points in the
x
-,
y
- and
z
- directions, respectively, such that
N=N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{3}
. For such a grid, the discrete gradient, curl, and divergence operators on the primal grid can be denoted by
\mathbb{G}
,
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}
. These are built using Kronecker products of the
\mathbb{d}
and
\mathbb{I}
matrices of appropriate sizes as follows

\mathbb{G}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{d}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{3}}\end{pmatrix},

(49)

\mathbb{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{N}&-\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{3}}&\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{3}}&\mathbb{O}_{N}&-\mathbb{d}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}\\
-\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}&\mathbb{d}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}&\mathbb{O}_{N}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(50)

\mathbb{D}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{d}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}&\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{3}}&\mathbb{I}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{d}_{N_{3}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(51)

The adjoint operators of these operators on the dual grid are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{G}}=-\mathbb{D}^{T},

(52)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{T},

(53)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{D}}=-\mathbb{G}^{T}.

(54)

Due to our degrees of freedom being defined on discrete spaces associated with the de Rham complex, the discrete gradient, curl and divergence operators and their dual operators are always exact.

4
Particle in Cell (PIC) discretization

Let us now consider the particle discretization of the Vlasov equation, where we write

f_{s}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{s}}w_{p}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\delta(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{p}(t)),

(55)

where
w_{p}
is the particle weight,
N_{s}
is the number of particles of species
s
and
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. This function is clearly not continuous and smooth. However, in order to use particle information to calculate fields such as
\rho
,
\bm{J}
and
\mathbb{S}
in their discretized forms, we would require a smoothing kernel, typically a spline. Such a spline would effectively spread out the influence of the particle’s mass and charge over a volume centered at the particle location, rather than keep it concentrated at a single point. These splines would also be used to calculate electric and magnetic fields at particle locations using the discretized forms of the fields. As each particle carries its own charge
q_{p}
, mass
m_{p}
and weight
w_{p}
, we shall not distinguish the species in the discrete sum and
\sum_{p}
will denote in the sequel the sum over all particles of all species. Each particle then obeys equations (
14
) and (
15
). The splines used in this work are based on the well-known cardinal B-splines. Fundamental cardinal B-splines of an arbitrary degree
d
centered at
x=0
can be defined recursively using the convolution

S^{(d)}(x)=S^{(0)}*S^{(d-1)}(x)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}S^{(d-1)}(x-x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime},

(56)

with

S^{(0)}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }-\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(57)

Cardinal B-splines have the property

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S^{(d)}(x)\,dx=1.

(58)

The coupling of particles and fields involves two kinds of B-splines: node splines and cell splines. These splines are built using the cardinal B-spline described above.

These splines are key to evaluation of
\bm{E}
,
\bm{B}
fields at particle positions and evaluation of field variables like charge density
\rho
and current density
\bm{J}
, as defined in their respective spaces.
Consider the mesh size in the
x
-direction to be denoted as
h
. Node splines are centered on grid nodes, and are defined as

S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})=S^{d}((x_{p}-x_{i})/h).

(59)

This spline is supported on the interval
[x_{i}-(d+1)h/2,x_{i}+(d+1)h/2]
, and its values are independent of the grid size.
Cell splines are centered on cell midpoints, and are defined as

S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})=\frac{1}{h}S^{d-1}\left(\frac{x_{p}-x_{i+1/2}}{h}\right).

(60)

This spline is supported on the interval
[x_{i+1/2}-dh/2,x_{i+1/2}+dh/2]
, and its volume-integrals are independent of the grid size. Node and cell splines for the
y
- and
z
- directions can be similarly defined given the respective mesh sizes in those directions.

The charge density
\rho
is defined as volume-integrals on the dual grid i.e.
\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}(\rho)\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
. This is calculated as

\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}(t)_{i,j,k}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}(\rho(t))_{i,j,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)).

(61)

The current density
\bm{J}
is defined as face integrals on the dual grid i.e.
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(\bm{J})\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. This is calculated as

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}^{x}(t)_{i+1/2,j,k}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(J^{x}(t))_{i+1/2,j,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,x}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)),

(62)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}^{y}(t)_{i,j+1/2,k}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(J^{y}(t))_{i,j+1/2,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,y}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{c}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)),

(63)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}^{z}(t)_{i,j,k+1/2}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(J^{z}(t))_{i,j,k+1/2}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,z}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}(t)).

(64)

The components of the divergence of the stress tensor
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}
are also defined as face integrals on the dual grid. The matrix
\mathbb{S}
can be written in terms of its components as

\mathbb{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{S}^{x}\\
\mathbb{S}^{y}\\
\mathbb{S}^{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{S}^{xx}&\mathbb{S}^{xy}&\mathbb{S}^{xz}\\
\mathbb{S}^{yx}&\mathbb{S}^{yy}&\mathbb{S}^{yz}\\
\mathbb{S}^{zx}&\mathbb{S}^{zy}&\mathbb{S}^{zz}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly, the vector
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}
can be written as

\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}=\begin{pmatrix}(\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S})^{x}\\
(\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S})^{y}\\
(\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S})^{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}(\mathbb{S}^{xx})+\partial_{y}(\mathbb{S}^{xy})+\partial_{z}(\mathbb{S}^{xz})\\
\partial_{x}(\mathbb{S}^{yx})+\partial_{y}(\mathbb{S}^{yy})+\partial_{z}(\mathbb{S}^{yz})\\
\partial_{x}(\mathbb{S}^{zx})+\partial_{y}(\mathbb{S}^{zy})+\partial_{z}(\mathbb{S}^{zz})\\
\end{pmatrix}.

If we denote
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}
as
\bm{Q}
and its discrete dual face equivalent as
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}
, we have
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(\bm{Q})\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. We denote the dual equivalent of the term
\partial_{y}(\mathbb{S}^{xy})
as
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xy}
and so on for other terms. Thus,
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}
can be written as

\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{x}\\
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{y}\\
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xx}+\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xy}+\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xz}\\
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{yx}+\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{yy}+\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{yz}\\
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{zx}+\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{zy}+\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{zz}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The individual components of
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}
are calculated as

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xx}(t)_{i+1/2,j,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,x}v_{p,x}\frac{\partial S_{i}^{c}(x_{p}(t))}{\partial x_{p}(t)}S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)),

(65)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xy}(t)_{i+1/2,j,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,x}v_{p,y}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p}(t))\frac{\partial S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))}{\partial y_{p}(t)}S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)),

(66)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{xz}(t)_{i+1/2,j,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,x}v_{p,z}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))\frac{\partial S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t))}{\partial z_{p}(t)},

(67)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{yx}(t)_{i,j+1/2,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,y}v_{p,x}\frac{\partial S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))}{\partial x_{p}(t)}S_{j}^{c}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)),

(68)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{yy}(t)_{i,j+1/2,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,y}v_{p,y}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))\frac{\partial S_{j}^{c}(y_{p}(t))}{\partial y_{p}(t)}S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t)),

(69)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{yz}(t)_{i,j+1/2,k}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,y}v_{p,z}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{c}(y_{p}(t))\frac{\partial S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}(t))}{\partial z_{p}(t)},

(70)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{zx}(t)_{i,j,k+1/2}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,z}v_{p,x}\frac{\partial S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))}{\partial x_{p}(t)}S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}(t)),

(71)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{zy}(t)_{i,j,k+1/2}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,z}v_{p,y}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))\frac{\partial S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))}{\partial y_{p}(t)}S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}(t)),

(72)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}}^{zz}(t)_{i,j,k+1/2}=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}v_{p,z}v_{p,z}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p}(t))S_{j}^{n}(y_{p}(t))\frac{\partial S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}(t))}{\partial z_{p}(t)}.

(73)

The values of the electric and magnetic fields at the particle locations, i.e.
\bm{E}^{S}=(E_{x}^{S},E_{y}^{S},E_{z}^{S})
and
B^{S}=(B_{x}^{S},B_{y}^{S},B_{z}^{S})
, come from an integration of the fields over the particle shape functions. The exact expressions given here can be derived from the discrete Lagrangian presented in the next section. These are used to update particle velocities. These are also calculated at particle positions using node and cell splines. These fields are calculated in terms of the edge-integrals
{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}
and face-integrals
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
.

\displaystyle E_{x}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}),

(74)

\displaystyle E_{y}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}),

(75)

\displaystyle E_{z}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j,k+1/2}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}).

(76)

\displaystyle B_{x}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k+1/2}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}),

(77)

\displaystyle B_{y}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k+1/2}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}),

(78)

\displaystyle B_{z}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}).

(79)

The dual face equivalents of the Lorentz force
\bm{J}\times\bm{B}
can be obtained by simply replacing the components of
\bm{v}_{p}
in equations (
62
)–(
64
) with components of
\bm{v}_{p}\times\bm{B}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})

5
Semi-discretized governing equations

In this section, we use the semi-discrete action principle to obtain the semi-discrete governing equations. The discretization is only in space, while all quantities are still varying continuously with time. The semi-discrete electric field equation is also derived. Conservation of energy for our proposed semi-discretization is mathematically proven.

5.1
Semi-discrete action principle

In this framework, in the same way as in
33
, we can write a discrete action principle from which our model will follow:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{h}=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\left[w_{p}(m_{p}\bm{v}_{p}+q_{p}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}})\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\dot{\bm{x}}_{p}S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})\right)-\frac{1}{2}m_{p}\bm{v}_{p}^{2}-q_{p}{\bm{\mathsf{\upphi}}}\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}(S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}))\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}(\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}})\cdot(\mathbb{H}_{1}\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}).

(80)

The discretized Euler-Lagrange equations for the particles, obtained by taking the variations with respect to
\bm{x}_{p}
and
\bm{v}_{p}
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{v}_{p},

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{q_{p}}{m_{p}}\left(\bm{E}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})+\bm{v}_{p}\times\bm{B}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})\right).

(82)

In our discrete setting, the electromagnetic fields and the potentials, all defined on the primal grid, are related by

{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}=-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}-\mathbb{G}{\bm{\mathsf{\upphi}}},~~~~{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}.

(83)

This immediately yields the discrete Faraday equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}=0.

(84)

Similarly, the definition of the magnetic field, coupled with the discretization of the magnetic field on primal grid faces, allows us to write the discretized Gauss law of magnetism

\mathbb{D}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=0.

(85)

Taking the variations of
\mathcal{L}_{h}
with respect to
{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
, we find Ampère’s law

\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=\mu_{0}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}},~~~\mbox{where }\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}(t)=\sum_{p}w_{p}q_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\bm{v}_{p}(t)S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t)\right).

(86)

Taking the variations with respect to
{\bm{\mathsf{\upphi}}}
leads to the discretized quasineutrality condition

\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}=0,~~~\mbox{where }\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}=\sum_{p}w_{p}q_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{3}\left(S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t)\right).

(87)

Just like the continuous counterpart, this results in the discrete divergence of the current density being 0 i.e.

\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}=0.

(88)

While equation (
4
) is satisfied at the continuous level of the quasineutral model, at the discrete level equation (
87
) is not satisfied to machine precision. The discrete charge density volume integrals are obtained by depositing a finite number of particles onto the grid, introducing statistical sampling noise. The resulting deviations are typically small, and since quasineutrality is embedded in the electric and magnetic field equations, they do not affect the consistency of the quasineutral limit. In PIC codes like the one presented here, these fluctuations decrease with increasing number of particles per cell, scaling approximately as
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N_{p}})

(
6
)
. Instead, our numerical scheme ensures that the discrete quasineutrality divergence constraint given by equation (
88
) is satisfied up to machine precision at the discretized level, as explained later in Section
6
. A divergence-free
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
also leads to a divergence-free
{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
. This simplifies the discretized curl-curl operator in equation (
86
) to a discrete Laplace operator
\mathbb{L}
. Accordingly, the discretized counterpart of equation (
12
) becomes the linear system

(\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{H}_{1}\mathbb{G}\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{3}\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{H}_{1}){\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=-\mathbb{L}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}.

(89)

After obtaining the divergence-free
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
, we use the above equation to obtain the divergence-free
{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
. The discretized magnetic field
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
is then obtained using
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
. This ensures that the discretized Ampère’s law, equation (
86
) is exactly satisfied by our structure-preserving scheme. Maintaining this consistency between the discretized current and Ampère’s law is essential to avoid unphysical behaviour in the numerical solution of the quasineutral model, as has been pointed out by
10
. We reiterate that our use of periodic boundary conditions here makes it easy to impose the discrete divergence-free condition on
{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
i.e.
\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{H}_{1}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=0
. This makes the discrete Laplace operator in equation (
89
) invertible to a constant, and also maintains the exactness of the discrete mimetic de Rham structure. Non-periodic boundaries would necessitate modifications in equation (
89
) to incorporate boundary effects.

5.2
Semi-discrete electric field equation

Equations (
84
)-(
86
) do not yield a direct way to update the electric field. For this reason, following
18
and
22
, the problem is reformulated to get an equation for the electric field. The time derivative of the discrete Ampère’s equation (
86
) using the particle current gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mu_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mu_{0}\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\bm{v}_{p}(t)S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})-\bm{v}_{p}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\nabla S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}\sum_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\frac{q_{p}^{2}}{m_{p}}(\bm{E}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})+\bm{v}_{p}\times\bm{B}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p}))S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})-q_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\cdot\nabla S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})\right).

(90)

Then, taking the discrete curl of (
84
) yields the following equation for the electric field:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}+\mu_{0}\sum_{p}\frac{w_{p}q_{p}^{2}}{m_{p}}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\bm{E}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\right)=

\displaystyle-\mu_{0}\sum_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\frac{q_{p}^{2}}{m_{p}}(\bm{v}_{p}\times\bm{B}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p}))S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})-q_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\cdot\nabla S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})\right).

(91)

This is a large, sparse, linear system that can be solved to obtain
{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}
in the numerical algorithm. The second term on the left hand side is a particle ‘mass’ matrix, that requires looping over all the particles. This constitutes a significant portion of the overall computational cost. A cheaper alternative would be to instead use a diagonal matrix,
\mathbb{M}
, build from the discretized charge density contributions of the individual species,
\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s}
, defined on the dual grid as volume-integrals. The resultant linear system using this approach would be

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}+\mathbb{M}{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}=

\displaystyle-\mu_{0}\sum_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\frac{q_{p}^{2}}{m_{p}}(\bm{v}_{p}\times\bm{B}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p}))S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})-q_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\cdot\nabla S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})\right).

(92)

Here, the diagonal terms of
\mathbb{M}
, corresponding to the coefficients of the
x
-direction component of the discretized electric field, i.e.
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}
, or
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{x}_{i,j,k}
, are given by

m_{i+1/2,j,k}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\bar{\rho}_{s,i+1/2,j,k}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\frac{\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s,i,j,k}+\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s,i+1,j,k}}{2\Delta V}.

(93)

Here,
\bar{\rho}_{s,i+1/2,j,k}
is an interpolated value of the charge density contribution of species
s
, obtained at the
x
-direction edge
(i+1/2,j,k)
using the
\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s}
volume integrals from the dual cells
(i,j,k)
and
(i+1,j,k)
, and the cell volume
\Delta V
. Similarly, the coefficients of
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{y}_{i,j,k}
and
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}^{z}_{i,j,k}
are given by

\displaystyle m_{i,j+1/2,k}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\bar{\rho}_{s,i,j+1/2,k}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\frac{\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s,i,j,k}+\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s,i,j+1,k}}{2\Delta V},

(94)

\displaystyle m_{i,j,k+1/2}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\bar{\rho}_{s,i,j,k+1/2}=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\frac{\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s,i,j,k}+\tilde{\bm{\uprho}}_{s,i,j,k+1}}{2\Delta V}.

(95)

Although the linear system from equation (
92
) is not obtained from a least action principle, it is a convenient approximation on account of its lower computational cost.

5.3
Semi-discrete energy conservation

For a periodic domain, the total magnetic field energy of the system can be defined as

E_{M}=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}.

(96)

Its rate of change becomes, using equation (
84
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{M}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}\cdot\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}.

(97)

The total kinetic energy of the system is defined as

E_{K}=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\left[\frac{1}{2}w_{p}m_{p}\bm{v}_{p}^{2}\right].

(98)

Using equations (
3.2
), (
82
) and (
86
), its rate of change becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{K}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}\left[w_{p}q_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\cdot\left(\bm{E}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})+\bm{v}_{p}\times\bm{B}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})\right)\right]=\sum_{p=1}w_{p}q_{p}\bm{v}_{p}\cdot\bm{E}^{S}(t,\bm{x}_{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}w_{p}q_{p}\begin{pmatrix}v_{p,x}\\
v_{p,y}\\
v_{p,z}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p})\\
\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p})\\
\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j,k+1/2}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k}\sum_{p=1}w_{p}q_{p}\begin{pmatrix}v_{p,x}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p})\\
v_{p,y}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p})\\
v_{p,z}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}\\
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k}\\
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j,k+1/2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}^{x}(t)_{i+1/2,j,k}\\
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}^{y}(t)_{i,j+1/2,k}\\
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}^{z}(t)_{i,j,k+1/2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i+1/2,j,k}\\
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j+1/2,k}\\
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{i,j,k+1/2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}\cdot{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}=(\mathbb{C}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{2}{\bm{\mathsf{B}}})\cdot{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}=(\mathbb{C}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}})\cdot{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}=(\mathbb{C}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}})^{\top}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}^{\top}\mathbb{C}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}=\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{H}}}}\cdot\mathbb{C}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}.

(99)

From equations (
97
) and (
5.3
), it can be seen that the sum of magnetic and kinetic energies is conserved by our proposed semi-discrete formulation. It must be noted that the exact conservation of energy only holds true in the semi-discrete case. After discretizing in time, the energy conservation error would depend on the time-step size
\Delta t
, up to the order of accuracy of the discretization scheme. We also point out that the above condition of semi-discrete energy conservation exactly holds for periodic boundary conditions. This condition would not automatically hold true for non-periodic boundaries and would need modifications to account for boundary fluxes.

6
Time-stepping scheme

We assume that particle positions and velocities are known at time
t_{n}
. This also includes the initial time
t=t_{0}=0
. To update the particle velocities and thus update their positions, we first need the electric and magnetic fields to calculate the forces acting on particles. The discrete Ampère’s equation (
86
) can be used to calculate the magnetic field. However, the discretized current density,
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
, satisfied the divergence-free condition equation (
88
) only in the semi-discrete formulation that is continuous with respect to time. This divergence-free property does not hold for finitely sized time-steps. The divergence error would depend on the time-step size
\Delta t
, up to the order of accuracy of the scheme. Therefore, to proceed,
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
must first satisfy the divergence-free condition equation (
88
). This is necessary for the quasineutrality assumption to hold true and also for obtaining a divergence-free magnetic field. This is achieved with the help of a ‘divergence-corrector’ electric field,
{\bm{E}}_{\star}
, and its corresponding potential,
{\psi}_{\star}
where
{\bm{E}}_{\star}=-\nabla{\psi}_{\star}
. We define their discrete counterparts on the primal grid edges and nodes and refer to them as
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star}
and
{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star}
, respectively. They also satisfy the condition

{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star}=-\mathbb{G}{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star}.

(100)

The ‘corrector’ electric field should be such that updating the particle velocities using the force exerted by this field over the time-step
\Delta t
leads to a discretized current density
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
with zero discrete divergence, i.e. it would satisfy equation (
88
). This gives

\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}=\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left((\bm{v}_{p,\star}(t)+\frac{q_{p}}{m_{p}}\bm{E}_{\star}^{S}(t_{n},\bm{x}_{p}(t_{n}))\Delta t)S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\right)=0.

(101)

Here,
\bm{E}_{\star}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=(E_{\star,x}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p}),E_{\star,y}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p}),E_{\star,z}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p}))
is written in terms of the edge-integrals
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star}
as

\displaystyle E_{\star,x}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star,i+1/2,j,k}S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}),

(102)

\displaystyle E_{\star,y}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star,i,j+1/2,k}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}),

(103)

\displaystyle E_{\star,z}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star,i,j,k+1/2}S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}).

(104)

In terms of
\psi_{\star}
, (
101
) becomes

\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left((\bm{v}_{p,\star}(t)-\frac{q_{p}}{m_{p}}\nabla\psi_{\star}^{S}(t_{n},\bm{x}_{p}(t_{n}))\Delta t)S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\right)=0.

(105)

Defining an uncorrected current density
\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}_{\star}
in terms of the contributions from the uncorrected particle velocities
\bm{v}_{p,\star}
, we get

\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}_{\star}(t)=\sum_{p}q_{p}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\bm{v}_{p,\star}(t)S(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\right).

(106)

Substituting this in equation (
105
), we get

\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\sum_{p}\frac{q_{p}^{2}}{m_{p}}w_{p}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\left(\nabla\psi_{\star}^{S}(t_{n},\bm{x}_{p}(t_{n}))\Delta tS(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p}(t))\right)=\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\tilde{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}_{\star}(t_{n}).

(107)

The
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star}
edge-integrals can be written in terms of
{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star}
as

\displaystyle E_{\star,x}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=-\nabla\psi_{\star,x}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}({{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star,i,j,k}-{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star,i+1,j,k})S_{i}^{c}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}),

(108)

\displaystyle E_{\star,y}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=-\nabla\psi_{\star,y}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}({{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star,i,j,k}-{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star,i,j+1,k})S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{c}(y_{p})S_{k}^{n}(z_{p}),

(109)

\displaystyle E_{\star,z}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=-\nabla\psi_{\star,z}^{S}(\bm{x}_{p})=\sum_{i,j,k}({{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star,i,j,k}-{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star,i,j,k+1})S_{i}^{n}(x_{p})S_{j}^{n}(y_{p})S_{k}^{c}(z_{p}).

(110)

Hence, equation (
107
) becomes a large, sparse linear system that is solved to obtain
{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star}
, and
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star}
is then obtained from equation (
100
). The particle velocities are now updated as

\bm{v}_{p}^{n}=\bm{v}_{p,\star}^{n}+\frac{q_{p}}{m_{p}}\bm{E}_{\star}^{n}(\bm{x}_{p})\Delta t.

(111)

We now have the corrected velocities at time
t_{n}
that give a divergence-free
{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
, calculated using equations (
62
)–(
64
). This corrected
{{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}}
can now be used for calculating
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
. The discretized vector potential
{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
is now calculated using equation (
89
) and then
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
is calculated using the discretized magnetic field definition given in equation (
83
) i.e.
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\mathbb{C}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
. The electric field at time
t_{n}
can now be calculated using the linear system in equation (
91
). All the large, sparse linear systems from equations (
89
), (
91
), (
92
) and (
107
) are solved in parallel using the
HYPRE
library
(
25
;
24
;
31
)
.

After the electric and magnetic fields at time
t_{n}
are calculated, the positions and velocities of the particles of species
s
are evolved using the following split scheme:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{p}^{-}=\bm{v}_{p}^{n}+\frac{q_{p}}{m_{p}}\bm{E}^{n}(x_{p}^{n})\frac{\Delta t}{2},

(112)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{R}(\bm{B}^{n}(x_{p}^{n}),\;((q_{p}/m_{p})|\bm{B}^{n}(x_{p}^{n})|\Delta t/2))\bm{v}_{p}^{-},

(113)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{p}^{n+1}=\bm{x}_{p}^{n}+\bm{v}_{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Delta t,

(114)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{p}^{+}=\mathbb{R}(\bm{B}^{n}(x_{p}^{n+1}),\;(q_{p}/m_{p})|\bm{B}^{n}(x_{p}^{n+1})|\Delta t/2)\bm{v}_{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(115)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{p}^{n+1}=\bm{v}_{p}^{+}+\frac{q_{p}}{m_{p}}\bm{E}^{n}(x_{p}^{n+1})\frac{\Delta t}{2}.

(116)

Equations (
113
) and (
115
) are
\bm{v}\times\bm{B}
rotation updates, where the magnitude of
\bm{v}
is retained after the update. The rotation matrix
\mathbb{R}(\bm{B},\alpha)
where
\bm{B}
is the magnetic field vector and
\alpha
is a dimensionless parameter, is given by

\mathbb{R}(\bm{B},\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}b_{x}b_{x}+(b_{y}b_{y}+b_{z}b_{z})c_{\alpha}&b_{z}s_{\alpha}+b_{x}b_{y}(1-c_{\alpha})&-b_{y}s_{\alpha}+b_{x}b_{z}(1-c_{\alpha})\\
-b_{z}s_{\alpha}+b_{y}b_{x}(1-c_{\alpha})&b_{y}b_{y}+(b_{x}b_{x}+b_{z}b_{z})c_{\alpha}&b_{x}s_{\alpha}+b_{y}b_{z}(1-c_{\alpha})\\
b_{y}s_{\alpha}+b_{x}b_{z}(1-c_{\alpha})&-b_{x}s_{\alpha}+b_{y}b_{z}(1-c_{\alpha})&b_{z}b_{z}+(b_{x}b_{x}+b_{y}b_{y})c_{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}.

(117)

Here,
c_{\alpha}=\cos(\alpha)
,
s_{\alpha}=\sin(\alpha)
and
{\bf b}=(b_{x},b_{y},b_{z})=\bm{B}/|\bm{B}|
, i.e. the unit vector in the direction of the magnetic field. Once the particle positions and velocities are available at time
t_{n+1}
, the entire procedure described above is repeated for the next time-step. This time-stepping scheme is second order accurate. For better readability, the time-stepping scheme is summarized as follows:

1.

Particle positions and velocities are available at time
t_{n}
. Equation (
107
) is solved to obtain The ‘divergence-corrector’ potential,
{{\bm{\mathsf{\uppsi}}}}_{\star}
. The ‘divergence-corrector’ electric field,
{{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}}_{\star}
, is then obtained by solving equation (
100
).

2.

Particle velocities are updated using equation (
111
).

3.

The divergence-free discrete current density,
{\bm{\mathsf{J}}}
, is calculated using equations (
62
)–(
64
).

4.

The discretized vector potential
{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
is calculated from equation (
89
) and
{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
is calculated using equation (
83
).

5.

{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}
is calculated using equation (
91
).

6.

Particle positions and velocities at time
t_{n+1}
are calculated using the standard second order Boris-push as per equations (
112
)–(
116
), completing the time-step.

The time-step restriction for the time-stepping scheme is given by

\Delta t\leq\frac{C}{|\Omega_{c,e}|},

(118)

where
C
is a constant. This is because the electron cyclotron wave with frequency
\Omega_{c,e}
is the fastest wave in the quasineutral model.

7
Dispersion relation

It is possible to perform a linear perturbation analysis of the Vlasov-Maxwell system to obtain a dispersion relation describing the various eigenmodes generated by the system. This has been shown by various authors such as
9
,
26
and
47
, to name a few. It must be noted that while these authors derive the dispersion relation for the non-quasineutral, full Vlasov-Maxwell system, the dispersion relation required here can be obtained from the full system by simply taking the quasineutral limit i.e.
\epsilon_{0}\to 0
. However, for the sake of completeness, we still describe the steps required to obtain the dispersion relation for the quasineutral Vlasov-Maxwell system. An equilibrium state is assumed where
\bm{E}=\bm{E}_{0}
,
\bm{B}=\bm{B}_{ext}
and
f_{s}=f_{s,0}
. Perturbations from equilibrium quantities are denoted by the subscript ‘1’. To obtain the dispersion relation, plane-wave perturbations of the form

\bm{E}_{1}=\hat{\bm{E}}e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}-\omega t)}

(119)

can be assumed, where
\bm{k}
is the wavenumber and
\omega
is the angular frequency for the plane-wave perturbation. Applying such perturbations to (
7
), (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
), we get

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{J}}=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int\hat{f_{s}}\bm{v}d\bm{v},

(120)

\displaystyle-i\omega\hat{f_{s}}+i\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{X}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\bm{k}\hat{f_{s}}+\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\left[{(\bm{V}\times\bm{B}_{ext})}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f_{s}}}{\partial\bm{V}}+{(\hat{\bm{E}}+\bm{V}\times\hat{\bm{B}})}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{s,0}}{\partial\bm{V}}\right]=0,

(121)

\displaystyle i\bm{k}\times\hat{\bm{B}}=\mu_{0}\hat{\bm{J}},

(122)

\displaystyle\omega\hat{\bm{B}}=\bm{k}\times\hat{\bm{E}}.

(123)

Eliminating
\hat{\bm{B}}
,
\hat{f_{s}}
and
\hat{\bm{J}}
from these equations, we obtain the equation

\bm{k}\times\bm{k}\times\hat{\bm{E}}+(\omega/c)^{2}\underline{\underline{\bm{\epsilon}}}\cdot\hat{\bm{E}}=0,

(124)

where
\underline{\underline{\epsilon}}
is the dielectric tensor of the plasma, that can be written as

\underline{\underline{\bm{\epsilon}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{xx}&\epsilon_{xy}&\epsilon_{xz}\\
\epsilon_{yx}&\epsilon_{yy}&\epsilon_{yz}\\
\epsilon_{zx}&\epsilon_{zy}&\epsilon_{zz}\end{bmatrix}.

(125)

The plasma dielectric tensor is a matrix that describes the behaviour of a magnetized plasma in the presence of an electric field. It determines the dispersion relation and contains all the information on linear wave propagation in a uniform magnetized plasma. Denoting the magnitude of
\bm{k}
as
k
, we can define the refractive index
n=kc/\omega
and the wavevector
\bm{\kappa}=\bm{k}/k
, equation (
124
) becomes

n^{2}\bm{\kappa}\times\bm{\kappa}\times\hat{\bm{E}}+\underline{\underline{\bm{\epsilon}}}\cdot\hat{\bm{E}}=0.

(126)

Taking the determinant of this linear system gives us the hot plasma dispersion relation

\det|n^{2}(\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}-\delta_{ij})+\epsilon_{ij}|=0.

(127)

Given the equivalence of all directions perpendicular to the static background magnetic field, we write
\underline{\underline{\bm{\epsilon}}}
in terms of
k_{\perp}
and
k_{\parallel}
, which are the components of
\bm{k}
perpendicular and parallel to the magnetic field, respectively. We assume
\theta
to be the angle between
\bm{k}
and the static background magnetic field. Without loss of generality, we define
k_{x}=k_{\perp}
,
k_{y}=0
and
k_{z}=k_{\parallel}
, and therefore get

\displaystyle k_{x}=k_{\perp}=k\sin(\theta)\Longrightarrow\kappa_{x}=\sin(\theta),

(128)

\displaystyle k_{z}=k_{\parallel}=k\cos(\theta)\Longrightarrow\kappa_{z}=\cos(\theta).

(129)

The background magnetic field is therefore along the
z
-direction. The hot plasma dispersion relation, thus becomes

\det\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{xx}-n^{2}\cos^{2}(\theta)&\epsilon_{xy}&\epsilon_{xz}+n^{2}\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)\\
\epsilon_{yx}&\epsilon_{yy}-n^{2}&\epsilon_{yz}\\
\epsilon_{zx}+n^{2}\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)&\epsilon_{zy}&\epsilon_{zz}-n^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)\end{bmatrix}=0.

(130)

We also define
n_{\perp}=n\sin(\theta)
and
n_{\parallel}=n\cos(\theta)
.
In the quasineutral case, the individual terms of the dielectric tensor
\underline{\underline{\bm{\epsilon}}}
are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xx}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\frac{n^{2}}{\lambda_{s}}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})e^{-\lambda_{s}}(-x_{0,s}Z(x_{n,s})),

(131)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xy}=-\epsilon_{yx}=-i\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}n[I^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda_{s})-I_{n}(\lambda_{s})]e^{-\lambda_{s}}(-x_{0,s}Z(x_{n,s})),

(132)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xz}=\epsilon_{zx}=-\frac{1}{2}n_{\perp}n_{\parallel}\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega\Omega_{c,s}}\frac{v_{th,s}^{2}}{c^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\frac{n}{\lambda_{s}}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})e^{-\lambda_{s}}(x_{0,s}^{2}Z^{\prime}(x_{n,s})),

(133)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{yy}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\left[\frac{n^{2}}{\lambda_{s}}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})-2\lambda_{s}[I^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda_{s})-I_{n}(\lambda_{s})]\right]e^{-\lambda_{s}}(-x_{0,s}Z(x_{n,s})),

(134)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{yz}=-\epsilon_{zy}=\frac{i}{2}n_{\perp}n_{\parallel}\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega\Omega_{c,s}}\frac{v_{th,s}^{2}}{c^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}[I^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda_{s})-I_{n}(\lambda_{s})]e^{-\lambda_{s}}(x_{0,s}^{2}Z^{\prime}(x_{n,s})),

(135)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{zz}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})e^{-\lambda_{s}}(x_{0,s}x_{n,s}Z^{\prime}(x_{n,s})).

(136)

In the above expressions,
\lambda_{s}
and
x_{n,s}
are dimensionless quantities given by

\lambda_{s}=\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}v_{th,s}^{2}}{2\Omega_{c,s}^{2}},

(137)

and

x_{n,s}=\frac{\omega-n\Omega_{c,s}}{k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}.

(138)

The terms
\omega_{p,s}
and
\Omega_{c,s}
are the plasma and cyclotron frequencies of species
s
. These frequencies, for the two species, are given by

\omega_{p,i}=\sqrt{\frac{Z_{i}^{2}n_{i}e^{2}}{\epsilon_{0}m_{i}}},~~~~\omega_{p,e}=\sqrt{\frac{n_{e}e^{2}}{\epsilon_{0}m_{e}}}

(139)

and

\Omega_{c,i}=\frac{eZ_{i}B_{ext}}{m_{i}},~~~~\Omega_{c,e}=-\frac{eB_{ext}}{m_{e}},

(140)

where
Z_{i}
is the ion atomic number,
e
is the electronic charge, and
B_{ext}
is the magnetic field magnitude. Note that the electron cyclotron frequency,
\Omega_{c,e}
, is defined as a negative value. The terms
n_{i}
and
n_{e}
are the ion and electron particle number density, respectively. Similarly,
m_{i}
and
m_{i}
denote the respective particle masses. Also,
v_{th,s}
is the thermal velocity of the species
s
given by

v_{th,s}=\sqrt{\frac{2T_{s}}{m_{s}}},

(141)

where
T_{s}
is the temperature of the species.
Also,
Z
is the plasma dispersion function and
I_{n}
denotes the modified Bessel function of order
n
.

7.1
Cold plasma approximation

To obtain a simplified dispersion relation for the case of a cold plasma, we assume the limit
T_{s}\rightarrow 0
. This also implies
\lambda_{s}\rightarrow 0
and
|x_{n,s}|\rightarrow\infty
, causing the components
\epsilon_{xz}
,
\epsilon_{zx}
,
\epsilon_{yz}
and
\epsilon_{zy}
of the dielectric tensor to vanish, i.e.

\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{xz}=\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{zx}=\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{yz}=\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{zy}=0.

(142)

The remaining terms simplify as

\displaystyle\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{xx}=\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{yy}=S=\frac{1}{2}(R+L)=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}-\Omega_{c,s}^{2}},

(143)

\displaystyle\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{xy}=-\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{yx}=-iD=\frac{1}{2i}(R-L)=-i\sum_{s}\frac{\Omega_{c,s}}{\omega}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}-\Omega_{c,s}^{2}},

(144)

\displaystyle\lim_{T_{s}\rightarrow 0}\epsilon_{zz}=P=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}.

(145)

Here the terms
R
and
L
are given by

\displaystyle R=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+\Omega_{c,s})},

(146)

\displaystyle L=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega(\omega-\Omega_{c,s})}.

(147)

Therefore, for a cold plasma, equation (
130
) simplifies to

\det\begin{bmatrix}S-n^{2}\cos^{2}(\theta)&-iD&n^{2}\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)\\
iD&S-n^{2}&0\\
n^{2}\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)&0&P-n^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)\end{bmatrix}=0.

(148)

This is the cold plasma dispersion relation. This approximation is helpful also in the case of warm plasmas, in obtaining simplified expressions for various wave frequencies. In the following two subsections, we analyze the warm and cold plasma dispersion relations to describe the waves that are obtained propagating in directions parallel and perpendicular to the background equilibrium magnetic field. Wherever possible, we use the cold plasma dispersion relation to obtain simplified expressions for the
k
-
\omega
relations. This works well for waves existing in cold plasmas. In other cases, i.e. for waves existing only in warm plasmas, we use the warm plasma dispersion relation to describe them.

7.2
Waves
\parallel
to
\bm{B}_{ext}

The dispersion relation for waves propagating in a direction parallel the background magnetic field can be obtained by substituting
\theta=0
in equation (
148
). We thus get the equation

\det\begin{bmatrix}S-n^{2}&-iD&0\\
iD&S-n^{2}&0\\
0&0&P\end{bmatrix}=0.

(149)

For the quasineutral case, there is no solution to the equation
P=0
. For the non-quasineutral case, the solution for this equation would have been the Langmuir wave. The remaining factor simplifies to the equations

\displaystyle n^{2}=S+D=R,

(150)

\displaystyle n^{2}=S-D=L.

(151)

For the R-mode wave in equation (
150
), since
\Omega_{c,e}
is negative, a positive solution for
\omega
exists only for
\omega<|\Omega_{c,e}|
. It is clear that
n\rightarrow+\infty
as
\omega\rightarrow|\Omega_{c,e}|^{-}
. This is a right-handed, transverse, circularly polarized wave, called the electron cyclotron wave (ECW), with the eigenvector
(E_{x},iE_{x},0)
. Similarly, for the L-mode in equation (
151
), a positive solution for
\omega
exists only for
\omega<|\Omega_{c,i}|
. In this case,
n\rightarrow+\infty
as
\omega\rightarrow|\Omega_{c,i}|^{-}
. This is a left-handed, transverse, circularly polarized wave and the corresponding eigenvector is
(E_{x},-iE_{x},0)
. This is called the ion cyclotron wave (ICW). Both these waves are seen below in Figures
3(b)
and
3(a)
in Section
8.1.2
. For a single-species plasma with kinetic electrons and stationary ions in the background, only the electron cyclotron wave (ECW) is observed, as can be seen in Figures
2(b)
and
2(a)
in Section
8.1.1
. The full, non-quasineutral Vlasov-Maxwell system generates two more solutions with components
E_{x}
and
E_{y}
, from the system of equations (
150
) and (
151
). These are a left-handed and a right-handed circularly polarized electromagnetic wave wherein
\omega\to ck
as
k\to\infty
.

The above cyclotron waves have been obtained from the cold plasma dispersion relation. However, there are other modes that do not exist in the cold plasma limit. Describing these waves requires the more generic hot plasma dispersion relation given in equation (
130
). Substituting
k_{\perp}=0
in the plasma dielectric tensor, the terms
\epsilon_{xz}
,
\epsilon_{yz}
,
\epsilon_{zx}
and
\epsilon_{zy}
simplify to 0, while the other terms are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xx}=\epsilon_{yy}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\left[Z\left(\frac{\omega-\Omega_{c,s}}{k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\right)+Z\left(\frac{\omega+\Omega_{c,s}}{k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\right)\right],

(152)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xy}=-\epsilon_{yx}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\left[Z\left(\frac{\omega-\Omega_{c,s}}{k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\right)-Z\left(\frac{\omega+\Omega_{c,s}}{k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\right)\right],

(153)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{zz}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}v_{th,s})^{2}}Z^{\prime}\left(\frac{\omega}{k_{\parallel}v_{th,s}}\right).

(154)

The dispersion relation becomes

((\epsilon_{xx}-n^{2})(\epsilon_{yy}-n^{2})-\epsilon_{xy}\epsilon_{yx})\epsilon_{zz}=0.

(155)

Besides the warm plasma generalizations of the cyclotron waves described above, the other solutions of this equation are the heavily damped, higher-order modes of Alfvén-cyclotron waves that have been studied by various researchers such as
1
,
3
and
42
. The modes obtained from the factor
((\epsilon_{xx}-n^{2})(\epsilon_{yy}-n^{2})-\epsilon_{xy}\epsilon_{yx})=0
, with eigenvector
(E_{x},E_{y},0)
manifest as straight lines emanating from (
k
,
\omega
) = (0,
\Omega_{c,e}
), forming a cone-shaped structure on the
k
-
\omega
plot. For a double-species plasma with ions, these modes also exist for the second cyclotron frequency, thus emanating from (
k
,
\omega
) = (0,
\Omega_{c,i}
). This structure can be clearly seen in Figures
2(b)
and
2(a)
in Section
8.1.1
and Figures
3(b)
and
3(a)
in Section
8.1.2
. The mode obtained from
\epsilon_{zz}=0
, with eigenvector
(0,0,E_{z})
also forms a similar cone-shaped structure emanating from (
k
,
\omega
) = (0, 0). This can be seen in Figures
2(c)
and
3(c)
. These higher-order modes are a thermal effect wherein the cone-angle decreases with temperature, reducing to zero in the cold plasma limit.

7.3
Waves
\perp
to
\bm{B}_{ext}

The dispersion relation for waves propagating in a direction parallel the background magnetic field can be obtained by substituting
\theta=\pi/2
in equation (
148
). We thus get the equation

\det\begin{bmatrix}S&-iD&0\\
iD&S-n^{2}&0\\
0&0&P-n^{2}\end{bmatrix}=0.

(156)

Again, for the quasineutral case, there is no solution for
P-n^{2}=0
. For the non-quasineutral case, this would have resulted in what is known as the ordinary or O-mode, which is an electromagnetic plasma wave with the component
E_{z}
. The remaining factor simplifies to

n^{2}=\frac{RL}{S}.

(157)

For a single-species plasma with kinetic electrons and stationary ions in the background, this reduces to

n^{2}=-\frac{\omega_{p,e}^{2}}{\omega^{2}},

(158)

which has no solution. For the two-species case with kinetic electrons as well as ions, there exists a solution for
\omega<\Omega_{H}
, where
\Omega_{H}
is termed as the hybrid frequency and is given by

\Omega_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{c,i}^{2}\omega_{p,e}^{2}+\Omega_{c,e}^{2}\omega_{p,i}^{2}}{\omega_{p,e}^{2}+\omega_{p,i}^{2}}}.

(159)

This value can be found by limiting equation (
157
) to
\infty
i.e.
S=0
. It can also be seen that
n\rightarrow+\infty
as
\omega\rightarrow|\Omega_{H}|^{-}
. The eigenvector for this wave is
(E_{x},-i(S/D)E_{x},0)
. We see this wave in Figures
6(a)
,
6(b)
,
6(c)
,
6(d)
,
7(a)
and
7(b)
in Section
8.2.2
below. For the full, non-quasineutral Vlasov-Maxwell system, equation (
157
) produces three more solutions. For a two-species plasma, there is a second hybrid wave, wherein the eigenfrequency
\omega
approaches a constant value as
k\to\infty
. There are also two more solutions that are called the extraordinary or X-modes. These X-modes are electromagnetic modes wherein
\omega\to ck
as
k\to\infty
.

We now use the hot plasma dispersion relation to look at waves that do not exist in the cold plasma limit. Substituting
k_{\parallel}=0
in the plasma dielectric tensor, the terms
\epsilon_{xz}
,
\epsilon_{yz}
,
\epsilon_{zx}
and
\epsilon_{zy}
simplify to 0, while the other terms are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xx}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{s}}}{\lambda_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\frac{n^{2}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})}{\omega-n\Omega_{c,s}},

(160)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{xy}=-\epsilon_{yx}=-i\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega}e^{-\lambda_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\frac{n[I^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda_{s})-I_{n}(\lambda_{s})]}{\omega-n\Omega_{c,s}},

(161)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{yy}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{s}}}{\lambda_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\frac{n^{2}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})+2\lambda_{s}^{2}I_{n}(\lambda_{s})-2\lambda_{s}^{2}I^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda_{s})}{\omega-n\Omega_{c,s}},

(162)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{zz}=-\sum_{s}\frac{\omega_{p,s}^{2}}{\omega}e^{-\lambda_{s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\frac{I_{n}(\lambda_{s})}{\omega-n\Omega_{c,s}}.

(163)

The dispersion relation becomes

(\epsilon_{xx}(\epsilon_{yy}-n^{2})-\epsilon_{xy}\epsilon_{yx})(\epsilon_{zz}-n^{2})=0.

(164)

We first consider the equation
(\epsilon_{zz}-n^{2})=0
, with eigenvector
(0,0,E_{z})
. From equation (
163
), it can be clearly seen that the refractive index
n=n_{\perp}
becomes infinite at all harmonics of the cyclotron frequencies i.e.
\omega=m\Omega_{c,s}

where
m
is a positive integer and
s
can be electrons or ions. Besides these cyclotron harmonic resonances, there are no wave solutions to this equation. Without the quasineutral assumption, an electromagnetic plasma wave would have been a solution to this equation. The other factor, i.e.
(\epsilon_{xx}(\epsilon_{yy}-n^{2})-\epsilon_{xy}\epsilon_{yx})=0
, also gives rise to these resonances on account of the
(\omega-n\Omega_{c,s})
terms in the denominators seen on the RHS of equations (
160
)–(
162
). The other solutions to this equation are the Bernstein waves
(
5
)
with the associated eigenvector
(E_{x},E_{y},0)
. We note that the
x
-direction is the direction of wave propagation, and therefore the
E_{x}
Bernstein waves are longitudinal in nature, while the
E_{y}
Bernstein waves are transverse. The cyclotron harmonic resonances can be seen in Figures (
4
) and (
6
)
in the wave spectra of all field components. The Bernstein waves can be clearly seen in Figures (
4(b)
), (
4(d)
), (
6(b)
) and (
6(d)
). The Bernstein waves also exist in Figures (

), (
4(c)
), (
6(a)
) and (
6(c)
) but cannot be distinctly seen on account of overlap with the cyclotron harmonic resonances.

8
Numerical tests

In this section, we perform various tests to validate our algorithm, by drawing comparisons between the various waves obtained from simulations and the corresponding analytical relations obtained from the dispersion relation. In these tests, a uniform quasineutral plasma with Gaussian distributions for each species, under constant initial background magnetic fields is considered. The simulation is allowed to run for a long time and the Fast-Fourier Transform (FFT) of the resultant electric field time-series data is obtained. The FFT spectrum can then be plotted on a
k
-
\omega
plot along with their analytical counterparts. For all these simulations, the particles are generated using a quasi-random Sobol sampler. The computational domain is a quasi-1D domain with domain size
[0,10]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
and a computational grid of
256\times 8\times 8
cells. The higher 256-cell resolution is taken in the direction along which the wave spectrum is to be studied. Periodic boundary conditions are applied at all boundaries. Next, we perform a test where a single sinusoidal perturbation is initialized and its damping rate is compared with the theoretical result obtained from the dispersion relation. Finally, we conduct studies to assess the scalability of the code.

8.4
Parallelization strategy and scalability

The implementation used in this work is based on the
AMReX
software framework
(
52
)
, which provides support for block-structured parallel simulations using distributed-memory parallelism via MPI. The computational domain is decomposed into grid patches that are distributed across MPI ranks using the load-balancing mechanisms provided by
AMReX
. Particle management, inter-process communication, and particle redistribution are handled through the framework’s built-in parallel infrastructure. Linear systems arising from the numerical model are solved using the
HYPRE
library through the interfaces provided by
AMReX
.

As expected in most PIC codes, the dominant portion of the overall computational cost arises from particle loops. The key particle operations for this model include pushing particles using electromagnetic fields calculated at particle positions, and particle-to-mesh depositions to calculate particle-based quantities defined on the grid spaces. These particle-based quantities include charge density, current density, the Lorentz force and stress tensor terms on the right hand side of the curl-curl equation (
91
), the particle contributions to the charge matrix on the left hand side of the curl-curl equation (
91
), and the particle contributions to the ‘divergence-corrector’ potential matrix on the left hand side of equation (
107
). For a double-species simulation using 500 particles per cell, the computational costs of the costliest operations as percentages of the overall cost in descending order are:

1.

Calculating particle contributions to the charge matrix on the left hand side of the curl-curl equation (
91
) and building matrix
\approx 40\%
.

2.

Calculating particle contributions to the ‘divergence-corrector’ potential matrix on the left hand side of equation (
107
) and building matrix
\approx 40\%
.

3.

Pushing particles using electromagnetic fields
\approx 3\%
.

4.

Solving linear systems in equations (
89
), (
91
) and (
107
)
\approx 3\%
.

5.

Calculating charge density, current density, the Lorentz force and stress tensor terms on the right hand side of the curl-curl equation (
91
)
\approx 3\%
.

With increasing number of particles per cell, the overall percentage of the cost of solving the linear systems reduces even further. While these percentages vary slightly, depending on the individual problem parameters, they are a reliable representation of the relative computational costs for all the problems considered in this work. Thus, overall performance and scalability are primarily governed by the efficiency of particle operations.

Strong and weak scaling studies were conducted to evaluate the parallel performance of the code. The case with the perpendicular wave propagation spectrum for a double-species plasma with
m_{i}/m_{e}=4

v_{th,e}=0.5c

A total number of 500 particles per cell was used for each species. For strong scaling studies, a mesh size of
1024\times 8\times 8

For a fixed number of time steps, the simulation was run using 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128 MPI tasks, and the times required for each case were recorded. The metric used to assess strong scaling performance is the speedup, given by

Speedup=t(1)/t(N)

9
Summary, discussion and conclusions

A new numerical scheme based on the geometric, structure-preserving Particle-in-Cell (PIC) model has been developed for the quasineutral, fully kinetic Vlasov-Maxwell equations using mimetic finite differences on dual grids. This capability has been added to the C
++
-based parallel framework,
GEMPICX
. The scheme has been successfully tested by comparisons between the dispersion relation and results obtained from numerical simulations. This work also demonstrated the capabilities of the scheme in capturing a higher-order process such as damping, through a test case involving the damping of a cyclotron wave.
Scaling tests have also been conducted, demonstrating encouraging parallel performance on the tested computing platform, although additional studies will be required to assess performance for exascale applications.

Our model uses a vector potential
\bm{A}
to obtain the magnetic field using the
\nabla\times\bm{A}
. This results in a curl-curl equation in
\bm{A}
i.e. equation (
10
). We then use the Coulomb gauge condition
\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0
that reduces the curl-curl operator to a Laplace operator resulting in equation (
12
). This Laplace operator is invertible up to a constant, making it easy to solve for
\bm{A}
and obtain a unique
\bm{B}
. This, however, is only possible due to the periodic boundary conditions in the test cases considered in this work. The periodic boundaries make the imposition of
\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0
and its discrete counterpart
\tilde{\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{H}_{1}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=0
straightforward. They also ensure that the discrete de Rham structure is exactly preserved, keeping in line with the idea of structure-preserving methods. However, the use of this module for more realistic scientific applications, such as fusion energy, requires more general, physically accurate non-periodic boundary conditions. With non-periodic conditions, the Coulomb gauge condition
\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0
would not be automatically satisfied and therefore the curl-curl system would not have a unique solution. To obtain meaningful and unique solutions, we would require additional compatibility constraints on
\bm{A}
and the modification of equation (
89
). For example, a perfectly conducting wall with unit normal
\bm{n}
would have
\bm{A}\times\bm{n}={\bf 0}
, while a perfect magnetic conductor would have
\bm{n}\times(\nabla\times\bm{A})={\bf 0}
. Such non-periodic boundaries would also require appropriate boundary trace spaces within the discrete de Rham structure
(
2
)
. The treatment of boundary conditions for the vector potential in a discrete de Rham framework has been extensively discussed in the context of computational electromagnetism
(
8
;
29
;
44
)
, where gauge compatibility and tangential constraints must be handled carefully in non-periodic domains. The principal goal of the current work has been to extend the capabilities of the
GEMPICX
framework to the quasineutral regime. Introducing non-periodic boundary conditions and the corresponding treatment of the discrete vector potential at boundaries would require a deeper analysis, and this is left for follow-up studies.

While the present work only employs second-order Hodge operators, it is worth emphasizing that the mimetic framework naturally admits Hodge discretizations of arbitrary order. Since the Hodge operators determine the spatial accuracy of the discrete Maxwell operator, their order directly influences the accuracy of the underlying field discretization and therefore of wave propagation. In the context of geometric discretizations of the full Vlasov-Maxwell system, higher-order Hodge operators have been shown to improve the representation of short-wavelength modes and reduce numerical dispersion errors, leading to more accurate discrete dispersion relations
(
33
)
. However, the quasineutral approximation considered here removes the high-frequency electromagnetic modes that are most directly affected by such dispersion errors. Consequently, the extent to which higher-order Hodge operators improve the accuracy or efficiency of geometric quasineutral PIC simulations remains an open question. Investigating their impact on low-frequency quasineutral plasma dynamics would require a dedicated study and is left for future work. The numerical implementation of these operators involves denser matrices for the operators themselves and also for linear systems such as equation (
91
), increasing computational complexity as compared to the low-order formulation used here.

Quasineutral fluid models such as magnetohydrodynamics are known to contain sharp gradients and discontinuities such as shocks. Fluid models employ techniques such as limiting, shock-capturing, artificial dissipation and Godunov-type solvers to handle such regimes
(
48
;
4
)
. On the other hand, PIC methods, such as the one presented in this work, are not primarily shock-capturing schemes. In regimes where strong gradients or shock-like structures arise, PIC methods represent such features through the underlying particle distribution rather than as true discontinuities in macroscopic fields
(
6
)
. While the present work focusses on smooth test cases in order to verify the dispersion properties and consistency of the proposed discretization, various PIC studies on shock formation are available in the existing literature
(
27
;
6
;
51
;
12
;
45
)
. Geometric discretizations are built using differential and integral operators on the basis of vector calculus identities, and therefore assume smooth fields. Therefore, strong gradients may reduce the accuracy of such spatial discretizations and produce oscillations. In PIC simulations, strong gradients can also amplify particle noise. Numerical stabilization in such regimes typically focuses on improving robustness and controlling particle noise through techniques such as higher-order particle shapes, filtering, or current smoothing
(
30
)
.
We note that structure-preserving approaches for problems with reduced regularity have been investigated in related Eulerian settings, including Hamiltonian and energy-conserving discontinuous Galerkin discretizations for plasma kinetic equations
(
15
;
28
)
. However, the treatment of shocks and strongly non-smooth solutions within geometric PIC frameworks remains largely unexplored. Investigating the behavior of the present structure-preserving formulation in strongly nonlinear regimes is therefore an interesting direction for future work.

While there are several semi-implicit
(
37
;
40
)
and fully implicit
(
13
;
12
)
PIC approaches that enforce energy conservation at the discrete level, our current scheme maintains energy conservation exactly only at the semi-discrete level. After time discretization, energy conservation is only accurate up to the order of the time integrator. This is because the action principle we used for the numerical algorithm was discretized in space but not in time. The time integrator was then obtained by further discretizing in time the semi-discrete governing equations obtained from the action principle. While the focus of this work was extending de Rham structure-preserving methods to quasineutral models, it would be beneficial to extend such methods to exact discrete energy conservation as well. A next logical extension of our method could be to make the method variational in time as well, by discretizing time at the level of the action principle itself, rather than discretizing the resulting equations. Such a fully variational discretization would preserve exact energy conservation after time discretization, significantly enhancing long-term stability
(
34
;
46
;
41
)
.

There are several other interesting avenues for further research. The current work only performs tests that use a Cartesian grid on slab geometry. Application of this model to more realistic magnetic confinement fusion applications such as tokamaks or stellarators, would involve toroidal geometries and therefore require discretizations on curvilinear meshes. This could be achieved by extending the present structure-preserving discretization framework to curvilinear elements, similar to the work of
35
. Another interesting avenue could be the study of wave propagation in a non-constant background magnetic field, which would be of interest to the edge physics community. More recently,
GEMPICX
has been extended by
43
to the gyrokinetic model in the Zero Larmor Radius limit, namely the drift-kinetic (DK) model
(
36
)
. They also coupled the DK model for electrons with the fully kinetic model for ions, resulting in a hybrid drift-kinetic model. A possible future direction for our structure-preserving scheme is its extension to the quasineutral limit of this hybrid drift-kinetic model. Apart from the electromagnetic and Langmuir waves, the quasineutral hybrid model would remove the description of electron cyclotron waves and their harmonics, further reducing time and length scale restrictions on the numerical model.
