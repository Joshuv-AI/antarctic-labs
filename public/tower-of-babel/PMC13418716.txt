Mechanical forces in bacterial pathogenesis: Sensing, tropism, and intervention
Authors: Yixin Jiang, Gaixin Xu, Haobo Liang, Yuying Wang, Guowen Liu, Xinyuan Pan, Lan Yang, Hongxia Liu, Youwen Liu, Yanfang Ren, Xiaolin Wang, Qiusheng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBacterial pathogenesis unfolds within mechanically complex host niches, yet physical forces are frequently overlooked as active drivers of virulence. Here, we argue that mechanical cues—shear, confinement, and matrix rheology—serve as essential regulatory inputs that dynamically reprogram bacterial physiology. We propose a multi-scale framework connecting force-dependent receptor kinetics and envelope-stress signaling to community-level biofilm viscoelasticity. This synthesis elucidates how bacteria interpret the specific force landscapes of human tissues, providing a mechanistic rationale for tissue-tropism that biochemical models alone cannot resolve. Finally, we posit that the “physicality” of infection presents an unexploited therapeutic frontier. By outlining strategies to target mechanotransduction nodes or exploit biofilm mechanical fragilities, we demonstrate how mechanics-based interventions offer orthogonal strategies to bypass antimicrobial resistance, shifting the paradigm from chemical inhibition to physical disruption.KEYWORDS: Antimicrobial resistance, bacterial pathogenesis, biofilm mechanics, host-pathogen interaction, mechanobiology, mechanotransduction

Introduction

Bacterial pathogenesis unfolds within a physically dynamic theater. While bacteria are traditionally viewed as chemical processors that integrate biochemical cues, they are equally defined by their interaction with mechanical inputs—ranging from fluid shear stress and osmotic pressure to tissue stiffness and confinement forces [1–3]. Decades of research have established that these physical forces are not merely obstacles to be endured but are decisive regulatory signals that reprogram bacterial survival, adaptation, and virulence [4,5]. Bacteria possess a sophisticated mechanotransduction apparatus—comprising mechanosensitive ion channels, flagellar motors, pili, and envelope-spanning complexes—that detects and translates mechanical loads into intracellular biochemical cascades, thereby coordinating adaptive cellular responses [6–9].

Despite this progress, the role of mechanics in infection has often been studied in isolation, focusing on specific sensors or single species. Only recently has a more systemic view emerged: mechanical cues act as continuous inputs that orchestrate the entire infection cycle [5,10]. In the early adhesion phase, force-induced conformational changes in surface adhesins function as “mechanical locks,” strengthening attachment under flow [11–13]. As colonization progresses, the viscoelasticity of the biofilm matrix and the stiffness of host tissues modulate motility patterns and interspecies cooperation, shaping the architecture of infectious foci [14–16]. Crucially, mechanical stress is now known to tune virulence directly—regulating pathogenicity islands and toxin secretion via two-component systems and second messengers like cyclic dimeric guanosine monophosphate (c-di-GMP)—allowing pathogens to optimize their phenotype for specific physical microenvironments [17–19]. This interaction is bidirectional: just as hosts deploy mechanical defenses like epithelial extrusion, bacteria actively remodel the host’s mechanical state to evade immunity, creating a dynamic physical contest that determines infection outcomes [20–22].

A major unresolved question is how these universal mechanical mechanisms drive tissue-specific infection phenotypes. The force landscapes of the human body are highly heterogeneous—contrasting the turbulent shear of the cardiovascular system with the compressive loading of the oral cavity or the static confinement of the urinary tract [23,24]. How bacteria interpret these distinct force profiles to exhibit tissue tropism remains insufficiently defined [25,26]. This gap limits our ability to predict pathogenesis and underscores the need for a mechanics-informed framework, particularly in the face of escalating antimicrobial resistance [27]. As pathogens fortify themselves through biofilm formation and efflux pumps, traditional biochemical antibiotics face diminishing returns [28–30].

To address these challenges, we propose that the “physicality” of infection offers an orthogonal therapeutic frontier. Mechanics-based anti-infective strategies provide three complementary levers: (i) targeting the mechanotransduction nodes (e.g. MscL and MscS) to blind bacteria to environmental cues [9,31–33]; (ii) applying mechanical disruption to breach biofilm barriers and potentiate drug penetration [34,35]; and (iii) engineering biomaterials with “mechano-bactericidal” topographies or deploying active micro/nanorobots to physically clear infections [36–38].

In this review, we advance a multi-scale framework that treats mechanical forces as causal drivers of pathogenesis. We first synthesize the molecular and cellular mechanisms of bacterial mechanotransduction, connecting sensors like pili and channels to downstream transcriptional control [6,13]. We then map these modules to the characteristic force landscapes of major organ systems, providing a mechanistic rationale for tissue-selective tropism that biochemical models alone cannot resolve [14,26,39]. Finally, framing infection biology as a quantitative and engineerable mechanical system, we outline a paradigm shift from chemical inhibition to physical disruption, distilling actionable principles to guide next-generation antimicrobial strategies [36,40,41].

Mechanosensing and mechanotransduction: from interface structures to gene regulation

To navigate the physical landscape of infection, bacteria deploy a diverse array of mechanosensory architectures—including mechanosensitive ion channels, envelope-spanning complexes, flagellar motors, and adhesive pili—that interface directly with the host environment. This apparatus functions not merely as a passive detector but as a rapid transducer, converting mechanical inputs (such as shear stress, osmotic pressure, and surface contact) into intracellular electrophysiological and biochemical signals. These immediate physical stimuli are subsequently consolidated by global regulatory networks, including two-component systems (TCS) and second messenger pathways (e.g. c-di-GMP and cAMP). This multi-layered integration links instantaneous mechanical reception to long-term transcriptional reprogramming, orchestrating essential pathogenic behaviors ranging from motility and metabolic adaptation to virulence factor secretion and biofilm development. Mechanosensory structures and interfaces at the bacterial surface

Bacteria interface with their physical environment through a specialized array of mechanosensory architectures. At the forefront are mechanosensitive ion channels, which function as distinct sensor–transducer units [42]. Embedded within the cytoplasmic membrane, these proteins operate via a “force-from-lipid” mechanism, where lateral tension in the lipid bilayer drives conformational rearrangements that gate the pore [43–48]. The two principal families, MscL and MscS, govern complementary tension regimes [49]. MscL acts as a high-threshold emergency release valve; upon acute hypo-osmotic shock, it undergoes a large-scale opening to permit the massive efflux of osmolytes, thereby preventing lysis [43,50]. Conversely, MscS is tuned to lower tension thresholds, regulating routine osmoregulatory homeostasis through subtle gating transitions [42,47,51,52]. Together, they convert membrane mechanics directly into electrochemical fluxes.

Beyond the membrane, the cell wall and envelope-spanning complexes maintain mechanical continuity [2,53]. These structures do not merely support cell shape but actively transmit physical stress to inner-membrane receptors. Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), coupled to the peptidoglycan matrix, modulate envelope-stress signaling to maintain structural integrity [2,54,55]. The transmission logic differs by phylogeny: in Gram-negative bacteria, lipoprotein tethers relay mechanical information from the peptidoglycan to the outer membrane, stabilizing the envelope against shear; in Gram-positive bacteria, the cell wall functions as a thick, porous hydrogel (approx. 60 nm pores) where distinct connector proteins span the wall–membrane interface to initiate intracellular responses [56].

Motility organelles also serve as sophisticated force probes. The bacterial flagellum functions as a dual-purpose mechanochemical motor [8,57,58]. Its basal body detects fluid shear and viscous load through the stator (MotA/MotB) and the rotor switch complex (FliG, FliM, FliN) [59,60]. For instance, in Salmonella, increases in load (surface contact or high viscosity) trigger conformational changes in the switch complex, which recruit additional stators to increase torque. This mechanosensitivity feeds into the chemotaxis network, remodeling gene expression to tune motility patterns and virulence phenotypes [58,61–63]. Similarly, Type IV pili (T4P) act as retractile force sensors [64]. During surface exploration, cycles of extension and retraction generate tensile loads [65]. Upon surface engagement, the resistance to retraction induces conformational stretching of the major pilin subunits. This mechanical stress is transduced into the cytoplasm, activating the cAMP signaling pathway to upregulate virulence factors and initiate biofilm formation, effectively coupling surface contact to pathogenic colonization [9,66,67].

Mechanotransduction pathways: two-component systems and second-messenger networks

While structural sensors detect immediate physical cues, the interpretation and integration of these signals rely on global transduction networks—primarily TCS and second messengers. TCSs, composed of a sensor histidine kinase (HK) and a cognate response regulator (RR), serve as the primary logic gates for envelope stress [68–70]. In many contexts, TCSs sense mechanics indirectly: primary sensors (like ion channels or pili) alter the ionic or periplasmic environment, which is then detected by HKs such as EnvZ or PhoQ [7,71]. Mechanical perturbations, such as osmotic shifts or envelope deformation, induce conformational activation and autophosphorylation of the HK, driving a phosphorelay to the RR that reprograms transcriptional landscapes [72–75]. This system functionally couples physical stress to adaptive gene regulation [71].

To coordinate complex multicellular behaviors, bacteria utilize second-messenger networks, most notably c-di-GMP and cAMP. The c-di-GMP network functions as a central decision-making hub for the “lifestyle switch” between motility and sessility [76,77]. Its architecture allows for parallel processing: distinct mechanosensory inputs differentially activate specific diguanylate cyclases (DGCs) or phosphodiesterases (PDEs). This creates localized or global shifts in c-di-GMP pools, which bind effector proteins to arrest motility, trigger matrix production, and orchestrate biofilm development [15,78–80]. In parallel, the cAMP pathway integrates surface-sensing signals. Rapid fluctuations in cAMP levels, modulated by adenylyl cyclases and specific phosphodiesterases, regulate effectors like the cAMP receptor protein (CRP) [81]. Together with quorum sensing systems, these second messengers consolidate mechanical inputs (shear, attachment) with chemical cues, forming the core information-processing scaffold that allows bacteria to optimize fitness and virulence within dynamic host microenvironments [67,82–84].

Mechanoregulation of bacterial pathogenic mechanisms

Mechanical forces are integral physical components of the host microenvironment and serve as a central axis linking mechanics to infection biology. Acting across scales—from macroscopic tissue distortion and fluid shear to microscopic intercellular tension and intra-biofilm stress—these forces dictate bacterial pathogenic potential (Table 1) [85–92]. Mechanistically, force does not merely act as a physical constraint but serves as a regulatory input that orchestrates adhesion, colonization, dissemination, and host–pathogen crosstalk. Mechanical regulation also extends to bacterial cell-cycle control. Recent work showed that growth-induced buckling in filamentous E. coli modulates Min-protein patterning and thereby affects divisome positioning and cell division, indicating that mechanical strain can feed directly into core bacterial morphogenetic programs [93] (Figure 2).

Mechanoregulation of the bacterial infection process

Adhesion mechanics and molecular switching

Adhesion represents the primary checkpoint for infection, determining whether a pathogen overcomes hydrodynamic washout to establish a foothold. This process relies on the precise mechanosensing and structural remodeling of bacterial surface adhesins. Bacterial organelles, such as flagella and pili, function not only as propulsion units but also as mechanoprobes that gauge substrate properties. Simultaneously, mechanical loads remodel the host extracellular matrix (ECM) and cell surface receptors, often exposing cryptic ligand sites that favor bacterial binding.

To transition from transient attachment to stable colonization, bacteria must accommodate local mechanical features. This adaptation is driven by force-induced conformational changes in surface proteins. In P. aeruginosa, the T4P tip protein PilY1 acts as a mechanosensor containing a von Willebrand A-like (vWA) domain. Under mechanical load, this domain undergoes sustained conformational rearrangement, which triggers an elevation in intracellular c-di-GMP levels and activates biofilm-associated pathways. A single cysteine-to-alanine substitution in PilY1 diminishes this force-triggered switching, directly linking molecular mechanosensing to the colonization phenotype [85]. Reciprocally, forces remodel host ligands to enhance susceptibility. For instance, the interaction between Staphylococcus aureus Protein A (SpA) and host von Willebrand factor (vWF) is shear-enhanced: fluid flow unfolds vWF, exposing high-affinity cryptic binding sites for SpA. This force-dependent “unmasking” significantly potentiates bacterial adhesion to platelets and sites of vascular injury [86] (Figure 3(A)).

Biophysical adaptation and bond dynamics

As bacteria engage with a surface, they actively tune their geometry and binding kinetics. In P. aeruginosa, fluid shear modulates T4P dynamics to optimize cell orientation. Under low shear, pili retraction tilts the cell body away from the surface, promoting detachment; conversely, high shear counteracts this torque, flattening the cell against the substrate to prolong residence time and secure attachment [89].

To withstand these forces, bacterial adhesins have evolved specialized mechanical properties. In S. epidermidis, adhesins contain “sacrificial domains” that unfold under tension. These domains function as molecular shock absorbers, dissipating energy to protect the structural integrity of the adhesin–ligand complex. Simulation and deletion mutants demonstrate that losing these domains compromises adhesion stability under flow [87] (Figure 3(B)). Furthermore, many bacteria utilize “catch bonds”—bonds that strengthen under tensile load—to resist washout. A classic example is the FimH adhesin of E. coli [25]. Similarly, intestinal bacteria employ dockerin–cohesin complexes that undergo allosteric regulation to switch between binding modes (rupture forces of ~200 pN vs ~500 pN) depending on the loading rate, thereby securing colonization amidst the turbulent shear of the gut [94] (Figure 3(C)).

Community mechanics and biofilm architecture

Colonization is rarely a solitary event; it involves complex community mechanics. Mechanical cues fundamentally reshape biofilm architecture by modulating motility and interspecies cooperation. In multispecies contexts, mechanics governs cooperative succession. In oral biofilms, viable S. oralis enzymatically degrades salivary conditioning films, mechanically exposing the substrate for S. mutans adhesion. The viability and metabolic activity of the pioneer species are thus mechanically translated into the structural stability of the entire consortium [95].

In C. crescentus biofilms, fluid shear dictates clonal organization: low shear permits motility and extensive clonal mixing, facilitating cooperation; high shear suppresses motility and enforces spatial segregation between lineages. This mechanically driven segregation reduces cooperation, resulting in dispersed, less stable microcolonies [90].

Dissemination: hijacking and evasion

Dissemination marks the expansion of infection from a localized niche, a process governed by the interplay between bacterial mechanotransduction and host tissue mechanics. While host-pathogen signaling is well-studied, the “mechanical hijacking” of host machinery is an emerging paradigm.

Intracellular pathogens actively subvert host cytoskeletal mechanics to drive spreading. In Shigella flexneri, the effector IpaH7.8 retains the host GTPase Rab13 on the remnants of the bacteria-containing vacuole (BCV). Rab13 then recruits the dynein motor complex via specific adaptors. The resulting dynein-mediated forces strip the vacuolar remnants from the bacterial surface, facilitating cytosolic escape and efficient cell-to-cell spreading [96] (Figure 4(A)).

Conversely, the host deploys mechanical defenses to contain infection. In epithelial monolayers, uninfected neighbors generate collective compressive forces that squeeze softer, infected cells. This mechanical mismatch drives the extrusion of the infected cell from the monolayer, sacrificing the individual to preserve barrier integrity [20]. However, ECM mechanics can tip this balance. On soft substrates, infection remains predominantly cluster-type, and the relatively intact or polymerized cytoskeleton restricts bacterial movement within the primary infected-cell cluster. In contrast, stiff substrates generate increased tensile force and promote cytoskeletal remodeling and depolymerization. This depolymerized cytoskeletal state facilitates bacterial dispersal to neighboring cells and reduces intracellular antibiotic accumulation, thereby linking tissue mechanics to both bacterial dissemination and therapeutic failure [97] (Figure 4(B)).

Mechanoregulation of bacterial virulence

Bacterial virulence is not a static property but a dynamic response governed by the precise spatiotemporal regulation of virulence factors. While traditional models emphasize biochemical cues, mechanical forces – including fluid shear, hydrostatic pressure, and membrane tension – serve as equally critical co-regulators. Bacteria utilize these physical inputs as navigational beacons to identify host niches, activating signaling cascades that reprogram the transcriptional landscape. This mechanochemical integration allows pathogens to fine-tune virulence programs, such as toxin secretion and invasion, to match the specific physical context of the infection site [26,91].

From an evolutionary perspective, mechanochemical discrimination may help bacteria activate costly virulence programs only within permissive host niches. By sensing shear, surface contact, and confinement, pathogens can couple local mechanical cues to virulence activation, as shown by EHEC, in which host attachment and fluid shear promote LEE-dependent gene expression [25,98]. Because virulence-gene expression can increase membrane permeability and envelope-stress sensitivity, restricting these programs to appropriate mechanical contexts may conserve resources, reduce premature immune exposure, and improve the timing of pathogenic activation [99].

Surface organelles act as the primary antennas for this regulation. Extensive evidence demonstrates that flagella and pili function as dual-purpose structures: beyond locomotion and adhesion, they relay mechanical stress to downstream regulatory networks. Broadly, the flagellar basal body detects rotational load (viscosity/friction), while pili transduce tensile stress generated during retraction. At the molecular scale, these inputs trigger conformational changes that activate virulence pathways. For instance, in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, force-dependent remodeling of the surface protein PilY1 activates the cAMP pathway and downstream virulence programs. This direct coupling ensures that energy-expensive virulence factors are deployed only when the bacterium physically engages with a target interface [85].

Mechanoregulation extends beyond the individual cell to the multicellular community. In S. aureus biofilms, periodic mechanical loading reshapes physical architecture and modulates metabolic gradients. Under nutrient-limited conditions, mechanical deformation restricts the diffusion of autoinducing peptides (AIP), leading to their local accumulation. This triggers the accessory gene regulator (agr) quorum-sensing system – specifically upregulating agrC and RNAIII—to drive exotoxin and protease expression. Simultaneously, nutrient fluctuations tune the SaeRS two-component system to enhance adhesin production. Thus, mechanical stress acts as a master switch that coordinates agr and SaeRS to reprogram the global virulence profile, balancing sessile persistence with aggressive dissemination [88] (Figure 5(A)).

Transcriptional reprogramming is also sensitive to the magnitude of fluid shear, which varies drastically across host tissues. This “shear-sensing” capability allows bacteria to distinguish between niches. In Salmonella enterica, the transition from the low-shear environment of the intestinal lumen to the high-shear hemodynamic environment of the bloodstream triggers a profound transcriptomic shift. High shear stress selectively remodels the expression of Salmonella Pathogenicity Islands (SPI-1, SPI-4, and SPI-5), enabling the pathogen to adaptively tune its invasion and survival strategies to the prevailing hydrodynamic forces [26,91,100].

A striking example of molecular mechanotransduction governing virulence is found in enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC). Here, the transcription factor GrlA is normally sequestered at the membrane in an inactive state. Mechanical force generated during initial adhesion physically triggers the liberation of GrlA into the cytoplasm, where it binds to the LEE1 promoter within the Locus of Enterocyte Effacement (LEE). Fluid shear further amplifies this activation, increasing LEE1 output by 3- to 4-fold compared to static conditions. This initiates a positive feedback loop: LEE-encoded Intimin and Tir proteins strengthen adhesion, which generates higher traction forces, further boosting GrlA release and virulence expression. Recent studies indicate that the BaeSR two-component system also integrates these mechanical signals during adhesion, providing a parallel pathway to ensure robust colonization of the human colon [92,98,101] (Figure 5(B)). Finally, bacterial mechanosensing extends beyond detecting absolute force magnitude to “rheosensing” – the ability to detect the rate of shear flow. P. aeruginosa utilizes the fro operon to sense flow dynamics with high sensitivity, allowing fine-tuned control of gene expression in dynamic mucosal or vascular environments [102] (Figure 5(C)).

Mechanoregulation of bacteria–host cell interactions during infection

The interface between bacteria and host cells functions as a dynamic mechanical theater. While traditionally viewed through the lens of immune signaling, this interaction is equally defined by a bidirectional “mechanical tug-of-war.” Host cells actively constrain infection by remodeling tissue tension and deploying mechanically driven clearance mechanisms; reciprocally, pathogens adapt by tuning their intrinsic stiffness or by sensing host mechanical vulnerabilities to subvert immunity.

Host mechanical defenses: extrusion and surveillance

The host epithelium serves as the first line of mechanical defense. When infected by cytosolic pathogens like Listeria monocytogenes or Rickettsia parkeri, epithelial cells undergo a distinct biomechanical transformation: the cytoskeleton softens, and contractility decreases. Detecting this mechanical mismatch, neighboring uninfected cells generate collective compressive forces that squeeze the softer, infected cell upward. This process culminates in apical extrusion, physically ejecting the pathogen-laden cell to preserve monolayer integrity and restrict dissemination.

Beyond active extrusion, the static mechanical properties of the microenvironment also dictate infection patterns [5,20,103]. On soft substrates, host cells exhibit minimal actin remodeling, effectively confining bacteria to localized clusters [97]. Furthermore, geometric constraints create landscapes of spatial heterogeneity; for instance, in micropatterned monolayers, bacteria preferentially adhere to peripheral cells where intercellular tension is highest (~75 nN), revealing that host stress distributions passively bias bacterial colonization sites [104].

Innate immune effectors, particularly polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs), utilize mechanotransduction to enhance bactericidal potency [105]. During extravasation, PMNs experience substantial physical stress as they navigate endothelial junctions. This tension is actively sensed by the mechanochannel Piezo1, which triggers calcium influx and amplifies NADPH oxidase activity, thereby priming the oxidative burst before the neutrophil even reaches the pathogen [106]. Once at the site, environmental cues such as ECM stiffness and cyclic stretch promote chromatin decondensation, facilitating the formation of Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) and sensitizing PMNs to bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS) for efficient clearance [107] (Figure 6(A)).

Bacterial countermeasures: biophysical camouflage and co-sensing

To survive this mechanical surveillance, bacteria have evolved sophisticated biophysical countermeasures. One strategy involves “mechanical masking” to evade immune recognition. In S. aureus, the adhesin SdrE binds the host complement regulator Factor H (FH). Under the shear stress of blood flow, this interaction acts as a “catch bond,” where bond lifetime increases with force up to ~1400 pN. This mechanically stabilized complex creates a shield that masks regulatory sites on FH, effectively suppressing complement activation [108]. Similarly, Yersinia pestis assembles the F1 antigen into a hydrated, high-density polymer brush. This structure functions as a “mechanically slippery” shield that resists macrophage compression and creates steric hindrance, physically blocking immune receptors from accessing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [109]. Even in Streptococcus pyogenes, the pilus tip adhesin Spy0125 utilizes an internal thioester bond as a reversible mechano-switch. This stabilization allows the bacterium to distinguish between transient soluble inhibitors and robust host ligands under load, ensuring specific adhesion amidst the inflammatory chaos [110].

Finally, intracellular pathogens achieve precise niche adaptation by integrating mechanical signals with chemical cues – a process of “coincidence detection.” The virulence factor VapA of Rhodococcus equi exemplifies this synergy. Efficient membrane insertion of VapA requires two simultaneous conditions: the acidic pH (~4.5) typical of the phagosome and low lateral membrane pressure. Acidic pH induces a conformational change in VapA, while regions of reduced lipid packing provide mechanically favorable insertion sites. This dual-sensing mechanism ensures that VapA selectively targets and disrupts the phagosomal membrane, facilitating bacterial escape into the cytosol. Thus, by optimizing their own mechanics and exploiting the physical properties of the host, bacteria effectively disrupt immune recognition and secure intracellular survival [111] (Figure 6(B)).

Mechanically driven tissue tropism in bacterial infection

The ability of a pathogen to colonize a specific organ is governed not only by biochemical receptor compatibility but also by its “mechanical competence” – the capacity to sense and adapt to the unique physical forces of that niche. Bacteria deploy specialized mechanosensory apparatuses to transduce tissue-specific cues into intracellular signals (via TCS and c-di-GMP networks), regulating core pathogenic processes such as adhesion, virulence, and host modulation. Importantly, the translation of mechanical input into pathogenic behavior is strictly conditioned by the physical context of the surrounding tissue. Human organs display diverse mechanical landscapes: from the high-velocity hemodynamic shear of the vasculature to the cyclical hydrodynamic washout of the urinary tract and the interfacial stresses of the oral cavity. These distinct force profiles act as physiological filters, selecting for pathogens with matching mechanoadaptive strategies and thereby driving tissue-specific tropism (Figure 7).

Cardiovascular system

The cardiovascular system represents a high-energy mechanical environment dominated by hemodynamic shear stress [112,113]. In healthy vessels, endothelial cells experience stable laminar flow with wall shear stresses ranging from 1 to 7 Pa, a regime that maintains homeostasis [114,115]. However, anatomical features such as heart valves and arterial bifurcations generate abrupt transitions in flow velocity and direction. These regions are characterized by turbulent shear (5–20 Pa) and transient spikes exceeding 20 Pa during valve closure [116]. Such high-intensity mechanical events can compromise endothelial integrity, exposing the subendothelial matrix to create prime anchoring sites for colonization [115].

S. aureus, the predominant pathogen in infective endocarditis, has evolved to exploit this hydrodynamic volatility. Its surface adhesin, Clumping factor A (ClfA), functions as a shear-sensitive molecular switch. Within the physiological shear range of 5–20 Pa, ClfA forms a “catch bond” with plasma fibrinogen. Under low shear, the ClfA–fibrinogen bond remains in a weak, short-lived state (~0.1 nN). However, as shear stress escalates, tensile force drives conformational remodeling in ClfA, exposing high-affinity binding pockets that increase bond strength by approximately 15-fold (~1.5 nN) [117,118]. This mechanosensitive strengthening allows S. aureus to withstand the potentially dislodging forces of turbulent blood flow, enabling sustained adhesion on heart valves and the establishment of the infectious nidus [119].

Urinary system

Compared with the relatively continuous perfusion of the vasculature, the urinary tract is characterized by discrete filling–voiding cycles, episodic hydrodynamic washout, and marked osmotic fluctuations. The mechanical environment is dominated by the periodic filling and voiding of the bladder, generating low wall shear stresses (0.01–0.1 Pa) that act to scour unattached bacteria [120]. Simultaneously, osmolality varies drastically (300–1200 mOsm/kg) depending on metabolic state, forcing pathogens to maintain strict osmotic homeostasis [121]. In this dynamic milieu, shear stress, osmotic pressure, and tissue tension form a composite mechanical barrier [122,123].

Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), the primary driver of urinary tract infections (UTIs), utilizes the adhesin FimH to navigate these forces via a “force-induced allostery” mechanism [13]. Under low shear, the lectin domain of FimH is tightly coupled to its pilin domain, maintaining a low-affinity conformation [124]. As urine flow accelerates and tensile forces on the pilus rise to 10–30 pN, mechanical loading triggers domain separation. This structural shift snaps the lectin domain into a high-affinity conformation, boosting receptor binding strength by ~100-fold [13,124,125]. This catch-bond transition allows UPEC to anchor robustly to bladder epithelial cells specifically during high-flow voiding events, preventing washout. Furthermore, UPEC strains bearing Dr fimbriae employ a “rolling–detachment–refilling” strategy. By engaging type IV collagen through multivalent contacts, these bacteria maintain adhesion at shear stresses of 0.1–1 Pa [126]. Crucially, when infected epithelial cells exfoliate—a host mechanical defense—these bacteria rapidly bridge to adjacent cells via pilus-mediated connections. This mechanics-driven “hopping” enables swift recolonization and is clinically linked to the high recurrence rates observed in chronic UTIs [127,128].

Implant-associated tissues

The implantation of abiotic materials introduces a mechanical discontinuity into the host environment, creating a distinct interfacial niche in which bacterial adhesion and biofilm persistence may be favored under certain conditions [129–131]. The genesis of Implant-Associated Infections (IAIs) is governed by the unique tribological and mechanical conditions at the device–tissue interface. Three physical factors dominate this microenvironment: (i) stress shielding, arising from the Young’s modulus mismatch between rigid implants (e.g. titanium) and compliant bone, which alters local strain fields; (ii) cyclic loading, generated by daily weight-bearing activities, which continuously pumps fluid and deforms the peri-implant space; and (iii) persistent interfacial shear, which modulates both bacterial adhesion and the efficacy of host immune surveillance [132]. Collectively, these forces act as selective pressures that drive biofilm recalcitrance [133].

S. epidermidis, a primary etiological agent of IAIs, actively exploits these mechanical cues [134]. During the initial “race for the surface,” the bacterium deploys specialized adhesins such as SdrG and Embp. These surface proteins recognize host matrix components (fibrinogen and fibronectin, respectively) that rapidly coat the implant [135,136]. Crucially, local fluid shear stabilizes these adhesin–ligand complexes, likely via catch-bond mechanisms, transforming hemodynamic drag into an adhesive advantage [131].

As colonization progresses, the mechanical microenvironment shapes biofilm architecture and persistence. S. epidermidis upregulates the icaADBC operon to synthesize Polysaccharide Intercellular Adhesin (PIA), assembling a viscoelastic Extracellular Polymeric Substance (EPS) matrix. In later stages, compressive stresses within the peri-implant niche compact this matrix, fostering high-density bacterial clustering [132]. Furthermore, mechanical loading can induce microdamage at the tissue interface, creating “mechanically sheltered” micro-niches. These zones effectively dampen local immune surveillance, generating immune-privileged pockets where bacteria evade phagocytosis and serve as reservoirs for chronic recurrence [137].

Conversely, the physical topography of the implant surface offers a lever for therapeutic intervention. Engineering surfaces with specific nanotopographies can modulate the effective contact area and generate localized stress concentrations sufficient to rupture bacterial envelopes. This “mechano-bactericidal” approach leverages contact mechanics to inhibit colonization without reliance on antibiotics [138].

Oral cavity

The oral cavity represents an extreme biomechanical theater where tissues are subjected to high-magnitude forces from mastication and occlusion, superimposed on salivary hydrodynamic shear. Available estimates suggest that saliva-generated shear at the tooth surface is approximately 0.076 Pa, with oral biofilm flow models producing comparable values in the range of ~0.02–0.06 Pa. Thus, salivary shear is broadly similar in order of magnitude to urinary wall shear, but is substantially lower than vascular shear under laminar or disturbed flow conditions [26,139]. In health, these mechanical inputs maintain periodontal homeostasis [26,140,141]. However, mechanical disequilibrium—such as traumatic occlusion or abnormal shear—can trigger a “dysbiotic shift,” tipping the balance from commensal symbiosis to pathogenic destruction [142–144].

Pathological loading serves as a primary instigator of this infection progression [144]. Excessive occlusal stress disrupts the epithelial barrier and elevates shear forces within the periodontal pocket [145]. This physical trauma exposes cryptic collagenous motifs within the connective tissue, which act as high-affinity ligands for Gram-negative anaerobes. Pathogens like Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum utilize collagen-binding adhesins to anchor firmly to these exposed matrices [146]. Under these high-shear conditions, the biofilm architecture is compacted and stabilized, which in turn potentiates quorum sensing and the secretion of tissue-degrading enzymes (gingipains), accelerating the breakdown of the periodontal ligament [143].

At the molecular level, the synergy between mechanics and immunity provides a critical mechanistic insight into bone loss. Mechanical stress acts in concert with P. gingivalis LPS to hyperactivate the mechanosensitive channel Piezo1 in osteoblasts. This coincident signaling triggers the MEK–ERK pathway, significantly increasing the ratio of RANKL to OPG. This molecular shift promotes osteoclastogenesis, directly coupling mechanical loading to alveolar bone resorption [147].

Thus, the progression of periodontitis is driven by a dual-mechanical mechanism: externally, forces enhance bacterial adhesion and biofilm virulence; intracellularly, forces synergize with bacterial toxins to hijack host signaling axes, propelling the tissue toward irreversible inflammatory destruction.

Mechanics-based strategies for combating bacterial infection

Mechanical forces serve as master regulators throughout the pathogenic trajectory, governing survival, adhesion, dissemination, and virulence expression while shaping host–pathogen crosstalk. Although antibiotics have historically been the cornerstone of infection control, their overuse has accelerated the global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), rendering conventional therapies increasingly futile [148,149]. In this landscape, mechanics-based interventions offer a promising, orthogonal pathway to circumvent therapeutic bottlenecks [150]. Multistage interventions targeting bacteria–host mechanical systems

Mechanics-based anti-infective strategies can be organized according to the level at which they interrupt the infection process: membrane-level mechanosensors, force-generating surface structures, intracellular signaling networks, and host–pathogen mechanical interfaces [7,25]. This multistage view avoids treating bacterial mechanotransduction as a single drug target and instead frames it as a layered system in which mechanical sensing, adhesion, virulence regulation, and host responses can each be therapeutically intercepted.

Among bacterial mechanosensors, mechanosensitive ion channels—particularly the mechanosensitive channel of large conductance—represent especially attractive therapeutic targets [7,42,49]. MscL is especially notable because it is widely conserved among bacteria and lacks close mammalian homologs [151–153]. As a large-conductance tension-gated channel, MscL normally functions as an emergency release valve during hypo-osmotic shock [43,50,153]. However, inappropriate or sensitized opening of this channel can become detrimental by causing uncontrolled leakage of ions, metabolites, and osmoprotectants, while also increasing the cytoplasmic entry of otherwise poorly permeable antibacterial agents [153–155]. Several small molecules and antibiotics have therefore been investigated as MscL-active agents or adjuvants. For example, K05 and 011A modulate MscL gating and can enhance antibiotic access to the bacterial cytoplasm; dihydrostreptomycin directly binds to and passes through the MscL pore; curcumin promotes MscL-dependent membrane permeabilization and antibiotic synergy; and SCH-79797 induces MscL-mediated membrane permeabilization. Importantly, these agents should be interpreted primarily as proof-of-concept permeability-enhancing or stress-sensitizing strategies rather than fully validated stand-alone therapeutics, because species specificity, membrane-context dependence, off-target membrane perturbation, and in vivo efficacy remain incompletely resolved [153,155–157].

Beyond membrane channels, force-generating bacterial surface structures provide another intervention layer. Flagella and pili are not only motility or adhesion organelles but also mechanical sensors that link surface engagement to colonization and virulence programs [8,9]. Thus, disrupting flagellar assembly, pilus extension – retraction cycles, or adhesin – receptor interactions can weaken force-dependent attachment and impair early colonization [158,159]. At the intracellular level, targeting two-component systems or second-messenger networks provides a downstream strategy to uncouple mechanical inputs from transcriptional outputs [15,71]. Inhibitors of histidine kinase autophosphorylation, response-regulator activity, or c-di-GMP turnover enzymes may suppress mechanosensitive biofilm switching and virulence expression [160,161].

Host- and interface-directed strategies further complement these bacterial targets. For instance, modulation of host mechanotransduction pathways such as Piezo1 can alter infection-associated inflammatory or uptake response [33,162]. In parallel, interface-blocking biomaterials or hydrogel systems can physically disrupt bacterial anchorage to host matrices. A representative example is a biohydrogel co-delivering Arg – Gly – Asp – Ser (RGDS) peptides and antibiotics, which exemplifies a “block-and-kill” strategy. In this system, RGDS peptides competitively interfere with the interaction between bacterial fibronectin-binding proteins (FnBPs) and host integrins, thereby mechanically decoupling bacteria from the host matrix, while the antibiotic component suppresses bacterial growth. This combined adhesion-blocking and antibacterial design illustrates how pathogen – matrix mechanics can be exploited therapeutically [163]. Together, these approaches suggest that mechanics-based therapy should be viewed not as a single MscL-centered strategy, but as a multilevel framework targeting membrane permeability, adhesive force transmission, signal transduction, and host – interface mechanics. Bacterial resistance mechanisms and mechanically driven breakthrough strategies

Even mechanics-targeted agents must contend with evolved resistance mechanisms that blunt therapeutic efficacy [149,180,181]. Bacteria deploy a multilayered defense: structurally, they fortify cell walls and construct biofilm matrices to impede drug penetration; functionally, membrane-embedded efflux pumps actively expel toxins; and metabolically, they rewire pathways to neutralize lethal agents. These adaptive strategies necessitate a combinatorial approach where mechanical interventions serve to breach these defenses (Figure 9).

Mechanical forces can counter resistance via three mechanisms: structural disruption, stress sensitization, and enhanced delivery. For example, the application of defined octahedral shear stress to bacterial envelopes has been shown to destabilize efflux pump assemblies, thereby diminishing their export capacity and restoring sensitivity to metals like copper and silver. This suggests that micro-scale mechanical stress can functionally disable the “pumping” machinery essential for resistance.

Physiological shear forces can also induce intrinsic sensitization without exogenous drugs [182]. Fluid shear mimicking arterial flow (~800 s−1) renders bacteria hypersensitive to endogenous H2O2 (lowering tolerance by 100–1000-fold compared with static conditions), suggesting a context-dependent vulnerability under flow conditions. However, the relationship between shear and infection is intrinsically bidirectional. In the cardiovascular niche, hemodynamic shear can promote infection by stabilizing S. aureus ClfA–fibrinogen catch bonds, as seen in infective endocarditis. Conversely, arterial-level shear may stress planktonic or weakly attached bacteria, increasing membrane permeability, redox imbalance, and H2O2 susceptibility. Thus, shear-enhanced oxidative killing should be considered species-, state-, and niche-dependent rather than a universal response. Exploiting this vulnerability therapeutically will therefore require careful consideration of bacterial species, attachment state, and infection niche [117,183]. Beyond shear-mediated sensitization, biophysical tools such as mechanical waves have also been proposed to modulate collective bacterial behaviors, including biofilm attachment, maturation, and swarming. Ultrasound (US) technologies further exemplify this “delivery-facilitation” route. Low-frequency noncontact US induces cavitation to physically dismantle biofilm matrices in IAI. Conversely, high-frequency US transiently permeabilizes membranes (sonoporation), promoting drug uptake. When coupled with sonosensitizers, these modalities enable targeted, spatiotemporally controlled eradication, providing a clinical solution to the diffusion barriers that typically limit chemotherapy [184–189].

Material-driven mechanical antibacterial strategies

Implantable devices – from cardiac stents to orthopedic prosthetics – are frequent sites of biofilm-associated complications. The physicochemical properties of abiotic surfaces often promote preferential adhesion, leading to chronic inflammation and device failure [190,191]. To combat this, material engineering has evolved two complementary paradigms: static nanostructured surfaces that exert passive bactericidal forces, and dynamically actuated micro/nanorobots that deliver active, on-demand disruption.

Static strategies exploit “mechano-bactericidal” topographies. The primary mechanism is membrane stretching and rupture: bioinspired nanopillar arrays (mimicking dragonfly wings) fabricated on titanium or polyetheretherketone can strain bacterial envelopes beyond their elastic limit, achieving > 90% killing efficiency [192–194]. A secondary mechanism involves physical cutting, exemplified by graphene nanosheets with atomically sharp edges that slice bacterial membranes. Crucially, clinical translation hinges on selective biocompatibility; advanced designs, such as peptide-modified titanium dioxide nanotube arrays, have been engineered to mechanically kill bacteria while simultaneously promoting gingival fibroblast integration, resolving the conflict between bactericidal potency and tissue integration [195].

Dynamic strategies represent the frontier of “active matter” therapeutics. Micro/nanorobots combine self-propulsion with physical disruption to penetrate hard-to-reach niches. For instance, magnetically guided, sea-urchin-like microrobots utilize surface microspikes to mechanically scour biofilms from biliary stents [196]. Similarly, self-locomotive antimicrobial microrobots (SLAM) generate thrust via catalytic H2O2 decomposition, driving deep penetration into P. aeruginosa biofilms. The resulting bubble collapse imparts shear stress that disrupts the EPS, opening channels for co-administered antibiotics [197].

Recognizing that single-mode strategies have limitations – such as the inability of nanostructures to reach deep biofilm layers – the field is shifting toward multimodal synergistic designs [198]. Hybrid platforms now integrate mechanical rupture with chemical or photothermal modalities. For example, combining topographic stress with photocatalytic reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation compensates for the limitations of purely physical contact [199,200]. In temporal sequence, an initial burst of ion-mediated or photothermal inactivation can be followed by sustained mechanical clearance. Especially for recalcitrant biofilms, the coupling of robotic mechanical scouring with localized antibiotic release generates a synergistic effect: physical disruption compromises the biofilm architecture, thereby potentiating chemical eradication [201–203]. These paradigms underscore that integrating mechanics into combinatorial therapies is key to developing next-generation, resistance-resistant antibacterial systems.

Perspectives and conclusions

While the role of mechanical forces in driving bacterial pathogenesis is now indisputable, bridging the chasm between fundamental mechanobiology and clinical intervention presents formidable challenges. At the mechanistic level, a primary epistemological bottleneck is the lack of spatiotemporal resolution. Bacterial responses to force are integrated across hierarchies – from single-molecule conformational shifts to community-level viscoelastic remodeling – yet current technologies often fail to capture these events simultaneously under physiological conditions. Consequently, the “encoding logic” by which bacteria decipher complex mechanical inputs remains largely a black box. Furthermore, the disparity between reductionist in vitro models and the in vivo host environment limits translational success. Static culture systems cannot replicate the dynamic coupling of biochemical and mechanical cues – such as the interplay of mucus rheology, airflow shear, and immune cell extrusion in respiratory infections – leading to a fragmented understanding of pathogenesis that often fails validation in clinical pathology [204,205].

At the application level, mechanics-based antibacterial strategies face rigorous engineering and safety hurdles. The transition from bench to bedside is constrained by the need for material stability and biocompatibility; for example, nanostructured bactericidal coatings must resist wear and delamination under chronic physiological loading to prevent implant failure [206]. A critical paradox exists wherein surface topographies potent enough to rupture bacterial membranes may inadvertently compromise host eukaryotic cell integrity, necessitating precise optimization of the bio-interface [36]. Similarly, the deployment of active micro/nanorobots is complicated by the challenge of navigation within the heterogeneous mechanical milieu of the body, where non-Newtonian fluids and tortuous tissue architectures impede localization and actuation [207].

Looking forward, overcoming these limitations requires a convergence of quantitative biology, advanced manufacturing, and artificial intelligence. First, we must move from description to decoding. Future research should prioritize establishing a “mechanical signal – phenotype atlas,” quantifying how specific force patterns – such as the frequency and amplitude of shear or gradients in stiffness – map onto pathogenic outcomes including adhesion strength and virulence expression. This will enable the design of molecular modulators that can precisely mimic or mask mechanical cues to confuse bacterial decision-making. Second, physiological fidelity is paramount. The integration of organ-on-chip platforms with microfluidic actuation offers a route to recapitulate tissue-specific mechanical landscapes, providing high-throughput testbeds for screening mechanics-adapted therapeutics. Finally, the ultimate frontier lies in intelligent, closed-loop intervention. By coupling artificial intelligence with micro-robotics, we envision systems capable of diagnosing biofilm mechanics in real-time (identifying stress concentration points) and delivering targeted physical disruption. This shift – from generalized chemical inhibition to precision physical disruption – establishes a new paradigm for combating antimicrobial resistance in the post-antibiotic era.
