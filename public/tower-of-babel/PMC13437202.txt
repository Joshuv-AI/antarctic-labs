Standardizing Obesity Imaging: From Confirmation of Excess Adiposity to Integrated Body Composition Phenotyping
Authors: Thanh Duy Nguyen, Woojin Yi
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the review by Kim et al. [1] on the role of imaging in adiposity evaluation and anti-obesity drug development. Their timely overview of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and bioelectrical impedance analysis clearly shows that imaging-based body composition assessment is moving closer to routine clinical and trial use. Building on their review, we would like to emphasize that the next challenge is not merely the broader use of imaging, but also the standardization of how imaging confirms excess adiposity and how it is integrated into obesity phenotyping.

The 2025 Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology Commission proposed a major shift in obesity diagnosis by moving beyond body mass index (BMI) alone [2]. In this framework, confirmation of excess adiposity should not rely solely on BMI, but should incorporate additional anthropometric measures or direct assessment of body fat, including imaging-based methods when appropriate [2]. Therefore, CT, MRI, and DXA are no longer merely research tools; they are becoming increasingly relevant for diagnostic classification and therapeutic decision-making [12].

At present, however, the infrastructure for standardized imaging-based adiposity assessment remains underdeveloped. As noted by Kim et al. [1], CT and MRI are regarded as reference standards for quantifying visceral and ectopic fat. However, its broader clinical use remains limited by the unresolved heterogeneity in anatomical landmark selection, single-slice versus volumetric analysis, segmentation strategy, and diagnostic cutoff values. Lee et al. [3] reviewed these issues in detail and emphasized the lack of consensus regarding imaging modalities, measurement protocols, anatomical levels, and threshold definitions in body composition imaging. Thus, if imaging is to support obesity diagnosis within the new framework, acquisition, analysis, and interpretation must be more clearly standardized.

Importantly, cross-sectional imaging provides information beyond fat quantification alone. Lee et al. [3] also highlighted that CT and MRI can simultaneously assess visceral fat, subcutaneous fat, and skeletal muscle, thereby enabling integrated body composition phenotyping rather than adiposity measurement alone. This broader perspective is clinically relevant. Jang et al. [4] showed that sarcopenic obesity measured using clinically acquired CT or MRI was an independent predictor of postoperative pancreatic fistulas after pancreaticoduodenectomy. Kim et al. [5] further demonstrated that myosteatosis was associated with poor overall survival and recurrence-free survival in patients with resectable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, whereas sarcopenia alone was less consistently prognostic. These findings suggest that obesity imaging may be the most informative when it captures not only fat burden, but also muscle depletion and muscle quality.

Ethnic and racial variations in body composition render this an even more important issue. Asian populations often accumulate greater visceral and ectopic fat at lower BMI thresholds than European populations, whereas individuals of African descent may exhibit lower visceral fat and greater lean mass at similar BMI values [6]. Population-scale CT analyses have further shown substantial age-, sex-, and race-related differences in the reference distributions of visceral fat, subcutaneous fat, and skeletal muscle [7]. Given the central role of visceral and ectopic fat in cardiometabolic risk, a single global imaging cut-off is unlikely to be appropriate [8]. Even the commonly cited visceral fat area threshold of 100 cm2, used in East Asian populations including Korean cohorts, should not be assumed to transfer directly across populations [9].

The need for standardization is further reinforced by both regulatory and technical developments. As Kim et al. [1] highlighted, the 2025 U.S. FDA draft guidance on obesity drug development recommends body composition assessment by DXA or an appropriate alternative in a representative sample of clinical trial participants, emphasizing that the therapeutic target is fat mass reduction rather than weight loss alone. Simultaneously, deep learning–based body composition analysis is making large-scale implementation increasingly feasible. Park et al. [10] developed and validated an automated CT segmentation system for abdominal muscles, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat, demonstrating high performance across validation cohorts. Along with population-scale deep learning analyses [7], these tools create a timely opportunity to establish robust normative databases and clinically meaningful thresholds.

We therefore propose that the radiology community, together with endocrinologists, obesity medicine specialists, and regulatory agencies, should prioritize three goals: standardized imaging acquisition and analysis protocols for body composition assessment; large-scale multiethnic normative databases with age-, sex-, and ethnicity-specific reference values and diagnostic thresholds; and consensus standards for quality assurance, reproducibility, and validation of automated body composition tools intended for clinical use.

Kim et al. [1] have provided an excellent foundation by clarifying the current role of imaging in adiposity assessment and anti-obesity drug development. The next step is to ensure that when imaging is used within the new obesity framework, it moves beyond adiposity quantification alone toward standardized, reproducible, and biologically integrated body composition phenotyping.
