ABSTRACT
We present a tour de force of atomistic molecular dynamics simulations involving the coordinated effort of 14 research groups of the Ascona B-DNA Consortium (ABC). This initiative provides a complete characterization of the 2080 DNA hexamers embedded in 190 carefully selected 20-mer duplexes, each simulated in replicate for at least 10 microseconds in explicit solvent. The consortium generates 0.25 petabytes of data, capturing millisecond-scale ensembles at the oligomer level and dynamics up to 10−1 seconds at the base-pair level. Analysis yields a comprehensive description of sequence-dependent DNA properties, including rare events such as backbone transitions, reversible base-pair changes, and partial unfolding. Processing these atomistic ensembles reveals a hidden physical code of DNA, helping explain rules of genome composition and evolution beyond coding regions. This community effort delivers unprecedented, validated FAIR data to support coarse-grained and AI models of DNA at cellular scale.

Introduction

At the start of the millennium, there was already a bulk of evidence showing that DNA is a highly polymorphic and flexible polymer whose physical properties are defined by a complex choreography of sequence-dependent correlated movements sampling a vast variety of sub-states, some of them stabilized by non-canonical interactions1–13. The sequence-dependent physical properties of DNA modulate specific and non-specific protein binding to DNA14,15, the effect of epigenetic variants16–22, mismatches23–27, lesions5,28–36, the placement of nucleosomes5,30–37, the binding of effector proteins14,38, and even the compaction of chromatin21,39,40. Unfortunately, the combinatorial explosion of possible sequences severely limits our understanding of how sequence determines the physical properties of DNA, hampering a genome-scale representation of DNA physics41 and the understanding of evolutionary pressure beyond simple maintenance of coding information.

The first attempts to describe sequence-dependent properties of DNA relied on the analysis of the variability in experimental structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB)1,5,6,42 and were focused on the description of the equilibrium and harmonic deformability of the 10 unique base pair steps (bps). These pioneer studies delivered the mesoscopic model of sequence-dependent physical properties of the DNA duplex6. However, it soon became evident that an independent BPS model was not accurate enough and at least tetramer effects should be considered. This motivated the Ascona B-DNA Consortium (ABC)3,8,10–12 to use atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to characterize the physical properties of 136 unique DNA tetramers7,8,10,12,43. Leading-edge at the time, trajectories were collected using consensus protocols and were stored and made available, something that was instrumental in further force-field refinement as well as for the development of coarse-grained and mesoscopic models of DNA44–54 and chromatin37,40. However, major limitations have now become apparent in these models, for example: poor description of correlated movements55–57, limited knowledge on neighbour effects beyond the tetramer58, poor representation of rare events, such as fraying, opening, unfolding, or the formation of alternative base-pairing schemes. Consequently, our understanding of the sequence-dependent properties of DNA is incomplete and unable to reliably describe and correlate DNA properties at the genome scale.

We present here an extensive simulation project, where 14 experienced groups joined efforts to describe the physical properties of DNA at the hexamer level. Through an unprecedented simulation tour de force, we provide not only the complete catalogue of harmonic and non-harmonic deformations for the entire set of 2080 unique DNA double-stranded hexamers, but describe previously unknown dynamic features of DNA, define sequences showing anomalous behaviour, and determine a similarity tree between DNA sequences in terms of their structure, dynamics and interaction properties. The massive amount of data collected here under FAIR (Findable Accessible Interoperable and Reusable) principles59, validated by a large part of the DNA-community, and accessible through a powerful database infrastructure, provides a set of information for training AI methods and the development of mesoscopic and coarse-grain models of DNA.

Results

Stability and convergence of the simulations

The 190 duplexes simulated here are shown in Supplementary Information Table S1, where the labels r01, r02, r03 and r04 refer to the different replicas, and (*) labelled simulations extended for double simulation times. RMSD analyses indicate that MD simulations are sampling well the expected B-fibre geometry without any major drift along the trajectories, as shown in RMSD distributions for the first and last 200 ns of trajectories (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The slightly larger values obtained for the 20-mer compared with the 16-mer highlight the presence of fraying effects (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Only in a very few cases, these fraying effects affect the central 16-mer (see below and Supplementary Fig. S1) in a stochastic manner, appearing only in one of the replicas (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Overall, we obtained stable trajectories in the vast majority of simulations, and additional replicas and extension of trajectories was required only in 3% of the cases, suggesting good convergence of trajectories in the 10 μs range. Projection of the 190×2 average structures on the PCA map (1st and 2nd mode, PC1 and PC2) of the meta-trajectory obtained by joining the individual 190 × 2 simulations for the central 16-mer segments shows that replicas are nearly indistinguishable, and that the impact of sequence on the duplex dynamics is not negligible (see Supplementary Fig. S2A-B). Among the different sequences, the analysis shows that seq134 (GCGTCCCGAAGGCCAAGAGC, see Supplementary Table S1) displays most accurately the average characteristics of a DNA duplex, while duplexes Seq047 and Seq144 are, despite their similar composition (see Supplementary Table S1), the ones showing the most diverse conformations. Projection of the average structures on the PCA map of eq134 shows that the structural variability sampled in a single trajectory is larger than that inferred by looking at sequence dependent variability of the average structures in the meta-trajectory (see Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting that flexibility descriptors extracted from the analysis of structural variability in experimental databases may underestimate the true flexibility.

Global conformation of the duplex

The integration of all trajectories in a global ensemble allowed us to obtain (time and sequence averaged) helical properties of DNA duplexes. The results (see Supplementary Table S2) fit very well accepted fibre data60, except for a lower twist (33.9 degrees in average), which in fact agrees better with the average value obtained from the analysis of PDB structures (33.6 degrees), and the value inferred from the analysis of the electrophoretic behaviour of long DNA fibres (c.a. 34 degrees)61. Persistence lengths (PLs; see Supplementary Fig. S3) computed here using the Noy-Golestanian formalism62 yielded average values slightly larger than the accepted experimental estimate (around 60 nm vs around 50 nm, respectively). This discrepancy may arise from force-field errors or from limitations inherent in mapping experimental persistence lengths onto atomistic observables. A bias toward larger persistence lengths associated with the use of the Noy-Golestanian algorithm cannot be neglected, as slightly lower values were reported by Maddocks and co-workers63 using a different persistence-length definition applied to BSC1-based coarse-grained ensembles (45-70 nm, with an average of approximately 56 nm). In any case, even major changes are expected by considering or not terminal base pairs in the calculations. Thus, when we repeated the same computations considering terminal base pairs, PL values below 50 nm were obtained (Supplementary Table S3), warning against quantitative comparisons between experimental and theoretical PLs, or even between theoretical predictions, where not detailed description of the calculations are provided. Furthermore, a recent study found a very good agreement between flexibility descriptors derived from MD-Parmbsc1 simulation and optical tweezer experiments, arguing against a systematic overestimation of stiffness in current simulations63. Results in Supplementary Fig. S3 show that there is a large dispersion of PL values derived for the 190 different hexABC sequences. We defer to the literature64 for a full explanation of this fact, which is primarily due to the strong contribution of intrinsic bends to estimates of (apparent) PL at short length scales. The presence of short A-tracts, such as d(A·T)4, is known to induce high localized intrinsic curvature of the double helix nearby, which is why seq021 with a single A-tract has a rather low PL of 43 nm. In the presence of multiple A-tracts the phasing between them strongly influences the overall intrinsic bend of the oligomer, so that seq149, which has two A-tracts separated by 3 bps, has a rather high PL of 71-72 nm, while seq077 with two A-tracts separated by one bp has an average persistence length; see Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S3. These data highlight the complexity of A-tract-induced curvature, in which both the internal A-tract sequence and the surrounding sequence context contribute subtly to the resulting global curvature65,66. They also indicate that persistence length alone cannot be taken as a simple proxy for DNA flexibility.

To assess the impact of base composition on global DNA flexibility, we grouped duplexes (16-bp central segments) into A/T-rich and G/C-rich sequences (base pair fraction >70%) and analysed their mechanical properties separately. Persistence length, RMSD, and force constants associated with the four dominant deformation modes (obtained from covariance matrix diagonalization, PC1 to PC4) show no systematic differences between the two groups (Supplementary Figs. S4-S5). A/T-rich duplexes exhibit a slightly larger dispersion in all metrics, whereas G/C-rich duplexes show marginally increased flexibility along specific collective motions. Overall, base composition alone (A/T vs G/C content) does not provide a reliable predictor of global duplex flexibility. To move beyond a simple G/C vs A/T classification, we analysed sequence-dependent variations in RMSD, PL, and the force constant associated with the first principal component (PC1 in Principal Component analysis; PCA) for the central 16 bps of each sequence. Local sequence effects were quantified using a k-mer analysis (k = 4), considering tetramers present at least once within the central region and represented by a minimum of three sequences. Results in Supplementary Fig. S6 show an inverse correlation between stiffness along PC1 and RMSD, indicating that both metrics describe similar deformation physics. Duplexes containing tetramers such as AGGG, TATA, ATAG, and CCTA display enhanced flexibility (high RMSD and low PC1 force constant), whereas AAAA, AATT, and AAAT are associated with increased stiffness. In general, analysis of the most flexible sequences reveals enrichment in C-rich motifs (CpC/GpG) and in TpA, CpA/TpG steps, whereas the stiffest duplexes preferentially contain ApT, ApA/TpT and TpC/GpA motifs, typically within mixed A/T/C contexts. Notably, TpA steps are prevalent in flexible duplexes, while ApT motifs dominate rigid ones, revealing a marked directional asymmetry within A/T-rich sequences. Poly(A)-containing sequences show only moderate PL, indicating that A-tracts alone do not confer high stiffness. Overall, these results demonstrate that local sequence context and base-step orientation, rather than global A/T or G/C content, govern the mechanical response of DNA duplexes to deformation.

To understand the origin of sequence dependence on flexibility, stacking energies (intra-strand, at the base level; and total, at the base pair step level) were computed, explicitly accounting for hexameric sequence context. The results, which agree reasonably well with QM data in solution for isolate base or base pair step67 show a marked dependence on neighbouring bases (Supplementary Fig. S7). In particular, base pair steps known to display large variability in quantum calculations, such as CpG, also show the strongest context-dependent modulation of stacking energies in our analysis. These findings are further supported by melting temperature estimates obtained with OligoCalc (https://oligocalc.eu/), where sequences with the same number of hydrogen bonds display markedly different stabilities; for example, (CpG)5 exhibits a Tm of 49 °C, whereas (CpC/GpG)5 melts at 33 °C, highlighting the dominant contribution of stacking interactions to duplex stability.

Rare events in duplex dynamics

The exhaustive 10−1 s ensemble allowed us to explore rare events usually inaccessible to standard MD simulations. Overall hydrogen bonding (HB) in the central 16-mer duplex is very well preserved, with most of the duplexes displaying a fraction Watson-Crick (WC) pairing maintained along the simulation of 95-100% (Fig. 1A). We found that central d(G·C) and d(A·T) pairs are in an open conformation compatible with solvent exchange in 0.06% (d(G·C)) and 0.25% (d(A·T)) of the meta-ensemble generated by combining the 190×2 simulations (see Fig. 1B). Opening events are rare, short-lived (0.16 ns on average for the central 16mer, 0.87 ns overall) and stochastic, often coupled to terminal fraying, the most common distortion found in DNA (see examples in Fig. 1C, D). Overall, terminal d(C·G) pairs are opened around 9% of the time (see Fig. 1B), a value that agrees perfectly with opening estimates from NMR experiments68. The simultaneous opening of both ends is rare (1.2 %), but slightly more probable than expected (1.0%) for fully independent events. If only long-lived (more than 10 ns) events are considered, the figures reduce to 6% opening of one end, and 0.5% for both ends (0.36% expected for independent events). Interestingly, while most of the fraying events are short-lived, reversible and restricted to the terminal base pair step, a small portion last much longer (up to 5 μs for Seq186_r01, Fig. 1C). Furthermore, in some cases openings are propagated to the central d(A·T) pairs, leading to reversible partial unfolding (up to 8 base pairs are lost in Seq152_r01 and 7 in Seq040_r01; see Figs. 1D and 2). Fraying at terminal (loss of all the hydrogen bonds, at 5′ or 3′) d(G·C) base pairs ([G]C/[C]G) occurs predominantly in GCG/CGC trimers. The same trimer preference is observed for opening of the second base pair at duplex ends (G[C]/C[G]), suggesting that perturbations at terminal base pairs propagate to their nearest neighbours.

Opening of the third base pair in the terminal trimer GCX/CGX′ is rare, but becomes more likely when this position is occupied by an d(A·T) or d(T·A) base pair (Supplementary Fig. S8). The average opening d(A·T) and d(T·A) at the third base pair (around 2%) is slightly above the average opening of the same pairs in central position of the duplex (see Fig. 1), suggesting a moderate next-to-nearest-neighbour effect. Central trimers exhibit consistently reduced fraying compared to terminal regions, confirming higher internal stability (Fig. 1), yet show motif-specific opening predominantly in A/T-rich triads (ATA/TAT and TAA/TTA), indicating A/T content as the main driver of central base-pair opening (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Opening of central d(A·T) steps leads in some cases to the formation of Hoogsteen pairs (see examples in Fig. 2), instead of the canonical Watson-Crick ones. The WC→H transitions are very rare, as they were found in only 3 of the c.a. 400 individual simulations (more than 500 if those used to refine the simulation protocol are considered). Furthermore, when WC→H transitions happen in one replica, they are not detected in any of the other replicas collected for the same oligo. On average, d(A·T) base pairs predominantly adopt the Watson-Crick (WC) pairing scheme, as defined by an apparent equilibrium constant: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{{eq}}=\frac{\left[H\right]}{\left[{WC}\right]}\approx {10}^{-6}$$\end{document}Keq=HWC≈10−6, indicating a nearly negligible role of the Hoogsteen pairing in defining the global characteristics of the duplex. However, WC→H transitions are not so rare in certain A/T contexts, such as d(TAAT), where (combining all the ensembles collected in this study) we obtain an apparent equilibrium constant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{{eq}}=0.013$$\end{document}Keq=0.013, very close to the value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{{eq}}=0.010$$\end{document}Keq=0.010 obtained from averaging NMR estimates on the same triads69. It is important to note that even for those sequences the overall occurrence of Hoogsteen pairing is too low to significantly affect global metrics. To get further support for these theoretical results, we synthesized a short oligo containing one of the segments where WC→H transitions appeared spontaneously in simulations, and collected the 1H NMR spectrum, which was compared with that obtained for a control duplex with the same length and A/T vs G/C composition. Imino signals (Supplementary Fig. S10) corresponding to the central d(A·T) segment are wider and displaced to very unusual positions, indicative of polymorphism in the H-bonding pattern, something that was confirmed by melting experiments (Supplementary Fig. S10). Finally, while our simulations are too short to determine the real time-scale of WC→H transitions, as we are just collecting the tails of the transition rate distribution70, a few conclusions can be reached: (i) once WC→H happens the new H-bonding scheme is quite stable71,72, generating little distortion in the B-DNA helix73,74; (ii) at least two mechanisms for WC→H transition are possible: one following the local paradigm suggested by Al-Hashimi and co-workers69,75, and another, involving partial unfolding of an A/T segment (see different cases in Fig. 2)76.

Classical simulations performed here do not allow to explore WC→H transitions in d(G·C) pairs, as a stable Hoogsteen d(G·C) requires the protonation of cytosine. However, mining our vast ensemble, a few events of partial opening of a central d(G·C) pair were found, leading to guanosine in the syn conformation and the cytosine exposed to the solvent (Supplementary Fig. S11) ready to accept a proton. As no protonation is possible in classical MD, the Hoogsteen d(G·C) pairing is not stable in our simulations, the guanosine moves to the anti-conformation, and the WC pairing scheme is recovered (last case in Fig. 2). However, we checked further if upon protonation the Hoogsteen base pairing would be stable. To do so, on the open structure, we forced the protonation of cytosine and we ran a simulation. A fully stable duplex is obtained (see Supplementary Fig. S11), in agreement with Al-Hashimi results on similar oligos69,75, proving that once exposed to the solvent the cytosine could be protonated and then it could form a stable Hoogsteen pairing.

The hexamer helical space

Our extensive ensembles allowed characterizing the complete hexameric configurational space of DNA. The central base steps of the 2080 unique hexamers sample for 97.8% canonical helical structures. Disruptions are in most cases related to stochastic fast breathing events breaking H-bond schemes (Fig. 1), and just in a few cases non-helical conformations last for a significant time. Removing those snapshots provides a slightly reduced ensemble, which can be processed to generate helical ensembles for the 6 degrees of freedom (translational and rotational base pair step parameters: shift, slide, rise, tilt, roll and twist) at the central pair of the 2080 unique hexamer environments. Analysis of the entire 6-dimensional dataset demonstrated that simple harmonic approximations are not able to fully describe the helical space sampled by DNA, due to its multimodality (see Fig. 3). In fact, only around 15% of the hexamers (identical results in both replicas) can be accurately represented by a single 6-dimensional Gaussian (a single helical state, unimodal in Fig. 3A). Most of the hexamers are represented by 2 (bimodal, 48%) and 3 (trimodal, 31%) Gaussian distributions, and more than 3 clusters are required only in a very small (quadrimodal, around 6%) number of cases. The intrinsic multimodality of the helical space1–3,77 is already visible in the 2D and 1D projections of the helical space (see “Methods” for details and examples in Fig. 3B–D). The individual helical parameters showing higher bimodality are: twist, slide and shift (as an example see the twist distribution in Fig. 3B–D and Supplementary Fig S12-S14). As a general trend, bimodality is frequent in CpG, CpA and TpA steps (twist, slide and shift respectively), but tetramer effects are quite large (see reference examples in Supplementary Figs. S12 and S13), confirming the need to introduce neighbour effects to accurately describe helical space. Hexamer effects are important in just a few cases (see Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S14), and octamer effects are expected to be negligible (see Supplementary Fig. S15). Finally, we computed the force-constant matrix associated with the set of base pair step helical parameters9 for the complete hexameric helical space (available in the Supplementary Material). Consistent with the global analyses of flexibility detailed above, hexamers containing A-tracts are generally more rigid in the elastic regime than other sequences. However, the primary determinant distinguishing flexible from rigid hexamers is the presence of TpA versus ApT steps: TpA steps are associated with increased flexibility, whereas ApT steps confer the highest rigidity. This distinction is quantitatively captured by the product of the diagonal elements of the force-constant matrix describing helical motions, which is systematically lower for TpA-containing hexamers and higher for those containing ApT steps (see Supplementary Fig. S16).

Correlation effects

Contrary to assumptions used in early mesoscopic models6, correlations in the helical movements between neighbouring base pair steps (i and i + 1) are far from being negligible51,52,55,56,78. In general, intra-parameter (same base pair helical parameter) correlations between neighbouring base pair steps i → i + 1 are negative (anti-correlations) and in some cases quite significant, while inter-parameter correlations are less important, and can be either positive or negative (see examples in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S17) Overall, negative correlations dominate versus the positive ones (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum }_{k,l}{r}_{i\to i+1}=-1.5$$\end{document}∑k,lri→i+1=−1.5, with i standing for a given bps and k, l for the different helical parameters) and just a few i→i + 2 correlations, and i→i + 3 correlations are nonnegligible (|r | > 0.1; see Fig. 4A), which means that local perturbations do not propagate at large distances along the helix. Frustration in the helical space

Neighbour effects can generate frustration in the central bps, i.e. a given bps may sample helical regions in one sequence context, which are impeded for the same bps in a different one. This can enhance specificity, in cases where certain neighbouring bases prevent a given bps from adopting the bioactive conformation, which is, in contrast, the most stable for other neighbouring contexts. This can be quantitatively analysed using a harmonic simplification, the frustration energies (deformation energies, see Methods) like those shown in Fig. 5, where we estimate the energy cost required to move the geometry of a central bps in a given sequence environment to that which is the optimum one for the same bps in all the other potential sequence environment. Results in Fig. 5A illustrate that tetramer environment effects, that is, how the same central base pair step behaves when embedded in different tetrameric contexts (i.e., varying the two flanking base pairs immediately adjacent to it) are moderate on average (<0.5 kcal/mol, i.e. around 1 kbT) but can be remarkably large in specific cases. For instance, we detected frustration energies up to 3.5 kcal/mol, corresponding to the energy cost required for the central CpC step of the d(TCCCAT)·d(ATGGGA) hexamer to adopt the equilibrium geometry of the same bps in the d(CACCTT)·d(AAGGTG) one. Such sizable frustration energies highlight the influence of the tetrameric environment on local DNA mechanics and caution against using base-pair level descriptors that neglect these context-dependent effects. In contrast, hexameric environment effects, which account for next-to-nearest neighbour interactions (i.e., how the same tetrameric core behaves when embedded in different hexameric sequences), are significantly milder. On average, the corresponding frustration energies are below 0.05 kcal/mol (within thermal noise), and only rarely do hexamer-hexamer comparisons exceed 0.3 kcal/mol. The backbone torsion angles

MD simulations of all the double-stranded DNA hexamers lead to the expected sampling of backbone conformations reproducing well the expected values from experimental data (see dial plots in Supplementary Fig. S18A-B). The lengths of the dynamics allow the DNA backbone to sample nonstandard regions. Most glycosidic torsion angles sample the traditional anti region (see χ (chi) torsion angle in Supplementary Fig. S18C), but exploration of the syn conformations is not rare (χ ≈ 0° to +90°), typically linked to terminal fraying and to the formation of Hoogsteen pairings (see Fig. 2). For example, while average values indicate a South puckering, dynamics allow the DNA to sample phase angles in the entire South-East region (see Supplementary Fig. S18D) with non-negligible sampling of the C3’endo conformation. If a traditional two-state puckering model is used, North states account for 18% of the total, very close to the recent estimates obtained by the analysis of experimental DNA backbones79. The largest and most likely relevant variability is related to the ε/ζ concerted movement that leads to the well-known BI/BII polymorphism, responsible for the high twist–low twist bimodality. We found little variation in BI/BII populations with the position along the oligomer (see Supplementary Fig. S19), and a very remarkable dependence on the sequence. Thus, while on average 20% of the bps are in the BII conformation (very close to average experimental estimates80) there are dramatic sequence differences. For example, 48% of BII is expected for the d(GpA) step, very close to 40% from NMR estimates at the same temperature81, while no significant BII is expected in d(YpY) steps or some d(RpY) steps such as d(ApT) or d(GpT), again in perfect agreement with accurate NMR experiments82.

Hidden physical rules in genome evolution

While the rules governing sequence prevalence, conservation and evolution of DNA in coding regions are clear and related to maintenance of the sequence of proteins and/or efficiency of translation, those governing non-coding regions are unknown. In other words, it is easy to determine the evolutionary pressure against a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in a coding region simply by translating the SNP into an amino acid substitution. However, it is unclear in the case of non-coding regions (extending these to the associated regulatory segments) where the “evolutionary pressure” should be related exclusively to the intrinsic properties of DNA and not their genetic information. Our massive analysis allows us to compare all the hexamers from a physical point of view, either considering strand independent (2080) or dependent (4160) hexamers and decipher how the need to maintain physical properties have modulated the sequence of non-coding regions, and the evolutionary constraints that they enforced.

Firstly, we analysed the hexamer similarities using different descriptors, including dihedral angles, principal component analysis (PCA), and ion distributions (see Methods), each capturing distinct aspects of hexamer structure, flexibility, and interaction properties. These descriptors enable more meaningful comparisons among hexamers than their base sequence (see Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S20). The most informative of these descriptors were derived from the analysis of the latent coordinate derived from autoencoders and the density of cations (K+) in the major groove (MG). Autoencoders based on dihedral angles provide simultaneously information on structure and flexibility of the hexamers, while major groove ion densities (maj_ions) contain both information on direct (H-bond donors and acceptors) and indirect (structure based) recognition of the DNA by proteins. Interestingly, the similarity computed from the different descriptors provides a different landscape of hexamer similarities, as the global correlation between autoencoder latent variables and MG ion distributions is negligible (r < 0.1, Supplementary Fig. S21). Only a few hexamer pairs appear as consistently similar or different (see Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S22) in all metrics. We then analysed the impact of physical properties in guiding evolutionary variability in non-coding regions. To this end, we computed the sensitivity to mutation of different hexamers (defined as the maximum dissimilarity that can be achieved through a single nucleotide mutation; see “Methods”) considering both structural/dynamics (dihedral-based autoencoder) and recognition (ion densities) metrics. Figure 6C plots the sensitivities for all hexamers compared with their prevalence in a human chromosome (using as example chromosome 10 as a representative of intermediate length) excluding coding and regulatory regions. Clearly, the most frequently occurring hexamers are among the most mutation-resilient, exhibiting lower sensitivity across both comparison metrics. This relationship is further supported by population-level statistical analyses, which demonstrate a significant enrichment of highly abundant hexamers within the low-sensitivity regime (Supplementary Fig. S24). Notably, an increase in A/T content appears to correlate with reduced sensitivity. In other words, our genome seems to be enriched in sequences that function as buffers against mutations, preserving the physical (structural/dynamic and recognition-related) properties of DNA.

Finally, we quantified the intrinsic affinity of DNA for nucleosome formation by computing the elastic energy required to deform DNA from its naked to its nucleosomal conformation (see “Methods”)84. This deformation energy was evaluated using sequence-dependent equilibrium geometries and stiffness constants for each base-pair step in its hexameric context, and subsequently converted into a nucleosome formation probability (affinity), providing a direct measure of DNA-histone affinity. We applied this framework to analyze nucleosome formation around transcription start sites across the yeast genome. Comparison of the normalised nucleosome affinity values with experimental MNase-seq nucleosome coverage (GEO: GSE255857) shows good agreement, with low nucleosome formation probabilities in nucleosome-free regions and a marked increase at well-positioned nucleosomes (see Supplementary Fig. S27).

Discussion

The central dogma of biology presents DNA as a regular helical structure acting as a blind carrier of genetic information, where the role of DNA sequence is limited to coding proteins. However, research over the last decade has shown that the DNA sequence does much more than just code for proteins, it also defines the physical properties of DNA and influences its interactions with effector proteins, as well as the structure of chromatin, which is a key element in gene regulation. In other words, it is now clear that a secondary code exists in DNA85, a code that is much more complex to decipher than the primary genetic code, as it is encoded in pattern longer than the three-letter codons of the genetic alphabet. Determining the physical properties of DNA involves in principle solving a 4n problem (with n is the size of the chromosome), which is far beyond the capabilities of current experimental approaches. In this paper we present an unprecedented community effort in which 14 of the most active groups in DNA simulation joined efforts to determine the minimum length of DNA required to accurately extrapolate the properties of long DNA segments. The resulting 4096 hexamers (reduced to 2080 through symmetry rules) were simulated using accepted community protocols, with individual trajectories at least 20 times longer than those used in current state-of-the-art simulations, and all resulting data were deposited in FAIR format and made openly available to the community. The analysis of the 0.25 petabytes of data reveals the presence of non-canonical distortions, characterized in detail here (and validated experimentally), deciphers the canonical physical properties of DNA, and uncovers previously unknown conservation rules beyond the coding regions. Perhaps more importantly, our large-scale simulation effort, conducted over three years and involving 14 experienced groups, provides reference data for developing a generation of mesoscopic and coarse-grained models as well as AI methods designed to bridge the gap between DNA simulation and chromatin structure.

Methods

Systems setup

Applying an adapted version of the Orenstein and Shamir algorithm86, we generated an optimal sequence covering all of the 2080 unique hexamers of DNA (of length 2145 bp). This optimal sequence was subsequently split into 190 oligomers of 16 bp each, and GpC terminal pairs were added on both ends to facilitate convergence. This choice of oligomer length provides an optimal balance between number of simulations and total computational effort, and is particularly compact, with only 10 of the 2080 hexamers appearing exactly twice in the dataset (the other 2070 appear exactly once). We generated 100 sets of 190 20 bp oligomers in this way, and chose the one featuring the least extreme GpC content per oligomer, resulting in %G/C values ranging from 25% to 80% (excluding terminal GpCs) (see Supplementary Table 1 for the full list of sequences). Duplexes were built in the canonical B-like conformations using Arnott’s parameters as implemented in the Nucleic Acids Builder programme in AMBER suite of programmes87. All systems were surrounded by an octahedral box of water (at least 15 Å distance between any DNA residue and the faces of the octahedron), each system was neutralized by adding ions and 150 mM additional KCl to mimic physiological conditions. Simulation boxes containing around 6 × 105 atoms were optimized, thermalized and equilibrated using Roe & Brooks procedure88. After a careful pre-analysis and benchmark (to be published) considering a variety of recent force fields (FFs) for DNA, ion and water parameters we decided to use parmbsc1 force field for DNA44, OPC water model89 and Li-Merz90 and in particular Joung-Cheatham for TIP4P-EW for monovalent ion parameters91.We should note that similar results are expected from OL15 and OL21 FFs, while the use of three-point water models such as TIP3P or SPC/E leads to slightly more rigid ensembles.

Molecular dynamics protocol

DNA structures were prepared for explicit solvent molecular dynamics simulations using AMBER tools. Hydrogen Mass Repartitioning (HMR) was applied to the topology files using parmed_hmassrepartition from the AMBER suite, tripling hydrogen atom masses and scaling heavy atoms accordingly to enable a 4 fs integration timestep. Simulations were run using the AMBER20 suite of programmes. The systems, comprising DNA, water, and counterions, underwent a 10-step equilibration protocol adapted from Roe & Brooks, standardized by the ABC consortium. This protocol included progressive energy minimizations and NVT/NPT equilibration stages with gradually reduced positional restraints on the DNA. Initial minimizations were performed with force constants up to 5 kcal/mol·Å², followed by NVT and NPT dynamics with decreasing restraints and increasing timesteps. The final step consisted of a 1 ns unrestrained NPT simulation with a 2 fs timestep. Equilibration ensured structural relaxation and system stability before production simulations. Constant-pressure equilibration (10 ns) and production (10 microseconds) were performed under periodic boundary conditions at 1 bar using the Monte Carlo barostat92 and Langevin dynamics to maintain the temperature at 310 K. SHAKE93 constraints were applied on bonds involving hydrogen atoms and a 10 Å cutoff for non-bonded interactions. Trajectories and restart files were written in NetCDF format at regular intervals (20 ps).

Production runs

Equilibrated systems were used as the starting point for each production run. Based on a prior pilot study with palindromic sequences, showing general convergence within 2–3 ms, each simulation was extended to 10 ms and sampled every 20 ps (following again conclusions from a careful benchmarking), the 10 ms were used for analyses of the simulation if not differently stated for some analyses. Simulations were performed in two independent replicas at 310 K and 1 atm. When significant distortions were observed in one of the replicas, the trajectory was extended to 20 microseconds and/or 2–3 additional replicas were launched. Overall, c.a. 4 ms of trajectories were collected, accounting for 10–1 s at the base pair level dynamics. Following FAIR principles, all the set-up, simulation and analyses can be reproduced using BioExcel Building Blocks technology (10.48546/workflowhub.workflow.196.6)94,95.

Molecular dynamics analyses

Canonical analyses

Trajectories were analysed at three levels: (i) the entire 20-mer duplexes, (ii) the central 16-mer duplexes and (iii) each one of the 2080 unique hexamers. Analyses include standard metrics such as the RMSD, helical coordinates96, principal component analysis (PCA), groove dimensions and persistence length (PL)97. All analyses were performed considering the entire trajectories. The hydrogen bonds were calculated using the programme cpptraj of Amber. The same method was used for both terminal and internal base pairs. This programme considers Watson and Crick hydrogen bonds disrupted whenever the donor-acceptor distance exceeded 3.0 Å or the donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle dropped below 135°. In the study, to avoid terminal effect we did not considered the terminal bases (GpC) for the selection of the hexamers, they only consider the central 16mers, as well as analysis at helical base-pair level were obtained by removing those snapshots where the canonical hydrogen bonding (HB) scheme was lost.

Persistence lengths (PL) were calculated following the Noy-Golestanian formalism62, using its implementation in the SerraNA programme97. In this approach, the worm-like chain (WLC) model is adapted to atomic-resolution DNA structures, and directional decay is evaluated between the directional vectors defined at each base pair step. Previous studies have demonstrated that the transition from local to global bending regimes in PL occurs within approximately one helical turn as DNA length increases62,97. Beyond this length, PL values converge, indicating that a 16-mer fragment is adequate for estimating the global persistence length.

Helical parameters analyses

The helical distributions and stiffness force constants of all bps (considering only snapshots where at least two of the Watson and Crick hydrogen bonds were preserved) in all hexamer environments were collected in the normalized six-dimensional helical space (rise, slide, shift, roll, twist and tilt). The deviation of the distributions from normality were assessed using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)-Helguero analysis98, as described elsewhere3,99. Bimodality, which refers to the existence of two sub-states for a given degree of freedom, was determined both at tetramer and hexamer level. In the first case all the data on duplicated tetramers were collected and the whole ensemble was analysed. To evaluate structural diversity across hexamers in a 6-dimensional helical space, k-means clustering was applied to each hexamer’s conformational ensemble. The optimal number of clusters was determined using Gap Statistic100; pooled normalized inertia was used as the dispersion measure within this calculation, normalizing each cluster’s variance by its size to assess overall compactness. Correlation between bps helical movements were explored using linear or circular correlation coefficients as a function of the sequence distance55.

Deformation/frustration energy

Frustration in the helical space at the base pair step (bps) level refers to the energetic cost required to deform a DNA base pair step from its preferred conformation in one sequence context to that in another. To quantify this effect, we calculated the deformation (or “frustration”) energy, defined as the energy needed to shift a given base pair step (XY) from its most populated (equilibrium) structural state in one hexamer (i) to the most populated (equilibrium) state of the same step in another hexamer (j) within the helical parameter space. This approach allows us to determine how the conformational preferences of a base pair step vary as a function of sequence context, revealing which steps are more constrained or more adaptable to local structural changes. For instance, if the central base pair step CpG is compared between two different hexamers, for example AGCGTT and TGCGAA, the frustration energy quantifies how much elastic deformation energy is required for the step CpG in the hexamer AGCGTT to adopt the equilibrium conformation of the CpG step in the hexamer TGCGAA. In this example keeping the same central base pair but varying the neighbouring base pairs we could evaluate the tetrameric effect. A larger frustration energy indicates stronger context-dependent deformation or greater incompatibility between the local structural preferences. We note that frustration plays a central role in the harmonic rigid-base coarse-grain model cgDNA, as developed in refs. 49,50,52,53.

The energetic cost (deformation energy) required to wrap a 147-bp DNA fragment into the nucleosomal conformation was quantified using the same an elastic energy formalism Eq.( 1). DNA conformations were described in terms of local helical parameters. The deformation energy was evaluated by defining the equilibrium reference state using the sequence-dependent base pair step parameters for each base pair in its hexameric sequence environment, together with the corresponding force-constant matrices obtained from molecular the dynamics simulations16,84. The final deformed state was defined by the base pair parameters of DNA in the nucleosome-wrapped conformation. Substituting these quantities into the deformation energy expression allowed a direct estimation of the energetic penalty associated with adopting the nucleosomal geometry. To relate deformation energy to nucleosome affinity, the elastic energy was converted into a Boltzmann-weighted probability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P=\exp \left(\right.-E/{k}_{{{\rm{B}}}}T$$\end{document}P=exp−E/kBT), where kB is the Boltzmann constant, T is the temperature. This probability, here called affinity, provides a quantitative measure of the intrinsic affinity of a DNA sequence for nucleosome formation, with higher probabilities corresponding to lower energetic penalties for nucleosomal wrapping.

Principal component analyses (PCA)

PCA were done in Cartesian space using standard procedures, to check the global flexibility of the 20-mer oligos and to determine the importance of hexamer effects on the essential deformation mode of the central tetramer, as well as to compare the dynamics of different hexamers. In this latter case instead of a heavy atom representation we use a sequence-agnostic representation defined by all the backbone plus N1/N9 C6/C1 atoms of the nucleobases (Pur/Pyr). The similarity between two essential dynamics was performed using Hess metrics101,102 with eigenvectors required to explain 90% of variance.

Interaction properties

DNA-cation interactions were estimated from the density of cations along the major and minor grooves at each bps, which define a 10-dimensional vector for each unique hexamer (2 grooves times 5 bps per hexamer). Cation distributions are analysed using curvilinear helicoidal coordinates83,103, which provide a precise picture of the proximal environment of DNA by accounting for the coupling of ion dynamics with the thermal fluctuations of DNA.

Variational autoencoders (VAE)104

VAE were used to compress the dynamics of the different hexamers into a 6-dimensional deep-learned latent space. To this end a VAE was trained with 8 dihedral angles per nucleotide (backbone α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ, glycosidic χ, and puckering pseudorotation angle P), converted to sin/cos pairs to avoid discontinuities. At this point a single hexamer is described by a vector of 2 strands × 6 residues × 8 dihedrals × 2 trigonometric functions = 192 values between –1 and 1. Both the encoder and decoder feature 5 hidden layers of dimension 4096 each, with a ReLU nonlinearity. The training was performed for 93 epochs on the Watson-strand hexamers (replica 1). The encodings of each hexamer trajectory (subsampled so as to yield 50,000 frames) define a probability distribution in the 6-dimensional latent space. Thus, we use the Hellinger distance105 between two such distributions (0 corresponding to identical, and 1 to fully non-overlapping distributions) as a measure of (dis-)similarity between two hexamers. To calculate this distance, probability density is sampled (through kernel density estimation with optimized bandwidth) at 105 points drawn from the multivariate normal distribution, and the result is calculated as the scaled Euclidean norm of the difference of the square roots of the vectors of probability density at the sampled points.

Dissimilarities

Dissimilarities between each hexamer were calculated and for technical reasons, different dissimilarity metrics were applied for different datasets. For ion distributions, the chosen metric was the root-mean-square difference between the two density profiles, each composed of 5 points. For PCA, the chosen metric was 1 minus the normalized overlap between the principal axes (20 eigenvectors that explain around 90% of the variance). Similarly, for the dihedral distributions, the chosen metric was 1 minus the normalized overlap between the distributions in the low-dimensional latent space. Note that, for the above reasons, dissimilarity metrics are normalized and constrained for PCA and dihedrals, but unnormalized and unconstrained for ion distributions.

Consistency of dissimilarity patterns

For each hexamer, and for each pair of features, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between the given rows of the corresponding dissimilarity matrices. The resulting product of these 6 correlation coefficients is reported as the “cross-feature consistency score”, indicating whether for a given hexamer all features follow the same dissimilarity pattern against all other hexamers.

Sensitivity

The sensitivities of different hexamers were calculated as the maximum dissimilarity that can be achieved through a single nucleotide mutation.

Clustering methods

To identify sequence patterns that yield similar descriptors, we performed clustering based on (dis-)similarity matrices in each descriptor space, and presented the clusters as a dendrogram. The number of clusters was optimized for each dataset (PCA, minor groove ions, major groove ions, dihedrals) by calculating the sums of intra-cluster dissimilarities for cluster numbers between 2 and 20, and choosing the number after the largest drops in this metric, indicating the identification of large meaningful distinctions (as in the “elbow” method). Then, cluster divisions and identities were iteratively reconstructed starting from the smallest to the optimal number of clusters to create the dendrogram. The reported cluster entropy was calculated as the sum of the 6 entropies of the individual bases’ probability distributions. The affinity propagation algorithm, using the precomputed dissimilarity matrix, was used as implemented in Python’s scikit-learn106. We note that analogous dendrograms for tetramer sequences have previously been presented for crystal structure data and cgDNA coarse grain energies in ref. 107.

Dendrogram construction

Dendrograms were constructed by following consecutive cluster splitting events in the hierarchical clustering algorithm, using the custom (precomputed) dissimilarity matrix. Vertical position of the split on the dendrogram was determined based on the population-weighted average of mean inter-cluster dissimilarity. To that end, the average dissimilarity between all pairs of samples in each cluster was first calculated, and a population-weighted average of these per-cluster values was then computed for increasing numbers of clusters. These values were then normalized, i.e., mapped onto the 〈0,1〉 interval via a linear transformation. For each final cluster, the total cluster population and sequence entropy (in bits, with a maximum of 6 × log2(4) = 12) are reported, along with an IUPAC-style consensus sequence. The latter was calculated by representing the probability distribution of the 4 DNA bases at each position in a 3-simplex, dividing up the 3-simplex into equal-volume regions corresponding to each combination (A, T, C, G for 1 base; R, Y, K, M, S, W for 2 bases; B, D, H, V for 3 bases; and N for 4 bases) and assigning the code of the region where the probability distribution mapped to. Sequence logos were generated using logomaker108, and cluster centres (representatives) were chosen as the ones with the lowest mean dissimilarity to all other members of the cluster49.

All analyses were done using standard software (AMBER analysis suite, Curves+96, MDTraj109) as well as “in house” scripts. Analyses were integrated in workflows using BioExcel Building Blocks infrastructure (BioBB)94,95.

UV and NMR experiments

Unlabelled DNA oligonucleotides d(CGACTCAGCG)·d(CGCTGAGTGC) and d(CGCATACGCG)·d(CGCGTATGCG) were purchased from IDT with a standard desalting purification. The samples were dissolved in a buffer containing 50 mM NaCl and 25 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7) and hybridized. They were then heated to 90 °C and slowly cooled down to allow duplex formation. Melting experiments were performed by heating from low temperature to 95 °C at 0.5 °C/min, monitoring absorbance at 260 nm every 0.5 °C with samples at 5 μM. NMR spectra were acquired in the described buffer with 10% of D2O at 5 °C, 15 °C, 25 °C, 35 °C and 45 °C. Spectra were acquired in a Bruker spectrometer operating at 600 MHz, equipped with cryoprobe and processed with the TOPSPIN software. Water suppression was achieved by including an excitation sculpting module in the pulse sequence110.

Data storage

All trajectories were stored in a non-relational database using MongoDB infrastructure and MDDB protocols111. All trajectories are stored in a FAIR format with detailed provenance. This includes the group performing the simulation, validation, and all the individual steps followed to launch the simulation. Overall, we have collected around 0.25 PB of trajectories, which are stored in Barcelona, with a backup located at the Centre for High Performance Computing (University of Utah, Salt Lake City, Utah, USA).
