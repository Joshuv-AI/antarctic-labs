Local LMO: Constrained Gradient Optimization via a Local Linear Minimization Oracle
Peter RichtárikKAUSTKaja Gruntkowska

Abstract
We design Local LMO – a new projection-free gradient-type method for constrained optimization. The key algorithmic idea is to replace the global linear minimization oracle over the constraint set used by Frank–Wolfe ( FW ) with a local linear minimization oracle over the intersection of the constraint set and a “small” ball centered at the current iterate. In particular, when minimizing f : ℝ d → ℝ f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} over a constraint ∅ ≠ 𝒳 ⊆ ℝ d \emptyset\neq\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} , Local LMO performs the iteration x k + 1 ∈ arg ⁡ min z ∈ 𝒳 ∩ ℬ ⁡ ( x k , t k ) ⁡ ⟨ ∇ f ​ ( x k ) , z ⟩ , x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\rangle, where x 0 ∈ 𝒳 x_{0}\in\mathcal{X} , and t k > 0 t_{k}>0 is a suitably chosen radius which can be interpreted as an effective stepsize. While designed as an alternative to FW , Local LMO is perhaps best viewed as a generalization of Gradient Descent ( GD ) rather than a modification of FW . Indeed, it is easy to see that Local LMO reduces to GD in the unconstrained setting and, more generally, to GD restricted to an affine subspace if the constraint 𝒳 \mathcal{X} is affine. We prove that this simple algorithmic scheme transfers the known (unaccelerated) convergence rates of Projected Gradient Descent ( PGD ) to the projection-free world in several important regimes, some of which are beyond the reach of FW . In contrast to FW theory, i) our guarantees hold without requiring the feasible set 𝒳 \mathcal{X} to be bounded (and unlike FW , our rates therefore do not depend on its diameter), ii) our theory does not require the “curvature” assumption, which allows us to establish a standard sublinear rate for convex functions with bounded gradients ( FW is not known to converge in this regime), iii) we obtain a linear rate in the smooth strongly convex regime ( FW obtains a linear rate under special assumptions on the geometry of 𝒳 \mathcal{X} only). Furthermore, we obtain sharp sublinear rates in the smooth convex and non-convex regimes, in the ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smooth convex regime, and in stochastic and non-differentiable settings.

Introduction

Constrained optimization problems play a fundamental role in optimization theory and machine learning (ML)
(

)
. In this work, we revisit the classical formulation

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x),

(1)

Such problems arise throughout ML

Constrained formulations play an increasingly important role in modern ML systems. As learning algorithms are deployed in safety-critical domains—including finance, healthcare, and autonomous systems—there is a growing demand for models that are safe, robust, interpretable, and fair

Naturally, the extremely rich optimization literature offers a multitude of algorithms for handling such problems. A useful way of mapping out the landscape of these methods is to group them by the oracle used to access the constraint set

Related work

Here we give an overview of closely related literature, with a broader discussion in
Appendix

B
.

Our method is a
local projection-free first-order method
. It is naturally positioned between Projected Gradient Descent (
PGD
)
(
67
)
and Frank–Wolfe (
FW
)
(
18
;
48
)
.

PGD
performs the update

x_{k+1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\bigr),

where
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\coloneqq\arg\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-z\|
, and thus relies on a
projection oracle
. Under standard assumptions, it matches the optimal convergence rates of (unaccelerated) unconstrained Gradient Descent (
GD
), without dependence on global geometric properties of
\mathcal{X}
, such as its diameter
\diam\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}{\left\|x-y\right\|}
, and naturally applies to unbounded constraints. Its main drawback, however, is that projections can be computationally expensive or even intractable for many constraints.

By contrast, classical Frank–Wolfe computes

\displaystyle s_{k}\in{\rm LMO}_{\mathcal{X}}(\nabla f(x_{k}))

and then updates
x_{k}
by moving toward
s_{k}
, thus relying on a
global linear minimization oracle
(see
Section

B.3
, the original paper of
(
18
)
and the modern overview by
(
39
)
). Thus,
FW
avoids projections altogether.
Classical
FW
is typically analyzed for problems of the form (
1
) under the assumptions that
\mathcal{X}
is nonempty, convex, and compact, that
f
is convex and differentiable on
\mathcal{X}
. A large body of work shows that, if
f
is additionally smooth, one obtains the classical sublinear rate
f(x_{K})-f(x_{\star})=\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{K}})
, which is generally unimprovable
(
37
;
46
;
39
)
. This smoothness assumption is often expressed via the
FW
curvature constant
C
(see (
33
)), rather than a global gradient Lipschitz constant
(
39
)
.
Stronger guarantees, such as linear convergence, generally require substantially stronger assumptions, e.g, that the solution lies in the interior of
\mathcal{X}

(
33
)
, or that
f
is strongly convex together with favorable geometry of the feasible set (most often polyhedral structure)
(
48
)
. Such rates are typically established not for vanilla
FW
, but for enhanced variants such such as away-step, pairwise, or fully corrective methods. Moreover, there are examples where no rate improvement is possible even when
f
is strongly convex
(
46
)
.
Overall, the simplicity of
FW
-type methods comes with well-known limitations (not shared by
PGD
), summarized in
Table

1
.
This highlights a fundamental distinction between projection- and LMO-based methods.

It is also useful to distinguish our method from other linearly convergent projection-free variants, such as away-step
FW

(
77
)
, pairwise
FW
, and fully corrective
FW
methods
(
44
)
. Those methods still rely on the
global
linear oracle, but add away/correction mechanisms to overcome the slow boundary behavior of vanilla
FW
; their analyses typically rely on polyhedral geometric constants such as pyramidal width
(
44
)
. Our method instead enforces progress by restricting the search to a
local trust region
. In this sense, it is closer in spirit to localized
FW
methods than to away-step or pairwise
FW
.

A new projection-free constrained optimization framework.

We introduce the
Local Linear Minimization Oracle
(
Local LMO
) framework, which replaces the global linear minimization used in

with a local oracle restricted to
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})

GD

While related ideas exist in the literature on

(
LLOO

variants. In contrast, our motivations, theoretical results, and interpretations arise from a different geometric perspective; see the detailed discussion in
Section

B.7
.

2

We developed the

\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{K}})

3.1
Master theorem

We start with a fundamental one-step result that underlies all subsequent convergence guarantees: under appropriate radius choices, each iteration produces a decrease in distance to the solution set. Notably, this result holds without having to assume convexity of

Theorem 3.1

(One-step behavior of
Local LMO
)
.

Let
3.1

,
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{\star}
, and let one of the following radius admissibility conditions be satisfied:

Type-I admissibility:

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0,

\displaystyle\quad 0<t_{k}\leq\frac{\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\,x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|},

(5)

Type-II admissibility:

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{k})\neq\nabla f(x_{\star}),

\displaystyle\quad 0<t_{k}\leq\frac{\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star}),\,x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}.

(6)

Let

be obtained from

(

, i.e.,

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}\limits_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\rangle.

Then

(i)

(7)

(ii)

\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=t_{k}

In the Type-I Admissibility case, the proof can be found in

, and in the Type-II admissibility case, refer to
Section

D.1.2
.
∎

The key inequality (
8
) shows that each step yields a
Fejér-type decrease
with respect to the solution set
\mathcal{X}_{\star}
.
Note that (
7
) follows from (
8
). However, while (

A subtle point concerns the admissibility conditions in
Theorem

3.1
, which involve ratios of the form (
5
)-(
6
). These expressions require that the corresponding gradients (or gradient differences) are nonzero. When the denominator in (
5
) vanishes, the situation can be treated separately: if
\nabla f(x_{k})=0
, then
x_{k}
is a first-order stationary point, which in our setting implies optimality.
This highlights an interesting structural feature of the framework. When
\nabla f(x_{\star})=0
, the constraint set plays no active role at the solution, since the unconstrained minimizer already lies in
\mathcal{X}
. In this case, the problem effectively reduces to unconstrained optimization, and one could in principle discard the constraint and run vanilla
GD
instead. Nevertheless, even when the constraint is inactive at optimality, it may still provide algorithmic benefit: prior knowledge that
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}
can be exploited to improve performance.
More generally, our method can leverage such structural information by inheriting the improved conditioning of the restricted problem. For instance, when
\mathcal{X}
is an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
Algorithm

1
reduces to
GD
on that subspace, and its convergence is governed by the corresponding subspace condition number rather than the ambient one. This leads to acceleration whenever the objective is better conditioned along the constraint set.
In contrast,
PGD
applies a full-dimensional gradient step followed by projection, which yields a theory governed by global geometric quantities rather than the geometry of the active subspace.

The Type-II admissibility condition (
6

Since it involves gradient differences, the denominator can vanish without implying optimality, and hence such a condition cannot be used as a stopping criterion without additional assumptions. 3.6
Practical considerations

Across all regimes considered above,

matches the strong convergence guarantees of

while avoiding projections. However, for a fair comparison with projection-based methods, it is important to acknowledge that the stepsize (radius) rules prescribed in

3.6

Indeed, these rules depend on quantities that are typically unknown, such as

and

suggests choosing

and

Section

5
Conclusions

One of the main limitations of our results is the dependence of the theoretical radii (see
Table

1
) on generally unknown problem-specific quantities, such as
f(x_{\star})
,
\nabla f(x_{\star})
, and
{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}
. While our preliminary experiments with a toy quadratic problem suggest that a heuristic radius rule motivated by our theory may work well in the smooth strongly convex setting, much more further theoretical and empirical work is clearly needed. Further, our
Local LMO
framework may provide new insights into recently proposed LMO-based deep learning optimizers such as
Muon
,
Scion
and
Gluon
. In particular, in
Section

B.9
we show how intersecting spectral constraints with local trust-region balls leads to a damped LMO update that may be useful for neural network training. These insights are also of a preliminary nature, and are merely meant to hint at interesting future work directions. In general, we view this work as the foundational paper for a new class of projection-free gradient-type methods, which means that a lot is yet to be discovered. In fact, the structure of our method hints at deeper and potentially far-reaching insights: we show in Appendix
A
that
Local LMO
admits a natural composite-optimization interpretation as a forward–backward scheme for minimizing
f+\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
, where
\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
is the indicator function of
\mathcal{X}
, in which the usual proximal backward step
(
52
)
is replaced by a broximal backward step
(
31
)
. This perspective further clarifies the connection of our method to projected/proximal gradient methods, while highlighting its hard-constrained trust-region nature.
We invite the community to contribute to further development of this promising new direction.

Appendix A
Local LMO as a forward–backward method with a broximal backward step

In this section we explain that
Local LMO
can be viewed as a forward--backward scheme
5
5

5

All results of this paper, including those in this section, can be formulated in the Hilbert space setting. We resort to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for simplicity of exposition only.
for the composite problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;F(x)\coloneqq f(x)+g(x),

(17)

where

g(x)\coloneqq\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\;\coloneqq\;\begin{cases}0,&x\in\mathcal{X},\\
+\infty,&x\notin\mathcal{X}.\end{cases}

(18)

Problem (
17
) is of course equivalent to the constrained problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x).

The point of view developed below is useful for two reasons.
First, it clarifies the relation between
Local LMO
and classical forward–backward / proximal-gradient methods.
Second, it shows that
Local LMO
can be interpreted as a
hard-constrained
analogue of proximal gradient descent, in exactly the same spirit in which the broximal operator introduced by
[
31
]
is a hard-constrained analogue of the classical proximity operator
[
52
]
.

A.1
Classical forward–backward splitting

Let us first recall the usual forward–backward update for (
17
) in the case when
f
is differentiable and
g
is proper, closed, and convex. Given a stepsize
\gamma_{k}>0
, the method takes the form

x_{k+1}=\prox_{\gamma_{k}g}\left(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

(19)

Equivalently,
x_{k+1}
is the minimizer of the regularized first-order model

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{g(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma_{k}}{\left\|z-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}\right\}.

(20)

Thus, the forward–backward method can be interpreted as follows:

•

the
forward step
is the linearization of
f
at
x_{k}
,

•

the
backward step
is the minimization of this linearized model regularized by the quadratic penalty
\frac{1}{2\gamma_{k}}{\left\|z-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}
.

In the special case (
18
), we have

\prox_{\gamma_{k}g}(y)={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(y),

and hence (
19
) reduces to Projected Gradient Descent:

x_{k+1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

A.2
From prox to brox

The broximal operator
[
31
]
replaces the quadratic penalty by a hard ball constraint.
Given a proper function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, a center
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a radius
t>0
, define

\brox_{h}^{t}(y)\;\coloneqq\;\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{B}(y,t)}h(z),

(21)

where

\mathcal{B}(y,t)\coloneqq\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;:\;{\left\|z-y\right\|}\leq t\right\}.

Hence, whereas the proximity operator solves the penalized problem

\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z}\left\{h(z)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}{\left\|z-y\right\|}^{2}\right\},

the broximal operator solves the hard-constrained counterpart

\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z}\left\{h(z)\;:\;{\left\|z-y\right\|}\leq t\right\}.

Thus, moving from the proximity to the broximity operator should be thought of
as replacing a
soft quadratic penalty
by a
hard trust-region constraint
.

A.3
A forward–backward interpretation of Local LMO

We now apply this idea to the composite problem (
17
). At iteration
k
, define the first-order model of
F=f+g
around
x_{k}
by

m_{k}(z)\coloneqq g(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle.

(22)

The natural broximal forward–backward step associated with this model is

x_{k+1}\in\brox_{m_{k}}^{t_{k}}(x_{k}).

(23)

Using the definition (
21
), this means

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\{g(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle\right\}.

(24)

Substituting
g=\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
, we obtain

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\{\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle\right\}.

(25)

Since
\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(z)=+\infty
outside
\mathcal{X}
, the feasible set in (
25
) collapses to
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
, and hence

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle.

(26)

Finally, since
-\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}\right\rangle
is constant with respect to
z
, (
26
) is equivalent to

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle.

(27)

But (
27
) is exactly the
Local LMO
update.

We have therefore proved the following interpretation.

Proposition A.1

(Forward–backward–brox interpretation of
Local LMO
)
.

Consider the constrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x),

and rewrite it in composite form as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)+\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(x).

Then the
Local LMO
iteration

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle

is equivalent to the forward–backward step

x_{k+1}\in\brox_{m_{k}}^{t_{k}}(x_{k}),\qquad m_{k}(z)=\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle.

In other words,
Local LMO
is obtained from classical forward–backward splitting by replacing the proximal backward step with a broximal backward step.

Proof.

The equivalence follows immediately from (
23
)–(
27
).
∎

A.4
Two variants of the broximal backward step

It is important to emphasize that the interpretation above is
not
the same as writing

x_{k+1}\in\brox_{g}^{t_{k}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

Indeed, this latter expression would minimize only
g
over a ball centered at the forward point
x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})
, and hence would correspond to a direct replacement of
\prox_{\gamma_{k}g}(y)
by
\brox_{g}^{t_{k}}(y)
inside the classical formula (
19
). This is
not
what
Local LMO
does. Instead,
Local LMO
keeps the center of the ball at the current iterate
x_{k}
, linearizes the smooth part
f
, adds the non-smooth term
g
, and then applies the broximal operator to this linearized composite model:

x_{k+1}\in\brox_{\,g+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\cdot-x_{k}\right\rangle}^{t_{k}}(x_{k}).

Thus, the correct interpretation is:

Local LMO
is a forward–backward method in which the backward step is performed not on
g
itself, but on the
linearized composite model

g+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\cdot-x_{k}\right\rangle
, using a broximal operator instead of a proximal one.

A.5
Comparison with classical PGD

The distinction between
PGD
and
Local LMO
can now be expressed in one line.

For
g=\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
, projected gradient descent solves

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma_{k}}{\left\|z-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}\right\}.

(28)

By contrast,
Local LMO
solves

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(z)+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle\;:\;{\left\|z-x_{k}\right\|}\leq t_{k}\right\}.

(29)

Thus:

•

PGD
uses a
soft quadratic regularization
,

•

Local LMO
uses a
hard trust-region constraint
.

This is exactly analogous to the relation between the proximity operator and the broximal operator.

A.6
A monotone inclusion viewpoint

There is also a useful inclusion interpretation. The optimality condition for (
17
) is

0\in\nabla f(x)+\partial g(x).

Since
g=\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
, we have
\partial g(x)=N_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
, and therefore the problem is equivalent to the monotone inclusion

0\in\nabla f(x)+N_{\mathcal{X}}(x).

(30)

For classical forward–backward, the implicit step reads

0\in\nabla f(x_{k})+\partial g(x_{k+1})+\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}(x_{k+1}-x_{k}).

For
Local LMO
, the first-order optimality condition for (
27
) gives

0\in\nabla f(x_{k})+\partial g(x_{k+1})+N_{\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}(x_{k+1}).

(31)

Since
g=\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
, this becomes

0\in\nabla f(x_{k})+N_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k+1})+N_{\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}(x_{k+1}).

(32)

Hence the Euclidean penalty term

\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})

from proximal forward–backward is replaced by the normal cone to the trust-region ball,

N_{\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}(x_{k+1}).

Appendix B
Related work

B.1
Constrained optimization in machine learning

Constrained optimization is a fundamental tool in machine learning, used to encode prior knowledge, enforce structure, and ensure desirable properties of learned models. Rather than relying solely on unconstrained objectives with regularization, many modern applications impose explicit constraints of the form

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x),

where the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
captures domain-specific requirements. In this section, we provide a brief and non-exhaustive overview of key applications, focusing on representative examples rather than a complete survey.

Constraints are commonly used to impose structure on model parameters. Classical examples include sparsity constraints for feature selection
[
73
]
, low-rank constraints in matrix completion and representation learning
[
64
]
, and simplex constraints in probabilistic models such as topic models
[
8
]
. In deep learning, structured constraints are used to enforce sparsity, low-rank structure, or orthogonality in neural networks, improving training stability, generalization, and interpretability
[
74
,
4
,
29
]
.

A prominent example arises in adversarial robustness. In this setting, adversarial examples are generated by maximizing the loss over a norm-bounded perturbation set, typically an
\ell_{p}
ball around a data point
[
24
,
50
]
. The constraint set encodes the allowable perturbations, and robust training then amounts to solving a constrained min–max optimization problem, where the outer minimization seeks model parameters that perform well under worst-case perturbations
[
50
]
.

In sensitive applications, constraints are used to enforce fairness, safety, and reliability requirements
[
83
,
2
]
. For instance, one may impose constraints on prediction disparities across demographic groups, or enforce monotonicity and boundedness conditions in high-stakes decision systems. These constraints encode requirements that cannot be captured by the loss function alone and are increasingly important in real-world ML deployments.

Many ML models require parameters to lie in structured domains, leading naturally to constrained optimization formulations. A prominent example is the enforcement of orthogonality constraints on weight matrices, which can be formulated as optimization over the Stiefel manifold and has been used to stabilize training and improve generalization in deep networks
[
1
,
4
]
.

Another important class of constraints arises from enforcing Lipschitz continuity
[
26
]
, which is closely tied to robustness and stability. This leads to optimization problems with spectral or norm constraints on network weights, such as bounding operator norms
[
27
,
66
]
.

In many of the settings above, the constraint set
\mathcal{X}
is structured but complex: projections may be computationally expensive, while simpler primitives (e.g., linear minimization over
\mathcal{X}
) can be efficient. As a result, there is strong practical motivation for optimization methods that can exploit such structure without requiring costly projections.
This motivates the study of alternative oracle models for constrained optimization that better align with the computational structure of ML applications. In particular, methods that avoid global projections while retaining strong convergence guarantees are of significant interest.

B.2
Projected Gradient Descent

Projection-based first-order methods are among the most classical and widely used approaches for solving constrained optimization problems of the form (
1
). They combine a gradient step with a projection onto the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
, typically defined via a Bregman divergence
D_{\phi}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\phi}(x)\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}}D_{\phi}(z,x).

In the Euclidean setting
\phi(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, this reduces to the standard projection

{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)=\arg\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-z\|.

This leads to the well-known Projected Gradient Descent (
PGD
) iteration

\displaystyle x_{k+1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\bigr),

where
\gamma_{k}>0
is a stepsize, typically chosen as
\gamma_{k}\equiv\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
for
L
–smooth convex objectives.

At a high level,
PGD
performs an unconstrained gradient step followed by a correction that enforces feasibility. This simple structure has made it a foundational method in constrained optimization, with extensive theoretical and algorithmic development; see, e.g., the classical references
59
,
5
,
58
.

PGD
enjoys convergence guarantees that match those of unconstrained gradient methods:
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
in the smooth convex case,
\mathcal{O}(\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}))
in the smooth strongly convex case,
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})
in the bounded-gradient convex case, and
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})
for finding an
\varepsilon
-stationary point in the smooth non-convex case, measured by the projected gradient mapping
G_{\gamma}
. These rates are known to be optimal for unaccelerated first-order methods under the corresponding suboptimality or stationarity criteria.

A key implication is that constraints do not degrade the oracle complexity of the method:
PGD
achieves these guarantees without additional cost beyond projection operations. This makes it one of the few methods that cleanly separates optimization difficulty from feasibility constraints in the oracle complexity sense.

Despite its strong theoretical properties, the practical efficiency of
PGD
critically depends on the cost of computing the projection step. For simple constraint sets–such as norm balls, boxes, simplices, or other polyhedral structures–projections admit closed forms or can be computed efficiently
[
16
,
80
,
76
,
69
]
.
However, for many modern applications, the projection itself requires solving a nontrivial optimization problem over
\mathcal{X}
. In such cases, each
PGD
iteration may be as expensive as solving a full auxiliary problem, making the projection step the computational bottleneck. This limitation is particularly pronounced when
\mathcal{X}
is high-dimensional.

B.3
Frank–Wolfe

The main limitation of
PGD
is not its convergence rate, but its reliance on efficient projections. While its oracle complexity is optimal, its per-iteration cost can be prohibitive. This has motivated a broad line of work on projection-free methods, which replace projections with different oracles. These methods trade off dependence on the geometry of
\mathcal{X}
for cheaper iterations, but typically at the cost of weaker convergence guarantees.

The Frank–Wolfe (
FW
) method
[
18
,
48
]
, also known as the conditional gradient method
[
11
]
, is a canonical example of such a projection-free first-order algorithm for constrained convex optimization. Unlike projected gradient methods, which require computing projections onto the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
,
FW
replaces this step with a single call per iteration to a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO). Specifically, at iteration
k
, it computes

\displaystyle s_{k}\in{\rm LMO}_{\mathcal{X}}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\coloneqq\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle

and updates

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\gamma_{k}(s_{k}-x_{k}),

where
\gamma_{k}\in[0,1]
is a stepsize.
The LMO solves a linear problem over
\mathcal{X}
, which is often significantly cheaper than projection. As a result,
FW
is particularly well suited for large-scale problems where projections can be computationally prohibitive.

Due to this favorable structure,
FW
has found numerous applications across machine learning and optimization, including large-scale learning problems
[
43
,
53
]
, pruning large language models
[
68
]
, matrix completion
[
21
]
, optimal transport
[
49
]
, optimal experiment design
[
38
]
, image processing
[
41
]
, submodular function maximization
[
17
,
3
]
, and quantum physics
[
14
]
.

From a theoretical perspective,
FW
is known to achieve a sublinear convergence rate in general. For smooth and convex objectives, it requires at least
\Omega(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
gradient and LMO calls to compute an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution
[
39
,
46
]
. The smoothness assumption is often expressed through the
FW
curvature constant
C

[
39
]
, defined as

\displaystyle C\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,s\in\mathcal{X},t\in[0,1],\\
y=x+t(s-x)\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{t^{2}}\left(f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\right),

(33)

rather than a global Lipschitz constant for the gradient.
The same projection-free structure also extends to smooth non-convex objectives, but with a stationarity rather than primal-gap guarantee: over a compact convex set
\mathcal{X}
, the minimum
FW
gap satisfies
\bar{g}_{K}=\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{K}}}\right)

[
45
]
.

More recently,
75
studied
FW
under the
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smoothness condition and proposed an
(L_{0},L_{1})
-aware short-step variant. For compact convex feasible sets, they proved an
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
primal-gap guarantee in the general convex case, together with faster rates under additional assumptions. This extends
FW
analysis beyond the classical globally smooth setting. However, the result relies on a modified short-step rule adapted to the
(L_{0},L_{1})
geometry, rather than the standard curvature-based framework.

Faster convergence can only be achieved under additional structural assumptions. In particular, when the constraint set
\mathcal{X}
is strongly convex, linear convergence is possible if the solution lies in the interior of
\mathcal{X}
and the objective is strongly convex, or if the unconstrained minimizer lies outside
\mathcal{X}

[
48
,
77
]
. These results highlight that the location of the optimizer plays a crucial role in the behavior of
FW
.
It remained unclear whether the strong convexity of
\mathcal{X}
alone can yield faster convergence rates without additional assumptions on the optimizer’s location. This question was partially resolved by
19
, who showed that when both the objective function and the feasible set are strongly convex,
FW
achieves a rate of
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}})
.

Recently,
34
proved that this rate is in fact optimal: for smooth and strongly convex objectives over smooth and strongly convex sets,
FW
with exact line search or short-step variants requires at least
\Omega(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}})
iterations. This result establishes that the uniform convergence rate of
\Omega(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}})
is tight with respect to
\varepsilon
, and further shows that the dependence on the optimizer’s location is intrinsic rather than an artifact of earlier analyses. Moreover, since the lower bound applies to smooth sets, it rules out any acceleration stemming from the smoothness of
\mathcal{X}
in this setting.
A concurrent work by
28
derived a similar lower bound of
\Omega(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}})
for a broader class of LMO-based methods using a zero-chain construction. Their result covers a broader algorithm class, but only applies in the high-dimensional regime and its extension to smooth constraint sets is limited to more restrictive settings where the smoothness parameter scales with the target accuracy.

Beyond strong convexity, other structural assumptions can also lead to improved rates. For example, when
\mathcal{X}
is a polytope, several
FW
variants, such as away-step, pairwise, and fully corrective methods, achieve faster convergence rates under suitable conditions. We refer to
11
for a comprehensive overview of these variants and their theoretical guarantees.

For a modern perspective on lower bounds and their historical development, see
34
,
28
.

Frank–Wolfe curvature.

We provide a simple example showing that bounded gradients on
\mathcal{X}
do not guarantee finite Frank–Wolfe curvature.

Example B.1

(Bounded gradients do not imply finite Frank–Wolfe curvature)
.

Consider the convex function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
defined over
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
by

\displaystyle f(x)=x^{3/2}.

Then
f
is differentiable on
\mathcal{X}
with gradient
\nabla f(x)=\nicefrac{{3\sqrt{x}}}{{2}}
. Hence, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\frac{3}{2},

so the bounded gradient condition (
13
) holds with
G=\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}
.

We now show that the Frank–Wolfe curvature constant (
33
) is infinite. Fix
x=0
and
s=1
, and let
y=x+t(s-x)=t
for
t\in[0,1]
. Since the right derivative
\nabla f(0)=0
, we have

\displaystyle f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle=t^{3/2}.

Substituting into (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t^{2}}\left(f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\right)=2t^{-1/2}.

Taking
t\to 0
yields divergence, and therefore
C=\infty
, showing that bounded gradients alone do not control the second-order behavior captured by the Frank–Wolfe curvature constant.

B.4
Interior-point methods

When additional structure of
\mathcal{X}
is available, one can exploit it to design more specialized algorithms. A common setting is when
\mathcal{X}
admits an explicit representation of the form

\mathcal{X}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;:\;\psi_{i}(x)\leq 0,\;i=1,\dots,m,Ax=b\}.

Interior-point methods approach such problems by solving a sequence of approximations to the original constrained problem, typically using Newton-type methods.

A canonical example is the barrier method
[
55
,
10
]
. The key idea is to replace the inequality constraints with a penalty term in the objective, yielding a problem to which Newton’s method can be applied.
To motivate this, consider rewriting the problem by incorporating the constraints via indicator functions

\displaystyle I_{-}(z)=\begin{cases}0&z\leq 0,\\
\infty&z>0.\end{cases}

This leads to the equivalent formulation

\displaystyle\min_{x:Ax=b}\left\{f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}I_{-}\left(\psi_{i}(x)\right)\right\}.

While this removes the explicit constraints, the resulting objective is not differentiable, preventing the direct application of Newton’s method.
The barrier method replaces
I_{-}
with the approximation

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{-}(z)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{t}\log(-z)&z\leq 0,\\
\infty&z>0,\end{cases}

where
t>0
controls the accuracy of the approximation. The function
\hat{I}_{-}
is convex, nondecreasing, differentiable and closed, and becomes a better approximation of
I_{-}
as
t\to\infty
.
This leads to the
logarithmic barrier function

\phi(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\log(-\psi_{i}(x)),

and the associated problem

\min_{x:Ax=b}\left\{tf(x)+\phi(x)\right\}.

Barrier methods approach (
1
) by approximately solving a sequence of such problems for an increasing sequence of scalars
t
, typically using second-order methods. This requires access to the first- and second-order derivatives of the functions
\psi_{i}
representing the constraints.

Barrier methods are only one class within interior-point methods. Another important class is primal–dual interior-point methods
[
78
]
, which update primal and dual variables by solving perturbed KKT systems. For a comprehensive overview, see
10
.

B.5
Splitting and primal-dual approaches

Another closely related line of work studies projection-free methods for problems whose feasible region is described by several simpler constraints.
In our case, even when linear minimization over
\mathcal{X}
and over the ball
\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
are individually tractable, linear minimization over their intersection can be substantially harder.
Splitting methods address this difficulty by decomposing a complicated feasible structure into simpler components.

In the Frank–Wolfe setting, this idea has been developed through augmented-Lagrangian and primal-dual formulations.
For example,
22
consider problems in which the feasible region is represented through multiple convex sets coupled by a linear constraint.
Instead of requiring a single LMO over the full intersection, their method calls LMOs over the separate component sets and enforces consistency through an augmented Lagrangian mechanism.
Related primal-dual conditional-gradient methods, such as those of
81
and
72
, similarly combine LMO-based primal updates with multiplier or proximal mechanisms for handling affine and composite constraints.

These methods are different from the
Local LMO
approach studied here.
Splitting and primal-dual approaches avoid a difficult joint oracle and recover feasibility through consistency enforcing mechanisms, whereas our
Local LMO
keeps the intersection
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
inside the oracle and therefore enforces local feasibility directly.

B.6
Penalty approaches

Penalty methods provide a related but conceptually distinct comparison point.
Rather than decomposing the feasible region into simpler oracle calls, penalty methods replace hard constraints by additional objective terms that measure constraint violation
[
59
,
7
]
.
For example, for equality constraints
h(x)=0
and inequality constraints
g_{i}(x)\leq 0
, a quadratic penalty formulation considers a sequence of problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}{\left\|h(x)\right\|}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i}\left(\max\{0,g_{i}(x)\}\right)^{2}\right\},

where
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter.
As
\rho
increases, infeasible points become increasingly expensive, and solutions of the penalized problems are driven toward the feasible region.
Exact penalty methods instead use non-smooth penalties, such as
\ell_{1}
-type constraint violation terms, and can recover constrained solutions for sufficiently large finite penalty parameters under suitable assumptions.

From the perspective of
Local LMO
methods, a penalty based analogue would replace the exact local feasible subproblem by a softened model. Instead of solving

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle,

one could consider a penalized surrogate of the form

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle+\rho\dist^{2}(z,\mathcal{X})+\rho\left(\max\{0,{\left\|z-x_{k}\right\|}-t_{k}\}\right)^{2}\right\}.

Such a surrogate may be easier to optimize or decompose, but it does not enforce feasibility exactly for finite
\rho
.
Thus, while penalty methods control feasibility through violation terms,
Local LMO
treats both
z\in\mathcal{X}
and
z\in\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
as hard constraints of the linearized subproblem.

B.7
Local linear optimization oracle

20
propose a conditional gradient method closely related to our
Local LMO
for smooth and strongly convex optimization over a polyhedral set
\mathcal{P}
, achieving linear convergence.

The main algorithmic ingredient of the algorithm is the
local linear optimization oracle
(
LLOO
). For
x\in\mathcal{P}
,
t>0
, and
g\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an oracle
\mathcal{A}(x,t,g)
is an
LLOO
for the polytope
\mathcal{P}
with parameter
\rho\geq 1
if it returns a feasible point
p\in\mathcal{P}
such that

(i)

\forall y\in\mathcal{B}(x,t)\cap\mathcal{P}
it holds that
\left\langle g,y\right\rangle\geq\left\langle g,p\right\rangle
,

(ii)

{\left\|x-p\right\|}\leq\rho t
.

Thus, the LLOO relaxes our local subproblem

\displaystyle{\rm LMO}_{\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t)}(g)\coloneqq\mathop{\arg\!\min}\limits_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t)}\left\langle g,z\right\rangle

(34)

by solving a linear problem over a larger set.

One of the main contributions of
20
is to show that such an oracle can be constructed with
\rho
depending only on the dimension
d
and a polytope-dependent parameter
\mu(\mathcal{P})
, using a single call to the standard LMO (
2
) (that in this case returns a vertex of
\mathcal{P}
minimizing a given linear objective). Hence, the per-iteration oracle complexity matches that of classical
FW
.
Their construction uses one LMO call together with a convex decomposition of the input
x
: the atoms are sorted by their inner product with
g
, and mass is shifted, subject to the ball constraint, from atoms with largest values to the atom minimizing
\left\langle g,\cdot\right\rangle
.

They establish that, for
\rho=d\mu(\mathcal{P})
, the iterates of their
LLOO
-based algorithm applied to problem (
1
)m with
f
being
L
–smooth and
\mu
–strongly convex over
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{P}
, satisfy

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f(x_{\star})=\mathcal{O}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{\mu K}{4L\rho^{2}}\right)\right).

B.8
Comparison with Local LMO

The line of work on local linear optimization oracles is the closest prior literature to our
Local LMO
. Indeed,
20
explicitly proposed using a local oracle that, given a convex and compact set
\mathcal{X}
, radius
t
, and linear objective, returns a feasible point whose linear objective value is at least as good as that of every point in
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t)
, while remaining within a controlled multiple of the radius. Their motivation was precisely that a localized linear oracle can yield much faster projection-free methods than vanilla
FW
, including linear convergence over polytopes
[
20
]
. Thus, at the level of the oracle model, the idea of replacing projections by a
local
linear minimization problem is not new. However, we arrived at
Local LMO
independently and became aware of
LLOO
only during the literature review.

That said, our formulation differs conceptually from the usual
LLOO
presentation. The
LLOO
literature typically frames the method as an improved
FW
variant and analyzes it via dual gaps, objective decrease, and polyhedral geometry
[
20
,
44
]
. By contrast, our method is cast as a
brox-style trust-region linearization scheme

[
31
,
32
]
. Each step solves the linearized problem over the exact set
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
, and the analysis is driven by a Fejér-type geometric decrease of the form

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-t_{k}^{2}.

(35)

This viewpoint makes the connection to
PGD
especially transparent: our method may be interpreted as a projection-free
local
alternative to
PGD
, rather than a variant of
FW
.
Compared to standard global
FW
oracle (
2
), which is often relatively cheap for structured feasible regions
[
39
]
, our method relies on a stronger oracle, since solving (
34
) can be substantially harder (though in some cases it may be simpler). This oracle-strength issue is emphasized explicitly in the LLOO literature:
20
note that exact optimization over
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t)
may be significantly more difficult than over
\mathcal{X}
. This likely explains why local linear oracle methods, despite their conceptual importance, have not become standard textbook baselines in the same way as
PGD
or vanilla
FW
.

As discussed above, our formulation differs conceptually from the
LLOO
perspective. Rather than relying on objective decrease and polyhedral geometry, we view the method as a
prox-style trust-region linearization scheme

[
31
,
32
]
, applicable to arbitrary nonempty, closed, and convex sets
\mathcal{X}
. Each step solves the linearized problem over the exact set
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
, and the analysis is based on a Fejér-type decrease (
35
), which yields

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}\right)^{2k}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}

for radii
t_{k}=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{L+\mu}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|
(see
Section

3.3
).

B.9
Modern motivation: LMO methods in deep learning

Although the projection-versus-LMO dichotomy has been known for decades, recent developments in large-scale machine learning have renewed interest in LMO-based optimization. Several modern optimizers for deep learning explicitly rely on LMOs, including the
Muon
optimizer
[
40
]
and related methods such as
Scion

[
60
]
and
Gluon

[
65
]
.

Scion
is closely related to
FW
through its use of LMO over a norm ball.
In the constrained variant, the update takes the form

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=(1-\gamma_{k})x_{k}+\gamma_{k}{\rm LMO}_{\mathcal{X}}(d_{k}),

which coincides with a stochastic or momentum version of
FW
over the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
. In particular, when
d_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
, this reduces exactly to one step of the classical
FW
update over
\mathcal{X}
.
These developments have sparked a resurgence of interest in
FW
-type and projection-free methods more broadly. This renewed practical relevance motivates revisiting the theoretical foundations of LMO-based methods and addressing the classical limitations of the
FW
framework.

1. Whole space.

2. Singleton.

.
Since
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we necessarily have
x=c
, and therefore

z^{\star}=c.

3. Affine subspace.

Let
\mathcal{X}=a+\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear subspace, and let
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}
denote the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{V}
.
Since feasible displacements from
x
must lie in
\mathcal{V}
, the problem reduces to minimizing the linear form over the Euclidean ball in
\mathcal{V}
.
Hence

z^{\star}=\begin{cases}x-t\dfrac{{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}g}{\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}g\|},&{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}g\neq 0,\\[8.53581pt]
x,&{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}g=0.\end{cases}

(see
Appendix

F
).

4. Hyperplane.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ a^{\top}z=b\}
,
a\neq 0
.
This is a special case of an affine subspace.
If we define the component of the gradient tangent to the hyperplane by

g_{\top}\coloneqq g-\frac{a^{\top}g}{\|a\|^{2}}a,

then

z^{\star}=\begin{cases}x-t\,\dfrac{g_{\top}}{\|g_{\top}\|},&g_{\top}\neq 0,\\[8.53581pt]
x,&g_{\top}=0.\end{cases}

5. Affine line.

Let
\mathcal{X}=a+\operatorname{span}\{v\}
,
v\neq 0
.
Denote
\hat{v}\coloneqq v/\|v\|
. Since feasible displacements are one-dimensional, the solution is obtained by moving to one endpoint of the feasible segment:

z^{\star}=\begin{cases}x-t\,\hat{v},&\langle g,\hat{v}\rangle>0,\\[2.84526pt]
x+t\,\hat{v},&\langle g,\hat{v}\rangle<0,\\[2.84526pt]
\text{any point in }\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t),&\langle g,\hat{v}\rangle=0.\end{cases}

6. Ray.

Let
\mathcal{X}=a+\{\alpha v:\ \alpha\geq 0\}
,
\|v\|=1
.
Write
x=a+\alpha_{x}v
,
\alpha_{x}\geq 0
.
Then feasible points in
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t)
are of the form
a+\alpha v
, where
\alpha\in\left[\max\{0,\alpha_{x}-t\},\,\alpha_{x}+t\right]
.
Hence

z^{\star}=a+\alpha^{\star}v,

where

\alpha^{\star}=\begin{cases}\max\{0,\alpha_{x}-t\},&\langle g,v\rangle>0,\\[2.84526pt]
\alpha_{x}+t,&\langle g,v\rangle<0,\\[2.84526pt]
\text{any feasible }\alpha,&\langle g,v\rangle=0.\end{cases}

7. Line segment.

Let
\mathcal{X}=[a,b]\coloneqq\{(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b:\ \lambda\in[0,1]\}
, suppose
x=(1-\lambda_{x})a+\lambda_{x}b
.

Then feasible points are parameterized by

\lambda\in[0,1]\cap\left[\lambda_{x}-\frac{t}{\|b-a\|},\,\lambda_{x}+\frac{t}{\|b-a\|}\right].

Thus

z^{\star}=(1-\lambda^{\star})a+\lambda^{\star}b,

where
\lambda^{\star}
is the left or right endpoint of this interval depending on the sign of
\langle g,b-a\rangle
:

\lambda^{\star}=\begin{cases}\text{left endpoint},&\langle g,b-a\rangle>0,\\[2.84526pt]
\text{right endpoint},&\langle g,b-a\rangle<0,\\[2.84526pt]
\text{any feasible }\lambda,&\langle g,b-a\rangle=0.\end{cases}

8. Euclidean ball.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{B}(c,R)\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \|z-c\|\leq R\}
.
The local problem is

\min_{z\in\mathcal{B}(c,R)\cap\mathcal{B}(x,t)}\langle g,z\rangle,\qquad u\coloneqq\frac{g}{\|g\|}.

There are three cases:

•

If the minimizer over
\mathcal{B}(x,t)
remains in
\mathcal{X}
, namely if
\|x-tu-c\|\leq R
,
then

z^{\star}=x-tu.

•

If
x-tu\notin\mathcal{X}
, but the minimizer over
\mathcal{X}
lies in
\mathcal{B}(x,t)
, namely if
\|c-Ru-x\|\leq t
, then

z^{\star}=c-Ru.

•

In the remaining case, neither of the two single-ball minimizers is feasible for the other constraint. Hence, both constraints are active at the optimum, and
z^{\star}
lies on the intersection
\|z-x\|=t
and
\|z-c\|=R
.
In this case
x\neq c
, so the following quantities are well defined:

d\coloneqq c-x,\quad\rho\coloneqq\|d\|,\quad e_{1}\coloneqq\frac{d}{\rho},\quad\alpha\coloneqq\frac{\rho^{2}+t^{2}-R^{2}}{2\rho},\quad s\coloneqq\sqrt{t^{2}-\alpha^{2}}.

Let
p\coloneqq u-(u^{\top}e_{1})e_{1}
be the component of
u
orthogonal to
e_{1}
. If
p\neq 0
, then

z^{\star}=x+\alpha e_{1}-s\,\frac{p}{\|p\|}.

If
p=0
, then every point in the sphere intersection is optimal. In the degenerate case
s=0
, this point is unique.

9. Slab between two parallel hyperplanes.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \ell\leq a^{\top}z\leq r\},a\neq 0
.
If the unconstrained minimizer
x-tu
lies in the slab, then

z^{\star}=x-tu.

Otherwise, at least one of the two slab boundaries is active at the solution.
In that case, the solution can be constructed explicitly in two steps: first move from
x
along the direction
-u
until the active boundary hyperplane is reached, and then use the remaining radius budget along the tangential component of
-u
within that hyperplane. Since the second step reduces exactly to the hyperplane case treated above, this yields an explicit closed-form expression for
z^{\star}
.

C.3
Generic distance decomposition

The following simple lemma plays an important role in the convergence proof.

Lemma C.1

(Generic decomposition)
.

For any three points
x_{+},x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|x_{+}-z\|^{2}=\|x-z\|^{2}-2\langle x-x_{+},x_{+}-z\rangle-\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}.

In particular,

\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}=\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{k}-x_{k+1},\,x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\rangle-\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}.

(37)

Proof.

Observe that
x-z=(x-x_{+})+(x_{+}-z).

Hence, by expanding the squared norm, we get

\|x-z\|^{2}=\|(x-x_{+})+(x_{+}-z)\|^{2}=\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+\|x_{+}-z\|^{2}+2\langle x-x_{+},x_{+}-z\rangle.

Rearranging this identity yields

\|x_{+}-z\|^{2}=\|x-z\|^{2}-2\langle x-x_{+},x_{+}-z\rangle-\|x-x_{+}\|^{2},

which proves the first claim. The second identity follows immediately by substituting

x_{+}=x_{k+1},\qquad x=x_{k},\qquad z=x_{\star}.\qed

C.4
Strict convexity

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper function.
Recall that
f
is
strictly convex
if for every pair of distinct points
x,y\in\dom f
and every
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)<\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y).

Lemma C.2

(Strict convexity implies strict monotonicity of the gradient)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable and strictly convex on a convex set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),\,x-y\rangle>0,\qquad\nabla f(x)\neq\nabla f(y)

for any two distinct points
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Since
f
is convex and differentiable, the first-order inequality holds at every point:

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\rangle,

(38)

and

f(x)\geq f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),\,x-y\rangle.

We claim that if
x\neq y
, then both inequalities are in fact strict:

f(y)>f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\rangle,

(39)

f(x)>f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),\,x-y\rangle.

(40)

Indeed, suppose for contradiction that equality holds in (
38
), i.e.,
f(y)=f(x)+\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle
.
Let
z_{t}\coloneqq(1-t)x+ty
,
t\in[0,1]

By convexity of
f
,

f(z_{t})\leq(1-t)f(x)+tf(y).

Using the assumed equality, the right-hand side becomes

(1-t)f(x)+t\bigl(f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\bigr)=f(x)+t\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle.

On the other hand, applying (
38
) with
z_{t}
in place of
y
, we get

f(z_{t})\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),z_{t}-x\rangle=f(x)+t\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle.

Hence

f(z_{t})=f(x)+t\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle=(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)\qquad\forall t\in[0,1].

Thus
f
is affine on the segment
[x,y]
, which contradicts strict convexity since
x\neq y
. Therefore (
39
) holds.
The proof of (
40
) is identical. Now add (
39
) and (
40
):

f(y)+f(x)>f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle.

Canceling
f(x)+f(y)
from both sides yields

0>\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle.

Rearranging gives
\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle>0
, which proves the claim.
∎

C.5
Reverse Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality says that

\langle u,v\rangle\leq\|u\|\|v\|

for any vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In general, Cauchy–Schwarz does not admit a reverse form. However, when
u
and
v
are special, a reverse form of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality holds. In particular, such a reverse inequality holds for

v=x-y,\qquad u=\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),

where
f
is
L
–smooth and
\mu
–strongly convex, stated and proved below. It plays an important role in our convergence proof of
Local LMO
in the smooth and strongly convex regime.

Lemma C.3
.

If
f
is differentiable,
L
–smooth, and
\mu
–strongly convex, then

\theta\,\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\,\|x-y\|\leq\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(41)

where
\theta\coloneqq\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{L+\mu}
.

Proof.

Let us start with the standard inequality satisfied by all
L
–smooth and
\mu
–strongly convex functions:

\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq\frac{1}{L+\mu}\left(\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}+\mu L\|x-y\|^{2}\right),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(42)

Applying the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab

with
a=\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|
and
b=\sqrt{\mu L}\,\|x-y\|
, we obtain

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}+\mu L\|x-y\|^{2}\geq 2\sqrt{\mu L}\,\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\,\|x-y\|.

Substituting this into (
42
) proves (
41
).
∎

Appendix E
Convergence theory of Projected Gradient Descent

E.1
Smooth convex case

We first consider the smooth convex regime.

Proposition E.1

(Convergence for
PGD
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty closed convex set.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L
–smooth on an open set containing
\mathcal{X}
. Consider the
PGD
update

x_{k+1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right),\qquad 0<\gamma\leq\frac{1}{L}.

Define the projected gradient mapping by

G_{\gamma}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma}\Bigl(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x-\gamma\nabla f(x))\Bigr),

and let
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{\star}\coloneqq\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
. Then the following hold for all
k\geq 1
:

(i)
One-step descent:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(70)

(ii)
Fejér-type inequality:

\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\gamma\bigl(f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\bigr).

(iii)
Function value rate:

f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2\gamma k}.

(iv)
Best-iterate projected-gradient rate:

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2\bigl(f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)}{\gamma k}.

(v)
Distance-based projected-gradient rate for strict stepsizes:

if
0<\gamma<\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, then

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma L)\,k}.

(vi)
Ergodic function value rate:

for the average
\bar{x}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}
, one has

f(\bar{x}_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2\gamma k}.

(vii)
Gradient-difference bound:

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}k}.

Remark E.1
.

In the special case
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, these bounds become

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2k},

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|G_{1/L}(x_{i})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L\bigl(f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)}{k},

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}_{k})-f(x_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2k},

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{k}.

(71)

Proof.

Let
z_{k}\coloneqq{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k}))
, so that
z_{k}=x_{k+1}
and
G_{\gamma}(x_{k})=\frac{1}{\gamma}(x_{k}-z_{k})
.

(i). By the
L
–smoothness of
f
,

f(z_{k})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z_{k}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Since
\gamma\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, we have
\nicefrac{{L}}{{2}}\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\gamma}}
, hence

\displaystyle f(z_{k})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z_{k}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(72)

On the other hand, the optimality condition for projection gives

\displaystyle\left\langle x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})-z_{k},x-z_{k}\right\rangle\leq 0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

(73)

Since
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, setting
x=x_{k}
yields

\left\langle x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})-z_{k},x_{k}-z_{k}\right\rangle\leq 0.

Expanding this inequality, we obtain
\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z_{k}-x_{k}\rangle\leq-\frac{1}{\gamma}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Substituting this into (
72
) gives

f(z_{k})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{\gamma}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}=f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Therefore,

f(x_{k+1})=f(z_{k})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}=f(x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(ii).
Setting
x=x_{\star}
in (
73
), we obtain

\langle x_{k}-x_{k+1},x_{\star}-x_{k+1}\rangle\leq\gamma\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{\star}-x_{k+1}\rangle.

Using the identity

2\langle a-b,c-b\rangle=\|a-b\|^{2}+\|c-b\|^{2}-\|a-c\|^{2}

with
a=x_{k}
,
b=x_{k+1}
, and
c=x_{\star}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\gamma}\Bigl(\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\Bigr)\geq\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\rangle.

(74)

By convexity,
f(x_{\star})\geq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{\star}-x_{k}\rangle,

hence

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle.

(75)

Also, by
L
–smoothness,

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(76)

Subtracting
f(x_{\star})
from both sideds of (
76
) and combining if with (
75
),

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\leq\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(77)

Since
\gamma\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, we have
\frac{L}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}
, so

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\leq\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Therefore, using (
74
)

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\Bigl(\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\Bigr),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\gamma\bigl(f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\bigr).

(78)

(iii). Summing (
78
) from
i=0
to
k-1
, we get

2\gamma\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\bigl(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}.

Since
f(x_{i})
is nonincreasing by (
70
), we have

k\bigl(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\bigl(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{\star})\bigr).

Therefore,
f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2\gamma k}
.

(iv). Summing the descent inequality (
70
) for
i=0,\dots,k-1
, we obtain

\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq f(x_{0})-f(x_{k})\leq f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star}).

Hence

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2\bigl(f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)}{\gamma}.

Dividing by
k
and noticing that the minimum is upper bounded by the average yields the desired result

(v).
Combining (
74
) with (
77
),

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\Bigl(\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\frac{1}{2\gamma}-\frac{L}{2}\Bigr)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Equivalently,

\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\gamma\bigl(f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)-(1-\gamma L)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

If
0<\gamma<\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, then
1-\gamma L>0
, and therefore

\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-(1-\gamma L)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Since
x_{k+1}-x_{k}=-\gamma G_{\gamma}(x_{k})
, we get

\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-(1-\gamma L)\gamma^{2}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\|^{2}.

Summing for
i=0,\dots,k-1
, we obtain

(1-\gamma L)\gamma^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2},

and dividing by
k
yields

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|G_{\gamma}(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma L)k}.

(vi). By convexity,
f(\bar{x}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(x_{i})
.
Hence

f(\bar{x}_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(f(x_{i})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\bigl(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{\star})\bigr)\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2\gamma k}.

(vii). Since
x_{\star}
minimizes
f
over the closed convex set
\mathcal{X}
, the first-order optimality condition implies

\langle\nabla f(x_{\star}),x-x_{\star}\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

Equivalently,
x_{\star}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(x_{\star}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{\star})\bigr)
.
Using the nonexpansiveness of the projection, we get

\|x_{i+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{i}-x_{\star}-\gamma(\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star}))\|^{2}.

Expanding,

\|x_{i+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{i}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\gamma\langle\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star}),x_{i}-x_{\star}\rangle+\gamma^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}.

Since
f
is convex and
L
–smooth,

\frac{1}{L}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Because
\gamma\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, it follows that

\gamma\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star}),x_{i}-x_{\star}\rangle.

Hence,

\|x_{i+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{i}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\gamma^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}.

Summing for
i=0,\dots,k-1
, and dividing by
k
,

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}k}.\qed

Remark E.2
.

Most of the results above are known and can be found in prior literature
[

,

,

,

]
.
Among these bounds, the most standard headline results are the

E.2
A negative result for Projected Gradient Descent

We now show that the quadratic dependence on
L
in the gradient-difference bound

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{L^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{k}

cannot, in general, be improved to a linear dependence on
L
.
More precisely, we prove that there is no universal constant
C>0
such that, for every
L
–smooth convex constrained problem, the iterates of Projected Gradient Descent satisfy

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{CL\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{k}.

(79)

Construction.

Let the feasible set be
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and consider the family of convex quadratic functions

f_{\alpha}(u,v)\coloneqq\frac{\alpha}{4}u^{2}+\alpha uv+\alpha v^{2}+v=\frac{\alpha}{4}(u+2v)^{2}+v,\qquad\alpha>0.

The gradient and Hessian are

\nabla f_{\alpha}(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\alpha}{2}u+\alpha v\\[3.99994pt]
\alpha u+2\alpha v+1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\nabla^{2}f_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha/2&\alpha\\
\alpha&2\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

The Hessian has eigenvalues
0
and
\frac{5\alpha}{2}
. Hence
f_{\alpha}
is convex and
L
–smooth with

L=\frac{5\alpha}{2}.

We claim that the constrained minimizer is
x_{\star}=(0,0)
.
Indeed,

f_{\alpha}(u,v)=\frac{\alpha}{4}(u+2v)^{2}+v.

Since
v\geq 0
for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}
, and the quadratic term is always nonnegative, we have

f_{\alpha}(u,v)\geq 0\qquad\forall(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}.

Moreover,
f_{\alpha}(0,0)=0
, so
(0,0)
is a constrained minimizer.

PGD with the standard stepsize.

Let
x_{0}=(1,0)\in\mathcal{X}
and run Projected Gradient Descent with the standard stepsize

\gamma=\frac{1}{L}=\frac{2}{5\alpha}.

We first compute the gradient at
x_{0}
:

\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{0})=\nabla f_{\alpha}(1,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha/2\\
\alpha+1\end{pmatrix}.

Hence the unconstrained gradient step is

x_{0}-\gamma\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{0})=\left(1-\frac{\gamma\alpha}{2},-\gamma(\alpha+1)\right).

Since the second component is negative, projection onto
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
sets it to zero, and therefore

x_{1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(x_{0}-\gamma\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{0})\bigr)=\left(1-\frac{\gamma\alpha}{2},\,0\right).

Using
\gamma=\frac{2}{5\alpha}
, we get

\frac{\gamma\alpha}{2}=\frac{1}{5},\qquad\text{so}\qquad x_{1}=\left(\frac{4}{5},0\right).

Gradient differences.

We next compute the gradients at
x_{\star}
,
x_{0}
, and
x_{1}
.
At the constrained minimizer,

\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})=\nabla f_{\alpha}(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore

\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{0})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha/2\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix},

and thus

\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{0})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})\|^{2}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}+\alpha^{2}=\frac{5\alpha^{2}}{4}.

Now evaluate the gradient at
x_{1}=(4/5,0)
:

\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{5}\\[3.00003pt]
\alpha\cdot\frac{4}{5}+1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\alpha/5\\[3.00003pt]
4\alpha/5+1\end{pmatrix}.

Hence

\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{1})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})=\begin{pmatrix}2\alpha/5\\[3.00003pt]
4\alpha/5\end{pmatrix},

and so

\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{1})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})\|^{2}=\left(\frac{2\alpha}{5}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{4\alpha}{5}\right)^{2}=\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{25}+\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{25}=\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{5}.

Therefore, for
k=2
,

\min_{0\leq i\leq 1}\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{i})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})\|^{2}=\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{5}.

Contradiction with a hypothetical linear-in-
L
bound.

Now suppose that there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that (
79
) holds for every
L
–smooth convex constrained problem.
Applying (
79
) to the present example with
k=2
, we would obtain

\min_{0\leq i\leq 1}\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{i})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{CL\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2}.

Since
L=\nicefrac{{5\alpha}}{{2}}
and
\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}=\|(1,0)-(0,0)\|^{2}=1
, the right-hand side becomes

\frac{CL\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{2}=\frac{C}{2}\cdot\frac{5\alpha}{2}=\frac{5C\alpha}{4}.

But we already computed that

\min_{0\leq i\leq 1}\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{i})-\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{\star})\|^{2}=\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{5}.

Hence (
79
) would force

\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{5}\leq\frac{5C\alpha}{4}.

Equivalently,

\alpha\leq\frac{25}{16}C.

Therefore, if we choose

\alpha>\frac{25}{16}C,

then (
79
) fails.

Since
C
was arbitrary, no universal constant
C
can make (
79
) valid for all
L
–smooth convex constrained problems.
We have thus shown that there is no universal constant
C>0
such that

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{C\,L\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{k}

holds for all
L
–smooth convex constrained problems.
Thus, the quadratic dependence on
L
in the bound

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla f(x_{i})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{L^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{k}

is, in general, unavoidable.

E.3
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smooth convex case

Proposition E.2

(
PGD
under asymmetric
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smoothness)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty closed convex set.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and asymmetrically
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth (
H.1
) on a open set containing
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by the
PGD
iteration

\displaystyle x_{k+1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\right),

where
\gamma_{k}>0
.
If

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}+\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|},\right)

(80)

then for every
K\geq 1

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma_{k}^{2}}.

In particular, for

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}+\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}\right),

(81)

the iterates satisfy

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}+\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{4\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{K}.

Moreover, if
\sqrt{K}>L_{1}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|\leq\frac{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|\right)\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|}{\sqrt{K}-L_{1}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|},

and if
K\geq 4L_{1}^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|\right)^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{K}.

Proof.

Denote
\Delta_{k}:=\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})
.
Since
x_{\star}
is a minimizer of
f
over
\mathcal{X}
, for every
\gamma_{k}>0
we have
x_{\star}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{\star}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{\star})\right)
(Refer to the proof of
Proposition

E.1
(vii)).
Therefore, by the nonexpansiveness of the projection,

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\right)-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{\star}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{\star})\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}-\gamma_{k}\left(\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\gamma_{k}\langle\Delta_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle+\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}.

Next, by
Lemma

H.1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}+\frac{\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}\right)\leq\langle\Delta_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle.

Hence, under the choice (
80
),

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}+\frac{\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}\right)\leq\langle\Delta_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle.

Substituting this into the previous bound yields

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\Delta_{k}\|^{2}.

Summing for
k=0,1,\dots,K-1
and dropping the nonnegative term
\|x_{K}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2},

and hence

\displaystyle\left(\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma_{k}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma_{k}^{2}},

proving the first part of the statement.
Now, choosing
\gamma_{k}
as in (
81
), the bound above becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}+\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|}\right)^{2}\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}.

The rest of the proof follows directly from the proof of
Theorem

H.1
.
∎

E.4
Non-convex case

Projected gradient descent also admits convergence guarantees in the non-convex setting.
The following result is obtained by specializing the proximal-gradient convergence theorem of
5
.

Proposition E.3

(
PGD
in the smooth non-convex case)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty closed convex set.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
–smooth on an open set containing
\mathcal{X}
, and assume that
f_{\star}\coloneqq\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
is finite.
Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by the
PGD
iteration

\displaystyle x_{k+1}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right),

where
0<\gamma\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, and define the projected gradient mapping by

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x-\gamma\nabla f(x)\right)\right).

Then, for every
K\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f_{\star}}{\gamma\left(1-\frac{L\gamma}{2}\right)\,K}.

In particular, for
\gamma=\frac{1}{L}
,

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{1/L}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{2L\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)}{K}.

Moreover,
G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Consequently, every accumulation point of
\{x_{k}\}
is a first-order stationary point.

Proof.

The result is an immediate specialization of
5
to the composite problem

\displaystyle F(x)=f(x)+\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(x),

where
\iota_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the indicator function of
\mathcal{X}
. In
5
’s notation, the non-smooth term is
g(x)\equiv\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
,
and the proximal-gradient update with constant parameter
\bar{L}\geq L
is

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\prox_{\iota_{\mathcal{X}}/\bar{L}}\left(x_{k}-\frac{1}{\bar{L}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right)={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\frac{1}{\bar{L}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

Choosing
\bar{L}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
, we recover exactly the
PGD
iteration.
Notice that gradient mapping in
5
is

\displaystyle G_{\bar{L}}(x)=\bar{L}\left(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x-\frac{1}{\bar{L}}\nabla f(x)\right)\right)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x-\gamma\nabla f(x)\right)\right)=G_{\gamma}(x).

Therefore,
[
5
, Theorem 10.15]
yields
G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\to 0
, every accumulation point of
\{x_{k}\}
is stationary, and

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f_{\star}}{MK},

where, in the constant-stepsize case,

M=\frac{\bar{L}-\nicefrac{{L}}{{2}}}{\bar{L}^{2}}.

Substituting
\bar{L}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
gives
M=\gamma\left(1-\nicefrac{{L\gamma}}{{2}}\right)
,
and hence

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f_{\star}}{\gamma\left(1-\frac{L\gamma}{2}\right)\,K}.

Setting
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
yields

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{1/L}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{2L\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)}{K}.

∎

Appendix F
Gradient descent in a subspace

Assume that the feasible set is an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}=a+\mathcal{V}
, where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear subspace.
We have the following result.

Proposition F.1

(Explicit form of Local
LMO
on an affine subspace)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{X}=a+\mathcal{V}
,
where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear subspace.
Let
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}
denote the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{V}
. Consider one step of
Algorithm

1
, i.e.,

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\rangle,

(82)

where
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
and
t_{k}>0
. Then

x_{k+1}=\begin{cases}x_{k}-t_{k}\dfrac{{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})}{\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\|},&\text{if }{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0,\\[8.53581pt]
x_{k},&\text{if }{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})=0.\end{cases}

In particular, if
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0
, then the step is unique.

Proof.

Since
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}=a+\mathcal{V}
, every point
z\in\mathcal{X}
can be written uniquely in the form
z=x_{k}+s
,
s\in\mathcal{V}
,
{\left\|s\right\|}\leq t_{k}
.
Therefore the subproblem (
82
) can be written as

\min_{s\in\mathcal{V},\ {\left\|s\right\|}\leq t_{k}}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}+s\right\rangle.

Since the term
\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}\right\rangle
does not depend on
s
, this is the same as

\min_{s\in\mathcal{V},\ {\left\|s\right\|}\leq t_{k}}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),s\right\rangle.

Now, for every
s\in\mathcal{V}
, we have

\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),s\right\rangle=\left\langle{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k}),s\right\rangle,

because
s\in\mathcal{V}
and
\nabla f(x_{k})-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\in\mathcal{V}^{\perp}
.
Hence the problem further reduces to

\min_{s\in\mathcal{V},\ {\left\|s\right\|}\leq t_{k}}\left\langle{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k}),s\right\rangle.

If
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})=0
, then the objective is identically zero over the feasible set.
Hence every
s\in\mathcal{V}
satisfying
{\left\|s\right\|}\leq t_{k}
is optimal.
Equivalently, every point in
x_{k}+\{s\in\mathcal{V}:{\left\|s\right\|}\leq t_{k}\}
is a solution of (
82
).
In particular,
s=0
yields the valid choice
x_{k+1}=x_{k}
.
Assume now that
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0
.
Then by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\left\langle{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k}),s\right\rangle\geq-{\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}{\left\|s\right\|}\geq-{\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}t_{k}

for every feasible
s
.
Equality holds if and only if

{\left\|s\right\|}=t_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=-t_{k}\frac{{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})}{{\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}.

Therefore,

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-t_{k}\frac{{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})}{{\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}.\qed

The above proposition shows that, when
\mathcal{X}
is an affine subspace, the method in
Algorithm

1
coincides with normalized
GD
on the restriction of
f
to that affine subspace whenever the projected gradient is nonzero.
At points where the projected gradient vanishes, this identification holds under the natural rule
x_{k+1}=x_{k}
.
Indeed, consider the restricted problem

\min_{x\in a+\mathcal{V}}f(x).

The tangent space of the feasible set is
\mathcal{V}
, and the gradient of the restricted objective is precisely the projection of the ambient gradient onto this tangent space:

\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x).

Therefore, whenever
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0
, the update from
Proposition

F.1
can be rewritten as

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-t_{k}\frac{\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})}{{\left\|\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})\right\|}}.

Thus the method moves in exactly the same direction as
GD
for minimizing
f
over the affine subspace
\mathcal{X}
, namely the negative projected gradient direction, but with step length prescribed directly by the radius
t_{k}
.
For comparison, ordinary
GD
on the restricted problem would take the form

x_{k+1}^{\rm GD}=x_{k}-\eta_{k}{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})=x_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k}),

for some scalar stepsize
\eta_{k}>0
.
Hence, whenever
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0
, the two methods differ only in how the step length is parameterized.
In fact, the update from
Algorithm

1
is exactly a
GD
step with effective stepsize

\eta_{k}=\frac{t_{k}}{{\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}=\frac{t_{k}}{{\left\|\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})\right\|}}.

This discussion can be summarized formally as follows.

Remark F.1

(Connection with
GD
on the affine subspace)
.

When
\mathcal{X}=a+\mathcal{V}
is an affine subspace,
Algorithm

1
coincides with normalized
GD
applied to the restriction of
f
to
\mathcal{X}
.
More precisely, if
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k})\neq 0
, then

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-t_{k}\frac{\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})}{\|\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})\|},\qquad\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla f(x_{k}).

Equivalently, the same step can be written as an ordinary
GD
step

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta_{k}\,\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k}),\qquad\eta_{k}=\frac{t_{k}}{\|\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}f(x_{k})\|}.

Hence, in the affine subspace case,
Algorithm

1
is exactly
GD
on the restricted problem
f|_{\mathcal{X}}
, expressed in trust-region form.

Finally, the unconstrained case appears as the special case
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in which
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}=\operatorname{Id}
is the identity mapping. Then the formula reduces to

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-t_{k}\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|},

which is precisely normalized
GD
in the ambient space.

Appendix G
Non-differentiable case

3.1
requires the function
f
to be convex and differentiable.
Under this assumption, we then consider three settings: (i) smooth objectives, (ii) strongly convex and smooth objectives, and (iii) objectives with bounded gradients.
The first two explicitly require smoothness, and hence differentiability of
f
.

In this section, we focus on the last setting and remove the differentiability assumption. It is known that, in general,
FW
does
not
converge in this setting
[
57
, Example 1]
. Nesterov’s counter-example is simple:

d=2,\quad f(u,v)=\max\{u,v\},\quad\mathcal{X}=\{(u,v)\;:\;u^{2}+v^{2}\leq 1\}.

See also
[
82
, Appendix 3]
.

We make a slight modification to
Algorithm

1
.
At iteration
k
, if
0\in\partial f(x_{k})
, then
x_{k}
is already a minimizer of
f
, and the algorithm terminates. Otherwise, the method selects a subgradient
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
and defines the next iterate as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle g_{k},z\right\rangle.

(83)

We now show that the bounded gradient analysis extends directly to this non-smooth setting.

Proposition G.1

(Well-posedness in the non-smooth case)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex.
For every
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, every
t_{k}\geq 0
, and every choice of subgradient
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
, the set
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})
is nonempty, closed, and bounded.
Consequently, the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle g_{k},z\right\rangle

admits at least one solution.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of
Theorem

C.1
.
∎

Proposition G.2

(Type-I admissibility with subgradients)
.

Let
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
and
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{\star}
.
Assume that

\displaystyle g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k}),\qquad 0\notin\partial f(x_{k}),\qquad 0<t_{k}\leq\frac{\left\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\rangle}{{\left\|g_{k}\right\|}}.

Define

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle g_{k},z\right\rangle.

Then the conclusions of the Type-I part of
Theorem

3.1
remain valid, namely,

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}

\displaystyle\geq t_{k},\qquad{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|}=t_{k},\qquad{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}\leq{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}-t_{k}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of the Type-I admissibility part in
Theorem

3.1
, after replacing
\nabla f(x_{k})
by
g_{k}
.
Indeed, the argument uses only the admissibility condition, the optimality conditions of the local linear minimization subproblem, and the geometry of the ball constraint.
No differentiability of
f
is needed.
∎

Theorem G.1

(Rate under bounded subgradients)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex, and let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by the modified
Local LMO
method in (
83
), with
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Assume that the subgradients of
f
are bounded on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\left\|g\right\|}\leq G\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\ \forall g\in\partial f(x),

for some constant
G>0
.
Let
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{\star}
, and assume that whenever
0\notin\partial f(x_{k})
, the radius is chosen as

\displaystyle t_{k}\coloneqq\frac{f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})}{{\left\|g_{k}\right\|}},\qquad g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k}).

If
0\in\partial f(x_{k})
, then
t_{k}=0
and the method terminates.
Then, for every
K\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\right)^{2}\leq\frac{G^{2}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K}.

(84)

Moreover, the average iterate
\hat{x}_{K}\coloneqq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle f(\hat{x}_{K})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{G{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}}.

(85)

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 0
. If
0\in\partial f(x_{k})
, then
x_{k}
is a minimizer of
f
, and there is nothing to prove.
So assume that
0\notin\partial f(x_{k})
.
Since
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
, the subgradient inequality gives
f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq\left\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\rangle
.
Dividing by
{\left\|g_{k}\right\|}
, we obtain

\displaystyle t_{k}=\frac{f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})}{{\left\|g_{k}\right\|}}\leq\frac{\left\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\rangle}{{\left\|g_{k}\right\|}}.

Hence the Type-I admissibility condition in
Proposition

G.2
is satisfied.
Therefore,

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}\leq{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}-t_{k}^{2}.

Substituting the definition of
t_{k}
yields

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}\leq{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}-\frac{\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\right)^{2}}{{\left\|g_{k}\right\|}^{2}}.

Using the bounded subgradients assumption, we get

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}\leq{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}-\frac{\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\right)^{2}}{G^{2}}.

Summing this inequality for
k=0,1,\dots,K-1
, we obtain after rearranging,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\right)^{2}\leq\frac{G^{2}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K}.

This proves (
84
).
The bound (
85
) for the average iterate follows exactly as in the proof of
Theorem

3.6
, by Jensen’s inequality.
∎

Appendix H
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smooth case

We now consider the generalized smoothness regime in place of the standard
L
–smoothness assumption.
Throughout this section, we assume that
f
is defined on an open convex set containing
\mathcal{X}
and satisfies the following assumption:

Assumption H.1
.

The function

is asymmetrically
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smooth, meaning that there exist constants
L_{0}>0,L_{1}\geq 0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle{\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|}\leq\left(L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(y)\right\|}\right){\left\|x-y\right\|}.

We refer the readers to
25
for further details on this assumption.

It is known that for a convex function

Lemma H.1

(Lemma 2.2 of
25
)
.

Let
f
be convex, satisfying
H.1
.
For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|}^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(y)\right\|}}+\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|}^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|}}\right)\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\right\rangle.

Theorem H.1

(Rate under asymmetric
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smoothness)
.

Let Assumptions
3.1
and
H.1
hold, and
f
be convex. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the iterates generated by
Algorithm

1
. Assume that the radii are chosen as

\displaystyle t_{k}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right),&\nabla f(x_{k})\neq\nabla f(x_{\star}),\\[5.16663pt]
0,&\nabla f(x_{k})=\nabla f(x_{\star}).\end{cases}

Then, for every
K\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K},

and hence

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K}.

Moreover, if
\sqrt{K}>L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\leq\frac{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\right){\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}-L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}.

Thus, if
K\geq 4L_{1}^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{4(L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|})^{2}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K}.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 0
. If
\nabla f(x_{k})=\nabla f(x_{\star})
, then
t_{k}=0
, and the proof is trivial.
Assume now that
\nabla f(x_{k})\neq\nabla f(x_{\star})
. By
Lemma

H.1
with
x=x_{k}
and
y=x_{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}+\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}\right)\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star}),x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\rangle.

Dividing both sides by
{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}
, we obtain

\displaystyle t_{k}\leq\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star}),x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\rangle}{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}.

Hence the Type-II admissibility condition of
Theorem

3.1
is satisfied. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}\leq{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}-t_{k}^{2}.

Summing this inequality for
k=0,1,\dots,K-1
,
rearranging and substituting the definition of
t_{k}
yield

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{K}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)\right)^{2}\leq{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}.

Dropping the nonnegative term
{\left\|x_{K}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}
and dividing by
K
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K}.

Now we apply

\min\limits_{0\leq k\leq K-1}a_{k}\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}a_{k},

with

\displaystyle a_{k}:=\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)\cdot{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\right)^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\dfrac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}^{2}}{K}.

To interpret the result, notice that by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|},

\displaystyle L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}.

We can thus lower bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\geq\frac{1}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}}+\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\right).

Hence

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\leq\frac{{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}}.

Now fix an index
k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
attaining the minimum above, and suppose that
\sqrt{K}>L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}{L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}}\leq\frac{{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}}.

Rearranging gives

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}}\right){\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\leq\frac{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\right){\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}}.

Since
1-\frac{L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}}>0
, we conclude that

\displaystyle{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\leq\frac{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\right){\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}-L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\leq\frac{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}\right){\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{\sqrt{K}-L_{1}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}}.

Finally, if
K\geq 4L_{1}^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\sqrt{K}-L_{1}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|\geq\frac{\sqrt{K}}{2},

and hence

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|\leq\frac{2\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|\right)\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|}{\sqrt{K}}.

Squaring both sides gives

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{\star})\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{\star})\|\right)^{2}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{K}.

∎

Appendix I
Non-convex case

We next analyze the non-convex case under the Polyak–Łojasiewicz assumption, which is standard in nonconvex optimization.

The problem with standard PŁ.

The usual Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PŁ) inequality is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|}^{2}\geq\mu\left(f(x)-f(x_{\star})\right),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

For unconstrained problems, this is natural, because if
x_{\star}
is a minimizer, then necessarily
\nabla f(x_{\star})=0
.
Therefore, the norm of the full gradient is a valid measure of stationarity.
However, for constrained problems, a constrained minimizer
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{\star}
does not in general satisfy
\nabla f(x_{\star})=0
.
Instead, what holds is the first-order optimality condition

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{\star}),z-x_{\star}\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{X}.

A natural first attempt to extend the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality to the constrained setting is to impose

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}^{2}\geq\mu\left(f(x)-f(x_{\star})\right),\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

However, this condition is not the most intrinsic constrained analogue of the PŁ inequality.
The quantity
{\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x_{\star})\right\|}
measures whether the ambient gradient at
x
matches the ambient gradient at one fixed minimizer
x_{\star}
.
But constrained stationarity is not characterized by matching
\nabla f(x_{\star})
.
Rather, it is characterized by the condition
0\in\nabla f(x)+N_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
, where the normal cone depends on the current point
x
.
This suggests that a constrained PŁ condition should be formulated in terms of a residual whose zero set coincides with the constrained first-order condition, rather than in terms of agreement with the ambient gradient at a fixed minimizer.

Projected PŁ.

A more principled way to extend the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality to the constrained setting is to view the problem through the Projected Gradient Descent framework.
For a stepsize
\gamma>0
, the
PGD
update is
x^{+}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(x-\gamma\nabla f(x)\bigr)
.
This naturally leads to the projected gradient mapping

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x-\gamma\nabla f(x))\right).

This object is the constrained analogue of the gradient.
When
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x-\gamma\nabla f(x))\right)=\nabla f(x).

Moreover, for a closed convex set
\mathcal{X}
, the condition
G_{\gamma}(x)=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle x={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x-\gamma\nabla f(x)\right).

The above identity, from the perspective of projection onto a closed convex set, is equivalent to
\left\langle-\gamma\nabla f(x),z-x\right\rangle\leq 0
for all
z\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x),z-x\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{X},

which is precisely the variational inequality form of first-order stationarity for minimizing
f
over
\mathcal{X}
.
More explicitly, the above condition is equivalent to
-\nabla f(x)\in N_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
, or
0\in\nabla f(x)+N_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
.

Assumption I.1

(Projected PŁ)
.

Let
3.1
hold without
f
being convex.
Furthermore, assume that there exist constants
\mu>0
and
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x)\right\|}^{2}\geq\mu\left(f(x)-f(x_{\star})\right),\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},

(86)

where

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(x-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x-\gamma\nabla f(x)\right)\right).

Theorem I.1

(Linear convergence under Projected PŁ)
.

Let
I.1
hold for some
\gamma\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}]
, and let
f
be
L
–smooth on an open set containing
\mathcal{X}
.
Assume that the constrained minimum
f_{\star}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
is attained.
Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle,

with radii

t_{k}\coloneqq\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}.

(87)

Then, for every
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Consequently, for any
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1-\gamma\mu\right)^{k}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right).

In particular, if
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, then

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu}{L}\right)^{k}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right).

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 0
, and for simplicity, define

\displaystyle y_{k}\coloneqq{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

Then, by the definition of
G_{\gamma}
,

\displaystyle y_{k}=x_{k}-\gamma G_{\gamma}(x_{k}),\qquad{\left\|y_{k}-x_{k}\right\|}=\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}\overset{\eqref{eq:-0-08uyh9f8y9gfg9_09uv}}{=}t_{k}.

(88)

In addition,
y_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}_{k}
, and thus

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y_{k}\right\rangle,

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y_{k}-x_{k}\right\rangle.

(89)

By the optimality condition of projection onto a convex set, for
y_{k}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right)
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle y_{k}-\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right),z-y_{k}\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{X}.

Choosing
z=x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle y_{k}-x_{k}+\gamma\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-y_{k}\right\rangle\geq 0.

Expanding this inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y_{k}-x_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\frac{1}{\gamma}{\left\|y_{k}-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}\overset{\eqref{eq:-iny7dX079fg}}{=}-\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

(90)

By combining (
89
) and (
90
), we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

(91)

Since
f
is
L
–smooth, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}.

(92)

Because
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, we also have

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|}\leq t_{k}\overset{\eqref{eq:-0-08uyh9f8y9gfg9_09uv}}{=}\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}.

(93)

Substituting (
91
) and (
93
) into (
92
) yields

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\gamma\left(1-\frac{L\gamma}{2}\right){\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Since
\gamma\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, we have
1-\nicefrac{{L\gamma}}{{2}}\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and thus

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Finally, by (
86
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\geq\mu\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\right).

Combining this with the descent estimate gives

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\gamma\mu\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\right).

Rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1-\gamma\mu\right)\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\right).

Iterating this inequality yields the result.
∎

The non-convex smooth case without PŁ.

Even without
I.1
, the same radius choice based on the projected gradient mapping still yields global descent and a sublinear convergence guarantee to first-order stationarity.

Theorem I.2

(Sublinear convergence in the smooth non-convex case)
.

Let
3.1
hold without
f
being convex.
Let
f
be
L
–smooth on an open set containing
\mathcal{X}
.
Assume that the constrained minimum
f_{\star}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
is attained.
Fix some
\gamma\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}]
and let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\arg\!\min}_{z\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k},t_{k})}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z\right\rangle,

with radii

\displaystyle t_{k}:=\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}.

Then, for every
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Consequently, for every
K\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{2\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)}{\gamma K}.

In particular, if
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{1/L}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{2L\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)}{K}.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 0
, and for simplicity, define

\displaystyle y_{k}:={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

Then, by the definition of
G_{\gamma}
,

\displaystyle y_{k}=x_{k}-\gamma G_{\gamma}(x_{k}),\qquad{\left\|y_{k}-x_{k}\right\|}=\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}=t_{k}.

Hence
y_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{B}_{k}
, and by the optimality of
x_{k+1}
for the local linear minimization problem,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y_{k}\right\rangle.

Equivalently,

\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y_{k}-x_{k}\right\rangle.

By the optimality condition of projection onto the closed convex set
\mathcal{X}
for
y_{k}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle y_{k}-\left(x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})\right),z-y_{k}\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{X}.

Choosing
z=x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle y_{k}-x_{k}+\gamma\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-y_{k}\right\rangle\geq 0.

Expanding this inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y_{k}-x_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\frac{1}{\gamma}{\left\|y_{k}-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}=-\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Since
f
is
L
–smooth, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|}^{2}.

Because
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, we also have

\displaystyle{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|}\leq t_{k}=\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}.

Substituting the previous two estimates yields

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\gamma{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}+\frac{L\gamma^{2}}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\gamma\left(1-\frac{L\gamma}{2}\right){\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Since
\gamma\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
, we have
1-\nicefrac{{L\gamma}}{{2}}\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and thus

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{2}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}.

Summing this inequality for
k=0,1,\dots,K-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq f(x_{0})-f(x_{K})\leq f(x_{0})-f_{\star}.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{2\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)}{\gamma}.

Dividing by
K
, we get

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}\leq\frac{2\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)}{\gamma K}.

The last claim follows by setting
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{L}}
.
∎

Remark I.3

(Interpretation of the non-convex guarantee)
.

The conclusion of
Theorem

I.2
is a rate to approximate first order stationarity, measured by the projected gradient mapping. The proof of the theorem shows that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}^{2}<\infty,

and hence

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}{\left\|G_{\gamma}(x_{k})\right\|}=0.

Therefore, if
x_{k_{j}}\to\bar{x}
along some subsequence, then by continuity of
\nabla f
and of the Euclidean projection onto a closed convex set,

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(\bar{x})=\lim_{j\to\infty}G_{\gamma}(x_{k_{j}})=0.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\bar{x}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\bar{x}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{x})\right),

and hence

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x}),z-\bar{x}\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{X},

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle 0\in\nabla f(\bar{x})+N_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x}).

Thus, every accumulation point is a first order stationary point of the constrained problem.
