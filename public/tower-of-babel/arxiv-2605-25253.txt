Algebraic Characterization of FO-definable Languages of Higher-Dimensional Automata
Enzo Erlich, Jérémy Ledent, Krzysztof Ziemiański

Abstract
Higher-dimensional automata (HDA) are a model of concurrency that models simultaneous execution of events using higher dimensional cells. HDA recognize languages of pomsets, a generalization of finite words whose letters are partially ordered. We prove a new algebraic characterization of HDA languages: a language of pomsets is regular if and only if it is the inverse image of a functor from the category of pomsets into a finite category. Furthermore, the language is definable in first-order logic exactly when it is recognized by an aperiodic category, generalizing the McNaughton-Papert theorem to HDA languages. We also investigate a notion of counter-free HDA, and show that if a language is accepted by a counter-free HDA, it must be definable in first-order logic. The converse, however, is still open.

Introduction

Over finite words, the class of languages definable in First-Order (FO) logic is a strict subclass of regular languages, which admits several characterizations.
For a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

L
is recognized by a star-free regular expression,

2.

L
is recognized by an aperiodic monoid,

3.

L
is recognized by a counter-free automaton,

4.

L
is definable in first-order logic (FO),

5.

L
is definable in linear temporal logic (LTL).

The proof of
(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)
is due to Schützenberger
[
15
]
; the equivalence between
(2),(3)
and
(4)
was proved by McNaughton and Papert
[
13
]
; and Kamp
[
11
]
showed the last equivalence
(4)\Leftrightarrow(5)
.

In this paper, we are interested in extending these results to
Higher-Dimensional Automata
(HDA).
HDA are a model of concurrency that enriches standard finite-state automata with higher-dimensional cells, allowing to specify that some events may occur simultaneously.
Although they were originally introduced by Pratt
[
14
]
in 1991, a proper language theory for HDA was only developed recently
[
8
]
.
Many classic results of automata theory carry over to HDA languages
[
9
,
10
,
3
,
2
,
5
]
.
Most relevant for our purpose is the work developed in
[
5
]
, which proves a variant of Kamp’s theorem for HDA.
In that paper, the authors define a temporal logic on pomsets that has the same expressive power as first order logic.
Following McNaughton and Papert, our goal is to find two additional characterizations of the class of FO-definable HDA languages: (i) via an algebraic characterization akin to aperiodic monoids, and (ii) using a notion of
counter-free HDA
.
We leave open the question of finding a notion of star-free regular expression for HDA that captures the same class of languages.

Pomset languages of HDA.

While standard finite state automata recognize languages of words, HDA recognize languages of
partially ordered multi-sets
, or
pomsets
.
A word can be viewed as a special case of pomset, where the letters are totally ordered; such pomsets correspond to sequential executions.
But in general, two letters in a pomset need not be ordered: when two letters are incomparable, we think of them as two events occurring concurrently.
More precisely, events are ordered according to Lamport’s happens-before partial order
[
12
]
. We say that event
a

precedes
event
b
, written
{a\to b}
, if event
a
is terminated before
b
starts.
Thus, two events are incomparable (a.k.a.
concurrent
) when the two intervals during which they are executed overlap.

Gluing of pomsets and their interval representation

Additionally, pomsets are equipped with
interfaces
.
The
starting interface
is a set of events that are already started at the beginning of the computation. They are required to be minimal w.r.t. the precedence partial order.
Symmetrically, the
terminating interface
is a set of events, maximal w.r.t. precedence, that are still ongoing at the end of the computation.
When two pomsets
P
and
Q
have matching interfaces (that is, the terminating interface of
P
is the same as the starting interface of
Q
), we can
glue
them together to obtain a new pomset
P\ast Q
.

Figure
1
depicts the gluing of two pomsets, viewed as partial orders (top), and as intervals (bottom).
Events that belong to the starting (resp., terminating) interface are marked with a dot to the left (resp., right) of the letter.
Gluing plays the role of word concatenation.
However, since it is defined only for pomsets with matching interfaces, the underlying algebraic structure is that of a category rather than a monoid.
We write
\mathsf{iiPoms}
for the category whose objects are interfaces, and morphisms are pomsets.

Contributions.

We first prove in
section

3
the following algebraic characterization of HDA languages: a language of pomsets is regular if and only if it is the inverse image of a functor from
\mathsf{iiPoms}
into a finite category (
Theorem

11
).
The left-to-right implication follows the standard construction of the syntactic monoid associated with a language; in our setting, this is now a
syntactic category
.
The converse direction requires a bit more work however.
While, for finite words, a morphism into a finite monoid can immediately be viewed as an automaton using the Cayley graph of the monoid; this is not the case for HDA.
What we get instead is what we call an
\mathsf{iiPoms}
-module. To turn it into an HDA, we rely on the canonical suffix presentation of a language of pomsets
[
10
]
.

Then, in
section

4
we investigate the class of languages that are recognized by a functor into an aperiodic category.
A category is aperiodic when every endomorphism
x
is such that
x^{n}=x^{n+1}
for some
n
.
We show in
theorem

28
that the class of languages recognized aperiodic categories is also the class of FO-definable languages.
To prove this, we use the so-called ST-decomposition of a pomset.
This allows us to view a language of pomsets as a language of words, and relying on results from
[
5
]
, we can transport the McNaughton-Papert theorem from words to pomsets.

Lastly in
section

5
, we define a notion of counter-free HDA, and show that every language accepted by a counter-free HDA is aperiodic.
However, the converse direction remains an open question.
This is because the geometric structure of HDAs interacts with counter-freeness in a non-trivial way.
We explain why a naive attempt at solving the open question fails, and conclude with an example of a pomset language that exhibits some form of counting, despite being aperiodic.

Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly recall the definitions about pomsets and HDA that we will need in the rest of the paper.
We encourage readers unfamiliar with pomset languages of HDA to read a more gentle introduction to those notions, e.g.
[
9
]
.

Notations.

For a category
\mathcal{C}
, we write
\mathsf{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
for the objects of
\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
for the morphisms.
Composition is written in diagrammatic order, i.e., given morphisms
f:A\to B
and
g:B\to C
, their composition is denoted
fg
or
f\fatsemi g
rather than
g\circ f
.

2.1
Higher Dimensional Automata (HDA)

Fix a finite alphabet
\Sigma
.
An HDA is built from higher-dimensional cells.
Each cell is labeled with a finite list of elements of
\Sigma
, that we think of as the currently running events.
This totally ordered list of events is called a concurrency list, or
conclist
.

Definition 1

(Conclist)
.

A conclist
(U,\dasharrow,\lambda)
consists of a finite set
U
equipped with a strict total order
\dasharrow
, and a labeling function
\lambda:U\to\Sigma
.

A conclist may have several occurrences of the same letter
a\in\Sigma
running in parallel, in which case the total order
\dasharrow
is crucial to differentiate the corresponding events.
We write
\mathsf{CList}
for the set of conclists.

Definition 2

(HDA)
.

An HDA is a tuple
(X,\mathsf{ev},\{\delta^{0}_{A,U},\delta^{1}_{A,U}\mid A\subseteq U\in\mathsf{CList}\},\bot,\top)
, where:

•

X
is a finite set of cells, and
\mathsf{ev}:X\to\mathsf{CList}
assigns a conclist to each cell. For a conclist
U
, we write
X[U]=\{x\in X\mid\mathsf{ev}(x)=U\}
for the set of cells of type
U
.

•

For each
U\in\mathsf{CList}
and
A\subseteq U
,
\delta^{0}_{A,U}:X[U]\to X[U-A]
is called the lower face map, and
\delta^{1}_{A,U}:X[U]\to X[U-A]
the upper face map.
We often omit the subscript
U
when it is clear from context.
The face maps must satisfy the precubical identities: for all
\mu,\nu\in\{0,1\}
and
A\cap B=\varnothing
, we require
\delta^{\mu}_{A}\delta^{\nu}_{B}=\delta^{\nu}_{B}\delta^{\mu}_{A}
.

•

\bot,\top\subseteq X
are sets of initial and accepting cells, respectively. Often,
\bot\subseteq X[\varnothing]
.

We often denote an HDA by its underlying set
X
.
The
dimension
of an HDA is the maximal number of events active in a cell,
\dim(X)=\max\{|\mathsf{ev}(x)|\mid x\in X\}
.
A path in an HDA is a sequence of cells, where consecutive cells can be connected in two ways: either by starting some events, that is, moving from a lower face of
x
to the cell
x
; or by terminating some events, that is, moving from a cell
x
to an upper face of
x
.

Definition 3

(Path)
.

A path in an HDA
X
is a sequence
\alpha=(x_{0},\varphi_{1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},\varphi_{n},x_{n})
,
where each
x_{i}\in X
, each
\varphi_{i}\in\{\nearrow^{A},\searrow_{A}\mid A\in\mathsf{CList}\}
, such that for all
1\leq i\leq n
, either

•

\varphi_{i}=\nearrow^{A}
for some
A\subseteq\mathsf{ev}(x_{i})
, and
x_{i-1}=\delta^{0}_{A}(x_{i})
, or

•

\varphi_{i}=\searrow_{A}
for some
A\subseteq\mathsf{ev}(x_{i-1})
, and
\delta^{1}_{A}(x_{i-1})=x_{i}
.

The
source
and
target
of a path are
\mathsf{src}(\alpha)=x_{0}
and
\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)=x_{n}
, respectively.
A path is
accepting
when
\mathsf{src}(\alpha)\in\bot
and
\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)\in\top
.
To define the language accepted by an HDA, we first need to introduce pomsets.

2.2
Pomset languages

HDA recognize languages of partially ordered multi-sets, or
pomsets
, a.k.a. labeled partial orders.
In fact, we require additional structure: an
event order

\dasharrow
to distinguish autoconcurrent events; source and target interfaces to allow for concatenation (or
gluing
) of pomsets; and the partial order is always assumed to be an interval order.
For conciseness, we overload the word “pomset”, always assuming the whole structure.

Definition 4

(iiPomset)
.

A pomset over
\Sigma

is a tuple
(P,<_{P},\dasharrow_{P},\lambda_{P},S_{P},T_{P})
where:

•

(P,<_{P})
is a finite set equipped with a strict partial interval order called precedence.

•

\dasharrow_{P}
is an acyclic relation on
P
called the event order, such that for all
x\neq y\in P
, if
x\not<_{P}y
and
y\not<_{P}x
, then either
x\dasharrow_{P}y
or
y\dasharrow_{P}x
.

•

\lambda_{P}\colon P\to\Sigma
is a labeling function.

•

S_{P},T_{P}\subseteq P
are the source (resp. target) interfaces, such that elements of
S_{P}
(resp.
T_{P}
) are minimal (resp. maximal) elements w.r.t.
<_{P}
.

When there is no ambiguity, we denote
<_{P},\dasharrow_{P}
and
\lambda_{P}
as

<
,
\dasharrow
and
\lambda
.

An example pomset is depicted in
fig.

2
. In pictures, precedence
<_{P}
is represented using full arrows, and event order
\dasharrow_{P}
as dotted arrows, with transitive arrows omitted.
The source (resp. target) interfaces are represented as bullets before (resp. after) the label of the event.

A pomset
P
is called
discrete
when it has an empty precedence order.
In that case, the event order
\dasharrow
is a strict total order.
Given a conclist
(U,\dasharrow,\lambda)\in\mathsf{CList}
and
A\subseteq U
, we define the following discrete pomsets:

•

\mathsf{id}_{U}=(U,\varnothing,\dasharrow,\lambda,U,U)
is an
identity
,

•

{}_{A}\!\!\uparrow\!{U}=(U,\varnothing,\dasharrow,\lambda,U-A,U)
is a
starter
,

•

{U}\!\!\downarrow_{A}\;=(U,\varnothing,\dasharrow,\lambda,U,U-A)
is a
terminator
.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{id}_{U}
is an elementary pomset where the events in
U
are currently active in parallel. In
{}_{A}\!\!\uparrow\!{U}
, we just started the events
A
, so that the events
U
are now active.
In
{U}\!\!\downarrow_{A}
, the events
U
were active, and we terminate the events
A
.
In small dimensions, we write discrete pomsets using a bracket notation, where the event order implicitly goes from top to bottom. For instance,
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}a\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\\
\phantom{\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}}b\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
denotes the starter
{}_{\{b\}}\!\!\uparrow\!{U}
, where
U
is the two element conclist
a\dasharrow b
.

The source and target interfaces
S_{P},T_{P}
, together with the event order
\dasharrow
and labeling map
\lambda
, can be viewed as conclists. For
U,V\in\mathsf{CList}
, we write
\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,V)
for the set of pomsets with source interface
U
and target interface
V
(up to isomorphism).
We define the
gluing
operation on pomsets,
\ast:\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,V)\times\mathsf{iiPoms}(V,W)\to\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,W)
.

Definition 5

(Gluing)
.

Let
P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,V)
and
Q\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(V,W)
. Let
f:T_{P}\to S_{Q}
be the unique conclist isomorphism between the interfaces of
P
and
Q
.
Then, we define the pomset
P\ast Q=(R,<_{R},\dasharrow_{R},\lambda_{R},S_{R},T_{R})
, where

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=(P\sqcup Q)/_{x\equiv f(x)}

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{P}\cup\lambda_{Q}

\displaystyle<_{R}

\displaystyle=\;<_{P}\cup<_{Q}\cup\;(P-T_{P})\times(Q-S_{Q})

\displaystyle S_{R}

\displaystyle=S_{P}

\displaystyle\dasharrow_{R}

\displaystyle=\;\dasharrow_{P}\cup\dasharrow_{Q}

\displaystyle T_{R}

\displaystyle=T_{Q}

See
Figure

1
for an example of gluing (event order implicitly goes from top to bottom). Gluing is associative and
\mathsf{id}_{U}
is a neutral element, this data assembles into a category
\mathsf{iiPoms}
, whose objects are conclists, morphisms are pomsets, and composition is gluing.

The
dimension
of a pomset
P
is the size of a maximal
<_{P}
-antichain.
We write
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
for the subcategory of
\mathsf{iiPoms}
consisting of pomsets of dimension at most
k
.
Note that
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
has a finite number of objects: they are conclists of length at most
k
.

Definition 6

(Language, subsumption, downward-closure)
.

A
language
of pomsets is a set of morphisms
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}
.
For
P,Q\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,V)
, we say that
P

is subsumed by

Q
, written
P\sqsubseteq Q
, when there is a bijection
f:P\to Q
preserving the interfaces, labeling and event order, such that
f(x)<_{Q}f(y)
implies
x<_{P}y
.
Intuitively, the pomset
Q
is “more concurrent” than
P
.
A language
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}
is
downward-closed
when
Q\in L
and
P\sqsubseteq Q
implies
P\in L
.
For a language
L
, we define
L\!\!\downarrow\;=\{P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}\mid{P\sqsubseteq Q}\text{ for some }Q\in L\}
, called the downward-closure of
L
.

Let
X
be an HDA, and
\alpha
a path in
X
.
We define the pomset associated with
\alpha
,
\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(\mathsf{ev}(\mathsf{src}(\alpha)),\mathsf{ev}(\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)))
, by induction on the length of
\alpha
:

•

If
\alpha=(x_{0})
, then
\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)=\mathsf{id}_{\mathsf{ev}(x_{0})}
.

•

If
\alpha=(\delta^{0}_{A}(x),\nearrow^{A},x)
, then
\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)={}_{A}\!\!\uparrow\!{\mathsf{ev}(x)}
.

•

If
\alpha=(x,\searrow_{A},\delta^{1}_{A}(x))
, then
\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)={\mathsf{ev}(x)}\!\!\downarrow_{A}
.

•

If
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\ast\ldots\ast\alpha_{n}
is a concatenation, then
\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)=\mathsf{ev}(\alpha_{1})\ast\ldots\ast\mathsf{ev}(\alpha_{n})
.

Definition 7

(HDA language)
.

The language accepted by an HDA
X
is

\mathsf{Lang}(X)=\{\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)\mid\text{$\alpha$ is an accepting path in $X$}\}

A language of pomsets is
regular
if it is accepted by a finite HDA.
It can be shown
[
8
]
that all regular languages are downward-closed.

Global assumptions.

Throughout the paper, we only consider languages of pomsets of bounded dimension,
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
. This is a crucial assumption, as it is not yet known how to properly model concurrent programs with an unbounded numbers of processes using HDA
[
4
]
.
Also, some of our results require the language to be downward-closed, while others do not. We explicitly indicate whenever this assumption is needed.

2.3
First-Order logic on pomsets

Another way to describe pomset languages is via logical characterizations.
MSO logic over pomsets was introduced in
[
2
]
and shown to be as expressive as HDA, for downward-closed languages.
The fragment of First Order (FO) logic has been studied in
[
5
]
, together with an equivalent LTL-like logic.
Here, we focus on the class of FO-definable languages of pomsets.
FO formulas are generated by the following grammar, where
a\in\Sigma
and
x,y
are variables:

\varphi,\psi::=a(x)\mid\mathbf{s}(x)\mid\mathbf{t}(x)\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\wedge\psi\mid\exists x.\varphi\mid x<y\mid x\dasharrow y

The semantics is straightforward: variables range over events of the pomset;
<
and
\dasharrow
stand for precedence and event-order, respectively; and
a
,
\mathbf{s}
,
\mathbf{t}
stand for the labeling, starting interface and terminating interface, respectively.

Definition 8

(Semantics of FO over pomsets)
.

Consider an FO formula
\varphi
, a pomset
P=(P,<_{P},\dasharrow_{P},\lambda_{P},S_{P},T_{P})
, and a valuation
\nu
mapping variables to events of
P
.
Then, the satisfaction relation
P,\nu\models\varphi
is defined inductively as follows:

\displaystyle P,\nu\models a(x)

\displaystyle\text{ if }\lambda_{P}(\nu(x))=a

\displaystyle P,\nu\models\exists x.\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ if }\exists p\in P.\;\;P,\nu[x\mapsto p]\models\varphi

\displaystyle P,\nu\models\mathbf{s}(x)

\displaystyle\text{ if }\nu(x)\in S_{P}

\displaystyle P,\nu\models\mathbf{t}(x)

\displaystyle\text{ if }\nu(x)\in T_{P}

\displaystyle P,\nu\models\neg\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ if }P,\nu\not\models\varphi

\displaystyle P,\nu\models\varphi\wedge\psi

\displaystyle\text{ if }P,\nu\models\varphi\text{ and }P,\nu\models\psi

\displaystyle P,\nu\models x<y

\displaystyle\text{ if }\nu(x)<_{P}\nu(y)

\displaystyle P,\nu\models x\dasharrow y

\displaystyle\text{ if }\nu(x)\dasharrow_{P}\nu(y)

When
\varphi
has no free variable, we write
P\models\varphi
, and
\mathsf{Lang}(\varphi)=\{P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}\mid P\models\varphi\}
.

FO-definable languages may not be downward-closed.
In fact, the class of FO-definable languages is not closed under downward-closure, as shown in
proposition

9
.
Thus, when comparing expressivity of HDA models with logical (or algebraic) characterizations, we will focus on the class of downward-closed FO-definable languages.

Proposition 9
.

The language
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
a\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{*}
is FO-definable, but
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
a\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{*}\!\!\downarrow
is not.

Proof.

The language
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
a\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{*}
is recognized by the following FO formula:
\forall x.a(x)\wedge\exists!y.x\parallel y
,
where
\exists!
means “exists unique”, defined as usual, and
x\parallel y:=\neg(x<y\vee y<x\vee x=y)
.
However,
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
a\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{*}\!\!\downarrow\cap\;a^{*}=(aa)^{*}
, which is not FO-definable
[
6
]
. Since FO-definable languages are closed under intersection,
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
a\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{*}\!\!\downarrow
cannot be defined in FO.
∎

3
Recognizability for HDA languages

In this section, we propose a notion of algebraic recognizability for pomset languages.
Over words, a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is recognized by a finite monoid
M

if there exists a monoid morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow M
and a subset
J\subseteq M
such that
L=\varphi^{-1}(J)
.
It is a well-known result that recognizable languages are exactly the regular languages
[
7
]
.
In higher dimension, the set
\mathsf{iiPoms}
of all pomsets over a given alphabet has the structure of a category rather than a monoid.
The objects are interfaces, and the arrows are pomsets whose domain and codomain are the starting and terminating interfaces, respectively.
Thus, we get the following notion of recognizability for pomset languages:

Definition 10
.

A language
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}
is
recognizable
if there exists a finite category
\mathcal{D}
, a set of morphisms
K\subseteq\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{D})
, and a functor
F:\mathsf{iiPoms}\to\mathcal{D}
such that
L=F^{-1}(K)
.

The rest of the section is dedicated to proving the following
Theorem

11
.
The first implication (
Lemma

13
) follows the standard construction of the syntactic monoid.
The second implication (consequence of
Proposition

19
) is less straightforward and requires the notion of
\mathsf{iiPoms}
-module.

Theorem 11
.

A pomset language
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
is regular if and only if it is recognizable.

3.1
From regularity to recognizability

To show that any regular language is recognizable, we use a construction similar to the usual syntactic monoid.
Taking into account the interfaces, we describe in
Definition

12
the
syntactic category
of a language.
Then, we prove in
Lemma

13
that if the language is regular, then its syntactic category is finite.
Since any language is recognized by its syntactic category, this concludes the first direction of
Theorem

11
.

The definition of the syntactic category can be formulated for any category
\mathcal{C}
. We will then specialize to the case of
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{iiPoms}
.
Given a language
L\subseteq\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
and two morphisms
\alpha,\beta:U\to V
in
\mathcal{C}
, we write
\alpha\sim\beta
when
\forall\gamma,\delta.\;\gamma\alpha\delta\in L\Leftrightarrow\gamma\beta\delta\in L
.
This is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{C}(U,V)
, called the syntactic congruence.

Definition 12
.

The
syntactic category

\mathrm{Syn}(L)
of a language
L\subseteq\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
has objects
\mathsf{Ob}(\mathrm{Syn}(L))=\mathsf{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
, and morphisms

\mathrm{Syn}(L)(U,V)=\mathcal{C}(U,V)/{\sim}

with the composition
[\alpha]\fatsemi[\beta]=[\alpha\fatsemi\beta]
.

The syntactic category comes with an obvious projection
\pi_{L}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{Syn}(L)
and a language

K=\{[\alpha]\mid\alpha\in L\}\subseteq\mathrm{Syn}(L)

such that
\pi_{L}^{-1}(K)=L
.

Lemma 13
.

If
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
is regular, then
Syn(L)
is finite. As a consequence,
L
is recognizable.

Proof.

Let
X
be a finite HDA such that
\mathsf{Lang}(X)=L
. For
P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,V)
, let

t(P)=\{(x,y)\in X[U]\times X[V]\mid\text{there is a path }\alpha\text{ s.t.\ }\mathsf{src}(\alpha)=x,\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)=y\text{ and }\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)=P\}.

First,
t(P)=t(P^{\prime})
implies
P\sim P^{\prime}
; second, there is only a finite number of possible values of
t
, so a finite number of equivalence classes for
\sim
.
∎

3.2
From recognizability to regularity

Now that we have established that any regular language is recognizable, we aim to prove the converse. For languages of words, translating a finite monoid
M
together with a homomorphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{*}\to M
into a finite automaton is fairly straightforward: the states of the automaton are the elements of
the monoid, and the transitions are given by
x\overset{a}{\rightarrow}y
if
x\cdot\varphi(a)=y
.
For pomset languages, however, the situation is different.
Given a functor
F:\mathsf{iiPoms}\to\mathcal{D}
into a finite category
\mathcal{D}
, the analogue of the above construction does not yield an HDA directly.
Instead, what we get is what we call an
\mathsf{iiPoms}
-module, that is, a set equipped with a right action of
\mathsf{iiPoms}
.
This can then be turned into an HDA, but it requires some work.

Definition 14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. A
\mathcal{C}
-module is a set
M
equipped with

•

Functions
\mathsf{src},\mathsf{tgt}:M\to\mathsf{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
. For
U,V\in\mathsf{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
let
M(U,V)=\mathsf{src}^{-1}(U)\cap\mathsf{tgt}^{-1}(V)
.

•

Functions
\mu_{U,V,W}:M(U,V)\times\mathcal{C}(V,W)\to M(U,W)
. We write
\mu
for the partial function
M\times\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{C})\to M
, and
m\cdot\alpha
for
\mu(m,\alpha)
.

such that, for all
m\in M(U,V)
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}(V,W)
, and
\beta\in\mathcal{C}(W,T)
,

•

m\cdot\mathsf{id}_{V}=m
, and

•

(m\cdot\alpha)\cdot\beta=m\cdot(\alpha\fatsemi\beta)
.

Definition 15
.

A homomorphism of
\mathcal{C}
-modules is a function
\varphi:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
such that for any
m\in M
and
\alpha\in\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
,
\mathsf{src}(\varphi(m))=\mathsf{src}(m)
,
\mathsf{tgt}(\varphi(m))=\mathsf{tgt}(m)
and
\varphi(m\cdot\alpha)=\varphi(m)\cdot\alpha
.

Remark 16
.

In other words, a
\mathcal{C}
-module
M
is a family of functors
M(U,-):\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{Set}
indexed by
U\in\mathsf{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
, and a homomorphism of
\mathcal{C}
-modules
M\to M^{\prime}
is a family of natural transformations
M(U,-)\Rightarrow M^{\prime}(U,-)
.

Notice that, for
\mathcal{C}=\Sigma^{*}
viewed as a category with one object, a
\Sigma^{*}
-module is a set
M
together with a right monoid action of
\Sigma^{*}
, that is, a deterministic finite state automaton.
Here, we are interested in
\mathsf{iiPoms}
-modules instead.
For instance, the syntactic category
\mathrm{Syn}(L)
is an
\mathsf{iiPoms}
-module, with
[P]\cdot Q=[P\ast Q]
.
More generally, any functor
F:\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\to\mathcal{D}
can be viewed as an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module, by taking
M(U,V)=\{F(P)\mid P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}(U,V)\}
for all
U,V
, and then letting
m\cdot Q=m\fatsemi F(Q)
.
Finally,
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
itself is an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module, with the right action
P\cdot Q=P\ast Q
.

Definition 17
.

A language
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
is recognized by an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module
M
if there exists a subset
J\subseteq M
and a homomorphism of
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-modules
\varphi:\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\to M
such that
L=\varphi^{-1}(J)
.

Note that in the above definition, the morphism of
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-modules
\varphi:\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\to M
is entirely determined by the image of the identity pomsets
\mathsf{id}_{U}\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}(U,U)
for each interface
U
.
These play the role of the “initial states”, if we think of the
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module
M
as some sort of deterministic automaton.
When a language
L
is recognized by an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module, we call the tuple
(M,J,\varphi)
a
presentation
of
L
.

Moreover, notice that one may always assume that the morphism
\varphi:\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\to M
is surjective.
If that is not the case, we can simply discard the elements of
M
that are not in the image of
\varphi
, so that
(\varphi(\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}),J\cap\varphi(\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}),\varphi)
is a surjective presentation of
L
.

Lemma 18
.

Any language
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
recognizable by a finite category can be recognized by a finite
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module.

Proof.

Suppose
L
is recognized by a functor
{F:\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\to\mathcal{D}}
into a finite category, with
K\subseteq\mathsf{Mor}(\mathcal{D})
.
As we have seen, this gives rise to an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module whose elements are the morphisms of
\mathcal{D}
in the image of
F
.
Then
F
can be seen as a morphism of
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-modules, and since
F^{-1}(K)=L
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Canonical suffix presentation

The reason for introducing the notion of
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module is that every finite presentation of a pomset language can be turned into a
canonical suffix presentation
.
Given a language
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
and pomset
P
, the
prefix quotient

P\backslash L
is defined by
P\backslash L=\{Q\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\mid P*Q\in L\}
.
Since pomset languages have a Myhill-Nerode theorem
[
10
]
, a pomset language
L
is regular if and only if the set
\mathsf{suff}(L)=\{P\backslash L\mid P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\}
is finite.
While the set
\mathsf{suff}(L)
does not have the structure of a category, it can be equipped with the structure of an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module.
This module can then be turned into an HDA using the construction from
[
10
]
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
be a language.
Then, the presentation
(\mathsf{suff}(L),J,\varphi)
, called the canonical suffix presentation of
L
, defined by:

•

\mathsf{src}(P\backslash L)=S_{P}
and
\mathsf{tgt}(P\backslash L)=T_{P}
,

•

P\backslash L\cdot Q=(P\ast Q)\backslash L
,

•

J=\{P\backslash L\mid P\in L\}
,

•

\varphi(P)=P\backslash L
,

recognizes
L
. Additionally, for any presentation
(M,K,\psi)
recognizing
L
where
\psi
is surjective, there exists a module homomorphism
f:M\to\mathsf{suff}(L)
such that
(f(M),f(K),f\circ\psi)
is
isomorphic to the canonical suffix presentation
(\mathsf{suff}(L),J,\varphi)
of
L
.

Proof.

First,
(\mathsf{suff}(L),J,\varphi)
is well-defined since
P\backslash L\cdot\mathsf{id}_{T_{P}}=(P\ast\mathsf{id}_{T_{P}})\backslash L=P\backslash L

and
(P\backslash L\cdot Q)\cdot R=(P\ast Q\ast R)\backslash L=P\backslash L\cdot(Q\ast R)
. Then, it is clear from the definitions that
L=\varphi^{-1}(J)
.
Now, let us prove that any presentation
(M,K,\psi)
admits a homomorphism
f
such that
(f(M),f(K),f\circ\psi)=(\mathsf{suff}(L),J,\varphi)
.

Fix, for any
m\in M
,
f(m)=\psi^{-1}(m)\backslash L
. This is well-defined since if
\psi(P)=\psi(Q)=m
, then
P\backslash L=\{R\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\mid m\cdot R\in L\}=Q\backslash L
.
First,
f
is a module homomorphism.
Observe that
\mathsf{src}(f(\psi(P)))=\mathsf{src}(\psi^{-1}(\psi(P))\backslash L)=\mathsf{src}(P\backslash L)=S_{P}=\mathsf{src}(\psi(P))
as
\psi
is itself a homomorphism. The same goes for
\mathsf{tgt}(f(\psi(P)))
. Now, for any
Q\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
,

f(\psi(P))Q=(\psi^{-1}(\psi(P))\backslash L)Q=(P\backslash L)Q=PQ\backslash L=\psi^{-1}(\psi(PQ))\backslash L=f(\psi(PQ))=f(\psi(P)Q)
.

It is immediate that
f\circ\psi(P)=\psi^{-1}(\psi(P))\backslash L=P\backslash L
. Hence,
f(M)=\mathsf{suff}(L)
and
f\circ\psi=\varphi
.
Now,
f(K)=\{\psi^{-1}(\psi(P))\backslash L\mid\psi(P)\in K\}=\{P\backslash L\mid P\in L\}=J
.
∎

Proposition

19
suffices to conclude that recognizable languages of finite dimension are regular.
Indeed, if we show that the set
\mathsf{suff}(L)
is finite, then we can conclude by the Myhill-Nerode theorem for HDA
[
10
]
that
L
is regular.
If
L
is recognizable, it is recognized by a finite presentation
(M,K,\psi)
where
\psi
is surjective.
Then,
Proposition

19
says that there is a module homomorphism
f:M\to\mathsf{suff}(L)
such that
f(M)=\mathsf{suff}(L)
.
Since
M
is finite, this implies that
\mathsf{suff}(L)
is also finite.

The proof of the Myhill-Nerode theorem from
[
10
]
essentially shows how to construct an HDA from the canonical suffix presentation
(\mathsf{suff}(L),J,\varphi)
.
In
Section

3.3
, we adapt this proof to directly turn any finite module recognizing
L
into an HDA accepting the same language.

3.3
Direct construction from modules to HDA

In this section, we give another, more direct proof that recognizable languages are regular.
The construction of the HDA from an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module is a generalized version of the one in
[
10
]
.
In this section only, we restrict to languages of pomsets whose source interface is
\varnothing
, meaning that no processes are active at the start of computation.
This restriction is made solely for ease of presentation, as it prevents some technicalities (see
[
10
]
for details).
We write
{}_{\varnothing}\mathsf{iiPoms}
for the set of pomsets whose source interface is
\varnothing
.

Given a finite presentation
(M,J,\varphi)
, the
U
-cells of the resulting HDA are elements of
M(\varnothing,U)
. Face maps are then
added in the same way as in
[
10
]
. However, for the down faces to be well-defined, we need an additional condition called coherence.
In the following, for
P
a pomset and
A\subseteq P
a subset of events of
P
, we write
P-A
for the subpomset induced by the events of
P
not belonging to
A
.

Definition 20
.

A presentation
(M,J,\varphi)
is
coherent
if
for all
U
and all
P,Q\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(\varnothing,U)
:

\varphi(P)=\varphi(Q)\implies\text{for all }A\subseteq U.\;\;\varphi(P-A)=\varphi(Q-A),

Lemma 21
.

Any recognizable language has a coherent presentation.

Proof.

Fix a presentation
(M,J,\varphi)
recognizing a language
L
.
Given
P,Q\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
, we write
P\sim Q
if
S_{P}=S_{Q}
,
T_{P}=T_{Q}
and, for any
A\subseteq T_{P}
,

\varphi(P-A)=\varphi(Q-A)
. Then,
\sim
is an equivalence relation with a finite number of classes, since
M
is finite and
the dimension of
L
is bounded. Moreover,
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}/{\sim}
is an
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}
-module. We now prove that together with
\psi:P\mapsto[P]
and
K=\varphi^{-1}(J)
,
it defines a coherent finite presentation for
L
.

First,
{}_{\varnothing}\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\xrightarrow{\psi}\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}/{\sim}\supseteq K
is coherent.
If
\psi(P)=\psi(Q)
, then by definition
P\sim Q
, which implies that for any
A\subseteq T_{P}=T_{Q}
,

P-A\sim Q-A
.
Therefore,
\psi(P-A)=\psi(Q-A)
.
Finally, it recognizes
L
:
\psi(P)\in K
if and only if there exists
Q\sim P
such that
\varphi(Q)\in J
. Since
\varphi(Q)=\varphi(P)
,
this is equivalent to
P\in L
.
∎

We can now construct an HDA from a coherent presentation.
Observe that if
(M,J,\varphi)
is the canonical suffix presentation of
L
, the resulting HDA is the Myhill-Nerode HDA of
[
10
]
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
(M,J,\varphi)
be a coherent presentation of a downward-closed language
L
, where
\varphi
is surjective.
Then
X
defined by

X[U]=M(\varnothing,U),\delta^{0}_{A}(\varphi(P))=\varphi(P-A),\delta^{1}_{B}(m)=m\cdot{U}\!\!\downarrow_{B},\\
\bot_{X}=\{\varphi(\mathsf{id}_{\varnothing})\},\top_{X}=J,

is a well-defined HDA such that
\mathsf{Lang}(X)=L
.

Proof.

First, note that since
\varphi
is surjective,
X[U]=M(\varnothing,U)=\varphi(\mathsf{iiPoms}(\varnothing,U))
.
The map
\delta^{0}_{A}
is well-defined: if
\varphi(P)=\varphi(Q)
, then
\varphi(P-A)=\varphi(Q-A)
because the presentation is coherent.
The precubical identities are straightforward to check. For example, if
m=\varphi(P)\in M(\varnothing,U)
, and
A,B\subseteq U
are disjoint,
\delta^{0}_{A}(\delta^{1}_{B}(m))=\varphi((P\ast{U}\!\!\downarrow_{B})-A)={\varphi((P-A)\ast{(U-A)}\!\!\downarrow_{B})}=\delta^{1}_{B}(\delta^{0}_{A}(m))
.
So
X
is a well-defined HDA.

To prove that the language accepted by
X
is
L
, we first prove the following claim: for any path
\alpha
in
X
,
\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)\backslash L\subseteq\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)\backslash L
.
Note that
\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)\in M
, and
m\backslash L
is defined by
m\backslash L=\{P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq k}\mid m\cdot P\in J\}
.
We prove this by induction over
\alpha
.

•

If
\alpha=(\varphi(\mathsf{id}_{\varnothing}))
, then
\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)\backslash L=L=\mathsf{ev}(\alpha)\backslash L
.

•

If
\alpha=(\beta,\searrow_{B},m)
, then
m=\delta^{1}_{B}(\mathsf{tgt}(\beta))=\mathsf{tgt}(\beta)\cdot{U}\!\!\downarrow_{B}
, where
\mathsf{tgt}(\beta)\in M(\varnothing,U)
.
Let
R\in\mathsf{tgt}(\alpha)\backslash L
, that is,
m\cdot R\in J
.
Since
m\cdot R=(\mathsf{tgt}(\beta)\cdot{U}\!\!\downarrow_{B})\cdot R=\mathsf{tgt}(\beta)\cdot({U}\!\!\downarrow_{B}\ast R)
, this yields
{U}\!\!\downarrow_{B}\ast R\in\mathsf{tgt}(\beta)\backslash L
.
By induction hypothesis, we obtain

4
Aperiodic categories and First-Order logic

For languages of finite words, algebraic characterizations have led to the study of several strict subclasses of regular languages, defined by the properties of their recognizing monoids.
Perhaps the most prominent is the class of languages recognized by aperiodic monoids.
This class can equivalently be characterized using star-free regular expressions, counter-free automata and first-order logic
[
15
,
13
]
.
Intuitively, these languages can be recognized without needing to count letters modulo a constant
k>1
.
For instance,
a^{*}
is star-free, whereas
(aa)^{*}
is not, because in the latter requires counting the number of
a
’s modulo 2.

In this section, we extend this class of languages from words to pomsets.
Recall that a finite monoid
M
is
aperiodic
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
x\in M
,
x^{n+1}=x^{n}
.
Extending this definition to categories and modules is straightforward.

Definition 23
.

A finite category
\mathcal{C}
is
aperiodic
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
x\in\mathcal{C}(U,U)
,
x^{n+1}=x^{n}
.

Definition 24
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-module
M
is
counter-free
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
m\in M(V,U)
and
c\in\mathcal{C}(U,U)
,
m\cdot x^{n+1}=m\cdot x^{n}
.

For modules, we prefer to use the term
counter-free
, traditionally used for automata, because we consider them to be closer to automata than to categories. In this section, we aim to prove that aperiodic categories, counter-free modules, and FO logic recognize the same pomset languages.
Expressivity of counter-free HDA will be explored in
Section

5
.

Aperiodic categories and counter-free HDA

In the context of words, a deterministic automaton
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,I,F,\delta)
is
counter-free
if
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and state
q\in Q

\delta(q,w^{n+1})=\delta(q,w^{n})

[
13
]
.
Essentially, this says that the transition monoid of the automaton is aperiodic, so the proof that counter-free automata recognize aperiodic languages is fairly straightforward.
It is, however, limited to deterministic automata, and it has been established that not all HDA can be determinized
[
10

.
In
[
6
]
, Diekert and Gastin discussed two possible definitions of counter-freeness in the non-deterministic setting of Büchi automata. We adapt the less restrictive of them, Definition 11.5, for HDA.

5.1
Counter-free HDA recognize aperiodic languages

Given an HDA
X
, a cell

and a pomset
P\in{}_{U}\mathsf{iiPoms}
, we denote by
d(x,P)
the set of cells accessible from

by paths labeled by
P
.

Definition 29
.

An HDA
X
is
counter-free
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any cell
x\in X[U]
and pomset
P\in\mathsf{iiPoms}(U,U)
,

d(x,P^{n+1})=d(x,P^{n})
.

Proposition 30
.

For any counter-free HDA accepting a language
L
, there exists an aperiodic category recognizing
L
.

Proof.

If
L
is recognized by a counter-free HDA, then the associated ST-automaton is counter-free. Hence, the word language
\mathsf{ST}(L)
is defined by an FO formula
[
13
]
. Thus, so is the pomset language
L

[
5
]
.
The conclusion comes from
Theorem

28

If

Intuitively,
P_{2n}
is the 2-dimensional pomset containing
2n
events labeled by
a
such that the
i
-th event is concurrent with
the
(i-1)
-th and the
(i+1)
-th events. Furthermore, they are alternating: odd events are on the top (i.e., they are minimal according to event order)
and even events are at the bottom (i.e., they are maximal).

Lemma 33
.

The (non-downward-closed) language
\{P_{2n}\mid n\geq 0\}
is aperiodic.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that this language is described as a set of pomsets
P
satisfying the following first order formula.
For every
x
,
a(x)
and there exist
y,y^{\prime}
such that one of the following four conditions hold:

•

y\dasharrow x\dashleftarrow y^{\prime}\text{ and for all }z\neq x,y,y^{\prime},\text{either }z<x\text{ or }x<z
,

•

y\dashleftarrow x\dasharrow y^{\prime}\text{ and for all }z\neq x,y,y^{\prime},\text{either }z<x\text{ or }x<z
,

•

x\dasharrow y\text{ and for all }z\neq x,y,\;x<z
,

•

y\dasharrow x\text{ and for all }z\neq x,y,\;z<x
. ∎

The key idea is that we do not actually need to count the parity of the number of
a
’s. Instead, we keep track of whether the event is up or down according to the event order.
If we start up, alternate positions, and finish down, the number of
a
’s will be even.
Note that the language
\{P_{2n}\}\!\!\downarrow
is not aperiodic, since
\{P_{2n}\}\!\!\downarrow\mathop{\cap}a^{*}=(aa)^{*}
is not aperiodic.

Lemma 34
.

The language
L=\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq 2}(\varnothing,\varnothing)\setminus\{P_{2n}\}
is downward-closed and aperiodic.

Proof.

The difference of aperiodic languages is aperiodic. Furthermore,
L
is downward-closed since
P_{2n}
are maximal pomsets with respect to subsumption in
\mathsf{iiPoms}_{\leq 2}
.
∎

Thus, there exists an aperiodic downward-closed language
L
such that (1) it contains all pomsets of odd length, and (2) for every
n\geq 0
, there is a pomset of length
2n
that does not belong to
L
.
We conclude from this example that aperiodic pomset languages admit a limited form of “counting”, in contrast to word languages. The language
L
from
lemma

34
, however, can be recognized by a counter-free HDA, so it still does not invalidate the conjecture.

6
Conclusion and future work

We have proved an algebraic characterization of regular languages of HDA: a pomset language is regular if and only if it is the preimage of a functor into a finite category.
Furthermore, the finite category recognizing a language is aperiodic if and only if the language is definable in first order logic.
This generalizes the McNaughton-Papert theorem to a higher-dimensional setting.
Aperiodic pomset languages are peculiar in that they allow for some restricted form of counting, unlike word languages.

We have defined a notion of counter-free HDA, and shown that every language accepted by a counter-free HDA is aperiodic.
However, the converse is still an open question: it is not yet well understood how aperiodicity interacts with downward-closure, which is intrinsic to HDAs.
One possible way to solve it would be to prove that the HDA constructed from the canonical suffix presentation using
Lemma

22
is always counter-free.
There does not seem to be a specific reason why this should be the case, but we have not yet found counterexample.
Another, perhaps more promising, approach could be to show that, if an aperiodic language is recognized by a non-counter-free HDA, it is always possible to add some extra cells so as to eliminate the counter without changing the language.

Finally, another avenue for future work would be to find a notion of star-free regular expressions that captures the same class of pomset languages.
HDA languages do not behave well w.r.t. complementation
[
3
]
, so finding a good notion of star-free regular expression is not straightforward.
