2. Executive Summary

GismoPower’s Final Research Report documents the outcomes of a DOE SBIR Phase
II project focused on advancing the MEGA® (Mobile Electricity Generating Appliance), a
trailerable, plug-in solar canopy appliance designed to deliver appliance-class electricity
generation for homes, small businesses and renters. The project’s core objective was to
remove the technical and regulatory barriers that have historically prevented plug-in
solar systems from being safely certified, permitted, and interconnected in the United
States.

A principal accomplishment of this Phase II effort was the completion and issuance of
UL-3700 Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems, an Outline of
Investigation (OOI) that establishes, for the first time, a certifiable national safety and
standards pathway for appliance-class, cord-and-plug-connected photovoltaic systems.
UL-3700 directly enables certification of high-power plug-in solar appliances such as the
MEGA®, independent of customized building wiring upgrades or site-specific
engineering, thereby removing a primary commercialization barrier for GismoPower’s
technology and for the broader category of portable, appliance-based DERs.

Project Motivation and Context​
At project inception, plug-in and portable solar technologies faced a systemic regulatory
gap. While inverter performance standards and grid interconnection rules had evolved,
no U.S. product safety standard addressed cord-and-plug-connected generation under
bidirectional power flow. As a result, emerging technologies were excluded by utilities
and permitting authorities due to the absence of certifiable requirements rather than
demonstrated safety failures.
Regulatory and Standards Engagement​
The project engaged extensively with U.S. and international standards bodies, including
the IEC TC 64/WG48, UL 1741 Supplement SC, and UL 9741 technical committees.
These engagements clarified that existing inverter-centric and installation standards
could not readily accommodate plug-in or appliance-class DERs, reinforcing the need
for UL 3700 Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems as a separate,
fit-for-purpose standard.​
​
UL-3700 - Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems ​
As one of the main accomplishment of this SBIR Phase II grant, UL Solutions issued a
Safety Science white paper documenting risks unique to plug-in PV systems and
identifying mitigation strategies suitable for certification and the initial Outline of
Investigation (OOI) for UL-3700 Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems
on December 11, 2025. The UL-3700 defines construction requirements, performance
testing, markings, and installation instructions for Interactive Plug-In PV systems and

This SBIR Phase II project delivered measurable reductions in MEGA frame

improvements reduce total system cost, shorten deployment timelines, and materially

Increasing per Unit output​
Upgrading the PV modules from 540 W to 710 W increased the system’s DC wattage
per MEGA from 4.86 kW to 6.39 kW. In parallel, modernization of the electronic
components delivered measurable performance gains. A comparison of field data at
Pine Ridge/SD from Red Cloud Renewable Energy Center using the 2023 MEGA
configuration and 102 EastRidge Loop using the 2025 MEGA configuration indicates an
approximate 50% (!) increase in electricity generation at low light conditions during
December 2025, which is significantly higher than expected.
Utility, State, and Federal Findings​
The project documented regulatory barriers through utility and commission proceedings,
including NV Energy and Nevada NPUC determinations and a Connecticut PURA IES
proposal denial based on programmatic fit and cost-effectiveness rather than technical
infeasibility. Federal engagement with FERC and policy outreach through the ACORE
Grid Forum highlighted misalignment between federal energy policy and
non-jurisdictional electrical codes and standards.
DOE-Leveraged Impact​
The project leverages SBIR funding through a future PNNL Technical Assistance
Agreement (TA-CRADA 747), extending Phase II findings into multi-state regulatory
analysis and development of model permitting and interconnection frameworks informed
by UL 3700. The SBIR Phase II project enabled a nationally recognized certification
framework for Interactive Plug-In PV systems. The completion of UL 3700 Interactive
Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems addresses a critical commercialization
barrier for consumer-deployable clean energy technologies and advances DOE
objectives related to distributed energy access, grid resilience, and regulatory
modernization.​

3.1 Standards Development (G/NG-1)
Long awaited: UL Solutions published the white paper “Safety Considerations for
Plug-In Photovoltaic (PIPV) Systems“ and the Outline of Investigation (OOI) for
“UL-3700 Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems“
Transition to Published UL Framework​
During the SBIR Phase II period, GismoPower’s engagement with UL Solutions
advanced from exploratory and preliminary investigations to a formally published draft
for safety analysis and a completed certification framework for Interactive Plug-In
Photovoltaic (PIPV) equipment. These outcomes represent the first US-specific,
UL-issued technical foundation addressing plug-in PV systems connected via
cord-and-plug interfaces.
GismoPower’s UL Project No.4790844670, completed in June 2024 during Phase I of
this SBIR award, served as the foundational technical input for the subsequent
development of the UL-3700 Outline of Investigation (OOI). In parallel, Lawrence
Berkeley National Laboratory’s April 2025 publication, “Barriers to Balcony Solar and
Plug-In Distributed Energy Resources in the United States,” widely recognized within
the regulatory and standards community, was co-authored by the Principal Investigator
associated with this SBIR project, extending the technical analysis to include systemic
regulatory and code-based barriers.
UL White Paper – Safety Science Foundation​
UL’s white paper, “Interactions of Plug-In PV with Protection of Existing Power Systems”
(December 2025), documents safety risks unique to PIPV systems, including
undetected branch-circuit overloads, GFCI blinding during bidirectional power flow, UL
concludes that unrestricted connection of PIPV systems to existing residential branch
circuits is not supported without additional engineered safeguards. The paper identifies
mitigation strategies such as dedicated circuits, uniquely configured PIPV receptacles,
integrated overcurrent protection, power control systems, and touch-safe connector
designs.
It is especially encouraging that UL included the federal policy mandate for “two-way
power flow” by FERC Order 2222 for equipment and standards as one of the core
principles in UL-3700. Tenacious and sustained outreach to UL executives before, at
and after RE+ 2025 in Las Vegas was not ignored.
While many of the technical issues identified through UL Project No.4790844670 were
incorporated and further examined within the UL-3700 white paper, the personal
electric-shock protection implications of electronic anti-islanding behavior did not
receive proportional scrutiny. In particular, the safety function of rapid inverter AC
voltage collapse as it relates to human exposure at attachment plugs remains
under-recognized. Utility lineworkers and members of the public are equally subject to

 5

electric shock hazards, and fault current propagates at the speed of light from PV
inverters, through connector prongs, and into downstream conductors, including
downed power lines.
UL White Paper Page 10: “Utility grid interconnection performance requirements
and tests focus exclusively on the suitability of connections to the grid and do
not address electric shock, energy and fire hazards that could pose a risk to the
general public from accessing the inverter outputs. This presents a critical safety
concern for PIPV products due to the unconventional use of the attachment plug
as a power output interface”.
UL White Paper Page 12: “IEEE 1547 utility export limits are not designed to
protect people from electric shock hazards. Under islanded or open phase
conditions, the inverter is allowed up to two seconds to cease output current. The
two-second performance allowance and lack of a single-fault shock safety
reliability evaluation can create a risk of electric shock on the exposed
attachment plug blades used in a PIPV product.”
​
The above UL narrative claims that IEEE 1547 interconnection performance
requirements do not constitute a product shock-safety evaluation for standard “plug-in”
consumer-accessible output interfaces (e.g., connector plug blades used as an output).
However, this narrative becomes misleading when it recasts anti-islanding as a
requirement “to protect the grid” unrelated to personal shock protection. The grid does
not need protection from UL-1741 compliant PV inverters exporting electricity under
normal operating conditions. Anti-islanding requirements were specifically adopted in
IEEE 1547 for line-worker shock protection to prevent distributed resources like PV
inverters from energizing a de-energized portion of the utility system during outages and
restoration events, a scenario that can create shock and arc-flash risk for utility
personnel and can also disrupt protection coordination and reclosing practices.
As documented in GismoPower’s contribution to RFI DE-FOA-0003167 - Grid
Integration of Solar Energy Systems and Other Inverter-based Resources (November
2023) on page 5 and in the first SBIR Research Performance Progress Report on page
27, we demonstrated that both grid-following inverters, such as the SolarEdge SE7600,
and grid-forming inverters, such as the Sol-Ark 12K, reduce AC output voltage
substantially faster than the two-second cessation allowance permitted under IEEE
1547. Newer Sol-Ark 12K inverters additionally incorporate an integrated Microgrid
Interconnect Device (MID), providing an extra layer of protective functionality beyond
anti-islanding circuits alone.

 6

Measured results showed that the SolarEdge SE7600 reduced output voltage in less
than ~40 ms, while the Sol-Ark 8K achieved voltage reduction in less than ~60 ms. In
comparison, the inductive electric drill press used as a reference load exhibited a slower
voltage decay by approximately 20 ms under similar measurement conditions. These
results indicate that modern PV inverters already operate well within timeframes that are
significantly shorter than the IEEE 1547 anti-islanding limit.
GismoPower maintains that an electric shock protection requirement originally intended
to safeguard utility lineworkers located several feet downstream from the point of
disconnection should, at a minimum, provide equivalent protection to individuals who
may come into contact with an exposed connector prong at the inverter’s AC output
interface.
Shortening the IEEE 1547 two-second anti-islanding voltage cessation requirement to
e.g. sub-100 ms levels would therefore not materially affect commercially available PV
inverters. Instead, such a change could render large inductive loads like electric drill
presses “non-compliant” with an amended IEEE “anti-islanding” voltage drop
requirement.

UL 3700 Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems​
Issued December 11, 2025, UL 3700 establishes a formal certifiable product category
for Interactive Plug-In PV Equipment and Systems. The Outline of Investigation (OOI)
defines scope, construction requirements, performance testing, and marking and
installation instructions. Importantly, UL 3700 does not assume the availability of
market-ready bidirectional GFCI breakers; instead, it defines device-level and
system-level mitigation approaches that can be certified independently of legacy
electrical infrastructure.
Alignment with SBIR Phase II Objectives​
The publication of the UL White Paper and UL 3700 Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV)
Equipment and Systems OOI demonstrates that the SBIR Phase II effort materially
contributed to standards development by translating research findings into safety
science and testable requirements. While final product certification occurs beyond the
SBIR performance period, the project successfully enabled a viable UL certification
pathway where none previously existed.
Relevance to DOE Objectives​
This work supports DOE goals related to distributed energy access,
consumer-deployable clean energy technologies, and regulatory modernization. By
establishing a durable UL framework for PIPV systems, the SBIR Phase II project
delivers system-level impact beyond a single product and provides a foundation for
future commercialization and code adoption.

3.3 Stakeholder Discovery & Outreach (G/NG-3)

During the SBIR Phase II performance period, GismoPower advanced a plug-in,
behind-the-meter (BTM) solar technology that encountered regulatory barriers
disproportionate to its technical risk. No other US-based patent-holding company with
an SBIR Phase II supported BTM solar solution faces such a high concentration of
non-technical obstacles across utilities, codes, and permitting authorities.

 10

This experience underscored a core tension explored throughout this project: while
distributed energy resources are broadly supported in principle, appliance-class plug-in
solar systems challenge long-standing regulatory assumptions that were developed for
permanently installed infrastructure. GismoPower’s approach: requiring a dedicated
circuit rather than relying on branch circuit connections, addresses known safety
concerns such as breaker masking through branch-circuit overload. Although this
design choice differentiates the MEGA® system from low-wattage 120V plug-in
products, it also establishes a technically credible pathway for higher-wattage, safe
plug-in solar deployment in the United States.

The SBIR Phase II effort combined technical development with extensive outreach and
market validation activities to ensure that emerging regulatory frameworks evolve in
parallel with innovation. The following activities reflect how the project engaged
(rejected) investors, policymakers, utilities, tribal partners, and the broader clean energy
ecosystem to validate both the technology and its market relevance. Providing direct
access to the original documents or links to videos for each topic enables verification
and traceability of the underlying documentation. Please refer to the individual Google
Docs links provided as needed.

systems. While the outreach did not result in formal Commission action, it increased
awareness among FERC staff and leadership and reinforced a core Phase II
conclusion: standards development and certifiable product pathways are prerequisites
for effective execution of federal DER policy.

3.3.7 ​ Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC)
During the SBIR Phase II performance period, GismoPower engaged with the Federal
Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) to assess whether federal energy policy,
specifically FERC Order No. 2222 and FERC Order N. 901, were being effectively
implemented for emerging plug-in and appliance-like distributed energy resources
(DERs, or whether conflicts with non-FERC-jurisdictional electrical codes and product
standards were creating unintended barriers.
Petition for Declaratory Order EL-25-110-000​
On June 25, 2025, GismoPower filed a Petition for Declaratory Order requesting
clarification on whether certain provisions of the National Electrical Code (NEC) and
related UL product standards could constitute barriers to DER participation in wholesale
markets, contrary to the intent of FERC Order No. 2222. The petition argued that
technology-neutral federal policy supporting behind-the-meter DERs was being
undermined in practice by Code and Standards requirements that predated widespread
bidirectional two-way power flow and plug-in solar technologies. The petition cited
pioneering state policy developments like Utah H.B. 340 and national laboratory
research identifying regulatory barriers to plug-in solar adoption, and requested
Commission guidance on harmonizing federal market policy with safety and installation
standards.
Request for Fee Waiver and Commission Action​
Because the required $40,900 filing fee posed a material hardship for a pre-commercial,
SBIR-funded small business, GismoPower submitted a Request for Fee Waiver on July
31, 2025. The request cited economic hardship, reliance on DOE SBIR funding, and the
public-interest nature of the issues raised.
On August 15, 2025, FERC issued an Order Denying Waiver of Filing Fee, concluding
that the company had not met the regulatory threshold for severe economic hardship
under 18 C.F.R. § 381.106(a). Absent payment of the fee, the Commission did not act
on the merits of the Petition.
Informal Outreach from FERC Leadership​
Following issuance of the waiver denial, senior staff from the Office of the FERC
Chairman initiated direct outreach to GismoPower to discuss the technology and the
jurisdictional questions raised. Correspondence confirms that the outreach was
exploratory and informational in nature, reflecting Commission interest in understanding
how emerging behind-the-meter technologies intersect with existing federal authority,
including Orders 2222 and 901.

 17

Draft Municipal Petition (Not Filed)​
In parallel, GismoPower prepared a draft joint Petition for Declaratory Order with the
City of Palmetto, Georgia, intended to present the issue from a municipal perspective.
This draft expanded the legal framing to include inverter-based resource reliability
requirements under Order No. 901. The draft petition was not submitted to FERC and is
included solely as background documentation of regulatory exploration during Phase II.
Relevance to SBIR Objectives​
Although no declaratory relief was issued, the FERC engagement clarified procedural,
financial, and jurisdictional barriers facing small SBIR-funded firms seeking federal
clarification of cross-domain regulatory conflicts. The experience informed the project’s
strategic emphasis on standards development and certification pathways (e.g., UL
3700) as the most viable near-term mechanism for enabling compliance with federal
energy policy and supporting commercialization of plug-in solar technologies.​

3.3.8​ International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC)​
​
During the SBIR Phase II performance period, GismoPower engaged directly with the
International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) to address how emerging plug-in
photovoltaic (PV) and portable distributed energy resource (DER) technologies are
treated within international electrical installation standards. This engagement focused
on ensuring that IEC installation requirements appropriately reflect current safety
science, appliance-class product standards, and evolving national policy frameworks for
bidirectional power flow, rather than implicitly excluding plug-in solar technologies
through assumptions developed for permanently installed generation systems.
Engagement with IEC TC64/WG48 and USNC TAG 64​
GismoPower’s Principal Investigator participated as a technical expert for the IEC
Technical Committee 64 (Electrical Installations of Buildings) and the corresponding
U.S. National Committee (USNC) Technical Advisory Group (TAG 64). In this role,
GismoPower contributed to the U.S. review of IEC 60364-7-751 (Low-Voltage
Generating Sets), a proposed international installation standard with potential
implications for plug-in solar systems.
In July 2025, USNC TAG 64 formally reviewed the draft standard. While the U.S.
supported the majority of the document, the committee submitted a unanimous
comment opposing specific clauses (751.550.101.1 and 751.550.101.2) that would
prohibit connecting generating equipment to final circuits via standard receptacles. The
U.S. position emphasized that such language could unintentionally prevent deployment
of plug-in solar technologies already governed by national product safety standards and
installation codes, and could restrict innovation where national standards provide
appropriate technical safeguards.

 18

Documentation of National and International Context​
As part of this engagement, GismoPower contributed technical context to committee
deliberations and published materials documenting the emergence of U.S. state-level
policy enabling plug-in solar, including Utah’s H.B. 340, which created a legal category
for “Portable Solar Generating Devices” subject to UL listing and NEC compliance.
Comparative information was also provided regarding large-scale deployment of plug-in
PV systems in Europe under national product standards such as Germany’s DIN VDE V
0126-95:2025-12, demonstrating that such systems can be regulated safely through
product-level standards rather than installation prohibitions.
IEC Working Group Deliberations​
In December 2025, GismoPower participated in IEC TC 64 Working Group 48
deliberations in Obernai/FR as the document advanced toward the Final Draft
International Standard (FDIS) stage. During these meetings, GismoPower presented
recent technical developments, including UL’s published white paper on plug-in PV
safety interactions, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s analysis of regulatory
barriers to balcony solar and plug-in DERs, and the completion of UL 3700 Interactive
Plug-In PV Equipment and Systems establishing construction and test requirements for
Interactive Plug-In PV technologies.

3.3.13 UL-1741 SC Task Group​
​
During the SBIR Phase II performance period, GismoPower engaged in multiple
national and international standards development activities to address regulatory and
technical barriers affecting plug-in and mobile distributed energy resource (DER)
technologies. This section documents participation in the UL Standards process for UL
1741 Supplement SC, aligned in structure and tone with other regulatory and standards
engagement activities reported under UL 3700, IEC, NV-Energy/NPUC, and FERC.
Overview of Engagement​
GismoPower participated directly in the UL Standards ballot and comment process for
the proposed addition of Supplement SC to UL 1741, which governs grid-interactive
inverters and power conversion equipment. Engagement focused on evaluating whether
proposed requirements appropriately addressed emerging DER configurations,
including plug-in solar, mobile DER systems, and bidirectional EV charging
technologies.
Ballot Participation and Position​
In September 2025, GismoPower submitted a formal ballot response and technical
comments during the UL 1741 Supplement SC voting cycle. The UL-1741 draft
Supplement SC proposed new requirements specifying that Electric Vehicle Supply
Equipment (EVSE) and related DER equipment must be “permanently connected” to the
premises wiring.
GismoPower was the sole “No” vote and provided detailed technical objections. The
comments stated that the permanent-connection requirement lacked a demonstrated
safety basis and would function as a structural exclusion of cord-and-plug-connected
DER technologies. These concerns were raised in the context of maintaining technology
neutrality while ensuring equivalent levels of electrical safety.
Interaction with Existing Standards and Policy​
The submitted comments noted that UL 1741 Supplement SC was intended to align
with existing UL standards, yet the proposed language conflicted with other ongoing
standards efforts and regulatory developments, including:​

 26

- UL 9741 (Bidirectional Electric Vehicle Charging Systems),​
- UL’s parallel development of safety frameworks for plug-in photovoltaic (PIPV)
equipment,​
- State and federal policy initiatives supporting behind-the-meter DER participation.
GismoPower further noted that photovoltaic systems historically fell outside the scope of
UL 1741 Supplement SC, and that expanding the standard to impose
permanent-connection requirements narrows innovation pathways without addressing
underlying technical limitations.
Technical Gaps and Safety Considerations​
The ballot comments identified the absence of UL-listed bidirectional ground-fault circuit
interrupters (GFCIs) as the primary technical limitation affecting safe deployment of
plug-in DERs. This gap, rather than any inherent safety deficiency of plug-in or portable
systems, was cited as the root cause of current compliance challenges. These findings
are consistent with independent analysis by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory
identifying unidirectional protection devices as a barrier to balcony solar and plug-in
DER adoption in the United States.
Outcome of the Standards Process​
During the ballot cycle, a task group strawpoll proposal was introduced to expand
Supplement SC to include safety and performance requirements specific to plug-in
photovoltaic technologies. The strawpoll proposal was rejected, and the committee
elected not to expand the scope of Supplement SC to address plug-in DER
technologies at that time.
Implications for SBIR Objectives​
The outcome of the UL 1741 Supplement SC engagement clarified the limitations of
existing inverter standards in accommodating plug-in and mobile DER configurations.
This experience reinforced the project’s strategic emphasis on pursuing fit-for-purpose
standards development through separate frameworks, including UL 3700, as a more
effective pathway to enable safe, compliant deployment and commercialization of
plug-in solar and mobile DER technologies within the SBIR program timeframe.

3.3.15 Connecticut Public Utilities Regulatory Authority (PURA) – ​
Innovative Energy Solutions (IES)

GismoPower submitted a proposal under Cycle 3 of the Innovative Energy Solutions
(IES) Program administered by the Connecticut Public Utilities Regulatory Authority
(PURA). The proposal was not selected for Phase 3 deployment.

In its written decision (Docket No. 24-08-07), PURA stated that certain proposed
projects, including GismoPower’s, were not selected because their demonstration of
potential cost effectiveness lacked sufficient detail. PURA further indicated that it could
not evaluate projects where key economic inputs or assumptions had changed without
additional explanation.

With respect to GismoPower’s proposal, PURA specifically cited a lack of detail
regarding ratepayer benefits as defined within the IES program framework, including
renewable energy credits (RECs) and household upgrades. PURA noted that while
RECs can provide value to solar generation assets, customers would be unable to
receive REC incentives in the absence of an interconnection agreement with the electric
distribution company (EDC), which is a prerequisite under existing Connecticut utility
programs.

The PURA determination did not dispute the existence of customer-level return on
investment (ROI) for the MEGA® system, nor did it identify technical infeasibility or
safety concerns. Rather, the decision reflects a structural limitation of the IES evaluation
framework: benefits that accrue primarily to customers or communities behind the meter
are not readily credited unless they also produce measurable, utility-administered
ratepayer benefits such as utility-controlled RECs or grid-side investments.

Because PURA operates within a regulatory construct sponsored and administered
through incumbent utilities, behind-the-meter generation that displaces utility-owned or
utility-compensated energy resources presents limited incentive alignment under current
program rules. As a result, customer-financed, appliance-class solar systems despite
demonstrable economic value to end users are disadvantaged relative to projects that
expand utility-visible assets or utility-managed incentive streams.

Implications for SBIR Outcomes

This outcome reinforced a core finding of the SBIR Phase II effort: state pilot programs
optimized for utility-centric benefit accounting are poorly suited to evaluate
appliance-class, customer-owned distributed generation, even where such systems
demonstrate clear economic returns, safety, and deployability. ​

7. Technical Challenges and Lessons Learned

7.1 UL-Standard-Setting in the Absence of a Prior Product Category
A foundational challenge of the project was that the technology did not fit within any
existing U.S. product safety standard category. Appliance-class, plug-in photovoltaic
(PV) and distributed energy resource (DER) systems occupy a hybrid no-man's land
between permanently installed generation equipment and consumer appliances.
Existing standards assume one of two conditions:
 1.​ Permanently installed equipment evaluated in conjunction with the premises
 wiring, or
 2.​ Portable consumer appliances that do not export power onto the electrical
 system.
The project’s technology satisfied neither assumption. As a result, early regulatory and
utility interactions consistently failed not due to identified hazards, but due to the
absence of a certifiable framework under which hazards could be evaluated.
This gap created a circular barrier: Utilities and Authorities Having Jurisdiction (AHJs)
deferred to product standards that did not exist, while standards bodies lacked
precedents to scope requirements without real-world deployment experience.
The project demonstrated that when no prior category exists, standards development
becomes a daunting engineering task in itself, not merely a documentation exercise.
Translating system-level safety concepts into testable, enforceable requirements
required:
 ●​ Recognition of infrastructure limitations (e.g., lack of bidirectional ground-fault
 protection devices);
 ●​ Willingness to decouple product safety from assumptions about building wiring
 upgrades.
Completing the Outline of Investigation (OOI) for UL 3700 illustrated that fit-for-purpose
standards can be developed without waiting for wholesale changes to codes or
infrastructure, but doing so requires close integration between engineering design and
standards logic from the outset.​

9. Commercialization Status and Outlook
Commercialization Risk Reduction Through Federal Vehicle Classification
 To reduce regulatory uncertainty and enable scalable national
 deployment, GismoPower joined the National Association of Trailer
 Manufacturers (NATM) and initiated the process to classify the MEGA®
 as a federally regulated trailer under National Highway Traffic Safety
 Administration (NHTSA) jurisdiction.
GismoPower is intentionally shifting the MEGA from a site-specific “structure” paradigm
into a federally regulated vehicle paradigm. This is a critical strategic move that
materially simplifies nationwide deployment and underscores the importance of the
scope enhancement for UL-9741 and UL-1741 SC to include plug-connected
appliances and ALL plug-connected vehicles, including forklifts, trailers and boats as
NEC recognized and UL standardized categories.

This approach materially reduces commercialization risk by eliminating site-specific
structural permitting, shortening deployment timelines, and enabling repeatable
manufacturing and registration across all 50 states. The VIN-based vehicle classification
also supports insurability, asset tracking, and standardized enforcement recognition,
thereby improving market bankability and lowering barriers to adoption for tribal, rural,
and commercial customers.
The NHTSA guide for manufacturers of motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment
provides the legal and procedural foundation for this transition.
Under National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) regulations, a trailer is
defined as:
“A motor vehicle with or without motive power, designed for carrying
persons or property and for being drawn by another motor vehicle.”

11. Energy Impact and Increased Power Generation
The SBIR Phase II project delivered measurable increases in electricity generation per
deployed unit, improving the productivity of customer-owned, behind-the-meter power
assets. Upgrading photovoltaic modules from 540 W to 710 W increased per-unit DC
wattage from 4.86 kW to 6.39 kW. In parallel, modernization of power electronics
produced substantial real-world performance gains. Field data comparing earlier and
later MEGA® designs demonstrated up to 50 percent higher electricity generation under
low-light winter conditions, exceeding initial performance expectations.
These gains increase locally produced electricity without requiring additional land
disturbance, new transmission infrastructure, or utility-funded capital investments.
Because MEGA® systems are privately financed and customer-sited, all incremental
generation is achieved with zero capital expenditure by utilities or ratepayers, while
reducing demand on centralized generation during peak and shoulder periods if
combined with battery storage.
The system’s appliance-class, non-permanent design further limits environmental and
infrastructure impacts by avoiding foundations, roof penetrations, or permanent site
modifications. This deployability supports flexible use cases including semi-permanent
installations, mobile applications, and rapid redeployment while preserving land use
options and reducing long-term site obligations.​

11.1 Affordability, Access, and Customer Choice

The project advances affordable access to locally produced electricity by enabling
deployment models that are structurally compatible with renters, low- and
moderate-income households, Tribal communities, and other customers who lack
access to conventional rooftop solar. By eliminating requirements for roof ownership,
major electrical retrofits, and site-specific structural permitting, the MEGA® platform
reduces both upfront and soft costs that commonly limit participation in customer-owned
generation.

Engagement with Tribal partners and community-based organizations confirmed that
portability, non-permanence, and standardized certification are critical for aligning
energy solutions with local governance, land-use constraints, and affordability priorities.
Planned community-led deployment programs, supported by post-SBIR philanthropic
funding and federal technical assistance, provide a credible pathway for delivering
practical economic and resilience benefits. These include reduced household energy
expenditures, improved reliability during outages, and opportunities for local workforce

12. Conclusions and Future Work​
​
12.1 Conclusions
This DOE SBIR Phase II project successfully met all proposed technical, regulatory, and
commercialization objectives, with all Phase II milestones completed and verified in
accordance with the approved Statement of Work. Deliverables associated with
standards development (G/NG-1), engineering validation (G/NG-2), stakeholder and
market engagement (G/NG-3), and cost optimization (G/NG-4) were achieved and
documented through UL-issued materials, signed and sealed engineering
documentation, validated cost models, and demonstrated field deployments
A central Phase II outcome was the completion and issuance of UL-3700, Interactive
Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems, establishing the first U.S. certifiable product
safety framework for appliance-class, cord-and-plug-connected photovoltaic and
distributed energy resource (DER) systems. This outcome directly addresses the
primary commercialization barrier identified at project inception, the absence of a
nationally recognized safety standard for bidirectional, plug-connected generation and
represents a durable, system-level contribution aligned with DOE objectives for
regulatory modernization and expanded DER access.
From an engineering and manufacturing readiness perspective, the project
demonstrated measurable progress consistent with late-stage prototype to early
commercial readiness. Design-for-manufacturing and assembly (DFMA) improvements
reduced structural component count, lowered per-unit material and assembly costs, and
improved scalability. Concurrent system upgrades increased per-unit energy output and
validated performance under real-world operating conditions. These outcomes
collectively reduce technical and manufacturing risk and support transition beyond
SBIR-funded R&D.

The project also achieved market, regulatory, and cost readiness through extensive
engagement with end-users, regulators, utilities, standards bodies, federal agencies,
Tribal partners, and national laboratories. Utility-approved deployments and formal
regulatory interactions demonstrated that remaining barriers to adoption are not rooted
in technical feasibility, but in standards availability and permitting consistency, both of
which were materially advanced during Phase II. Documented post-SBIR demand
signals and secured funding pathways further validate market relevance and near-term
deployment potential.
Taken together, these results confirm that the Phase II effort substantially reduced
commercialization risk across technical, regulatory, market, and cost dimensions,
fulfilling the core intent of the DOE SBIR Phase II program.

12.2 Phase III Readiness
This Phase II project explicitly enables a Phase III path focused on certification
completion, scaled manufacturing, and multi-site deployment rather than continued
feasibility research. With a certifiable UL framework established, engineering designs
validated, cost structures optimized, and market demand demonstrated, the project is
positioned to transition into Phase III activities supported by a combination of private,
philanthropic, and DOE-leveraged resources, including national laboratory technical
assistance. Phase III efforts will concentrate on execution, replication, and scale,
consistent with DOE objectives for accelerating the commercialization and impact of
distributed clean energy technologies.

ERRATUM
Report: GismoPower LLC, SBIR Phase II Final Report to the U.S. Department of Energy,
Award DE-SC0022708, December 2025.

Affected section: Chapter 10, "Reassessing Net Energy Metering (NEM 3.0)," Section 10.6,
"California Example" (inputs listed on page 46; numerical results reported on page 47).

Date of erratum: July 26, 2026.

Nature of the correction
The numerical results reported in Section 10.6 do not follow from the stated inputs when
evaluated with equations (3) and (7) of the same chapter. The reported results are also
internally inconsistent: the three printed components (+1.70%, +0.19%, and −1.25%) sum to
+0.64%, not to the reported total of +1.13% in equation (9), while the reported bill impact ($2.40
per month on a $210 bill) corresponds to the total rather than to the sum of the components.
This internal inconsistency confirms that the error arose in the evaluation step and cannot be
attributed to an alternative, unstated parameter set. The equations, the methodology, and all
stated input values are unchanged and remain as printed. Only the evaluated results are
corrected; no input, assumption, or conclusion of the report is otherwise affected.

For completeness of scope: this erratum does not concern equation (8), the societal overlay
ri_soc. Section 10.5 presents that equation analytically only; no numerical values for the
externality terms X_U,land, X_U,nr, X_D,land, or X_D,nr are stated anywhere in the chapter, the
example's equation (9) is the total from equations (3) and (7) rather than ri_soc, and the chapter
itself notes that the X terms do not change rates absent regulatory action. Equation (8) therefore
requires no correction and plays no role in the discrepancy corrected here.

Corrected values

 Quantity (Section 10.6) As printed Corrected

 BTM self-consumed PV contribution to average rates +1.70% +4.57%
 BTM export contribution to average rates +0.19% −0.46%
 Utility-scale solar contribution to average rates −1.25% −2.86%
 Net rate impact, ri_total (equation 9) +1.13% +1.25%
 Monthly impact on a $210 residential bill $2.40 $2.63
 Net rate impact excluding self-consumed generation not stated −3.32%

For verification, using the printed inputs: ​
D = 0.12, U = 0.20, s = 0.55, r = 35, r_exp = 7, CoS₀ = 35, A_E = 9.0, A_L = 0.5, A_T&D = 5.0, η
= 0.25, η′ = 0.10, C_U = 3.0, VoS_U = 8.0, (all in cents/kWh where applicable)

CE_self = 9.0 + 0.5 + 0.25 × 5.0 = 10.75 and ​
CE_exp = 9.0 + 0.5 + 0.10 × 5.0 = 10.0.

Equation (7) then gives the self-consumption term: ​
0.12 × 0.55 × (35 − 10.75)/35 = +4.57% ​
​
and the export term ​
0.12 × 0.45 × (7 − 10.0)/35 = −0.46%.

Equation (3) gives the utility-scale term: ​
0.20 × (3.0 − 8.0)/35 = −2.86%. ​
​
The corrected net is +1.25%, or approximately $2.63 per month on a $210 residential bill.

Effect on findings and conclusions
The corrections leave the chapter's qualitative findings intact and strengthen its central
recommendation. First, the corrected export term is negative: under the stated export credit of 7
cents/kWh, which is below the utility avoided cost of exports (CE_exp = 10.0 cents/kWh),
exported behind-the-meter energy reduces average rates rather than raising them. Second, the
chapter recommends excluding self-consumed generation from rate-impact calculations on the
ground that on-site load reduction is conservation, not a cost; applying that recommendation to
the corrected figures yields a net rate impact of −3.32% (exports plus utility-scale only), a
stronger version of the result the chapter argued for. Third, the sensitivity statement regarding
an increase of η from 0.25 to 0.40 remains directionally correct; the corrected net under η = 0.40
is +1.11%.

We regret the error and appreciate the opportunity to correct the record. Please associate this
erratum with the report file for Award DE-SC0022708.
