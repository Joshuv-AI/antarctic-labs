One-Step Score-Based Density Ratio Estimation
Wei Chen weichen001.work@foxmail.com, Qibin Zhao qibin.zhao@riken.jp, John Paisley jpaisley@columbia.edu, Junmei Yang yjunmei@scut.edu.cn, Delu Zeng dlzeng@scut.edu.cn

Abstract
Density ratio estimation (DRE) is a useful tool for quantifying discrepancies between probability distributions, but existing approaches often involve a trade-off between estimation quality and computational efficiency. Classical direct DRE methods are usually efficient at inference time, yet their performance can seriously deteriorate when the discrepancy between distributions is large. In contrast, score-based DRE methods often yield more accurate estimates in such settings, but they typically require considerable repeated function evaluations and numerical integration. We propose One-step Score-based Density Ratio Estimation (OS-DRE), a partly analytic and solver-free framework designed to combine these complementary advantages. OS-DRE decomposes the time score into spatial and temporal components, representing the latter with an analytic radial basis function (RBF) frame. This formulation converts the otherwise intractable temporal integral into a closed-form weighted sum, thereby removing the need for numerical solvers and enabling DRE with only one function evaluation. We further analyze approximation conditions for the analytic frame, and establish approximation error bounds for both finitely and infinitely smooth temporal kernels, grounding the framework in existing approximation theory. Experiments across density estimation, continual Kullback-Leibler and mutual information estimation, and near out-of-distribution detection demonstrate that OS-DRE offers a favorable balance between estimation quality and inference efficiency.

The main contributions of this work are:

•

We propose OS-DRE, a solver-free score-based DRE framework that approximates the path integral in one step and aims to combine the estimation quality of score-based methods with the efficiency of classical one-pass estimation.

•

We utilize the theory of frame basis approximation to achieve a spatiotemporal representation of time score, and convert the log-density ratio path integral into a single inner product owing to isomomorphism characteristic of Hilbert space, thereby enabling solver-free inference and also supporting analytic temporal derivatives for training.

•

We obtain analytic frames with an RBF-based construction and identify conditions for a computable and well-posed approximation, by justifying their completeness, finite-term stability, and analytic tractability. By relating this construction to existing approximation results, we also obtain approximation error bounds for the finitely and infinitely smooth regimes considered in OS-DRE.

•

Extensive experiments show that OS-DRE achieves competitive performance with reduced computational cost across density estimation, continual KL divergence estimation, OOD detection, and MI estimation benchmarks.

Finally, Proposition

Related Works

Existing methods for DRE can be broadly categorized by how directly they compare the endpoint densities. Early direct approaches either estimate the two densities separately, e.g.,, by kernel density estimation
(
22
)
, and then form their ratio, or estimate the ratio itself without modeling each density individually
(
26
)
. Representative direct estimators include KLIEP
(
63
)
and uLSIF
(
25
)
, together with broader formulations based on Bregman divergence and
f
-divergence
(
62
)
.
A major conceptual advance was the connection between DRE and class-probability estimation, which showed that minimizing suitable proper composite losses in binary classification yields consistent ratio estimators
(
41
)
.
This perspective led to discriminative and neural DRE methods such as NCE
(
17
)
and InfoNCE
(
49
)
, and also motivated subsequent analyses of truncation
(
38
, TR-DRE)
, bias in classification-based ratio learning
(
59
)
, and DRE with missing data
(
13
)
. More recently, theoretical work has begun to clarify the statistical behavior of deep direct estimators.
For example,
77
establish non-asymptotic error bounds for deep ReLU
(
1
)
DRE under Bregman divergence
(
2
)
and extend the analysis to unbounded settings, with connections to KL estimation and telescoping estimators.
Nevertheless, these methods still compare the two endpoint densities directly, which have been discovered to probably lead to numerical instability and sensitivity to regularization. Particularly, some literature discuss therapies when the distributions have weak overlap
(
53
;
6
)
, large discrepancy
(
61
)
, or complex high-dimensional geometry
(
67
)
.

A substantial body of work therefore seeks to improve the estimation quality of direct DRE while retaining its basic formulation. A central challenge is the density-chasm problem, in which the supports of
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
overlap poorly, making direct ratio learning unstable and biased
(
38
;
53
)
. Existing remedies include trimmed DRE
(

)
, adaptive and regularized Bregman objectives
(

;
46
)
, iterated regularization to improve convergence behavior in kernel settings
(
15
)
, robust objectives such as

-DRE
(

)
, binary-loss refinements that reduce bias toward small density ratios
(
73
)
, and projection-based methods that transform the data into more favorable representation spaces for ratio estimation, either by identifying low-dimensional subspaces
(
67
)
or by mapping samples into a learned feature space before estimation, as in FDRE
(
8
)
. Although these methods substantially strengthen the classical pipeline, they remain variants of direct endpoint-ratio estimation and therefore do not substantially mitigate the fundamental difficulty of comparing
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
head-on.

A different line of work addresses this issue by introducing intermediate distributions, thereby replacing a difficult global ratio problem with a sequence or continuum of easier local problems. In the discrete setting, TRE
(
53
)
decomposes the target ratio into a telescoping product of intermediate ratios, and this idea has subsequently been extended through adaptive decomposition in AM-DRE
(
69
)
, multinomial formulations in MDRE
(
61
)
, and generalized-geodesic interpolation in IMDRE
(
30
)
. The continuous counterpart is score-based DRE, in which the log-density ratio is expressed as a path integral of a time-dependent score field along an interpolation between
p_{0}
and
p_{1}

(
9
)
. Because this formulation avoids direct comparison of the endpoint distributions and instead estimates smoother local changes along the path, it often yields improved estimation quality under high discrepancy
(
9
;
6
)
. Within this framework, the choice of interpolation path and training objective can affect both estimation quality and optimization stability. Several studies therefore examine how to improve these aspects, for example by designing lower-variance training objectives
(
5
)
, modifying objective formulations to accelerate training
(
71
)
, or proposing principled criteria for selecting more suitable probability paths
(
6
;
29
)
. More recently,
4
propose heuristic path-adaptation strategies motivated by minimum-variance principles. Despite these advances, inference in score-based DRE still typically requires numerical quadrature
(
4
)
or ODE solvers
(
9
;
71
)
, which leads to substantial computational cost
(
36
;
37
)
.

In summary, direct estimators are efficient but degrade under large discrepancy, while score-based methods are more accurate but computationally expensive. OS-DRE bridges this gap by retaining the estimation quality of score-based DRE while removing numerical integration via partly analytic temporal integration. Closely related,
16
study time-varying energy modeling via dual score matching, although their setting differs from general DRE between non-Gaussian distributions. Overall, OS-DRE combines the estimation quality of score-based methods with the efficiency of direct DRE methods.

One-Step Score-Based Density Ratio Estimation

3.1
Problem Statement

Score-based DRE rewrites the density ratio
r({\bm{x}})=p_{1}({\bm{x}})/p_{0}({\bm{x}})
as a temporal integral.
Given a continuously interpolating path of densities
\{p_{t}({\bm{x}})\}_{t\in[0,1]}
from
p_{0}
to
p_{1}
, we have

\log r({\bm{x}})=\log\frac{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}{p_{0}({\bm{x}})}=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}t.

(3)

The integrand
\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
, called the
time score
, is unknown in practice and is estimated by a neural network trained via time score matching
(
9
)
.
Evaluating
\log r({\bm{x}})
then requires numerically integrating the trained network, which is the main computational bottleneck as it demands many forward passes (e.g., via ODE solvers
(
9
)
or quadrature methods
(
4
;
47
)
).
Our goal is to eliminate this bottleneck by reducing the integral to a finite weighted sum via a temporal frame,
so that the weights can be precomputed once and reused across all
{\bm{x}}

4
Finite-Term Approximation for OS-DRE via Analytic RBFs

In
Section

3

\log r({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\bar{g}_{k}

\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t

To turn this infinite expansion into a practical one-step estimator, we should (i) approximate it by finitely many temporal atoms,
and (ii) choose those atoms so that the resulting approximation remains convergent and well-posed.
This section provides such a construction using analytic RBF families, together with approximation guarantees and concrete kernels.

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t)=s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},t),

(11)

\displaystyle\log r({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\bar{g}_{k}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\bar{g}_{k}=\log r_{K}({\bm{x}}),

(12)

where
\bar{g}_{k}
depends only on the chosen temporal family and the subscript
K
indicates approximation using
K
terms.
In practice, for each
{\bm{x}}
, we approximate
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
by an element of
\mathcal{V}_{K}
as in
Eq.

11
, forming the basis of our numerical implementation.

What Makes This Approximation Valid?

To be a faithful finite approximation of the frame-based representation in
Section

3
,
the family
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
should satisfy two requirements:
(i)
completeness
(as
K\to\infty
, the span can approximate any element of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
),
and (ii)
finite-term well-posedness
(for each
K
, the coefficient representation in
\mathcal{V}_{K}
is unique).
We now instantiate
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
with RBFs and formalize (i-ii) via Lemma
7

and Proposition
8
.

4.2
RBF-Based Temporal Frames

General RBF Construction.

We consider an infinite RBF family on
[0,1]
:

g_{k}(t)=\phi\left(\frac{|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right),\qquad t\in[0,1],

(13)

where
c_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
are the center and shape parameters.

Next, we will demonstrate that this family is expressive enough in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.

Lemma 7
.

Consider
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
defined by

g_{k}(t)=\phi(|t-c_{k}|/\sigma_{k})
on
[0,1]
, subject to:

(i)

The generating function
\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous, non-negative, not identically zero, and integrable, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\phi(r)\mathrm{d}r<\infty
.

(ii)

For any point
t_{0}\in(0,1)
, there exists a subsequence of indices
\{k_{n}\}_{n=1}^{+\infty}
such that the centers
c_{k_{n}}\to t_{0}
and the corresponding shape parameters
\sigma_{k_{n}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Then
\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.

See the proof in
Section

A.6
.
Lemma
7
establishes the
completeness
side of the construction:
as the family provides arbitrarily fine localized atoms around every interior location, the span of
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
can approximate arbitrary functions in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.

Finite-Term Stability and A Well-Posed Approximation Scheme.

Denseness alone does not ensure that the approximated score is numerically well-posed.
For OS-DRE, the finite-term representation in
\mathcal{V}_{K}
should also form a stable approximation scheme.
We formalize this requirement as follows.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}\subset\mathcal{H}_{t}
be defined by
g_{k}(t)=\phi(|t-c_{k}|/\sigma_{k})
.
This family yields a convergent and well-posed approximation scheme if:
(i) Denseness: The infinite family’s linear span is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, i.e.,
\overline{\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}}=\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
(ii) Finite-term stability: For any finite
K\geq 1
, the subset
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is linearly independent.

See the proof in
Section

A.7
.
In practice, (i) is ensured by Lemma
7
, while (ii) can be guaranteed by choosing
\phi

to correspond to a strictly positive definite kernel: for distinct centers, strict positive definiteness implies that the associated Gram matrix is non-singular
(
58
)
, hence
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is linearly independent.

Analytic Formulas for One-Step Estimation.

Beyond (i)–(ii), OS-DRE benefits from RBF families that admit analytic temporal integrals and derivatives.
For
g_{k}
in
Eq.

13
,

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\phi\left(\frac{|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

(14)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(t-c_{k})}{\sigma_{k}}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right),

(15)

so once
\phi
is fixed,
\bar{g}_{k}
and
g_{k}^{\prime}(t)
reduce to closed-form expressions and could be easily precomputed in advance (derived in
Appendix

B
).
This property is exactly what enables one-step inference in
Eq.

12
(via precomputing
\{\bar{g}_{k}\}
) and as well partly analytic computation of
\partial_{t}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)
in training.

Summary of Basic Approximation Conditions.

Before introducing specific kernel families, we summarize the essential properties that a generating function
\phi
must possess to form a valid and computable OS-DRE scheme.

Remark 9

(Basic Approximation Conditions)
.

To effectively bridge the gap between theoretical framework and practical implementation, an admissible RBF generating function
\phi
should satisfy three conditions:

1.

Completeness:
The infinite family should be sufficiently rich to ensure the denseness of the induced spaces, as established in Lemma
7
.

2.

Finite-term Stability:
Any finite-term approximation must remain numerically well-posed, forming a stable approximation scheme as formalized in Proposition
8
.

3.

Analytic Tractability:
The temporal operators must be analytically resolvable, meaning each atom
g_{k}
admits a closed-form temporal integral
\bar{g}_{k}
and derivative
g_{k}^{\prime}(t)
.

Guided by these conditions, the next section details specific choices for
\phi
that not only satisfy the above structural conditions but also cover different regularity regimes to accommodate varying target score distributions.

4.4
Approximation Error Bounds Analysis

Approximating the infinite expansion to
K
temporal atoms introduces approximation error. Building on the concrete kernel families introduced in
Section

4.3
, we now characterize how
\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}
decays as
K
increases, and how this decay leads to an error bound for
\log r({\bm{x}})
. For each fixed
{\bm{x}}
,
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
denotes the best approximation to
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
. This formulation separates the approximation capacity of the temporal family from the statistical and optimization errors that arise when the coefficients are learned in practice.

The approximation analysis proceeds in three steps. We first establish qualitative consistency from the denseness of the temporal family. We then derive an explicit algebraic rate for kernels whose native spaces are equivalent to Sobolev spaces of finite order. Finally, for infinitely smooth kernels such as the Gaussian and generalized IMQ families, we invoke the corresponding kernel-specific interpolation estimates.

Lemma 14

(Approximation Consistency)
.

Assume that the approximation spaces are nested, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}_{1}\subset\mathcal{V}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{H}_{t}
, and that their union is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, i.e.,
\overline{\bigcup_{K=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{V}_{K}}=\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
For each fixed
{\bm{x}}
, let
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
be the best approximation of
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
in
\mathcal{V}_{K}
.
Then

\left\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }K\to\infty.

(24)

Consequently,

\left|\log r({\bm{x}})-\log r_{K}({\bm{x}})\right|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }K\to\infty.

(25)

See
Section

A.8
for a proof.

While Lemma
14
ensures asymptotic consistency, it does not specify how rapidly the approximation error decays for a finite
K
.
To obtain explicit rates, one must quantify both the regularity of the target function
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
and the approximation power of the temporal kernel family.
For RBF approximation, the relevant notion of kernel regularity is the native space
\mathcal{N}_{\phi}
, i.e., the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) induced by the kernel generated by
\phi
.
We first consider the finitely smooth Sobolev regime following classical RBF error estimates
(
44
)
.

Proposition 15

(Approximation Error Bounds for Finitely Smooth Kernel)
.

Let
\phi
be a strictly positive definite radial kernel on
\mathbb{R}
such that
its native space
\mathcal{N}_{\phi}
is norm-equivalent to

\mathcal{W}^{\tau,2}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\tau>1/2
.
Let the target function satisfy

s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{W}^{\beta,2}([0,1])
with
1/2<\beta\leq\tau
.
Let
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
be the best approximation of
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
in

\mathcal{V}_{K}=\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, where the center set

\mathcal{C}_{K}=\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset[0,1]
is quasi-uniform.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
{\bm{x}}
,
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
, and
K
, such that

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq C\,K^{-\beta}\,\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{W}^{\beta,2}([0,1])}.

(26)

See
Section

A.9
for a proof.
Proposition
15
refines the qualitative convergence in
Lemma
14
by providing an explicit algebraic decay rate.
Its key feature is that the achievable exponent is limited by the kernel regularity: the target smoothness
\beta
can be exploited only up to the native-space smoothness
\tau
(i.e.,
\beta\leq\tau
in Proposition
15
).
This algebraic rate directly applies to the finitely smooth Matérn kernel introduced in
Section

4.3
.

Remark 16
.

In our implementation, we place centers
\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
on a quasi-uniform grid over
[0,1]

(to match the approximation setting of Proposition
15
),
and learn the shape parameters
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
to provide adaptive multi-scale coverage in time.
As
K
increases, the resulting sequence of center sets becomes dense in
(0,1)
.
Together with sufficiently small shape parameters, this stronger construction is consistent with the interior accumulation requirement in Lemma
7
.

We next turn to infinitely smooth kernels (such as the Gaussian, IMQ, and RQ kernels detailed in Section
4.3
), for which the natural approximation theory is no longer based on a fixed Sobolev order.
Instead, one obtains super-algebraic or exponential-type interpolation estimates in terms of the fill distance.

Proposition 17

(Approximation Error Bounds for Infinitely Smooth Kernel)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{K}
be quasi-uniform.
Assume therefore that there exists a constant
c_{\ast}>0
, independent of
K
, such that
h_{K}\leq c_{\ast}K^{-1},\forall K\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

For each fixed
{\bm{x}}
, let
I_{\mathcal{C}_{K}}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{V}_{K}

denote the RBF interpolant of
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
on
\mathcal{C}_{K}
.

(i) Gaussian kernels.

Let
\phi(r)=e^{-r^{2}}
, and assume

s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])
with
\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])
being the native space over
[0,1]
.
Then there exist constants
C,c,h_{0}>0
,
independent of
{\bm{x}}
,
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
, and
K
, such that whenever
h_{K}\leq h_{0}
,

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq C\exp\left(-c\frac{|\log h_{K}|}{h_{K}}\right)\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])}.

(27)

Consequently, there exist constants
\widetilde{C},\widetilde{c}>0

such that, for all sufficiently large
K
,

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq\widetilde{C}\exp\bigl(-\widetilde{c}K\log K\bigr)\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])}.

(28)

(ii) Standard inverse multiquadric (IMQ) kernels.

Let
\phi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1/2}
, and assume
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])
and that the classical exponential-type IMQ interpolation estimate holds on
[0,1]
, namely, there exist constants
C>0
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and
h_{0}>0
such that whenever
h_{K}\leq h_{0}
,

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq C\lambda^{1/h_{K}}\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])}.

(29)

Then there exist constants
\widehat{C},\widehat{c}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
K
,

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq\widehat{C}\exp(-\widehat{c}K)\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])}.

(30)

(iii) Generalized inverse multiquadric kernels.

Let
\phi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, and assume
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])
.
Then there exist constants
C,c,h_{0}>0
,
independent of
{\bm{x}}
,
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
, and
K
, such that whenever
h_{K}\leq h_{0}
,

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{c}{h_{K}}\right)\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])}.

(31)

Consequently, there exist constants
\widetilde{C},\widetilde{c}>0

such that, for all sufficiently large
K
,

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)-s^{({\mathbf{t}})}_{K}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}\leq\widetilde{C}\exp(-\widetilde{c}K)\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}([0,1])}.

(32)

See proof in
Section

A.10
.
In particular, the RQ kernel

\phi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1}
is the special case
\alpha=1
and is covered by

Eqs.

31
and
32
.
In all three cases, we have derived explicit convergence rates by combining interpolation error bounds from the literature with the quasi-uniformity condition.
The Gaussian kernel yields super-algebraic convergence (
\exp(-cK\log K)
), while both the generalized IMQ and standard IMQ kernels yield exponential convergence (
\exp(-cK)
).

5.1
Model, Objectives, and Training Protocol

This subsection summarizes the implementation choices needed to reproduce OS-DRE:
(i) the temporal basis and neural network parameterization, (ii) the training objectives, and
(iii) the sampling schedules and training/inference procedures.

Neural Network Parameterization.

We parameterize the spatial coefficients with a neural network
\text{NN}(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}})
, which outputs the
K
coefficients

{\bm{h}}_{1:K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})=[h_{1}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),\dots,h_{K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]=\text{NN}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}).

(33)

For brevity, we denote the vector of temporal atoms and their derivatives evaluated at time
t
as

{\bm{g}}_{1:K}(t)=[g_{1}(t),\dots,g_{K}(t)],\quad{\bm{g}}_{1:K}^{\prime}(t)=[g_{1}^{\prime}(t),\dots,g_{K}^{\prime}(t)].

(34)

Then, for a given pre-specified temporal atoms
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the time score, its derivative, and its integral admit closed forms:

\displaystyle s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})g_{k}(t)=\left\langle{\bm{h}}_{1:K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{g}}_{1:K}(t)\right\rangle,

(35)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})g_{k}^{\prime}(t)=\left\langle{\bm{h}}_{1:K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{g}}_{1:K}^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\bar{g}_{k}=\left\langle{\bm{h}}_{1:K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{1:K}\right\rangle,

where
\bar{g}_{k}
is precomputed from the chosen kernel family (cf.
Section

4.3
).
The trainable parameters include the network weights
{\bm{\theta}}
and the basis shape parameters
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, while the centers
\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are fixed on a quasi-uniform grid over
[0,1]
to satisfy Proposition
15
.

The network consists of a shared backbone and task-specific heads.
The backbone extracts a feature embedding from
{\bm{x}}
.
For time-score matching and neural DRE tasks we use a ResNet backbone (e.g., ResNet-18)
(
18
)
, while for joint estimation of time
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}
and data scores
{\bm{s}}^{({\mathbf{x}})}({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
we adopt a U-Net backbone
(
55
)
to better capture spatial structure in high-dimensional data.
A coefficient head maps the feature embedding to the
K
coefficients, and when joint score matching (see
Eq.

36
) is used, an additional head predicts the data score
{\bm{s}}^{({\mathbf{x}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})
using time positional encoding
(
66
)
and residual blocks.

Training Objective.

We train
{\bm{\theta}}
(and
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
) using a task-dependent score-matching objective.
When both the time score
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)
and the data score
{\bm{s}}^{({\mathbf{x}})}({\bm{x}},t)
are modeled, we use the joint score matching objective
(
9
)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\lambda(0)s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}}_{0},0;{\bm{\theta}})-\lambda(1)s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}}_{1},1;{\bm{\theta}})\right]

(36)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{v}})}\left[2\lambda(t)\partial_{t}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})+2\lambda^{\prime}(t)s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\lambda(t)\|{\bm{s}}^{({\mathbf{x}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}+2\lambda(t){\bm{v}}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{s}}^{({\mathbf{x}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}}){\bm{v}}\right],

where

p({\bm{v}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
, and
\lambda(t)
is a weighting function.
The term
\partial_{t}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})
is an approximation of
\partial_{t}^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
.
The time variable is sampled from a task-dependent distribution
t\sim p(t)
following
(
5
)
.

Our framework provides computational advantages in both training and inference.
The derivative
\partial_{t}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})
is computed via
Eq.

35
, eliminating the need for automatic differentiation over
t
and the second-order gradients required by prior methods (e.g., DRE-
\infty
), thereby reducing optimization to a first-order problem with improved stability.
For large-scale tasks such as OOD detection, we adopt conditional score matching
(
71
)
, which is more efficient while retaining the one-step inference property of OS-DRE.

Sampling from
p_{t}({\bm{x}})
with Path Schedules.

To evaluate the training objective, we sample from the interpolated distribution
p_{t}({\bm{x}})
by constructing
{\bm{x}}_{t}
from independent samples
{\bm{x}}_{0}\sim p_{0}
,
{\bm{x}}_{1}\sim p_{1}
, and
t\sim p(t)
via a task-dependent interpolation scheme.
For simple settings we use fixed schedules such as the linear or variance-preserving (VP) path; for DRE, common choices include the diffusion interpolant (DI) and dequantified diffusion bridge interpolant (DDBI).
We generally adopt DDBI, as it does not assume
p_{0}
to be Gaussian
(
6
)
, better matching typical DRE settings.
For harder tasks such as OOD detection, we apply the minimum-variance path (MVP) principle
(
4
)
to make path parameters learnable.
MVP converts a prescribed scheme (e.g., DDBI) into a learnable one by optimizing its path parameters, yielding in our OOD experiments a learnable DDBI path that improves optimization and score calibration in Near-OOD regimes.
As these interpolation schemes are standard components rather than our main contribution, we omit their explicit formulas; details can be found in
4
.

Inference Protocol.

After training, OS-DRE evaluates the log-ratio by a single pass:

\log\hat{r}({\bm{x}})=\int_{0}^{1}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t;{\bm{\theta}})\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{\star})\bar{g}_{k}=\left\langle{\bm{h}}_{1:K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}),\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{1:K}\right\rangle,

(37)

where
{\bm{h}}_{1:K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{\star})=\text{NN}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{\star})
(see
Eq.

33

Because
\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{1:K}
are precomputed analytic constants, evaluation requires only one network evaluation (
\text{NFE}=1
), regardless of the particular time-sampling strategy or path family used during training.

5.2
Application to Density Estimation

Problem Setup and Evaluation Metric.

We consider density estimation where
p_{0}({\bm{x}})
is a simple reference distribution, e.g.,
\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
, and
p_{1}({\bm{x}})
is a complex, intractable data distribution.
The log-likelihood of
p_{1}
can be written as
\log p_{1}({\bm{x}})=\log r({\bm{x}})+\log p_{0}({\bm{x}})
, where
r({\bm{x}})={p_{1}({\bm{x}})}/{p_{0}({\bm{x}})}
is the density ratio.
After training, OS-DRE provides a one-step estimate of the log-ratio

\log\hat{r}({\bm{x}})

(cf.
Eq.

37
), and we evaluate log-likelihood as

\log p_{1}({\bm{x}})\approx\log\hat{r}({\bm{x}})+\log p_{0}({\bm{x}})
.
We report negative log-likelihood (NLL; lower is better) on tabular datasets, and bits-per-dimension (BPD; lower is better) for energy-based modeling.

We first evaluate on nine synthetic benchmarks
(
28
;
6
)
covering diverse geometric challenges.
OS-DRE employs
K=400
temporal atoms with a RQ kernel and performs inference with a single function evaluation (
\text{NFE}=1
).
Baselines rely on numerical quadrature with varying budgets (
\text{NFE}\in\{2,5,10\}
).

5.3
Application to Continual Kullback-Leibler Divergence Estimation

Problem Setup and Evaluation Metric.

We evaluate OS-DRE on continual Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence estimation under online distribution-shift scenarios, a setting highly relevant to real-time change-point detection
(
7
)
and continuous covariate-shift adaptation
(
76
)
.
In this setup, the target distribution evolves over discrete steps
t=0,1,2,\dots
, while the source distribution
p_{0}
remains fixed.
The goal is to continuously estimate the KL divergence from
p_{0}
to the current target
p_{t}
in real time:
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{t}\|p_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim p_{t}}\left[\log\frac{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{p_{0}({\bm{x}})}\right]
, where the inner log-ratio is directly approximated by DRE.

We consider three controlled benchmarks with a fixed source
p_{0}({\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
that isolate different types of nonstationarity:
(i) linear drift, where the target
p_{t}=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}}_{t},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t})
has linearly shifting mean and covariance,
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}=t\Delta{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}=(1-t\Delta\sigma){\bm{I}}_{d}
with constant drift rates
\Delta{\bm{\mu}}
and
\Delta\sigma
;
(ii) noise corruption, motivated by
19
, where variance inflates progressively with
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}={\bm{0}}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}=(1+t\Delta\sigma^{2}){\bm{I}}_{d}
;
and (iii) controlled divergence, which enforces a prescribed KL increase
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{t}\|p_{0})=t
by setting
\|{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|=\sqrt{2t}
with random directions
(
76
)
.

Continual KL Divergence Estimation.

Fig.

4
shows KL divergence estimation results across three benchmarks.
With only
\text{NFE}=1
, OS-DRE produces stable, accurate estimates that closely follow the ground truth, whereas solver-based baselines remain unreliable even at
\text{NFE}=50
.
In particular, OS-DRE tracks the drift horizon smoothly (
Fig.

4(a)
), detects discrete change points clearly (
Fig.

4(c)
), and maintains competitive accuracy as corruption increases, where baselines suffer from systematic underestimation and high variance (
Fig.

4(b)
).
The key reason is that OS-DRE computes the density ratio analytically rather than relying on numerical solvers, avoiding the error accumulation that makes solver-based methods unstable under tight inference budgets.

5.4
Application to Mutual Information Estimation

Problem Setup and Evaluation Metric.

We estimate mutual information (MI) between two random variables
{\mathbf{x}}\sim p({\bm{x}})
and
{\mathbf{y}}\sim q({\bm{y}})
via DRE by treating the joint distribution
p_{1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\triangleq p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
as the target and the product of marginals
p_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\triangleq p({\bm{x}})\,q({\bm{y}})
as the reference.
The MI is then
\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\sim p_{1}}[\log r({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})]
, where
r({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=p_{1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})/p_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. After training OS-DRE to obtain
\log\hat{r}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
, we approximate MI by Monte Carlo over held-out joint samples. Negative samples from
p_{0}
are obtained by shuffling within minibatches, while positive pairs are drawn from the true joint
(
9
)
. We report mean squared error (MSE) against ground-truth MI.

Geometrically Pathological Distributions.

We evaluate OS-DRE on four MI estimation benchmarks with geometrically pathological distributions
(
10
)
, featuring heavy tails (Half-Cube Map), sharp curvature (Asinh Mapping), non-smooth boundaries (Additive Noise), and asymmetric nonlinear dependencies (Gamma–Exponential).
These properties violate the local smoothness assumptions of many standard estimators.

As shown in
Fig.

5
, OS-DRE achieves consistently lower MSE than solver-based baselines across all dependency levels while using only
\text{NFE}=1
. Notably, the Matérn kernel often provides the best fit for asymmetric structures like Gamma–Exponential, while infinitely smooth kernels (Gaussian/RQ) remain competitive on moderate dependencies. These results confirm that OS-DRE’s partly analytic temporal representation is robust to geometric pathologies that destabilize numerical quadrature.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented OS-DRE, a partly analytic, solver-free framework for score-based density ratio estimation that offers a favorable balance between estimation quality and computational efficiency.
By decomposing the time score into spatial coefficients and an analytic temporal frame based on the isomomorphism characteristic and frame basis approximation theory of Hilbert space, the proposed method converts the path integral into a closed-form weighted sum.
This formulation obviates the need for numerical solvers and enables density ratio estimation with one single function evaluation.
The theoretical analysis studies conditions related to completeness, stability, and analytic tractability of the temporal frame.
By examining specific kernels in both finitely and infinitely smooth regimes, we further obtain approximation error bounds with algebraic and exponential decay.
These results clarify the approximation behavior of the finite-dimensional subspaces and provide guidance for selecting temporal kernels in practice.
Empirically, OS-DRE shows competitive performance across density estimation, continual KL divergence estimation, mutual information estimation, and challenging near out-of-distribution (OOD) detection benchmarks, while offering substantial speedups relative to solver-based baselines.
Taken together, these findings suggest that OS-DRE is a practical option for probabilistic inference under strict time or resource constraints.

The current framework also has limitations. Its approximation quality depends on the regularity of the target time score function
\{\partial_{t}\log p_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
, as analyzed in Proposition
15
. In particular, less smooth time scores may require a larger basis dimension
K
to maintain estimation quality, which can increase model capacity. Looking ahead, although the present work focuses on density ratio estimation, the underlying mechanism may be applicable more broadly. Whenever a target quantity admits a path-integral representation of a learned integrand, an analytic temporal representation may replace expensive numerical solvers with a single inner product. From this perspective, OS-DRE provides a concrete example of solver-free analytic temporal integration and suggests a more general design principle for efficient path-integral estimators beyond DRE.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

We want to show that for
\forall s^{({\mathbf{t}})}\in\mathcal{S}
. It satisfies:

\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}\times[0,1])}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}<\infty.

(38)

Based on
1
and
2
, we have: (1)
p_{t}({\bm{x}})\geq C>0
, so
\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\leq\frac{1}{C}
; (2)
\left|\partial_{t}p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right|\leq D
. Then, using the chain rule, we write
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)
as:

\left|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}=\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right|^{2}=\left|\frac{\partial_{t}p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\right|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{D}{C}\right)^{2}.

(39)

We now integrate over
\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}\times[0,1])}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

(40)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{D}{C}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D}{C}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\left(\frac{D}{C}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

Since
\mathcal{X}
is measurable and bounded, then
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}<\infty
holds, and
\|s^{({\mathbf{t}})}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}\times[0,1])}^{2}<\infty
.
Therefore, the space
\mathcal{S}
of functions
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}
is a subset of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X}\times[0,1])
. This complete the proof.

∎

A.2
Proof of Lemma
2

Proof.

The target log-density ratio is defined as the temporal integral of the time score function:
\log r({\bm{x}})=\int_{0}^{1}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\mathrm{d}t
.

For a fixed
{\bm{x}}
, we can express this integral as an inner product in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{t}=L^{2}([0,1])
between the function
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
and the constant function
{\bm{1}}(t)\equiv 1
.

\log r({\bm{x}})=\langle s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},\cdot),{\bm{1}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}.

(41)

Since
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is a complete orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, the time score has the series expansion
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t)
, which converges in the
L^{2}
-norm. Due to the continuity of the inner product in a Hilbert space, we can interchange the inner product and the infinite summation:

\displaystyle\log r({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t),{\bm{1}}(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}

(42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\langle h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t),{\bm{1}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\langle g_{k}(t),{\bm{1}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

This completes the proof.
∎

A.3
Proof of Lemma
4

Proof.

We begin with the
algebraic tensor product

\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}\hat{\otimes}\mathcal{H}_{t}
, consisting of finite sums of elementary tensors
f\otimes g
with
f\in\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}
and
g\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
. Although dense in
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
, this space is not complete. Its completion under the induced inner product is the
Hilbert tensor product
,
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}\hat{\otimes}\mathcal{H}_{t}
, which is isometrically isomorphic to the space
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}

(
24
)
.
This isomorphism allows us to construct a frame for
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
from frames of
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}\hat{\otimes}\mathcal{H}_{t}
.

Let
F
be an arbitrary element in
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}\hat{\otimes}\mathcal{H}_{t}\cong\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
. Let
h_{k}({\bm{x}})\triangleq\langle F({\bm{x}},\cdot),g_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}
. The sum of the squared frame coefficients for
F
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}|\langle F,f_{l}\otimes g_{k}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}|\langle h_{k},f_{l}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}}|^{2}\right)\quad(\bm{\star})

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}B_{f}\|h_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}}^{2}=B_{f}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\|h_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}}^{2}\quad(\bm{\star\star})

\displaystyle=B_{f}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}|\langle F({\bm{x}},\cdot),g_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\quad(\bm{\star\star\star})

\displaystyle\leq B_{f}\int_{\mathcal{X}}B_{g}\|F({\bm{x}},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\quad(\bm{\star\star\star\star})

\displaystyle=B_{f}B_{g}\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}}^{2}.

An analogous derivation provides the lower bound,
A_{f}A_{g}\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}}^{2}\leq\sum_{l,k}|\langle F,f_{l}\otimes g_{k}\rangle|^{2}
. The key steps are:

(\bm{\star})
Rewriting the sum by substituting the definition of
h_{k}
.

(\bm{\star\star})
Applying the upper frame bound for the spatial frame
\{f_{l}\}
for each fixed
k
.

(\bm{\star\star\star})
Using Fubini’s theorem to swap the summation and integration.

(\bm{\star\star\star\star})
Applying the upper frame bound for the temporal frame
\{g_{k}\}
for each fixed
{\bm{x}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem
5

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three steps: establishing the existence of the expansion for
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}
, deriving the corresponding expansion for
\log r({\bm{x}})
, and simplifying the expressions.

By Lemma
4
, since
\{f_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
and
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are frames for
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{t}
respectively, the set of elementary tensors
\{f_{l}\otimes g_{k}\}_{l,k=1}^{\infty}
forms a frame for the spatiotemporal space
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
. A fundamental property of a frame is that any element in the Hilbert space can be represented as a series expansion of the frame elements. Therefore, for any
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
, there exist coefficients
\{c_{l,k}\}
such that:

s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}(f_{l}\otimes g_{k})({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t),

(44)

where the series converges in the norm of
\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
.

The log-density ratio is obtained by integrating the time score. For a fixed
{\bm{x}}
, the integration operator
I:h(t)\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\mathrm{d}t
is a continuous linear functional on
\mathcal{H}_{t}
. The continuity allows us to interchange the functional with the infinite summations:

\displaystyle\log r({\bm{x}})=\int_{0}^{1}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t

(45)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})\bar{g}_{k}.

By defining the spatial coefficient functions
h_{k}({\bm{x}})\triangleq\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})
, we can group the terms in the double summations. This simplification yields the final expressions for the time score and the log-density ratio as presented in the theorem statement:

\displaystyle s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})\right)g_{k}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t),

(46)

\displaystyle\log r({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}c_{l,k}f_{l}({\bm{x}})\right)\bar{g}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\bar{g}_{k}.

(47)

This completes the proof.
∎

A.5
Proof of Corollary
6

Proof.

Let
v({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}^{\prime}(t)
. By assumption, this series converges to a function
v\in\mathcal{H}_{{\bm{x}},t}
. We must show that
v
is the weak derivative of
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)
with respect to time.

By definition, this requires showing that for any smooth test function
\psi\in C_{c}^{+\infty}((0,1))
, the following equality holds for almost every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
:

\int_{0}^{1}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{1}v({\bm{x}},t)\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(48)

Let’s evaluate the left-hand side. For a fixed
{\bm{x}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}(t)\right)\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t\quad(\bm{\star})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})\left(-\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}^{\prime}(t)\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)\quad(\bm{\star\star})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}h_{k}({\bm{x}})g_{k}^{\prime}(t)\right)\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t\quad(\bm{\star\star\star})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}v({\bm{x}},t)\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t.

The key steps are justified as follows:

(\bm{\star})
The interchange of summation and integration is permitted because the series for
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}
converges in
L^{2}
, and the operator
h\mapsto\int h\psi^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t
is a continuous linear functional on
L^{2}
.

(\bm{\star\star})
Since each
g_{k}\in\mathcal{W}^{1,2}([0,1])
, it has a weak derivative
g_{k}^{\prime}
. By the definition of the weak derivative and the fact that
\psi
has compact support in
(0,1)
(meaning boundary terms vanish), we can apply integration by parts.

(\bm{\star\star\star})
The interchange of integration and summation is again justified by the assumed
L^{2}
convergence of the series defining
v({\bm{x}},t)
.

This confirms that
v({\bm{x}},t)
is the weak derivative of
s^{({\mathbf{t}})}({\bm{x}},t)
, completing the proof.
∎

A.6
Proof of Lemma
7

Proof.

We prove denseness by
contradiction
, adapting standard approximate identity arguments from approximation theory
(
23
)
to our temporal frame setting.

Assume that
\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is not dense in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
. Then its closure
\overline{\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}}
is a proper closed subspace of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{t}
. Therefore,
\Bigl(\overline{\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}}\Bigr)^{\perp}\neq\{0\}.

Hence there exists a non-zero
u\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
(
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}>0
) such that

\langle u,g_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}=\int_{0}^{1}u(t)g_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0,\quad\forall k\geq 1.

(50)

We will show that this forces
u
to be the zero function, establishing the contradiction.

Since
u\in L^{2}([0,1])\subset L^{1}([0,1])
, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (see, e.g.,
12
) implies that almost every point in
(0,1)
is a Lebesgue point of
u
.
Let
t_{0}\in(0,1)
be such a Lebesgue point. By condition (ii), there exists a subsequence
\{k_{n}\}_{n=1}^{+\infty}
with
c_{k_{n}}\to t_{0}
and
\sigma_{k_{n}}\to 0
.
Denote
g_{n}(t)=g_{k_{n}}(t)
,
c_{n}=c_{k_{n}}
, and
\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{k_{n}}
, and define the normalized kernels:

F_{n}(t)=\frac{g_{n}(t)}{D_{n}},\quad\text{where }D_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{n}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(51)

These satisfy
F_{n}(t)\geq 0
and
\int_{0}^{1}F_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t=1
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

Using the change of variables
v=(t-c_{n})/\sigma_{n}
, we analyze
D_{n}
:

\displaystyle D_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\phi\left(\frac{|t-c_{n}|}{\sigma_{n}}\right)\mathrm{d}t=\sigma_{n}\int_{-c_{n}/\sigma_{n}}^{(1-c_{n})/\sigma_{n}}\phi(|v|)dv.

(52)

Since
c_{n}\to t_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\sigma_{n}\to 0
, we have:
-\frac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\to-\infty,
and
\frac{1-c_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\to+\infty.

Therefore,

\frac{D_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}=\int_{-c_{n}/\sigma_{n}}^{(1-c_{n})/\sigma_{n}}\phi(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v\triangleq C_{\phi}.

(53)

By condition (i),
C_{\phi}\in(0,\infty)
, and hence
D_{n}>0
for sufficiently large
n
.

We now show that the mass of
F_{n}
concentrates at the interior point
t_{0}
.
Let
0<\delta<\min\{t_{0},1-t_{0}\}
. Since
c_{n}\to t_{0}
, for all sufficiently large
n
we have
|c_{n}-t_{0}|<\delta/2
; then in this case, whenever
|t-t_{0}|\geq\delta
,

\displaystyle|t-c_{n}|

\displaystyle\geq|t-t_{0}|-|c_{n}-t_{0}|\geq\delta/2.

(54)

Thus, for all sufficiently large
n
, the tail mass can be bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\int_{\{t\in[0,1]:|t-t_{0}|\geq\delta\}}F_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{D_{n}}\int_{\{t\in[0,1]:|t-c_{n}|\geq\delta/2\}}\phi\left(\frac{|t-c_{n}|}{\sigma_{n}}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{n}}{D_{n}}\int_{\{v:c_{n}+\sigma_{n}v\in[0,1],|v|\geq\delta/(2\sigma_{n})\}}\phi(|v|)dv

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{n}}{D_{n}}\int_{|v|\geq\delta/(2\sigma_{n})}\phi(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v.

(55)

Since
\frac{D_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\to C_{\phi}\in(0,\infty)
(
Eq.

53
), the sequence
\frac{\sigma_{n}}{D_{n}}
is bounded for all sufficiently large
n
. Therefore,

\int_{\{t\in[0,1]:|t-t_{0}|\geq\delta\}}F_{n}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(57)

Thus,
\{F_{n}\}
is a sequence of nonnegative normalized kernels on
[0,1]
whose mass concentrates at
t_{0}
. Hence it forms an approximate identity on
[0,1]
centered at
t_{0}
. Since
t_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
u\in L^{1}([0,1])
, the standard approximate identity argument yields

\int_{0}^{1}u(t)F_{n}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}u(t_{0}).

(58)

Contradiction:

By the orthogonality condition
Eq.

50
and the definition of
F_{n}
:

\int_{0}^{1}u(t)F_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{D_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}u(t)g_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t=0,\quad\forall n.

(59)

Combining with
Eq.

58
gives
u(t_{0})=0
.

Since
t_{0}\in(0,1)
was an arbitrary Lebesgue point of
u
, we obtain
u(t_{0})=0
at every Lebesgue point of
u
. Since almost every point in
(0,1)
is a Lebesgue point of
u
, it follows that
u(t)=0
a.e. on
[0,1]
. Hence
u=0
in
L^{2}([0,1])=\mathcal{H}_{t}
, contradicting the assumption that
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{t}}>0
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{span}\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{t}
. This finishes the proof.

∎

Appendix B
Analytic Formulas for RBF Kernels

This section provides a summary of the RBF generating functions,
\phi(r)
, used and referenced in this work. All kernels listed below satisfy the conditions of our approximation framework. Their respective closed-form integrals and derivatives are detailed in the subsequent sections.

B.1
Matérn RBFs

The Matérn family of RBFs is widely used in machine learning, particularly in Gaussian processes, as their smoothness is controlled by a parameter
\nu
. We consider the common case where
\nu=3/2
, which corresponds to a once-differentiable function. The generating function is strictly positive definite and is given by
\phi(r)=(1+\sqrt{3}r)\exp(-\sqrt{3}r)
.
The basis functions, which are in the Sobolev space
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})
, are:

g_{k}(t)=\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right).

(77)

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Integral.

The integral
\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t
is computed by splitting the integral at the center
c_{k}
due to the absolute value. The indefinite integral of the generating function is
\int\phi(r)\mathrm{d}r=-re^{-\sqrt{3}r}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}e^{-\sqrt{3}r}
. Evaluating this over the respective intervals yields the final closed form.

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Derivative.

The derivative of the generating function is
\phi^{\prime}(r)=-3r\exp(-\sqrt{3}r)
. Applying the chain rule, we find the derivative:

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(t-c_{k})}{\sigma_{k}}

\displaystyle=-3\frac{|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\cdot\frac{t-c_{k}}{|t-c_{k}|}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3(t-c_{k})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}|t-c_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}}\right).

B.2
Gaussian RBFs

The Gaussian RBF is defined by the generating function
\phi(r)=\exp(-r^{2})
. The basis functions are therefore given by:

g_{k}(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{|t-c_{k}|^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right),

(78)

where
c_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}>0
are the center and shape parameters of
g_{k}
.

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Integral.

The integral
\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t
is calculated as follows. We use the substitution
u=(t-c_{k})/\sigma_{k}
, which implies
\mathrm{d}t=\sigma_{k}\mathrm{d}u
.

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left(-\frac{|t-c_{k}|^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}t=\sigma_{k}\int_{-c_{k}/\sigma_{k}}^{(1-c_{k})/\sigma_{k}}\exp(-u^{2})\mathrm{d}u.

This integral can be expressed using the error function,
\mathrm{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}\exp(-x^{2})\mathrm{d}x
. Since
\int_{a}^{b}\exp(-u^{2})\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}(\mathrm{erf}(b)-\mathrm{erf}(a))
, we have:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{1-c_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)-\mathrm{erf}\left(-\frac{c_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\right]=\frac{\sigma_{k}\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{1-c_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{c_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\right],

where the last step uses the property
\mathrm{erf}(-z)=-\mathrm{erf}(z)
.

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Derivative.

The derivative
g_{k}^{\prime}(t)
is found by applying the chain rule:

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp\left(-\frac{(t-c_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{(t-c_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(-\frac{(t-c_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{(t-c_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\cdot\left(-\frac{2(t-c_{k})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right).

B.3
Inverse Multiquadric RBFs

The Inverse Multiquadric (IMQ) RBF is defined by the generating function
\phi(r)=(r^{2}+1)^{-1/2}
. The basis functions are therefore given by:

g_{k}(t)=\left(\frac{(t-c_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}+1\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{k}}{\sqrt{(t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}.

(79)

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Integral.

The temporal integral
\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t
is calculated using the standard integral for the inverse hyperbolic sine function. We use the substitution
u=t-c_{k}
, which implies
\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}u
.

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{\sqrt{(t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\mathrm{d}t=\sigma_{k}\int_{-c_{k}}^{1-c_{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\mathrm{d}u.

The integral of
1/\sqrt{u^{2}+a^{2}}
is
\ln(u+\sqrt{u^{2}+a^{2}})
. Applying this, we get:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\left[\ln\left(u+\sqrt{u^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]_{-c_{k}}^{1-c_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\left(\ln\left((1-c_{k})+\sqrt{(1-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)-\ln\left(-c_{k}+\sqrt{c_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\ln\left(\frac{(1-c_{k})+\sqrt{(1-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}{-c_{k}+\sqrt{c_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\right).

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Derivative.

The derivative
g_{k}^{\prime}(t)
is found by applying the chain rule to
g_{k}(t)=\sigma_{k}\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}
:

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{-3/2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{-3/2}\cdot 2(t-c_{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma_{k}(t-c_{k})}{\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}.

B.4
Rational Quadratic RBFs

The Rational Quadratic (RQ) kernel can be viewed as an infinite sum of Gaussian kernels of different scales. This property makes it a robust choice, capable of modeling data at multiple scales. It is strictly positive definite, and its generating function is
\phi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1}
.
The basis functions are therefore given by:

g_{k}(t)=\left(1+\frac{(t-c_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{(t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}.

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Integral.

The temporal integral
\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}t
is calculated using the standard integral for the arctangent function. We use the substitution
u=t-c_{k}
, which implies
\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}u
.

\bar{g}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{(t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t=\sigma_{k}^{2}\int_{-c_{k}}^{1-c_{k}}\frac{1}{u^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u.

The integral of
1/(u^{2}+a^{2})
is
\frac{1}{a}\arctan(\frac{u}{a})
. Applying this, we get:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{k}=\sigma_{k}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\arctan\left(\frac{u}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\right]_{-c_{k}}^{1-c_{k}}=\sigma_{k}\left(\arctan\left(\frac{1-c_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)+\arctan\left(\frac{c_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)\right),

where the last step uses the property
\arctan(-z)=-\arctan(z)
.

Closed-Form Expression for the Temporal Derivative.

The derivative
g_{k}^{\prime}(t)
is found by applying the chain rule to
g_{k}(t)=\sigma_{k}^{2}\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{-1}
:

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\prime}(t)=\sigma_{k}^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{-1}=-\frac{2\sigma_{k}^{2}(t-c_{k})}{\left((t-c_{k})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}.
