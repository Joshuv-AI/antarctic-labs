Hydrogen-assisted nannochloropsis sp. microalgae biodiesel to operate cleaner diesel engines
Authors: Karthikeyan Sathasivam, Ilhami Colak, J Arunprasad, S Rajkumar, Muthuraman Subbiah, Rabaa Mzahim Mizher, V Thirusuthan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe need to find sustainable energy sources has taken the center stage in the light of the growing energy global requirements. The renewable energy sources have also taken much interest in biofuels, as they promise to add renewable and environmentally benign sources of energy. The feedstock used in biodiesel is usually a wide range of inexpensive feedstock. In the current research, a new synthesis method using a feedstock with a green microalga, namely, Nannochloropsis sp. is defined. According to the authors, bio-diesel blends based on this microalgal source would help in producing less toxic fuels, increase the functionality, and at the same time, minimize emissions. The present paper examines production of biodiesel using Nannochloropsis sp. a microalga with high calorific value thus qualifying as a feedstock. After one hour of ultrasonication at 500 Hz frequency, the recovery of bio-oil was around 720 mL. Afterwards, the resulting bio-oil was undergone to a single-pot transesterification with sodium hydroxide as the catalyst in methanol to extract the bio-oil with an extraction efficiency of 92%. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy were used to determine physicochemical properties of the resultant biodiesel ensuring it was suitable as a fuel. The combustion stability of the resulting B20 + 7.5H2 blend was better than that of the diesel fuel, with peak in-cylinder pressure of 47 bar and a net heat release rate of 20.51 J/°CA compared to 46.5 bar and 19.98 J/°CA for diesel. The emission analysis showed that B20 resulted in a decrease in smoke (from 62 to 44.8%) and an increase in CO (from 0.012 to 0.3%) and hydrogen supplementation (2.5–7.5 L m−1) further improved combustion. Notably, B20 + 7.5H2 reduced CO₂ (from 9.3 to 8.5%), while NOx remained within 1763–1694 ppm. These results demonstrate that hydrogen-enriched algae biodiesel blends can improve engine efficiency and reduce harmful emissions, providing a viable, cleaner alternative for diesel engines.Keywords: Nannochloropsis sp., Biodiesel, Hydrogen, Combustion, EmissionSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Materials and methods

The time-honored microalgae can be used as an alternative to fossil fuels, which have their economic benefits and greenhouse effect reduction. These are organisms that survive in high photic conditions. In addition to the lipid development, they help in the sequestration of carbon and the formation of oxygen18. It is possible to control the rise and fall of the growth of the microalgae as well as the biochemical structure of a microalgae under a variety of physico-chemical conditions. There are industrial, pharmaceutical and energetic benefits achievable through interspecies variations as a result of cultivating these organisms over time gradients. The lipid storage in the Nannochloropsis sp. is quite more evident and therefore this species is highly applicable in the production of biodiesel21. The fact that the cell wall of the organism is not very strong in the case of the organism in the genus Nannochloropsis sp. makes it difficult to deal with the application of conventional laboratory methods to study organisms with large cell wall thus making it difficult to study intracellular processes. Nannochloropsis sp. is a halophilic, saline-adaptive and unicellular microalga with a greenish-orange coloration. It is a member of the family Dunaliellaceae and the order Chlamydomonadales, the family, class, Chlorophyceae, and division Chlorophyta. The alga has a high biotechnological and nutritional potential as it has a high biomass production, high carotenoid and lipid content and is also resistant to high salinity17. The process of converting traditional feedstocks into biodiesel has been revealed not so beneficial in comparison to utilizing a relatively cheap, yet environmentally friendly, but not so cost-effective, feedstock like or Nannochloropsis sp. The organism also has several environmental and economic advantages such as a better lipid profile, the ability to survive in sub-optimal conditions of growth, low resource needs, and co-products.

Transesterification

The extraction of bio-oil was done through ultrasonic to extract lipid in biomass that had been collected in the form of Nannochloropsis sp. The resultant of the process was an algal oil fraction. In order to optimize the extraction level, 100 g of Nannochloropsis sp. biomass were mixed with 30 mL of methanol, double-distilled water, and chloroform in the following volumes; 1:0.7:2. The ultrasonication frequency of 60 min at which the oil had been liberated was allowed to rise and break up the cell membranes22. The obtained sediment and the recovered oil were moved into a beaker, where 100 mL of methanol was introduced to mix with them. The filter paper was used to filter the algal oil using Whatman filter paper to take out particulate residues in the algal oil. Then 60 mL of acetone was added and the mixture was heated to remove any remaining moisture. Bio-oil of Nannochloropsis sp. yielded a total of 650 mL (Fig. 1). The first analytical test revealed that bio-oil had a low amount of free fatty acids. The bio-oil which was obtained after growing the Nannochloropsis sp. was then subjected to base-catalysed transesterification in the presence of methanol and sodium hydroxide to give the respective mono-alkyl methyl esters. The choice of transesterification conditions was informed by literature that provided all the optimum parameters including a methanol to bio-oil molar ratio of 1:8, catalyst concentration of less than 0.6wt% NaOH, reaction temperature of 50° C, and the residence time of 180 min. A second vessel of 1.2 g of sodium hydroxide pellets was then mixed with 100 mL of methanol and stirred at 450 rpm during 20 min. The necessary molar ratio of 0.4 was measured and 40 mL of sodium methoxide solution was added to 120 mL of algal oil, thus keeping the ratio of methanol and bio-oil at 1:8. The mixture was then moved to a conical flask having a flat bottom and transesterification was initiated by heating the mixture to 50 °C during 180 min. After the reaction was complete, it was transferred into a separating funnel and left to stand 180 min to cool. A co-precipitation of glycerol at the bottom and algal biodiesel in the top layer was observed during the separation of the phases and a circular heterogeneity was achieved23. The overall yield of the transesterification of the bio-oil of Nannochloropsis sp. was 92%.

Results and discussion

Optimization of bio-diesel conversion

The oil obtained using the algae, Nannochloropsis sp. was refined to biodiesel by transesterifying it with a base catalyst, as done in the previous section. The conversion process has been supported by the optimization of the parameters used in the transesterification. Factors that affected the conversion involved the type of catalysts, the ratio of methanol to oil, the temperature and period of time used to carry out the conversion28. The following experimental variations were introduced during the optimization of conversion by the adjustment of these parameters. This experiment analysis used the following methanol to oil ratios that are usually used in the preparation of biodiesel; 1:7, 1:6, and 1:5. The weight concentration on catalysts was 0.5%, 1.0% and 1.5%. The temperatures that were treated included 50 °C, 60 °C, and 70 °C whereas the reaction time taken was 1 h, 2 h, and 3 h. The parameters investigated are operating ones and they are summarized in Table 1. The highest biodiesel concentration was achieved at a temperature of 60 °C, 1:7 weight concentration of the methanol-to-oil mixture, and a time period of 3 h to produce biodiesel in an optimum state of 90.02%. With a similar set up having a ratio of 1:6 and using 1.5 wt. catalyst, 60 °C temperature and a reaction time of 2 h, the yield was 89.91 °C. The yield of biodiesel was recorded to be 88.81% when using the following conditions: a 1:6 ratio, 1.5% wt. catalyst, temperature of 50 °C, and a duration of 2 h.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS)

The marine alga sp. that was used to extract the bio-oil was Nannochloropsis sp. and thereafter the bio-oil was subjected to the single-stage transesterification process using sodium hydroxide and methanol to extract biodiesel29. The resulting biodiesel was then subjected to an esterification test in order both to determine the presence of a wide range of FAMEs and to evaluate the efficiency of the transesterification reaction; the mixture was then found to be subjected to a GC 9000-MS analysis. The mass chromatogram demonstrated the presence of nine different FAMEs whose retention times decreased between 19.90 and 30.40 min. The FAMEs were structurally elucidated using the analysis of the patterns of fragmentation. All the spectra repeatedly displayed a base peak at m/z 74, which is an indication of a McCafferty rearrangement. The most common way of fragmentation was the loss of the methoxy group through B-cleavage as shown in Fig. 4. The presence of multiple peaks in other spectra was due to ejection of the methoxy groups and the reposition of carbon and hydrogen atoms during the transesterification reaction (Fig. 3). These observations were structural correspondence of proton movement and rearrangement of the carbonyl moiety in the organization of the protons, especially in 14,17-octadecadienoic acid methyl ester that had a retention time of 22.28 min. Combustion characteristics

In-cylinder pressure

The in-cylinder pressure represents a critical performance parameter used to obtain and explain the combustion behavior of various fuel blends in a single cylinder, four-stroke diesel engine. The combustion characteristics are of interest to mixtures of diesel and algae biodiesel and algae biodiesel and hydrogen, through the in-cylinder pressure profile. The current experiment involved the comparison of full-spec diesel (100%) and a mixture of 20% and 80% of algae biodiesel and diesel at different flow rates of 2.5 L m−1, 5.0 L m−1, and 7.5 L m−1 with and without hydrogen32. The state of high in-cylinder pressure is caused by the high energy density and autoignition tendencies of diesel fuel. The combustion is characterized by a rapid increase in pressure at the start of the power stroke followed by a stabilization that is representative of an effective conversion of energy. The highest pressure was measured at 46.5 bar at 8 degrees after top dead center in the D100 mixture. The biodiesel mixes of algae gave slightly lower peak in-cylinder pressure, which can be explained by the low calorific value of this type of algae in comparison to traditional diesel33. The increased oxygen concentration in biodiesel is also probable to explain the higher amount of combustion as shown by the peak in-cylinder pressure of 47 bar as shown in Fig. 7. The diesel–biodiesel blend was enriched with hydrogen at a lower volumetric flow rate of 2.5 L min−1, a process that triggers propagation of the flames and in turn speeds up the buildup of cylinder pressure by reducing the ignition delay. The mixture was highly homogeneous and thus the process started combusting very fast, probably because the diffusivity of hydrogen was so high, and its activation energy was low. The peak pressures were kept within a reasonable range as no more than a limited amount of hydrogen was added; the highest pressure recorded was 47 bar on the eighth cycle, which matched the point of top dead center (TDC). The diesel algal biodiesel mixture pressure shot up to 46 bar at an induction rate of 5 L min−1 and 47 bar at 7.5 L min−1. It increases the rate of pressure increase, which results in the boosting of the fuel oxidation rate, therefore, hydrogen can be regarded as carbon neutral when generated out of algae21.

Rate of pressure rise

The pressure rate is a very important parameter that dictates the behavior of the combustion of a four-stroke single-cylinder diesel engine. In circumstances of diesel/ algae biodiesel mixtures, i.e. 100% petroleum diesel and 80:20 mix of diesel and algae biodiesel, the combination kinetics in the combustion are in the form of certain rates of a pressure increase34. The rate of increase of pressure-rate with height is relatively steep with conventional diesel fuel, which can be explained by its high energy density and due to the in-compression rate of auto-ignition of the fuel. The speed of pressure growth in diesel-only combustion is so dominated by the lift-off of the rapid pressure rise during the early phases of premixed combustion. The pressure-rate increment is lower in case the algae biodiesel is mixed with diesel compared to the case of pure diesel. This was determined by high oxygen level in biodiesel and a low calorific value which makes the combustion process more regulated35. This blending, in turn, leads to a smoother increase in pressure, in turn, enhancing combustion stability; the respective value is 1.74 bar/°CA at 3° centigrade post TDC. The addition of hydrogen to the diesel-algae biodiesel mixture at a rate of 2.5 L min−1 increases the rate of pressure, which is attributed to the high flame speed and low ignition energy of hydrogen as shown in Fig. 8. The presence of hydrogen stimulates ignition, which enhances velocity of push up of pressure. The values of pressure-rate were determined to be 1.74 bar/°CA at 2 °C after TDC, 1.64 bar/°CA at 5 L min−1 after TDC and 1.74 bar/°CA at 7.5 L min−1after TDC.

Net heat release rate

The heat release rate (HRR) inside a single-cylinder engine is used to gauge the energy released during combustion, which is very important in measuring the engine efficiency, performance, and emission. They tested several fuel blends such as 100% diesel, an 80% not to mention 20% diesel / algae biodiesel mixture and the latter mixture with different levels of Hydrogen enrichment of 2.5, 5 and 7.5 L min −1. These differences affected ignition features, flame spread and efficiency of combustion, thus bringing variability in the HRR36. The maximum HRR with diesel fuel is also generally very large: this can be explained by the high density of its energy and also since some well-defined premixed period of combustion exists. When diesel fuel burns, it expands energy at a fast rate generating a somewhat sharp HRR profile. As of a result, combustion of diesel is characteristically more thermal-efficient and releases more heat. The speedy emission of the heat is typical of the traditional diesel burns, most of the heat being released as part of the premixed period. In the case of D100 albeit, cooling with 19.98 J/°CA heat release at 1 degree post TDC was measured. The combination of algae-derived biodiesel and diesel also provided more oxygen in the mixture, thus allowing fuller combustion and resulting in a softer heat-release curve. The associated burning process was also very easy, with a slow development of HRR which guaranteed full oxidation of the fuel. The heat release attained 20.91 J/°CA at 1 degree after TDC (Fig. 9). Hydrogen addition at 2.5 L min−1 on hydrogen-diesel differentiation enhanced the HRR significantly than diesel fuel, a phenomenon that could be explained by the fact that hydrogen has a low ignition energy demand and a high flame speed which combined to allow a higher rate of combustion to incline towards a faster beginning of combustion37. The corresponding HRR was, therefore, enriched earlier in the combustion cycle, with the initial phase of heat-release being higher when compared to diesel alone. The Hydrogen serves as a combustion accelerator, improving the efficiency on combustion, and thermal production, resulting in a greater extent of full combustion and maximum HRR at relatively high levels. The HRR at these concentrations of hydrogen was also kept at manageable levels without any significant pressure peaks forming as 20.51 J/◦CA of 2.5 L min−1, 19.88 J/◦CA of 5 L min−1 and 20.49 J/◦CA of 7.5 L min−1 as 20.51 J/◦CA at 1 degree after TDC. The comparison between various fuel blends reveals that despite the fact that the incorporation of algae biodiesel in the diesel providing a constant baseline further with a slightly higher peak HRR, the addition of hydrogen considerably changes combustion by altering the profile of the HRR.

Cumulative heat release rate

A four-stroke diesel single-cylinder engine with a cumulative heat release rate cumulative heat release rate. It is a summation of the total energy dissipated during a complete combustion cycle and it is a crucial measure of the completeness of combustion and the energy utilization of a given fuel mixture. Pure diesel fuel has a high CHRR most of the time, which is due to high calorific value and effective utilization of energy in combustion. The rapid rise in cumulative heat release characterizes combustion especially in the stage of the premixed burn which constitutes the takeoff the stage of diffusion-burn38. Comparison of the fuel blends of other fuel blends with the diesel CHRR could hence be performed on baseline. The blends of 20% algae bio-diesel in diesel generally show a small rise in CHRR compared to diesel itself, due to the low energy density of biodiesel. However, the oxygen content per mole of biodiesel is intrinsic, which would enhance complete combustion, consuming to some extent the impact of its lower energy density. The buildup of CHRR with this mixture is slower than with 100 per cent diesel; there is therefore only slight incongruities in the energy release per cycle. This advantage is enhanced by 2.5 L min−1 hydrogen, which increases a steep front of the CHRR curve. This is due to the fact that hydrogen is able to increase the rate of ignition and complete oxidation of the fuel. This leads to cumulative energy release as there is a reduction in the combustion incompleteness leading to a high CHRR as compared to the pure diesel–biodiesel mix. The hydrogen is added which allows an increased percentage to be emitted in a shorter period of time in combustion. Since the combustion ends faster, sharp bursts of extreme temperatures are counteracted, resulting in better efficiency in combustion. The same increase was seen at 5 L min−1 and 7.5 L min−1 of hydrogen concentration. The high CHRR at such hydrogen levels requires extremely sensitive adjustment of engine settings in order to avoid discontinuous combustion and mechanically harmful peak pressure and temperature regimes (Fig. 10).

Mean gas temperature

The MGT is one such performance metric and the most significant and defines the quality of combustion and the level of emissions as well as the level of the mechanical stresses in the parts of the engine. Changes in the fuel mixture add fluctuation in MGT that can be explained by the differences in the combustion properties and the process of heat release39. The level of MGT is normally high with the use of pure diesel fuel (D100) because diesel has got a high consistency of energy and because of the high quality of its combustion. Diesel burning is one in which high peak temperatures are observed with the premixed period enhancing the release of energy. Even though high MGTs have the power to increase thermal efficiency, they simultaneously encourage the use of NOx emission by leaving hot spots in the combustion chamber. Homogeneous diesel combustion can be used to give relatively stable values of MGT, which can be used as a metric against which the ratios of alternative fuels can be measured. Addition of 20% algae-based biodiesel to diesel will cause some slight change in the MGT since the oxygen content of the biodiesel will enable further complete combustion to a reduced amount of residual carbon; such change is expected to happen slightly lower than the maximum temperature. MGT in single cycle four stroke diesel engines forms one of the main pointers to combustion efficiency, which in turn has a direct impact on emissions and the thermal loads that are exquisitely caused to engine components40. The control of MGT in different percentages of fuel is not quite easy, due to the presence of different combustion properties and heat-discharge processes. The configurations of the high MGTs utilizing D100 are associated with the high energy content and clean burning nature of diesel (Fig. 11). The high rate of releasing energy of the mixture combustion phase creates a high peak temperature in the diesel burning. High MGTs enhance the efficiency of thermal but also cause the emission of high NOx as a result of the high temperature zones of the cylinder. Compared to other fuels mixes, pure diesel combustion has relatively constant MGTs, and creates a basis of meaning by which to compare these terms (Fig. 11). MGT variation: as a rule, a 20% mixture of diesel and biodiesel is characterized by a moderate effect on MGT explained by the presence of oxygen in this compound, which is beneficial to the completeness of combustion, to the reduction of the amount of residual carbon and the slight decline in the maximal temperature.

Emission characteristics

Unburned hydrocarbon

The emission of UBHC emissions occurring in one cylinder of a diesel engine could be used as a measure of the rate of combustion and fuel burn-out efficiency. Hydrogen injection rates of 2.5, 5 and 7.5 L min−1 were utilized in the determination of trends of emissions and in the process of developing strategies on reducing the emissions (Fig. 12). The naturally long chains of carbon in diesel engines and the involved complicated combustion processes generally produce high levels of UBHC. The formation of UBHC is related to the presence of incomplete combustion taking place in either the low-temperature or fuel-rich locations in the cylinder41. Despite the fact that due to the high level of energy density diesel starts burning readily and efficiently, the formation of the residual UBHC is still possible by quenching on the cylinder walls and in the crevices. In order to make a comparative evaluation of the emission of UBHC with various fuel blends, it is assumed that diesel is used as the reference case. It is expected that incorporation of 20% of algae-based biodiesel to diesel will ensure a reduced level of UBHC emissions since the inherent oxygen percentage will enhance high level of combustion and thus lower the number of unburnt hydrocarbons in the mixture42. Biodiesel availability will reduce the tendency of constituting fuel-rich mixtures, hence reducing UBHC. Besides, the lower ignition delay of biodiesel promotes the ratio of full combustion, which stifles the formation of UBHC further. In the test conditions, the measurements of the UBHC of both D100 (pure diesel) and B20 were 50 ppm respectively at a fuel density of 100 kg/m3. The process of introducing UBHC emissions by adding hydrogen flow at 2.5 L min−1 (alongside diesel–biodiesel blend) is anticipated to reduce the diesel emissions. High levels of hydrogen increase the rate of flame spread, as well as the lower the energy required to start the combustion, which in turn facilitates fast ignition and lowers fuel enriched low temperature regions that naturally produce UBHC. Hydrogen also enhances the development of homogeneous combustion which results in a significant decrease of the UBHC level in the combustion chamber43. In B20 mixture, the values of UBHC were measured, and the observed average values were 52, 53 and 54 ppm at 2.5, 5 and 7.5 L min−1 flow rates of hydrogen respectively. Even though such large rates of hydrogen flux considerably reduce UBHC, caution is necessary to prevent untenable effects, including knock or thermal stress related to improperly-timed combustion or unfavorable ratios of the mixtures. This was demonstrated to be caused by increased overall combustion efficiency that was caused by hydrogen which inhibits the number of unburned hydrocarbons and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and as such engine wear was alleviated.

Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide (CO) is the main measure of the efficiency of combustion and the level of oxidation of the fuel. The formation of CO is normally happening in half burned areas when oxygen mixing with fuel is inadequate or ineffective. CO emissions during the three fuel conditions, i.e. 100 per cent diesel, diesel-algae biodiesel and mixtures of the aforementioned fuel ratios with different rates of hydrogen flow, differed between each of them because of their different combustion properties and oxidation abilities (Fig. 13). The pure diesel is likely to result in the moderate amounts of CO due to large amounts of unburned hydrocarbons. Maximum oxidation in diesel combustion web needs adequate pressure and temperature, nevertheless, at extreme temperatures and pressure, low temperature levels often prevail in particular areas to furnish CO. A 20% algae bio-diesel fraction reduces the CO emission since the natural oxygen present in it is naturally increased with the aim of ensuring further combustion and conversion of carbon into CO2. Besides, biodiesel has the propensity of stifling fuel enriched areas through the augmentation of fuel–air blend, thus reducing CO formation even more when compared to unsaturated diesel22. The CO level in B20 blend was 0.012 ppm and 0.013 ppm respectively. Hydrogen supplementation in the diesel–biodiesel mixture lowers the ignition energy requirements and increases flame speed and improves combustion effectiveness, as well as the dynamics of CO emissions. A slight rise in CO concentration can be expected when the hydrogen flow rates are high because of the reduced time to be spent on the oxidation process; the measured CO levels were 0.2, 0.25 and 0.3% in 2.5, 5 and 7.5 L min−1 decrease in oxidation respectively.

Carbon dioxide

Single-cylinder based CO2 emissions give an indirect measurement of the fuel oxidation performance. Under fully combusted states, the entire amount of carbon is changed to CO2 instead of CO. Commercial comparison of CO2 emissions between pure diesel, diesel-algae biodiesel, and mixed-blends under different rates of hydrogen flow depicts relative performance and environmental advantage of every fuel plan31. This can be attributed to the fact that the mass of CO2 that is emitted by diesel is more due to its richness in carbon and energy density supporting full oxidation to CO2 as the key combustion product. The algae biodiesel mixture of 20% has a low value of CO2 emission than the use of pure sunflower diesel, which is explained by the reduced carbon rate (Fig. 14). Being an oxygenated fuel, algae biodiesel works as an efficient burner with a moderate impact on cutting CO2. At full load, the CO2 emissions obtained were 8.9% with straight diesel and 9.3% with diesel-algae biodiesel mix. The CO2 generation per kilogram me of fuel combusted is decreased with the introduction of a hydrogen injection rate of 2.5 L min−1 (diesel)-biodiesel mixture. The fact that hydrogen is free of carbon, therefore it provides energy without increasing CO2 emission and hence reduces the overall energy output to CO2 emission ratio. Hydrogen is very reactive making it undergo thorough combustion and reduces the formation of CO. The observed results of 8.83%, 8.5%, and 8.8% of CO gas emission at 1, 2, and 4 min of hydrogen offering on diesel and biodiesel blends, shows that the addition of hydrogen to diesel and biodiesel in blends is able to inhibit the formation of CO2 gases without the deleterious effects on the performance of combustion. However, there is still need to conduct a thorough evaluation of the nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions and thermal impacts of high-hydrogen concentration.

Oxides of nitrogen

The main ecological issue related to NOx discharging of diesel engines is an unfavorable effect of NOx on the air quality and human health well-established in the literature. The formation of NOx mainly happens when nitrogen and oxygen are present and combusted in high-temperature condition in a cylinder of the engine. The emissions of NOx were measured in five fuel formulations, (i) pure diesel, (ii) mixture of diesel and algae biodiesel, (iii) a collection of blends with different amount of hydrogen added to it12. The aim was to clarify how the fuel structure and enrichment of hydrogen affected the NOx formation as the NOx levels had the tendency to increase during the diesel combustion. Another attribute of this combustion is the high rate of release and high temperatures. As a result, efficient diesel engines can work even above the temperatures that are reached by considerable production of baseline NOx. The major responsibility of NOx is also seen in the situation with pure diesel combustion where the peak activities of NOx occur during the premixed combustion phase where the high-energy emitted by the combustion process maintain the primary NOx-forming processes. Replacement of diesel with 20 per cent of algae biodiesel tends to decrease NOx emissions by a few percent as compared to pure diesel. High oxygen content of the biodiesel increases burning and decreases the amount of soot. Nevertheless, there are no significant rises in the combustion temperatures. The increased oxygen incorporated in the algae biodiesel increases the further oxidation at slightly lower temperatures; this leads to a small adjustment in the emission of NOx. The impact of biodiesel on NOx, however, is not entirely predictable, it depends on conditions of certain combustion and in some cases, high local oxygen concentration can increase the temperatures29. In high-load conditions, pure diesel gave 1745 ppm NOx, and the diesel-algal in 1763 ppm. Increments in the NOx emissions were induced by hydrogen injections of 2.5 L m−1, 5 L m−1 and 7.5 L m−1 on the mix of diesel–biodiesel (Fig. 15). The rate of flame propagation of the hydrogen increases and prolongs the combustion which makes the peak temperatures high and thus increases the formation of the NOx; the NOx concentration of 2.5 L m−1, 5 L m−1 and 7.5 L m−1 added hydrogen to the mixture was measured as 1768 ppm, 1798 ppm and 1694 ppm respectively (Fig. 15).

Smoke

Smoke emission mainly comes when fuel is not burned completely, thus leading to the production of PM. In this study, the emission rates of smoke on the different fuels, both 100% diesel, and combinations of diesel and algae biodiesel, with varying rates of hydrogen additions are reported, displaying the effect of the composition of fuels and the levels of hydrogen on the levels of smoke (Fig. 16). The compound nature of the hydrocarbon architecture, high fraction of aromatic, and propensity to produce soot in the areas with lots of diesel fuel make the emission of smoke by the diesel fuel relatively high, hence increased restriction of the EPA emission limits. Under high load condition that only involves operation with diesel, local fuel-enriched pockets may occur because of the insufficiency of air–fuel mixture leading to production of large amount of smoke18. Long burning time and high amount of carbon in diesel fuel are also a factor to base smoke emissions. The addition of algae biodiesel to the diesel fuel is likely to decrease the level of smoke emissions. The increased oxygen percentage increases the efficiency of combustion to minimize the amount of soot’s due to a higher degree of hydrocarbon oxidation. The diesel–biodiesel combination will also encourage fuller combustion and thus fewer particulates will be generated as compared to straight diesel39. The improvement in oxygenation and decadence of aromatic component in biodiesel is synergistic in nature and reduces the amount of smoke and at the same time has a strong combustion performance. The experiment resulted in 62% smoke emission of straight diesel, compared to 49% in diesel-algal biodiesel blend. The diesel–biodiesel can be further reduced to hydrogen to reduce the amount of smoke. The high diffusivity and clean-burning property of hydrogen due to the lack of carbon do not allow the development of soot and therefore less production of direct particulates. The addition of hydrogen helps in facilitating a more homogenous air and fuel mixture and fully combusting the mixture finally reducing the amount of smoke emissions. The percent decreases between the smoke emissions of the observed 2.5 L m−1, 5 L m−1 and 7.5 L m−1 of hydrogen additions were 52.7%, 44.8% and 58.6% of the control diesel–biodiesel blend. When the flow of hydrogen is high, especially at 5 L m−1 and 7.5 L m−1, maximum smoke reduction is obtained which can be explained by properties of hydrogen as a non-soothing gas and a homogeneous environment after which combustion occurs17. Blends made out of diesel, biodiesel and hydrogen also show a positive reaction considering the reduction of smoke; however additional control measures are needed with regards to high levels of hydrogen so as to limit the production of NOx.

Conclusion

The analysis shows that the microalga Nannochloropsis sp. may be used as an affordable and environmentally friendly feedstock for biodiesel production, with a conversion efficiency of 90.02%. The spectroscopy techniques were used to confirm the conversion of the bio -oil fraction to FAME, FTIR and NMR spectroscopy. A single-cylinder diesel engine was used to do the analysis of performance. The burning behavior’s emission, and fuel economy were contrasted with the conventional diesel, algae-based biodiesel as well as the hydrogen-enriched mixtures which gave different data. It has been shown that diesel fuel, due to its large energy density, gave the highest in-cylinder pressures and promoted efficient combustion process, but it also exhibited high concentrations of NOx. On the other hand, bio-diesel made of algae also increased the combustion stability along with a significant binary with UBHC, CO, and soot, but with relatively lower NOx. The hydrogen injection enhanced the behavior of combustion by increasing the rate at which the pressure increased, heat was released and the reduction in the rate of soot formation due to increased oxidation. This can be blamed on the dilute concentration of NOx due to an increment in cell combustion temperatures. A hybrid fuel that includes diesel, algae bio-diesel, and hydrogen will show a high potential to achieve its goal of realizing maximum combustion, and reducing its level of toxic emissions (CO, particulate matter), and this will provide diesel engines with a more environmentally friendly mode of operation. The increase in the NOx levels related to a hydrogen enrichment can be alleviated by optimizing the flow of hydrogen and the additional use of post-treatment technologies. The use of a bio-diesel hydrogen hybrid blend increases the flexibility of the fuel and the security of power compared to the bio-diesel obtained fuels as well as Hydrogen obtained fuels. Besides, the mixture has also better utilization efficiency, evidenced by less CO, HC, and particulate emissions than undiluted biodiesel, though with excess NOx because of higher combustion temperatures. The adoption of this hybrid approach poses significant challenges especially over hydrogen networking, storage facilities as well as increased cost of operation. The rapid flow of hydrogen would also require recalibration of the engines, which would place a practical limitation on its utilization. Principal results reestablish the claim that a base-catalyzed transesterification of Nannochloropsis sp. provides a bio-oil yield of 92% and a biodiesel yield of 90.02% with an argument that Nannochloropsis sp. is a very attractive biofuel feedstock. The biodiesel produced by algae has a high possibility of reducing the CO, UBHC and soot emissions making it a cleaner substitute to the regular diesel. The low increment of NOx is associated with elevated combustion temperatures. Combustion properties: The in-cylinder pressure was higher, and the combustion cycle was shorter as compared to diesel; the quality of combustion with biodiesel made of algae was better and hence the decrease in emissions. A small increase in NOx was realized though fuel efficiency and soot were minimized modestly. Future activities must be done on the reduction of NOx, expansion of large-scale cultivation, engine testing, analysis of fuel blend durability and stability, and incorporation of renewable inputs into the energy mixture, and scale-up of the process. A way forward to more sustainable and cleaner biodiesel propulsion engines is found in the development of hydrogen-based biodiesel blends as is depicted in this study leading to additional innovation in fuel chemistry and combustion engineering.
