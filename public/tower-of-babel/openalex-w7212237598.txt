Abstract
Background: Artificial intelligence [AI] has enhanced contemporary dental practice by providing advanced tools for
diagnosis, treatment planning, and patient management. Objective: This narrative review examines the current
applications of AI in dentistry, evaluates its diagnostic efficacy, identifies challenges for clinical implementation, and
discusses future perspectives. Methods: A narrative literature review was conducted by searching PubMed, Scopus,
Web of Science, SciELO, and LILACS for studies published between 2020 and 2026. Observational studies
randomized clinical trials, systematic reviews, and methodological studies were included. DeCS/MeSH descriptors
were combined using the Boolean operators AND and OR. Study selection was purposive, prioritizing recent
publications with higher methodological quality and scientific relevance. Results: AI demonstrated high accuracy in
detecting dental caries, periapical lesions, periodontal diseases, and other oral conditions. Applications span all dental
specialties, including radiology, endodontics, orthodontics, periodontics, and oral and maxillofacial surgery. Despite
these advancements, significant barriers persist, including ethical concerns regarding patient privacy, the need for
rigorous clinical validation, and integration into existing clinical workflows. AI should be considered a
complementary tool that enhances clinician capabilities rather than a substitute for clinical judgment. The future of
dentistry will involve the integration of explainable AI systems, federated learning for data protection, and digital
education for the dental workforce. Conclusion: AI holds transformative potential to improve access, quality, and
efficiency in dental care, provided it is implemented with methodological rigor, continuous human oversight, and
appropriate ethical considerations.
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence; Dentistry; Machine learning; Dental diagnosis; Deep learning.

1. Introduction
Artificial Intelligence [AI] refers to the capability of machines to execute tasks that traditionally require human
intelligence (Schwendicke et al., 2020). In healthcare, particularly in dentistry, AI has emerged as a transformative technology
with the potential to revolutionize diagnosis, treatment planning, and patient management (Samaranayake et al., 2025; Shan et
al., 2021).
A core subdomain of AI is machine learning [ML], which teaches intrinsic statistical patterns within data to make
predictions on unseen datasets (Schwendicke et al., 2020). Deep learning [DL], a subset of ML, utilizes multilayered
mathematical operations to learn and infer from complex data structures, such as radiographic images (Schwendicke et al.,
20201; Shan et al., 2021). While truly autonomous medical robotic systems remain beyond current reach, the virtual
component of AI, namely software-based algorithms, constitutes the primary application currently used in dentistry (Shan et
al., 2021).
Recently, the evolution of Generative Artificial Intelligence [GAI], particularly through Large Language Models
[LLMs], has significantly expanded the scope of AI applications in dentistry. Unlike traditional algorithms designed for image
classification, these models support activities such as clinical documentation, education, patient communication, and scientific
writing, although they have limitations in data reliability and traceability (Samaranayake et al., 2025).
Dental professionals are increasingly relying on AI technology to assist in diagnosis, clinical decision-making,
treatment planning, and prognostic prediction across ten dental specialties (Bonny et al., 2023). One of the most significant
advantages of AI in dentistry is its ability to analyze large volumes of data rapidly and accurately, providing practitioners with
valuable insights to enhance their decision-making processes (Bonny et al., 2023; Ahmed et al., 2021). Despite the growing
volume of publications, there remains a shortage of reviews that critically integrate image-based AI advancements with
emerging generative AI applications in dental practice.
Therefore, this narrative review aims to examine the current applications of AI in dentistry, evaluate its diagnostic and
therapeutic efficacy, identify challenges for clinical implementation, and discuss the future perspectives of this emerging
technology.

2. Methodology
A study with a qualitative approach (Risemberg et al., 2026a; Pereira et al., 2018) was conducted as an exploratory
study with limited systematization (Risemberg et al., 2026b). A narrative literature review (Ogassawara et al., 2025;
Fernandes, Vieira & Castelhano, 2023) was conducted by searching the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science
databases using the descriptors "Artificial Intelligence", "Dentistry", "Machine Learning", and "Deep Learning", combined via
the Boolean operators AND and OR. Articles published between January 2020 and June 2026 in English, Portuguese, and
Spanish addressing clinical applications of artificial intelligence in dentistry were included.
The study selection was purposive, prioritizing systematic reviews, meta-analyses, clinical validation studies,
consensus documents, and recent high-impact articles relevant to AI applications in dentistry. Screening was conducted by
evaluating titles, abstracts, and subsequently full-text articles. Selected studies were analyzed based on their methodological
design and target population. This strategy allowed for the synthesis of high-impact scientific evidence and clinical relevance
without attempting to exhaustively map the entire available literature.
This study utilized ChatGPT [OpenAI (GPT-5.5)] as a supporting tool for text organization, grammatical review, and
structural suggestions. The AI tool was not used to generate scientific content or interpret data. All scientific content was
reviewed by the author and grounded exclusively in reliable bibliographical references.

3. Results
The search strategy identified many records across the databases consulted. Given the narrative nature of this review,
a purposive selection of studies with the highest methodological relevance and scientific impact was performed, resulting in
the inclusion of 31 articles that form the basis of this synthesis [Table 1]. Thus, the articles represent a qualitative sample of the
primary available evidence rather than a quantitative mapping of total scientific production on the topic. High methodological
heterogeneity was observed among the studies, particularly in the datasets used, validation criteria, algorithm types, and
imaging modalities, which hindered direct comparisons of outcomes.

4. Discussion
Recent literature demonstrates a significant expansion of AI applications across virtually all dental specialties,
consolidating this technology as a promising tool for diagnostic support, therapeutic planning, and clinical monitoring (Cosola
et al., 2025; Shan et al., 2021; Schwendicke et al., 20201). AI encompasses various computational approaches that simulate
human cognitive functions such as learning, pattern recognition, and decision-making (Shan et al., 2021; Ezhov et al., 2021).
Among these approaches, ML stands out as a paradigm in which algorithms are trained on data to identify patterns and
generate predictions without explicit task-specific programming.
A more advanced subset of ML is Deep Learning [DL], which is based on artificial neural networks composed of
multiple processing layers (Samaranayake et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Pitchika et al., 2024). This architecture allows the
algorithm to automatically learn complex patterns from large datasets without requiring manual feature selection, making it
exceptionally efficient for analyzing medical and dental images (Samaranayake et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Shan et al.,
2021).
In dentistry, DL models known as Convolutional Neural Networks [CNNs]algorithms specialized in image analysis—
have demonstrated high performance in detecting caries, periapical lesions, periodontal bone loss, fractures, and other
abnormalities in radiographic and tomographic examinations, achieving accuracy comparable to specialists in several studies
(Pitchika et al., 2024; Shan et al., 2021; Schwendicke et al., 2020). The evaluated studies show that DL-based algorithms,
particularly CNNs, exhibit diagnostic performance comparable to that of experienced dental surgeons across various image
interpretation tasks (Samaranayake et al., 2025; Schwendicke et al., 2020; Pitchika et al., 2024)
Consequently, dental radiology currently represents the area of greatest AI development. Models trained on periapical,
panoramic, and cone-beam computed tomography [CBCT] images have shown high capability in identifying carious lesions,
periapical changes, periodontal bone loss, impacted teeth, and other maxillofacial pathologies. Beyond increasing diagnostic
sensitivity, these systems help reduce inter-examiner variability and standardize image interpretation, promoting greater
clinical reproducibility and serving as an important decision-support tool for the dentist (Samaranayake et al., 2025; Arzani et
al., 2025; Zaborowicz et al., 2025; Shujaat et al., 2025).
The detection of carious lesions represents another extensively studied application with advanced clinical maturity.
Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrate that DL algorithms, specifically CNNs, possess high diagnostic
capacity for identifying caries across imaging modalities, with average sensitivities of approximately 85% and specificities of
approximately 90%. These results highlight the potential of AI as a tool for early diagnosis, contributing to more standardized
image interpretation and evidence-based treatment planning (Sezen-Hulsmans et al., 2026; Samaranayake et al., 2025; Arzani
et al., 2025; Zaborowicz et al., 2025; Shujaat et al., 2025)
Despite these promising results, the literature still highlights major methodological limitations that restrict widespread
clinical implementation. Considerable heterogeneity exists across studies regarding the algorithms used, the size and
representativeness of image repositories, diagnostic criteria, and model training and validation methods. These differences
impede direct comparison of outcomes, compromise reproducibility, and limit the generalizability of algorithms to distinct
populations, imaging equipment, and clinical contexts. This underscores the need for multicenter studies and standardized
protocols for external validation before routine adoption in dental practice (Saikia et al., 2025; Pitchika et al., 2024; Ahmed et
al., 2021; Surlari et al., 2023).
Beyond radiology and cariology, consistent applications have been described in periodontics, endodontics,
orthodontics, implantology, oral and maxillofacial surgery, forensic dentistry, and prosthodontics [Table 2].

4.1 Generative Artificial Intelligence
One of the major shifts observed in recent years involves the incorporation of Generative Artificial Intelligence [GAI]
into dental practice. Unlike traditional AI models that predominantly focus on image classification and interpretation, Large
Language Models [LLMs]—such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Microsoft Copilot—perform tasks in natural language
processing, clinical document generation, educational material development, scientific research support, and clinical decisionmaking assistance. This evolution has significantly expanded the potential applications of AI in dentistry, particularly in
education, research, clinical management, and patient communication (SE et al., 2025; Umer et al., 2024; Brozovic et al.,
2024).
In dental education, these tools are being utilized to construct simulated clinical cases, generate examination
questions, design educational materials, and personalize learning. Within the clinical environment, they show potential to
optimize patient charting, summarize electronic health records, and facilitate interprofessional and patient-provider
communication (Ma et al., 2026; Khurshid et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025).
However, LLMs exhibit major limitations, including the generation of incorrect information ["hallucinations"], biases
derived from training datasets, and a lack of source traceability to substantiate their outputs. Consequently, it is recommended
that their use remain restricted to supporting professional activities and never replace clinical judgment or therapeutic decisionmaking (Khurshid et al., 2026; Najeeb & Islam, 2025)
The findings of this review reinforce the view that artificial intelligence is one of the most promising technologies for
transforming contemporary dental practice. However, while high diagnostic performance is frequently emphasized in
literature, clinical implementation still faces scientific, technological, regulatory, and ethical challenges (Li et al., 2026; Cosola
et al., 2025; Surlari et al., 2023; Ahmed et al., 2021; Schwendicke et al., 2020). Overall, the analyzed studies demonstrate that
AI offers important benefits for diagnosis, treatment planning, education, and clinical management, but implementation
remains constrained by data quality, clinical validation, algorithm interpretability, ethical issues, and regulation (Li et al., 2026;

Despite the high diagnostic performance demonstrated by AI algorithms, their widespread clinical translation depends
on overcoming critical methodological obstacles (Li et al., 2026; Vázquez-Sebrango et al., 2025). A primary hurdle relates to
the quality of training data. Many models are developed using retrospective image repositories obtained from single
institutions with low demographic diversity. This narrow focus increases the risk of overfitting, reducing the generalizability of
models when applied across different clinical settings (Ahmed et al., 2021; Pitchika et al., 2024; Saikia et al., 2025; Surlari et
al., 2023).

emerged as a fundamental strategy to enhance transparency, practitioner trust, and clinical safety (Di Cosola et al., 2025;
Ezhov et al., 2021; Shan et al., 2021).
From a regulatory standpoint, the rapid expansion of AI-driven solutions demands continuous updates to frameworks
governing data protection, professional liability, and clinical validation. Compliance with legislation such as the General Data
Protection Law (LGPD) and international standards is indispensable to guarantee the ethical utilization of these technologies
(Li et al., 2022; Schwendicke & Büttner, 2023).
Furthermore, most studies currently available focus on evaluating algorithmic diagnostic accuracy, leaving a
noticeable gap in research on patient-centered clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, workflow impacts, and professional
acceptability (Huo et al., 2025; Thurzo et al., 2023; Umer & Adnan, 2024). Prospective, multicenter clinical trials and
implementation studies will be critical to determine the true utility of AI in dental practice (Ahmed et al., 2021; Pitchika et al.,
2024; Saikia et al., 2025; Samaranayake et al., 2025; Surlari et al., 2023). Therefore, it must be emphasized that the integration
of AI should not be viewed as a replacement for the dental surgeon, but rather as a complementary tool designed to enhance
evidence-based decision-making (Di Cosola et al., 2025; Khurshid et al., 2026; Vázquez-Sebrango et al., 2025). The synergy
between clinical expertise, scientific knowledge, and computational support represents the most promising pathway toward
precise, personalized, and patient-centered dentistry (Bonny et al., 2023).

5. Future Perspectives
AI-driven applications will optimize dental care by relieving the workforce of laborious, routine tasks, thereby
improving health outcomes at lower cost for a broader population and ultimately facilitating personalized, predictive,
preventive, and participatory dentistry (Ezhov et al., 2021; Schwendicke et al., 2020; Umer et al., 2024). In oral health
management, AI technology spans diverse applications, including oral health education and counseling, monitoring, screening,
diagnosis, treatment, follow-up of oral diseases, and health data collection and management (Li et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026;
Shujaat et al., 2025).
By raising public awareness of oral health and improving self-management capabilities, AI can enhance diagnostic
accuracy, facilitate tailored treatments, support teledentistry, optimize dental resource allocation, and provide early warning
systems for oral pathologies (Li et al., 2026; Najeeb & Islam, 2025; Shariff et al., 2025). Dental education will need to adapt
alongside the introduction of clinical AI solutions, fostering digital literacy across the future dental workforce (Schwendicke et
al., 2020; Thurzo et al., 2023). Currently, the primary contemporary applications of AI in dentistry remain rooted in
undergraduate dental education and academic research (Pitchika et al., 2024; Surlari et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025).

6. Conclusion
Artificial intelligence represents a highly promising technology for dentistry, with applications spanning diagnosis,
therapeutic planning, education, and clinical management. The current evidence demonstrates its high potential to improve
diagnostic precision and optimize decision-making, particularly in image-based analyses. However, challenges regarding
clinical validation, data quality, ethical aspects, and regulatory compliance still limit its widespread implementation.
Thus, AI must be used as a tool that complements the dentist's clinical judgment, contributing to a more efficient,
personalized, and evidence-based practice. The advancement of artificial intelligence will likely reshape dental practice
profoundly in the coming years. Nevertheless, its incorporation must occur ethically and transparently, and be based on robust
scientific evidence, preserving the central role of the dental surgeon’s clinical judgment.
