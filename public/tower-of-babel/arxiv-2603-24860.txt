Efficient all-electron Bethe-Salpeter implementation using crystal symmetries Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jörn Stöhler, Stefan Blügel, Christoph Friedrich

Abstract
We describe an all-electron implementation of the Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE) for the calculation of optical absorption spectra in the full-potential linearized augmented-plane-wave (FLAPW) method. So far, FLAPW implementations have resorted to a simple plane-wave basis for the bare and screened Coulomb potentials, thereby forgoing the all-electron description to some extent. In contrast, we expand the interaction potentials in the all-electron mixed basis. As in most implementations, the BSE is solved by the diagonalization of a two-particle Hamiltonian matrix, whose dimension is proportional to the number of 𝐤 \mathbf{k} points. Due to the large number of 𝐤 \mathbf{k} points required to converge the BSE, the resulting matrix becomes large even for small unit cells. We describe a method that exploits the crystal symmetries to accelerate the construction and diagonalization of the two-particle Hamiltonian. In particular, we employ group theoretical tools to bring the Hamiltonian into block-diagonal form. Furthermore, it is shown that often only one of the blocks needs to be taken into account for the optical absorption spectrum leading to a considerable speedup of the diagonalization step. The code allows for the inclusion of spin-orbit coupling and is parallelized with the possibility of storing the Hamiltonian in distributed memory over many nodes, keeping the memory demands low. To validate our implementation, we show optical absorption spectra and report exciton binding energies for bulk Si, LiF, and MoS 2 . By exploiting the crystal symmetries, we can reduce the dimension of the Hamiltonian matrix of Si by a factor of five, resulting in a 125-fold speedup in its diagonalization. The calculated spectra agree with available theoretical and experimental spectra from the literature. The calculated exciton binding energies of 22 meV and 76 meV for Si and MoS 2 are closer to experimental values than in previous BSE studies.



I
Introduction

The Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE)
24
;
35
;
44
;
36
enables the accurate computation of optical absorption spectra, electron energy-loss spectra, and exciton binding energies from first principles. It provides a unified description of the response of the interacting many-electron system to the electric field of photons or beam of electrons, treating plasmonic and excitonic excitations on the same footing. Diagrammatically, the former are described by a summation of Green-function bubbles and the latter by a summation of ladder diagrams to all orders. The BSE also incorporates all possible mixtures of the diagrams, which leads to a mutual renormalization of excitons and plasmons.

To date, most implementations of the BSE for periodic systems rely on a plane-wave
14
;
33
;
20
(or Gaussian
45
) basis with a pseudopotential approximation for the Kohn-Sham effective potential. In such an implementation, only the valence electrons are treated explicitly, whereas the contribution of the core electrons to the effective potential is approximated and fixed by the chosen pseudopotential. Furthermore, due to the smoothed form of the potential, the valence wavefunctions are pseudized close to the atomic nuclei, where they miss the rapid variations of the true energy eigenstates. Likewise, high-lying empty states, relevant for the construction of the screened interaction, differ substantially from true energy eigenstates because the pseudopotential approximation is made to work well only for the valence states, while it gets less accurate for high-lying states
18
.

Full all-electron implementations of the BSE for periodic systems are scarce.
Implementations based on the LAPW basis set
42
;
30
;
51
and on the linearized muffin-tin orbitals (LMTO) basis set
37
have been reported. More recently, an implementation of the BSE based on numerical atomic orbitals has also been introduced
55
.
Both implementations based on the LAPW basis deviate from a full all-electron description, however, in that they project the (bare and screened) interaction potentials onto an auxiliary plane-wave basis, which cannot resolve the rapid variations close to the atomic nuclei. In our implementation, we do not resort to a plane-wave basis but expand all quantities in the LAPW basis and (in the case of the interaction potentials) its related mixed basis
29
;
16
;
9
.

In a common approach, one reformulates the BSE as an eigenvalue problem with an effective electron-hole Hamiltonian. This Hamiltonian is represented in a basis of electron-hole wavefunction products.
In periodic systems, typically a large number of
\mathbf{k}
points is required to converge the results, which makes the construction and diagonalization of the dense Hamiltonian matrix computationally expensive even for small systems.
A possibility to reduce the computational cost without sacrificing accuracy is by exploiting crystal symmetries. For example, it is possible to restrict the
\mathbf{k}
points to an irreducible wedge of the Brillouin zone, which is routine in density-functional theory (DFT) codes. It is also possible in implementations of hybrid functionals
10
and the
GW
approximation
16
, where an additional extended irreducible wedge has to be introduced because of nested
\mathbf{k}
summations.

Here, we describe our use of crystal and time-reversal symmetries to reduce the cost of BSE calculations.
First, we speed up the construction of the two-particle Hamiltonian. We reduce the number of entries that need to be calculated explicity, and calculate the rest by applying suitable symmetry transformations.
Second, we bring the large and dense Hamiltonian into a block-diagonal form by a transformation to a symmetry-adapted basis. Often, only one of the blocks contributes to the spectrum. This significantly accelerates the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian, which is the most time-consuming part of the BSE calculation for large systems (or calculations with large
\mathbf{k}
grids).

We would like to emphasize that the symmetry-accelerated construction of the Hamiltonian and its transformation to the symmetry-adapted basis are exact. The computational speedup does not come at the expense of accuracy. (The smaller matrix size may even help reduce numerical rounding errors.)
This stands in contrast to other techniques such as double-grid methods
44
;
2
, which necessarily introduce approximations. The approaches are, of course, compatible and can be combined.

III

\times

60

The BSE implementation based on group theory has previously been described in Ref.
49

III
Implementation

III.1
Basis sets

We employ the LAPW basis to represent the single-particle states
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}n}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)
. The FLAPW method
4
;
27
;
53
divides space into two regions, the non-overlapping atom-centered muffin-tin (MT) spheres and the remaining interstitial region. In the latter, the basis functions are simple plane waves
e^{i({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{G}}){\mathbf{r}}}/\sqrt{V}
with a reciprocal cutoff radius
|{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{G}}|\leq G_{\mathrm{max}}

u_{lp}(r)Y_{lm}(\hat{{\mathbf{r}}})
in the MT spheres (
{\mathbf{r}}
measured from the MT center). Here,
Y_{lm}(\hat{{\mathbf{r}}})
are spherical harmonics, and
u^{\sigma}_{lp}(r)
are numerical functions defined on a radial grid. The
l

0\leq l\leq l_{\mathrm{max}}
, where
l_{\mathrm{max}}
is a convergence parameter. The index
p
enumerates different radial functions. The standard LAPW basis employs
u^{\sigma}_{l0}(r)
, which is the solution of the radial scalar-relativistic Dirac equation with the spherically averaged spin-
\sigma
effective Kohn-Sham potential inside the MT sphere, and its energy derivative
u^{\sigma}_{l1}(r)
. So-called local orbitals
48

u^{\sigma}_{lp}(r)
with
p\geq 2
can be included to augment the basis in the MT spheres
48
. No approximations are made to the shape of the effective potential
53

As already discussed in the previous section, we formulate the BSE as an eigenvalue problem [Eq. (
7
)] in a two-particle product basis, defined as pairs of occupied and unoccupied states

\psi_{{\mathbf{k}}uo}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma,{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})=\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)\varphi^{*}_{{\mathbf{k}}o}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})~.

(9)

Formally, the single-particle wavefunctions are orthonormal and complete in the electron Hilbert space. This implies that the two-particle product basis is orthonormal and complete in the electron-hole Hilbert space with the orthonormality condition

\langle\psi_{{\mathbf{k}}uo}|\psi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}o^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{{\mathbf{k}}{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\delta_{uu^{\prime}}\delta_{oo^{\prime}}\,.

(10)

As a third basis set, we introduce the mixed basis

;
16
, which is designed to represent wavefunction products of the form

\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)\varphi^{*}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)

9

{\mathbf{r}}
and the same spin
\sigma
(but not necessarily at the same
{\mathbf{k}}
). The mixed basis is helpful for the evaluation of the interaction matrix elements Eq. (
8

9

The mixed-basis functions, denoted by

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{I}|M^{\mathbf{q}}_{I}\rangle\langle\tilde{M}^{\mathbf{q}}_{I}|=1\,,\quad\langle M^{\mathbf{q}}_{I}|\tilde{M}^{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}_{J}\rangle=\delta_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\delta_{IJ}\,

III.2
Coulomb divergence

With the completeness relation Eq. (
11

8

\displaystyle w_{{\mathbf{k}}n,{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}n^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{q}}m,{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}m^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{IJ}\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{q}}m}M_{{\mathbf{q}}I}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times\langle\tilde{M}_{{\mathbf{q}}I}|w|\tilde{M}_{{\mathbf{q}}J}\rangle\langle M_{{\mathbf{q}}J}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}n^{\prime}}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}m^{\prime}}\rangle\,.

(12)

The matrix
\langle\tilde{M}_{{\mathbf{q}}I}|w|\tilde{M}_{{\mathbf{q}}J}\rangle
is precalculated.
The prefactor
N_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1}
originates from the mixed-basis representation
w({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=(N_{\mathbf{k}})^{-1}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}},IJ}\langle\tilde{M}_{{\mathbf{k}}I}|w|\tilde{M}_{{\mathbf{k}}J}\rangle M_{{\mathbf{k}}I}({\mathbf{r}})M^{*}_{{\mathbf{k}}J}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

The computation of the vectors

12

By calculating the interaction matrix elements using the mixed basis, SPEX maintains the all-electron description and avoids a mapping to a pure plane-wave basis.
One may wonder why previous FLAPW implementations chose a plane-wave basis. An aspect in this choice might have been the fact that the Coulomb interaction is long-range, which gives rise to singularities in the Coulomb matrix.
The plane-wave representation has the advantage that there is only one divergent element, namely the head element (
{\mathbf{G}}={\mathbf{G}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
), in the bare Coulomb matrix, and the screened interaction additionally has divergent wing elements (
{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{0}
or
{\mathbf{G}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
) of lower order. This simple structure is lost in the mixed-basis representation.
However, it can be recovered if a unitary transformation from the mixed basis to the Coulomb eigenbasis is performed
19
. The mixed basis is defined in such a way that this transformation leads to the same matrix structure as in the pure plane-wave basis with divergent head and wing elements, while all other matrix elements remain finite. We can thus treat the Coulomb singularity as if we had a pure plane-wave basis.

According to the Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
), one needs matrix elements of
w=\overline{v}
only for
{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}
, whereas the matrix elements of
w=W
are required for all elements of the
{\mathbf{q}}
-point set because of the condition
{\mathbf{q}}={\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
, and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
runs over all wave vectors. Since
\overline{v}
is a modified Coulomb potential that specifically lacks the long-range Fourier component, there is no singularity in
\overline{v}
. However, the divergent elements of
W
do play a role.

In the limit of small momentum transfers
{\mathbf{q}}
,
the projections
\langle e^{i{\mathbf{q}}{\mathbf{r}}}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}n^{\prime}}\rangle
of the hole-hole (

,

) (orthonormality) and proportional to

As a consequence, divergent terms proportional to

,

, and

We are thus left with the

To get an understanding of how to treat the singularity appropriately, we push the size of the simulation cell, in which periodic boundary conditions shall hold for any finite size, to infinity. As a consequence, the wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}

{\mathbf{k}}

Viewed from a different perspective, when considering a finite
{\mathbf{k}}

{\mathbf{k}}

The

divergence in 3D yields a finite value, but the question arises over which region to integrate. To avoid double counting, the integration region should be somehow limited to
{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}
and its neighborhood without including any of the non-zero
{\mathbf{q}}
points of the
{\mathbf{q}}
mesh, since each
{\mathbf{q}}
point possesses its own matrix element in Eq. (
7
).
One might consider integrating over a region stretching halfway to the neighboring
{\mathbf{q}}
points. To simplify the integration, one might furthermore define the integration region to be spherical. However, we find that such a treatment of the divergence gives rise to bad
{\mathbf{q}}
-point convergence. Furthermore, it would not account for anisotropic (e.g., layered) systems or unevenly distributed
{\mathbf{q}}
-point meshes.

We basically employ the same treatment of the divergence as the one introduced in Ref.
16
for the
GW
method. Here, we describe an extended version that allows for anisotropy in the screening, i.e., tensors
\mathbf{L}
that are not just a multiple of the identity matrix.
The basic idea is that the region of integration is extended over the whole reciprocal space. As this integration comprises all other
{\mathbf{q}}
points, too, we have to introduce a double-counting correction. Another difficulty is that
q^{-2}
does not integrate to a finite value if the integration extends over the whole space. We address this difficulty by replacing
1/q^{2}
in Eq. (
13
) by
e^{-bq^{2}}/q^{2}
with a small parameter
b>0
. This is a modification of an idea introduced in Ref.
34
in the context of the Hartree-Fock method. The exponential factor guarantees that both integral and
{\mathbf{q}}
sum remain finite in the contribution of the singularity

W_{{\mathbf{k}}n,{\mathbf{k}}n^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}n,{\mathbf{k}}n^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{div}}=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int d^{3}q\frac{e^{-bq^{2}}}{q^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}}-\frac{4\pi}{N_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathrm{uc}}}\sum_{q\neq 0}\frac{e^{-bq^{2}}}{q^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}}

(14)

to the diagonal terms of Eq. (
12
) for
w=W
, where the sum over
{\mathbf{q}}
is the double-counting correction. To evaluate the integral, we first consider the angular integration. The only angle-dependent term is
\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}
. Its expansion in terms of spherical harmonics can be written as

\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}=\sum_{l=0,2}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}L_{lm}Y_{lm}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})\ .

(15)

Likewise, we make the general ansatz

\frac{1}{\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}}=\sum_{l=0,2,4,\dots}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}H_{lm}Y_{lm}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})

(16)

for its reciprocal with unknown coefficients
H_{lm}
. Multiplication and using the Gaunt coefficients
G_{LM,lm,l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}=\int Y^{*}_{LM}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})Y_{lm}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})Y_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})d^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}
gives

\displaystyle 1=

\displaystyle\sum_{L=0,2,4,\dots}\sum_{M=-L}^{L}\left[\sum_{l=0,2}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\sum_{l^{\prime}=|L-l|}^{L+l}G_{LM,lm,l^{\prime},M-m}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.L_{lm}H_{l^{\prime},M-m}\vphantom{\sum_{l^{\prime}=|L-l|}^{L+l}}\right]Y_{LM}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})\ ,

(17)

which, by equating the coefficients on both sides [note that
\sqrt{4\pi}Y_{00}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})=1
], yields a system of linear equations. The upper bound of the
l
sum (
L
sum) in Eq. (
16
) [Eq. (
17
)] is a convergence parameter. Since all
Y_{lm}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})
integrate to zero on the unit sphere except
Y_{00}(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}})
, it is sufficient to converge only the coefficient
H_{00}
with respect to the summation upper bound. We find that convergence is very fast. Using the spherical-harmonic expansions of Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) generally accounts for anisotropic dielectric screening.

We finally get

\int_{q<q_{\mathrm{max}}}d^{3}q\frac{e^{-bq^{2}}}{q^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}}=\pi\frac{H_{00}}{\sqrt{b}}\mathrm{erf}(\sqrt{b}\,q_{\mathrm{max}})\,.

(18)

with the error function
\mathrm{erf}(x)=(2/\sqrt{\pi})\int_{0}^{x}e^{-x^{\prime 2}}dx^{\prime}
.
We have introduced a cutoff radius
q_{\mathrm{max}}
up to which the
{\mathbf{q}}
integration and summation are carried out. The reason for introducing
q_{\mathrm{max}}
is that, even with the exponential factor, the addends do not fall off quickly enough to enable a fast evaluation of the
{\mathbf{q}}
sum. In addition, we smoothen the upper limit of the sum by an additional function

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\neq 0\\
q<q_{\mathrm{max}}-\Delta\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{-bq^{2}}}{q^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}}\,+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\neq 0\\
|q-q_{\mathrm{max}}|\leq\Delta\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{-bq^{2}}}{q^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}}\,f\left(\frac{q-q_{\mathrm{max}}}{\Delta}\right)

(19)

with
\Delta=(V_{\mathrm{BZ}}/N_{\mathbf{k}})^{1/3}
, the Brillouin-zone volume
V_{\mathrm{BZ}}=8\pi^{3}/V_{\mathrm{uc}}
, and
f(x)=(x^{3}-3x+2)/4
.
To minimize the number of parameters,
b
and
q_{\mathrm{max}}
are linked by the condition
b=e^{-bq_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}
. We have found
b=0.005
to be a good choice.

III.3
Usage of symmetries

BSE calculations are computationally demanding. The most expensive steps are (1) the calculation of the screened interaction within the random-phase approximation, (2) the construction of the electron-hole Hamiltonian Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
), and finally (3) the diagonalization of the electron-hole Hamiltonian. All of these steps can be significantly accelerated by exploiting spatial and time-reversal symmetries, which is an elegant way of reducing the computational burden without sacrificing accuracy.

How spatial and time-reversal symmetries can be utilized in the calculation of the screened interaction has already been described in Ref.
16
. For the sake of completeness, we note that we make use of the block-diagonal structure of
W
in the basis of Coulomb eigenfunctions
\{C_{{\mathbf{k}}\mu}\}
:
\langle C_{{\mathbf{k}}\mu}|W|C_{{\mathbf{k}}\nu}\rangle=0
unless
C_{{\mathbf{k}}\mu}({\mathbf{r}})
and
C_{{\mathbf{k}}\nu}({\mathbf{r}})
fall into the same irreducible representation (irrep) of group theory.
The Coulomb eigenfunctions are defined as linear combinations of the mixed-basis functions
C_{{\mathbf{q}}\mu}({\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{I}c_{{\mathbf{q}},\mu I}M_{{\mathbf{q}}I}({\mathbf{r}})
with the matrix
c_{{\mathbf{q}},\mu I}
of eigenvectors of
v_{{\mathbf{q}},IJ}=\langle M_{{\mathbf{q}}I}|v|M_{{\mathbf{q}}J}\rangle
. Obviously,
\overline{v}
becomes diagonal in this basis.
We will discuss irreps in Sec.
III.3.3
in more detail. Presently, it suffices to understand the irreps as a classification scheme that allows us to bring the matrix representation of
W
into block-diagonal form.
A similar technique can be used for nonlocal operators
10
in general, including the
GW
self-energy.
The block-diagonal structure of
W
accelerates the evaluation of matrix-matrix products. However, since this aspect is not the focus of the present paper, we defer a more detailed discussion to future work.

The evaluation of the matrix elements Eq. (
12
) is the most expensive step in the construction of the electron-hole Hamiltonian of Eq. (
7
).
Fortunately, many of the
W
matrix elements are related to each other via symmetry operations. Thus, instead of calculating all matrix elements explicitly, we may calculate only a few and generate the other elements from these with the help of symmetry transformations.

Furthermore, we apply
group theoretical tools to make the two-particle Hamiltonian block-diagonal and, in this way, speed up the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian. It is important to note that, in this case, the Hamiltonian is a two-particle Hamiltonian. It is represented in a basis of two-particle functions, each depending on two points in space (see Sec.
III.1
). So, the block-diagonality of the electron-hole Hamiltonian goes beyond what was explained above about the
W
matrix, which is represented in mixed-basis functions, i.e., regular local functions that depend on a single point in space. To bring the Hamiltonian into block-diagonal form, we have to perform a unitary transformation of the product functions. The new functions — linear combinations of Eq. (
9
) — can then be classified uniquely into irreps.

III.3.1
Symmetry transformation matrix

Before explaining the techniques in detail, we introduce the notation. We write a crystal symmetry operation as
s=(A,{\mathbf{T}}+{\mathbf{a}},\alpha)
with a
3\times 3
(proper or improper) rotation matrix
A\in\mathrm{O}(3)
, a lattice vector
{\mathbf{T}}
, a translation vector
{\mathbf{a}}
(which is not a lattice vector), and
\alpha
, which is 0 (1) if the symmetry operation excludes (includes) time reversal. The complete set of symmetry operations forms a symmetry group
S
.

The action of a symmetry operation on a real-space and reciprocal-space vector as well as on a single-particle function
\varphi({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)
is declared as

\displaystyle s{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{T}}+{\mathbf{a}}

(20)

\displaystyle s{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\alpha}A{\mathbf{k}}+\overline{{\mathbf{G}}}

(21)

\displaystyle s\varphi({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}}B_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\,c^{\alpha}\,\varphi(s^{-1}{\mathbf{r}},\sigma^{\prime})

(22)

with
s^{-1}{\mathbf{r}}=A^{-1}({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{T}}-{\mathbf{a}})
and the conjugation operator
c
[
c^{0}f({\mathbf{r}})=f({\mathbf{r}})
and
c^{1}f({\mathbf{r}})=f^{*}({\mathbf{r}})
]. The vector
\overline{{\mathbf{G}}}
(if not the nullvector) folds the
{\mathbf{k}}
vector back into the first Brillouin zone. Obviously,
\overline{{\mathbf{G}}}
depends on
s
and
{\mathbf{k}}
but has been written without these dependencies to simplify the notation. The matrix
B\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
is the
2\times 2
rotation matrix in spin space, which derives from
A
and
\alpha
. If spin-orbit coupling is neglected, the
B
matrix is simply the identity matrix.

When we let a symmetry operation
s
that leaves the Hamiltonian invariant act on an eigenstate, it maps this eigenstate onto another eigenstate with the same energy eigenvalue.
The symmetry transformation, when represented in the basis of eigenstates, is thus a sparse unitary matrix, which only mixes degenerate states

\displaystyle s\,\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)=\sum_{n^{\prime}\sim n}\Gamma(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}n}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\,\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}n^{\prime}}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma),

(23)

where
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}=s{\mathbf{k}}
and the notation
n^{\prime}\sim n
means that
n^{\prime}
runs over all states at
s{\mathbf{k}}
[Eq. (
21
)] that are (energy) degenerate with the
n
th state at
{\mathbf{k}}
.
It suffices to calculate
\Gamma(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}n}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}n^{\prime}}=\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}n^{\prime}}|s\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}\rangle
for the generators
\{s_{\nu}\}
of
S
. The generators are elements of
S
from which all symmetry operations can be generated: For any
s\in S
, there is a representation
s=s_{\nu}s_{\nu^{\prime}}s_{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\cdots
(some of the generators may be identical). The transformation
\Gamma(s)
can then be constructed by multiplication
\Gamma(s)=\Gamma(s_{\nu})\Gamma(s_{\nu^{\prime}})\Gamma(s_{\nu^{\prime\prime}})\cdots
(in simplified notation). The number of generators of
S
is usually much smaller than the total number of operations in
S
.

Applying the symmetry transformation to an electron-hole basis function yields

\displaystyle s\,[\varphi_{\mathbf{k}u}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)\varphi_{\mathbf{k}o}^{*}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u^{\prime}\sim u}\,\sum_{o^{\prime}\sim o}\,\tilde{\Gamma}(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}uo}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}o^{\prime}}

(24)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)\varphi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}o^{\prime}}^{*}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})

again with a sparse unitary transformation matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}uo}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}o^{\prime}}=\Gamma(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}u}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}\Gamma^{*}(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}o}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime}}\,.

(25)

The full symmetry group
S
has thus representations
\tilde{\Gamma}(s)
of unitary and anti-unitary operators in the space of electron-hole wavefunction products. The translations
s_{\mathbf{T}}:{\mathbf{r}}\mapsto{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{T}}\in S_{\mathbf{T}}

have a trivial representation

\tilde{\Gamma}(s_{{\mathbf{T}}})^{{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime}o^{\prime}}_{{\mathbf{k}}uo}=\delta_{uu^{\prime}}e^{i{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{k}}\delta_{oo^{\prime}}e^{-i{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{k}}=\delta_{uu^{\prime}}\delta_{oo^{\prime}}\,.

(26)

We can therefore switch from
S
to the group quotient
S/S_{\mathbf{T}}
, which removes the lattice translations. The resulting quotient group is now finite and the representations
\tilde{\Gamma}(sS_{\mathbf{T}})
of the coset
sS_{\mathbf{T}}
are simply
\tilde{\Gamma}(s)
. For simplicity, we will refer to the quotient group as
S
in the following.

III.3.2
Hamiltonian matrix elements

The effective electron-hole Hamiltonian matrix and its elements must obey the crystal symmetries.
This fact can be used to accelerate its construction. We first turn to the screened interaction matrix
W
.
Let us consider a matrix element
W_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u,{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o}
(note the change of notation
{\mathbf{k}}\leftrightarrow{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
with respect to Eq.
7
). Comparison with Eq. (
8
) gives
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}
and shows that the
w
matrix (here,
w=W
) depends on
\mathbf{q}
. The loop over
{\mathbf{q}}
should be the outer loop. We restrict
{\mathbf{q}}
to the irreducible Brillouin zone (IBZ), which is the minimal set of
{\mathbf{k}}
points from which all other
{\mathbf{k}}
points can be generated via Eq. (
21
). In addition, we restrict
{\mathbf{k}}
to the
extended irreducible Brillouin zone
[EIBZ(
{\mathbf{q}}
)], which is the minimal set of
{\mathbf{k}}
points from which all other
{\mathbf{k}}
points can be generated via Eq. (
21
) with the restriction that the operations
s
are elements of the so-called ”little group”. The little group is a subgroup of
S
, which contains all symmetry operations
s
that map
{\mathbf{q}}
onto itself,
s{\mathbf{q}}={\mathbf{q}}
. In general, the EIBZ(
{\mathbf{q}}
) is larger than the IBZ because the little group is smaller than the full symmetry group.

We now show that all other matrix elements (with
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathrm{BZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{BZ}
) can be generated from the subset [
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{EIBZ({\mathbf{q}})}
] by applying suitable symmetry operations. Given general
{\mathbf{q}}
and
{\mathbf{k}}
, there is a symmetry operation
s^{\prime}
that rotates
{\mathbf{q}}
into the IBZ,
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}=s^{\prime}{\mathbf{q}}
, and there is another symmetry operation
s^{\prime\prime}
of the little group that rotates
s^{\prime}{\mathbf{k}}
into the EIBZ(
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
). By definition of the little group,
s^{\prime\prime}
leaves
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
invariant, and we have
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}=s{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}=s{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{EIBZ({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})}
with
s=s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}
.

Let us write an arbitrary matrix element as

\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}u}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime}}|W|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime}}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}o}\rangle
. Substitution of the integration variables
{\mathbf{r}}\rightarrow s^{-1}{\mathbf{r}}
(likewise for
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
) does not change the integral. Then, using Eq. (
22
), the symmetry invariance of
W({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
(
s\,W\,s^{\dagger}=W
), and the fact that
W({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
is real, we can write

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}u}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime}}|W|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime}}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}o}\rangle

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{\alpha}\langle s\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}u}\,s\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime}}|W|s\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime}}\,s\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}o}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u^{\prime\prime}\sim u\\
u^{\prime\prime\prime}\sim u^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o^{\prime\prime}\sim o\\
o^{\prime\prime\prime}\sim o^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\Gamma^{*}(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}u}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}}\Gamma^{*}(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime\prime\prime}}\Gamma(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime}}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}o}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}o^{\prime\prime}}\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime\prime\prime}}|W|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime\prime}}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}o^{\prime\prime}}\rangle

with the irreducible representations Eq. (
23
). We have taken into account the possibility of
\alpha=1
, i.e., the symmetry operation
s
involves time reversal. The spin rotation matrix
B_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
can be ignored because the integrals of the form
\langle\varphi|\varphi M\rangle
in Eq. (
8
) involve a trace over the spins (in the SOC case), which is invariant with respect to SU(2) spin rotations.
Since the four summations only run over the degenerate subspaces (including a maximum of three states), Eq. (
27
) requires practically no computing time.

In practice, we loop over
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{EIBZ({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})}
and calculate the corresponding matrix elements of the right-hand-side of Eq. (
7
), which we call the ”seed” matrix elements. For each pair
({\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
, we determine a minimal set of symmetry operations
\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots\}
with which all pairs
({\mathbf{k}}_{1},{\mathbf{k}}_{2})
symmetry equivalent to
({\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
can be generated, e.g.,
{\mathbf{k}}_{1}=s_{1}{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}=s_{1}({\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
. Here, ”minimal” means that if there is another
s
that would generate the same pair, this
s
is discarded from the set. The set does
not
form a subgroup, since, for example, the neutral element is not an element of the set.
We then loop over
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
, etc. and calculate the corresponding ”rotated” matrix elements following Eq. (
27
). In this way, all matrix elements of the screened Coulomb interaction are calculated.

An analogous strategy is applied to the
matrix elements of the (modified) bare Coulomb interaction
\overline{v}_{{\mathbf{k}}u,{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}o,{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}
. Furthermore, it is possible to accelerate the computation of the seed matrix elements. The different order of
{\mathbf{k}}
indices compared to
W_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u,{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o}
leads us to another strategy to evaluate them, namely one that minimizes the number of integrals
\langle M\varphi|\varphi\rangle
and matrix-vector products.
A comparison to Eq. (
12
) (now,
w=\overline{v}
) shows that
{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}
. If symmetry is used to distribute the matrix elements as in the case of
W_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u,{\mathbf{k}}o^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}u^{\prime},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o}
, then
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{EIBZ({\mathbf{k}})}
. However, it is simpler in a first implementation to generate all matrix elements explicitly. Therefore, we adopt the more general assumption
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{BZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{BZ}
in the following. First, we calculate the integrals
\langle M_{\mathbf{0}I}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}m}\rangle
for all
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
and multiply them with the Coulomb matrix

[\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}I}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}m}]:=\sum_{J}\langle\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}I}|\overline{v}|\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}J}\rangle\langle M_{\mathbf{0}J}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}m}\rangle.

(28)

Here, we can make use of a sparse representation of the
\overline{v}
matrix
10
. Obviously, the matrix elements for
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
can be obtained directly by scalar products

\overline{v}_{{\mathbf{k}}u,{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime};{\mathbf{k}}o,{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}=\sum_{I}\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o}M_{\mathbf{0}I}\rangle[\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}I}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime}}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}]

(29)

For
{\mathbf{k}}
outside the IBZ, the wavefunctions
\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)
are not stored in computer memory, but they are generated by the operation
\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}n}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)=s_{\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}n}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)
according to Eq. (
22
) with a suitable operation
s_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{IBZ}
(
\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}
is the ”parent” of
{\mathbf{k}}
with
s_{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}={\mathbf{k}}
). With an analogous notation as the one used above for the
W

matrix elements, we thus have

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o}|\overline{v}|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}o^{\prime}}\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle s_{\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}u}s_{\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}o}|\overline{v}|s_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}o^{\prime}}s_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}u^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{\alpha}\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}u}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{k}}o}|\overline{v}|s\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}o^{\prime}}s\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}u^{\prime}}\rangle\,,

where
s=s^{-1}_{\mathbf{k}}s_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}
, and
\alpha=1
takes into account time reversal in the symmetry operation
s_{\mathbf{k}}
, otherwise
\alpha=0
. We have used that
\overline{v}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
is real and invariant with respect to all
s
(
s\overline{v}s^{\dagger}=\overline{v}
). The symmetry transformation has to be applied only to the wavefunctions on the right-hand side of the equation. We do this by transforming the known
[\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}I}\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}o^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}u^{\prime}}]
to
[\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}I}s\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}o^{\prime}}|s\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}u^{\prime}}]
. We let
s^{-1}
act on all quantities in
[...]
, which corresponds to a change of integration variables. The transformation can then be performed in the space of the mixed-product basis (
I
index), giving
c^{\alpha^{\prime}}[(s^{-1}\tilde{M}_{\mathbf{0}I})\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}o^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\mathbf{p}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}u^{\prime}}]
with
\alpha^{\prime}=1
if
s
involves time reversal, otherwise
\alpha^{\prime}=0
.

It seems now that a very large number of such transformations have to be carried out because of the dependence of
s=s^{-1}_{\mathbf{k}}s_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}
on
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathrm{BZ}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{BZ}
. Note that the number of
{\mathbf{k}}
points can be very large in BSE calculations. Of course, the number of symmetry operations for different combinations of
{\mathbf{k}}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
cannot be larger than the total set of symmetry operations. It is therefore sufficient to perform the transformation for all
s\in S

and store the results. The calculation of the matrix elements Eq. (
29
) then amounts to simple scalar products of vectors stored in memory.

III.3.3
Irreducible representations

One of the computationally most expensive steps in the BSE calculation is the diagonalization of the electron-hole Hamiltonian — note that the dimension of the Hamiltonian matrix is large, it grows with
N_{\mathrm{o}}N_{\mathrm{u}}N_{\mathbf{k}}
where
N_{\mathrm{o}}
,
N_{\mathrm{u}}
, and
N_{\mathbf{k}}
are the numbers of occupied and unoccupied bands as well as the number of
{\mathbf{k}}
points.
The computational cost thus grows cubically with the number of
{\mathbf{k}}
points, whereas the construction of the screened interaction
W
and the electron-hole Hamiltonian
H
exhibit only a quadratic scaling.
The computation time can be reduced if the electron-hole Hamiltonian is brought into block-diagonal form. To achieve this, one has to construct a symmetry-adapted basis, in which the Hamiltonian acquires the desired form.
This is indeed possible in the present case of a four-point operator, which might be surprising given the fact that group theory is usually applied in theoretical solid-state theory to mathematically less complex matrices, such as the phonon dynamical matrix or single-particle Hamiltonian.

A symmetry-adapted basis can be generated once we know the irreducible representations (irreps) of the symmetry operations (or their traces). As the name suggests, the irreps are the smallest possible matrix representations of the symmetry operations. Equation (
25
) defines valid representations, which, moreover, are already quite sparse, but they are
not
the smallest possible representations. In other words, the representations are reducible. So, the electron-hole products Eq. (
9

\tilde{\Gamma}

{\mathbf{k}}o

) states, but the maximum irrep dimension in space groups is three. Before we explain how a symmetry-adapted basis can be constructed, we have to introduce some concepts of group theory and start by restating the great orthogonality theorem.

We restrict ourselves to linear symmetry operations (excluding time-reversal symmetries) for simplicity. Let

III.3.4
Decomposition into irreps

To relate these general results of group theory to our present case, the (reducible) representation
\tilde{\Gamma}
of the symmetry group is given by Eq. (
25

\Gamma
is still unknown. It will not be our goal to determine the irreps explicitly. For our purpose, it will suffice to perform a decomposition of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
into symmetry-adapted subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
, as this already makes the electron-hole Hamiltonian block-diagonal.

There is a unique canonical decomposition into subspaces.
Consider a representation
\tilde{\Gamma}
of the group
S
on the space
\mathcal{H}
.
Consider one of the irreps, say with the index
\mu
.
We will now construct the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
.
At first, we define the characters
\chi_{\mu}(s)
of the representation
\Gamma_{\mu}
by

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu}(s)=\Tr[\Gamma_{\mu}(s)]\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(35)

An important property of the characters is that they are orthonormal in the sense

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{S}}\sum_{s}\chi^{*}_{\mu}(s)\chi_{\nu}(s)=\delta_{\mu\nu}

(36)

with
|S|
the number of elements of
S
. Equation (
36
) is also called the
little orthogonality theorem
.
In the following, we only need the character tables instead of the complete irreps. Character tables are available for all space groups from the Bilbao crystallography server
5
, the International Tables for Crystallography
25
, or similar publications.

With the characters
\chi_{\mu}(s)
, we can now declare an operator on
\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle P_{\mu}=\frac{d_{\mu}}{\absolutevalue{S}}\sum_{s}\chi^{*}_{\mu}(s)\tilde{\Gamma}(s)

(37)

where
d_{\mu}
is the dimension of
the
\mu
th irrep.
The operator commutes with any matrix
\tilde{\Gamma}(t)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(t)^{-1}P_{\mu}\tilde{\Gamma}(t)=\frac{d_{\mu}}{\absolutevalue{S}}\sum_{s}\chi^{*}_{\mu}(s)\tilde{\Gamma}(t^{-1}st)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mu}}{\absolutevalue{S}}\sum_{s}\chi^{*}_{\mu}(t^{-1}st)\tilde{\Gamma}(t^{-1}st)=P_{\mu}\,.

(38)

Let us assume that we already know a basis set in which the reducible representations
\tilde{\Gamma}(s)
block-diagonalize into
\Gamma(s)
of Eq. (
32
), which would amount to replacing
\tilde{\Gamma}\rightarrow\Gamma
in Eq. (
37
). In this basis,
P_{\mu}
is also block-diagonal.
In particular, we have
(P_{\mu})^{\nu^{\prime}i}_{\nu j}=(P_{\mu})^{\nu i}_{\nu i}\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}
.
The great orthogonality theorem applies to
P_{\mu}
and tells us that each block
(P_{\mu})_{\nu,i}^{\nu,i}
is a multiple of the identity matrix. We can determine the prefactor by taking the trace

\displaystyle\Tr[(P_{\mu})_{\nu,i}^{\nu,i}]=\frac{d_{\mu}}{\absolutevalue{S}}\sum_{s}\chi^{*}_{\mu}(s)\Tr[\Gamma_{\nu,i}(s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mu}}{\absolutevalue{S}}\sum_{s}\chi^{*}_{\mu}(s)\chi_{\nu}(s)=d_{\mu}\cdot\delta_{\mu\nu}\,.

(39)

As a consequence,
(P_{\mu})_{\nu,i}^{\nu,i}=0
if
\mu\neq\nu
, otherwise it is the identity matrix.
This means that
P_{\mu}
is a projection operator that projects an arbitrary function into the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
.

A basis for
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
can thus be obtained by applying
P_{\mu}
of Eq. (
37
) to a complete set of vectors. Such a set is simply given by (1,0,…,0), (0,1,…,0), etc.; each unit vector corresponds to one of the product basis functions Eq. (
9
).
Note that the resulting set of the projected functions is highly linearly dependent, in general. Many of the functions will just vanish. Many of them will be identical.
We use singular value decomposition to obtain an orthonormal basis
\{\mathbf{v}_{\mu,k}\}_{k\leq n_{\mu}\cdot d_{\mu}}
. It may appear computationally costly to apply the projector
P_{\mu}
in the product basis space given the large dimensionality of this space. However, the action of
P_{\mu}
on a particular product function
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}u}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)\varphi_{\mathbf{k}o}^{*}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})
acts only within the space of the so-called
orbit
associated with that function: the orbit is the set of all product functions obtained by applying the symmetry operations to
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}u}({\mathbf{r}}\sigma)\varphi_{\mathbf{k}o}^{*}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})
. The orbit consists of all product basis function, whose constituent occupied and unoccupied states are energy degenerate to
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}u}
and
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}o}
. Each orbit can thus be regarded as an equivalence class that groups together all degenerate product functions. The orbit dimension is much smaller than the product-basis dimension and is at most
|S|d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}
, where
d_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximal degree of degeneracy;
d_{\mathrm{max}}=3
is the maximum value among all space groups. Specifically, the size of
\mathcal{H}_{[{\mathbf{k}}uo]}
does not scale with the size of the
{\mathbf{k}}
point grid or the number of bands that define the two-particle basis.

By repeating the above procedure for all irrep classes, we obtain orthonormal symmetry-adapted basis sets for all irreps. The subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
themselves are orthogonal to each other, as well, so that we finally obtain an orthogonal basis transformation matrix

\displaystyle U=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\vb{v}^*_{1,1} \\ \dots\\ \vb{v}^*_{r,n_r d_r}\endlx@physics@matrix)

(40)

which decomposes our representation
\tilde{\Gamma}
into the irreps. The transformation
U^{\dagger}HU

then yields the block-diagonal form of the electron-hole Hamiltonian of Eq. (
34
).

The transformation
U^{\dagger}HU
can be performed ”irrep-wise”,
U_{\mu}^{\dagger}HU_{\mu}
. Furthermore, the vectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{\mu,k}\}
have maximally
|S|d_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}
non-zero components. This upper bound on the number of non-zero components does not increase with the system size or the
\mathbf{k}
grid. The transformation matrix
U
is therefore sparse.

III.4.1
Optical absorption spectrum

Diagonalizing the

with a parallel solver gives the full set of eigensolutions of

, categorized into irreps.
Assuming that all

In fact, in the present examples, we find that only one of the irreps contributes to the absorption spectrum, while all others do not. The estimated speedup for the diagonalization is therefore as large as
r^{3}
.

Each of the obtained eigenfunctions belongs to a single irrep, which guarantees that their symmetry is fully preserved and no averaging step is needed in the visualization.

Other approaches that circumvent the diagonalization of the electron-hole Hamiltonian, for example, the time-evolution approach

For example, in the case of the Lanczos-Haydock method, the final absorption spectrum is simply given by the sum of the ”Lanczos-Haydock spectra” calculated for each contributing block separately. Here, too, only one irrep may need to be taken into account.

Equation (
3

III.4.2
Excitonic energies and loss spectrum

So far, we have discussed the poles of the optical absorption spectrum or macroscopic dielectric function

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{N_{\mathbf{k}}V}\frac{\langle\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}u}|-i(\hat{{\mathbf{q}}}\nabla)|\varphi_{{\mathbf{k}}o}\rangle}{\epsilon^{0}_{{\mathbf{k}}u}-\epsilon^{0}_{{\mathbf{k}}o}}

Equation (

It seems that this gives rise to yet another eigenvalue equation of the same dimensions, which would double the cost of solving the BSE (if we want the eigenvalue spectrum of both the optical absorption spectrum and the excitonic eigenvalue spectrum).
Fortunately, transforming the resulting modified electron-hole Hamiltonian

Before we discuss the secular equation, we first have a closer look at the modified eigenvalue problem. The transformation to the eigenspace of

yields

If one of the

\lambda

For the same reason, only the eigensolutions of the irrep(s) relevant for the optical absorption spectrum (see previous paragraph) need to be taken into account.
Finally, given a set of

Applying the matrix determinant lemma to the characteristic equation of

Calculating the eigenvalue spectrum from the secular Eq. (

III.5
Parallel storage

Due to the quadratic scaling of the number of product basis functions with respect to the system size and its cubic scaling with respect to the number of
{\mathbf{k}}
points, it is essential to parallelize the code in order to treat large systems and/or achieve high accuracy. In particular, the large size of the electron-hole Hamiltonian requires the matrix to be stored in parallel across multiple compute nodes, since the limited memory of a single node is quickly exhausted.

We use a two-step process. In the first step, the Hamiltonian
H
is calculated in the standard two-particle product basis. When
H
is complete, it is transformed to the symmetry-adapted basis in a second step. Both matrices are stored in distributed memory, which requires a suitable data distribution strategy using MPI as described in the following.

As we use the Tamm-Dancoff approximation, the Hamiltonian matrix
H
is Hermitian, and we can afford to just store its upper triangle in the first step. The storage of this upper triangle is evenly distributed across the compute nodes. Each node keeps its part in shared memory (MPI 3.1), i.e., shared among the processes on that node: All processes of the node can access this memory by load/store operations as if it were an array allocated by the process itself.

The calculation of the matrix elements is parallelized over the processes. All processes independently calculate matrix elements with a minimum of communication between the processes (and without calculating any element twice). However, due to the usage of symmetries in the calculation of the matrix elements, see Sec.
III.3.1
, the matrix elements calculated by a given process are not contiguous in memory. It is therefore not possible for the processes to calculate only the matrix elements that are stored in the memory of the local node. All processes eventually need to send their values to all nodes (except their own).

Here, we use the following strategy:
A given process writes the matrix elements with fast store operations either to the (node-)local part of the matrix or to a buffer array. (Each process has
N-1
buffer arrays, where
N
is the number of nodes.)
There is an outer loop in the calculation of the BSE direct and exchange matrix elements, namely over the
{\mathbf{k}}
points (of the irreducible wedge) in the former [corresponding to the contribution of
W({\mathbf{k}})
] and over the processes in the latter. At the end of each loop iteration, several send-receive (MPI Sendrecv) operations are executed to transmit buffer data to the corresponding remote nodes, where a local process receives it and writes it to the local portion of the matrix stored on that node.
In this way, the communication always transfers contiguous memory arrays (the buffer arrays) and the (non-contiguous) writing to the matrix is performed by fast store operations.
The communication overhead is well below the computation time of the matrix elements.

In the second step,
H
is transformed to the symmetry-adapted basis using the (sparse) transformation matrix Eq. (
40
. The result of the transformation is stored in block-cyclic form as required by the parallel linear-algebra libraries ScaLAPACK
12
or ELPA
6
;
32
. Specifically, the right-multiplication of the
U
matrix is calculated in a round-robin fashion giving, in each iteration and process, a very thin matrix with
b
columns where
b
is the chosen block size, typically 64, 96, or 128. The left-multiplication of
U^{\dagger}
is then done by the same process in chunks of
b
rows so that, in each step, the result is a complete
b
\times
b
block, which is then sent to the process that owns that block (according to the block-cyclic distribution). Before calculating the next block, each process receives its own blocks from other processes. In this way, the transformed Hamiltonian is immediately stored in block-cyclic form and can be passed to the parallel linear algebra library. In this step, the communication overhead can be significant for very large matrix sizes, but it is still far below the total computation time.

V
Summary

In this paper, we described our implementation of the Bethe-Salpeter equation approach for calculating optical absorption and electron-energy loss spectra within the all-electron full-potential linearized augmented-plane-wave (FLAPW) method. To represent the bare and screened Coulomb interaction potentials, we employ the mixed basis, which is derived from the LAPW basis and adapted to represent products of wavefunctions.

It was demonstrated how crystal symmetries can be exploited to accelerate the construction and diagonalization of the electron-hole Hamiltonian in the Bethe-Salpeter equation.
Only a small number of interaction matrix elements (both exchange and direct terms) need to be computed explicitly; all others can be generated by applying symmetry transformations to these elements.
Moreover, transforming the conventional electron-hole product basis to a symmetry-adapted product basis using a projection operator from group theory brings the Hamiltonian
into a sparse, block-diagonal form, one block per irreducible
representation (irrep).
For the systems considered in this study, only one of the blocks turns out to contribute to the optical absorption spectrum. The other blocks do not contribute because the oscillator strengths of their corresponding eigenstates vanish due to symmetry.
As a result, the dimension of the electron–hole Hamiltonian for bulk silicon is reduced by a factor of five, leading to a 125-fold speedup in the diagonalization step. In the case of bulk MoS
2
, this speedup even reaches a factor of 216.

We also showed how the divergence of the screened Coulomb interaction
at the point
{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbf{0}
can be treated accurately, including the case of anisotropic screening described by a non-diagonal dielectric tensor. Among the systems studied in this work, bulk MoS
2
is an example of a material with such a non-diagonal dielectric tensor.
Once a set of eigensolutions is available for the optical
absorption spectrum, determining the corresponding set of eigensolutions for the
electron-energy loss spectrum requires only negligible additional computational effort.

Due to the large size of the electron–hole Hamiltonian, simulations of big systems or calculations with dense
{\mathbf{k}}
-point meshes require the matrix to be distributed across multiple compute nodes.
We therefore developed efficient parallel algorithms (1) to distribute
the Hamiltonian matrix elements – whose computation is non-contiguous due to the exploitation of crystal symmetries – across compute nodes and (2)
to transform the electron-hole Hamiltonian into a symmetry-adapated basis and, at the same time, distribute the matrix into a block-cyclic layout across the processes,
suitable for the application of diagonalization routines from
ScaLAPACK or ELPA.

The speedup achieved by the usage of symmetry enabled BSE calculations for Si to be converged to a very fine
{\mathbf{k}}
-point set of 60
\times
60
\times
60 using a product basis
band cutoff of
n_{\mathrm{occ}}n_{\mathrm{unocc}}=5.6
(see Sec.
IV
).
The resulting optical absorption spectrum closely reproduces the experimental lineshape and yields an exciton binding energy of 22.5 meV, in better agreement with the experimental value of 14 meV than in previous BSE studies.
Similarly good agreement is obtained for LiF. The optical absorption spectrum of MoS
2
was calculated including spin–orbit coupling, yielding the characteristic double-peak structure at the onset of the spectrum. The calculated exciton binding energy of 76 meV is significantly closer to the experimental value of
\sim
85 meV than in previous BSE studies.
The most demanding calculation involved an electron–hole Hamiltonian with a dimension of nearly two million for LiF.
