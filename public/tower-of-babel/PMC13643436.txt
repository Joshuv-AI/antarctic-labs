Second Transapical Mitral Valve-in-Valve With a Stented Bovine Jugular Venous Valve in Shone's Complex

Published: 2026

Severe mitral valve disease in infants and young children is exceptionally challenging given limited prosthetic options. Mechanical valves implanted in infants and young children lead to high risk for thrombosis, anticoagulation complications, and outgrowth. Traditional bioprosthetic valves are available only in limited small sizes and tend to degenerate quickly, necessitating early replacement.1 Off-label use of the Melody valve (Medtronic; a stented bovine jugular vein conduit, designed for the pulmonary position) in the mitral position has emerged as an alternative for mitral valve replacement in this population.2 The Melody valve can be adapted and implanted surgically in the mitral position, avoiding long-term anticoagulation with the potential for future balloon dilations.3

Experience with the Melody valve in the mitral position (MiM) have shown encouraging short-term results.4 Late dilations of Melody mitral valves have been performed, significantly reducing the mean inflow gradients to accommodate somatic growth.4 However, the approach is not without limitations: structural valve deterioration is accelerated under systemic pressures, with series noting up to 17% to 28% of Melody valves developing significant dysfunction by 1 year postimplant.2,3

After MiM valve dysfunction, “valve-in-valve” replacement with another MiM is uncommon and was first reported in 2019, marking a new frontier in managing degenerated MiMs.5 Our case, involving a 6-year-old boy with Shone's complex who received a third MiM (second valve-in-valve), adds to this small body of evidence.

Past History

A 6-year-old, 18-kg boy (body surface area: 0.78 m2), born with Shone's complex with critical aortic valve stenosis and mitral stenosis, presented with cardiogenic shock and reduced left ventricular (LV) systolic function requiring balloon aortic valvuloplasty on day of life 2, followed by aortic arch augmentation and mitral valve repair at 2 weeks of age. Because of significant residual aortic valve stenosis and insufficiency, at 3 months of age, he underwent a Ross-Konno procedure with surgical placement of a 14-mm MiM (Melody #1), reinforced with a pericardial skirt and creation of an atrial septal defect to facilitate future access. At 2 years of age, he underwent transcatheter valve-in-valve implant (Melody #2) into the degenerated MiM via a transapical approach for mitral regurgitation. The current procedure represents the fourth mitral-level intervention overall and the third Melody valve (Melody #3; second transcatheter valve-in-valve).

Current Presentation

After Melody #2, the patient remained asymptomatic for 4 years until he developed progressive mixed MiM dysfunction with moderate to severe stenosis (mean inflow gradient: 15 mm Hg, in context of atrial septal defect with a mean gradient of 10 mm Hg) and moderate regurgitation (Video 1). The MiM measured 13.6 mm at the inner stent (z-score: −2.7) and 17 mm at the outer valve frame (z-score: −1.1).

The case was discussed in a joint cardiosurgical multidisciplinary team meeting. Although a mechanical prosthesis was an option at this stage and had been our approach in a previous similar case, the patient's parents were very keen to avoid systemic anticoagulation given his young age and asked if a further MiM was an option. Because the dominant lesion was stenosis, the agreed strategy was to proceed with balloon dilation of the existing MiM, with an explicit plan to re-valvulate during the same procedure if dilation worsened regurgitation, to defer further valve implantation if the stenosis could be relieved by dilation alone. The preexisting MiM had not produced LV outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction on prior imaging, therefore the neo-LVOT obstruction risk of implanting a further valve was considered acceptable without dedicated cross-sectional imaging.

Procedure

The patient was brought to the hybrid catheterization laboratory and placed under general anesthesia. The procedure was performed under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and biplane fluoroscopy guidance. Systemic heparinization was achieved with a 100 IU/kg dose of heparin. Access was obtained in the left femoral vein using a 6-F sheath and a Multipurpose catheter was used to cross the atrial septum and access the left upper pulmonary vein. We exchanged the short sheath and catheter for an 8.5-F Oscor deflectable long sheath (Baylis) to the left atrium. The Melody valve was crossed using a balloon end-hole catheter, and an 0.035-inch Safari wire (Boston Scientific) was positioned in the left ventricle. An 18 × 40 mm AltoSa balloon (AndraTec) was positioned across the MiM and inflated (Figure 1), resulting in worsening of the regurgitation to severe (Video 2).

Transfemoral Approach for Balloon Redilation and Attempted Valve Implantation

The MiM was crossed with a balloon-tipped end-hole catheter to avoid passing through stent struts (A and B). This was facilitated by previously created atrial septal defect and deflectable sheath. With wire position in the LV, an AltoSa balloon was inflated cross the MiM to postdilate it (C). Using a purely transfemoral approach was not successful in advancing the delivery sheath across the MiM given the highly unfavorable angles across the atrial septum (D).

The decision was made to proceed with a third MiM implant: A Melody valve was mounted on a 20-mm Ensemble delivery system (Medtronic), which was chosen to achieve optimal expansion within the existing MiM frame given somatic growth, and was advanced to the atrial septum. Attempts to deliver the MiM using a transfemoral approach were not successful given the unfavorable angles across the atrial septum (Figure 1).

A limited left anterior thoracotomy through the 5th intercostal space was performed, and an 8-F sheath was introduced into the LV apex. A 15 mm gooseneck snare was used to capture the Safari wire from the LV, creating a stable venoarterial rail (inferior vena cava→right atrium→left atrium→LV apex). The Ensemble system was advanced over the rail, across the previous MiM with fluoroscopic and TEE guidance. The Melody #3 was then successfully deployed within the existing MiM, without complication (Figure 2).

VA Rail Creation and MiM Valve-in-Valve Deployment

Snaring of the 0.035-inch Safari guidewire to create a venoarterial rail (A), which provided an improved angle of attack for delivery system positioning (B and C). Inner-balloon inflation (C and D) with optimal device position, followed by outer-balloon inflation (E). Final fluoroscopy showed valve-stent in good position (F).

Postdeployment TEE confirmed excellent positioning, with no regurgitation, paravalvular leak (Video 3), or LVOT obstruction, and a mean mitral inflow gradient of 5 mm Hg with an invasive left atrial pressure of 13 mm Hg. The LV apex puncture site was secured with a 6-0 purse-string suture.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was extubated in the catheterization laboratory and admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit with an uneventful recovery, transitioning to the cardiology ward on postoperative day 2. He was discharged home on postoperative day 5 with aspirin 75 mg daily.

At the most recent follow-up, 11 months after the procedure, the patient remained asymptomatic and was growing well. Echocardiography demonstrated mean MiM inflow gradient of 10 mm Hg (likely overestimated based on previous correlation with invasive gradients and color flow appearance), with no regurgitation (Video 4), paravalvular leak, or LVOT obstruction. Surveillance was ongoing at 6-month intervals. Transition to a mechanical mitral valve replacement will be considered when structural valve deterioration supervenes.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the expanding role of MiM as a “repeatable” substitute for mitral valve replacement in small children whose annuli, particularly with prior complex repairs, fall below the threshold for standard prosthesis. Since its original surgical adaptation, the appeal of this strategy has been its ability to provide acceptable early hemodynamics in small annuli while preserving the possibility of future catheter-based reintervention as the child grows.6,7

In contemporary series, MiM has shown consistently low early left atrioventricular valve inflow gradients across different congenital substrates, with uncommon LVOT or pulmonary venous obstruction in the largest multi-institutional experience.2,4 That same evolving experience has clarified 2 practical constraints: limited durability under systemic pressure, and the need for reintervention (balloon dilation or redo replacement) as children grow and valve dysfunction develops.

Our patient's course highlights both issues. After Melody #1 at age 3 months and Melody #2 (first hybrid transcatheter valve-in-valve) at age 2 years, he developed progressive mixed dysfunction 4 years later. Balloon dilation was a rational first step to improve stenosis and accommodate growth; however, dilation can unpredictably worsen regurgitation through stent deformation, leaflet damage, or creation/exacerbation of paravalvular leak.4,7 This underscores the need for real-time TEE assessment and a clear backup plan for immediate replacement when regurgitation becomes clinically significant. In our case, planned escalation to a third MiM implantation resulted in prompt restoration of acceptable valve function.

To overcome limitations of a transfemoral approach, hybrid access with left anterior thoracotomy, apical LV access, and wire externalization to create a stable rail enabled controlled MiM delivery. Our case extends that concept, demonstrating feasibility even after 2 prior MiM valves (ie, a third MiM), suggesting that repeat transcatheter valve therapy can remain viable in bridging select children until they can receive appropriate prosthesis.

Nevertheless, repeated valve-in-valve MiM introduces risks that should be acknowledged. Each additional stent frame increases metal burden, may reduce effective orifice area, and could complicate future expansion or surgical explant. Stent-related failure, although uncommon, has been also reported in the mitral position, including complete stent fracture presenting as acute severe mitral stenosis.7 Patient selection and procedural planning should also address LVOT obstruction risk using echocardiography-derived anatomic predictors to aid decision-making for optimal sizing.8

Antithrombotic therapy and infectious risk are important considerations. Postoperative antiplatelet/anticoagulation strategies widely vary across centers, and emerging multicenter experience has raised concern for late infective endocarditis after MiM, emphasizing the need for careful counseling and strict endocarditis prophylaxis.9

Beyond Melody-based strategies, the balloon-expandable Edwards Sapien 3 valve has also been used off-label in the pediatric mitral position, particularly relevant for patients whose annular dimensions exceed those accommodated by the Melody valve. Early pediatric experience reported in recent series10 highlights important limitations, including early bioprosthesis dysfunction with rising gradients independent of somatic growth. Valve functionality over a range of diameters may not be maintained with the Sapien valve. In patients anatomically suitable for either platform, valve choice remains an individualized multidisciplinary team decision.

Conclusions

Our case adds to the limited experience with MiM valve-in-valve therapy by demonstrating that a third MiM implantation can be performed safely and effectively using a hybrid transapical approach when transfemoral delivery is not feasible. Although MiM remains an important growth-accommodating bridge for children with small annuli and complex anatomy, repeated valve-in-valve interventions should be pursued with clear recognition of durability limitations, failure modes, and the need for structured long-term follow-up to optimize timing and selection of future catheter-based versus surgical options.
