Timehash: Hierarchical Time Indexing for Efficient Business Hours Search CCS: Information systems Information retrieval CCS: Information systems Retrieval models and ranking
Jinoh Kim, Jaewon Son

Abstract
. Temporal range filtering is a critical operation in large-scale search systems, particularly for location-based services that need to filter businesses by operating hours. Traditional approaches either suffer from poor query performance (scope filtering) or index size explosion (minute-level indexing). We present Timehash , a novel hierarchical time indexing algorithm that achieves over 99% reduction in index size compared to minute-level indexing while maintaining 100% precision. Timehash employs a flexible multi-resolution strategy with customizable hierarchical levels. Through empirical analysis on distributions from 12.6 million business records of a production location search service, we demonstrate a data-driven methodology for selecting optimal hierarchies tailored to specific data distributions. We evaluated Timehash on up to 12.6 million synthetic POIs generated from production distributions. Experimental results show that a five-level hierarchy reduces index terms to 5.6 per document (99.1% reduction versus minute-level indexing), with zero false positives and zero false negatives. Scalability benchmarks confirm constant per-document cost from 100K to 12.6M POIs, while supporting complex scenarios such as break times and irregular schedules. Our approach is generalizable to various temporal filtering problems in search systems, e-commerce, and reservation platforms.

1.
Introduction

Temporal filtering has become a fundamental requirement in modern location-based search services. Users routinely search for “restaurants open now” or “stores open late,” expecting immediate and accurate results that reflect real-time business availability. This expectation poses a significant challenge for large-scale search systems that must efficiently filter millions of businesses by their operating hours while maintaining query performance and precision.

We encountered this challenge while building a location search service that handles millions of business listings. The service handles over 12 million business listings and serves tens of millions of daily queries from users seeking location-based information.
Timehash
was deployed in this production service from June 2023 to December 2024 (18 months), indexing 12.6 million POI records. A substantial portion of queries involves temporal constraints, such as finding businesses that are currently open or will be open at a specific time. The ability to accurately and efficiently filter businesses by operating hours directly impacts user satisfaction and engagement, making it a business-critical feature.

However, real-world business operating hours introduce substantial complexity that traditional range filtering approaches struggle to handle. Many businesses operate with break times—for example, a restaurant might be open from 11:00 AM to 2:00 PM, closed from 2:00 PM to 5:00 PM, and then open again from 5:00 PM to 9:00 PM. Some businesses have irregular schedules where weekday hours differ from weekend hours. Others operate across midnight boundaries (e.g., 10:00 PM to 2:00 AM), while convenience stores and gas stations operate 24 hours a day.

Traditional range filters, which represent operating hours as simple start and end times, cannot adequately capture these complex patterns. A single range filter cannot represent break times or multiple discontinuous time ranges. This limitation forces system designers to either sacrifice precision (by approximating complex schedules with single ranges) or resort to inefficient workarounds (such as storing multiple range filters per business, which multiplies index overhead). Neither approach scales well to millions of businesses while maintaining the minute-level precision that users expect when searching for “restaurants open now.”

1.1.
Current Approaches and Limitations

Given the limitations of scope filtering, we explored index-based approaches. Existing strategies face a tension among index efficiency, query performance, and precision. Coarse-grained indexing (e.g., hourly buckets) achieves small index size and fast queries but sacrifices precision and cannot represent break times. Fine-grained (minute-level) indexing achieves precision and supports break times but suffers from index explosion—660 terms per document for an 11-hour business, billions of terms at scale
(
Zobel and Moffat 2006
)
. Multiple range filters multiply index overhead and complicate query processing. Table

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Temporal Indexing in Search Systems

Temporal indexing has been extensively studied in the database community. R-trees
(
Guttman 1984
)
and interval trees provide efficient access methods for indexing time intervals in traditional database systems
(
Ramakrishnan and Gehrke 2003
)
. However, these structures are designed for general-purpose databases and do not address the specific challenges of inverted indexes in search systems
(
Zobel and Moffat 2006
;
Büttcher et al. 2010
)
. R-trees require maintaining separate tree structures that cannot be directly integrated into inverted indexes, and they are optimized for point and range queries in database contexts rather than term-based retrieval in search engines.

Temporal database research
(
Snodgrass et al. 1995
;
Jensen and Snodgrass 1999
)
has focused on managing valid time and transaction time in database management systems, addressing concepts such as temporal queries and versioning. While this work provides important foundations for temporal data management, it targets different requirements: database systems prioritize query correctness and transaction semantics, whereas search systems require efficient term-based indexing with minimal storage overhead. Our work addresses the specific challenge of minimizing index size in inverted indexes while maintaining minute-level precision for temporal range queries, a problem that has not been adequately addressed by existing temporal indexing approaches.

2.2.
Hierarchical Data Structures

Hierarchical data structures have been successfully applied to spatial data representation and indexing
(
Samet 2006
)
. Quadtrees
(
Finkel and Bentley 1974
;
Samet 1984
)
and their variants partition space hierarchically, enabling efficient spatial queries. Geohash
(
Niemeyer 2008
)
encodes geographic locations into hierarchical strings, allowing proximity search by sharing common prefixes. The key insight of Geohash—that hierarchical encoding can efficiently represent spatial information—inspired our approach to temporal indexing.

However, there are fundamental differences between spatial and temporal hierarchical encoding. Geohash encodes a single point (latitude, longitude) into a single hash string, whereas
Timehash
encodes a time range into multiple hash keys at different resolutions. Geohash uses ASCII character encoding (e.g., “9q8yy”), which is not human-readable, while
Timehash
uses numeric encoding (e.g., “0809” for 8:09 AM) that directly represents time values, facilitating debugging and system maintenance. More importantly, Geohash is designed for proximity search in spatial contexts, while
Timehash
is specifically optimized for range query filtering in inverted indexes, addressing the challenge of minimizing index terms while maintaining precision.

The multi-resolution approach in
Timehash
shares the hierarchical decomposition principle with spatial structures, but adapts it to the one-dimensional temporal domain and the specific requirements of search engine indexing. Rather than encoding a single point,
Timehash
decomposes ranges into hierarchical buckets, enabling efficient representation of arbitrary time intervals through a small set of multi-resolution hash keys.

2.3.
Time Encoding Schemes

Various schemes exist for encoding temporal information. The timehash library
(
Usher 2019
)
applies Geohash-inspired principles to temporal data, encoding a single timestamp into a variable-precision ASCII string (e.g., “ae0f0ba1fc”). While useful for time-series data binning and aggregation, this approach has two fundamental limitations for our use case. First, it generates a single hash per timestamp and lacks native support for range queries; to index a business operating from 11:40 AM to 9:00 PM, one must hash each individual minute, producing 560 opaque terms. Second, the ASCII encoding is not human-readable—a hash like
ae0f0ba1fc
provides no intuitive indication of the time it represents, making debugging and system maintenance in production environments prohibitively difficult.

Standard time representations such as Unix timestamps and ISO 8601
(
International Organization for Standardization 2004
)
provide universal time encoding but are not designed for efficient range query filtering in inverted indexes. Unix timestamps require exact matching and cannot represent ranges efficiently, while ISO 8601 strings generate too many distinct terms when used directly in indexes.

3.
Problem Definition

3.1.
Business Hours Search Problem

We formalize the temporal range indexing problem for business hours search in large-scale search systems. Given a collection of documents (businesses)
D=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\}
, each document
d_{i}
is associated with one or more time ranges
R_{i}=\{r_{i1},r_{i2},\ldots,r_{ik}\}
representing its operating hours. Each range
r_{ij}=[s_{ij},e_{ij}]
is defined by a start time
s_{ij}
and end time
e_{ij}
, where times are represented at minute-level granularity.

A query
q
can be either a point query
q=t
(find businesses open at time
t
) or a range query
q=[t_{1},t_{2}]
(find businesses open during the time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
). A document
d_{i}
matches query
q
if there exists at least one range
r_{ij}\in R_{i}
such that
r_{ij}
overlaps with
q
. For a point query
q=t
, this means
s_{ij}\leq t\leq e_{ij}
for some
r_{ij}\in R_{i}
. For a range query
q=[t_{1},t_{2}]
, this means
[s_{ij},e_{ij}]\cap[t_{1},t_{2}]\neq\emptyset
for some
r_{ij}\in R_{i}
.

The challenge is to design an indexing scheme that enables efficient query processing while minimizing storage overhead. In an inverted index, each document is associated with a set of index terms (keys). For temporal range queries, we need to generate a set of index terms
T_{i}
for each document
d_{i}
such that: (1) query processing can be performed through efficient term-based retrieval, (2) the number of terms
|T_{i}|
is minimized to reduce index size, and (3) query results maintain high precision and recall (ideally 100% recall with minimal false positives).

For example, a restaurant operating from 11:40 AM to 9:00 PM (represented as
[1140,2100]
in minutes since midnight) must be indexed with terms that allow queries at any time within this range to efficiently retrieve the document, while minimizing the total number of terms across all documents in the collection.

3.2.
Requirements

An effective temporal range indexing scheme must satisfy four key requirements:

Precision
: The system must support minute-level granularity to match user expectations. When a user searches for businesses open at 11:40 AM, the system should accurately distinguish between businesses that open at 11:40 AM versus those that open at 11:45 AM. This requirement precludes coarse-grained approaches that sacrifice precision for efficiency.

Scalability
: The indexing scheme must scale to large-scale datasets containing millions or billions of documents. Index size must remain manageable—excessive index terms per document lead to prohibitive storage overhead. Query latency must remain low even as the dataset grows, requiring efficient term-based retrieval without query-time filtering over large document sets.

Flexibility
: Real-world business operating hours introduce substantial complexity that the indexing scheme must accommodate. Many businesses operate with break times (e.g., a restaurant open 11:00 AM–2:00 PM, closed 2:00 PM–5:00 PM, then open 5:00 PM–9:00 PM). Some businesses have irregular schedules where weekday hours differ from weekend hours. Others operate across midnight boundaries (e.g., 10:00 PM to 2:00 AM), while convenience stores and gas stations operate 24 hours a day. The indexing scheme must support these complex patterns without multiplying index overhead.

Query Performance
: Query processing must be efficient, leveraging the inverted index structure for fast term-based retrieval. The system should avoid query-time filtering that requires scanning large document sets, as this degrades performance and does not scale to large collections.

These requirements create a fundamental tension: achieving minute-level precision while maintaining small index size and supporting complex temporal patterns. Existing approaches satisfy some but not all requirements, motivating the need for a novel solution that can balance these competing objectives.

3.3.
Metrics

We evaluate temporal range indexing schemes using three key metrics:

Index Size
: The primary efficiency metric is the number of index terms per document. For a document
d_{i}
with index terms
T_{i}
, we measure
|T_{i}|
, the cardinality of the term set. The average terms per document across the collection,
\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i=1}^{|D|}|T_{i}|
, indicates the storage overhead of the indexing scheme. A smaller value indicates better index efficiency. Total index size is also measured in terms of storage (e.g., megabytes or gigabytes) for the entire collection.

Query Latency
: Query performance is measured by latency (time to process a query) and throughput (queries processed per second). We report percentiles (P50, P95, P99) to capture the distribution of query latencies, as tail latencies are critical for user experience. Query latency should remain low even as the dataset scales, indicating that the indexing scheme enables efficient term-based retrieval without query-time filtering.

Precision and Recall
: Accuracy is measured using precision and recall. For a query
q
, let
R_{q}
be the set of documents returned by the system and
C_{q}
be the set of correct documents (ground truth). Precision is defined as
\frac{|R_{q}\cap C_{q}|}{|R_{q}|}
, the fraction of returned results that are correct. Recall is defined as
\frac{|R_{q}\cap C_{q}|}{|C_{q}|}
, the fraction of correct results that are returned. False positives occur when
R_{q}\setminus C_{q}\neq\emptyset
, and false negatives occur when
C_{q}\setminus R_{q}\neq\emptyset
. An ideal indexing scheme achieves high precision (minimal false positives) and 100% recall (zero false negatives), ensuring that all relevant documents are retrieved while minimizing irrelevant results.

These metrics capture the fundamental tradeoffs in temporal range indexing: index size versus query performance versus accuracy. An effective solution must optimize all three dimensions simultaneously.

4.
Timehash Algorithm

4.1.
Overview: Hierarchical Multi-Resolution Approach

Our approach,
Timehash
, addresses the temporal range indexing problem through hierarchical multi-resolution decomposition. The key insight is that time ranges can be efficiently represented by decomposing them into a small set of hierarchical buckets at multiple resolutions rather than indexing every individual minute.

The algorithm operates on a set of predefined measures (granularities) arranged in a hierarchy, such as
M=\{4h,1h,15m,5m,1m\}
for business hours search. Given a time range
[start,end]
,
Timehash
iteratively selects the largest measure that fits within the remaining range, generates a hash key for that block, and advances the current position. This greedy approach ensures that long contiguous ranges are covered by coarse-grained blocks (reducing the number of keys), while range boundaries are handled by fine-grained blocks (maintaining precision).

For example, consider a restaurant operating from 11:40 AM to 9:00 PM (21:00). Naive minute-level indexing would generate 560 index terms.
Timehash
, using the five-level hierarchy, generates 5 keys:
\{08113040,081145,12,16,2020\}
by hierarchically combining:

•

Coarse-grained blocks: 4-hour blocks (12, 16) covering large contiguous portions

•

Fine-grained composite keys: Hierarchical keys (08113040, 081145, 2020) handling boundaries through multi-level refinement

This represents a dramatic reduction in index size (from 560 to 5 keys) while maintaining minute-level precision. Figure
1
illustrates this hierarchical decomposition process for a simpler example (08:00–21:00). The algorithm is level-agnostic: it works with any hierarchical level configuration, allowing adaptation to different applications within the daily time range domain. For instance, resource booking systems might benefit from 30-minute blocks instead of 15-minute blocks, while shift-based operations might use 6-hour blocks for specific shift patterns. Through empirical analysis on application-specific data, optimal hierarchies can be determined that balance index efficiency with query precision for the target application. Note that
Timehash
focuses on indexing time-of-day ranges within a 24-hour period, not date-level or week-level temporal information.

4.2.
Time Hierarchy Design

Timehash
employs a flexible and customizable architecture that allows users to define hierarchical levels based on their specific requirements. The algorithm is agnostic to the specific granularities chosen, enabling adaptation to different domains and use cases. However, through empirical analysis on 12.6 million business records from a production location search service, we identified optimal five-level hierarchies that balance index size reduction with query precision for business hours search.

The optimal hierarchy consists of five levels with decreasing granularity:

•

Level 1 (4-hour blocks)
: Keys represent 4-hour periods (00, 04, 08, 12, 16, 20), covering large contiguous time blocks efficiently. This level aligns with common business hour patterns such as morning shifts, afternoon shifts, and evening shifts.

•

Level 2 (1-hour blocks)
: Keys represent hourly periods (00–23), matching natural business hour boundaries (e.g., “open at 9 AM”, “close at 5 PM”).

•

Level 3 (15-minute blocks)
: Keys represent quarter-hour periods (00, 15, 30, 45), providing intermediate precision common in scheduling systems.

•

Level 4 (5-minute blocks)
: Keys represent 5-minute periods (00, 05, 10, …, 55), enabling fine-grained precision without excessive key generation.

•

Level 5 (1-minute precision)
: Keys represent individual minutes (00–59), providing maximum precision for edge cases and exact time matching.

The optimization process systematically evaluated various level combinations, testing 2-level to 6-level hierarchies with different granularity configurations. Metrics included total index terms across all documents and query precision. The five-level hierarchy achieved the optimal tradeoff; the specific granularities are selected through data-driven analysis based on the target dataset’s temporal distribution (see Section
7
).

The encoding format uses human-readable numeric representation with hierarchical key composition. Keys are formed by combining components from different levels, read from left to right. For example, a key “1212” represents a 4-hour block “12” refined to a 1-hour block “12”, while “08113040” represents a 4-hour block “08” refined through multiple levels to a 5-minute block “40”. This hierarchical structure directly represents time values, making keys human-readable and significantly simplifying debugging and system maintenance compared to opaque ASCII encodings used in existing schemes.

4.3.
Hash Key Generation Algorithm

The
Timehash
generation algorithm takes as input a time range
[start,end]
and a set of measures
M=\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k}\}
ordered by decreasing granularity. The algorithm iteratively decomposes the range by selecting the largest measure that fits within the remaining portion, generating a hash key for that block.

The
encode
function generates hash keys by combining components from different hierarchy levels. At each level, the function extracts the relevant time components (hours, minutes) and appends them to form a composite key. The key is read from left to right, with each component representing a finer granularity level. For example, a key “1212” represents a 4-hour block “12” (12:00–16:00) refined to a 1-hour block “12” (12:00–13:00). Similarly, “08113040” represents a 4-hour block “08” (08:00–11:59) refined to a 1-hour block “11” (11:00–11:59), then to a 15-minute block “30” (11:30–11:44), and finally to a 5-minute block “40” (11:40–11:44). The encoding format directly represents time values in a hierarchical structure, making keys human-readable and facilitating debugging.

Consider a simple example: a business operating from 12:00 PM to 4:00 PM. The entire range aligns with the 4-hour block 12:00–16:00, so the algorithm generates a single key “12”. For a narrower range 12:00–13:00, the 4-hour block is too coarse; the algorithm recurses to the 1-hour level and generates “1212” (4h block “12” refined to 1h block “12”).

For a complex example, consider a restaurant operating from 11:40 AM to 9:00 PM (21:00). The algorithm decomposes this into 5 keys:

(1)

Interior blocks
: 12:00–16:00 and 16:00–20:00 each fill a complete 4-hour block, generating keys “12” and “16”.

(2)

Start boundary
(11:40–12:00): Falls within 4h block “08” (08:00–12:00), which is not fully covered. Recursive refinement reaches 1h block “11”, then 15m block “30” (11:30–11:44) is still too coarse, then 5m block “40” (11:40–11:44) fits, yielding “08113040”. The remaining 11:45–12:00 fits exactly into 15m block “45”, yielding “081145”.

(3)

End boundary
(20:00–21:00): Falls within 4h block “20” (20:00–24:00), not fully covered. Refinement to 1h block “20” (20:00–21:00) fits exactly, yielding “2020”.

The final key set is
\{08113040,081145,12,16,2020\}
—5 keys instead of 560 minute-level terms. Figure

4.4.
Query Term Generation

For query processing,
Timehash
generates query keys that match index keys at various hierarchy levels. Given a query time
t
(e.g., 14:30, encoded as 1430), the algorithm generates all possible query keys that could match documents operating at time
t
.

The query key generation process works as follows:

(1)

Point query
: For a query at time
t
, generate keys at all hierarchy levels that contain
t
. For example, for query time 14:30 (1430):

•

Level 1 (4-hour): “12” (12:00–16:00 contains 14:30)

•

Level 2 (1-hour): “1214” (14:00–15:00 contains 14:30)

•

Level 3 (15-minute): “121430” (14:30–14:44 contains 14:30)

•

Level 4 (5-minute): “12143000” (14:30–14:34 contains 14:30)

•

Level 5 (1-minute): “1214300000” (14:30–14:30 contains 14:30)

(2)

Query processing
: All five query keys are used to lookup documents in the inverted index. The union of posting lists from all query keys contains all documents that operate at time 14:30.

(3)

Range query
: A range query
[t_{1},t_{2}]
finds documents with any temporal overlap (Section
3
). This is implemented by generating index-side keys for
[t_{1},t_{2}]
and matching against document keys. In practice, point queries dominate production workloads; range queries are a straightforward extension.

The query key generation ensures completeness: any document operating at query time
t
will have at least one index key that matches at least one query key. This guarantees 100% recall (zero false negatives).

4.5.
Handling Complex Scenarios

Timehash
handles various complex temporal patterns through its hierarchical decomposition strategy:

Break Times
: For businesses with break times (e.g., open 11:00–14:00, closed 14:00–17:00, open 17:00–21:00),
Timehash
processes each time range independently. The algorithm generates separate key sets for each range:
K_{1}
for 11:00–14:00,
K_{2}
for 17:00–21:00. The document’s complete term set is the union
K_{1}\cup K_{2}
. Query processing remains the same: query keys match any of the document’s index keys, correctly identifying businesses open at the query time.

Irregular Schedules
: For businesses with different hours on different days (e.g., weekday vs. weekend),
Timehash
can store separate key sets per day type. The indexing process generates keys for weekday hours and weekend hours separately, and query processing considers the appropriate key set based on the query day.

Midnight Spanning
: Ranges that cross midnight (e.g., 22:00–02:00) are decomposed into two ranges:
[22:00,24:00]
and
[00:00,02:00]
. Each range is processed independently, generating separate key sets that are then combined.

24-Hour Operations
: Businesses operating 24 hours a day are indexed with keys covering the entire day. The algorithm generates keys at coarse-grained levels (4-hour, 1-hour) that efficiently cover the full 24-hour period.

Day/Date Extensions
: While
Timehash
focuses on daily time-of-day ranges, the encoding scheme can be extended to include day-of-week or date information by prefixing time hash keys. For example, keys like
mon1212
(Monday 12:00–13:00, 1-hour block) or
Jan281212
(January 28, 12:00–13:00) combine day/date prefixes with time hash keys, enabling efficient filtering by both temporal context and time-of-day. The prefix acts as an additional coarsest level in the hierarchy, allowing queries to first filter by day/date before refining to time granularity.

5.
Theoretical Analysis

5.1.
Complexity Analysis

We analyze the complexity of
Timehash
in terms of space (number of keys generated) and time (computation cost). The analysis is based on the recursive decomposition algorithm implemented in the
cover
function, which processes a time range
[from,to]
at each hierarchy level.

Space Complexity
: For a time range of length
T
minutes, naive minute-level indexing generates
O(T)
keys (one key per minute). We analyze how
Timehash
reduces this through hierarchical decomposition.

Proof
: Consider the recursive
cover
function. For a range
[from,to]
of length
T
, the algorithm first tiles the interior with complete blocks of size
m_{1}
(the coarsest measure). Because
m_{i-1}
is a multiple of
m_{i}
in our hierarchy, the inner boundaries of these complete blocks are aligned to all finer levels. Consequently, only the two
outer
boundaries (start and end of the original range) require refinement, and each boundary propagates
exactly one
sub-boundary to the next finer level.

The key count decomposes into two parts:

(1)

Interior blocks
at the coarsest level: at most
\lfloor T/m_{1}\rfloor+1
.

(2)

Boundary refinement keys
: at each level
i\geq 2
, each of the two boundaries contributes at most
m_{i-1}/m_{i}-1
complete blocks and propagates one sub-boundary to level
i+1
.

The total boundary key count is therefore:

(1)

B=2\sum_{i=2}^{k}\left(\frac{m_{i-1}}{m_{i}}-1\right)

which depends only on the hierarchy definition and is
independent of
T
. The total key count satisfies:

(2)

|H|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{T}{m_{1}}\right\rfloor+1+B=O\!\left(\frac{T}{m_{1}}\right)

This represents a constant-factor
m_{1}{\times}
reduction over naive
O(T)
indexing.

Concrete bound
: For the hierarchy
M=\{240,60,15,5,1\}
,
B=24
. With at most 7 interior blocks for
T\leq 1440
, the total is bounded by 31. Empirically, we observe a worst case of
28 keys
(Table
6
), versus 1,440 for naive indexing—a
>
97% reduction.

Time Complexity for Generation
: The time complexity of generating keys for a range
[from,to]
is
O(T/m_{1})
where
T=to-from
.

Proof
: The
cover
function iterates through complete blocks at the coarsest level in
O(T/m_{1})
time. Boundary processing adds
O(B)
work where
B
is the constant defined above. Total time is
O(T/m_{1}+B)=O(T/m_{1})
. For the bounded 24-hour domain, this is
O(1)
.

Query Complexity
: For a point query at time
t
, generating query keys takes
O(k)=O(1)
time where
k
is the number of hierarchy levels, as
getQueryTerms
iterates through all measures once. For a range query
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, generating query keys takes
O(Q/m_{1})
time where
Q=t_{2}-t_{1}
, using the same analysis as index key generation; for the bounded domain this is again
O(1)
.

Retrieving documents from the inverted index for
|H|
query keys takes
O(|H|+|\mathit{results}|)
time, where
|\mathit{results}|
is the number of matching documents. Since
|H|
is bounded by a small constant (at most 28 for our hierarchy), the lookup overhead is negligible and the total query complexity is dominated by the result set size
O(|\mathit{results}|)
.

5.2.
Optimality Analysis

The optimality of the five-level hierarchy (4h, 1h, 15m, 5m, 1m) is established through both theoretical analysis and empirical validation. The hierarchy selection process balances two competing objectives: minimizing the number of index keys while maintaining query precision.

Theoretical Optimality
: For a given set of hierarchy levels, the
Timehash
algorithm generates the minimum number of keys needed to cover a time range while maintaining minute-level precision. This is achieved through the greedy decomposition strategy: at each level, the algorithm selects the largest possible block that fits within the remaining range, ensuring that no smaller number of keys can cover the same range at the same precision level.

The specific granularities (4h, 1h, 15m, 5m, 1m) are chosen to align with natural time boundaries and business patterns:

•

4-hour blocks
(240 minutes): Divides a 24-hour day into 6 segments aligned with shift patterns (morning: 08–12, afternoon: 12–16, evening: 16–20), efficiently covering long contiguous operating hours.

•

1-hour blocks
(60 minutes): Matches natural business hour boundaries, as businesses typically open and close at round hours (e.g., 9 AM, 5 PM). This reduces the need for fine-grained keys at common boundary times.

•

15-minute blocks
: Provides quarter-hour precision, common in scheduling systems and sufficient for most business hour queries. This intermediate granularity balances precision with key count.

•

5-minute blocks
: Enables fine-grained precision for edge cases (e.g., businesses opening at 11:40 AM) without generating excessive keys. The 5-minute granularity is a good compromise between 15-minute and 1-minute levels.

•

1-minute blocks
: Provides maximum precision for exact time matching, ensuring that queries at any minute can be accurately answered.

Empirical Validation
: The optimality of five-level hierarchies was validated through systematic evaluation on 12.6 million production business records (Section
7
). We tested configurations from 2 to 6 levels with various granularity choices. Five levels consistently achieved the best balance: minimizing total index terms while maintaining full precision. Fewer levels produce significantly more terms (e.g., 3-level yields
3\times
more keys), while adding a sixth level provides negligible improvement (
<
1%). The hierarchical decomposition ensures every time point is precisely covered by at least one key. Alternative granularities at each level (e.g., 3-hour or 2-hour coarsest blocks, 10-minute or 30-minute intermediate blocks) were tested, and the optimal choices depend on the dataset’s temporal distribution.

Generalizability
: While the specific hierarchy (4h, 1h, 15m, 5m, 1m) is optimal for business hours search, the
Timehash
algorithm framework supports arbitrary level configurations. Different applications within the same temporal domain (daily time-of-day ranges) may require different hierarchies optimized for their specific patterns. The data-driven optimization methodology we describe can be applied to identify optimal hierarchies for other domains.

5.3.
Precision Guarantees

Timehash
provides strong correctness guarantees: 100% precision (zero false positives) and 100% recall (zero false negatives). These guarantees are ensured by the algorithm’s design.

Theorem 5.1 (Zero False Negatives).

Timehash
guarantees zero false negatives. For any query time
t
within a document’s operating hours
[start,end]
, at least one query key will match at least one index key.

Proof.

The
cover
function recursively processes the entire range
[start,end]
, ensuring that every minute is covered by at least one key at some hierarchy level. For any query time
t\in[start,end]
, the query key generation process creates keys at all hierarchy levels that contain
t
. Since the index key generation covers the entire range
[start,end]
, there exists at least one index key that covers time
t
, and at least one query key that matches that index key. Therefore, the document will be retrieved, ensuring 100% recall.
∎

Theorem 5.2 (Zero False Positives).

Timehash
guarantees zero false positives. A query key matches a document’s index key only if the query time falls within the document’s operating hours.

Proof.

The
cover
function only generates keys for time blocks that are fully contained within the operating range
[start,end]
or that require refinement at finer granularities. Keys are never generated for time outside the operating range. For a query time
t\notin[start,end]
, no query key can match any index key, as all index keys represent time within
[start,end]
. Therefore, documents not operating at time
t
will not be retrieved, ensuring 100% precision.
∎

These theoretical guarantees ensure that
Timehash
maintains correctness for all time ranges and patterns, not just those observed in evaluation datasets. The hierarchical decomposition strategy ensures completeness (every time point is covered) and correctness (only time points within the range are covered).

6.
Implementation

6.1.
System Architecture

Timehash
is implemented as a lightweight C++ library that integrates seamlessly with existing search engine infrastructure
(
Baeza-Yates and Ribeiro-Neto 2011
)
. The system architecture consists of three main components:

Timehash
Library
: The core implementation provides two primary functions:

•

getIndexTerms(from, to)
: Generates hierarchical hash keys for a time range
[from,to]
, where
from
and
to
are strings in
hhmm
format (e.g., “1140” for 11:40 AM, “2100” for 9:00 PM).

•

getQueryTerms(hhmm)
: Generates query keys for a point query at time
hhmm
, returning all hierarchy-level keys that could match.

Indexer
: The indexing component processes business data and generates
Timehash
keys via
getIndexTerms
. For each business document, the indexer:

(1)

Parses operating hours (potentially multiple ranges for break times)

(2)

Calls
getIndexTerms
for each time range

(3)

Combines all keys into a document’s term set

(4)

Adds these terms to the inverted index with the document ID

Query Processor
: The query processing component handles temporal queries:

(1)

Receives a query with a time constraint (e.g., “find businesses open at 2:30 PM”)

(2)

Calls
getQueryTerms
to generate all possible query keys

(3)

Looks up each query key in the inverted index

(4)

Unions the posting lists from all query keys

(5)

Returns the final result set

The implementation is designed for minimal overhead: the
Timehash
library is stateless (except for the configurable measures), making it thread-safe and suitable for high-throughput indexing and query processing. The library’s interface is simple and requires no external dependencies, facilitating easy integration into existing search systems.

6.2.
Integration with Search Engine

Timehash
integrates with search engines through the standard inverted index structure. The integration is straightforward:
Timehash
keys are treated as regular index terms, requiring no modifications to the underlying index infrastructure.

Inverted Index Structure
: Each
Timehash
key (e.g., “1212”, “08113040”) becomes a term in the inverted index. The posting list for a term contains all document IDs that have that term in their index term set. For example, the posting list for term “12” contains all businesses that operate during the 12:00–16:00 time block, while the posting list for “1212” contains businesses that operate specifically during 12:00–13:00.

The inverted index structure remains unchanged: it is a standard term-to-document mapping.
Timehash
keys are simply added as additional terms alongside other document attributes (e.g., location terms, category terms). This design choice enables seamless integration without requiring specialized index structures or query processing logic.

Query Processing Pipeline
: When a query includes a temporal constraint (e.g., “find restaurants open at 2:30 PM”), the query processor:

(1)

Extracts the query time (e.g., 14:30, encoded as 1430)

(2)

Calls
getQueryTerms(1430)
to generate query keys:
12
,
1214
,
121430
,
12143000
,
1214300000

(3)

For each query key, looks up the corresponding posting list in the inverted index

(4)

Unions all posting lists to get the candidate document set

(5)

Optionally applies additional filters (location, category, etc.) to refine results

(6)

Returns the final ranked result set

The union operation can be efficiently implemented using hash sets or sorted lists to avoid duplicate document IDs. Since the number of query keys is small (at most 28 for our hierarchy, typically 5 for point queries), the overhead is minimal.

Integration Benefits
: This integration approach provides several advantages:

•

No infrastructure changes
: Works with existing inverted index implementations

•

Backward compatibility
: Existing queries without temporal constraints continue to work unchanged

•

Composability
: Temporal filtering combines with other filters (location, category) via standard boolean operations

•

Scalability
: Leverages existing index infrastructure optimized for large-scale search

6.3.
Indexing Pipeline

The indexing pipeline processes business data to generate
Timehash
keys and populate the inverted index. The pipeline is designed for batch processing of large-scale datasets.

Input Format
: Business data includes operating hours in
hhmm
format. For businesses with break times, multiple time ranges are provided. For example, a restaurant might have operating hours:

•

Weekday: 11:00–14:00, 17:00–21:00

•

Weekend: 12:00–21:00

Processing Steps
:

(1)

Parse operating hours
: Extract time ranges from business data, handling different formats (e.g., “11:00-14:00” or “1100-1400”).

(2)

Key generation
: Call
getIndexTerms
for each range. The recursive
cover
function:

•

Processes the range at each hierarchy level (4h, 1h, 15m, 5m, 1m)

•

Generates keys for blocks completely contained within the range

•

Recursively refines boundary blocks at finer granularities

•

Combines keys hierarchically (e.g., “08113040” = 08 + 11 + 30 + 40)

(3)

Combine keys
: For businesses with multiple time ranges (e.g., break times), union all generated key sets to form the document’s complete term set.

(4)

Add to inverted index
: For each key in the document’s term set, add the document ID to the corresponding posting list.

Performance Considerations
: The indexing pipeline is optimized for throughput:

•

Batch processing
: Processes multiple documents in parallel, leveraging multi-core architectures

•

Efficient key generation
: The recursive
cover
function generates at most 28 keys for any range within the 24-hour domain, ensuring constant-time key generation regardless of range length

•

Memory efficiency
: Keys are generated on-the-fly and immediately added to the index, avoiding large intermediate data structures

•

Incremental updates
: The pipeline supports incremental indexing, allowing updates to individual documents without full reindexing

Scalability
: The pipeline scales linearly with the number of documents. For 12 million businesses, the indexing process completes in reasonable time (e.g., hours rather than days), making it practical for large-scale deployments. The key generation cost per document is bounded by a small constant (at most 28 keys), and the inverted index update operations are standard and well-optimized in existing search engine implementations.

6.4.
Query Processing

Query processing in
Timehash
is designed for low latency and high throughput. The process leverages the inverted index structure for efficient term-based retrieval.

Query Flow
: For a query requesting businesses open at time
t
:

(1)

Query key generation
: Call
getQueryTerms(t)
to generate all hierarchy-level keys that could match documents operating at time
t
. For example, for query time 14:30, generate keys:
\{12,1214,121430,12143000,1214300000\}
.

(2)

Index lookup
: For each query key, retrieve the corresponding posting list from the inverted index. This involves
k
lookups where
k
is the number of hierarchy levels (typically 5).

(3)

Result union
: Union all posting lists to obtain the candidate document set. Since documents may appear in multiple posting lists (e.g., a document operating 12:00–16:00 appears in both “12” and “1214” posting lists), deduplication is necessary.

(4)

Result filtering
: Optionally apply additional filters (location, category, etc.) to refine results.

(5)

Result ranking
: Rank results according to relevance criteria (e.g., distance, rating, popularity).

Optimization Techniques
:

•

Caching
: Query keys for common query times (e.g., current time) can be cached to avoid regeneration.

•

Parallel lookup
: The
k
index lookups can be performed in parallel, reducing query latency.

•

Early termination
: If a query key’s posting list is empty, skip remaining lookups for that key.

•

Posting list compression
: Standard inverted index compression techniques (delta encoding, variable-byte encoding) reduce memory usage and improve cache performance.

Query Performance
: The query processing time is dominated by posting list retrieval rather than key generation. Since key generation is
O(k)=O(1)
for point queries and posting list retrieval is efficient in modern inverted index implementations,
Timehash
achieves low query latency suitable for production search systems.

8.
Discussion

8.1.
Generalizability

Timehash
’s hierarchical decomposition strategy is domain-agnostic, enabling adaptation to various temporal indexing applications beyond business hours search.

Domain Adaptation
: The customizable level configuration allows optimization for specific requirements. Resource booking systems might use 30-minute blocks aligned with typical appointment durations. Shift-based operations could use 6-hour blocks matching shift boundaries (e.g., morning, afternoon, night shifts). High-frequency trading or IoT applications could extend to second-level precision through modified encoding schemes.

Temporal Extensions
: The encoding scheme can be extended to include day-of-week or date information by prefixing time hash keys. For example,
mon1212
represents Monday 12:00–13:00, while
Jan281212
represents January 28, 12:00–13:00. The prefix acts as an additional coarsest level in the hierarchy, enabling efficient filtering by both temporal context and time-of-day. This extension naturally fits the hierarchical structure and maintains the algorithm’s efficiency properties.

8.2.
Limitations and Tradeoffs

Temporal Scope
:
Timehash
targets daily time-of-day ranges (00:00–23:59). Multi-day or weekly patterns require combining
Timehash

Precision Boundary

9.
Conclusion

We presented
Timehash
, a hierarchical time indexing algorithm for temporal range filtering in large-scale search systems. Our contributions include: (1) formalizing the temporal range indexing problem and demonstrating limitations of existing approaches; (2) introducing a flexible hierarchical multi-resolution algorithm with customizable level configurations; (3) demonstrating a data-driven hierarchy optimization methodology validated on 12.6 million production records; and (4) providing a rigorous complexity analysis showing
O(T/m_{1})
worst-case key count with a constant upper bound of 28 keys for the 24-hour domain, together with guaranteed 100% precision and recall.

Our evaluation on 12.6 million synthetic POIs (generated from production distributions) demonstrates 99.1% reduction in index terms versus minute-level indexing (from 609.7 to 5.6 terms per document), while supporting complex patterns including break times, irregular schedules, and midnight-spanning ranges. Scalability benchmarks confirm constant terms per document (5.6) and linear build time from 100K to 12.6M POIs.

Broader Impact
:
Timehash
addresses a fundamental challenge in location-based search services, where temporal filtering is critical for user experience. The 99.1% reduction in index size directly translates to reduced storage costs, improved cache efficiency, and better scalability. The open-source implementation in multiple languages (C++, Python, Java, Go, JavaScript, Rust, Kotlin, Scala) facilitates adoption and further research.

Future Work
: Several directions emerge from this work: (1) extending to day/date-level encoding (e.g.,
mon1212
for Monday 12:00–13:00) for weekly patterns; (2) adaptive hierarchies that adjust based on query patterns or data distributions; (3) second-level precision through extended encoding; (4) automated hierarchy selection based on data characteristics; and (5) integration with spatial indexing for multi-dimensional filtering in location-based services.

Timehash
demonstrates that hierarchical decomposition can effectively address the temporal range indexing challenge, achieving dramatic improvements in index efficiency while maintaining precision and supporting complex patterns. We believe this work opens new directions for temporal indexing research and provides a practical solution for large-scale search systems requiring efficient temporal filtering.
