Guardians of the canopy: two new turtle ant species (Hymenoptera: Formicidae: Cephalotes), from Ecuador’s Chocó and Amazonia, revealed by morphology and DNA barcoding

Adrian Troya, Akayky Yumbay, Elian Ilguan, Alex Pazmiño-Palomino, Fernanda Salazar-Buenaño, David A. Donoso, Andrew Mitchell

Abstract

We describe two new species of arboreal ants of the genus Cephalotes Latreille, 1802, from Ecuador: Cephalotes esthelae Ilguan & Troya, sp. nov. and Cephalotes sacha Yumbay & Troya, sp. nov. The former, a member of the pinelii + grandinosus-group, was collected from lowland Chocó and Amazonian forests, while the latter, belonging to the atratus-group, is an inhabitant of the Amazonian region. Diagnoses and detailed morphological descriptions of workers and soldiers are provided, illustrated by high-resolution images. Species identification was further corroborated with mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (CO1) sequences, which confirmed their distinctiveness from morphologically similar congeners. Both species exhibit unique combinations of mesosomal and gastric characters. In addition, we provide the first description of the soldier of the rarely collected C. dentidorsum De Andrade, 1999. These discoveries expand the known diversity of the genus in Ecuador to 33 species, highlighting this region’s role as a hotspot for Neotropical myrmecofauna. A fully illustrated key and checklist of all Cephalotes species known from Ecuador is also presented. The new taxa occur in well-preserved but increasingly threatened forests, ecosystems subject to high rates of deforestation and mining activity, potentially endangering their populations. The integration of molecular and morphological evidence provides a robust framework for delimiting species such as in this morphologically complex and species-diverse genus. We highlight the need for further exploration in understudied regions such as the Chocó and Andean–Amazonian foothills. Continued morphology- and DNA-based research will be crucial to elucidate phylogenetic relationships, assess biogeographic barriers such as the Andes, and inform conservation strategies for Ecuador’s arboreal ant fauna.

Introduction

Cephalotes Latreille, 1802, usually referred to as turtle ants due to their armor resembling a shell (), is currently comprised of 118 extant and 18 fossil, valid species distributed throughout the Neotropical realm, but reaching also the southern Nearctic: Arizona, Texas, and Florida (; ). The countries with most species records of this genus are Brazil (64) and Colombia (45) (), and the biomes with the highest species richness are Amazonia and the Atlantic Forest ().

Cephalotes is polymorphic, both morphologically and behaviorally (), and while species distinction based on workers is relatively straightforward, this is not necessarily the case for the other castes, perhaps because these are not as abundant in collections as are the workers. Recent revisions of the regional Cephalotes fauna of Colombia (), and Brazil () provide excellent tools for the identification of these ants. In general, Cephalotes workers are distinguishable from other similar genera, for example Procryptocerus, its sister genus, by the presence of strongly expanded frontal lobes, hiding the lateral portions of the clypeus, dorsoventrally slightly flattened bodies, and most species bearing spines or projections on the mesosoma, petiole and postpetiole (; ).

Cephalotes is adapted to the arboreal life (; ; ) with all known species nesting in preexisting cavities in the stems of canopy or understory trees (). In general, their diet is primarily composed of extrafloral nectar and hemipteran honeydew, but they also routinely consume pollen and nitrogenous vertebrate waste, such as bird droppings and mammalian urine that accumulate on plant surfaces (). According to , their ecological specialization is based on the colonization of pre-existing wood cavities, which has been particularly important for the evolution of the soldier caste. The soldiers use their armored heads to block the nest entrances against competitors. In contrast, the flattened legs and body with lateral projections present in the workers of many species evolved possibly as a consequence of gliding (). These integrated adaptations to arboreal life possibly influenced their diversification and evolutionary success, enabling them to occupy a wide range of microhabitats across the Neotropics ().

Cephalotes was first proposed by Latreille (1802) based on Linnaeus (1758) Formica atrata (currently Cephalotes atratus). The genus has a long taxonomic history with numerous names being synonymized under it (see and , for a review). Although the monophyly of Cephalotes as sister to Procryptocerus has been demonstrated before (; ; ), its internal classification in 24 species-groups, proposed by , were tested by , later confirmed in , who showed that some of these groups are not monophyletic. More recently, , based on the analysis of ultraconserved elements, proposed an internal conformation of 15 species-groups which need further taxonomic work to stabilize the internal nomenclature of the genus. The current knowledge about the taxonomy and species distribution of Cephalotes in northwestern South America is mainly covered by the works of , , and . For Ecuador, provided the first list with distribution records for 20 species. Subsequently, recorded six additional species in the country. Since then, some potential new taxa have been identified from several collections in this country.

The present study advances the knowledge of the Cephalotes fauna of Ecuador by describing two new species from the coastal and Amazonian regions using an integrative approach based on morphological and molecular evidence. These taxa are assigned to the pinelii + grandinosus and atratus groups (sensu
). Furthermore, we provide the first description of the soldier caste for the rarely collected C. dentidorsum De Andrade, 1999. Finally, we build a comprehensive identification key and uptaded checklist for all 33 recorded species in Ecuador.

DNA sequencing and phylogenetic reconstruction

We used the subunit one of the cytochrome c oxidase (CO1) mitochondrial gene complex to infer the possible phylogenetic placement of our new species in the Cephalotes CO1 tree, but also their placement within their respective species-groups sensu
: atratus-group for C. sacha sp. nov., and pinelii + grandinosus-group for C. esthelae sp. nov.

Except for C. esthelae, the sequences of all species, including the outgroups, were downloaded from Bold Systems, version 4 (boldsystems.org). Of the 2,300 available sequences we selected only 294 ( and ) based on the following criteria: sequences without contamination and stop codons; sequences with more than 300 base pairs; in case of species or morphospecies with numerous sequenced specimens from a single site, we selected only two representatives per site, per country; sequences lacking voucher images were not selected unless these were linked to previously published studies related to the systematics of Cephalotes, e.g., , , ; and finally, we did not select sequences of species clearly misplaced in the topology when performing prior phylogenetic tests. Of the 294 analyzed sequences, 85 were generated in the present study, and are labeled with “MEPN” in the column “Collection Code” of the .

Genomic DNA of C. esthelae sp. nov. was extracted from two legs per specimen using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen Sciences, Germantown, MD, USA), with the following protocol: (1) Legs immersion in 100 μL of lysis solution in a 1.5 mL tube; (2) Legs grinding; (3) Addition of 0.5 μL of proteinase K and vortex for 2 s; (4) Incubation in water bath overnight at 55 °C; (5) Addition of 0.5 μL RNAase and water bath for 30 min/37 °C; (6) Addition of 35 μL of protein precipitation and centrifuge for 3 min/13,000 rpm; (7) Extraction of supernatant and transfer to a sterilized 1.5 mL tube; (8) Addition of 150 μL of isopropanol for DNA precipitation, and centrifuge for 3 min/13,000 rpm; (9) Isopropanol extraction; (10) DNA washing through addition of 150 μL of 70% ethanol, and centrifuge for 3 min/13,000 rpm; (11) Ethanol extraction and drying of ethanol remnants at room temperature for 3–5 min; (12) DNA elution in 20 μL of ultrapure water. The gene region was amplified following a two-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR): first step: 1 min/94 °C; five cycles (30 s/94 °C; 40 s/47 °C; 10 min/72 °C); second step: 30 s/94 °C; 30 cycles (40 s/52 °C; 1 min/72 °C; 10 min/72 °C). Forward primer LCO1490: 5′-GGT CAACAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTGG-3′ and reverse primer HC02198: 5′-TAA ACT TCA GGG TGACCA AAA AAT CA-3′ from . The PCRs and DNA isolations were carried out at the Zoologische Staatssammlung München, Germany. The amplicons were sent to the University of Munich’s Biozentrum facility for Sanger sequencing.

The sequences were aligned using MAFFT version 7 () and the resulting block was visually inspected for unaligned sites which were removed. The length of the final block consisted of 654 base pairs.

We built three trees based on the following sets of sequences: 1. ‘Cephalotes_total’ represented by the whole set of 294 sequences (); 2. ‘Cephalotes_atratus-group’ represented by the available sequenced species in the atratus species-group (); and 3. ‘Cephalotes_pinelii-grandinosus-group’ represented by the available sequenced species in the pinelii + grandinosus species-group (). The aligned sequences were submitted to the Cyberinfrastructure for Phylogenetic Research—CIPRES (), and the dataset was analyzed with IQ-TREE (; ), a maximum likelihood-based tree inference package, using the following parameters: model of molecular evolution TIM2 + F + I + G4, based in the Bayesian information criterium—BIC, as implemented in ModelFinder (). The node supports were calculated using 1,000 rounds of ultrafast bootstrap () replicates (as recommended in ), together with the Shimodaira–Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test- SH-aLRT, and the Bayesian-like transformation of the aLRT–aBayes () branch support methods. The trees were rooted with species of Pheidole (P. zeteki Smith, P. biconstricta Mayr) and Procryptocerus (P. adlerzi Mayr, P. regularis Emery), which according to are the closest lineages to Cephalotes. The topologies were visualized and edited in FigTree () and Adobe Illustrator® (Adobe Inc., Park Avenue, San Jose, CA, USA).

Species concept

We follow ’s concept, which defines species as groups of interbreeding natural populations which are reproductively isolated from others. Since reproductive isolation cannot be directly assessed in the examined material, species boundaries were inferred from morphology. We interpret the examined specimens as being members of new species based on their morphological similarity, expressed in unique sets of external traits differentiating them from all other known closely related species, thus suggesting reproductive isolation.

Results

CO1 Cephalotes tree and molecular discrimination of the new species

Our CO1 Cephalotes tree contains 58 valid species and ten morphospecies (). Overall, within group species composition is in agreement with 11 of the 15 species-groups proposed in . These four conflicting groups are the pallens, pinelii (South American) + grandinosus, texanus + bimaculatus + pinelii (Central American), and wheeleri. Besides this, the pallens and the pinelii + grandinosus groups came out as paraphyletic in our analysis: C. pallidus, a member of the pallens group, is sister to C. umbraculatus (umbraculatus group), whereas the newly described species, C. esthelae, came out as sister to most species of the pallens group, although from a morphological perspective we suspect it might belong to the pinelii + grandinosus group (see the diagnosis of this group under the taxonomic account). For this reason, we suggest a provisional placement of C. esthelae under such group. Another major disagreement from our analysis with respect to that of consists in the internal arrangement of species-groups. For example, in our phylogeny the atratus group is not at the base of the tree and is sister to the basalis group. In contrast, in , the atratus group is recovered as sister to all other species-groups ( of their phylogeny). There are some potential causes for these inconsistencies, among them, the lower phylogenetic resolution of the CO1 above species level. We will expand on this topic in the discussion.

As to the placement of species within each species-group we noted some variations with respect to phylogeny. however, include in their work 24 species and various other morphospecies which we do not include here because there are no available CO1 sequences for them yet. Possibly, the most striking difference in within-group species relationships between our study and that of , is in the pallens group, where the included species are split in two separate groups: the first consisting of C. pallens, C. porrasi, C. patellaris, and two unidentified species from Belize and Costa Rica, and a second group consisting of C. pallidus and an unidentified, possibly new species, C. YASsp1, from French Guiana (). The first pallens group comes out as sister to the new species, C. esthelae, while the second pallens group comes out as sister to C. umbraculatus, a monotypic species-group.

The new Amazonian species, C. sacha, which belongs to the atratus species-group, comes out as sister to the Central American C. alfaroi in our phylogeny. Both are genetically closer to C. serraticeps, the nodal support values for these relationships are relatively high (). Cephalotes alfaroi shows about 14% of genetic divergence from C. sacha, as measured by the number of substitutions per site (). Cephalotes serraticeps, on the other hand, which is also morphologically similar to C. sacha, has about 16% genetic divergence with it. These three species are closer to C. atratus than they are to C. opacus and C. placidus. Cephalotes oculatus is the only member of the atratus group not represented in the atratus species-group.

Cephalotes esthelae is morphologically most similar to C. persimilis De Andrade, 1999 and C. persimplex De Andrade, 1999 (see further details under the taxonomic treatment of C. esthelae), but as said before, it does not come out as sister, not even closer to them in the Cephalotes CO1 tree (). Cephalotes esthelae shows about 17–20% of genetic divergence from C. persimilis and C. persimplex, and about 20% from C. grandinosus (), which is also morphologically similar to C. esthelae. Despite the morphological resemblance among these four species, C. esthelae exhibits a pronounced genetic divergence from them. However, the node subtending this specific lineage shows low support values, indicating that its phylogenetic placement between the pallens and pinelii + grandinosus groups remains unstable in this topology ().

Comments

The workers of C. esthelae, as compared to members of the pinelii + grandinosus species-group, the group to which we suspect this species might be part of, are medium-sized (WL: 1.01–1.16 mm), and considering only the head size, this species is amongst the largest (HL: 0.92–1.09 mm; HW: 1.15–1.28 mm). In contrast, the average head size of the workers of the other pinelii + grandinosus members is HL: 0.79–1.12 mm, and HW: 0.87–1.25 mm. The soldiers, however, exhibit a significantly longer head (HL: 1.60 mm) compared to that of the other known soldiers of the pinelii + grandinosus species-group (HL: 1.12–1.37 mm). From a morphological perspective C. persimilis and C. persimplex are the closest species to C. esthelae, which differs from them in the following: in frontal view, the head is broader than the pronotum by about one maximum eye length (), while in both C. persimilis and C. persimplex the head is almost as broad as the pronotum (CASENT0173700, CASENT0922544); the posterior cephalic margin has a median concavity ( and ), which is absent in C. persimilis and C. persimplex. The worker of C. foliaceus is the only known caste in the pinelii + grandinosus group with a similarly big head (HL: 1.10 mm; HW: 1.43 mm), but this is easily distinguished from the worker of C. esthelae by the lamella of the first gastral tergite, which in C. esthelae runs posteriad to about the median gastral region (), whereas in the worker of C. foliaceus the lamella surrounds the complete gastral margin (CASENT0904925). Cephalotes esthelae and C. persimplex are the only species in the genus showing subtriangular-shaped canaliculate hairs on the dorsum of head and mesosoma. These are usually discernible with high magnification (≥60×).

A single nontype worker (), collected near Lumbaqui town (Sucumbíos province) in a well-preserved Amazonian cloud forest, to the eastern Andean chain, shows a longer gaster (GL: 1.51 mm) as compared to the workers of the type material (GL: 1.31–1.42 mm) which were collected in lowland rain forests in Esmeraldas province, to the western side of the Andes. In addition, the gastric hairs of this Amazonian specimen are smaller and less abundant () as compared to those of the specimens of the type series.

Distribution notes

Cephalotes esthelae has so far been found exclusively in Ecuador, inhabiting well-preserved terra firme forests with an elevation range between 120 m and 550 m. In the northern part of this country, its distribution reaches pristine forests near a small river called Puchuchoa, close to the town of Lumbaqui, in the western side of the province Sucumbíos. This is a transition zone between the lowland rain forests of Amazonia and the Andean foothills, mostly dominated by pre-mountainous cloud forests. To the west, this species is found in lowland well-preserved forests of the Reserva Ecológica Cotacachi Cayapas, province of Esmeraldas, which is part of the southern section of the Chocó Biogeographic. These ecosystems, characterized by their high biodiversity and endemism, are currently under increasing anthropogenic pressure due to high deforestation rates and changes in land use (; ), with still unknown, but likely negative impacts on the populations of many species.

Comments

Within the atratus species-group, C. sacha is relatively small (WL: 2.20–2.62), and except for C. serraticeps and C. alfaroi, its morphologically closest taxa, C. sacha differs significantly from the other group members: C. atratus, C. placidus, C. oculatus, and C. opacus. In regards to C. serraticeps, C. sacha differs in the following characters: in dorsal view, the promesonotum of the worker lacks a Y-shaped carina (present in C. serraticeps) (); the anterior projection of the pronotal spine is almost six times shorter than the spine’s length (in C. serraticeps it is about three times shorter than the spine’s length); in lateral view, the mesonotum and propodeum make an obtuse angle of about 150° () (in C. serraticeps is about 180° or more, sometimes no angle is noticed) (see Fig. 11C in ); in frontal view, the interspinal distance () is narrower than the corresponding in C. serraticeps. In the soldiers, the occipital corner has a single acute carina (in C. serraticeps it has three distinct pointed teeth); the meso- and metapleural plates are foveate (pleural foveae are absent in C. serraticeps).

As of Cephalotes alfaroi we examined the image of the holotype worker (CASENT0904899) on AntWeb, and distinguished the following differences with respect to C. sacha: well-marked pleural striae (in C. sacha the pleural striae are shallower, less marked than in C. alfaroi); dorsum of gaster smooth and glossy, and in general, body relatively shiny (in C. sacha the gastral dorsum is opaque and striate, as well as the rest of the body); the propodeal spiracle is about three spiracular diameters off the propodeal dorsum (about 1.5–2 spiracular diameters in C. sacha). Regarding a non-type C. alfaroi worker from Corcovado National Park, at the pacific southwestern coastal region of Costa Rica (CASENT0040145), identified by Brian Fisher on AntWeb, we noticed the gastral dorsum has slight longitudinal striae anteriorly, but the rest of the integument is smooth and glossy, as in the holotype, which is from Jimenez, Limón province, in the northeastern Atlantic side of that country.

As to the soldier of C. alfaroi, its posterior vertexal border (near the occipital foramen) has a pair of well-developed teeth (absent in C. sacha); the propodeal spines of C. alfaroi are longer than those of C. sacha; the petiole of C. alfaroi is devoid of spines or teeth dorsally (in C. sacha the petiole has two well-developed, but short spines).

Distribution notes.
Cephalotes sacha is so far, only known from Ecuador. It inhabits well-preserved Amazonian rainforests, from approximately 190 m up to 600 m of elevation. To the northern range its distribution reaches the forests of the Aguarico river, near Lumbaqui, a well-preserved area in western Sucumbíos province, close to the southern Colombian border. These forests are located in a transition zone between the Amazonian lowlands and the Andean foothills, which are currently subject to strong anthropic pressure (). To the south its distribution reaches the southern region of Parque Nacional Yasuní, near the Cononaco river, just at the border of Orellana and Pastaza provinces, and close to the western Peruvian border. This area is mostly composed by pristine, lowland rainforests showing a high annual rainfall (). This latter region of Parque Nacional Yasuní is arguably the most well-preserved site of the whole Ecuadorian Amazonia.

Natural history notes

The two specimens from Cononaco river were collected from a Myrtaceae tree, close to a stream. In winter, this collection site is probably flooded as judged by the water marks on the base of the Myrtaceae tree trunk.

Comments

Our first line of evidence that led us to determine the examined soldiers are C. dentidorsum is based on the comparison of a C. dentidorsum worker (MEPN28208) from the eastern Ecuadorian Amazon, previously reported by , which matches the morphology of the worker CASENT0922532, from the eastern Amazonian Peru, imaged in AntWeb. This specimen and a soldier were found by Phil Ward on the same plant of the genus Tachigali Aubl. (“Tachigalia” on Ward’s label), thus bearing the same collector’s code: P. S. Ward 8657. Based on key to the Brazilian Cephalotes, the C. dentidorsum soldier (MEPNINV28455), from Orellana, Ecuador, keyed out as C. adolphi. This is not surprising because these two sister species are highly similar (see details below) but inhabit different biomes in terms of mostly plant composition. Cephalotes adolphi is restricted to mid-eastern Brazil, mainly the Cerrado biome (, ), whereas C. dentidorsum have only been found in Amazonian habitats () did not examine C. dentidorsum specimens, it was not the scope of their work, so the characters they propose in their key, which lead to C. adolphi, might also lead to C. dentidorsum in absence of additional informative characters.

This rarely collected species is phylogenetically placed in the C. angustus group sensu
, confirmed in , but sensu
 is placed in the angustus + fiebrigi + bruchi group. Workers and soldiers of C. dentidorsum are highly similar to those of the also rarely collected C. adolphi. The workers differ only in the sculpture of the first gastral segment being opaque and with shallow punctae in C. adolphi, and smooth and shining in C. dentidorsum. However, the soldiers apparently differ in their cephalic index (longer head in C. adolphi, slightly wider in C. dentidorsum) and in the shape of the anapleural groove (well-impressed in C. adolphi, weakly impressed in C. dentidorsum). To us, these traits do not seem clear enough to distinguish between these two species. Their status as good taxa, in our opinion, is supported mainly by their differing geographic distribution, and not necessarily by their morphology. Although western Amazonia and the Cerrado differ indeed in their climatic regimes and floristic composition, there is no current evidence of barriers to gene flow between populations in this South American region. Since C. dentidorsum and C. adolphi have been so rarely collected, and thus far, only two specimens, one of each species, have been CO1 sequenced (see the group angustus + fiebrigi + bruchi in ), both with the same BIN (Barcode Index Number), and a pairwise genetic distance of less than 2%, we stress on the need to obtain additional material from both biomes because it remains to be clarified whether they are different species and not populational variants of a single species.

Discussion

About the placement of the new species in their respective species-groups

Molecular data usually enables detection of genetic divergence that may not be apparent from morphology alone, and this is particularly relevant for species that exhibit certain phenotypic plasticity or have evolved in isolation (). From our phylogeny, C. sacha forms a well-supported group with its similar species C. alfaroi and C. serraticeps (). Conversely, we could not reach a consensus as to the placement of C. esthelae in a species-group because our morphological observations and genetic analyses lead us to differing conclusions. However, C. esthelae exhibits a highly pronounced genetic divergence, >17%, from its known morphologically closest relatives, C. persimilis, C. persimplex, and C. grandinosus (), which supports the status as a distinct species. Except for a few characters in the worker caste, notably the bigger head of C. esthelae as compared to the other three species, they are overall very similar. Thus, we expected these taxa to come out closely related in our Cephalotes tree, but they did not. This could be explained, in part, due to the lower phylogenetic resolution of a single mitochondrial locus at deeper nodes, compared to the multi-loci phylogenomic approach, based on Ultraconserved Elements (UCEs), used by .

Single mitochondrial loci have inherent limitations for inferring phylogenetic relationships at deep taxonomic levels, these limitations stem from the fact that a single gene captures only one genealogy, which do not necessarily reflect that of the taxon, missing the variance in tree structure across the genome (). In addition, mitochondrial genes exhibit a lower substitution rate, making them less phylogenetically informative (). Although our CO1-based tree partially recovered the species composition of Cephalotes species-groups in 11 of the 15 clades recognized in , its resolution was insufficient to resolve the relationships among species in the genus, and this was particularly evident in the variable position of C. esthelae in the two trees depicted in and .

A closer examination of the four sequences of C. esthelae in the alignment () shows various non-homology sites as compared to its known morphologically closest species. The resulting genetic distance may be explained by the long geographic distance, more than a thousand kilometers, separating the Chocoan populations of C. esthelae from those of C. persimilis from Minas Gerais (Cerrado and Atlantic Forest biomes, southeastern Brazil), and from the Amazonian C. persimplex. Both the Andes as dispersal barrier, as well as differing climatic, ecological and physiognomic conditions of the habitats where these species live, are potential explanatory variables. However, with respect to C. grandinosus, geographic distance may not be sufficient to explain the observed genetic divergence because the examined records of C. esthelae are relatively close to site records of C. grandinosus, for example, in Panama and Costa Rica. Also, these two species inhabit the same biome. Other factors like incomplete taxon sampling, structure of the habitats and microhabitats (), changes in feeding strategies (), unidentified geographic-ecological barriers, and even the non-optimal field and museum preserving conditions of the C. esthelae specimens, could hold cues to explain such result.

In addition, the sequences of C. esthelae showed some ambiguous sites which we assume could be the result of a suboptimal preservation in the museum benches. Before sequencing, these specimens were stored in 75% ethanol for about twenty years, but it was never replaced with new, fresh ethanol (A. Troya, 2025, personal observation). This pattern of DNA fragmentation and degradation has been documented before in museum specimens preserved under such conditions ().

Despite these limitations, mitochondrial barcoding remains a valuable tool for species delimitation and discovery of cryptic diversity in ants (; ). The molecular uncertainty associated with C. esthelae underscores the need to obtain further fresh material, and thus higher-quality DNA of that species, which may eventually confirm our proposal of its placement in the pinelii + grandinosus group.

Putative signals of divergence in a C. esthelae population

Cephalotes esthelae and C. sacha are currently endemics for Ecuador, and further sampling will likely unveil additional site records in both Chocó and in Amazonia. As to C. esthelae, we here consider both populations, from Chocó and Amazonia, as belonging to a single species. However, the Amazonian population, represented by a unique individual (, MEPNINV18178), differs from those of Chocó in the shape of the gaster, which is longer in such Amazonian specimen (see comments under C. esthelae description), and its gastric pilosity is smaller and less abundant as compared to that of the Chocoan populations. These differences in morphology possibly represent signatures of lineage divergence, which could be occurring due to a potential decline or complete breakdown of interbreeding between the Chocoan and Amazonian populations that are separated by the Andean mountain chain. We do not have DNA data of the Amazonian specimen to support this assumption, however, the Andes as a barrier restricting gene flow (; ), and the distance separating these populations, approximately 200 km, are factors to consider in future analyses.

Evidence of speciation events promoted by vicariance or dispersion in the context of the Andes uplift in northwestern South America has been demonstrated before with birds (), mammals (), frogs (), among other groups, but also with ants, for example, Atta cephalotes ().

Because of the variation in morphology of the Amazonian population of C. esthelae as compared to the populations of Chocó, we excluded this specimen from the type material. This responds to the need to avoid taxonomic ambiguities by prioritizing stable diagnostic characters over geographic variants, a criterion applied in groups with morphological plasticity ().

Additions to current knowledge in the distribution of other Cephalotes species

While examining the geographic distribution of species records in our dataset, we noted that some historical records of C. alfaroi originating in Brazil (Roraima and Amazonas), which are currently listed on AntWeb, are misidentifications. To our current knowledge, that species occurs only in Costa Rica and Panama (; AntWeb.org). Besides locality, coordinates and collection date, no other information is provided on AntWeb, and more importantly, the four records are not validated by a taxonomist. did not mention this species in their Cephalotes revision for Brazil. Through the examination of ant collections from Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Brazil, we did not find records of C. alfaroi. We suggest the Brazilian records could be either C. sacha or C. serraticeps.

Implications for the conservation of the habitats home of C. esthelae and C. sacha

The regions where the new species originate from are highly anthropized and threatened (A. Troya, 2020, personal observation), thus both species may be considered potentially vulnerable. In the Chocó region, mining concessions have caused severe deforestation and fragmentation, while in the Amazonian Lumbaqui town, agriculture expansion and urbanization continue to degrade natural habitats (). These impacts threaten the animals that depend on tree canopies for nesting and dispersal (). Although the populations of C. sacha live in the overall well-preserved Parque Nacional Yasuní, it faces significant threats due to extractive activities, particularly oil exploitation, which compromise both ecosystem integrity and local indigenous communities (). Deforestation remains a leading cause of biodiversity loss in Neotropical insects, disrupting ecological functions and reducing habitat availability ().

The known populations of C. esthelae are represented by only 12 individuals collected from four sites: three in the Chocó region and one in the Amazon. All specimens were collected more than 20 years ago, at a time when these habitats were already under threat from human-driven activities such as commercial deforestation, slash-and-burn agriculture, and illegal mining. Although the sampled Chocoan sites, Salto del Bravo, La Tabla, and Pajonal, were relatively pristine and showed little direct human impact, there was already concern about imminent disturbance, particularly from mining companies seeking to expand operations in the San Miguel River basin, the main access route to these areas. Local Afro-descendant and Indigenous Chachi communities have been increasingly targeted by such companies, often attracted by short-term economic incentives. These temporary benefits, offered in exchange for access to their territories, risk causing long-term damage to fragile ecosystems. Consequently, effective conservation in these areas requires policymakers to adopt frameworks that prioritize the enduring integrity of nature while ensuring sustainable local livelihoods.

Conclusion

The exploration and documentation of new species is essential to filling gaps in taxonomic-biodiversity knowledge. The present new species hypotheses add to such aim. This study enhances our understanding of Cephalotes diversity in Ecuador and highlights the need to conserve endangered habitats and increase field research in understudied regions. To address these challenges, it is crucial to implement conservation strategies that promote habitat restoration, involving local communities in the protection and sustainable management of both degraded and well-preserved ecosystems. Further research will be required to confirm the presence of the current two new species in other Neotropical regions, thus improving our understanding of their distribution and biogeography.
