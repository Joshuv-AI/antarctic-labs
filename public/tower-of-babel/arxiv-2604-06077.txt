Simulating Thermal Properties of Bose–Hubbard Models on a Quantum Computer
Simon Becker, Cambyse Rouzé, Robert Salzmann

Abstract
While recent advances have established efficient quantum algorithms for preparing Gibbs states of finite-dimensional systems, comparable complexity results for bosonic and other infinite-dimensional models remain unexplored. We introduce the first general rigorous Gibbs sampling framework for bosonic many-body systems, showing that physically relevant bosonic models admit gapped dissipative generators, enabling efficient preparation of thermal states. Although our results hold for broad classes of models, we illustrate them using Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonians, both within and beyond the mean-field regime. In both cases, we show that the associated dissipative generators maintain a positive spectral gap, thereby implying exponential convergence to the thermal state. Our argument in the multi-mode case is based on a finite-rank reduction of the dissipative dynamics, which allows us to control the generator via compact perturbations and deduce the discreteness of the spectrum and the stability of the gap. We apply our results to provide efficient preparation of the corresponding Gibbs state on qubit hardware, and by that a quantum algorithm to compute thermal properties of the associated model. This provides the first mathematically controlled route to Gibbs sampling in infinite-dimensional systems, with implications for quantum simulation, thermalization, and many-body complexity, where quantum advantages may arise.

Introduction

The simulation of ground and thermal states of many-body quantum systems is widely regarded as one of the most promising routes toward near-term quantum advantage. Recent progress has occurred on several fronts: advances in the complexity-theoretic understanding of quantum many-body systems

;
Gilyén et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2025a

;

;
Mann and Minko 2024

While most existing results focus on locally finite-dimensional systems, such as spin models or fermionic lattices, bosonic systems have received far less attention in the computational complexity literature. Historically, discrete systems have dominated quantum algorithmic studies because they parallel the classical digital-computation paradigm, while bosonic systems were mainly explored in quantum communication and information theory

Recently, however, a rigorous complexity-theoretic framework for bosonic quantum systems has begun to emerge

From a computational standpoint, bosonic systems present unique challenges. Classical approximation techniques that are highly effective for spin systems often fail in infinite-dimensional settings. II
Quantum Gibbs sampling for continuous-variable systems

The primary task considered in this letter is that of the preparation of the Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}(H):=e^{-\beta H}/\Tr(e^{-\beta H})
of the Hamiltonian
H
of an infinite dimensional quantum system at inverse temperature
\beta>0
. In order for
\sigma_{\beta}(H)
to be well-defined, we further assume the Gibbs hypothesis, namely that
\Tr(e^{-\lambda H})<\infty
for all
\lambda>0
. To achieve this, we consider a family of dissipative quantum Gibbs samplers
Ding et al. 2025a
;
Gilyén et al. 2024
extended in
Becker et al. 2026a
to infinite-dimensional systems. In a nutshell, the jumps associated with the generator
\mathcal{L}_{\smash{\sigma_{E},\widehat{f},H}}
of the dissipation formally consist of the following dressing of a family of bare jump operators
\{A^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}=\{(A^{\alpha})^{\dagger}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle L^{\alpha}(H)\!:=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itH}\!A^{\alpha}e^{-itH}f(t)\,dt,

(1)

where the filter function
f
is chosen so that the Gibbs state is stationary:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{E},\widehat{f},H}(\sigma_{\beta}(H))=0.

(2)

In fact, the so-called KMS condition
\overline{\widehat{f}(\nu)}\!=\!\widehat{f}(-\nu)e^{-\beta\nu/2}
on the Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\nu)=\int f(t)e^{it\nu}dt
ensures that the generator is self-adjoint with respect to a well-chosen inner product
\langle.,.\rangle_{\sigma_{\beta}(H)}
(see Appendix
A
). The parameter
\sigma_{E}>0
controls both the Hamiltonian simulation time required to implement the dissipation on a quantum computer and the spectral gap,
\operatorname{gap}(L_{\smash{\sigma_{E},\widehat{f},H}}),
of the generator acting on the Hilbert space associated with
\langle.,.\rangle_{\sigma_{\beta}(H)}
. The latter controls the mixing time of the dynamics when initialized in a well-chosen state
\rho_{\operatorname{ini}}
of the system with
\rho_{\operatorname{ini}}\leq\mathfrak{c}\,\sigma_{\beta}(H)
:

\displaystyle t_{\operatorname{mix}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\inf\big\{t\geq 0:\,\big\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{E},\widehat{f},H}}(\,\rho_{\operatorname{ini}}\,)-\sigma_{\beta}(H)\big\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\log\big({\mathfrak{c}}/{\varepsilon}\big)}{\operatorname{gap}(L_{\sigma_{E},\widehat{f},H})}\,,\qquad\text{ given }\varepsilon\in(0,1)\,.

(3)

Intuitively, as
\sigma_{E}\to 0
, the amount of resources required to implement the evolution up to time
t_{\operatorname{mix}}(\varepsilon)
blows up, while the spectral gap decreases as
\sigma_{E}
increases. In our companion paper
Becker et al. 2026a
,we also identified single-mode settings where a bad choice of filter
f
may lead to a vanishing gap, resulting in infinite mixing times.
Building on these insights, our main goal is to prove that, in the limit
\sigma_{E}\to\infty
, the resulting generator
\mathcal{L}_{\smash{\widehat{f},H}}
remains gapped for a broad class of physically relevant bosonic systems, and to develop end-to-end schemes for computing their key physical properties.

In Section
III
below, we detail our findings concerning the positivity of the spectral gap for the Bose–Hubbard model. The common strategy underlying our results is as follows. First, we identify solvable or approximately solvable reference models, typically Gaussian or number-diagonal ones, whose associated Gibbs samplers admit explicit spectral control. Second, we show that physically relevant perturbations preserve positivity of the dissipative gap and their fixed points stay close to that of the unperturbed dynamics. Third, we apply the finite-dimensional approximation scheme from
Becker et al. 2026a
to connect the infinite-dimensional dynamics to algorithmically tractable approximations, thereby demonstrating that Gibbs states of Bose-Hubbard models can be prepared efficiently on qubit-based quantum computers, see Section
V
.

III
Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonians

A broad class of examples is provided by the Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonians: on a finite lattice
\Lambda=\llbracket 1,L\rrbracket^{D}
with
n=L^{D}
modes, its Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{\operatorname{BH}}\!=\!-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\mathrm{h.c.})\!+\!\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{2}-N_{i})\!-\!\mu\sum_{i}N_{i}.

Here
a_{i}
and
a_{i}^{\dagger}
are bosonic annihilation and creation operators,
N_{i}=a_{i}^{\smash{\dagger}}a_{i}
,
J
is the hopping amplitude,
U>0
the on-site repulsion, and
\mu
the chemical potential. For such Hamiltonians, the ground-state problem restricted to finite-particle sectors is known to be computationally hard
Childs et al. 2014
, while finite-temperature quantities necessarily involve all particle-number sectors and therefore the full infinite-dimensional Fock space. This infinite-dimensionality creates a serious obstacle for classical algorithms. In particular, existing classical partition-function algorithms typically rely on truncating local boson numbers with a cutoff that grows with system size, leading at best to quasi-polynomial runtime in relevant regimes
Tong and Kuwahara 2025
. This motivates the search for quantum algorithms that avoid such classical truncation barriers.

III.1
Mean-field regime

For the mean-field decoupling of the Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonian, the effective single-site model is

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{MF}}=-\mu N+\frac{U}{2}N(N-1)-\bar{\psi}\,a-\psi\,a^{\dagger}+|\psi|^{2}.

We perform a perturbative spectral analysis in the superfluid order parameter
\psi
, which corresponds to a genuinely unbounded perturbation of the Hamiltonian. For sufficiently small
|\psi|
, the spectrum remains discrete, high-energy eigenvalues stay isolated, and the corresponding eigenvectors remain close to the Fock basis. Comparing the resulting Dirichlet form with that of a diagonal reference model yields a positive spectral gap for the associated self-adjoint Gibbs sampler (see Appendix
C
for details):

Theorem III.1
.

The mean-field Bose–Hubbard sampler
L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},H_{\operatorname{MF}}}
associated to the filter function
\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(\nu)=\exp\big(-{\sqrt{1+(\beta\nu)^2}+ \beta\nu}/{4}\big)
and bare jumps
\{a,a^{\dagger}\}
is gapped for sufficiently small
|\psi|
.

Our analysis likely applies to the full Bose-Hubbard model for finite lattice sites, though not necessarily in the thermodynamic limit. It remains an important open problem to show that the generator
L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},H_{\operatorname{BH}}}
remains gapped independently of the number of modes.

III.2
Regularized Bose–Hubbard models

In order to make a step in the direction of proving the spectral gap for the full Bose-Hubbard model, we consider two physically relevant regularizations, designed to model both superfluid and Mott-insulating regimes while controlling high-occupation sectors
Fisher et al. 1989
. First, we choose the following parametrization of the Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonian over a
D
-dimensional lattice of side-length
L

\displaystyle H_{\operatorname{BH}}

\displaystyle=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\operatorname{h.c.}+\eta\sum_{i}N_{i}+V,

where
\eta,\eta^{\prime},J\in\mathbb{R}
,
U>0
, and
V:=\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i}
. One regularizes the on-site interaction to study the
superfluid phase

\begin{split}H_{\operatorname{SF}}&\equiv H^{(M^{\prime})}_{\operatorname{SF}}\!=\!-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}\!+\operatorname{h.\!c.}\!+\eta\sum_{i}N_{i}+\Pi^{b}_{M^{\prime}}V\Pi^{b}_{M^{\prime}},\end{split}

for some increasing family of projections
\Pi^{b}_{M^{\prime}}
onto finite dimensional subspaces that we specify below, and where
\eta-2|J|D>0
. We also consider the regularization of the hopping-term to emphasize the on-site interaction, which is particularly relevant for the
Mott-insulating phase

\begin{split}H_{\operatorname{MI}}&\!\equiv\!H^{(M^{\prime})}_{\operatorname{MI}}\!\!=\Pi^{a}_{M^{\prime}}\!\!\left(\!-J\!\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\!a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}\!+\operatorname{h.\!c.}\!+\eta\sum_{i}\!N_{i}\!\right)\!\!\Pi^{a}_{M^{\prime}}\!\!+\!V,\end{split}

for some finite dimensional projections
\Pi^{a}_{M^{\prime}}
. In words,
H_{\operatorname{SF}}
is a finite-rank perturbation of a quadratic Hamiltonian, while
H_{\operatorname{MI}}
is a finite-rank perturbation of a commuting sum of quartic operators
\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i})
. In Appendix
D
, we verify that both truncations approximate the full Bose Hubbard model for sufficiently large truncations.

Lemma III.2
.

Assume
U>0
and
\eta-2D|J|>0
. Then

\displaystyle\Big\|\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{BH}})-\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}})\Big\|_{1}\!\!\leq\!\varepsilon\,\text{ for }\,M^{\prime}=\Omega\Big(n+\log\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)\Big).

The same holds for the Mott-insulator truncation
H_{\operatorname{MI}}
under the only assumption that
U>0
.

Thus, it is enough to prepare the Gibbs state of either
H_{\operatorname{SF}}
or
H_{\operatorname{MI}}
at truncation level
M^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(n)
in order to get an accurate approximation of the Gibbs state of
H_{\operatorname{BH}}
. In our next main result, we prove that at any truncation
M^{\prime}
, the Gibbs samplers associated to either of the two truncations eventually converge to their respective fixed points.

Theorem III.3
.

There exist filter functions
\widehat{f}
such that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, bare jumps
\{a_{i},a_{i}^{\dagger}\}_{i\in[n]}
and truncation
M^{\prime}
, the generator
L_{\widehat{f},H_{\operatorname{SF}}}
and
L_{\widehat{f},H_{\operatorname{MI}}}
are gapped, i.e.

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}(L_{\widehat{f},H_{\operatorname{SF}}})>0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\operatorname{gap}(L_{\widehat{f},H_{\operatorname{MI}}})>0.

Since both models discussed are in particular number preserving, the vacuum
\ket{0}
is an eigenstate with eigenvalue
0
in both cases. Therefore
\outerproduct{0}{0}\leq\mathfrak{c}\sigma_{\beta}(H)
with
\mathfrak{c}\leq\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}(H)=e^{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
for
H\in\{H_{\operatorname{BH}},H_{\operatorname{SF}},H_{\operatorname{MI}}\}
. In view of the upper bound (
3
), choosing
\rho_{\operatorname{ini}}\equiv\outerproduct{0}{0}
, it suffices to lower bound
\operatorname{gap}(L_{\widehat{f},H})
in order to derive explicit bounds on the mixing time of the sampler.

IV
Finite-rank spectral gap under finite-rank perturbations

The proof of Theorem
III.3
follows a general strategy that we summarize now: let a quantum system with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
be described by a Hamiltonian

H=H_{0}+R,

where
H_{0}
is exactly solvable and
R=\Pi R\Pi
acts only on a finite-dimensional low-energy sector selected by an orthogonal projection
\Pi
commuting with
H_{0}
. Writing
\overline{\Pi}:=I-\Pi
,
H_{0}
and
H
are decomposed according to

\mathcal{H}=\overline{\Pi}\mathcal{H}\oplus\Pi\mathcal{H},

and they coincide on
\overline{\Pi}\mathcal{H}
. The two Hamiltonians differ only through a finite-dimensional block, and in particular, their spectral projections agree outside that block. This structural property is inherited by the dressed jump operators. Let
A^{\alpha}
be a possibly unbounded bare jump such that

\Pi A^{\alpha}
extends to a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}
, and consider its associated decorated jump
L^{\alpha}(H)
. Denoting

L^{\alpha}_{\pm,H}:=e^{\pm\beta H/4}L^{\alpha}(H)e^{\mp\beta H/4},

we show that
L^{\alpha}_{\pm,H}=L^{\alpha}_{\pm,H_{0}}+\mathcal{R}^{\alpha}_{\pm}
, where each remainder
\mathcal{R}^{\alpha}_{\pm}
is of finite rank and has vanishing
\overline{\Pi}
-
\overline{\Pi}
block.

IV.1
Perturbations of Gaussian models

To illustrate our method we consider as example the simplest case of a single-mode system (
n=1
), choose the set of bare jumps as
\{a,a^{\dagger}\}\equiv\{A^{-},A^{+}\}
,
\Pi_{M^{\prime}}:={P^{a}_{M^{\prime}}}
as the projection onto the first
M^{\prime}+1
single-mode Fock states, and consider the quadratic Hamiltonian
H_{0}\equiv N=a^{\dagger}a
. Using the canonical commutation relations, it was shown in
Cipriani et al. 2000
that the generator
L_{\widehat{f},H_{0}}
is lower bounded by the so-called ladder-block operator

\displaystyle L_{\operatorname{LB}}(\bullet):=\frac{1}{2}(\nu_{+}-\nu_{-})^{2}(N\bullet+\bullet N)\;-\;\nu_{+}(\nu_{-}-\nu_{+})\,\bullet

where
\nu_{+}:=|\widehat{f}(1)|^{2}
and
\nu_{-}:=|\widehat{f}(-1)|^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\smash{L_{\operatorname{LB}}(\outerproduct{n}{m})}\!=\!\frac{\smash{\nu_{-}\!\!-\!\nu_{+}}}{\smash{2}}\big((\smash{\nu_{-}\!\!\!-\!\nu_{+}})\smash{(n+m)\!-\!2\nu_{+}\!}\big)\smash{\outerproduct{n}{m}}.

(4)

Moreover, we make use of the following:

Lemma IV.1
.

Let
L^{\prime}=L_{\widehat{f},N}+L_{\operatorname{pert}}
be a perturbation of the generator
L_{\widehat{f},N}
of the semigroup converging to the Gibbs states of the number operator
N
. If there exists some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\|L_{\operatorname{pert}}(L_{\operatorname{LB}}-\lambda)^{-1}\|<1
, then
L^{\prime}
has a discrete spectrum and, hence, is gapped.

Then, we consider
H=N+V
for a perturbation of the form
V=\Pi_{M^{\prime}}h(N)\Pi_{M^{\prime}}=\sum_{k\leq M^{\prime}}h(k)\outerproduct{k}{k}
, for some function
h
of the number operator
N
. Given
\omega_{n}:=1+h(n)1_{n\leq M^{\prime}}-h(n-1)1_{n-1\leq M^{\prime}}
,
\alpha_{n}:=\widehat{f}(-\omega_{n})e^{-\beta\omega_{n}/4}
,
\beta_{n}:=\widehat{f}(\omega_{n+1})e^{\beta\omega_{n+1}/4}
,
\gamma:=\sqrt{\nu_{+}\nu_{-}}
, and
\delta_{n}:=n\bigl(|\alpha_{n}|^{2}-\nu_{-}\bigr)+(n+1)\bigl(|\beta_{n}|^{2}-\nu_{+}\bigr)
, we can express

\displaystyle\bigl(L_{\widehat{f},N}-L_{\widehat{f},H}\bigr)(\outerproduct{n}{m})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sqrt{nm}\,\Bigl(\gamma-\alpha_{n}\overline{\alpha_{m}}\Bigr)\outerproduct{n-1}{m-1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{(n+1)(m+1)}\,\Bigl(\gamma-\beta_{n}\overline{\beta_{m}}\Bigr)\outerproduct{n+1}{m+1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{n}+\delta_{m})\outerproduct{n}{m}.

(5)

Moreover, since
\omega_{n}=1
for all
n\geq M^{\prime}+2
, one has

\alpha_{n}=\widehat{f}(-1)e^{-\beta/4}=\widehat{f}(1)e^{\beta/4}=\beta_{n},\qquad\delta_{n}=0.

Therefore
(L_{\widehat{f},N}-L_{\widehat{f},H})(\outerproduct{n}{m})\neq 0
only if
n\leq M^{\prime}+1
or
m\leq M^{\prime}+1
. Combining (
5
) with the eigenvalue decomposition (
4
) and applying Lemma
IV.1
for
\lambda
large enough, directly leads us to the conclusion that
\operatorname{gap}(L_{\widehat{f},H})>0
.

For the superfluid truncation
H_{\operatorname{SF}}
, the reference Hamiltonian is the quadratic hopping model

H_{0}=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\mathrm{h.c.})+\eta\sum_{i}N_{i}.

After diagonalizing its one-particle hopping matrix,
H_{0}
becomes a sum of independent bosonic modes,

H_{0}=\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{k}\,b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}

for some normal modes
b_{k}=\sum_{x}\varphi_{k}(x)\,a_{x}
with orthonormal single particle eigenfunctions

\varphi_{k}(x)=\smash{\big(\frac{2}{L+1}\big)^{D/2}\prod_{\mu=1}^{D}\sin(k_\mu x_\mu)}
and single-particle energies
\varepsilon_{k}=\eta-2J\sum_{\mu}\cos k_{\mu}
. We get immediately

V=\sum_{k}\Bigl(-\eta^{\prime}+\frac{U}{2}\Bigr)b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{k,q,r,s}\Lambda_{kqrs}\,b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{q}^{\dagger}b_{r}b_{s},

where
\Lambda_{kqrs}=\sum_{x}\varphi_{k}(x)\varphi_{q}(x)\varphi_{r}(x)\varphi_{s}(x)
. Choosing
\Pi^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}=(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}
the
n
-fold product projection on to the first
M^{\prime}+1
Fock states associated to the mode operators
\{b,b^{\dagger}\}
, we can extend the previous analysis to the present
n
-mode setting.

IV.2
Perturbation of number-diagonal models

For the Mott-insulating truncation
H_{\operatorname{MI}}
, the reference model is instead the number diagonal Hamiltonian

V=\sum_{i}h(N_{i}),\qquad h(n)=\frac{U}{2}n^{2}-\eta^{\prime}n.

For such models, we prove in the companion paper
Becker et al. 2026a
that filter functions whose Fourier transform decays for negative frequencies inevitably lead to gapless samplers. Instead, we consider a Metropolis-type filter function already introduced in
Ding et al. 2025a
:

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(\nu)=\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{1+(\beta\nu)^{2}}+\beta\nu}{4}\right)

(6)

so the jumps are well defined only in the frequency domain: given the set of Bohr frequencies
B(H)=\operatorname{Sp}(H)-\operatorname{Sp}(H)
of
H:=\sum_{E\in\operatorname{Sp}(H)}EP_{E}
,

\displaystyle L^{\alpha}(H)=\sum_{\nu\in B(H)}\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(\nu)\sum_{E-E^{\prime}=\nu}P_{E}A^{\alpha}P_{E^{\prime}}.

In
(
Becker et al. 2026a
, Theorem 3.5)
, we show that, by choosing the set of bare jumps as
\{a_{i},a_{i}^{\dagger}\}_{i\in[n]}
, the corresponding sampler
L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},V}
is gapped. Here, we show that the system remains gapped when adding the truncated quadratic part
H_{\operatorname{MI}}-V
, with
\Pi_{M^{\prime}}=(P^{a}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}
, where
P^{a}_{M^{\prime}}
denotes the projection onto the first
M^{\prime}+1
Fock states corresponding to the mode operators
\{a,a^{\dagger}\}
. First, in Lemma
B.5
, we show that

\displaystyle L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N_{i})}=-\{A(N_{i}),\bullet\}+K_{i}

(7)

where
K_{i}
is compact on
\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
and
A(N_{i})
is diagonal in the Fock basis and its eigenvalues diverge at large occupation. Note that the decomposition (
7
) is enforced by the quartic nature of
h
, and does not hold for the Gaussian models of Section
IV.1
. Since the anticommutator map above has discrete spectrum given by sums of pairs eigenvalues of
A(N_{i})
(cf. Corollary
B.6
), we have that its resolvent
(\{A(N_{i}),\bullet\}-i)^{\smash{-1}}
is compact on
\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
. By the second resolvent identity (see Lemma
B.7
), we directly get that the resolvent
(L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N_{i})}-i)^{\smash{-1}}
is compact, which implies that
L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N_{i})}
has discrete spectrum and is thus gapped. Finally, in Corollary
B.8
, we argue that the
difference
K^{\prime}:=L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},H_{\operatorname{MI}}}-L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},V}
is compact, so that the above argument extends to

\displaystyle L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},H_{\operatorname{MI}}}=\sum_{i}\big(-\{A(N_{i}),\bullet\}+K_{i}\big)+K^{\prime},

which proves
\operatorname{gap}(L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},H_{\operatorname{MI}}})>0
.

V
End-to-end runtimes

for physical properties

The above findings open the door to rigorous runtime analysis of quantum algorithms based on implementing our constructed Gibbs samplers generated for the estimation of thermal properties of bosonic quantum systems. In Appendix
E
, we combine Theorem
III.3
with
(
Becker et al. 2026a
, Corollary 4.33)
to show that Gibbs states of Bose-Hubbard models can be prepared efficiently on a qubit-based quantum computer:

Theorem V.1
.

Let
M^{\prime}=\operatorname{poly}(n,\log(1/\eps)).
Then the Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}(H^{(M^{\prime})}_{\operatorname{SF}})
can be prepared within
\varepsilon
-trace distance on a quantum computer with

VI
Outlook

In this Letter, we introduced a general framework for quantum Gibbs sampling in continuous-variable many-body systems and proved positive spectral-gap results for the paradigmatic Bose–Hubbard model. These results provide a first rigorous foundation for analyzing the runtime of quantum algorithms that prepare Gibbs states of interacting, infinite-dimensional quantum systems.

Our approach shows that exactly solvable Gaussian or number-diagonal reference models, combined with perturbative stability arguments and finite-rank approximations, offer a robust strategy for establishing fast thermalization in infinite-dimensional settings.

An important next challenge is to obtain quantitative lower bounds on the spectral gap, both for the Bose–Hubbard model and more broadly for other continuous-variable many-body systems. At present, our results establish positivity of the gap, but do not control its scaling with the number of modes.

More broadly, our work opens several directions. On the algorithmic side, it yields rigorous convergence guarantees for quantum algorithms aimed at estimating free energies, response coefficients, and other thermal observables in bosonic systems. On the complexity side, it points to continuous-variable models as promising candidates for quantum advantages in thermal-state simulation
Tong and Kuwahara 2025
. On the physical side, it applies to experimentally relevant platforms such as optical-lattice bosons. Altogether, these results mark a first step toward a complexity theory of thermal-state preparation for interacting continuous-variable quantum matter.

Appendix B
Spectral gap under finite-rank perturbations

Next, we summarize the mechanism underlying spectral gap stability under finite-rank perturbations, referring to our companion paper
Becker et al. 2026b
. The results below are stated with proof sketches and emphasize the structural ideas: finite-rank perturbations of the Hamiltonian only produce finite-rank corrections in the conjugated Lindblad structure, which is sufficient to preserve discreteness of the spectrum and hence a spectral gap.

The first result shows that perturbing the Hamiltonian in a finite-dimensional sector does not affect the jump operators on the infinite-dimensional bulk, and only creates a finite-rank correction.

Lemma B.1

(Finite-rank stability of conjugated jump operators)
.

Let
H_{0}
be self-adjoint, bounded from below, with discrete spectrum, and let
P
be a finite-rank orthogonal projection with
[P,H_{0}]=0
,
Q:=1-P
. Let
R=PRP
be bounded self-adjoint and set
H:=H_{0}+R
. Let
A^{\alpha}
satisfy
P\mathcal{H}\subset D(A^{\alpha})
and
PA^{\alpha}
bounded. Fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
and assume
L_{s}^{\alpha}(H)
,
L_{s}^{\alpha}(H_{0})
are well-defined. Then

L_{s}^{\alpha}(H)=L_{s}^{\alpha}(H_{0})+\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\alpha},\qquad Q\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\alpha}Q=0,

with
\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\alpha}
finite rank.

Proof sketch.

The perturbation only modifies the spectral data inside the finite-dimensional subspace
P\mathcal{H}
; inserting this into the Bohr-frequency expansion shows that all corrections factor through
P\mathcal{H}
, hence are finite rank, as proved in
(
Becker et al. 2026b
, Lemma 3.2)
.
∎

The next step shows that this finite-rank stability propagates to the quadratic expressions appearing in the Lindblad generator. This is crucial, since the generator depends on terms such as
L^{\dagger}L
and
LL^{\dagger}
.

Lemma B.2

(Finite-rank stability of quadratic coefficients)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
B.1
, write
L=L_{s}^{\alpha}(H)
,
L_{0}=L_{s}^{\alpha}(H_{0})
. Then,

L^{\dagger}L-L_{0}^{\dagger}L_{0}=L_{0}^{\dagger}\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{R}^{\dagger}L_{0}+\mathcal{R}^{\dagger}\mathcal{R},\quad LL^{\dagger}-L_{0}L_{0}^{\dagger}=L_{0}\mathcal{R}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{R}L_{0}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{R}\mathcal{R}^{\dagger},

and both differences are finite rank.

Proof sketch.

Each term contains at least one factor of
\mathcal{R}
, so it factors through a finite-dimensional space; this is exactly the mechanism formalized in
(
Becker et al. 2026b
, Lemma 3.3)
.
∎

We can now combine these two ingredients to understand how the full conjugated Lindblad generator changes: all modifications remain confined to finite-rank coefficients.

Lemma B.3

(Finite-rank perturbation of the conjugated generator)
.

With the above notation, let
L_{\widehat{f},H}
,
L_{\widehat{f},H_{0}}
be the conjugated generators and assume

L_{\pm,H}^{\alpha}=L_{\pm,0}^{\alpha}+\mathcal{R}_{\pm}^{\alpha}

with
\mathcal{R}_{\pm}^{\alpha}
finite rank. Then, the difference
L_{\widehat{f},H}-L_{\widehat{f},H_{0}}
decomposes into commutator terms and quadratic contributions whose coefficients are all finite-rank perturbations.

Proof sketch.

Insert the decomposition of the jump operators into the Lindblad structure and expand; finite-rank stability of all coefficients follows from Lemma
B.2
and
(
Becker et al. 2026b
, Prop. 3.4)
.
∎

B.1
Perturbations of Gaussian Hamiltonians

To make this structure concrete, we specialize to the Gaussian reference case. Here the unperturbed generator is explicit, and the perturbation inherits a very rigid structure.
Moreover, this structural control is strong enough to preserve the spectral properties of the generator.

Theorem B.4

(Persistence of discrete spectrum and spectral gap)
.

Let
H_{0}=\sum_{i}N_{i}
,
H=H_{0}+V
with
V=PVP
, and take
A^{(i,-)}=a_{i}
,
A^{(i,+)}=a_{i}^{\dagger}
. If
L_{\widehat{f},H_{0}}
has compact resolvent, then
L_{\widehat{f},H}
also has compact resolvent; in particular the spectrum remains discrete, and simplicity of the eigenvalue
0
implies a nonzero spectral gap.

Proof sketch.

The perturbation splits into finite-rank terms and operators that are infinitesimally bounded with respect to the reference generator; hence it is relatively bounded with bound
0
, which preserves compactness of the resolvent and therefore discreteness and the spectral gap, see
(
Becker et al. 2026b
, Theorem 3.6)
for details.
∎

B.2
Perturbations of powers of the number operator

For powers of the number operator, we restrict ourselves to the filter function (
10

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(\nu)=\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{1+(\beta\nu)^{2}}+\beta\nu}{4}\right).

(26)

Lemma B.5

(Generator for
H=h(N)
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
, set
H:=h(N)
, and let
\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}
be a filter function. Then

\displaystyle L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N)}(X)

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}g_{+}(N)\,X\,g_{+}(N)a+a\,g_{-}(N)\,X\,g_{-}(N)a^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{A_{+},X\}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{A_{-},X\},

(27)

where

g_{+}(N)=\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N+1)-h(N))e^{\beta(h(N+1)-h(N))/4},\quad g_{-}(N)=\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N-1)-h(N))e^{-\beta(h(N)-h(N-1))/4},

and

A_{+}=(N+1)|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N+1)-h(N))|^{2},\qquad A_{-}=\frac{N|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N-1)-h(N))|^{2}}{2}.

Assume

\sqrt{n+1}\,|g_{+}(n)|\to 0,\qquad\sqrt{n}\,|g_{-}(n)|\to 0,\text{ and }(n+1)\,|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(n+1)-h(n))|^{2}\to 0.

Then
a^{\dagger}g_{+}(N),\,g_{+}(N)a,\,a\,g_{-}(N),\,g_{-}(N)a^{\dagger}
are compact on
\mathcal{H}
, the gain terms define compact maps on
\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, and
A_{+}
is compact. Consequently,

L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N)}=-\{A_{-},\cdot\}+K,

where
K
is compact on
\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

Let
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the number basis. Since
H=h(N)
is diagonal, the Bohr frequencies of
a^{\dagger}
and
a
are
h(n+1)-h(n)
and
h(n-1)-h(n)
, hence

L_{+}(H)=a^{\dagger}\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N+1)-h(N)),\qquad L_{-}(H)=a\,\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N-1)-h(N)).

Conjugation gives

e^{\beta H/4}a^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H/4}e_{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\,e^{\beta(h(n+1)-h(n))/4}e_{n+1},

hence
e^{\beta H/4}a^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H/4}=a^{\dagger}e^{\beta(h(N+1)-h(N))/4}
, and similarly for
a
. Since
\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N\pm 1)-h(N))
commutes with
H
, this yields the stated form with
g_{\pm}
. Moreover,

L_{+}(H)^{\dagger}L_{+}(H)=(N+1)|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N+1)-h(N))|^{2},\quad L_{-}(H)^{\dagger}L_{-}(H)=N|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N-1)-h(N))|^{2},

which commute with
H
, hence are unchanged by conjugation. For compactness,

a^{\dagger}g_{+}(N)e_{n}=\sqrt{n+1}g_{+}(n)e_{n+1},\quad a\,g_{-}(N)e_{n}=\sqrt{n}g_{-}(n)e_{n-1},

and similarly for adjoints, so these are weighted shifts with weights tending to zero and thus compact. If
K_{1},K_{2}
are compact on
\mathcal{H}
, then
X\mapsto K_{1}XK_{2}
is compact on
\mathscr{T}_{2}
, hence both gain terms are compact. The assumption on
(n+1)|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(n+1)-h(n))|^{2}
implies that
A_{+}
is compact as a diagonal operator. Therefore
X\mapsto-\tfrac{1}{2}\{A_{+},X\}
is compact on
\mathscr{T}_{2}
, and the claim follows.
∎

The previous result shows that, under certain conditions,

L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N)}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\{A_{-},\cdot\}+K,

with

a compact operator. We may thus analyze the spectrum of the anticommutator
\tfrac{1}{2}\{A_{-},\cdot\}
in the next Lemma and see it is discrete, as well.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
, bounded from below, with purely discrete spectrum.
Let
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors,

Ae_{n}=\lambda_{n}e_{n}
, where
\lambda_{n}\to+\infty
.
Define an operator
\Phi
on the Hilbert–Schmidt space

\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
by

\begin{split}\Phi(X)=AX+XA,\quad X\in D(\Phi):=\{X\in\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H}):AX+XA\in\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})\}.\end{split}

Then
\Phi
is self-adjoint with compact resolvent, and its spectrum is
given by

\operatorname{Sp}(\Phi)=\{\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{n}:m,n\in\mathbb{N}\},

where each value
\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{n}
appears as an eigenvalue with
eigenvector
E_{mn}:=|e_{m}\rangle\langle e_{n}|
.

Proof.

The rank-one operators
E_{mn}:=|e_{m}\rangle\langle e_{n}|
form an
orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{T}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, and one computes

\begin{split}\Phi(E_{mn})&=AE_{mn}+E_{mn}A=\lambda_{m}E_{mn}+\lambda_{n}E_{mn}=(\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{n})E_{mn}.\end{split}

Thus
\Phi
is diagonal in this basis, with eigenvalues

\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{n}
. Since
\lambda_{n}\to+\infty
, the set

\{\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{n}:m,n\}
has no finite accumulation point,
so
\Phi
has a compact resolvent. Self-adjointness follows because

\Phi
is unitarily equivalent to the diagonal multiplication operator
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{2})
with real diagonal entries

(\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{n})_{m,n}
.
∎

We can apply this Lemma to

A_{-}(N)=\frac{N|\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(h(N-1)-h(N))|^{2}}{2}

and define

\Phi(X):=-(A_{-}(N)X+XA_{-}(N)).

We thus conclude that

Corollary B.7
.

Under the assumption of Lemma
B.5
and if
A_{-}(N)
has compact resolvent, then the generator
L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N)}
has a compact resolvent and thus a purely discrete spectrum. In particular, any perturbation of
\Phi
that is relatively
\Phi
-bounded, with a relative bound
<1
, leads to a generator with a discrete spectrum and thus a spectral gap.

Proof.

By Lemma
B.5
, the difference
L_{\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},h(N)}-\Phi
is compact and by Lemma
B.6
the operator
\Phi
has discrete spectrum.
Since
L_{\widehat{f}_{M},h(N)}=\Phi+K

with
K
compact, the second resolvent identity gives

(L_{\widehat{f}_{M},h(N)}-i)^{-1}-(\Phi-i)^{-1}=-(L_{\widehat{f}_{M},h(N)}-i)^{-1}K(\Phi-i)^{-1}.

Because
K
is compact and both resolvents are bounded, the right-hand side is compact. Since
(\Phi-i)^{-1}
is compact, it follows that
(L_{\widehat{f}_{M},h(N)}-i)^{-1}
is compact as well. Hence
L_{\widehat{f}_{M},h(N)}
has compact resolvent, and therefore discrete spectrum.
∎

By the same argument combined with Lemma

B.3
Gap estimates via Dirichlet form perturbative analysis

Beyond perturbations of Gaussian models, in Lemma

We consider the derivation operator for

With that, we can write the Dirichlet form

Becker et al. 2026a

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\widehat{f},H}(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\langle\partial^{\alpha}_{t}(x),\partial^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\rangle}{\beta\cosh(2\pi t/\beta)}\,dt.

(28)

We consider below Hamiltonians

\widetilde{H}

Lemma B.9
.

Let

and

be such that the difference

of their corresponding derivations is bounded with operator norm satisfying the bound

(29)

Then, for all

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(x)\geq\tfrac{(1-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2})\mathcal{E}(x)-(\kappa_{1}^{-1}+\kappa_{2}^{-1})\frac{\Delta}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{1+\frac{\kappa_{2}}{2}}.

Assume further that

Then also

has a positive spectral gap that satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}(\widetilde{L})\geq\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{gap}(L)}-\sqrt{\Delta}\right)^{2}.

By the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, we see for all

that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\|(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}-\widetilde{\partial}^{\alpha}_{t})(x)\|_{2}\|\partial^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\|_{2}+\|(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}-\widetilde{\partial}^{\alpha}_{t})(x)\|_{2}\|\widetilde{\partial}^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\|_{2}\right)}{\beta\cosh(2\pi t/\beta)}dt

and

From this, we immediately see (
30

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)\geq\operatorname{gap}(L)\|x\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}(\widetilde{L})\geq\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{2}}{2}\right)^{-1}\left((1-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2})\operatorname{gap}(L)-(\kappa_{1}^{-1}+\kappa_{2}^{-1})\frac{\Delta}{2}\right).

Inserting these into (

Appendix C
Mean-field Bose-Hubbard model

We first consider the generator corresponding to the mean-field Bose-Hubbard model
H_{\operatorname{MF}}
obtained by decoupling the hopping term in terms of the superfluid order parameter
\psi=\langle a_{i}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}
. By a thorough perturbative spectral analysis, we show that for small enough
|\psi|
,
L_{\smash{\sigma_{E},\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}},H_{\operatorname{MF}}}}
is gapped. Our results also extend beyond the mean-field regime, for certain regularizations of the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian
H
which prove relevant when studying the superfluid and Mott-insulating phases, see Section
D
for more details.

More precisely, the mean-field description is obtained by decoupling the hopping term in terms of the superfluid order parameter
\psi=\langle a_{i}\rangle

a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}\approx\psi^{*}a_{j}+a_{i}^{\dagger}\psi-|\psi|^{2},

(33)

which neglects quadratic fluctuations around
\psi
. This reduces the problem to an effective single-site Hamiltonian

H_{\text{MF}}=\sum_{i}-J\left(\psi a_{i}^{\dagger}+h.c.-|\psi|^{2}\right)+\tfrac{UN_{i}^{2}}{2}-(\mu+\tfrac{U}{2})N_{i},

where the mean-field phase diagram follows from the self-consistency condition
\psi=\langle a_{i}\rangle_{\text{MF}}

H:=-\mu N+U\tfrac{N(N-1)}{2}-\overline{\psi}a-\psi a^{\dagger}+|\psi|^{2}

(34)

for some parameters
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,

, and
\psi\in\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, we consider
\mathcal{A}=\{+,-\}
, bare jumps
A^{+}=a^{\dagger}
, and
A^{-}=a
, as well as the filter function

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}(\nu)=\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{1+(\beta\nu)^{2}}+\beta\nu}{4}\right),\qquad\qquad\beta>0.

(35)

\widehat{f}_{\mathscr{M}}
in (

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{MF}}
be the corresponding generator in the Schrödinger picture as defined in Section

\displaystyle\max_{m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\left|E_{m_{1}}-E_{m_{2}}\right|\leq 144\,\delta\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)^{2}

(46)

and

\displaystyle\min_{m_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\left|E_{m_{1}}-E^{(0)}_{m_{2}}\right|>2\delta\left(m_{2}+1\right)

(47)

for all
m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{n}.

Lastly, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta_{E}=\sup_{|\psi|\in I}\max_{0\leq j,m\leq 6+\frac{8\mu}{U}}|E_{j}-E_{m}|<\infty

(48)

for all closed intervals
I\subseteq[0,\frac{U}{16}).

Proof.

We start by proving that
H
has discrete spectrum. For this, we use that for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Sp}(H_{0})

we have for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(H_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}a\ket{m}=\frac{\sqrt{m}}{E^{(0)}_{m-1}-\lambda}\,\ket{m-1}

and

(H_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}a^{\dagger}\ket{m}=\frac{\sqrt{m+1}}{E^{(0)}_{m+1}-\lambda}\,\ket{m+1},

and therefore

\displaystyle\left\|(H_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}V\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\psi|\left(\sup_{m\geq 1}\frac{\sqrt{m}}{|E^{(0)}_{m-1}-\lambda|}+\sup_{m\geq 0}\frac{\sqrt{m+1}}{|E^{(0)}_{m+1}-\lambda|}\right)\leq 2|\psi|\sup_{m\geq 0}\frac{\sqrt{m+1}}{|E^{(0)}_{m}-\lambda|}=:|\psi|M(\lambda).

Hence, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Sp}(H_{0})
such that

\displaystyle|\psi|M(\lambda)<1,

(49)

we have, by
a standard von Neumann series argument, that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Sp}(H)
with resolvent given by the convergent series

\displaystyle(H-\lambda)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-(H_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}V)^{k}(H_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}.

(50)

Below, we show that
\lambda
satisfying (
49
) always exists, which in particular implies by (
50
)
that
(H-\lambda)^{-1}
is compact since
(H_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}
is compact. This already shows that the spectrum of
H
is discrete, i.e. consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity.

We proceed to locate the eigenvalues of
H:
For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
large enough, i.e. satisfying (
43
), let
\Gamma_{n}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a circle in the complex plane with center of origin
E^{(0)}_{n}
and radius
r\sqrt{n+1}
for some
r\in(2|\psi|,\frac{U\sqrt{n+1}}{8}].
By (
38
) of Lemma
C.2
we have for all
m\neq n

\displaystyle\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma_{n}}|E^{(0)}_{m}-\lambda|

\displaystyle\geq|E^{(0)}_{m}-E^{(0)}_{n}|-r\sqrt{n+1}\geq\frac{U}{4}(m+n+1)-r\sqrt{n+1}\geq\frac{U}{8}(m+n+1).

(51)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|\psi|\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma_{n}}M(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq 2|\psi|\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma_{n}}\max\!\left\{\frac{1}{r},\;\sup_{m\neq n}\frac{\sqrt{m+1}}{\left|E^{(0)}_{m}-\lambda\right|}\right\}\leq 2|\psi|\max\!\left\{\frac{1}{r},\;\frac{8}{U\sqrt{n+1}}\right\}=\frac{2|\psi|}{r}<1

(52)

which by (
50
) gives that
(H-\lambda)^{-1}
exists for all
\lambda\in\Gamma_{n}.

For
\psi^{\prime}\in[0,\psi]
, we use that by holomorphic functional calculus, the spectral projection of
H(\psi^{\prime})=H_{0}+\overline{\psi^{\prime}}a-\psi^{\prime}a^{\dagger}
corresponding to spectral values in the interval
(E^{(0)}_{n}-r\sqrt{n+1},E^{(0)}_{n}+r\sqrt{n+1})
is given by

\displaystyle P_{n}(\psi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{n}}(\lambda-H(\psi^{\prime}))^{-1}d\lambda.

By (
50
) and (
52
), we see that
\psi^{\prime}\mapsto P_{n}(\psi^{\prime})
is continuous (in fact, analytic) on the interval
[0,\psi]
, and therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}(P_{n}(\psi^{\prime}))=\operatorname{rank}(P_{n}(0))=1

(53)

for all
\psi^{\prime}\in[0,\psi].
This in particular implies that
also
H\equiv H(\psi)
has a unique eigenvalue,
E_{n},
in the interval
(E^{(0)}_{n}-r\sqrt{n+1},E^{(0)}_{n}+r\sqrt{n+1})
which furthermore has multiplicity 1. Further, using the same argument as for (
51
) we see (
45
).

Similarly, we can proceed to locate the low eigenenergies of
H:
For
n<3+\tfrac{4\mu}{U}
,
|\psi|<\tfrac{U}{16}
and
\delta\in[|\psi|,\frac{U}{6})
we consider the subset
\mathcal{C}_{n}\subseteq\{0,\cdots,3+\lfloor\frac{4\mu}{U}\rfloor\}
constructed in Lemma
C.2
.
By (
39
) the eigenvalues
E^{(0)}_{m}
of
H_{0}
for
m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
are separated from the ones
E_{m^{\prime}}^{(0)}
satisfying
m^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{C}_{n}.
In particular, (
39
) gives that we can choose a path
\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}\subset\mathbb{C}
surrounding exactly all
E^{(0)}_{m}
for
m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
and no other eigenvalues of
H_{0}
and further satisfies

\displaystyle\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}}|E^{(0)}_{m}-\lambda|>2\delta(m+1)

(54)

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In particular this gives that
\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}}|\psi|M(\lambda)<1
which gives by (
50
) that
(H-\lambda)^{-1}
is well-defined for all
\lambda\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}.
Arguing the same way as for (
53
), we see that
H
has the same number of eigenvalues (including multiplicity) as
H_{0}
being surrounded by
\Gamma^{\prime}_{n},
i.e. for all
m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
the Hamiltonian
H
has an eigenvalue
E_{m}
in the interior of
\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}
. Using (
54
) we already see that (
47
) holds true. Furthermore, by choosing
\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}
to be the smallest circle around the

such that (

) holds, and using (

) we see that

\displaystyle\max_{m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\left|E_{m_{1}}-E_{m_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\left|E^{(0)}_{m_{1}}-E^{(0)}_{m_{2}}\right|+4\delta\left(4+\frac{4\mu}{U}\right)\leq 128\,\delta\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)^{2}+4\delta\left(4+\frac{4\mu}{U}\right)\leq 144\,\delta\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)^{2}

which shows (
46
).

Lastly, since the path
\Gamma_{n}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}_{n}
to locate the eigenvalues
E_{n}
of

were independent of the specific
\psi
with
|\psi|\in[0,\frac{U}{16})
we see that

\Delta_{E}=\sup_{|\psi|\in I}\max_{0\leq j,m\leq 6+\frac{8\mu}{U}}|E_{j}-E_{m}|<\infty

for all closed intervals
I\subseteq[0,\frac{U}{16}).

∎

By the previous lemma, we know that
H
has a discrete spectrum
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}.
Further, for
n
large enough, i.e., (
43
), we have seen that the
E_{n}
are non-degenerate and separated from each other. For small
n
, the
E_{n}
can, in principle, be degenerate. To take this into account, we consider
\operatorname{Sp}(H)=(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to be the spectrum of
H
, including multiplicities; i.e.,
E_{n}
appearing multiple times if it is degenerate. We then fix some eigenbasis of
H
, which we denote by
(\ket{E_n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by slight abuse of notation (e.g.
|E_{n}\rangle\neq|E_{n+1}\rangle
, even if the energies may coincide
E_{n}=E_{n+1}
). We use the convention that
c_{n}:=\langle n|E_{n}\rangle\geq 0
by possibly changing the phase factor of
\ket{E_n}.
In the following, we study the state
\ket{E_n}
as a perturbation of the corresponding Fock basis vector
\ket{n}
.

Lemma C.4

(Eigenvector perturbation theory)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\psi\in\mathbb{C}
, and
U>0
be such that
\frac{16|\psi|}{U}<1.
Then for
n\geq 3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
and using the convention
c_{n}=\bra{n}E_{n}\rangle\in[0,1]
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{E_n}-\ket{n}\right\|\leq\frac{32|\psi|}{U\sqrt{n+1}}.

(55)

Furthermore, taking
\frac{16|\psi|}{U}\leq 1-\kappa
for some
\kappa>0
, we have for all sequences
\left(u_{m}\right)_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that
|u_{m}|\leq 1
for all
m
that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\!\!u_{m}\sqrt{m}\left(\ket{E_{m-1}}\!\bra{E_m}\!-\!\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\!\leq\!C_{\kappa}\frac{|\psi|}{U},

(56)

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{m\geq 3+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\!\!\!\!\!\!u_{m}\sqrt{m+1}\left(\ket{E_{m+1}}\!\bra{E_m}\!-\!\ket{m+1}\!\bra{m}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!\!\leq\!C_{\kappa}\frac{|\psi|}{U},

(57)

for some
C_{\kappa}\geq 0
depending only on
\kappa.

For
n<3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
,
\delta\in(|\psi|,\frac{U}{16})
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}\subseteq\left\{0,\cdots,3+\lfloor\frac{4\mu}{U}\rfloor\right\}
defined in Lemma
C.2
, the projections
P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}|E_{m}\rangle\!\langle E_{m}|
and
P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}|m\rangle\!\langle m|
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{24|\psi|}{\delta}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right),\left\|a\left(P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{48|\psi|}{\delta}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)^{3/2},

\displaystyle\left\|a^{\dagger}\left(P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{48|\psi|}{\delta}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)^{3/2}.

(58)

Furthermore, for
n,m\geq 3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
and
\frac{16|\psi|}{U}\leq 1-\kappa
for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle|\bra{ E_m}a\ket{E_n}-\sqrt{n}\delta_{m+1,n}|\leq C_{\kappa}\frac{|\psi|}{U}\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{|m-n|},

(59)

\displaystyle|\bra{ E_m}a^{\dagger}\ket{ E_n}-\sqrt{n+1}\delta_{m,n+1}|\leq C_{\kappa}\frac{|\psi|}{U}\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{|m-n|}

(60)

for some
C_{\kappa}\geq 0
depending only on
\kappa.

Lastly, for
n<3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
,
m\geq 3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
,
\psi,\delta
, and
U
such that
\frac{|\psi|}{\delta}\leq 1-\kappa
for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we have

\begin{split}&\left\|P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}a\ket{E_m}-\sqrt{m}P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\ket{m-1}\right\|\leq C_{\kappa}\left(\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)^{3/2}+1\right)\,\frac{|\psi|}{\delta}\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{\operatorname{dist}(\mathcal{C}_{n},m)}\\
&\left\|P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}a^{\dagger}\ket{E_m}\right\|\leq C_{\kappa}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right)\,\frac{|\psi|}{\delta}\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{\operatorname{dist}(\mathcal{C}_{n},m)}\end{split}

(61)

for
\operatorname{dist}(\mathcal{C}_{n},m):=\min_{k\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}|k-m|
and some
C_{\kappa}\geq 0
only depending on
\kappa.

Proof.

As established in Lemma
C.3
, the mean-field Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian,
H=H_{0}+V
, has a discrete spectrum with eigenvalues denoted by
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}.
For
n\geq 3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
and
Q_{n}=\mathbbm{1}-|n\rangle\!\langle n|
, we define

\displaystyle R_{n}=Q_{n}(E_{n}-H_{0})^{-1}Q_{n}V,

where we interpret
Q_{n}(E_{n}-H_{0})^{-1}Q_{n}
as the resolvent of
H_{0}
in the space
Q_{n}\mathcal{H}
, which is well-defined by (
45
) of Lemma
C.3
.
Since, given
V=-\overline{\psi}a-\psi a^{\dagger}
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}

R_{n}|m\rangle=-\psi(E_{n}-E^{(0)}_{m+1})^{-1}\delta_{m+1\neq n}\sqrt{m+1}|m+1\rangle-\overline{\psi}(E_{n}-E^{(0)}_{m-1})^{-1}\delta_{m-1\neq n}\sqrt{m}|m-1\rangle

we see by (
45
) that, by the Schur test,

\displaystyle\|R_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{16|\psi|}{U\sqrt{n+1}},\ \|aR_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{16|\psi|}{U},\text{ and }\|a^{\dagger}R_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{16|\psi|}{U}.

(62)

From the eigenvalue equation
H\ket{E_n}=E_{n}\ket{E_n}
, we find that
(E_{n}-H_{0})\ket{E_n}=V\ket{E_n}
and

\displaystyle Q_{n}\ket{E_n}=R_{n}\ket{E_n}.

Therefore

\ket{E_n}=\ket{n}\!\bra{ n}\ket{E_n}+Q_{n}\ket{E_n}=c_{n}\ket{n}+R_{n}\ket{E_n}

(63)

with
c_{n}=\langle n|E_{n}\rangle\in[0,1].
From this and (
62
), we already see that for large
n
, the
\ket{E_n}
essentially becomes
\ket{n}
as

c_{n}\geq 1-\frac{16|\psi|}{U\sqrt{n+1}}.

(64)

In particular, using the triangle inequality with (
63
), together with (
64
) and (
62
), we get (
55
):

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{E_n}-\ket{n}\right\|\leq(1-c_{n})+\|R_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{32|\psi|}{U\sqrt{n+1}}.

Using that, by (
62
), we see
\|R_{n}\|_{\infty}<1,

\ket{E_n}=c_{n}\left(\mathbbm{1}-R_{n}\right)^{-1}\ket{n}=c_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(R_{n})^{j}\ket{n}.

(65)

We use this to prove (
56
). Note that

\begin{split}\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}u_{m}\sqrt{m}\Big(\ket{E_{m-1}}\!\bra{E_m}-\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}\Big)&=\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}u_{m}\sqrt{m}(c_{m}c_{m-1}-1)\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}\\
&\quad+\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}u_{m}c_{m}c_{m-1}\sqrt{m}\sum_{(j,l)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R^{\dagger}_{m})^{l}.\end{split}

(66)

Since we have
|u_{m}|\,\sqrt{m}|1-c_{m}c_{m-1}|\leq\frac{32|\psi|}{U}
, we see that the first term in (
66
) is a bounded operator satisfying

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}u_{m}\sqrt{m}(c_{m}c_{m-1}-1)\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{32|\psi|}{U}.

(67)

For the second term in (
66
), we denote
\widetilde{u}_{m}=u_{m}c_{m}c_{m-1}
which satisfies
|\widetilde{u}_{m}|\leq 1
and write

\begin{split}\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sum_{(j,l)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\sqrt{m}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R_{m})^{l}&=\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sum_{0<j+l<4}\sqrt{m}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R_{m})^{l}\\
&\quad+\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sum_{j+l\geq 4}\sqrt{m}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R_{m})^{l}.\end{split}

(68)

We bound both terms individually. For the first term, we use the fact that from (
C
), we have for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle(R_{m})^{j}\ket{m}=\sum_{-j\leq k\leq j}d^{(j)}_{k,m}\ket{m+k}

for some coefficients
d^{(j)}_{k,m}
satisfying
|d^{(j)}_{k,m}|\leq\|R_{m}\|_{\infty}^{j}\leq\left(\frac{16|\psi|}{U\sqrt{m+1}}\right)^{j}
by (
62
), and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sqrt{m}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R_{m})^{l}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-j\leq k_{1}\leq j\\
-l\leq k_{2}\leq l\end{subarray}}\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sqrt{m}d^{(j)}_{k_{1},m-1}d^{(l)}_{k_{2},m}\ket{m-1+k_1}\!\bra{m+k_2}.

Using that for
j+l>0
and
\frac{16|\psi|}{U}<1
, we have
|\widetilde{u}_{m}|\,\sqrt{m}\,|d^{(j)}_{k_{1},m-1}d^{(l)}_{k_{2},m}|\leq\frac{16|\psi|}{U}
; we see that this operator is, in fact, bounded, which, in particular, gives for the first term in (
68
) that

\left\|\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\sum_{0<j+l<4}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sqrt{m}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R_{m})^{l}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{0<j+l<4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-j\leq k_{1}\leq j\\
-l\leq k_{2}\leq l\end{subarray}}\frac{16|\psi|}{U}=\frac{1104|\psi|}{U}.

(69)

Denoting
\gamma:=\frac{16|\psi|}{U}<1
, the second term in (
68
) can be estimated using (
62
) as

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\widetilde{u}_{m}\sum_{j+l\geq 4}\sqrt{m}(R_{m-1})^{j}\ket{m-1}\!\bra{m}(R_{m})^{l}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{m\geq 4+\frac{4\mu}{U}}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{3/2}}\right)\left(\sum_{k=4}^{\infty}(k+1)\widetilde{\gamma}^{k}\right)\lesssim\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}^{4}}{(1-\widetilde{\gamma})^{2}}.

Combining this with (
67
) and (
69
) proves (
56

57
) follows analogously.

We continue to prove (
59
): Using (
65
), we have for

\displaystyle\bra{ E_m}a\ket{ E_n}-\sqrt{n}\delta_{m+1,n}=(c_{n}c_{m}-1)\sqrt{n}\delta_{m+1,n}+c_{n}c_{m}\sum_{(j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{0}\setminus\{(0,0)}\}\bra{m}(R^{\dagger}_{m})^{l}a(R_{n})^{j}\ket{n}.

Using (
64
), the first term can be bounded as
|c_{n}c_{m}-1|\sqrt{n}\delta_{m+1,n}\leq\frac{32|\psi|}{U}\delta_{m+1,n}.

For the second term in (

), we first note that, by the orthogonality of the Fock states and by the definition of

, we have that

\displaystyle\bra{m}(R^{\dagger}_{m})^{l}a(R_{n})^{j}\ket{n}=0

for all

. Hence, denoting once again
\gamma:=\frac{16|\psi|}{U}<1
and

\displaystyle\left|c_{n}c_{m}\sum_{(j,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{0}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\bra{m}(R^{\dagger}_{m})^{l}a(R_{n})^{j}\ket{n}\right|

Using

for some

and
\sup_{K\geq 0}\left(\frac{1-\kappa}{1-\kappa/2}\right)^{K-1}\frac{K+1}{\kappa^{2}}<\infty

60

For
n<3+\frac{4\mu}{U}
and
\delta\in(|\psi|,\frac{U}{16})
, we consider the set
\mathcal{C}_{n}
from Lemma
C.2
and

and define
P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}|m\rangle\!\langle m|
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{n}=Q^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}(E_{n}-H_{0})^{-1}Q^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}V,

(70)

where we interpret
Q^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}(E_{n}-H_{0})^{-1}Q^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}

, which is well-defined by (

) of Lemma

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{R}_{n}\|_{\infty},\,\|a\widetilde{R}_{n}\|_{\infty},\,\|a^{\dagger}\widetilde{R}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{|\psi|}{\delta}<1.

(71)

From the eigenvalue equation
H\ket{E_n}=E_{n}\ket{E_n}
, we find that
(E_{n}-H_{0})\ket{E_n}=V\ket{E_n}
,

\displaystyle Q^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\ket{E_n}=\widetilde{R}_{n}\ket{E_n}

and therefore

\displaystyle\ket{E_n}=P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\ket{E_n}+\widetilde{R}_{n}\ket{E_n},

(72)

which gives

\ket{E_n}=\left(\mathbbm{1}-\widetilde{R}_{n}\right)^{-1}P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\ket{E_n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(\widetilde{R}_{n})^{j}P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\ket{E_n}.

(73)

Denoting
P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}|E_{m}\rangle\!\langle E_{m}|,
, we

\begin{split}\left\|P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}P_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\right\|_{\infty}&\leq\left\|\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}|E_{m}\rangle\!\langle E_{m}|\widetilde{R}_{m}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\widetilde{R}_{m}|E_{m}\rangle\!\langle E_{m}|P^{(0)}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\right\|_{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!\!+\!\left\|\sum_{m\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\widetilde{R}_{m}|E_{m}\rangle\!\langle E_{m}|\widetilde{R}_{m}\right\|_{\infty}\\
&\leq\frac{12|\psi|}{\delta}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{U}\right),\end{split}

(74)

where in the last inequality we have used that

.
Since

has support in

with eigenvalues lying above in

Lemma E.1
.

Let

(99)

\displaystyle\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\sigma^{-1}_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}.

We continue with bounding the operator norm of the perturbation

\displaystyle E_{j}(H_{\operatorname{SF}})\geq E_{j}(H_{0})-\|V^{\prime}\|\geq E_{j}(H_{0})-\left|\frac{U}{2}\right|(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)(nM^{\prime})^{2},

smallest eigenvalues of

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\Tr\left(e^{-\beta H_{\operatorname{SF}}}\right)\leq e^{\beta|\frac{U}{2}|(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)(nM^{\prime})^{2}}\Tr\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right)=e^{\beta|\frac{U}{2}|(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)(nM^{\prime})^{2}}\prod_{k}\frac{1}{1-\exp(-\beta\eps_k)},

where the

. As by definition we have for all

we see (

∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\beta,U>0,

\eta,\eta^{\prime},J\in\mathbb{R}
with
\eta^{\prime}\leq 1
and
\eta-2D|J|>0
and consider for
M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle H_{\operatorname{SF}}:=H_{0}+\frac{U}{2}(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i})(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}

where
H_{0}
is defined in Section
D
,

being the projector onto the first
M^{\prime}+1
Fock states with respect to the ladder operators
\{b_{k},b^{\dagger}_{k}\}

). Then we have for all

\displaystyle E_{\operatorname{Gibbs}}:=\Tr\left(e^{4N_{\operatorname{tot}}^{\kappa}}\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}})\right)\leq\exp(p_3(n,M'))

(102)

for some function
p_{3},
which is polynomially bounded in its entries and whose finite coefficients can depend on the other parameters specified above.

Proof.

Note that as
a_{i}
and
b_{k}
are related via unitary transformation, we have

N_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}=\sum_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k},

By that we see that

commutes with
a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i}
and
b^{\dagger}_{k}b_{k}
for all

and, therefore, also with
H_{0}
and
\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i}).
Furthermore, as also
[(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n},N_{\operatorname{tot}}]=0

\displaystyle[H_{\operatorname{SF}},N_{\operatorname{tot}}]=0.

(103)

By the diagonalization argument in Section
D
we can write

H_{0}=\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{k}b^{\dagger}_{k}b_{k}

and by definition we have for all

Fix any

Therefore, we have

\Tr\!\bigl(e^{4N_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\kappa}}e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{SF}}}\bigr)\leq\exp\!\Bigl(C_{\kappa}\,t^{-\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}}\Bigr)\Tr\!\bigl(e^{tN_{\mathrm{tot}}}e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{SF}}}\bigr)=\exp\!\Bigl(C_{\kappa}\,t^{-\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}}\Bigr)\Tr\!\Bigl(e^{-\beta\bigl(H_{\mathrm{SF}}-\frac{t}{\beta}N_{\mathrm{tot}}\bigr)}\Bigr),

where we in the last equality we have used that

N_{\mathrm{tot}}
and
H_{\mathrm{SF}}
commute .

Since

where we denoted by

Combining these estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle\Tr\!\bigl(e^{4N_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\kappa}}\sigma_{\beta}\bigr)\leq\exp\!\Bigl(C_{\kappa}\,t^{-\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}}+2\beta\|V^{\prime}\|\Bigr)\frac{\Tr(e^{tN_{\mathrm{tot}}}e^{-\beta H_0})}{\Tr(e^{-\beta H_0})}.

E.2
End-to-end simulation cost for free energies

In this section, we consider the task of computing the difference in free energies of two Hamiltonians
(H_{0},D(H_{0}))
and
(H_{1},D(H_{1}))
with
H_{0},H_{1}\geq-h_{0}
, over a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
at inverse temperature
\beta>0
:

\displaystyle\Delta F(\beta,H):=F(\beta,H_{1})-F(\beta,H_{0}),\qquad\text{ where }\quad F(\beta,H):=-\beta^{-1}\log\Tr(e^{-\beta H}).

We consider a path
H(s):=(1-s)H_{0}+sH_{1}
, so that

\displaystyle\Delta F(\beta,H)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}F(\beta,H(s))\,ds

\displaystyle=-\beta^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}\,\frac{1}{\Tr(e^{-\beta H(s)})}\frac{d}{ds}\Tr(e^{-\beta H(s)})\,ds

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}\,\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{1}{\Tr(e^{-\beta H(s)})}\Tr\Big(e^{-(\beta-\tau) H(s)}(H_1-H_0)e^{-\tau H(s)}\Big)\,d\tau ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\,\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H(s))(H_1-H_0)\Big)\,ds.

(106)

where we denote the Gibbs state of
H
at inverse temperature
\beta
by
\sigma_{\beta}(H)
. In words, it suffices to estimate the average of the potential
V:=H_{1}-H_{0}
in the Gibbs state at different values of
s
to obtain a good estimate of the free energy difference.

To illustrate the method, we consider the
n
-mode Hamiltonians

\displaystyle H_{0}=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\Big(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\operatorname{h.c.}\Big)+\eta\sum_{i}N_{i}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad H_{1}=H_{0}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i},

(107)

and assume that
\eta>2DJ
. We first need to truncate
H_{1}-H_{0}
into a finite-rank operator
H_{M}
such that the error induced on the integral (
106
) remains small:

Lemma E.4
.

Assume
U>0
and
\kappa:=\beta(\eta-2D|J|)>0
, let
P_{M}
be the projection onto the first
M+1
lowest-energy single-mode Fock states, and denote
H_{M}:=(P^{a}_{M})^{\otimes n}(H_{1}-H_{0})(P^{a}_{M})^{\otimes n}
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left|\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H(s))\bigl(H_1-H_0-H_M\bigr)\Big)\right|\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{ for }\qquad M={\Omega}\Big(n+\log\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)\Big).

(108)

The same conclusion holds when replacing
H(s)
by
H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s):=H_{0}+s\frac{U}{2}(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i})(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}
for any
M^{\prime}>0
.

Proof.

Below, we denote
P_{M}\equiv P_{M}^{a}
for sake of simplicity. Then, it suffices to observe that

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H(s))\bigl(H_1-H_0-H_M\bigr)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{U}{2}\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H(s)) \big(1-P_M^{\otimes n}\big)\sum_i \big(N_i^2-\eta' N_i\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{U}{2}\,\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H(s))\,(1-P_M^{\otimes n})\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\Tr\Big(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))\Big(\sum_i N_i^2-\eta' N_i\Big)^2\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Then, we denote
Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i}\bigr)
so that
H(s):=H_{0}+\frac{sU}{2}\,Q
. Since
Q\geq-C_{\eta^{\prime}}n
, and
Q\leq(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)N^{2}
, and therefore
Q^{2}\leq(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)^{2}N^{4}
. Moreover, as proved in (
92
),

H(s)\geq H_{0}-C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n\geq(\eta-2D|J|)N-C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n=\frac{\kappa}{\beta}N-C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n,

and
[N,H(s)]=0
. Hence, for every
0<\lambda<\kappa
,

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))(1-P_{M}^{\otimes n})\bigr)\leq e^{-\lambda(M+1)}\bigl(1-e^{-(\kappa-\lambda)}\bigr)^{-n}e^{\beta C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n}.

Next, for every
0<\tau<\kappa
, using
x^{4}\leq\frac{24}{\tau^{4}}e^{\tau x}
,
x\geq 0
, we get

Q^{2}\leq(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)^{2}N^{4}\leq\frac{24(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)^{2}}{\tau^{4}}e^{\tau N}
. Therefore

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))Q^{2}\bigr)\leq\frac{24(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)^{2}}{\tau^{4}}\,\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))e^{\tau N}\bigr).

Since
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}(H(s))\geq 1
,

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))e^{\tau N}\bigr)\leq\Tr\bigl(e^{-\beta H(s)}e^{\tau N}\bigr)\leq\Tr\bigl(e^{-(\kappa-\tau)N}\bigr)e^{\beta C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n}=\bigl(1-e^{-(\kappa-\tau)}\bigr)^{-n}e^{\beta C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n}.

Thus, we have proved that

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))Q^{2}\bigr)\leq\frac{24(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)^{2}}{\tau^{4}}\bigl(1-e^{-(\kappa-\tau)}\bigr)^{-n}e^{\beta C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n}.

Combining the two bounds yields, for all
0<\lambda,\tau<\kappa
,

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))(1-P_{M}^{\otimes n})\bigr)^{1/2}\,\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))Q^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{5(1+|\eta^{\prime}|)}{\tau^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}(M+1)}\bigl(1-e^{-(\kappa-\lambda)}\bigr)^{-n/2}\bigl(1-e^{-(\kappa-\tau)}\bigr)^{-n/2}e^{\beta C_{\eta^{\prime},U}n}.

Choosing
\lambda=\tau=\kappa/2
, we obtain

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))(1-P_{M}^{\otimes n})\bigr)^{1/2}\,\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))Q^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\leq\frac{20}{\kappa^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{\kappa}{4}(M+1)}\bigl(1-e^{-\kappa/2}\bigr)^{-n}.

If
\kappa\geq 2\log 2
, then
1-e^{-\kappa/2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, and thus

\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))(1-P_{M}^{\otimes n})\bigr)^{1/2}\,\Tr\bigl(\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))Q^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\leq\frac{20}{\kappa^{2}}\,2^{\frac{2n-M-1}{2}}.

The result follows.
∎

By an argument identical to that of Lemma
E.4
, we also argue that on the observable
H_{M}
, the states
\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))
and
\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s))
have comparable statistics: assuming
\kappa>0
, then

\displaystyle\Big\|\sigma_{\beta}(H(s))-\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s))\Big\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\qquad\text{ for }\qquad M^{\prime}=\Omega\Big(n+\log\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon'}\Big)\Big).

We have shown that,

\displaystyle\Big|\Delta F(\beta,H)-\int_{0}^{1}\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s))H_M\Big)\,ds\Big|\lesssim\varepsilon+\|H_{M}\|^{2}\,\varepsilon^{\prime}\lesssim\varepsilon+nM^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}.

In order for this bound to be below
\varepsilon
, we thus need to choose
M=\Omega(n+\log(1/\varepsilon))
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon/(nM^{2})
, which means
M^{\prime}=\Omega(n+\log(1/\varepsilon'))=\Omega(n+\log(1/\epsilon))
. Moreover, using similar bounds, by standard Riemann-sum approximation

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s))H_{M} \Big)\,ds-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(k/L))H_{M} \Big)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{2L}\,\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\Big|\frac{d}{ds}\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s))H_{M} \Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{2L}\,n^{2}M^{2}(M^{\prime})^{2}.

This implies the following:

Proposition E.5
.

Assume
U>0
and
\kappa:=\beta(\eta-2|J|D)>0
. Then, choosing
M,M^{\prime}=\widetilde{\Omega}(n+\log(1/\varepsilon))
, and thus
L=\widetilde{\Omega}\Big(\frac{n^{6}}{\varepsilon}\Big)
, we get that

\displaystyle\Big|\Delta F(\beta,H)-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\Tr\Big(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(k/L))H_{M} \Big)\Big|\leq\varepsilon

where
H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s):=H_{0}+s(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i})(P^{b}_{M^{\prime}})^{\otimes n}
.

It remains to estimate the number of samples of
\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(s))
for different values of
s\in[0,1]
needed and the required circuit depth for preparing each Gibbs state to bound the runtime for estimating
\Delta F(\beta,H).
This is obtained in the following theorem.

Theorem E.6
.

The free energy
F(\beta,H_{1})
can be estimated with accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and probability of failure bounded by
\delta>0
on a quantum computer with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n\log n\,\log\log(1/(\lambda^{\min}_{2}\eps))\right)
many qubits with total runtime of order

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\min}_{2}\varepsilon^{3}}\log\left(1/\delta\right)\operatorname{poly}\left(n\right)\right)

where
\lambda^{\min}_{2}:=\min_{s\in[0,1]}\lambda_{2}(s)>0
and
\lambda_{2}(s)\equiv\operatorname{gap}(L_{\widehat{f},H^{(M^{\prime})}_{\operatorname{SF}}(s)})
denotes the spectral gap of the Gibbs sampler defined in Theorem
E.3
.
Moreover, the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
notation hides constants independent of the displayed parameters and additionally suppresses subdominant
\operatorname{poly}\log
in the leading order.

Proof.

Note that we can analytically compute
F(\beta,H_{0})
from Section
D
as

\displaystyle\Tr\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right)=\prod_{k}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{k}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad F(\beta,H_{0})=\beta^{-1}\sum_{k}\log\left(1-e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{k}}\right).

Hence, in order to estimate
F(\beta,H_{1})=\Delta F(\beta,H)+F(\beta,H_{0}),
it remains to estimate the difference of free energies
\Delta F(\beta,H):

For
M\in\mathbb{N}
we denote
H_{M}=\frac{U}{2}\left(P^{a}_{M}\right)^{\otimes n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(N_{i}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}N_{i}\right)\left(P^{a}_{M}\right)^{\otimes n}
. Using

\|H_{M}\|\lesssim n^{2}M^{2}

and Hoeffding’s inequality we see that for all
k=0,\cdots,L-1
we can estimate the expectation value
\Tr(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(k/L)) H_M)
with accuracy
\varepsilon/3
and probability of failure
\delta>0
using a number of samples of
\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(k/L))
and measurements in the eigenbasis of
H_{M}
of order

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\,\frac{\|H_{M}\|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\,\frac{n^{4}M^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,\right).

(109)

Therefore, using the union bound, we can estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\Tr(\sigma_\beta(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(k/L)) H_M)

(110)

with same accuracy and probability of failure using

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\,\frac{Ln^{4}M^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{L}{\delta}\right)\,\right).

(111)

many samples of the Gibbs states and measurements in the eigenbasis of
H_{M}.
Using Proposition
E.5
we can estimate
\Delta F(\beta,H)
by (
110
) with accuracy
\varepsilon/3
with

\displaystyle L=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{n^{6}}{\varepsilon}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad M=\mathcal{O}\left(n+\log(1/\eps)\right).

(112)

Lastly, using Theorem
E.3
for varying on-site repulsion parameter
U,
we can prepare for each
k=0,\cdots,L-1
the Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}(H_{\operatorname{SF}}(k/L))
with accuracy
\varepsilon/(3\|H_{M}\|)
in trace distance on a quantum computer with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n\log n\,\log\log(1/(\lambda_2(k/L)\eps))\right)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n\log n\,\log\log(1/(\lambda^{\min}_2\eps))\right)
many qubits using a circuit depth of order

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(k/L)}\operatorname{poly}\left(n,\log(1/\eps)\right)\right)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\min}_{2}}\operatorname{poly}\left(n,\log(1/\eps)\right)\right).

Multiplying this with the required numbers of samples of the Gibbs states in (
111
) and inserting the scaling of
L
and
M
in (
112
) yields the result.
Note that since
H_{M}
is diagonal with respect to the Fock basis corresponding to
\{a_{i},a_{i}^{\dagger}\},
the required eigenbasis measurements are simply computational basis measurements of our quantum computer, and hence do not require additional circuit depth.

∎
