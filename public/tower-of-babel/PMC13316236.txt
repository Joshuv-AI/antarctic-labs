Mitochondrial OXPHOS integrates immunometabolic cascade for bone regeneration via coupled ATP production and ROS homeostasis
Authors: Jingrong Chen, Ping He, Jiayi Gu, Jingqi Wu, Yuxiao Liu, Wei Lei, Tong-Chuan He, Liwen Zheng, Joost D de Bruijn, Huipin Yuan, Hongmei Zhang, Mingzheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoinductive calcium phosphate ceramics (CaPs) hold great promise for bone repair, yet the metabolic principles governing their efficacy are poorly defined. Here, we identify mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as an early and indispensable pathway activated specifically by an osteoinductive tricalcium phosphate (TCPS) in material-induced bone formation. Our findings demonstrate that OXPHOS critically governs the M2 macrophage-osteoclast axis for bone formation by regulating both cellular energy supply and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis. Disrupting either function-by inhibiting OXPHOS (reducing ATP production) or by disrupting ROS balance (including ROS scavenging or accumulation)-uncouples this metabolic-immunological cascade and abrogates bone formation. Our work establishes mitochondrial OXPHOS as a central metabolic hub that integrates bioenergetics with ROS balance to orchestrate material-induced bone formation via M2 macrophage polarization-osteoclastogenesis axis. Consequently, these insights provide a rational basis for therapeutic modulation of the immunometabolic cascade in bone regeneration, via strategies designed to co-activate OXPHOS and fine-tune ROS dynamics.Keywords: Oxidative phosphorylation, Reactive oxygen species, Macrophage polarization, Osteoclast, Osteoinductive materials

Introduction

The repair of critical-sized bone defects remains a major clinical challenge. Acellular, growth factor-free (bio) materials capable of de novo bone formation in non-osseous sites have thus emerged as a promising osteoinductive strategy. Among these, calcium phosphate ceramics, particularly tricalcium phosphate (TCP), are considered potential bone repair materials due to their chemical similarity to natural bone and inherent osteoinductive property once a submicron-scaled surface topography is achieved [1,2]. For instance, TCP ceramics with submicron-scaled grains and submicron-scaled pores on their surface (TCPS) become osteoinductive, while its counterpart with micron-scaled grains and micron-scaled pores (TCPB) is non-osteoinductive [3]. This contrast highlights the decisive role of surface microstructure in determining osteoinductive performance, making it crucial to elucidate the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms for novel bone regeneration approaches with osteoinductive materials.

Successful material-induced bone formation depends not only on the intrinsic osteogenic properties of materials but also on its dynamic interplay with the host microenvironment, ultimately enabling the transition from an inactive implant to functional living bone tissue [4]. Accordingly, research has shifted from studying the material in isolation to decoding the biological cascade it triggers. Macrophages, as central mediators of the innate immune response, act as primary “sensors” and “conductors” of the early immune microenvironment around implants [[5], [6], [7]]. They are known to support bone formation by secreting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), which promote angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation [8,9]. Intriguingly, emerging evidence indicates that osteoclasts-often derived from macrophage fusion-play a pivotal role in calcium phosphate-driven osteogenesis [10]. Rather than being merely resorptive, osteoclasts are now recognized as active regulators of bone regeneration, being the core participants in the “osteoclast-osteoblast coupling” process [[11], [12], [13]]. Furthermore, the hypoxic niche established upon implantation can promote both M2-like macrophage polarization and osteoclast formation around calcium phosphate ceramics, thereby facilitating material-induced bone formation [14]. Together, these insights outline a coherent sequence for material-induced bone formation of macrophage recruitment, M2 polarization, osteoclast formation, and subsequent bone formation [3], providing a chronological framework for mechanistic investigation.

Advances in immunometabolism have illuminated the central role of cellular energy metabolism in regulating bone regeneration processes, including immune response and cell differentiation [15,16]. Undifferentiated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) maintain high glycolysis and low oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in physiological hypoxic niches, a mechanism that prevents oxidative senescence and preserves their self-renewal capacity. Conversely, shifting this metabolic balance toward elevated OXPHOS is necessary for osteogenic differentiation [17]. Building on this principle, strategies have been designed to activate OXPHOS in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), enhancing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) metabolism to promote osteogenic differentiation and bone regeneration [[18], [19], [20]]. As the first responders to implants, macrophages undergo functional polarization being also tightly coupled to metabolic reprogramming. Specifically, enhanced OXPHOS is a metabolic hallmark of anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, whereas a glycolytic shift and a disrupted tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle characterize pro-inflammatory M1 states [21,22]. Redirecting macrophage metabolism from glycolysis toward OXPHOS can thus promote M2 polarization, establishing an immunomodulatory microenvironment conducive to bone repair [23,24].

Notably, osteoclasts derived from the fusion of macrophages also undergo energy metabolic reprogramming. Osteoclast differentiation is a biological process that requires substantial energy to support both their maturation and functional activity. Studies have shown that efficient OXPHOS serves as the key energy source driving osteoclast differentiation. Despite a shift toward glycolysis during the resorption phase for rapid ATP production [25,26], the foundational importance of OXPHOS is emphasized by its central role throughout the osteoclast life cycle. For instance, deletion of the mitochondrial complex I subunit Ndufs4 not only impedes osteoclast differentiation but also weakens their resorptive capacity, thereby reducing bone resorption in osteoporotic mouse models [27]. Similarly, inhibiting salt-induced kinase 3 (SIK3)-a key regulator of intracellular energy homeostasis-downregulates OXPHOS-related gene expression in osteoclasts, subsequently suppressed their differentiation and resorptive function [28]. In summary, OXPHOS plays a crucial role throughout the entire life cycle of osteoclasts, spanning their differentiation and functional execution. Considering that OXPHOS plays an important role in both M2 polarization and osteoclast formation, and the M2-osteoclast axis is critical in materials-induced bone formation [14,29], we hypothesized that, by orchestrating the M2-osteoclast axis, OXPHOS-driven metabolic reprogramming in macrophages could be a key mechanism underpinning the differential osteoinductive performance of materials.

To test the hypothesis, we employed a murine muscle pouch model to systematically investigate the causal links between OXPHOS-mediated metabolic reprogramming, macrophage M2 polarization, osteoclast formation and coupling, and the final bone formation in osteoinductive material implants. Meanwhile, we verified further the in vivo findings with in vitro osteoclastogenesis, using selective chemicals. As a central metabolic hub, OXPHOS drives the M2-macrophage to osteoclast immunometabolic cascade via coordinated ATP production and reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, enhancing the osteoinductive efficacy of calcium phosphate ceramics (Scheme 1). This study is trying to provide novel mechanistic insights into osteoinductive materials and to establish a theoretical foundation for novel bone regeneration strategies with either material optimization or chemicals.

Experimental section

Materials

Porous TCPS and TCPB (Φ5.0 × 2.5 mm) were provided by Kuros Biosciences BV (Bilthoven, The Netherlands). Preparation of the materials was reported previously [3]. The materials were dry sterilized at 160 °C for 120 min prior to use.

Animals

Kunming (KM) mice (male, 6-7 weeks old, Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co.,Ltd.) were used in this study. Animal experiments were conducted following the ARRIVE guidelines and the studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Chongqing Medical University [2025(069)].

In vivo study

Surgical implantation

This study employed an intramuscular pouch implantation model. Mice were subjected to inhalation of 2% isoflurane (ISOTHESIA, UK) for anesthesia and subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg, Buprenex Injection, USA) for analgesia. After shaving the surgical site, the operative area was sterilized sequentially with povidone-iodine and 75% ethanol. Bilateral intramuscular pouches were then created in the gluteus maximus muscles, into which TCPS and TCPB materials (Φ5.0 × 2.5 mm, disc-shaped) were implanted in the left and right sides of the same animal, respectively. The wound was closed with 5-0 non-tension sutures.

Pharmacological treatments

To study the effects of OXPHOS and ROS inhibitors on bone formation in vivo, mice were given either oligomycin (1 nM, MCE, USA) or Mito-Tempo (70 μM, Abmole, USA). Oligomycin was dissolved in a solvent solution containing 0.2% carboxymethylcellulose and 0.25% Tween-80 in sterile H2O, Mito-Tempo was dissolved in PBS. Injections were given every other day around the material for one week or two weeks, and control group was injected with an equal amount of PBS.

Sample harvesting

Mice were euthanized at predetermined time points by excess CO2. The implants were carefully separated from the surrounding tissues and stored in 1.5 mL Eppendorf (EP) tubes containing RNALater Animal Tissue RNA Stabilization Preservation Solution (Beyotime, China) for RNA isolation or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China) for histology and immunofluorescence analysis.

Histological analysis

Samples collected from muscle pouches were decalcified with EDTA decalcification solution (slow decalcification) (ServiceBio, China), embedded in paraffin (Leica, Germany), and sectioned for subsequent Masson's trichrome staining, TRAP staining, and immunofluorescence staining.

Immunofluorescence staining

Decalcified paraffin sections were dewaxed with xylene and ethanol, followed by pretreatment using an immunohistochemical detection system kit (Bioss, China) prior. Briefly, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies included CD206 (1:500, Proteintech, China); CTSK (1:500, Proteintech, China); OCN (1:500, Servicebio, China). The following day, after rewarming, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, the secondary antibodies involved goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Flour 647 (1:500, Abcam, UK). DAPI stain was applied to visualize nuclei (Beyotime, China). Images were captured under a fluorescent microscope (Leica, Germany).

TRAP staining

The deparaffinized paraffin sections were first dewaxed with xylene and ethanol, incubated with pure water at 37 °C for 2 h, and then stained for TRAP using a commercial TRAP staining kit (Servicebio, China) by incubating in the dark at 37 °C for 20-30 min according to the instructions. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, China) and mounted with neutral resin.

Masson's trichrome staining

The decalcified paraffin sections were first dewaxed with xylene and ethanol, then stained with a Masson's trichrome staining kit (Solarbio, China) following the instruction for use. Sections were mounted with neutral balsam.

Detection of ROS

The decalcified sections were dewaxed in xylene and ethanol and incubated with 5 μM dihydroethidium (DHE) solution (MCE, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min protected from light, and then, the nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 10 min at room temperature protected from light. Images were captured under a fluorescent microscope (Leica, Germany). The fluorescence intensity of 3-5 random areas of each group were quantified and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

RT-qPCR

The sample (0.1 g) was introduced into a 1.5 mL RNAase-free grinding tube containing 600 μl of lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) and RNAase-free grinding beads. Subsequently, the mixture was ground to homogenate in a frozen tissue grinder (JingXin, China) at −50 °C and 60 Hz settings for 30 s with a 10 s interruption for five times. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using an RNA extraction kit (Beyotime, China), and cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (Takara, Japan) under the following thermocycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 1 min (annealing/extension). Gene expression was quantified using a CFX 96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). Gapdh served as the internal reference gene for normalization, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was used to quantify the targeted OXPHOS-related genes, osteoclast-related genes, macrophage polarization-related gene [M2: Cd206] and ROS-related gene [Sod2]. Their primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

TCPB and TCPS were harvested from the muscle pouches of mice for transcriptome analysis 2 weeks after material implantation. Total RNA was extracted from the tissue using TRIzol® Reagent at Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co.,Ltd (Shanghai, China) according the manufacturer's instructions. Then RNA quality was determined by 5300 Bioanalyser (Agilent) and quantified using the ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies). Only high-quality RNA sample (OD260/280 = 1.8-2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RQN≥ 6.5, 28S:18S ≥ 1.0, >1 μg) was used to construct sequencing library at Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co.,Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Histomorphometry

Quantification of TRAP staining and immunofluorescence staining was performed with decalcified sections. Briefly, the stained decalcified sections were digitally scanned (Olympus, Japan) to images for analysis, positive cells per field of view (FOV) with the view size of 1438 μm × 1204 μm in the representative sections were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Quantification of bone was conducted with decalcified sections obtained. In short, the sections were digitally scanned (Olympus, Japan) to get overviews, and percentage of bone in available space was quantified (Area of bone × 100/area of region of interest-area, i.e., the area of CaP ceramic implants) with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

In vitro study

RNA sequencing

Mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (mBMDMs) were inoculated on TCPB and TCPS, and cells cultured in osteoclast medium for 4 days (approximately 1.5 × 106 cells per sample) were subjected to transcriptome sequencing. Total RNA was isolated from the specified biological materials using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. 15596026) following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. For library preparation, the total RNA was used as input for RNA sequencing library preparation utilizing the KCTM Digital mRNA Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth Tech. Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The library preparation included the enrichment of PCR products corresponding to fragments ranging from 200 to 500 base pairs. The enriched libraries were quantified and sequenced on a Novaseq X Plus platform (Illumina) using the PE150 sequencing mode to generate paired-end reads.

Culture of mBMDMs

After euthanizing the mice with carbon dioxide, the femurs and tibias of 6-7-week-old male mice were aseptically collected, and the bone marrow was flushed out using complete α-MEM medium (α-MEM medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCellent Bio, Australia) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, USA) (v/v)). The bone marrow cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and erythrocytes were lysed using erythrocyte lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). The cells were then resuspended in 10 mL of complete α-MEM medium and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 24 h, the non-adherent cells were collected and cultured for 3 days in the presence of macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, PeproTech, USA) at a final concentration of 70 ng/mL to generate mBMDMs for further uses.

Polarization of mBMDMs under different treatment conditions

For immunofluorescence staining, 24-well plates were used, while 12-well plates were employed for Rt-qPCR analysis. The mBMDMs were seeded at a density of 1.25 × 105 cells/cm2 in the culture plates with 50 ng/mL M-CSF to assess macrophage polarization. The cells were treated with the following compounds at the indicated final concentrations: 1 nM oligomycin (MCE, USA), 70 μM Mito-Tempo (Abmole USA), and 1 μM MitoPQ (MCE, USA).

Osteoclastogenesis of mBMDMs with different treatments

For RT-qPCR analysis, mBMDMs were cultured in 12-well plates at a seeding density of 1.25 × 105 cells/cm2. For immunofluorescence staining and TRAP staining, cells were incubated in 96-well plates at a seeding density of 1.25 × 105 cells/cm2. Osteoclast differentiation was induced starting on day 0 using medium supplemented with 50 ng/mL M-CSF (PeproTech, USA) and 100 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL, PeproTech, USA). The medium was replaced every other day for 4 days. For chemical treatments, the following compounds were added to the osteoclast induction medium: oligomycin, (MCE USA), 1 nM; Mito-Tempo, (Abmole, USA) 70 μM; MitoPQ, (MCE, USA), 1 nM, 5 nM, 10 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, 500 nM, 1 μM; 2-DG, (MCE USA), 1 μM.

Influence of different conditioned culture medium on osteogenic differentiation of CRL-12424 cells

The CRL-12424 cell line (ATCC), derived from mBMSCs, was maintained in DMEM medium (Sigma, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin, and cells were then harvested using 0.25% trypsin/EDTA (Sigma, USA) for subsequent use. The CRL-12424 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and co-cultured with various conditioned media under the following experimental conditions as described previously [30,31]: The negative control was complete DMEM; the positive control was osteogenic medium [complete DMEM supplemented with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (β-GP), 1.0 × 10−8 M dexamethasone (Dex), and 50 μg/mL vitamin C (VitC), all from Beyotime, China]; and the experimental groups were treated with a 1:2 mixture of either osteoclast-conditioned or macrophage-conditioned medium and complete DMEM, without osteogenic supplements (i.e., β-GP, Dex, VitC). After 7 days of culture, samples were collected for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) to evaluate the osteogenic differentiation potential of CRL-12424 cells.

Osteoclastogenesis on the surface of TCPS and TCPB

TCPS and TCPB (Φ5.0 × 2.5 mm) were placed in 96-well plates and immersed in 100 μL complete medium for 2 h. Subsequently, non-adherent mononuclear cells were seeded at a density of 1.25 × 105 cells/cm2 in 200 μL complete α-MEM medium supplemented with 75 ng/mL M-CSF (PeproTech, USA), followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 3 days. The medium was subsequently replaced with 200 μL complete α-MEM medium containing 37.5 ng/mL M-CSF (PeproTech, USA) and 75 ng/mL RANKL (PeproTech, USA). Medium changes were performed every other day for a total culture period of 4 days.

TRAP staining

The cells were fixed in 4% tissue cell fixative (Solarbio, China) for 30 min, treated with 0.2%-0.3% TritonX-100 permeabilization solution (Solarbio, China) for 30 min, and stained using TRAP staining kit (Servicebio, China). Images were captured under 5× and 10× microscopes and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software for both the TRAP-positive cell numbers and the TRAP positive area per view of field at 5×.

Immunocytochemistry

The cells were fixed with 4% tissue cell fixative (Solarbio, China) for 30 min, treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 permeabilization solution for 20 min. Non-specific antigens were blocked with 5% goat serum (diluted in PBS, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 60 min, and then the samples were incubated with primary antibodies (CD206, Proteintech, China, 1:200; CTSK, Proteintech, China, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, samples were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG H + L, Proteintech, China, 1:200) and Actin-Tracker Red-Rhodamine (Beyotime, China) for 2 h. Cell nuclei were then stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. Images were taken under a fluorescence microscope at 10×, 20× and 40×.

ALP staining

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 37 °C for 30 min. ALP staining was performed using the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase chromogenic kit (Beyotime, China). Images were taken under the microscope at 5× (Leica, Germany).

RT-qPCR

Steps were similar to in vivo samples. Gapdh served as the internal reference gene for normalization, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was used to quantify the targeted OXPHOS-related genes, macrophage polarization-related genes [M2: Cd206], osteoclast-related genes [Trap, Ctsk], ROS-related gene [Sod2] and osteogenesis-related genes [Alp] in MSC. Their primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Detection of ROS

The mBMDMs (1.25 × 105 cells/cm2) were inoculated into 96-well plates and materials (TCPS or TCPB). Cells were treated with oligomycin (1 nM), Mito-Tempo (70 μM), and MitoPQ (100 nM). The cells were incubated in DHE solution (MCE, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then, the cells were washed twice with PBS. The nuclei were stained by Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min at room temperature avoiding light. Images were taken under a fluorescence microscope at 10×, 20× and 40×. The fluorescence intensity of 3-4 random areas of each group were quantified and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Mitochondrial ROS content detection

MitoSOX Red staining revealed mROS generation. The mBMDMs (1.25 × 105 cells/cm2) were inoculated into 96-well plates. Cells were treated with oligomycin (1 nM) for 48 h. The cells were incubated in MitoSOX Red solution (MCE, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then, the cells were washed twice with PBS. The nuclei were stained by Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min at room temperature avoiding light. Images were taken under a fluorescence microscope at 20×.

ATP generation capacity assay

The ATP concentration was calculated using an enhanced ATP assay kit (Beyotime, China) by measuring chemiluminescence with a multi-function microplate reader with chemiluminescence detection function.

Mitochondrial staining

The mBMDMs (1.25 × 105 cells/cm2) were inoculated into 96-well plates or materials (TCPS or TCPB). The cells from plates or materials (TCPS or TCPB) were incubated in MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, China) at 37 °C for 30 min in darkness. Then, the cells were washed twice with PBS. The nuclei were stained by Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min at room temperature avoiding light. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 60× oil immersion objective.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements

The OCR was measured using a Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer (Agilent, USA) with Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent, 103015-100) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, mBMDMs from plates and materials (TCPS or TCPB) were seeded in XF96-well Seahorse assay plates (at a concentration of 1.5 × 104 cells/well) cultured overnight for attachment. On the assay day, the medium was replaced with XF base medium containing 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and 1 mM pyruvate, followed by incubation in a CO2-free incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. Oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (2 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM) were prepared from the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit and injected sequentially.

Real-time ATP rate assay

The Real-Time ATP production rate was measured using a Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer (Agilent, USA) with Seahorse XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay kit (Agilent, 103592-100), following the manufacturer's instructions. Cell seeding, culture conditions, medium replacement, and CO2-free incubation were performed as described in 2.4.15. Oligomycin (1.5 μM) and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM) were injected sequentially. The ATP production rate was calculated using Seahorse Wave pro software.

Statistical analysis

According to different scenarios, t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test and two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test were performed by using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. All the data were represented by mean ± SD, with statistical significance denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

TCPS, but not TCPB, induces robust ectopic bone formation in mice

To evaluate the osteoinductive potentials of TCPB versus TCPS, porous discs (Φ5.0 × 2.5 mm; n = 4 per group) were implanted into the gluteal muscle pouches of mice up to 7 weeks. Histological and immunofluorescence analyses were performed. Masson's trichrome staining revealed substantial new bone formation in all TCPS implants, whereas bone formation was not observed in any TCPB implant (Fig. 1b; Fig. S1, Supporting Information). Furthermore, strong expression of osteocalcin (OCN), a specific osteoblast marker, was detected in the TCPS group (Fig. 1c). These results demonstrate that TCPS is osteoinductive in vivo, while TCPB is not.

Yellow asterisks denote the implant material; Red arrows indicate newly formed bone. New bone around the implant is observed in the TCPS group, whereas no bone formation is detected in TCPB. c) Immunofluorescence staining for OCN (green) and nuclei (DAPI, blue) in 7-week implants (n = 3). Strong OCN deposition is evident in the bone matrix surrounding TCPS implants, while TCPB exhibits negligible OCN immunoreactivity. (d, e) Heatmaps showing the expression of d) OXPHOS-related genes and e) osteoclast-related genes in implants harvested at 2 weeks (n = 6 per material). f) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between TCPS and TCPB at 2 weeks (n = 6 per material). g) RT-qPCR validation of selected OXPHOS-related genes in 2-week implants (n = 5). Two-tailed Student's t-test. h) RT-qPCR validation of selected osteoclast-related genes in 2-week implants (n = 5). Two-tailed Student's t-test. Each error bar represents the mean ± SD. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

TCPS implants induce increased osteoclast formation and elevated OXPHOS levels at early intramuscular implantation time

To uncover the mechanistic basis for the differential osteoinductive potentials of TCPS and TCPB, we conducted transcriptomic profiling of implants harvested two weeks after intramuscular implantation (n = 6 per group). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant upregulation of biological pathways associated with osteoclast differentiation and OXPHOS in TCPS compared to TCPB (Fig. 1f). Consistent with this, heatmap analysis showed elevated expression of key osteoclast-related genes (e.g., Calcr, Acp5, Oscar, Ocstamp, Mmp9, Dcstamp) and OXPHOS-related genes (e.g., mt-Atp6, mt-Co3, Cox7a2l, Atp6v1f, Atp6v0b, mt-Nd3, Atp5f1e, Ndufa6) in the TCPS group (Fig. 1d–e). This distinct transcriptional signature was independently validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1g–h; Fig. S2a–b, Supporting Information). The readouts indicate that TCPS uniquely initiates a coupled molecular response involving osteoclast programming and enhanced mitochondrial energy metabolism.

TCPS enhances osteoclast differentiation and promotes mitochondrial energy metabolism in vitro

To evaluate the osteoclastogenic and metabolic responses to materials in a controlled setting, mBMDMs were cultured on TCPB or TCPS discs in vitro (n = 3 per group) and differentiated into osteoclasts using M-CSF and RANKL. TRAP staining on day 4 showed significantly enhanced osteoclast formation on TCPS compared to TCPB (Fig. 2a). Transcriptomic profiling of the 4-day cultures further revealed upregulation of the OXPHOS pathway and elevated expression of key OXPHOS-related genes (e.g., mt-Atp6, mt-Atp8, mt-Nd2, mt-Nd3, mt-Nd6, mt-Co3, Atp5md) in cells grown on TCPS (Fig. 2b–d). Consistent with these findings, the real-Time ATP rate assay (n = 8) demonstrated significantly higher ATP production rates from mitochondrial respiration (mitoATP) (Fig. 2e) in the TCPS group compared to TCPB, while no significant difference was observed in ATP production rates from glycolysis (glycoATP) (Fig. 2f) between the two groups. These results indicate that the elevated ATP production on TCPS was primarily driven by enhanced mitochondrial OXPHOS activity rather than glycolysis, further confirming that TCPS promotes osteoclastogenesis and mitochondrial energy metabolism in vitro.

OXPHOS inhibition impairs mitochondrial function and suppresses osteoclast differentiation in vitro

To characterize the dynamic changes in energy metabolism during osteoclastogenesis, we measured ATP production rates using the Real-Time ATP rate assay. The mitoATP production rates were elevated in osteoclasts mature stage (day 4) and osteoclast formation stage (day 2) compared to macrophages stage (day 0) (Fig. S3a–b). Notably, the mitoATP production rate peaked at the osteoclast formation stage (day 2) and declined upon further maturation (day 4) (Fig. S3b). In contrast, the glycolytic ATP production rate increased after osteoclast differentiation while no significant difference was observed during osteoclastogenesis (Fig. S3c). To determine the functional contribution of OXPHOS-derived ATP versus glycolysis-derived ATP to osteoclast formation, we additionally examined the effect of the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG) on osteoclastogenesis in vitro. 2-DG treatment did not significantly impair osteoclast formation (Fig. S3d–e), demonstrating that glycolysis-derived ATP is dispensable for the differentiation process.

Correspondingly, extracellular flux analysis showed that osteoclast precursors exhibited markedly higher OCR than undifferentiated mBMDMs. Mitochondrial stress tests confirmed that basal respiration, proton leak, maximal respiration, ATP production and non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption were all elevated in osteoclast precursors (Fig. 2g–h). To directly investigate the functional role of OXPHOS, we inhibited ATP synthase using oligomycin (OM). OM suppressed OCR in both mBMDMs and osteoclast precursors, with a more pronounced effect in osteoclast precursors, indicating their heightened OXPHOS dependence (Fig. 2g–h). The number and area of TRAP+ and CTSK+ cells were significantly reduced (Fig. 3a–b, d-e) and the expression of osteoclast genes Ctsk and Trap was downregulated (Fig. 3c) when mBMDMs were cultured on routine culture plates or on TCPS in the presence of OM in culture medium for 4 days.

Given that OXPHOS is a core mitochondrial process, we next examined the effects of OM on mitochondrial integrity. Four-day treatment with OM decreased mitochondrial number in cells cultured on culture plates (Fig. 3f–g) and reduced the fluorescence intensity of the OXPHOS subunit ATP6 (Fig. 3h), demonstrating OM-induced mitochondrial impairment. Mitochondrial dysfunction frequently involves impaired OXPHOS. To assess this, we performed OCR measurements using the Seahorse XF Mito Stress Test. The OCR analysis revealed that cells at day 0 exhibited significantly lower basal and maximal respiration than those at days 2 and 4, confirming the reliance on OXPHOS during osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 3i–j). Furthermore, OM treatment reduced OCR at all time points examined, further corroborating the inhibitory effect of OM on mitochondrial OXPHOS activity (Fig. 3i–j). In summary, pharmacological inhibition of OXPHOS by OM compromises mitochondrial function, reduces OCR, and attenuates osteoclastogenic differentiation, underscoring the essential role of oxidative metabolism in osteoclastogenesis.

Early pharmacological inhibition of OXPHOS suppressed TCPS-induced osteoclastogenesis and subsequent bone formation

To investigate the requirement of OXPHOS in material-induced bone formation, we locally administered OM (ATP synthase inhibitor) around intramuscular TCPS implants in mice at the early postoperative phase up to 2 weeks with peri-implant injections every other day (OM vs. PBS control, n = 3-5 per group per time point). OM treatment nearly abolished the appearance of TRAP+ and CTSK+ cells around the implants harvested at week 2, compared with PBS-injected controls (Fig. 4b-c, g). Consistent with this morphological suppression, Trap gene expression was significantly downregulated (Fig. 4f) in the OM-treated samples at week 2, confirming that early OXPHOS inhibition potently blocks osteoclast formation. Crucially, this transient inhibition during the first two weeks also impaired subsequent bone induction. By week 8, TCPS implants in the OM group exhibited significantly less new bone compared to the PBS-treated group (Fig. 4d-e, h). These findings demonstrate that early osteoclast activation, which depends on functional OXPHOS, is essential for bone formation in TCPS implants.

OXPHOS inhibition impairing early osteoclastogenesis by disrupting M2 macrophage polarization

To identify the critical temporal requirement for OXPHOS activity during osteoclast differentiation, OM (ATP synthase inhibitor) was administered at different stages of in vitro osteoclastogenic differentiation. TRAP staining demonstrated that early inhibition (from day 0) strongly suppressed osteoclast formation, whereas later addition (days 2-4) had minimal effect (Fig. 5a–b), indicating that OXPHOS is specifically essential at the initial phase of osteoclast commitment. Given that osteoclasts originate from the macrophage lineage, we further investigated whether OM affects macrophage polarization. In vitro, OM treatment significantly reduced both the expression of the M2 marker gene Cd206 and the number of CD206+ cells (Fig. 5c–d). This polarization inhibition by OM was also observed in vivo. At week 1 post-implantation, CD206+ cells were scarcely detected around TCPB implants or OM-treated TCPS implants, in contrast to the prominent CD206 signal in TCPS controls (n = 3 per group) (Fig. 5f). Consistently, Cd206 mRNA levels were markedly lower in both the TCPB and OM-treated TCPS groups than that in TCPS controls (Fig. 5g).

OXPHOS inhibition disrupts redox homeostasis and elevates ROS levels in vivo and in vitro

To investigate the impact of OXPHOS inhibition on local redox balance, OM (ATP synthase inhibitor) was administered around TCPS intramuscular implants in mice every other day for up to 2 weeks. DHE staining of the implants harvested at week 2 showed a substantial increase of ROS within the OM-treated group (Fig. 5h and i). This rise of ROS was accompanied by elevated expression of the antioxidant gene Sod2 (Fig. 5j). Consistent with these in vivo observations, OM treatment also induced a significant increase of ROS levels in mBMDMs cultured on culture plates (Fig. 5k; Fig. S4a–b, Supporting Information). These data demonstrate that pharmacological suppression of mitochondrial OXPHOS perturbs redox homeostasis, leading to significant ROS accumulation in both in vivo implants and in vitro cell cultures.

We next assessed ROS levels in vivo intramuscular implants in mice. DHE staining of TCP implants harvested at day 5 and post-implantation revealed higher ROS in TCPS compared to TCPB. Local OM injection further augmented ROS accumulation around TCPS implants relative to untreated TCPS controls (Fig. 6e-f; Fig. S5a–b, Supporting Information), indicating that ROS levels are modulated by both materials and OXPHOS activity.

To directly test whether ROS exerts a dose-dependent (biphasic) effect on osteoclastogenic differentiation, mBMDMs were treated with a gradient of MTPQ concentrations during osteoclastogenesis in vitro on culture plates. TRAP staining demonstrated that low-dose MTPQ (<100 nM) enhanced osteoclast formation, whereas higher concentrations (>100 nM) strongly inhibited it (Fig. 6g and h). CCK-8 assays confirmed that this inhibition was not attributable to cytotoxicity within the tested range (Fig. 6i). Notably, the pro-osteoclastogenic MTPQ concentration also elevated ROS levels of cells on culture plates (Fig. 6j–l) and rescued osteoclast formation on the otherwise poorly osteoinductive TCPB (Fig. 6k).

Pharmacological scavenging of ROS impairs M2 macrophage polarization in vitro and in vivo

To determine the requirement of ROS signaling in osteoclastogenesis, we employed the mitochondria-targeted ROS inhibitor, Mito-Tempo (MT). MT significantly reduced the formation of TRAP+ and CTSK+ cells, downregulated the expression of Ctsk and Trap (Fig. 7a-c, e), and suppressed osteoclast differentiation of mBMDMs on TCPS (Fig. 7d). To identify the critical stage at which ROS regulate osteoclastogenesis, MT was introduced at different phases during in vitro osteoclast differentiation. TRAP staining revealed that early intervention during the macrophage stage (day 0) strongly suppressed osteoclast formation. In contrast, treatment at later stages (day 2 and day 4) had no significant inhibitory effect (Fig. 7f-g), indicating that osteoclast formation requires early ROS signaling sustained by OXPHOS. Moreover, MT treatment markedly decreased the number of CD206+ cells in M-CSF–cultured mBMDMs on culture plates (Fig. 7h-i). Consistent with its antioxidant function, MT also lowered intracellular ROS levels and downregulated Sod2 expression (Fig. 7j-l). Together, these data demonstrate that ROS signaling is essential for M2-like macrophage polarization.

At week 2, MT treatment nearly abolished the appearance of TRAP+ and CTSK+ cells around TCPS implants compared to PBS controls (Fig. 8b-c, g) and significantly downregulated the expression of the osteoclast genes Ctsk and Trap (Fig. 8f), confirming that early ROS scavenging potently suppresses osteoclast formation. The mitochondrial electron transport chain generates ATP through OXPHOS, a process accompanied by the production of ROS [33]. Since macrophages cultured on TCPS exhibited significantly higher OXPHOS levels than those on TCPB, we hypothesized that ROS levels would be correspondingly increased. To compare ROS production between the two substrates, we cultured macrophages on TCPS and TCPB in osteoclastogenic induction medium for 2 days. The results confirmed that ROS levels were significantly higher in cells on TCPS than in those on TCPB (Fig. 9e–i). To clarify whether this TCPS-induced ROS is indeed beneficial and falls within a “physiological window”, we supplemented TCPS cultures with a mitochondrial ROS scavenger (MT, Mito-Tempo) or with ROS accumulation (OM, oligomycin). MT treatment drastically reduced ROS, whereas OM sharply increased ROS (Fig. 9e–i). In these four groups, cells cultured on TCPS without any treatment displayed the strongest osteoclast differentiation capacity. In contrast, both ROS depletion (MT) and excessive ROS (OM) suppressed osteoclast formation (Fig. 9f). This confirms that an appropriate ROS level—as induced natively by TCPS—is optimal for osteoclastogenesis, while too low or too high ROS is detrimental. We further substantiated these findings in an in vivo implantation model. After implantation, TCPS scaffolds exhibited higher ROS levels than TCPB, but markedly lower than the OM-treated group. The MT-treated group displayed ROS levels comparable to TCPB (Fig. 9g–j). Consistent with the in vitro data, proper ROS levels in the TCPS group favored osteoclast formation, while either insufficient ROS (TCPB, MT) or excessive ROS (OM) compromised osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 9h). These results demonstrate that TCPS, via its surface topography, maintains a favorable ROS homeostasis that supports osteoclast formation and subsequent bone formation in vivo.

Conditioned media from osteoclasts promote osteogenic differentiation of MSCs

To evaluate the influence of osteoclast- and macrophage-derived signals on osteogenesis, MSCs were cultured for 7 days in conditioned media from osteoclasts (OC-CM) or macrophages (Mφ-CM) (n = 3 per group). ALP staining on day 7 revealed that OC-CM enhanced ALP activity compared to the conventional growth medium (DMEM control), whereas Mφ-CM showed no such pro-osteogenic effect (Fig. S6b, Supporting Information). Consistent with this, Alp gene expression followed the same trend (Fig. S6c, Supporting Information).

To exclude the direct effect of OXPHOS on osteogenic differentiation, MSCs were cultured in osteogenic medium with or without OM (ATP synthase inhibitor)-using a concentration previously shown to significantly inhibit osteoclast formation-for 7 days (n = 3 per group). ALP staining demonstrated that OM did not impair the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs (Fig. S7a, Supporting Information). ATP levels remained comparable between the test and control groups (Fig. S7b, Supporting Information), indicating that MSCs are less sensitive to OXPHOS activity than cells relevant to osteoclastogenesis.

Discussion

OXPHOS as a bioenergetic driver of osteoclastogenesis

OXPHOS serves as a central hub in cellular energy metabolism. This process begins with nutrients-such as sugars, lipids, and amino acids-being progressively oxidized through pathways including glycolysis, β-oxidation, and the TCA cycle, yielding high-energy electron carriers NADH and FADH2. Electrons from these carriers are then transferred via the mitochondrial electron transport chain (complexes I-IV) in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The energy released during electron transfer drives proton pumping across the membrane, establishing an electrochemical gradient that powers ATP synthase to produce ATP [34,35]. Mitochondria, as the primary site of OXPHOS, dictate cellular energy output through their functional state-reflected in membrane potential, morphology, and oxygen consumption rate [[36], [37], [38], [39]].

This bioenergetic capacity is fundamentally linked to osteoclastogenesis. Prior studies demonstrate that RANKL-induced differentiation relies on transcriptional coactivators such as PGC1β, PPAR-γ, and ERRα, which drive mitochondrial biogenesis and enhance OXPHOS to meet the high energy demands of cell fusion and early differentiation [40,41]. Furthermore, Tfr1-mediated iron uptake sustains mitochondrial OXPHOS in osteoclasts, with its deficiency impairs mitochondrial membrane potential and content [42]. Collectively, these reports underscore that osteoclast differentiation entails extensive metabolic reprogramming, wherein OXPHOS activation supplies indispensable bioenergy [40,43].

Building on these findings, we mapped the trajectory of OXPHOS-derived ATP and glycolysis-derived ATP across distinct stages of osteoclastogenesis, confirming the upregulation of OXPHOS-derived ATP during osteoclastogenesis. Interestingly, after osteoclast formation, mitochondrial ATP production declines while glycolytic ATP production increases (Fig. S3b–c). In addition, we performed Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Tests to directly measure OCR, a more sensitive and quantitative method than JC-1 to evaluate OXPHOS activity [44]. The OCR analysis revealed that macrophages at day 0 exhibited lower basal OCR compared to preosteoclasts and osteoclasts at days 2 and 4, further confirming the reliance on OXPHOS during osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 3i–j). More importantly, the glycolysis inhibitor (2-DG) treatment did not significantly impair osteoclast formation (Fig. S3d–e), demonstrating that glycolysis-derived ATP is dispensable for the differentiation process. This conclusion is in agreement with the comprehensive metabolic characterization of human osteoclasts by Lemma et al. [45], who showed that osteoclast differentiation is primarily sustained by mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, as evidenced by elevated expression of electron transport chain complexes and increased oxygen consumption rates. Notably, Lemma et al. further established that while OXPHOS is the main bioenergetic source for osteoclast formation, glycolysis becomes functionally important at the later stage of bone resorption, when glycolytic enzymes localize near the actin ring to facilitate matrix degradation [45]. Our results fully align with this biphasic metabolic model. Taken together, these data establish that OXPHOS-derived ATP, rather than glycolytic ATP, is the primary energetic driver of osteoclast differentiation.

Real-time monitoring of mitochondrial respiration confirmed significantly elevated mitochondrial respiration in osteoclasts precursors compared to macrophages (Fig. 2g-h). Pharmacological inhibition of OXPHOS using OM (ATP synthase inhibitor) robustly suppressed osteoclast formation in vitro (Fig. 3a-e), concomitant with impaired mitochondrial function (Fig. 3f-j). Notably, the inhibitory effect of OM exhibited strict temporal dependency: early administration (from day 0) potently blocked osteoclastogenesis, whereas later addition (days 2-4) had little impact (Fig. 5a-b). This temporal specificity suggested that OXPHOS functions at the early upstream stage, potentially by shaping macrophage to polarizing to the precursor stage of osteoclasts. We examined whether OXPHOS influences macrophage polarization. Indeed, OM significantly suppressed M2 polarization both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 5c-d, f-g), positioning OXPHOS-driven macrophage polarization as an early gatekeeper in the osteoclastogenic cascade.

The dual role of OXPHOS: ATP production and ROS homeostasis

As the core source of cellular energy, OXPHOS is intrinsically coupled to the generation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS). During electron transport in the inner mitochondrial membrane-particularly at complexes I and III-a fraction of electrons can “leak” and react directly with oxygen, forming superoxide anions (O2•−), a major intracellular ROS source [46,47]. When OXPHOS is impaired, mROS levels rise sharply, potentially damaging mitochondrial components and triggering mitochondrial stress [48,49]. Consistently, our in vitro and in vivo data confirmed that OXPHOS inhibition leads to increased ROS levels (Fig. 5h–k; Fig. S4, Supporting Information).

ROS are established promoters of osteoclastogenesis [50]. RANKL-induced ROS production via Rac1/NOX1 activates NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K pathways, upregulating osteoclast-related genes [[51], [52], [53]] while antioxidant treatment suppresses such differentiation [54]. Our data corroborate that elevated ROS can enhance osteoclast formation. However, we observed a biphasic regulatory effect: beyond a critical threshold, further increases of ROS inhibited osteoclastogenic differentiation (Fig. 6g–h). To determine if reduced ROS would also impair the process, we employed MT (mitochondria-targeted ROS inhibitor) in vitro and found that ROS suppression indeed inhibited osteoclast formation (Fig. 7a-e), with time-course analysis revealing this inhibition occurs primarily during the early stages (Fig. 7f-g). Subsequent experiments confirmed that reduced ROS levels suppress M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 7h-i).

In summary, using a mouse bone marrow-derived monocyte model, we demonstrate that mitochondrial OXPHOS orchestrates osteoclastogenesis by serving as a central metabolic hub. It drives early M2 macrophage polarization through coupled ATP production and the maintenance of a permissive ROS homeostasis.

OXPHOS mediates material-driven osteoclastogenesis

A direct metabolic comparison between TCPS and TCPB revealed that the submicron-scale TCPS surface significantly increased mitochondrial abundance and enhanced OXPHOS relative to the micron-scale TCPB surface, resulting in a physiological elevation of intracellular ROS (Fig. 9a–e). To determine whether this endogenous ROS elevation lies within a functionally beneficial range, we employed ROS depletion (MT) and excessive ROS (OM) as orthogonal pharmacological tools, not to mimic the biomaterial's effect, but to map the ROS response spectrum. MT scavenging reduced ROS below the level intrinsically induced by TCPS, while OM provoked a pathological ROS surge, both extremes impaired osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 9e–f). These results confirm that TCPS itself establishes a pro-osteoclastogenic optimal redox window, in contrast to the exogenous disruption caused by OM or MT. This concept aligns with the emerging view that ROS are not exclusively harmful, moderately elevated ROS can act as signaling molecules to maintain physiological functions, whereas excessive ROS scavenging may disrupt redox homeostasis [55].

These in vitro findings were fully corroborated in vivo. Mirroring the culture results, the ROS level maintained in the TCPS group favored robust osteoclast formation, while both insufficient ROS (TCPB group and MT treatment) and excessive ROS (OM treatment) impaired this process (Fig. 9g–h). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the submicron-scale TCPS surface drives osteoclastogenesis by upregulating mitochondrial OXPHOS, which generates a controlled, pro-osteoclastogenic ROS signal. In contrast, the micron-scale TCPB surface fails to achieve this metabolic and redox state, providing a mechanistic explanation for its inferior osseointegration.

Osteoclasts as coupling agents in material-induced bone formation

Osteoclasts are indispensable for bone remodeling, fulfilling roles that extend beyond mere resorption. While classically viewed as pioneers that clear the path for subsequent osteoblast-dependent bone formation [56], accumulating evidence highlights their active signaling functions. Osteoclasts secrete factors such as PDGF-BB [57], C3a [58] and Cthrc1 [14]-and engage in bidirectional EphrinB2-EphB4 crosstalk that fine-tunes the bone resorption-formation balance [59]. This paradigm shift helps explain why moderate, well-coupled osteoclast activity can enhance-rather than impair-bone regeneration [14,29,60].

Consistent with this emerging view, we observed that the osteoinductive TCPS, but not non-osteoinductive TCPB, triggered significant upregulation of osteoclast-differentiation pathways in vivo as early as 2 weeks post-implantation (Fig. 1f). In vitro, TCPS directly promoted osteoclast formation (Fig. 2a), and conditioned medium from these osteoclasts potently enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs (Fig. S6a–c, Supporting Information), underscoring the essential coupling role of osteoclasts in (bio) material-driven osteogenesis.

OXPHOS as the metabolic foundation for material-induced bone formation by enhancing osteoclastogenesis

The central role of OXPHOS extends to serving as the essential metabolic engine for material-induced bone formation, primarily by fueling the osteoclastogenesis that initiates the regenerative cascade. Our study establishes mitochondrial OXPHOS as a decisive metabolic switch underlying the distinct osteoinductive capacities of submicron-structured TCPS versus micron-structured TCPB. Following implantation into murine muscle pouches, robust ectopic bone formation was observed exclusively around TCPS after 7 weeks (Fig. 1b and c), consistent with prior reports [29]. This reproducible disparity-where TCPS reliably induces bone while TCPB remains inert-provided a model system to dissect the governing metabolic mechanisms.

Crucially, local delivery of the OXPHOS inhibitor OM in vivo abolished the material-induced bone formation. To determine whether this inhibition resulted from a direct impairment of osteoblastic differentiation, we assessed the effect of OM on MSCs. MSCs were cultured in osteogenic medium supplemented with or without OM-using a concentration previously shown to potently inhibit osteoclast formation-for 7 days. ALP staining revealed that OM did not impair the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs (Fig. S7a, Supporting Information). This key finding indicates that OXPHOS inhibition blocks bone formation not by directly targeting osteoblast lineage cells, but rather by disrupting an essential upstream process. Together, the in vivo and in vitro evidence positions OXPHOS as the foundational metabolic driver of material-induced bone formation, specifically through its indispensable role in enabling the osteoclastogenesis required to launch the coupled bone-formation cascade.

General discussion and concluding perspectives

Our findings collectively reveal a sophisticated, stage-specific role for OXPHOS-regulated ROS in biomaterial-mediated bone regeneration. Prevailing literature has largely emphasized the detrimental effects of excessive ROS, which inhibit the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs by reducing ALP activity, mineralization, and the expression of key genes like Runx2, Alp, Ocn, and Bmp2 [61,62]. This perspective has driven the development of antioxidant (bio) materials to scavenge ROS but to promote MSCs differentiation [63]. Notably, the studies advocating ROS clearance to enhance bone repair have predominantly relied on pathological models characterized by chronically elevated ROS (possibly with too high levels of ROS) and abundant active osteoclasts, such as osteoporosis or inflammatory injury models [64,65].

In contrast, the repair of critical-sized bone defects-the focus of this study-presents a distinct biological scenario. It involves regeneration at sites distant from host bone, where resident osteoclasts are initially absent and the inflammatory phase post-surgery is transient. Although surgical operation inevitably triggers inflammation and ROS production [66], our work demonstrates that a spatiotemporally controlled, moderate elevation of early ROS is crucial for bone regeneration. This controlled ROS signal, sustained by functional OXPHOS, avoids oxidative damage while steering early immune responses toward productive osteoclastogenesis-a key initiating event for material-induced bone formation.

Therefore, we propose an “indirect osteogenic pathway” for critical-sized bone defect repair. In this model, osteoinductive (bio) materials like TCPS first promote osteoclastogenesis via OXPHOS-maintained ROS homeostasis. These osteoclasts, in turn, initiate the bone-formation cascade in osteoinductive materials through coupling signals. This positions osteoclasts-rather than MSCs-as the essential “founding cells” of bone regeneration in critical-sized bone defect with osteoinductive materials.

Consequently, our study calls for a paradigm shift in the design logic for osteoinductive materials: from a blanket “simple antioxidant” strategy toward a more nuanced “spatiotemporal ROS modulation” approach. The goal is not to eliminate ROS indiscriminately, but to harness its physiological signaling role at the correct time, place, and concentration to orchestrate favorable immunometabolic crosstalk. This refined perspective provides a new theoretical foundation for designing next-generation (bio) materials capable of repairing complex and critical-sized bone defects.

Conclusion

This work establishes mitochondrial OXPHOS as the central metabolic hub underlying the material-induced bone formation. OXPHOS drives bone formation in osteoinductive materials through a dual, synergistic role: it furnishes the bioenergetic currency (ATP) required for M2 macrophage polarization and osteoclast differentiation, while concurrently maintaining a moderate window of ROS essential for signaling and functional coupling within this immunometabolic cascade. Disruption of either OXPHOS activity or ROS homeostasis abrogates bone formation. Thus, targeting the OXPHOS-ROS axis offers a fundamental metabolic engineering strategy for designing next-generation bone grafting materials with more potent and predictable bone regeneration performance.
