Hierarchical Planning with Latent World Models
Wancong Zhang, Basile Terver, Artem Zholus, Soham Chitnis, Harsh Sutaria, Mido Assran, Randall Balestriero, Amir Bar, Adrien Bardes, Yann LeCun, Nicolas Ballas

Abstract
Model predictive control (MPC) with learned world models has emerged as a promising paradigm for embodied control, particularly for its ability to generalize zero-shot when deployed in new environments. However, learned world models often struggle with long-horizon control due to the accumulation of prediction errors and the exponentially growing search space. In this work, we address these challenges by learning latent world models at multiple temporal scales and performing hierarchical planning across these scales, enabling long-horizon reasoning while substantially reducing inference-time planning complexity. Our approach serves as a plug-in planning abstraction that applies across diverse latent world-model architectures and domains. We demonstrate that this hierarchical approach enables zero-shot control on real-world non-greedy robotic tasks, achieving a 70% success rate on pick-&-place using only a final goal specification, compared to 0% for a single-level world model. In addition, across physics-based simulated environments including push manipulation and maze navigation, hierarchical planning achieves higher success while requiring up to 3 × 3\times less planning-time compute.

Introduction

Planning with learned world models has emerged as a powerful paradigm for embodied decision making. By learning predictive models of environment dynamics from experience and using them to simulate future outcomes, world models enable agents to reason about the long-term consequences of actions
(
Sutton 1981
;
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
)
. Recent advances in representation learning and latent-space modeling have improved the scalability of this approach, making it possible to train world models directly from high-dimensional observations such as images and videos
(
Ha and Schmidhuber 2018
;
Hafner et al. 2023
;
Hansen et al. 2023
)
.
More recently, self-supervised learning has enabled training world models from large collections of unlabeled, task-agnostic offline trajectories, allowing zero-shot deployment in downstream robotic control and navigation
(
Assran et al. 2025
;
Sobal et al. 2025
;
Bar et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Terver et al. 2026b
)
. Despite this progress, learned world models continue to struggle with long-horizon control due to two fundamental challenges: (i) prediction errors compound over time, leading to increasingly inaccurate rollouts
(
Talvitie 2014
;
Janner et al. 2019
)
, and (ii) even under a perfect model, planning becomes computationally expensive as the search space grows exponentially with horizon
(
Ichter et al. 2020
)
.

A classic approach to long-horizon control is to introduce
hierarchy
into the decision-making structure. By reasoning at multiple temporal scales, agents can plan coarsely over long horizons while retaining fine-grained control at short horizons. This intuition underlies a large body of work on temporal abstraction in reinforcement learning, including options, skills, and hierarchical policies
(
Sutton et al. 1999
;
Bacon et al. 2017
;
Hafner et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2023
)
, as well as hierarchical world models that capture dynamics at multiple timescales
(
Gumbsch et al. 2023
;
Chitnis et al. 2024
;
Schiewer et al. 2024
;
Hansen et al. 2024
)
. In parallel, the optimal control literature has explored hierarchical model predictive control (MPC) for multi-task settings
(
Fang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Kögel et al. 2025
)
.
However, these approaches remain limited in complementary ways. Reinforcement learning methods typically rely on task-specific rewards, which restrict their applicability in zero-shot settings; even goal-conditioned and zero-shot RL methods
(
Park et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2024b
)
still struggle to generalize to environments and tasks beyond the training distribution
(
Sobal et al. 2025
)
. Conversely, existing hierarchical MPC methods are not task-specific but are largely restricted to low-dimensional state spaces, hand-engineered representations, or known dynamics, limiting their applicability to high-dimensional observation spaces such as raw pixels. As a result, it remains unclear how to realize hierarchical planning for zero-shot control directly on learned world models from high-dimensional observations.

In this work, we propose
Hierarchical Planning with Latent World Models
(HWM), a framework for zero-shot hierarchical model predictive control using learned latent world models. Rather than learning hierarchical policies, HWM introduces temporal hierarchy directly at inference time by planning over multiple timescales on task-agnostic world models trained via next-latent prediction.
Crucially, our approach is defined by how the hierarchical levels interact. HWM learns world models operating at different temporal resolutions within a shared latent space, enabling a direct coupling between levels: high-level predictions act as subgoals for low-level MPC via latent-state matching. This removes the need for inverse models, skill learning, or goal-conditioned policies, and allows hierarchical planning to operate entirely on pretrained world models.
To support efficient long-horizon planning, we further introduce a learned action encoder that compresses sequences of primitive actions between waypoint states into latent macro-actions. This reduces the dimensionality of the high-level search space and makes planning more tractable. By decoupling long-horizon reasoning from fine-grained control, HWM mitigates error accumulation and reduces overall planning complexity.

2
Hierarchical Planning with Latent World Models

2.1
Problem Setting

We consider a Markov decision process (MDP)
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mu,p)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
is the initial state distribution, and
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}
denotes the transition dynamics. We work in the offline setting with access to a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of state–action trajectories
\tau=(s_{1},a_{1},s_{2},\ldots,a_{T-1},s_{T})
.
Our objective is to use
\mathcal{D}
to enable zero-shot control across a variety of goal-conditioned tasks. Rather than learning an explicit parametric policy, we learn world models to approximate the true transition dynamics
p
, and select actions at inference time via model predictive control (MPC), which induces a goal-conditioned policy. We consider goal-reaching tasks at evaluation time, where the agent is provided with a goal state
s_{g}
and succeeds by reaching it. In this setting, the task space
\mathcal{Z}
coincides with the state space
\mathcal{S}
.

2.2
Latent World Models

A
world model
is a learned dynamical model that approximates how an environment evolves in response to an agent’s actions, estimating transition dynamics of the form
p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t})
. In high-dimensional settings, world models are typically learned in a
latent space
to improve efficiency and robustness. An encoder maps observations
s_{t}
to latent states
z_{t}
, and a latent world model predicts future latents conditioned on actions,
p(z_{t+1}\mid z_{t},a_{t})
. Recent work has shown that latent world models learned from reward-free, task-agnostic offline data can support zero-shot, goal-conditioned control in downstream planning tasks
(
Assran et al. 2025
;
Sobal et al. 2025
;
Bar et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Terver et al. 2026b
)
. However, planning directly with such models using flat MPC becomes increasingly brittle as task horizons grow, due to compounding prediction errors and the expanding search space.

2.3
Top-Down Hierarchical Planning

Latent world models naturally support hierarchical planning, as different levels of abstraction can reason over different temporal horizons within a shared predictive framework. By planning at an appropriate temporal resolution, hierarchical methods avoid direct optimization over long sequences of primitive actions while retaining fine-grained control.

In this work, we adopt a
top-down
hierarchical planning strategy that operates entirely in a learned latent space (
fig.

2
). At a high level, the agent plans abstract latent actions toward a final goal using a long-horizon world model. The resulting predicted latent states serve as intermediate subgoals. A low-level planner then optimizes short-horizon sequences of primitive actions to reach the next subgoal, repeating this process in a receding-horizon MPC manner.

We now describe the latent representations and world models used by the hierarchical planner. Let
E
denote an encoder that maps observations
s_{t}
to latent states
z_{t}=E(s_{t})
. We assume access to two learned latent world models operating at different temporal resolutions:

(i)
a low-level model
P^{(1)}(z_{t+1}\mid z_{t},a_{t})
that predicts short-horizon transitions conditioned on primitive actions, and

(ii)
a high-level model
P^{(2)}(z_{t+h}\mid z_{t},l_{t})
that predicts long-horizon transitions conditioned on latent macro-actions
l_{t}
.

The macro-actions
l_{t}
are produced by a learned action encoder that summarizes temporally extended sequences of low-level actions. Unlike fixed-stride temporal abstraction methods, we do not assume a fixed high-level horizon
h
, allowing each high-level transition to correspond to a variable-length segment of low-level execution.

High-Level Planning.

Given an initial observation
s_{1}
and a goal observation
s_{g}
, we encode them into latent space as
z_{1}=E(s_{1})
and
z_{g}=E(s_{g})
. At the high level, we plan over a sequence of
H
latent actions
\hat{l}_{1:H}
by minimizing a goal-conditioned energy function in latent space:

\mathcal{E}_{2}(\hat{l}_{1:H};z_{1},z_{g})\;\triangleq\;\left\|z_{g}-P^{(2)}(\hat{l}_{1:H};z_{1})\right\|_{1}

Here,
P^{(2)}(\hat{l}_{1:H};z_{1})
denotes the final latent state obtained by autoregressively unrolling the world model
P^{(2)}
starting from
z_{1}
with latent actions
\hat{l}_{1:H}
. The optimal latent action sequence is given by:

l^{*}_{1:H}=\arg\min_{\hat{l}_{1:H}}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\hat{l}_{1:H};z_{1},z_{g}).

Unrolling the optimized latent plan yields a sequence of intermediate latent subgoals at the high-level temporal resolution:

\tilde{z}_{i}\;\triangleq\;P^{(2)}(l^{*}_{1:i};z_{1}),\ i=1,\ldots,H
.

Low-Level Planning.

At execution time, the agent plans primitive actions to reach the first latent subgoal
\tilde{z}_{1}
. Starting from the current latent state
z_{1}
, we define a low-level energy function over a horizon of
h
steps:

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\hat{a}_{1:h};z_{1},\tilde{z}_{1})\;\triangleq\;\left\|\tilde{z}_{1}-P^{(1)}(\hat{a}_{1:h};z_{1})\right\|_{1}

The optimal low-level action sequence is obtained via

a^{*}_{1:h}=\arg\min_{\hat{a}_{1:h}}\mathcal{E}_{1}(\hat{a}_{1:h};z_{1},\tilde{z}_{1})
, which is fed into the controller. The agent re-plans every
k
interactions using the hierarchical planner.

2.4
Low-Level Latent World Model

The low-level latent world model
P^{(1)}_{\theta}
predicts short-horizon transitions in latent space conditioned on primitive actions. Given an observation
s_{t}
, we encode it into a latent representation
z_{t}=E(s_{t})
and use
P^{(1)}_{\theta}
to model
p(z_{t+1}\mid z_{t},a_{t})
. The model is trained using standard latent world-model objectives, including teacher-forcing and multi-step autoregressive rollout losses, following prior work on JEPA-style predictive models (
section

B.1
). In our experiments, we reuse the training recipes and architectures of the underlying backbone world models (VJEPA2-AC, DINO-WM, and PLDM), ensuring that any performance gains are attributable to hierarchical planning rather than changes in the low-level dynamics model.

3.1
Franka Arm with Robotiq Gripper

We evaluate HWM on real-world multi-stage manipulation with a Franka arm equipped with a Robotiq gripper, resembling the Droid setup. We run pick-&-place and drawer opening/closing. These tasks require
non-greedy
behavior, where successful execution depends on intermediate motions that temporarily increase distance to the goal—a regime in which single-level planners typically fail.

Platform and Task.

We follow the VJEPA2-AC deployment setup and evaluate on real-world pick-&-place and drawer manipulation using a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda arm with a two-finger gripper. Pick-&-place includes 10 start–goal pairs across two objects (cup and box), while drawer manipulation covers 7 opening and closing tasks (see
appendix

G
). Success is defined as reaching the goal.

Baselines.

VJEPA2-AC

(
Assran et al. 2025
)
is a single-level, world-model-based planner.
Because we use the same architecture and training data for our low-level world model, this comparison isolates the effect of hierarchical planning.
We additionally evaluate three vision–language-action models (VLA) baselines
Octo
,
\bm{\pi}_{0}
-FAST-DROID, and
\bm{\pi}_{0.5}
-DROID (see
section

J.1
for details).

Data.

World models are trained on approximately 130 hours of unlabeled real-robot manipulation data from
DROID
(
Khazatsky et al. 2024
)
and RoboSet
(
Kumar et al. 2023
)
.
Observations include RGB images and end-effector proprioception; actions correspond to end-effector
delta poses (see
section

B.2
for details).

Hierarchical Planning Setup.

The agent is provided with the current and goal observations, each specified by RGB images and end-effector poses. Agent’s actions are end-effector delta poses that are mapped to joints delta poses via inverse kinematics. For each action, the corresponding joints delta is executed in a blocking fashion. The high-level planner optimizes over latent actions of dimension 4, using 3000 CEM samples with a prediction horizon of 2, while the low-level planner uses 800 CEM samples with a prediction horizon of 2. The agent replans every
k=1
step in standard MPC manner. Planning rollouts are executed in parallel across GPUs, making the approach computationally efficient even with large numbers of trajectory samples. Full CEM hyperparameters are provided in
appendix

C
.

World Model Architectures.

We follow the VJEPA2-AC training recipe for the low-level world model (see
section

B.2
).
To train the high-level world model,
We use
N=3
waypoint states sampled from trajectory segments spanning up to
4
seconds, with the middle waypoint chosen uniformly at random. Latent actions are encoded from the intervening low-level actions using a transformer-based action encoder, with the CLS token projected to the latent action space.
For visualization, we decode latent representations into RGB images using a ViT decoder; these reconstructions are used solely for qualitative analysis and are not involved in planning or training (details in
appendix

E

A key challenge in long-horizon planning with learned world models is error accumulation during autoregressive rollouts, where small one-step errors compound over time. We hypothesize that models trained at longer temporal scales yield more accurate long-horizon predictions by reducing the number of autoregressive steps required. To test this, we condition both low-level and high-level world models—trained on the DROID dataset—on held-out initial observations and predict future states up to 2 seconds ahead, measuring

4.3
How to generate good subgoals?

Figure 7
:

Effect of latent action dimension on hierarchical planning.

Left:

When the latent space has very low capacity, the high-level planner fails to produce valid plans.
As

For hierarchical planning to be effective, the high-level planner must propose subgoals that lie on a feasible path to the final goal and are reachable by the low-level planner. High-level plans therefore fall into three categories: invalid plans, valid plans with unreachable subgoals, and valid plans with reachable subgoals.

As shown in
fig.

7
, when the latent action space has sufficient capacity (
\geq 4
dimensions), the high-level planner typically produces valid plans. However, these subgoals are not always reachable by the low-level planner, as they may require non-greedy action sequences. Restricting the latent-action dimensionality biases the planner toward proposing subgoals that are achievable with greedy behavior. This suggests an optimal regime in which the latent space is expressive enough to encode useful trajectories, but not so expressive that it enables subgoals requiring complex non-greedy execution. Empirically, a latent action dimension of 4 strikes this balance for the Franka tasks.

Notably, reconstruction fidelity is not tightly correlated with latent-action dimensionality. Lower-dimensional latent spaces yield noisier predictions, reflected in higher
\ell_{1}
error and blurrier reconstructions (
fig.

7
), as expected under stronger compression. Nevertheless, these predictions often preserve coarse semantic structure, such as contact events or motion direction, which is sufficient for hierarchical planning despite reduced visual precision.

5
Related Works

World Models and Planning.

Learning predictive models of environment dynamics is a core idea in model-based reinforcement learning
(
Sutton 1981
)
, with early work focusing on state-space dynamics for planning and policy optimization
(
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
;
Sutton 1991
)
. More recent approaches learn dynamics from high-dimensional observations, either directly in pixel space or in a learned latent space
(
Ebert et al. 2017
;
Ha and Schmidhuber 2018
;
Hafner et al. 2019
;
Schwarzer et al. 2020
;
Hafner et al. 2023
;
Watter et al. 2015
;
Nair et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Bagatella et al. 2026
)
, enabling efficient learning and planning from pixels in both simulation and real-world robotics.

Recent work further demonstrates that task-agnostic generative world models trained on large-scale, real-world video can produce realistic and diverse simulations of physical environments
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Bruce et al. 2024
;
Bai et al. 2025
)
, highlighting the potential of scaling predictive modeling beyond task-specific datasets. Closely related to our setting, a line of work on latent world models shows that models trained on reward-free offline data via next-latent prediction can generalize zero-shot to downstream control tasks. These approaches leverage the learned dynamics to simulate action outcomes and select actions through cost-based optimization, without requiring task-specific policy training
(
Assran et al. 2025
;
Sobal et al. 2025
;
Terver et al. 2026b
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
.

Limitations of Long Horizon Planning.
Learned world models remain brittle when used for long-horizon planning due to compounding prediction errors
(
Talvitie 2014
;
Janner et al. 2019
)
. Moreover, long-horizon planning is challenging even under a perfect model because the search tree grows exponentially with the branching factor and horizon (a manifestation of the curse of horizon)
(
Ichter et al. 2020
)
.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning.

To address long-horizon control, prior work has explored hierarchical reinforcement learning through temporal abstraction via options and skills
(
Sutton et al. 1999
;
Bacon et al. 2017
;
Hafner et al. 2022
)
. In parallel, many model-based RL approaches incorporate hierarchical world models to capture dynamics at multiple temporal resolutions
(
Gumbsch et al. 2023
;
Chitnis et al. 2024
;
Schiewer et al. 2024
;
Hansen et al. 2024
)
. While these methods enable improved long-horizon reasoning, they typically rely on task-specific policy learning, which limits their applicability to zero-shot and general-purpose control.

Hierarchical Model Predictive Control.
In the optimal control literature, hierarchical model predictive control (MPC) has been explored for long-horizon, multi-task settings
(
Fang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Kögel et al. 2025
)
. However, existing hierarchical MPC approaches are largely restricted to low-dimensional state spaces, hand-engineered representations, or known dynamics, limiting their applicability to high-dimensional observation spaces such as raw pixels.

We provide a more detailed comparison of these approaches, including their relationship to our method and a summary across key design axes, in
appendix

A
.

6
Conclusion

We presented HWM, a hierarchical planning framework that performs model predictive control over learned latent dynamics at multiple temporal scales. By decoupling long-horizon reasoning from fine-grained action execution, HWM mitigates compounding prediction errors and the exponentially growing search spaces that limit single-level world-model-based planners. Across diverse world-model architectures, hierarchical planning consistently outperforms flat planners on non-greedy, long-horizon tasks while substantially improving inference-time efficiency.

Despite these gains, long-horizon manipulation remains challenging, and performance degrades for all methods as horizons increase. Addressing this limitation will likely require advances in representation learning, including more abstract representations for higher-level world models; uncertainty-aware planning to better handle the increased stochasticity and model uncertainty inherent to long-horizon real-world prediction; and improved hierarchical planning algorithms that allow feedback and interaction across levels of the hierarchy instead of strictly top-down decomposition. We view HWM as a step toward scalable, general-purpose planning with learned world models, and hope this work encourages future research on hierarchical abstractions for long-horizon decision making in complex environments.

Hierarchical Model Predictive Control methods
(e.g., CAVIN, LAT, Safe HMPC) typically operate in a zero-shot setting, but are largely restricted to low-dimensional or structured state representations, often with known or simplified dynamics. This makes it challenging to directly apply them to high-dimensional perceptual inputs.

Hierarchical Model-Based Reinforcement Learning methods
(e.g., Director, Puppeteer, IQL-TD-MPC) address this limitation by learning dynamics in latent representation spaces derived from high-dimensional observations. However, these approaches rely on reward supervision and are typically trained for specific task objectives, which can limit their applicability in zero-shot settings.

In contrast, HWM operates on learned latent world models trained purely via next-latent prediction from high-dimensional observations, without reward supervision. This enables a task-agnostic formulation in which planning is performed directly in the learned latent space. Concretely, both levels of the hierarchy optimize a shared objective that minimizes the distance between predicted latent states and a goal latent representation, with high-level predictions serving as subgoals for the low-level planner (
section

2.3
). This allows the system to compose behaviors at test time and adapt to new goals and environments in a fully zero-shot manner.

Among prior work, THICK is most closely related to our setting, as it also learns hierarchical latent world models from pixel observations. However, it is developed within a reward-predictive model-based RL framework, where models are trained with reward signals and optimized for reward-based objectives. As a result, its generalization is primarily evaluated within a fixed task distribution. In addition, its MPC-based evaluation focuses on relatively simple simulated settings with aligned training and test distributions and fixed goals.

Visualizing World Model Predictions

Franka Arm with Robotiq Gripper.

To reconstruct RGB images from latent representations, we train on DROID a ViT decoder that maps the frozen V-JEPA 2 encoder features back to pixel space. The decoder takes as input the encoded features of dimension 1408 (matching the V-JEPA 2 ViT-Giant encoder output) and projects them to an intermediate dimension of 1024 via a linear layer. The architecture consists of 12 transformer blocks, each with 32 attention heads and an MLP ratio of 4, using GELU activations, LayerNorm and sinusoidal 2D positional embeddings. The decoder operates on a
32\times 32
grid of spatial tokens (corresponding to patch size 8 for
256\times 256
images). The model is trained using an
L_{1}
reconstruction loss on normalized pixel values. We train the decoder for 100 epochs on the DROID dataset using the AdamW optimizer with a peak learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
, linear warmup over 2 epochs, cosine decay to
10^{-6}
, and weight decay of
0.1
. Training is performed on 8 nodes with a total batch size of 512.

Push-T.

Similarly as for the DROID decoder depicted above, we train a ViT decoder that maps the frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder features back to pixel space on the Push-T dataset. The decoder is a ViT-Base of intermediate dimension of 768 with 12 transformer blocks, each with 16 attention heads. It operates on a
28\times 28
grid of spatial tokens (corresponding to patch size 8 for
224\times 224
images). The model is trained using an
L_{2}
reconstruction loss on normalized pixel values for 40 epochs on the PushT dataset with a total batch size of 256.

Appendix I
Offline Planning Evaluation for Franka

Since planning on a real robot is time-consuming and not parallelizable, we introduce a quantitative offline planning evaluation that enables faster iteration over model variants and planning hyperparameters. We evaluate on the same 10 pick-&-place tasks used in the interactive experiments, with manually defined subgoals corresponding to the grasp point. Expert actions are defined as the robot’s delta pose from the initial observation to the subgoal,
\tilde{a}_{1}
, and from the subgoal to the goal,
\tilde{a}_{2}
.
Conditioned on the initial and goal observations, the hierarchical planner produces a sequence of subgoals along with a sequence of primitive actions
(a_{1:h}^{*})
toward the first subgoal. To assess high-level planning quality, we compare the aggregate inferred action
\sum_{i=1}^{h}a^{*}_{i}
with the expert action
\tilde{a}_{1}
using cosine similarity and
\ell_{1}
distance. High cosine similarity or low
\ell_{1}
distance indicates that the planner has identified a plan that moves toward the non-greedy subgoal, which strongly correlates with real-world task success.

J.1
Vision-Language-Action Models

For our baselines, we reproduce the same setup as in the Droid platform. Specifically, we use a Franka Panda arm with a bi-finger Robotiq gripper, dual camera setup (one wrist camera plus one side camera looking at the robot from the left). Our wrist camera setup is identical to Droid (the same mount and model - ZED Mini), however, for the side camera, we use Intel RealSense. While RealSense has a different aspect ratio (4:3 instead of ZED Mini’s 16:9) we found that simply padding the side image to get 16:9 aspect ratio yields good success rate for VLAs trained on Droid with this aspect ratio.
