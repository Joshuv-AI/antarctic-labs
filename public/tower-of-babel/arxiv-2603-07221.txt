Margin in Abstract Spaces
Yair Ashlagi, Roi Livni, Shay Moran, Tom Waknine

Abstract
Margin-based learning, exemplified by linear and kernel methods, is one of the few classical settings where generalization guarantees are independent of the number of parameters. This makes it a central case study in modern highly over-parameterized learning. We ask what minimal mathematical structure underlies this phenomenon. We begin with a simple margin-based problem in arbitrary metric spaces: concepts are defined by a center point and classify points according to whether their distance lies below r r or above R R . We show that whenever R > 3 ​ r R>3r , this class is learnable in any metric space. Thus, sufficiently large margins make learnability depend only on the triangle inequality, without any linear or analytic structure. Our first main result extends this phenomenon to concepts defined by bounded linear combinations of distance functions, and reveals a sharp threshold: there exists a universal constant γ > 0 \gamma>0 such that above this margin the class is learnable in every metric space, while below it there exist metric spaces where it is not learnable at all. We then ask whether margin-based learnability can always be explained via an embedding into a linear space – that is, reduced to linear classification in some Banach space through a kernel-type construction. We answer this negatively by developing a structural taxonomy of Banach spaces: if a Banach space is learnable for some margin parameter γ ≥ 0 \gamma\geq 0 , then it is learnable for all such γ \gamma , and in infinite-dimensional spaces the sample complexity must scale polynomially in 1 / γ 1/\gamma . Specifically, it must grow as ( 1 / γ ) p (1/\gamma)^{p} for some p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 , and every such rate is attainable.

Introduction

Margin-based learning provides a canonical example of parametric learning in high dimensions due to it being one of the primary settings where generalization does not depend on the number of parameters. Certain hypothesis classes, most notably linear functions, whose learning complexity typically grows with dimensionality, become markedly easier to learn when their effective domain is restricted to points lying a fixed distance away from the decision boundary, i.e., when a margin condition is imposed.
Due to the appealing nature of dimensionality-independent generalization, margin assumptions have been a topic for extensive research in the context of halfspaces and kernel methods in Euclidean and Hilbert spaces. Classic maximal margin algorithms, such as the Support Vector Machines, enjoy dimension-independent generalization guarantees when the data satisfies a margin condition. Simply put, when the margin exceeds a universal threshold, the distance-based threshold class is learnable independently of the underlying metric space and, in this regime, has VC dimension one. In other words, for sufficiently large margins, the learnability of these abstract margin-based classifiers relies only on the triangle inequality and not on any additional linear or geometric structure. Margin-Based Learning in Linear Spaces.

Since our analysis of margin-based learning in general metric spaces provides only a partial characterization of margin based learnability, we shift our focus to the more structured setting of linear spaces. A well studied case of margin learning is the one of linear functionals on Hilbert spaces. Although infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces are not totally bounded, linear classification with margin in these spaces provide a canonical example for margin-based learning, a fact that cannot be accounted for by the metric space perspective.
This motivates our study of linear spaces, where additional geometric structure plays a central role.

At the same time, linear spaces arise naturally even when dealing with abstract, non-linear learning problems. A central paradigm in learning theory is to embed a classification problem into a linear space, most prominently via kernel methods, so that hypotheses are realized as linear functionals and margin-based generalization guarantees apply. This perspective raises the question of whether such linear embeddings merely provide a convenient analytical framework, or whether they fundamentally characterize margin-based learnability.

These considerations motivate a more focused study of margin problems in the context of Banach spaces, both in terms of their intrinsic sample complexity and their role as a potential universal model for margin-based learning via embeddings. We therefore ask the following question:

To what extent is linear classification with margin universal in the context of margin-based learning? For example, can every learnable margin-based class be realized as linear functionals over an appropriate Banach space?

As a first step toward this goal, we develop a more refined understanding of the sample complexity of linear classification in Banach spaces. We derive structural results which may be of independent interest. In particular, we show that if a Banach space is
\gamma
-learnable for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then it is learnable for all
\gamma
, and the dependence of the sample complexity on the margin is necessarily polynomial in
\frac{1}{\gamma}
.

Moreover, we provide a complete taxonomy of the possible learning rates in Banach spaces as a function of
\gamma
: we show that the sample complexity always scales as
\gamma^{-p}
for some exponent
p\geq 2
, and that every such rate is attainable. In particular, we derive tight bounds on the sample complexity of linear classification in
\ell_{p}
spaces as a function of the margin
\gamma
, complementing known upper bounds with matching lower bounds.

We then return to the universality question and show that margin-based learnability does not, in general, reduce to linear embeddings into Banach spaces. Using the taxonomy of sample complexities developed above, we construct learnable margin-based classes whose learning rates cannot arise from any Banach space. This yields a negative answer to the universality question.

To establish these results, we introduce a geometric notion of shattering. This notion applies in an abstract framework of margin spaces that we define, which strictly generalizes metric spaces, and serves as a key tool in proving both the separation result and our structural results in the Banach setting.

3.2
Learnability in Banach spaces

We now turn to the question of the universality of linear classification with margin.
Linear classifiers play a central role in learning theory, both due to their favorable sample complexity properties and because many abstract learning problems are approached via linear embeddings, for instance through kernel methods.

A notable feature of linear classification is that, unlike the general metric-space setting, margin-based learning in Hilbert spaces is possible for every
\gamma>0
, despite the fact that such spaces are infinite-dimensional and not totally bounded.
This naturally leads to the question of whether general margin-based learning problems can always be reduced to linear classification in normed spaces.
More precisely, we ask whether every
\gamma
-learnable margin-based concept class can be embedded - possibly via an appropriate kernel - into a Banach space in which linear classification with margin is learnable.

We show that this universality does not hold in general: there exist concept classes that are learnable for every
\gamma>0
, yet cannot be embedded into any Banach space admitting learnable linear classification with margin.

3.2.1
Embedding margin problems into Banach spaces

Embedding learning problems into linear spaces is a classical technique that has played a particularly influential role in machine learning, most notably through kernel methods. In this framework, nonlinear concept classes are analyzed via linear predictors in a suitable feature space.
Given the central role of kernel methods in explaining margin-based generalization, it is natural to ask to what extent margin-based learnability can - or must - be understood through such linear embeddings.

To formalize this idea, we define an embedding of a margin problem
\mathcal{F}
over a domain
\mathcal{X}
into a Banach space
\mathcal{B}
as a pair of maps
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\Psi:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\star}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\star}
denote the closed unit balls of
\mathcal{B}
and its dual
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
, respectively. We require that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

c_{1}\,\Psi(f)\big(\Phi(x)\big)\;\leq\;f(x)\;\leq\;c_{2}\,\Psi(f)\big(\Phi(x)\big).

When
c_{1}=c_{2}=1
, this condition reduces to
\Psi(f)(\Phi(x))=f(x)
, i.e., the class
\mathcal{F}
is realized exactly as the unit ball of linear functionals on some Banach space
\mathcal{B}
. Allowing
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
to differ relaxes this requirement, permitting controlled distortion when representing
\mathcal{F}
as a family of linear functionals.

The guiding principle behind this definition is that of reduction of learning problems: an embedding of a margin problem
\mathcal{F}
into a Banach space
\mathcal{B}
reduces the task of learning
\mathcal{F}
to that of learning linear classifiers in
\mathcal{B}
. Indeed, if a labeled sample
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
\gamma
-realizable by
\mathcal{F}
, then the transformed sample
\{(\Phi(x_{i}),y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
(C\gamma)
-realizable in
\mathcal{B}
, where
C=1/\max(c_{1},c_{2})
, so

\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)\leq\dim_{\mathcal{B}}(C\gamma).

As a consequence of theorem
3.2
, we know that if a Banach space is
\gamma
-learnable for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then it is learnable for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Thus, if
\mathcal{F}
admits an embedding into a learnable (for any or all
\gamma
) Banach space
\mathcal{B}
, then
\mathcal{F}
itself is
\gamma
-learnable for all margins
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

This naturally raises the converse question:
can every margin problem
\mathcal{F}
that is learnable for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
be embedded into a learnable Banach space?
Unfortunately, the answer is negative, as shown by the following result.

Theorem 3.6:
Learnable class with no learnable Banach embedding

There exists a symmetric and convex set of functions
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
that is
\gamma
-learnable for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
, yet admits no embedding into any learnable Banach space.

The full proof of theorem
3.2.1
, is given in
4.4
. The main argument is an application of theorem
3.2
, with a construction of a set of functions
\mathcal{F}
for which
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)
, is not polynomial. Specifically, given a decreasing function
\phi:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, define a class
\mathcal{F}
of functions on the natural numbers by

\mathcal{F}=\bigl\{f\in(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}\;:\;\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ \phi(f(n))\geq n\bigr\}.

One can verify that
\mathcal{F}
is symmetric and convex, and moreover, satisfies
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)=\phi(\gamma)
. Consequently, if
\phi(\frac{1}{x})
grows faster than any polynomial of
\frac{1}{x}
as
x\rightarrow 0
, then
\mathcal{F}
cannot be embedded into any learnable Banach space
\mathcal{B}
, since by theorem
3.2
, any such embedding would enforce a polynomial upper bound on
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)
.

be any Banach space,let
\gamma\in(0,1)
, be such that
\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)<\infty
, and
define
p=\frac{\log(\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)+1)}{\log\frac{1}{\gamma}}
, so

\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)+1=\frac{1}{\gamma^{p}}.

By proposition
3.4
, the function
\gamma\to\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)+1
, is submultiplicative, so
\phi(\gamma^{n})\leq\phi(\gamma)^{n}
. Hence, for any
\gamma^{\prime}\in(0,1)
we may choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\gamma^{n}\leq\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma^{n-1}
, and by the above get

\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma^{\prime})\leq\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma^{n})+1\leq\big(\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)+1\big)^{n}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{np}}\leq(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{\prime}})^{p}.

Which gives the desired result.

Note that by corollary
3.8
, if
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is
\gamma
-shattered we have that
\left\lVert\sum\lambda_{i}x_{i}\right\rVert\geq\gamma\sum|\lambda_{i}|
, hence they are linearly independent. As a result if
\mathcal{X}
is
d
-dimensional we indeed have
\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)\leq d
, for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
. If
\mathcal{X}
is infinite dimensional we apply Dvoretzky’s theorem
6
, which states that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
n>0
, every infinite dimensional Banach space
\mathcal{X}
contains an
1+\varepsilon
-isomorphic copy of a
n
-dimensional Hilbert space. In particular we have

\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)\geq\dim_{\ell_{2}^{n}}((1+\varepsilon)\gamma)=\min\big(n,\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\gamma^{2}}\big).

where the identity for
\dim_{\ell_{2}^{n}}(\gamma)=\min(n,\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}})
, was shown in
2
, and also follow from proposition
3.5
. By taking
n
large enough and
\varepsilon
small enough we get the desired bound.
The fact that any rate for
p\geq 2
can be achieved follow directly from proposition
3.5
by taking
\mathcal{X}=\ell_{q}
where
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{p}=1
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

It is clear by Proposition
3.7
, that the standard basis vectors
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are
\gamma
-shattered by
\ell_{1}
for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, hence it is not
\gamma
-learnable. By standard duality arguments this implies that the dual learning problem is also not learnable. Since
\ell_{\infty}
is the dual of
\ell_{1}
we deduce it is also non learnable for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
(note that the notion of duality in the learning theory and functional analysis coincide in our setup).
For the upper bounds one can use Theorem 2 in
3
, to bound the fat shattering dimension, which is always larger than our notion of shattering. Indeed, recall that the
\gamma
-fat shattering dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is the maximal size of a set
S=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
if there is a function
r:S\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any labeling
y\in{-1,1}^{n}
, the dataset
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}
is
\gamma
-realized by
f-r
for some
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Taking
r=0
, it is clear that the
\gamma
-fat shattering dimension upper bound
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)
.

Note that the implied constant in
3
, is
B_{p}^{2}
the square of the best constant in the upper bound of the Khintchine inequality, which by
9
is
1
if
p\leq 2
and at most
p
if
p>2
.

For the lower bounds we note that if
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are
n
standard basis vectors in
\ell_{p}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
\sum|\lambda_{i}|=1
then using a standard application of Hölder’s inequality we have

\left\lVert\sum\lambda_{i}e_{i}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{p}\geq n^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{1}=n^{\frac{1}{p}-1}.

Hence if
n\leq\gamma^{\frac{p}{1-p}}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}
, we have that
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
\gamma
-shattered, so

\mathtt{VC}(\mathcal{H}_{(\ell_{p})_{1}^{\star}}^{\gamma})\geq\frac{1}{\gamma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}-1
, which gives a tight bound for the case of
1<p\leq 2
.

Finally it remains to give a lower bounds for the case
p>2
. Let
n=2^{m}
be some natural number, which is a power of two and satisfies
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
Denote
\mathcal{X}=\ell_{p}^{n}
, and we will show that
\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}
, then the result will follow from the natural embedding of
\ell_{p}^{n}
in
\ell_{p}
. Let
H
be the
n
on
n
Hadamard matrix (which exists since
n
is a power of two). Recall that
H_{i,j}\in\{\pm 1\}
for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and that
\left\lVert Hx\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{n}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
(Those are the only properties of the Hadamard matrix we need). Define
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
by
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(j)=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{p}}}H_{i,j}
, and we claim that this will be our shattered set. Indeed, a simple computation yields

\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{i}(j)|^{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}=1.

So we have that
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are a collection of unit vectors. Now to check it is shattered we recall that by Holder inequality we have for any
1\leq r<p
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the following inequality

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{r}\leq n^{\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{p}.

Hence for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{1}=1
we get

\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{p}}}H\lambda\right\rVert_{p}\geq\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}H\lambda\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

So we see that
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are
\gamma
-shattered for
\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, implying that if
n
is any power of two less then
\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}
we have that
\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)\geq n
. In particular we always have
\dim_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}
, from which we deduce the desired result.
∎

Proof for
Section

3.2.1
- non embeddability of margin spaces.

Let
\phi:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
be some monotone decreasing function, and define
\mathcal{F}\subset(-1,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
by

\mathcal{F}=\{f\in(-1,1)^{\mathbb{N}}\;:\;\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\;\phi(|f(n)|)\geq n\}

Clearly
\mathcal{F}
is symmetric, and since
\phi
is monotone, it is convex. Indeed, for any
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\phi\big(|\lambda f(n)+(1-\lambda)g(n)|\big)\geq\phi\Big(\max\big(|f(n)|,|g(n)|\big)\Big)\geq n.

So
\lambda f+(1-\lambda)g\in\mathcal{F}
, and it is a convex set. Note that a sample
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
\gamma
-shattered by
\mathcal{F}
if and only if
\phi(\gamma)\geq x_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. So

\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)=\phi(\gamma).

Hence if we take
\phi
to be some super polynomial function, such as the integer part of
e^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
, we will get that there is no embedding of
\mathcal{F}
into a learnable Banach space
\mathcal{B}
. Indeed any such embedding will imply that there is some constant
C>0
such that (below
[\cdot]
denotes the integer part)

[e^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}]=\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma)\leq\dim_{\mathcal{B}}(C\gamma).

But by Theorem
3.2
, this is impossible as
\dim_{\mathcal{B}}(\gamma)=O(\frac{1}{\gamma^{p}})
for some
p>0
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.7
- Characterization of shattering in margin spaces.

(3)\implies(2):
Assume toward contradiction that
(3)
holds but
(2)
does not hold, so there is some
y\in[-\gamma,\gamma]^{n}
such that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
there is some
i\in[n]
such that
f(x_{i})\neq y_{i}
. Consider the set

\mathcal{F}|_{S}=\{\big(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2})\dots f(x_{n})\big)\;:\;f\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is convex so is
\mathcal{F}|_{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and by assumption
y\notin\mathcal{F}|_{S}
. Hence by the Hahn-Banach theorem there is some vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and scalar
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}=a.

But for any
f\in\mathcal{F}|_{S}
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}f_{i}<a.

By normalization we may assume
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|=1
. Hence, since we assumed
(3)
, we get that there is some
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})\geq\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|\gamma\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}=a

In contradiction.

(2)\implies(1):
Obvious.

(1)\implies(3):
Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|=1
.
Since we assume that
S
is shattered by
\mathcal{F}
, for any sign pattern
s\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, there is some
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that
s_{i}f(x_{i})\geq\gamma
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. In particular we may find such
f
for the sign pattern

(choosing arbitrarily in the cases where
\lambda_{i}=0
). Then for this

we get for all

From which we deduce

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|\gamma=\gamma.

Which gives the desired result.
