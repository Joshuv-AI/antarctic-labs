Radiomics analysis of CT imaging for predicting MIT family translocation renal cell carcinoma: a multicenter retrospective clinical study
Authors: Shi-Wei Lin, Xiao-Hui Wu, Wen-Qi Liu, Dong-Ning Chen, Yan Lin, Zheng-Sheng Liu, Ji-yuan Wang, Hu-bin Yin, Xue-Yi Xue, Ning Xu, Shao-Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the imaging characteristics of Microphthalmia Transcription Factor (MIT) Family Translocation Renal Cell Carcinoma (MIT-RCC), assess the relationship between CT-based radiomics and MIT-RCC, and develop a predictive model to improve preoperative non-invasive diagnosis.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using preoperative computed tomography (CT) images and clinical data from 746 RCC patients (77 MIT-RCC and 669 other RCC subtypes) across multiple centers. Regions of interest (ROIs) in both tumor and normal renal parenchymal during non-contrast and arterial phases were manually segmented using ITK-SNAP. Radiomics features were extracted via Python, and the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method was applied for feature selection. A combined model incorporating radiomics scores and clinical predictors (age, stage) was developed using logistic regression and visualized as a nomogram. Patients from two centers formed the training cohort (n = 539), and three other centers served as the validation cohort (n = 207).ResultsFrom 1316 features per ROI, 22 tumor and 24 normal renal parenchymal features were selected. The nomogram integrating Tumor_Radscores, Kidney_Radscores, age, and clinical stage showed strong predictive performance in the training cohort (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.952, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.927–0.971) and validation cohort (AUC = 0.914, 95% CI: 0.879–0.942), with good calibration and clinical utility.ConclusionBy integrating radiomics and clinical data, the proposed nomogram achieved accurate, non-invasive prediction of MIT-RCC, offering valuable support for preoperative diagnosis and personalized treatment planning.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16334-2.

Introduction

Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) is the most common primary malignant tumor of the kidney, accounting for approximately 85–90% of renal malignancies [1]. It ranks third among malignant tumors of the urinary system [2], with its incidence and mortality rates increasing annually. According to global statistics, over 400,000 new cases of renal cancer are diagnosed each year, and nearly 180,000 patients die from the disease [3]. Most of these patients are over 60 years old, with two-thirds being male. Translocation Renal Cell Carcinoma (tRCC), associated with the Microphthalmia Transcription Factor (MIT) family, is a rare subtype of RCC characterized by chromosomal translocation involving Transcription Factor E3 (TFE3) or Transcription Factor EB (TFEB) genes (located at Xp11.2 and 6p21, respectively) fused with various partner genes [4]. Due to the clinical, histopathological, and genetic similarities between tRCC with TFE3 and TFEB gene fusions, the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) Vancouver Classification in 2013 grouped these subtypes into a single category termed MIT Family Translocation Renal Cell Carcinoma (MIT-RCC) [5]. The ISUP consensus meeting in 2012 revised the 2004 renal tumor classification, resulting in the latest 2016 WHO classification standards, which designated MIT-RCC as a new clinically significant subtype of RCC [6]. Common types of RCC include Clear Cell Renal Carcinoma (ccRCC), Chromophobe Renal Cell Carcinoma (ChRCC), and Papillary Renal Cell Carcinoma (pRCC), with ccRCC being the most prevalent. MIT-RCC, however, is rare in clinical practice and exhibits high malignancy [7]. Its imaging features overlap with those of ccRCC and pRCC. Currently, pathological examination, such as biopsy, remains the gold standard for diagnosing RCC. However, due to the fragile nature of renal tissue, biopsy can lead to complications such as renal bleeding and needle tract metastasis, making routine preoperative biopsy for renal tumors generally inadvisable.

In 2012, Dutch scholar Lambin introduced the concept of Radiomics [8]. Radiomics refers to the rapid extraction of quantitative features from imaging modalities such as Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Positron Emission Tomography (PET), and Ultrasound (US). Statistical or machine learning methods are then used to screen the most valuable radiomic features, which are correlated with lesion characteristics to construct predictive models, aiding clinicians in making treatment decisions [9]. The underlying principle of radiomics is that medical images contain information reflecting the underlying pathophysiology of diseases, and quantitative image analysis can reveal these relationships. Radiomics can be integrated with genetic and clinical data to assist in diagnosis, treatment evaluation, and prognosis prediction. Its main advantages include: (1) Almost all cancer patients undergo digital imaging, providing a large potential source of radiomic data; (2) Radiomics offers a non-invasive evaluation of tumor temporal and spatial changes; (3) Radiomics is routinely performed throughout the treatment process, enabling continuous monitoring. The radiomics workflow includes image data acquisition, Region of Interest (ROI) segmentation, feature extraction, feature selection, model construction, and evaluation [10].

Currently, CT is widely used for preoperative assessment and clinical staging of RCC patients. However, radiomics models combining CT texture and non-texture features for predicting MIT-RCC have yet to be developed. Therefore, this study aims to develop and compare radiomics models based on CT imaging features to achieve individualized prediction of MIT-RCC. Radiomics technology addresses the limitations of traditional imaging by transforming visual imaging information into quantifiable objective data for in-depth analysis. By combining high-throughput extraction and analysis of imaging and clinical data, radiomics comprehensively explores tumor information, enabling objective tumor classification predictions and non-invasive diagnose. Accurate preoperative imaging diagnosis is crucial for helping clinicians formulate scientific treatment plans and provides essential support for selecting appropriate surgical or perioperative treatment strategies for MIT-RCC patients.

Materials and methods

Research population

The study was approved by the ethics committees of each participating center (Approved No. of ethic committee: MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2025]749), and all patients provided informed consent. Retrospective data collection was conducted from January 2009 to December 2023 at five hospitals, involving a total of 908 RCC patients. Ultimately, 746 RCC patients (age, 51.32 ± 10.34 years; 456 males) were included for analysis, comprising 77 cases (including 74 TFE3 and 3 TFEB RCC) of MIT-RCC and 659 cases of other types of RCC. The study cohort was divided according to the source institutions into a training cohort of 539 cases and a validation cohort of 207 case (Fig. 1).

Inclusion exclusion process of subjects in this study

All cases were pathologically confirmed after biopsy or surgery at the participating centers. The diagnosis of MIT-RCC was established according to contemporary histopathological criteria, based on hematoxylin–eosin morphology in combination with immunohistochemical and/or molecular evidence when available, including TFE3 and TFEB testing [11–13]. In our retrospective multicenter cohort, the diagnostic workup varied across institutions and time periods, reflecting real-world clinical practice. To reduce diagnostic heterogeneity, all available pathology reports were reviewed and cases meeting the diagnostic criteria for MIT-RCC were included in the study. Typical H&E, IHC, and FISH images of non-MIT-RCC and MIT-RCC were presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Inclusion Criteria: Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) untreated prior to CT examination; (2) CT images without significant noise or artifacts; (3) pathologically confirmed RCC; (4) complete clinical and pathological data available; (5) availability of non-contrast and contrast-enhanced CT images from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Exclusion Criteria: Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) treated prior to CT examination; (2) poor-quality or missing CT images; (3) lack of pathological results or incomplete clinical data for other reasons; (4) concurrent malignancies in other organs. The study protocol was registered in ClinicalTrials, gov. registration number: NCT07181954.

Clinical and pathological data

The collected clinicopathological data include age, gender, body mass index (BMI), tumor location, history of diabetes, history of hypertension, American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) clinical staging, and pathological tumor subtype. Pathological data were obtained from the pathology department after biopsy or surgery and were reviewed by two experienced pathologists to ensure diagnostic accuracy and consistency.

Instruments and methods

A 64-slice multi-detector CT scanner was used for imaging. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage of 120–140 kV, tube current of 550 mA, slice thickness and spacing of 5 mm each. Patients were positioned supine for abdominal non-contrast and contrast-enhanced scans, covering the area from the diaphragm to the anterior superior iliac spine. Non-ionic contrast agent iodixanol (iodine concentration: 350 mg/mL) was injected via the antecubital vein using a high-pressure injector at a flow rate of 3.0–3.5 mL/s, with a dose of 1.5 mL/kg. The arterial phase was acquired 25–28 s after injection, the venous phase 65–70 s after injection, The arterial phase was acquired 25–28 s after injection, the venous phase 65–70 s after injection, and the delayed phase 170 s after injection. Non-contrast and arterial phases provide fundamental information on tumor density and vascular characteristics. The selection of these two phases ensures sufficient data while minimizing redundant information, thereby improving the efficiency and stability of the model [14, 15]. Therefore, we obtained non-contrast and arterial phase kidney CT images from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and extracted features from these phases to construct the model [16].

CT image segmentation

ROI segmentation was performed using ITK-SNAP (https://www.itksnap.org) by three radiologists with more than five years of experience. The whole tumor was delineated slice-by-slice to generate a three-dimensional volume of interest. Normal renal parenchymal ROIs were delineated on the homolateral kidney, avoiding major vessels, collecting system, and artifacts, to serve as reference tissue. Two radiologists with over ten years of experience reviewed and confirmed the ROIs. In cases where the two reviewers disagreed, a third radiologist with 20 years of experience participated in a joint discussion to finalize the ROI. Finally, all manually delineated ROIs were automatically fused by the software to generate three-dimensional VOIs (Figs. 2 and 3).

Radiomics feature extraction

Radiomics features were extracted using PyRadiomics version 3.0 implemented in Python (version 3.7.3). In addition to applying two basic image filters, namely Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) and wavelet filters, features were also derived from the original images. To eliminate scale differences and ensure comparability, all features were standardized using Z-score transformation. To evaluate the reproducibility of ROI delineation and radiomics feature extraction, observer variability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which was calculated with the R package “psych” (version 2.4.3) (Supplementary Table 1). Features with ICC > 0.8 were considered reproducible and were selected for subsequent analysis. Highly reproducible radiomics features significantly associated with MTT-RCC were further screened using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, implemented with the R packages “glmnet” (version 4.1-7) and “pROC” (version 1.18.0).

Construction and validation of the radiomics model

Radiomics model development was performed in a stepwise manner. First, radiomics features with good reproducibility (ICC > 0.8) were retained. Subsequently, the LASSO regression was used to further select the most informative features, and the selected features were combined to construct the radiomics signature. The penalty parameter λ in the LASSO model was determined by cross-validation. Univariate logistic regression analysis was then performed to evaluate the associations between the radiomics signature, clinical characteristics, and MIT-RCC. Variables with potential predictive value were further entered into multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors, based on which a radiomics-clinical combined nomogram was constructed. No additional class imbalance correction method was applied during model development. Missing data were handled by excluding incomplete cases before analysis. The diagnostic performance of the nomogram was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the calculation of the area under the curve (AUC). The DeLong test was used to compare the differences in AUC between the radiomics-clinical combined nomogram and various predictors. The calibration of the nomogram was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration curves [17]. To assess the clinical utility of the nomogram, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted [18]. The radiomics-clinical nomogram was constructed and validated using R software packages, including “Hmisc” (version 5.0–1), “car” (version 3.1-2), “rms” (version 6.6-0), “pROC” (version 1.18-0), “survival” (version 3.5-5), “survminer” (version 0.4.9), and “rmda” (version 1.6).

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 was set for all tests.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among the 77 MIT-RCC cases, 74 were TFE3-rearranged and 3 were TFEB-rearranged. Because the number of TFEB-rearranged cases was limited, separate subgroup modeling was not performed. This study analyzed the general clinicopathological characteristics of patients in the training set (539 cases) and validation set (207 cases) to explore the differences between MIT-RCC and other types of renal cell carcinoma (RCC). The results showed that MIT-RCC patients exhibited a significantly younger age profile. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of BMI, gender, tumor location, or prevalence of metabolic diseases (P > 0.05). However, a significant difference was observed in AJCC clinical staging (P < 0.001), with stage III-IV accounting for 58.5% (31/53) of cases in the MIT group of the training set, which was 3.3 times higher than that of the non-MIT group. This trend persisted in the validation set (20.8% vs. 8.2%).

Radiomics feature selection

We extracted radiomic features from the renal tumor and normal renal parenchymal for each patient from the pre-contrast and arterial phase sequences. A total of 1316 features were extracted from each ROI. Features with intra-observer or inter-observer ICC values below 0.8, indicating low reproducibility, were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, the number of radiomic features for normal renal parenchymal was reduced to 855, while the number for tumor features was reduced to 931. Subsequently, using the LASSO regression method (Fig. 4A-D), 24 radiomic features were identified from the normal renal parenchymal ROI and 22 features were selected from the tumor ROI (Table 2). The distribution relationships between the kidney radiomics features, tumor radiomics features, and clinical characteristics are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Radiomic feature selection result

Nomogram construction

This study utilized univariate and multivariate Logistic regression analyses to evaluate clinical factors for predicting the risk of MIT-RCC (Table 3). Results from both univariate and multivariate analyses indicated that age, AJCC clinical staging, and radiomics features are significant predictors of MIT-RCC. Other variables such as BMI, gender, tumor location, hypertension, and diabetes showed no significant correlation in either univariate or multivariate analyses (P > 0.05). In summary, younger age, higher AJCC staging, tumor radiomics features, and normal renal parenchymal radiomics features are critical predictive factors for MIT-RCC. A nomogram was constructed by integrating age, AJCC clinical staging, tumor radiomics features, and normal renal parenchymal radiomics features (Fig. 4E). For instance, a patient with a total score of 162.5 points—Tumor_radiomics score of 6.5 (70 points), Kidney_radiomics score of 7.8 (42.5 points), age of 31 years (39 points), and AJCC clinical stage III (11 points)—has an estimated probability of MIT-RCC of approximately 0.9. Pathological findings ultimately confirmed XP11.2 translocation/TFE3 gene fusion-associated renal carcinoma, as illustrated in Fig. 5A-D.

Model evaluation

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the predictive performance of the nomogram model, which integrated four key factors: age, AJCC clinical stage, tumor radiomics score, and kidney radiomics score. The results demonstrated that the nomogram model exhibited excellent predictive capability in both the training and validation cohorts, with overall performance significantly superior to single-factor models. Compared to traditional clinical models based solely on age or AJCC clinical stage, the nomogram model enhanced the ability to distinguish MIT-RCC by incorporating clinical and radiomics features.

In the training cohort, the nomogram model achieved an AUC of 0.952 (95% CI: 0.927–0.971) (Fig. 6A), significantly outperforming single-factor models, including age (AUC = 0.768, z = 3.959, P < 0.001), AJCC clinical stage (AUC = 0.706, z = 5.665, P < 0.001), kidney radiomics (AUC = 0.851, z = 3.937, P < 0.001), and tumor radiomics (AUC = 0.745, z = 4.254, P < 0.001). In the validation cohort, the nomogram model achieved an AUC of 0.914 (95% CI: 0.879–0.942) (Fig. 6B), also significantly outperforming age (AUC = 0.722, z = 4.513, P < 0.001), AJCC clinical stage (AUC = 0.683, z = 5.442, P < 0.001), kidney radiomics (AUC = 0.807, z = 4.485, P < 0.001), and tumor radiomics (AUC = 0.730, z = 5.295, P < 0.001) (Table 4). These results indicated that the nomogram model possesses significantly higher predictive performance in distinguishing MIT-RCC compared to other univariable models.

Discussion

This study conducted a detailed analysis of the clinical characteristics of MIT-RCC patients in the training and validation cohorts and explored the clinical features of this disease in depth in combination with relevant literature. MIT-RCC is a rare subtype of renal cell carcinoma with unique molecular features, and its clinical manifestations, imaging characteristics, and molecular mechanisms have received increasing attention in recent years. The study data showed that MIT-RCC patients in both the training and validation cohorts exhibited a specific age distribution pattern, suggesting that this disease is more common in younger patients, consistent with findings from previous studies [19, 20]. Furthermore, no significant differences in gender distribution were observed in this study; however, a review by Rizzo et al. noted that although MIT-RCC can occur in patients of any gender, it may be more common in females [19]. Differences in gender distribution may reflect variations in sample size, study populations, or statistical methods, indicating the need for further large-scale multicenter studies to validate the gender differences in MIT-RCC. Metabolic factors such as BMI, hypertension, and diabetes did not show significant predictive value in this study, suggesting that the pathogenesis of MIT-RCC may be less associated with traditional metabolic diseases. AJCC clinical staging was found to have significant prognostic value for MIT-RCC patients. This study revealed that as clinical stage increases (from Stage II to Stage IV), the risk of MIT-RCC significantly rises, which is closely related to the tumor’s invasiveness and advanced characteristics. This finding is supported by the literature, with Rizzo et al. and Wang et al. indicating that MIT-RCC often presents at diagnosis with large tumor size or local invasion, and some patients may even exhibit distant metastases. Additionally, the imaging characteristics of MIT-RCC are somewhat specific [19]. Wang et al. analyzed MRI and CT imaging of MIT-RCC patients and found that this tumor type typically exhibits pronounced enhancement features, suggesting a rich blood supply that may be related to its unique molecular mechanisms [19]. The pathogenesis of MIT-RCC is primarily associated with chromosomal translocations resulting in TFE3 or TFEB gene fusions, which provide critical diagnostic and differential diagnostic markers. Argani and Ladanyi further noted that histopathologically, MIT-RCC often exhibits mixed features of clear cells and eosinophilic cells, accompanied by unique immunohistochemical markers (e.g., TFE3 positivity) [21]. These characteristics were significantly correlated with the tumor radiomics score and kidney radiomics score observed in this study, providing essential support for the non-invasive diagnosis of MIT-RCC.

With the increasing understanding of MIT-RCC, attention to its diagnosis and treatment has also grown. Currently, the definitive diagnosis of MIT-RCC primarily relies on pathological results. However, radiomics, a cutting-edge technology at the intersection of computer science and medicine, has demonstrated significant potential in evaluating treatment efficacy and predicting cancer prognosis [22]. By employing complex mathematical algorithms, radiomics can extract valuable medical imaging data from various imaging modalities such as CT, MRI, and PET [23–26]. Radiomics not only compensates for the subjectivity of traditional imaging but also provides new tools for RCC detection, risk assessment, and treatment planning. For instance, radiomics has shown remarkable potential in distinguishing benign and malignant renal tumors. Nassiri et al. analyzed preoperative CT images from 684 patients with localized renal masses, using decision tree analysis to identify key variables and construct a radiomics model capable of reliably differentiating benign renal masses from renal cell carcinoma [27]. Radiomics also holds great promise in diagnosing RCC subtypes. Menglin Chen et al. developed a multiphase CT-based radiomics model to differentiate clear cell renal cell carcinoma [28], while Xiaoli Li et al. utilized a radiomics nomogram to distinguish small renal oncocytomas (< 4 cm) from clear cell renal cell carcinoma [29]. Beyond CT-based radiomics, a study extracted radiomics features from MRI images of 77 patients, establishing a radiomics model that improved the differentiation of RCC subtypes by combining information from three MRI sequences [30]. Radiomics also has applications in predicting RCC prognosis. Bolin Liu et al. demonstrated the enhanced ability of multi-slice spiral CT imaging to predict Fuhrman pathological grades of clear cell renal cell carcinoma [31]. Ruizhi Gao et al. developed a radiomics nomogram to predict the prognosis of ccRCC patients [32], indicating that radiomics applications can provide critical references for clinical management and prognostic evaluation in RCC patients. The selected radiomics features may partly explain the imaging phenotype of MIT-RCC. In this study, most retained features were first-order and texture features derived from original, LoG-filtered, and wavelet-transformed images, mainly including GLCM-, GLDM-, GLRLM-, and GLSZM-based features. These features reflect gray-level distribution and spatial heterogeneity, and may therefore be associated with the histological heterogeneity and complex internal composition of MIT-RCC. Notably, features were retained from both the tumor and the ipsilateral renal parenchyma excluding the tumor, suggesting that diagnostically relevant information may exist not only within the lesion itself but also in the normal renal parenchymal environment. However, the exact biological basis of these associations still requires further radiologic-pathologic validation.

This study developed a comprehensive nomogram prediction model based on age, AJCC clinical staging, tumor radiomics features, and kidney radiomics features to differentiate MIT-RCC from other types of renal cancer. By comparing the predictive performance of single-factor models and multifactor combined models, we validated the superiority of the nomogram model in both the training and validation sets, further highlighting its potential value in clinical applications.

Our study has several limitations. First, MIT-RCC is a rare tumor subtype, resulting in a markedly imbalanced dataset. We did not apply artificial resampling techniques in order to preserve the real-world disease distribution and avoid potential bias from synthetic data; however, the effect of class imbalance on model robustness cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the retrospective multicenter design may have introduced limitations related to data completeness, confounding control, and heterogeneity in imaging acquisition, scanner types, and operational protocols. Third, only CT imaging was included, which may have limited the diversity of diagnostic information compared with multimodal approaches. In addition, our model did not incorporate deep learning architectures, which may have restricted its ability to capture higher-level imaging features. Fourth, pathological evaluation was not fully uniform across centers or time periods, and some degree of diagnostic heterogeneity cannot be ruled out given the known diagnostic complexity of translocation-associated renal cell carcinomas. Finally, TFE3-rearranged RCC and TFEB-rearranged RCC may represent biologically distinct entities, but were analyzed together because of the limited number of cases, particularly TFEB-associated tumors, which may have obscured subtype-specific imaging characteristics.

In summary, this study is the first to construct a hybrid model for predicting MIT-RCC by integrating radiomics features of renal tumors, radiomics features of normal renal parenchymal tissues, and clinical data. The results demonstrated that the nomogram model exhibited superior predictive performance in both the training and validation cohorts compared to single predictive factors, with good discrimination and calibration. Radiomics quantifies the characteristics of tumors and normal renal parenchymal tissues, addressing the subjectivity of traditional imaging, and further improves diagnostic accuracy when combined with clinical data. Although this study has limitations, such as a limited sample size, the findings provide important references for the preoperative diagnosis of MIT-RCC and lay the foundation for future individualized treatment.
