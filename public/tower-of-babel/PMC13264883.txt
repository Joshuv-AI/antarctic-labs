Societal costs for the closest family relatives of patients with brain disorders in Denmark: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Mette Kielsholm Thomsen, Thomas Bøjer Rasmussen, Jens Olsen, Niels Skipper, Sandra Benkjer Stallknecht, Oleguer Plana-Ripoll, Christian Fynbo Christiansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBrain disorders, including neurological conditions and mental disorders, pose a considerable economic burden in Denmark. However, the costs of illness are not limited to the patients themselves, as the consequences of living with a brain disorder may also impact close relatives.We aimed to describe any societal costs related to healthcare utilisation and income loss for the closest family relatives of patients with brain disorders.MethodsThis population-based cohort study included the closest family relatives (proxies for caregivers) of patients with prevalent (by 1 January 2021) or incident (during 2016–2021) brain disorders, using registry data. They were compared with corresponding relatives of matched population comparisons. Patients were categorised into three age strata: children and young people (0–24 years), adults (25–64 years) and older adults (65+years). We specified criteria for identifying the closest family relative for each age stratum. Using data from national registries, we estimated attributable healthcare costs and, for working-age relatives (18–65 years), income loss.ResultsIn 2021, close relatives of patients with brain disorders included 125 495 fathers and 96 154 mothers of children and young people, 880 661 relatives of adults and 378 826 relatives of older adults. The pooled attributable costs of brain disorders incurred by closest family relatives were €2407 million for prevalent disease in 2021 and €794 million for incident disease the first year following incidence. The dominating cost component was income loss for working-age relatives.ConclusionsThe higher healthcare costs and especially the lower income for relatives of patients with brain disorders could be associated with the societal economic burden of brain disorders in Denmark. Informal care provided by relatives should be considered in discussions of healthcare prioritisation.Keywords: Economics, Health Transition, Mental Health, Public Health

Introduction

Brain disorders, including neurological conditions and mental disorders, are a considerable health burden which incurred more than 500 million disability-adjusted life years worldwide in 2021. The high overall disability burden is driven both by disease severity and a high prevalence.1 The prevalence of brain disorders in Europe is considerable, with the three most common types being headaches (152.8 million persons affected), anxiety disorders (69.1 million affected) and sleep disorders (44.9 million affected) in the total population of 514 million inhabitants of 30 European countries.2 In Denmark, brain disorders affected one-third of the population in 2021.3

Brain disorders have previously been found to pose a considerable economic burden across countries.2,4 Costs of brain disorders in Europe were estimated at €798 billion, or €5550 per inhabitant, in 2010.2 In the Danish population of ~6 million people, the total healthcare costs attributable to brain disorders are €7.5 billion yearly, in addition to an average income loss of €17 587 per person with a brain disorder in Denmark.3 However, the costs of an illness are not limited to the patients themselves, as the consequences of suffering from a brain disorder impact close relatives as well, not least from the patient’s need for support and informal care in daily life and disease management.5

Informal caregiving is most often provided by a close family member and more seldom by a neighbour or friend.6 Family roles, and thus the type of family relative most likely to provide informal care, change over the life course. A Danish survey among informal caregivers of patients treated in outpatient psychiatric services found that for adult patients, 35% of their caregivers were partners and 49% were parents.7 For patients in later life, a German study of primary informal caregivers of patients with dementia found that the primary caregiver was most often the partner or an adult child, accounting for 93% of patients.8

Keating et al have established a taxonomy for economic costs of informal caregiving, identifying categories of economic costs of caregiving, namely loss of income, out-of-pocket expenses and the value of time spent undertaking informal care, in addition to health and social consequences of care labour.9 Family relatives undertaking informal care may be subject to consequences for their own health as well as their ability to undertake paid work,10 thus adding to the sum cost of brain disorders.

A survey-based study from Sweden found that even in a relatively expansive and universal welfare system, significant resources went toward providing informal care across the population, with costs of €14.5 billion per year, equalling 3% of the Swedish gross domestic product (GDP).11 Likewise, replacement costs of caregiving (costs, if all time spent on informal care were to be replaced by paid care) were estimated in Australia in 2020 at 4% of GDP.12

Previously, the economic burden incurred by relatives of patients with brain disorders in Denmark has only been evaluated in selected mental disorders and stroke, and generally focusing on one type of relative (eg, parents or partners).13,19

We aimed to extend the current evidence on societal costs of all brain disorders in Denmark, by assessing the costs related to health and lower income for the closest family relatives of patients with brain disorders across stages of life.

Materials and methods

Setting

In Denmark, healthcare is primarily tax-funded and provided free of charge at the point of use. All residents are assigned a unique and universal civil person registration number at birth or immigration, which enables data linkage across national registries.20

Study design

In this registry-based cohort study, we assessed the societal costs among assumed closest family relatives of persons with brain disorders in Denmark. They were compared with corresponding relatives of matched population comparisons. We estimated the excess costs in family relatives of patients with incident and prevalent brain disorders, respectively, and considered costs related to healthcare and loss of income.

Study cohorts

Brain disorder cohorts

We considered 25 types of brain disorders (online supplemental table 1) as previously defined.3

We established an incident and a prevalent cohort for any brain disorder and for each subtype of brain disorder. The prevalent cohorts included persons with the brain disorder diagnosed within the past 20 years, among people alive and residing in Denmark on 1 January 2021. The incident cohorts included persons with a first-time diagnosis of the brain disorder of interest during 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2021, among those with no diagnosis of the specific disorder in the previous 15 years.

Definition of closest family relatives by age strata

We used age as a stratifying factor throughout, assessed on diagnosis date for incident cohorts and on 1 January 2021, for the prevalent cohorts. Age was categorised into three categories by ‘life phases’, according to WHO and National Health Services definitions: (1) children and young people 0–24 years,21 (2) adults 25–64 years and (3) older adults 65+ years.22

For each patient, we identified the one or two family relatives that we assumed to be the closest relatives, likely acting as informal caregivers. As relatives of patients with brain disorders may be of increased risk of brain disorders themselves, we sought the closest relatives among those that had not previously had such a diagnosis themselves. As criteria to identify who would typically be the primary relative(s) of a patient, we considered both age strata, type of family relation, geographical proximity and age of family relatives (table 1). For children and young people (0–24 years), we included both parents if possible. For adults (25–64) we included one relative, prioritised as (1) partners, (2) parents, (3) children and (4) adult siblings as closest relatives. For older adults (65+), we also included one relative, prioritised as (1) partners, (2) adult children and (3) adult siblings as closest relatives.

Population comparisons and comparison relatives

We matched each set of patient and patient relative(s) with one set of comparison person and comparison relative(s).

Due to computational restrictions, this was done in two steps. First, to allow for a sufficient number of individuals to identify family relatives of same type as the patient relative, we matched each patient with up to 100 persons from the general population, among those who had not previously been diagnosed with the brain disorder of interest. Matching was done by sex and birth year using sampling with replacement (ie, a person can be a control for multiple cases, but only once for each case). Second, for each person in the comparison cohort, we identified their family relatives without pre-existing the brain disorder of interest and restricted to the closest relatives according to the same criteria as for patient relatives. Then we restricted the comparison relatives to those who were of the same relation-type, year of birth (within 5 years), sex and education level as the patient relative. If this resulted in more than one eligible set of comparison person and comparison relative(s), we drew one set at random (online supplemental figure 1).

Data sources and variables

We retrieved data from the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR) (including diagnoses from both somatic and psychiatric hospital contacts)23 and the prescriptions registry24 to identify diagnoses of brain disorders (online supplemental table 1). From the DNPR, we also retrieved information on comorbidities in the past 10 years and calculated the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score based on these data.25 CCI score was categorised as ‘0’, ‘1-2’ and ‘3+’.

To assess healthcare costs, we included primary sector healthcare, hospital care and filled prescriptions. For primary sector healthcare, we used data from the Danish Health Service Registry26 to identify subsidised services provided by general practitioners, private practising specialists and dentists. The cost of each type of service was retrieved from payment-agreements between Danish regions and provider unions.

Hospital-based healthcare was assessed as admissions, outpatient contacts and emergency room visits from the DNPR, and linked to diagnosis-related group and Danish Ambulatory Grouping System tariffs. Tariffs for psychiatric hospital care were only available until 2019. We therefore imputed their values for the period 2020–2022 based on data from the years prior, following the same method as described in a previous study.3

Filled prescriptions were identified using data from the Prescription Registry, which contains information on all prescriptions filled at community pharmacies, including the retail price (the market price including the Danish value-added tax of 25%) of each specific drug.24

Data on income was retrieved from the Income Registry27 and defined as individual earnings (not including social benefits) before taxes. An overview of data tables and variables used to calculate costs is available in online supplemental table 2.

To adjust for inflation, all costs—except prescription medication costs due to its fluctuating price-structure (elaborated in online supplemental table 2)—were adjusted to the 2023 level using the Danish Consumer Price Index.28 Original cost data were in Danish krone (DKK), and we used a fixed exchange rate of €1=7.45 DKK for conversion to euro.

Statistical analysis

We characterised patient and comparison cohorts as well as patient relatives and comparison relatives according to sex, age, education level and CCI score. In the main analysis, we focused on any brain disorder and secondarily reported corresponding results for specific brain disorders.

We described the societal costs of brain disorders in family relatives including costs related to both healthcare services and income loss. For the prevalent cohorts, costs were assessed for the calendar year 2021; for the incident cohorts, costs were assessed for the year following diagnosis.

We computed both the actual and attributable costs of healthcare services. The attributable, or excess, healthcare costs were defined as the crude difference in healthcare service costs between the patient relatives and their matched comparison relatives.

We estimated attributable income loss in family relatives of working age, that is, 18–65 years. The attributable income loss was calculated as the crude difference between yearly income (earnings) before taxes for patient relatives, and the yearly income (earnings) before taxes of comparison relatives. Secondarily, we estimated the relative income loss as the mean attributable income loss proportional to the mean income of comparison relatives.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the planning or conduct of this study.

Results

All results are provided by type of brain disorder in online supplemental tables 6–305.

Prevalent brain disorder cohort

We identified 263 026 children and young people (0–24 years), 1 103 732 adults (25–64 years) and 566 848 older adults (65+ years) with any prevalent brain disorder on 1 January 2021. Among these, 34%, 10% and 28% in each age stratum, respectively, had no available closest relative alive, living in Denmark and without prior brain disorders (online supplemental table 3a). The proportions of patients without an eligible closest family relative were lower when considering specific prevalent brain disorders in the two youngest age strata (ranging from 1% for adults with eating disorders to 19% for children and young people with depression), but were up to 36% and 68% for older adults with cerebral palsy or intellectual disabilities, respectively (online supplemental tables 60 and 168).

Subcohorts of patient relatives each consisted of 125 495 fathers and 96 154 mothers of children and young people, 880 661 relatives of adults and 378 826 relatives of older adults with any type of brain disorder. The median age of fathers and mothers of children and young people with any brain disorder was 49.9 years (IQR 44.9–54.6) and 48.1 years (IQR 43.2–52.5), respectively. The majority had a low comorbidity burden, with 90.9% and 90.0% having a CCI score of 0 (online supplemental table 4a).

The closest relatives of adults with any brain disorder were mostly partners (39.5%) and adult children (31.7%), and 15.9% and 12.9% were parents and siblings, respectively. Overall, 56.4% of them were men, median age was 42.7 years (IQR 25.8–58.0) and 88.9% had a CCI score of 0. The closest relatives of older adults were 37.1% partners, 60.6% adult children and 2.3% siblings, with overall 55.9% men and median age 58.1 years (IQR 48.3–70.2). More relatives of older adults had comorbidities compared with both relatives of children and young people and relatives of adults with only 81.9% having a CCI score of 0. Comparison relatives had similar characteristics as patient relatives (online supplemental table 4a).

Costs of prevalent brain disorders

In sum, the total attributable costs of brain disorders incurred by closest relatives of patients with any brain disorder were €2407 million in 2021.

Mothers and fathers of children and young people with any brain disorder had total attributable healthcare costs of €14.7 and 25.2 million in 2021, which corresponds to €116 and €262 per person, respectively. The attributable healthcare costs in 2021 were €134.1 million in total and €152 per person for closest relatives of adults, and €34.0 million in total and €90 per person for closest relatives of older adults (table 2). The costliest component of healthcare was consistently hospital care (figure 1).

Incident brain disorder cohort

The proportions of patients with incident brain disorders who did not have a closest family relative were similar to those for patients with prevalent brain disorders (online supplemental table 3b). The incident cohorts of patient relatives each consisted of 79 433 fathers and 63 931 mothers of children and young people (0–24 years), 188 475 relatives (53.4% men) of adults (25–64 years) and 102 748 relatives (50.7% men) of older adults (65+years) with incident brain disorders of any type (online supplemental table 4b). Patients with incident brain disorders were younger and had less comorbidities at index, compared with patients in the prevalent cohort (online supplemental table 5).

Costs of incident brain disorders

In sum, the total attributable costs of brain disorders incurred by closest relatives in the first year after incidence of any brain disorder were €794 million.

By patient age strata, total attributable healthcare costs for relatives amounted to €8.7 million and €11.4 million for fathers and mothers of children and young people, €31.6 million for relatives of adults and €19.5 million for relatives of older adults in the first year (table 3 and figure 2). Per person, attributable healthcare costs in the first year after incidence varied from €111 for fathers of children and young people to €191 for relatives of older adults. Income loss posed the highest costs with €328.2 million for fathers and €137.6 million for mothers of children and young people, €209 million for relatives of adults and €47.6 million for relatives of older adults in the first year after incidence. Per person, income loss varied from €792 for relatives of older adults to €4219 for fathers of children and young people. The proportional income loss was 5.0% for fathers and 3.8% for mothers of children and young people, 2.6% for relatives of adults and 1.1% for relatives of older adults in the first year.

Discussion

In 2021, close relatives of patients with brain disorders included 125 495 fathers and 96 154 mothers of children and young people, 880 661 relatives of adults and 378 826 relatives of older adults. The pooled attributable societal costs of brain disorders incurred by closest family relatives were €2407 million for prevalent disease in 2021 and €794 million for incident disease the first year. The dominating cost component was income loss, which was proportionally highest for fathers of children and young people with brain disorders.

We have used the term attributable costs, which is a widely used term for excess costs in the economic cost-of-illness literature; however, the results should not be interpreted causally but rather as a descriptive measure.

Our study adds to the existing literature by addressing societal costs in relatives across 25 disorders in a comprehensive nationwide cohort.

Previously, Fuglsang et al estimated the patient-incurred attributable costs of brain disorders which were measured in billions rather than millions of euro.3 Our cost estimates from the family-relative perspective add an important component to the overall economic burden of brain disorders in Denmark. At the same time, the costs illustrate both the importance of informal care provided by family relatives and the personal health and income consequences of caregiving for a family member with a brain disorder. The previous study by Fuglsang et al demonstrated a high degree of excess costs for patients with brain disorders.3 Therefore including relatives who themselves had brain disorders would influence our results, depending on the type and severity of brain disorders among relatives. When considering any brain disorder, a fair proportion of patients had no close relative alive and with no previous brain disorder of their own; this was overall less of an issue when considering specific brain disorders.

At the cost of representativeness of patients with brain disorders in general, our inclusion criteria led to relatives of patients with brain disorders being similar to the identified comparators on age, sex and education level. However, there may be some residual confounding that we did not account for in this descriptive study, as, for example, Komulainen et al previously found that parents of children with mental disorders had lower income compared with population comparators, even 5 years before the child was diagnosed.19 This could be due to chronic disease persisting years before diagnosis, or due to an excess risk of mental disorders in lower income families.

We used registry data to identify close family members whom we assumed were most likely to act as caregivers. This approach allowed us to identify all family members of persons with brain disorders, rather than relying on patient-referred caregivers. However, as family members were used as proxies for caregivers, we may have underestimated the societal costs of brain disorders incurred by caregivers, as there may be misclassification in that not all who were defined as closest family members in our study provided informal care. Such care may not have been provided at all or may have been provided by another person in the family or social network, whom we were not able to account for. In addition, we lacked direct measures of caregiving time.

We considered societal costs of the health and income consequences of informal caregiving, whereas other approaches include estimating the value of time spent caregiving, such as a multinational European study which estimated the cost of care overall per dementia patient at €7820 (95% CI €7194 to €8446), of which 54% was the value of informal care and only 16% was direct medical costs.13 As we were not able to consider the value of time spent on informal caregiving and other non-monetised costs, our cost estimates are likely conservative.

An Australian caregiver study found that the longer a person is a caregiver, the greater the consequences it has for paid work and thereby income,29 which is in line with our finding that the income loss was higher when looking at prevalent brain disorders compared with incident brain disorders, for relatives of children and young people and of adults.

Denmark traditionally had a higher degree of institutionalised care, as opposed to family-based informal care playing a larger role in Southern Europe,13 however care is increasingly moving out of hospitals, and a higher degree of family involvement is expected.30 The costs of brain disorders incurred from patient relatives may therefore increase in the future in Denmark.

Due to the high prevalence of brain disorders in the population, it is likely that many families suffer from more than one case of brain disorders. Accumulation of brain disorders in families or couples, as well as the burden of caregiving in ‘sandwich’ generations caring for both offspring and parents with brain disorders at the same time, pose important topics for future research on brain disorders. The societal cost in relatives of individuals with brain disorders in Denmark may only be applicable to countries with similar healthcare and societal systems and should therefore be confirmed in other settings.

Conclusions

We found that relatives of patients with brain disorders incur higher healthcare costs, which could be associated with the overall societal burden of brain disorders in Denmark. Informal care provided by relatives should be considered when discussing caregiving burden and future healthcare prioritisations, particularly if care responsibilities increasingly involve patient relatives.
