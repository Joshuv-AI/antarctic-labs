Dual-temperature H2 sensing with a wide dynamic range enabled by PdO/Pd-ZnO nanorod arrays
Authors: Yingyi Wang, Lin Liu, Chenfei Guo, Kejie Guan, Fuqin Sun, Yifan Li, Ju Bai, Xiaoshuang Gou, Xiaowei Wang, Ting Zhang, Sujie Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen (H2) safety monitoring demands sensors that operate reliably across the entire trace-to-percent-level concentration range, a persistent challenge for conventional metal-oxide systems. Herein, a temperature-gated sensing strategy is demonstrated using PdO/Pd-decorated, metal–organic framework (MOF)-derived ZnO nanorod arrays. Conformal coating of vertically aligned ZnO nanorods with a conductive Zn-catecholate (Zn-CAT) MOF, followed by thermal conversion, yields highly porous ZnO@ZnO homojunctions functionalized with PdO/Pd nanoparticles. This optimized hierarchical architecture enables a single chemiresistive device to operate in two distinct regimes. At 125 °C, it achieves trace H₂ detection (0.02–50 ppm) with a theoretical limit of 49 ppb. At 250 °C, it delivers an ultrahigh response (3632 to 1% H2) while maintaining near-linear behavior up to 2.5%. Crucially, operando near-ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (NAP-XPS) explicitly unravels the underlying mechanism, revealing a transition from low-temperature oxygen-mediated pathways to high-temperature PdHx-assisted dissociative spillover. This continuous detection window, spanning approximately 5 orders of magnitude, bridges the gap between early-warning and catastrophic-release monitoring for full-scenario H2 safety.

Introduction

Driven by global population growth and industrialization, the global demand for energy from both fossil fuel-based and renewable sources continues to rise. Hydrogen (H2), a carbon-free energy carrier with a high gravimetric heat of combustion (142 kJ g-1), is widely regarded as a key component of low-carbon and sustainable energy systems1–3. Due to these attributes, H2 is already extensively utilized in various sectors, including medical facilities, research laboratories, the automotive and aerospace industries, space propulsion, oil refining, semiconductor manufacturing, metallurgy, chemical processing, and nuclear technologies2,4. However, its low minimum ignition energy (0.017 mJ), combined with its colorless and odorless nature, renders H2 highly flammable and potentially explosive when its concentration in air exceeds 4%5. Consequently, hydrogen leakage in production, storage, and utilization infrastructures poses serious safety risks. This underscores the urgent need for H2 sensors that offer high sensitivity and selectivity, fast response and recovery, and reliable continuous operation for effective monitoring and early leak detection.

Chemiresistive H2 sensors are predominantly based on metal-oxide semiconductors (MOS), owing to their low cost, CMOS compatibility, simple readout, and capability for continuous monitoring6–8. It has been demonstrated that a variety of metal-oxide semiconductors, such as SnO29, ZnO10,11, WO312, and other related materials, can exhibit high sensitivity toward H2 detection. In particular, ZnO-based gas sensors have been widely studied owing to their distinctive electrical properties, low cost, excellent stability, and nontoxicity13,14. Nevertheless, the H2 sensing performance of traditional ZnO-based gas sensors remains constrained by insufficient sensitivity and selectivity. To overcome these issues, various strategies have been explored to enhance the performance of ZnO-based gas sensors, including the incorporation of secondary components, engineering crystal facets, optimizing nano- and micro-architectures, and tuning the operating temperature15–17. Among these, functionalization with a secondary phase has proven particularly powerful, as it directly affects both the chemical properties (catalytic activity, defect chemistry, surface acidity/basicity) and physical properties (morphology, specific surface area, particle size) of the sensing layer18–20.

In particular, the derivation of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) onto MOS surfaces to construct homo- or heterojunctions has emerged as an appealing route, because MOFs offer well-defined pore structures, high specific surface areas, and tunable chemical compositions that are highly beneficial for gas adsorption, diffusion, and interfacial charge transfer21–23. Beyond their direct use as sensing materials, MOFs can be transformed into functional derivatives, such as porous metal oxides, carbons, or metal/oxide nanocomposites, while partially inheriting the framework’s morphology and porosity24. For instance, Shah et al. constructed the NiO@CuO NFs with porous sharp tips by using a MOF as a precursor-based H2 gas sensor, which showed 28.3% higher than that of a porous Ni-MOF-based H2 gas sensor. However, the fabricated sensor showed a 300 ppb low detection limitation (LOD) and narrowed H2 detection windows. Similarly, Sharma et al.25 fabricated ZnO decorated with graphitic carbon (GC) via a wet-chemical strategy by the self-assembly of a zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF-8). The ZnO@GC-based H2 sensor exhibited a 124.6% response (ΔR/R0) to 10 ppm of H2 under a humid environment at room temperature. However, existing MOF‑derived chemiresistive sensors are generally tailored to a single analyte and a single working temperature, and their dynamic ranges are usually confined to two to three orders of magnitude in gas concentration. Systematic engineering of MOF-derived MOS architectures to achieve wide H₂ detection windows has been rarely explored.

Although MOF-derived architectures offer a high surface area and interconnected porosity, the aforementioned studies still primarily focus on single-temperature operation and relatively narrow concentration windows, and their performance under realistic hydrogen-storage conditions (from trace leaks to high-concentration releases) remains to be further improved. Constructing multi-scale junction structures has recently been recognized as an effective strategy to improve chemiresistive performance, as coupled homojunctions, heterojunctions, and metal/oxide interfaces can synergistically modulate charge separation, depletion layer width, surface band bending, and catalytic reaction pathways26. For example, Karuppasamy et al.27, developed the GC anchored spherical Pd@PdO core-shells over γ-Fe2O3 microcube (Pd@PdO/γ-Fe2O3@GC) from MOF-derived H2 sensor. The high specific surface area of sensing materials results in good gas-sensing performance (0.1 to 5 ppm) with a relative LOD (100 ppb). Meanwhile, Liu et al.28, reported the bimetallic MOF-derived mesoporous Ru-doped SnO2-based Triethylamine sensor. Under 250 °C, the sensor has a good sensitivity of 125.5 to 100 ppm to triethylamine at 80% RH. However, in such systems, and particularly in MOF‑derived ZnO/Pd architectures, the temperature‑dependent transition from oxygen‑mediated sensing at low H₂ concentration to Pd‑assisted dissociative adsorption and H2 spillover at high concentrations has not been comprehensively explained, which limits the sensors capable of spanning both trace‑ and high‑concentration H2 detection regimes.

Herein, we report a temperature-gated H2 sensing architecture based on PdO/Pd-decorated, MOF-derived ZnO nanorod arrays, specifically designed for full-scenario hydrogen leakage monitoring. By conformally growing an electrically conductive Zn-CAT MOF on vertically aligned ZnO nanorods and subsequently converting it into a porous shell, a defect-rich ZnO@ZnO homojunction is constructed and further sensitized with highly active PdO/Pd nanoparticles. This optimized ZnO@ZnO3/Pd architecture allows a single chemiresistive device to transition between two distinct sensing regimes: a low-temperature (125 °C) mode for trace H2 detection down to 49 ppb, and a high-temperature (250 °C) mode delivering ultrahigh responses (3632 to 1% H2) with near-linear behavior up to 2.5%. Furthermore, operando near-ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (NAP-XPS) is utilized to clarify this dual-temperature mechanism, providing direct evidence for the synergistic interplay between surface oxygen-mediated reactions and Pd-assisted hydrogen spillover. Achieving a continuous dynamic range across around 5 orders of magnitude, this work provides a robust material platform and mechanistic foundation for next-generation, wide-range H2 safety sensors.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemicals and solvents were purchased from commercial sources and used directly without additional purification. Zinc nitrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) (≥99.8%) was purchased from Shanghai Hushi Laboratorial Equipment Co., Ltd; Hexamethylenetetramine (C6H12N4, HMTA) (≥99.8%) and zinc acetate (Zn(CH3COO)2, Zn(OAc)2) (≥99.8%) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich; Ethanol (≥99.8%) and acetone (≥99.8%) were purchased from JingXie High-tech Electronic Materials Co., LTD. The 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexahydroxytriphenylene (HHTP) ligand was obtained from J&K Scientific. All aqueous solutions were prepared with deionized Milli-Q water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C).

Experiments

In-situ growth of ZnO NR arrays

A modified hydrothermal method was employed to achieve the in-situ growth of ZnO NR arrays directly on the sensing region of the device substrate. Prior to growth, Si/SiO₂ substrates pre-patterned with interdigitated electrodes (15 nm Ti/100 nm Au) were sequentially cleaned with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water, followed by O2 plasma treatment (400 W, 3 min) to remove residual surface contaminants and enhance hydrophilicity. A 20 mM Zn(CH3COO)2 ethanol solution was then spin-coated onto the substrates and dried at 150 °C for 15 min; this process was repeated four times to form a uniform ZnO seed layer. The seeded substrates were subsequently annealed at 400 °C for 30 min in air to improve adhesion and crystallinity of the ZnO seeds.

For the hydrothermal growth, the seeded substrates were placed face-down in an aqueous precursor solution containing 25 mM Zn(NO3)2·6H2O and 25 mM HMTA, and maintained at 90 °C for 3 h. After growth, the ZnO NR-coated chips were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and ethanol, and then dried under ambient conditions.

In-situ growth of ZnO@ZnO NR arrays

To fabricate the MOF-derived porous ZnO@ZnO NR arrays, ultrathin Zn-CAT films were conformally deposited onto the ZnO NR surfaces through a layer-by-layer (LbL) liquid-phase epitaxy (LPE) process. In a typical cycle, the ZnO NR arrays were sequentially immersed in Zn(CH3COO)2 (0.1 mM) and HHTP (0.01 mM) precursor solutions for 10 and 20 min during the initial deposition step, followed by alternating immersions of 5 and 10 min in each subsequent LbL cycle. After each cycle, the samples were rinsed thoroughly with ethanol to remove uncoordinated species, ensuring the formation of a uniform and well-defined Zn-CAT network. The resulting Zn-CAT-coated ZnO NR arrays were then dried at ambient conditions. By precisely tuning the number of LbL cycles, Zn-CAT films with controllable thicknesses were obtained. Finally, thermal annealing at 400 °C for 1 h in air converted the Zn-CAT coating into a porous ZnO shell, yielding the hierarchical porous ZnO@ZnO NR arrays.

Synthesis of ZnO@ZnO/Pd

To functionalize the porous ZnO@ZnO NR arrays with Pd nanoparticles, a Pd precursor solution was first prepared by dissolving PdCl2 in ethanol. The ZnO@ZnO NR arrays were subsequently immersed in a 0.75 mM PdCl2 solution for 2 h to allow sufficient precursor adsorption. Following immersion, the samples were exposed to UV irradiation (λ = 365 nm, 3.57 mW) for 30 min to induce the photoreduction of Pd²⁺ into metallic Pd nanoparticles. After irradiation, the Pd-loaded porous ZnO@ZnO NR arrays were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried in air, yielding samples with Pd nanoparticles.

Characterizations

The surface morphology of the ZnO@ZnO/Pd heterostructure was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8230) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, Oxford Aztec X-max 80 SDD EDX detector). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) imaging and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) were conducted on an FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN microscope. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker AXS D8 Advance diffractometer using filtered Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA; λ = 1.54056 Å) over a 2θ range of 3°–55° with a step size of 0.01° in air at room temperature. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a PHI 5000 VersaProbe II spectrometer in the Nano-X Vacuum Interconnected Workstation at the Suzhou Institute of Nano-Tech and Nano-Bionics (SINANO), Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS). Raman spectra were collected at room temperature using a He-Cd laser (excitation at 523 nm) on an Edinburgh FLS980 spectrometer (United Kingdom).

Sensor fabrication and measurement

Gas sensing measurements were performed by mounting the fabricated sensors in a sealed chamber integrated with a hot-cold stage (HCS621G-PM, Instec, USA), which was controlled by an mK200B temperature controller (−170 to 600 °C). The chamber volume was approximately 100 cm3, equipped with a gas inlet connected to the test gas source and an outlet linked to an exhaust collector. Real-time sensor current was recorded using a Keithley 2636B source meter.

A total flow of 500 sccm synthetic air (79% N2 and 21% O2) or target gases with defined concentrations was introduced into the chamber. Desired gas concentrations were obtained by diluting high-concentration certified gas cylinders with synthetic air. After exposure to 500 sccm of the target gas for a period, the flow was switched back to 500 sccm synthetic air until the baseline was recovered. The gas sources (1% H2, 1% CH4, 1% CO, 100 ppm H2S, 100 ppm NH3, 100 ppm NO2, and 100 ppm SO2), all balanced in dry synthetic air, were purchased from Shanghai Wetry Standard Gas Analysis Technology Co., Ltd. The sensing response was defined as Response = (Ia − Ig)/Ig for oxidizing gases and Response = (Ig − Ia)/Ia for reducing gases, where Ig and Ia correspond to the sensor current in the target gas and in the air, respectively.

The crystalline structure of ZnO@Zn-CAT/Pd heterostructure is prepared. PXRD patterns (Figure S3) show diffraction peaks at 2θ = 31.88°, 34.54°, 36.38°, and 47.64°, corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), and (102) planes of wurtzite ZnO (JCPDS 36-1451). A weak low-angle peak at 4.7°, attributed to Zn-CAT (100) plane, is observed prior to pyrolysis, indicating low crystallinity of the Zn-CAT islands. Thus, XPS was employed to further analyze the chemical states of the ZnO@Zn-CAT composites (Figure S4). All the peaks were calibrated by using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV as the reference. ZnO@Zn-CAT samples from the first synthesis processes were investigated first. As shown in the survey spectra (Figure S5a), signals for Zn, C, and O are clearly observed, indicating the high purity of the synthesized ZnO@Zn-CAT composites, which is consistent with the EDS results (Figure S1). The high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Figure S4b) can be deconvoluted into components at 283.25, 284.79, 286.32, 288.34, and 291.08 eV, which are assigned to C=C, C-C, C-O, C=O, and π–π* transitions, respectively. The deconvoluted C=C, C-C, and π-π* satellite components are attributable to the π-conjugated benzene ring originating from the HHTP ligand and appear most prominently in ZnO@Zn-CAT12 samples with the thickest Zn-CAT shells. The O 1s spectra (Figure S4c) consist of three components: lattice oxygen (OL, 530.5 eV), oxygen vacancies (VO, 531.8 eV), and chemisorbed oxygen species (OC, 533.0 eV). With increasing Zn-CAT cycles, the OL peak shifts by ~0.24 eV to higher binding energy, suggesting increased metal-oxygen interactions and surface chemical modification30,31. The Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 peaks at 1021.38 and 1044.48 eV, respectively, confirm the divalent state of Zn (Figure S4d).

The Raman spectra were used to study the chemical bonds in the series of ZnO and ZnO@Zn-CAT heterostructures during material fabrication. A slight decrease of the -OH vibration band at 1372 cm-1 suggests the formation of coordination bonds accompanied by deprotonation between oxygen atoms and metal cations (Figure S5)32,33. This interpretation is corroborated by the emergence of the C = C and C = O vibration feature at ~1584 cm-1, indicative of electron redistribution at the oxygen atoms via in-situ partial oxidation of the ligands to maintain charge equilibrium during synthesis33,34. Notably, the C = C/C = O band intensity increases with the growth cycles, consistent with the increased thickness of the Zn-CAT layer.

After calcination to form the ZnO@ZnO/Pd heterostructure, the diffraction peaks remain consistent with wurtzite ZnO (JCPDS NO. 36–1451) (Fig. 1d), while the Zn-CAT-related peak at 4.7° disappears, confirming complete decomposition of the Zn-CAT. XPS analysis (Fig. 1e, f and Figure S6) verifies the presence of Zn, O, and Pd (Figure S6a). XPS analysis (Fig. 1e, f and Figure S6b) verifies the presence of Zn, O, and Pd (Figure S6a). The O 1s spectra of all samples consist of OL, VO, and OC components. Interestingly, while ZnO@ZnO/Pd samples generally exhibit higher VO contents than pristine ZnO, the optimal ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sample displays the lowest proportion of OC (13.3%) in the ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) environment of the XPS measurement (Fig. 1e). This apparent contradiction is resolved by analyzing the Pd 3d spectra (Fig. 1f), which reveal the coexistence of metallic Pd (Pd0), Pd2+ (Pd-O), and intermediate Pd species, confirming the formation of a mixed Pd/PdO catalytic system. Among all samples, ZnO@ZnO3/Pd possesses the highest proportion of Pd0 (61.4%). The abundant Pd0 species induce a strong chemical sensitization, generating highly active but weakly bound OC species on the surface. These highly reactive species are prone to rapid desorption under UHV testing conditions, leading to a suppressed apparent OC signal. To evaluate the defect chemistry and active surface species, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was conducted. The spectra exhibit a dominant signal at g = 2.005 (Figure S7), characteristic of chemisorbed oxygen radicals (OC, e.g., O2− and O−), which is different from ionized VO which appears at g = 1.99635,36. This indicates that its abundant metallic Pd0 sites efficiently catalyze oxygen dissociation via the spillover effect, accumulating massive amounts of highly reactive OC radicals under ambient conditions. These reactive species extract electrons to widen the depletion layer and, upon H2 exposure, undergo rapid redox reactions to instantly release trapped electrons. This dynamic process fundamentally drives the ultrahigh sensing response observed in the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor.

Dual-temperature sensing regimes for high- and low-concentration H2 detection

H2 sensing behaviors at high concentrations

The sensing performance of the sensors was straightforwardly demonstrated by their response to H2. Working temperature could be an essential factor in optimizing the sensor’s operating condition. As shown in Fig. 2a, the response of all sensors toward 2500 ppm H2 exhibits a clear temperature-dependent behavior in the range of 25–300 °C. The ZnO@ZnO3/Pd heterostructure delivers the highest response at 250 °C, which is identified as the optimal operating temperature for high H2 concentrations in order to balance the reaction kinetics and gas-phase diffusion at moderate temperatures37. As the temperature continues to rise, the desorption process of oxygen molecules becomes dominant, which reduces the response value38. Figure 2b presents the dynamic gas-sensing response characteristics of the ZnO and ZnO@ZnO/Pd-based sensors toward high concentrations of H2 gas ranging from 10 ppm to 2.5% at 250 °C. Notably, the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor exhibits the highest response, reaching 3632.3 toward 1% H2-8.04, 1.69, and 6.15 times higher than ZnO, ZnO@ZnO8/Pd, and ZnO@ZnO12/Pd, respectively. This superior performance correlates with the enhanced Vo concentration in the MOF-derived ZnO layers, as supported by XPS analysis, and the abundant surface Pd0 species, which jointly promote catalytic H2 dissociation and charge transfer. These results highlight the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd heterostructure as the optimal material platform for further studies. The ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor also displays fast kinetics for high-concentration H2, with response and recovery times (defined as the time to reach 90% of the total current change) of 32 s and 21.5 s, respectively, toward 1000 ppm H2 at 250 °C (Fig. 2c), while the other devices exhibit noticeably slower dynamics (Figure S8). The fast response and recovery kinetics of the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor are driven by the synergistic effect of its high metallic Pd0 content in Fig. 1f, which accelerates H2 dissociation and spillover rates (compared with ZnO sensor), and its optimal shell thickness that minimizes gas diffusion resistance. Conversely, thicker MOF-derived shells (e.g., ZnO@ZnO8/Pd and ZnO@ZnO12/Pd) severely prolong recovery times due to the sluggish outward diffusion and delayed desorption of water byproducts trapped within their extended porous networks.

The sensitivity, quantified by the slope of the response-concentration plots (Figure S9), further highlights the advantage of the optimized architecture: ZnO@ZnO3/Pd achieves the highest sensitivity (0.045 ppm−1), markedly surpassing ZnO (0.05 ppm−1), ZnO@ZnO8/Pd (0.22 ppm−1), and ZnO@ZnO12/Pd (0.26 ppm−1). All sensors exhibit excellent linearity, with R2 values of 1 for ZnO and ZnO@ZnO3/Pd, and 0.9997 and 0.9998 for ZnO@ZnO8/Pd and ZnO@ZnO12/Pd, respectively, indicating accurate quantification over a broad ppm-percent-level range. The ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor also demonstrates robust stability. Short-term cycling at 5000 ppm H2 (Fig. 2d and Figure S10) reveals a gradual baseline shift, but a stabilized response of ~2330 after ~150 cycles, confirming its reliable signal output under repeated operation. Long-term aging tests further demonstrate that the response to 1000 ppm H2 remains nearly unchanged over 8 months of continuous evaluation (Fig. 2e), indicating outstanding operational durability for practical H2 monitoring.

H2 sensing behaviors at low concentrations

For early warning of hydrogen leaks in storage and transport scenarios, the sensitive detection of trace H2 is equally critical. We therefore re-optimized the working temperature for low concentrations by monitoring the response to 2 ppm H2 from 25 to 300 °C (Fig. 3a). As shown in Fig. 3a, the response of all sensors toward 2 ppm H2 exhibits a temperature-dependent trend over 25–300 °C, with the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor delivering the maximum response at 125 °C. This temperature was therefore selected as the optimal operating condition for detecting low-concentration H2. Dynamic gas-sensing response characteristics toward 0.02–50 ppm H2 at 125 °C are presented in Fig. 3b. Consistent with the high-concentration measurements, the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor exhibits the highest response, reaching 2.49 at 2 ppm, 3.04, 1.12, and 7.09 times higher than ZnO, ZnO@ZnO8/Pd, and ZnO@ZnO12/Pd, respectively. This enhancement is attributed to the high density of oxygen vacancies and the abundance of surface Pd0 species, which together facilitate efficient adsorption, activation, and reaction of H2. The transient response curves (Figure S12a) show that at 125 °C, the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor achieves response and recovery times of 242.5 s and 239 s, respectively, longer than at 250 °C, reflecting slower surface reaction kinetics and different populations of adsorbed oxygen species at lower temperatures, but still suitable for continuous safety monitoring. To evaluate anti-interference capabilities, the selectivity of the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor was assessed against common interfering gases from H2 storage environments, including CH4, CO, H2S, NH3, and SO2. As shown in Fig. 3e, the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor exhibits a significantly higher response to H2 than to any other analyte, demonstrating strong selectivity. The good selectivity of the sensor toward H2 originates from the specific catalysis of a high proportion of metallic Pd0. While the H-H bond energy is ~436 kJ mol−1, Pd catalytically lowers the activation energy for H2 dissociative adsorption to near-zero, enabling massive spillover effects even at 250 °C40,41. In contrast, the thermal energy provided at 250 °C is entirely insufficient to overcome the high bond dissociation energies of stable interfering molecules, such as the C-H bond in CH4 (~439 kJ mol−1)42 and the N-H bond in NH3 (~391 kJ mol−1)43. Furthermore, gases with lone-pair electrons like CO and H2S tend to strongly coordinate with Pd active sites, causing transient catalyst poisoning rather than undergoing rapid redox reactions at this temperature44,45. At the same time, the humidity influence on ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor has been tested (Figure S13). Although humidity does have some effect on the device, which is an intrinsic property of highly active site materials, its impact on the device is extremely small compared to the sensor’s high response. Furthermore, comparative Ragone plots (Fig. 5f) and literature benchmarking (Table S1) indicate that the response of the ZnO@ZnO3/Pd sensor at high H2 concentrations surpasses most previously reported materials25,46–55. Compared with the recent MOF-derived metal oxide-based H2 sensor, the fabricated sensor shows two orders of magnitude higher at high H2 concentration.

To unravel the origin of the dual-temperature sensing characteristics observed in the Pd-doped ZnO homojunction nanorod array, a comprehensive model integrating surface reactions and energy-band modulation is proposed. Initially, in a vacuum state, the isolated n-type ZnO and p-type PdO possess their intrinsic band structures (Fig. 5b (1)). Upon exposure to ambient air and thermal activation, oxygen molecules adsorb onto the surface of materials. By capturing free electrons from the ZnO conduction band, these molecules convert into various chemisorbed oxygen species (O2− or O−) dictated by the specific operating temperature (Eqs. 2–4). Macroscopically, this catalytic spillover process releases the trapped electrons back into the ZnO conduction band, significantly narrowing the surface depletion region. Simultaneously, the chemical transformation from oxidized Pd species to metallic Pd0 and PdHx modulates the work function of the catalytic overlayer. This electronic sensitization dramatically alters the interfacial band alignment, significantly reducing the p-n heterojunction potential barrier height (Fig. 5b (3)). This dual modulation accelerated surface redox reactions and dynamically lowered interfacial barriers and synergistically dictates the sensor’s exceptional H2 response.

Conclusion

In summary, a temperature-gated sensing strategy has been successfully demonstrated using PdO/Pd-decorated, MOF-derived ZnO nanorod arrays for full-scenario H2 safety monitoring. By utilizing a Zn-CAT MOF as a self-template, a defect-rich ZnO@ZnO homojunction functionalized with PdO/Pd nanoparticles was engineered to create a highly active, multi-scale catalytic interface. This optimized architecture (ZnO@ZnO3/Pd) allows a single chemiresistive device to transition seamlessly between two distinct operating regimes. Specifically, it utilizes a low-temperature (125 °C) window for trace detection (0.02–50 ppm; LOD = 49 ppb) and a mid-temperature (250 °C) window for high-concentration monitoring (response: 3632 to 1% H2, linear up to 2.5%). Together, these modes achieve an exceptionally broad and continuous dynamic range spanning approximately 5 orders of magnitude. Crucially, operando NAP-XPS provides direct evidence for the underlying mechanism, explicitly revealing the thermally driven shift from oxygen-mediated surface reactions to PdHₓ-assisted dissociative spillover. This work not only establishes a robust material platform for comprehensive H2 leakage detection, but also provides fundamental mechanistic insights that can be extended to the rational design of environment-adaptive, multi-functional gas sensors.
